Qualitative Framework for Evaluating Clinical Data Science Systems: Beyond Technical Validation
Authors: Samita M Heslin
Published: 2026

I. Introduction

The proliferation of data science and artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare has led to the rapid deployment of predictive models and clinical decision-support tools [1–3]. Although advanced technical performance metrics are now available, real-world failures of healthcare AI systems of-ten do not arise from algorithmic weaknesses alone [4,5]. Instead, such failures are commonly driven by limitations in how these systems are evaluated and implemented, particularly with respect to system safety, clinical workflow integration, and human-AI interactions [6–8]. Increasing evidence suggests that failures in clinical AI result not only from algorithm design, but also from inadequate evaluation, governance, and implementation processes [4,9].

This communication presents a conceptual framework for evaluating clinical data science systems in healthcare. It draws on established evaluation frameworks in health informatics as well as the author’s direct experience implementing and evaluating clinical data science systems in healthcare settings.

II. CARES Framework: A Qualitative Approach to System Evaluation

The CARES framework (Clinical relevance, Appropriateness of data, Reliability and safety, Engagement and usability, and Sustainability and governance) is a conceptual evaluation framework (Figure 1) that addresses five key limitations in current approaches to evaluating clinical data science systems:

CARES framework with five domains for evaluating clinical data science systems. Arrows indicate that the evaluation domains are iterative and interdependent.

1) C (clinical relevance): Evaluation of clinical data science systems must begin with a formal identification of the clinical problem and decision-making context. These systems should address meaningful clinical needs, integrate into clinical workflows, and align with clinicians’ cognitive processes [8,10].

2) A (appropriateness of data and representativeness): Healthcare data are inherently heterogeneous, incomplete, and shaped by documentation practices within healthcare organizations. Accordingly, evaluations should consider data sources, patterns of missingness, and demographic representativeness to identify potential biases and inequities [11,12]. If clinical data science systems are trained on nonrepresentative data, they may exacerbate existing health disparities [12].

3) R (reliability and safety): Evaluations should determine how clinical data science systems fail. This includes identifying the clinical situations in which a system is likely to fail, establishing pathways for issue escalation, and implementing guardrails that support human monitoring and intervention [6,7]. Scenario-based testing and failure-oriented evaluation may be effective for identifying rare but severe errors [7,9].

4) E (engagement and usability): The performance of clinical data science systems depends on how clinicians interact with and respond to system outputs. Accordingly, evaluations should assess the transparency and interpretability of system outputs, the number of alerts generated, and the degree of automation bias introduced by the system [6,13].

5) S (sustainability and governance): Once deployed, clinical data science systems require continuous monitoring. Governance structures, performance monitoring, and mechanisms for updating or decommissioning models in response to evolving clinical contexts are essential [4,14,15]. To sustain the effectiveness and trustworthiness of these systems, organizational readiness and accountability are critical.

The CARES framework differs from other evaluation models in several important respects. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) focuses primarily on perceived usefulness and ease of use as determinants of technology adoption, but it does not address issues such as data quality, safety-related failure modes, or post-deployment governance [10]. The Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability (NASSS) framework provides a sociological analysis of the non-adoption and abandonment of health technologies; however, it is primarily descriptive rather than structured as an evaluation instrument [8]. The ABCD validation approach offers rigorous statistical guidance for model development and validation and focuses primarily on technical performance, but it does not explicitly address integration into clinical workflows or accountability structures [16]. The Fit between Individuals, Task, and Technology (FITT) framework is used to evaluate the fit among the individual, the task, and the technology in a clinical setting; however, it does not account for data representativeness, safety-related failure modes, or governance structures [17]. FUTURE-AI provides a comprehensive international consensus guideline for trustworthy AI development and deployment. Although it serves as a development and reporting standard, it is not structured as an evaluation tool for assessing a specific system before and after clinical implementation [18]. In contrast, the CARES framework is designed as a practical evaluation tool that incorporates both the technical performance of AI systems and their functioning within real clinical environments, from pre-deployment assessment through post-deployment governance. Recent work has emphasized the limitations of single-dimensional approaches. Frameworks that evaluate AI tools across clinical, organizational, and social domains are increasingly recognized as necessary for sustainable implementation [19,20].

As a practical example of how the CARES framework can be applied, consider an AI prediction model for sepsis integrated into an emergency department workflow. In the clinical relevance (C) domain, the evaluator would confirm that the model addresses a clear, well-defined clinical need, such as early identification of sepsis risk, and that its outputs align with clinician workflows [1,4]. In the appropriateness of data (A) domain, the evaluator would determine whether the model’s training data are representative of the local patient population, including the demographic and socioeconomic diversity of that population. In the reliability and safety (R) domain, the evaluator would assess how the model performs across heterogeneous clinical presentations of sepsis, including among high-risk patient groups [21]. The evaluator would also examine the escalation pathways available when model outputs are erroneous or uncertain [6,7]. In the engagement and usability (E) domain, the evaluator would assess alert fatigue and determine whether the model’s output format, frequency, and integration into emergency department workflows support appropriate clinical action rather than being ignored or overridden [6,22]. Finally, in the sustainability and governance (S) domain, the evaluator would assess whether governance structures are in place to monitor the model over time, including monitoring for model drift as patient populations change, sepsis care protocols evolve, or the broader health system context shifts [4,14]. Taken together, the CARES framework enables evaluators to assess multiple areas of risk associated with the development and implementation of AI models in clinical settings and to identify dimensions of risk that purely technical validation cannot capture.

III. Conclusion

The evaluation of clinical data science systems must extend beyond technical performance metrics. The CARES framework serves as a general model that can guide the implementation of clinical data science systems through structured, multidomain pre-deployment evaluation and post-deployment governance. However, although the CARES framework provides a structured approach to evaluation, successful adoption of AI in clinical practice ultimately depends on engaging clinicians as active partners in the process.
