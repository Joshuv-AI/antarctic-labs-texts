A novel FeTiZn ternary oxide nanocomposite for spontaneous and efficient adsorptive removal of hexavalent chromium from polluted water
Authors: Md Arifuzzaman, Ammar Fayez Al-Shayeb, Ganesh Jethave, Umesh Fegade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the adsorption performance of a FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite for the efficient removal of toxic hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] from aqueous media. The nanocomposite was synthesized via a simple aqueous co-precipitation route and characterized using XRD, FTIR, SEM, TEM, and EDX techniques, confirming its crystalline structure and uniform nanostructured morphology. Batch adsorption studies were conducted to evaluate the effects of pH, contact time, adsorbent dosage, and temperature on Cr(VI) removal. The maximum adsorption capacity (qmax) determined from the Langmuir model was 256.41 mg g⁻¹ at 298 K, indicating a high affinity toward Cr(VI) ions. Kinetic modeling revealed that adsorption followed a pseudo-second-order model (R² = 0.998), suggesting chemisorption as the rate-determining mechanism. Thermodynamic analysis yielded ΔG° = − 4.35 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH° = +24.18 kJ mol⁻¹, confirming that the adsorption process is spontaneous and endothermic in nature. The FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite exhibited excellent stability, maintaining 84.6% of its initial adsorption efficiency after five regeneration cycles. Statistical analyses, including ANOVA and t-tests, validated the reliability of the experimental results and model predictions. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite is a promising and reusable adsorbent for Cr(VI) removal, offering a sustainable approach for water purification and environmental remediation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28367-x.Keywords: Cr(VI) adsorption, Pseudo-second-order, Langmuir, Freundlich models, Hypothesis testing, SustainabilitySubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Contamination of natural water sources by heavy metals is a serious environmental issue today. Hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] stands out among these pollutants due to its high toxicity, strong movement in water, and known cancer-causing effects. Activities like electroplating, pigment manufacturing, leather tanning, and some mining operations can release large amounts of Cr(VI) into the environment. Once it enters the water, it is hard to remove and can lead to long-term health issues, including respiratory problems and a higher risk of cancer1.

Traditional treatment methods, such as chemical precipitation, ion exchange, and membrane filtration, have been used to tackle Cr(VI) contamination. While these methods can work under certain conditions, they often need expensive infrastructure, create secondary waste streams, or have difficulty achieving high removal rates at low contaminant levels2. On the other hand, adsorption has become a more practical method for removing heavy metals. This approach is relatively straightforward, can be scaled for different treatment amounts, and works well across a wide range of conditions3.

In recent years, there has been increased interest in nanomaterials for use as adsorbents, mainly because of their high surface area, adjustable surface chemistry, and improved reactivity4,5. Metal oxides like Fe2O3, TiO2, and ZnO have been studied on their own for their ability to hold heavy metal ions6,7. However, combining these oxides into one nanocomposite may lead to synergistic effects, where the combined properties enhance performance beyond what each material can achieve alone8. Despite this potential, few studies have looked at Fe–Ti–Zn oxide nanocomposites for Cr(VI) removal, and even fewer have paired these tests with a thorough statistical analysis of the adsorption process9,10.

Despite extensive work on single-component metal oxides and some binary composites, limitations remain in achieving both high capacity and mechanistic understanding under practically relevant conditions. Recently, researchers have explored multi-component and engineered metal-oxide systems to combine complementary functionalities (e.g., enhanced redox activity, increased surface hydroxyl density, and improved stability) and thereby overcome these limitations11–14. For example, engineered Fe-based clusters and Fe-rich nanostructures have been reported to give rapid and selective removal of certain pollutants15,16. At the same time, Fe–Ti and other mixed oxide systems have shown improved performance relative to their single-oxide counterparts. In contrast to these prior studies, the present work develops a ternary FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite. It demonstrates (i) superior Cr(VI) uptake at alkaline pH (≈ 8), (ii) combined isotherm/kinetic/thermodynamic and statistical-physics analysis to elucidate the adsorption mechanism, and (iii) regeneration performance and mechanistic illustration that together provide stronger evidence for practical applicability than many existing reports. These points distinguish our study from earlier single-oxide and binary systems and address the current gap in multi-oxide adsorbents that are both high-performing and mechanistically well-characterized17,18.

In this study, we explore the adsorption behavior of a FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite for Cr(VI) in water19. We examine kinetic and equilibrium characteristics using Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms, assess the feasibility from a thermodynamic perspective, and apply various statistical tools such as ANOVA, t-tests, Chi-square analysis, and correlation studies to interpret our findings20,21. By merging a new composite material with thorough statistical validation, this research seeks to enhance our understanding of adsorption mechanisms and present a promising option for effective water treatment technologies.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of fetizn oxide nanocomposite

The FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite was synthesized via a simple aqueous co-precipitation method. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O), titanium dioxide (TiO2, anatase phase), and zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) were used as the metal precursors.

Initially, 0.1 M solutions of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O and Zn(NO3)2·6H2O were prepared separately in 100 mL of deionized water under continuous magnetic stirring. In a separate beaker, 0.5 g of TiO2 was dispersed ultrasonically in 50 mL of deionized water for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. The Fe precursor solution was then added dropwise to the TiO2 suspension under constant stirring at 500 rpm. After 30 min of mixing, the Zn precursor solution was added gradually at the same rate. The mixed suspension was stirred continuously for 3 h at 80 °C under reflux conditions to promote the formation of the mixed oxide network. The resulting brownish precipitate was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min, washed three times each with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted species, and then dried in an oven at 100 °C for 12 h. The dried solid was gently ground to obtain a fine powder and subsequently calcined at 500 °C for 3 h in air to enhance crystallinity and stability.

Characterization of fetizn oxide nanocomposite

The synthesized FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite was characterized using various analytical techniques. FE-SEM was employed to examine the morphology and size distribution of the nanoparticles, crucial for understanding their adsorption capacity and surface area. XRD of the nanocomposite was done to identify the nature and phase composition of the nanocomposite. EDS spectra were used to understand the elemental composition. FTIR analysis identified the functional groups present on the nanocomposite’s surface, shedding light on potential interactions during the adsorption process.

Statistical analysis of experimental data

The concept of the null hypothesis (Ho) is introduced under the assumption of its truth, providing a qualitative and quantitative statistical framework. Simultaneously, an alternative hypothesis (Ha) is formulated, taking into account whether the alternative hypothesis gains acceptance in the event of null hypothesis rejection, or vice versa. Before hypothesis testing, the significance level, or the highest probability of rejecting the null hypothesis, is established in advance22,23. Hypothesis testing, or experimental data analysis significance testing can be divided into two broad classes: (1) parametric tests, including the t-test, Chi-square test etc., and (2) distribution-free tests, including hypotheses tested under no assumptions based on population properties24,25. t-test was applied to compare the means of two sets of experimental conditions (e.g., adsorption at two different pH levels) to determine if differences were statistically significant. ANOVA was used when comparing adsorption efficiencies across more than two experimental conditions (e.g., different temperatures) to identify significant differences among multiple groups. Chi-square test was applied to assess the association between categorical variables (e.g., adsorbent reuse cycle and adsorption efficiency categories).

The z-test functions to contrast the mean of a sample with a hypothesized population mean value. It finds utility in scenarios where the sample size is larger than 30 (n > 30), becoming a crucial tool for determining mean significance25. On the other hand, when dealing with a finite and unknown population variance coupled with a small sample size (n < 30), the t-test is the preferred choice. Particularly when the population variance is undisclosed, the t-test serves as a suitable method for assessing the significance of differences between two sample means25. Addressing cases with limited sample sizes, the paired t-test enters the stage by comparing two related samples, even when their normality is not a strict requirement. The equality of variances between the two populations is not mandatory. The paired t-test is often deployed to analyze data from both experimental treatments and control groups26,27.

Adsorption of Cr(VI) using batch method

Preparation of Cr(VI) solution

An amount of 0.283 g Potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was dissolved in deionized water to obtain a 100 ppm stock solution of Cr(VI). To get the required (10 ppm) concentrations of Cr(VI) for the adsorption studies, the stock solution was further diluted.

Adsorption experiments

Adsorption experiments were conducted on an orbital rotary shaker apparatus. Predetermined amounts of FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite were added in Cr(VI) solutions with varying initial concentrations. The mixtures were agitated using a shaker for a specified duration to achieve equilibrium adsorption. The adsorption process was monitored at controlled temperature and pH conditions.

Measurement of remaining Cr(VI) concentration

The Cr(VI) solutions concentration was determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer before and after the adsorption. A calibration curve was established using standard solutions of known concentrations. The absorption intensity at a specific wavelength was measured and % adsorption was calculated.

XRD analysis

XRD (Bruker D8-advance) analysis was performed to clarify the crystal structure and phase composition of the FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite. The X-ray diffraction pattern of the FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite (Fig. 1e) exhibits multiple well-defined diffraction peaks, indicating a crystalline material composed of mixed oxide phases. All prominent peaks in the pattern were identified and indexed by comparison with standard JCPDS cards. The major reflections in the 2θ ranges 25–38° and 43–63° can be attributed to a combination of Zn-containing spinel/oxide and Ti/Fe oxide phases. Specifically, the sharp peaks near ~ 30–36° are consistent with the (220), (311) and (400) reflections of the ZnFe2O4 spinel phase (JCPDS No. 22-1012)30 and overlap with the characteristic (100), (002) and (101) reflections of hexagonal ZnO (wurtzite, JCPDS No. 36-1451)31. The peak at ~ 25° corresponds to the (101) plane of TiO2-anatase (JCPDS No. 21-1272)32 while weaker reflections near ~ 27–41° may indicate the presence of minor rutile TiO2 or iron oxide (hematite, JCPDS No. 33–0664)33. Peaks observed in the higher 2θ region (≈ 50–63°) further support the coexistence of spinel and oxide phases (e.g., ZnFe2O4 (422), (511), (440)).

The combined XRD results indicate that the synthesis produced a mixed Fe–Ti–Zn oxide system in which spinel formation (ZnFe2O4) and TiO2 coexist a structural scenario that can produce the observed adsorption behavior via increased active sites and synergistic surface chemistry34,35. The obtained peaks were consistent with the reference data from the JCPDS, confirming the crystalline nature of the nanocomposite. The calculated crystallite size using the Scherrer equation was approximately 10 nm36,37. This crystallite size correlates with the FE-SEM results, reinforcing the nanocomposite’s well-defined nanoscale structure.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was utilized to examine the functional groups present in the FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite (Fig. 1f). The FTIR (Bruker, Alpha) spectrum revealed characteristic peaks at specific frequencies. The 538 cm− 1 peak revealed the existence of Fe-O stretching vibrations, while 838 cm− 1 peak revealed Ti-O stretching vibrations. Also, the peak at 1419 cm− 1 was responsible for Zn-O stretching vibrations. The broad absorption band in 3488 cm− 1 revealed the existence of hydroxyl (OH) groups, ascertaining the surface hydroxylation of the nanocomposite38,39. These groups are crucial for adsorption through the creation of binding sites for Cr(VI) ions. The collective results of these characterization techniques contribute to a comprehensive understanding of the FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite’s structure, composition, and potential for effective Cr(VI) adsorption.

Thermodynamic parameters.

The thermodynamic parameters (ΔG°, ΔH°, and ΔS°) provide valuable insights into the nature and feasibility of the Cr(VI) adsorption process on FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite. The negative values of ΔG° at all studied temperatures confirm that the adsorption process is spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable. The magnitude of ΔG° slightly decreases with increasing temperature, suggesting that higher temperatures enhance the adsorption efficiency, likely by facilitating the mobility of Cr(VI) ions and their interaction with active sites.

The positive value of ΔH° indicates that the adsorption process is endothermic, implying that heat absorption promotes the interaction between Cr(VI) ions and the nanocomposite surface. The obtained ΔH° value lies within the typical range for chemisorption (40–800 kJ·mol⁻¹) rather than physisorption (< 40 kJ·mol⁻¹), suggesting that the adsorption mechanism primarily involves chemical bonding or surface complexation between Cr(VI) species and the metal–oxygen functional sites (Fe–O, Ti–O, Zn–O) on the nanocomposite surface.

Furthermore, the positive ΔS° value reflects an increase in randomness at the solid–solution interface during adsorption, which may result from the displacement of water molecules and the redistribution of ions as Cr(VI) is adsorbed. This entropy gain supports the endothermic nature of the process and further confirms the spontaneous uptake of Cr(VI).

Taken together, the thermodynamic results indicate that the adsorption of Cr(VI) onto FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite is a spontaneous, endothermic, and predominantly chemisorptive process, consistent with the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and isotherm analysis.

Statistical physics parameters n, NM, and Qsat

NM represents the number of moles of adsorbate required to form a single uniform layer over the adsorbent surface, while Qsat corresponds to the maximum quantity of adsorbate that can be accommodated, including both monolayer and multilayer adsorption. The adsorption orientation of the Cr(VI) molecules on the FeTiZn oxide surface, whether horizontal or vertical, greatly affects the parameter ‘n’. If the value of ‘n’ was either less than or more than one, it could be explained by the adsorption of Cr(VI) molecules in a horizontal or vertical orientation on the surface of FeTiZn oxide43. Below, we will explain the many options available.

Possibility 1: If n is less than 0.5, it suggests that there is a potential for the interchange of FeTiZn oxide with Cr(VI), leading to the parallel alignment of adsorption.

Possibility 2: When n is within the range of 0.5 to 1, it encompasses all possible orientations of adsorption for Cr(VI) molecules on the surface of FeTiZn oxide, including both parallel and non-parallel orientations.

Possibility 3: When n is greater than or equal to 1, it signifies a total lack of parallel alignment in the adsorption of Cr(VI) molecules on the surface of FeTiZn oxide.

Figure 5a illustrates the relationship between the parameter ‘n’ and the solution temperature, while Table 3 provides a values and summary of model parameters. The values of ‘n’ at temperatures of 298, 308, and 318 K are determined to be 0.234, 0.232, and 0.230, respectively. It is worth noting that all of these numbers are less than 1. Therefore, when Cr(VI) interacts with FeTiZn oxide, it involves horizontal molecular orientation and multi-locking processes44–46. Figure 5b demonstrates the observed change in active sites number (as shown by the ‘Nm’ parameter) for the adsorption temperature in the FeTiZn oxide. Table 3 displays the Nm values at temperatures of 298, 308, and 318 K are 546.72, 315.27, and 272.92 mg/g, respectively. These values represent the adsorbed Cr(VI) amount onto the surface of FeTiZn oxide. A reduction in the value of ‘n’ leads to a corresponding drop in the value of Nm. The decrease in the number of functional locations on the surface of FeTiZn oxide, together with the constant adsorption process, often leads to a fall in the ‘n’ parameter and consequently reduces the Nm parameter. The decline in the Nm values often signifies the deterioration of the newly formed active sites of the adsorbent (FeTiZn oxide) for the adsorption of Cr(VI) molecules47.

Adsorption energies

The ε1 and ε2 correspond to the interactions occurring between Cr(VI) molecules and the surface of the first and subsequent layers, respectively. ε1 is superior to ε2, indicating that ε1 and ε2 are linked to adsorbent-adsorbate and adsorbate-adsorbate interactions (Fig. 5d). The ε2 adsorption energy is seen to increase as the temperature rises, attributed to thermal movements50–52. The adsorption energies at temperatures of 298, 308, and 318 K were determined to be 13.22, 20.62, and 25.28 kJ/mol, respectively. The rising patterns in energy levels were elucidated by the impact of temperature on the surface, which stimulated the atoms and facilitated the detection of adsorbed atoms on the adsorbent’s surface. Experimental evidence shows that the adsorption energy of ε2 rises between 298 and 308 K, but decreases at 318 K. The adsorption energies measured at temperatures of 298, 308, and 318 °K were found to be 4.45, 8.92, and 2.90 kJ/mol, respectively. The drop in adsorption energy at 318 K can be attributed to the increased thermal motion of Cr(VI) molecules on the ε2 layers, which relates to how the dye molecules interact with them53.

Regeneration study

The regeneration efficiency and stability of FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite were evaluated over five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles (Fig. 11). In the first cycle, the fresh FeTiZn oxide achieved a Cr(VI) adsorption efficiency of 97.61% within 90 min. After each cycle, the spent adsorbent was regenerated by gentle washing with a dilute alkaline solution (0.01 M NaOH) to desorb bound Cr(VI) ions, followed by thorough rinsing with deionized water and drying at 60 °C before reuse.

Reusability study of FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite for Cr(VI) adsorption over five successive cycles.

Across the five reuse cycles, adsorption performance gradually decreased, with the efficiency dropping to 84.59% by the fifth cycle. The observed decline can be attributed to partial blockage of active sites, incomplete desorption, and possible structural changes to the surface during repeated washing. Despite this reduction, the FeTiZn oxide retained over 86% of its original capacity after five cycles, demonstrating excellent structural stability and reusability under basic pH (8) conditions.

The desorption efficiency in each cycle remained above 80%, confirming that the regeneration process effectively recovered most of the active sites. These results highlight the feasibility of FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite for multiple-use applications, reducing operational costs and enhancing sustainability in Cr(VI) removal from contaminated water.

Proposed mechanism:

At pH ~ 8 the dominant aqueous Cr(VI) species are oxyanions such as CrO42− (and to a lesser extent HCrO4⁻). Under these alkaline conditions, the surface hydroxyl groups of the FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite are largely deprotonated, generating negatively charged surface sites (≡ M–O⁻) (Fig. 12). Despite the electrostatic repulsion expected between negatively charged surfaces and CrO42−, adsorption is observed; this can be rationalized by specific adsorption (inner-sphere complexation) at exposed metal centers (Fe, Ti, Zn) and by ligand exchange reactions in which CrO42− coordinates directly to surface metal ions, displacing loosely bound surface oxygen or water. Localized Lewis-acid sites (unsaturated metal centers) provide strong binding points that overcome the electrostatic barrier70.

Proposed adsorption mechanism of Cr(VI) on FeTiZn nanocompoites at pH = 8.

In addition, if reduced iron sites (Fe(II)) or surface electron donors are present from the synthesis, partial redox conversion of Cr(VI) → Cr(III) can occur even at alkaline pH, producing surface-bound Cr(III) species or precipitated Cr(OH)3; however, this reduction pathway is likely slower and less extensive at pH 8 than under acidic conditions. Overall, Cr(VI) removal at pH ~ 8 appears to proceed mainly via inner-sphere complex formation at exposed metal sites, with limited contribution from reduction to Cr(III) depending on the availability of reducing sites71.

Conclusion

The FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite demonstrated excellent Cr(VI) removal performance, achieving an adsorption efficiency of 81.23% under optimal conditions (200 ppm initial concentration, 240 mg/L adsorbent dose, pH 8, 60 min contact time). The material showed good stability, retaining 84.59% efficiency after five reuse cycles, indicating strong regeneration potential. Adsorption was well described by both Langmuir and Freundlich models, with a maximum capacity of 256.41 mg/g from Langmuir fitting. Statistical physics analysis identified the double-layer model as the most suitable, suggesting combined monolayer and multilayer adsorption.

The double-layer model is the most suitable statistical physics model for our system. This conclusion was based on three main factors: (i) the double-layer model provided the highest coefficient of determination (R²) and the lowest residual error values compared with other tested models, indicating superior fitting accuracy; (ii) the physical parameters obtained (NM and Qsat) were consistent with experimentally observed adsorption trends and with the Langmuir qmax value, supporting the model’s reliability; and (iii) the model accounts for both monolayer and multilayer adsorption processes, which aligns with our experimental observations of possible multilayer formation at higher Cr(VI) concentrations. By capturing this dual adsorption behavior, the double-layer model offered both the best statistical fit and the most physically meaningful interpretation for our system.

Thermodynamic parameters confirmed the spontaneity of the process, and kinetic studies indicated pseudo-second-order behavior. The high adsorption capacity compared with conventional adsorbents, along with reusability and effectiveness at near-neutral pH, highlights the potential of FeTiZn oxide nanocomposite for practical application in water treatment and environmental remediation.
