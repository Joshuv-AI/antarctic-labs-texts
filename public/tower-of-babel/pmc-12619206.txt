Comparative effectiveness of metformin and alogliptin combination therapy versus metformin monotherapy in patients with type 2 diabetes: an emulated target trial
Authors: Jaehyeong Cho, Yerin Hwang, Selin Woo, Tae Hyeon Kim, Kyeongmin Lee, Yejun Son, Seoyoung Park, Hyunjee Kim, Ju-Young Shin, YongHyun Cho, Dahye Shin, Dosang Cho, Kyung-Jae Lee, Chang Hyun Kim, Sang Youl Rhee, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has emerged as a global health challenge, various treatment strategies, such as the use of metformin and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, have been widely adopted. However, evidence on the effectiveness of their combination therapy remains limited, particularly in studies utilizing target trial emulation approaches within the South Korean population.MethodsThis emulated target trial included 68,372 patients with T2DM identified between 2001 and 2024 from four university hospitals in South Korea. After applying exclusion criteria, patients were classified into two groups: metformin monotherapy and metformin plus alogliptin dual therapy. A 1:2 propensity score matching approach was used to balance baseline covariates between groups. The outcome of this analysis was the achievement of glycemic control, defined as haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) < 6.5% during the follow-up period. Clinical parameters, including glycemic indices, were assessed over 24 weeks from the initiation of therapy using unpaired t-tests. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the likelihood of achieving glycemic control.ResultsAfter applying exclusion criteria and performing 1:2 propensity score matching, 1230 patients were included in the final analysis (371 in the dual therapy group and 662 in the monotherapy group). Over the 24-week follow-up period, the dual therapy group showed greater reductions in HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, with the greatest improvement observed at 8 weeks. No significant differences were found between groups in changes across other clinical parameters. Although the reduction in HbA1c during 24-week follow-up period was greater in the dual therapy group, the difference did not reach statistical significance. However, over the extended follow-up period, patients receiving dual therapy exhibited a higher likelihood of achieving glycemic control (HbA1c < 6.5%) compared to those on monotherapy (aHR, 2.41; 95% CI, 1.64–3.55).ConclusionBy emulating a target trial, this study showed that dual therapy with metformin and alogliptin improved glycemic control in patients with T2DM in South Korea. Given the growing number of available treatment regimens, future research should incorporate a wider range of antidiabetic agents and explore the effectiveness of various therapeutic combinations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic and progressive metabolic disorder characterized by impaired insulin secretion due to β-cell dysfunction [1]. Notably, in 2021, an estimated 529 million people were living with T2DM worldwide, corresponding to a global prevalence of 6.1% [2]. Due to its high prevalence and association with a wide range of life-threatening complications, [3] T2DM has become a major global health challenge, prompting the continuous development and revision of management strategies and clinical guidelines [4].

As a result of these efforts, various pharmacologic agents, including metformin and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, have been developed and are widely used in the management of T2DM [5]. However, despite the established benefits of individual agents and combination therapies [6, 7], their comparative effectiveness has not been thoroughly evaluated in the South Korean population. However, ethnic differences in T2DM pathophysiology and treatment response have been well documented, particularly among East Asian populations who generally exhibit lower insulin secretory capacity and greater sensitivity to β-cell dysfunction compared with Western populations [8]. These characteristics may lead to differential effectiveness of antidiabetic therapies, underscoring the importance of country-specific evidence, such as South Korea, to guide clinical decision-making. Notably, no randomized controlled trials have directly compared these treatment strategies among South Korean patients with T2DM. While several studies have assessed the strength of this combination in broader East Asian populations [9, 10], most were not fully implemented in real-world clinical settings and lacked analyses specific to individual populations. Moreover, real-world observational data are often not structured to support causal inference, highlighting the need for rigorously designed comparative studies using target trial emulation.

Thus, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of metformin monotherapy versus combination therapy with metformin and alogliptin among patients with T2DM in South Korea. We utilized clinical data from four independent tertiary hospitals for this analysis. To address the limitations of observational research and emulate the strengths of randomized controlled trials [11], we emulated target trial using retrospective data. In addition, changes in key clinical parameters relevant to T2DM management and the probability of achieving successful glycemic control were evaluated over a 24-week follow-up period.

Methods

Data source

This study analyzed clinical data from four tertiary hospitals in South Korea: Kyung Hee University Medical Center (KHUMC), Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center (KUDMC), Ewha Womans University Medical Center (EWUMC), and Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital (CNUHH). All datasets were converted to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP-CDM), which standardizes electronic medical records (EMRs) and claims data to a common structure and vocabulary, facilitating multicenter analyses [12]. the OMOP-CDM for four hospitals were consists of 68,372 patients with 31,360 for KHUMC, 12,372 for KUDMC, 17,004 for EWUMC, and 7,636 for CNUHH. Eligible records were collected between January 1, 2001, and December 31, 2024.

Study design and participants

This study was conducted as a retrospective, observational emulation of a target trial to compare the effects of metformin monotherapy versus dual therapy with metformin and alogliptin on glycemic control in patients with T2DM [13]. Patients with T2DM from the OMOP-CDM dataset were identified using the standardized Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine - Clinical Terms (SNOMED) concept ID 201,826 for “type 2 diabetes mellitus,” and all descendant concepts within the SNOMED hierarchy, including relevant subtypes and complications, were included to ensure comprehensive case ascertainment [14].

Of the 68,372 patients with T2DM identified from the four hospitals, 67,142 were excluded during the washout period based on the following criteria: (1) prescription of medications other than metformin or alogliptin; (2) age < 20 or > 65 years; (3) history of severe cardiovascular disease or malignancy; (4) elevated blood pressure (≥ 180/≥110 mmHg); and (5) baseline hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) < 6.9% or > 10.4% before the index date (Fig. 1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Kyung Hee University Medical Center (KHUH-2023-10-077), Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center (DSMC 2024-02-056), Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital (SEUMC 2023-12-002-003), and Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital (CNUHH-2024-012). All procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Boards of Kyung Hee University Medical Center, Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center, Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital, and Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital due to the retrospective design and use of de-identified data, as permitted by the approved protocols.

Exposures and outcomes

This study included patients with T2DM, with the primary exposure defined as treatment with either metformin monotherapy or dual therapy comprising metformin and alogliptin. For the dual therapy group, the index date was defined as the date of the first alogliptin prescription. For the monotherapy group, the index date corresponded to the first metformin prescription among individuals who were never prescribed alogliptin. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 8, 12, and 24 weeks following the index date. Clinical parameters collected during the follow-up period included body weight, body mass index (BMI), HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), uric acid, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine levels. The primary outcome of interest was glycemic control. To capture both early and sustained effects, we evaluated outcomes in two complementary ways. First, short-term changes in HbA1c were analyzed as continuous outcomes by assessing the mean change from baseline at weeks 8, 12, and 24. Second, to assess long-term glycemic control, we conducted a time-to-event analysis in which achievement of HbA1c < 6.5% was defined as the first occurrence of this threshold at any of the scheduled follow-up visits [15, 16], without requiring confirmation at multiple time points [17]. This dual approach allowed us to evaluate both the magnitude of glycemic improvement at specific time intervals and the durability of achieving clinically meaningful glycemic control during follow-up.

Propensity score matching

To ensure balanced covariate distribution between the monotherapy and dual therapy groups, a 1:2 exposure-driven propensity score matching approach was applied. Propensity scores were estimated using age, sex, and baseline HbA1c, and matching was performed using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement, with a caliper of 0.001 standard deviations (SDs) [18]. Matching quality was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values below 0.1 indicating acceptable balance [19]. Although a 1:2 matching ratio was specified, some treated patients could only be matched to a single control due to the strict caliper and balance requirements, resulting in a final matched sample that was slightly below the intended ratio.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate longitudinal changes in clinical parameters and assess differences between the treatment groups, a combination of paired and unpaired statistical tests was applied [20]. Baseline characteristics were compared using unpaired t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables [21], based on data type and distributional assumptions. Within-group changes from baseline at 8, 12, and 24 weeks were analyzed using paired t-tests, while between-group comparisons of change magnitudes were conducted using unpaired t-tests. The proportion of patients achieving glycemic control, defined as HbA1c < 6.5%, was examined using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compared between groups using the log-rank test [22]. To quantify differences in the likelihood of achieving intensive glycemic control, adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [23]. All analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and graphical outputs were generated with R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Participant and public involvement

Study participants were not involved in the development of the research question or study design. Results will be available to all participants or relevant communities upon request.

Results

Following 1:2 propensity score matching, a total of 1,230 patients with T2DM were included in the final analysis, comprising 371 patients in the dual therapy group and 662 in the monotherapy group (Fig. 1). The matched cohort showed satisfactory balance across covariates, with all SMDs below the threshold of 0.1, indicating adequate matching quality. For example, the mean age was 55.58 years (SD, 7.34) in the dual therapy group and 54.85 years (SD, 7.86) in the monotherapy group with SMD of 0.095; the sex showed an SMD of 0.016; and baseline HbA1c levels were 7.73 (SD, 0.69) for dual therapy group and 7.73 (SD, 0.70) in the monotherapy group, respectively (SMD, 0.010) (Table 1).

Figure 2 illustrates the mean changes in HbA1c and FPG over time for both dual therapy and monotherapy groups. Overall, the dual therapy group exhibited greater reductions in HbA1c and FPG compared to the monotherapy group throughout the follow-up period. In the dual therapy group, the most pronounced decrease in both HbA1c and FPG was observed at 8 weeks from baseline, followed by a rebound at 12 weeks. A similar pattern was observed in the monotherapy group, with the greatest reduction at 8 weeks, although subsequent changes were minimal over the remaining follow-up period.

Changes in various clinical parameters did not differ significantly between the dual therapy and monotherapy groups (Table 2). This included HbA1c (dual therapy: − 0.75% [SD, 1.07] vs. monotherapy: − 0.62% [SD, 0.93]; p = 0.489) and FPG (dual therapy: − 32.78 mg/dL [SD, 46.32] vs. monotherapy: − 17.61 mg/dL [SD, 48.82]; p = 0.201). Among the evaluated parameters, a statistically significant difference was found only for LDL cholesterol (dual therapy: − 0.16 µmol/L [SD, 1.48] vs. monotherapy: 0.78 µmol/L [SD, 1.40]; p = 0.023). However, the dual therapy group exhibited more effective glycemic control than the monotherapy group in long-term follow-up period. As illustrated in Fig. 3, patients receiving dual therapy achieved glycemic control, defined as HbA1c < 6.5% during the follow-up period, significantly higher than those receiving monotherapy, with an aHR of 2.41 (95% CI, 1.64–3.55).

Discussion

Key findings

This study conducted an emulated target trial to compare the effectiveness of metformin monotherapy and combination therapy with metformin and alogliptin for glycemic control in patients with T2DM. Four independent tertiary hospital data were utilized in our analysis. After applying exclusion criteria and performing 1:2 propensity score matching, a total of 1,230 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 371 in the dual therapy group and 662 in the monotherapy group. Throughout the follow-up period, the dual therapy group showed greater reductions in HbA1c and FPG, with the most pronounced improvement observed at 8 weeks. Although the early reduction in HbA1c was not statistically significant, the observed divergence over time underscores the importance of sustained combination therapy. Additionally, patients receiving dual therapy achieved glycemic control significantly higher than those in the monotherapy group. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in changes across other clinical parameters. Subgroup analyses showed consistent benefits, remaining significant in older patients (≥ 50 years) and those with preserved renal function.

Comparison with other studies

As one of the most prevalent chronic conditions with potentially life-threatening complications [2], T2DM has led to the development of a wide range of therapeutic strategies. Among these, metformin and alogliptin are widely prescribed agents, and numerous studies have investigated their individual effects as well as the efficacy of their combined use. In line with the findings of our study, prior research has also reported enhanced glycemic control with the combination of metformin and alogliptin compared to monotherapy [6, 24, 25]. However, most of these studies have primarily focused on broader Asian populations [24] or specific countries such as Japan [6], leaving a gap in evidence specific to South Korean patients. Given that metformin and DPP-4 inhibitors, including alogliptin, are among the most commonly prescribed medications for T2DM in South Korea [26], our study focused on the South Korean population to evaluate the effectiveness of dual therapy of metformin and alogliptin. Furthermore, in the absence of randomized controlled trials directly comparing dual therapy with monotherapy, we emulated a target trial using observational data to assess their comparative effectiveness.

Plausible mechanisms

The enhanced glycemic control observed with metformin and alogliptin dual therapy may be attributed to their complementary mechanisms of action. The primary mechanism of metformin is the inhibition of hepatic gluconeogenesis [27], while recent studies have highlighted its role in activating AMP-activated protein kinase via lysosomal pathways [28]. In contrast, alogliptin exerts its effects by prolonging the activity of incretin hormones such as GLP-1 and GIP [29], which enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppress glucagon release [30]. The simultaneous targeting of both hepatic glucose production and incretin pathways may produce a synergistic effect on glycemic control.

Additionally, DPP-4 inhibitors like alogliptin are known to have a low risk of hypoglycemia and are generally weight-neutral, which complements metformin’s favorable metabolic profile [31]. The combination may also help mitigate potential compensatory mechanisms, such as increased glucagon secretion or insulin resistance, that can occur during monotherapy [28]. By addressing multiple pathophysiological pathways involved in T2DM, dual therapy may offer more durable and effective glycemic regulation than either agent alone.

Although the dual therapy group showed a greater reduction in HbA1c at 8 weeks, the between-group difference was not statistically significant at 24 weeks. This attenuation over time may be explained by improvements in glycemic control in both groups through ongoing clinical care and lifestyle modification, as well as regression to the mean and variability in treatment adherence [32]. These factors likely contributed to the narrowing of differences between the two treatment strategies during longer follow-up [33].

Clinical implications

Given that metformin and alogliptin are among the most commonly prescribed medications for patients with T2DM in South Korea [26], this study provides real-world evidence on their effectiveness in achieving glycemic control. This dual approach enables improved glycemic control with a low risk of hypoglycemia, making it particularly suitable for patients who require intensification of therapy beyond metformin monotherapy. Given the rising prevalence of T2DM and the frequent early failure of monotherapy among South Korea populations, [34]the use of metformin, alogliptin combination therapy may serve as an effective and safe option for optimizing glucose control in routine clinical practice.

Limitations

Although this study provided effectiveness of metformin and alogliptin dual therapy compared to metformin monotherapy, this study has several limitations. First, this study conducted emulated target trial to overcome the limitations from the observational study, this study cannot fully eliminate all inherent biases of observational designs. To mitigate these, standardized SNOMED concept IDs were employed for cohort definition, and strict exclusion criteria were applied during the washout period, thereby emulating a randomized controlled trial setting. Second, the number of eligible patients was limited due to the wide variety of diabetes treatment regimens beyond metformin and alogliptin. Third, the generalizability of the findings may be limited, as the study population was restricted to individuals from South Korea. Nevertheless, the use of a multicenter cohort drawn from major tertiary hospitals and the application of a standardized OMOP-CDM contribute to the methodological rigor and enhance the relevance of the results to similar healthcare systems. Fourth, this study did not account for the severity of T2DM at baseline. Although the included patients generally had suboptimal glycemic control, incorporating diabetes severity into future analyses may yield more nuanced and informative results. Fifth, unlike randomized controlled trials, dual therapy in real-world settings is often initiated in patients with poor glycemic control, which may introduce baseline treatment selection bias. To address this, baseline HbA1c levels were included in the matching process to enhance comparability between groups. Lastly, as metformin’s glycemic effect is known to be dose-dependent, unmeasured variability in dosing between the monotherapy and dual therapy groups in our dataset, could have influenced the observed outcomes.

Conclusion

Using data from four independent hospitals in South Korea, this study emulated a target trial to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of metformin monotherapy versus dual therapy with metformin and alogliptin in patients with T2DM. The findings indicated that the dual therapy resulted in greater reductions in HbA1c and FPG levels, suggesting superior glycemic control. In light of the increasing burden of T2DM and the common early limitations of monotherapy in the South Korean population, the combination of metformin and alogliptin may represent a clinically valuable approach for enhancing glycemic management in everyday practice.
