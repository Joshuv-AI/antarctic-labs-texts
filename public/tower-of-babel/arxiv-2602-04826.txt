Abstract
. We define a distance analogous to the Gromov–Hausdorff distance that enables the comparison of arbitrary quasi-isometric spaces. We also investigate properties preserved under limits with respect to this distance, as well as properties of the entire class of metric spaces equipped with this distance. For this purpose, we introduce the notion of quasi-isometric distortion for correspondences. Using this notion, we prove that the class of all metric spaces is path-connected; in fact, any two metric spaces can be connected by a curve of finite length.



1.
Introduction

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance defines a metric on the set of equivalence classes of compact metric spaces, providing a way to measure the resemblance between metric spaces.

Recent research has extensively developed the Gromov–Hausdorff framework, including computations of the distance for specific metric spaces and estimates in various general settings
[
16
,
1
,
19
,
20
]
, the study of the geometry of the space of metric spaces endowed with this distance
[
5
,
3
]
, as well as investigations of limit-preserving properties and compactifications
[
17
,
12
]
. In addition, numerous modifications of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance have been proposed, often in situations where metric spaces carry additional structure
[
14
,
15
,
21
,
4
]
.
Connections between quasi‑isometric invariants of groups and their algebraic properties, such as those studied via derivations in group algebras
[
2
]
, also motivate further investigation.

While for non-compact metric spaces, the Gromov-Hausdorff distances are often infinite, the convergence of pointed metric spaces doesn’t preservs the coarse structure. So in this paper, we introduce a distance based on the notion of quasi-isometry. By “distance” we mean a symmetric function that does not necessarily satisfy the triangle inequality. The constructed distance allows to compare arbitrary quasi-isometric spaces. Convergence with respect to this distance will be called
quasi-isometric convergence
.

We show that Gromov–Hausdorff convergence implies quasi-isometric convergence, and that quasi-isometric convergence implies pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, i.e.,

GH\quad\Rightarrow\quad QI\quad\Rightarrow\quad PGH.

Precise formulations are provided in Corollary
3.6
and Proposition
3.7
. Consequently, we show that several important properties are preserved under limits with respect to the quasi‑isometric distance. These include separability, properness, the intrinsic metric property, and several others; see Corollary
3.8
.

1.1.
Main results

Our first main result concerns the behavior of metric properties under quasi-isometric convergence. We show that several natural properties are preserved under limits in the quasi‑isometric distance. More precisely, if

X_{k}\xrightarrow[\rm q.i.]{}Y
,
then several metric properties of the spaces
X_{k}
are inherited by the limit space
Y
. These include total boundedness, boundedness of the diameter (with convergence of diameters), separability, properness (under the assumption that
Y
is complete), the intrinsic metric property, geodesicity and properness (again under completeness of
Y
), as well as
\delta
-hyperbolicity and the
\mathrm{CAT}^{\kappa}
condition; see Corollary
3.8
.

Our second main result shows that the quasi-isometric distance is topologically equivalent to an actual metric. We construct a metric
D
(Proposition
5.3
) on the class of metric spaces and prove that it induces the same topology and the same coarse structure as the quasi-isometric distance
\hat{d}
. In particular, we prove the following mutual estimates (Proposition
5.4
):

\hat{d}(X,Y)=r\;\Rightarrow\;D(X,Y)\leq\ln(1+2r),\qquad D(X,Y)=r\;\Rightarrow\;\hat{d}(X,Y)\leq e^{2r}-e^{r}.

Finally, we obtain structural results for the space of all metric spaces endowed with the metric
D
. In particular, we prove the following theorem concerning continuous deformations.

Theorem
5.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be metric spaces, and let
R
be a correspondence between
X
and
Y
with
\operatorname{q-dis}R\leq r.
Then the map
t\mapsto R_{t}
is a continuous path in
(\mathfrak{M},D)
connecting
X
and
Y,
and its length does not exceed
e^{2r}-e^{r}.

A direct corollary is that the space (
\mathfrak{M},D
) is linearly connected. Furthermore, the specific construction provided by the theorem shows that its coarse structure is monogenic.

Structure of the paper

In Section

2.
Gromov–Hausdorff distance and basic definitions

We first recall the classical definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, list some of its properties and equivalent formulations, and then proceed to the definition of the quasi-isometric distance.

The Hausdorff distance measures how close two subsets of a given metric space are.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of a metric space
X
. The
Hausdorff distance
between
A
and
B
is defined by

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\{r>0\mid A\subseteq U_{r}(B),\,B\subseteq U_{r}(A)\},

U_{r}(A)=\{x\in X\mid\exists a\in A:d(x,a)<r\}.

This notion allows one to define a (possibly infinite) distance between arbitrary metric spaces. The following distance was first introduced by Edwards in 1975 and later independently by Gromov in 1981. A detailed account of its history may be found in
[
26
]
. We follow the notation of
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
Gromov–Hausdorff distance
between nonempty metric spaces
X
and
Y
is defined as

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf\left\{d_{H}\left(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\right)\mid X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\subset Z,X^{\prime}\approx X,Y^{\prime}\approx Y\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all metric spaces
Z
and isometric embeddings of
X
and
Y
into
Z
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be arbitrary metric spaces. Then

1.

d_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(Y,X);

2.

d_{GH}(X,Z)\leq d_{GH}(X,Y)+d_{GH}(Y,Z).

Note that reflexivity does not hold, even after passing to the quotient by the relation of isometry.

Example 1
.

Let
\overline{X}
be the completion of a metric space
X
. Then
d_{GH}(X,\overline{X})=0.

However, on the set of equivalence classes of
compact
metric spaces modulo isometry, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance indeed defines a metric
[
7
, Theorem 7.3.30]
.

2.1.
Reformulations

We now present several equivalent ways to compute or estimate the Gromov–Hausdorff distance. For the first of them, we need the notion of a correspondence. Almost all of the definitions below are taken from Sections 7.4 and 8.1 of
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two sets. A
correspondence
between
X
and
Y
is a subset
R\subseteq X\times Y
satisfying the following condition: for every point
x\in X
there exists at least one point
y\in Y
such that
(x,y)\in R
, and similarly, for every point
y\in Y
there exists a point
x\in X
such that
(x,y)\in R
.

A correspondence can be viewed as a surjective multivalued map from
X
to
Y
, associating to each

the set
\{y\mid(x,y)\in R\}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
R
be a correspondence between metric spaces
X
and
Y
. Its
distortion

\operatorname{dis}R
is defined by

\operatorname{dis}R=\sup\left\{\left|d_{X}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)-d_{Y}\left(y,y^{\prime}\right)\right|\mid(x,y),\left(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\right)\in R\right\},

where
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
are the metrics on
X
and
Y
, respectively.

For a map
f\colon X\to Y
the
distortion
is defined by

\operatorname{dis}f=\sup\left\{\left|d_{X}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)-d_{Y}\left(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\right)\right|\mid x,x^{\prime}\in X\right\}.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
7
]
, Theorem 7.3.25)
.

For any metric spaces
X
and
Y
one has

(2.1)

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{R}(\operatorname{dis}R),

where the infimum is taken over all correspondences
R
between
X
and
Y
.

Example 2
.

A pair of maps
\varphi\colon X\to Y
and
\psi\colon Y\to X
determines a correspondence

R_{\varphi\psi}=R_{\varphi}\cup R_{\psi}
, where
R_{\varphi},R_{\psi}\subseteq X\times Y
are the graphs of the maps
\varphi
and
\psi
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The following equalities hold:

(2.2)

\operatorname{dis}R_{\varphi,\psi}=\max\left\{\operatorname{dis}R_{\varphi},\operatorname{dis}R_{\psi},C_{\varphi,\psi}\right\},\text{ where }C_{\varphi,\psi}=\sup_{x\in X,y\in Y}\left|d_{X}(x,\psi(y))-d_{Y}(\varphi(x),y)\right|;

(2.3)

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\varphi,\psi}\max\left\{\operatorname{dis}R_{\varphi},\operatorname{dis}R_{\psi},C_{\varphi,\psi}\right\}.

This proposition was formulated in
[
18
,
23
]
; we omit the straightforward proof.

2.2.
Properties inherited by limits

We will write

if

.

Let

(2)

(3)

(4)

(5)

(6)

(7)

\delta

2.3.
The non-compact case

We now pass to the definition of Gromov–Hausdorff convergence for pointed spaces.

Definition 2.11
.

A
pointed metric space
is a pair
(X,p)
consisting of a metric space
X
together with a fixed base point
p\in X
.

Definition 2.12
.

A sequence
\left\{\left(X_{n},p_{n}\right)\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of pointed metric spaces
converges in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense
to a pointed metric space
(X,p)
if the following conditions hold: for every
r>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a natural number
n_{0}
such that for each
n>n_{0}
, there exists a (not necessarily continuous) map
f:U_{r}\left(p_{n}\right)\rightarrow X
such that:

(1)

f\left(p_{n}\right)=p
;

(2)

\operatorname{dis}f<\varepsilon
;

(3)

the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the set
f\left(U_{r}\left(p_{n}\right)\right)
contains the ball
U_{r-\varepsilon}(p)
.

We shall use the notation
\left(X_{n},p_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[GH]{}(X,p)
for this convergence.

Let
\mathbb{S}_{r}^{n}
denote the
n
–sphere of radius
r
. The following example of convergence of pointed spaces is typical.

Example 3
.

Let
\{r_{k}\}
be a sequence tending to infinity. Then

\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}_{r_{k}},p_{k}\right)\xrightarrow[GH]{}(\mathbb{R}^{n},p),

where
p_{k}
and
p
are arbitrarily chosen points on the spheres and on the plane, respectively.

Note that if in item 3 of Definition
2.12
one replaces
U_{r-\varepsilon}
by
U_{r}
, one obtains a different, non-equivalent definition. The difference appears for spaces with non-intrinsic metrics. For instance, consider

X=\{1\}\cup\{2^{m}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\},\quad p=1,\qquad X_{k}=\{1\}\cup\{2^{m}+\tfrac{1}{k}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\},\quad p_{k}=1

we have
\left(X_{k},p_{k}\right)\xrightarrow[GH]{}(X,p)
, but without subtracting the constant
\varepsilon
convergence fails.

Proposition 2.13
.

Properties 3–8 of Theorem
2.10
remain valid under pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

Proof.

To prove separability of
Y
, it suffices to use the fact that each ball is separable by Theorem
2.10
. In the remaining cases, it is enough to work inside a sufficiently large ball of finite radius. On finite-radius balls we have ordinary Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, so the properties pass to the limit by Theorem
2.10
.
∎

If one additionally assumes that
X_{k}
and
Y
have intrinsic metrics, then the convergence of diameters
\operatorname{diam}X_{k}\to\operatorname{diam}Y
also holds. A proof can be found in
[
13
]
. However, that paper uses a definition of Gromov–Hausdorff convergence which is not equivalent to ours; but if one restricts to spaces with intrinsic metrics, the definitions become equivalent. Boundedness, of course, is not preserved under taking limits; see Example
3
.

Pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence induces a topology on the class of all pointed spaces. It was shown in
[
14
,
22
]
that this topology is metrizable on the class of proper metric spaces.

3.
Quasi-isometric distance

There are several equivalent definitions of quasi-isometry. We will use a definition of quasi-isometry close to that in
[
6
]
, see p. 138.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces, and let
A\geq 1,B\geq 0,C\geq 0
be some numbers.

(1)

A map
f\colon X\to Y
is called an
(A,B)
-quasi-isometric embedding
if for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
:

(3.1)

\frac{1}{A}d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})-B\leq d_{Y}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))\leq Ad_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+B.

(2)

Maps
f,g\colon X\to Y
are
C
-close
if

\sup_{x\in X}d_{Y}(f(x),g(x))<C.

(3)

The spaces
X
and
Y
are
(A,B,C)
-quasi-isometric
if there exist
(A,B)
-quasi-isometric embeddings
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
such that
g\circ f
is
C
-close to
\mathrm{id}_{X}
and
f\circ g
is
C
-close to
\mathrm{id}_{Y}
.

Spaces
X
and
Y
are called
quasi‑isometric
if there exist
A,B,C
such that they are
(A,B,C)
-quasi‑isometric.
Using the definition of
(A,B,C)
-quasi‑isometry, it is easy to define
quasi‑isometric convergence
of spaces.
We write
X_{k}\xrightarrow[\mathrm{q.i.}]{}Y
if, starting from some index
N,
the spaces
X_{k}
and
Y
are
(A_{k},B_{k},C_{k})
-quasi‑isometric with
A_{k}\to 1,

B_{k}\to 0,

C_{k}\to 0.

In what follows, by a
distance
we mean a symmetric function
d\colon X\times X\to[0,+\infty].

Distances that satisfy all the usual axioms of a metric space and take only finite values will be called
metrics
.
When a metric may take infinite values, we use the term
generalized metric
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
quasi-isometric distance

\hat{d}
between metric spaces
X
and
Y
is

\hat{d}(X,Y)=\inf\{r>0\mid X\text{ is $(1+r,r,r)$-quasi-isometric to }Y\}.

Obviously
\hat{d}(X_{k},Y)\to 0
is equivalent to
X_{k}\xrightarrow[\mathrm{q.i.}]{}Y.

The next proposition describes how quasi‑isometry constants behave under composition.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
X
is
(A,B,C)
-quasi‑isometric to
Y,
and
Y
is
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})
-quasi‑isometric to
Z.
Then
X
is
(AA^{\prime},AB^{\prime}+A^{\prime}B,AC^{\prime}+A^{\prime}C+B+B^{\prime})
-quasi‑isometric to
Z.

Proof.

We check by direct computation.
Let
f\colon X\to Y,

f^{\prime}\colon Y\to Z,
then

\displaystyle d_{Z}(f^{\prime}fx_{1},f^{\prime}fx_{2})\leq

\displaystyle A^{\prime}d_{Y}(fx_{1},fx_{2})+B^{\prime}\leq A^{\prime}Ad_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+A^{\prime}B+B^{\prime}\leq A^{\prime}Ad_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+A^{\prime}B+AB^{\prime};

\displaystyle d_{Z}(f^{\prime}fx_{1},f^{\prime}fx_{2})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A^{\prime}}d_{Y}(fx_{1},fx_{2})-B^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{A^{\prime}A}d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})-\frac{B}{A^{\prime}}-B^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{A^{\prime}A}d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})-BA^{\prime}-AB^{\prime};

\displaystyle d_{X}(gg^{\prime}f^{\prime}fx,x)\leq

\displaystyle d_{X}(gg^{\prime}f^{\prime}fx,gfx)+d_{X}(gfx,x)\leq AC^{\prime}+B+C\leq AC^{\prime}+A^{\prime}C+B+B^{\prime}.

Similar inequalities hold for
g,g^{\prime},
hence
X
is
(A^{\prime}A,A^{\prime}B+AB^{\prime},AC^{\prime}+A^{\prime}C+B+B^{\prime})
-quasi‑isometric to
Z.

∎

Corollary 3.4
.

The distance
\hat{d}
satisfies:
\forall X,Y,Z

(1)

\hat{d}(X,X)=0;

(2)

\hat{d}(X,Y)=\hat{d}(Y,X);

(3)

if
\hat{d}(X,Y)=r,

\hat{d}(Y,Z)=r^{\prime}
then
\hat{d}(X,Z)\leq 2(r+r^{\prime}+rr^{\prime}).

One can verify that the constructed distance is not a generalized metric, as it fails to satisfy the triangle inequality.
To see this, it suffices to consider spaces consisting of two points with different distances between them.
We say that metric spaces
X
and
Y
are equivalent if
\hat{d}(X,Y)=0.

Later, we shall show that using
\hat{d}
, one can define a topology and a coarse structure on any collection of equivalence classes of metric spaces that constitutes a set, and also that there exists a generalized metric
D
that induces the same topology and coarse structure as
\hat{d}.

If metric spaces
X
and
Y
are quasi‑isometric, then
\hat{d}(X,Y)<\infty.
Thus, we obtain a finite distance on the class of quasi‑isometric
spaces. However, unlike pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, convergence with respect to our distance still tracks the coarse structure of the space, in the sense that
d(X_{k},X)\to 0
implies that from some index onward, the spaces
X_{k}
and
Y
are coarsely equivalent.
It suffices to choose an index beyond which all values
d(X_{k},X)
are finite.

It is well known that the existence of a quasi‑isometric embedding with dense image implies quasi‑isometry of the spaces.
However, we are interested in concrete estimates on the constants; therefore, we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be an
(A,B)
-quasi‑isometric embedding whose image is
R
-dense.
Then the spaces
X
and
Y
are
(A,B+2R,AR+B)
-quasi‑isometric.

Proof.

Define
g\colon Y\to X.
For each point
y\in Y

d_{Y}(y,f(x))<R.
Set
g(y)=x.
For the map
g

(3.2)

\displaystyle d_{X}(g(y_{1}),g(y_{2}))\leq Ad_{Y}(f(g(y_{1})),f(g(y_{2})))+B\leq Ad_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})+B+2R;

(3.3)

\displaystyle d_{X}(g(y_{1}),g(y_{2}))\geq\frac{1}{A}d_{Y}(f(g(y_{1})),f(g(y_{2})))-B\leq\frac{1}{A}d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})-B-\frac{2R}{A}.

Hence

For the compositions, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle d_{Y}(fg(y_{1}),y_{1})\leq R\quad\text{ (by definition of $g$);}

(3.5)

\displaystyle d_{X}(gf(x_{1}),x_{1})\leq Ad_{Y}(fgf(x_{1}),f(x_{1}))+B\leq AR+B,

which completes the proof.

Let

, on the ball of radius

\operatorname{dis}\left.{f_{k}}\right|_{B_{r}(p_{k})}\to 0.
Condition 3 holds because
f_{k}
is
2R_{k}
-dense and
R_{k}\to 0.

∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
X_{k}\xrightarrow[\rm q.i.]{}Y
.
If each space
X_{k}
possesses one of the following properties, then the limit space
Y
also possesses that property (provided
Y
is complete, if indicated):

(1)

total boundedness;

(2)

boundedness of diameter; moreover,
\operatorname{diam}(X_{k})\to\operatorname{diam}(Y)
;

(3)

separability;

(4)

properness of the space, if
Y
is complete;

(5)

the property of being a length space, if

is complete;

(6)

properness and geodesicity of the space, if

is complete;

(7)

(8)

the

That is, the metric

satisfies conditions 2–9 of Theorem

.

Proof.

Properties 3–8 hold because they hold for pointed convergence.
As noted earlier, under quasi‑isometric convergence, the coarse structure is preserved.
Therefore, if

then from some index onward

If

then from some moment the diameters

Hence, items 1 and 2 remain valid. ∎

Thus, convergence with respect to

is a weaker convergence than Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, but it still preserves many important properties, i.e., it generalizes the Gromov–Hausdorff distance.

4.
Topology and coarse structure

We now proceed to describe the topology and coarse structure induced by our distance.
We work with the set
\mathfrak{M}
of equivalence classes of separable metric spaces under the relation of being at zero quasi‑isometric distance.

4.1.
Coarse structure

First, we recall the definition of a coarse structure.

Definition 4.1
.

A
coarse structure
on a set
X
is a family of subsets
\mathcal{E}
of the Cartesian product
X\times X
satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

The diagonal
\Delta_{X}=\{(x,x)\mid x\in X\}
belongs to
\mathcal{E}.

(2)

Symmetry:

If
E\in\mathcal{E}
, then
E^{t}=\{(y,x)\mid(x,y)\in E\}
also belongs to
\mathcal{E}
.

(3)

Closure under finite unions:

If
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{n}\in\mathcal{E}
, then
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\in\mathcal{E}
.

(4)

Closure under taking subsets:

If
E\in\mathcal{E}
and
F\subseteq E
, then
F\in\mathcal{E}
.

(5)

Closure under composition:

For any
E,F\in\mathcal{E}
we have
E\circ F\in\mathcal{E}
, where

E\circ F=\{(x,z)\mid\exists y\in X,(x,y)\in E\text{ and }(y,z)\in F\}.

We provide simple examples of coarse structures.

Example 6
.

The
maximal coarse structure
consists of all subsets of
X\times X.

Example 7
.

The
minimal coarse structure
consists of all subsets of
\Delta_{X}.

Example 8
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
The system
\mathcal{E}_{d}
consisting of sets
E\subseteq X\times X
such that

(4.1)

\exists r>0:E\subseteq E_{r},\quad\textnormal{where}\quad E_{r}=\{(x,x^{\prime})\mid d(x,x^{\prime})\leq r\}.

is a coarse structure. It is called the
bounded metric coarse structure
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a set of equivalence classes of metric spaces.
Analogous to the metric coarse structure, we define the family
\mathcal{E}_{\hat{d}}
.
That is, a set
E\in\mathcal{E}_{\hat{d}}
if and only if it satisfies condition (
4.1
) for
\hat{d}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The family
\mathcal{E}_{\hat{d}}
defines a coarse structure on
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Conditions 1–4 are trivially satisfied. By Corollary
3.4

E_{r_{1}}\circ E_{r_{2}}\subseteq E_{2(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{1}r_{2})},

hence property 5 also holds.
∎

We now make a few remarks about the metrizability of coarse structures.

Definition 4.3
.

A coarse structure
\mathcal{E}
on a set

is called

if there exists a generalized metric

such that
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{d}.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq 2^{X\times X}.
The coarse structure
generated
by
\mathcal{S}
is the coarse structure obtained as the intersection of all coarse structures containing
\mathcal{S}.

Theorem 4.5

(Theorem 2.55.

[
24
]
)
.

A coarse structure is metrizable if and only if it is generated by some countable family of sets
\mathcal{S}.

Example 9
.

The coarse structure
\mathcal{E}_{\hat{d}}
on
\mathfrak{M}
is countably generated by the sets

E_{n}=\{(X,Y)\mid\hat{d}(X,Y)<n\},

therefore, there exists a generalized metric that induces this coarse structure.

It is easy to give an explicit formula for a generalized metric that induces the same coarse structure as
\hat{d}.

Proposition 4.6
.

The distance
\rho=2+\ln(\hat{d}+1)
for
\hat{d}\neq 0,
and zero otherwise, is a generalized metric and induces the same coarse structure as
\hat{d}.

Proof.

Let
\hat{d}(X,Y)=r,

\hat{d}(Y,Z)=r^{\prime}.
According to Corollary
3.4

\rho(Y,Z)\leq\ln(2(r+r^{\prime}+rr^{\prime})+1)+2\leq\ln(r+1)(r^{\prime}+1)+4=\rho(X,Y)+\rho(Y,Z).

Thus

∎

However, the constructed metric induces the discrete topology; thus, by passing to such a metric, we lose information about the convergence of spaces.

4.2.
Topology

We turn to the description of the topology on the set
\mathfrak{M}.

Definition 4.7

(Topology
\mathcal{T}_{\hat{d}}
)
.

A set
\mathfrak{U}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
is called
open
if for each
X\in\mathfrak{U}
and every sequence

such that
X_{k}\in\mathfrak{U}
for
K\geq n_{0}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

A base for this topology is formed by the sets

\displaystyle B_{r}(X)=\{Y\mid\hat{d}(X,Y)<r\}.

Proof.

Obviously for each
r>0
and
X\in\mathfrak{M}
, the set
B_{r}(X)
lies in
\mathcal{T}_{\hat{d}}.
Let
\mathfrak{U}
be an open subset of
\mathfrak{M},

X\in\mathfrak{U}.
Then there exists
r>0
such that
B_{r}(X)\subseteq\mathfrak{U}.
Indeed, otherwise one could find a sequence
X_{k}\in B_{\frac{1}{k}}(X)\backslash\mathfrak{U}
with
X_{k}\to X,

X_{k}\not\in\mathfrak{U}.
This contradicts the openness of
\mathfrak{U}.

∎

From the results of
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
, it follows that the topology on

is metrizable. We shall not elaborate on this now, because in the next section we will present an explicit construction of a generalized metric that induces the same topology and coarse structure as
\hat{d}.

Thus, we have defined on

a topology and a coarse structure and have verified that each of them separately is metrizable.
We now proceed to prove that they are simultaneously metrizable.

5.
Correspondences and metrization

We now show how to define a metric equivalent to the quasi‑isometric distance using the notion of correspondence (see Definition
2.4
). To simplify notation, we denote the distance between points
x
and
x^{\prime}
by
|x,x^{\prime}|
. The metric space in which the distance is taken will be clear from the context or stated explicitly.

Definition 5.1
.

The
quasi‑isometric distortion
of a correspondence
R
is the number

(5.1)

\operatorname{q-dis}R=\inf\left\{r>0\mid\frac{1}{e^{r}}|y,y^{\prime}|-e^{r}+1\leq|x,x^{\prime}|\leq e^{r}|y,y^{\prime}|+e^{2r}-e^{r}\quad\forall(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R\right\}.

Definition 5.2
.

Define the distance from
X
to
Y
by

(5.2)

D(X,Y)=\inf_{R}\{\operatorname{q-dis}R\},

where the infimum is taken over all correspondences
R
between
X
and
Y.

Proposition 5.3
.

The constructed distance is a metric.

Proof.

Reflexivity and symmetry are trivial. Let us check the triangle inequality.
Suppose
D(X,Y)=r_{0},

D(Y,Z)=s_{0}.
Then there exists a correspondence
R
between
X
and
Y
with
\operatorname{q-dis}R=r<r_{0}+\varepsilon
and a correspondence
S
between
Y
and
Z
with
\operatorname{q-dis}S=s<s_{0}+\varepsilon.
Define a correspondence between
X
and
Z
by

(5.3)

T=\{(x,z)\mid\exists y:(x,y)\in R,(y,z)\in S\}.

Now we estimate its quasi‑isometric distortion.

(5.4)

|x,x^{\prime}|\leq e^{r}|y,y^{\prime}|+e^{2r}-e^{r}\leq e^{r+s}|z,z^{\prime}|+e^{r}(e^{2s}-e^{s})+e^{2r}-e^{r}

It is easy to see that

(5.5)

e^{r}(e^{2s}-e^{s})+e^{2r}-e^{r}\leq e^{2r+2s}-e^{r+s}.

Indeed, by rearranging and grouping terms, we see that this is equivalent to the inequality

(5.6)

e^{2r}-e^{r}\leq e^{2s}(e^{2r}-e^{r}).

The reverse inequalities are established analogously.
Thus,
\operatorname{q-dis}T\leq r+s,
and because
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we get
D(X,Z)\leq r_{0}+s_{0}.

∎

We now show that
\operatorname{q-dis}R
induces the same topology and coarse structure as the quasi‑isometric distance.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
X,

Y
be metric spaces.

(1)

If
\hat{d}(X,Y)=r,
then
D(X,Y)\leq\ln(1+2r);

(2)

If
D(X,Y)=r,
then
\hat{d}(X,Y)\leq e^{2r}-e^{r}.

Thus, the topologies and coarse structures induced by
\hat{d}
and
D
coincide.

Proof.

Indeed, suppose there is a correspondence
R
between the spaces
X
and
Y
with parameters
(A,B).
That is, for any pairs
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R
, we have

\frac{|y,y^{\prime}|}{A}-\frac{B}{A}\leq|x,x^{\prime}|\leq A|y,y^{\prime}|+B.

By definition, for a correspondence, we have
\pi_{X}R=X,

\pi_{Y}R=Y,
where
\pi_{X},\pi_{Y}
are the projections from the Cartesian product
X\times Y
onto the corresponding factor.
Hence there exist maps
f\colon X\to Y,

g\colon Y\to X,
such that
\Gamma_{f},

\Gamma_{g}^{T}\subseteq R.
Since
R
is an
(A,B)
-correspondence,
f,

g
are
(A,B)
-quasi‑isometric embeddings.
Let us check the closeness of the compositions
g\circ f
and
f\circ g
to
\mathrm{id}_{X},\mathrm{id}_{Y}.
Because
(gfx,fx)
,
(x,fx)\in R,
we have the inequality

(5.7)

|gfx,x|\leq A|fx,fx|+B.

Therefore,
X
and
Y
are
(A,B,B)
-quasi‑isometric.
The estimate for the second composition is completely analogous. Thus, from
D(X,Y)=r
, it follows that

(5.8)

\hat{d}(X,Y)\leq\max\{e^{r}-1,e^{2r}-e^{r}\}=e^{2r}-e^{r}.

Conversely, let
X
and
Y
be
(A,B,C)
-quasi‑isometric spaces.
That is, there exist
(A,B)
-quasi‑isometric embeddings
f\colon X\to Y,

g\colon Y\to X
such that the compositions are
C
-close to the identity maps.
Define

(5.9)

R=\Gamma_{f}\cup\Gamma_{g}^{T}

and estimate
\operatorname{q-dis}R.
Let
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R.
If
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
lie in the graph of one of the functions, then

(5.10)

\frac{1}{A}|x,x^{\prime}|-\frac{B}{A}\leq|y,y^{\prime}|\leq A|x,x^{\prime}|+B,

because the functions are
(A,B)
-quasi‑isometric embeddings.
If
(x,y)\in\Gamma_{f},

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{g}^{T},
then

(5.11)

|x,x^{\prime}|=|x,g(y^{\prime})|\leq|gfx,gy^{\prime}|+|x,gfx|=|gy,gy^{\prime}|+C\leq A|y,y^{\prime}|+B+C.

The estimate for
|yy^{\prime}|
is done similarly.
Thus, we have found an
(A,B+C)
-correspondence between
X
and
Y.
Hence if
\hat{d}(X,Y)=r,
then

e^{D(X,Y)}\leq 1+r,\quad e^{2D(X,Y)}-e^{D(X,Y)}\leq 2r,

whence
D(X,Y)\leq\ln(1+2r).

Therefore, both distances induce the same topology and coarse structure.
∎

5.1.
Continuous deformations of metric spaces

We now show that any two quasi‑isometric metric spaces can be connected by a continuous path of finite length.
Let
R
be a correspondence between metric spaces
X
and
Y.
Denote the set
R
equipped with the metric

|(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{t}=(1-t)|xx^{\prime}|+t|yy^{\prime}|

by
R_{t}.
For
t=0
and
t=1
, one generally obtains pseudometric spaces.
But we identify them with the corresponding metric spaces obtained by identifying points at distance zero.
Then
X=R_{0}
,
Y=R_{1}.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be metric spaces, and let
R
be a correspondence between
X
and
Y
with
\operatorname{q-dis}R\leq r.
Then the map
t\mapsto R_{t}
is a continuous path in
(\mathfrak{M},D)
connecting
X
and
Y,
and its length does not exceed
e^{2r}-e^{r}.

Proof.

First, we show continuity.
Let
t,s\in[0,1]
,
|t-s|=\delta
.
The graph of the identity map
\mathrm{id}\colon R_{t}\to R_{s}
gives a correspondence between
R_{t}
and
R_{s}.
We estimate its distortion.

(5.12)

|(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{s}=(1-s)|x,x^{\prime}|+s|y,y^{\prime}|=(1-t)|x,x^{\prime}|+t|y,y^{\prime}|+(s-t)(|y,y^{\prime}|-|x,x^{\prime}|)\leq\\
\leq|(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{t}+\delta||x,x^{\prime}|-|y,y^{\prime}||

We estimate the term with
\delta
separately; without loss of generality assume
|x,x^{\prime}|>|y,y^{\prime}|.

(5.13)

\displaystyle|x,x^{\prime}|-|y,y^{\prime}|=t|x,x^{\prime}|+(1-t)|x,x^{\prime}|-t|y,y^{\prime}|-(1-t)|y,y^{\prime}|\leq

(5.14)

\displaystyle\leq te^{r}|y,y^{\prime}|+t(e^{2r}-e^{r})+(1-t)|x,x^{\prime}|-t|y,y^{\prime}|-\frac{(1-t)}{e^{r}}|xx^{\prime}|+(1-t)(e^{r}-1)=

(5.15)

\displaystyle=t(e^{r}-1)|y,y^{\prime}|+(1-t)(1-\frac{1}{e^{r}})|x,x^{\prime}|+(1-t)(e^{r}-1)+t(e^{2r}-e^{r})\leq

(5.16)

\displaystyle(e^{r}-1)|(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{t}+(e^{2r}-e^{r})

Thus,

|(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{s}\leq(1+\delta(e^{r}-1))|(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{t}+\delta(e^{2r}-e^{r}).

Hence as
\delta\to 0,
the distortion
\operatorname{id}\colon R_{t}\to R_{t+\delta}
tends to
0,
so the curve is continuous.
Now, we write asymptotic estimates that will help us compute the length of the curve.
Let
\Delta=\operatorname{max}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}),
where
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
are determined from the equations

e^{\Delta_{1}}=1+\delta(e^{r}-1),\quad e^{2\Delta_{2}}-e^{\Delta_{2}}=\delta(e^{2r}-e^{r}).

Clearly
D(R_{t},R_{s})\leq\Delta.
As
\delta\to 0,
we have

\Delta_{1}=\delta(e^{r}-1)+o(\delta),\quad\Delta_{2}=\delta(e^{2r}-e^{r})+o(\delta).

Therefore,

\Delta=\delta(e^{2r}-e^{r})+o(\delta).

Using the inequality
D(R_{t},R_{s})\leq\Delta
and passing to sufficiently fine partitions of the curve, we obtain that the length of the curve does not exceed
e^{2r}-e^{r}.

∎

Corollary 5.6
.

The set of equivalence classes
\mathfrak{M}
of separable metric
spaces is monogenic.

Proof.

The coarse structure is generated by the set
E_{1}=\{(X,X^{\prime})\mid D(X,X^{\prime})\leq 1\}.
Indeed, from the fact that any two metric spaces at distance at most
r
can be connected by a curve of length at most
e^{2r}-e^{r},
it follows that

E_{r}\subseteq E_{1}\circ\dots\circ E_{1},

where the right‑hand side is the composition of
k
sets with
k>e^{2r}-e^{r}.

∎

The above reasoning allows one to pass to an intrinsic metric, i.e., a metric defined as the infimum of lengths of curves between points.
From the estimates on curve lengths, it follows that this generalized metric will induce the same topology and coarse structure as
D,
and hence as
\hat{d}.

Example 10
.

Let
E_{p}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{p})
denote the
n

\|x\|_{p}=\sqrt[p]{|x_{1}|^{p}+\dots+|x_{n}|^{p}}.

Consider the curve
\gamma(p)=E_{p},

We will show that this curve is a geodesic (though not naturally parametrized) on the interval
[0,2]
and on the ray

n=2
their union forms a closed loop (since
E_{1}
is isometric to

Recall that the Banach–Mazur distance between isomorphic Banach spaces

D_{BM}(X,Y)=\inf_{T}\ln\max\{\|T\|,\|T^{-1}\|\},

where the infimum is taken over all isomorphisms

We now explain why the distance

between finite‑dimensional vector spaces

of the same dimension equals the Banach–Mazur distance. First, observe that the space

is isometric to

Consequently,

coincides with its asymptotic cone
\operatorname{ascone}X
. Moreover, to every
(A,B,C)
-quasi‑isometry
\tilde{f}\colon X\to Y
corresponds a bi‑Lipschitz homeomorphism
f\colon\operatorname{ascone}X\to\operatorname{ascone}Y,
with Lipschitz constants
\operatorname{L}(f)
and
\operatorname{L}(f^{-1})
do not exceed
A

[
9
]
[Lemma 10.83]. Since
f
and
f^{-1}
are Lipschitz, they are differentiable almost everywhere, there exists a point
x_{0}
of differentiability s.t. the derivative
df_{x_{0}}
is a linear isomorphism between
X
and
Y
with
\operatorname{L}(df_{x_{0}})\leq\operatorname{L}(f)\leq A.
Thus, from each
(A,B,C)
-quasi‑isometry we obtain a linear isomorphism with norm is at most
A.
In Proposition
5.4
we showed that every
(A,B)
-correspondence gives rise to a pair of quasi‑isometries with the same parameter

which yields the required equality.

It is known
[

[Proposition 37.6] that for

and for

and this value is attained by the identity map.

Considering a broken line

Hence, the supremum of the lengths of broken lines equals the distance, so the curve is a geodesic.

Now consider the curve

where

is

endowed with the norm

Fix

where

Observe that

(5.17)

\displaystyle D_{BM}(E_{0},E_{1})

\displaystyle\leq D_{BM}(R_{t_{0}},R_{t_{1}})+\dots+D_{BM}(R_{t_{n-1}},R_{t_{n}})

(5.18)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\dfrac{1-t_{1}+t_{1}M}{1}\cdot\dfrac{1-t_{2}+t_{2}M}{1-t_{1}+t_{1}M}\cdot\dots\cdot\dfrac{M}{1-t_{n-1}+t_{n-1}M}\right)

\displaystyle=\ln M=D_{BM}(E_{0},E_{1}).

Therefore, the curve

is also a geodesic.

Thus, the estimates obtained in the previous section are not optimal in general.
