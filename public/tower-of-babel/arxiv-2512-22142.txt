Abstract
. The ecosystem behind foundation model development today is highly centralized and limited to large-scale cloud data center operators: training foundation models is costly, needing immense compute resources. Decentralized foundation model training across edge devices, leveraging their spare compute, promises a democratized alternative. However, existing edge-training approaches fall short: they struggle to match cloud-based training performance, exhibit limited scalability with model size, exceed device memory capacity, and have prohibitive communication overhead. They also fail to satisfactorily handle device heterogeneity and dynamism. We introduce a new paradigm, Cleave , which finely partitions training operations through a novel selective hybrid tensor parallelism method. Together with a parameter server centric training framework, Cleave copes with device memory limits and avoids communication bottlenecks, thereby enabling efficient training of large models on par with the cloud. Further, with a cost optimization model to guide device selection and training workload distribution, Cleave effectively accounts for device heterogeneity and churn. Our evaluations show that Cleave matches cloud-based GPU training by scaling efficiently to larger models and thousands of devices, supporting up to 8x more devices than baseline edge-training approaches. It outperforms state-of-the-art edge training methods by up to a factor of 10 in per-batch training time and efficiently handles device failures, achieving at least 100x faster recovery than prior methods.

1.
Introduction

“Foundation models” (FMs)
5
are driving the AI revolution
25
, enabling powerful generative systems across language
43
, vision
49
, networks
65
, and software
14
.
Trained on large-scale data and adapted to diverse tasks, they have raised growing concern with the centralization of the current ecosystem
3
.
This is rooted in the significant compute resources required in training and the associated enormous economic cost
56
, affordable only to a handful of global entities, mostly those who operate the cloud computing data centers.
As a response to such centralization, decentralized foundation model training has emerged
53
;
12
, leveraging volunteered edge devices to pool compute resources. This paradigm taps into the scale, energy efficiency, and underutilization of modern edge hardware
68
, offering a more inclusive alternative to centralized training.

Decentralized foundation model training with edge devices would be appealing only if it satisfies
three key requirements
:

First
, it should offer an equivalent platform (in terms of training time), on par with the de facto cloud-based training.

Second
, it should match the accuracy of training in the cloud by allowing the use of the same model architecture, optimizers and training hyperparameters (
e.g.,
batch size, sequence length).
This rules out using typical edge-device oriented optimizations like compression or of models or gradients as those risk accuracy loss.

Third
, it must support training large models on foundation-scale datasets, in line with the scaling laws that underpin the performance of modern neural architectures
23
.

However, with the current methods for foundation model training at the edge, the above requirements are not met, thereby limiting the potential of this paradigm.
This is due to
three challenges
:

1. Excessive per-device memory consumption.
Existing edge training approaches (
e.g.,
DTFM
69
, EDDL
18
), employ data parallelism (DP), pipeline parallelism (PP) or a combination of both.
While the typical usable memory on phones is around 512MB
33
, the memory demand of training a model can be hundreds of GB.
Even with commonly adopted model parallelism in edge training, the per-device memory demand cannot be met for all sizes of models (detailed in §
2.3
).

2. High communication volume among devices.

Although tensor parallelism (TP) used in cloud training approaches (
e.g.,
Alpa
72
) is a key enabler for reducing per-device memory consumption, it shifts the problem to communication.
In addition to gradient AllReduce in DP, TP introduces additional AllReduce and AlltoAll at each layer in both backward and forward propagation (detailed in §
2.3
).
So, leveraging TP naively to fit model training into device capability results in high communication overhead, making it challenging to use it in an edge setting with constrained network bandwidth.

3. Handling device heterogeneity and churn.

Current approaches (
e.g.,
DTFM, Alpa) involve
all
devices in training without fully considering their heterogeneity in compute and communication characteristics.
This ends up including stragglers in the communication-intensive DP and TP, slowing down the training process.
Moreover, edge environments are inherently dynamic – devices can disconnect, fail, or join at any point during training.
Current approaches either assume a static device set (
e.g.,
Alpa, DTFM) or lack fine-grained fault recovery and seamless integration of newly available devices (
e.g.,
SWARM
51
, Asteriod
68
), making them inefficient in real-world edge deployments.

Motivated by the above, we propose
Cleave
that makes decentralized large-scale foundation model training possible with edge devices by addressing the above challenges.
Our key insight is that foundation model training is dominated by generalized matrix multiplication (GEMM) operations with inherently high degree of parallelism embedded.
Fine-grained sharding and distribution of this operation enables lower per-device memory footprint and agile fault tolerance under device churn.
Combining this with a server centric training framework that handles workload distribution and intermediate result aggregation reduces per-device communication demand.

Cleave
brings together these ideas to introduce a novel
selective hybrid tensor parallelism
technique
that flexibly leverages the scale of edge devices while accounting for their diverse resource constraints and characteristics.
The result is the capability to train large foundation models with edge devices equivalent to cloud based training (in terms of accuracy and training time).
Specifically, we make four contributions:

Contribution #1:
We bring tensor parallelism into the scope of edge training in a communication-efficient manner.
Our intuition is that each device can handle as little as a single row and column of General Matrix Multiplication (GEMM) operations, that dominate foundation model training computation, to reduce communication related latency as well as to minimize the device memory footprint (§
3.1
).

Contribution #2:
We adopt a parameter server (PS) centric training framework (§
3.2
), shifting away from the current distributed edge training approaches that rely on peer-to-peer communication among devices (
e.g.,
DTFM).
Our key idea is to use PS as the aggregator for AllReduce and AlltoAll operations, avoiding the need for exchanging intermediate data between devices.
In addition, the PS centric approach aligns well with the link bandwidth asymmetry typical in the edge environment, leveraging the asymmetric input and output sizes of GEMMs.

Contribution #3:
We design a cost model to select devices for training and distribute workload among them (§
4
). The model accounts for compute and communication heterogeneity, and the dynamism of edge devices due to churn. It enables stragglers to be sparingly used or excluded entirely by redistributing their workload to other devices, thereby avoiding stalls in the training process. It also leverages fine-grained sharding with TP and DP to ensure fast recovery under device churn.

Contribution #4:

We evaluate
Cleave
against state-of-the-art (SOTA) edge (DTFM
69
) and cloud (Alpa
72
) methods considering training two foundation language models of various sizes – OPT
71
and Llama2
62
(§
5
).
Our results show that
Cleave
not only enables training of larger models than SOTA edge training methods but also
achieves the same training performance as with cloud GPUs.
Furthermore,
Cleave
is 4-10X faster than the current edge training methods.
In addition, the design of
Cleave
leveraging TP and PS-based architecture allows it to scale to thousands of devices, supporting 2-8X more devices than the baseline methods.
We also evaluate the training time impact of device churn with
Cleave
against SOTA edge (SWARM
51
and Asteroid
68
) and cloud (Bamboo
61
and Mario
34
) methods.
Our results show that
Cleave
achieves at least 100x faster recovery than prior methods.

To summarize,
Cleave
enables scalable, accurate, and efficient training of large foundation models on edge devices by addressing the core challenges of memory constraints, communication overhead, and device heterogeneity—delivering cloud-comparable performance in decentralized settings.

2.
Background and Motivation

2.1.
Edge Devices and Characteristics

The edge can be broadly viewed as the set of devices outside datacenters
8
.
For our purpose of foundation model training, we focus on the subset of edge devices that are network-connected, equipped with AI accelerators, and being charged.
Examples include laptops and smartphones with GPUs (e.g., Apple M4/A16).
Such edge devices collectively offer compute capabilities comparable to the cloud, due to their massive scale
45
and their ample idle periods each day
2
.

Memory constraints.

Smartphones’ application-level memory is limited.
Although 8-12GB memory is advertised, each application can use at most 512MB
33
. Similarly, for laptops, while 32-64GB is the advertised value, but the usable memory is 10GB or less, with memory fragmentation and backend applications consuming most of it.

Device heterogeneity.

We consider the following:
(i)
Compute
, referring to variations in FLOPS performance across devices—for example, mobile devices offer 5–7 TFLOPS, while laptops like the Apple M3 Pro provide up to 27 TFLOPS
22
; and
(ii)
Communication
, which includes (1) differences in network link capacity, where both bandwidth and latency vary across devices
7
, and (2) network link asymmetry, where per-device uplink (UL) speeds are typically 2–10× slower than downlink (DL), with typical downlink bandwidths of 10–100 MB/s and uplink bandwidths of 5–10 MB/s
57
.

2.2.
Existing Solutions and their Limitations

Distributed training can involve three forms of parallelism:
(i)
Data Parallelism (DP)
, which partitions input data into distinct batches, with each DP group holding a full model replica
32
;
18
;
(ii)
Pipeline Parallelism (PP)
, which partitions models by layers, and pipelines forward and backward computations across micro-batches to minimize device idle time
21
;
39
; and
(iii)
Tensor Parallelism (TP)
, which splits model parameters within layers across devices, allowing per-device memory consumption to be reduced
55
;
72
.

Existing distributed training approaches fall in two categories: (1) native cloud approaches and (2) cloud approaches adapted to edge.
Current cloud-based approaches (
e.g.,

55
;
72
;
9
use 3D parallelism (DP+PP+TP).
But they make strong assumptions that do not reflect the edge environment: (i) homogeneity in compute and communication among devices, and (ii) availability of high bandwidth communication between devices (
e.g.,
NVLINK).
So, using these approaches as is in the edge setting leads to degraded performance.
Existing edge distributed training methods limit themselves to 2D parallelism, specifically DP
18
;
12
and PP
69
;
51
).
They do not consider TP, as the communication overhead in peer-to-peer TP negates its memory reduction benefit, as shown in
Figure 1
.
Consequently, current edge training approaches inherit the drawbacks of cloud-based approaches with high device memory requirements and peer-to-peer communication overhead (due to DP AllReduce) as the key limitations.

With regard to device heterogeneity, existing training systems address different aspects.
AMP
31
, HetPipe
44
, and SDPipe
37
focus on compute heterogeneity using PP.
DTFM
69
and FusionAI
58
target network heterogeneity, with DTFM combining DP and PP.
DeDLOC
12
optimizes DP AllReduce for communication heterogeneity but uses asynchronous updates, risking accuracy loss.

Existing approaches for handling device churn either employ checkpoint-restore, or replication and recomputation.
Mario
34
and Tenplex
64
represent the cloud methods of checkpointing all training states.
Bamboo
61
replicates the per-layer compute in the context of spot instances.
SWARM
51
adopts rewiring, where failed hidden states are rerouted to devices holding the same layer for recomputation. Asteroid
68
employs resharding and redistributed layers in addition to recomputation.
Learning@home
53
has a particular focus on expert recompute for Mixture-of-Expert type foundation models.

Federated Learning (FL)
27
;
54
is akin to DP and has a focus on data privacy, making it orthogonal to FM training on edge devices with public data leveraging additional forms of parallelism. Our focus is on the latter.
The fault tolerance strategy in FL can be lossy due to dropping gradients, similar to asynchronous gradients
52
, while our focus is on the fully synchronous and lossless version.
While incorporating Split Learning (SL) adds scalability and capacity with offloading to the cloud
60
, it is still equivalent to the DP+PP scheme, falling short in performance and accuracy.

3.
Cleave
Training Framework and Method

3.1.
Key Insights

To enable us to address the core challenge highlighted in the previous section, we make the key observation that training foundation models is dominated by generalized matrix multiplication (GEMM) operations, which are highly parallelizable and structurally independent. As shown in
Table 3
, over 99% of the total floating-point operations in LLaMA models are attributable to GEMMs, with non-GEMM operations such as layer normalization, activation functions, and softmax contributing negligibly to overall computation. Further,
Table 4
details representative GEMMs within a single transformer layer, highlighting their uniformity and independence—each of these operations can be executed without memory dependencies, making them ideal for fine-grained task decomposition.

This structure presents a significant opportunity: large-scale parallelism can be exploited across devices or execution units by partitioning and scheduling these GEMM tasks independently. In particular, the absence of inter-GEMM dependencies during forward and backward passes allows for asynchronous or decentralized execution.
To adapt tensor parallelism to the edge setting without sacrificing accuracy, our key insight is that those GEMM operations can be partitioned into smaller sub-matrix level operations, such that each device is only responsible for computing a minimal amount of units.
Given each GEMM has an upper limit of total communication volume, as more devices join, each handles fewer rows and columns per GEMM (through tensor and data parallel sharding), thereby lowering communication volume with more devices, shown as the ideal case in
Figure 1
.

Such an idealized training method models training as a series of operators in a directed acyclic graph (e.g., Alpa), with a controller distributing the computation of operations across devices. The communication load includes the model size and all intermediate results. The controller can aggregate gradients locally during forward and backward propagation without additional communication, as devices transmit output from each operator back to the controller without peer-to-peer broadcasting.
In addition, the gradient for each parameter is transmitted only once and the intermediate result in each layer is transmitted from devices only once so that the total data transmitted is model size + (intermediate size * number of layers) for each batch.

3.2.
Cleave
Design

Our proposal dubbed
Cleave
mimics the above outlined idealized training method by using a typical client-server communication architecture, as opposed to the peer-to-peer communication approach adopted in the cloud-based and current edge distributed training approaches.
At the core of
Cleave
is a parameter server (PS) that, as a server, plays several key roles: (i) scheduling training operations across devices, (ii) maintaining training parameters and eliminating aggregation over network (AlltoAll and AllReduce) operations, and (iii) tracking device availability and capabilities (compute resources and downlink/uplink speeds).
Devices as clients carry out training operations assigned by the PS, and tensor parallelism enables an arbitrary number of participants.

We term this strategy
selective hybrid tensor parallelism
, where “selective” refers to the straggler-aware scheduling via cost model (§
4.1
).
When assigning even a single row-column pair to a straggler degrades overall performance,
Cleave
redistributes the its workload to more capable devices to maintain system efficiency.
In
Cleave
, we handle device churn following a similar strategy, reactively (§
4.2
).
Particularly, failed sub-GEMMs in the current round are distributed across remaining live devices through the cost model, while newly joined devices wait for the next round of GEMM.

Figure 2
.
The workflow of
Cleave
from model defined in training script to DAG of GEMMs. Edges in the DAG represents the memory dependency. Each GEMM is scheduled selectively across devices with best effort communication and computation overlap.

Seamless training as in cloud.

To ensure parity with cloud-based training,
Cleave
represents the model as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of GEMM operations
72
;
31
;
58
, as shown in
Figure 2
for a transformer layer, where each node denotes a GEMM (e.g., QKV projections) and edges capture memory dependencies.
This DAG can be extracted using software hooks (e.g., PyTorch) to trigger custom scheduling routines.

Cleave
preserves the size and numerical semantics of each GEMM to ensure consistency with standard cloud-based training.
At each DAG node, the PS reads pre-computed decisions from the cost model to determine optimal scheduling, dispatches the corresponding rows and columns to participating devices, and aggregates the computed outputs.
When the device set changes,
Cleave
overlaps cost model recomputation with device-side computation, and update the scheduling decisions on the successive batch.

Scheduling Workflow.

Cleave
requires devices to register upon joining and report their compute and communication capabilities, including uplink (UL) and downlink (DL) bandwidth.
Since GEMM shapes repeat across layers, the cost model optimization is solved once per device set and reused thereafter.
Our hybrid tensor parallelism adapts DL-to-UL bandwidth asymmetry for each device by shaping the row–column assignments accordingly.
To maximize efficiency,
Cleave
overlaps communication and computation using parallel threads and a stream-based protocol (
e.g.,
gRPC
15
): rows and columns are downloaded, GEMMs computed, and results uploaded concurrently.
Device departures are detected via disconnect events; unfinished GEMM outputs are identified using bookkeeping, and the remaining work is rescheduled by re-invoking the cost model.

Cleave

4.2.
Handling device churn

Cleave

.

We treat each occurrence of device failure as a new snapshot of the scheduling problem.
Suppose that a failed device was responsible for computing a subset of the GEMM, denoted by
\mathbf{A}_{s}^{*}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{s}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{B}_{s}^{*}\subseteq\mathbf{B}_{s}^{\prime}
.
Our goal is to reschedule the computation of the submatrix
\mathbf{A}_{s}^{*}\circ\mathbf{B}_{s}^{*}
.
We define binary matrices
\mathbf{R}_{s}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathbf{D}|\times m_{s}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{s}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathbf{D}|\times q_{s}}
to represent the presence of row and column caches on each device, corresponding to mappings of
\mathbf{A}_{s}
and
\mathbf{B}_{s}
across the device set
\mathbf{D}
.
That is,
\mathbf{R}_{s}[k,i]=1
if device
k
holds row
i
of
\mathbf{A}_{s}
, and similarly
\mathbf{C}_{s}
reflects cached columns of
\mathbf{B}_{s}
.
Given the memory constraint in
Equation 6
, we assume that no cache replacement occurs during the execution of a GEMM at level
s
. Accordingly, we enforce the constraints:

\alpha_{s,k}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{R}_{s}[k,i],\quad\beta_{s,k}=\sum_{j}\mathbf{C}_{s}[k,j]
,
where
\alpha_{s,k}
and
\beta_{s,k}
denote the number of row and column blocks cached on device
k
, respectively.

We aim to handle both single device and multiple simultaneous device failures.
For all failed row and column computations across devices, we concatenate the affected blocks to form
\mathbf{A}_{s}^{*}
and
\mathbf{B}_{s}^{*}
. This reduces the problem to the same scheduling formulation described in
subsection 4.1
, but with a modified computation criterion for estimating the device communication costs.
For each device
d_{k}\in\mathbf{D}
, we define the DL communication cost
C_{\textsc{Comm}}^{d}(s,k)
as:

\displaystyle C_{\textsc{Comm}}^{d}(s,p,k):=\frac{\alpha_{s,p}n_{s}b}{W^{d}_{k}}\mathbf{R}_{s}[k,\alpha_{s,p}]+\frac{n_{s}\beta_{s,p}b}{W^{d}_{k}}\mathbf{C}_{s}[k,\beta_{s,p}]

Here,
\alpha_{s,p}
and
\beta_{s,p}
denote the number of rows and columns needed from other devices,
n_{s}
is the size of the current GEMM,
b
is the block size,
W^{d}_{k}
is the bandwidth of device
d_{k}
, and
L^{d}_{k}
captures its latency overhead. The uplink cost
C_{\textsc{Comm}}^{u}(s,p,k)
can be computed similarly.
If the required row or column has already been transmitted to the target device, or is scheduled to be sent to another device, we can further reduce the downlink communication cost by reusing its on-device cache.
We use the same Gurobi solver as in §
4.1
.

Tensor parallelism (TP).

Eliminating TP increases the communication volume by 273% for each device and runtime by 413%. Without TP, each device needs to receive a matrix rather than rows and columns, and there’s less asymmetry in vector-matrix multiplication (GEMV) compared to GEMM.

Parameter server architecture (PS).

Replacing the PS architecture with peer-to-peer communication results in a 342% increase in communication volume and a 543% increase in runtime. Peer-to-peer approaches require broadcasting model parameters, matrix reshaping, and AllReduce operations across devices, significantly increasing communication overhead. The PS architecture reduces this by centralizing parameter storage and using optimized downlink (DL) transmission, which better utilizes bandwidth and reduces runtime.
The memory increase is due to the optimizer being loaded on devices rather than being kept on PS.

Device heterogeneity awareness.

When removing device heterogeneity awareness in distributing GEMM computations, runtime increases significantly (up to 325%), and communication volume rises by 21%. Without load balancing, stragglers receive the same workload as other devices, significantly delaying synchronization. Moreover, model parameters are replicated to more devices rather than skew to strong devices, further increasing communication volume.

6.
Discussion on Practical Concerns

Assumptions of parameter server.

We assume a realistic and widely available datacenter network bandwidth of 200 Gbps per CPU-only parameter server with 128 CPU cores (e.g., AWS EC2 M6in instances). Given this configuration, and assuming edge devices operate under typical fixed broadband downlink speeds of 250 Mbps
57
, a single server can concurrently support approximately 1,000 to 2,000 devices.
At larger scales, a distributed or hierarchical parameter server (PS) architecture may be necessary.
Cleave
supports horizontal scaling by sharding data and replicating model parameters across multiple servers—distributing both bandwidth and computation. This is aligned with techniques used in systems like Beldi
70
, and ensures scalable training throughput without central bottlenecks.

Robustness to poisoning attacks.

Cleave
can defend against poisoning attacks in distributed GEMM by employing random projection-based verification
38
. Specifically, for matrix multiplication
C=AB
,
Cleave
samples random vectors
r,s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and verifies correctness by checking whether
r^{\top}(AB)s=(Ar)^{\top}(Bs)
. This probabilistic check can detect even single-entry corruptions with high probability, incurring only
\mathcal{O}(n)
overhead per verification, where
n
is the largest dimension of
A
or
B
. The probability of a false negative is at most
\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})
, as successful evasion requires reconstructing the entire random vector. Since vectors are freshly generated at runtime, the scheme remains effective even against white-box adversaries. As the check reduces to GEMV operations, it is lightweight and compatible with real-time execution on modern CPUs
66
.

Energy consumption and carbon footprint.

Decentralized training using edge devices as an alternative to a single high-end GPU can offer improved energy efficiency and lower environmental impact
67
. In contrast, cloud-based training often suffers from sub-linear performance scaling—as observed in systems such as Alpa
72
, Megatron
55
, and DeepSpeed
47
—resulting in disproportionate energy costs. For example, scaling from 1 to 16 GPUs may reduce training time by 9×, yet increase total power consumption by 15×. As shown in
Figure 8
,
Cleave
achieves linear scalability in both performance and energy efficiency, owing to its design that keeps total computation and communication volumes fixed regardless of the number of participating devices. Even when accounting for communication overhead, energy costs remain minimal: smartphone WiFi modules consume roughly 0.06W at 250 Mbps, while desktop Ethernet cards draw around 0.25W at 1 Gbps. These values are negligible relative to the compute energy, and so do not undermine the overall energy savings.

7.
Conclusions

We have presented
Cleave
, a novel method for training foundation models in a decentralized manner that accounts for all the unique characteristics of the edge environment, including memory constraints, compute/network heterogeneity, and dynamic availability.
To this end,
Cleave
introduces a novel selective hybrid tensor parallelism technique and builds on a parameter server-based training framework to support scalable and efficient workload distribution.
Cleave
explicitly addresses the presence of stragglers and handles device churn by enabling fast recovery and seamless integration of new devices, ensuring robustness throughout training.
Our findings indicate that
Cleave
is effective for large-scale distributed foundation model training with edge devices, equivalent to that with cloud-based training, underscoring its potential for democratizing foundation model development.

A.3.

The bounds in
Equation 7
and
Equation 8
compare aggregate communication volumes, providing sufficient but not necessary conditions. We derive tighter characterizations by analyzing the temporal structure enabled by

The streaming mechanism (illustrated in
Figure 2
) enables overlap between downlink transmission, computation, and uplink transmission. Let
T_{\text{DL}}
,
T_{\text{comp}}
, and
T_{\text{UL}}
denote the time for downloading, computing, and uploading one row-column pair respectively. For a device processing
k

19
:

(9)

T_{\text{pipeline}}(k)=T_{\text{DL}}+(k{-}1)\cdot\max(T_{\text{DL}},T_{\text{comp}},T_{\text{UL}})+T_{\text{comp}}+T_{\text{UL}}

This captures the pipeline fill phase (
T_{\text{DL}}
), steady-state execution at the rate of the slowest stage, and drain phase (
T_{\text{comp}}+T_{\text{UL}}
). The refined crossover condition compares this pipelined makespan against the baseline’s AllReduce latency, which scales as
O(\alpha\cdot\lceil\log_{2}D\rceil)
for ring-based implementations
59
, where
\alpha
is the per-message latency.

The DAG structure (
Figure 2
) imposes
S
synchronization barriers corresponding to levels with memory dependencies. The total
Cleave

(10)

T=\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\left(T_{\text{GEMM}}(s)+T_{\text{sync}}(s)\right)

where

captures waiting time at level
s

Combining Equations (9) and (10), the tightened condition for

(11)

where

\alpha

\beta

Appendix B
Communication Efficiency: Heterogeneous

The heterogeneous setting requires fundamentally different analytical tools because the optimization problem—minimizing makespan subject to memory and workload constraints—is a variant of the unrelated parallel machine scheduling problem, known to be NP-hard
30
.

B.1.
Problem Formulation and Complexity

The workload allocation problem in
Cleave
can be formulated as a generalized assignment problem. Let
x_{ik}
denote the fraction of GEMM
i
assigned to device
k
, with constraints
\sum_{k}x_{ik}=1
for all
i
and
x_{ik}\geq 0
. The objective is to minimize the maximum completion time (makespan) across devices:

(17)

\min_{x}\max_{k}\sum_{i}\frac{x_{ik}\cdot W_{i}}{F_{k}}+C_{\text{COMM}}(x,k)

subject to memory constraints
\sum_{i}x_{ik}\cdot M_{i}\leq M_{k}
for all devices
k
.

This problem admits a
(2-\frac{1}{m})
-approximation via the Longest Processing Time (LPT) heuristic
16
, where
m
is the number of machines. However,
Cleave
’s structure enables better guarantees.

B.2.
Exploiting Structural Properties

Cleave
’s DAG structure provides two properties that enable tighter analysis: GEMMs within a level are independent (no memory dependencies), and workload is arbitrarily divisible at the row-column granularity. Under these conditions, the optimal makespan at each level satisfies the lower bound:

(18)

T^{*}_{\text{level}}(s)\geq\max\left(\frac{\sum_{i}W_{i}(s)}{\sum_{k}F_{k}},\max_{i}\frac{W_{i}(s)}{F_{\max}}\right)

where
W_{i}(s)
is the workload of GEMM
i
at level
s
,
F_{k}
is device
k
’s compute capability, and
F_{\max}=\max_{k}F_{k}
. The first term represents the parallelism-limited bound (total work divided by total capacity), while the second represents the serialization-limited bound (largest indivisible unit).

The Gurobi solver
17
achieves a makespan within a factor of
(1+\epsilon)
of this lower bound for any
\epsilon>0
given sufficient solver time, leveraging the convexity of the continuous relaxation and the effectiveness of branch-and-bound for the integrality constraints.

B.3.
Stochastic Performance Bounds

When device capabilities are drawn from a distribution, as in our evaluation using AI-Benchmark data
22
, we derive expected-case bounds using order statistics theory
10
.

Let
F_{k}\sim\mathcal{F}
with mean
\mu_{F}
and variance
\sigma^{2}_{F}
. The coefficient of variation
c_{v}=\sigma_{F}/\mu_{F}
characterizes heterogeneity. For a load-balanced allocation where each device receives workload proportional to its capability, the expected makespan scales as:

(19)

\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{hetero}}]\approx T_{\text{homo}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{c_{v}^{2}}{2}\cdot g(D)\right)

where
g(D)
is a decreasing function capturing the load-balancing benefit of additional devices.

For
Cleave
’s fine-grained allocation at row-column granularity, concentration inequalities
6
yield
g(D)\approx 1/\sqrt{D}
. This reflects the law of large numbers: with many small tasks, deviations from optimal balance average out. In contrast, for coarse-grained baselines like DTFM where the minimum allocation unit is a full layer,
g(D)\approx 1
, providing no asymptotic improvement from additional devices.

This analysis explains the empirical observation (
Figure 6
) that
Cleave
deviates by only 5% from the ideal case under straggler presence, while baselines experience
10\times
slowdowns.

Appendix C
Distributional Latency Modeling

Network latency in edge environments exhibits heavy-tailed behavior due to variable wireless conditions, congestion, and device-level scheduling artifacts. The cost model in Section 3.2 treats latency as deterministic constants
L^{d}_{k}
and
L^{u}_{k}
, which underestimates tail effects that dominate synchronous training performance.

C.1.
Distributional Model

Empirical studies of mobile network latency
20
;
40
demonstrate that round-trip times follow heavy-tailed distributions. We adopt the Pareto distribution as our analytical model:

(20)

\mathbb{P}(L>x)=\left(\frac{x_{m}}{x}\right)^{\alpha},\quad x\geq x_{m}

where
x_{m}
is the scale parameter (minimum latency) and
\alpha
is the shape parameter governing tail heaviness. When
\alpha\leq 2
, variance is infinite; when
\alpha\leq 1
, even the mean diverges. Measurements from the MobiPerf dataset
36
and related studies suggest
\alpha\in[1.5,3]
for typical mobile networks.

The Pareto distribution satisfies the maximum domain of attraction property for extreme value theory
11
, making it a canonical choice for analyzing tail behavior in distributed systems.

C.2.
Impact on Synchronization Barriers

At each synchronization barrier in
Cleave
’s DAG traversal, the PS waits for all
D
assigned devices. The barrier completion time is determined by the maximum latency:

(21)

T_{\text{barrier}}=\max_{k=1}^{D}L_{k}

For Pareto-distributed latencies with
\alpha>1
(finite mean), the expected maximum scales according to extreme value theory
28
:

(22)

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{k=1}^{D}L_{k}\right]\sim x_{m}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\cdot D^{1/\alpha}

This scaling is substantially worse than the
O(\log D)

C.3.
Tail-Aware Cost Model

We augment the cost model (Equation 2) to account for tail risk using Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), also known as Expected Shortfall
48
:

(23)

C_{\text{GEMM}}(s,p)=\text{CVaR}_{\beta}\left[\max\left(C_{\text{COMM}}(s,p),C_{\text{COMP}}(s,p)\right)\right]

where
\text{CVaR}_{\beta}
denotes the expected value in the worst
\beta
-fraction of outcomes. For operational relevance in training stability, we recommend
\beta=0.05
, capturing 95th percentile behavior.

For Pareto-distributed latencies, the CVaR admits closed form:

(24)

\text{CVaR}_{\beta}[L]=\frac{x_{m}}{\beta^{1/\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}

Alternatively, the objective can incorporate a variance penalty for risk-averse optimization:

(25)

\min_{\{x_{ik}\}}\quad\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{total}}]+\lambda\cdot\sqrt{\text{Var}(T_{\text{total}})}

where
\lambda\geq 0
controls risk aversion. When latencies are independent across devices, this formulation can be solved via second-order cone programming
35
, maintaining computational tractability.

C.4.
Straggler Mitigation Guarantees

Cleave
’s approach of excluding stragglers (Section 3.1) represents one mitigation strategy. We analyze the theoretical tradeoffs of alternative approaches.

Speculative Execution.
Assign each row-column pair to
r
devices and use the first response. The probability that all
r
copies experience tail latency decreases as:

\mathbb{P}(L>t)^{r}=\left(\frac{x_{m}}{t}\right)^{r\alpha}

The expected completion time for
r
-way replication is:

(26)

\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{j=1}^{r}L_{j}\right]=x_{m}\cdot\frac{r\alpha}{r\alpha-1}\cdot r^{-1/\alpha}

The optimal redundancy factor balances latency reduction against communication overhead. Setting marginal benefit equal to marginal cost yields:

(27)

r^{*}\approx\left(\frac{C_{\text{comm}}}{C_{\text{tail}}\cdot\alpha}\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)}

where
C_{\text{comm}}
is the per-replica communication cost and
C_{\text{tail}}
is the cost of experiencing tail latency. For
\alpha=2
and moderate tail penalty, this suggests
r^{*}\in[2,4]
.

Coded Computation.
Rather than full replication, erasure codes enable recovery from any
k
of
n
responses
29
. The makespan becomes the
k
-th order statistic of
n
latencies. For Pareto distributions:

(28)

\mathbb{E}[L_{(k:n)}]\sim x_{m}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(1-1/\alpha)}{\Gamma(n-k+1+1/\alpha)\Gamma(k)}

Setting
n-k=O(n^{1-1/\alpha})
yields expected latency of
O(x_{m})
with redundancy overhead
O(n^{-1/\alpha})
, providing a principled tradeoff between communication cost and tail latency mitigation.

C.5.
Implications for
Cleave
Design

Incorporating fat-tailed latency analysis yields three design insights for
Cleave
deployments.

First, the optimal device count depends on the tail parameter
\alpha
. With heavier tails, marginal benefit from additional devices diminishes faster. The refined scaling condition becomes:

(29)

D^{*}\approx\left(\frac{W_{\text{GEMM}}}{L_{\text{median}}\cdot W_{d}}\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)}

where
W_{\text{GEMM}}
is workload per GEMM,
L_{\text{median}}
is median latency, and
W_{d}
is downlink bandwidth. For
\alpha=2
, this yields
D^{*}\propto(W_{\text{GEMM}})^{2/3}
rather than linear scaling.

Second, the PS architecture provides a natural control point for straggler mitigation. The PS can maintain empirical latency distributions for each device using exponential moving averages and dynamically adjust assignments. A Thompson sampling approach
50
balances exploration (learning device characteristics) with exploitation (assigning to reliable devices).

Third, bandwidth asymmetry interacts multiplicatively with tail effects. If uplink latencies exhibit heavier tails than downlink—plausible given wireless upload characteristics and contention –
Cleave
’s design choice to minimize uplink communication provides compounding benefits beyond mean-case analysis. Specifically, if downlink has tail parameter
\alpha_{d}
and uplink has
\alpha_{u}<\alpha_{d}
, the effective benefit of reducing uplink volume by factor
\gamma
scales as:

\gamma^{1+1/\alpha_{u}-1/\alpha_{d}}

Appendix D
Implementation Details

System design choices.

We focus on GEMM operations, particularly in attention and MLP layers, as they are the most computationally intensive, exhibiting cubic time complexity with respect to model parameters, while other operations have quadratic complexity
55
;
24
. While most work on partitioning GEMM focuses on sparse matrices
1
;
13
, our approach targets the dense matrices typical in foundation models and addresses challenges unique to edge training.

Our approach uses synchronized communication, with the PS waiting for responses from all assigned workers. The system processes training batches with a fixed optimal batch size globally. It also supports asynchronous training, synchronizing forward and backward passes while allowing asynchronous gradient accumulation.

Managing a large number of devices has been extensively studied in edge orchestration frameworks. Existing systems like KubeEdge
26
and Orchestra
4
support large-scale cloud-edge communication, typically relying on protocols such as MQTT
42
or AMQP
41
for communication.
We adopt these protocols to broadcast rows and columns to specific groups of devices as determined by the solver. Parameter server frameworks, such as FedScale
27
and LIFL
46
, provide functionality for device management in federated learning contexts. We leverage device registration and keep-alive mechanisms from these frameworks in our system.

Solver Implementation.

We use the Gurobi
17
solver to compute the optimal solution. For the largest tested search space—1024 devices and a model size of 70B—the solver completes in approximately 10 minutes, considering six types of GEMM operations involved in training.
For a given configuration, including the number of devices, device compute capabilities, bandwidth, model batch size, and sequence length, the solver needs to be run only once. Since the training process typically involves thousands to millions of batches, with each batch taking over a minute, the solver’s runtime overhead is negligible.
