Application of large language models in clinical decision-making for dry eye disease
Authors: Haoqiang Cui, Yuyang Yang, Taichen Lai, Xuemei Zhang, Kunhong Xiao, Li Liu, Ziyi Qi, Catherine Jan, Yuqing Wang, Beiou Zhang, Jiahao Liu, Zhuoting Zhu, Yan Huang, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the diagnostic, classification, and treatment decision-making performance of large language models (LLMs) in dry eye disease (DED) and compare their performance with that of junior ophthalmologists to assess their feasibility as clinical support tools.MethodsOne hundred standardized DED cases from Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital (Aug 2023–Aug 2025) were analyzed. Four LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and ERNIE Bot-4.5-turbo) underwent an initial 20-item test with a 95% accuracy threshold. Only models meeting this criterion advanced to case-based evaluation. Diagnostic and therapeutic outputs were rated by senior ophthalmologists using the Global Quality Score (GQS) and a clinical safety score. Meanwhile, the response times of both the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists were recorded to evaluate their respective efficiency.ResultsChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Gemini-2.5-Pro met the screening threshold. In determining treatment necessity, the accuracies of the LLMs (96–99%) were comparable to those of junior ophthalmologists (97%). For DED classification, DeepSeek-V3 achieved the highest accuracy (92%) and agreement with experts (kappa = 0.80), outperforming ChatGPT-4o and junior ophthalmologists (71%, kappa ≈ 0.53; p < 0.01). Gemini-2.5-Pro showed strong performance (accuracy 89%), the highest GQS (4.87 ± 0.37), and the best safety rating, with 89% of its outputs rated as “good”. The decision efficiency of the LLMs was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.001), with response times (16–36 s) much shorter than those of junior ophthalmologists (315 ± 57 s).ConclusionsGemini-2.5-Pro and DeepSeek-V3 demonstrated high accuracy, safety, and efficiency in case-based DED management, showing strong potential as auxiliary tools to enhance clinical decision-making and support less experienced clinicians.Trial registrationNot applicable. This study is a retrospective observational study and does not involve any intervention requiring trial registration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03586-y.

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common, chronic, and progressive disorder of the ocular surface with a multifactorial etiology. Its core pathophysiological features include tear film instability, ocular surface inflammation, and neurosensory abnormalities [1, 2], which manifest as a wide spectrum of subjective symptoms and objective signs. The disease mechanism is complex and influenced by multiple factors, thus requiring comprehensive evaluation and individualized management in clinical practice. Patients with DED often present with symptoms such as ocular dryness, discomfort, fluctuating vision, fatigue, and a burning sensation [3, 4]. DED significantly impairs quality of life [5] and, if left untreated or in severe cases, may lead to complications such as corneal damage and ocular infection [6, 7]. According to the TFOS DEWS II report, the global prevalence of DED is increasing, with estimates ranging from 5% to 50% [8, 9]. The condition is particularly common among the elderly and individuals with prolonged electronic device use. The etiologies of DED are diverse, and the disease can be classified into aqueous-deficient dry eye (ADDE), evaporative dry eye (EDE), and mixed type, each of which has distinct mechanisms and therapeutic strategies [3]. Although various treatment modalities are available [10], such as warm compresses, intense pulsed light (IPL), and punctal plugs, their proper application requires solid professional knowledge. In clinical practice, junior ophthalmologists and primary care providers often face challenges in managing patients with diverse subtypes of DED. Given the marked inter-individual variability of the disease, achieving consistent treatment outcomes remains a significant challenge. Therefore, accurate diagnosis and the development of personalized treatment strategies are essential for improving therapeutic efficacy.

In recent years, artificial intelligence has made remarkable progress in healthcare applications [11]. Trained on massive corpora comprising articles, monographs, and medical literature, large language models (LLMs) are capable of capturing and modeling complex patterns within language and data [12]. When integrated with multidimensional clinical information, they can support a variety of tasks, including diagnostic assistance, documentation, decision support, patient communication, as well as personalized learning and skill enhancement in educational training [13–15]. For instance, ChatGPT-4o has demonstrated broad utility in medical diagnosis, covering fields such as gastroenterology, neurology, emergency medicine, and infectious diseases, while achieving varying levels of accuracy and effectiveness. Notably, 93.4% of its responses for gastroesophageal reflux disease differentiation were deemed completely or mostly appropriate, its accuracy in neuropathology reached 84%, and its accuracy in emergency severity classification was 89% [16–20]. Recently, numerous studies have also explored the applications of LLMs in ophthalmology, highlighting their strong potential for medical question answering, examinations, and case analysis. For example, ChatGPT-4o was found to outperform attending ophthalmologists in ocular surface disease knowledge testing [21], and ChatGPT-4o demonstrated excellent performance in answering myopia-related questions and showed self-correction capabilities [22]. Furthermore, both ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Advanced successfully rewrote complex medical materials into more comprehensible content, significantly improving the readability and accuracy of patient education materials for DED [23]; similarly, another study found that GPT-4 and Baichuan 2 achieved high correctness, completeness, and safety when addressing inquiries from Chinese patients with DED [24]. However, these studies have primarily focused on knowledge-based questioning and patient education, without addressing clinical diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making. Given the complex etiology, diverse subtypes, and high demand for individualized management in DED, there remains a lack of systematic evaluation of LLMs in diagnostic classification and treatment decision-making for this condition.

Building on this background, this study aimed to evaluate the performance of LLMs in the diagnosis, classification, and treatment decision-making for DED using standardized clinical cases, and to investigate their potential utility as clinical decision-support tools. By comparing the performance of LLMs with that of junior ophthalmologists and an expert reference group, this study sought to assess the clinical reasoning capabilities of LLMs in DED decision-making and provide scientific evidence for the future development of AI-assisted DED management systems. The ultimate goal is to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of DED management. Particularly in resource-limited settings, AI technologies may eventually help compensate for the insufficient clinical experience or heavy workloads faced by ophthalmologists.

Methods

Study population and data collection

The study population comprised adult patients who attended the outpatient or inpatient clinics of the Department of Ophthalmology at Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital between August 2023 and August 2025. Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) and diagnostic testing systems, and all records were anonymized. Inclusion criteria included: a diagnosis of DED or presenting with DED-related symptoms (e.g., dryness, grittiness, pain, visual fatigue); and complete clinical information, including symptom scores (e.g., OSDI), tear function tests (e.g., Schirmer’s test, NIBUT), corneal staining scores, and prior treatment history. Exclusion criteria were as follows: severe ocular diseases that could potentially interfere with the assessment or treatment of DED (e.g., active corneal infections, advanced corneal pathology); incomplete clinical data that prevented the acquisition of key diagnostic information. After standardizing formats, normalizing variables, and performing logical checks, any abnormal or defective records were excluded, leaving a total of 100 cases for analysis (Fig. 1).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital (Approval No. K2025-09-016), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and the use of anonymized clinical data.

Preliminary model screening

Prior to the case-based analysis, the research team developed a 20-item single-choice test of high difficulty that covered key knowledge of DED, including diagnostic criteria, classification basis, treatment principles, and contraindications. The test items were derived from established guidelines, clinical practice, and the TFOS DEWS II report [3, 10, 25].

All test items were input into the LLMs, including ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and ERNIE Bot-4.5-turbo, which were accessed through their official web-based chat interfaces between July 28 and August 1, 2025. The screening was based on accuracy, with a predefined threshold of 95%. Only the models that met this criterion proceeded to the subsequent case-based testing phase.

Experimental groups and input mode

Two comparison groups were established: an LLM group and a junior ophthalmologist group. In the LLM group, each standardized clinical case was independently input into each evaluated LLM to generate diagnostic classification and treatment recommendations. The models were required to provide both the diagnosis and the treatment plan in a single response, without any additional interaction or iterative clarification. In the junior ophthalmologist group, three junior ophthalmologists participated. All were residents enrolled in the standardized Chinese ophthalmology residency training program, equivalent to PGY-2 ophthalmology residents, with approximately two years of postgraduate clinical training, including one year of dedicated ophthalmology training. All had completed formal rotations in cornea and ocular surface disease and held valid physician licenses.

To ensure fairness and comparability, all cases were converted into a standardized structured case format and presented identically to both the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists. Each case included patient age, sex, chief complaint, symptom scores, ocular surface examination findings, and auxiliary test results. For the LLMs, the context was cleared before each case to prevent information carryover. All LLMs were queried using a unified prompt: “Is treatment necessary? What type of DED does this case represent? Please recommend a treatment strategy.” The junior ophthalmologists were asked to answer the same three clinical questions based on the identical case information.

To enable fair comparison between free-text LLM outputs and human responses, all answers from both groups were transformed into a structured evaluation format comprising three dimensions: (1) whether treatment was indicated, (2) DED subtype (ADDE, EDE, mixed type, or unclassified), and (3) treatment recommendation. Treatment recommendations were further categorized into predefined clinical domains, including basic therapy, anti-inflammatory therapy, physical treatment, surgical treatment, and follow-up or precautions, allowing standardized expert grading across models and clinicians. At the same time, to account for the inherent stochasticity of LLM outputs, response stability was assessed using a BERTScore-based semantic consistency framework on a randomly selected subset of 30 cases, in which each LLM generated two independent responses under identical prompts and their semantic similarity (BERTScore F1) was averaged as an indicator of intrinsic consistency [26].

Clinical reference standard and DED subtyping

The clinical reference standard for DED in this study was established using a rule-based workflow derived from the TFOS DEWS II diagnostic guidelines. All cases first underwent a triaging step to exclude potential confounders, including infectious or allergic conjunctivitis, transient postoperative dryness, and systemic medications known to affect tear film homeostasis. Cases then proceeded to the diagnostic entry stage, where DED was diagnosed only when both subjective symptoms (OSDI ≥ 13 or DEQ-5 ≥ 6) and at least one objective marker of tear film instability were present, including non-invasive tear breakup time (NIBUT) < 10 s, corneal fluorescein staining > 5 dots, or lid margin staining ≥ 2 mm [25].

After confirmation of DED, cases were further classified according to the TFOS DEWS II pathophysiology-based subtyping framework. ADDE was determined based on reduced tear production assessed by Schirmer I test or tear meniscus height, whereas EDE was determined based on tear film instability, blink patterns, and meibomian gland assessment. Cases meeting criteria for both ADDE and EDE were classified as mixed type, and all remaining cases were categorized as unclassified.

Meibomian gland structure was assessed using infrared meibography with the Oculus Keratograph 5 M imaging system in a dark-room setting. High-resolution images of both the upper and lower eyelids were obtained for each eye. Meibomian gland morphology and dropout were graded according to the Meiboscore system described by Arita et al. [27]. Gland loss was classified as Grade 0 (no gland loss), Grade 1 (loss < 1/3 of the total gland area), Grade 2 (loss between 1/3 and 2/3), and Grade 3 (loss > 2/3). These standardized meibography-based grades were used as part of the EDE subtyping criteria in accordance with the TFOS DEWS II pathophysiological framework.

Scoring system and evaluation metrics

A dual evaluation framework was adopted to comprehensively assess the overall quality and clinical accuracy of the recommendations. The GQS was used to evaluate overall quality, with scores ranging from 1 to 5. The key assessment criteria included coherence, clinical relevance, and consistency with the provided case information. A score of 1 indicated poor quality with clear deviations from clinical standards, whereas a score of 5 indicated excellent quality, highly consistent with expert expectations and sufficient for clinical application [28] (Supplementary Table 1).

In parallel, a clinical safety score was used to evaluate the feasibility and potential risk, based on a three-tier standard: “Good” (fully accurate and clinically applicable); “Marginal” (containing minor factual errors but not affecting clinical decision-making, with controllable risk); and “Poor” (containing major errors or deviations that may mislead clinical judgment or pose potential harm) [29].

All scoring was conducted by two senior ophthalmologists, each with ≥ 10 years of clinical experience in DED. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the consensus served as the gold standard reference against which the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists were evaluated.

Assessment of decision efficiency

Decision efficiency was defined as the ratio of the GQS score to the time (in seconds) required to complete each case. This metric was designed to reflect the balance between speed and quality in clinical application. For the LLM group, the automated response time of each model was recorded, whereas for the junior ophthalmologist group, the time from case review to the completion of a full recommendation was measured. All timing was conducted in a standardized and quiet environment by independent research assistants using a digital stopwatch application on a mobile device to ensure comparability and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

For treatment accuracy comparisons, McNemar’s test was applied to evaluate the differences between the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists on the same set of cases. For DED classification consistency, Cohen’s kappa coefficients were calculated for each model relative to the expert group. Pairwise Z-tests were then used to compare the kappa values between the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists to assess statistical significance. Classification accuracy was also compared using McNemar’s test. For the analyses of GQS and efficiency, the Shapiro–Wilk test was first applied to assess normality. The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was then used for pairwise comparisons to evaluate significant differences in GQS and efficiency between the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 100 eligible patients with DED were included in this study. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the demographic characteristics (age and sex) and distribution of DED subtypes (ADDE, EDE, mixed type, and unclassified). Of these participants, 40 (40%) were male and 60 (60%) were female. Regarding age distribution, 39 patients (39%) were aged 20–39 years, 41 (41%) were aged 40–59 years, and 20 (20%) were 60 years or older. In terms of DED subtype classification, 11 patients (11%) were categorized as ADDE, 3 (3%) as EDE, 74 (74%) as mixed type, and 12 (12%) as unclassified.

Preliminary screening of LLMs

The multiple-choice test accuracies of the four candidate models were as follows: ChatGPT-4o (100%), DeepSeek-V3 (95%), Gemini-2.5-Pro (95%), and ERNIE Bot-4.5-turbo (90%) (Fig. 2). Accordingly, the first three models proceeded to the case-based testing phase.

Reliability and consistency of evaluation

The inter-rater reliability between the two senior ophthalmologists was first evaluated based on the initial classification and treatment decisions for the 100 cases. The experts demonstrated almost perfect agreement in case classification, with a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.74–0.96). Regarding the clinical decision of whether to initiate treatment, the level of consensus was even higher, yielding a kappa value of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.76–1.00).

Furthermore, the consistency of the experts’ safety scores for each study group (the LLMs and the junior ophthalmologists) is summarized in Table 1. Statistical analysis indicated that the experts maintained a highly unified standard when assessing safety performance across all groups. The weighted Cohen’s kappa values ranged from 0.696 to 0.918, representing “almost perfect agreement” according to the Landis and Koch criteria. These results confirm the robustness and reliability of the primary data used for subsequent comparative analyses (Table 1).

The internal consistency analysis across 30 clinical cases revealed that all three models maintained satisfactory response reproducibility, with mean BERTScore F1 scores exceeding 0.82. Specifically, DeepSeek-V3 demonstrated the highest consistency, with a mean F1 score of 0.8807 (95% CI: 0.8705–0.8909; SD = 0.0273). Gemini-2.5-Pro ranked second, achieving a mean F1 score of 0.8735 (95% CI: 0.8646–0.8823; SD = 0.0236). ChatGPT-4o exhibited a mean F1 score of 0.8236 (95% CI: 0.8092–0.8380; SD = 0.0386).

Accuracy of treatment necessity and classification

In determining treatment necessity, all groups demonstrated high accuracy: ChatGPT-4o (0.99), DeepSeek-V3 (0.98), Gemini-2.5-Pro (0.96), and all junior ophthalmologists (0.97) (Supplementary Table 3). Among them, ChatGPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy.

For DED classification, DeepSeek-V3 achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 92% (95% CI: 85–97) and strong agreement with expert assessment (kappa = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.67–0.93). In contrast, ChatGPT-4o (accuracy 71%, 95% CI: 61–80; kappa = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.38–0.67), junior ophthalmologist 1 (71%, 95% CI: 61–80; kappa = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.38–0.67), junior ophthalmologist 2 (65%, 95% CI: 55–74; kappa = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.30–0.60), and junior ophthalmologist 3 (70%, 95% CI: 60–79; kappa = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.37–0.66) showed significantly lower accuracy than DeepSeek-V3 (p < 0.001) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (accuracy 89%, 95% CI: 81–94; p < 0.01). Regarding consistency, the kappa values differed significantly between ChatGPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 (p = 0.006), as well as between each junior ophthalmologist and DeepSeek-V3 (p = 0.005, p < 0.001, and p = 0.004, respectively) (Supplementary Table 3).

In the overall evaluation metrics, DeepSeek-V3 exhibited the strongest performance (Macro F1 = 0.896), followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro. Overall, the LLMs demonstrated comparable, if not superior, accuracy in DED classification compared to that of the junior ophthalmologists. All groups showed extremely high specificity for mixed-type DED, though differences in sensitivity were observed. Specifically, both Gemini-2.5-Pro and DeepSeek-V3 exhibited near-perfect sensitivity for mixed-type classification (sensitivity > 0.97), whereas the sensitivity of the junior ophthalmologists in this subtype was relatively lower (Supplementary Table 4).

In the misclassified cases, the models demonstrated different error patterns. Gemini-2.5-Pro misclassified 7 ADDE cases as mixed type, 1 mixed case as ADDE, and 3 unclassified cases as EDE. DeepSeek-V3 misclassified 2 EDE, 2 ADDE, and 1 unclassified case as mixed type; it also misclassified 2 mixed cases as ADDE and 1 EDE case as unclassified. By contrast, ChatGPT-4o showed the most concentrated error pattern, predominantly misclassifying mixed cases: 16 as EDE, 12 as ADDE, and 1 ADDE case as unclassified (Fig. 3).

Quality of treatment recommendations (GQS)

In the GQS score analysis, to integrate the overall performance of the junior ophthalmologists, the median GQS score across the three junior ophthalmologists was calculated for each case. This approach was adopted because GQS is an ordinal variable, making the median more appropriate than the mean, and because the performance of the three junior ophthalmologists showed low consistency (ICC = 0.351, 95% CI: 0.225–0.477), supporting the use of the median.

Significant differences in GQS scores were observed among the groups (Fig. 4). Gemini-2.5-Pro achieved the highest score (4.87 ± 0.37), followed by DeepSeek-V3 (4.48 ± 0.59) and ChatGPT-4o (4.17 ± 0.71), with the junior ophthalmologist group scoring the lowest (3.42 ± 0.50). All pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Clinical safety

As shown in Fig. 5, Gemini-2.5-Pro demonstrated the best safety performance, with 89% of its recommendations rated as “good” and the remainder (11%) rated as “marginal.” For DeepSeek-V3 and ChatGPT-4o, the proportions of “good” responses were 60% and 37%, respectively, while “marginal” responses accounted for 40% and 62%. Junior ophthalmologist 1 showed the lowest overall performance. For junior ophthalmologist 1, only 1% of the recommendations were rated as “good,” 43% as “marginal,” and 56% as “poor.” The corresponding proportions for junior ophthalmologists 2 and 3 were 36%, 46%, and 18%, and 28%, 44%, and 28%, respectively.

Decision-making time and efficiency

In the analysis of response time, the median value of the three junior ophthalmologists was calculated for each case to represent the overall performance of the junior ophthalmologists. This approach was adopted because the response times among the three junior ophthalmologists showed low consistency (ICC = 0.544, 95% CI: 0.412–0.658), supporting the use of the median.

Across the 100 cases, Gemini-2.5-Pro required the shortest average time (16.4 ± 3.23 s), followed by ChatGPT-4o (20.0 ± 7.64 s) and DeepSeek-V3 (36.0 ± 6.59 s). The junior ophthalmologist group required the longest time (314.8 ± 56.77 s) (Fig. 6A). For efficiency scores, Gemini-2.5-Pro ranked highest (0.31 ± 0.094), followed by ChatGPT-4o (0.25 ± 0.146), DeepSeek-V3 (0.13 ± 0.034), and the junior ophthalmologist group (0.01 ± 0.003). The differences among the groups were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the performance of LLMs in clinical decision-making for DED and compared their performance with that of junior ophthalmologists. The results showed that all tested models demonstrated a solid knowledge base of DED, with a multiple-choice accuracy of ≥ 95%. In judging treatment necessity, the LLMs (with accuracies of 96%–99%) performed comparably to, or slightly better than, the junior ophthalmologists (97%). However, in the more challenging classification task, significant differences emerged: DeepSeek-V3 (92%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (89%) achieved significantly higher accuracy than both ChatGPT-4o (71%) and the junior ophthalmologist group (71%). Regarding the quality and safety of recommendations, Gemini-2.5-Pro performed best, achieving the highest GQS score (4.87 ± 0.37) and clinical safety rating (89% “good”). In terms of decision efficiency, all LLMs (16.4–36.0 s) greatly outperformed the junior ophthalmologist group (314.8 s), with Gemini-2.5-Pro being the most efficient.

These findings are consistent with recent literature highlighting the potential of LLMs in various ophthalmic subspecialties, such as glaucoma, retinal diseases, and cataract management [28, 30]. However, most prior studies have focused primarily on factual accuracy or diagnostic performance. Current research on LLMs within the DED landscape primarily addresses information delivery and health equity. Prior studies comparing general-purpose and search-oriented models (e.g., ChatGPT-4 vs. Perplexity AI) demonstrated their utility in patient counseling but revealed limitations in scientific substantiation. Additionally, documented variations in cultural sensitivity and readability across different LLMs further emphasize the necessity of addressing demographic disparities in digital healthcare guidance [31, 32]. With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence in ophthalmology, multimodal LLMs are further expanding their application to complex clinical tasks. Recent studies have demonstrated that multimodal LLMs, by integrating both textual and imaging data, can be applied to complex decision-making tasks such as intraocular lens power calculation in cataract surgery, highlighting their potential in real-world clinical settings. Compared with the unimodal LLMs evaluated in the present study, multimodal LLMs may offer additional advantages by incorporating objective imaging data in ophthalmic practice. For DED, imaging modalities such as corneal fluorescein staining, meibography, and tear film assessments play a crucial role in disease evaluation. The integration of such multimodal data with structured clinical information may further improve the accuracy, robustness, and clinical applicability of AI-assisted diagnostic and decision-making systems [33]. This study further reveals differences among LLMs in more complex tasks, such as clinical classification and treatment strategy generation. Notably, the best-performing LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Pro) demonstrated classification accuracy that was comparable to, or better than, that of junior ophthalmologists in DED. This does not challenge the central role of ophthalmologists in clinical decision-making but rather provides empirical evidence supporting the use of LLMs as efficient tools for “auxiliary screening” or “preliminary classification.” This suggests that LLMs could effectively assist ophthalmologists with routine diagnostic tasks, enabling them to devote more attention to complex case analysis and patient communication.

The outstanding classification performance of DeepSeek-V3 and Gemini-2.5-Pro may be attributable to their architectures and training data being optimized for multi-factorial and nonlinear reasoning [34]. DED classification (e.g., ADDE, EDE, mixed type) requires the integration of multiple indicators, such as symptoms, Schirmer’s test, NIBUT, and meibomian gland function, which differs from binary judgments. These models may be better at capturing subtle associations among the indicators. In contrast, the relatively weaker performance of ChatGPT-4o on this task suggests that while different LLMs perform similarly in general domains, significant variation may exist in specialized reasoning tasks. Although Gemini-2.5-Pro and DeepSeek-V3 performed well overall in classification, they tended to over-interpret borderline cases. Specifically, when indicators were near threshold values (e.g., NIBUT slightly above 10 s but close to an abnormal reading), Gemini-2.5-Pro incorporated lifestyle factors (e.g., prolonged screen use with a reduced blink rate) and nonspecific complaints (e.g., occasional dryness) to infer possible evaporative components. This led to 7 ADDE cases being misclassified as mixed type and 3 unclassified cases being misclassified as EDE. DeepSeek-V3 showed similar tendencies, referencing more lenient criteria (e.g., NIBUT < 14 s) [35], which contributed to inconsistencies. Importantly, despite occasional misclassifications, both models demonstrated prudence in their treatment recommendations, avoiding aggressive interventions for borderline indicators and thereby maintaining good clinical safety. In the management of mixed-type DED, LLMs were able not only to perform basic classification but also to integrate different pathogenic components, such as aqueous deficiency and excessive tear evaporation, and provide individualized treatment recommendations accordingly. Furthermore, in cases involving systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus), LLMs were able to incorporate systemic medical history and propose management strategies that included systemic treatment considerations and multidisciplinary follow-up, suggesting a preliminary capacity for integrative clinical reasoning.

High GQS and safety scores are essential for the clinical applicability of LLMs. Most recommendations generated by Gemini-2.5-Pro were rated as “good” and were associated with higher GQS scores, which indicated not only accurate answers but also clear, coherent, comprehensive, and clinically relevant strategies (e.g., specific drug recommendations, dosages, and lifestyle advice). Its performance was significantly better than that of the junior ophthalmologist group, whose recommendations were often overly generic (e.g., recommending “artificial tears” without specifying type or frequency), overlooked key therapeutic points, or carried potential risks, reflecting decision uncertainty caused by limited experience. Thus, LLMs can serve as tools for “standardization” and “knowledge augmentation,” thereby supporting junior ophthalmologists. However, notable limitations remain regarding the completeness and up-to-dateness of LLM treatment suggestions. For instance, in recommending corticosteroid therapy, all models rarely mentioned the crucial safety consideration of intraocular pressure monitoring. In addition, for patients with immune-related disease and corneal staining, the latest guidelines clearly indicate that scleral lenses may be an option [36–38]. However, none of the models recommended this approach, which revealed insufficient awareness of the latest clinical consensus guidelines.

This study confirms that Gemini-2.5-Pro and DeepSeek-V3 can serve as reliable clinical decision-support tools. In the diagnosis and treatment of DED, they not only provide highly accurate and consistent reference judgments for complex diagnostic tasks like subtyping but also generate high-quality, individualized treatment recommendations for clinicians to review. Despite their excellent performance, this technology should be positioned as an auxiliary tool, supporting the entire decision-making process from diagnosis to treatment. Its fundamental goal is to help ophthalmologists reduce inter-individual judgment variations and cognitive biases, thereby improving the standardization and quality of overall medical services. Furthermore, the superior diagnostic performance and recommendation quality of LLMs compared to that of junior ophthalmologists highlight their potential as effective training aids. They can provide inexperienced doctors with real-time, structured, expert-level clinical reasoning, helping them to rapidly enhance their clinical thinking skills. More importantly, the combination of the LLMs’ extremely rapid response speeds and high-quality outputs leads to orders-of-magnitude gains in efficiency. Specifically, during peak outpatient periods, LLMs can act as super-assistants, quickly generating preliminary management plans. This allows ophthalmologists to focus their valuable time on reviewing plans, patient communication, and complex decision-making, which in turn can significantly optimize workflow and alleviate clinical workload. However, this marked improvement in efficiency may also represent a double-edged sword in clinical practice. The rapid generation of high-quality recommendations could inadvertently encourage over-reliance among clinicians, particularly those with less experience, thereby potentially weakening the critical judgment required for managing complex cases. This issue is especially relevant in conditions such as DED, which are highly heterogeneous and often involve discrepancies between symptoms and signs as well as potential neuropathic mechanisms, requiring careful and nuanced clinical assessment. Therefore, in real-world applications, LLMs should be integrated within a human-in-the-loop framework, ensuring that final clinical decisions remain under physician supervision, thereby maximizing efficiency while maintaining patient safety.

This study has several limitations. First, relying on retrospective, static cases may not fully reflect the real-world performance of LLMs in dynamic clinical workflows, nor assess their impact on long-term patient outcomes. The diagnosis and management of DED is complex, requiring flexible, interactive history-taking to identify discrepancies between symptoms and signs or potential neurosensory abnormalities. However, the lack of dynamic interaction in our study may overestimate the LLMs’ decision-making capabilities. Additionally, the written test format artificially prolonged junior ophthalmologists’ response times, potentially underestimating their true clinical efficiency. Thus, our results primarily highlight the potential of LLMs as decision-support tools. Future studies should incorporate real-world data and dynamic interactions to evaluate their robustness. Second, the single-center, unbalanced dataset (predominantly mixed type DED) limits the generalizability of the findings to different geographic regions and heterogeneous populations, necessitating future multicenter validation. Third, the “black-box” nature of LLMs restricts their interpretability. Future integration of Explainable AI techniques could improve their clinical trustworthiness. Fourth, the manual grading of meibomian glands and corneal staining introduces subjectivity. Future multimodal LLMs could standardize these assessments by directly integrating imaging data [39, 40]. Finally, the LLMs were not fine-tuned for medical applications, and the results reflect the performance of specific versions. Furthermore, the models were accessed via web interfaces, limiting parameter control and potential output stability. However, our model-agnostic evaluation framework allows for future extension through API integration and parameter optimization, thereby overcoming this interface limitation and ensuring clinical reliability.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that advanced LLMs, particularly Gemini-2.5-Pro and DeepSeek-V3, exhibit substantial potential in the clinical management of DED. When evaluating standardized cases, they showed superior performance compared to junior ophthalmologists in classification accuracy, recommendation quality, and decision-making efficiency. They may serve as effective decision-support tools for ophthalmologists, particularly junior ophthalmologists and primary care providers. However, it is important to emphasize that LLMs should function as enhancers rather than replacements for clinical judgment. When combined with ophthalmologists’ expertise, LLMs hold the potential to more efficient, precise, and personalized care for patients.
