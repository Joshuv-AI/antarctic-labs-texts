Renally Generated Ammonia as a Cause of Hyperammonaemic Non‐Hepatic Encephalopathy
Authors: Victoria E. Nkunu, Kevin Y. L. Mak, Kuldeep Dodia, Fraulein D. Morales, Alicia K. J. Chan, R. Todd Alexander, Peter M. Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEncephalopathy with high ammonia levels is most commonly associated with cirrhosis or urea cycle disorders, but it can also result from renal ammoniagenesis when renal excretion of ammonium is impaired. In the literature, this has been shown to occur in the presence of (1) urinary diversion to a segment of intestine; (2) urea‐splitting microorganism overgrowth in the bladder or (3) renal tubular acidosis. However, hyperammonaemic encephalopathy can still occur without any of these overt factors. We present the case of a 76‐year‐old male who developed two episodes of hyperammonaemic encephalopathy in the setting of high output ileostomy. The first instance occurred with acute kidney injury (AKI) in the setting of post‐operative sepsis, with vomiting causing hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis that subsequently evolved into normal anion gap metabolic acidosis (NAGMA). The patient recovered but presented months later with AKI, NAGMA and elevated ammonia. This resolved with fluids and bicarbonate replacement. Hypokalaemia, acidosis, corticosteroids and aldosterone stimulate renal ammoniagenesis. In some patients, renal ammonium excretion is sufficient under baseline conditions but not with decreased renal perfusion and increased ammoniagenesis. In such situations, impaired excretion of renally generated ammonium is suggested by elevated serum ammonia level accompanied by more‐positive‐than‐expected urine anion gap in the setting of hypokalaemia or acidosis. Identifying patients who might benefit from immediate correction of these factors is important to permit attempts to suppress renal ammoniagenesis.

Introduction

Hyperammonaemia is classically associated with ammonia (NH3) liberation in the gut that is not adequately metabolised by hepatic urea cycle enzymes in the setting of chronic liver disease. First‐line therapy is lactulose, which promotes acidification of the colon and expulsion of ammonium ions (NH4 +) and the bacteria producing them. However, NH4 + can also be generated and secreted into the urine by the kidney, but under exceptional circumstances, ammonia can escape the confines of the urinary tract and result in systemic hyperammonaemia, even to a degree causing coma or death [1]. Thus, the kidney is a clinically significant producer of ammonia that deserves more attention.

In this case report, we describe a patient with high ileostomy output who curiously developed hyperammonaemic encephalopathy without any evidence of chronic liver disease or urea cycle disorder.

Ileostomies can be complicated by excessively high output, leading to volume contraction causing acute kidney injury (AKI), as well as metabolic derangements like hypokalaemia‐alkalosis or hyperkalaemia‐acidosis [2]. High output from an ileostomy results in loss of sodium and chloride ions, and subsequent activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system promotes sodium resorption via epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) in the principal cells of the collecting ducts, giving rise to a lumen‐negative electrical potential that favours secretion of either K+ or NH4 + [3]. Hypokalaemia and metabolic acidosis stimulate renal uptake of glutamine to produce HCO3 − and NH4 +, favouring secretion of NH4 + [4].

It is unusual for kidney‐generated NH4 + to leave the urinary tract in large quantities, requiring one of three exceptional circumstances: (1) urinary diversion to an intestinal pouch or tract that is more likely to absorb ammonia than the urinary bladder; (2) overgrowth of urea‐splitting microorganisms in the bladder, with or without urinary obstruction; or (3) renal tubular acidosis (RTA), which interferes with acidification and excretion of NH4 + by the kidney.

Hyperammonaemic encephalopathy due to a urinary diversion was first described in 1972 for a 36‐year‐old woman with ureterosigmoidostomy at the age of 8 [5]. Notably, this patient was in a coma with an ammonia level of 146 μM, and she was hypokalaemic with a mild respiratory alkalosis, pH 7.45. Following replacement of electrolytes and fluids, her ammonia level dropped sharply and she regained consciousness. Since then, there have been many similar cases reported, including a 41‐year‐old woman with a remote ureterorectal diversion [1] and another with an ileal conduit [6], both of whom had concurrent hypokalaemia and metabolic acidosis that would stimulate renal ammoniagenesis. Intestinal mucosa is more likely to uptake urea and ammonia than the native bladder, and animal studies suggest that colonic uptake is greater than small intestinal [7].

Another notable cause of hyperammonaemia is urinary tract infection with urea‐splitting organisms such as Proteus mirabilis , Klebsiella pneumonia or Pseudomonas aeruginosa [8]. Hyperammonaemia has also been observed in UTIs with common non‐urea‐splitting organisms in the Enterobacteriaceae family such as E. coli , with urinary obstruction thought to be a contributor [9]. In almost all cases, the potassium and acid–base status of such patients was not reported, though since the purported mechanism of hyperammonaemia is urea‐splitting, such factors may not be relevant. Given the common occurrence of bacteriuria and urinary tract infections, however, one has to wonder whether other unrecorded factors are at work.

Distal renal tubular acidosis (RTA), a genetic or acquired defect in the acidification of urine in the collecting ducts, is also associated with hyperammonaemia. In a literature review of distal RTA patients, plasma ammonia level was roughly correlated with the degree of acidosis (as assessed by plasma bicarbonate level) [9]. RTAs can also be an adverse effect of various medications, and there are case reports of concurrent drug‐induced RTA and hyperammonaemia for acetazolamide [10], deferasirox [11], topiramate [12], zonisamide [13] and omeprazole [14]. Of these, acetazolamide, deferasirox and topiramate inhibit carbonic anhydrase to cause a proximal RTA, whereas zonisamide and omeprazole are associated with distal RTA. Topiramate and zonisamide were found to increase the risk of hyperammonaemia in 2944 children with epilepsy [15]. As a class, anti‐epileptics are associated with hyperammonaemic encephalopathy, and of these, valproic acid is the most common culprit, especially when combined with other anti‐epileptics [12, 13, 15, 16, 17]. We hypothesise that anti‐epileptics alter kidney function in subtle ways even if they do not overtly cause an RTA, being designed specifically to inhibit the activity of ion channels.

In summary, there are many known cases of hyperammonaemic encephalopathy caused by renally generated ammonia, and the phenomenon may be more prevalent than is realised. Herein we present the case of a patient who twice experienced hyperammonaemic encephalopathy without cirrhosis or urea cycle disorder, and without any of the above risk factors known to predispose to non‐hepatic hyperammonaemic encephalopathy (urinary diversion, UTI ± obstruction and RTA).

Case

A 76‐year‐old man had a subtotal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis for superficially invasive adenocarcinoma of the rectosigmoid with polyposis and high‐grade dysplasia, which was subsequently complicated by ileorectal anastomotic leak and intraabdominal sepsis. Five days later, he underwent laparotomy and resection of the ileorectal anastomosis with creation of an end ileostomy (POD 0), after which he was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). Prior to the OR, he vomited and became drowsy and confused, and then 3 L of nasogastric output was suctioned. He had a persistently decreased level of consciousness for the next 25 days. He initially displayed metabolic alkalosis secondary to the vomiting, but this evolved into metabolic acidosis, reaching a bicarbonate nadir of 12 mM on Day 11 (Table 1). On Day 10, he underwent another repeat laparotomy with washout of pelvic abscess and debridement of necrotic rectal stump and pelvic tissue. After this, his metabolic acidosis slowly improved, and he became alert again by Day 26. During this time, liver synthetic function remained intact as indicated by a maximum INR of 1.3. He did experience a cholestatic injury indicated by a bilirubin level of 86 μM accompanied by elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) of 204 U/L (reference range 40–120 U/L), but this was not due to shock liver as alanine transaminase (ALT) remained low at 31 U/L maximum (reference range < 60 U/L).

The patient had a complicated course of recovery but was discharged to a rehabilitation facility after about 3 months and then returned home. Approximately 10 months later, he was admitted to a general medicine ward with confusion and falls at home. He was noted to have a severe acute kidney injury (AKI), with creatinine at presentation of 645 μM due to high ostomy output (Table 2), complicated by hypovolemic hyponatremia (sodium nadir 123 mM), though his lactate level was within normal range at presentation. Given persistent confusion and new asterixis on exam after the initial fluid resuscitation, an ammonia level was ordered which was elevated at 82 μM. His liver synthetic function was also normal—bilirubin 10 μM, international normalised ratio (INR) 1.1. His liver enzymes were in the normal range—alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 7 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 23 U/L and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 96 U/L.

He received intravenous bicarbonate and fluids, and lactulose was started for hyperammonaemia with encephalopathy, and his cognition and biochemical indices continued to improve (Table 2). Unfortunately, as the patient was transitioned off IV fluids, his kidney function started to worsen again as he was still experiencing high ostomy output. Lactulose treatment was discontinued after 7 days, as it was felt that it was increasing ostomy output. The purported mechanism of action of lactulose, acidifying the colon to prevent colonic uptake of ammonia, was not relevant in this patient with high output end ileostomy and absent colon. Intravenous fluids resumed, along with bicarbonate replacement. With time, as his kidney function improved, his ammonia levels decreased and his confusion cleared entirely, and he was discharged home.

Given the elevated ammonia levels in the absence of cirrhosis or acute liver failure, a geneticist was consulted to assess for other etiologies, including urea cycle disorders. Investigations on Day 6 were negative (normal plasma amino acids, urine organic acids, urine amino acids, urine orotic acid). Of note, the glutamine level, which is usually elevated in acute exacerbations of urea cycle disorders, was within normal range at 617 μM (439–893). Also of note, it would be highly unusual to diagnose a genetic cause of hyperammonaemia in a patient in their seventies.

The patient improved and was discharged home on potassium and 2 g per day bicarbonate supplementation. Interestingly, the patient was admitted once again 6 months later with high ostomy output with a serum creatinine of exactly 800 μM, but with less severe acidosis and no hyperammonaemia or confusion on exam, and the AKI resolved quickly with fluids. After this admission, the patient was never again admitted with AKI, bicarbonate supplementation was stopped, and the patient has been stable in independent seniors living for the past 3 years.

Discussion

In summary, our patient twice developed confusion associated with hyperammonaemia in the absence of chronic liver disease or urea cycle disorder. Both episodes were associated with decreased perfusion (sepsis or hypovolaemia) and AKI, as well as electrolyte abnormalities, specifically, hypokalaemia (in the first episode) and metabolic acidosis (in both). Given that it is unlikely that intestinal ammonia generation and absorption were significant in this patient with no colon and a high output end ileostomy, we surmised that the elevated ammonia levels were renally generated, with hypokalaemia and metabolic acidosis further stimulating renal ammoniagenesis. However, there were none of the factors known to interfere with renal clearance of ammonia (see Introduction): numerous CT scans of the abdomen and pelvis never provided any indication of the presence of urinary diversion, obstruction or kidney stone formation; urine cultures throughout admission never showed significant bacterial growth; the patient was not on any medications associated with hyperammonaemia [17]; and under normal circumstances, the patient did not have any acid–base imbalance that would suggest an underlying renal tubular acidosis.

Impaired excretion of renally generated ammonium and a more‐positive‐than‐expected urine anion gap are criteria of distal renal tubular acidosis, which results in a failure to acidify urine to pH < 5.3 due to a defect in proton transport. However, urinalysis consistently showed a pH of 5.0, which is the lowest pH observable by our hospital urinalysis testing. Thus, our patient did not have a defect in urine acidification consistent with distal RTA, even though there were many functional abnormalities, such as severe acidosis, normokalaemia during severe acidosis, and hyperammonaemia, that would have been consistent with distal RTA. Instead, we suggest that our patient had a more subtle defect in ammonium excretion unrelated to acidification and propose that it could be called ‘incomplete ammoniagenic renal tubular acidosis’.

In the setting of AKI, our patient was unable to sufficiently excrete renally generated ammonium, as evidenced by a more‐positive‐than‐expected urine anion gap (urine [Na+] + [K+] − [Cl−]) [18], given the degree of acidosis. After volume resuscitation reversing the AKI, the urine anion gap became slightly more negative, suggesting an increase in ammonium excretion, though this may have been offset by an increase in ammonium generation, as serum ammonia levels remained elevated into Day 4 of the admission (Table 2). By Days 8 and 9, metabolic acidosis had resolved, thereby removing the stimulus for renal ammoniagenesis and excretion, as evidenced by a strongly positive urine anion gap.

Renal ammoniagenesis and ammonium excretion are complex processes for which the molecular details continue to emerge [4]. Proximal convoluted tubule cells are stimulated to catabolise glutamine by corticosteroids, angiotensin II, aldosterone, acidaemia and hypokalaemia. Glutamine is broken down to NH4 + and bicarbonate, with a significant amount of NH4 + secreted into the tubule lumen by the apical NHE3 Na+/H+ exchanger [4].

NH4 + ions move through the renal tubule until they reach the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, where they can be transported into the tubular epithelial cell by the Na+‐K+‐2Cl− co‐transporter, isoform 2 (NKCC2 or SLC12A1) [4], the target of loop diuretics. Because of a similar ionic radius, NH4 + can substitute for K+, so that loop diuretics cause both K+ and NH4 + to be retained in the tubular lumen. Re‐uptake of NH4 + in the thick ascending limb sets up a counter‐current gradient with increasing concentrations towards the medulla, enhancing cumulative secretion of NH4 + as the filtrate progresses through the collecting ducts [19]. One study has indicated an important role for sulfatides in maintaining the medullary NH4 + gradient [20].

Rhesus (Rh) glycoproteins, Rh C glycoprotein (Rhcg, SLC42A3) and Rh B glycoprotein (Rhbg, SLC42A2) have been found to be essential for transporting both NH3 and NH4 + into both principal and intercalated cells of the collecting ducts, with Rhcg most important for secreting NH3 into the tubule lumen [4]. Effective secretion of ammonia into the collecting ducts requires urine acidification by H+ pumps and exchangers, which is impaired in classic familial distal RTA. Rhcg and Rhbg are supposed to be upregulated in conditions that require increased ammonium secretion, including metabolic acidosis and hypokalaemia.

There are multiple sites along the proximal and distal tubules in which a subtle genetic defect or drug interaction could reduce the efficiency of ammonium excretion. It is important to note that decreased renal function due to AKI or chronic kidney disease (CKD) also impairs ammonium excretion, though these conditions do not routinely cause hyperammonaemia because renal ammoniagenesis is also compromised. Accumulation of ammonia only occurs when there is an additional defect that causes an uncoupling of ammonium excretion from ammonium production, resulting in a more severe acidosis than expected for the degree of renal impairment. In our patient, decreased renal perfusion due to sepsis or severe hypovolemia certainly contributed to the impairment in ammonium excretion. Thus, this phenomenon is most likely to occur in critically ill patients with compromised global perfusion, impacting not only kidney function, but also liver and cognitive function as well, making it challenging to separate out hyperammonaemic encephalopathy from a complex clinical picture. Technically, our patient could have also been diagnosed as having uremic encephalopathy. It is interesting to speculate that, since not all patients with AKI or CKD present with uremic encephalopathy, perhaps it is those with impaired excretion of renally generated ammonium that do.

Conclusion

Severe cases of hyperammonaemia cause confusion, coma, or even death, so it is important to consider renal ammoniagenesis as a possible culprit even in non‐cirrhotic patients. While it may be difficult to surmise all possible genetic causes or drug effects that could give rise to impaired renal ammonium excretion, it is relatively straightforward to address the physiologic factors that increase renal ammoniagenesis. First of all, it is important to identify and discontinue any medications known to be associated with hyperammonaemia [17]. Next, prompt correction of hypokalaemia or metabolic acidosis is important to minimise proximal tubule ammoniagenesis.

It is possible that renally generated ammonia might also contribute to hepatic encephalopathy in cirrhosis patients, so it should be considered in this setting as well.
