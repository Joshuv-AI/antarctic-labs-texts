Participant-centered tolerability of transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation: insights from two crossover studies
Authors: Gabriel Gonzalez, Maximillian O’Malley, Jessica Bolanos, Danielle Taylor, Lisa M. McTeague, Adrian Canepa, Marlon L. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a promising noninvasive technique for modulating parasympathetic activity, yet optimal stimulation parameters and comprehensive tolerability profiles remain undefined. Traditional assessments of taVNS tolerability have focused narrowly on safety and adverse events, overlooking participant comfort and subjective experience, which are critical factors for adherence and clinical translation.MethodsWe conducted two randomized crossover trials in healthy adults to evaluate the tolerability of taVNS across different stimulation parameters. Study 1 compared two monophasic duty cycles (30 s on/30 s off vs. 10 s on/10 s off), while study 2 compared monophasic and biphasic waveforms. Tolerability was assessed using questionnaires and semi-structured interviews, with secondary outcomes including heart rate variability (HRV), pain sensitivity, and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) measures of corticospinal excitability and intracortical inhibition/facilitation.ResultsBoth studies demonstrated high tolerability across all taVNS conditions, with low median ratings for pain and irritation. The most common sensations were tingling, tickling, or pricking, reported by the majority of participants but rated as mild. Qualitative analysis revealed nuanced preferences related to comfort, perceived effectiveness, and ease of adjustment to stimulation. No significant differences in secondary physiological outcomes were observed between conditions, though trends suggested parameter-specific effects on HRV and pain sensitivity.ConclusionsOur findings highlight that taVNS is well tolerated in healthy adults, regardless of duty cycle application or waveform, when tolerability is defined to include comfort and participant experience. Incorporating these dimensions into tolerability assessments may enhance protocol optimization and support broader adoption of taVNS in clinical practice. Further research in clinical populations is warranted.Trial registrationhttps://clinicaltrials.gov, identifiers (NCT06381102 and NCT06614933).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42234-026-00206-y.

Background

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is a promising intervention for numerous conditions, including: epilepsy, depression, obesity, heart failure, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, migraine, cluster headache, peripheral neuropathy, and other chronic pain conditions (Hilz 2022; Yang et al. 2024; Marsal et al. 2021; Courties et al. 2022; Straube et al. 2015; Costa et al. 2024). Initially VNS was only available as a surgical implant (Hilz 2022; Yap et al. 2020); however, over the last 20 years, noninvasive VNS has proliferated (Hilz 2022). The two most common forms of noninvasive VNS are transcutaneous cervical and transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS). While both these methods may be effective in stimulating the vagus nerve, stimulation in the cervical region yields additional challenges due to the deep positioning of the vagus nerve in the cervical region, where it lies within the carotid sheath beneath the sternocleidomastoid muscle, making selective stimulation difficult (Yap et al. 2020). Therefore, taVNS may represent a more favorable stimulation approach, owing to its anatomically superficial access to the vagus nerve at the ear.

taVNS involves stimulation of the tragus and/or cymba concha parts of the ear as these areas are innervated by the ascending auricular branch of the vagus nerve (ABVN) (Yap et al. 2020; Evensen et al. 2022; Tavares-Figueiredo et al. 2024). The ABVN contains sensory vagal endings (Evensen et al. 2022) making it an optimal location for transcutaneous stimulation and activation of the parasympathetic nervous system. Like the invasive version of VNS, transcutaneous stimulation of the ABVN in taVNS activates the nucleus tractus solitarii (Evensen et al. 2022; Atanackov et al. 2025; Prott et al. 2025; Duff et al. 2024; Geng et al. 2022), resulting in downstream activation of brain areas associated with heart rate variability (Atanackov et al. 2025; Geng et al. 2022), pain perception (Tavares-Figueiredo et al. 2024; Prott et al. 2025; Duff et al. 2024), increased intracortical inhibition (Evensen et al. 2022), and emotional/mood regulation (Evensen et al. 2022; Croft et al. 2025). taVNS also has a favorable safety profile according to recent reviews (Yap et al. 2020; Redgrave et al. 2018). The most common side effects associated with taVNS include skin irritation, itching, and transient pain over the stimulation site (Yap et al. 2020; Redgrave et al. 2018). Other side effects occurring in < 1% of participants include nasopharyngitis, headache, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, facial drooping, heart palpitations, and vocal hoarseness (Yap et al. 2020; Redgrave et al. 2018).

While taVNS has the potential to be a simple, safe, and effective intervention, its tolerability is understudied. In several studies taVNS tolerability has been deemed to be favorable from reported adverse events (Yap et al. 2020; Tavares-Figueiredo et al. 2024; Atanackov et al. 2025; Gerges et al. 2024). Although adverse event reporting is essential, participant comfort and subjective experience are also important components of tolerability and may influence uptake and treatment adherence with taVNS. Because taVNS is applied utilizing small electrodes to fit the tragus and cymba concha, there is a relatively high current density (amperes per square meter) that is more likely to produce noxious or unpleasant sensations at higher intensities. Furthermore, ion build up may occur more rapidly with smaller electrodes and with longer pulse trains of stimulation, potentially increasing risk for adverse skin reactions (Fary and Briffa 2011). Therefore, participant tolerability may limit the ability to achieve therapeutic doses or may adversely affect compliance with taVNS treatment protocols, as several studies report mixed results (Evensen et al. 2022; Tavares-Figueiredo et al. 2024; Prott et al. 2025; Badran et al. 2022).

The parameters used for taVNS may also have a significant effect on tolerability. While several recent studies have reported physiologic effects of specific taVNS parameters (Costa et al. 2024; Atanackov et al. 2025; Duff et al. 2024; Geng et al. 2022; Badran et al. 2022, 2018; Borges et al. 2019; Capone et al. 2015; Clancy et al. 2014; De Couck et al. 2017; Dumoulin et al. 2021; Gerges et al. 2025; Roy et al. 2023; Mertens et al. 2022; Midden et al. 2023), the optimal stimulation settings have yet to be established (Yap et al. 2020). Most studies to date have reported using monophasic waveform, often decided by the specifications of the stimulation device, with less studies using biphasic (Yap et al. 2020). Application of monophasic waveforms has a risk of ion build up that may result in skin damage (Fary and Briffa 2011; Yuan et al. 2021), thus needing a pulsed delivery or duty cycles below 100%. Biphasic waveforms do not have this risk, due to alternating polarity that prevents ion accumulation (Wang et al. 2022), and therefore can be delivered continuously. Due to these considerations, an improved understanding of how duty cycle and waveforms affect tolerability would help parameter optimization and guide dosage considerations in future studies or clinical application of taVNS. Thus, the aim of this research was to investigate participant tolerance and experience with differing duty cycle applications using a monophasic waveform and to compare the tolerability between monophasic and biphasic waveforms.

Methods

This research consisted of two consecutive studies: both utilized crossover designs with two visits. The first study investigated differences in tolerability between two different applications of 50% duty cycles, with on/off times of 30 s (condition A) and 10 s (condition B) using a square monophasic waveform. The second study investigated differences in tolerability between monophasic and biphasic waveforms. Tolerability was assessed via questionnaire and semi structured interviews. We also explored the effects on heart rate variability (HRV), pain sensitivity, and corticospinal inhibition in subgroups of each study (n = 12). Both studies took place in Miami, FL in a university laboratory setting.

Participants

Healthy individuals were recruited from an academic health care system (i.e., students, clinicians, and faculty) using flyers. The first study, on monophasic duty cycle, took place between April and September of 2024, while the second study (monophasic vs. biphasic) occurred during December 2024 to May 2025. The inclusion criteria consisted of age 18–80 and English speaking. The exclusion criteria included the presence chronic pain, acute pain of intensity greater than 3/10, poorly controlled inflammatory conditions (e.g., autoimmune disease), any unstable medical condition or medical contraindication, pregnancy, implants (i.e., pacemaker or cochlear), head or neck metastasis, recent ear trauma, history of epilepsy, history of autonomic nervous system dysfunction, and the presence of cognitive impairment or language barrier that impairs full autonomy in the consent process or in the ability to participate in detailed interviews. A sample size of 30 was chosen for both studies as this was estimated to provide 80% power to detect small to moderate effect size for preferences between conditions (g = 0.25, α = 0.05) and sufficient to reach saturation with qualitative analysis.

Study design and stimulus parameters

In study 1, a duty cycle of 30 s on and 30 s off (condition A) was compared to 10 s on and 10 s off (condition B), with both conditions using the same monophasic wave form, pulse width of 500 μs, frequency of 25 Hz, intensity of 2 mA, and duration of 60 min using the Soterix Medical taVNS Stimulator. All participants received both conditions, in randomized order, and separated by at least one week to provide an adequate washout period.

In study 2, the monophasic wave form used a pulse width of 500 μs, frequency of 25 Hz, duty cycle of 50% (30 s on/30 s off). The biphasic wave form used a pulse width of 500 μs, frequency of 25 Hz, duty cycle of 100%. Both the monophasic and biphasic conditions were applied with an intensity of 2.5 mA and duration of 60 min using the Digitimer DS8R stimulator (Digitimer, Welwyn Garden City, UK). These shared parameters were chosen as constants as they have demonstrated effectiveness in modulating targeted physiologic processes and are within the ranges of taVNS parameters included in a 2020 review (Yap et al. 2020; Badran et al. 2018). The intensity was set at 2.0 mA and 2.5 mA for studies 1 and 2, respectively, as this was estimated to be within the therapeutic threshold and consistent with dosing parameters in prior studies (Badran et al. 2019). All parameters aside from the length of pulse trains in study 1 and waveform in study 2 were held constant in order to focus specifically on tolerability pertaining to these differences in current delivery. For all conditions in both studies, stimulation was delivered through self-adhesive 9 × 12 mm hydrogel stimulation electrodes (RELI-stick, Soterix Medical) with the anode placed over the cymba concha and the cathode on the anterior tragus to minimize off-target stimulation (Black et al. 2023).

Outcomes

TaVNS tolerability

TaVNS tolerability was the primary outcome for both studies. Tolerability was assessed immediately after each condition using by an unvalidated questionnaire that included questions on ear pain, irritation, discomfort, and any sensations felt either during or after application of taVNS (Appendix 1). Participants were asked to rate the intensity of these sensations on a 0–10 scale with 0 representing none and 10 representing the worst imaginable. Additionally, semi-structured interviews were used to gain additional contextual information on participant preferences and differences in experience between conditions. Thus, questions regarding description of the sensation, changes in sensations throughout taVNS application, comfortability, feelings on taVNS personal usage, and preferences between conditions were also included (Appendix 1).

Heart rate variability (HRV)

HRV was also assessed to explore if the effect on HRV varied between conditions. HRV variables root mean square of successive differences between normal heartbeats (RMSSD) and the standard deviation of the inter-beat interval of normal sinus beats (SDNN) were measured using the Polar H10 (Polar, Kempele, Finland) and Kubios HRV software (Kubios, Kuopio, Finland) for 10 min prior and 10 min post taVNS for all conditions with the participant in quiet sitting.

Quantitative sensory testing (QST)

QST involved both thermal and mechanosensory testing based on an established testing protocol (Rolke et al. 2006). Thermal testing included cold and hot pain threshold assessment using the Medoc TSA2 Air (Medoc, Israel). Mechanosensory testing included mechanical pain threshold for pinprick stimuli, wind up ratio, and pain pressure threshold (PPT) using the MRC Innovations PinPrick stimulators (MRC Innovations, USA) and the Medoc computerized pressure pain algometer (Medoc, Israel). All QST testing was done on the left forearm with the participant seated upright in a chair, and these measures were performed pre and post each condition in a subset of the sample.

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation Measures (TMS): Short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) and intracortical facilitation (ICF)

Paired pulse transcranial magnetic stimulation (ppTMS) protocols were used to measure intracortical inhibition and facilitation (Burke et al. 2019). Participants were seated in an armchair with the head resting on the headrest throughout the visit. TMS was delivered through a MCB70 angled focal figure-of-eight shaped magnetic coil connected to a Magpro X100 (MagVenture, Alpharetta, GA). Motor evoked potentials (MEP) were recorded with the PowerLab system (AD Instruments, New Zealand) using surface EMG Ag/AgCl electrodes placed over the right first dorsal interosseous muscle in a belly tendon montage. The coil position resulting in both visible twitch of the target muscle and the highest peak-to-peak amplitude of the MEP (“hotspot”) was marked using the TMS navigation system (Localite, Germany) to ensure accurate coil positioning throughout the testing (Wong et al. 2025). Confounding factors

Potential confounding variables (e.g., hours of sleep, quality of sleep, caffeine intake, exercise participation, substance use, etc.) were asked at the beginning of each session for both studies. These results can be found in Appendix 2. In study 1, the only significant difference between conditions was in hours of sleep. This was due to two outliers reporting 2.5 and 11 h of sleep prior to condition A and B, respectively. These two outliers were kept in all data analyses as they did not significantly affect results. All other baseline comparisons of confounders across conditions were non-significant for study 1 and two and unlikely to influence the observed effects.

Cognitive/affective questionnaires were also included as confounding factors in both studies as affective states can influence how the brain responds to neuromodulation (Schutter et al. 2023). In study 1, the Perseverative Thinking Questionnaire (PTQ) was administered, and for study 2 the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) was given. The PTQ is a 15-item, Likert-type scale designed to measure the broad idea of repetitive negative thought. PTQ total scores range from 0–60, representing level of repetitive negative thinking, with higher scores indicative of more frequent and intensive preservative thought (Ehring et al. 2011). The PANAS is a 20 item self-report, Likert-type scale designed to measure the recent or general state of positive and negative emotion (Watson et al. 1988). Participants were asked to answer both questionnaires with regard to how they were feeling in the moment.

Interviews and qualitative analysis

The interviews were audio recorded and transcribed for analyses using rapid qualitative analysis (Hamilton 2020). Rapid qualitative analysis was systematically applied in this study according to established protocols (Hamilton 2020). The interview guide was used to create structured templates and matrix displays to facilitate data condensation, synthesis, and theme development. Templates were developed collaboratively by the team (MW and GG) and pilot tested to ensure consistency, usability, and relevance. Once consistency was demonstrated, MW completed summaries of transcripts. The summaries were aggregated by MW to populate the matrices that enabled systematic comparison between participants. The interview guides and participant matrices can be found in Appendices 5, 6a, & 6b.

Quantitative data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize study samples, and Chi-square goodness-of-fit tests were performed to determine if the distribution of participants’ preference (e.g., condition A, B, or no preference) differed from an equal distribution across conditions, with significance set at p < 0.05. Both studies were designed and powered for assessing tolerability; however, the sample size provided 75% power (α = 0.05) to detect a medium effect (d ≥ 0.5) for differences in HRV, QST, and TMS measures between conditions. Therefore, we focus on descriptive statistics (i.e., means, medians, standard deviations, and confidence intervals), and we provide tests of significance, with reported effect sizes, to serve as a useful guide for future studies. Wilcoxon signed-ranks test was used to assess for differences between conditions for secondary outcomes in both studies with significance set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) v28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

taVNS tolerability

In study 1, taVNS tolerability was similar between conditions A and condition B. Seven individuals reported ear pain during condition A and eight during condition B (median ± SD) numerical pain rating scale (NRS) value of 2.00 ± 2.06 and 3.00 ± 1.69, respectively) (Table 3). A feeling of tingling, tickling, and/or pricking during taVNS was by far the most prevalent shared sensation between the two conditions with 26 and 25 individuals reporting this for conditions A and B, respectively. However, the median NRS for irritation from tingling, tickling, and/or pricking was low and identical for both (2.00 ± 1.77). At the second session, participants were asked if they experienced pain after the first session, and no participants reported pain after condition A and only 1 individual reported pain after condition B with an NRS score of 3.

In study 2, taVNS tolerability was also similar between monophasic and biphasic waveforms. Five individuals reported ear pain during taVNS for both monophasic and biphasic. Median NRS value for ear pain during taVNS were 2.00 ± 1.79 and 2.00 ± 2.07, respectively. The sensation of tingling, tickling, and/or pricking during taVNS was again the most prevalent shared sensation between the two waveforms with 26 and 24 individuals reporting this for monophasic and biphasic, respectively. Again, the median NRS for irritation from tingling, tickling, and/or pricking was low (Table 2). One participant reported pain after monophasic with an NRS score of 6, and two individuals reported pain after biphasic with a median NRS score of 1.50 ± 0.71. Adverse events and side effects

In study 1, one participant did not complete the crossover session due to reports of headache and anxiety post visit 1 (Condition B) that may or may not have been associated with the stimulation. Eight additional participants reported side effects, with 4 reports during condition A and 4 reports during condition B. Side effects from condition A included changes in hunger, itching, headache, and runny nose with a median NRS intensity of 1.50 ± 1.71. Side effects for condition B included a sense of calm and focus, tiredness and sleepiness, headache, and itchy ear with a median NRS intensity of 4.50 ± 2.94. For study 2, no adverse events or side effects were reported during or after monophasic or biphasic conditions.

Heart rate variability

In study 1, the median heart rate change was slightly lower with condition B (greater decrease), while the median SDNN and RMSSD change scores was higher for condition A (Table 3). Despite the small trend suggesting a greater HRV response with condition A, none of the condition comparisons were significant and all had negligible effect sizes (Cohen’s d < 0.2) (Table 3). Baseline HRV results can be found in Appendix 7.

In study 2, the median SDNN and RMSSD change score was greater for monophasic compared to biphasic; however, none of the condition comparisons were significant, and all comparisons had negligible effect sizes (Cohen’s d < 0.2) (Table 4).

Quantitative sensory testing

In study 1, the median change score for PPT was negative for condition A (greater sensitivity to pressure pain) and positive for condition B (decreased sensitivity to pressure pain). All other QST measures had similar changes between conditions (Table 3). While none of the condition comparisons for QST change scores were significant, PPT had a moderate effect size for both pressure and NRS.

In study 2, the median change score for wind up demonstrated a slight increase for monophasic and decrease for biphasic with median values of 0.28 ± 0.54 and −0.10 ± 0.09, respectively. This difference in wind up change scores between monophasic and biphasic conditions was significant (p = 0.028) and had a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.636) (Table 4).

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

TMS measures in study 1 were mixed with condition A showing both increased SICI (% inhibition) and ICF (% facilitation), while condition B demonstrated a shift towards greater facilitation with decreased SICI and increased ICF. RMT and spTMS remained constant with both conditions. None of condition comparisons were significant, and all had small to negligible effect sizes except for RMT (Cohen’s d = 0.592) (Table 3).

Study 2 also demonstrated mixed results for TMS measures. RMT and spTMS remained consistent in both monophasic and biphasic. The monophasic condition was associated with decreased SICI and increased ICF, while the biphasic condition demonstrated increased SICI and decreased ICF. Again, none of the condition comparisons were significant, although there was a moderate effect size for SICI (Cohen’s d = 0.623) (Table 4).

Perception threshold

In study 2, the intensity at which participants first perceived the stimulation (perception threshold) was also assessed. There were no differences in the perception threshold (p = 0.929) between the monophasic waveform (median of 1.00 ± 0.45) and biphasic waveform (median of 1.00 ± 0.40). Additionally, Spearman’s rank-order correlation was conducted to assess the relationship between perception threshold and severity of irritation from tingling, tickling, and/or pricking. The correlation was not significant for monophasic or biphasic conditions (rs(26) = −2.68, p = 0.185 and rs(24) = −2.02, p = 0.343, respectively).

Discussion

To our knowledge, the crossover studies in this project were the first to primarily focus on taVNS tolerability in terms of patient comfort, preference, and experience between differing duty cycle applications of monophasic waveform and between monophasic and biphasic waveforms. In study 1, both duty cycle conditions (30/30 and 10/10) were generally well tolerated with low reports of pain, discomfort, and other side effects during or after taVNS. Participants seemed to equally prefer the conditions, indicating that the on/off times did not play a significant role in the participants’ experience with taVNS. For study 2, both monophasic and biphasic conditions were also well tolerated and equally preferred. These results also add support to other studies that report favorable tolerance in terms of adverse events with taVNS (Wong et al. 2024). However, it is important to note that the different parameters in study 2 also resulted in differences in stimulation dosage. Specifically, in monophasic sessions participants received half the amount of stimulation compared to biphasic sessions due to 50% duty cycle. This should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results.

Stimulation current intensity is an obvious parameter to consider regarding taVNS tolerability, and unsurprisingly a prior study by Borges et al. (Borges et al. 2019) has shown that lower intensity (0.5 mA) was perceived as less unpleasant than higher intensities (1.0 mA and 1.5 mA) (Borges et al. 2019). However, lower intensities may not always achieve a therapeutic dosage, and some have suggested that higher intensities (i.e., 200% of the perception threshold) are optimal (Badran et al. 2019). In study 1, we held the stimulation current intensity constant for all participants across both conditions at 2.0 mA, and it was well tolerated by the vast majority of participants. Thus, in study 2, we increased the prescribed intensity to 2.5 mA and added the assessment of perception threshold. The perception threshold was higher than 0.5 mA for most participants using both monophasic and biphasic waveforms, which could explain why the study by Borges et al. reported favorable tolerability at a subthreshold intensity. It is also noteworthy that using a higher intensity in study 2 than in study 1 did not result in a greater number of participants reporting pain or discomfort, and that we did not observe a relationship between perception threshold and severity of irritation with taVNS. While some participants did report pain and irritation from tingling, tickling, and pricking sensations, the NRS ratings were low, indicating only mild discomfort, and in general all 4 conditions tested were well tolerated.

Attaining the targeted physiologic effects with taVNS is best achieved with repeated sessions of taVNS; thus, participant adherence is important (Roy et al. 2023; Reis et al. 2024). Several recent studies report mixed results in participant compliance to taVNS protocols (Evensen et al. 2022; Tavares-Figueiredo et al. 2024; Prott et al. 2025), with one of the positive studies using low standards and defining good compliance as when 80% of the sample achieves 50% of stimulation volume prescribed (Prott et al. 2025). While tolerability has not been directly mentioned as a factor of noncompliance with taVNS, it is understudied and has been shown to influence compliance with other neuromodulation techniques. For example, Paneri et al. (Paneri et al. 2016) investigated tolerability of repeated application of transcranial direct stimulation (tDCS), modulated high frequency transcranial pulsed current stimulation (MHF-tPCS), and sham tDCS in healthy subjects. Tolerability and compliance were significantly better for the MHF-tPCS group compared to the sham group (Paneri et al. 2016). This study highlights the importance of studying tolerability in neuromodulation to enhance participant experience and adherence, and our findings expand this understanding to taVNS research. Continued investigation of the specific elements contributing to taVNS tolerability is needed to ensure viability of real-world application of taVNS as a medical treatment.

Secondary outcomes (HRV, QST, and TMS)

While there were no statistically significant differences between conditions in HRV for either study in this research, there were notable trends. All conditions resulted in decreased heart rate post taVNS as well as increased RMSSD and SDNN. These findings are consistent with prior studies in healthy individuals (Geng et al. 2022; Badran et al. 2018; Borges et al. 2019; Clancy et al. 2014; De Couck et al. 2017; Roy et al. 2023). It is important to note that most of our sample were young healthy individuals, with relatively high baseline HRV, which may explain why relatively small increases in HRV outcomes were seen. Lower baseline HRV is associated with a greater HRV response to taVNS; (Soltani et al. 2023) therefore, our sample demographics and high baseline values may have contributed to a ceiling effect.

taVNS is also a promising intervention for pain management (Costa et al. 2024). Thus, we examined changes in pain sensitivity in subgroups of participants via QST. In both of our studies QST changes were small to negligible, with only wind up (a measure of nociceptive temporal summation) showing differences between conditions in study 2. Specifically, participants had a greater decrease in wind-up with biphasic taVNS. These findings support prior findings that taVNS does not induce significant changes in static QST measures (i.e., heat detection threshold, cold detection threshold, pinprick, and vibrotactile stimulation) (Dumoulin et al. 2021). However, our findings pertaining to wind up suggest that additional research on the effects of taVNS on dynamic QST measures (i.e., wind up and conditioned pain modulation) is warranted.

Although exploratory, our findings on changes in TMS measures add valuable data to the limited existing research. In study 1 there seemed to be a global shift towards cortical facilitation with the exception for SICI in condition A. In study 2, both SICI and ICF increased after monophasic, with negligible changes observed for both after biphasic. Our findings differ from those prior studies. Gerges et at. (Gerges et al. 2025) reported increased SICI and no change in ICF after 60 min of taVNS in a group of 23 healthy individuals; (Gerges et al. 2025) Capone et al. reported increased SICI in a group of 10 participants; (Capone et al. 2015) and van Midden et al. reported a 96.3% probability of increased SICI during active taVNS using Baysian statistics (Midden et al. 2023). These differences are noteworthy as our measures of resting and general excitability (RMT and spTMS) remained relatively constant across all four conditions with slight trends only seen in SICI and ICF. It is important to note that while we did not see differences between conditions in change of TMS measures in either study, we were likely underpowered to do so. In addition, while our results are interesting and differ from those of other studies regarding the effects of ppTMS, they should be interpreted with caution due to our small and non-clinical sample sizes.

Limitations

The primary focus of these studies was to assess tolerability and the participant experience with taVNS, and our results are only applicable to the stimulation parameters included. In addition, both studies consisted of mostly young healthy individuals which potentially limited the amount of change seen in our secondary physiologic outcomes. Moreover, these findings may not be generalizable to clinical populations. Lastly, our results should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample sizes, especially for the secondary outcomes.

Conclusion

The results from this research provide an in-depth description of tolerability and participant experience with taVNS. Improved understanding of factors that influence taVNS tolerability may help researchers and clinicians to optimize the selection of stimulation parameters for comfort and effectiveness for best participant compliance and outcomes. Importantly, all parameters tested were tolerated well and resulted in increased HRV. However, the effects on other measures (QST and TMS) were inconsistent and warrant further investigation.
