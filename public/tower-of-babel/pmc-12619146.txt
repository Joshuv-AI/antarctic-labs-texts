Unraveling the influence of offline and online social support on the connection between negative life events and suicidal ideation: a cross-sectional and gender-based examination among Chinese students amid the COVID-19 crisis
Authors: Moye Xin, Chengxi Yang, Lijin Zhang, Xueyan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate whether offline and online social support mediate the relationship between negative life events (NLEs) and suicidal ideation among Chinese students during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a focus on gender differences.MethodsUsing stratified sampling, 1,800 middle-high school and university students across China were surveyed. Structural equation modeling (SEM) with bias-corrected bootstrapping (5,000 samples) tested mediation effects, while multigroup SEM evaluated gender invariance. The Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC), Scale of Suicidal Ideation (SSI), and modified social support scales were administered.ResultsOffline social support significantly mediated NLEs’ impact on suicidal ideation: among males, for punishment (indirect effect = -0.007, 95% CI [-0.012, -0.003]) and adaptation (0.009, [0.004, 0.015]); among females, for learning pressure (0.008, [0.003, 0.013]), loss (0.013, [0.006, 0.021]), interpersonal relationships (0.024, [0.015, 0.034]), and adaptation (0.015, [0.008, 0.023]). Online social support showed no mediating effects for either gender. Multigroup SEM confirmed gender-invariant mediation structures (Δχ2 = 3.29, p = 0.17).ConclusionOffline social support serves as a critical mediator of NLEs’ effects on suicidal ideation, with gender-specific pathways. Online support cannot substitute for tangible networks during crises. Findings emphasize the need for gender-tailored offline support interventions and longitudinal research to inform suicide prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03545-8.Keywords: Suicidal ideation, Offline social support, Online social support, Negative life events, Gender analysis

Introduction

Globally, suicide is a leading cause of disability or death, accounting for 1.4% of the annual mortality rate [1]. The COVID-19 pandemic has amplified the impact of negative life events (NLEs)—such as academic pressure, family conflict, or loss—on young students’ mental health, elevating risks of suicidal ideation [2–4]. This is particularly critical in China, where youth suicide is a major public health concern: suicidal ideation affects 13.2–28.0% of middle-high school students and 12.9–21.5% of university students [5–9], yet the mechanisms linking NLEs to suicidal ideation (and potential protective factors like social support) remain understudied, especially compared to Western research [10–13]. Suicide is the leading cause of death among Chinese individuals aged 14–28, with suicidal ideation prevalence ranging from 13.2% to 28.0% in middle-high school students [5–7], posing a significant public health concern and endangering the lives of more young students, indicating that suicidal ideation serves as a risk factor for subsequent suicidal behavior, underscoring the critical need to identify protective mechanisms. Similarly, the suicide rate among Chinese university students has also shown an upward trend. A study involving 867 students randomly selected from two well-known universities in China discovered a 12.9% prevalence of suicidal ideation, with a higher number of female students reporting such thoughts compared to males [8]. Another study indicated that 21.5% of university students reported suicidal ideation, while 15.8% had attempted suicide [9]. These findings highlight a significant and concerning prevalence of suicidal behavior among young students in China, which has become a distressing response to the life problems or difficulties they face.

From a theoretical perspective, the emergence of suicidal behavior follows a gradual process involving the accumulation and interaction of psychological and environmental factors. To clarify key constructs, suicidal ideation is defined as the desire or motivation for death without active implementation, serving as the earliest psychological precursor to suicidal behavior [14]. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) outlines three sequential stages of suicidal behavior: 1) suicidal ideation, 2) suicidal attempt/failure, and 3) suicidal death [15]. This framework underscores the critical role of suicidal ideation as a preventable stage in the pathway to lethal outcomes [14, 16].

Considering the significant impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health and well-being of both minors and adults worldwide [17], potential stressors such as negative life events can act as risk factors that potentially exacerbate existing anxieties and predict the prevalence of life-threatening behaviors among youths psychosocially, such as non-suicidal self-injury and suicidality [18, 19].

Given that suicidal ideation is the earliest preventable stage of suicidal behavior [14–16], identifying how to interrupt its development is urgent. Theoretical frameworks like the Stress Process Model guide this inquiry: they posit that NLEs do not directly cause suicidal ideation but operate through mediating mechanisms—with social support (both offline and online) emerging as a key potential protector [15]. Offline support (e.g., family/peer interaction) may mitigate NLE-related distress, while online support (e.g., virtual communities) offers alternative connection—but whether either mediates the NLEs-suicidal ideation relationship (rather than merely moderating it) remains unclear, particularly amid pandemic-related restrictions on offline interaction [17]. This study thus focuses on testing a mediation model: whether offline and online social support transmit the effects of NLEs on suicidal ideation, with attention to gender differences.

For social support specifically, the model emphasizes that sufficient offline social support acts as a protective mediator by interrupting the stress-transmission pathway: when individuals encounter NLEs (e.g., academic pressure, interpersonal conflict), access to emotional validation (e.g., empathy from family/peers) and practical assistance (e.g., guidance from teachers) reduces the psychological strain of these events, thereby lowering the likelihood of developing suicidal ideation [15, 20, 21]. In contrast, low social support would function as a risk-enhancing mediator—but our study focuses on adequate support as a protective pathway, consistent with prior research that frames social support as a proactive mechanism to buffer stress via mediation [20, 22].

Online social support, by comparison, offers virtual avenues for connection but operates through distinct mechanisms: while it may provide emotional belonging, its lack of tangible interaction and potential for risks (e.g., cyberbullying, disinhibition) mean its ability to mediate NLEs’effects is less certain [23, 24]. This distinction underscores the need to test both offline and online support as potential mediators—rather than merely moderators—of the NLEs-suicidal ideation relationship, especially amid the COVID-19 pandemic, where offline interactions were restricted and online engagement surged [17].

Negative life events, suicidal ideation, and gender dynamics

NLEs, such as punishment, learning pressure, or interpersonal conflicts, accumulate psychological strain and increase suicidal risk [25, 26]. Gender adds complexity: male students exhibit higher rates of non-suicidal self-injury but may underreport emotional distress, while female students face more intense NLEs (e.g., relational conflicts) yet receive greater offline support [27–29]. These differences may shape how social support mediates NLEs’ effects.

The pressures faced by young students due to negative life events encompass diverse domains, from micro-level family dynamics (e.g., parental expectations), meso-level school and social environments (e.g., academic competition, peer relationships), to macro-level societal conditions (e.g., cultural norms, pandemic-related disruptions)— a framework that directly guided our selection of NLE factors for investigation. These cumulative pressures align with the five categories of negative life events we intentionally focused on in this study—interpersonal relationships, learning pressures, punishment, loss, and adaptation—each reflecting stressors at different ecological levels and chosen to address our research question about how contextually relevant NLEs interact with social support to influence suicidal ideation. This ecological framework highlights how negative life events across micro-to-macro systems collectively impact mental well-being and suicidal risk, irrespective of gender.

Social support and suicidal ideation

Research has shown that offline social support effectively provides psychological counseling and relief for individuals with suicidal thoughts, reducing the prevalence of such ideation [20]. Online social support, which offers identity and belonging through virtual emotional interactions, has grown popular among youth due to its novelty, convenience, and privacy. It enhances well-being for those affected by negative life events and serves as a preventive measure against self-harm and suicide [30, 31].

Both offline and online social support exhibit mixed effects on mental health outcomes. Offline support from family members, while helpful, can sometimes impose burdens through unsolicited advice or psychological impacts [32]. Online support carries unique vulnerabilities: anonymity enables "disinhibition," prompting impulsive sharing of distressing or self-harm-related content that may normalize or worsen suicidal ideation [23]; excessive personal disclosure ("high self-exposure") lacks offline nonverbal cues, increasing vulnerability to judgment or misunderstanding [23, 33]. Additionally, online traits like weak connections, lack of physical contact, and easy access to pro-suicide content can outweigh emotional validation benefits, negatively affecting psychological well-being [23].

Social support as a mediator: offline and online pathways

Offline social support’s mediating role is well-supported by research, as it transmits the stress-reducing effects of NLEs through family and peer interactions—distinct from a moderation role that merely adjusts stress intensity [20–22]. For example, when individuals face NLEs like parental conflict or peer victimization, offline support does not just “buffer” stress in isolation; it actively serves as a pathway through which the negative impact of NLEs on suicidal ideation is reduced [22]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic reduced in-person contact, potentially weakening this offline mediating mechanism and raising questions about whether online support could serve as a substitute. Online support offers accessibility and anonymity [30] but lacks the emotional depth and tangibility of offline relationships, risking ineffectiveness in transmitting stress-reducing effects for severe distress or even exacerbating strain via misinformation [23, 32]. A key empirical gap remains in clarifying whether offline/online support transmits (mediates) the effects of NLEs on suicidal ideation, particularly across genders, as this distinction directly informs whether support systems can be targeted to interrupt the stress-ideation pathway.

Emerging research on online social support highlights its potential to mediate stress-related physical and mental health outcomes among younger populations [24, 34, 35]. By indirectly influencing health risk behaviors in stressed students, online support’s mediating role in the NLEs-suicidal ideation pathway requires empirical exploration. Such research is critical for understanding how stressors and support networks interact to shape suicidal thoughts, informing interventions to reduce youth suicide risk.

Controversies in offline support’s mediating role reflect divergent findings across stress types and cultures. While consistent evidence links it to buffering academic and family stress [20, 21], its efficacy for severe stressors (e.g., trauma) is debated, with some studies showing full mediation of parental conflict’s effects [22] and others only partial mediation for peer victimization [36]. This study addresses these gaps by testing offline support’s mediation across diverse NLEs (punishment, learning pressure, adaptation) in a Chinese sample, where collectivist norms prioritize familial and peer support. By examining gender differences in mediation across stressor types (e.g., punishment for males, relational stress for females), the research offers granular insights into offline support’s protective mechanisms.

Research gaps and hypotheses

The specific mechanism underlying the relationship between the variables mentioned above, as well as the impact of different types of social support on the association between negative life events and suicidal ideation, remains unclear. In summary, the current research has several gaps or deficiencies, which can be outlined as follows:

Gap 1. Integrated mechanism

Few studies use a unified social psychology framework to categorize NLEs and social support types and assess their mediating roles in suicidal ideation. While concepts such as "negative life events," "suicidal ideation," and "social support" are widely used in various disciplines such as sociology, demography, psychology, medicine, and biology, there is a lack of a unified framework that combines these concepts to categorize negative life events and social support into different types from a common perspective. This framework could provide a comprehensive assessment of the mental health status of young students from a social psychology perspective.

Gap 2. Mediation vs. moderation

Existing research on social support often conflates mediation (causal pathways) with moderation (buffering effects), particularly for online support among adolescents. Firstly, while offline social support has been shown to have a significant mediating role between negative life events and suicidal ideation among young students, there are controversies regarding its mediating effect on individuals experiencing life stress, depending on different methods, groups, and sources of stress [36, 37]. Therefore, it is important to determine if such a mediation effect exists and whether it can be applied to a wider range of adolescent groups. Secondly, although some research has been conducted on the mediating role of online social support, mostly focusing on its relationship with other internet variables (such as internet altruistic behavior and online self-disclosure) [38], such research is primarily limited to university students who frequently engage in online social interaction. There is little investigation into the possible relationship between psychological constructs like stress appraisal, emotional regulation, and online social support in predicting mental health outcomes such as suicidal ideation. Therefore, it remains unanswered whether online social support also mediates the association between negative life events and suicidal ideation among young students.

Gap 3. Gender-specific mediation

While gender differences in NLEs and support seeking are documented [28, 29], whether these lead to divergent mediating pathways for suicidal ideation remains untested. As mentioned earlier, individuals of different genders may have different perceptions of negative life events and social support, which can result in varying effects. It has not been established whether gender differences exist throughout the mechanism involving different types of negative life events, suicidal ideation, and various sources of social support, across different genders. This is crucial in determining whether gender differences would lead to variations in specific support methods and priorities when formulating and revising health intervention policies for young students facing the pressure of negative life events.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants consisted of 1,800 students (886 males, 914 females; 935 middle-high school students, 865 university students; Mage = 17.2 years; age range: 11–24 years) recruited from three middle-high schools and two universities in Northwestern China (June 2022, after the outbreak of COVID-19). Parents or legal guardians gave permission for high school students’ participation by signing consent forms. The study was approved by the institutional ethics review board within the university and the schools where the research was conducted.

Descriptive statistics for control variables were as follows: 1. Single-child status: 68.3% (n = 1,230) of participants were only children, while 31.7% (n = 570) had siblings; 2. Parental marital status: 82.5% (n = 1,485) of participants reported their parents were in a first marriage, 9.2% (n = 166) had divorced parents, 5.8% (n = 104) had parents who were remarried, and 2.5% (n = 45) had a widowed parent; 3. Grade distribution: In middle-high schools, participants included 328 eighth graders (18.2%), 307 ninth graders (17.1%), and 300 high school students (16.7%). In universities, 289 first-year (16.1%), 237 s-year (13.2%), 186 third-year (10.3%), and 153 fourth-year students (8.5%) were included.

Measures

Suicidal ideation

The Scale of Suicidal Ideation (SSI) developed by Beck (1979) was used to assess suicidal ideation [39]. This measure consists of 14 items (e.g., I think suicide can end the current pain; I have taken some strange or dangerous drugs to suicide on purpose) rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). Higher scores indicate stronger suicidal ideation intensity. The SSI has been proved to contain excellent reliability (α = 0.86).

Negative life events

Guided by our research question— which aimed to explore how NLEs across micro-to-macro ecological levels (e.g., micro-level family dynamics, meso-level school/social environments, macro-level societal disruptions) impact Chinese students’ suicidal ideation— we operationalized negative life events using the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) [40], a widely validated tool for assessing youth-specific stressors in Chinese contexts. The original scale consists of 27 items categorized into 6 factors: interpersonal relationships, learning pressures, punishment, loss, adaptation, and other (e.g., got misunderstood or wronged; got discriminated against or treated coldly for interpersonal relationships; failure in an exam for learning pressures; got criticized or punished at school for punishment; sudden death of relative or friend, experienced theft or lost items for loss; transfer or suspension, major changes in daily routines for adaptation; family financial problems for other). Respondents rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale based on the extent to which each event impacted their life, from 1 (no impact) to 5 (extremely severe impact).

To enhance construct validity and ensure alignment with our research focus on ecologically relevant NLEs (i.e., stressors across micro-to-macro systems), we conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal axis factoring and Varimax rotation, yielding a modified 5-factor solution—we excluded the "Other" category for three key reasons: 1) Low item convergence: Items in the "Other" factor (e.g., family financial problems, accidental injuries) showed weak factor loadings (< 0.40) in our sample, failing to form a coherent latent construct; 2) Theoretical misalignment: These items did not clearly map to our ecological framework (micro-level family dynamics, meso-level school/social environments, macro-level societal disruptions), as they represented idiosyncratic or contextually specific stressors not central to our research question; 3) Statistical improvement: The 5-factor model explained 68.2% of total variance (vs. 62.5% for the original 6-factor structure), with all remaining items loading ≥ 0.55 on their target factors and no cross-loadings > 0.40 (see Table A1 in Supplementary Materials), indicating better fit.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) further supported the revised structure (χ2(190) = 485.3, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.052 [90% CI: 0.048–0.057], SRMR = 0.049), and reliability estimates (Cronbach’s α) for the five factors ranged from 0.78 (punishment) to 0.85 (adaptation)—comparable to or higher than the original 6-factor ASLEC (α = 0.72–0.83). Regarding comparability with prior research: While the original ASLEC’s "Other" factor is sometimes used to capture miscellaneous stressors, most Chinese studies focusing on adolescent NLEs and mental health [20, 41] prioritize the same five factors we retained, as they reflect the most common stressors for this population. Excluding the "Other" factor thus minimally impacts cross-study comparisons for our core research question, while improving the scale’s psychometric properties for our sample. Items from the "Other" factor (e.g., family financial problems) were not reclassified to avoid diluting the validity of the retained factors, resulting in a 5-factor ASLEC that balances construct relevance, statistical rigor, and alignment with key prior work.

Offline social support

A modified version of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) was used to assess offline social support [42, 43]. The original measure includes 12 items which divide into 3 subscales related to the source of support, namely family, friends, or significant other. This scale was adapted for Chinese contexts by Xiao [43], in order to facilitate understanding and cultural division, by modifying the original subscales (family, friends, significant other) to teachers, friends, and family members. The adapted scale consists of 16 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from "completely inconsistent" to "fully consistent." The total score for offline social support is obtained by calculating the mean of item responses, where higher mean scores indicate higher levels of perceived social support. The modified scale showed good reliability in the present study (α = 0.92).

Online social support

The 23-item online social support scale designed by Liang [44] was adopted to assess the degree to which participants have obtained emotional and practical support through the Internet. Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they have received online social support using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (no) to 5 (always). Sample items include, “I found more friends through common interests and hobbies on the Internet” and “Comparing to offline life communication, I choose to spend more time in online communication, because it’s more reliable and relaxing” A mean score is calculated whereby the higher the score, the more online social support received. The scale has reported sufficient reliability (α = 0.85).

Additionally, control variables included in all models were: 1) Demographic factors: age (continuous), gender (male/female), school type (middle-high school/university), grade level (ordinal), and single-child status (yes/no); 2) Family background: parental marital status (first marriage/divorced/remarried/widowed).

Clustering at the school level was not explicitly modeled due to the study’s cross-sectional design and lack of significant variance partitioning across schools in preliminary analyses. However, school type (urban/suburban) was implicitly controlled via stratified sampling. All models used robust standard errors to account for potential heteroscedasticity.

Data collection

To ensure representativeness across key demographic strata, we employed stratified sampling with predefined quotas based on gender (male/female) and school type (middle-high school/university). The sampling frame included three middle-high schools and two universities in Northwestern China, selected to cover urban and suburban areas. Specific procedures were as follows:

Strata definition. 1) Gender: Two strata (male, female), with target quotas to achieve a roughly 1:1 gender ratio. 2) School type. Two strata (middle-high school: grades 8–12; university: undergraduate years 1–4), with quotas proportional to the approximate enrollment sizes of these institutions in the region.

Quota allocation. 1) Middle-high school students constituted 52% of the sample (n = 935), reflecting their larger population in the target region; 2) university students comprised 48% (n = 865). Data analysis strategy

Firstly, we employed the bivariate correlation method to compare the correlations between negative life events, online social support, and suicidal ideation across different genders as a prerequisite for analyzing the mediating effect. Next, to test the mediating effects of offline and online social support, also to address the hypothesis that social support mediates the effect of negative life events (NLEs) on suicidal ideation "regardless of gender," we adopted bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (BCa CIs) via structural equation modeling (SEM) to formally test whether mediation paths (NLEs → social support → suicidal ideation) were equivalent across males and females with Mplus 8.4, aligning with contemporary recommendations [45, 46]. This method directly estimates the indirect effect (ab path) and avoids reliance on the traditional Baron-Kenny stepwise approach, which may underpower small-to-moderate effects. Moreover, to address the limitations of the stepwise regression approach, we adopted a single structural equation model (SEM) per gender using Mplus 8.4, which estimates all paths (direct effects of negative life events [NLEs] on suicidal ideation, NLEs on social support, and social support on suicidal ideation) simultaneously. For each gender, the model includes control variables (age, school type, single-child status, parental marital status) and employs bias-corrected bootstrap resampling (5,000 samples) to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for direct and indirect effects. This approach reduces Type I error by avoiding sequential hypothesis testing and provides a more efficient, comprehensive assessment of mediation. Results are reported as standardized path coefficients with bootstrapped CIs, enabling direct evaluation of both total and indirect effects.

Procedures

Model specification: Separate SEM models were constructed for males and females, including: 1) Negative life events (NLEs) as the independent variable; 2) Social support (offline/online) as the mediator; 3)Suicidal ideation as the dependent variable; 3) Control variables (age, school type, single-child status, parental marital status).

Bootstrapping

For each model, 5,000 bootstrap samples were generated to compute 95% BCa CIs for indirect effects. A significant mediation effect was inferred if the CI excluded zero.

Gender invariance testing

Multigroup SEM was used to test whether mediation paths (NLEs → social support → suicidal ideation) differed significantly between genders by comparing unconstrained and constrained models (with paths fixed equal across genders), and using chi-square difference tests (Δχ2) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) to evaluate model fit. A non-significant Δχ2 (p > 0.05) would support gender invariance of mediation effects.

This approach enhances statistical rigor by directly estimating mediated effects and accounting for sampling variability, as well as tests the hypothesis of equivalent mediation across genders while accounting for potential gender differences in specific NLEs (e.g., punishment for males, learning pressure for females) as observed in results and in the literature.

Mediating effect test of social support

Offline social support as a mediator

Structural equation modeling (SEM) with bootstrapping (5,000 samples) revealed significant gender-specific mediation effects for offline support (Tables 3 and 4). Among males, offline support partially mediated the impact of punishment (indirect effect = −0.007, 95% CI [−0.012, −0.003]) and adaptation (indirect effect = 0.009, 95% CI [0.004, 0.015]) on suicidal ideation, explaining 35% and 7.4% of total effects, respectively. For females, offline support mediated four NLEs: learning pressure (indirect effect = 0.008, 95% CI [0.003, 0.013]), loss (indirect effect = 0.013, 95% CI [0.006, 0.021]), interpersonal relationships (indirect effect = 0.024, 95% CI [0.015, 0.034]), and adaptation (indirect effect = 0.015, 95% CI [0.008, 0.023]), accounting for 7.5%, 12.4%, 16.8%, and 12.9% of total effects. Multigroup SEM confirmed gender-invariant mediation paths (Δχ2 = 3.29, p = 0.17), indicating consistent mediating roles despite differing NLE types.

For females, online support also showed no mediation for the four NLEs (learning pressure, loss, interpersonal relationships, adaptation) mediated by offline support. Indirect effects were near zero (learning pressure: 0.0003, loss: 0.0001, interpersonal relationships: 0.0002, adaptation: 0.0002) with CIs including zero (all ranges spanning negative and positive values). Unlike males, females showed no significant direct paths from NLEs to online support (e.g., learning pressure → online support: β = 0.068, p > 0.05; Table 6), suggesting they did not seek more online support when facing stressors.

Across both genders, the consistent null mediation—paired with weak or non-significant NLEs-online support paths—reinforces that online support, even when accessed, lacked the capacity to transmit stress-reducing effects, justifying its inclusion as a core construct to contrast with offline support’s robust mediation.

Gender differences in mediation patterns

While offline support mediated NLEs’ effects in both genders, males showed significant mediation for punishment and adaptation, whereas females exhibited broader mediation across learning pressure, loss, interpersonal relationships, and adaptation. This aligns with the hypothesis that offline support’s mediating role is universal but targets gender-specific stressors. Online support’s lack of mediation suggests limited utility in buffering NLEs’ impact during the pandemic, possibly due to its virtual nature and associated risks (e.g., disinhibition, high self-exposure).

Discussion

Key findings summary

The study’s key findings center on distinct mediating roles of offline versus online social support in the relationship between negative life events (NLEs) and suicidal ideation, with clear gender differences: Offline social support emerged as a significant mediator for specific NLEs across both genders—for males, this included punishment and adaptation stressors; for females, it extended to learning pressure, loss, interpersonal conflicts, and adaptation. In contrast, online social support showed no meaningful mediating effects for any NLEs in either gender, with indirect effects too small to be clinically or statistically relevant.

Offline social support as a critical mediator: gender-differentiated pathways

The study’s findings partially support Hypothesis H1a, confirming that offline social support mediates the impact of specific NLEs on suicidal ideation for both genders, but through gender-specific stressor profiles. For males, offline support primarily mediated the effects of punishment and adaptation stressors—consistent with cultural expectations in China, where male adolescents face heightened pressure to conform to disciplinary norms, making punitive events (e.g., school discipline) or adaptation challenges (e.g., routine disruptions) more salient stressors [47]. The mediating role here suggests structured interventions targeting these stressors could disrupt the pathway to suicidal ideation. The rebellious tendencies commonly observed in this group may lead to more frequent encounters with punitive events (e.g., school discipline) or adaptation challenges (e.g., routine disruptions), prompting them to seek tangible support from peers or family to mitigate emotional distress. The mediating role of offline support here suggests that structured interventions targeting male students—such as mentorship programs or peer counseling—could disrupt the pathway from punishment/adaptation stress to suicidal ideation [48].

For females, offline social support exhibited broader mediating effects across learning pressure, interpersonal relationships, and adaptation, accounting for 7.5% to 16.8% of total effects. This aligns with research with traditional gender roles that often cast females as more emotionally vulnerable and academically pressured [49]. Female students may internalize academic failures more intensely, leading to increased distress that offline support—such as family encouragement or teacher guidance—can alleviate [41]. Similarly, the stronger mediating effect for interpersonal conflicts reflects females’ higher sensitivity to relational stress, as they may rely more on offline networks for emotional validation during peer disputes [50–53]. These findings underscore the need for gender-tailored support systems, such as female-focused counseling workshops to address learning pressure and relational challenges.

Theoretical implications: aligning with the stress process model

The results strongly support the Stress Process Model [15], illustrating that NLEs do not directly cause suicidal ideation but operate through mediating mechanisms like offline social support. For both genders, NLEs such as punishment (males: β = 0.198***) and learning pressure (females: β = 0.107***) showed direct effects on suicidal ideation, which were partially reduced by offline support. This transmission mechanism highlights the importance of targeting social support networks to interrupt the stress-ideation pathway. Notably, the multigroup SEM analysis confirmed gender-invariant mediation structures (Δχ2 = 3.29, p = 0.17), indicating that while the specific NLEs mediated differ by gender, the underlying role of offline support as a mediator remains universal. This finding extends prior cross-cultural research [20] by demonstrating that collectivist cultural norms in China—where family and peer support are highly valued—strengthen the buffering role of offline support, even amid pandemic-related disruptions.

Online social support: the limits of virtual connection

The null mediating effect of online social support—despite its inclusion as a core construct to test pandemic-era virtual support’s utility—warrants nuanced interpretation, with three key factors explaining this pattern:

Measurement limitations

The 23-item online social support scale [44] used in this study focuses on general emotional/practical support (e.g., “finding friends via shared interests online”) but lacks items capturing crisis-specific support (e.g., “seeking urgent emotional help for suicidal thoughts online”). This mismatch may have underestimated online support’s potential to mediate severe stressors like NLEs, as the scale did not assess the type of targeted support needed during distress. Additionally, the scale’s reliance on self-reported “frequency of support received” (1 = “no” to 5 = “always”) does not account for perceived support quality—a critical distinction, as low-quality online support (e.g., superficial validation) may fail to reduce suicidal ideation [23, 33].

Cultural context

Chinese collectivist norms prioritize tangible, in-person support (e.g., family visits, peer meetings) over virtual interactions [20, 50]. This cultural preference is reflected in our sample: 47.2% of students (58.7% of females) reported preferring offline support, citing “lack of emotional trust” in online relationships. For males, even when punitive events or adaptation stressors increased online support seeking (β = 0.108*, Table 5), cultural expectations to “handle stress independently” may have prevented them from fully engaging with online support, rendering it ineffective [28, 47].

Alternative explanatory mechanisms

Online support may operate as a moderator (buffering NLEs’ direct effects) rather than a mediator—an untested pathway in our model. For example, online support could reduce the strength of the NLEs-suicidal ideation link without transmitting effects between variables. Additionally, residual confounding (e.g., unmeasured digital literacy, which correlates with both online support use and mental health) may have obscured potential mediation; students with low digital literacy may struggle to access high-quality online support, even if they seek it [17, 24].

Notably, the null effect is not due to insufficient statistical power: With N = 1,800 and bootstrapped 95% CIs, the study had > 80% power to detect small indirect effects (Cohen’s f2 = 0.02) [45]. Future research should use crisis-tailored online support scales and test moderation models to further explore online support’s role in NLEs-related distress.

Cultural and methodological considerations

The study’s focus on Northwestern China provides unique insights into regional cultural factors, such as collectivist values and educational pressures, that shape NLEs and support-seeking behaviors. However, this regional focus may limit generalizability to more urban or Eastern contexts, where digital literacy and online support utilization might be higher.

Critical to our findings is the modified 5-factor ASLEC [40], whose exclusion of the "Other" dimension (e.g., family financial problems, accidental injuries) aligns with the gender-specific NLE patterns we observed. By retaining only ecologically relevant factors (interpersonal relationships, learning pressure, punishment, loss, adaptation), the modified scale enhanced the clarity of offline support’s mediation: For example, the absence of idiosyncratic "Other" stressors prevented dilution of the link between gender-specific NLEs (e.g., punishment for males, relational conflict for females) and suicidal ideation, allowing us to more precisely identify which stressors offline support effectively mediates. This modification also improves comparability with prior Chinese adolescent studies [20, 41], which similarly prioritize these five NLE factors—strengthening the broader relevance of our findings. That said, future research could reintroduce the "Other" factor with refined items (e.g., pandemic-specific stressors like “school closure-related financial strain”) to capture contextually unique NLEs that may interact with social support differently.

Conclusions, practical implications, and future directions

This study reveals that offline social support acts as a critical mediator between negative life events (NLEs) and suicidal ideation among Chinese students, with distinct gender dynamics: males show mediation for punishment and adaptation stressors, while females exhibit broader mediation across learning pressure, loss, interpersonal conflicts, and adaptation. In contrast, online social support did not mediate these relationships in our sample—this finding highlights contextual limits of online support rather than absolute ineffectiveness, as these limits are tied to the study’s specific conditions: the COVID-19 pandemic’s restrictions on in-person interaction (which may have altered online support quality), our focus on general (vs. crisis-specific) online support measurement, and Chinese cultural preferences for tangible offline connection. Notably, this does not negate online support’s potential utility in other contexts (e.g., non-crisis periods, populations with high digital literacy), but rather underscores that offline support networks are particularly critical for mitigating NLE-related suicidal ideation during crises like the pandemic. The findings inform targeted, contextually relevant interventions to mitigate suicidal ideation among Chinese students amid crises like COVID-19:

For male students, given that offline social support mediates the impact of punishment and adaptation stressors, schools could implement structured mentorship programs—for example, pairing male students facing disciplinary issues (e.g., school criticism for rule violations) or adaptation challenges (e.g., transferring to a new class/school) with trained teacher mentors or senior peers. These mentors would provide one-on-one offline support, such as helping students process punitive experiences constructively or navigate new environments, directly addressing the stressors most relevant to males.

For female students, as offline support mediates learning pressure, loss, and interpersonal conflicts, schools and communities could develop female-focused support workshops—for instance, organizing monthly “academic stress management sessions” (led by school counselors) to help females cope with exam anxiety or homework pressure, and “relational conflict resolution groups” where female students can discuss peer disputes or family tensions in a safe offline setting, with counselors guiding emotional validation and problem-solving.

Beyond school settings, families (82.5% of our sample have parents in first marriages, per " Participants" section) should be engaged through family-centered support initiatives—such as parent education workshops that teach caregivers to recognize gender-specific stress signals (e.g., irritability in males due to punishment, withdrawal in females due to interpersonal conflicts) and provide tangible offline support (e.g., open face-to-face conversations about academic struggles instead of unsolicited advice).

Educators and policymakers should also prioritize training for school staff to identify these gender-specific NLE impacts and integrate offline support into existing mental health frameworks—for example, updating school counseling protocols to include gender-stratified risk assessments, ensuring that support resources (e.g., offline counseling hours, peer support groups) are allocated to address the unique stressors of each gender [54–64]. These targeted interventions leverage the irreplaceable role of offline support identified in our study, maximizing their potential to reduce suicide risk among young students.

Longitudinal research is essential to explore causal pathways between NLEs, social support, and suicidal ideation over time, particularly to test whether online support’s role evolves beyond the pandemic context. Cross-cultural studies should examine how collectivist values influence support efficacy, while developing crisis-tailored online platforms—integrating video counseling (to emulate offline emotional depth) and crisis-specific support modules (e.g., suicidal ideation hotlines)—could address the contextual limits identified in our study. Additionally, investigating combined offline-online support strategies (e.g., pairing in-person peer mentorship with virtual check-ins) may enhance youth mental health interventions, particularly for students with limited access to offline networks.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The data were collected from a health risk survey in Shaanxi Province, China, primarily focusing on adolescents in Western China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to Eastern cities where cultural and socioeconomic contexts differ. The sample predominantly included students from provincial capital and prefecture-level city schools, underrepresenting those from county and township regions, potentially biasing the results. Additionally, the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-report questionnaires introduce methodological constraints, as self-reported data may be subject to response biases, and the design does not allow for causal inferences or tracking of temporal changes in suicidal ideation. Furthermore, the study did not assess the impact of psychotherapy or counseling services, which are critical protective factors in suicide prevention, leaving a gap in understanding how such interventions might influence the relationship between negative life events, social support, and suicidal ideation. Future research should address these limitations by adopting longitudinal designs, expanding sampling to diverse geographical areas, and incorporating evaluations of mental health interventions to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying suicidal ideation among Chinese students.
