Antemortem diagnosis combining computed tomography and air sac fluid aspiration for aspergillosis: a case report in a gentoo penguin (Pygoscelis papua)
Authors: Naoya MATSUMOTO, Megumi ITOH, Takahito TOYOTOME, Kenichi WATANABE, Manabu YAMADA, Kyogo HAGINO, Sakurako NEO, Kazutaka YAMADA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary aspergillosis is a fatal disease with high mortality in captive penguins in zoos and aquariums; however, antemortem identification of Aspergillus spp. remains challenging. A gentoo penguin (Pygoscelis papua) exhibiting respiratory clinical signs showed fluid accumulation in the interclavicular air sac on computed tomography (CT). The fluid was aspirated via puncture using a sterile needle in the prone position, as during CT. The fluid sample was positive for A. fumigatus in fungal culture. After the penguin died, infection of A. fumigatus was confirmed in the air sac fluid collected postmortem pathological examination. These findings suggest that combining computed tomography and air sac aspiration is a promising approach for the antemortem diagnosis of aspergillosis in penguins.Keywords: air sac fluid, aspergillosis, aspiration, computed tomography, penguin

Pulmonary aspergillosis in captive penguins living in zoos and aquariums is a fatal disease with high mortality. Pathological findings of aspergillosis, caused by Aspergillus spp., include nodules in the lungs and air sacs, and accumulation of fluid in the air sacs [11, 12, 23, 25]. Given that the major clinical signs of aspergillosis are nonspecific, such as inappetence, coughing, open-mouth breathing, and weight loss, distinguishing it from other diseases and establishing a definitive diagnosis remain difficult [3, 6, 7, 11, 20]. Furthermore, by the time clinical signs emerge, the clinical condition of the animal may have already worsened to the point of imminent mortality [2, 10, 11]. Captive penguins showing respiratory clinical signs or abnormal blood parameters are often administered antifungal drugs without identifying the causative fungus, assuming the worst possible scenario (i.e., Aspergillus infection). In other cases, healthy penguins are given prophylactic antifungal treatments. These practices have become routine and may lead to antifungal medication being administered not only to clinically ill penguins but also to healthy individuals. Consequently, extensive use of antifungal drugs promotes the development of drug resistance [12, 17, 18, 22] and can potentially cause adverse effects in penguins. Long-term drug administration may also result in economic losses, not only in terms of medication costs but also through the loss of valuable animal resources [8, 19, 21].

A gentoo penguin (Pygoscelis papua) housed at an aquarium in Hokkaido, Japan was suspected of respiratory disease given presentation of respiratory clinical signs, such as coughing. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the practice guidelines of the aquarium’s veterinary department. A blood examination was subsequently performed using blood collected from the medial metatarsal vein, after which aspergillosis parameters were evaluated [6, 20]. Accordingly, total protein, albumin-to-globulin ratio (A/G), white blood cell (WBC), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatine kinase (CK), uric acid (UA), sodium, potassium, calcium, and phosphorus were measured. All blood parameters were within the reference range: total protein (5.9 g/dL), A/G (1.48), WBC (4,330/μL), AST (172 IU/L), CK (164 IU/L), UA (6.2 mg/dL), sodium (158 mEq/L), potassium (2.0 mEq/L), calcium (11.1 mEq/L), and phosphorus (4.3 mEq/L). For a more detailed information, CT was performed on the 17th day of admission. After immobilizing the penguin via an intramuscular injection of midazolam at 2.0 mg/kg (Takeda Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), the penguin was placed in the prone position on the CT table, and respiratory rate was monitored. CT images were then obtained during spontaneous breathing using a four-row multidetector CT scanner (Asteion Super 4, Cannon, Ohtawara, Japan) at 80 kV, 150 mA, and 1.0 mm slice thickness. No contrast study was performed. The images were reconstructed using a soft-tissue algorithm for the mediastinal window image (window level: +30, window width: 350) and a lung algorithm for the lung window image (window level: −300, window width: 1,600). DICOM data were sent to a viewer (OsiriX-N, Newton-Graphics, Sapporo, Japan) to read the images. Accordingly, CT revealed fluid accumulation in the interclavicular air sac, an 11 × 13 mm cavitary lesion in the right lung parenchyma, and thickening of the air sac membrane (Fig. 1). No abnormalities were detected in tissues other than the respiratory tract. The air sac fluid had a CT value of 11 ± 6 Hounsfield unit (HU) and a volume of 9.0 mL measured using CT images. To collect the fluid, a virtual line was drawn from the fluid to the body surface of the air sac using CT images to measure the distance and angle [15]. During fluid collection, the penguin was sedated using an intramuscular injection of midazolam at 2.0 mg/kg. After disinfecting the surface around the puncture area with 70% isopropanol, the penguin was punctured with a 20 G, 38-mm-long sterile needle in the prone position as during CT. In total, 2.0 mL of fluid was collected from the interclavicular air sac. The color of the air sac fluid was faint pink with a specific gravity of 1.022 (Fig. 2A). Although bacterial and fungal cultures of the air sac fluid were negative, microscopic smear examination revealed the presence of mycelia, leukocytes, and erythrocytes. At this point, although the causative pathogen was not identified, the detection of mycelia in the air sac fluid suggested a fungal respiratory infection. The penguin was tentatively diagnosed with aspergillosis, and oral itraconazole (5.0 mg/kg once daily for 120 days) treatment was initiated [16].

On the 115th day, blood parameters were still within the normal reference range; however, the clinical signs were unchanged. CT was therefore performed again to reassess the condition. Aside from the abnormal findings observed on CT on the 17th day, fluid in the right bronchus, fluid with gas bubbles in the posterior thoracic air sac, and infiltrations in the lungs were observed on CT (Fig. 3). The fluid had a CT value of 27 ± 11 HU and a volume of 5.4 mL measured using CT images. In total, 4.0 mL of fluid was collected from the interclavicular air sacs. The air sac fluid was hematoid with a specific gravity of 1.016 (Fig. 2B). Although numerous erythrocytes, macrophages, and eosinophils were observed microscopically, the pathogen was not observed via smear examination of the air sac fluid. While bacterial cultures remained negative, fungal culture detected Aspergillus fumigatus.

On the 156th day of admission, inappetence, vomiting, and open mouth breathing even at rest were observed, with the penguin dying on the following day. Pathological examination revealed macroscopic findings of airsacculitis (Fig. 4A), pleuritis, peritonitis, and caseous nodules in the right caudal lung lobe. Histopathological examination demonstrated pyogenic granulomatous airsacculitis (Fig. 4B), bronchopneumonia with focal necrosis and fibrin exudation (Fig. 4C), and intratracheal hemorrhage. Gomori methenamine silver staining revealed numerous fungal hyphae and conidia within the lesions (Fig. 4D). Bacterial colonies were diffusely observed throughout the respiratory system, and A. fumigatus and Escherichia coli were detected in the air sac fluid collected postmortem examination. Genotypic analysis of A. fumigatus isolates obtained postmortem and from the air sac fluid collected on the 115th day revealed identical microsatellite profiles, indicating persistence of a single strain throughout the course of infection [4].

Studies in mammals have shown that bronchoalveolar lavage can be used for the definitive diagnosis of respiratory infection [26]. In birds, however, bronchoalveolar lavage is impractical given that washing fluid introduced into the trachea can spread into the air sac, making the subsequent collection of this fluid impossible. Alternatively, studies in raptors have reported pathogen identification via endoscopic biopsy of lesions; however, this method has not been used clinically given that it requires cutting open the body, making it highly invasive, and places too much stress on penguins with suspected respiratory diseases [6, 15, 20]. Another study reported that nasal lavage can identify pathogens responsible for respiratory disease in parrots [9]; however, aspergillosis mainly develops in the lower respiratory tract, including the air sacs and lungs, in penguins [11, 15]. Nasal, pharyngeal, and tracheal swabs are susceptible to contamination by ubiquitous fungal conidia and are therefore of low diagnostic value [20]. In the current case, after collecting the air sac fluid observed on CT via sterile needle puncture from the body surface, mycelium was observed on the 17th day, whereas A. fumigatus was isolated on the 115th day. Moreover, we confirmed that A. fumigatus isolated from air sac fluid collected on the 115th day had identical microsatellite profiles. Based on our review of the literature, this is the first report of antemortem isolation of Aspergillus spp. in a penguin. The combination of CT and air sac fluid aspiration was found to be useful for the identification of the pathogen and diagnosis of aspergillosis. This method can avoid contamination by exogenous spores during swabbing of the upper respiratory tract and is expected to become useful not only for penguins but also for other birds in which endoscopic examination, which requires cutting open the body, is difficult.

Despite the long-term administration of itraconazole, pathological examination confirmed A. fumigatus, indicating that antifungal treatment did not provide sufficient therapeutic benefit. In such cases, drug resistance must be considered. However, drug susceptibility testing of the A. fumigatus isolate in this case confirmed its susceptibility to itraconazole, amphotericin B, and voriconazole [1, 22]. The ineffectiveness of antifungal treatment in this case could presumably be attributed to poor penetration of antifungal drugs into inflamed tissue and limited blood flow to the air sacs [5, 14]. Considering the cost and burden of treatment on the penguin, itraconazole was selected. However, had effective penetration of antifungal drugs into the inflamed area been prioritized, topical treatment and early initiation of nebulized antifungal therapy may have been useful [24].

Antemortem air sac fluid examination could not identify bacterial infections observed in the postmortem examination. The gas-producing E. coli detected postmortem examination may explain the gas findings in the posterior thoracic air sac seen on CT on the 115th day [13]. In this case, fluid was collected only from the interclavicular air sac, not the posterior thoracic air sac. If fluid had been collected from the posterior thoracic air sac where gas was detected on CT, E. coli might have been identified before death. This highlights a limitation of the approach, namely that pathogens may be missed if all suspicious air sacs are not sampled.

Nevertheless, the combination of CT and air sac aspiration represents a promising approach for the antemortem diagnosis of aspergillosis in penguins.
