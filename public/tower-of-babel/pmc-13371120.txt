Decoupled accumulation of chlorophylls and total carotenoids between floral and vegetative organs in Dendrobium
Authors: Feng-Ping Zhang, Le Yu, Qi-Yong Huang, Han-Run Li, Shi-Bao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChlorophylls and carotenoids constitute the predominant plant pigments, playing pivotal roles in photosynthesis, pollinator attraction, and defense against biotic and abiotic stress. However, differences in pigment composition between floral and vegetative organs, along with their functional coordination, remain poorly understood. We quantified chlorophyll (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll) and total carotenoid contents in flower organs (petals, sepals and floral lips), roots, stems and leaves across 36 Dendrobium species. The results revealed significantly higher variation in pigment contents of flowers, particularly petal total carotenoids, compared to conserved vegetative pigment patterns. Leaves exhibited the highest chlorophyll, whereas floral lips contained the highest total carotenoid content. Ancestral state reconstruction further supported strong phylogenetic conservatism in vegetative pigment traits and marked evolutionary lability in floral pigment traits. Strong positive correlations were observed among pigments of flowers (petals, sepals, floral lips), in contrast to the non-significant correlations between pigment contents of flowers and vegetative organs. Among vegetative organs, leaf pigment levels were positively correlated with stem pigment levels, but with root pigment levels. Based on principal component analysis (PCA), the first axis of multivariate variation was primarily defined by floral chlorophylls and total carotenoids, whereas the second axis represented variability derived from pigment profiles of vegetative organ. Floral and vegetative pigments exhibited clear evolutionary and functional decoupling, a pattern robust to phylogenetic correction. Introduction

Pigments are central to plant biology, mediating critical ecological interactions and physiological processes [1]. Chlorophylls, the engines of photosynthesis, facilitate light capture in vegetative organs [2], carotenoids, by contrast, fulfill dual roles: acting as accessory photosynthetic pigments [3–5] and serving as visual signals to attract pollinators to flowers [6, 7]. This functional dichotomy suggests potential evolutionary trade-offs: selection for photosynthetic efficiency in vegetative organs (e.g., leaves) may constrain pigment diversification of flower, and vice versa [8, 9]. Carotenoids were likely present in the vegetative tissues, their primary roles were probably unrelated to pollinator attraction and instead included functions such as photosynthesis [10]. Yet whether chlorophyll and carotenoid pigments evolve independently between plant organs remains unexplored, particularly in lineages exhibiting extraordinary floral diversity like Orchidaceae. Orchid flowers possess complex structure, including the highly modified median petal known as the floral lip, a specialized and modified petal which act as a primary pollinator landing platform and key visual signal for pollinators [11, 12].

Among orchids, the genus Dendrobium stands out for its horticultural and ecological significance, displaying a striking range of floral colors from vibrant yellows to subtle greens [13]. Most Dendrobium species are epiphytic orchids distributed in tropical and subtropical montane forests, where they experience variable light conditions ranging from shaded understory to exposed canopy microhabitats. These environmental gradients impose strong selective pressures on both photosynthetic function and pollinator attraction [14–16]. Pigments of flowers primarily function as visual signals to attract specific pollinators, subjecting them to strong biotic selection pressures linked to reproductive success [17]. However, floral coloration is also shaped by abiotic factors and plays additional roles in protecting reproductive structures and maintaining gamete viability, with many plant systems experiencing a combination of biotic and abiotic selective pressures rather than pollinator‐mediated selection alone [18]. Most Dendrobium are pollinated by daylight-active bees (Hymenoptera: Apoidea), which rely strongly on visible-light color cues to locate and recognize flowers [19]. Dendrobium flowers, comprising petals, sepals, and the floral lip, a highly modified and specialized petal that functions as the key visual attractant and pollinator landing platform [12, 20]. The bee pollinator Ctenoplectra davidi visiting D. chrysotoxum flowers was firstly found [19], D. chrysanthum was pollinated by male Ctenoplectra cornuta [21]. Meanwhile, Dendrobium vegetative organs (roots, leaves, stems) vary in pigmentation, where pigments serve critical survival roles [22–24]. For example, chlorophyll-rich aerial roots, supports photosynthesis under low-light canopy conditions, an adaptation thought to enhance survival in epiphytic habitats [23, 25]. This divergence in functional creates distinct evolutionary trajectories between flowers and vegetative organs. Despite this divergence, the evolutionary relationship between pigments of flowers and vegetative organs in Dendrobium remain unexamined.

To address this question, we focus on two major pigment classes: chlorophylls, the essential pigments for photosynthesis, and total carotenoids, which function in photoprotection and act as visual signals for pollinator attraction. Chlorophylls are primarily chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, two structurally and functionally distinct subtypes that we measured separately to better understand their differential accumulation across organs and potential ecological implications. Specifically, chlorophyll a is the core pigment directly involved in light energy conversion during photosynthesis, while chlorophyll b acts as an accessory pigment that expands the range of light wavelengths captured, particularly in low-light environments [26, 27]. While this pigment class mediates light capture in photosynthetic tissues such as leaves, chlorophylls also accumulate within floral organs. In these floral tissues, their green coloration may serves as a visual cue to pollinators in shaded habitats [5, 28, 29]. By comparing chlorophylls and carotenoids across multiple organs and species, we can assess whether their evolution is coordinated or decoupled. Previous studies on plant pigment evolution have largely focused on flowers [30] or leaves alone [31, 32]. For instance, research on Antirrhinum and Petunia has highlighted pollinator-mediated selection driving floral color divergence, with flower color evolving adaptively to match pollinator perception [33]. Few studies have integrated pigment evolution across both reproductive and vegetative organ types, particularly in diverse genera like Dendrobium. Furthermore, no study has combined phenotypic analyses with ancestral state reconstruction to distinguish conserved ancestral traits from derived, labile innovations in organ-specific pigmentation. Within the genus, pigment variation is predominantly interspecific, with minimal intraspecific variation in flower color observed in the studied taxa. Floral lip color, a key floral structure, exhibits substantial interspecific diversity, ranging from pale yellow, golden yellow, and orange to pink, purple, and white.

In this study, we address this gap by characterizing chlorophyll and total carotenoid evolution across key reproductive and vegetative organs (petal, sepal, floral lip, root, leaf and stem) in Dendrobium, exploring the independent and correlated patterns of pigment diversification across these compartments. We further integrate ancestral state reconstruction to evaluate phylogenetic conservatism in vegetative pigments and evolutionary lability in floral pigments. Our research focused on three specific objectives: (1) to compare the variation in chlorophyll and total carotenoid contents between floral and vegetative organs. We hypothesized that: (a) chlorophyll and total carotenoid contents of flowers exhibit higher variability than pigments of vegetative organs; (b) flowers particularly floral lips contain higher total carotenoid contents, potentially adapting to diverse pollinators; (c) vegetative organs show lower chlorophyll and total carotenoid content variability, and with leaves maintaining peak chlorophyll content to optimize light capture and energy conversion. (2) to test correlations of chlorophyll and total carotenoid contents within floral organs (floral petal, sepal, lip), within vegetative organs (root, leaf, stem), and between floral and vegetative organs. We expect modular correlations, with strong associations within floral or vegetative organs but weak cross-organ correlations, indicating independent co-ordinations. (3) to examine whether chlorophyll and total carotenoid contents of flowers and vegetative organs form distinct dimensions in multivariate trait space, after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness among Dendrobium with such a clear separation serving as a key empirical test of the hypothesis that pigments of flower and vegetative organs follow independent evolutionary trajectories. Our findings will provide a framework for understanding pigment trait modularity in flowering plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The cultivated 36 Dendrobium species (Fig. 1.) were collected from the greenhouse at Kunming Botanical Garden (KBG), Kunming Institute of Botany (KIB), Chinese Academy of Sciences in Kunming, Yunnan Province, southwestern China. The studied species were formally identified by Prof. Fengping Zhang. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the Herbarium of Kunming Institute of Botany (KUN), Chinese Academy of Sciences with deposition number (Table S1).

Pigment content measurement

For each species, at least six independent individual plants were randomly selected. For each individual, healthy mature leaves, newly opened flowers and healthy white-green aerial root tips were collected separately, and placed in a cold box with ice and then taken to laboratory. Petals (excluding the floral lips), sepals, floral lips, root tips, leaves and middle stem segments were sampled, weighed immediately for fresh weight (FW), cut into small fragments, and placed into 15-ml centrifuge tubes. N,N-dimethylformamide was added to a final volume of 5 ml. Tubes were placed in a room at 4℃ under complete darkness for seven days to ensure complete pigment extraction, and absorbance values were measured using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-2550, Shimadzu, Japan) at 450 nm, 647 nm, and 664.5 nm [34, 35]. Data analysis

Differences in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophylls (chlorophyll a + b) and total carotenoid contents among different organs were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by LSD multiple comparisons test. Correlations between pigment contents were conducted using Pearson’s correlation tests across all species. All analyses were performed in R v.4.4.2 [36]. To account for phylogenetic relationships among species, a phylogenetically independent contrast (PIC) analysis was performed by using the R package “ape” [37], based on a published molecular phylogenetic tree of Dendrobium inferred from whole genome re-sequencing data [38]. For each pair of pigment traits, PICs were computed for both traits separately. The correlation between the two sets of contrasts was then assessed using Pearson’s correlation forced through the origin, as required under the Brownian motion model of traits evolution [39]. The correlation coefficient and its significance were used to evaluate the relationship after phylogenetic correction. To evaluate the correlations both within and among chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll (chlorophyll a + b) and total carotenoid contents defining petal, sepal, floral lip, leaf, stem, and root tissues, a principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted with the “prcomp” function in the R package “vegan” on all 24 traits. The phylogenetic principal component analysis (pPCA) was also performed [40] to account for the phylogenetic influence. Ancestral state reconstruction for quantitative pigment traits was carried out using the R package “phytools” [41], based on the phylogenetic topology from Zhang et al. [38].

Ancestral state reconstruction was performed for chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll (a + b), and total carotenoid contents of petals, sepals, floral lips, leaves, stems, and roots across 36 Dendrobium species (Figs. S1–S6). Floral organs (petals, sepals, and floral lips) showed pronounced evolutionary lability, with frequent and marked state transitions. The total carotenoid contents of petal and floral lip exhibited the most dramatic evolutionary shifts, corresponding to high interspecific variation in floral coloration. In contrast, vegetative organs (leaves, stems, and roots) exhibited strong phylogenetic conservatism in the contents of chlorophylls and total carotenoids, with closely related species showing similar values and relatively gradual evolutionary shifts. The ancestral Dendrobium was reconstructed to possess moderate to high leaf chlorophyll contents and stable pigment profiles in stems and roots, consistent with conserved photosynthetic and photoprotective functions. These patterns reveal clear evolutionary decoupling between conserved vegetative pigmentation and labile floral pigmentation, supporting the hypothesis that reproductive and vegetative traits evolve under divergent selective regimes in Dendrobium.

Discussion

The evolution of plant pigmentation is a dynamic interplay between ecological function and physiological constraint, with distinct selective pressures shaping pigment profiles across different organs [42]. This general pattern has been widely documented in flowering plants, wherein floral pigment variation is driven by both biotic pollinator pressure and abiotic environmental filtering [43], while vegetative pigment traits are primarily constrained by physiological and photosynthetic requirements [2]. This study indicated distinct modular phenotypic patters in the accumulation of chlorophylls and total carotenoids between floral and vegetative organs across the studied Dendrobium species. Ancestral state reconstruction further supported that vegetative pigment traits were evolutionarily conserved across the phylogeny, whereas floral pigment traits exhibited high lability with frequent evolutionary shifts among lineages. These findings offer new insights into how orchids balance phenotypic diversification in reproductive traits with functional stability in vegetative traits, a key evolutionary strategy that may underlie the wide ecological adaptation and horticultural diversity of Dendrobium species.

This study reveals a pronounced disparity in chlorophyll and total carotenoid content variability between floral and vegetative organs. Pigments of flowers, particularly the contents of total carotenoids in petal, exhibit substantial quantitative variation, while pigments of vegetative organs, such as root chlorophyll b, showed comparatively constrained variation in their accumulated amounts. This pattern aligns with divergent ecological roles of these organs: Pigments of flowers primarily function in pollinator attraction, where high variability can be favored to adapt to diverse pollinator sensory preferences [28, 44], yet floral pigmentation is also shaped by abiotic conditions [18, 45]. In contrast, pigments of vegetative organs are critical for core physiological processes like photosynthesis, where stability is advantageous for sustaining consistent efficiency [1, 2]. This partitioning of selective regimes agrees with earlier studies on model flowering plants, which demonstrated that vegetative pigment profiles are canalized by strong stabilizing selection, whereas floral pigments evolve under a combination of biotic and abiotic pressures [2, 43].

The organ-specific specialization of pigments further supports this functional divide. Leaves, the primary photosynthetic organs, have higher chlorophyll content, reflecting their roles in light capture and energy conversion [29]. In contrast, floral organs (petals, sepals, floral lips) have significantly lower chlorophyll levels, minimizing green coloration that would reduce contrast with a background of green leaves and hinder pollinator detection [5, 28, 46]. Notably, the floral lip, a specialized floral structure that often serves as pollinator landing platforms and main attractive organs [12], exhibits higher carotenoid content. At the same time, carotenoids also contribute to photoprotection and oxidative stress resistance, highlighting roles beyond visual signaling that may interact with both biotic and abiotic selective pressures [47]. Species with high leaf chlorophyll contents are typically found in high-light habitats, where increased chlorophyll content enhances light capture and photosynthetic efficiency [48–50]. Conversely, species with lower leaf chlorophyll concentrations are adapted to shaded understory environments, where lower chlorophyll levels may optimize light utilization under limited light conditions [50]. Similarly, species with high floral carotenoid concentrations are often associated with nutrient-rich habitats, suggesting that increased nutrient availability may support higher carotenoid synthesis for enhanced pollinator attraction [51, 44].

Correlation analyses revealed a clear modular structure in phenotypic accumulation patters of chlorophyll and total carotenoid: strong positive correlations among pigments of flowers, significant correlations between pigments of leaves and stems, and minimal overlap between floral and vegetative pigment associations. This floral–vegetative modularity matches the evolutionary decoupling hypothesis proposed in comparative plant studies [40, 52], and our results further extend this framework to epiphytic Dendrobium orchids. This pattern is further reinforced by ancestral state reconstruction, which recovered conserved vegetative pigment states and labile floral pigment states across the phylogenetic tree. This modularity is consistent with divergent phenotypic differentiation between floral and vegetative organ systems. Within floral organs, chlorophyll concentrations co-vary significantly across petals, sepals, and floral lips, with parallel correlation patterns observed for total carotenoids. These associations remained relatively consistent even after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness, which suggests that coordinated chlorophylls and total carotenoids distribution across floral organs may represent adaptive tendencies rather than simply reflecting shared ancestry. Coordinated coloration likely enhances the integrity of floral signals: a consistent pigment profile across petals, sepals, and floral lips ensures that pollinators perceive a unified visual cue, increasing foraging efficiency [28, 53–55]. Species with high carotenoid levels in petals consistently show elevated carotenoid accumulation in the floral lips as well, which may strengthen the overall floral display and improve pollinator detection.

In vegetative organs, pigments of leaves and stems are significantly correlated, while pigments of roots vary independently from both. This pattern suggests a phenotypic module linking above-ground vegetative tissues, which share similar light environments and physiological roles [56, 57]. Roots, which function in nutrient uptake and grow in shaded or soil environments [58] exhibit distinct adaptations in epiphytic orchids like Dendrobium, where they evolve as aerial roots exposed to light [16]. Their pigment profiles are optimized for non-photosynthetic functions such as photoprotection or antioxidant defense [59, 60]. Chlorophyll in these aerial roots, though present at lower levels than in leaves, may play a role in sensing light quality to regulate root development, a function distinct from its role in photosynthesis [61, 62]. Such independent pigment adaptation in aerial roots represents a unique feature of epiphytic orchids that differs greatly from terrestrial model plants, adding novel comparative evidence to organ-specific pigment evolution [23].

Principal component analysis (PCA) confirms the modular separation of floral and vegetative total chlorophyll and carotenoid profiles. The first PCA axis predominantly captures pigment variation of flower, while the second axis captures pigment variation of vegetative organ. This clear separation in multivariate trait space aligns with correlation-based evidence of modularity, establishing chlorophyll and carotenoid-based profiles of flower and vegetative organ as distinct phenotypic modules. Together with ancestral state reconstruction, these multivariate results confirm that floral and vegetative pigment systems have undergone independent evolutionary trajectories. The second PCA axis, capturing pigment variation of vegetative organ, likely reflects optimization for photosynthetic efficiency and stress tolerance. For chlorophyll content, D. falconeri had the highest concentration in leaves and D. polyanthum the lowest; in roots, D. sulcatum ranked highest and D. cariniferum lowest; and in stems, D. henryi showed the maximum while D. chrysotoxum showed the minimum. For carotenoid content, D. hancockii had the highest leaf concentration and D. chrysotoxum the lowest; in roots, D. christyanum ranked highest and D. gratiosissimum lowest; and in stems, D. wattii exhibited the maximum and D. chrysotoxum the minimum.

The tight clustering of leaf and stem pigments along this axis suggests that these organs may exhibit coordinated variation in pigment profiles, which could potentially reflect responses to environmental variation, such as light intensity or nutrient availability, thereby helping to maintain stable physiological function in above-ground vegetative tissues [56, 57]. Consistency between conventional and phylogenetic PCA reflects functional divergence. This modularity suggests that orchids may: (1) display diversified chlorophyll- and carotenoid-based floral coloration without compromising photosynthetic efficiency, (2) exhibit distinct adaptations of pigments of vegetative organs to light environments, free from pollinator selection constraints, (3) develop specialized root pigment strategies with minimal apparent trade-offs with floral traits. This phenotypic modularity may underpin the hyperdiversity of Dendrobium, one of the largest orchid genera, by supporting rapid floral diversification while conserving core vegetative functions. Our quantitative analysis is restricted to chlorophylls and total carotenoids. Floral coloration in Dendrobium is also strongly modulated by anthocyanins and flavonoids, which were not measured in the present study. Accordingly, the modular patterns observed in this study apply specifically to the chlorophylls–total carotenoid axis and do not encompass the full spectrum of floral pigment diversity. Future work integrating targeted metabolomics of flavonoids and anthocyanins, alongside transcriptomic regulatory data, will enable a more holistic interpretation of floral color evolution and the evolutionary relationships of pigmentation across orchid organs.

Conclusion

This study reveals distinct phenotypic patterns of chlorophylls and total carotenoid pigments between floral and vegetative tissues in Dendrobium. Ancestral state reconstruction further confirms that vegetative pigment traits exhibit strong phylogenetic conservatism, whereas floral pigment traits show remarkable evolutionary lability. Pigments of flowers exhibit high variability and coordinated accumulation, with higher total carotenoid content, particularly in the floral lip, consistent with a putative role in visual signaling potentially associated with pollinator attraction. In contrast, variation in vegetative chlorophylls and total carotenoid appears relatively constrained, forming a stable functional module that likely contributes to maintaining photosynthetic efficiency and physiological robustness in leaves and stems. These evolutionary patterns, supported by both phenotypic correlation and ancestral state reconstruction, demonstrate clear evolutionary decoupling between floral and vegetative pigment systems. These findings advance our understanding of how phenotypic modularity relates to divergent reproductive pigment variation and vegetative physiological stability in flowering plants. Our study also provides a biochemical reference for the targeted horticultural improvement of floral color traits related to total carotenoid-mediated hue variation. This study did not include quantification of anthocyanins and flavonoids, which are also important determinants of full-spectrum floral coloration in Dendrobium. Future research combining multi-pigment metabolomics, gene regulatory characterization, and comparative genomic analyses (e.g., ortholog screening, regulatory variation, and dN/dS calculation) will help further elucidate the evolutionary patterns of floral pigmentation and more comprehensively address the functional trade-offs underlying organ-specific color.
