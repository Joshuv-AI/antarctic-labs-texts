Availability, accessibility, and utilization of mental health services or support among university students in Africa: a mixed methods systematic review with meta-analysis and meta-synthesis
Authors: Hesborn Wao, Melvin A Wao, Steve Bicko Omollo, Carolyne K Mutua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe literature is replete with studies documenting the magnitude of mental illness among university students in Africa. However, it is unclear what mental health services or support (MHSS) are available to students and the extent to which they access and utilize them. We determined the availability, accessibility, and utilization of MHSS among university students in Africa and explored factors associated with the accessibility and utilization of MHSS.MethodsWe obtained data through a systematic search of five databases and included studies that reported on the availability, accessibility, or utilization of MHSS among university students in Africa. We extracted data on sociodemographic characteristics, MHSS focus (availability, accessibility, or utilization), mental health outcomes, and factors associated with MHSS accessibility and utilization. Quantitative data were meta-analyzed, and qualitative data were meta-synthesized.Results and conclusionWe included 18 studies. More informal than formal sources of MHSS are available, accessible, and utilized by African university students. While parents and lecturers are often seen as the available informal sources of MHSS for university students in Africa, access to these services is hampered by instrumental-related barriers such as inadequate mental health literacy and financial constraints. These findings underscore the need for targeted, low-cost interventions, such as faculty training and peer counseling, that improve mental health literacy and access to services. Providing psychoeducation to parents can help them guide students toward appropriate help without being an obstructive force. Rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach, universities and policymakers can consider developing segmented mental health promotion campaigns that target specific student subpopulations (i.e., those with different religious, cultural, or ethnic backgrounds) with messages that resonate with their preferred help-seeking pathways. There is a need to prioritize policies that expand access to mental health insurance and services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07529-1.Keywords: Anxiety, Counseling, Help-seeking behavior, Mental health services, Psychosocial distress, Public health, University students, Young adults

Background

Mental illnesses account for 13% of the global disease burden [1], and about 35% of university students worldwide experience mental health problems [2]. In Africa, mental illnesses, including anxiety-based disorders, mood disorders, disruptive behaviors, and substance use disorder, continue to be a significant concern [3]. For example, while Africa’s population grew by nearly 50% between 2000 and 2015, the number of years lost to mental health disability increased by over 50% during the same period [4]. Mental illnesses are more prevalent among university students than in the general population [5]. For example, based on a systematic review of the prevalence of depression among university students in low and middle-income countries, about one-quarter (24.4%) of university students grapple with mental health issues [6]. Similarly, over one-third of university students in Ethiopia suffer from common mental disorders [7]. In Nigeria, the prevalence of mental health problems varies (i.e., psychological distress 25.2%, anxiety 28.8%, depression 33.5%, and perceived stress 60.5%) [8].

Whereas numerous systematic reviews [5, 7–9] and prevalence studies [10–18] have examined the prevalence of mental illness and associated factors in African university students, these studies have notable limitations. For example, of the 44 included studies in a systematic review examining university students’ use of MHSS, only one was from Africa [19]. Another review that examined university students’ mental health focused solely on sub-Saharan Africa, and thus may not provide an accurate picture of the situation on the continent [20]. The most recent review examined help-seeking intentions, behaviors, and barriers in college students broadly, excluding help-seeking from informal sources such as family and friends [21]. Based on existing evidence, it is unclear what sources of MHSS are available to university students and the extent to which they access and utilize them. Notably, students can only utilize MHSS when access is provided [22]. Similarly, students can only access MHSS that are available to them (e.g., if they are aware that these MHSS exist).

Regarding the availability or awareness of MHSS, Gebreegziabher and colleagues found that more than half of the students at Jimma University in Ethiopia who had common mental health disorders (52.9%; n = 402) reported that they were not aware of available MHSS at the student clinic [23]. With respect to accessibility, in a study examining access to support services offered at the Dar es Salaam College of Education in Tanzania, approximately 67% of students never sought help from the college’s support services or resources [24]. With reference to utilization, MHSS utilization rate among students varied: 16.4% based on a multi-country WHO Mental Health Survey [25], 24.6% based on another WHO World Mental Health International College Student Initiative [26], and 28.9% based on a study examining mental healthcare utilization among first-year university students in South Africa [22]. A study investigating the utilization of counseling services by university students in Kenya found that more women (91.5%) than men (8.5%) utilize counseling services [27].

The dearth of data on MHSS availability, accessibility, and utilization by university students in Africa justifies conducting this systematic review that sought to answer four research questions: (1) What sources of MHSS are available to university students in Africa? (2) To what extent do students access available MHSS? (3) To what extent do students utilize accessible MHSS? (4) What are the drivers of accessibility and utilization of MHSS by university students? Examining the mental health of university students is critical because mental health challenges transcend beyond the classroom and into the community. Mental health problems such as depression and anxiety are strongly associated with poor academic performance, increased dropout, and heightened vulnerability to suicidal ideation [20]. Untreated mental illness among students shape their postgraduation trajectories with long-term effects on employability, economic participation, and engagement in community life [28]. Given that mental illnesses are associated with educational attainment [26, 29–33] and suicide [34–36], knowing what MHSS are available, accessed, and utilized, and identifying drivers of MHSS access and utilization may help policy-makers and program managers to design evidence-based MHSS programs, especially in resource-constrained settings typical of most African universities. The knowledge gained from this systematic review can inform the design of effective psychosocial and mental health interventions that reduce the burden of mental illneses, improve academic achievement, and enhance the quality of life of university students [37–39].

Methods

Registration of study protocol

This mixed methods systematic review, including meta-analysis and meta-synthesis, was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [39] (see Appendix 1). Its protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022296870), followed by a peer-reviewed publication [40].

Study design

This review was conducted in accordance with the updated methodological guidance developed by the JBI Mixed Methods Review Methodology Group [41]. Specifically, given its focus on three dimensions of MHSS (availability, accessibility, and utilization) and factors associated with MHSS access and utilization, we employed a convergent segregated approach. Specifically, we conducted separate quantitative syntheses (including meta-analysis or narrative review) and qualitative syntheses (via narrative summary), followed by the integration of evidence derived from both syntheses to gain a deeper understanding of the topic.

Definition of variables

For this review, mental illnesses refer to health conditions that affect an individual’s thinking, mood, or behavior (or a combination of these) and can be temporary or chronic [3]. Examples of mental illnesses include depression, generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, social anxiety disorder, specific phobias, psychological distress, and other mental disorders. By MHSS, we refer to the delivery of an intervention, therapy, information, or social support designed to address a mental illness. This includes inpatient services, outpatient services, day treatment, university counseling services, and community mental health services. A MHSS is said to be available if it is known to exist (e.g., at a clinic facility on- or off-campus and possibly in the form of mental health information), irrespective of whether it is accessible. A MHSS is considered accessible if students can seek the service or support, notwithstanding whether it is received or not. Finally, an MHSS is utilized if students receive it, with indicators including admissions, treatment usage, and costs of treatment.

Data sources and search strategies

We conducted a systematic search of published studies from PubMed, ERIC, Cochrane Library, Trip database, and Semantic Scholar for the years 2000–2024. As indicated in a recent systematic review of university students’ mental health in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the period 2000–2024 is suitable for researching studies on student mental health in SSA because it includes a significant amount of time during which there have been changes in global health policies and trends [20]. A comprehensive search strategy was developed using keywords and controlled vocabulary adapted to five electronic databases. In PubMed, the following search terms were used: ((university student*) OR (college student*)) AND ((depression [MeSH Terms]) OR (depressive symptoms [MeSH Terms]) OR (depressive disorder [MeSH Terms]) OR (dysthymia [MeSH Terms]) OR (mental disorder [MeSH Terms]) OR (mental distress) OR (anxiety [MeSH Terms]) OR (stress) OR (psychological distress)) AND ((availability of health services [MeSH Terms]) OR (accessibility of health services [MeSH Terms]) OR (access to health care [MeSH Terms])) OR (health care utilization [MeSH Terms]) OR (health service utilization [MeSH Terms]) OR (treatment seeking [MeSH Terms])) AND (Africa [MeSH Terms]) NOT (child*). In Cochrane Library and ERIC, we used the terms “Mental Health AND Africa.” In Trip Database, “Mental Health AND Africa AND student*” were used, whereas in Semantic Scholar, “Mental health” AND (“college” OR “university”) AND (student*) AND “Africa*” AND “access*” OR “health-seeking” were used. In addition, we employed the snowballing technique, whereby the reference lists of retrieved studies were manually examined and additional articles meeting the inclusion criteria were added. Given the paucity of mental health studies focusing on availability, accessibility, or utilization of MHSS by university students in Africa, we did not limit the search by language, year of publication, or study design.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion or exclusion of studies was determined using the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Setting (PICOS) framework. That is, the review included: any study including college or university students, excluding studies focusing on general population, children, or refugees (population); any study examining an intervention aimed at addressing availability, accessibility, and/or utilization of MHSS (intervention); any study reporting mental health-related problem such as psychological distress, stress, depression, and anxiety (outcomes); and any study focusing on students enrolled in an undergraduate or postgraduate degree–granting institution or tertiary institution, including a multicounty study provided it has data on students in Africa (setting). We excluded studies where it was not possible to extract data on the availability, accessibility, or utilization of MHHS or where participants were not university/college students.

Data extraction and management

We uploaded the results from electronic database searches into EndNote X9 to facilitate the removal of duplicate records. Next, we followed a two-step screening process. First, a pair of reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies against the inclusion criteria to determine broad eligibility. We identified retained studies as either ‘include’ or ‘unclear.’ Next, two reviewers independently screened the full text of all retained studies against the eligibility criteria. Disagreements regarding study inclusion or exclusion were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached, or, in cases of disagreement, a third reviewer mediated the discussion.

Two reviewers experienced in systematic review methods pilot-tested a data extraction form using a sample of included studies. A high level of agreement was obtained (Cohen’s kappa = 0.85). A pair of coders independently extracted the required data from each included study using the pilot-tested data extraction form managed in Google Form. The following data were extracted for all included studies: author’s name; year of publication; country where the study was conducted; research type (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods research); study design; program level (undergraduate vs. graduate); discipline or field of study (medicine, engineering, mixed, etc.); gender; age (mean, SD, range); sample size; MHSS focus (availability, accessibility, or utilization, or a combination of these); mental health outcome (depression, anxiety, stress, etc.); and factors associated with accessibility and utilization of MHSS. For each study, data were independently extracted by two investigators, and any discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached or a third reviewer arbitrated. The level of agreement was assessed by computing Cohen’s kappa.

To accommodate potential heterogeneity due to varying measures used in quantitative studies included, we employed multiple approaches in extracting quantitative data. Where numerical data were reported, we extracted descriptive statistics, correlations, odds ratios, or risk ratios. Given our interest in the empirical data and authors’ interpretations thereof, we extracted qualitative data from the results and discussion sections verbatim. When a mixed methods approach was used, quantitative and qualitative data were collected separately.

Assessment of methodological quality

The risk of bias in included studies was examined by having two reviewers independently assess each study using relevant tools. For cross-sectional studies or the quantitative component of mixed methods studies, we used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies [42]. Whereas this tool contains eight items (each scored using a 4-point scale), given that all items were applicable, we modified the tool to include a 3-point scale scored as low risk of bias (“+”) corresponding to “Yes,” high risk of bias (“-“) corresponding to “No,” and unclear risk of bias (“?“) corresponding to “Unclear.” For qualitative studies or the qualitative component of mixed methods studies, we used the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) – Qualitative Checklist [43]. This tool contains 10 items, each scored using a 3-point response scale as follows: low risk of bias (“+”), high risk of bias (“-“), and unclear risk of bias (“?“). We used RevMan 5.3 software to present the results of the methodological quality of included studies graphically [44]. Where pairs of reviewers appraising the methodological quality of included studies disagreed, a third reviewer discussed the issue with them until a consensus was reached.

Data analysis and assessment of heterogeneity

Quantitative data analysis – Data extracted from the Google Form were downloaded as MS Excel for use in data analysis. Frequencies and percentages of the constructs measured, the tools used, and basic descriptive statistics reported were presented. By comparing the results of at least two studies focusing on similar mental health problems, we performed a meta-analysis of the proportion of students accessing or utilizing specific MHSS using the metanprop function in Stata, and expressed the results as estimated pooled proportions using forest plots. To accommodate study heterogeneity, we used random effect models to estimate the pooled proportion as they provide more equal weighting across studies [45]. Results from the multivariate test were preferred over those from the bivariate test in cases where both statistical analyses were performed. Test for heterogeneity between studies was examined using the I2 statistics [46], with I2 >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity.

Qualitative data analysis – We employed thematic analysis methods, adopting a critical realist epistemological stance that emphasizes reflection on meaningful patterns within the qualitative data. Adopting an interpretive meta-synthesis, we performed line-by-line, open, and complete coding, creating a thematic map. We grouped codes into meaningful patterns and compared them across studies to identify patterns and contradictions. To inductively refine themes, they were reviewed in consultation with at least two review authors.

Results

Study selection process

Figure 1 presents a flow diagram illustrating the literature search process, based on PRISMA guidelines [47]. Searches from five databases, including a hand search, identified 7,437 potentially relevant studies. Excluding 1,731 duplicates, the titles and abstracts of the remaining 5,706 studies were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 5,582 records for the reasons outlined. Inter-rater agreement for data screening indicated strong agreement, with a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.85. Further assessment of the full texts of the remaining 124 studies for eligibility resulted in the exclusion of 106 studies. Altogether, 18 studies met our inclusion criteria (13 quantitative [cross-sectional] studies, 1 qualitative study, and 4 mixed methods studies).

Characteristics of included studies

Table 1 presents the basic characteristics of 18 studies involving 57,949 students from eight countries: South Africa (4 studies), Ethiopia (3 studies), Egypt (3 studies), Nigeria (2 studies), Sudan (2 studies), Tanzania (2 studies), Kenya (1 study), and Cameroon (1 study). These studies were published between 2010 and 2024, with over half (56%, n = 10) published between 2019 and 2024. The sample size ranged from 18 to 28,516 students (Median = 639, Mean = 3219, SD = 7529). Two-thirds (67%) of these studies were conducted in public universities, 16 studies (89%) included undergraduate students, and five studies (28%) included students in a medical program. The percentage of female students ranged from 40% to 71% (Mean = 60%). For most studies, the mean age of students was approximately 21 years. In eight studies, random sampling techniques were employed. In terms of type of mental health-related problem, three studies each focused on common mental disorders [22, 24, 48], psychological distress [49–51], and stress [25, 28, 52]; two studies focused on mental distress [48, 53]; and one study each focused on anxiety and depression [23], schizophrenia [53], and emotional distress [54]. Different self-reporting screening tools were used to assess mental health outcomes, including the 10-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) [23]; self-reporting questionnaire to evaluate mental disorders [16, 55]; General Health Questionnaire to assess psychological distress [49]; investigator developed questionnaires to assess stress [25, 28]; three questionnaires (Medical Outcomes Survey social support survey, Arabic Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale, and Arabic WHO Quality of Life Brief questionnaire) to assess depression, anxiety, and stress [51].

Methodological quality of included studies

We judged the risk of bias in the included quantitative studies as moderate, with 17 studies having clearly defined inclusion criteria, subjects and settings being described in detail, and appropriate statistical analysis being used (Fig. 2 upper panel). However, in about one-half of these studies, strategies to deal with confounding factors were not clearly stated. Similarly, the risk of bias in the included qualitative or mixed methods studies (Fig. 2, lower panel) was judged to be moderate, with all five studies having a clear statement of the research purpose, appropriate use of qualitative methods, a research design suitable for the research aim, and an appropriate recruitment strategy. However, in over one-half of the studies, it was unclear whether the relationship between the researcher and participants was adequately considered.

Available sources of MHSS

Based on our review, availability of MHSS was defined in terms of existence of a facility where the service is provided (e.g., “Mental health services were available at the student clinic… [p.4]” [23]); existence of formal or informal sources of MHSS (e.g., “… students mainly rely on informal sources of help such as their fellow students…[p.105]” [24]); or nature of service being provided (e.g. “… Kenyan universities offer social support to students in the form of counselling… [p.142]” [27]). Data on available MHSS were extractable from 61% (11/18) of included studies (Table 2). Informal sources of MHSS were the most frequently cited category, cited 25 times. This was followed by the facility/office where the service is available, cited 19 times. Formal sources and nature of MHSS were the least cited categories, cited 15 and 11 times, respectively. By formal sources, we refer to people who have accredited professional training to provide MHSS (e.g., psychiatrist, psychologist, etc.) whereas informal sources refer to those without such training (e.g., friends, parents, family members, etc.).

Number and percentage of available sources of mental health services or support

Available informal sources of MHSS, including friends (classmates, roommates, and intimate partners) and parents (family, elders, siblings, and other relatives), were the most frequently cited, mentioned in seven (39%) and five (27%) studies, respectively. Administrative staff/auxiliary police and other sources (e.g., mass media, reading a book, or watching TV) were the least cited, each mentioned in one study.

Available formal sources of MHSS, including mental health professionals (counselors) and doctors (including general practitioners, physicians, or healthcare providers), were the most frequently cited, mentioned in five (28%) and four (22%) of the studies, respectively. Interestingly, only one study mentioned a psychiatrist as an available formal source of MHSS.

Facility/office including counseling unit and referral district hospitals were the most frequently cited available sources of MHSS, each cited in four (22%) studies. The least cited sources of MHSS were psychosocial support center, students’ union office, and the dean of students’ office, each mentioned in one study.

Nature of MHSS including social support and use of medication were the most frequently cited nature of MHSS, each mentioned in three (17%) studies. Psychiatric care, individual interpersonal psychotherapy (talk therapy), and personal coping strategies were each cited in one study.

Accessible sources of MHSS and associated factors

In this review, the extent to which students accessed MHSS was measured by the proportion of students seeking mental help from formal or informal sources. Mental help seeking is referred to as “the tendency of students to reach out for assistance amidst stressful situations (p.99) [24] or “the act of seeking assistance for mental health issues, which may involve accessing professional mental health services, consulting friends or family members, or engaging with support groups” (p.2) [56]. Where data on accessibility were available from at least two studies, meta-analysis was performed using a random effects model and the pooled proportion was reported with a 95% confidence level.

Accessible informal sources of MHSS - seven accessible informal sources of MHSS were identified: religious leader or spiritual help; friend (classmate or roommate); parent (family, elders, siblings, or other relative); traditional healer; intimate partner; institution-based sources (lecturer, academic advisor, or instructor); and others (mass media, TV watching, reading a book, calling phone line). Meta-analyzable data were obtained from nine studies, including 4,650 students. The overall pooled proportion (estimated using a random effect model) of students who accessed informal sources of MHSS was 21% (95% CI; 16% − 25%) (Fig. 3). However, given the significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99.2%, p = 0.000), this finding should be interpreted with caution. In one study [24], three other accessible informal sources of MHSS were cited: student union (36%), dean of students (33%), and administrative staff/auxiliary police (28%). A second study [57] identified four additional accessible informal sources of MHSS: private hospital (50.19%), district hospital (39.7%), medical social center (5.06%), and pharmacy shop (2.81%).

Forest plot of pooled proportion of students accessing informal sources of mental health services or support (based on random effect models with 95% confidence interval)

Accessible formal sources of MHSS - Five accessible formal sources of MHSS were identified: institutional (mental health service from the college); psychiatrist; psychologist; physician (doctor, general practitioner, or healthcare provider); and counselor (mental health professional). Meta-analyzable data were obtained from nine studies (4,650 students). The pooled proportion of students who accessed formal sources of MHSS was 15% (95% CI: 11% − 18%), however, given the evidence of significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98.1%, p = 0.000), this finding should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of pooled proportion of students accessing formal sources of mental health services or support (based on random effect models with 95% confidence interval)

Instrumental-related barriers to access of MHSS – Seven types of instrumental-related barriers were identified, with meta-analyzable data obtainable from six studies (3,855 students): (a) mental health literacy (not knowing where to seek professional care or ignorance of health services); (b) financial constraints (inability to afford the financial costs involved or lack of transport); (c) having no one who could help one access professional care; (d) lack of time (e.g., difficulty taking time off education); (e) being too unwell to ask for help; and (f) lack of professionals from one’s own ethnic or cultural group. The pooled proportion of students citing these seven barriers was 48% (95% CI; 38% − 58%) (Fig. 5). In addition, in one study, it was reported that 42% of the students had no information at all about mental illness [23]. In one qualitative study [50], it was reported that students were not informed about the availability and benefits of guidance and counseling services in universities. In this study, a student focus group discussant commented, “If the services are there, they are not known, we should be made aware of such.” Another student remarked, “We should be told about where the guidance and counselling office are, and the services offered there so that we know who to see when we have problems.” Complementary findings from one mixed methods study [27] supported the findings related to barriers, including a lack of knowledge about counseling services, financial constraints, and insufficient time. In one study [24], 48% of students cited the location of the counseling center or the lack of a good environment as a barrier. Adverse social events were cited as a barrier in one study [50], and an insufficient number of qualified personnel and the use of denial as a coping style were identified as barriers in another study [27].

Forest plot of pooled proportion of students citing instrumental-related barriers to access of mental health services or support (based on random effect models with 95% confidence interval)

Attitudinal-related barriers to access of MHSS – Eight types of attitudinal-related barriers were identified, with meta-analyzable data obtainable from seven studies (4,288 students): (a) prefer to deal with the problem alone (i.e., wanting to solve the problem on their own); (b) prefer to get help from family or friends; (c) prefer to get al.ternative forms of care (e.g., traditional or religious healing or complementary therapies); (d) dislike talking about own feelings, emotions, or thoughts; (e) concern about medication side effects; (f) fear/afraid of being put in hospital against one’s will; (g) mistrust in health system (thinking professional care would not help); and (h) discouraging experience (e.g., previous bad experiences with professional care for mental health). The pooled proportion of students citing these five barriers was 47% (95% CI: 37%-58%) (Fig. 6). In addition, a mixed-methods study [25] found that unfair treatment from college sources of help was a barrier to accessing MHSS, and 31% of the students cited a lack of caring language as a significant barrier. In addition, other attitudinal-related barriers reported included thinking the problem would getter by itself (74.4%) [16]; being worried of uncertainties surrounding mental health diagnosis or fear of the unknown (59%); and not recognizing or knowing what signs or symptoms are related to psychological problems (58%) [49]. Finally, in a mixed-methods study [28], three attitudinal-related barriers reported included a lack of confidence in counselors, peer pressure, and a preference for seeking help from significant others and the Internet. Other attitudinal-related barriers to access to MHSS included methods of communication used to seek help from institutional sources when in stressful situation (e.g., face-to-face [83%], phone call [37%], email [16%], and letter writing [11%] [24] and student’s gender and other demographic facxtors such as religion, ethnicity, marital status, residence, area of growing, family history of mental illness, and substance use [16].

Forest plot of pooled proportion of students citing attitudinal-related barriers to access of mental health services or support (based on random effect models with 95% confidence interval)

Stigma-related barriers to access of MHSS - Eight types of stigma-related barriers were identified, with meta-analyzable data obtainable from six studies (3,531 students): (a) concern about what family might think, say, do, or feel; (b) concern that one might be seen as weak for having a mental health problem; (c) concern about what students or people at work might think, say, or do; (d) lack of confidentiality (concern that people known might find out); (e) feeling embarrassed or ashamed (fear of asking for help); (f) concern that people might not take one seriously if they found out one was having professional care; (g) concern that one might be seen as ‘crazy’; and (h) peer pressure (concern about what one’s friends might think, say, or do e.g. friends might think less of one). The pooled proportion of students citing these eight barriers was 44% (95% CI: 35%-54%) (Fig. 7). In addition, in one study [49], four stigma-related barriers were cited: not wanting a mental health problem to be on one’s medical records (63.5%); concern that it might harm one’s chances when applying for jobs (58.7%); concern that one might be seen as a bad parent (49.3%); and concern that one’s children may be taken into care or one may lose access or custody without one’s agreement (47.1%).

Utilized sources of MHSS and associated factors

Access to MHSS does not necessarily translate to MHSS utilization: “We know that having help or support resources is one thing, and the utilization of the existing help resources is the most essential and important thing in addressing stress (p.100).” [24] Thus, in this review, the extent to which students utilized different sources of MHSS was measured by the proportion of students receiving treatment [22], adhering to treatment [55], utilizing services [22], [24], or attending intervention sessions [55]. As with accessibility, a higher proportion of students utilized informal sources of MHSS than formal sources.

Utilized informal sources of MHSS – This review identified two utilized informal sources of MHSS: (a) religious leader/group or spiritual help and (b) traditional healer (to counter evil spirits). Using meta-analyzable data from four studies, which included 22,790 students, the overall pooled proportion (estimated using a random effects model) of students utilizing these two informal sources of MHSS was 42% (95% CI: 25%-59%) (Fig. 8, upper panel). Other studies identified several utilized informal sources of MHSS. A study exploring the level of utilization of college help or support resources among undergraduate student teachers experiencing psychosocial stressors identified [24]: friends (61%), student union (36%), lecturers (35%), dean of students (33%), academic advisor (28%), and administrative staff (28%). According to a study examining healthcare utilization among first-year university students in South Africa [22], the proportion of students utilizing MHSS were grouped into three categories: type of mental disorder (bipolar spectrum disorder – 64.3%, drug use disorder – 55.8%, generalized anxiety disorder – 36.1%, and major depressive disorder – 36.1%); number of disorders (exactly one disorder – 22.7%, exactly two mental disorders – 30.2%, and three or more mental disorders – 47.9%); and suicidal thoughts and behaviors (suicidal ideation without plan or attempt – 25.4%, suicidal plan without attempt – 41.6%, and suicide attempt – 52.9%). In one mixed methods study [55], good treatment adherence was reported, whereby virtually all students completed all eight treatment sessions, with two-thirds (67%) of them rating the quality of intervention received as excellent. Some qualitatively expressed their satisfaction: “…the care that I received is like [changing a dried tree to a tree with wet leaf]. I received my previous identity; I am confident, strong, have a good communication with my friends.” For some, the treatment received was beyond expectation: “When I came to this office, it was because of my friend’s advice. I was not expecting to get such kind of service. I just came to tell my [internal turmoil] if somebody is ready to listen to me. However, what I was thinking was completely different from what I received. It is beyond my expectation.” In a study examining mental healthcare utilization among first-year university students in South Africa, it was found that despite having access to free student counselling services on campus, less than one-third (28.9%) of first-year students with common mental disorders utilized MHSS in the preceding 12 months [22]. In a study exploring mental health stigma among university students in Nigeria [52], only 13% of students sought immediate medical care for active suicidal ideation. Onditi and colleagues [24] found that only about one-third (36%) of students used the student union as a source of help.

Forest plot of pooled proportion of students utilizing formal (upper panel) and informal (lower panel) sources of mental health services or support (based on random effect models with 95% confidence interval)

Utilized formal sources of MHSS - Two utilized informal sources of MHSS were identified: (a) pharmaceutical/biological intervention or formal medical resources, and (b) psychological interventions, psychotherapy, or counseling. Using meta-analyzable data from four studies, which included 22,790 students, the overall pooled proportion (estimated using a random effect model) of students utilizing these two formal sources of MHSS was 28% (95% CI: 20%-35%) (Fig. 8, lower panel). Several other utilized formal sources of MHSS were reported. Two studies explored the use of digital mental health solutions. A study examining the level of interest in using electronic mental health among Egyptian university students reported the following as reasons for using the Internet as a source of web-based mental health help [58]: convenience (95.6%), user-friendly (85.4%), privacy (84%), rapid response (79.5), cost-effective (79.1%), decreased stigma (74.8%), anonymity (72.7%), and interactivity (59.4%). A study exploring university students’ attitudes towards and perceptions of digital mental health solutions [59] established that 68.5% of students have used the Internet to find information about physical and mental health problems, 9.2% have consulted a mental health professional using an online video conferencing platform, 12.4% have experience of using mental health applications (apps), 4.5% have made use of mental health chatbots.

Factors associated with utilization of MHSS - in one study [22], factors related to utilization of MHSS were categorized based on sociodemographic, types of mental disorder, suicidal thoughts and behaviors, and other factors. Multivariate analysis results showed that female students were more likely to utilize MHSS than their male counterparts (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI = 1.27–2.42) and students with atypical sexual orientations were more likely to utilize MHSS compared to those with typical sexual orientations (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI = 1.04–2.33). First-generation students (neither of their parents had completed tertiary education) were less likely to utilize MHSS than were second-generation students (either or both parents held a university degree) (aOR = 0.29, 95% CI = 0.13–0.64). We found that more female than males students utilized counseling services, 92% versus 9%. With regard to type of mental disorder, the odds of MHSS utilization were as follows: bipolar spectrum disorder: aOR = 4.07, 95% CI = 1.10–15.1; drug use disorder: aOR = 3.45, 95% CI = 1.53–7.80; generalized anxiety disorder: aOR = 2.34; 95% CI = 1.42–3.86; and major depressive disorder: aOR = 1.88; 95% CI = 1.10–3.21, net of other variables in the model. The odds of mental health utilization increased with severity of suicidal thoughts and behaviors: suicidal ideation without plan or attempt: aOR = 2.00, 95% CI = 1.32–3.03; suicidal plan without attempt: aOR = 3.64, 95% CI = 2.44–5.43; and suicide attempt: aOR = 4.57, 95% CI = 2.05–10.2). Another factor associated with mental health utilization was the suitability of the location or environment in which the service was provided. For example, in one study, participants lauded the environment: “Our study was conducted in a building where a student’s general medical clinic is located, so students are not seen to be attending a mental health clinic. This potentially minimized stigma and increased the likelihood of attending the IPT-E sessions regularly (p.10)” [55].

Discussion

Previous reviews examining university students’ use of MHSS have been limited in scope [19–21]. One systematic review included only one African study out of 44 [19], while another review focused narrowly on sub-Saharan Africa [20]. A more recent review of help-seeking intentions, behaviors, and barriers in college students excluded informal sources and qualitative studies [21]. To our knowledge, our systematic review is the first to examine the availability, accessibility, and utilization of MHSS among university students in Africa. Additionally, it identified the drivers of MHSS accessibility and utilization. Given the heterogeneity of the studies reviewed, more rigorous research studies are required to investigate the availability, accessibility, and utilization of MHSS by university students in Africa, establish causal relationships, and inform the development of evidence-based policies.

More informal than formal sources of MHSS are available to students

Our findings reveal that university students in Africa have greater awareness of informal sources of MHSS (e.g., friends and parents) than formal sources (e.g., mental health professionals and general practitioners). Consistent with our finding, a study exploring sources of help for mental illness showed that participants relied more on informal sources (e.g., social support networks and family) than formal sources to address their mental health issues [60]. Our findings suggest that the availability of both informal and formal sources of MHSS is essential. A study comparing the mechanisms of formal and informal help in the context of mental health recovery also concluded that informal and formal sources of MHSS are complementary [61]. While informal sources of MHSS are essential, given the potential risk of exposure to inaccurate or unhelpful information from these sources, it is also necessary to ensure that students are informed about the available formal sources of MHSS within the university. A recent systematic review emphasized the need to increase students’ awareness of available support services within and outside the university [19]. Given the significant role of family as an informal source of MHSS, policymakers and universities should consider integrating family engagement initiatives into mental health interventions for university students.

Emphasizing awareness of formal sources can help students navigate the potential pitfalls of relying solely on informal networks. Many students fail to access formal sources of MHSS due to a lack of awareness about their availability. Information about available sources of MHSS on campus can be disseminated through written materials such as leaflets and posters, face-to-face contact with peer support groups, and information provided on the institution’s website. Other strategies include engaging in talks about available and accessible sources of MHSS, displaying relevant materials via bulletin boards, and conducting seminars on MHSS. Findings from this study suggest that more efforts are needed by universities to enhance students’ awareness of the MHSS available on campus and how to access them.

More informal than formal sources of MHSS are accessed by students

Similar to MHSS availability, we observe that students tend to access informal sources of MHSS (e.g., intimate partners, lecturers, and religious leaders) more frequently than formal sources (e.g., institutional counseling services, psychiatrists, and psychologists). This finding coincides with prior research examining help-seeking preferences among college students with a history of suicide ideation, which showed that 87% received informal help, with family (65%) and friends (54%) being the leading sources [62]. It is also consistent with another study showing that most participants prefer to seek help from informal sources rather than formal sources of care [63].

While informal sources of MHSS are more accessible, they lack the professional expertise needed for effective management of mental health issues. We recommend a multifaceted approach that encourages access to formal MHSS while also integrating informal support systems. While informal support can be a positive first step, it should not be a substitute for professional care. Research is needed to (1) characterize the nature and quality of support offered by informal sources such as lecturers and religious leaders; (2) identify the risks associated with relying solely on informal support, including potential for misinformation, breaches of confidentiality, and ineffective advice; and (3) explore how informal networks can be leveraged as a positive conduit to professional services rather than a replacement. In addition, given the high demand for and limited capacity of formal MHSS, future research should explore the effectiveness of task-sharing models. This may be in the form of studying the outcomes of training non-specialists (e.g., peer counselors, lecturers, and student affairs staff) in mental health basic intervention skills and conducting randomized controlled trials evaluating the efficacy of evidence-based mental health interventions delivered by trained non-specialists within the university context. University mental health policy should not view informal and formal support as mutually exclusive but as parts of a coordinated continuum of care. This can be achieved by developing clear referral pathways from informal supporters to formal services. This includes training faculty and staff to recognize signs of distress and guide students toward professional help.

Students face instrumental-related barriers in accessing MHSS

Of the three categories of barriers that students face in attempting to access MHSS, the most frequently cited are instrumental-related (48%), followed by attitudinal (47%), and stigma-related barriers (44%). The three most significant barriers to accessing mental health support are strikingly clear: a lack of mental health literacy—meaning many individuals don’t know where to find help—a struggle to afford the financial costs involved, and the absence of a supportive companion to guide them in navigating mental health services. These findings are supported by studies across Africa, which identify a lack of knowledge or information about where to get MHSS [64] and financial barriers [65] as leading barriers for many university students. Given that the proportion of students citing these barriers is very close, ranging from 44% to 48%, this finding may underscore the urgent need for further research to determine mental health interventions that address these barriers among university students in Africa.

More informal than formal sources of MHSS are utilized by students

As with MHSS availability and accessibility, this review revealed that students are more inclined to utilize informal sources of MHSS than formal sources. Notably, the top two informal sources they frequently turn to are religious leaders and traditional healers. Conversely, when it comes to formal MHSS, the most cited options are pharmaceutical interventions and psychological therapies. Our findings are supported by previous research which established that university students often turn to more informal sources such as intimate partners, friends, and religious leaders [24, 54]. Similarly, in a study examining MHSS utilization among college students with a history of suicide ideation, majority (87%) of students indicated that they received informal help, 73% received formal treatment, and 61% received both [62].

Students may turn to informal sources of MHSS because they align with their cultural beliefs and provide a sense of familiarity, trust, and community-based support that formal services may lack. They may perceive religious leaders and traditional healers as more effective for specific issues, especially those with spiritual or non-biomedical explanations. These findings underscore the substantial impact of informal support networks on students’ approaches to managing their mental health. They suggest a need for more integrated services, culturally competent formal care, enhanced education on formal options, and collaboration with informal providers to bridge trust and accessibility gaps. Formal service providers need to collaborate with religious leaders and traditional healers to build trust and facilitate referrals. Given that students may continue using informal MHSS, the therapy should be designed to complement, not conflict with informal sources. Future research should investigate why students are hesitant to utilize formal sources and suggest how best to address these issues.

Policy implications

Given that students tend to access and utilize informal MHSS more than formal sources, universities should enhance the accessibility and appeal of formal MHSS. They need to address the structural and perceptual barriers that deter students from utilizing formal MHSS. Examples of policy changes could include increasing funding for counseling centers to reduce waiting times and ensure services are available when needed; improving mental health literacy and anti-stigma campaigns to increase student awareness of and access to MHSS; and offering a variety of MHSS including telehealth, group therapy, and online resources that accommodate diverse student needs and preferences. Given that students rely more on informal networks, universities should formalize training and provide clear guidance to these individuals to maximize the benefits and minimize the risks. This may involve offering mandatory mental health first aid training to faculty and academic advisors, equipping them with the skills to listen, assess, and guide students encountering mental health problems. They can develop resources and clear protocols for staff and faculty who receive mental health disclosures, outlining the appropriate boundaries and referral processes. They can provide support and supervision for non-specialist staff involved in MHSS to ensure they operate within their scope. Finally, universities need to prioritize mental well-being across the institution by employing a comprehensive approach that ensures mental well-being is embedded into the fabric of university life. This can be achieved by integrating mental health education into the curriculum and new student orientation programs; implementing flexible academic policies that accommodate mental health needs without penalty; and fostering a culture of wellness by promoting a sense of belonging and community, which is a known protective factor for student mental health.

Study strengths and limitations

A key methodological strength of this study is that it is the first mixed methods systematic review (including meta-analysis and meta-synthesis) of the availability, accessibility, and utilization of MHSS among university students in Africa. However, some limitations are noteworthy. First, although we included 18 studies that focus on mental health problems, there was substantial heterogeneity among the studies. We attempted to address this by using a random effects model [66]. Two, whereas limited time and staff prevented us from searching grey literature and unpublished studies, and this might have led to missing necessary evidence, our expanded search of five databases and the use of the snowballing technique likely minimized the risk of missing relevant studies. Three, whereas the quality of included studies was assessed using relevant tools, there was potential subjectivity in the methodological appraisal process. To address this, we had two reviewers independently assess the quality of the included studies and resolve any disagreements through discussion. Finally, although planned, it was not feasible to conduct a subgroup analysis due to the limited number of applicable studies and small sample sizes.

Conclusion

There is evidence that more informal than formal sources of MHSS are available, accessible, and utilized by university students in Africa. Given that students encounter numerous barriers in accessing MHSS, especially instrumental-related barriers, more studies could explore the use of digital mental health interventions that adopt self-help approaches and are likely to be accessed privately.
