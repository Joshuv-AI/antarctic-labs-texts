Reconciling nitrogen fertilization effects on soil nitrification with acidification
Authors: Liangping Wu, Yuanyuan Huang, Yuanyuan Huang, Jing Wang, Lu Lu, Yves Uwiragiye, Meiqi Chen, Quan Tang, Nyumah Fallah, Zihan Liu, Hang Jing, Ahmed S Elrys, Yinfei Qian, Yi Cheng, Zucong Cai, Scott X Chang, Minggang Xu, Christoph Müller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen (N) fertilization supports nearly half of the global population but disrupts the N cycle. While N addition is assumed to stimulate soil nitrification by increasing substrate supply and nitrifier activity, it may also inhibit nitrification via soil acidification. Resolving this paradox is key to understanding the nitrification response to N addition (βnit). Using a global dataset, we show that βnit is best explained by final soil pH and ecosystem type. The βnit-pH relationship is positive in croplands, weak in forests, and negative in grasslands. Notably, βnit shifts from negative to positive above a pH threshold of 5.7 in croplands. Structural equation modeling reveals that final pH affects βnit in croplands by altering ammonia-oxidizing bacteria abundance and ammonium concentration in response to N addition. Our findings underscore the critical role of pH in regulating βnit and improving predictions of ecosystem N dynamics and plant productivity under future N inputs.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Element cycles

Introduction

Nitrogen (N) is a critical nutrient element governing the structure and functioning of terrestrial ecosystems1. This nutrient input can relieve ecosystem N limitation and thus increase productivity2. However, N inputs exceeding ecosystem demand can promote nitrate (NO3−) leaching, deplete base cations, disrupt soil proton (H+) budgets, and ultimately accelerate soil acidification3,4. These negative effects are closely linked to soil nitrification, a microbial process in which ammonium (NH4+) is oxidized to NO3− with the simultaneous release of H+5,6. As a central step in the global N cycle, soil nitrification influences N availability for plants and microbes, and it also results in the production of the potent greenhouse gas nitrous oxide (N2O)5,7. These products (NO3−) and byproducts (N2O and H+) can trigger a cascade of feedback loops that regulate climate dynamics and environmental sustainability5,6. In particular, enhanced nitrification accelerates fertilizer N losses and soil acidification in croplands, thereby reducing fertilizer use efficiency and threatening food security8. Globally, about half of the applied N fertilizer is lost to the environment primarily by nitrification products and byproducts9. N-induced soil acidification associated with nitrification has also emerged as a widespread global concern10,11. Therefore, a better understanding of the response of soil nitrification to N addition (βnit) is critical for predicting future changes in soil N retention, global N cycling, and plant productivity.

Existing meta-analyses have demonstrated a globally positive βnit primarily driven by increased NH4+ availability and enhanced activity of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB)12–14. However, N addition also frequently accelerates soil acidification4,10,11, which may partly offset or even outweigh the overall stimulatory effect of N addition on soil nitrification (Supplementary Fig. 1), particularly in acidic soils15,16. This is because decreasing soil pH reduces the availability of ammonia (NH3) for ammonia-oxidizing microorganisms17,18. Furthermore, soil pH has been identified as a key regulator of the effects of N addition on N2O emissions19,20, which can be produced through nitrification under aerobic conditions21. Many studies have reported concurrent declines in soil nitrification and pH after N addition22,23, suggesting that N-induced soil acidification may regulate βnit. In addition, whether βnit is positive or negative may depend on the ecosystem type. For instance, N addition has been shown to stimulate nitrification in grassland soils but inhibit it in cropland soils despite both being associated with soil acidification22–24. These results suggest that other factors may interact with soil acidification, affecting βnit. Furthermore, N-induced soil acidification varies with ecosystem type, initial soil pH, climate, N application rate, N form, and experimental duration4,10,25. In particular, initial soil pH determines the dominant buffering mechanisms, such that similar N inputs may result in contrasting soil pH responses4. Therefore, the observed decline in soil pH does not necessarily reflect the intensity of N-induced soil acidification26. Consequently, meta-analyses that considered only the stimulatory effects of N addition on nitrification, without accounting for the counteracting influence of soil acidification, may have overestimated βnit12,13,27. However, the extent to which this inhibitory influence contributes to global variation in βnit, and whether this regulation depends on environmental conditions, remain unclear.

Here, we tested the hypothesis that N-induced soil acidification would regulate βnit at a global scale. To do this, we assembled a global dataset comprising 682 observations reporting βnit and 371 observations on N-induced changes in soil pH (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2a). We therefore conducted a meta-analysis to investigate the role of N-induced acidification and environmental factors in regulating βnit. We further hypothesized that under N addition, there exists a threshold of soil pH: below this threshold, nitrification is inhibited, while above it, nitrification is stimulated. Our main finding is that βnit is interactively affected by final soil pH (defined as the soil pH after N addition) and ecosystem type. This research provides insights into reconciling the variation in βnit with soil acidification across terrestrial ecosystems.

Results

Across all studies, N addition enhanced soil nitrification rates by 60% (95% CI: 50–70%), NH4+ concentrations by 23% (95% CI: 15–31%), and AOB abundances by 153% (95% CI: 99–223%), but did not change ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA) abundances (Fig. 1b). In contrast, N addition lowered soil pH by 46% (95% CI: 41–51%; Fig. 1b). Moreover, βnit exhibited a positive relationship with final soil pH and a hump-shaped relationship with pH unit change between N fertilizer treatment and control (βH+) (Supplementary Figs. 2b and 3a and Supplementary Table 2).

Model selection analyses showed that βnit was best predicted by final soil pH and ecosystem type (Fig. 2). We further found a strong interaction between final soil pH and ecosystem type on βnit (p < 0.0001). Ecosystem-specific patterns of βnit along the final soil pH gradient were observed, with contrasting trends in cropland, forest, and grassland ecosystems (Figs. 3 and 4). In cropland ecosystems, βnit was significantly and positively related to final soil pH and shifted from negative to positive above a final threshold pH of 5.7 (95% CI: 5.3–6.0) (Fig. 4a). In forest ecosystems, βnit was not related to final soil pH (Fig. 4b). In grassland ecosystems, βnit was significantly and negatively related to final soil pH and characterized by a final soil pH threshold of 8.1 (95% CI: 7.6–9.7), above which βnit was negative (Fig. 4c). Therefore, the relationship between βnit and final soil pH was positive in croplands, weak in forests, and negative in grasslands (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Consistent with our first hypothesis, we provide global-scale evidence that variation in βnit is associated with soil pH after N addition. The two known phenomena of N-induced soil acidification4,10,11 and N-induced stimulation of soil nitrification12,13,27 have been well-documented worldwide. Although acidification frequently inhibits nitrification in acidic soils (pH < 5.5)14–16,28, the relationship between N-induced soil acidification and changes in soil nitrification has not been comprehensively evaluated, limiting our understanding of global βnit. Here, we show that βnit is best explained by an interaction between final soil pH and ecosystem type. One possible explanation is that soil buffering mechanisms decouple N-induced acidification from changes in soil pH26 (see Supplementary Discussion 1). And the relationship between βnit and final soil pH was ecosystem-specific. In croplands, βnit shifted from negative to positive when final soil pH exceeded a threshold of 5.7, supporting our second hypothesis. In contrast, grasslands showed an opposite pattern: βnit declined from positive to negative above a threshold of 8.1. Our findings highlight the critical role of final soil pH as an integrated predictor of βnit.

βnit responds differently to final soil pH across ecosystems

To understand why βnit responds differently to final soil pH across ecosystems, we examined both biotic and abiotic mechanisms. In general, both the availability of substrate NH4+ and the activities of AOA and AOB play critical roles in regulating soil nitrification5,13. Similarly, SEM encompassing all ecosystems also confirmed that final soil pH influenced βnit by mediating changes in βAOB. The effects of N addition on AOB rather than AOA could be due to the fact that AOB have lower NH3 affinity, and thus, a high NH3 environment following N addition favors the survival of AOB29,30, resulting in AOB responding more strongly to N addition than AOA (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). In addition, the divergent relationships between final soil pH and βnit across ecosystems may also be ascribed to differences in N application rates, durations, and forms, climate variables (e.g., MAP), initial soil pH, or SOC concentrations (details in Supplementary Figs. 7–12 and Supplementary Table 6). These variations likely reflect the differing structures and functions of ecosystems in response to N addition, shaped by factors such as plant species, soil properties, and management practices (Supplementary Figs. 13–15)31,32. It has also been shown that different ecosystems respond differently to N addition in terms of soil acidification and the N cycle because of differences in N input intensity and ecosystem sensitivity11,33. These responses suggest that distinct mechanisms are likely involved in regulating βnit by final soil pH across ecosystem types. This pattern was further reinforced by SEM, showing that final soil pH positively influenced βnit through changes in βAOB and βNH4+ in croplands, but negatively affected βnit by altering βNH4+ in grasslands.

We, therefore, propose a framework that integrates final soil pH, substrate (NH4+ concentration) availability, and microbial driver (AOB abundance) as proximal controls explaining the varying relationships between βnit and final soil pH across ecosystems. These controls may be further influenced by distal drivers, such as N inputs and initial soil properties. One of the biggest differences between grasslands and forests is that croplands typically receive substantially higher amounts of N (Supplementary Fig. 13), primarily as chemical fertilizer, to boost crop yields3,34. Consistently, the highest βnit values were observed when N application rates reached or exceeded 250 kg N ha−1 (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). The varying mechanisms of final soil pH, NH4+ availability, and AOB abundance in various ecosystems could, therefore, be attributed to different N inputs. At higher N input levels, N supply may exceed biological demand, resulting in transient NH4+ accumulation and greater substrate availability for nitrifiers when final soil pH remains conducive to nitrification. In addition, higher N inputs in croplands likely led to greater increases in βAOB compared to grasslands and forests35,36, reflecting the competitive advantage of AOB under high NH4+ availability. This is indirectly supported by a recent meta-analysis showing that the relative contribution of AOB to nitrification was highest in cropland, followed by grassland and forest37. Alternatively, variations in nitrification measurement methods may have introduced biases. Nitrification was predominantly calculated using potential nitrification rate (PNR) in croplands, while net nitrification rate (NNR) was more common in forests and grasslands (Supplementary Fig. 18). The relative contribution of AOB to nitrification has been reported to be higher when measured using PNR compared with NNR37. This difference may arise from the slurry assay conditions used during PNR calculation, where adequate aeration and uniform substrate distribution amplify the differences in βAOB. This could also explain why βAOB is more important in croplands, whereas βNH4+ plays a larger role in grasslands.

In addition, soil properties may provide an explanation for the contrasting βnit-pH relationships across ecosystems. NH4+ concentrations in the control treatments were much higher in forests (47.2 mg kg−1) than in croplands (11.8 mg kg−1) and grasslands (7.8 mg kg−1) (Supplementary Data 1). This difference suggests that βnit in croplands and grasslands may be more sensitive to variation in substrate addition than in forests. N addition significantly decreased the soil C/N ratio27,38 from 15.3 to 15.0, with a smaller decrease in forests than in croplands and grasslands, suggesting a smaller increase in N availability for nitrifiers in forests. A higher βnit was observed in conifer-dominated forests, which could be attributed to conifers being sensitive to N-induced lower soil C/N ratio compared to deciduous-dominated forests (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). Moreover, despite decreasing soil pH across ecosystems, soil acidification was generally less severe in forests than in croplands and grasslands under N addition (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This is primarily due to lower N inputs and initial pH (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14), as well as greater acid-weathering processes in forests39. In particular, boreal and conifer-dominated forests showed no significant changes in soil pH under N addition11. Therefore, the relatively minor change in soil pH in forests was likely insufficient to elicit a significant response of βnit to final soil pH.

Finally, initial soil pH and SOC concentration were associated with variation in final soil pH across all ecosystems, particularly in croplands and grasslands (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 21). Croplands and grasslands differed markedly in their initial pH ranges, with croplands including more acidic soils and grasslands occurring predominantly above pH 6.5 (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 14). These contrasting pH ranges may partly explain the contrasting relationship between final soil pH and βnit between the two ecosystems (see Supplementary Discussion 2). Taken together, these results highlight the complex interactions between soil properties and final pH changes in regulating βnit, as they differentially affect acidification, substrate, and microbial drivers across ecosystems. However, it should be noted that SEM and meta-regression are statistical tools and do not establish cause-effect relationships40,41. Therefore, the proposed pathway through which final soil pH changes may regulate βnit across ecosystems should be interpreted with caution. Complex interactions and feedback loops often limit causal inference in ecological studies42. Future manipulative experiments are needed to validate the mechanisms suggested here.

βnit switches between positive and negative in cropland and grassland ecosystems

One of the central findings of this study is that βnit varies between positive and negative across cropland and grassland ecosystems. What drives this switch? Our results suggest it reflects a trade-off between two opposing processes: stimulation of nitrification due to increased substrate NH4+ availability as well as enhanced AOB abundance13, and inhibition of nitrification due to soil acidification from N inputs4,10,11. These opposing processes are embedded within the final soil pH gradient and are also modulated by distal drivers, which influence the relative dominance of each process across ecosystems. In croplands, we detected a shift in βnit from negative to positive when final soil pH exceeded 5.7, suggesting that nitrification was stimulated under moderate acidification but inhibited once pH fell below a critical tipping point. In contrast, in grasslands, βnit shifted from positive to negative when final soil pH exceeded 8.1. Croplands with comparable final soil pH generally maintained positive βnit, suggesting that the negative βnit observed in high-pH grasslands was ecosystem-specific rather than a general feature of alkaline soils. Geographically, negative βnit was observed in regions with a large area in South and East Asia, northern Sub-Saharan Africa, the central United States, and eastern Brazil. These regions have also been identified as global hotspots of soil acidification under current and future atmospheric N deposition4,34,43. Previous studies have shown that long-term N fertilization can substantially acidify soils, with soil pH declining from approximately 6.0 to 4.0, leading to strong inhibition of both PNR and gross nitrification rate (GNR) in acidic upland soils22,44. Zhu et al.45 found that GNR in vegetable soils was significantly stimulated when pH decreased moderately from 6.2 to above 5.6, primarily due to N inputs. However, further acidification to pH 4.3 resulted in significant suppression of GNR. This phenomenon suggests that the negative effects of soil acidification on nitrification intensified progressively with decreasing pH, eventually overriding any stimulatory effects from increased substrate availability. Similarly, Duan et al.46 reported that soil pH decreased gradually with increasing N application rates, while NNR initially increased and then decreased in vegetable fields.

In contrast to croplands, grasslands showed positive βnit values when the final soil pH was below 8.1. This discrepancy can be ascribed to the differences in N addition rates and initial soil properties between the two ecosystem types. Croplands typically receive substantially higher N inputs, resulting in more pronounced soil acidification4,34. Lower final soil pH (<4.5) was observed in cropland with initial soil pH <5.5 (Supplementary Fig. 14). Under such acidic conditions, most added N remains in the form of NH4+, which is less accessible to AOB that preferentially utilize NH347. In addition, soil acidification can induce aluminum toxicity, which further inhibits nitrification by suppressing nitrifier activity48,49. Increased osmotic pressure and salt stress due to excessive N fertilization may further inhibit soil microbial activity50,51. This was indirectly supported by the results showing that nearly all βnit values were positive when N application rates were <250 kg N ha−1 in croplands (Supplementary Fig. 16d). These findings suggest that moderate N inputs can mitigate soil acidification and associated microbial stress, thereby sustaining soil health and crop productivity. In addition, soil organic matter plays a crucial role in buffering pH changes, primarily due to the presence of functional groups such as hydroxyl, phenolic, and carboxylic acids52,53. The generally higher SOC content in grasslands compared to croplands likely contributed to a greater soil pH buffering capacity, resulting in a smaller pH shift following N addition (Fig. 4). Indeed, the minimum final soil pH observed in croplands was generally as low as 3.71, whereas it was up to 4.90 in grasslands. This suggests that the inhibitory effect of N-induced soil acidification on nitrification may have been offset by other positive effects of N addition in most grasslands. Nevertheless, acidification can still become an important constraint in intensively managed grasslands when final soil pH falls below 5.4 (e.g., pasturelands; Supplementary Fig. 23).

It is surprising that βnit was negative when the final soil pH exceeded 8.1 in grasslands. This counterintuitive pattern may reflect complex interactions among N input rates and environmental constraints. Notably, annual N input rates of grasslands were significantly higher at the final soil pH range of 4.5–6.5 (on average 202 kg N ha−1) than at the final soil pH range of 6.5–7.5 (88 kg N ha−1) and >7.5 (80 kg N ha−1) (Supplementary Fig. 13). This suggests that when final soil pH falls below the threshold, higher N inputs alleviate N limitation and increase substrate NH4+ availability for nitrifiers, which is common in typically N-limited grasslands. This interpretation is also consistent with the contrasting patterns observed between pasturelands and rangelands (Supplementary Fig. 23), which likely reflect differences in management intensity and associated N inputs. Conversely, when final soil pH exceeds 8.1, lower N inputs may fail to relieve N limitation, while alkaline conditions may also enhance NH3 volatilization losses, jointly reducing NH4+ availability for nitrification. In addition, urea, a primary N form, undergoes hydrolysis. This reaction transiently increases soil pH by consuming H+, but also raises the risk of NH3 volatilization54, especially under alkaline conditions, which may reduce NH4+ availability for nitrifiers. Water availability may further constrain nitrification under these conditions. Zhang et al.55 suggested that the inhibitory effects of N addition or irrigation on nitrification could be alleviated by their interaction in alkaline semiarid steppe soils. In our dataset, these inhibitory effects were predominantly observed in regions with MAP below 500 mm, where N-induced soil acidification was also minimal (Supplementary Data 1), thereby supporting the role of co-limitation by water and N. Although these mechanisms offer plausible explanations, direct evidence explaining the negative βnit in grasslands when the final soil pH is above 8.1 is still lacking. Further research is needed to identify the underlying mechanisms driving this response.

Study implications

This study provides a global synthesis investigating the response of βnit to final soil pH. Our proposed framework integrates soil acidification, substrate availability, and microbial factors to explain the varying responses of βnit to final soil pH across ecosystems. Our findings, which show that N addition inhibits nitrification in certain ecosystems with critical final soil pH, reveal that stimulatory βnit are not universal across ecosystems. In particular, when final soil pH drops below 5.7 in croplands, the inhibitory effects of N-induced soil acidification outweigh the stimulatory effects of increased substrate availability and microbial factors, leading to negative βnit. Incorporating these acidification effects into soil N dynamic models could improve predictions of nutrient cycling and plant productivity under future N input scenarios. Such refinements are especially relevant in croplands where N management decisions directly impact crop yield and environmental outcomes.

The findings have clear implications for land management. In croplands, both excessively low and high βnit values were associated with unfavorable outcomes, specifically suboptimal crop yields or an increased risk of NO3−-N loss (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25)56. The stronger relationship between final soil pH and βnit at higher N inputs suggests that managing N application rates to prevent soil pH from falling below 5.7 may help maintain favorable conditions for nitrification and thereby sustain crop productivity. Alkaline amendments (e.g., liming) may alleviate acidification-related suppression of nitrification, while nitrification inhibitors, such as 3,4-dimethylpyrazole phosphate, may be appropriate where βnit is excessively high57. In some cases, combined strategies may be needed to optimize N retention and minimize N losses58. In contrast, forests appear more prone to N loss as βnit increases, suggesting that reducing atmospheric N deposition remains important for minimizing ecosystem N leakage. Grasslands may avoid such trade-offs due to synchronization between plant uptake and N availability (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). Nevertheless, N-induced acidification remains a concern in intensively managed pasturelands that receive substantial N inputs, similar to croplands.

Together, these insights support more nuanced, ecosystem-specific N management strategies to safeguard soil health, optimize fertilizer efficiency, and advance sustainable food production under increasing N pressures.

Study limitations and uncertainties

The present study has several limitations and uncertainties. First, although we conducted a comprehensive literature search without geographic restrictions, studies on βnit were disproportionately concentrated in regions with high N addition rates (e.g., South and East Asia). Although publication bias analyses suggested that the overall results were robust, potential bias was detected in several subgroup analyses (Supplementary Figs. 26–28 and Supplementary Table 7). This may partly reflect the uneven sample distribution among ecosystem types, final soil pH levels, regions, and N application rates. In addition, limited datasets from Africa, Europe, Australia, and South America increased uncertainties in the global spatial patterns of βnit (Fig. 6b). Future βnit studies in these areas are therefore needed to improve global representativeness.

Second, there were three commonly used soil nitrification rates (GNR, PNR, and NNR), which differ in methodology and ecological implications59. For example, GNR, typically measured using 15N dilution or tracing methods, reflects the actual rate of NO3− production in situ, whereas PNR represents the maximum nitrification rate under ideal conditions. In contrast, NNR is the balance between GNR and gross NO3− consumption. Although nitrification type was identified to be an unimportant predictor in the mixed-effects model (Fig. 2), the responses of βnit to final soil pH varied across the three methods (Supplementary Fig. 29). Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future research with more comprehensive data is warranted.

Third, differences in the frequency and timing of N addition among ecosystem types may influence βnit response to final soil pH. For instance, nitrification responses increased initially but leveled off following N addition in grassland soil, whereas they remained consistently stimulated under frequent N inputs in forest soil60,61. These findings suggest that split applications of N fertilizer, adjusted based on real-time plant nutrient status, could be an effective strategy to enhance soil N availability and promote crop growth62.

Methods

Literature search

We used Web of Science (Core Collection; http://www.webofknowledge.com) and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI; https://www.cnki.net) for an exhaustive search of articles published before January 2023. We also searched within these publications for references. Studies included in previous meta-analyses examining βnit were also included in our synthesis12,13,27. We used the following search terms: “nitrification” AND “soil” AND “N deposition” OR “N addition” OR “N amendment” OR “N enrichment” OR “N fertilize” OR “N elevate.” A flowchart of the process and outcome of the literature search is provided in Supplementary Fig. 3063.

Criteria for the inclusion or exclusion of studies in the meta-analysis

To be included in our dataset, articles had to meet several requirements: (1) N fertilizer was directly added to the ecosystem in the field (better-representing βnit under natural conditions; greenhouse and mesocosm studies were not included in our analysis); (2) studies contained at least one pair of data (N addition and control treatments); (3) the N addition experimental design included at least three replicates; (4) the N addition and control treatments were established under the same conditions to avoid confounding factors. The N & phosphorus (P) or N & P & potassium treatments were also included in our analysis due to the limited data in croplands. In addition, experiments conducted at different locations within a single publication, each with independent controls, were treated as separate studies. If identical data were reported in multiple publications, we recorded them only once. We included each level independently if a publication reported nitrification rates for different “experimental durations” and “N addition rates.” In total, 150 publications with 682 observations met these criteria (Supplementary Data 1).

Data extraction

We extracted and stored the following data: the means, the sample size, and, depending on the study, the variance, SD, or standard error (SE). Where available, we also tabulated response variables including soil pH, AOA amoA, AOB amoA, NH4+, NO3−, microbial biomass C (MBC), microbial biomass N (MBN), MBC/MBN ratio, TN, SOC, C/N ratio, total P concentration (TP), crop yield, and aboveground biomass (Supplementary Data 1). Some observations of soil pH were measured using solutions of KCl or CaCl2, and their values were converted to soil pH measured in water based on established equations64,65. When results were presented graphically, we used GetData Graph Digitizer 2.26 (http://getdata-graph-digitizer.com/) to extract the data. If only the SE was reported, SD was calculated as SD = SE × n. In addition to these response variables, our database also included site characteristics such as location (longitude, latitude), climatic variables (climate type, MAP, and mean annual temperature (MAT)), and initial soil properties (pH, SOC, TN, C/N ratio, and TP). We also gathered data on experimental conditions, including N addition rate, duration, and form (urea, NH4NO3, NH4+, and NO3− based fertilizers, as well as other forms such as controlled-release N, mixed N, and unspecified N). Ecosystem types included cropland, forest (conifer, mixed, and other species), and grassland (including both rangeland and pastureland, with grazing excluded during the experiments). Additionally, we recorded the methods used to determine soil nitrification rate, including 15N isotope dilution analysis (GNR), NO3− concentration changes (NNR), the chlorate inhibition method (PNR), and the shaken slurry method (PNR). These methods were also collected (see Supplementary Data 1). We also considered the year of publication to investigate whether there were discrepancies between early and subsequent estimates of the relationship between βnit and the development of the discipline (Supplementary Fig. 31). All these factors were selected to help explain the variation of βnit (Supplementary Tables 1–5).

In such cases, we applied the “Bracken1992” approach to impute SD using the coefficient of variation derived from all complete cases67.

We calculated the βi and its variance using the “rma” function in the R package “metafor”68. There was a significant response for the variable when the CI of βi did not overlap with zero. Some results were reported as percentage changes with N addition [i.e., 100 × (eβi − 1)] to facilitate interpretation.

Using the random-effects meta-analysis, we assessed the overall effect of N addition on soil nitrification rates (Supplementary Fig. 31). We assessed heterogeneity using Cochran’s formal Q-test (QE), which tests whether the variability in the observed effect sizes or outcomes is larger than would be expected based on sampling variability alone. As expected for a biological meta-analysis69, significant residual heterogeneity was observed in the random-effects meta-analysis for the total dataset (QE = 77604, pE < 0.001), which we attempted to explain using different moderators (Supplementary Table 2).

We ran single mixed-effects meta-regression models to examine the associations between βnit and individual categorical and continuous moderators (Supplementary Fig. 33 and Supplementary Tables 2–5). Continuous variables were fitted as linear terms. Additionally, we ran models with categorical factors without an intercept to obtain the parameter estimates (mean effect sizes) for each level. The heterogeneity explained by the moderators of each meta-regression was assessed using omnibus tests (QM) (Supplementary Tables 2–5). Publication bias was assessed using “contour-enhanced” Funnel plots and Egger’s tests, which included precision (1/SE) as a covariate in the rma function, and meta-analytic residuals70. Rosenberg’s fail-safe numbers were calculated to assess the robustness of our results against publication bias71.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to address potential collinearity among variables that showed significant correlations with βnit. Variables with VIF values exceeding 5 were excluded from the analysis72. Among correlated predictors, final soil pH explained more variation in βnit than initial soil pH (R2 = 0.13 versus R2 = 0.08; Supplementary Figs. 2b and 33e) and was therefore retained in the subsequent models. We then performed multiple mixed-effects meta-regression models to identify the predictors that best explained variation in βnit. All possible combinations of the remaining predictors were evaluated using the R packages “glmulti”73 and “metafor”68. To avoid overfitting, interaction terms were not included. Instead, variation in βnit and in its relationship with final soil pH was further examined across ecosystems under different nitrification types and environmental and management conditions (Supplementary Figs. 7–12 and 29). Model selection was based on the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). The relative importance value for a particular predictor is equal to the sum of the Akaike weights (the probability that a model is the most plausible model) for the models in which the predictor appears. Hence, a predictor included in models with large Akaike weights will receive a high importance value. These values provide overall support for each variable across all models. A cut-off of 0.8 was set to differentiate between important and unimportant predictors. All parameter estimates are reported for the best models run using Maximum Likelihood.

SEM was used to evaluate hypothesized pathways linking final soil pH to βnit through changes in βNH4+ and βAOB globally and across terrestrial ecosystems. Variables included in the SEM were selected based on hypothesized mechanisms and the results of meta-regression analyses (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 1–5). Due to the large proportion of missing data, certain variables were excluded from our SEM. For the global SEM, we omitted the initial soil Olsen-P (AP). In the cropland SEM, AP, βMBC, and βMBN were excluded, and in the grassland SEM, βAOB was excluded. The least-squares method was used to fit the data to the model using the AMOS 24.0 software (Amos Development Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA). In general, a qualified SEM is indicated by a low ratio of chi-square (χ2) to degree of freedom (df) (χ2/df < 3)74, a non-significant χ2 test (p > 0.05)75, and a low root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08)76. All SEMs met these criteria for acceptable fit.

We predicted the spatial variation of βnit following a digital soil mapping approach. This approach is referred to as “SCORPAN-SSP Fe” model (where SCORPAN stands for Soil Information, Climate, Organism, Relief, Parent Material, and Time, and SSP Fe refers to Soil Spatial Prediction Function with spatially autocorrelated errors)77. Random forest was chosen for its strong predictive performance and ability to capture complex relationships among variables, and it has been widely applied for both variable importance analysis and mapping soil resources78,79. The developed models were built in five steps: (1) βnit and 51 environmental covariates related to soil-forming factors were compiled (Supplementary Table 7). The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (75%) and an independent testing set (25%). (2) A regression matrix for βnit and the covariates was constructed. (3) Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was applied to identify the subset of predictors that minimized root mean squared error (RMSE). Starting with all 51 covariates, RFE selected 15 covariates, as including additional covariates did not further improve predictive accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 34). (4) Random Forest hyperparameters were tuned using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. The optimal settings were num.trees = 500, mtry = 1, min. node.size = 5, and splitrule = variance. Cross-validation model performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination, RMSE, mean absolute error, and Concordance Correlation Coefficient (Supplementary Fig. 35). (5) The final model was trained on the full training set using the optimal hyperparameters and then applied to predict the spatial distribution of βnit across global terrestrial ecosystems, producing the corresponding SD map (Fig. 6).
