Awareness regarding common eye diseases among university students at a public university in Riyadh City, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Khalid B. Abdulrahman, Layan Y. Alharbi, Abeer M. Alanazi, Haifa H. Allahem, Sadeem K. Alquraini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Numerous ocular disorders, such as glaucoma, cataracts, and diabetic retinopathy, are among the leading causes of blindness worldwide. There needs to be more healthcare understanding about these conditions and their consequences to ensure the potential for early management and prevention by delaying medical treatment seeking. As a result, boosting public awareness of eye diseases is critical for early detection and management, helping to lessen the burden of vision disability upon the community. Misunderstandings regarding distinct blinding eye disorders have been examined in Arab nations, particularly Saudi Arabia; individuals should be informed of them and encouraged to seek available eye care facilities and increase awareness of the condition. The usage of healthcare services in the country considerably helps eliminate blindness induced by ocular disorders. Therefore, this study sought to assess public awareness of common ocular diseases such as glaucoma cataracts, diabetic retinopathy, and dry eye among university students at a public university in Riyadh City, Saudi Arabia.Method:Cross-sectional research was performed from 20-12-2022 to 19-2-2023. The survey was distributed to participants from a public university in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. This research used an electronic self-administered survey distributed randomly to evaluate people’s knowledge of major ocular disorders. Seven hundred twenty-six respondents completed the study survey. All participants should meet the following criteria: adults 18 years or older. Individuals who did not consent to participate in this study would be excluded.Result:A total of 585 women and 141 men were surveyed. Knowledge and awareness of ocular illnesses were below acceptable; according to the responses, one-third of them did not know if there are signs and symptoms associated with glaucoma patients, and 56% were unsure of whether having huge eyeballs is normal or not; in addition, medical students showed a higher level of knowledge comparing with other students, similarly, 82.6%, as opposed to 59.3%, were aware that uncontrolled diabetes could result in vision loss due to disorder in the retina. Compared with two-thirds (67.7%) of students from other schools, medical school students correctly identified that various ocular diseases can develop with aging 80.5% (P = 0.001). Education and nationality were not significantly associated with knowledge (P = 0.43 and 0.337, respectively). On the other hand, women had a better understanding than men (12.1 vs. 9.98, P < 0.001). Respondents with eye or systemic disease that could impair eye health had a considerably higher average knowledge score (13.03 3.72 vs. 11.71 3.61, P = 0.04).Conclusion: Universities should give healthcare education and promote knowledge regarding ocular diseases among students to increase knowledge and comprehension of frequent ocular illnesses. In addition, getting counsel from an ophthalmologist is the most crucial step for reducing vision problems since it may aid in early detection, immediate management, compliance, and prevention.

Introduction

Numerous eye conditions, including glaucoma, cataracts, and diabetic retinopathy, are among the world’s significant causes of blindness. Health knowledge of these illnesses and their sequelae reduces opportunities for early intervention and prevention by delaying seeking medical care. Therefore, increasing public understanding of ocular disorders is crucial for their early diagnosis and treatment; thus, it helps to reduce the burden of visual impairment on a society’s population.[123]

The worldwide awareness of prevalent eye illnesses has been reported to vary.[4567] The level of awareness for ocular diseases was estimated at 69% for cataracts and 41% for glaucoma in affluent nations such as Canada.[6] The urban Indian people had limited knowledge of eye illnesses. For instance, a report from southern India found that 90% of glaucoma patients were unaware of the disease’s complications.[89] Deficient awareness of glaucoma (3.2%) and diabetic retinopathy (27%) was also noted in a study conducted in India.[1]

Studies have identified numerous factors potentially affecting how much people know and understand ocular illnesses. Age, sex, educational attainment, and socioeconomic status are a few of these. For instance, it was recommended that elderly individuals and women have more understanding of glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy.[5]

In Arab countries, especially Saudi Arabia, misconceptions related to specific blinding eye diseases have been studied[1011]; people should be aware of them and encouraged to use accessible eye care services and improve their understanding of the disease. The country’s use of health services significantly reduces blindness caused by eye diseases.[12]

It has been observed that both developed and developing nations are aware of eye illnesses. Studies evaluating the awareness of eye conditions and presbyopia in the Middle East region are scarce. In addition, in Saudi Arabia, public knowledge low about glaucoma (14.8%), pediatric eye problems (51.5%), and refractive errors (63%) compared with their knowledge about eye problems in diabetes, ocular trauma, and general eye diseases (88.6, 81.7, and 91.3%, respectively).[13] Therefore, this study sought to assess public awareness of common ocular diseases such as glaucoma cataracts, diabetic retinopathy, and dry eye among university students at a public university in Riyadh City, Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted from 20-12-2022 to 19-02-2023. This study was conducted using an electronic self-administered questionnaire that was distributed randomly to assess awareness regarding common eye diseases. Participants from a public university in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, were invited to complete the questionnaire.

Study questionnaire

The study questionnaire was adapted from multiple previous studies and was modified after, and a pilot study will be conducted to check the validity and reliability of this questionnaire. The self-administered questionnaire contains two sections: (1) personal information and demographic data and (2) knowledge regarding eye health information. The first section included 16 questions related to the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents. The eye health information section contained 26 questions. The first twenty-five questions had three possible answers: yes/no/I do not know. These questions assessed standard methods to protect the eyes and questions related to their awareness regarding common eye diseases. In addition, one question was related to the source of knowledge.

Knowledge score calculation

Each of the 25 knowledge-related questions was scored, and respondents were awarded one point for each correct answer. The knowledge score was calculated by summing the number of correct answers with a maximum possible score of 25.

Sampling technique and sample size calculation

Convenience sampling was used in this study. The total number of all Imam Mohammad Ibn Saud Islamic University undergraduate students is 73938. The sample size calculation with a 99% confidence interval and 0.05 precision rate is = 727. All participants must meet the following criteria: adults ≥18 years old. Those who did not agree to participate in this study will be excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R v 3.6.3. Counts and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to summarize continuous variables. The Chi-square test of independence was used to assess the association between categorical variables. Linear regression was used to determine factors associated with knowledge scores. No missing data were observed, as respondents were not allowed to submit the response unless all responses were completed. Hypothesis testing was performed at a 5% level of significance.

Results

Seven hundred twenty-six respondents completed the study questionnaire. Of these, 19.4% (n = 141) were men, and 80.6% (n = 585) were women. Regarding age, one-third (37.7%) of the respondents were aged 18–20, and one-half (54.3%) were aged 20–25. Most included respondents were Saudis (97%, n = 704) and single (94.6%, n = 687). Regarding education, approximately one-third of the respondents were in their junior year (49.3, n = 358), and 42.4% (n = 308) were in their senior year. Less than 10% were in their preparatory year. One-quarter of the respondents (26.2%) were studying at the College of Medicine. Most of the respondents lived with their families (95.3%). Only 4.68% of the respondents had eye or systemic diseases that might affect eye health, while more than one-half (52.3%) had first or second-degree relatives with eye or systemic diseases [Figure 1]. One-half of the respondents did not have relatives with eye or systemic diseases that could affect eye health, while one-quarter had a first-degree relative with such disease (n = 199, 27.4%).

Responses [Figure 2] showed less-than-acceptable knowledge and awareness regarding eye problems. Approximately all respondents (94.08%) knew that wearing protective gear during wielding or smithing could protect the eye from serious injury, and roughly the same number knew that wearing contact lenses, while sleeping could lead to severe eye infections (94.2%). Most respondents also knew that cheap fireworks could hurt the eye (89.94%). Interestingly, ~90% of the respondents thought wearing glasses with an incorrect prescription could damage children’s eyes.

Responses to knowledge questions

There were split responses regarding many questions, such as whether good visual acuity in patients with diabetes indicates good visual health and whether refractory eye correction techniques using surgery could cure all types of eye problems. Three-quarters of the respondents did not know whether glucocorticoids could cause cataracts and whether using laser treatment to treat diabetic retinopathy could cause blindness. In addition, 60% and 56% of the respondents did not know whether glaucoma might cause symptoms and whether having extensive eyeballs in children is normal. Only two-thirds (66.8%) knew that contact lenses could not be worn safely during swimming.

Results [Table 2] showed a higher level of knowledge in college of medicine students than in other colleges for most questions. Three-quarters of medical school students (72.6%) knew that an untreated increase in ocular pressure could lead to visual impairment or permanent blindness compared with half of those from other schools (55.8%, P < 0.001). Similarly, 82.6% knew that uncontrolled diabetes could lead to diminished or lost vision due to problems in the retina, compared with 59.3%. Regarding aging as a developing factor for some ocular diseases, 80.5% of medical school students answered correctly compared with two-thirds (67.7%) of other schools (P = 0.001). A significant difference was observed in other questions, although the absolute difference in knowledge between groups was minor. No significant difference was observed between groups for the eleven questions. A comparison of knowledge scores based on study level is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Three-quarters of the women (72%) knew that contact lenses could not be worn during swimming or diving compared with only 45.4% of men (P < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for the question related to eyeliners (P < 0.001) and other questions, such as the effect of aging and diabetes on developing eye diseases. Lower rates of correct answers were observed for medical questions such as using ophthalmic glucocorticoids and laser therapy in managing diabetic neuropathy.

Linear regression analysis [Figure 3] showed that the average knowledge score was significantly lower in men than women (B = −2.02, P < 0.001), while age and level of education were not significantly associated with knowledge. Discussion

Blindness and visual impairment are significant worldwide health concerns associated with death, illness, and a lower quality of life, resulting in significant economic loss and productivity.[14] Most people disregard early indicators of eye disease and do not seek treatment in time to avert permanent vision loss.[4] Blindness can result from a lack of knowledge about prevention or a delay in obtaining medical care for eye illnesses such as glaucoma or cataracts.[15] Knowledge regarding glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, strabismus, and cataracts was extensively studied in the literature.[161718] However, most research focused on older people and excluded younger persons. Some were conducted in eye clinics or hospitals[17] and did not represent knowledge of the community’s healthy population. The current study included a homogenous population of university students. This might explain why the results may differ from other studies, as all respondents were university students, which might explain the lack of association between study level and knowledge observed in several other studies.[218]

The current cross-sectional study explored awareness regarding common eye problems in a public university in Riyadh City, Saudi Arabia. Results showed a variable level of knowledge regarding the different aspects of eye problems. Most respondents knew about the importance of wearing protective eye gear and the harm that could be caused by wearing contact lenses during sleep and using cheap fireworks. On the contrary, there was an apparent lack of knowledge in other areas, such as the harm that could be caused by wearing glasses with an incorrect prescription in children, where 89.67% were misguided to believe that it could.

Interestingly, two-thirds of the respondents did not know that patients with glaucoma might not show any signs or symptoms. These results are consistent with those reported in the general population in Riyadh city in 2016 and showed that only 14.8% had acceptable knowledge regarding glaucoma.[13] Similarly, a systematic review found significant knowledge gaps in glaucoma patients. The controlled and observational research found that several educational approaches for enhancing glaucoma knowledge were effective.[19] Our data further suggests that there is a need for eye healthcare interventions that focus on areas of knowledge that are lacking, particularly glaucoma.

In Saudi Arabia, several studies explored awareness regarding eye problems. In the Hail region, Alshammari[3] explored the knowledge regarding different eye problems such as glaucoma, cataracts, dry eyes, and diabetic retinopathy. The general populace in Saudi Arabia’s Hail Area moderately understands prevalent eye problems. The study concluded that there is a lack of understanding of preventable sight-threatening eye illnesses, particularly cataracts and glaucoma. A similar study was conducted in Jordan and showed that the level of awareness of the previous four diseases was high. The level of education and the participant’s age were the main factors associated with awareness regarding ocular diseases.[15]

Nonetheless, the current study showed that medical students were more aware than students from other universities. The study was also more comprehensive than the previous ones, included twenty-five questions related to eye problems, and was completed by ~700 respondents. Contradictory to other studies, we had questions about eye protection, contact lens storage/use, and strabismus.

The study also explored the association between gender and knowledge regarding eye problems and showed that women were generally more knowledgeable than men. Previous studies failed to show such an association[20] or even find contradictory results.[3] The reason might be the nature of the study questionnaire. The current questionnaire included questions related to contact lenses and eyeliners, which women are more likely to use and know about than men.[212223] In contrast, previous questionnaires did not include such questions.

Overall, responses showed a less than moderate knowledge regarding common eye problems, with a mean knowledge score of ~12 correct questions, a maximum of 22 correct answers, and no respondent answering all questions correctly. Health promotion can help minimize the burden of eye illnesses, thereby limiting preventable causes of blindness and impaired vision. Interestingly, only 25% of the students obtained their information from health awareness campaigns, and the average knowledge score of these respondents was similar to that of those who received information from other sources. Educational activities to increase public awareness of ocular illnesses can boost the effectiveness of health promotion, preventing avoidable blindness.

Conclusion

Universities should provide health education and raise student awareness of eye problems to boost awareness and understanding of common eye disorders. Seeking ophthalmologist consultation is the most critical step in preventing visual impairment, as it can help with early diagnosis, timely treatment, compliance, and prevention.

Recommendation

We recommend raising awareness regarding common eye diseases and eye health information and encouraging people to get medical attention when necessary for early management and prevention. We also recommend conducting longitudinal studies to determine the frequency of eye diseases, determine misconceptions regarding standard Methods to protect the eye and validate efficient prevention measures.

Limitation

This study has a potential limitation. The sample for this study was taken from only one location, the Riyadh region, specifically from Imam Mohammad Ibn Saud Islamic University students. As a result, the findings may not be applicable in a different area. Furthermore, various confounders may impact on how the results are interpreted. The study also looked at the relationship between gender and understanding of eye issues and found that women were generally more aware than men. The current questionnaire included questions about contact lenses and eyeliners, which women are more likely to use and know about than men. By contrast, previous questionnaires did not include such questions. The results indicate that the study population had a high awareness of eye diseases, but their knowledge of them still needed to be improved.
