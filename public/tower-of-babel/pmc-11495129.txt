A novel supraorbital surgical approach for enucleation in dromedary camels (Camelus dromedaries): cadaveric study
Authors: Mohamed Marzok, Mohamed A Nazih, Adel I Almubarak, Mohamed W El-Sherif
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnucleation and exenteration are widely utilized ophthalmic procedures in veterinary practice, involving the complete removal of the eye and comprehensive extraction of orbital contents, respectively. These procedures are indicated for pain relief, excision, and management of neoplasia metastases, and addressing severe medically untreatable conditions.AimThis study aimed to develop an orbital enucleation surgical approach. The study evaluated the applicability of the new approach and investigated the impact of the surgical procedure.MethodsAnatomical dissection of three cadaveric heads and surgical orbital enucleation in four cadaveric heads were performed. Anatomical data was collected, and feasibility, safety and applicability of the procedure were assessed.ResultsAnatomical dissection showed a distinctive large orbital fossa, providing a suitable surgical window to access orbital structures. The procedure was found to be feasible, facilitating the complete removal of the orbital content. Notably, the preservation of intact eyelids was prioritized to enhance cosmetic appearance.ConclusionThe presented orbital enucleation technique proved to be feasible, safe, reproducible, and required basic surgical skills to perform.Keywords: Camel, Enucleation, Exenteration, Ophthalmic, Orbital

Background

Enucleation involves the complete surgical extraction of the eye, while exenteration entails the comprehensive removal of all contents within the orbital cavity [1, 2]. These procedures are clean-contaminated procedures [3] and indicated to relieve pain, control neoplasia metastases, and address severe trauma that cannot treated surgically and severe inflammation/ infection that cannot managed medically [4–9]. In different animal species, the two well-established surgical enucleation techniques are the transconjunctival [8, 10] and transpalpebral approaches [6, 8, 11, 12]. The enucleation procedure is pursued with two distinct objectives: the first is to address ophthalmic conditions with minimal intra and postoperative complications [8, 9], and the second is to preserve cosmetic appearance post-procedure [8, 13, 14]. For the later purpose, an ocular prosthesis is implanted to maintain the shape and size of the orbital socket [14]. The eyes of camels feature a distinctive structure with three eyelids and double rows of eyelashes, adapting to the challenging conditions of the desert environment [15]. Despite the limited literature available, various congenital [16] and acquired ocular disorders, ranging from curable to untreatable, have been documented in camels with an incidence rate up to 12% of the studied sample [17]. Primary or secondary keratoconjunctivitis and nonulcerative keratitis, always treated medically [15, 17, 18]. However, recorded perforating wounds of the cornea and intra or extraoral tumors of the ocular structures needs surgical enucleation with an incidence rate up to 25% of investigated ocular conditions [15, 17–19]. Although enucleation is generally safe, potential complications such as bleeding, hematoma, infection, wound dehiscence meningitis, and blindness of the contralateral eye may arise [4, 5, 20]. Similar to other animal species, enucleation procedure in camels performed through transpalpebral approach [21]. The distinctive cranial anatomy of the camel, characterized by a prominent and deep temporal fossa that extends rostrally as the orbital fossa, is demarcated by the zygomatic and orbital processes. This configuration serves as the caudo-dorsal entrance to the orbital cavity [22, 23]. The aim of this study is to introduce a novel surgical enucleation approach utilizing the orbital fossa and assess its feasibility and safety.

Materials and methods

Study design

Seven freshly slaughtered cadaveric camel heads intended for meat consumption were used in this study. Anatomical dissection of the tempro-orbital fossa region was done in three heads. Anatomical findings proceeded the orbital enucleation procedure in four heads.

Anatomical surgery

The caudao dorsal area of the head of camel was dissected and the anatomy of the superficial and deep structures of the tempro-orbital region were documented. Evaluation of the ocular structures surrounding the eye, including the muscles, nerves, blood vessels, and connective tissues was done. Anatomical findings are shown in Fig. 1.

Surgical procedure

A modified enucleation approach was developed based on the anatomical findings. The left supraorbital fossae were aseptically prepared through clipping, disinfection, and draping. A semi-circular skin incision was performed at the orbital fossa and over the orbital process, directing the free part rostrally. The skin was sharply dissected to reveal the underlying orbital fascia. The fascia was sharply dissected, and the created flap flipped backward to create a window into the orbital cavity, exposing the orbital fat. Subsequently, the orbital fat was carefully grasped with forceps and excised using curved Mayo scissors. The orbital cavity was then thoroughly rinsed with saline solution. Afterward, the Tenon’s capsule, housing the eyeball and extraocular muscles, was dissected sharply using 20 cm curved Metzenbaum scissors, starting from its bony attachment. When the posterior attachment of the eye was performed, scissors were used to dissect into the dorsal bulbar conjunctive. The free hand of the surgeon was used to push the eye backward to facilitate further dissection. As the eyeball freed, grasping forceps were used to retract the eyeball through the created orbital fossa window and further sharp dissection performed till complete freeing of the eyeball. The remaining tissue were meticulously removed through sharp dissection using scissors. The orbital cavity was then filled with sterile gauze, followed by suturing of the orbital fascia using a number 2 − 0 polyglactin suture (Egycryl, EgyMed, Egypt). The skin incision was closed with a number 0 silk suture, employing an interrupted pattern. The surgical procedure is shown in Fig. 2.

Results

Anatomical findings

The orbital cavity extends laterally from the orbital rim to the optic foramen medially, remaining concealed beneath the frontal bone, sinus, and zygomaticofrontal process. The circular perimeter of the orbital rim measures approximately 17 cm, with a depth ranging from 8.7 to 9 cm towards the optic foramen. Within this cavity, the eyeball resides along its associated structures, including the lacrimal gland, eyelids, and periorbita. The anatomical relationship between the temporo-orbital fossa and the orbit is illustrated in Fig. 3. The periorbita comprises three layers (external periorbital, middle collateral, and internal muscular), envelopes, and secures these structures in their anatomical position, forming the orbital fasciae and spaces. The thickest external periorbital layer is a cone-shaped capsule that closely adheres to the periosteum of the orbital cavity. Caudally, it interfaces with the periorbital fat. The base of this cone attaches externally to the orbital rim, where it reflects and inserts into the substance of the eyelids, thus closing the orbital rim and forming an orbital septum. The apex was attached internally to the optic foramen. The longitudinal axis of the periorbital sac forms an acute angle of approximately 63–65 degrees with the axis of the vomer process and approximately 115–117 degrees with that of the basisphenoid.

The camel skull showing the anatomical relationship between the temporo-orbital fossa (red arrow), where the skin flap is created to access the periorbital space, and the orbit (yellow arrow), which represents the entrance to the ocular socket

Surgical findings

The feasibility of the procedure was evident, as it was easily applied using ordinary general surgery tools, without requiring specialized professional expertise. Additionally, better exposure to the surgical field, allowing for more efficient manipulation and tissue handling. The technique’s operator experience requirements appeared to be minimal, as the improved exposure of the surgical field facilitated easier access during the procedure. Leaving the eyelids intact provides favorable cosmetic and functional outcomes, makes it a promising approach for ocular surgery in camels. However, further clinical research and larger-scale studies are recommended to validate these findings.

Discussion

The current study introduces a novel orbital enucleation technique for camels, designed to enhance navigation within the orbital cavity and simplify operations on orbital content. The study’s findings reveal positive outcomes across diverse evaluation categories, affirming the feasibility, safety, and applicability of the technique. To our knowledge, this technique is the first to report on orbital enucleation in camels, adding a unique perspective to the existing literature on enucleation procedures. Consistent with prior approaches, the current technique aims to fulfill the dual objectives of enucleation surgery: effectively removing orbital structures with minimal complications while preserving postoperative cosmetic appearance [2, 10, 13]. Earlier techniques showcased enucleation conducted via a transpalpebral incision, involving the excision of the eye, and associated ocular structures [11, 12], followed by the suturing of eyelids. Another method involves a subconjunctival approach, preserving the eyelids for potential subsequent prosthesis implantation [8, 10]. Our current technique, owing to the orbital approach, facilitates superior navigation within the orbital cavity, allowing for the comprehensive removal of its contents. This approach not only enables the implantation of a prosthesis through the incision area but also ensures the preservation of the eyelids.

The anatomy of the temporo-orbital region exhibited an absence of vital structures, ensuring the safety of the surgical incision. Additionally, the orbital cavity, filled posteriorly with an extensively large periorbital fat, when surgically extracted, provided a clear view of the orbital structures. These findings highlight the superiority of this approach in minimizing the likelihood of postoperative inflammation and infection. This is achieved by complete removal of all orbital structures and the direct application of an orbital prosthesis, consequently enhancing cosmetic outcomes. In the previous technique, specific surgical instruments were recommended to manage intraoperative bleeding, a prevalent complication of the procedure [12, 14]. Another intraoperative challenge involves manipulating ocular structures during dissection, attributed to the limited operating space [13, 21]. In our technique, the operator can utilize their non-dominant hand to stabilize the eye or apply pressure during the dissection of ocular structures from the bony attachment, overcoming this obstacle. Common reported postoperative complications are hemorrhage, hematoma, cyst formation, infection, and inflammation [1]. Most reported postoperative complications are typically associated with inadequate hemostasis and incomplete removal of orbital structures. Herein, it is hypothesized that these complications will be minimal or completely absent. Other serious complications reported in previous techniques include trauma to the optic chiasm leading to subsequent blindness in the contralateral eye and meningitis arising from forceful traction of the eye during excision [5]. In our present technique, the ease of cutting the optic nerve, along with the posterior attachment of ocular structures through the wide surgical window in the orbital fossa, is emphasized, eliminating the need for traction on the optic chiasm. Aligned with previous reports, our results indicate that the technique appears to demand minimal operator experience. The enhanced exposure of the surgical field allows for easier access, potentially reducing the learning curve for surgeons adopting this new approach. In contrast to previous reports where orbital implants were applied through the bulbar aperture [8, 14], herein, the current approach facilitates the application of the prosthesis from the dorsal orbital aperture. This method is expected to offer better filling, preserve the size of the orbital fossa, minimize any potential implant loss and better cosmetic outcomes. Although the flap is large due to the size of the temporo-orbital fossa, bleeding may occur during its creation. Adequate hemostatic techniques are essential to control bleeding and prevent complications. However, further research and larger-scale studies are recommended to validate these findings and explore potential applications in a broader clinical setting. The modified technique’s advantages in providing better exposure and tissue handling make it a valuable addition to the existing armamentarium of camel ophthalmic procedures.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of this study highlight the potential of the orbital enucleation technique as a safe, efficient, and effective approach in camels. The positive outcomes observed in terms of feasibility, safety, and applicability.
