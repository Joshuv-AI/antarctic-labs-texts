Core Elements of the Stunting Prevention Model to Improve Adolescents’ Quality of Life
Authors: Eva Santi Hutasoit, Puspa Sari, Yenni Zuhairini, Dany Hilmanto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStunting is a public health problem influenced by biological, behavioral, family, social, environmental, and health system factors throughout the life cycle. Factors such as poor reproductive health, anemia in adolescents, low health literacy, early marriage, socioeconomic conditions, and limited access to health services increase the risk of intergenerational stunting. However, adolescent-focused stunting prevention approaches as pre-pregnancy strategies are still limited. This study aims to identify core elements for developing a stunting prevention model to improve adolescents’ quality of life.MethodsThis is a descriptive qualitative study with an exploratory approach. The study was conducted through focus group discussions and in-depth interviews with 27 purposively selected informants, including adolescents, parents, school principals, community members, health workers, community health center heads, and policymakers. Data collection was conducted until data saturation was reached, and the data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis.ResultsThe analysis yielded 73 codes, 28 categories, and eight main themes: adolescent factors, family factors, social support, institutional commitment, governance, quality of health services, access to health services, and the environment. Taken together, these themes represent the key interrelated components of an adolescent-centered approach to stunting prevention, with potential pathways to improving adolescent quality of life.ConclusionCombining these eight core elements can offer a comprehensive, cross-sectoral, and life-cycle approach to supporting adolescent well-being and addressing the intergenerational risk of stunting.

Introduction

Stunting is a child’s growth and development problem that arises form chronic malnutrition and frequent illness, reflecting long-term exposure to various factors such as biological, nutritional, social, environmental, and health systems from before conception, during pregnancy, and until early life.1,2 This condition is influenced by interrelated elements, including maternal malnutrition, poor diet, recurrent infections, unhealthy environments, poverty, low education levels, and difficulty in accessing quality health services, so that efforts to prevent stunting require a cross-sectoral approach that not only meets children’s nutrition needs but also involves factors that influence life.1–3 Stunting is also an intergenerational health issue because various risk factors have existed since adolescence, before pregnancy.2 Malnutrition, anemia, early marriage, a lack of understanding of reproductive health, and difficult access to health services, information, and community support for adolescents can increase the risk of reproductive health problems, pregnancy complications, stunted growth, premature birth, early childhood malnutrition, and impaired brain development in subsequent generations.2,4,5 Therefore, stunting prevention must be viewed as a lifelong health strategy by strengthening the health and well-being of adolescents before reproductive age, thereby breaking the chain of stunting between generations.2,4,6

Adolescence is a crucial stage in child development, marked by the second-fastest physical growth spurt after infancy, as well as rapid biological, cognitive, and psychosocial development.5 These characteristics make adolescence a golden moment to improve nutritional and health status before they enter childbearing age.5 In addition to being a primary target in the global nutrition program,1 adolescence is an important time to build health literacy, healthy lifestyle habits, and reproductive health that support optimal nutrition before reproductive age.4 Thus, intervention efforts during adolescence offer a great opportunity to improve their quality of life in the near future.4 Furthermore, improving adolescent health also contributes to improving maternal and child health in the future.5

Therefore, the success of program interventions during adolescence should be measured not only by clinical indicators and nutritional status but also by improvements in adolescents’ overall health and well-being.7 Within this framework, quality of life is increasingly recognized as an important outcome in measuring the success of interventions because it can indicate the overall physical, psychological, social, and environmental conditions.8 Various studies have shown that the more healthy behaviors adolescents adopt, including physical activity, healthy eating patterns, good sleep quality and duration, and low sedentary behavior, the higher their health-related quality of life (HRQoL).9 Improving quality of life through better nutrition, reproductive health, and healthy lifestyles is expected to strengthen adolescents’ readiness to enter reproductive age, thereby reducing potential factors that cause stunting in future generations.3,5 Therefore, interventions that encourage the formation of healthy lifestyle habits in adolescence can improve current quality of life, strengthen reproductive health, and reduce the risk of stunting in future generations.5,9

Although adolescence is a critical period for ensuring adequate nutrition to support growth and development and prevent stunting, adolescent health remains underrepresented in global nutrition strategies. Most nutrition policies and interventions have conventionally focused on pregnant women, infants, and young children. In contrast, large-scale programs for school-aged children and adolescents remain limited in many low- and middle-income countries.2,3 This gap deserves attention because various determinants of stunting, such as anemia, poor dietary quality, low health literacy, early marriage, and early pregnancy, often begin before conception.2 Youth-centered prevention strategies are crucial to stop the pattern of stunting that repeats across generations.2,6 To achieve these targets, an integrated, life-cycle-focused approach is needed that can address the complexity of the causes of stunting, including those pre-conception.4,5 Indonesia has chosen a cross-sectoral strategy through targeted and sensitive interventions to accelerate the reduction of stunting rates in line with global efforts.10 However, the majority of interventions still occur in pregnant women and children under five, while programs specifically targeting young people in the period before conception are still rare.3,5 Iron and folic acid supplementation is one of the main actions to improve the nutritional status of adolescent girls; its implementation in various countries, including Indonesia, is still hampered by low beverage fulfillment, weak monitoring, and a lack of institutional support, so that its impact on adolescent nutritional status is not optimal.3,11 These results indicate that preventing stunting in young people cannot rely solely on a single nutritional intervention, but requires a more comprehensive, integrated, and cross-sectoral model, that addresses the causes of stunting before conception.3,4

Cross-sector collaboration is increasingly recommended to advance adolescent health and nutrition by engaging the medical, education, food, and social protection sectors.12 However, most intervention findings focus on a single aspect, such as nutrition education, iron-folic acid supplementation, and reproductive health,3 while effectiveness data on integrated approaches in low- and middle-income countries are scarce.13 Adolescent health programs that focus on adolescents themselves and integrate multiple sectors are still rarely implemented consistently in low- and middle-income countries.14 Health and education systems in many countries continue to operate in silos, hindering effective cross-sector collaboration to improve adolescent health and well-being.15 Therefore, existing evidence on a comprehensive framework that encompasses the roles of adolescents, families, schools, health services, communities, and policymakers within a single lifelong stunting-prevention strategy remains limited.3,14,16

In addition, most preventive planning for adolescents still focuses on nutritional and health guidelines, while adolescents’ quality of life is no longer prioritized as the primary outcome.3,13 In this regard, the standard of living is increasingly recognized as the result of various dimensions that describe physical, psychological, social and environmental health, and is the basis for adolescents’ willingness to enter reproductive age and adulthood.7,15 These limitations highlight the need for a comprehensive, youth-centered stunting prevention model that integrates all elements of lifelong stunting prevention.

The uniqueness of this study lies in the development of the core element of the a stunting prevention model, which positions adolescents as central actors in an integrated, community-based prevention system, oriented toward a lifecycle approach, rather than simply as prospective parents or passive recipients of nutrition education. Unlike approaches that typically focus on mothers, children under five, families, or primary health care, this model integrates the roles of adolescents, families, peers, teachers, health workers, communities, and policymakers through a multisectoral approach, while being tailored to the unique cultural characteristics of the population.4,6 Furthermore, the proposed model positions adolescent quality of life as an intended long term outcome of adolescent centered stunting prevention. Achieving this outcome requires a model that integrates biological components (nutrition, reproductive health, anemia prevention), healthy behaviors, psychosocial support, access to health services, institutional commitment, and community governance as interacting elements in addressing the determinants of stunting before pregnancy.

The development of this model is based on evidence that adolescent health remains underutilized in policy, programs, and research, despite the potential for investment in adolescent health to improve health across the life cycle, strengthen capacity as potential parents, and break the cycle of intergenerational transmission.4–6 This gap, especially in low- and middle-income countries, necessitates a more youth-centered, multisectoral stunting prevention model.17,18 This study aims to identify and describe the core elements needed to develop an adolescent-based stunting prevention model to improve adolescents’ quality of life, as a strategy to break the intergenerational cycle of stunting.

Materials and Methods

Research Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design with an exploratory approach19 to provide a comprehensive description of the experiences and perspectives of adolescents, parents, health workers, teachers, and other stakeholders related to stunting prevention among adolescents, and to identify the core elements needed to develop a prevention model. This research was situated within a constructivist paradigm, recognizing that participants’ experiences, perceptions, and meanings regarding stunting prevention in adolescents are socially constructed through interactions within their families, schools, communities, and the health system.

Data Collection

Tembilahan was selected as the research location. It is the administrative center of Indragiri Hilir Regency, also known as the “Regency of a Thousand Parits”, and is located 288 km from Riau’s provincial capital. The research was conducted from February to April 2025 using focus group discussions with some adolescents. In-depth community interviews were also conducted with other adolescents, parents, school principals, community health workers, community health center heads, heads of health centers, heads of health offices, heads of education offices, and heads of the Ministry of Religious Affairs. Focus group discussions were used with adolescents to encourage interaction and collective reflection among peers. At the same time, individual interviews were conducted with parents, teachers, health professionals, and policymakers to explore their experiences and professional perspectives in greater depth. Participants were selected because they represented key stakeholders involved in adolescent health policy and implementation, as well as stunting prevention. Interviews were conducted by the first author, a female midwifery researcher with qualitative research training and prior experience conducting in-depth interviews. Informants were contacted in person or by telephone, provided with information, and asked to sign consent forms. There was no prior relationship between the researcher and informants before the survey. Research information was also provided to the adolescents’ parents or legal guardians after they agreed, and consent forms were signed by both the parents or legal guardians and the adolescents. In-depth interviews were conducted face-to-face to explore the existing phenomena or constructs. The interview process continued until data saturation indicated that no additional information was needed.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the Integrated Health Service System Model for the Prevention of Food Deficiency Anemia (IDA)20 and existing literature. This guide ensured clarity and relevance for qualitative research experts. The main questions in this study included “What forms of stunting prevention programs will be implemented for adolescents?”; “What are the barriers faced in stunting prevention programs?”; “What types of health services are available to adolescents?”; and “What are their expectations regarding the implementation of stunting prevention programs?” All interviewees were asked these questions. Interviews lasted 45–60 minutes. Data collected were audio-recorded. The resulting recordings and written notes served as research tools. Interviews with adolescents and their parents were conducted at their homes. Interviews with teachers, health workers, and policymakers were conducted at their workplaces. The interviews took place in a comfortable and quiet room.

Participants were selected using a purposive sampling method with maximum variation. This sampling strategy was used to ensure a broad range of informants with diverse roles and experiences related to adolescent stunting prevention. The involvement of participants from various sectors enabled this study to capture diverse perspectives on biological, behavioral, family, community, health system, and policy factors influencing adolescent stunting prevention. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached, at which point no new information or themes relevant to the research objectives emerged. Some participants were re-interviewed if the data lacked depth or clarity. General inclusion criteria required that participants have lived in Tembilahan for at least 1 year, ensuring familiarity with adolescent health services.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Padjadjaran University, Bandung, approved this study under code number 1271/UN6.KEP/EC/2024. All study subjects provided informed consent before the study began. Their identities were encrypted, and secure data storage was used to ensure privacy and anonymity.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke (2020).21 Consistent with the qualitative descriptive design and constructivist paradigm, the analysis focused on identifying patterns of meaning grounded participants experience and perspectives. The analysis process included: (1) identifying and understanding the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) developing initial themes, (4) reviewing and developing themes, (5) defining and collecting themes, and (6) compiling a report of the analysis results. The data analysis process was conducted using NVivo 14 software. The criteria used to determine trustworthiness were credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Iterative data analysis, source triangulation, and research team discussions during theme development helped enhance credibility. Providing a thorough explanation of the study setting and participant characteristics helped support transferability. An audit trail that recorded analytical and methodological assessments helped confirm reliability. In line with this, Nowell et al (2017) supported confirmability by linking interpretations to appropriate participant quotes and keeping reflective analytical notes throughout the research process.22

The data analysis process was carried out through a series of stages, namely: (1) All conversations were recorded using a voice recorder and written down word for word. The written results included 18 conversation files totaling 207 pages. The researcher read all the writings repeatedly, comparing them with the voice recordings to ensure accuracy and to gain an in-depth understanding of the informants’ experiences, views, and ideas. During this activity, the researcher took analytical notes on initial ideas, patterns, and key issues that emerged from the data. (2) Next, all writings were analyzed in a structured manner to identify parts of the data related to the research objectives. Each meaningful quote was labeled (initial code) using NVivo 14. The labeling process was carried out inductively, using data from the conversations, so that labels were developed from the informants’ experiences and perspectives rather than predetermined by theory. Labeling and development of main ideas were carried out by the first author and repeatedly discussed with the research team until agreement was reached on their interpretation. (3) Labels that had similar meanings were then integrated into a broader framework through a continuous process of communication and grouping. From this activity, 73 labels were produced and arranged into 28 frameworks. These frameworks were then organized into initial main ideas describing patterns of meaning related to factors influencing stunting prevention among adolescents. (4) The initial main ideas were then reviewed by comparing them to all initial data to ensure that each main idea was supported by adequate data and had internal consistency. During this phase, several frameworks were combined, separated, or refined until a set of main ideas emerged that best represented the experiences of all sources. (5) After the review process was completed, each main idea was clearly described according to the main meaning contained therein and given a name that reflected the content of the main idea. This process resulted in eight main ideas that describe the core components of the stunting prevention model in adolescents, namely adolescent elements, family elements, social support, institutional commitment, governance, quality of health services, reach of health services, and environmental elements, (6) The final stage was carried out by analyzing the relationships between important ideas to explain how these various elements play a role in preventing stunting in adolescents. Each main idea is supported by direct quotes from sources as concrete evidence, then documented with citations to research findings and relevant literature to provide a complete understanding of the core components of the adolescent-based stunting prevention model.

In-depth interviews were conducted among teenagers representing each level of education, namely junior high school (JHS), senior high school (SHS), vocational high school (VHS), and Islamic high school (IHS) in Tembilahan. Additionally, interviews were conducted with representatives of parents, teachers, community leaders, healthcare workers, and program manager at the Health Department, the Ministry of Religious Affairs, and the manager of Family Resilience and Welfare Program at the Department Of Population Control, Family Planning, Women’s Empowerment And Child Protection (DPCFPWECP) of Indragiri Hilir Regency. A total of 29 participants were recruited; 2 participants withdrew due to illness before completing the interview, resulting in a final participant count of 27. Theme 1. Adolescent Factors

Knowledge

Adequate understanding can encourage someone to live a healthy lifestyle. Many adolescents under study lacked a sufficient understanding of stunting; they only recognized it as a condition of short stature, without concern about its definition, prevention methods, or related symptoms. Such condition was reflected in the following statement: Stunting is just a name, perhaps, but what is stunting? What’s new about it? And what are the consequences? Well, as far as I know, many people still don’t know about it. (TC1)

Stunting is just a name, perhaps, but what is stunting? What’s new about it? And what are the consequences? Well, as far as I know, many people still don’t know about it. (TC1)

Healthy Lifestyle

Healthy lifestyles are a crucial element in stunting prevention efforts through an improvement in the quality of life of the younger generation. Adolescents involved here had not adopted the principles of a balanced diet; the majority often skipped breakfast before starting activities and tended to choose fast food for lunch at school. Similarly, buying food outside the home remained a common choice among adolescents. Some adolescents were also reluctant to eat vegetables, and some even engaged in smoking and experienced bullying. Such conditions were expressed through the following statement: They don’t have time for breakfast at home before going to study at school. If it is made in percentage, 50% of them don’t have time for breakfast at home. (TC1)

They don’t have time for breakfast at home before going to study at school. If it is made in percentage, 50% of them don’t have time for breakfast at home. (TC1)

Attitude

Attitude can be defined as an individual’s tendency to react positively or negatively to any object, person, or event. Many adolescents often refused to take iron tablets, as expressed by the following informant: Then, some of our friends also don’t want to consume the tablets. (TN5)

Then, some of our friends also don’t want to consume the tablets. (TN5)

Interest

Adolescent interest referred to the desire, attention, and attraction to a particular object, activity, or field that motivated them to participate voluntarily. Some adolescents were less enthusiastic or interested during counseling sessions, while others preferred to be active in extracurricular sports activities rather than participating in YICC (Youth Information and Counseling Center) activities. Such condition was in line with the following statement: YICC is unattractive. I’m not really interested in it. I am just interested in joining extracurricular activities. (TN7)

YICC is unattractive. I’m not really interested in it. I am just interested in joining extracurricular activities. (TN7)

Motivation

Motivation is a crucial element or primary factor that drives someone to engage in activities to achieve a specific goal. Several adolescents believed that knowledge about stunting was not urgent for them, as they believed that having children was still a long way off and would be a matter for after marriage. Male adolescents also assumed that stunting prevention was the responsibility of adolescent girls. This opinion was in line with the following statement: Even when we explain, they seem to be imagining things. They think it’s not important, like, ‘Oh, it’s not my son or daughter’. Maybe, ah, hopefully it won’t affect me. If it’s my son or daughter, yeah maybe I will consider it. Well, even if we give leaflets, brochures and so on, they might not keep them. (HW3)

Even when we explain, they seem to be imagining things. They think it’s not important, like, ‘Oh, it’s not my son or daughter’. Maybe, ah, hopefully it won’t affect me. If it’s my son or daughter, yeah maybe I will consider it. Well, even if we give leaflets, brochures and so on, they might not keep them. (HW3)

Theme 2. Family Factors

Family Knowledge

Parental knowledge has a significant impact on children’s health, given that parents are primarily responsible for child care, nutrition, and the development of healthy behaviors. However, some parents still did not understand the concept of stunting, as expressed by the following informant: I don’t know about that (stunting) (PF3)

I don’t know about that (stunting) (PF3)

Some parents still do not understand how to prepare food, as expressed by the following informant: Yes, they can’t prepare the food they have, but they don’t know how to cook or serve it to their children. They don’t understand (PHS)

Yes, they can’t prepare the food they have, but they don’t know how to cook or serve it to their children. They don’t understand (PHS)

Healthy Lifestyle

Parental positive behavior surely can prevent stunting, while negative behavior may increase a child’s likelihood of experiencing stunting. Some parents or families of adolescents still defecated in rivers and used contaminated river water to wash clothes and eating utensils, as reported by the following informant: There are still many of them. Well, they put it in the trash, ma’am. That’s right. Yes, yes. This side, as long as the tide. This one, this one, along the riverbank. They often defecate in the river, and wash everything with that water. (HW4)

There are still many of them. Well, they put it in the trash, ma’am. That’s right. Yes, yes. This side, as long as the tide. This one, this one, along the riverbank. They often defecate in the river, and wash everything with that water. (HW4)

Economics

Household income or economic circumstances will influence access to food, which is linked to low purchasing power. Teenage marriage is still common due to economic factors. When an adolescent marries, he or she is no longer considered a dependent of the parents. Such condition was conveyed by the following informant: Sometimes, it’s not realized by some people. They don’t understand. They think it’s the happy ending, isn’t it? That’s it, this means there are no more dependents. It is considered as the responsibility of the husband. (HW5)

Sometimes, it’s not realized by some people. They don’t understand. They think it’s the happy ending, isn’t it? That’s it, this means there are no more dependents. It is considered as the responsibility of the husband. (HW5)

Family Motivation

Motivation from parents or family is a crucial factor in encouraging parents to build healthy families, ensure proper development for their children, and create a quality life. Generally, men who were reluctant to participate in counseling activities assumed that the role of childcare was entirely the women’s responsibility, as expressed by the following informant: Yes, so wherever there are counseling activities, they are more frequently attended by women. Men are rare to join. Sure, it is important to ask men to come, they should be invited. (PHS)

Yes, so wherever there are counseling activities, they are more frequently attended by women. Men are rare to join. Sure, it is important to ask men to come, they should be invited. (PHS)

Parenting

Parenting patterns are a collection of behaviors and habits implemented by families to meet physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs for optimal growth and development. Families play a crucial role in preventing stunting at various stages of life. In fact, some parents still did not realize the importance of healthy meals for adolescents. The following informant expressed it: Grandma didn’t have time to make it. Grandma was in the garden. But her father gave her snacks. For example, she was going to leave in the morning, her father had already delivered the money the night before. (P2)

Grandma didn’t have time to make it. Grandma was in the garden. But her father gave her snacks. For example, she was going to leave in the morning, her father had already delivered the money the night before. (P2)

Theme 3. Social Support

Teacher/School Support

Teachers play a crucial role in encouraging, facilitating, and monitoring iron supplement consumption among adolescent girls as a preventative measure against anemia and stunting. Teachers or schools can provide this support by providing information and time for adolescent health programs. However, teachers often lacked the time to participate in adolescent health program activities due to their heavy workload. This was consistent with the following informant’s statement: He’s not well-monitored because the teachers were busy. Because all the teachers, as well as the homeroom teacher, were always busy teaching. (HW7)

He’s not well-monitored because the teachers were busy. Because all the teachers, as well as the homeroom teacher, were always busy teaching. (HW7)

Community Support

Community support can take the form of direct community participation that helps to create an ideal environment for children’s growth and development. Community or health cadre involvement in health education activities is still quite limited, as expressed by the following informant: Our cadres are also integrated with health post cadres. Discussion

The study findings indicated that stunting prevention among adolescents was a highly complex issue and was influenced by various factors, including adolescent factors, family factors, social support, institutional commitment, governance, access to health services, quality, and the environment (Table 2). There were several similarities between the elements of the Model of Integrated Health Care System to Prevent IDA20 and the study findings. However, this study identified differences between the basic theory and the IDA model. Such differences lie in adolescent factors, family factors, and the environment. Based on the finding, the proposed conceptual model comprises eight interconnected core elements: adolescent factors, family factors, social support, institutional commitment, governance, access to health services, quality, and the environment, which together form a multidimensional framework for adolescent centered stunting prevention (Figure 2). The intervention model proposed in this study aims to prevent stunting through an improvement in the quality of life of adolescents.

Adolescent Factors

This study found that most adolescents know stunting only as a condition of being “short”, without understanding its definition, causes, long-term consequences, or prevention methods. Adolescents are also unable to connect current health with reproductive health and the future health of their children. A similar situation is also seen in many low- and middle-income countries, where a lack of literacy skills and behaviors to prevent malnutrition in adolescents can impact reproductive health and the well-being of future generations.5 Although knowledge from adolescence can drive the development of preventive attitudes through increased health literacy 2023),23 behavioral change cannot be achieved solely through increased knowledge; it also requires strengthening adolescents’ motivation, skills, and active participation in health behaviors.24

These findings emphasize the high level of adolescent ignorance about the link between current conditions and the health of future generations, demonstrating the need for education about stunting with an approach that considers the entire lifespan. This approach not only has the potential to prevent stunting but also encourages healthy lifestyle behaviors that are beneficial throughout life.5 These healthy lifestyle behaviors are associated with a better quality of life for adolescents in physical, psychological, and social aspects.9 Therefore, improving adolescents’ quality of life and well-being should be one of the main goals of adolescent health interventions, as it reflects optimal physical, psychological, and social development.15

In addition to a lack of knowledge, adolescents also exhibit unhealthy lifestyles, such as skipping breakfast and consuming low-nutritious snacks. The results of this study align with research in the Netherlands, which found that teenagers who often skip breakfast and rarely eat fruit and vegetables are less physically active. They tend to lead unhealthy lifestyles, especially among those who have problems using social media.25 Eating habits formed during adolescence have a significant impact on health throughout life and for future generations, so it is important to integrate education with strategies for behavior change and environmental support.5 These strategies must also be supported by school and family environments that facilitate healthy lifestyle choices.4 This is because improvements in diet, physical activity, and health behaviors are closely related to improvements in adolescent quality of life.9 The results of this study indicate that life-course-focused health literacy is a core element of the developed model, as it links increased knowledge, behavior change, and adolescent quality of life.

Research results indicate that adolescents show low interest in extracurricular activities provided by the Youth Information and Counseling Center (YICC). Therefore, information about stunting needs to be designed in an engaging manner using digital and social media. Developing digital educational content about stunting on social media is expected to stimulate adolescent participation and increase their knowledge and role in stunting prevention efforts.26 A review in Canada shows that social media-based nutrition education interventions that involve peers and are tailored to adolescents’ characteristics can increase adolescents’ nutritional knowledge, engagement, and participation. However, evidence on changes in eating behavior remains limited.27 Adolescents in the United States prefer health interventions delivered via social media platforms, especially TikTok and Instagram, because the content format is considered more interesting, visual, interactive, and aligned with their digital media usage habits.28 The findings of this study reflect the low interest in YICC, indicating the importance of transforming educational media into digital platforms that better align with adolescent preferences. This is in line with Hargreaves et al (2022), who emphasized that digital media can be used to increase adolescent engagement, social support, and health literacy as part of adolescent health intervention strategies.4 Therefore, utilizing digital media is one strategy in a stunting prevention model aimed at improving adolescent quality of life.

Adolescent motivation is not only determined by family support but also by the perception that stunting issues only become important after marriage and are considered women’s responsibility. This perception negatively impacts adolescent boys’ participation in early health promotion efforts, so lifecycle strategies should target both boys and girls equally.4,12 Unlike previous studies that focused more on low knowledge and dietary habits, this study shows that gender norms also play a role in shaping adolescent motivation towards stunting prevention. Therefore, stunting prevention strategies need to incorporate aspects of gender equality, increased motivation, participation of adolescent boys, changes in perceptions of risk, and the promotion of sustainable healthy lifestyles. These findings indicate that gender equality is an important supporting factor in the development of stunting prevention models because it influences adolescent motivation, participation, and quality of life.

Family Factors

The findings of this study indicate that family members influence each other in creating a conducive environment or actually become a barrier to stunting prevention efforts. This conclusion is consistent with systematic observations that revealed a link between family cohesion and health literacy in improving health communication, shared decision-making, parental participation, and healthy lifestyles in adolescents and other family members.29 Inadequate family understanding of stunting may be due to the focus of program interventions, which are still largely directed at pregnant women, infants, and toddlers. At the same time, attention to the preconception and adolescence periods remains limited.3 Consequently, families with adolescents have not been prioritized in health promotion and education, thus underutilizing opportunities to foster health literacy before conception.30 A methodological review revealed that adolescents’ eating habits are significantly influenced by parental knowledge, parenting styles, and family food practices.31 Thus, improving health literacy within the family has the potential to support improvements in adolescents’ quality of life by creating a family environment that supports their health and well-being.32 This conclusion indicates that family literacy is a fundamental element in this model because it influences adolescents’ healthy lifestyle habits from the preconception stage.

Findings regarding family habits regarding the use of river water for activities such as bathing, defecating, and washing indicate that sanitation remains a major obstacle in stunting prevention efforts. Poor sanitation increases the risk of exposure to pathogens, which can trigger environmentally induced intestinal dysfunction (EID), thus disrupting nutrient absorption and physical development in children.33 This situation aligns with systematic and meta-analyses in Indonesia, which suggest that inadequate sanitation, lack of access to safe drinking water, and socioeconomic conditions play crucial roles in the causes of stunting.34 Global evidence also indicates that poor water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) practices increase the likelihood of stunting in children in low- and middle-altitude countries, so nutritional interventions need to be aligned with improvements in environmental sanitation.35 Improving family sanitation can also create a residential environment that supports adolescents’ quality of life.36 This indicates that sanitation behavior within families remains a challenge despite the implementation of various stunting prevention programs and is a fundamental element in stunting prevention models.

Economic pressures in this study not only limit families’ ability to provide nutritious food but also drive some families to enter into early marriage to reduce their economic burden. This situation has the potential to increase the risk of early pregnancy and reinforce the cycle of stunting across generations.2 These findings align with international evidence that poverty and low socioeconomic status are key determinants of child marriage in many low- and middle-income countries,37 while economic constraints increase household food insecurity and limit access to nutritious food, increasing the risk of stunting.34,38 The life-course approach suggests that improving socioeconomic conditions before pregnancy is a crucial part of the cross-generational cycle of stunting.2 In addition to increasing the risk of stunting, economic pressures also have the potential to reduce adolescents’ quality of life by hindering their opportunities for optimal growth and development.32 These findings indicate that the decision to marry off adolescents is not only influenced by cultural factors but also reflects a family’s adaptation strategy to these conditions. Therefore, family economic resilience needs to be positioned as a core element of stunting prevention models.

Research on parenting practices shows that parents pay little attention to the nutrition and food quality of their adolescent children. Adolescents with unhealthy eating habits are more likely to have parents who provide fewer healthy foods, attend family meals less frequently, and exhibit less supportive parenting practices related to food.31 A report by Arseniou et al (2026) also supports this conclusion, showing that family health literacy and family harmony impact health communication, shared decision-making, and parent-adolescent engagement in disease prevention efforts.29 Therefore, parenting practices that support healthy lifestyles can not only improve adolescents’ dietary habits but also foster a family environment that enhances quality of life.32 Therefore, positive parenting is an essential part of a model to support adolescent quality of life and prevent stunting.

Research findings reveal that gender roles still limit fathers’ participation in childcare, thus preventing stunting from being viewed as the mother’s responsibility. A systematic review showed that fathers are rarely involved in childcare and health programs in low- and middle-income countries because most programs are mother-centered and not designed to maximize father involvement effectively.39 Gender roles, stereotypes, and traditional role allocations still limit fathers’ involvement in childcare and nutrition programs.40 Conversely, active father involvement improves parenting and supports mothers,41 which can foster a positive family environment that enhances adolescents’ quality of life.32 These findings confirm that parenting is a crucial component of the stunting prevention model.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the family is not only a place for adolescents to grow and develop but also a primary means of connecting biological, behavioral, social, and environmental factors in the process of stunting prevention throughout the life cycle. The developed model expands the stunting prevention approach, which has focused on pregnant women and the First 1000 Days of Life, by making the family the primary support system from adolescence onward. This integrated family approach is expected to not only to contribute to breaking the intergenerational cycle of stunting but also to support adolescent health, well-being, and quality of life as intended outcome of the proposed model.

Social Support

The findings of this study indicate that teachers’ limited time is a barrier to implementing health programs in schools due to competing academic demands, resulting in inconsistent health education. However, the school environment is a strategic setting for shaping adolescent health behaviors through nutrition education and improving health literacy delivered by teachers.26 School-based nutrition education interventions have been shown to increase adolescents’ knowledge of iron deficiency anemia and its prevention42 and to have the potential to improve adolescents’ subjective well-being.43 Therefore, integrating nutrition education into the curriculum, along with teacher training and cross-sectoral support, is crucial for more sustainable program implementation.26 These findings underscore that teacher support is a core element of the model, as it strengthens adolescent health literacy, fosters healthy lifestyle habits, and improves adolescent quality of life.

The low participation of cadres in this study indicates that community support for adolescent health programs is suboptimal, indicating a lack of human resources, mentoring systems, and institutional support. Systematic observations confirm that capacity building for cadres requires supportive supervision, feedback, and ongoing institutional support,44 as well as structured supervision, balanced workload sharing, and a clear mentoring system to support cadre performance.45 Program success is also influenced by collaboration between health workers, schools, families, and the community. This cross-sector partnership supports program implementation coordination, clarifies the roles of each party involved, and increases the willingness of health interventions in schools.46 Therefore, adolescents’ perceived social support is associated with better quality of life and can help them overcome various developmental challenges.47 Therefore, ongoing cadre support is a crucial element in increasing program effectiveness while supporting adolescents’ physical, psychological, and quality-of-life development.

This study also found that peer support is still limited among members of the Youth Information and Counseling Center (YICC), so the potential for social influence in shaping healthy norms and behaviors in adolescents has not been optimally utilized. A systematic analysis showed that peer education, peer mentoring, peer counseling, and group activities can improve adolescents’ knowledge and some health behaviors, although their impact on behavior change varies.48 This is also evident in a study in Ethiopia, which showed that a school-based nutrition education intervention through classroom learning, school clubs, peer-group mentoring, counseling, and parent-teacher meetings proved feasible and improved adolescent girls’ dietary well-being.49 Peer support facilitates the delivery of health information, making it more effective by leveraging established social relationships among adolescents.48 Furthermore, strengthening peer support can improve adolescents’ quality of life through psychosocial support.47 Therefore, peer support is a core element of the model for strengthening health behavior change while improving adolescents’ quality of life.

Overall, social support from peers, teachers, and the community complements one another in creating an environment that fosters healthy lifestyle behaviors, health literacy, and adolescent well-being. Support from parents, peers, and teachers is differentially related to adolescents’ physical, psychological well-being, self-esteem50 and adolescent life satisfaction.51 These findings expand the stunting prevention approach by positioning social support as a component of a system connecting schools, families, peers, and the community to improve adolescents’ quality of life across the life course. Therefore, stunting prevention models need to integrate all sources of social support to support intergenerational stunting prevention while simultaneously improving adolescents’ quality of life.

Commitment from Related Institutions

These findings suggest that limited space for services and counseling limits institutional support for adolescent health services. The success of school health programs depends on school readiness, organizational support, availability of time, training for teaching staff, and integration of the program into the school system.52 The availability of integrated school health services, including health education, counseling, and preventive health programs delivered by trained school personnel, provides adolescents with greater opportunities to access comprehensive health support.53 Conversely, weak coordination between the education and health sectors reduces adolescents’ access to integrated health services and limits the effectiveness of school-based health promotion initiatives.15 Therefore, adequate facilities support the implementation of school health programs and increase adolescents’ opportunities to receive services that contribute to improving their quality of life.

This study also revealed that a lack of funding resulted in fewer health worker visits to schools. These findings align with research in Yogyakarta, which identified a lack of stakeholder commitment, limited human resources, and budget constraints as key obstacles to accelerating stunting reduction.18 Similarly, global evidence shows that successful health programs require strong political leadership, sustained funding, and intersectoral coordination.1 Furthermore, organizational support and long-term funding strategies are crucial to sustaining health programs.54 Therefore, sustainable institutions act as key drivers of intersectoral coordination and the sustainability of adolescent health services, ensuring that the benefits of interventions are maintained and that adolescent health and quality of life are improved.

This study also found a limited number of health workers involved in adolescent health services. This situation is similar to research in Indonesia, which identified a lack of human resources as a major obstacle to accelerating efforts to reduce stunting.55 Health service capacity increases not only through increasing the number of health workers but also through human resource management, needs-based workforce planning, task allocation, training, and strong institutional support.56 Adolescents can access health education, counseling, preventive care, and appropriate referrals to youth-friendly health services largely thanks to comprehensive school health services.53 Therefore, the success of adolescent health services is more influenced by the institution’s ability to efficiently manage health workers than simply increasing their number, ensuring smooth service delivery and a broader positive impact on adolescents’ quality of life.

Conceptually, the success of adolescent health services depends on the integration of service facilities, funding, human resources, and institutional governance. Global evidence shows that successful health program implementation requires cross-sectoral coordination, sustainable financing, adequate human resource capacity, and effective governance to ensure synergy between all system components.1,54 Strengthening institutional capacity, intersectoral coordination, and organizational support are essential for program implementation to be sustained.56 The findings of this study indicate that strengthening institutional commitment is not only achieved through budget increases or additional health workers but also requires strengthening governance through cross-sectoral coordination to establish, maintain, and direct the implementation of adolescent health programs.57

Furthermore, needs-based health workforce planning, strong human resource governance, and sustained institutional investment are essential components to support the implementation of stunting prevention programs.54,56 These findings indicate that institutional commitment is determined not only by the availability of budget or human resources, but also by the governance capacity to integrate policies, financing, and cross-sectoral coordination. An integrated institutional approach enables more consistent implementation of adolescent health services by strengthening collaboration between the health and education sectors.52 Comprehensive school health programs improve adolescents’ access to health education, counseling, preventive care, and appropriate referral systems.53 Thus, institutional commitment serves as a mechanism that integrates facilities, funding, human resources, and governance so that adolescent health services can be implemented consistently and sustainably to improve adolescents’ quality of life.

Overall, these results suggest that the recommended stunting prevention model should include institutional involvement as a key component. Institutional support helps increase adolescents’ access to health education, counseling, and preventive care, thereby strengthening the delivery of school health services.53 Maintaining integrated adolescent health services also depends on good management and communication between the health and education sectors.15 This finding expands the approach to stunting prevention from focusing on individual interventions to strengthening institutional systems as a life strategy for improving adolescents’ quality of life.

Governance

This study found that monitoring of adolescent health programs in schools was not conducted sustainably, indicating that monitoring and evaluation mechanisms were not integrated, making it difficult to identify implementation barriers and needs for improvement. Continuous monitoring and evaluation play a role in identifying implementation barriers, increasing compliance with implementation standards, and maintaining the quality of school health programs.58 This condition aligns with Hargreaves et al (2022), who emphasized that a monitoring system involving adolescents, families, schools, and local governments is a crucial component for building accountability and ensuring the effectiveness of adolescent health interventions.4

This study also showed that the lack of training on stunting prevention for teachers limits the role of schools in adolescent health education. This indicates that teacher capacity is a critical factor in the successful implementation of school-based health programs. Research by Jafari et al (2023) showed that teacher involvement in an iron supplementation program significantly increased student iron tablet consumption,26 while ongoing training improved implementation quality and maintained fidelity to school-based health programs.59 Furthermore, strengthening teacher capacity is a strategy consistently linked to improving the quality of school health program implementation.58 Therefore, teacher training not only improves individual competency but also strengthens institutional capacity to maintain school health programs.

This study found that cross-sectoral coordination is not yet optimal, resulting in program implementation remaining sectoral. Although local governments have made a serious commitment to prioritizing stunting, weak coordination and differing perspectives regarding who is responsible for the stunting program remain obstacles.18 Research in Indonesia shows that cross-sectoral coordination is also a crucial element in accelerating stunting reduction because it allows for alignment of program planning, implementation, and evaluation across institutions.55 These findings are supported by global evidence confirming that cross-sectoral coordination enables policy alignment, resource sharing, and integration of health, education, and community empowerment services, leading to more effective and sustainable interventions.57 Thus, cross-sector coordination serves as an integration mechanism that reduces fragmentation of stunting prevention services.

The findings of this study also indicate that adolescent health programs are not supported by consistently applied implementation standards, resulting in inconsistent program implementation quality. The success of school health programs requires consistent implementation standards, monitoring of program compliance, and ongoing evaluation to maintain implementation quality.58 Therefore, implementing implementation standards tailored to the local context is a crucial component in strengthening institutional governance.

Comprehensive school health services provide a multicomponent approach to meeting the diverse health needs of children and adolescents that is associated with several beneficial health and behavioral outcomes.60 These services provide the foundation for achieving a broader range of health outcomes. One relevant outcome is health-related quality of life (HRQoL), which reflects physical and psychological well-being, social relationships, relationships with parents and autonomy, as well as adolescents’ experiences in the school environment.61 Conceptually, continuous monitoring, strengthening teacher capacity, consistent implementation standards, and cross-sector coordination work together to build implementation governance that maintains the quality of adolescent health services throughout the life cycle.

Overall, the successful implementation of a stunting prevention program for adolescents is determined not only by the availability of health interventions, but also by a monitoring and evaluation system capable of maintaining the quality of program implementation on an ongoing basis.58 Strengthening teacher capacity and providing comprehensive school health services are essential components to support the implementation of promotive, preventive, and counseling services for adolescents.60 Furthermore, cross-sector coordination and strong institutional governance are necessary to build, maintain, and guide the implementation of adolescent health programs sustainably.57 The integration of these components forms an institutional governance that supports the sustainability of stunting prevention programs throughout the adolescent life cycle. These findings expand the current nutrition-intervention-focused approach to stunting prevention by demonstrating that strengthening implementation governance through continuous monitoring, teacher capacity building, consistent implementation standards, and cross-sector coordination is a core element of a life-course-based stunting prevention model for improving adolescent quality of life.

Quality of Health Services

This study found that the quality of iron supplements, particularly the strong odor and nausea side effects, was among the main barriers to adolescent adherence to iron supplementation. These findings suggest that the success of supplementation programs depends not only on tablet availability but also on adolescents’ acceptance of the product’s characteristics. These results align with research in Kallur, India, which showed that the odor and taste of iron tablets influenced adherence. Some participants also suggested alternative formulations, such as iron syrup, to improve adolescent acceptance.62 Increasing acceptance of supplementation has the potential to improve adherence to iron consumption, thus supporting anemia prevention, maintaining physical and cognitive capacity, and improving adolescents’ quality of life.

This study showed that side effects after taking iron supplements contributed to low adolescent adherence. These findings indicate that low adherence is influenced not only by the pharmacological characteristics of the supplements but also by adolescents’ lack of understanding of the correct method of administration. Research by Hidayanty et al (2025) showed that clear communication about the benefits of IFA and education on how to reduce side effects, such as taking supplements after meals, can improve adherence.63 Therefore, adequate education and support from healthcare professionals are crucial to improving adherence to supplementation. Therefore, increasing adherence to supplementation not only supports the success of anemia prevention programs but also has the potential to maintain physical health and psychological well-being, key dimensions of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in adolescents.

This study also found that incomplete documentation and reporting hampered the monitoring and evaluation of adolescent health programs. This finding aligns with research in Indonesia that reported limited documentation of iron supplementation and anemia data among adolescents,20 and research in Ethiopia that also showed that poor-quality routine health information systems (RHIS) limited program monitoring and evaluation, and the use of data for evidence-based decision-making.64 Implementing stunting prevention programs requires effective coordination, monitoring, and oversight to improve program implementation at various levels of government.55 The availability of accurate and consistently documented health data allows for the precise identification of adolescent health needs, thus supporting evidence-based decision-making and strengthening cross-sectoral coordination in adolescent health services.4 Therefore, strengthening health information systems improves the quality of program implementation and ensures that adolescents receive services that are targeted, sustainable, and responsive to their needs.

Conceptually, quality health services, built on quality supplementation, adequate education, and a reliable health information system, enable adolescents to receive more effective, sustainable, and tailored services. These quality health services support anemia prevention, maintain physical and psychological health, and improve adolescents’ quality of life, the primary goals of the life-course-based stunting prevention model.

Access to Health Services

This study shows that adolescents still face various barriers to accessing health services, including limited access to health information on stunting, difficulty reaching health care facilities due to geographic location, and suboptimal distribution of iron supplements. These findings indicate that adolescent health care systems in many low- and middle-income countries still face gaps in the provision of nutrition and health interventions throughout the life cycle,1 thus indicating the need to strengthen health care systems through a life-course approach to support adolescent health, improve population health, and build human capital.17

The results indicate that limited access to health information in schools has the potential to reduce adolescent health literacy, thus lowering opportunities for receiving promotive interventions. Access to health for adolescents requires integrated, continuous services that are responsive to developmental stages and involve various sectors throughout the life cycle.65 These findings indicate that the provision of health information in school environments is suboptimal, even though adolescents obtain health information from various sources, including schools, health workers, families, peers, mass media, and digital media. This is in line with Armstrong et al (2021), who showed that adolescents use various digital sources, including search engines, social media, and websites, to obtain information about health and healthy lifestyles.66 However, their effectiveness depends on adolescents’ ability to evaluate the credibility of the information obtained.67 Health literacy is associated with adolescents’ health-related quality of life through improved health self-management skills.68

This study also found that some adolescents experience difficulties accessing health services due to geographic barriers. This finding aligns with Stierman et al (2021), who identified geographic access as a key determinant of adolescents’ access to health services in low- and middle-income countries. Distance to health facilities, limited transportation, and infrastructure barriers cause delays in accessing services and reduce the sustainability of promotive and preventive services.69 Based on this study’s findings, geographic barriers that delay access to health services may worsen adolescents’ health outcomes because health problems are not addressed promptly.

The study’s findings indicate that iron supplementation distribution to adolescents is suboptimal, leading some to fail to consume the tablets regularly. This finding is consistent with a systematic review conducted by Silitonga et al (2023), which showed that low adherence to iron supplementation among adolescent girls is influenced by tablet availability.11 Furthermore, Heidkamp et al (2021) emphasized that although iron and folic acid supplementation guidelines are available in many countries, effective delivery platforms, particularly for adolescents, remain a challenge, resulting in suboptimal intervention coverage.1 Inadequate iron intake and stores increase the risk of iron deficiency and anemia.70 Low iron status and anemia in adolescents have been associated with impairments in several aspects of cognitive function and academic performance71 and a lower quality of life, particularly through impairments related to health conditions and daily functioning.72 If this occurs during the preconception period, the risk of impaired fetal growth and stunting in subsequent generations can increase.2

These access barriers have the potential to reduce adolescents’ opportunities to receive interventions during the preconception period, which is a crucial phase in the life-course approach to breaking the intergenerational cycle of stunting.2 The Lancet Series on Optimizing Child and Adolescent Health and Development emphasizes that health investments from preconception to age 20 are the foundation for human capital formation. A life-course approach that integrates health, nutrition, education, and service systems is needed to optimize the health and development of children and adolescents into adulthood.17 Unlike most previous studies that position access to health services as a factor influencing service utilization or adherence to nutrition interventions,1,17,69 this study demonstrates that access to health services is a systemic element that integrates health literacy, geographic access, promotive and preventive services, and the distribution of nutrition interventions into a single model of stunting prevention based on adolescent quality of life.

Overall, the results of this study indicate that access to health services supported by adequate health information, continuous promotive and preventive services, optimal distribution of nutrition interventions, and service affordability are critical components in improving adolescent quality of life. Therefore, access to health services is not only a determinant of adolescent health, but also a core element of the stunting prevention model being developed because it supports preconception health, improves adolescent quality of life, and has the potential to break the intergenerational cycle of stunting.

The Environment

The results of this study indicate that poor sanitation can increase the likelihood of infection and reduce the success of nutrition programs among adolescents. Poor sanitation makes it easier to get germs and increases the risk of diarrhea. In contrast, efforts to provide clean air, sanitation, and hygiene can reduce the likelihood of diarrhea in children in low- and middle-income countries.73 Exposure to germs and environmental conditions can impact nutritional status, so nutrition programs need to be synergized with efforts to improve the availability of clean air, sanitation, and hygiene. However, the level of impact of WASH interventions on nutritional status can vary by region.74 Evidence in malnourished children suggests that exposure to enteric bacteria can be associated with enteric gut dysfunction (EED), a condition characterized by inflammation and damage to the structure and function of the intestinal lining.75 In this population, EED and a high burden of enteric infections are associated with growth problems.76 Although most findings on EED come from pediatric populations, this evidence suggests that improving sanitation remains crucial during adolescence to minimize exposure to infection and support the effectiveness of nutrition programs before the pre-pregnancy phase. A study in the Philippines revealed that a lack of sanitation facilities in schools, such as adequate clean water, toilet maintenance, hygiene education, and suboptimal handwashing practices, is associated with a high incidence of diarrhea.77 In addition to increasing the risk of infection, limited WASH facilities can contribute to frequent student absences and disrupt learning due to hygiene-related health issues.78 These results support the study’s conclusion that schools are a critical setting for implementing WASH interventions. In Indragiri Hilir Regency, the geographical conditions of water areas and settlements spread across many islands pose a risk of hindering the development of sanitation facilities, clean water provision, and access to environmental health services. Therefore, a WASH strategy tailored to the region’s specific characteristics is required.

For WASH interventions to be successful, an integrated approach is required, encompassing behavior change, effective program implementation, and collaboration across multiple sectors to ensure lasting benefits.73,74 Unlike many previous studies that viewed sanitation as a risk factor for infection or malnutrition in children, this study demonstrates that sanitation is an environmental element that supports the effectiveness of nutrition interventions, preconception health, and adolescent quality of life within a lifecycle-focused stunting prevention framework.

This study identified the role of early marriage norms, dietary patterns, and access to health services in stunting prevention. The study revealed that some communities still consider married adolescent girls to be no longer dependent on their parents. Early marriage increases the likelihood of pregnancy when a girl’s biological development is not yet complete, thus increasing maternal nutritional vulnerability.5 Children born to adolescent mothers are also at higher risk of being underweight, with maternal nutritional status being a possible intermediary pathway.79 In addition to heightened health risks, early marriage is also associated with reduced female empowerment and worsening mental health, including increased depressive symptoms and suicide threats.80 Findings from Yogyakarta suggest that early marriage does not act as a stand-alone risk factor for stunting but interacts with education, socioeconomic conditions, and feeding practices, increasing vulnerability to malnutrition.18 Based on these findings, regulatory enforcement and premarital education need to be integrated with the promotion of nutrition, reproductive health, and anemia prevention through schools, community health centers, and premarital services so that interventions can begin before the first pregnancy. Preventing child marriage requires actions that not only focus on individual adolescent girls but also change the social and gender norms that perpetuate the practice of child marriage through family and community involvement.81

Culture is also reflected in dietary patterns, which can influence adolescents’ nutritional status. The habit of drinking tea with meals has been shown to reduce bioavailable iron in women with and without iron deficiency anemia.82 Conversely, nutrition education can encourage the consumption of fruits or drinks rich in vitamin C along with iron-rich foods. Recent research shows that consuming ascorbate-rich fruits along with foods can increase hemoglobin and ferritin, although the effect on iron absorption is not always consistent.83

Refusal of immunization due to religious beliefs indicates that social norms, beliefs, and health literacy influence immunization decisions. Vaccine hesitancy among adolescents is linked to a lack of knowledge about vaccine-preventable diseases, low trust in vaccines, and minimal adolescent participation in the decision-making process.84 Therefore, communication about immunization must be delivered with religious values in mind, involving religious leaders and community leaders as trusted sources of information to increase immunization acceptance.

The findings of this study indicate that culture not only influences individual actions but also shapes social norms that influence decisions related to reproductive health, nutritional practices, and the use of health services. Combining changes in social norms with health interventions throughout the life cycle forms the basis for a stunting prevention model designed to strengthen preconception health and improve adolescents’ quality of life. Overall, the conclusions of this study indicate that sanitation and culture are complementary environmental factors in efforts to prevent stunting in adolescents. Unlike most previous studies that consider sanitation and culture as separate factors, this study shows that these two aspects interact through changes in social norms, health habits, and the physical environment to create a stunting prevention model that focuses on adolescents’ quality of life. The model produced in this study emphasizes that changes in social norms must go hand in hand with improvements in the physical environment through the participation of families, schools, medical personnel, religious leaders, community leaders, and local governments so that stunting prevention can be carried out continuously throughout the life cycle.

Although quality of life was not directly measured in this study, the proposed model may contribute to adolescents’ quality of life through several interconnected pathways supported by previous evidence. Healthy dietary practices and physical activity are associated with better health-related quality of life among adolescents.9 Moreover, adolescent quality of life encompasses broader dimensions of subjective well-being, supporting the importance of considering well-being beyond conventional clinical outcomes.7 Supportive relationships with parents, peers, and teachers are associated with different dimensions of adolescent well-being, including physical and psychological well-being and self-worth.50 At the institutional level, stronger integration between health and education systems may create more supportive environments for adolescent health and well-being,15 while strengthening school health services may improve adolescents’ access to health education, preventive services, counselling, and appropriate referral pathways.53 Collectively, these interconnected pathways provide a conceptual basis through which the proposed model may support adolescents’ current well-being while strengthening their health trajectories into adulthood and the health of future generations, consistent with a life-course perspective on adolescent nutrition.5

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several limitations. Because the study was conducted only in Tembilahan, the transferability of the findings to other Indonesian settings should be considered cautiously, as contextual differences may influence adolescent stunting-prevention needs. Given the qualitative design, the findings are intended to provide contextual and in-depth understanding rather than estimate prevalence or establish causal relationships. In addition, adolescent quality of life was not directly measured; therefore, its relationship with the identified core elements should be interpreted as a conceptual, evidence-informed pathway rather than an observed effect. Finally, the proposed model requires further validation and empirical testing across diverse populations and settings before its effectiveness in improving adolescent quality of life and reducing intergenerational stunting risk can be determined.

Conclusions

This study identified eight interconnected core elements of adolescent-centered stunting prevention: adolescent factors, family factors, social support, institutional commitment, governance, quality of health services, access to health services, and environmental factors. The findings indicate that stunting prevention during adolescence extends beyond nutrition-specific interventions and requires coordinated action across individual, family, school, community, health-system, and policy levels. Integrating these elements within a multisectoral and life-course framework may provide a practical basis for strengthening adolescent health and well-being while addressing preconception risks associated with the intergenerational cycle of stunting. In practice, the proposed framework can inform the development of coordinated adolescent health programs involving schools, primary health-care services, families, communities, and local policymakers. As quality of life was not directly measured, further research is needed to validate the proposed model and evaluate its effects on adolescent quality of life and subsequent health outcomes.
