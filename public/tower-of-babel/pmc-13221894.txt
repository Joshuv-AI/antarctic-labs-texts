Widefield Analysis of Choroidal Vascular Characteristics in Central Serous Chorioretinopathy With Different Choroidal Vein Drainage System
Authors: Xinlei Hao, Jiaxin Pu, Miaoling Li, Yuying Ji, Lan Mi, Xuenan Zhuang, Guiqin He, Yongyue Su, Feng Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore the choroidal vasculature changes in central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC) with symmetric and asymmetric choroidal venous drainage system using widefield optical coherence tomography angiography (WF-OCTA).MethodsIn this retrospective study, choroidal characteristics were evaluated in CSC eyes through WF-OCTA. Eyes were classified based on choroidal venous drainage patterns: symmetric, one dominant vortex vein (DVV), and two DVV patterns. The location of DVVs was mapped to four quadrants: superotemporal, inferotemporal, superonasal, and inferonasal. Quantitative parameters including choroidal thickness (CT), choroidal volume (CV), choroidal vascularity index, and choriocapillaris flow density (CCFD) were analyzed both globally and within each quadrant.ResultsOf 187 eyes from 146 patients, 43 eyes (22.99%) showed symmetric and 144 eyes (77.00%) demonstrated asymmetric choroidal venous drainage patterns. In the asymmetric cases, 78 eyes (41.71%) exhibited one-DVV and 66 eyes (35.29%) showed two-DVV patterns. One-DVV cases demonstrated significantly increased CT and CV, but decreased CCFD in DVV-related quadrants compared to symmetric cases (P < 0.05 for all), except for CCFD in cases with inferonasal-located DVV (P = 0.110). Moreover, when DVVs were located in superotemporal or superonasal quadrants, a significant reduction in overall CCFD was also observed (P = 0.010 and P = 0.021, respectively). In contrast, two-DVV cases exhibited predominant choroidal vascular alterations in the quadrants near the DVV locations. Overall CCFD was significantly lower in one-DVV cases (46.94% ± 1.30%) compared to two-DVV cases (47.92% ± 1.70%, P < 0.001).ConclusionsCSC eyes with asymmetric venous drainage demonstrated more significant choroidal alterations than symmetric cases, particularly when DVVs were located in superior quadrants. In contrast to one-DVV cases, two-DVV patterns showed more balanced choroidal circulation, likely through compensatory intervortex anastomoses.Translational RelevanceThis study investigates the relationship between choroidal parameters and varying patterns of preferential choroidal venous drainage in CSC eyes, showing that CSC eyes with asymmetric pattern demonstrated more significant choroidal alterations than symmetric cases.

Introduction

Central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC) is a sight-threatening macular disorder characterized by serous retinal detachment (SRD) and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) abnormalities.1 Recent advances in multimodal imaging have revealed that CSC belongs to the pachychoroid spectrum disorders, a group of entities sharing common choroidal features including increased choroidal thickness (CT) and dilated outer choroidal vessels.2 The pathogenesis of CSC primarily involves choroidal vascular alterations, with characteristic abnormalities including choroidal vascular hyperpermeability (CVH), delayed choriocapillaris filling, and congested vortex vein ampulla.3–5 Notably, areas of CVH with abnormal choroid serve as therapeutic targets for half-dose photodynamic therapy, which remains the standard treatment for chronic CSC.5,6 Therefore elucidating the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of CSC is crucial for understanding disease progression and optimizing therapeutic strategies.

According to published literature, distinct patterns of choroidal venous drainage have been identified in both healthy volunteers and CSC subjects: symmetric and asymmetric patterns.7–9 Among asymmetric cases, eyes can be further categorized into multiple modes based on the location of the dominant vortex vein (DVV) system.10,11 Ultra-widefield indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) studies have demonstrated that imbalanced choroidal venous drainage with specific congested vortex vein may contribute to choroidal venous insufficiency in CSC eyes.12,13 This venous congestion triggers compensatory mechanisms within the choroidal vasculature. The emergence of intervortex venous anastomoses connecting independent vortex vein systems represents a critical biomarker of CSC, serving as collateral vessels to compensate for imbalanced choroidal venous drainage.10,14

High penetrating swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) have enabled clinicians to acquire the full-thickness choroid imaging and quantitatively evaluate the vasculature components.4,15–17 Using this technology, prior studies showed that CSC eyes had thicker choroid compared to healthy eyes and fellow eyes both in the posterior pole and the periphery.18 Luo et al.19 have reported that the superotemporal quadrant system exhibits the greatest CT and choroidal vascular volume (CVV) among the four drainage quadrants, suggesting that this quadrant serves as the predominant choroidal drainage pathway in CSC. Understanding this anatomical characteristic may provide new insights for therapeutic targeting.

To date, there are no studies analyzing the choroidal vasculature characteristics of CSC with different choroidal venous drainage system, which could provide crucial insights into the pathogenesis of CSC. Hence, the objective of this study is to explore the choroidal vasculature changes in CSC with different drainage patterns and to understand how asymmetric venous drainage affects choroidal architecture using widefield optical coherence tomography angiography (WF-OCTA).

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective study enrolled participants diagnosed with CSC from September 2022 to November 2024 in the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center. The study was conducted in compliance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (2023KYPJ114). All participants provided written informed consent before any examinations.

The diagnosis of CSC was based on multimodal imaging-based classification introduced by Central Serous Chorioretinopathy International Group.1 The major criteria include: (1) current or previous evidence of SRD in the posterior pole on optical coherence tomography (OCT); (2) more than one area of RPE alteration on fundus autofluorecence/OCT/infrared imaging. Minor criteria include (1) placoid hyperfluorescence lesion on mid-phase widefield ICGA (WF-ICGA); (2) more than one focal RPE leakage on fluorescein angiography; (3) subfoveal choroidal thickness exceeding 400 µm. The diagnosis of CSC was made when two major criteria and at least one minor criterion were met. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) secondary macular neovascularization (MNV) detected by OCT/WF-OCTA; (2) prior treatment of photodynamic therapy or vitreoretinal surgery; (3) the spherical equivalent <−6.0 diopters or >3.0 diopters; (4) concomitant with other ocular comorbidities or severe systemic abnormalities; (5) any conditions affecting the quality of images (WF-OCTA images with quality score <7).

All enrolled participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (Topcon CT-1, Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), anterior segment slit-lamp examination, ocular fundus ophthalmoscopy, color fundus photography (Clarus 500, Zeiss, Germany or aTRC-50C, Topcon, Japan), OCT (Spectralis OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), WF-OCTA (TowardPi BM400K BMizar; TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China), fluorescein angiography and WF-ICGA (Spectralis HRA, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). BCVA was measured using a Snellen chart and converted into logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) for statistical analysis. Baseline demographics and multimodal imaging were collected.

Groupings

The grouping criteria were primarily established on widefield imaging using the fovea and optic disc center as landmarks.11 An asymmetric pattern was defined by the presence of DVV systems, which was determined when the terminal of temporal choroidal vein projected beyond the fovea or the terminal of nasal choroidal vein projected beyond the optic disc center (Figs. 1, 2).11 In a distinctive scenario, a DVV was identified when the termination of a temporal choroidal vein extended nasally across the optic disc center (Fig. 2A). Conversely, the absence of such findings indicated no DVV system and thus a symmetric pattern. Additionally, the demarcation of watershed zones between quadrants served as an auxiliary criterion for identifying DVVs, particularly in cases with extensive intervortex anastomoses (Figs. 1, 2).17 Although focal intervortex anastomoses does not compromise terminal vessels identification, extensive anastomoses obscure these boundaries, necessitating watershed zone delineation based on the inflection points and altered curvature of the anastomotic vessels.20 A DVV was considered present if the watershed zone demonstrated tortuosity, and absent if the watershed zone remained relatively smooth (Fig. 1A).

When a DVV traversed the reference point and spanned two quadrants, the quadrant containing the ampulla was considered the vortex vein's definitive quadrant. Depending on the number of DVV, the asymmetric pattern could be further divided into one DVV group (Figs. 1B–E) and two DVV group (Fig. 2). Furthermore, these groups were subdivided according to the anatomical location of the DVVs. The diagnosis and grouping were confirmed by two experienced investigators independently (X.H. and J.P.), and a third retina specialist (F.W.) made the final determination in cases of disagreement.

Measurements and Parameters

Widefield choroidal images were acquired using a SS-OCTA system equipped with a vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (1060 nm wavelength). The system features a transverse resolution of 10 µm, an axial resolution of 3.8 µm, and an acquisition speed of 400,000 A-scans per second. Images covering a 24 × 20 mm2 field of view (120°) were obtained using a raster scan protocol of 1536 × 1280 B-scans centered on the fovea, with each scan performed twice.15 All examinations were conducted by a single experienced investigator (X.H.).

After image acquisition, the machine could automatically identify the structure of each layer of the retina and choroid with the help of built-in artificial intelligence algorithms. If necessary, a manual segmentation correction was subsequently conducted (X.H. and J.P.). The upper and lower interfaces of the choroidal layer were Bruch membrane and choroid-scleral interface. The choriocapillaris slab was recognized as the layer between the Bruch membrane and 29 µm beneath the Bruch membrane. The choroidal medium-to-large vessel layer was recognized as the layer between 29 µm beneath the Bruch membrane and the choroid-scleral interface.

An artificial intelligence algorithm was applied to delineate capillary networks and medium-to-large vessels, enabling three-dimensional reconstruction and quantitative analysis of choroidal vasculature.4,21 The vascular-stromal structure could be identified on the B-scans, with hypo-reflective areas representing the vascular component and moderate-to-hyperreflective areas representing the stroma component. A comprehensive analysis was performed to evaluate multiple choroidal parameters, including CT, choroidal volume (CV), choroidal vascularity index (CVI), and choriocapillaris flow density (CCFD). CT was regarded as the perpendicular distance between the Bruch membrane (outer boundary of hyper-reflective RPE) and the choroid-scleral interface.22 CV was calculated as the sum of CVV and choroidal stromal volume. CVI was computed as the proportion of CVV to the total CV, with a higher CVI value indicating prominent choroidal vasodilation.4,19,22,23 CCFD was quantified as the ratio of blood flow signal-positive pixel area to the total delimited scan area, based on the flow signal detected by SS-OCTA.15,24

In the current study, four choroidal quantitative parameters were analyzed using two complementary approaches: (1) global measurements of the entire choroid, and (2) quadrant-specific measurements dividing the choroid into superotemporal, inferotemporal, superonasal, and inferonasal regions, as illustrated in Figure 3.13

Statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 26.0; IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive variables were represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and numbers with frequencies. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to validate the normality assumption for continuous variables. Independent sample t-test and one-way ANOVA were employed to evaluate the differences of continuous variables, such as CT, CV, CVI and CCFD. Following significant one-way ANOVA results, post-hoc analyses were performed using Tamhane's T2 test for unequal variances and Holm-Bonferroni test for equal variances. A χ2 test was used to analyze the categorical variables. Agreement between two investigators was assessed using the kappa statistic in terms of DVV-based grouping. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significance.

Results

Basic Information and Clinical Manifestations

This retrospective observational study included 187 eyes from 146 patients with CSC. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The average age (± SD) of the included patients was 48.25 ± 9.61 years, of which 116 were male, accounting for 79.45%. Of the 187 eyes included, 88 (47.06%) were right eyes. The mean baseline BCVA was 0.29 ± 0.32 LogMAR, and the mean duration from symptom onset to initial presentation to our hospital was 21.39 ± 29.88 months.

Of the 187 included eyes, 43 eyes (22.99%) showed symmetric and 144 eyes (77.00%) had asymmetric choroidal venous drainage patterns. In the asymmetric cases, 78 eyes (41.71%) exhibited one DVV and 66 eyes (35.29%) had two DVVs. The one DVV cases were distributed as follows: superotemporal in 28 eyes (14.97%), inferotemporal in 22 eyes (11.76%), superonasal in 22 eyes (11.76%), and inferonasal in six eyes (3.21%). In cases with two DVVs, the distribution patterns were: superotemporal + inferotemporal in two eyes (1.07%), superotemporal+superonasal in 23 eyes (12.30%), superotemporal + inferonasal in five eyes (2.67%), inferotemporal + superonasal in 25 eyes (13.37%), and inferotemporal + inferonasal in 11 eyes (5.88%). Kappa coefficient analysis demonstrated strong agreement between two investigators concerning DVV drainage pattern (κ = 0.870, P < 0.001). Of note, one eye (0.53%) demonstrated a posterior vortex vein exiting from the eyeball around the optic disc.

Comparison of Choroidal Parameters Among CSC Eyes With Symmetric, One DVV, and Two DVV Drainage Patterns

Table 2 and Figure 4 presented a comparative analysis of choroidal quantitative parameters among three groups: eyes presenting symmetric pattern, one DVV and two DVVs. No significant difference was observed in terms of overall and regional CT among three groups (Fig. 4A). Cases with one DVV showed significantly larger overall CV than cases with two DVVs (one DVV group: 112.47 ± 20.14 mm3, two DVVs group: 104.64 ± 17.88 mm3, P = 0.046; Fig. 4B). Compared to the other two groups, cases with two DVVs group had significantly lower value of regional CV in the superotemporal quadrant (two DVVs group vs. symmetry group: P = 0.039; two DVVs group vs. one DVV group: P < 0.001, respectively). Similar differences were observed regarding regional CVI in the superotemporal quadrant both between symmetry and two DVVs group (P = 0.030), and between one and two DVVs group (P = 0.049; Fig. 4C). Moreover, in the cases with one DVV group, the average overall CCFD (±SD) was 46.94% ± 1.30%, significantly lower than the two DVVs group (47.92% ± 1.70%, P < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 4D). Notably, there were significant differences in the regional CCFD between one and two DVVs groups in the inferotemporal (P = 0.041) and superonasal (P = 0.007) quadrants, respectively.

Besides, significant differences in CT, CV, CVI, and CCFD were observed among the four quadrants in both the overall cohort and within each subgroup (symmetry, one DVV, and two DVVs groups; P < 0.05 for all). These parameters demonstrated consistent spatial distribution patterns. For CT, CV, and CVI, values were highest in the superotemporal quadrant and progressively decreased in the order of superotemporal > superonasal > inferotemporal > inferonasal, with the lowest values in the inferonasal quadrant, except for CVI in overall cohort and two DVV groups where the values of superonasal quadrant were greater than those of superotemporal quadrant. Interestingly, CCFD showed an inverse spatial gradient in general, with maximum values in the inferonasal quadrant.

Supplementary Table S1 displayed the baseline characteristics of the three groups, while subgroup analyses for the acute (≤6 months) and chronic (>6 months) CSC cohorts were presented in Supplementary Table S2. Partial subgroup analysis revealed that chronic CSC tended to have thinner CT, lower CVI and higher CCFD (P < 0.05).

Quadrant-Specific Analysis of Choroidal Parameters in CSC Eyes With One DVV and Two DVV Patterns

Table 3 compares choroidal parameters between symmetric cases and one DVV patterns with DVVs located in different quadrants, as visualized in Figure 5. The regional CT and CV were significantly greater in quadrants containing DVVs compared to corresponding quadrants in the symmetry group (P < 0.05 for all; Figs. 5A, 5B). Specifically, increased CT was observed in DVV-related quadrants across all locations (Fig. 5A): superotemporal (P = 0.007), inferotemporal (P = 0.042), superonasal (P = 0.031), and inferonasal (P = 0.022). A similar pattern was observed for CV (Fig. 5B), with significant increases in the superotemporal (P < 0.001), inferotemporal (P = 0.016), superonasal (P = 0.032), and inferonasal (P = 0.010) quadrants. Interestingly, in cases with superotemporal-located DVV, the inferotemporal quadrant showed paradoxically reduced CT and CV compared to the symmetry group (P = 0.013 and P = 0.004, respectively). Interestingly, CCFD demonstrated an inverse relationship. It was significantly decreased in DVV-related quadrants, except for the inferonasal quadrant (P = 0.110), compared to corresponding quadrants in the symmetry group (Fig. 5D): superotemporal (P = 0.017), inferotemporal (P = 0.022), and superonasal (P = 0.001). Moreover, cases with DVVs in superotemporal and superonasal quadrants showed reduced overall CCFD (P = 0.010 and P = 0.021, respectively). Of note, in cases with superotemporal-located DVV, a significant reduction in CCFD was also observed in the diagonally opposite quadrant (inferonasal quadrant) (P = 0.020). However, CVI showed no significant differences between one DVV and symmetric cases, either in overall measurements or quadrant-specific analyses (P > 0.05 for all; Fig. 5C).

Discussion

In this study, we found that the majority of CSC eyes (77.00%) exhibited asymmetric choroidal venous drainage patterns, with preferential drainage routes of choroidal venous distributed across different quadrants. Compared to symmetrical eyes, the asymmetrical cases are more likely to cause overall or regional choroidal structural changes, and the extent and degree of damage varies when the DVV located in different quadrants. In one-DVV cases, significant choroidal structural alterations were primarily observed in quadrants containing DVVs, characterized by increased CT and CV, accompanied by a concurrent reduction in CCFD. In contrast, eyes with two-DVV patterns demonstrated predominant choroidal alterations in the quadrants near the DVV locations. Additionally, we found that the overall CCFD value was significantly lower in the one-DVV cases, mainly in cases with superotemporal-located and superonasal-located DVV. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation exploring the influence of different choroidal vein drainage system on choroidal vascular characteristics in CSC eyes, and the finding provided important insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CSC.

The structural and functional alterations in choroidal vasculature and their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms in CSC have garnered significant attention in recent years.12,19 Through the application of widefield SS-OCTA, we were able to comprehensively evaluate the number and distribution of DVVs, as demonstrated by our team's previous findings.17 Using this technology, we observed the universality of asymmetric choroidal venous drainage patterns,17 which aligns with previous studies that have documented various choroidal venous drainage patterns in both healthy subjects and CSC cohorts.7,8,11,25,26 In addition to the visualization of DVV, this noninvasive imaging modality also demonstrates advantages in the quantitative assessment of choroidal vascular characteristics,4,9,15 with reproducibility and accuracy that have been widely validated in prior studies on chorioretinal diseases.27,28

Previous studies have established various three-dimensional choroidal biomarkers as reliable indicators for evaluating choroidal vasculature alterations in CSC.4 These alterations manifest both structurally and spatially. Structurally, CT serves as a crucial indicator for disease progression to chronicity, with Ambiya and colleagues29 demonstrating that CSC eyes with subfoveal choroidal thickness ≤ 356 µm typically represent chronic cases requiring intervention rather than observation. Spatially, as reported by Funatsu et al.,18 choroidal thickening extends beyond the posterior pole to peripheral areas in CSC eyes when compared with healthy eyes.15 Our findings further elaborate on these spatial patterns in relation to vortex vein distribution. In one-DVV cases, we observed increased regional CT and CV specifically in DVV-related quadrants compared to symmetric cases (Table 3). This localized thickening aligns with our previous research, which demonstrated predominant distribution of CVH areas within DVV drainage territories among pachychoroid spectrum disorders.11 Experimental evidence supports these clinical observations, as Chen et al. demonstrated that induced vortex vein occlusion in cynomolgus monkeys led to early-phase choroidal thickening specifically in the territory of the occluded veins.30

Interestingly, in CSC eyes with two DVVs, we found no significant increases CT and CV in the DVV-related quadrants (Table 4). This difference might be explained by more efficient blood flow redistribution through two DVV systems, where pathologically developed intervortex venous anastomoses provide compensatory pathways, preventing significant venous stasis in any single quadrant. The significantly higher overall CV and lower overall CCFD in one-DVV compared to two-DVV cases serves as another finding which supports this hypothesis. This result suggests that greater blood accumulated in the choroidal vasculature in one-DVV eyes, which could induce an increase in overall choroidal thickness and potential mechanical damage to the choriocapillaris. In contrast, in the two-DVV groups, we did not observe these choroidal changes in the DVV-related quadrants, but rather in neighboring quadrants. This phenomenon may be explained by the presence of intervortex venous anastomoses in CSC which connect independent vortex vein system and facilitate choroidal blood transfer between them. We hypothesized that as the number of DVVs increased, there was greater potential for the formation of pathological anastomoses. Whether these intervortex venous anastomoses occur more frequently in cases with more DVVs remains to be elucidated in our ongoing investigation. It is plausible that the two-DVV pattern contributes to a more balanced choroidal venous circulation through compensatory redistribution via intervortex anastomoses. However, given that the presence and extent of such anastomoses were not directly evaluated in the present study, this interpretation remains hypothetical. Future investigations incorporating direct assessment of intervortex collateral networks are warranted to validate this proposed mechanism.

CVI, a noteworthy feature of choroidal vascularity, has been popularly applied in monitoring disease progression and assessing prognostication.22 In previous studies, the measurements obtained from traditional two-dimensional imaging and manual depictions were relatively subjective. Thus three-dimensional choroidal reconstruction could provide a more accurate representation of choroidal characteristics. Previous studies by Luo et al.19 demonstrated elevated CVI across all drainage quadrants in CSC eyes compared to healthy controls, indicating widespread choroidal vasodilation. Notably, the superotemporal quadrant exhibited both the thickest choroidal layer and highest CVI among the four quadrants, establishing its role as the preferred drainage system both in healthy individuals and in CSC.19,26 In our study, we observed significant differences in regional CV and CVI values specifically in the superotemporal quadrant among CSC cases with symmetric, one-DVV, and two-DVV patterns (Table 2). These findings suggest that choroidal vasodilation varies predominantly in the superotemporal quadrant across different venous drainage patterns, indicating that the anatomical distribution of DVV significantly influences regional choroidal remodeling. Given that the superotemporal quadrant serves as the primary drainage territory, structural alterations in this region may be the most sensitive and earliest detectable indicators of choroidal changes. The extent of superotemporal quadrant remodeling could potentially reflect disease progression and severity in CSC, although the precise relationship between superotemporal quadrant alterations and CSC characteristics warrants further investigation.

The choriocapillaris consists of wide-lumen sinusoidal vessels, enabling choroidal blood flow rates 20-fold higher than retinal circulation.31 Because of the absence of a tissue-capillary complex, the choriocapillaris is directly compressed between the engorged vortex venules and short posterior ciliary arterial branches.9 When vortex vein occlusion occurs, the resultant increase in hydrostatic pressure may cause significant choriocapillaris damage through enhanced fluid extravasation across the fenestrated endothelium of the inner vessel wall.9 Kishi et al.32 observed that choriocapillaris filling delay regions colocalized with the dilated vortex vein areas in acute CSC using ICGA, indicating the relationship between venous dilation and capillary damage.

In our research, we found that CSC eyes with one-DVV showed lower overall CCFD compared to symmetric cases despite the absence of statistical significance. This decrease was mainly attributed to cases with superotemporal-located and superonasal-located DVV. When the DVV was located in the superotemporal or superonasal quadrant, the reduction in CCFD value was observed not only in the DVV-related quadrant but also in the overall CCFD measurement. This may be related to the anatomical differences of the various venous drainage patterns. The superior drainage path overcomes gravity and travels upwards through the scleral outlet, which encounter high outflow resistance and severe vortex vein congestion, resulting in damage to choriocapillaris layer. Earlier studies have demonstrated that upper-dominant type of vortex veins dilation patterns had more severe macular choroidal thickening compared with symmetrical and lower-dominant type.18 However, the CT is mainly composed of Haller's vessels, so their study also proved that the excessive blood flow accumulating in the macular in the upper dominant type, consistent with our speculation.12,18 Previous studies have demonstrated that choroidal blood flow hypoperfusion may be associated with secondary MNV in the late stage of CSC.33 Understanding the relationship between choroidal drainage patterns and choriocapillaris perfusion is therefore crucial for predicting MNV development. Hence, in clinical practice, we should closely follow up CSC patients whose DVVs are located in the superior quadrants and timely intervene once complications such as MNV are observed.

There are several limitations of our study. First, owing to variations in the topographic distribution of DVVs, the sample sizes across different subgroups were heterogeneous, particularly for cases with inferonasal and superotemporal + inferonasal drainage systems, which might have limited our statistical power. Second, several confounding factors, including smoking history, alcohol consumption, sleep disorders, etc., were not incorporated, which might influence choroidal structural parameters. Third, we have to acknowledge that quadrant-based segmentation serves as an approximation rather than an exact representation of venous drainage territories. Forth, CCFD measurements obtained by WF-OCTA may be susceptible to signal attenuation or shadowing artifacts, particularly in eyes with SRD. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between choroidal parameters and varying patterns of preferential choroidal venous drainage in CSC eyes, providing novel insights into choroidal vascular pathophysiology.

Conclusions

Our study reveals distinct patterns of choroidal alterations in CSC eyes with different venous drainage system. In one-DVV cases, structural changes were predominantly observed in DVV-related quadrants, characterized by increased CT and CV with concurrent CCFD reduction. In contrast, two-DVV cases exhibited structural changes primarily in neighboring quadrants, suggesting different mechanisms of choroidal remodeling. Besides, when DVVs were located in superior quadrants, the decrease in CCFD occurs not only in the DVV-related quadrant, but also the entire choroid. These findings provide new insights into the relationship between choroidal venous drainage patterns and structural alterations in CSC, which may have implications for disease management and prognosis.
