Decolonial science in the Amazon demands active and equitable seats at the table
Authors: Deliane Penha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScientific collaborations in the Amazon often reproduce historical and structural inequalities that limit local leadership in knowledge production. Although funding and international attention have increased, local researchers are often confined to data collection while external actors define research priorities and receive intellectual recognition. Based on my trajectory as an Amazonian scientist, I argue that decolonial science requires structural transformation, shared decision-making, equitable recognition, and sustained investment in local scientific leadership, rather than symbolic inclusion.Subject terms: Ecology; Ecology; Science, technology and society; Scientific community

The Amazon at the center, yet peripheral in scientific authority

The climate crisis and rapid biodiversity loss have placed the Amazon at the center of global scientific attention. Governments, funding agencies, and research institutions now look to the region for answers. Funding has increased and large national and international projects have expanded across the world’s largest tropical forest. However, many initiatives reproduce old hierarchies under a new language. While institutions and agencies outside the region expand investment and cooperation, they also illustrate how agenda-setting power and scientific priorities often remain concentrated beyond the Amazon itself.

When scientific collaborations include local researchers, they often do so in the name of “capacity building”. In practice, local scientists and students are frequently assigned to data collection and field logistics. Data are essential to science, but they represent only a small part of it. Scientific leadership involves more than collecting data. It includes setting research agendas, defining key questions and conceptual frameworks, formulating hypotheses, interpreting results, and shaping the scientific narrative in peer-reviewed publications. When local researchers are excluded from these stages and confined to data collection, they remain indispensable but invisible as data collection is often treated as a purely operational task with little intellectual recognition.

This model of scientific collaboration and training limits our ability to assume leadership roles in research projects, perpetuates historical inequalities, and restricts the professional growth of local researchers. In short, this model reflects scientific colonialism. Unfortunately, the academic environment in which many Amazonian scientists work often treats this colonial modus operandi as normal, without recognizing that such practices reflect an ongoing structure shaping research in the region.

Brazil has regulatory frameworks that govern access to genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge (Biodiversity Law No. 13,123/2015)1, as well as research permits2,3. However, these requirements apply mainly to research conducted within federal conservation units. Studies carried out outside these protected areas—across large portions of the Amazon— often do not require formal authorization. Nor do they impose obligations regarding team composition or collaboration with scientists based in the Amazon. As a result, scientific extraction can occur entirely within national borders, reproducing colonial logics at the domestic level rather than the international one.

While a regulatory framework exists, it focuses mainly on biological material and access permits, without addressing how researchers distribute scientific authority, interpret data, assign authorship, or share leadership. The lack of policies that promote equity within the national scientific system helps explain why long-standing patterns of data extraction, limited local leadership, and unequal recognition persist.

For years, I believed that being a scientist in the Amazon meant collecting data with efficiency, rigor, and endurance. I took pride in facing the forest’s challenges—long field campaigns in remote and harsh conditions—and returning with data that few researchers have the opportunity to collect. My ability to manage complex field logistics reinforced this perception. Access to research sites in the Amazon often depends on detailed local knowledge. Researchers must navigate rivers, open trails, and understand bureaucratic and legal procedures. Because I grew up in the region, I knew people, routes, and institutions that made fieldwork possible. These skills made me feel essential to science, even though my role was largely limited to supporting others’ research.

This certainty began to fade during my Ph.D., when I met collaborators who showed genuine interest in my research questions, hypotheses, and interpretations. For the first time, I received an invitation to co-author a paper4 based on hydraulic data that I had collected. What surprised me was the discussion that followed: my data were treated as intellectual contributions, and my perspectives helped shape the scientific narrative. I realized how rare this experience was. Although I had taken part in many expeditions that resulted in publications, I was rarely invited to develop ideas or analyses. What I had accepted as my role in the scientific community revealed itself as a structural exclusion.

This pattern extends beyond individuals; therefore, it is a call to confront scientific colonialism from an insider’s perspective openly and to examine how we reproduce it within our own institutions.

The Tapajós case: a local portrait of scientific colonialism in the Amazon

The Tapajós National Forest is a conservation unit that offers relatively easy access and established infrastructure, including lodging, electricity, internet access, and trail networks that support research. The presence of the Federal University of Western Pará (UFOPA) has also turned the region into a research hub, attracting scientists from other parts of Brazil and abroad.

Throughout my academic career - from undergraduate studies to professor at UFOPA - I have observed remarkable hospitality toward visiting researchers from the South and Southeast of Brazil and from the Global North. We rolled out a red carpet for them. At first, this seemed natural and aligned with our cultural values. Over time, however, I noticed a troubling pattern: many local researchers began to see themselves—and train their students to see themselves—as intellectually subordinate. We were told that these collaborations are opportunities we cannot afford to miss. By accepting this narrative without question, we helped sustain the cycle.

In many collaborations, proposals are drafted and funded before local researchers are meaningfully involved. Students collect data but do not participate in proposal design, data analysis, interpretation, or authorship decisions. Indigenous peoples and traditional communities contribute territory, labor, and knowledge, yet remain marginal in recognition and leadership.

The hierarchy operates at multiple scales: between the Global North and Global South, within Brazil between regions (South/Southeast and the North/Northeast), and locally in relations with indigenous and traditional communities. These asymmetries determine who defines research agendas and who receives intellectual credit. In archeology, similar dynamics have already prompted legal reforms in Brazil5. In public health, researchers openly debate comparable inequalities6. In conservation biology, biodiversity, and ecology, however, the discussion remains limited7. We need to break this silence.

This silence is partly structural. Research in remote and biodiverse regions has long been organized around asymmetric funding and externally defined agendas. Within this model, extractive practices become normalized. The division between those who collect data and those who produce knowledge from them is rarely questioned.

Naming these asymmetries carries risks. Early-career researchers depend on the networks they might criticize. Senior researchers often maintain collaborations to secure publications and student training, even when leadership remains externally concentrated. Some researchers resist this logic through genuine co-creation with local scholars, but such practices remain exceptions.

Decolonial science: a call to transform scientific culture

In this final section, I reflect on responsibility, agency, and the possibilities for change. I write as a scientist based in the Amazon. Although I do not belong to an indigenous group or a traditional community, I was born and raised in a small town along the Amazon River, where science felt distant from everyday life. I built my career gradually within regional universities. My experience is personal, but it resonates with many Amazon-based researchers whose work remains closely tied to the territory while decision-making power remains concentrated elsewhere.

Across different social positions, Amazon-based scientists face a common constraint: limited intellectual leadership keeps us dependent on external actors to define research agendas and legitimize knowledge8. This dependence weakens both basic and applied research to respond to urgent local demands—such as strengthening productive chains, income generation, or region-specific socio-environmental challenges. Although colonial experiences differ across indigenous, traditional, and non-traditional Amazonian groups, they share the effects of concentrated scientific authority outside the region. This dynamic reinforces the perception of the Amazon as a field site rather than a center of knowledge production, even though research in the region depends fundamentally on local expertise, long-term relationships, and trust.

A decolonial approach to science requires more than denunciation. It requires awareness of these practices and the courage to challenge them. I use the term “decolonial” as both an analytical and political lens to confront entrenched structures reproducing inequity in knowledge production and to rethink how power, authority, and recognition are distributed. Decolonial science means co-creating research, reparative practices9, and ensuring equitable participation and recognizing Amazonian scientists and local people as co-producers of knowledge. It does not imply exclusion. Instead, it implies shared authority.

This shift also requires external collaborators to recognize the time local students need to develop as independent researchers. True collaboration and investment in training do not mean funding data collection to feed large projects while merely subsidizing a diploma. When students collect data for major projects without developing core research skills, they may become highly skilled field collectors. However, they do not become scientists prepared to lead projects, secure funding, and shape their own research agendas and publications.

Many Amazonian students welcome large research projects with enthusiasm, attracted by access to equipments, scholarships, and international collaborations. Yet they often discover that their role is limited to data collection. As local mentors, we also share responsibility. Our students are not cheap labor for external publications. We must trust their capacity to produce high-impact science and commit to creating real conditions for their intellectual development.

During a moment of confrontation within a colonial dynamic, I once heard the claim that an oppressor exists only because someone accepts oppression. Although provocative, this statement is problematic. It risks shifting responsibility away from the oppressor and reinforcing asymmetric power relations. At the same time, the statement reveals a difficult truth: structural domination can persist when silence, fear, or reluctant collaboration sustain it. It reminds us that we cannot wait for external saviors. When institutions promote inclusion, it often remains rhetoric, a showcase that does not confront the structural roots of inequality. In some cases, inclusion becomes a form of “box-ticking”, a symbolic gesture that signals diversity without changing power structures or authorship patterns. Such gestures may ease institutional conscience, but they rarely transform the hierarchies that keep Amazonian researchers at the margins of decision-making. Amazonian scientists - professors and students alike—face real constraints and structural barriers. However, if we want to change this scenario, we must move beyond a position of victim and assume responsibility for our own transformation. We must actively strengthen our research skills and take on leadership roles. How often have we invested in developing our analytical and writing skills? How frequently have we taken the risk of leading projects? Are we prepared to claim a role in defining agendas and setting priorities?

This article is more than a reflection on neocolonial practices. It is an invitation to dialog and empowerment. We do not seek symbolic inclusion. We seek recognition as scientists with the authority to shape knowledge about our own region. The climate crisis and biodiversity loss are global urgencies that demand strong regional leadership. We must realize our full creative and scientific potential to address these challenges. We cannot afford to reproduce new forms of colonization that restrict our leadership and intellectual growth. Building a more horizontal scientific culture requires prioritizing training and recognizing Amazonian students as future leaders rather than merely data collectors. When local researchers define research questions and interpret results, science advances. Such participation strengthens our understanding of biodiversity, conservation, and ecology10.

I recognize that leaders of large research groups often reproduce the practices they have learned, and that neocolonial practices in science are shaped by resource policies. For many years, these patterns operated with little scrutiny. Although a recent awareness is growing and these inequalities are increasingly discussed and challenged11, much of the change remains symbolic. Amazonian scientists are invited into discussions, but not consistently into decision-making. The structure persists.

As an early-career scientist in the Amazon, I feel both the weight of this structure and the urgency to transform it. Awareness alone is not enough; what we need is structural change, a reassessment of who defines research agendas, who sets priorities, and who holds decision-making power.

This transformation will be gradual and imperfect. But it requires deliberate action. To Amazonian scientists and students: question the roles you occupy and pursue leadership. To those who already hold positions of influence: remain engaged but create real opportunities for shared decision-making. We seek a seat at the table. More importantly, we seek the conditions to stand on equal footing.
