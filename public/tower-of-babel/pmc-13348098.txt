Caregiving burden and perceived stress in family caregivers of cardiac patients
Authors: Seyede Fatemeh Gheiasi, Mahmoud Rouhbakhsh, Soheila Rabie Siahkali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac diseases often require prolonged caregiving, imposing substantial burden and stress on family caregivers.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess caregiving burden and perceived stress among family caregivers of cardiac patients and to examine their associations with demographic and care-related factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 259 caregivers of cardiac patients (aged ≥ 18 years, ≥ 6 months of caregiving) at the emergency department of Ayatollah Mousavi Hospital, Zanjan, Iran, in 2024. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire, the Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI), and Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). Data were analyzed using t-tests, ANOVA, and Pearson correlation in SPSS version 16 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).ResultsThe mean total CBI score was 77.32 ± 16.16 (range: 35–94), and the mean PSS score was 31.57 ± 8.88 (range: 17–53). The highest burden was observed in the social (18.86 ± 3.28) and developmental (16.14 ± 5.10) subscales. Pearson correlation showed a statistically significant negative association between total CBI and PSS (r = − 0.745, p < 0.001). Significant differences in CBI and PSS were observed across caregiver characteristics, including gender, residence, living arrangement, education, economic status, government support, patient relationship, caregiving duration, and availability of substitutes (p < 0.05).ConclusionFamily caregivers of cardiac patients in emergency settings experience substantial caregiving burden and perceived stress, with a statistically significant negative association between these factors. Nurses can mitigate caregiver burden by providing timely education, emotional support, and tailored resources for high-risk caregivers, including including women, lower socioeconomic groups, and intensive caregivers.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain among the leading causes of morbidity, disability, and mortality worldwide, including in Iran, placing substantial burdens on families and healthcare systems [1–3]. Due to the chronic, progressive, and often unpredictable nature of cardiac conditions, affected individuals frequently require long-term care, predominantly provided by family caregivers [2, 4]. These caregivers play crucial roles in symptom management, medication adherence, and emotional support, and coordinate with healthcare services, highlighting their essential contribution to patient outcomes [2, 5].

Caregiver burden is a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, psychological, social, and financial strains, including time-dependent, physical, emotional, social, and developmental dimensions [4, 6]. Family caregivers of cardiac patients face prolonged demands, repeated hospitalizations, and uncertainty regarding disease prognosis, which may result in fatigue, anxiety, depression, social isolation, and financial strain, adversely affecting both caregiver well-being and patient care [5, 7–10].

Recent evidence demonstrates that caregiver burden is influenced by clinical, psychological, and social factors, and that interventions such as education, counseling, support groups, and structured coping strategies can reduce burden and improve outcomes for both caregivers and patients [6, 8, 11, 12].

Given these consequences, a theoretical framework is essential to systematically analyze caregiver experiences. The Stress–Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1990) suggests that chronic caregiving demands act as primary stressors, which, when coupled with limited coping resources, may result in psychological distress [13, 14]. Family-centered care models emphasize collaborative engagement between caregivers and healthcare teams, underscoring the reciprocal impact of caregiver well-being and patient outcomes [14]. NANDA-I nursing diagnoses, such as “Caregiver Role Strain,” provide structured recognition of caregiving challenges and guide nursing assessment and interventions [15, 16].

Caregiving for cardiac patients, particularly those with heart failure, presents unique challenges, including life-threatening disease, frequent acute exacerbations, emergency admissions, and ongoing fears of symptom recurrence or sudden death [10, 17, 18].

In Iran, family caregiving is deeply rooted in social, moral, and religious values and is often considered a familial obligation rather than a shared responsibility with formal care systems [19–21]. While this strong commitment may strengthen emotional bonds, it can also intensify caregiver burden and psychological strain [21].

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding caregiver burden, with some reporting low to moderate levels [22] and others, particularly in different cultural settings, reporting high burden [23]. Despite these studies, there is still a lack of research that simultaneously examines both caregiver burden and perceived stress among cardiac patient caregivers, especially in emergency department settings within the Iranian healthcare system, indicating a critical gap in understanding [10].

Emergency department (ED) settings pose additional stressors, such as acute symptom escalation, urgent decision-making, unfamiliar environments, and communication challenges with healthcare providers, which contribute to perceived burden and unmet needs [11, 12]. In emergency department settings, nurses are often the primary professionals interacting with family caregivers during acute cardiac events. Nurses play a key role in assessing caregiver burden and perceived stress, providing timely education, emotional support, and facilitating communication with the healthcare team [2, 11].

Most existing studies have focused on outpatient or general hospital contexts, with limited attention to the acute, high-stress environment of the ED. This gap is significant because caregivers in EDs face unique challenges, including urgent decision-making, acute symptom management, and unfamiliar clinical environments, which may intensify both burden and stress. Addressing this gap is essential for developing evidence-based, nursing-led interventions tailored to the specific needs of family caregivers in acute cardiac care.

Objective

This study aimed to systematically assess the relationship between caregiving burden and perceived stress among family caregivers of cardiac patients presenting to the emergency department in Iran. Additionally, it explored potential predictors and correlations of stress and burden within this caregiver population. Understanding these relationships can support nursing assessment, early identification of high-risk caregivers, and the development of nursing-led interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional descriptive single-center study was conducted at the emergency department (ED) of Ayatollah Mousavi Hospital, a tertiary care teaching hospital in Zanjan, Iran, from August to March 2024, chosen due to its high-stress, acute care environment. The study aimed to investigate the relationship between caregiver burden and perceived stress among family caregivers of cardiac patients. Caregivers were the primary unit of analysis.

Participants

Family caregivers were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had a first-degree relationship with the patient, and had at least six months of caregiving experience. Exclusion criteria included caregivers under 18 years, professional caregivers, caregivers of patients with other chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic respiratory diseases), those with a history of active substance use or psychotropic medication, and caregivers who had delegated patient care to healthcare professionals. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis. Among the participants, 22.6% were classified as ‘other relatives,’ but they were primary caregivers responsible for patient care and were included in the analyses. Their characteristics were carefully considered to ensure accurate representation of the caregiving context.

To determine the sample size for assessing the correlation between caregiver burden (CBI) and perceived stress (PSS), a conservative correlation coefficient of 0.2 was assumed. Previous studies reported higher correlations (≥ 0.5) [9, 24], which would have required a smaller sample. Considering a type I error of 0.05, a type II error of 0.1, and 90% statistical power, a minimum of 259 participants was deemed sufficient, and they were recruited using convenience sampling from the emergency department of Ayatollah Mousavi Hospital, Zanjan, Iran, between August and March 2024. A total of 275 questionnaires were distributed, of which 259 complete responses were included in the analysis.

Data collection and instruments

Data were collected using a demographic information questionnaire, the Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI), and Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (PSS).

Demographic questionnaire

Information was obtained on caregivers’ and patients’ age, gender, education, marital status, relationship to the patient, caregiving hours and duration, occupation, economic status, and place of residence.

Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI)

Developed by Novak and Guest in 1989, is a 24-item instrument measuring caregiver burden across five subscales: time-dependent, developmental, physical, social, and emotional burden. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from completely false (0) to completely true (4), with total scores ranging from 0 to 96; higher scores indicate greater caregiving burden. A total score of 36 or higher has been used in previous studies as an indicator of high caregiver burden [25, 26]. The CBI has demonstrated good reliability in international studies, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.69 to 0.87 for subscales and > 0.80 for the total scale [27, 28]. Among Iranian caregivers, the CBI exhibited excellent psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (α = 0.93) and robust test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.96) [27]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.91.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

Developed by Cohen et al. in 1983, was used to assess perceived stress related to unpredictable and uncontrollable life events during the past month [29]. The scale consists of 14 items scored on a four-point Likert scale ranging from never (0) to very often (4). Positive items are reverse-scored, and total scores range from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. A cutoff score of 21.8 has been suggested to indicate higher perceived stress [30]. The validity and reliability of the PSS have been confirmed in Iranian samples, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84 reported by Asghari et al. [31, 32]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the PSS was 0.81.

Data were collected on weekdays and weekends to capture variations in caregiver experiences. All working shifts (morning, afternoon, and night) were included to reflect different ED workloads. This ensured that data collection reflected varying levels of environmental intensity and workload in the emergency department. After explaining the study objectives and obtaining informed consent, questionnaires were distributed to eligible participants. Questionnaires were self-administered under the supervision of trained research assistants, who provided guidance as needed. Completion time was approximately 15–20 min.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as frequency (%) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Data were normally distributed, allowing the use of parametric tests (independent t-test, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlation). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 16 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zanjan University of Medical Sciences, zanjan, Iran (Ethics code: IR.ZUMS.REC.1403.177) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided written informed consent and were assured of confidentiality.

Results

Caregivers were the primary unit of analysis. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients under their care are presented solely to describe the clinical context, whereas all subsequent analyses and subgroup comparisons refer to family caregivers.

Characteristics of cardiac patients

Among the 259 cardiac patients referred to the emergency department, the mean age was 66.81 (11.4) years. The majority were male (54.4%), married (62.6%), and lived in urban areas (55.6%). Most patients lived with their families (75.7%) and had a high school education or lower (83.8%).

Regarding employment status, 38.2% of patients were unemployed, and 55.6% reported an average financial status. More than half were the main income earners of their families (56.0%). Rural insurance coverage was the most common type of health insurance (37.1%). In terms of functional ability, 35.1% of patients were completely independent, while only 4.7% were fully dependent. The mean number of hospitalizations was 5.02 (4.08), and the mean duration of cardiac disease was 8.60 (7.03) years (Table 1).

Characteristics of family caregivers

The mean age of family caregivers was 41.39 (10.36) years, with a mean caregiving duration of 7.95 (8.12) years. Most caregivers were female (59.1%) and married (66.4%), and the majority lived in urban areas (74.5%) and with their families (95.8%). The largest proportion provided continuous (24-hour) care (34.0%).

In terms of education, nearly half of caregivers held a bachelor’s degree (44.8%). Most caregivers were self-employed (40.2%) and reported an average financial status (69.9%). Only a small proportion received governmental support (3.9%). Most caregivers were the patient’s children (58.3%), while 22.6% were other relatives. The majority were not financially dependent on the patient (63.3%) and reported having access to a substitute caregiver (75.7%). Detailed caregiver characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Caregiver burden and perceived stress

The mean total caregiver burden score was 77.32 (16.16), with observed scores ranging from 35 to 94. The total score was calculated according to the standard Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI) scoring procedure, yielding a theoretical range of 0–96. Among the burden subscales, social burden showed the highest mean score, whereas physical and emotional burdens were comparatively lower. Cronbach’s α for the total CBI in this sample was 0.91.

The mean perceived stress score among caregivers was 31.57 (8.88), with scores ranging from 17 to 53, reflecting substantial variability in stress levels. Cronbach’s α for the PSS in this sample was 0.81. Descriptive statistics for caregiver burden subscales and perceived stress are presented in Table 3.

Association between caregiver burden and perceived stress

Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong and statistically significant negative association between total caregiver burden and perceived stress (r = − 0.745, 95% CI: − 0.79 to − 0.69, p < 0.001), indicating that higher caregiving burden was associated with lower perceived stress among family caregivers in this sample. Factors associated with caregiver burden

As shown in Table 4, male caregivers reported higher caregiver burden than females, with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.30–0.82; t (257) = 4.63, p = 0.001). No significant difference in burden was observed between single and married caregivers (Cohen’s d = 0.16, 95% CI: − 0.09–0.41; t (257) = − 1.086, p = 0.280). Caregivers residing in urban areas experienced greater burden compared with those in rural areas (Cohen’s d = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.72–1.32; t (257) = 6.828, p = 0.001). Caregivers of patients who lived with their families reported the highest levels of caregiver burden among all living arrangement categories (Cohen’s d = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.20–1.26; t (257) = − 2.002, p = 0.046).

Burden scores differed significantly across educational levels (η² = 0.165, 95% CI: 0.09–0.24; F (3,255) = 16.798, p < 0.001) and across economic status categories (η² = 0.107, 95% CI: 0.04–0.18; F (2,256) = 9.791, p = 0.001). Caregivers receiving governmental support reported substantially lower burden than those without support (Cohen’s d = 2.77, 95% CI: 2.23–3.31; t (257) = − 18.423, p = 0.001).

The caregiver–patient relationship was significantly associated with burden (η² = 0.042, 95% CI: 0.001–0.08; F (2,256) = 3.344, p = 0.037), with non-spouse and non-child relatives reporting higher scores. Duration of caregiving was also significant, whereby caregivers providing care for fewer than two days per week reported the highest burden (η² = 0.204, 95% CI: 0.13–0.28; F (3,255) = 18.247, p < 0.001). Finally, access to a substitute caregiver was associated with higher burden (η² = 0.062, 95% CI: 0.02–0.11; F (1,257) = 6.099, p = 0.001) (see Table 4).

Factors associated with perceived stress

As presented in Table 5, female caregivers reported higher perceived stress than males, with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.23–0.75; t (257) = − 3.595, p = 0.001). No significant difference in stress was observed between single and married caregivers (Cohen’s d = 0.15, 95% CI: − 0.10–0.40; t (257) = 1.409, p = 0.162). Caregivers residing in rural areas experienced significantly higher stress compared with urban caregivers (Cohen’s d = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.87–1.47; t (257) = − 7.664, p = 0.001). Caregivers of patients who lived alone reported the highest levels of perceived stress among all living arrangement categories (Cohen’s d = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.23–1.29; t (257) = 2.066, p = 0.040).

Perceived stress differed across educational levels (η² = 0.158, 95% CI: 0.08–0.23; F (3,255) = 15.267, p < 0.001) and across economic status categories (η² = 0.152, 95% CI: 0.08–0.22; F (2,256) = 14.965, p = 0.001), with higher education and better economic status associated with lower stress. The caregiver–patient relationship was significantly associated with perceived stress (η² = 0.081, 95% CI: 0.01–0.14; F (2,256) = 5.724, p = 0.001), as spouses and children reported higher stress levels than other relatives.

Duration of caregiving was significantly associated with perceived stress, with continuous 24-hour caregiving showing the strongest association (η² = 0.204, 95% CI: 0.13–0.28; F (3,255) = 21.761, p < 0.001). Finally, caregivers without access to a substitute caregiver had significantly higher stress compared with those who had available support (Cohen’s d = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.76–1.36; t (257) = − 6.300, p = 0.001) (see Table 5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional, descriptive study investigated caregiving burden and perceived stress among family caregivers of cardiac patients admitted to the emergency department of Ayatollah Mousavi Hospital in Zanjan, Iran. The findings revealed substantial levels of caregiver burden and perceived stress. According to the Stress–Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1990), primary stressors such as the acute and unpredictable nature of cardiac conditions, urgent decision-making, and limited social support contribute to psychological strain. When combined with limited coping resources, these stressors can result in heightened perceived stress, confirming the theoretical premise that caregiver outcomes are shaped by both demands and available resources [13, 14].

Previous studies report varying levels of caregiver burden, ranging from mild to high, depending on cultural context, care setting, and patient characteristics [33, 34]. Compared to caregivers of elderly patients in Jafari et al.’s study [17], our participants reported lower overall burden, suggesting that patient age, clinical severity, and care setting (acute vs. chronic) are important determinants of caregiver strain. These findings highlight the importance of cultural, gender, and socioeconomic factors. In the Iranian context, women often assume primary caregiving roles under economic constraints and with limited access to formal support [18, 35].

Analysis of the multidimensional caregiver burden revealed that social and developmental dimensions were particularly prominent. Social burden, including reduced social interactions, isolation, and limited support networks, can increase susceptibility to depression and anxiety. Developmental burden, reflecting impediments to personal and professional growth, was especially notable among younger or working caregivers. These patterns align with the Caregiver Role Strain framework, which emphasizes the relational, social, and role-related challenges of caregiving [15, 16]. Temporal, physical, and emotional burdens were also significant, reflecting the multifaceted nature of caregiving in acute cardiac contexts [9, 36, 37].

Correlation analysis demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between total caregiver burden and perceived stress (r = − 0.745, 95% CI: − 0.79 to − 0.69, p < 0.001), suggesting that the observed relationship may reflect the complex and multifaceted experiences of caregivers, including differences in coping, adaptation, and available support. Nurses can use caregiver stress screening tools, provide psychoeducational support, facilitate family-centered discharge planning, and develop structured caregiver education programs or workshops in acute cardiac settings [14]. Implementing these strategies aligns with the principles of Family-Centered Care, which emphasize collaborative engagement between caregivers and healthcare providers to optimize patient outcomes and caregiver well-being.

Socio-demographic factors further shaped caregiver experiences. Female caregivers were predominant, reflecting cultural expectations in Iran [9, 38]. Rural residence, lower education, and economic hardship were associated with greater stress, suggesting that nursing interventions should be tailored to address geographical, educational, and socioeconomic disparities. The caregiver–patient relationship also influenced outcomes: non-child caregivers reported higher burden, while child caregivers experienced greater emotional stress due to deeper familial bonds, highlighting the importance of relational and affective considerations beyond task intensity [38]. This pattern may reflect differences in social support, adaptation strategies, or coping resources, highlighting that burden and perceived stress may not always increase in parallel. Additionally, caregivers of patients who lived alone experienced the highest perceived stress, whereas caregivers of patients living with family reported the highest caregiver burden. These factors help explain why stress levels were particularly elevated in this sample.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides novel insights into family caregivers of cardiac patients in emergency settings, a population with limited prior research. Its strengths include the use of standardized instruments to assess caregiver burden and perceived stress, a multidimensional evaluation of caregiving, and integration with theoretical frameworks, including the Stress–Process Model, Caregiver Role Strain, and Family-Centered Care. By situating the findings within both domestic and international literature, the study highlights gender-sensitive and culturally-informed strategies for caregiver support.

However, several limitations should be considered. The use of self-report measures may have introduced reporting bias, and the single-center convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, patient clinical severity was not controlled for, which may have influenced caregiver burden and perceived stress levels. Additionally, the high-stress, acute nature of the emergency department setting may have influenced caregiver responses. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences and the examination of temporal changes in caregiver burden or perceived stress. Furthermore, this study was conducted within the Iranian emergency care system, where cultural norms, family caregiving roles, and the organization of healthcare services differ from those in other countries, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to different cultural or healthcare contexts. Despite these limitations, the study provides a solid foundation for nursing interventions and future research, emphasizing the importance of longitudinal, multicenter studies to explore temporal trends and evaluate the effectiveness of support strategies.

Conclusion

Caregivers in emergency cardiac settings face acute stressors that necessitate rapid nursing assessment and continuous support. Family caregivers in this context experience substantial caregiver burden and perceived stress, with a statistically significant negative association between these factors. This study specifically assessed caregiver burden and perceived stress among family caregivers of cardiac patients and examined their associations with socio-demographic and care-related factors.

Interpreted through the Stress–Process Model and the Caregiver Role Strain framework, these findings highlight the critical role of nurses in identifying caregivers with high burden and stress and providing emotional and educational support. Nurses should also coordinate referrals for psychosocial care, including systematic assessment and culturally tailored counseling. Interventions may include structured caregiver education programs, stress-management workshops, and integration of psychosocial support protocols, with particular attention to caregivers at higher risk, such as women, those with lower socioeconomic status, and caregivers providing intensive or prolonged care. Supporting family caregivers not only improves their psychological and physical well-being but also enhances patient outcomes and overall quality of care. Given the acute, unpredictable, and high-stress nature of emergency cardiac care, such nursing-led interventions are particularly important for timely assessment, emotional support, and prevention of caregiver overload.

Future research should adopt longitudinal and multicenter designs to evaluate the effectiveness of nursing-led interventions and explore temporal changes in caregiver burden and perceived stress, as causal relationships cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional study. Recognizing, supporting, and empowering family caregivers remains essential for high-quality, patient-centered cardiac care in acute and emergency settings.
