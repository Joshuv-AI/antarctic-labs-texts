Optimal Anytime-Valid Tests for Composite Nulls
Shubhanshu Shekhar

Abstract
We consider the problem of designing optimal level- α \alpha power-one tests for composite nulls. Given a parameter α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) and a stream of 𝒳 \mathcal{X} -valued observations { X n : n ≥ 1 } ​ ∼ i.i.d. ​ P \{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P , the goal is to design a level- α \alpha power-one test τ α \tau_{\alpha} for the null H 0 : P ∈ 𝒫 0 ⊂ 𝒫 ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) H_{0}:P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) . Prior works have shown that any such τ α \tau_{\alpha} must satisfy 𝔼 P ​ [ τ α ] ≥ log ⁡ ( 1 / α ) γ ∗ ​ ( P , 𝒫 0 ) \mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]\geq\tfrac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})} , where γ ∗ ​ ( P , 𝒫 0 ) \gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0}) is the so-called KL inf \mathrm{KL}_{\inf} or minimum divergence of P P to the null class. In this paper, our objective is to develop and analyze constructive schemes that match this lower bound as α ↓ 0 \alpha\downarrow 0 . We first consider the finite-alphabet case ( | 𝒳 | = m < ∞ |\mathcal{X}|=m<\infty ), and show that a test based on universal e e -process (formed by the ratio of a universal predictor and the running null MLE) is optimal in the above sense. The proof relies on a Donsker-Varadhan (DV) based saddle-point representation of KL inf \mathrm{KL}_{\inf} , and an application of Sion’s minimax theorem. This characterization motivates a general method for arbitrary 𝒳 \mathcal{X} : construct an e e -process based on the empirical solutions to the saddle-point representation over a sufficiently rich class of test functions. We give sufficient conditions for the optimality of this test for compact convex nulls, and verify them for Hölder smooth density models. We end the paper with a discussion on the computational aspects of implementing our proposed tests in some practical settings.

Introduction

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
denote some measurable space, and
\mathcal{P}_{j}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{j}\}
for
j=0,1
, denote a pair of disjoint classes of probability distributions on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
. Then, given a stream of observations
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P
for an unknown distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\cup\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we consider the task of distinguishing between

\displaystyle H_{0}:P\in\mathcal{P}_{0},\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}.

(2)

Given a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
, our goal is to construct a level-
\alpha
power-one test
\tau_{\alpha}
for this problem. Formally, this involves constructing a stopping time
\tau_{\alpha}
, such that the following two conditions hold:

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{P_{0}}\left(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\right)\leq\alpha,\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{P_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{P_{1}}\left(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\right)=1.

(3)

In other words,
\tau_{\alpha}
denotes the random data-driven time at which we stop collecting more observations, and reject the null. In addition to the power-one property, it is often important to control the expected value of the stopping time for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
. Prior works have established a fundamental impossibility result on the value that this quantity can take in the limit of small
\alpha
. In particular, it is known that any level-
\alpha

\tau_{\alpha}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]}{\log(1/\alpha)/\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})}\geq 1,\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})=\inf_{P_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel P_{0}),

(4)

and
d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel P_{0})
denotes the relative entropy (or KL divergence) between
P
and
P_{0}
. The term
\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})
is the appropriate measure of difficulty of distinguishing
P
from the null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. In this paper, our main objective is to develop a general constructive scheme to design tests that match this fundamental limit.

1.1
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we will work in an unspecified probability space that is large enough to allow the definition of all random variables that arise in our setup. As mentioned before, we assume that we have a stream of
i.i.d.
observations
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}
drawn from some unknown distribution
P
. Let
\{\mathcal{M}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
denote a filtration such that
\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\subset\mathcal{M}_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. This enlargement of the filtration is to incorporate any additional randomness in the design of the test. We consider a class of probability distributions
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
on
\mathcal{X}
, indexed by some parameter set
\Theta
. Note that we do not require
\Theta
to be finite dimensional, and in the most general case, elements of
\Theta
can simply be the probability measures themselves. We define the null and alternative classes
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
as distributions indexed by two disjoint subsets
\Theta_{0}\subset\Theta
and
\Theta_{1}\subset\Theta

P\equiv P_{\theta}
for an unknown
\theta\in\Theta_{0}\cup\Theta_{1}

\displaystyle\text{Given }\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P\equiv P_{\theta},\quad\text{decide between}\quad H_{0}:\theta\in\Theta_{0},\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\theta\in\Theta_{1}.

(5)

Depending on the specific problem instance, working with this parametrized formulation may be more natural than the equivalent setup of (
2

Our goal is to design a level-
\alpha
power one test for this problem for a pre-specified value
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Formally, this means that we need to design a stopping time
\tau_{\alpha}
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\right)\leq\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\left(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\right)=1.

(6)

In addition, for any
\theta\not\in\Theta_{0}
, we also want to ensure a small
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{\alpha}]
. Prior work, such as
Agrawal and Ramdas 2025

Let

Given a null class

The focus of this paper is to design and analyze power-one tests that are optimal in the sense defined above. Before proceeding to the main results, we present a brief discussion of related works in this area that will provide the appropriate context to place our contributions.

1.2
Related Work and Overview of Our Results

The study of power-one level-
\alpha
sequential tests was pioneered by Robbins and collaborators in the 1960s and 70s. For example, for the one-sided Gaussian mean testing problem with
\langle X_{n}\rangle=\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(\mu,1)
and
H_{0}:\mu\leq 0
versus
H_{1}:\mu>0
,
Darling and Robbins 1967
proposed tests that continue sampling indefinitely with probability at least
1-\alpha
under
H_{0}
, while stopping almost surely under
H_{1}
. Their construction used moment generating function (MGF) based martingales combined with a “peeling” argument that led to an iterated-logarithm boundary crossing of the running sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
In a follow-up work,
Darling and Robbins 1968
considered some nonparametric goodness-of-fit and stochastic dominance testing tasks, and developed sequential power-one variants of classical Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) style tests. Their construction essentially relied on a combinatorial deviation inequality for the KS statistic at any fixed
n
, combined with a simple union bound. They showed the level-
\alpha
and power-one property, and also obtained upper bounds on the expected stopping times of the resulting tests in terms of the effect-size and the inverse of the rejection boundary. Some early follow-up works include
Robbins 1970
, who developed tests based on mixture of likelihood-ratios (and more generally test martingales) for various parametric and nonparametric problems, and
Robbins and Siegmund 1972
who discussed an alternative approach based in generalized-likelihood ratio process for parametric problems, with predictable estimates in the numerator and a fixed (extremal) parameter in the denominator.

More recently, there has been a resurgence of interest in this area of anytime-valid inference over the last decade, and a detailed discussion can be found in the survey paper by
Ramdas et al. 2023
and the monograph by
Ramdas and Wang 2024
. Recent works design and analyze new power-one tests in both the parametric (e.g.,
Grünwald et al. 2024
;
Pérez-Ortiz et al. 2024
) and nonparametric (e.g.,
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2024
;
Shekhar and Ramdas 2023
;
Podkopaev and Ramdas 2023
;
Henzi and Law 2024
) settings. These works often involve bounds on the expected stopping time under the alternative, and establish some notions of optimality. For instance, in the case of two-sample testing,
Shekhar and Ramdas 2023
show the existence of “hard” problems for which the performance achieved by their test cannot be improved. In this paper, we are interested in analyzing the instance-wise optimality of level-
\alpha
tests in the limit of small
\alpha
, as we discussed in
Definition

1
. The most closely related works that address similar questions are
(
Agrawal and Ramdas 2025
)
and
(
Waudby-Smith et al. 2025
)
, and we discuss them below.

Waudby-Smith et al. 2025
studied the task of analyzing the performance under the alternative of a particular class of power-one tests, associated with
e
-processes of the form

\displaystyle W_{0}=1,\quad W_{n}=W_{n-1}\times\left(\lambda_{n}E_{n}^{(1)}+(1-\lambda_{n})E_{n}^{(2)}\right),

(9)

where
\{E_{n}^{(j)}:n\geq 1\}
for
j=1,2
are a collection of
i.i.d.

e
-values under the null, and
\langle\lambda_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\{\lambda_{n}:n\geq 1\}
denotes a sequence of predictable “bets”. This process can be interpreted as the wealth of an investor (who starts off with
W_{0}=\$1
) in a two-stock portfolio with price-relatives
E_{n}^{(1)}
and
E_{n}^{(2)}
in the
n^{th}
investing period, and
(\lambda_{n},1-\lambda_{n})
denotes the rebalancing done by the investor at the start of the
n^{th}
period. One of the results proved by
Waudby-Smith et al. 2025
is that if the betting (or investing) strategy
\langle\lambda_{n}\rangle
has vanishing per-sequence regret, then the expected stopping time of the resulting test satisfies
\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\alpha}]/\log(1/\alpha)\leq 1/\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}[\log(\lambda E^{(1)}+(1-\lambda)E^{(2)})]
under certain mild moment conditions
(
Waudby-Smith et al. 2025
, Theorem 3.3)
. The authors also show that this dependence cannot be improved for this particular class of tests. Our work differs from
Waudby-Smith et al. 2025
in scope as well as technical details: instead of working with a given class of
e
-values or processes which implicitly define the null, our goal is to start with a given composite null class and construct appropriate power-one tests that are optimal. The notion of optimality we care about is w.r.t. all feasible power-one tests, and not the restricted class that was the focus of
Waudby-Smith et al. 2025
.

Another related paper studying the fundamental limits of power-one tests is
(
Agrawal and Ramdas 2025
)
. They consider a general testing problem with a composite null
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, and show that any level-
\alpha
test
\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}
must satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime}]\geq\log(1/\alpha)/\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})
, as we recalled in
1
. Next, they show that for tests of the form
\tau_{\alpha}\coloneqq\inf\{n\geq 1:W_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
with
\langle W_{n}\rangle
denoting an
e
-process for the null
\mathcal{P}_{0}
,
Agrawal and Ramdas 2025
showed that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log W_{n}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\geq}}\;\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})\quad\implies\quad\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]}{\log(1/\alpha)/\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})}\leq 4.

(10)

Our contributions are complementary to
Agrawal and Ramdas 2025
. Rather than identifying sufficient conditions on
e
-processes for the associated tests to be optimal, we develop general design principles, via DV representation and minimax theorem, for building
e
-processes (and hence, power-one tests) that achieve the optimal lower bound as
\alpha\downarrow 0
with a sharp constant (note that this constant factor refinement is a consequence of the alternative analysis route developed in our paper, and not the primary driving goal of the paper).

Overview of our results.

We begin in
Section

by considering the technically simpler finite-alphabet setting, where the null is represented by a closed subset

A key observation underlying our proof of

2

-process construction (
Definition

2
) for arbitrary alphabets based on a predictable empirical-risk-minimization (ERM) based choice of test functions from a class
\mathcal{F}
that approximate
f^{*}
. Under certain conditions stated in

, we establish the optimality of tests associated with such
e
-processes in the sense of
Definition

1
. As a concrete nonparametric example, we discuss an application of our result to a problem in which the null

is a compact convex class of Hölder smooth densities over a compact domain, and the test functions lie in an RKHS associated with a universal kernel.

We end the paper with a brief discussion of the practical aspects associated with implementing our proposed

-processes in

. In particular, for certain finite dimensional problems, we can employ existing results from stochastic saddle-point optimization to obtain computationally feasible tests using our developed methodology. For larger problems, involving complex and high-dimensional data types (such as images, or videos), we briefly discuss a heuristic strategy that can directly leverage the well-established training pipelines of generative machine learning (ML) models, such as

2
Finite Alphabet Case

In this section, we work with the simplifying assumption that
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
denotes a finite alphabet. In this setting, the class of probability distributions
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is parametrized by the
(m-1)
-dimensional simplex
\Theta=\{\theta\in[0,1]^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta[j]=1\}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denote a class of distributions indexed by a convex, compact subset
\Theta_{0}\subset\Theta
; that is,
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
, where
P_{\theta}
is the probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
associated with the probability mass function
\theta
. Given
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{\theta}
and a confidence parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
, our goal is to construct a level-
\alpha
power-one test to decide between

\displaystyle H_{0}:\theta\in\Theta_{0},\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:\theta\in\Theta_{1},

(11)

for another subset
\Theta_{1}\subset\Theta
that is disjoint with
\Theta_{0}
.

For any pmf
\theta\in\Theta
, and a sequence
x^{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we will use the shorthand
\theta^{n}[x^{n}]
to represent
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta[x_{i}]
. For a distribution
P\equiv P_{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
with
\theta\not\in\Theta_{0}
, we will alternate between the notations
\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})
and
\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})
in this section. Both terms will be used to refer to the minimum divergence of
\theta
(or
P_{\theta}
) to the null
\Theta_{0}
(or
\mathcal{P}_{0}
):

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}\equiv\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})\equiv\gamma^{*}(P_{\theta},\mathcal{P}_{0})=\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\parallel P_{\theta_{0}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{0}^{\dagger}\equiv\theta_{0}^{\dagger}(\theta)\in\argmin_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\parallel P_{\theta_{0}}).

(12)

Since we have assumed that
\Theta_{0}
is a compact subset of
\Theta
, it implies that the infimum in the definition of
\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})
is achieved, and the term
\theta^{\dagger}(\theta)
is well defined. Within this context, our goal is to construct level-
\alpha
stopping times
\{\tau_{\alpha}:\alpha\in(0,1]\}
that are provably optimal in the sense discussed in
Definition

1
.

Construction of the test.

A general strategy for constructing power-one tests is to define them as the first
1/\alpha
crossing of a process
\langle W_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\{W_{n}:n\geq 0\}
adapted to the filtration
\langle\mathcal{M}_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\{\mathcal{M}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
, satisfying the following two conditions:

•

Under
H_{0}
, if
\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}
is the true parameter, then the process
\{W_{n}:n\geq 1\}
is unlikely to take large values.

•

Under
H_{1}
, if
\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1}
is the true parameter, then the
\{W_{n}:n\geq 1\}
grows rapidly at an exponential rate, that is approximately equal to
\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})
.

In other words,
\langle W_{n}\rangle
quantifies the accumulated evidence against the null based on the observations
\langle X_{n}\rangle
.
If
\Theta_{0}
and
\Theta_{1}
were singletons
\{\theta_{0}\}
and
\{\theta_{1}\}
, then the optimal choice would be the likelihood ratio process with
W_{n}=L_{n}(X^{n};\theta_{1},\theta_{0})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{1}^{n}[X^{n}]/\theta_{0}^{n}[X^{n}]
. For the case of a point null
\Theta_{0}=\{\theta_{0}\}
but composite alternative
\Theta_{1}
, a natural idea is to take mixtures

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{mix}}_{n}=\int_{\Theta}L_{n}(X^{n};\theta_{1},\theta_{0})\pi_{J}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\theta^{n}[X^{n}]\pi_{J}(\theta)d\theta}{\theta_{0}^{n}[X^{n}]},

(13)

where
\pi_{J}
denotes the Jeffreys prior over the probability simplex
\Theta
. The reason for this particular formulation is that classical results from universal compression imply that under any
\theta\in\Theta_{1}
, and
n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\log W^{\mathrm{mix}}_{n}\geq\log L_{n}(X^{n};\theta,\theta_{0})-\frac{m-1}{2}\log n-C_{m},

(14)

for some constant
C_{m}>0
. In other words, under any
\theta\in\Theta_{1}
, the mixture process
\{W^{\mathrm{mix}}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
grows essentially as quickly as the optimal likelihood ratio process modulo a small logarithmic regret term.
Finally, to deal with the most general case of composite null and alternative, we define
W^{\mathrm{UI}}
as

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}=\frac{\text{Mixture over all $\theta$}}{\text{Supremum over all null parameters}}=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\theta^{n}[X^{n}]\pi_{J}(\theta)d\theta}{\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\theta_{0}^{n}[X^{n}]}.

(15)

This simple idea, introduced by
Wasserman et al. 2020
, ends up giving us an (as we will show later) optimal test for the general setting of composite null and alternative. Using this process, given the stream of observations
\langle X_{n}\rangle
, we define our level-
\alpha
test as

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\geq 1:W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\},\quad\text{with}\quad W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{0}=1,\;\text{and}\;W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}=\frac{\theta_{J}^{n}[X^{n}]}{\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\theta_{0}[X^{n}]}.

(16)

Here,
\theta_{J}^{n}
denotes the mixture distribution over
\mathcal{X}^{n}
with respect to Jeffreys prior, i.e., the Krichevsky-Trofimov code
(
Krichevsky and Trofimov 1981
)
, and observe that the denominator is simply the maximum likelihood value based on the first
n
observations:

\displaystyle\theta_{J}^{n}[x^{n}]=\int_{\Theta}\theta^{n}[x^{n}]\pi_{J}(\theta)d\theta,\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{n}[x^{n}]=\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\theta_{0}^{n}[x^{n}].

(17)

Remark 1
.

Using the properties of
\mathrm{Dirichlet}(1/2,\ldots,1/2)
distribution (which is the Jeffreys prior in this case), it follows that the mixture
\theta_{J}^{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle\theta_{J}^{n}[x^{n}]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{J}[x_{i}\mid x^{i-1}],\quad\text{with}\quad\theta_{J}[x\mid x^{i-1}]=\frac{1/2+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{j}=x}}{m/2+i-1}.

(18)

That is, each conditional
\theta_{J}[\cdot\mid x^{i-1}]
is the so-called “add-
1/2
” predictor, where we simply add a fictitious count of
1/2
to each
x\in\mathcal{X}
to smooth out our estimate. Thus, the numerator in (
16
) can be updated incrementally in a computationally efficient manner.
However, the denominator requires recomputing the maximum likelihood value from scratch in each round for most problems, meaning that evaluating
W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}
can possibly end up being computationally demanding depending on the definition of
\Theta_{0}
. Since our focus in this paper is on the statistical performance of the test, we do not discuss the computational issues further.

We now proceed to the analysis of the tests
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle=\{\tau_{\alpha}:\alpha\in(0,1]\}
defined in (
16
).

Theorem 2.1
.

For the testing problem with composite null in (
11
), the class of tests
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle
defined in (
16
) satisfy the following for any
\theta\not\in\Theta_{0}
such that
\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})\coloneqq\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(\theta\parallel\theta_{0})>0
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\alpha,\;\;\forall\alpha\in(0,1],\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{\alpha}]}{J(\theta,\Theta_{0},\alpha)}\leq 1,\quad\text{where}\quad J(\theta,\Theta_{0},\alpha)=\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})}.

(19)

This establishes the optimality of the test
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle
in the sense of
Definition

1
. Additionally, note that the finiteness of the expected stopping times also implies the weaker power-one property of
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle
.

We present a detailed proof of this result in
Appendix

C
. An important fact about the process
\{W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
is that it is neither a martingale nor a supermartingale under the null. Yet, as noted by
Wasserman et al. 2020
, we can still employ Ville’s inequality on this process to control the type-I error because, for every null
\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}
, there exists a nonnegative supermartingale (or actually a nonnegative martingale) that dominates the process
\{W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
pointwise. This is one of the defining properties of
e-processes

(
Ramdas et al. 2020
, Lemma 6)
, which are fundamental tools in sequential anytime-valid inference.

3
Extension to General Alphabets

A closer look at the proof of
Theorem

2.1
(detailed in
Appendix

C
) reveals that, in addition to relying on the finiteness of the alphabet
\mathcal{X}
, the argument also relies strongly on two things: (i) the (weak) compactness of the null class, along with (ii) the application of Sion’s minimax theorem. Together, these two facts allow an interchange the
\inf
and
\sup
in the definition of
\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})
:

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}(\theta,\Theta_{0})=\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(X)]-\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[e^{f(X)}]\;=\;\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}}\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(X)]-\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[e^{f(X)}].

(20)

This, in turn, naturally leads to the definition of the “oracle
e
-process”
\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(f^{*}(X_{i})-\psi_{0}(f^{*})\right):n\geq 1\}
, with
f^{*}
denoting a function achieving the supremum. We can use this as a starting point to construct a new class of
e
-processes for general alphabets and establish their optimality under certain conditions. In this section, we first design and analyze such
e
-processes and then discuss their computational feasibility in
Section

3.2
.

Throughout this section, we work with a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a metric space endowed with a metric
\rho
, and
\mathcal{B}
is the corresponding Borel sigma algebra. While the metric structure is not strictly necessary in our argument, it allows some simplification of the proof of our main result since the DV representation of relative entropy between distributions on such spaces can be defined using continuous bounded functions (
2
).
Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta_{0}}:\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}\}
denote the class of null distributions on
\mathcal{X}
indexed by some parameter set
\Theta_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
denote a convex family of functions on
\mathcal{X}
, and define

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{F}}(P\parallel\mathcal{P}_{0})=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]-\psi_{0}(f)\right\},\quad\text{where}\quad\psi_{0}(f)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{f(X)}]\right).

(21)

We first record our assumptions formally, where we also define the class of alternatives that we consider in this section using the function class
\mathcal{F}
.

Assumption 1
.

We will work under the following assumptions:

•

(A1): Null Class.

\mathcal{P}_{0}
is a weakly compact and convex subset of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
.

•

(A2): Alternative Class.
For a given function class
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
and a null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, the set of alternative distributions,
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\setminus\mathcal{P}_{0}
, consists of
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
with

\displaystyle d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel\mathcal{P}_{0})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)=d_{\mathcal{F}}(P\parallel\mathcal{P}_{0})>0.

(22)

•

(A3): Uniform Moment Bound on
\mathcal{F}
under the alternative.
For every
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, there exists a constant
B_{P}<\infty
, and a
\delta>0
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\left\lvert f^{4+\delta}(X)\right\rvert\right]\leq B_{P}<\infty.

(23)

•

(A4): Uniform MGF Bound on
\mathcal{F}
under the null.
There exists a constant
B_{0}<\infty
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\psi_{0}(f)|=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\left\lvert\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[e^{f(X)}]\right)\right\rvert\leq B_{0}<\infty.

(24)

•

(A5): Learnability of
\mathcal{F}
.
For each
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, there exists a sequence
r_{n}\equiv r_{n}(P,\mathcal{F})\to 0
, such that for all
n\geq 1
, we have the following, with the same
\delta>0
from
(A3)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]\right\rvert\leq r_{n}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{2+\tfrac{\delta}{3}}},

(25)

where
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots
are
i.i.d.
draws from
P
.

Some of these conditions, such as
(A4)
, are not strictly necessary and can be weakened. We use the above statements as a balance between generality and simplicity of the proofs. Next, we propose a sequential test based on the variational representation of the divergence to null term in (
20
), which uses a sequence of “greedy” or empirical risk minimization (ERM) based approximations of
f^{*}
.

Definition 2

(DV
e
-process)
.

Consider a function class
\mathcal{F}
, and a null hypothesis represented by
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
. Given a stream of observations
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\cup\mathcal{P}_{1}
, we can define the process
\langle W_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\{W_{n}:n\geq 0\}
, with
W_{0}=1
,
f_{1}
an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{F}
, and

\displaystyle W_{n}=W_{n-1}\times\exp\left(f_{n}(X_{n})-\psi_{0}(f_{n})\right),\quad\text{with}\quad f_{n}\in\argmax_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(X_{i})-\psi_{0}(f),\quad\text{for }n\geq 2.

(26)

This can be used to define a level-
\alpha
test for
\mathcal{P}_{0}
as
\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\geq 1:W_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

In this section, we will establish that under
1
, the tests defined above are optimal in the sense of
Definition

1
. We touch upon some computational issues of implementing such tests in
Section

3.2
, and also discuss two important instantiations (for finite alphabets, and for testing means of bounded observations) in Appendix
B
.

Remark 2
.

In some cases, the
\argmax
above may not be well defined or may require appeals to a measurable selection theorem to be made completely rigorous. To avoid such cases, a more robust definition might be to work with an
f_{n}
such that
\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{n}(X_{i})-\psi_{0}(f_{n})\geq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(X_{i})-\psi_{0}(f)-s_{n}
, for some real-valued sequence
\langle s_{n}\rangle
converging to
0
. In fact, instead of using a specific ERM estimator/predictor as in the definition above, we may generalize the definition for any predictable sequence
\langle f_{n}\rangle=\{f_{n}:n\geq 1\}
, and study conditions on the quality of predictions
\langle f_{n}\rangle
to ensure optimality. We leave this investigation for future work.

The process
\{W_{n}:n\geq 0\}
and the associated tests
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle
depend on the ability to compute
\psi_{0}(f_{n})=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\log E_{Q}[e^{f(X)}]
, which may be computationally infeasible in some practical problems. We briefly discuss the computational aspects of implementing this scheme in
Section

3.2
, but for now, we focus on analyzing its statistical properties.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the conditions stated in
1
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the test
\tau_{\alpha}
introduced in
Definition

2
, is level-
\alpha
for any
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Furthermore, under
H_{1}
, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, the tests
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle
satisfy

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]}{\log(1/\alpha)/\gamma^{*}}\leq 1,\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma^{*}\equiv\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})\coloneqq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q).

(27)

This result can be interpreted as establishing the “first-order optimality” of the test introduced in
Definition

2
. Such results have been established for several composite parametric families, however, our scheme also applies in nonparametric settings as we illustrate in the next subsection. We defer the proof of this result to Appendix
D
.

Remark 3
.

The compactness and convexity assumption
(A1)
on the null distribution class allows the interchange of infimum and supremum in the definition of the terms
d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel\mathcal{P}_{0})
via the Donsker-Varadhan (DV) variational representation. It holds automatically in parametric models with compact parameter sets, and also in many interesting nonparametric problems as well. We discuss one such example in
Section

3.1
of uniformly bounded (from above and below) Hölder balls of densities.

Remark 4
.

Assumption
(A2)
says that the function class
\mathcal{F}
is rich enough so that the DV representation of the divergence to the null class is not loosened by restricting to
\mathcal{F}
. In other words, this holds when the log likelihood-ratio between the distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, and its reverse information projection
Q^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
can be approximated arbitrarily closely by a sequence of functions in
\mathcal{F}
. In
Section

3.1
, we present an example where the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with a universal kernel over a compact domain possesses this uniform approximation property.

Remark 5
.

The uniform bounded
(4+\delta)
moment assumption
(A3)
is a technical assumption; which in conjunction with a moment inequality (
5
) for discrete-time martingales gives us a key concentration result used in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
. Similarly, the uniformly bounded MGF condition under the null stated in
(A4)
guarantees that the one-step increments of our
e
-process in
Definition

2
is well-defined.

Remark 6
.

The assumption
(A5)
is a classical uniform law of large numbers assumption that requires the empirical processes indexed by
\mathcal{F}
to concentrate at rate
r_{n}
with high probability. This condition is satisfied by many “regular” function classes, such as the RKHS balls with bounded kernels that we consider in the next subsection. Importantly this condition is much weaker than the sequential learnability conditions, that are necessary to ensure the existence of prediction schemes with pathwise regret bounds. Our proof of
Theorem

3.1
proceeds without requiring the existence of such pathwise conditions.

Remark 7
.

We have made the specific choice of the ERM strategy in
Definition

2
and the corresponding learnability assumptions, such as condition
(A5)
, to keep our discussion concrete. With a view towards practical implementations, the statement and proof of
Theorem

3.1
can be generalized to allow for the choice of
f_{n}
to be an approximate ERM solution with vanishing error (as
n\to\infty
), or to other schemes for learning
f_{n}
with appropriate modifications to the assumptions. We leave a more thorough investigation of selecting
f_{n}
, and the minimal conditions needed for the optimality result like
Theorem

3.1
, to future work.

3.1
A Nonparametric Example

Definition 3
.

Throughout this section, we work with the compact alphabet
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
for some
d\geq 1
endowed with the usual Borel sigma algebra.

•

Let the null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
with
\Theta_{0}\equiv\Theta_{0}(c_{0},C_{0},L,\beta)
consists of densities (with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mu
) bounded away from
0
and
\infty
; that is,
\Theta_{0}=\{\theta:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty]:|\theta(x)-\theta(x^{\prime})|\leq L\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}^{\beta},\,c_{0}\leq|\theta(x)|\leq C_{0},\;\text{and}\;\int\theta d\mu=1\}
. Here, we have assumed
\beta\in(0,1]
,
L>0
,
0<c_{0}<C_{0}<\infty
.

•

Instead of choosing a single function class
\mathcal{F}
, we will work with a countable collection
\langle\mathcal{F}_{j}\rangle\equiv\{\mathcal{F}_{j}:j\geq 0\}
. Our tests will be based on a mixture of DV-
e
-processes associated with these function classes. In particular, for any
j\geq 0
, we set
\mathcal{F}_{j}
to be a
2^{j}
norm ball of an RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}
associated with a universal
(
Micchelli et al. 2006
)
positive-definite kernel
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. That is,
\mathcal{F}_{j}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}:\|f\|_{\kappa}\leq 2^{j}\}
. Additionally, we assume that the kernel
\kappa
is uniformly bounded
\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\kappa(x,x^{\prime})\leq 1
.

•

Finally, we consider a class of alternatives
\mathcal{P}_{1}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}=\left\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\setminus\mathcal{P}_{0}:\exists\theta\in\Theta_{0},\text{ with }P\ll P_{\theta},\;\frac{dP}{dP_{\theta}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathbbm{R}),\;\text{and}\;\frac{dP}{dP_{\theta}}>R_{0}>0\right\}.

(28)

Here,
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathbbm{R})
denotes the space of continuous real-valued functions on (the compact domain)
\mathcal{X}
.

With these terms, for any
j\geq 0
, we can define a process
\langle W_{n}^{j}\rangle=\{W_{n}^{j}:n\geq 0\}
following the approach described in
Definition

2
using the function class
\mathcal{F}_{j}
. These can be combined using weights
\langle c_{j}\rangle=\{c_{j}\in(0,1):j\geq 0\}
, with
c_{j}=\tfrac{6}{\pi^{2}(j+1)^{2}}
to define

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\geq 1:W_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\},\quad\text{with}\quad W_{n}=\sum_{j\geq 0}c_{j}W_{n}^{j},\quad\text{for all }n\geq 0.

(29)

Remark 8
.

The requirement that for every alternative
P
, there exists a null
\theta
, such that the likelihood ratio
dP/dP_{\theta}
is bounded away from
0
is imposed mainly for technical convenience. It ensures that the corresponding log-likelihood ratio is bounded continuous function on
\mathcal{X}
, which then simplifies the argument of the main result of this section stated below (
Proposition

1
). The statement of
Proposition

1
would remain valid even without this lower-bound condition. The proof, however, would then require a more delicate approximation argument, for instance, based on the truncation of the possibly unbounded log-likelihood ratios and an additional limiting step.

We can now state the main result of this section, which says that the tests defined in (
29
) are optimal in the limit of small
\alpha
.

Proposition 1
.

The tests
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle
defined in (
29
) are optimal (in the sense of
Definition

1
) for the problem with
(\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1})
as described in
Definition

3
.

The proof of this result is in
Appendix

E
.
As the proof of
Proposition

1
illustrates, the particular choice of a Hölder ball for the null density class is mainly a convenient way to obtain the structural properties required by our general theory. In particular, the uniform Hölder condition, together with the uniform bounds
0<c_{0}\leq\theta\leq C_{0}
implies that the family
\Theta_{0}
of null densities is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous on the compact domain
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
. By the Arzelá-Ascoli theorem (see
4
in
Appendix

A
),
\Theta_{0}
is therefore a compact subset of
\big(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathbbm{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)
and a simple continuity argument then shows that
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
is convex and weakly compact. This verifies the condition
(A1)
in
1
, which is needed to apply the DV variational representation and Sion’s minimax theorem in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

On the alternative side, the assumption that each
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
is absolutely continuous with respect to some
P_{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, with a bounded continuous likelihood ratio ensures that the optimal DV witness function exists and is a bounded continuous function. The universality of the kernel
\kappa
then implies that the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}
is dense (in
\sup
norm) in the space of continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
, which leads to a justification of
(A2)
in
1
. The remaining three conditions of
1
, namely
(A3), (A4)
and
(A5)
are straightforward consequences of the boundedness and learnability properties of RKHS balls. In this sense, the specific Hölder structure of the null is not essential for our result, and the fundamental requirements are (i) compactness in the appropriate topology of the null class, and (ii) the existence of a bounded continuous optimal log-likelihood ratio that can be well approximated by
\mathcal{F}
.

3.2
Practical Aspects

Our proposed construction of
W_{n}
in
Definition

2
relies on the ability to solve two optimization problems in each round:

•

Finding the test function
f_{n}
via the empirical risk minimization optimization task:
f_{n}\in\argmax_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(X_{i})-\psi_{0}(f)
.

•

Evaluating (or upper-bounding) the quantity
\psi_{0}(f_{n})=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[e^{f_{n}(X)}]
, used for defining the process
\langle W_{n}\rangle
in
Definition

2
.

In many problem instances, these operations can be computationally prohibitive. In this section, we briefly discuss this issue in two regimes. The first (§
3.2.1
) is a finite-dimensional setting where we may employ existing stochastic saddle-point methods to construct valid test supermartingales, and the second (§
3.2.2
) is an infinite dimensional setting where we discuss heuristic approaches based on large-scale generative models.

3.2.1
When Both
\Theta_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}
are Finite Dimensional

We first consider the special case in which both the null and the “test function class” admit finite dimensional and convex parameterizations, that is,
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
and
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\varphi}:\varphi\in\Phi\}
for some compact convex sets
\Theta_{0}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d_{0}}
and
\Phi\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d_{1}}
. The definition of
f_{n}
then becomes

\displaystyle f_{n}\in\argmax_{\varphi\in\Phi}\min_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\varphi,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{\varphi}(X_{i})-\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[e^{f_{\varphi}(X)}].

(30)

In practice, this task can be performed using a stochastic first-order ascent-descent scheme from the stochastic saddle-point literature, such as
(
Nemirovski et al. 2009
, § 3)
. Rather than studying a specific algorithm instantiation and its complexity analysis, we formalize the behavior of such algorithms through an
approximate optimization condition
: for some deterministic tolerance level
s_{n}\downarrow 0
, we assume that for all
n\geq 2
, we can construct an
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
-measurable test function
f_{n}=f_{\hat{\varphi}_{n}}
with
\hat{\varphi}_{n}\in\Phi
, such that we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{n}(X_{i})-\psi_{0}(f_{n})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\hat{\varphi}_{n},\theta)\geq\sup_{\varphi\in\Phi}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\varphi,\theta)-s_{n},\quad\text{w.p. }\geq 1-\epsilon_{n},

(31)

for some appropriately selected confidence level
\epsilon_{n}\in(0,1)
.
Here
s_{n}
may capture the upper bound on the duality gap ensured by the algorithm, and any other Monte Carlo approximations used in the implementation. Having obtained an
f_{n}
by the saddle-point solver (we can set
f_{1}
to be an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{F}
for
n=1
), the next task is to get an upper bound on
\psi_{0}(f_{n})
. Assuming sample-access to the null model class, we may estimate the map
\theta\mapsto\log E_{\theta}[e^{f_{n}(X)}]
with sufficient accuracy, and hence employ off-the-shelf convex solvers to obtain a random quantity
\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})
satisfying the high probability bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})\geq\psi_{0}(\hat{f}_{n})\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\right)\geq 1-\eta_{n},

(32)

for some small
\eta_{n}\in(0,1)
, and
n\geq 1
.
Now, plugging this
(f_{n},\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n}))
pair in
Definition

2
directly will not give us a valid
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}_{0}
since the required condition may be violated in the
\epsilon_{n}
-probability “bad” event. To address that, we will need to introduce an appropriate correction. We state one such formulation below under a boundedness assumption.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose there exist finite known constants
0<B_{n}<\infty
for all
n\geq 1
, such that

\displaystyle 0\leq\exp\left(f_{n}(X_{n})-\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})\right)\leq B_{n}\quad\text{almost surely},

(33)

where
(f_{n},\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n}))
were introduced in (
31
) and (
32
). Then, introduce the process

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{0}=1,\quad\widetilde{W}_{n}=\widetilde{W}_{n-1}\times\widetilde{E}_{n}\text{ for }n\geq 1,\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{E}_{n}=\frac{\exp\big(f_{n}(X_{n})-\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})\big)}{1+\eta_{n}B_{n}}.

(34)

Then the process
\langle\widetilde{W}_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\{\widetilde{W}_{n}:n\geq 0\}
is a test supermartingale under every
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Consequently,
\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\geq 1:\widetilde{W}_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
is a level-
\alpha
test for
\mathcal{P}_{0}
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Fix a null distribution
P_{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
(that is,
\theta\in\Theta_{0}
), and an
n\geq 1
, and let
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
also include any additional randomness used in the optimization algorithms for finding
(f_{n},\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n}))
. Then, if
G_{n}
denotes the “good event”
\{\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})\geq\psi_{0}(f_{n})\}
, we know that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(G_{n}^{c}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1})\leq\eta_{n}
. Then, observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{f_{n}(X_{n})-\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})}\boldsymbol{1}_{G_{n}}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{f_{n}(X_{n})-{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})}\boldsymbol{1}_{G_{n}}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of $G_{n}$})

(35)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\psi_{0}(f_{n})}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[e^{f_{n}(X_{n})}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle(f_{n}\text{ is }\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\text{-measurable})

(36)

\displaystyle\leq 1.

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of $\psi_{0}(f_{n})$})

(37)

Under the bad event, we use the boundedness assumption of
Proposition

2
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{f_{n}(X_{n})-\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})}\boldsymbol{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq B_{n}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(G_{n}^{c}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1})\leq B_{n}\eta_{n}.

(38)

Combining the previous two displays, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{f_{n}(X_{n})-\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\right]\leq 1+B_{n}\eta_{n},\quad\implies\quad\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\widetilde{E}_{n}\mid\mathcal{M}_{n-1}]\leq 1,

(39)

as required for any

Remark 9
.

While
Proposition

2
establishes the validity of the test based on
\langle\widetilde{W}_{n}\rangle
, for establishing the optimality, in addition to
1
a sufficient condition is that
s_{n}\to 0
,
\sum_{n\geq 1}\epsilon_{n}<\infty
, and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\eta_{n}B_{n}<\infty
. The summability of
\epsilon_{n}
ensures that our procedure uses “bad”
f_{n}
only finitely often, while
s_{n}\to 0
in addition to the summability of
B_{n}\eta_{n}
ensures that the long term drift of the process
\langle\log\widetilde{W}_{n}\rangle
is equal to
\gamma^{*}
. In particular,
\sum_{n\geq 1}\eta_{n}B_{n}<\infty
implies that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\log(1+\eta_{n}B_{n})<\infty
, and so the additional correction terms in the definition of
\widetilde{W}_{n}
only contribute an
\mathcal{O}(1)
term to the rejection (log-) boundary.

3.2.2
Heuristic for Larger Problems

To tackle the more practically relevant case when neither
\mathcal{P}_{0}
nor
\mathcal{F}
admit finite-dimensional parametrization, we may appeal to the recent advances in generative modeling in machine learning, such as diffusion models, invertible neural networks, or generative adversarial networks (GANs). In fact, the variational structure of the problem (
30
) closely resembles the training objective used in
f
-GANs.

A concrete practical strategy could be to replace the (possibly infinite dimensional) null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}
with a rich generative family
\{Q_{\eta}:\eta\in\mathcal{N}\}
. Here, each
Q_{\eta}
is implemented as a generator that maps some base distribution (like
Z\sim N(0,I_{d_{Z}})
for some
d_{Z}\geq 1
) into synthetic samples that approximate draws from a null distribution. Similarly, the test-function class
\mathcal{F}
may be approximated by another family of neural networks
\{f_{\varphi}:\varphi\in\Phi\}
with an appropriate architecture. This leads to the saddle-point problem in which the test-function network is trained to separate the observed data from the null samples, and the null generative-model
\eta
is trained to generate samples that are hard for the test-function network to distinguish. This exactly mirrors the GAN training pipeline, and we may use alternative stochastic gradient ascent-descent algorithms using appropriate mini-batches of data.
After sufficiently many iterations of this training, the learned critic function
f_{\hat{\phi}_{n}}
and the null generator
Q_{\hat{\eta}_{n}}
provide an approximate DV optimizer pair, and we can define a heuristic
e
-process by setting
f_{n}=f_{\hat{\phi}_{n}}
, and obtain
\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})
via a Monte Carlo approximation using the generator
Q_{\hat{\eta}_{n}}
.

Due to the nonconvexity/nonconcavity of the objective, the above approach does not yield a critic function
f_{n}=f_{\hat{\phi}_{n}}
satisfying the conditions required stated in
1
, nor is
\hat{\psi}_{0}(f_{n})
defined above guaranteed to be an upper bound on
\psi_{0}(f_{n})
. Nevertheless, this GAN-style construction is a natural and flexible heuristic to implement our general ideas to practical and high-dimensional problems, and we believe that exploring such implementations empirically as a promising direction for future work.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the problem of constructing optimal level-
\alpha
power-one tests for weakly compact and composite null hypotheses. Prior work has established a fundamental lower bound in the limit of small
\alpha
of the expected value of any such test in terms of the minimum relative entropy to the null (or
\mathrm{KL}_{\inf})
. That is, for any collection of tests
\langle\tau_{\alpha}\rangle=\{\tau_{\alpha}:\alpha\in(0,1)\}
for a given null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, and any
P\not\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we must have the following:

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]}{J(P,\mathcal{P}_{0},\alpha)}\geq 1,\quad\text{where}\quad J(P,\mathcal{P}_{0},\alpha)=\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)}.

(40)

In this paper, we established the existence of tests that are optimal in the sense that they satisfy
\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]/J(P,\mathcal{P}_{0},\alpha)\leq 1
. Towards this goal, we first considered the case of distributions supported on finite alphabets, and proved that a sequential test based on a class of
e
-processes proposed by
Wasserman et al. 2020
are optimal in the above-described sense. A crucial step in proving this result was a saddle-point representation of
\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})
, and an interchange of the order of
\inf
and
\sup
to obtain an “oracle
e
-process”. Using this as a starting point, we then considered a more general class of problems for which we can incrementally approximate the oracle
e
-process in a data-driven manner, and establish the optimality of the associated power-one test. To illustrate the power of this result, we discussed a detailed example of a nonparametric null parametrized by a Hölder ball of densities, and an alternative class for which the likelihood ratio is a continuous bounded function that can be approximated by elements of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Finally, we also discussed some computational aspects associated with implementing our proposed saddle-point based tests.

The results of this paper present several interesting directions for future work. Building on the discussion of
Section

3.2
, an immediate direction for investigation is to employ iterative algorithms from the literature on stochastic average approximation algorithms to design provably optimal tests for finite dimensional settings. On a more practical note, exploring how modern generative models may be used to construct tests for large-scale problems, such as by incorporating constraints associated with null classes is an important topic. Finally, extending our ideas beyond the
i.i.d.
case is another important direction.

Appendix A
Additional Background

In this section, we recall some key facts that serve as the foundations of the theoretical results presented in this paper. We begin by recalling a classical variational representation of relative entropy derived by
Donsker and Varadhan 1983
. We state a version of this result following the notation of
Polyanskiy and Wu 2025
.

Fact 2

(Donsker-Varadhan (DV) Representation)
.

Let
P,Q
denote two probability measures on some measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}})
, such that
P\ll Q
. Then, the relative entropy between
P
and
Q
is defined as
\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\log\tfrac{dP}{dQ(X)}\right]
, and satisfies the following variational representation:

\displaystyle d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]-\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{f(X)}],

(41)

where
\mathcal{C}_{Q}=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}:0<\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{f(X)}]<\infty\}
. Furthermore, if
P\ll Q
and
d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)<\infty
, the supremum in the expression above is attained at any
f^{*}\equiv f^{*}(P,Q)
of the form
f^{*}(x)=\log\tfrac{dP}{dQ}(x)+c
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. If
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
is a metric space with the Borel sigma-algebra, then we can replace
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
with
\mathcal{C}_{b}
, the class of all bounded continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
.

Next, we recall a general minimax theorem first obtained by
Sion 1958
, whose proof has subsequently been simplified, for example by
Komiya 1988
.

Fact 3

(Sion’s Minimax Theorem)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a compact convex subset of a topological vector space
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathbb{Y}
be a convex subset of a topological vector space
\mathcal{Y}
. Let
\Phi:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that

•

For every
x\in\mathbb{X}
, the map
y\mapsto\Phi(x,y)
is quasi-convex and lower semicontinuous on
\mathbb{Y}
.

•

For every
y\in\mathbb{Y}
, the map
x\mapsto\Phi(x,y)
is quasi-concave and upper semicontinuous on
\mathbb{X}
.

Then, we have the following:

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\;\sup_{y\in\mathbb{Y}}\Phi(x,y)\;=\;\sup_{y\in\mathbb{Y}}\;\inf_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\Phi(x,y).

(42)

We now recall a result
[
Simmons 1963
, Theorem C, Pg. 126]
that allows us to establish the compactness condition needed to apply the minimax theorem in our example discussed in
Section

3.1
.

Fact 4

(Arzelá-Ascoli)
.

Let
(\mathbb{X},\rho)
denote a compact metric space, and let
\Theta
denote a closed subset of
\big(C(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)
, the space of continuous functions on
\mathbb{X}
endowed with the
\sup
-norm
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}|f(x)|
. Then,
\Theta
is compact if and only if it is bounded and equicontinuous.

Finally, we recall a simplified version of a martingale inequality derived by
Pinelis 1994
that is used in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Fact 5

(adapted from Theorem 4.1 of
Pinelis 1994
)
.

Suppose
\{Y_{i}:i\geq 1\}
denotes a real-valued martingale difference sequence with respect to some filtration
\{\mathcal{M}_{i}:i\geq 0\}
. Define the partial sums,
S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}
for any
k\in[n]
, and the maximum values
S^{*}_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|S_{k}|
and
Y^{*}_{n}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Y_{i}|
. Then, for every
p\geq 2
, there exist finite positive constants
C_{1,p},C_{2,p}
(independent of
n
), such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left[|S^{*}_{n}|^{p}\right]\right)^{1/p}\leq C_{1,p}\left(\mathbb{E}[(Y^{*}_{n})^{p}]\right)^{1/p}+C_{2,p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{M}_{i-1}\right]\right)^{p/2}\right]\right)^{1/p}.

(43)

Appendix B
DV
e
-process Instantiations

In this section, we instantiate the general
e
-process introduced in
Definition

2
to two important settings: (i) finite alphabet with composite null, and (ii) testing the mean of bounded observations.

B.1
Finite Alphabet Setting

We return to the case of testing with observations lying in some finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
, and a null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
for some compact convex subset
\Theta_{0}
of the
(m-1)
-dimensional simplex (here
P_{\theta}
denotes the distribution on
\mathcal{X}
with pmf
\theta
). Then, let us introduce the class of functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{\log{\varphi}:\varphi\in\Phi\},\quad\text{with}\quad\Phi\coloneqq[\epsilon,1/\epsilon]^{m}.

(44)

This
\mathcal{F}
corresponds to all alternative distributions with pmf
\theta
, such that there exists a
\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}
with
\theta/\theta_{0}\in\Phi
. Then, the function
f_{n}
in round
n\geq 2
is defined as

\displaystyle f_{n}=\log\varphi_{n},\quad\text{with}\quad\varphi_{n}\in\argmax_{\varphi\in\Phi}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log\varphi[X_{i}]-\max_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\log\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\theta_{0}[x]\varphi[x].

(45)

It is easy to verify that this is a finite-dimensional convex-concave saddle point problem and can be solved with sufficient accuracy using existing methods. Having obtained the parameter
\varphi_{n}
(or function
f_{n}
), we can then approximate
\psi_{0}(f_{n})
, by solving

\displaystyle\max_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\log\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\theta_{0}[x]\varphi_{n}[x].

(46)

This is again a convex program over a compact convex domain, and can be solved using off-the-shelf solvers with high accuracy. With these two terms at hand, we can then construct the e-process, perhaps with some additional correction if necessary, as discussed in
Section

3.2.1
.

B.2
Bounded Mean Testing

We now discuss the application of our DV
e
-process for testing the mean of a stream of bounded observations. In particular, assume that
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
, and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\},\quad\text{for some known}\quad\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{X}=[0,1].

(47)

In other words, the null class consists of all distributions supported on
\mathcal{X}
with mean exactly equal to
\mu_{0}
. Let us introduce the test-function class for some small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\left\{x\mapsto\log(1+\varphi(x-\mu_{0})):\varphi\in\Phi_{\epsilon}\right\},\quad\text{with}\quad\Phi_{\epsilon}=\left[-\frac{1-\epsilon}{1-\mu_{0}},\frac{1-\epsilon}{\mu_{0}}\right].

(48)

The specific choice of this particular test function class is motivated by two reasons. The first is that for any
f\equiv f_{\varphi}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(f_{\varphi})=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\exp\left(\log(1+\varphi(X-\mu_{0}))\right)]\right)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\log\left(1+\varphi\left(EE_{Q}[X]-\mu_{0}\right)\right)=0.

(49)

The second reason is that a duality argument by
Honda and Takemura 2010
tells us that for any
P\not\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we have the following representation of the

term

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})=\sup_{\varphi\in\left[-\frac{1}{1-\mu_{0}},\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\right]}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\log(1+\varphi(X-\mu_{0}))].

(50)

Together, these two results imply that our choice of
\mathcal{F}
(with
\epsilon>0
) can be used to design optimal tests against alternatives for which
\varphi^{*}\equiv\varphi^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})
lies in the
\epsilon
-interior of the domain in the above dual representation.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\varphi^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})\in\Phi_{\epsilon}\},\quad\text{which implies}\quad d_{\mathcal{F}}(P\parallel\mathcal{P}_{0})=\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0}),

(51)

for all
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
. We can now state the main result of this section.

Proposition 3
.

Given a stream of
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
-valued observations
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}
drawn

from a distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\cup\mathcal{P}_{1}
, with the classes
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
introduced above in (
47
) and (
51
) respectively. Then, define the process
\langle W_{n}\rangle
, with
W_{0}=W_{1}=1
and

\displaystyle W_{n}=W_{n-1}\times\left(1+\varphi_{n}(X_{n}-\mu_{0})\right),\quad\text{with}\quad\varphi_{n}\in\argmax_{\varphi\in\Phi_{\epsilon}}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log\left(1+\varphi(X_{i}-\mu_{0})\right),

(52)

for all
n\geq 2
. Then, with
\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\geq 1:W_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tau_{\alpha}]}{\log(1/\alpha)/\gamma^{*}(P,\mathcal{P}_{0})}\leq 1,\quad\text{for all}\quad P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}.

(53)

In other words, the level-
\alpha
tests

are optimal in the sense of
Definition

1
.

Proof.

This result follows by simply verifying the conditions of
1
, and then appealing to
Theorem

3.1
. Since all the distributions are supported on

. The convexity of

is a simple consequence of the linearity of expectation. Together, these two observations show that the condition
(A1)
is satisfied by

. Condition

is ensured by definition of

, and

is also trivially satisfied since

Appendix C
Proof of
Theorem

2.1
(Finite Alphabet)

The level-
\alpha
property of this test has been established by
Wasserman et al. 2020
. We include the details here for completeness. The property essentially hinges on the observation that under
H_{0}
, if
\theta_{0}
is the true parameter, then

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}=\frac{\theta_{J}^{n}[X^{n}]}{\widehat{\theta}^{n}_{0}[X^{n}]}=\frac{\theta_{J}^{n}[X^{n}]}{\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\theta^{n}[X^{n}]}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\leq}}\;\frac{\theta_{J}^{n}[X^{n}]}{\theta_{0}^{n}[X^{n}]}\eqcolon L_{n}^{\theta_{0}}.

(54)

It is easy to verify that under
\theta_{0}

is a nonnegative martingale with an initial value of
L_{0}^{\theta_{0}}=1
, which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)=\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\exists n\geq 1:W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left(\exists n\geq 1:L_{n}^{\theta_{0}}\geq 1/\alpha\right)\leq\alpha,

(55)

where the last inequality follows from an application of Ville’s inequality. Taking a supremum over all
\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}
gives us the first part of
Theorem

2.1
.

Expected Stopping Time.

To study the expected stopping time property, consider the alternative with the true parameter
\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1}
. By the regret of the universal compression scheme, we know that

\displaystyle S_{n}\coloneqq\log W^{\mathrm{UI}}_{n}\geq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\log\theta^{n}[X^{n}]-\log{\widehat{\theta}^{n}_{0}[X^{n}]}-\mathrm{Regret}_{n},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathrm{Regret}_{n}=\frac{m-1}{2}\log n+C_{m},

(56)

for some constant
C_{m}
independent of
\theta_{1}
.
Let
\widehat{p}_{n}
denote the empirical probability mass function (pmf) based on
X^{n}
. Observe that for any
\theta\in\Theta

\displaystyle\log\theta^{n}[X^{n}]=n\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widehat{p}_{n}[x]\log\theta[x]=n\left(-H(\widehat{p}_{n})-d_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\theta)\right).

(57)

Here
H(\cdot)
denotes the Shannon entropy for discrete distributions, and
d_{\text{KL}}
is the relative entropy or KL-divergence.
By the nonnegativity of relative entropy, this implies that
\widehat{p}_{n}
is also the MLE (over all
\Theta

X^{n}
, which means that

\displaystyle S_{n}\geq-nH(\widehat{p}_{n})-\log\widehat{\theta}^{n}_{0}[X^{n}]-\mathrm{Regret}_{n}.

(58)

Now, we analyze the second term above to see that

\displaystyle\log\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\theta_{0}^{n}[X^{n}]

\displaystyle=n\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widehat{p}_{n}\log\theta_{0}[x]=-n\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\left(H(\widehat{p}_{n})+d_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\theta_{0})\right)

(59)

\displaystyle=-nH(\widehat{p}_{n})-nd_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\Theta_{0}),

(60)

where we define
d_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\Theta_{0})
as
\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\theta_{0})
. On combining (
58
) and (

), we get

\displaystyle S_{n}\geq nd_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\Theta_{0})-\mathrm{Regret}_{n}.

(61)

Now, we recall the Donsker-Varadhan (DV) variational representation
[
Polyanskiy and Wu 2025
, Theorem 4.6]
of relative entropy, to observe that

\displaystyle d_{\text{KL}}(p\parallel\Theta_{0})=\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]-\log E_{\theta_{0}}[e^{f}]\right)\eqcolon\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\Phi(f,\theta_{0}).

(62)

Now, observe that the function
\Phi
is concave in

\theta_{0}
(due to the concavity of

\Theta_{0}

\mathbbm{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
is convex. Hence, the hypotheses of Sion’s minimax theorem (recalled in Fact

\displaystyle d_{\text{KL}}(p\parallel\Theta_{0})=\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]-\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[e^{f}]=\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]-\psi_{0}(f),

(63)

where
\psi_{0}(f)\coloneqq\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[e^{f}]

, which gives a lower bound in the equality above, and using this with (

\displaystyle S_{n}\geq nd_{\text{KL}}(\widehat{p}_{n}\parallel\Theta_{0})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{p}_{n}}[f]-\psi_{0}(f)-\mathrm{Regret}_{n}=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-n\psi_{0}(f)}_{\coloneqq S_{n}(f)}-\mathrm{Regret}_{n}.

(64)

Thus, the stopping time

\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\geq 1:S_{n}\geq\log(1/\alpha)\}

\tau_{\alpha}
for arbitrary

\displaystyle f\in\argmax_{f^{\prime}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[f^{\prime}]-\psi_{0}(f^{\prime})\quad\implies\quad\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[f]-\psi_{0}(f)=d_{\text{KL}}(\theta_{1}\parallel\Theta_{0})=\gamma_{\theta_{1}}.

(65)

Next, let
\theta^{\dagger}
denote any minimizer in the definition of
\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})
; that is,

\displaystyle\theta^{\dagger}\in\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}d_{\text{KL}}(\theta_{1}\parallel\theta),

(66)

whose existence is justified by the compactness of
\Theta_{0}
, and the lower-semicontinuity of relative entropy. If
\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})<\infty
, then we must have
\theta^{\dagger}[x]>0
for all

such that
\theta_{1}[x]>0
. Finally, note that the optimal

defined in (
65
) is of the form
f(x)=\log\theta_{1}[x]/\theta^{\dagger}[x]+c
, and we can set

without loss of generality.
With these choices of

and
\theta^{\dagger}
, we can conclude the following:

•

Since
f(x)=\log\theta_{1}[x]/\theta^{\dagger}[x]
and
\theta^{\dagger}[x]>0
for all
x
such that
\theta_{1}[x]>0
, we can observe that

\displaystyle f(x)-\psi_{0}(f)\leq f(x)-\sup_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta_{0}}\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\theta_{0}[y]e^{f(y)}\leq f(x)-\log\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\theta^{\dagger}[y]\times\frac{\theta_{1}[y]}{\theta^{\dagger}[y]}=f(x).

(67)

Hence, the one-step increment of
S_{n}(f)
is upper bounded by
C_{f}\coloneqq\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\log\theta_{1}[x]/\theta^{\dagger}[x]
.

•

The stopping time
T_{f}
has finite expectation. To see this, note that
\mathrm{Regret}_{n}
is logarithmic in
n
, so there exists some finite
n_{0}
, such that for all

, we have
\mathrm{Regret}_{n}+\log(1/\alpha)\leq n\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})/2
. Hence, for all

larger than

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(T_{f}>n)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\left(S_{n}(f)<\log(1/\alpha)+\mathrm{Regret}_{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\left(S_{n}(f)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[S_{n}(f)]<-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[S_{n}(f)]\right).

(68)

Since the
i.i.d.
increments of

are bounded, we can apply Hoeffding’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[T_{f}]\leq n_{0}+\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\left(T_{f}>n\right)\leq n_{0}+\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}e^{-cn}<\infty,

(69)

for some constant
c>0
depending on the range of increments of

.

Together, these two facts imply that we can use Wald’s identity to conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[S_{T_{f}}(f)]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[T_{f}]\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[f(X)-\psi_{0}(f)]\leq\log(1/\alpha)+\frac{m-1}{2}\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[T_{f}]+C_{m}+C_{f}.

(70)

Let
\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})
denote
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[f(X)-\psi_{0}(f)]
, and observe that with
y=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[T_{f}]
, we have

\displaystyle y\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})\leq\log(1/\alpha)+\frac{m-1}{2}\log y+C_{m}+C_{f}.

(71)

By some standard calculations (see
Lemma

4
for details) we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[T_{f}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\gamma^{*}(\theta_{1},\Theta_{0})}\left(1+o(1)\right),

(72)

where
o(1)
indicates terms that go to zero as
\alpha\to 0
for a fixed
\theta_{1}
. This completes our proof. ∎

Throughout the proof, we will use

Lemma 1
.

The class
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{0}\}
is a weakly compact subset of

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
, and
\Theta_{0}
consists of
(L,\beta)
Hölder continuous functions, with
c_{0}\leq\theta(x)\leq C_{0}
for all

. Viewing
\Theta_{0}
as a subset of

endowed with the sup norm

•

\|\theta\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\theta|\leq C_{0}

\Theta_{0}
is a bounded subset of

•

If
\theta
denotes the limit of a sequence
\{\theta_{i}:i\geq 1\}\subset\Theta_{0}
in
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, then

is also
(L,\beta)
Hölder continuous. A similar argument shows that

must also be a density with
c_{0}\leq\theta(x)\leq C_{0}
. In other words,
\Theta_{0}
is a closed subset of
(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathbbm{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.

•

Finally, note that
\Theta_{0}
is also equicontinuous. That is, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta=(\epsilon/L)^{1/\beta}
, such that for all

with

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}|\theta(x)-\theta(x^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon

Hence, the conditions for applying the Arzelá-Ascoli theorem (recalled in Fact

is a compact subset of

To conclude the proof, let

denote any element of
(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X}),\mathbbm{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

converging to

in

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi dP_{\theta}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi dP_{\theta_{i}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\theta_{i}(x)dx-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\theta(x)dx\right\rvert\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\|\theta-\theta_{i}\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}dx\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\;0.

(96)

This implies that
\theta_{i}\to\theta
implies
P_{\theta_{i}}\Rightarrow P_{\theta}
; or, that the map

is continuous (w.r.t. the weak topology on

).

is the continuous image of a compact set, hence compact.
∎

Now, suppose that the true distribution is

\theta

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}(1-\varepsilon)-r_{n}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}_{n-1}}[f_{n}(X)]-\psi_{0}(f_{n})\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{P}[f_{n}(X)]-\psi_{0}(f_{n})+r_{n},

Lemma 4
.

For some positive constants
K
and
L
, let
y^{*}
denote the largest solution of the equation

\displaystyle y\;=\;K+L\log y.

(119)

Then, we have the following implication:

\displaystyle\frac{K}{L}>\max\left\{1-\log L,\;\frac{\log K+3}{2}\right\}\quad\implies\quad y^{*}\leq K+L\log K+2L.

(120)

In other words, as
K\uparrow\infty
with
L
fixed, we have
y^{*}=\left(K+L\log K\right)(1+o(1))
, with
o(1)
indicating a term that vanishes to
0
as
K\uparrow\infty
.

Proof.

The function
f(y)=y-K-L\log y
is convex on the domain
(0,\infty)
, and it satisfies
\lim_{y\downarrow 0}f(y)=\lim_{y\uparrow\infty}f(y)=+\infty
. Hence it achieves its minimum value at
y
such that
f^{\prime}(y)=1-L/y=0
or
y_{\min}=L
. The function value at
y_{\min}
is
L-K-L\log L<0
by the assumption on
K/L
. Thus the equation
f(y)=0
has two solutions, say
y_{0}<y_{1}
, and our goal is to get an upper bound on
y^{*}=y_{1}
. To do this, note that it is sufficient to find a
y>L
such that
f(y)>0
. Let us try a value
y=K+L\log K+C
for some constant
C>0
that we will specify later, and observe that
f(y)=L\log K+C-L\log(K+L\log K+C)=C-L\log\left(1+\frac{L\log K+C}{K}\right)\geq C-L\left(\frac{L\log K+C}{K}\right)
, where we used the fact that
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
. Now, suppose
2L\leq C\leq 3L
, which gives us the bound
f(y)\geq C-L^{2}\left(\frac{\log K+3}{K}\right)
which is greater than
0
by the assumption on
(L,K)
. Hence, we can conclude that
y^{*}\leq L\log K+K+2L
.

∎
