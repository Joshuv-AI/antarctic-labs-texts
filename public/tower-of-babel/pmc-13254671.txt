Direct assessment of total cavopulmonary connection power loss with interventional cardiac magnetic resonance in Fontan circulation
Authors: Hamza Dahshi, Tarique Hussain, Surendranath Veeram Reddy, Yousef Arar, Maria Gusseva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFontan physiology, reliant on a single ventricle and a passive total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) flow, is vulnerable to inefficiency. Linking TCPC power loss to cardiac function can provide valuable insights into Fontan hemodynamics. This study quantifies direct TCPC power loss from invasive cardiovascular magnetic resonance (iCMR) data and its relationship to conduit geometry, fenestration flow, and myocardial contractility pre- and post-Fontan fenestration test occlusion (FFTO).MethodsTwenty post-Fontan patients underwent iCMR, with 9 also having a FFTO. iCMR catheter-based pressures were combined with phase-contrast CMR flow in the vena cavae and pulmonary arteries. Three-dimensional (3D) whole-heart imaging provided conduit cross-sectional areas (CSA). A patient-specific biomechanical model estimated myocardial contractility from iCMR data. In-vivo TCPC blood flow indexed power loss (iPL) was calculated at baseline in 20 patients, and post-FFTO in 9.ResultsMean (±SD) at baseline was 0.24 ± 0.15 and decreased significantly from 0.27 ± 0.14 to 0.13 ± 0.10 in 9 patients post-FFTO (p = 0.010). Fenestration flow explained 46.5% of variation in iPL at baseline. Higher iPL was associated with higher myocardial contractility (R2 = 0.216, p = 0.004) pre-FFTO, and with lower myocardial contractility (R2 = 0.43, p = 0.054) post-FFTO. Higher iPL correlated with smaller conduit CSA (R2 = 0.26, p = 0.030) at baseline.ConclusionIncreased iPL was related to increased fenestration flow, increased contractility, and decreased conduit narrowing at baseline. Post-FFTO, increased iPL was associated with decreased contractility, suggesting that acute fenestration closure might cause preload suppression in the ventricle. This study offers a clinically relevant framework to obtain mechanistic insights into Fontan physiology and guide personalized strategies for fenestration management.Keywords: Fontan circulation, Total cavopulmonary connection, Interventional cardiac magnetic resonance, Power loss, Fenestration flow

Background

In patients with Fontan palliation, the cardiovascular system undergoes significant adaptation to maintain systemic, venous, and pulmonary circulations in series, all supported by a single functional ventricle [1]. Unlike a normal heart, where the right ventricle pumps blood into the pulmonary circulation, Fontan physiology relies on passive flow from the systemic veins into the lungs via the total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC). Over time, this unique configuration imposes considerable strain on the function of the cardiovascular system and can be associated with impaired systolic/diastolic ventricular function, inefficient pulmonary flow dynamics, distorted hepatic flow, and increased systemic venous pressure [2], [3]. Therefore, it is critical to understand the efficiency of flow dynamics in Fontan circulation and their association with mechanical function of the ventricle.

Numerous studies have analyzed flow power loss in Fontan circulation (sometimes referred to as the rate of energy loss), with particular focus on the TCPC region to identify compromised flow patterns [4], [5], [6], [7]. Power loss in the cardiovascular system arises from the viscous properties of blood, whereby mechanical power generated by ventricular contraction is dissipated as heat due to viscous friction of the blood flow [8]. In patients with Fontan circulation, several Fontan-specific vascular structures can exacerbate circulatory power loss. For example, flow collision from superior (SVC) and inferior (IVC) vena cavae has been shown to increase blood flow power loss within TCPC. Additional factors such as stenosis of right (RPA) or left (LPA) pulmonary arteries [9], [10], the presence of fenestration [9], narrowing of the Fontan conduit [5], [6] and development of venous collaterals can further reduce flow efficiency. Elevated TCPC power loss has been linked to reduced exercise capacity [11], [12], cardiac function [13], [14], and increased central venous pressure [14], suggesting that it may serve as a clinically relevant marker of Fontan circulation efficiency in patients with Fontan circulation.

Most insights into the relationship between TCPC power loss and Fontan conduit geometry were derived from computational fluid dynamic studies using retrospective imaging data [4]. These studies consistently demonstrated that conduit geometry is a key determinant of the blood flow power loss within the TCPC. Narrow conduit diameters (between 10–12 mm) [12], [15] or focal conduit stenosis [6], [10] were associated with increased blood flow power loss, primary attributable to the viscous energy dissipation of the blood. Conversely, excessively large conduit diameters (>20 mm) also demonstrated increased power loss due to increased blood flow recirculation and stagnation within the conduit [6], [15]. In addition to that conduit-caval alignment plays a critical role, as direct collision of SVC and IVC flows can induce recirculating and helical flow patterns increasing the power loss within TCPC [16], [17], [18]. While fenestration is often used to decompress TCPC and reduce central venous pressure [19], as well as to augment cardiac output [20], knowledge of its hemodynamic impact on the overall energetic efficiency of Fontan circulation remains limited. A recent computational fluid dynamic analysis revealed that a high fenestration flow was associated with increased blood power loss with increased turbulence of blood flow within TCPC, with only subtle reduction in the central venous pressure, suggesting a trade-off between the potential benefits and energetic costs of fenestration [21].

While computational fluid dynamics can estimate intravascular pressure [5] (i.e., an important component of energy loss equation) and provide patient-specific mechanical description of fluid dynamics, their physiological interpretation is limited by several assumptions [22], such as a choice of boundary conditions for the computational fluid dynamic simulations, mechanical properties of the vessel walls [23], and accuracy of generated geometric meshes, to name a few. Little published data exist on in vivo power loss computed directly from clinical data (e.g., using invasive catheterization pressure and Doppler flow velocity), independent of computational fluid dynamic framework. Moreover, most analyses have focused exclusively on the TCPC region, without exploring its association with the mechanical performance of the ventricle. Thus, there is a gap in understanding of in vivo TCPC power loss and its impact on cardiac function in patients with Fontan circulation.

This study aims to quantify in vivo TCPC blood flow power loss of 20 patients with Fontan circulation by using invasive cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (iCMR), which allows simultaneous acquisition of catheter pressure and volume data. Previous work demonstrated that iCMR provides reliable and reproducible hemodynamic assessment in patients with congenital heart disease [24]. We aim to investigate the relationship between TCPC power loss and Fontan conduit geometry (i.e., conduit narrowing). In addition to that, we will explore a system-level effect of Fontan circulation by analyzing a relationship between TCPC power loss and ventricular mechanical properties. In a subgroup of 9 patients undergoing Fontan fenestration test occlusion (FFTO), we will analyze the immediate response of fenestration closure on TCPC power loss and ventricular mechanics. A biomechanical model of the heart will be used to derive patient-specific ventricular mechanical properties (i.e., myocardial contractility). We hypothesize that TCPC power loss can be associated with Fontan conduit geometry and has a meaningful relationship with myocardial contractile function.

Methods

Study population

The study included 20 post-Fontan patients who underwent iCMR procedure. FFTO procedure was performed in 9 out of 20 patients. The data collection was approved by Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of the UT Southwestern Medical Center, Dallas (IRB STU 032017–061) and was performed at the CMR catheterization suite at Children’s Medical Center in Dallas, Texas, USA. Pediatric patients with completed Fontan palliation were retrospectively identified at our institution. All patients were referred for diagnostic or interventional iCMR either due to effort intolerance or desaturation. Acquired iCMR datasets were reviewed to ensure the availability of all data required for biomechanical model construction and TCPC power loss calculation. These data included catheterization pressures measurements in the main pumping ventricle and descending aorta (AO), left and right pulmonary arteries (LPA and RPA, respectively), and superior and inferior vena cavae (SVC and IVC, respectively), as well as CMR volumes of the main pumping ventricle and phase-contrast flows in descending AO, LPA, RPA, SVC and IVC. Only patients with complete datasets were included in the study. No exclusions were applied based on age, sex, presence of fenestration, or systemic ventricular morphology.

Interventional CMR data collection

Simultaneous acquisition of catheterization pressures, CMR volumes, and phase-contrast flows was achieved by iCMR procedure as described in previous works [24], [25]. Catheter pressure recordings were guided by the real-time 1.5T CMR and were projected at the bedside by using a shielded projector system (Philips Healthcare Best, the Netherlands). A 6-French Arrow, Balloon Wedge-Pressure Catheter (Model AI-07124 and/or AI-07126, Teleflex, Wayne, Pennsylvania, USA) was used. The catheter had a balloon-tip with dilute gadolinium (1-part gadolinium to 99-parts saline) and was guided with MR-conditional guidewire (angled-tip Emeryglide MRWire, Nano4Imaging, Aachen, Germany) to reach the required compartments of the heart and vasculature. CMR myocardial volumetric analysis employed retrospectively ECG-triggered cine bSSFP sequence (SENSE factor 2, slice-thickness 10 mm, special resolution 2 × 2 mm, 30 cardiac phases). Phase contract flow data were acquired over 40 cardiac phases, TE/TR = 2.7/4.4 ms, with two signal averages during free breathing, 2 × 2 × 8 mm resolution, SENSE factor 2, velocity encoding gradient at 25% above the maximum expected velocity in each vessel with standard acquisition for venous flow at 80 cm/s without contrast. Pressure and volume data were collected in the iCMR suite under the same anesthetic at 21% FiO2. All cases were done under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation, positive pressure ventilation with minimal positive end-expiratory pressure. A carefully titrated balanced anesthesia technique was used to maintain hemodynamic stability. A combination of sedative- hypnotics (such as midazolam, ketamine, dexmetetomidine, propofol), opioids (such as fentanyl), muscle relaxants (rocuronium), and inhalational agents (sevoflurane or isoflurane) was utilized.

Heart volumetrics and circulatory system hemodynamics

Cine CMR provided ventricular end-systolic/-diastolic volumes and ventricular myocardial mass. Phase-contrast CMR provided flow rates (Q) and vessel cross-sectional areas (CSA) from the following compartments: descending AO, RPA, and LPA, IVC, and SVC. (Fig. 1). Fontan conduit cross-sectional area (CSA) was obtained by a double-oblique measurement technique using 3D whole-heart inversion recovery bSSFP images (1.6 to 1.8 mm3 isotropic acquisition). Catheterization pressures were recorded in the main pumping ventricle, descending AO, femoral artery, RPA, LPA, IVC, and SVC.

During FFTO procedure, hemodynamic status was reevaluated following 10 min of Fontan fenestration occlusion. Post-FFTO pressures were consistently collected in femoral artery, LPA, RPA, IVC, and SVC. Note that pressures in the descending AO and ventricle were not available in post-FFTO conditions for the majority of patients. Post-FFTO phase-contrast flows were collected in LPA, RPA, IVC, and SVC. In addition, CMR was not re-acquired post-FFTO due to procedural constraints. Accordingly, ventricular mass and myocardial stiffness for the biomechanical model in post-FFTO state were extrapolated from pre-FFTO CMR acquisition (as described in the following section), whilst hemodynamic changes following the FFTO in descending AO, LPA, RPA, IVC, and SVC were reevaluated using post-FFTO simultaneously acquired catheter pressure and phase-contrast flow data.

Biomechanical model of the heart

A biomechanical model of the heart was used to derive patient-specific myocardial contractility from measured pressure and volume data in the ventricle and systemic circulation [26]. The model included three compartments as follows: (1) heart model compartment, representing contractile and viscoelastic behavior of a ventricular cavity [26], [27]; (2) the circulation model, representing distal and proximal mechanical properties of AO and systemic circulation; (3) the valve model, representing atrioventricular (AV) and AO valves mechanics, namely valvular stenosis and/or regurgitation (Fig. 2).

A ventricle is assumed to be a sphere with thickness (d) radius (R).Pat,Pv,Par,Pd and Pvs are pressures in the atria, ventricle, artery, distal circulation, and venous system, respectively. AV and VOT mechanics are given by forward and backward resistances (RforAV and RbackAV, RforVOT and RbackVOT, respectively). Circulation system is represented by a two-stage Windkessel, where Rpand Cpare proximal and distal resistance and capacitance, respectively and Rdand Cd are distal resistance and capacitance, respectively. Biomechanical model simulates a function of one cardiac beat. Myocardial contraction was represented using biophysical laws describing actin-myosin cross-bridge dynamics, inspired by Huxley’s sliding filament theory [28]. Myosin cross-bridges were modeled as series of springs generating active stress (τc) and active stiffness (kc) to reproduce active contraction of the myocardium:

where parameter u(t) is an electrical activation function representing intracellular calcium kinetics with u>0 producing contraction and with u<0 – relaxation. The function n0efib∈[0,1] represents the fraction of recruited myosin heads at a given sarcomere extension efib to represent the Frank-Starling law of a sarcomere length-dependent force generation [27]. Parameter α is a bridge destruction rate upon rapid sarcomere length change [27]. σ0 and k0 are the active stress and active stiffness, respectively, developed by the sarcomere during ventricular systole under optimal efib, when n0=1, reflecting the inotropic state of the myocardium. Thus, σ0 is an estimated parameter of the model reflecting intrinsic myocardial force-generating capacity. In this study, σ0 will be estimated through inverse modeling from measured ventricular pressure and volume data (see the subsequent paragraphs), and it will be further referred to as contractility [26], [27].

Myocardial relaxation was captured through viscoelastic mechanical laws describing the passive behavior of collagen-rich components of the myocardium [29].

To derive patient-specific myocardial contractility, model parameters form the heart, circulation, and valve model compartments were calibrated such that simulated ventricular pressure and volume, and AO pressure and flow waveforms corresponded to the patient-specific iCMR data. Model parameters were calibrated sequentially as described in our previous clinical works [30], [31], [32].

First, the measured AO flow was imposed on the circulation model (i.e., represented by a 2-stage Windkessel system [33] to adjust proximal resistance (Rp), and distal resistance and capacitance (Rd and Cd, respectively) until simulated AO pressure reached measured AO peak systolic and end-diastolic pressure (PSP and EDP, respectively). Then, the calibrated circulation model was coupled to the heart model.

In the heart model, the ventricle was assumed to be a sphere. Sphere thickness and radius in reference configuration (i.e., V0 - hypothetical ventricular geometry at 0 mmHg ventricular pressure) were derived from patient’s CMR measurements of ventricular mass and end-diastolic volume (EDV) using Klotz’s formula [34]. Myocardial stiffness parameter was calibrated by imposing measured EDP on the reference configuration and allowing the ventricle to passively extend until measured EDV was reached.

In patients with valvular insufficiency, the parameters of the valve model were calibrated by adjusting backward resistance of AV valve (RbackAV) and forward/backward resistance of AO valve (RbackVOT/RforVOT) to reproduce clinically observed degrees of valvular regurgitation and stenosis.

Finally, myocardial contractility was adjusted such that simulated ventricular peak systolic pressure and end-systolic volume, and AO forward flow matched measured data for each patient. The resulting set of model parameters defined the morphology of simulated ventricular pressure and volume and AO pressure and flow waveforms. The model was considered calibrated when model-derived waveforms closely matched peaks and EDV and EDP of measured waveforms. Calibrated parameters were considered descriptive of patient’s ventricular mechanical function (Fig. 3) with myocardial contractility being descriptive of patient’s systolic function [30]. Within this framework, myocardial contractility (in kPa) represents the maximum level of inotropic stress developed by the myocardium during systole [26].

Statistical analysis

Linear regression analysis was performed between myocardial contractility & iPL, and iPL & minimum conduit area, fenestration flow and iPL pre- and post-FFTO. The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was used to compare baseline data to post-FFTO measurements, and correlations were established using Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient. All statistical analysis was performed in R (v4.4.0, R Core Team 2024).

Results

Patient demographics

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are shown in Table 1. The study cohort included 15 patients with extracardiac TCPCs and 5 with lateral tunnels. Fifteen patients had hypoplastic left heart syndrome, four patients had tricuspid atresia, and one had atrioventricular septal defect. Five patients had left pumping ventricles, and fifteen had right pumping ventricles. Seventeen patients had patent fenestrations with fenestration flow ranging between 0 and 10.8 mL/beat. Mean ± SD age and body surface area (BSA) were 10.5 ± 3.9 years old and 1.04 ± 0.31 m2, respectively.

The calculated PL was 17.5 ± 10.8 mW at baseline, and iPL was 0.24 ± 0.15 at baseline for 20 patients. In 9 patients undergoing FFTO, iPL was 0.27 ± 0.14 and 0.13 ± 0.10 at baseline and post-FFTO, respectively (p = 0.01).

Linear regression analysis revealed that variation in iPL was significantly associated with model-derived contractility (R2 = 0.216, p=0.004), and fenestration flow (R2 = 0.465, p = 0.001) at baseline (Fig. 4). In post-FFTO, iPL was negatively associated with contractility (R2 = 0.432). Also, at baseline, iPL was negatively associated with minimum conduit area/BSA (R2 = 0.261, p = 0.025), (Fig. 5). In post-FFTO, there was a negative association between iPL and minimum conduit area/BSA, but the relationship was not significant (R2 = 0.277, p = 0.180).

Discussion

This study demonstrated an application of iCMR data to quantify in vivo TCPC power loss and its association with conduit narrowing and ventricular contractility in patients with single-ventricle physiology referred for post-Fontan assessment. Inclusion of patients undergoing FFTO procedure enabled discrimination of the effect of Fontan fenestration on TCPC hemodynamics and myocardial mechanics. Unlike prior computational fluid dynamic-based frameworks, this work leveraged iCMR technique to simultaneously acquire flow and pressure data for direct in vivo TCPC power loss calculation. Importantly, this study broadens the scope beyond non-fenestrated patients studied previously by Honda et al. [13], offering a more comprehensive characterization of TCPC hemodynamics, including the effect of Fontan fenestration, and their clinical implications. These findings highlight the translational value of a comprehensive iCMR-derived assessment coupled to biomechanical modeling of the heart to guide clinical decision-making in the management of patients with Fontan circulation.

In the current study, absolute values of in vivo TCPC PL (Eq. (1)) at baseline (mean ± SD, 17.5 ± 10.8 mW) were higher than reported by Honda et al. [13] (9.66 ± 8.50 mW). Since absolute values of blood flow PL are influenced by patients’ body size [8], the larger BSA of our cohort (1.04 ± 0.31 m2) compared to the Honda et al. [13] (0.50 ± 0.04 m2) likely accounts for the nearly twofold difference in PL. This underscores the importance of normalizing for intrasubject comparisons.

In patient-specific computational fluid dynamic studies utilizing the same formula for iPL as in eq. [2], Tang et al. [5] reported a mean iPL of 0.03, while Mercer-Rosa et al. [36] reported a mean iPL of 0.06. These values are in a range of iPL derived from post-FFTO data (0.13 ± 0.10) in the present study, but are lower than the range observed in pre-FFTO data (0.27 ± 0.13). Notably, these earlier studies exclusively analyzed patients without fenestration. Furthermore, computational fluid dynamics studies estimate vessel pressures using numerical methods rather than direct invasive measurements, which may further contribute to the difference in the reported values.

Our results demonstrated significant relationships between TCPC iPL and various functional components of Fontan cardiovascular system. At baseline, increased iPL in TCPC was associated with increased myocardial contractility and increased fenestration flow volume. Notably, fenestration flow volume pre-FFTO was associated with 46.5% of variation in iPL (with R2 = 0.465 between iPL and fenestration flow volume pre-FFTO). Following fenestration closure (post-FFTO) iPL significantly decreased and the relationship between iPL and myocardial contractility reversed compared to that at baseline, such that lower iPL became associated with higher myocardial contractility. This study used a previously employed definition of blood flow energy loss given by a difference between inlet and outlet flow energies across the TCPC, and occurring due to the viscous energy dissipation of blood [9], [13], [35]. However, in fenestrated patients, we demonstrated that 46.5% of iPL was likely attributed to the direct outflow of blood into the atrium through the fenestration. Consequently, in the presence of a fenestration, the applied definition of blood flow energy loss might be misleading as it reflects not only viscous dissipation within the TCPC but also direct loss of blood volume. Thus, in patients with fenestration post-FFTO, the calculated value of iPL likely reflects the actual viscous blood flow energy dissipation for these patients. Nevertheless, even though fenestration largely contributes to the direct loss of blood volume—and consequently to the increased blood flow power loss—within the TCPC, it also may stimulate the ventricular contraction by increasing ventricular preload. Notably, post-FFTO higher iPL was associated with lower contractility, and patients 1, 4, 5, and 7 (Fig. 4 post-FFTO) experienced a decrease in myocardial contractility, suggesting that removal of fenestration flow might provoke an acute restriction of preload.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have explicitly examined the relationship between TCPC power loss and ventricular systolic function while accounting for the fenestration flow volume. Honda et al. [13] reported a significant association between power loss and ventricular systolic function; however, their study had notable limitations. Their patient cohort consisted exclusively of non-fenestrated Fontan patients, which limits generalizability to the patients with fenestration.

Most previous computational fluid dynamics studies have analyzed TCPC power loss in isolation, primarily focusing on optimizing the caval connection to minimize blood flow energy dissipation within the TCPC [5], [6], [12], [16], [37], [38] and to avoid blood flow stagnation [4]. However, these studies have not explored the systemic-level effects of TCPC power loss, particularly its impact on ventricular function. In contrast, the present study leverages the richness of iCMR data and a high-fidelity biomechanical model [27], [39], enabling a comprehensive analysis of TCPC power loss and fenestration flow and their relationship with ventricular systolic function (i.e., given by model-derived contractility [30]).

Model-derived contractility is a mechanically estimated parameter reflecting force-generating capacity (i.e., inotropic state) of the myocardium derived from patients’ pressure and volume data. This quantity is not directly accessible from clinical measurements without the use of a biomechanical model. In our previous pre-clinical studies, this model-derived parameter was shown to be a descriptive biomarker of ventricular systolic function. For example, in patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot, myocardial contractility enabled discrimination of the effects of pulmonary regurgitation and stenosis on right ventricular mechanics [30]. In patients with congenitally corrected transposition of great arteries, contractility was used to predict the functional response of the left ventricle to increased afterload due to the double-switch operation [39]. Importantly, model-derived contractility is not an extrapolation of time-varying elastance theory [40] or a full end-systolic pressure–volume relationship (ESPVR) analysis [40]. Rather, it represents a geometry and biophysically informed estimate of myocardial force-generating capacity calibrated to patient-specific loading conditions [27], [41]. While ejection fraction and stroke volume quantify global pump performance from volumetric data and ESPVR characterizes phenomenological end-systolic behavior of the ventricle, present myocardial contractility captures a computed estimate of a mechanical force generated by the actin-myosin unit during contraction [27].

Fenestration flow is likely to play a critical role in Fontan hemodynamics by facilitating a right-to-left shunt, which reduces systemic venous pressure [21] while increasing ventricular preload. The results of the present study demonstrate that this mechanism can enhance myocardial contractility but at the cost of increased diversion of flow through the fenestration [21]. Our findings emphasize the clinical significance of fenestration management and its implications for optimizing Fontan circulation. Understanding the interplay between power loss, ventricular function, and fenestration status could inform personalized strategies for fenestration placement or closure. For example, determining the optimal timing of fenestration closure that could improve long-term hemodynamic efficiency and ventricular function. Future longitudinal studies are essential to evaluate these effects and provide evidence-based guidance for clinical decision-making.

This study demonstrated that increased iPL in TCPC was associated with decreased minimum conduit area normalized to BSA. While this finding is in line with predictions from prior computational fluid dynamic studies [5], [6], which predicted that narrower conduit CSA to be associated with power loss due to viscous dissipation, the observed relationship in our data was modest (R2 = 0.261, p = 0.025) and did not remain significant post-FFTO. This underscores the multifactorial nature of TCPC hemodynamics, where conduit narrowing likely represents only one contributor to power loss. Additional hemodynamic factors, such as stenosis of the LPA and RPA, flow collision from the SVC and IVC, and the development of venous collaterals, may substantially influence power loss in the TCPC region. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to better quantify the relative contribution of these factors and to determine whether optimizing conduit geometry could meaningfully enhance flow efficiency as well as overall cardiovascular health.

The present study advances prior computational fluid dynamic-based research by linking iPL not only to conduit geometry but also to myocardial contractility—an association rarely explored in computational studies. Unlike computational fluid dynamics, which derives intravascular pressure form theoretical assumptions, iCMR provides in vivo measurements that capture real-time hemodynamics, potentially offering a more clinically relevant framework for evaluating efficiency of Fontan circulation. Integrating iCMR-derived power loss with biomechanical modeling of the heart represents a significant step toward translating theoretical hemodynamic biomarkers of TCPC blood flow power loss into actionable insights for patient care. Broader adoption of this framework could inform optimized strategies for Fontan fenestration management. With further development and validation, the present modeling framework could enable real-time intra-procedural assessment of myocardial contractility [42] to support fenestration management. The current framework is fast in computation time (i.e., below 30 s in Matlab and near real-time in Python) and hence is suitable for real-time (or near–real-time) implementation in clinical settings. Automated extraction of pressure and imaging parameters, coupled with automated model calibration [42], could allow contractility estimates to update dynamically during iCMR procedures. In vivo calculation of power loss depends on clinical pressure and CMR flow data processing and could be available in real time once clinical reports are generated.

Limitations

The simultaneous acquisition of flow and pressure data given by iCMR modality is one of the key strengths of this study, yet it is essential to acknowledge inherent uncertainties in the assessment of fenestration flow and other flow parameters from iCMR data. Specifically, the detection of calculated residual fenestration flow post-FFTO in some patients (Fig. 4), despite clinical closure, highlights the sensitivity of iCMR to measurement noise or minor unobstructed flow pathways. This uncertainty is not unique to our method but reflects the broader challenge of accurately quantifying flow using calculations that employ multiple phase-contrast measurements in low-velocity states. Additionally, low-velocity PC-MRI measurements may be affected by physiologic variability across sequential free-breathing acquisitions, which can introduce inconsistency in flow quantification. Future validation studies would benefit from contrast-enhanced or 4D flow CMR techniques, which can directly resolve fenestration flow and pulmonary venous return. Despite these limitations, iCMR remains one of the most comprehensive techniques for simultaneous acquisition of flow and pressure data [24], [25], representing the current gold standard for invasive hemodynamic studies.

In the future work, automated 3D methods of conduit geometry reconstruction [12], [38] may improve the precision and reproducibility of conduit minimum CSA measurements and enable more comprehensive geometric characterization of the conduit shape (e.g., to identify uniform narrowing vs. focal stenosis, derive metrics of conduit curvature). In the present study, conduit CSA was derived using manual segmentation of 3D whole-heart inversion recovery bSSFP images. We acknowledge that such a method may introduce observer-dependent variability and limit measurement consistency across subjects.

This study was limited by the modest cohort size, underscoring the need for larger multicenter studies to validate the multifactorial relationships between TCPC power loss, myocardial function, fenestration flow, and to explore their longitudinal implications. Future studies should determine the reproducibility, prognostic value, and procedural utility of TCPC power loss measurements across diverse Fontan anatomies. Clinical translation will also require standardized acquisition protocols, automated analysis tools, and longitudinal correlation with outcomes.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the complex interplay between TCPC blood flow power loss, myocardial contractility, Fontan conduit geometry, and the presence of fenestration in Fontan physiology. By leveraging iCMR data prior to and after clinical FFTO, the results demonstrated that increased iPL in the TCPC is associated with higher myocardial contractility in the presence of fenestration, while this relationship reverses following fenestration closure. Notably, in the pre-FFTO state, 46% of the variation in iPL was explained by fenestration flow volume, highlighting its preload-stimulating effect at the expense of increased power loss within the TCPC. Also, increased iPL showed a modest association with Fontan conduit narrowing, underscoring multifactorial nature of TCPC hemodynamics.

By integrating iCMR-derived hemodynamic metrics with biomechanical modeling of the heart, this study advances the understanding of Fontan circulation power loss beyond computational fluid dynamics approaches, offering a clinically relevant framework to investigate the interplay between anatomical and physiological factors in vivo. Future work should validate these findings in larger cohorts, assess long-term impact of fenestration on the overall cardiovascular health, and explore patient-specific strategies for conduit optimization. Addressing these gaps will enable tailored interventions to improve the cardiovascular health and long-term outcomes for patients with Fontan circulation.
