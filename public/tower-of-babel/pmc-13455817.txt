Bridging Evidence Synthesis and Clinical Relevance in Blood-Letting Therapy for Knee Osteoarthritis: Methodological Reflections on a Meta-Analysis [Response to Letter]
Authors: Shuai Dong, Duan-Yu Mou, Dong-Sheng Qiu, Guo-Ping Ye, Xi Wu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao FY, Dr. Fu QQ, and Dr. Chen Z for their thoughtful and detailed commentary on our meta-analysis entitled “Efficacy and Safety of Bloodletting Therapy in Patients with Knee Osteoarthritis: A Meta-Analysis” (Journal of Pain Research 2026;19:600101).1,2 Their constructive critique raises important methodological considerations that merit careful discussion. Below, we provide a point-by-point response to each of their comments.

Comment 1 – Choice of Clinically Meaningful Comparators

Reviewer point: The comparators in the included RCTs (eg, acupuncture alone) are not guideline-recommended standard therapies for knee osteoarthritis, limiting clinical interpretability.

Response: We acknowledge this important limitation. In the original Discussion, we stated: Most of the included studies originated from China, and the results may be influenced by the context effect. Furthermore, the evidence is limited by the small sample sizes and low number of trials per comparison, as well as the exclusive conduct of all studies in China, which restricts geographic generalizability. (Discussion, page 16)1

Most of the included studies originated from China, and the results may be influenced by the context effect. Furthermore, the evidence is limited by the small sample sizes and low number of trials per comparison, as well as the exclusive conduct of all studies in China, which restricts geographic generalizability. (Discussion, page 16)1

We fully endorse the reviewers’ recommendation that future meta-analyses—and ideally, future primary RCTs—should prioritise guideline-recommended comparators (eg, topical/oral NSAIDs, intra-articular hyaluronan or corticosteroids per OARSI/ACR guidelines) to produce clinically actionable effect estimates.

Comment 2 – Clinical Heterogeneity in Control Interventions

Reviewer point: Pooling different acupuncture modalities (manual, warm, fire acupuncture) and different drug classes introduces clinical heterogeneity that may confound the summary estimate.

Response: We agree. In the original Results, we reported: Six trials reported this outcome using the WOMAC scale (MD = −5.57 scores, 95% CI −6.97 to −4.17 scores, 377 participants, I2 = 90%, P < 0.00001). A sensitivity analysis was performed on the WOMAC outcome by sequentially removing each study. (Results, page 13)1

Due to the limited number of trials per control-subtype, formal stratification was not feasible. We concur that a network meta-analysis would be a valuable next step to simultaneously compare multiple active interventions and rank efficacy.

Comment 3 – Stratification by Treatment Duration/Sessions

Reviewer point: Pooling outcomes across 9 days to 8 weeks is methodologically imperfect; dose–response patterns are obscured.

Response: We recognise this imperfection. In the original Methods, we explicitly stated: The treatment duration of included trials ranged from 9 days to 8 weeks, which is relatively short. Given that KOA is a chronic progressive disease, meaningful evaluation of long-term efficacy and safety generally requires follow-up of at least 6 months after treatment completion. (Methods, page 3)1

Given that KOA is a chronic progressive disease, meaningful evaluation of long-term efficacy and safety generally requires follow-up of at least 6 months after treatment completion. (Methods, page 3)1

We agree that prespecified subgroup analyses by treatment duration, or meta-regression examining session number and cumulative bleeding volume as dose proxies, would strengthen future syntheses.

Comment 4 – Pooling Response Rates Under Non-Uniform Grading Criteria

Reviewer point: Definitions of “response rate” and “ineffective rate” varied across trials and are not aligned with international instruments.

Response: We agree. In the original Methods, we documented: The definitions and calculation formulas for ‘inefficient rate’ and ‘response rate’ adopted in this meta-analysis were derived from two published Chinese clinical efficacy evaluation guidelines for traditional Chinese medicine. Such evaluation criteria have been widely used clinically in Chinese orthopedics and traumatology for a long time; however, they have not been validated against internationally recognized assessment instruments (eg, VAS, WOMAC, SF-36), nor aligned with the core outcome set for arthritis recommended by the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials (COMET) initiative. (Methods, page 4)1

Such evaluation criteria have been widely used clinically in Chinese orthopedics and traumatology for a long time; however, they have not been validated against internationally recognized assessment instruments (eg, VAS, WOMAC, SF-36), nor aligned with the core outcome set for arthritis recommended by the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials (COMET) initiative. We recommend that future BLT trials and meta-analyses adopt core outcome sets endorsed by OMERACT-OARSI (VAS, WOMAC, KOOS) and use standardised responder definitions—this is already reflected in our original “Revelations for Future Research” section (page 17).

Comment 5 – Technical Parameters of Bloodletting via Meta-Regression

Reviewer point: BLT is not a monolithic intervention; modality (pricking, wet cupping, Zhuang medicine BLT), acupoint selection, bleeding volume, and session frequency should be examined as covariates.

Response: We appreciate this suggestion. In the original Methods, we noted: Substantial clinical heterogeneity was observed across the included trials due to marked variations in bloodletting methods, acupoint selection, bleeding volume, and treatment frequency, which represented a major source of heterogeneity in this study. (Methods, page 4)1

Substantial clinical heterogeneity was observed across the included trials due to marked variations in bloodletting methods, acupoint selection, bleeding volume, and treatment frequency, which represented a major source of heterogeneity in this study. (Methods, page 4)1

Detailed descriptive data on BLT subtype, acupoints, bleeding volume, and frequency were presented in Tables 1 and 2. However, with ≤3 trials per BLT subtype, formal subgroup analysis or meta-regression was not feasible without overfitting. We agree that as the evidence base expands, these procedural parameters must be prespecified covariates—particularly bleeding volume and acupoint prescription, which likely drive both efficacy and adverse-event profiles.

Comment 6 – Publication Bias Assessment in Small-Sample Meta-Analyses

Reviewer point: With <10 trials per comparison, funnel plots lack power; alternative methods (Doi plot, LFK index) and geographic concentration (all trials from China) deserve discussion.

Response: We acknowledge the limitation. In the original Methods, we stated: If more than 10 trials were included in a meta-analysis, funnel plots were used to explore the possibility of publication bias. (Methods, page 5)1 Funnel plots were prespecified but unreportable when k < 10. We welcome the reviewers’ suggestion to use Doi plots/LFK index for small-sample asymmetry—this is a useful addition to our analytical toolkit for future updates. Regarding geographic concentration, we explicitly discussed this in the original Discussion: A systematic search of international databases did not identify any eligible randomized controlled trials of pure bloodletting therapy for knee osteoarthritis originating outside China. This geographical gap limits the global generalizability of our findings and underscores the need for international research on this traditional therapy. (Discussion, page 16)1

This geographical gap limits the global generalizability of our findings and underscores the need for international research on this traditional therapy. (Discussion, page 16)1

We accept that geographic and language restriction remains a source of potential bias we could not fully quantify.

Comment 7 – Overall Conclusion

We are grateful to Zhao, Fu, and Chen for their rigorous and scholarly critique. Their comments highlight the inherent tension between synthesising available evidence and the methodological constraints of the primary studies in complementary medicine. Our meta-analysis aimed to provide the best attainable estimate while transparently acknowledging its limits. We fully concur that future work should adopt more clinically structured and methodologically rigorous frameworks—appropriate comparators, standardised intervention and outcome definitions, and, where feasible, network meta-analysis with dose–response exploration. We hope this exchange advances evidence synthesis standards in this field.
