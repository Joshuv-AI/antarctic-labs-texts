Dysbiosis of vaginal and peritoneal fluid microbiota in patients with cervical lesions
Authors: Leilei Zhu, Zizhen Wu, Sensen Shi, Heng Xue, Lan Zhen, Yichen Wang, Jimiao Huang, Shihuang Liu, Huan Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to characterize disturbances in vaginal microbiota among patients with squamous intraepithelial lesions (SIL) or cervical cancer (CC), and to identify specific bacterial genera with potential as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers. We also explored microbiota of peritoneal fluid in CC patients across different pathological subtypes.MethodsA total of 76 participants were enrolled, including 44 SIL and 32 CC patients, further classified into four groups: low-grade SIL (LSIL; n = 14), high-grade SIL (HSIL; n = 30), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC; n = 18) and adenocarcinoma (ADC; n = 14). Vaginal secretions were collected from all participants, and free peritoneal fluid was obtained from 16 SCC and 12 ADC patients. Vaginal HPV status was monitored semiannually over two years in 25 HSIL patients. Microbial composition was analyzed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing.ResultsVaginal bacterial abundance was significantly higher in the CC group than in the SIL group. A non-significant decrease in Lactobacillus abundance (SIL: 60.89% vs. CC: 52.37%; p = 0.247) and an increase in anaerobic bacteria including Prevotella (SIL: 0.14% vs. CC: 0.79%; p < 0.001) and Sneathia (SIL: 0.21% vs. CC: 1.57%; p = 0.097) were observed in CC patients. Lactobacillus abundance was negatively correlated with these anaerobic genera. Among HSIL patients, those who cleared HPV infection had a significantly higher abundance of Atopobium (5.25% vs. 0.65%; p = 0.022). Furthermore, ADC patients showed significantly higher microbial abundance in both vaginal and peritoneal fluid samples compared to SCC patients. LEfSe analysis indicated that Prevotella was the most distinguishing genus in the ADC group.ConclusionsOur study suggests that vaginal microbiota diversity may be associated with the severity of cervical lesions. Additionally, ADC patients showed heightened microbial diversity in both vaginal and peritoneal fluid microbiota compared to SCC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04196-y.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Squamous intraepithelial lesions, Human papillomavirus, Vaginal microbiota, Microbiota of peritoneal fluid, 16S rRNA

Introduction

Globally, cervical cancer (CC) is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, with approximately 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths reported in 2022 [1, 2]. Over 90% of CC cases are associated with persistent human papillomavirus (HPV) infection [3]. A balanced vaginal microecology plays a crucial role in protecting against genital tract infections. Previous studies comparing vaginal microbiota between patients with squamous intraepithelial lesions (SIL) or CC and healthy individuals have demonstrated that the severity of cervical lesions is significantly correlated with increased vaginal microbial diversity [4, 5]. Histopathological type also substantially influences CC prognosis. However, limited studies to date have directly compared vaginal microbiota profiles between patients with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma (ADC) of the cervix. Anatomically, the female vagina, uterus, fallopian tube and pelvic cavity form a continuous tract. Emerging evidence suggests that endometrial microbiota dysbiosis is associated with both benign and malignant uterine conditions [6], and ascitic microbiota composition has been linked to prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and severity of cirrhosis [7, 8]. Although the microbiota in free peritoneal fluid from patients with benign gynecological diseases has been preliminarily characterized [9], its composition and potential role in CC remain unknown.

In this study, we collected vaginal secretions from patients with SIL, SCC and ADC, and conducted a series of comparative analysis of the vaginal microbiota. We aim to elucidate the correlation between vaginal microbial composition and the progression of cervical lesions, and to identify specific bacterial genera with potential diagnostic or prognostic value. Additionally, we explored differences in microbiota of peritoneal fluid between SCC and ADC patients.

Methods

Patient recruitment

The patients were recruited from the Gynecology Department of our hospital between June 2021 and December 2024. We collected data on participants’ age, menopausal status, reproductive history, comorbidities, cancer stage, routine leukorrhea tests, HPV infection types, and pathologic diagnosis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) age between 18 and 60 years; (b) no vaginal douching, medication, or sexual intercourse for at least 7 days prior to sampling; (c) no systemic use of antibiotics, antifungal agents, probiotics, or glucocorticoids within the preceding 3 months; and (d) testing positive for HPV upon initial screening and being diagnosed with SIL or CC. Exclusion criteria included: (a) a history of diabetes, autoimmune diseases, or other malignancies; and (b) pregnancy.

Diagnosis of HPV infection and cervical lesions

HPV genotyping was performed by the oncology laboratory of our hospital using PCR-RDB HPV genotyping (Yaneng Biotech) to identify 18 high-risk HPV (HR‐HPV) types (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82, and 83) and 5 low‐risk HPV (LR-HPV) types (6, 11, 42, 43, and 81) [10].

Patients diagnosed with low-grade SIL (LSIL), high-grade SIL (HSIL), SCC, or ADC were included. The final pathological diagnosis was based on the most severe findings among the cervical biopsies, endocervical curettage, conization, or hysterectomy tissues. Histologic endpoints were defined according to the 2014 WHO classification of tumors of the female reproductive organs (4th edition) [11] and the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) recommendations [12]. All pathological evaluations were confirmed by a senior pathologist in our department.

Sample collection

Vaginal secretion samples were collected from each participant using swabs to wipe the lateral vaginal wall near the fornix and the cervix [13]. For patients with CC undergoing surgery, 5–10 mL of free peritoneal fluid was aspirated. If insufficient fluid was present, the abdominal cavity was rinsed with 10 mL of sterile saline, which was then collected. All samples were immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Patient follow-up and subgroup analysis for HPV monitoring

A subgroup of patients with HSIL (n = 30) was monitored for HPV infection status over a period of 2 years, with assessments conducted at semi-annual intervals. Five patients were lost to follow-up due to irregular clinic attendance. Patients with LSIL were excluded from this longitudinal analysis because they all achieved HPV clearance within one year in our cohort. Patients with CC were also excluded from the HPV persistence analysis due to the potential confounding effects of their complex treatments (e.g., surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy) on HPV status; furthermore, their overall survival data are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Bacterial DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with primers 515 F (5’-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The quality and concentration of the purified DNA libraries were assessed using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen).

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

Alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and ACE) were calculated from the sequencing results using the vegan package in R and compared between groups using the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. Beta diversity was assessed through principal coordinate analysis (PCA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. To identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa across groups, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed, combining standard tests with linear discriminant analysis (LDA), using a logarithmic LDA score threshold of 3.0 for discriminative features.

Associations between microbial genera were evaluated by calculating Spearman’s correlation coefficients and visualized as heatmaps. Functional profiling of microbial communities was predicted from the 16 S rRNA data using PICRUSt2, which maps sequences to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) databases. All analyses followed the standard PICRUSt2 protocol. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Within the SIL group, although there was a significant difference in age between the LSIL and HSIL subgroups, no significant difference in vaginal microbiota abundance was observed. Within the CC group, the ADC subgroup exhibited significantly higher ACE and Chao1 scores compared to the SCC subgroup (Fig. 1 F-I)). The Venn diagram showed 73 genera were unique to the ADC group, 28 unique to the SCC group, 10 unique to the HSIL group, and 25 unique to the LSIL group (Fig. 1K).

Different microbial profiles between SIL and CC vaginal samples

A total of 363 genera were identified across all samples, with Lactobacillus being the most dominant genus. Compared with the SIL group, the proportion of Lactobacillus was lower in the CC group, but the difference was not statistically significant (CC: 52.37% vs. SIL: 60.89%; p = 0.247). Atopobium also exhibited a decreasing trend in the CC group (CC: 2.42% vs. SIL: 3.94%; p = 0.043). In contrast, the abundance of Prevotella was significantly higher in the CC group (CC: 0.79% vs. SIL: 0.14%; p < 0.0001). Although Sneathia also showed an increase in the CC group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (CC: 1.57% vs. SIL: 0.21%; p = 0.097).

LEfSe analysis revealed that Ochrobactrum was a representative genus in the SIL group, whereas Pseudomonas, Prevotella, Parvibaculum, and Bacteroides were among the top representative genera in the CC group (Fig. 2A). Further analysis among the four subgroups indicated that Prevotella was most notably enriched in the ADC group (Fig. 2B).

Among patients with SIL, a substantial proportion were infected with HPV16 (30.23%), HPV18 (25.58%), and HPV52 (34.88%). LEfSe analysis within the SIL group showed that the HPV16-positive group was characterized by Ochrobactrum, Paenibacillus, and Granulicatella, while the HPV52-positive group was associated with Fusobacterium, Megasphaera, Sneathia, Prevotella and Dialister (Fig. 2C).

HPV clearance follow-up analysis among patients with HSIL

Among patients with HSIL, 18 achieved HPV clearance, while 7 remained HPV-persistent. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the clearance and persistent groups (Supplementary Table 3). Although no notable differences in overall vaginal microbiota abundance were detected between the two subgroups (p > 0.05). The Atopobium was significantly more abundant in the HPV clearance group (5.25% vs. 0.65%; p = 0.022) (Fig. 2D).

Correlation analysis of vaginal microbiota

As illustrated in the heatmap, Lactobacillus showed a negative correlation with Neisseriaceae (r = −0.62), Prevotella (r = −0.46), Halomonas (r = −0.54) and Pseudomonas (r = −0.48). The Halomonas had a positive correlation between Neisseriaceae (r = 0.54) and Pseudomonas (r = 0.92) (Fig. 2E).

Potential biological functions of the vaginal microbiota

Functional prediction of microbial genes in vaginal samples from the CC and SIL groups was performed using the PICRUSt2 algorithm, with biological annotation based on the KEGG and COG databases. Differential enrichment analysis (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.05) indicated that although most functional pathways were similar between groups, the CC group showed significant enrichment in pathways related to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis in the CC group. While the SIL group exhibited enriched genes associated with the chloride channel protein EriC and pyridoxal/pyridoxine/pyridoxamine kinase, as shown in the COG functional classification (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a high-throughput microbiome assessment of vaginal microbiota in patients with SIL, as well as the vaginal and peritoneal fluid microbiota of in patients with CC. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that the patients with ADC exhibit higher microbial abundance in both the vaginal and peritoneal compartments compared to those with SCC.

Our data revealed an increasing trend in vaginal microbiota diversity with the progression from SIL to CC, consistent with previous reports [14, 15]. Furthermore, we observed that the ADC patients, who typically have a poorer prognosis [16], harbored a more complex vaginal microbiota than SCC patients. These findings align with the conventional view that increased vaginal microbiota diversity may reflect reduced resilience to ecological disturbances [17]. Histologically, SCC originates from the squamous epithelium, whereas ADC arises from glandular columnar epithelium [18]. Patients with ADC often experience vaginal discharge [19], which may contribute to disorders of the vaginal microbiota. Overall, the CC group showed a non-significant decrease in the proportion of Lactobacillus and significant increase in anaerobic bacteria, consistent with a community state type (CST) IV profile. Chen et al. also reported that HPV infection shifted the vaginal bacterial community from CST III to CST IV [20]. Our data indicated that this shift was more pronounced in ADC patients than in those with SCC.

Regarding the microbiota in peritoneal fluid, we found higher microbial abundance in patients with ADC compared to patients with SCC. We speculate this may be associated with the higher diversity of vaginal microbiota in patients with ADC. Previous studies have described a continuous microbial gradient from the vagina to the peritoneal cavity within individuals [21]. We further hypothesize that the ADC, originating in the endocervical canal, may disrupt the cervical mucus barrier [22], thereby facilitating the ascent of vaginal microbiota into the upper reproductive tract and pelvic cavity. Although often asymptomatic, such subtle changes can be detected through high-throughput sequencing. A case-control study by Abdul et al. demonstrated a correlation between chronic pelvic inflammatory disease and cervical dysplasia [23], supporting our hypothesis. We acknowledge that the relatively small sample size of peritoneal fluid samples limits the generalizability of these findings, and thus, further validation in larger independent cohort is warranted.

Using the LEfSe analysis, we identified several marker bacteria that were significantly different between SIL and CC groups, including Prevotella, Sneathia, Fusobacteriia, Atopobium, which have also been reported in other studies [24, 25]. In general, microbial environments characterized by a higher proportion of anaerobic bacteria and a lower proportion of Lactobacillus are more susceptible to HPV infection and associated with viral persistence [24]. Interestingly, our follow-up analysis revealed a significantly higher abundance of Atopobium in HSIL patients who cleared HPV infection. Chen‘s and Hu’s studies also indicated that the HPV-infected patients demonstrated a reduced vaginal Atopobium abundance [26, 27]. Atopobium may play a paradoxical role in vaginal health. Although it is considered an opportunistic pathogen linked to bacterial vaginosis development and recurrence [28], it may also possess properties that promote HPV clearance. In vitro studies by Libby et al. showed that Atopobium can stimulate an innate immune response in vaginal epithelial cells, inducing elevated levels of IL-6, IL-8 and antimicrobial peptide β-defensin 4, leading to localized cytokine and defensin production [29]. Given the limited sample size in our HSIL follow-up cohort, further research is necessary to clarify the role of Atopobium in HPV clearance.

Prevotella was identified as the most significant biomarker in the ADC group by LEfSe analysis, consistent with previous studies linking Prevotella-rich communities to genital inflammation and carcinogenesis [30]. Prevotella may promote ADC development by sustaining a pro-inflammatory microenvironment and through mucin-degrading metabolic activity that could disrupt the endocervical glandular epithelium. Our study also revealed distinct cervical microbiota profiles associated with different HR-HPV subtypes in patients with SIL. HPV52-positive cases were characterized by a higher abundance of Fusobacterium, Megasphaera, Sneathia, and Prevotella, which are strongly associated with cervical cancer and high-grade lesions established by previous literatures [31]. However, the association of unique microbes with HPV16, along with the overall concept of HPV type-specific microbiota, remains poorly explored. Therefore, more research is needed to elucidate these relationships.

KEGG pathway analysis indicated upregulation of LPS biosynthesis pathways in the vaginal microbiota of CC patients. Given the established role of LPS in promoting carcinogenesis [32], we hypothesize that enhanced LPS biosynthesis may contribute to malignant transformation and increased aggressiveness of cervical epithelial cells. COG analysis suggested enrichment of genes related to chloride channel protein EriC and pyridoxal/pyridoxine/pyridoxamine kinase in the SIL group. Pyridoxal kinase plays a key role in vitamin B metabolism, which can influence immune infiltration and affect the clinical course of locally advanced cancers [33]. Based on our clinical and bioinformatic findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying vaginal microbiota–cervical cell interactions warrant further investigation through functional studies.

As far as we know, this is the first study to characterize differences in vaginal microbiota between SCC and ADC patients, and the first to analyze the microbiota in peritoneal fluid of cervical cancer. Despite the biological relevance of these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is a descriptive report based on clinical observations from a relatively limited cases. Additionally, 16 S rDNA amplicon sequencing has inherent limitations in taxonomic resolution at the species level, CST classification, and function prediction. Future mechanistic studies using integrated approaches, such as metagenomics, metabolomics and in vitro/in vivo models, are needed to elucidate the differences between SCC and ADC and the detailed mechanisms of interactions between microbiota and the epithelium.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that vaginal microbiota diversity may correlate with the severity of cervical lesions. Furthermore, patients with ADC appeared to exhibit greater microbial diversity in both vaginal and peritoneal fluid microbiota compared to those with SCC, although further validation in larger cohorts is needed to confirm these findings.
