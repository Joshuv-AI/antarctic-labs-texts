Long term outcomes of older patients with classical hodgkin lymphoma: an analysis of the Texas cancer registry
Authors: Ethan A Burns, Hala Hassanain, Sunil Mathur, Darshil Choksi, Yuqi Zhang, Cesar Gentille Sanchez, Chih-hang Anthony Tang, Hanh Mai, Carrie Yuen, Shilpan Shah, Siddhartha Ganguly, Chih-chi Andrew Hu, Sai Ravi Pingali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment advances for classical Hodgkin Lymphoma (cHL) have improved outcomes, but studies are lacking in older patients. This analysis aims to investigate survival trends in older individuals with cHL using the Texas Cancer Registry (TCR). Adults ≥ 65 years with cHL were separated into three eras: 1995–2002, 2003–2010, and 2011–2017. Pearson chi-squared test and cox-proportional hazard models along with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) compared demographic factors, and survival trends were assessed by Kaplan-Meier methodology. There were 386 (37.4%), 336 (32.5%), and 311 (30.1%) individuals diagnosed in 1995–2002, 2003–2010, and 2011–2017, respectively. Median DSS per era was 97 months (95% CI 50.7, 143.3), 158 months (95% CI 132.5, 183.5), and not reached, (p=0.383), and median OS was 32 months (95% CI 23.4, 40.6), 37 months (95% CI 23.5, 50.5), and 40 months (95% CI 26.8, 53.1), respectively (p=0.693). Median OS was worse for blacks and Hispanics vs non-Hispanic whites (p=0.007), increasing age (p<0.001), and higher poverty index (p=0.006). While DSS and OS have not improved over the past two decades for older individuals, there are disparity dependent survival differences by race, age, and poverty index. Strategies to reduce these disparities are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06539-z.

Introduction

Although cancer is predominately a disease of the aging population, older patients are frequently underrepresented across clinical trials, limiting assessments of epidemiologic characteristics and long-term outcomes. This is also true of older patients with classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL). While the age-adjusted incidence rate for adults ≥ 65 years with cHL approaches 20%, studies assessing outcomes and survival trends are limited [1]– [2]. Over the past several decades, the evolution of stage-adapted multiagent chemotherapy regimens has resulted in a progression free survival (PFS) exceeding 90% in younger patients [3]. However, survival remains disproportionally inferior in the older population, with 5-year event free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) in adults ≥ 60 years ranging from 30 to 40% and 40–50%, respectively [4–8]. Data suggests that similar to younger patients, older patients benefit from multiagent regimens, as opposed to partial regimens, single agent chemotherapy, no systemic therapy, or radiation alone [9]. However, a multiagent, standard of care approach in older adults may be hampered in older individuals by diminished efficacy, poor therapeutic tolerance and toxicities, or histologic heterogeneity. For instance, Adriamycin, Bleomycin, Vinblastine, Dacarbazine (ABVD) has been demonstrated to have markedly increased toxicity and inferior outcomes in older individuals compared to younger individuals [10]. In the Echelon-1 trial comparing Brentuximab Vedotin, Adriamycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (Bv-AVD) to ABVD, there was a PFS benefit for the whole cohort, and improved survival for younger patients, but this was not seen in adults older than 60 years [11]– [12]. Further exploration into outcomes, demographic factors, and treatment disparities in older patients is needed.

By the year 2030, it is estimated that one-in-five adults residing in the United States (US) will be older than 64 years of age, and current population trends predict that the number of older adults will rise proportionally in Texas [13]– [14]. For these reasons, a state- based analysis utilizing data from the TCR to study long-term cHL outcomes in older patients is felt to be representative of the broader national aging population [15]. The aim of this study is to assess outcome trends and therapy patterns in adults ≥ 65 years through data provided by the Texas Cancer Registry (TCR).

Methods/materials

This was a retrospective study assessing outcomes of older patients ≥ 65 years old diagnosed with cHL from data provided by the TCR. The TCR is a statewide, population- based cancer registry that collects comprehensive patient data to monitor the Texas cancer burden, cancer control efforts, demographic disparities, treatment modalities, survivor- ship, and patient outcomes [16]. It is one of the largest cancer registries in the US and is funded by the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Program of Cancer registries (NPCR) [16]. For this study, a written application and protocol was submitted to the TCR, which provided the data upon receipt and approval of the application (Cancer incidence data was provided by the Texas Cancer Registry, Cancer Epidemiology and Surveillance Branch, Texas Department of State Health Services, 1100 West 49th Street, Austin, TX 78756, https://www.dshs.texas.gov/tcr/). Because this cancer registry data provided retrospectively collected patient data as a limited, deidentified aggregate of data, institutional review board approval was not required by the TCR. All patient data is available with written permission and approval of the TCR [16].

Patients ≥ 65 years diagnosed with histologically confirmed cHL (Lymphoma subtype recode/WHO 2008 1(a): 1(a) 1.1 for lymphocyte rich, 1(a)1.2 for mixed cellularity, 1(a)1.3 for lymphocyte depleted, 1(a)2 for nodular sclerosis) and reported to the TCR between the years 1995–2017 and follow-up data through November 8, 2020, were included. Patients were excluded from further analysis if they were < 65 years of age at diagnosis or had the following missing information: date of diagnosis, histologically proven disease, date of last follow-up, systemic treatment data, and unknown or unspecified subtype of cHL (Fig. 1). All others were included and divided into three distinct eras, 1995–2002, 2003–2010 and 2011–2017. Patient demographics including age groups (≥ 65–74 and ≥ 75 years), biologic sex (male/female), race (non-Hispanic white, Hispanic, black, and other), poverty index (defined as the census neighborhood of a residence with a poverty level of 0-<5%, 5-<10%, 10-<20, and 20–100%), and disease stage were extracted [17]. Relevant treatment patterns and trends overtime included systemic therapy (none, radiation alone, chemotherapy alone, and chemotherapy plus radiation) and chemotherapy administration (none, single agent, multiagent, not otherwise specified [NOS]). Specific data on individual chemotherapy regimens, number of treatment cycles, dosages used, radiation fractions, performance status, and toxicities were not available.

Consort Diagram

At the date of last follow-up, the patient status recode was reported as either alive or deceased. Causes of death were reported to the TCR and relevant ICD 7, 9, and 10 codes were provided. Patient cause of death recode was either attributed to the primary cancer or “non-cHL death. Non-cHL causes of death were collected and systematically categorized as “second primary cancer (SPC),” renal, neurologic, cardiovascular, respiratory, hematologic, infectious, and “other.”

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as medians for continuous variables, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in patient-level characteristics for cHL in the years 1995–2002, 2003–2010, and 2011–2017 including histologic subtype, age, sex, race, and poverty index, and stage were compared using the Pearson’s Chi-square test with continuity correction or Fisher’s exact test. Patient outcomes included median OS and disease specific survival (DSS) for the entire cohort and those that received only multiagent therapies, which were compared with Kaplan-Meier methodology, with significance assessed by Breslow and log-rank (Mentel-Cox) tests. The Cox proportional-hazards model using hazard ratios (HR) was used to investigate the association between the survival time of patients treated in each era and demographic factors. The Chi-square test was used to assess the significance of the difference. Descriptive statistics using 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used for OS, DSS, and HR. For the above-reported comparisons, a two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Out of 12,854 patients diagnosed with cHL, there were 1,033 (8.0%) identified between 1995 and 2017 that met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The median age was 74 (65–97) years, 537 (51.9%) were males, 671 (64.9%) were non-Hispanic Whites, and 302 (29.2%) were Hispanic. Nodular sclerosis was reported in 589 (57.1%), mixed cellularity in 335 (32.4%), lymphocyte rich in 59 (5.7%), and lymphocyte depleted in 50 (4.8%) patients. A total of 680 (65.8%) individuals were classified as residing in a region with a poverty index of 10% or higher. By era, 386 (37.4%), 336 (32.5%), and 311 (30.1%) were diagnosed 1995–2002, 2003–2010, and 2011–2017, respectively. Treatment patterns

A total of 302 (29.2%) patients received no treatment; 203 (52.6%), 199 (59.2%), and 182 (58.5%) received chemotherapy, and 28 (7.2%), 35 (10.4%), and 28 (9.0%) received chemotherapy + radiation in 1995–2002, 2003–2010, and 2011–2017, respectively. There was an increasing trend of multiagent therapies over time, with 155 (40.1%), 168 (50.0%), and 156 (50.2%), respectively per era, who received multiagent chemotherapy. There was a total of 56 (5.4%) who received radiation alone, 584 (56.5%) who received chemotherapy alone, and 91 (8.8%) who received chemotherapy + radiation (Figure S1A). The median age of those receiving no treatment, chemotherapy alone, radiation alone, and chemotherapy and radiation was 77 (65–97), 73 (65–93), 75 (65–87), and 72 (65–87) years, respectively.

Outcomes

Between 1995 and 2002, 2003–2010, and 2011–2017, median DSS was 97 months (95% CI: 50.7, 143.2), 158 months (95% CI 132.4, 183.5), and not reached (NR), respectively (p = 0.383) (Fig. 2A). The median OS for all patients was 32 months (95% CI 23.34840.6), 37 months (95% CI 23.5, 50.4), and 40 months (95% CI 26.8, 53.1) per era re spectively (p = 0.693) (Fig. 2B). For patients who received multiagent chemotherapy, median DSS was 174 months (95% CI 91.521, 256.479), and NR in either of the later era, respectively (p = 0.459) (Figure S2A). Median OS for patients who received multiagent systemic therapies was 34 (95% CI 18.4, 49.5), 36 months (95% CI 18.6, 53.3), and 33 months (95% CI 12.1, 53.8), respectively (p = 0.776) (Figure S2B).

Assessment of factors contributing to outcomes

By race, median OS was 42 months (95% CI 32.1 51.9) for non-Hispanic whites, 36 months (95% CI 12.0, 59.9) for blacks, 18 months (95% CI 7.6, 28.3) for Hispanics, and 18 months (95% CI 0.0, 41.3) for all others (p = 0.007) (Fig. 2C). By poverty index, median OS was 63 months (95% CI 47.5, 78.4) for 0-<5%, 37 months (95% CI 22.5, 51.4) for 5-<10%, 32 months (95% CI 18.9, 45.0) for 10-<20%, and was 28 months (95% CI 17.862, 38.138) for patients living in the 20–100% poverty index (p = 0.006) (Fig. 2D). Each incremental annual increase in age at diagnosis (range: 65–97 years) was associated with worse median OS (p < 0.001). There were no survival differences for patient sex (p = 0.264), histologic subtype (p = 0.095), or stage (p = 0.076) (Figure S2c-e).

At last follow-up, 836 (80.9%) patients died, of which 422 (50.5%) were attributed to a non-cHL death (Table 1, Table S3). The most common non-cHL causes of death included cardiac events in 113 (26.9%), neurologic events in 60 (14.3%), and second cancers in 67 (15.2%) patients (Fig 3). Median time to death due to cHL, cardiovascular diseases, second cancers, and neurologic events was 6 months (95% CI 4.8, 7.1), 34 months (95% CI 16.5, 51.4), 58 months (95% CI 51.1, 64.8), and 96 months (95% CI 63.2, 128.7), respectively (p < 0.001). Death due to second cancers (p = 0.606) and cardiovascular events (p = 0.685) were not influenced by receipt of radiation or chemotherapy in this cohort of patients (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this registry-based study assessing outcomes over the past 20 years in older patients with cHL, the following important findings were noted: There was a trend toward improving median OS and DSS over time, but neither of these were statistically significant; multiagent chemotherapy regimens did improve survival outcomes, but there was no difference in survival outcomes for patients who only received radiation therapy as opposed to single agent therapies; residing in regions of higher poverty index and advancing age at time of diagnosis was associated with poorer outcomes; there was race-dependent differences in median OS.

Older patients with cHL frequently have more comorbidities, poorer performance status, and a more aggressive disease biology than younger patients [18–22]. This may have influenced the 34.6% of patients who did not receive any form of systemic therapy in this study. This is not unique to our study; a recent SEER analysis reported that 25% of older patients received no systemic therapy. In this analysis, older age, patient frailty, and cardiac disease had significantly increased odds of receiving single agent chemotherapy or radiation therapy alone.

As reported in other studies, our study indicated a trend toward increasing utilization of multiagent regimens over time [23]– [24, 29]. It is possible that this reflects evolving treatment options. While the standard of care (SOC) has evolved in recent years to incorporate Brentuximab vedotin to AVD (Bv-AVD) in the front line setting based on results of the Echelon-1 study for advanced stage cHL, OS did not improve in adults ≥ 60 years [12]. An additional analysis of Echelon-1 also questioned the applicability of this regimen in the frontline for older adults and found that patients ≥ 60 years had a similar PFS between treatment cohorts (5-year PFS was 67.1% with BV + AVD versus 61.6% with ABVD (HR = 0.820 [95% CI: 0.494–1.362], P = 0.443), higher rates of peripheral neuropathy and neutropenia, but lower rates of pulmonary toxicity [25]. This data also reflects stagnant survival trends in older patients. More recently, Nivolumab-AVD (N-AVD) was found to improve PFS over BV-AVD (94% vs. 86% at 12.1 months, respectively), but it is important to note that the median age of this cohort was 27 (range 12–83 years) years. Of these patients, only 10% were older than 60, so subgroup assessment of tolerance, PFS, and OS in older patients is needed [11–12. Since then, there have been several studies looking at these different regimens. A recent phase II study looked at N-AVD in adults over 60 with advanced disease and found a 3 year PFS and OS of 79% and 97%, respectively, and notably found that 10% of patients stopped therapy due to toxicities. Importantly, baseline geriatric impairments did not correlate with outcomes [43]. A recent subgroup analysis of SWOG S1826 assessing outcomes of adults aged 60 years and older who received N-AVD compared to Bv-AVD, patients in the N-AVD had significantly lower rates of peripheral neuropathy, febrile neutropenia, sepsis, and infections, and significantly better 1 year PFS (93% vs. 64%, p = 0.022), EFS (93% vs. 57%, p = 0.0011), and trend toward better OS (95% vs. 83%, p = 0.091) [44]Other studies have also highlighted the improved toxicity profile of N-AVD vs. Bv-AVD in older patients [27]. While N-AVD and Bv-AVD may be newer treatment options in advanced cHL, not all patients can tolerate this combination, and alternative strategies should be considered. For instance, the combination of BV with nivolumab was assessed, reporting an overall response rate (ORR) of 64%, complete metabolic remission of 52%, and a durable remission with PFS of 18.2 months in all patients and not reached in patients with a complete response. Peripheral neuropathy rates were reported in 48% of patients [46]. Nivolumab as a single agent therapy was studied in a French trial for adults over the age of 60 years, which reported a complete metabolic response of 28.6% and median PFS of 9.8 months [47]. Unfortunately, the majority of patients eventually progressed. This data suggests that single agent nivolumab may provide temporary benefit, but it does not appear to provide sustained response of curative potential monotherapy has been studied in frail patients as well. In a phase II study of patients ≥ 60 years with cHL, frontline Bv monotherapy was used in up to 16 cycles, with an ORR of 92%, and a CR of 73%. However, the majority of patients relapsed, and the median PFS was 10.5 months [48]. In an effort to attenuate the toxicities of combination Bv-AVD in older frail patients, Evens et al. attempted to to a sequential approach with Bv monotherapy for 2 cycles, followed by AVD for 6 cycles, and then Bv consolidation. There was a 93% complete remission rate, a 2-year PFS of 84%, and only 8% discontinuation rate [26] This data highlights the unique and effective strategies needed to manage older patients. While our study did not include specific treatment protocols, it is a useful adjunct to better understand how implementing strategies may improve outcomes overtime. Older patients with cHL historically do not have equivalent outcomes to systemic treatment regimens when compared to age-matched cohorts. Aside from differences in disease biology, tolerance to therapy and toxicities likely play a major role. In older patients, the most common dose-limiting toxicities include leukopenia, cardiopulmonary events, and infections [19, 30–33]. Aside from treatment-limiting toxicities, other attributing factors have been suggested, including a poorer performance status, a more aggressive disease biology, advanced stage, Epstein-Barr virus mediation, or a higher proportion of mixed cellularity [18–20]. Comorbidities have been found to be an independent factor impacting both DSS and OS [22, 34, 35]. Evens et al. reported that age ≥ 70 years and loss of independence in activities of daily living were associated with poorer outcomes [1]. Moreover, Sykorova et al. and Proctor et al. identified additional factors—including a CIRS (Cumulative Illness Rating Score) greater than 3, lymphopenia < 8%, and an anthracycline-free chemotherapy regimen—that led to more unfavorable outcomes [35, 36]. While these are all possible reasons explaining poorer outcomes, other studies have found no differences in prognostic factors, suggesting the diverse heterogeneity of the older population [22].

In our analysis, while differences in sex, histology, and stage did not impact survival, poverty index, race, and increasing age negatively impacted OS, suggesting that health disparities exist in the older population. Our findings also align closely with U.S. registry-based studies—such as SEER–Medicare and NCDB analyses—which report 2–5-year OS between 58 and 65% in cHL patients aged ≥ 60, with persistently worse outcomes for Black and Hispanic individuals [45]. In a U.S. SEER analysis, Hispanics had the highest incidence rates of Hodgkin’s lymphoma among patients over 64 years old, followed by non-Hispanic Whites and then Blacks [45]. Moreover, Olszewski et al. and subsequently Rodday et al. examined SEER data for patients aged 60 to 65 and older, spanning roughly 1999 to 2015. Their analyses revealed that older patients were treated with less intensive chemotherapy and experienced worse survival outcomes than younger patients [49, 50].

Nearly half of the deaths reported in this study were attributed to non-cHL causes. There was a total of 836 (80.9%) deaths reported, of which 422 (50.5%) were not attributed to cHL. Of these deaths, over 25% were from cardiac causes, followed by second cancers and neurologic events. Lung cancer (34.3%) (Figure S3) was the most common secondary solid cancer. This supports findings in the literature, with solid tumors accounting for the majority of second cancers in cHL survivors, with breast, lung, and colon cancer being the most frequent. A comprehensive analysis of three significant studies comprising 6,292 patients showed that lung cancer contributed the greatest excess cancer risk, followed by leukemia. In women, breast cancer was the leading second cancer risk [37–39]. Currently, there are no guidelines informing upon appropriate screening times beyond what the American Cancer Society recommends for the general population [40].

Cardiovascular disease is the most common non-malignant cause of death in long- term survivors of cHL [41]. According to a retrospective study done in the UK that included over 7,033 patients with Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), the standardized mortality ratio for cardiac disease among HL survivors was 2.5 times higher (95% CI 2.1–2.9) and cardiac events were 10 times more common compared to the general population, with risk remaining elevated for a minimum of 25 years. For those who underwent supradiaphragmatic radiation therapy plus vincristine without anthracyclines, the risk was elevated by 15 times [41]. Our study showed that combination of chemotherapy and radiation therapies did not impact the risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.04 [95% CI 0.851, 1.279], p = 0.685) or second cancers (HR 1.069 [95% CI 0.830, 1.376], 95% CI 0.606). This could be related to improvement in radiation techniques and from longer time needed for radiation therapy related tissue injury. Median time to reported death event was 6 months (95% CI 4.8, 7.1) for cHL, 34 months (95% CI 16.5, 51.4) for cardiovascular events, 58 months (95% CI 51.1, 64.8) for second cancers, and 96 months (95% CI 63.239, 128.761) for neurologic events (p < 0.001). We speculate that the reported cardiovascular causes of death are likely exaggerated by pre-existing comorbidities that might further predispose to cardiac events.

Limitations of this study should be recognized. First, this is a retrospective study. If the data reported is inaccurate, it can lead to false assumptions and conclusions. For example, 32% of patients reported not receiving any therapy. Despite this number being relatively consistent with other studies including a recent SEER database analysis, this number may be inaccurate and limited by what is reported to the TCR. Neither the regimens, the number of cycles, patient tolerance, performance status, comorbidities, and subsequent lines of therapy are reported by the TCR and this important information needs to be further assessed in institutional studies. Moreover, whether the treatment line itself attributed to patient mortality or the timing of treatment initiation to time of death is not reported, and thus conclusions about treatment-related mortality cannot be assessed in this study. Furthermore, this database does report geriatric assessments, which predated the 2018 American Society of Clinical Oncology recommendation to provide a formal geriatric assessment in all adults 65 years and older who are to receive systemic therapy, in order to identify vulnerabilities not normally identified with routine oncology assessments [42]. Finally, ICD coding may be inaccurate, and therefore, not all causes of death may reflect the true cause of death in this cohort.

Conclusion

Despite evolving treatment strategies, survival has not improved for older patients with cHL over the past two decades. Socioeconomic status and racial disparities impact patient survival and strategies are needed to mitigate these factors. Non-cHL mortality was high, and given the high proportion of cardiovascular, neurologic, and second cancers contributing to patient deaths, standardization of long-term clinical monitoring after completion of therapy is needed.
