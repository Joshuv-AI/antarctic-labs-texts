The interaction of second trimester prenatal maternal inflammation and psychosocial stress on offspring depressive symptoms in adolescence
Authors: Rachel H Furlan, Madeline R Pike, Emily Lipner, Elizabeth C Breen, Barbara A Cohn, Piera M Cirillo, Nickilou Y Krigbaum, Ann M Kring, Thomas M Olino, Lauren B Alloy, Lauren M Ellman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Higher second trimester (T2) prenatal maternal inflammation (PNMI) and prenatal maternal psychosocial stress have been shown to independently contribute to offspring depression risk. Similarly, interactions between sources of inflammation and maternal daily life stress in T2, previously have been associated with increased offspring adolescent depressive symptoms. We aimed to extend previous findings by examining the potential interaction between exposure to higher T2 PNMI and maternal daily life stress on offspring depressive symptoms in adolescence.Methods:614 mother-offspring dyads from the Child Health and Development Studies (CHDS) had data available for T2 maternal serum interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra), and soluble TNF receptor-II (sTNF-RII), presence/absence of maternal reported daily life stress coded from interviews primarily conducted in T2, and adolescent offspring (ages 15–18 years) depressive symptoms assessed via self-report. Interactions were evaluated using hierarchical multiple regressions, controlling for maternal education.Results:Maternal daily life stress interacted with higher serum levels of maternal T2 IL-6 and T2 IL-8 to predict adolescent offspring depressive symptoms. Higher IL-6 and higher IL-8 were only associated with offspring depression in the presence of daily life stress. Maternal T2 IL-1ra and sTNF-RII were not associated with offspring adolescent depressive symptoms.Conclusion:The interaction of the adverse impacts of maternal daily life stress and higher maternal IL-6 and/or IL-8 levels during the second trimester may contribute significantly to exacerbate depression risk in adolescent offspring. These results have potential implications for multiple targets of future early intervention and prevention research.Keywords: Pregnancy, Inflammation, Maternal stress, Adolescence, Depressive symptoms

Introduction

Substantial evidence has suggested that risk for depression and related psychopathology in offspring can be influenced by maternal biopsychosocial stressors during gestation (Su et al., 2021; Monk et al., 2019; Fineberg et al., 2016; Coussons-Read, 2013). Findings have demonstrated a direct association between heightened prenatal maternal psychosocial stress—such as traumatic life events or daily life stress—and later offspring depressive symptoms (O’Connor et al., 2022; Davis et al., 2020; Kingsbury et al., 2016; Betts et al., 2014). Further, prenatal maternal psychosocial stress has been associated with several potential indirect pathways to offspring depression risk. Elevated prenatal maternal psychosocial stress has been correlated with an increase in obstetric complications linked to increased offspring depression risk, including premature birth, low birth weight, and abnormal development of the offspring hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Awad-Sirhan et al., 2022; Loret De Mola et al., 2014). Similarly, prenatal maternal psychosocial stress sequelae have been associated with early intermediate risk factors for depression, including lower cognitive abilities in toddlers (Laplante et al., 2018), increased shyness (Talge et al., 2007), and childhood externalizing and internalizing problems (Tung et al., 2023; O’Connor et al., 2002; Davis and Sandman, 2012; Rodriguez and Bohlin, 2005; Loomans et al., 2011). Despite evidence that prenatal maternal psychosocial stress may increase risk for offspring depression, the mechanisms contributing to these associations are not fully understood.

The role of Prenatal Maternal Inflammation (PNMI)

Several biological mechanisms may explain the relationship between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and offspring depressive symptoms, including glucocorticoid exposure (Kim et al., 2015), epigenetic changes (Monk et al., 2019), and prenatal maternal inflammation (PNMI; Hantsoo et al., 2019). Of these potential mechanisms, elevated PNMI—approximated by higher levels of circulating maternal markers of inflammation (e.g., cytokines)– has received increasing attention in its role in offspring depression outcomes (Flinkkilä et al., 2016; Lipner et al., 2024). Preclinical and human research has provided evidence for the involvement of PNMI in the emergence of offspring depressive symptoms. Multiple rodent studies demonstrate direct associations between higher PNMI and offspring depressive-like behaviors, typically in response to maternal immune activation (MIA; Babri et al., 2014; Lin and Wang, 2014; Enayati et al., 2012). Only two studies (to our knowledge) to date have directly examined the relationship between maternal inflammatory markers and offspring depression in a human sample (Gilman et al., 2016; Lipner et al., 2024). Gilman and colleagues (2016) first documented that a higher maternal proinflammatory state in mid-to-late gestation contributed to an increased risk for adult offspring MDD diagnosis, but lower risk in females (Gilman et al., 2016). Lipner et al. (2024) found that greater IL-6 in the second trimester (T2) was linked to higher reported adolescent depressive symptoms. This relationship was specific to T2 IL-6, with no significant associations with T1 and T2 IL-8, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra) and soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor-II (sTNF-RII) and depressive symptoms nor T1 IL-6. As IL-6 has been implicated consistently in offspring neurodevelopment (Enayati et al., 2020; Diz-Chaves et al., 2013; Smith et al., 2007; Rasmussen et al., 2019; Rudolph et al., 2018; Hsiao and Patterson, 2011), and is known to cross the placental barrier (Zaretsky et al., 2004), it is of interest to the current study to build upon these findings with T2 IL-6.

Studies of humans also have identified indirect effects of PNMI on offspring depression. For example, higher levels of maternal IL-8 during the first trimester (T1) were associated with increased offspring externalizing symptoms in childhood (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019). In addition, IL-1ra (a proxy for IL-1β) in T2 was associated with offspring internalizing symptoms (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019), and was associated with higher reported adolescent depressive symptoms via childhood cognitive performance (Pike et al., 2024). Child internalizing and externalizing symptoms (Cicchetti and Toth, 1991), as well as poorer childhood cognitive performance have been linked to higher risk for depression at later stages of development (Barlati et al., 2022; Hung et al., 2016). Deeper exploration of this relationship between PNMI and depressive symptoms is warranted to better understand these findings, especially in the critical period of adolescence (Oldehinkel et al., 1999; Lewinsohn, 1998).

Prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and PNMI in offspring depressive symptoms

The relationship between psychosocial stress and PNMI is complex and complicated by the unique and changing, immunomodulatory and hormonal state of pregnancy (Hantsoo et al., 2019). One source of PNMI may be prenatal maternal psychosocial stress, as psychosocial stress is shown to predict increased production of maternal proinflammatory cytokines (Walsh et al., 2016; Andersson et al., 2016; Coussons-Read et al., 2012; Coussons-Read et al., 2007). Reviews of non-pregnant adult studies show the impact of psychosocial stress on inflammation (Rohleder, 2019; Marsland et al., 2017; Segerstrom and Miller, 2004). Elevated inflammation may evoke psychosocial stress in non-pregnant individuals via sickness behaviors and heightened anxiety and depressive symptoms, suggesting a bidirectional relationship (Maydych, 2019; Eisenberger et al., 2010). Regardless of the debated direction, a growing body of evidence suggests that there are significant, likely causal (e.g., from experimental studies; Benschop et al., 1996), associations between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and PNMI.

With building evidence demonstrating positive associations between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and PNMI, new findings indicate that the interaction of psychosocial stress and PNMI may be driving their shared contributions to offspring depression (Maydych, 2019). Rodent models have uncovered an interaction between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and heightened IL-1β and TNF-α expression on heightened offspring depression-like symptoms (Diz-Chaves et al., 2013; Enayati et al., 2020). Human studies demonstrated independent links of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress (O’Connor et al., 2022; Van Den Bergh et al., 2020; Kingsbury et al., 2016; Betts et al., 2014) or PNMI (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019; Rudolph et al., 2018) with adverse offspring outcomes and depression. Nonetheless, no human study has investigated prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and PNMI in interaction on offspring depression risk (reviewed in Hantsoo et al., 2019).

Human studies examining both psychosocial stress and maternal inflammation with respect to early offspring risk factors of depression yield complex findings. One study demonstrated that greater third trimester (T3) maternal proinflammatory cytokines mediated the effect of high prenatal maternal depression on infant negative affect (Gustafsson et al., 2018). However, in a sample of 4902 mother–child dyads, Clayborne et al. (2023) did not find indirect effects of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and childhood internalizing and externalizing symptoms through PNMI. These studies differed in PNMI operationalization, examined different points of gestation, and did not directly examine the interaction of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and PNMI, which may contribute to the mixed findings.

Studies examining prenatal infection may provide additional insight into the interaction of PNMI and prenatal maternal psychosocial stress on depression. Because most (but not all) maternal infections do not cross the placenta (Kourtis et al., 2014; Robbins and Bakardjiev, 2012), yet several inflammatory biomarkers can (Zaretsky et al., 2004), the maternal inflammatory responses to infection have been suggested as a driving mechanism of the maternal-fetal transmission of developmental risk (Flinkkilä et al., 2016). A series of human studies have linked infections during pregnancy to increased offspring depression risk (Murphy et al., 2017; Simanek and Meier, 2015; Markham and Koenig, 2011; Bilbo and Schwarz, 2009; Machón et al., 1997). Our team previously discovered that maternal infection and reported daily life stress during T2 interacted to predict increased offspring adolescent depressive symptoms (Murphy et al., 2017). As inflammatory markers were not directly examined here, additional work is needed to understand PNMI mechanisms accounting for past T2 findings.

The current study

The current study aimed to expand our team’s previous work exploring T2 prenatal risk factors for depressive symptoms among adolescent-aged (15–18 years old) offspring in a large, prospectively collected birth cohort. Specifically, we investigated whether the interaction of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and four markers of T2 PNMI [IL-6, IL-8, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra, a proxy for IL1-β), soluble TNF-receptor II (sTNF-RII, a marker of TNF- α activity)] is related to higher offspring depressive symptoms during adolescence. Given that prior research in this birth cohort indicated that greater offspring adolescent depressive symptoms are specifically related to 1) an interaction between prenatal infection and daily life stress uniquely during T2 (Murphy et al., 2017), and 2) a direct relationship with greater T2 IL-6 and not T1 IL-6 (Lipner et al., 2024), our primary hypothesis was that higher levels of T2 IL-6 and the presence of daily maternal stress would interact to predict greater depressive symptoms in adolescent offspring. Second, acknowledging findings between IL-1ra, TNF- α and IL-8 with depressive symptoms (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019; Gilman et al., 2016; Pike et al., 2024), we also explored whether higher levels of T2 IL-1ra, sTNF-RII, and/or IL-8 and the presence of daily maternal stress would interact to predict greater depressive symptoms in adolescent offspring.

Methods

Description of cohort

The Child Health and Development Studies (CHDS) is a prospective and longitudinal cohort study that followed mother-offspring dyads (N=19,044 live births) from pregnancy through offspring development (van den Berg et al., 1988). Participants were pregnant women seeking prenatal care under the Kaiser Foundation Health Plan (KFHP) while living in the greater Alameda County, California and surrounding areas between 1959 and 1966. During this time, almost one third of the Alameda County population was receiving care through KFHP. CHDS followed up with 2020 of the mother-offspring dyads born between 1960 and 1963 in an “Adolescent Study,” when offspring were 15–19 years old (1977–1979; van den Berg et al., 1988).

The present analyses drew from 652 mother-offspring dyads from the CHDS Adolescent Study for whom there were inflammatory biomarker data assayed from archived T2 maternal serum samples (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019), which is a subset of 1711 dyads previously evaluated for prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and offspring adolescent depression symptoms (Murphy et al., 2017). Of the 652 dyads with available T2 maternal biomarker data, 38 were missing data on adolescent depressive symptoms, and therefore, were excluded, bringing the maximum possible sample to 614 (see Supplementary Materials, Fig. 1, for flow diagram of participant selection). Sample sizes utilized in the analyses varied based on the availability of maternal second trimester biomarker data (range 578–611 out of 614, depending on the biomarker) and maternal interview data for daily stress (n=601; see Table 1). Study procedures and analyses were approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Temple University, University of California, Los Angeles, and the Public Health Institute. Although the initial CHDS study predated IRBs and informed consent; therefore, individuals assented to participation after receiving a comprehensive description of the study.

Demographics variables

Sociodemographic data were collected through maternal reports largely during T2 (median = 101 days, mean weeks gestation = 15.3, SD = 6.8). Maternal education was used as a proxy for socioeconomic status (SES), and it was selected because it is non-missing for most participants, considered to be highly accurate, and is associated with other measures of SES in this dataset such as household income (Fineberg et al., 2016; Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019; Lipner et al., 2022). Maternal education has been used as a proxy for postnatal advantages in previous similar studies (Schlotz and Phillips, 2009). Maternal education was recorded as three categories: “Did not complete high school”, “completed high school”, and “completed more than high school”. In this sample, maternal race/ethnicity was categorized as “White”, “Mexican/Hispanic”, “Black”, and “Asian”. During data collection between 1959 and 1966, race and Hispanic ethnicity were not asked separately. Offspring sex assigned at birth (herein, referred to as “sex”) was recorded as “Male” or “Female.” Maternal depressive symptoms were measured at the adolescent follow up by self-report questionnaire (see Murphy et al., 2017 for details). The variable was examined as a potential covariate.

Prenatal Maternal Inflammation (PNMI)

PNMI in CHDS T2 maternal serum samples was previously approximated by four individual biomarkers of inflammation, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1ra, and sTNF-RII, as described in detail in supplementary materials in Lipner et al. (2024). Data were reported in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL). Importantly, the archived CHDS samples showed no evidence of serological degradation (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019; Lipner et al., 2024).

Prenatal maternal psychosocial stress

Daily life stress was previously extracted from maternal interviews conducted largely during T2 (median = 101 days, mean weeks gestation = 15.3, SD = 6.8) and coded as described in previous reports from our group (Fineberg et al., 2016; Murphy et al., 2017). Briefly, responses to the open-ended question “What kinds of things have been worrying you recently?” were coded as “present” for daily life stress if the mother reported discrete observable events that required adjustment in identity or routines (Cohen et al., 1997) or relatively minor events that disrupt daily life (Serido et al., 2004), such as financial stress, marriage difficulties, moving, or the chronic but non-life-threatening illness of a family member. Daily life stress was chosen as the measure of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress because it has been associated with depression (Betts et al., 2014; Betts et al., 2015; Buss et al., 2011; Watson et al., 1999) and schizophrenia (Fineberg et al., 2016) risk in offspring (the latter in the CHDS cohort) and has been shown to significantly moderate the relationship between maternal infection and offspring depressive symptoms (Murphy, et al., 2017) in the CHDS cohort.

Adolescent depressive symptoms scale

As previously reported and described (Murphy, 2017; Maxwell, 2018; Lipner, 2024), in the CHDS adolescent follow-up study, offspring between the ages of 15 and 19 (mean age = 16.4 years, SD = 0.7 years, range = 15=18 for n=614 offspring included in the current analyses) were given a 130-item questionnaire asking about their lives and their beliefs about themselves. Fourteen of those questions surveyed affective and cognitive symptoms of depression such as feelings of worthlessness, failure, and inadequacy (see Murphy et al., 2017 for a copy of the exact questions). Responses on those 14 questions were measured on a 4-point Likert scale and were combined into an overall depressive symptoms score, ranging from 14 to 56. This measure of depressive symptomatology has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81), high predictive validity to major depressive disorder (MDD) diagnoses using semi-structured interviews at a 20–30-year-old follow-ups of these offspring (data available upon request), and strong concurrent validity (Maxwell et al., 2018; Murphy et al., 2017).

Analytic plan

All analyses were conducted in R (v 4.2.2). The dependent variable, adolescent depressive symptoms score, was assessed for statistical normality by visualizing a graphic representation of the data and by examining skewness and kurtosis values. All PNMI biomarkers were natural log-transformed given skewness of variables and then were mean-centered. Daily life stress was dichotomous (present or absent).

Bivariate Pearson correlations were estimated between the outcome variable (adolescent depressive symptoms), the independent variables (PNMI biomarkers and daily life stress), and potential covariates (maternal education, maternal age at offspring birth, maternal depressive symptoms at adolescent timepoint, parity, and offspring sex). Maternal education was included in adjusted analyses as a covariate to account for a proxy for postnatal advantage (Pike et al., 2024). Other potential covariates (e.g., maternal age at offspring birth, maternal depressive symptoms at adolescent time point, parity and offspring sex) were identified as variables with sufficient theoretical link to the proposed analyses; however, of these additional potential covariates, only variables that were significantly correlated with adolescent depressive symptoms and at least one independent variable (PNMI biomarkers and daily life stress) were included as covariates in final analyses.

Four hierarchical multiple regressions were conducted, one for each biomarker, with unadjusted and adjusted (maternal education) models. Each model included 1) the individual T2 PNMI biomarker (IL-6, IL-8, IL-1ra, or sTNF-RII), 2) presence/absence of maternal daily life stress, and 3) PNMI biomarker by daily life stress interaction term, as predictors of adolescent depressive symptoms. For biomarkers with significant interaction terms, additional models were conducted stratifying by presence or absence of maternal daily life stress in a set of regressions to descriptively probe what was driving the significant interaction between daily life stress in the relationship between PNMI and adolescent depressive symptoms (i.e., with the biomarker as the independent variable and adolescent depressive symptoms as the dependent variable). All model results were examined for evidence of regression assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity, independence between observations, and normality of residuals. All tests were two-tailed with p < 0.05 indicating significance.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first human study to document interactions between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and measures of circulating levels of markers of PNMI in the development of offspring depressive symptoms in adolescence. We found significant interactions of higher maternal serum T2 levels of IL-6 or IL-8 with the presence of maternal daily life stress during pregnancy contributing to depressive symptoms in adolescent offspring. Stratified analyses on the presence or absence of reported maternal prenatal daily life stress, suggested that higher T2 IL-6 in particular, and IL-8 were driving the significant interactions that predicted higher depressive symptoms in adolescent offspring only when prenatal maternal stress had been present (the latter only approached significance in stratified analyses, p=.06). Therefore, our findings suggest that the influences of higher IL-6 and IL-8 in the second trimester may be conditional upon presence of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress in its impact on later offspring depressive symptoms. Although IL-8 only approached significance in predicting adolescent depressive symptoms in the presence of reported daily life stress, the interaction between T2 IL-8 and prenatal maternal psychosocial stress was significant; therefore, these stratified analyses (that reduced the sample sizes from the full analyses) still highlight that the significant interaction was driven by the influence of T2 IL-8 in the presence of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress.

It is well established that IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, and IL-8, a pro-inflammatory chemokine, contribute to implantation and maintenance of a healthy pregnancy via several shared processes (e.g., mediation of endometrial receptivity, feto-maternal immune-endocrine interactions) (Vilotić et al., 2022). Moreover, during T2, there is a relative decrease in the inflammatory state compared to T1, characterized by a reduction in some pro-inflammatory cytokines and an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines; this anti-inflammatory shift is thought to minimize the risk of immune rejection of the fetus by the mother’s immune system (Mor et al., 2011). Therefore, increases in maternal proinflammatory proteins during T2 may contribute to disruptions in fetal development. As both IL-6 and IL-8 have been shown to increase in response to chronic psychosocial stress in non-pregnant (Liu et al., 2022) and pregnant human samples (Wright et al., 2010; Andersson et al., 2016, Coussons-Read, 2013), higher levels of maternal psychosocial stress during T2 may contribute to dysregulated IL-6 and IL-8 expression, increasing risk of altered fetal development and obstetric complications seen in risk pathways to offspring depressive symptoms (Serati et al., 2020). Both IL-6 and IL-8 interacted with reported daily life stress to contribute to offspring depressive symptoms and only impacted offspring depressive symptoms when prenatal maternal psychosocial stress was present. These findings suggest that psychosocial stress is necessary for IL-6 and IL-8 to disrupt fetal development in the second trimester, potentially via numerous fetal, placental and/or maternal mechanisms. More research is needed to better determine how these inflammatory proteins specifically interact with 1) prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and 2) each other to contribute to offspring depressive symptoms.

In contrast, T2 IL1-ra and sTNF-RII were not independently associated with nor did they interact with daily life stress to predict offspring depressive symptoms. We cannot be certain why IL1-ra and sTNF-RII were not significantly associated with prenatal maternal psychosocial stress or depressive symptoms, as both biomarkers’ respective cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) have been independently connected to elevated stress (Goshen and Yirmiya, 2009; Huang et al., 2011) and depression risk (Berthold-Losleben and Himmerich., 2008; Farooq et al., 2017) in non-pregnant adult samples. However, since one or both of these cytokines may circulate at relatively low concentrations in pregnancy (Ross et al., 2022; Jarmund et al., 2021), which may be close to or below the level of detection, it is possible this has led to mixed results depending on the sample type and assay methods utilized. Additional investigations into the possible mechanisms of IL1-ra and sTNF-RII, as well as IL-1β and TNF-α, may reveal an explanation for their lack of significant associations in this study.

Our significant interaction results with IL-6 and IL-8 align with and extend a prior study within the same cohort by Murphy et al. (2017) that documented a significant interaction between second trimester prenatal maternal infection and prenatal maternal psychosocial stress to predict adolescent offspring depressive symptoms. This, in addition to the findings that IL-6 and IL-8 were not significantly associated with reported maternal daily life stress implies that some of the greater PNMI could be due in part to other factors associated with inflammation, such as (but not limited to) maternal infection during pregnancy. Despite previous studies associating prenatal maternal psychosocial stress with greater PNMI (Walsh et al., 2016; Andersson et al., 2016; Coussons-Read et al., 2012; Coussons-Read et al., 2007), the present study in the CHDS cohort did not find evidence of this direct relationship.

Although there is growing evidence supporting the independent adverse impacts of elevated PNMI (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2019; Rudolph et al., 2018) and prenatal maternal psychosocial stress (O’Connor et al., 2022; Van Den Bergh et al., 2020; Kingsbury et al., 2016; Betts et al., 2014) on offspring psychological outcomes, their potential interaction has not been well-researched. Only one preclinical study examined the independent and interactive effects of psychological and immune stress and found different effects of two hits (psychological and immune stress) compared to one hit (psychological or immune stress exposure) models on pregnancy outcomes and offspring physiology (Verstraeten et al., 2019). This supports the possibility for a prenatal psychosocial stress by inflammation interactive impact on offspring that is distinct from their independent effects. Fetal exposure to Maternal Immune Activation (MIA) is relatively common, but the incidence of significant neurodevelopmental changes measured in offspring due to MIA is decidedly less so (Meyer, 2019). Prenatal maternal psychosocial stress has been hypothesized to promote further susceptibility to such changes (Bauman and Buss, 2022; Meyer, 2019), but the mechanisms through which it may sensitize the fetus to the impacts of PNMI are not yet known. However, some evidence suggests that maternal daily life stress may sensitize the fetus to the adverse impacts of PNMI through increasing the permeability of the placenta to markers of inflammation such as proinflammatory cytokines. There is some evidence demonstrating that stress can compromise the permeability of the placental membrane (Aye and Keelan, 2013) and affect the activity of placental barrier enzymes that help to control the fetal environment (O’Donnell et al., 2009). IL-6 has been shown to cross the placental membrane (Zaretsky et al., 2004), and a membrane made more permeable by the effects of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress could enhance its access to fetal developmental pathways. Additionally, it is possible that greater PNMI and psychosocial stress may increase offspring susceptibility for the development of depressive symptoms after further insult, consistent with the multiple-hit model reviewed in Nabeshima and Kim (2013). In one study, adult offspring of prenatally stressed mice showed increased neuroinflammatory response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) administration relative to offspring of non-stressed mice, suggesting that offspring immune function and reactivity can be impacted well after the initial prenatal stress exposure (Diz-Chaves et al., 2012, 2013). A separate study similarly demonstrated that stress-exposed mice in adolescence that also were exposed to prenatal immune challenge developed pathological behaviors and neurochemical changes in adulthood (Giovanoli et al., 2013). Taken together, these preclinical findings indicate that prenatal exposures can impact offspring immune and stress reactivity long-term, thereby contributing to the development of offspring psychopathology.

Contrary to several previous studies noting associations between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and adverse offspring psychiatric outcomes such as depression risk (O’Connor et al., 2022; Van Den Bergh et al., 2020; Maxwell et al., 2018; Kingsbury et al., 2016; Betts et al., 2015, 2014), the present study did not detect an independent association between reported prenatal maternal daily life stress and offspring depressive symptoms specifically evaluated in adolescence. It is possible that the assessment of stress (outlined below) may have contributed to null results in our study; however, the demonstrated interactions between daily life stress and IL-6 and IL-8 were consistent with our expectations based on the results of our previous study (Murphy et al., 2017). Thus, higher PNMI, and specifically IL-6 and IL-8, may be a driving biological intermediary of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and neuropsychiatric risk, as supported by past animal studies (Coussons-Read, 2013; Hantsoo et al., 2019). Importantly, the current study replicated Lipner et al. (2024) by demonstrating a main effect of IL-6 on offspring depressive symptoms. This, combined with the fact that IL-6 strongly and significantly predicted increased offspring depression risk only in the presence of daily life stress, may imply a moderating role of psychosocial stress on the relationship between IL-6 and offspring depressive symptoms rather than an unspecified interaction. More research is necessary to further characterize this interaction.

Strengths and limitations

Our study results are strengthened by our large, prospective, longitudinal sample. Further, the CHDS sample interviewed mothers during their pregnancies, collecting information about maternal stress themes directly from mothers. Importantly, although our study was dependent on archived serum, our sample showed no evidence of serological degeneration (Lipner et al., 2024), increasing confidence in our PNMI samples.

This being said, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, although the scale of depressive symptoms among adolescent offspring measured the cognitive and thought-based depressive symptoms such as feelings of worthlessness or inadequacy, it did not include any somatic symptoms (e.g. issues with sleep, energy, and appetite), which have been linked to markers of inflammation in populations with depression (Andréasson et al., 2007; Motivala et al., 2005). However, higher scores on this scale were associated with depression diagnoses in follow-up studies with this cohort into adulthood (Murphy et al., 2017), supporting its validity. It is possible therefore that the inclusion of somatic symptoms of depression would have strengthened our findings. Future studies may wish to include a standardized and comprehensive measure of depression to account for clinical diagnoses of depression rather than depressive symptoms.

Furthermore, there are a few limitations associated with our measure of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress to consider. Although inter-rater reliability was high (Fineberg et al., 2016), the coding of stress themes was qualitative in nature and limited by the data available. Maternal interviews took place largely during the second trimester, but the precise timing or chronicity of the reported stresses cannot be determined, thus limiting possible interpretations of our results. It is possible that the lack of significant associations found between prenatal maternal psychosocial stress and offspring depressive symptoms was impacted by these factors, especially considering that this result is contrary to those of previous studies (Kingsbury et al., 2016; Sandman et al., 2013). To its advantage, our daily life stress variable captures mild-to-moderately stressful events of daily life, contributing to its applicability for the general population. However, its inclusiveness also renders the variable somewhat heterogeneous, making it complex to interpret. Further research is necessary to better parse apart the aspects of prenatal maternal psychosocial stress associated with offspring mental health outcomes.

The inflammatory biomarkers included in this study also were restricted. Though we included four prominent inflammatory factors, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1ra, and sTNF-RII that had strong theoretical and empirical support from previous preclinical and human studies, including other immune markers would have provided a deeper understanding of PNMI in this sample. Because our maternal serum was collected as part of the original CHDS study in the 1960s, subsequent analyses were limited to a finite amount of serum. The study investigators carefully considered biomarker selection while working within the constraints of the sample and utilized the analyses that were available and validated at the time (see Lipner et al., 2024 for more details).

Additionally, we did not explore the potential sources of inflammation in this study. Increases in inflammation could be due to a variety of factors, including but not limited to poor sleep, physical inactivity, smoking, vitamin D deficiency, infection, and air pollution (Berk et al., 2013). Further, the magnitude and specific impact (e.g., brain development, psychopathology, cognitive function) of elevated maternal serum concentrations of inflammatory biomarkers on offspring could vary due to multiple factors, including maternal and fetal genetics, chronicity of exposure, and combinations with other obstetric complications (Ellman et al., 2018). Finally, we cannot determine whether the PNMI is higher as a result of psychosocial stress (or even vice-versa) and/or co-occurs during T2 as a result of environmental stressors such as infection (Kemeny and Schedlowski, 2007) or poor maternal health behaviors (Christian, 2012) because the temporality of our measurement is not exact (e.g., blood samples were often not collected at the time of doctor appointments for infections). Overall, future research would benefit from examining more closely possible sources of PNMI to better understand the mechanisms through which offspring development is impacted.

There are demonstrated sex differences in the impact of PNMI on offspring depression risk (Gilman et al., 2016), and we have documented a sex-dependent main effect of PNMI on offspring depressive symptoms in this cohort (Lipner et al., 2024). Additionally, there are demonstrated sex differences in fetal responses to maternal stress (Ellman et al., 2008) and cortisol (Ellman et al., 2019) during pregnancy. Despite these findings and the plausible differential impacts of sex on offspring depression risk, we were unfortunately unable to explore potential sex-dependent effects in the present study. Our sample lacked sufficient power to include sex in our interaction model. Future studies with access to larger samples may wish to consider exploring three-way interactions between sex, inflammation, and maternal psychosocial stress.

Future directions

The current study contributes to our understanding of the complex interplay between prenatal risk factors for adolescent offspring depression. To add to this complexity, preclinical and clinical studies have shown that prenatal and postnatal factors (e.g. negative parenting strategies and postnatal stress exposure) may combine to further increased risk for the development of psychopathology (Debost et al., 2017; Giovanoli et al., 2013; Diz-Chaves et al., 2013). Overall, most offspring exposed to prenatal insults do not go on to develop psychopathology (Meyer et al., 2019) and effect sizes for responses to such exposures tend to be small (e.g. Ellman et al., 2018; Murphy et al., 2017). This could suggest that because an individual risk factor’s impact is small, it is when exposures across developmental time points combine that we see stronger influences on offspring psychopathology (Maxwell et al., 2018). Future directions therefore may include investigating postnatal risk factors at multiple timepoints in the years between the prenatal period and adolescence such as obstetric complications, cognitive functioning, and childhood mood and behavior, as they may contribute to the development of psychopathology in adolescence (Ellman et al., 2018).

Conclusions

In sum, our findings suggest that fetal exposure to the combination of maternal daily life stress during pregnancy and higher levels of maternal second trimester IL-6 and IL-8 are associated with increases in depression symptoms in adolescent-aged offspring. Although prenatal exposure is not always avoidable, the identification of potential maternal behavioral risk factors underscores the importance of mitigating and treating sources of both maternal stress and inflammation in the prenatal period. It is important to continue to characterize the relationships between PNMI, prenatal maternal psychosocial stress, and offspring depressive symptoms in both the human and animal literature in order to increase our understanding of the mechanisms involved and subsequently inform maternal healthcare practices, as well as understand risk factors for later mental health outcomes (which could ultimately contribute to early intervention strategies). Overall, the results of this study have potential implications for early prevention and intervention strategies seeking to improve offspring mental health outcomes.
