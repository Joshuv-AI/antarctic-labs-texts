Catalytic nano-metal interfaces drive pH-universal CO2-to-ethanol conversion
Authors: Ruihu Lu, Jiexin Zhu, Chenfeng Xia, Mengjiao Li, Ruohan Yu, Xingbao Chen, Yuhang Dai, Wei Zong, Yu Long, Bao Yu Xia, Liqiang Mai, Ziyun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renewably powered electrochemical CO2 reduction (CO2R) to alcohols presents a promising route to liquid fuels and chemicals. However, protonation of different intermediates often leads to mixed liquid and gas products, complicating separation and reducing economic feasibility. Here, we present a hydroxyl-affinity-tuning strategy that stabilizes hydroxyl-containing CO2R intermediates away from interfacial water through constructing nano-metal interfaces, thereby favoring ethanol production. Theory-guided catalyst design directs nano-cobalt/Cu surfaces, delivering CO2-to-ethanol Faradaic efficiencies (FE) of 62.17% and 45.32% under alkaline and acidic conditions, respectively, with ethanol partial current densities over ~300 mA cm−2. In acidic media, the catalyst maintains ethanol production for 235 hours with an average FE of 44.74%. In situ spectroscopy and theoretical calculations reveal that nano-Co/Cu interfaces favor binding hydroxyl groups and thereby enriches C-OH species on the copper surface, redirects CO2R pathways toward ethanol. This work provides a catalyst-centered strategy for designing interfacial architectures for selective alcohol synthesis. Future efforts should focus on translating this design principle into scalable systems for practical electrochemical CO2 utilization.

Introduction

Electrocatalytic reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2R), powered by renewable electricity to produce chemicals and fuel1,2, presents a highly promising approach to storing intermittent renewable energy and could help build the carbon-neutral cycle associated with human activities3. Of the products from CO2R, multi-carbon (C2+) liquid products, such as alcohol, are valuable industrial chemicals and fuels due to their high energy densities, ease of storage, purification, and transportability4. However, the formation of alcohol from CO2 is complex, involving the formation and competition among C-C, C-H, and O-H bonds within CO2R intermediates5. Selectively directing the formation or cleavage of these bonds towards alcohols under CO2R potential remains significantly challenging. So far, extensive effort has been made to improve the C-C coupling process to facilitate alcohols, achieved through alternative pathways, excluding direct *CO-*CO coupling6, including *CO-CO(H) mechanisms7, increased CO coverage8, and synergistic effects from multiple active sites9. Unfortunately, these strategies also promote multi-carbon (C2+) products without distinguishing between alcohols and hydrocarbons, and thus remain limited in selectively favoring alcohols in CO2R, particularly during the post-C–C coupling stage, with the FE ratio of alcohols to hydrocarbons often reported as 1 or even lower10.

Beyond C-C coupling, controlling O-H versus C-H formation is critical for producing liquid alcohol, as O-H groups are inherently hydrophilic while C-H groups are hydrophobic. Along with CO2R, the intermediates after C-C coupling undergo successive hydrogenation steps that ultimately determine the final C2+ products. In most cases, carbonous species usually tend to adsorb on the catalyst surface, typically copper, which is uniquely capable of converting CO2 into C2+ products11, whereas oxygenated species are often far from the surface and build interface hydrogen-bond networks with aqueous solvent. It has been reported that C-H and O-H bond formation shows distinct preferences for protonation, proceeding predominantly via the Langmuir–Hinshelwood (LH) or Eley–Rideal (ER) mechanisms, respectively. For example, the protonation barrier for *COCOH (* denotes adsorbed sites) is approximately 0.1 eV via ER and 0.8 eV via LH, whereas for *CCH2, it is about 1.2 eV via ER and significantly higher than that via LH (0.6 eV)12. It leads to the formation of O-H bonds via protonation from water, which easily occurs before C-H bonds. Moreover, the *H intermediates are essential for LH-type protonation. However, due to its weak *H adsorption on copper (with a barrier of approximately 0.7 eV for *H from water), the availability of *H intermediates on the surface is limited, thereby further restricting C-H bond formation via LH12,13. This consequently presents a challenge for preserving OH groups in CO2R intermediates until the formation of the final alcohol products.

Electrolyte regulation, including composite, additive, and cationic forms, plays a critical role in tailoring the interfacial interactions between adsorbates and the surrounding electrolyte14,15. From the bulk electrolyte, modulation of water-solvent interactions can suppress the availability of free water molecules for HER16. In addition, lowering the thermodynamic water activity has been demonstrated as an effective strategy to tune the C2+/C1 product distribution17. At the electrode–electrolyte interface, alkali cations can modify the electric field within the electric double layer (EDL), thereby influencing the stabilization of key intermediates involved in HER and CO2R15. Such interfacial electric-field effects have been widely recognized as promoting CO2R while suppressing HER. Despite these advantages, the influence of electrolyte engineering is fundamentally confined to the electric double layer and is highly sensitive to operating conditions. Under practical, high-current-density operation, cations tend to accumulate within the EDL, which can accelerate carbonate salt precipitation and compromise long-term stability18. Furthermore, excessive cation and additive accumulation promote local alkalization or disrupt the interfacial hydrogen-bonding network, both of which hinder proton transport from the bulk electrolyte to the catalyst surface18,19. thereby reducing CO2 utilization and limiting attainable current densities20,21. Notably, such EDL can inevitably fluctuate with variations in the applied potential22–24, further affecting the CO2R performance25. Together, electrolyte engineering is difficult to be a universal strategy for CO2R and significantly affects the CO2R as the EDL changes. To sustain CO2R operation with high ethanol selectivity, a catalyst that protects CO2R intermediates from interfacial interactions would enable robust operation irrespective of electrolyte pH and without the need to distinguish between different electrolyte systems, thereby enabling robust and versatile CO2R toward alcohols.

Here, we reported a hydroxyl affinity tuning strategy that stabilizes hydroxyl groups in CO2R intermediates far from interfacial water by constructing nanometal interfaces, thereby favoring ethanol production. The theory-guided catalyst directs the nano-cobalt/Cu surface (nano-Co/Cu). Using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we found that nano-Co/Cu interface preferentially binds hydroxyl groups in CO2R intermediates, resulting in low hydrogen-bond densities around *CHCOH, which hinders the protonation of C-OH species and facilitates the key steps that determine the CO2R pathway toward alcohol. The resultant nano-Co/Cu exhibited CO2-to-ethanol Faradaic efficiencies of 62.17% and 45.32% under alkaline and acidic conditions, respectively, with both delivering a high partial current density over ~300 mA cm−2 and, in acidic media, sustaining continuous ethanol production for 235 hours with an average FE of 44.74%. In-situ observations based on X-ray adsorption spectroscopy and infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) revealed the structural transformation of nano-Co/Cu under CO2R and the increased presence of -OH and C-OH species, which support the role of nano-Co/Cu in catalyzing CO2R toward selective ethanol production.

Results

Mechanism of nano-TM stabilizing hydroxyl

CO2-to-ethanol conversion proceeds through multiple proton-coupled electron transfer steps and C-C coupling, involving several key steps: the CO2 activation and protonation, *CO dimerization, and *CHCOH protonation. Among C2+ products26,27, the *CHCOH protonation is the key step, branching into *CCH and *CHCHOH depending on whether the protonation occurs at the oxygen or carbon atom, leading to ethanol and ethylene, respectively. At the interfacial aqueous surface, *CHCOH intermediates are surrounded by solvated water and surface hydrogen atoms (Fig. 1a)28, where the solvated hydrogen easily attacks their hydroxyl groups via an ER mechanism, leading to favoring the ethylene pathway (Supplementary Figs. 1-2 and Table 1) as reported in literature 12.Fig. 1Catalyst design stabilizing hydroxyl in intermediates.a Representative snapshots *CHCOH intermediates on Cu(111)-water interfacial structure. Besides, pH can regulate the interfacial solvated structure. As the pH decreases, the proton concentration increases, rather than the alkaline metal cation, to balance the potential, which increases the connectivity between *CHCOH and H2O while eliminating cation coordination29,30, both of which lead to weaker hydroxyl protection at low pH. As validation, the reaction energy calculations within a charged water layer on Cu(111) (Fig. 1b) suggest a solvated proton under acidic conditions can promote*CHCOH protonation towards *CCH more readily than solvated potassium ions under alkaline conditions, consistent with the result from full-water models (Supplementary Figs. 3–4). Consequently, as pH decreases, *CHCOH can be likely to be more easily protonated through hydrogen bonds at the aqueous interface, thereby facilitating dehydroxylation and yielding *CCH (Fig. 1c). Therefore, we sought a catalyst design that stabilizes the hydroxyl groups in *CHCOH, rather than allowing protonation by interfacial water, to promote ethanol production.

Based on these, we hypothesize that at an interface, Cu sites are responsible for generating key intermediates for C2+ products while the introduced transition-metal sites can stabilize the hydroxyl group in *CHCOH for ethanol production (Fig. 1c). To achieve this, nano-scale transition-metal atoms are incorporated onto the Cu surface (nano-TM/Cu), where they can form moderate chemical interactions with the hydroxyl group in *CHCOH, enabling hydroxyl protection. We note that, across the transition-metal series from left to right in the periodic table, the binding to adsorbates generally decreases31, which can lead to either excessively strong or overly weak adsorption, thereby either accelerating hydroxyl deoxygenation or failing to compete with hydrogen bonding. We collected extensive data mining from the Material Project database32 and gathered formation energies of metal oxides and hydroxides, which can be semi-quantitatively used to evaluate the metal-oxygen bonds (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Tables 2–3)33,34. Using the lowest formation energy values, we established a linear correlation between the formation energies of metal oxides and hydroxides (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 6), showing that lower formation energies correspond to stronger metal-oxygen bonds. Remarkably, the formation energies of cobalt oxides and hydroxides are comparable to, but slightly lower than, that of water, implying that Co-O bonds can be stronger than the covalent H–O bond. Consequently, the incorporation of nano-Co atoms could stabilize hydroxyl groups in *CHCOH rather than in solvated water, thereby favoring the ethanol pathway.

Theory-guided catalyst design

To realize this design concept, we explored the implications of the nano-Co atoms on the copper surface (nano-Co/Cu) using DFT calculations (Model details in Supplementary Data 1). We first calculated the formation energies of various nano-scale Co atoms on a Cu(111) surface (4 × 4-unit cell), a facet reported to have the lowest surface energy35. The surface energy of Co(0001) was found to be higher than that of Cu(111) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Among the configurations tested, a nano-metal interface with nine cobalt atoms on the copper surface exhibited the lowest formation energy (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 8), indicating its potential as a stable nano-Co/Cu structure. We then considered possible cobalt compounds as precursors for the electrochemical conversion to nano-scale Co atoms on copper, comparing their reaction energies for forming nano-Co/Cu (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 4). The calculated reaction energies show that as the formation energy of Co-related precursors is more positive, the corresponding reaction energies are likely to be lower. The cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc) molecules are located at the negative reaction energy-positive formation energy region, suggesting it could potentially contribute to the formation of nanoscale Co species on the Cu surface. Besides, to evaluate the stability of nano-cobalt atoms under non-CO2R environments, we tested the relative stability of various oxides to nano-Co/Cu as a function of potentials under pH = 7 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 9). At zero potential, the most stable configuration was identified as Co/Cu2O, suggesting that Cu is preferentially oxidized to Cu2O prior to the oxidation of Co, forming a Co/Cu2O. When the potential increases to approximately 0.4 V vs RHE, the Co/Cu2O is further oxidized into CoO/Cu2O. To validate the theoretical catalyst, we fabricated nano-Co/Cu from CoPc/Cu precursor. CoPc/Cu was prepared via a two-step sputtering-spraying method, in which metallic Cu was first sputtered onto polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) nanofibers, followed by spray deposition of CoPc onto the Cu/PTFE layer (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 10–14). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) elemental mapping shows a uniform distribution of Cu, Co, N, and C elements on Cu nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 15). During the CO2R, the CoPc molecules were electrochemically reduced into the metallic cobalt atoms (Supplementary Fig. 16), which subsequently aggregated to form nano-cobalt clusters, as evidenced by the uniform bright dots observed along the PTFE nanofibers. (Fig. 2e). High-resolution STEM images (Fig. 2f) clearly resolved atomistic metal clusters identified as metallic Co clusters by EDX elemental mapping. These results further confirmed the formation of theoretically guided nano-Co/Cu catalysts under CO2R operation.

Mechanism of electro-catalyzing CO2R to alcohol

To substantiate the role of nano-Co/Cu in facilitating CO2R pathway towards ethanol, we conducted DFT calculations to simulate the CO2R at the atomic level. Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulation was conducted for the water–Cu(111) and water–nano-Co/Cu interfaces, each with an adsorbed *CHCOH intermediate (Supplementary Figs. 17–20). In addition to a main peak at 1.2 Å ascribed to hydroxyl groups in *CHCOH, a peak at approximately 1.6 Å is attributed to hydrogen bonding (O··H) between the hydroxyl group of *CHCOH and solvated water36. The RDF results (Fig. 3a) showed a lower O··H density around *CHCOH for nano-Co/Cu compared with Cu(111). Furthermore, we observed that, in addition to interaction between *CHCOH and copper sites, the adjacent water also exhibited electronic interaction with *CHCOH on Cu(111) (Fig. 3b), suggesting hydrogen bonding. In contrast, on nano-Co/Cu, *CHCOH are likely to interact more strongly with Co atoms rather than with nearby water, implying that Co sites may help stabilize the hydroxyl group while reducing its direct interaction with interfacial water. The chemical Co-O bond (−0.63 eV) affords ~−0.3 eV stronger stabilization than the calculated hydrogen bonds (0.3 eV), with the hydrogen bond consistent with reported values. (Supplementary Fig. 21)37. We further calculated the *CHCOH protonation barriers (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 22). Compared with Cu(111), *CHCOH on nano-Co/Cu showed an increased value, suggesting the ethylene-forming protonation pathway may be kinetically less accessible on nano-Co/Cu. Besides, *CO dimerization on nano-Co/Cu proceeds with a relatively low energy barrier (Supplementary Figs. 23–24). Furthermore, we calculated reaction energy along the CO2R pathway (Supplementary Figs. 25–28) and identified three key steps determining ethanol formation: CO2 activation and protonation, C-C coupling, and *CHCOH protonation. All of these steps on nano-Co/Cu exhibited low reaction energies with a tendency towards *CHCHOH formation compared with Cu(111) (Fig. 3e), suggesting that nano-Co/Cu may facilitate CO2R kinetics toward ethanol while partially suppressing the competing ethylene pathway. Nevertheless, we note that these DFT and AIMD calculations are based on finite interfacial models and limited simulation timescales, we interpret these computational results as indicating a selectivity trend toward ethanol formation, which was further evaluated by electrochemical experiments.

Electrochemical test of nano-Co/Cu promoting alcohol production

We electrochemically tested the CO2R performance of the nano-Co/Cu electrode in the flow-cell reactor with a solution of 1 M KOH and a CO2 flow inlet. The nano-Co/Cu catalyst, as well as Cu as a reference, exhibited a potential-dependent product distribution over a range of applied potential spanning from −0.7 to −1.4 V versus RHE (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 29 and Supplementary Tables 5–6). Notably, the FE for ethanol on nano-Co/Cu increased with increasingly negative potential. An FE of 62.17% towards ethanol was achieved without other alcohol observed, with a partial current density of up to 350 mA cm-2 (Fig. 4b), along with total FEs of 73.06% and 96.38% towards liquid and C2+ products, respectively, corresponding to partial current densities of 413 and 558 mA cm-2 (Supplementary Figs. 30–32). We then switched the CO2R test to acidic conditions in the same flow cell reactor. Under acidic conditions, the nano-Co/Cu catalyst exhibited an FE of 45.32% toward ethanol, with no other alcohols products detected (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 7), with a partial current density of up to 336 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4f). In contrast, pure Cu primarily underwent HER, with a CO2R FE below 10% (Supplementary Fig. 33), consistent with previous work38. The near-exclusive ethanol production significantly lowers the cost of downstream separation39. To further exclude the support effect, the nano-Co/Cu on carbon-paper-based GDL also exhibited similar performance to that on PTFE-based GDL (Supplementary Figs. 34–35 and Supplementary Tables 8–9), delivering an ethanol FE of 56.72% and a partial current density of 384 mA cm-2 in alkaline media, while achieving an ethanol FE of 42.84% and a partial current density of 331 mA cm-2 in acidic media. Control experiments using a Nafion/Cu/PTFE electrode revealed the suppression of HER but only a slight increase in ethanol formation (Supplementary Fig. 36 and Supplementary Table 10), consistent with previous study40. This result indicates that the enhanced ethanol selectivity in the nano-Co/Cu catalyst originates primarily from the introduction of Co clusters. Comparison with the literatures showed that nano-Co/Cu, under both alkaline8,10,41–50 and acidic9,51–58 CO2R, exhibited higher alcohol FEs at high partial current densities than those reported catalysts that exhibited either FEs below 60% for alkaline CO2R and 40% for acidic CO2R, respectively, or partial current densities below 300 mA cm-2 ( ≥ 100 mA cm-2, Supplementary Fig. 37 and Supplementary Table 11). Besides, nano-Co/Cu also shows competitive performance compared with most previously reported catalysts within the same reactor configuration and electrolyte (Supplementary Tables 12–13). At a current density of 500 mA cm-2, the nano-Co/Cu catalyst delivered a stable operation for 235 hours with an average ethanol FE of 44.74% (Fig. 4g), indicating competitive durability compared with those previously reported catalysts for acidic CO2R (Supplementary Table 11).

Real-time Cu and Co K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy was further used to determine the Cu-Cu, Cu-Co, and Co-N bonds during the CO2R (Fig. 5b, c). Over the applied potential range relevant to CO2R, the Cu-Cu bonds remained unchanged, while the Cu-Co bonds were observed following the disappearance of Co-N bonds at a more negative potential. These results indicated that substantial CoPc molecules underwent electrochemical reduction to form nano-sized metallic clusters under operating conditions. The post-operation HAADF-STEM image further validates the presence of nano-Co/Cu structure during CO2R, while the XRD pattern shows significantly weakened CoPc signal, suggesting that a large portion of CoPc was converted into nano-Co clusters, while residual CoPc still coexisted with the derived nano-Co/Cu structure (Supplementary Fig. 39–43), consistent with the prediction of the Pourbaix diagram (Supplementary Fig. 44). The appearance of Cu-Co bonds further confirms the formation of various nano-metal interfaces between Co clusters and the Cu surface (Supplementary Fig. 45), which are responsible for effectively catalytic CO2R towards ethanol (Supplementary Figs. 46–48). Moreover, the Cu remained in a metallic state, and the reduced phthalocyanine did not affect catalytically active Cu under CO2R conditions (Supplementary Fig. 49).

We then performed in situ infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to detect the CO2R intermediates on the catalytic surface across a potential range from 0 to -1.4 V versus RHE. The in situ FTIR results revealed four different bands related to the OH-, *CO, C-OH, and -CH3 at ~3240, 2075, 1489/1038, and 1387 cm-1 on both Cu and nano-Co/Cu catalysts (Fig. 5d, e)62,63. For OH-, C-OH, and -CH3 species, the relative band intensities on nano-Co/Cu are higher than those on pure Cu over these potentials. Notably, two additional bands attributed to -CH2CH3 species were observed on nano-Co/Cu but not pure Cu. Besides, the intensities of the two additional bands assigned to -CH2CH3 species increase concurrently with the rise of the C-OH species, suggesting that the C-OH signals can be used to identify crucial intermediates toward alcohol formation. These results showed that more alcohol-related intermediates were retained on nano-Co/Cu than on pure Cu, which is significant for promoting the CO2R towards alcohol products.

Discussion

In summary, we reported a hydroxyl-affinity-tuning strategy in which nano-metal interfaces stabilize hydroxyl groups in CO2R intermediates, thereby promoting CO2-to-ethanol conversion. Through the theory-guided catalyst design, we successfully directed and electrochemically synthesized the nano-Co/Cu catalyst from reduction of partial CoPc during CO2R. DFT results revealed the role of nano-scale cobalt atoms for chemically stabilizing hydroxyl in CO2R intermediates, leading to protection of hydroxyl far from solvated water while promoting key steps in the CO2R pathway towards ethanol. Experimentally, the nano-Co/Cu catalyst delivered high FEs of 62.17% and 45.32% of ethanol selectivity under alkaline and acidic conditions, respectively, both delivering a high partial current density over ~300 mA cm-2. In situ spectroscopy confirmed the transformation of the nano-Co/Cu precursor and the higher presence of ethanol-related species for ethanol production.

Methods

Chemicals

Potassium hydroxide (KOH, reagent grade, 90%), Potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3, ACS reagent, 99.7%), Potassium chloride (KCl, 99.0%), and Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95%-98%) were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Inc (Shanghai, China). Nafion solution (5 wt.%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar Chemical Co. Analytical grade ethanol was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All the chemicals were used without further purification.

Materials characterizations

The microstructure of catalysts was characterized by JEOL-7100F scanning electron microscope (SEM) and double spherical aberration corrected transmission electron microscope (Titan Cubed Themis G2 300/Titan Cubed Themis G2 30). XPS measurements were performed on AXIS SUPRA, Kratos. XRD data were collected by D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). X-ray absorption data were collected at the beamline BL11B of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). In-situ XAS measurements were conducted using a commercial electrolytic cell (Zhongkewanheng). The preparation process of working electrode is similar to the preparation of Cu/PTFE and CoPc/Cu/PTFE, except the PTFE was replaced by carbon paper (CP, TGP-H-060, Toray), to ensure good conductivity. Pt plate and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. CO2-filled 0.5 M KHCO3 was used as the electrolyte. The flow rate of CO2 was set to 20 ml min–1.

Electrode preparation

Cu/PTFE

The Cu/PTFE electrode was prepared by sputtering metal Cu target (99.99%, Beijing Dream Material Technology Co., Ltd) onto a PTFE membrane (0.45 μm pore size, Zhongxingweiye Co., Ltd) using a magnetron sputtering machine (PD-200C, PDVACUUM Technologies Co., Ltd). The sputtering process was carried out at a power of 100 W for 20 min, yielding a thickness of Cu in 200 nm.

CoPc/Cu

The CoPc/Cu/PTFE electrode was prepared by spraying-coating cobalt phthalocyanine/nafion complex ink (a mixture with 1 mg of cobalt phthalocyanine, 1 ml of methanol, and 3 μl of 5 wt% Nafion solution) on Cu/PTFE electrode to form a CoPc loading of 0.1 mg cm–2. The CoPc/Cu/Carbon-based gas diffusion electrode (GDE, SGL 28BC) was prepared with the same procedure except that the PTFE was replaced by carbon-based GDE.

Nafion/Cu

The Nafion/Cu/PTFE electrode was prepared by spraying-coating nafion solution (1 ml of methanol and 3 μl of 5 wt% Nafion solution) on Cu/PTFE electrode.

DFT calculations

All computational calculations in this work were carried out on periodic slab models within the framework of first-principles density functional theory (DFT), as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package65–67. We used DFT calculations to calculate all reaction energetics, energy barrier and adsorption energetics with our DFT calculations. The generalized gradient approximation was used within the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional (GGA-PBE)68, while the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method69 of a plane-wave basis set within a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV was adopted to describe the ion–electron interactions. The valence electrons were modeled as follows: H(1s1), C(2s2sp2), O(2s22p4), N(2s22p5), Co(3d7, 4s2), and Cu(3d10, 4s1). To illustrate the long-range dispersion interactions between the adsorbates and catalysts, we employed the D3 correction method as described by Grimme et al.70. Methfessel–Paxton method method was used to improve the electronic convergence with a Fermi smearing width of 0.2 eV71. A Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh grid of 3 × 3 × 1 was employed for all structural optimizations72. Adsorption geometries within the charged water layer on Cu(111) slabs were optimized using a force-based conjugate gradient algorithm, whereas transition states were located with a constrained minimization technique73,74. The criteria of energy and force convergence were set to 1.0 × 10–5 eV per atom and 0.02 eV Å–1, respectively, for geometry optimization. Besides, to validate the hydrogen bond change around intermediates, we conducted Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations in a within the canonical (NVT, that is, a fixed number of atoms, a fixed volume and a fixed temperature) ensemble by Nosé–Hoover thermostats with a time step of 0.5 fs for 10 ps to to optimize the structure of water interfacial copper surface75. The Nosé–Hoover thermostat method was used to maintain the temperature at 300 K76. Radial distribution function g(r) of O-H bonds were including in the optimized geometry from AIMD simulations. More computational details are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Modeling

For the modeling of copper, the crystal structure was optimized and the equilibrium face-centered cubic lattice constants were found to be a Cu = 3.631 Å. The Cu(111) surface, corresponding to the low Miller index plane with the lowest surface energy77, was selected as a copper surface. For Cu(111) surface, a periodic four-layer p(3 × 3) slab model was used with the one lower layers fixed and the three upper layers relaxed. Nano-Co/Cu, was modeled with a periodic four-layer p(4 × 4) slab and added various cobalt atoms on the copper surface. A vacuum layer of 10 Å was built to avoid the artificial interactions between periodic slabs. At all intermediate and transition states, one charged layer of water molecules was added to the surface to account for the combined effects of the field and solvation: six water molecules were added near the surface, with three facing toward the surface and three parallel to the surface78. The charged layer of water molecules was optimized at all intermediate and transition states. For the full water interfacial copper surface, the total number of waters considered us 40, as seen in Supplementary Fig. 17, while keeping the density of ~1 g cm−3. To consider the oxidization configuration of nano-Co/Cu, two low Miller index planes of CuO(111) and Cu2O(111) were selected, with a periodic four-layer p(2 × 2) super cell and added cobalt clusters from hcp Co, CoO, Co3O4, and CoO2, with the total cobalt number of nine.
