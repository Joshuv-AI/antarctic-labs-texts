Lost in Transition
Authors: Jennifer Lewey, Leah Rethy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: hypertension, pregnancy, prevention

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDPs), including gestational hypertension and pre-eclampsia, are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), including coronary artery disease and heart failure.1 This elevated risk is largely mediated through the early development of hypertension, although other mechanistic pathways have also been implicated. National medical societies advocate for increased surveillance, screening, and timely treatment of individuals with a history of a HDP.2, 3, 4 Yet, the transition from obstetric care to primary care too often does not occur, particularly among individuals from minoritized racial/ethnic backgrounds and individuals without access to consistent insurance.5

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Attanasio et al6 present a cross-sectional analysis of contemporary data to characterize the transition to primary care after a recent pregnancy using data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. A total of 3,319 female respondents, representing a weighted sample of more than 3 million individuals, between the ages of 18 and 45 years were included. The primary exposure of interest was self-reported hypertension status, and respondents were categorized as having no hypertension (83%), chronic hypertension (7.6%), or pregnancy-only hypertension (9.4%). The outcomes of health care access and utilization were assessed by the following questions: 1) does the respondent have a “personal provider”; 2) has the respondent forgone care in the past 12 months due to cost; 3) has the respondent had a routine visit within past year; and 4) has the respondent had their lipids checked within the past year. The authors presented their primary findings as regression-adjusted predicted probabilities to improve interpretability and stratify the analysis by race/ethnicity.

Overall, rates of health care access were moderately high, with 79.6% identified as having personal provider and 70% reported having a routine visit in the past year. However, 14% reported deferring care due to cost and 48.8% reported having their lipids checked in the past year. Respondents with chronic hypertension reported greater health care utilization than those without any hypertension. In contrast, respondents with pregnancy only hypertension and those with no hypertension reported similar rates of health care utilization. In the analysis stratified by race/ethnicity, several notable findings were present. First, Hispanic respondents were less likely to report health care utilization compared to non-Hispanic White and Black participants. Second, Black and White individuals with hypertension had the greatest predicted probability of reporting health care utilization, a finding consistent with the overall analysis.

Although individuals with a HDP have a 2 to 3 higher odds of developing hypertension within 2 to 7 years postpartum, prior work has shown that individuals diagnosed with a HDP underestimate their risk of chronic hypertension as well as future CVD.7,8 The authors’ finding of the overall similarity in care patterns in those without a history of hypertension and those with a history of pregnancy-only hypertension, underscores the importance and ongoing need for care continuity and prevention.

The authors found that adults with self-reported hypertension had an increased probability of having a personal provider and having lipid testing. Despite this, approximately 25% reported no routine check-up in the past year. Individuals with hypertension should be seen at least 1 to 2 times annually to improve blood pressure control, screen for end-organ damage, and optimize prevention strategies.4 Only 40% of younger individuals with hypertension achieve optimal control. More frequent health care visits are associated with better BP control, suggesting this behavior as a key metric to improve future cardiovascular health for individuals with or at risk of hypertension.9

Importantly, the authors also found in race/ethnicity stratified analyses that there were low rates of routine care among Hispanic respondents and that there were no differences in care by hypertensive status. Although this analysis did not focus on the reasons why individuals may not be having routine visits, the authors did find that approximately 15% of those with a history of hypertension (including pregnancy-only hypertension) deferred care due to cost, with rates higher among Hispanic respondents. Strategies to improve care in the postpartum period will need to focus on methods to remove barriers to care, particularly among marginalized populations.

The authors also identified low-rates of lipid screening, with fewer than 50% of those with pregnancy-only hypertension reporting a lipid test within the past year. Although less frequent screening (ie at least every 5 years) may be appropriate for low-risk young adults, those with a history of a HDP, like those with chronic hypertension, should have yearly screening.3 Importantly, the recently released 2026 lipid guidelines from the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology identify a history of a HDP as a key risk enhancer for CVD and newly recommend using the PREVENT risk calculator (rather than the Pooled Cohort Equations), allowing for individual risk assessment starting at 30 years.3 These changes underscore the importance and relevance of both regular lipid screening and routine primary care in this young and at-risk population.

A major strength of this study is the use of a contemporary, nationally-representative survey, which is unconstrained by insurance continuity or type of coverage. Prior analyses have focused on individuals with only commercial or governmental insurance and excluded individuals with insurance changes. This allows individuals without insurance to be included in the analysis.

As the authors also acknowledge there are a number of limitations in this analysis. First, there may be misclassification in exposure status due to reliance on self-report, likely leading to an underestimation of the number of individuals with hypertension and potentially limiting power to detect between group differences. Second, respondents reporting care in the past year may have included nonprimary care visits, especially given the high rates of routine visits compared to prior studies. Finally, there were important expansions in insurance coverage in the postpartum period beginning in 2021 (American Rescue Plan Act) and so it is unclear if their findings, particularly relating to insurance status and deferral of visits due to costs, would hold in more recent years.

Nevertheless, this analysis of survey data representing over 3 million postpartum adults adds to our understanding of how recently pregnant individuals with hypertension or a history of a HDP are managed in the postpartum years. As has been shown previously, rates of routine primary-care, including lipid-assessment are inadequate, particularly among those whose hypertension resolves postpartum. By not effectively transitioning these at-risk individuals to primary care, we are missing critical opportunities for screening, treatment, and prevention. Future efforts must focus on methods to expand and maintain promising transitional care pathways, improve patient-level understanding of future CVD risk and remove persistent barriers to care.10
