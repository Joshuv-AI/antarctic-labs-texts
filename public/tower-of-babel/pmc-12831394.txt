Quantitative evaluation of trabecular meshwork pigmentation in unilateral Fuchs uveitis syndrome
Authors: Kubra Ozdemir Yalcinsoy, Mehmet Murat Uzel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo quantitatively analyze trabecular meshwork (TM) pigmentation in patients with unilateral Fuchs Uveitis Syndrome (FUS) and to compare the findings with those of the healthy fellow eyes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included patients with unilateral FUS. Demographic and ophthalmological data were recorded. Gonioscopic photographs of the anterior chamber angle of both eyes were taken under standardized conditions from the superior and inferior TM regions, using the same slit-lamp biomicroscope equipped with a mounted digital camera. TM pigmentation was quantitatively analyzed in the superior, inferior, and total (superior + inferior) regions using ImageJ software.ResultsOf the 30 patients with unilateral FUS, 5 were excluded due to poor image quality. The remaining 25 patients were analyzed, with FUS eyes classified as the FUS group and fellow eyes as controls. The mean age was 49.0 ± 12.3 years (range: 24–64); 60% were female. Common findings in FUS eyes included vitritis (100%), posterior subcapsular cataract (96%), stellate keratic precipitates (80%), and diffuse iris atrophy (80%). Heterochromia was observed in 40% of patients. Mean intraocular pressure (IOP) did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.182). TM pigmentation was higher in the FUS group across all regions, reaching statistical significance in the superior and total measurements (p = 0.006 and p = 0.040, respectively). TM pigmentation was lower in all regions in eyes with heterochromia compared to those without, with total values significantly reduced (p = 0.036). No significant correlation was found between IOP and TM pigmentation in the FUS group (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study demonstrated increased TM pigmentation in eyes affected by FUS, which may be due to ongoing pigment release. Conversely, eyes with heterochromia showed reduced pigmentation, likely reflecting chronic pigment loss.This quantitative method may aid in better understanding pigment distribution and iris changes in FUS, providing clinicians with a more objective assessment tool.

Introduction

Fuchs’ uveitis syndrome (FUS) is typically a unilateral, low-grade, chronic inflammation that affects the anterior uvea and vitreous [1]. Although it is relatively uncommon, FUS accounts for approximately 1% to 20% of all uveitis cases [2, 3]. It usually impacts middle-aged individuals, with no significant gender preference [1, 2]. The cause of FUS remains unknown; however, proposed mechanisms include sympathetic dysfunction, infectious agents, genetic predisposition, and immune-mediated processes [2, 3].

Diagnosis of FUS relies on characteristic clinical features, including stellate or medium-sized keratic precipitates, mild inflammation in the anterior chamber, diffuse iris atrophy with or without heterochromia, posterior subcapsular cataract, vitreous cells, and the absence of posterior synechiae or macular edema [1–3]. Among these, iris changes are considered a hallmark finding.² Slit-lamp biomicroscopic studies have described various iris alterations in FUS, including heterochromia, diffuse stromal atrophy, loss of iris crypts, nodules, irregular pupillary borders, and transillumination defects [1, 2]. In recent years, anterior segment imaging techniques have allowed for more detailed assessment of these iris changes. Several studies have documented decreases in iris thickness, smoothness, and pigment epithelium optical density in eyes with FUS, indicating structural atrophy and pigment loss [4–8].

Secondary glaucoma is among the most significant complications of FUS after cataract and can occur in up to 60% of patients [1, 2]. Various mechanisms for intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation have been proposed, including trabeculitis, angle neovascularization, trabecular sclerosis, steroid response, and collapse of Schlemm’s canal, but the exact cause remains uncertain [1–4, 6, 9–11].

Although iris changes in FUS have been thoroughly studied, alterations in trabecular meshwork (TM) pigmentation have not yet been assessed. Considering the potential role of pigment distribution in IOP dysregulation, such an investigation could reveal insights into the mechanisms of IOP elevation in eyes with FUS. Additionally, examining iris features with TM pigmentation might shed light on the pathophysiology of FUS. ImageJ, an open-source image analysis software, is increasingly used in ophthalmology for quantitative assessment of both anterior and posterior segment structures [5, 6, 12, 13]. Using ImageJ on gonioscopic images has been shown to objectively assess TM pigmentation [13, 14]. This study aims to quantitatively evaluate TM pigmentation in eyes with FUS using gonioscopic images analyzed with ImageJ and compare these results with those of healthy fellow eyes.

Methods

This cross-sectional observational study was performed at the Uvea Clinic of a tertiary eye hospital. The study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the study and provided written informed consent.

The inclusion criteria were phakic patients with unilateral FUS and a healthy fellow eye. The diagnosis of FU was based on clinical examination findings, including chronic, low-grade anterior chamber reaction; diffusely distributed small to medium-sized keratic precipitates on the corneal endothelium; diffuse iris atrophy with or without heterochromia; varying degrees of vitreous inflammation; and a fibrillary appearance of the vitreous [1, 2]. Diffuse iris atrophy was assessed as smoothness of the iris surface and the loss or absence of normal iris crypts.

Patients were excluded if they had a history of ocular surgery, laser treatment, trauma, bilateral FUS, any other type of uveitis, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, glaucoma, retinal disease, or abnormalities of the cornea, pupil, or lens (except posterior subcapsular cataract). Eyes with corneal edema, corneal opacity or extensive keratic precipitates that prevented visualization of the TM, or angle closure, were also excluded. The patients included in the study had no history of topical steroid or mydriatic use for at least 3 months.

All patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination, which included assessment of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) using the Snellen chart, measurement of intraocular pressure (IOP) by Goldmann applanation tonometry, slit-lamp biomicroscopy of the anterior segment, and a fundus examination. BCVA values were converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) for statistical analysis. Anterior segment photographs of the iridocorneal angle were obtained by the same experienced clinician for TM evaluation.

Image acquisition and quantitative analysis of the trabecular meshwork pigmentation

Gonioscopy was performed without pupil dilation in both eyes of each patient using the angle mirror of a Goldmann three-mirror lens to visualize the iridocorneal angle. Gonioscopic photographs of the angle were obtained using the same slit-lamp biomicroscope equipped with a mounted digital camera. Standardized gonioscopic images of the superior and inferior angles were captured at 40x magnification under consistent illumination and focus settings. At least three high-quality images were taken from each eye. The images were selected based on the clarity and appropriate visualization of the TM region.

The obtained iridocorneal angle photographs were transferred to ImageJ software (version 1.47, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) for quantitative analysis, as described by Kinori et al. [13]. The ImageJ software calculates an average intensity value by analyzing the pixel distribution within the defined region. Nevertheless, in the acquired images, a rectangular area of fixed dimensions was defined in both the superior and inferior TM regions, and the mean blackness intensity (in arbitrary units, AU) within each area was measured as an indicator of pigmentation (Fig. 1). Higher AU values corresponded to greater pigmentation. The sum of the superior and inferior values was defined as the total pigmentation (superior + inferior). All measurements were performed by an independent masked grader.

The obtained data were compared between the affected eyes in the FUS group and the fellow healthy eyes of the patients, which served as the control group.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, median (minimum–maximum), frequencies, and percentages, as appropriate. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. For normally distributed variables, the paired samples t-test was used to compare groups, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Associations between TM pigmentation and other variables, such as intraocular pressure (IOP), were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 60 eyes from 30 patients diagnosed with unilateral FUS were evaluated. Five patients were excluded due to poor-quality anterior segment images, leaving 25 patients for analysis. Among these, 25 eyes comprised the FUS group, and the fellow 25 healthy eyes served as controls. Of the patients, 15 (60%) were female and 10 (40%) were male, with a mean age of 28.4 ± 11.6 years (range: 24–64). The left eye was affected in 15 patients (60%) and the right eye in 10 patients (40%). According to LogMAR, the median BCVA was 0.05 (range: 0.0–1.8) in FUS eyes and 0.00 (range: 0.0–0.1) in healthy fellow eyes, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001). The mean IOP was 14.04 ± 4.4 mmHg (range: 10–21) in FUS eyes and 12.4 ± 3.3 mmHg (range: 10–19) in healthy eyes (p = 0.182). Gonioscopy revealed abnormal vascularization at the iridocorneal angle in 8 eyes (32%) and peripheral anterior synechiae in 4 eyes (16%) in the FUS group. Discussion

In this study, we quantitatively assessed TM pigmentation in eyes with FUS. To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide an objective measurement of TM pigmentation in FUS. Our results showed that TM pigmentation was significantly higher in FUS eyes compared to the healthy fellow eyes. However, in eyes with heterochromia, TM pigmentation was found to be lower. Additionally, no significant correlation was observed between IOP and TM pigmentation in FUS eyes.

Iris changes are a characteristic feature of FUS, and in recent years, anterior segment changes in these patients have been studied using various imaging methods [4–8, 15]. Basarir et al. [4] were the first to use anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) to assess iris structure and iridocorneal angle parameters in FUS eyes. They discovered that the thickest part of the iris was significantly thinner in FUS eyes compared to their healthy fellow eyes [4]. Additional findings included reduced iris convexity, a wider anterior chamber angle, and increased anterior chamber depth, which are consistent with diffuse iris atrophy. Similarly, Invernizzi and Ozer et al. [5, 6] used different AS-OCT-based methods to evaluate iris parameters in unilateral FUS. Invernizzi et al. [5] reported significantly reduced iris thickness in affected eyes compared to both fellow eyes and healthy control eyes. Although Ozer et al. [6] found no significant difference in iris thickness, they noted a significant decrease in the optical density of the iris pigment epithelial layer (PEL) in FUS eyes. Taken together, these findings suggest that iris atrophy in FUS involves not only stromal thinning but also pigment deficiency affecting the PEL. These structural and optical changes support the idea of progressive pigment loss in FUS. Since the iris pigment epithelium is the main source of pigment released into the anterior chamber, such changes may contribute to the alterations in TM pigmentation observed in our study.

Atrophic iris changes are more commonly observed in FUS than in heterochromia [2, 4, 16]. In line with previous studies [1, 7, 16], diffuse iris atrophy was present in the majority of our cases (80%), whereas heterochromia was observed in fewer patients (40%). Structural damage to the iris, particularly the stroma, anterior border layer and PEL, may lead to chronic pigment redistribution into the anterior chamber. Over time, these free pigment granules may accumulate in the TM, contributing to the increased pigmentation we observed [17, 18]. In our study, we observed that TM pigmentation was significantly increased in FUS eyes compared to their fellow eyes. Interestingly, TM pigmentation was lower in FUS eyes with heterochromia. This may suggest that TM pigment deposition is also reduced in eyes with more advanced pigment loss, manifesting clinically as heterochromia. Alternatively, this may reflect a later stage of the disease, characterized by diminished pigment distribution and a decreased TM pigment load. Previous studies have reported progressive degeneration across all iris layers in eyes with FUS [4, 19, 20]. Furthermore, anterior border layer atrophy occurs in the early stages of the disease, while ongoing pigment loss and advanced iris muscle changes have been reported to affect later stages [19, 20]. This further supports the idea that pigment distribution and TM pigmentation change with disease progression. Future prospective studies with larger samples may better explain this relationship.

Secondary glaucoma is a major, vision-threatening complication in eyes with FUS [1, 9, 21]. However, the precise cause of increased IOP in these patients remains unknown [2, 22]. TM pigmentation has been suggested as a contributing factor to IOP elevation, especially in the development of pigmentary glaucoma, by blocking aqueous humor outflow [17, 23]. Similarly, dense pigment accumulation in the TM has been associated with increased IOP in bilateral acute depigmentation of the iris (BADI) and bilateral acute iris transillumination (BAIT), both of which are characterized by acute pigment loss from the iris [18, 24]. Although the mechanism is not fully understood, it has been suggested that pigment accumulation in the trabecular meshwork may impair its phagocytic and regenerative abilities. This impairment may lead to an imbalance in extracellular matrix turnover [17, 23]. However, TM pigmentation in patients with FUS has not been evaluated to date. In the study by Yüksel Elgin et al. [25], no increase in TM pigmentation was observed on gonioscopy in FUS eyes, but detailed quantitative findings were not provided. Johnson et al. [11] evaluated aqueous humor dynamics in FUS and reported normal aqueous outflow rates and resistance despite markedly increased permeability of the blood-aqueous barrier. The authors proposed that a chronic barrier dysfunction might exist without a functional obstruction of outflow [11]. Previous histopathological studies have also suggested that IOP elevation in glaucomatous FUS may be associated with dysfunction or obstruction at the level of Schlemm’s canal [10, 11]. This finding indicates that the primary site of outflow resistance may be the Schlemm’s canal rather than the trabecular meshwork [10, 11]. Similarly, Ozer et al. [6] assessed Schlemm’s canal dimensions in FUS eyes using in vivo AS-OCT and found that the canal area was smaller in FUS eyes compared to healthy control eyes. In our study, no significant correlation was found between TM pigmentation and IOP in eyes with FUS. However, our cohort included only FUS eyes with normal IOP, limiting our ability to evaluate the effect of TM pigmentation on IOP elevation. The absence of a direct association between TM pigmentation and IOP may reflect a multifactorial mechanism of outflow dysfunction in FUS.

The primary limitation of this study is its relatively small sample size, which may impact the generalizability and statistical power of the results. The number of participants was limited to phakic patients without a history of intraocular surgery, as prior surgical interventions could have influenced TM pigmentation. Moreover, the study analysis was limited to comparisons between FUS eyes and unaffected fellow eyes, because pigment distribution can vary among individuals.

In conclusion, this study quantitatively evaluated TM pigmentation in eyes with FUS. Our results showed increased TM pigmentation in FUS eyes compared to healthy fellow eyes, which may reflect ongoing pigment release from the iris. Interestingly, TM pigmentation appeared lower in eyes with heterochromia, likely indicating a more advanced stage of pigment loss. The clinical significance of this assessment is that it improves understanding of disease pathogenesis and attempts to identify objective parameters for monitoring disease progression. This quantitative and noninvasive method may aid in better understanding pigment distribution and iris changes in FUS, providing clinicians with a more objective assessment tool.
