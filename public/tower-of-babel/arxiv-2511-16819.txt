Abstract
This paper presents a hybrid energy system (HES) experimental testbed developed at the University of Vermont to support prototyping and validation of advanced control and optimization strategies for grid services. The platform integrates hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation with a reconfigurable set of kilowatt-scale assets, including solar photovoltaic (PV), battery storage, an electrolyzer as a controllable load, and grid-tied inverters. A unified monitoring and communication architecture supports real-time data acquisition, model validation, and control implementation. The testbed’s capabilities are demonstrated through a controller hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) experiment in which a battery system participates in PV power smoothing.

Laboratory-based testbeds bridge this validation gap by providing controlled and repeatable environments to study complex device interactions. Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation in particular has become critical for these validation efforts. In an HIL configurations, physical hardware components, such as digital controllers or power electronic devices, are interfaced with a real-time digital simulation of a power system network. This approach enables the systematic evaluation of device-level performance, controller robustness, and overall system-level stability before field deployment.
Several experimental HIL testbeds have been developed, addressing different aspects of HES validation and inverter-dominated grid integration, as summarized in Table
I
. While these facilities demonstrate valuable capabilities, most focus on either large-scale grid integration or individual component validation. However, there remains a critical need for reconfigurable, intermediate-scale platforms that integrate multiple HES resources, including electrolyzers, and are explicitly designed to study coordinated multi-component HES control and co-optimization across multiple timescales.

This paper presents a new HES experimental testbed at the University of Vermont (
The Accelerated Testing Lab
), designed for the prototyping and validation of advanced control and coordination strategies to provide grid services. Compared to existing testbeds (Table
I
), the platform uniquely combines:

•

A dual-site architecture with field PV and weather data driving HIL experiments,

•

A reconfigurable HES platform integrating PV, battery, inverters,
and electrolyzer with plug-and-play interfaces,

•

A unified framework for multi-timescale HES control.

This paper provides a comprehensive overview of the key capabilities and features of this testbed, and experimentally validates battery participation in solar smoothing as a representative test case.

II
Experimental testbed and system architecture

The novel HES testbed features a distributed architecture composed of two physically distinct but tightly interconnected laboratory facilities. These facilities share real-time data, control signals, and HIL interfaces, enabling coordinated experiments that span devices, controllers, and grid-level scenarios.

•

The Accelerated Testing Laboratory (ATL), the primary on-campus HIL platform.

•

The Hybrid Solar Test Center (HSTC), an off-campus 23 kW solar and meteorological site, where a 100kW/100kWh battery storage will be installed in 2026.

This two-part structure enables model validation, rapid control prototyping, and HIL analysis at the ATL using real-world solar and weather data from HSTC. The overall system architecture is shown in Fig.

II-A

Accelerated Testing Laboratory

The ATL at the University of Vermont (Fig.
2
) integrates hardware and simulation capabilities to enable comprehensive testing of power electronics, energy storage, and control strategies in a reconfigurable cyber-physical environment. The bidirectional DC power supply feeds either the DC bus of two 5 kVA inverters connected in parallel or the batteries. The inverters, designed based on the UNIFI reference design
[
3
]
, enable testing advanced control strategies and detailed investigation of inverter dynamics and their impact on the grid. Each inverter is controlled by a Texas Instruments (TI) TMDSCNCD28379D microcontroller, which enables the implementation of advanced control strategies by sensing analog voltage and current signals through ADC cards and generating required PWM signals based on control modes
such as grid-forming (GFM) and grid-following (GFL) control, active/reactive power regulation, and coordinated multi-inverter operation.
In addition, the DC power supply is connected to
six 1.2 kWh Enphase LFP batteries, allowing for systematic charge, discharge, and storage co-optimization studies. On the AC side, the inverters are connected to a regenerative grid simulator (RGS).
The testbed is coupled to a 15 kW electrolyzer stack, which represents hydrogen production as a controllable load interfaced with the grid.

Furthermore, the Opal-RT simulator, with the OP4610 I/O expansion unit, provides the computational environment to emulate detailed grid behaviors. As shown in Fig.
2
, the testbed architecture is explicitly designed for ”plug-and-play” flexibility. This allows any component initially simulated within the Opal-RT to be seamlessly replaced by its physical hardware counterpart for full HIL validation.
This creates a robust cyber-physical loop where Opal-RT issues analog setpoints to the RGS and bidirectional DC supply, which act as the physical interface to the device under test. These controllable power supplies generate the precise power and voltage conditions dictated by the real-time simulation. Voltage and current sensors capture responses from the hardware and feed them back to the Opal-RT’s analog inputs, completing the HIL setup. This closed-loop configuration ensures that the real-time model dynamically reacts to physical hardware behavior and vice versa. This setup also facilitates integration of PV and weather profiles from the HSTC (discussed in the next subsection) to drive realistic validation scenarios.

II-B

Hybrid Solar Test Center

The ATL is supported by the HSTC located at the McNeil generating plant roughly one mile from campus. The HSTC hosts a 23 kW grid-connected solar PV array interfaced through SolarEdge inverters, along with an on-site weather station for real-time meteorological data acquisition. The weather station records solar irradiance, temperature, wind speed, and humidity every 5 seconds, providing synchronized environmental and electrical data for PV modeling and performance analysis. Future expansion includes the installation of a 100 kW/100 kWh battery energy storage system (BESS) in 2026, enabling field validation of battery-solar coordination strategies and grid services under real-world conditions.

Fig. 3:
Data and communication architecture between HSTC and ATL.

Environmental and operational data are collected from local and remote sources, as shown in Fig.
3
, to support real-time monitoring, control, and performance evaluation of all HES subsystems within the ATL. At the inverter and PV panel level, data from HSTC including AC and DC voltage, current, power, and frequency are measured using high-precision sensors and transmitted to the server through Modbus energy meter coupled with a communication gateway via Starlink network. All data streams are time-synchronized and stored on a dedicated server for visualization, analysis, and archival purposes.

The setup provides a unique and unified platform for validating HES control strategies and system models, as well as assessing system dynamic behavior and grid-integration performance under controlled conditions. The key capabilities of the testbed are discussed in the following section.

III
Platform Capabilities and Features

The experimental testbed provides a comprehensive environment for investigating HES integration and advanced control validation. The platform key capabilities span three interconnected domains:

•

Component and system-level modeling and validation

•

Control implementation and verification

•

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) integration

This section describes the platform capabilities and their application to contemporary challenges in power system operation.

III-A

Component and system-level modeling and validation

Accurate models of individual HES components and their interactions are fundamental to enabling informed decision-making in system design, control development, and operational optimization. The ATL provides comprehensive experimental capabilities to validate physics-based and data-driven models across multiple components and timescales. The bidirectional DC power supply enables experimental validation of battery models through controlled charge-discharge cycling, including the energy reservoir model
[
12
]
and electro-thermal equivalent circuit model
[
10
]
developed in prior work. For the electrolyzer subsystem, physics-based models describing hydrogen production rate, efficiency, and thermal dynamics have been developed and experimentally validated through systematic power cycling experiments on the 15 kW alkaline stack
[
1
]
.

The platform further enables validation of efficiency-based and detailed switching inverter models. Data from the HSTC support validation of PV models across diverse operating conditions and temperature dependencies. At the system level, aggregated circuit models capture interactions between PV, battery, and inverter systems through equivalent circuit modeling frameworks, facilitating consistent state estimation across component boundaries and robust parameter identification
[
13
]
. These validated models form the foundation for multi-objective optimization and control studies.

III-B

Control implementation and verification

HES change how controllers must be designed. While individual components can often be controlled independently, the strong electrical and thermal coupling in HES makes coordinated controller design both necessary and beneficial. As power systems become increasingly inverter dominated, traditional sources of inertia and voltage support disappear, and these services must be provided through tightly integrated, multi-layer control across HES components. Addressing these challenges requires experimental validation of advanced control and coordination strategies under realistic operating conditions. The ATL testbed bridges this gap by providing a flexible platform to validate the control algorithms at both the component and system levels. At the component level, ATL allows direct implementation and testing of inverter and battery control algorithms, such as GFM and GFL modes, active/reactive power regulation, Volt-VAR control, and multi-inverter coordination, under programmable conditions including voltage sags, weak grid operation, and fault events. At the system level, the platform supports hierarchical and distributed control schemes, including model predictive control and economic dispatch-based coordination of batteries, PV, and electrolyzers. III-C

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) integration

HIL validation bridges the gap between simulation and field deployment by integrating real hardware with real-time digital simulation. It enables realistic evaluation of controllers, converters, and system interactions while maintaining safety and flexibility. The ATL testbed, equipped with an OPAL-RT real-time simulator and I/O unit, supports both CHIL and HIL. The OPAL-RT simulator supports steady-state, transient, and dynamic grid models, enabling comprehensive evaluation of hardware interactions with the grid across different timescales. This enables detailed studies of inverter–grid interactions, coordination among HES components, and validation of advanced control strategies under realistic operating scenarios.

IV
Experimental Test Case: Solar Smoothing using Physical Battery Storage

The increasing penetration of PV generation introduces rapid power fluctuations from cloud transitions and irradiance variability, which challenges grid stability and power quality. A BESS can mitigate these fluctuations through fast response and bidirectional power capability, absorbing excess power during PV spikes and injecting power during drops. By coordinating PV generation with BESS operation, HES can smooth the power delivered to the grid. Grid operators impose ramp-rate limits to keep fluctuations within acceptable ranges, defined as:

V
Conclusion

This paper presented an experimental testbed for validating HES coordination and control strategies. The ATL platform integrates kW-scale PV, batteries, grid-tied inverters, and an electrolyzer with a real-time simulator. It enables modeling, control, and HIL validation of HES at both component and system levels. The solar smoothing experiment demonstrates ATL’s ability to test grid-service control strategies.

Future work will use the OPAL-RT real-time simulator to coordinate all subsystems within a unified control and communication framework targeting diverse operational objectives. Another direction will focus on implementing and validating GFL and GFM control strategies under weak-grid and transient conditions. These advancements will further establish the ATL as a comprehensive real-time facility for accelerated research and deployment of next-generation HES.
