Serum elemental profile‐based machine learning models for Alzheimer's disease identification and cognitive score prediction
Authors: Haotian Liu, Xinnan Liu, Yashuang Chen, Meng Pan, Ying Fu, Chao Ma, Wei Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent diagnostic approaches for Alzheimer's disease (AD) largely rely on cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers and neuroimaging, which may be invasive, costly, and not readily accessible in routine clinical settings. We investigated whether serum elemental profiling combined with machine learning could provide complementary information for AD identification and exploratory cognitive score prediction.MethodsThis retrospective cross‐sectional study included 874 participants enrolled between 2017 and 2023 from the Brain Aging National Cohort–Peking Union Medical College cohort, comprising 427 cognitively normal controls (NCs) and 447 patients with clinically defined AD. Serum concentrations of 20 elements were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. Associations between serum element concentrations and AD status were evaluated using age‐ and sex‐adjusted logistic regression models with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Associations with Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores were assessed using linear regression models with FDR correction. Machine learning classification models were developed for AD identification, whereas regression models were developed for exploratory MMSE and MoCA score prediction. Model performance was evaluated in an internal hold‐out test set.ResultsAge did not differ significantly between NC and AD participants (66.4 ± 9.9 vs. 67.1 ± 9.0 years; t(872) = −1.13, p = 0.260), whereas the proportion of women was higher in the AD group than in the NC group (255/447 [57.0%] vs. 211/427 [49.4%]; χ 2(1) = 5.11, p = 0.024). MMSE scores were significantly lower in patients with AD than in NC participants (AD: n = 277, median [interquartile range (IQR)] = 24 [19–27]; NC: n = 427, median [IQR] = 30 [30–30]; Mann–Whitney U = 3043.5, p < 0.001), as were MoCA scores (AD: n = 240, median [IQR] = 18.00 [14.00–20.25]; NC: n = 427, median [IQR] = 30 [30.00–30.00]; Mann–Whitney U = 13.5, p < 0.001). After adjustment for age and sex, higher serum lead (Pb) and tin (Sn) levels were associated with increased odds of AD (Pb: odds ratio [OR] = 4.95, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.42–7.37; Sn: OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.20–1.78), whereas higher serum antimony, nickel, manganese, cobalt, selenium, and calcium levels were associated with lower odds of AD (OR range: 0.50–0.81; all FDR‐adjusted p‐values < 0.05). Among the eight classification models evaluated in the internal hold‐out test set, the random forest model showed the highest apparent performance for distinguishing AD from NC, achieving an accuracy of 88% and an area under the receiver operating (AUC) (acharacteristic curv of 0.94. Among the three regression models evaluated, the random forest regression showed the highest apparent performance for cognitive score prediction, yielding the strongest correlations between predicted and observed scores for both MMSE (r = 0.48, p < 0.001) and MoCA (r = 0.62, p < 0.001).ConclusionSerum elemental profiles combined with machine learning may provide a minimally invasive and accessible complementary approach for AD identification and cognitive assessment.Keywords: Alzheimer disease, cognitive impairment, machine learning, serum elemental profiling

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most common cause of dementia, is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by gradual and irreversible cognitive decline. More than 55 million people worldwide are currently living with dementia, and this number is projected to nearly triple by 2050, making dementia a major public health challenge in aging societies. 1 Current diagnostic strategies often rely on invasive and resource‐intensive procedures, including cerebrospinal fluid biomarker testing and positron emission tomography (PET), to detect amyloid‐β and tau pathology. Although these approaches provide high diagnostic accuracy, their broader implementation is limited by cost, availability, and the need for specialized infrastructure and expertise. In many clinical and community settings, these resources remain inaccessible. Cognitive screening tools, such as the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), are more widely used; however, they require trained personnel, are time‐consuming to administer, and depend on patient cooperation, which may be compromised in older adults and individuals with more advanced cognitive impairment. Consequently, there is a need for noninvasive, accessible, and scalable approaches that can complement existing methods for AD detection and monitoring.

AD arises from a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures. Among environmental factors, disturbances in elemental homeostasis have been increasingly implicated in AD pathogenesis. 2 Several toxic metals, including lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg), are well‐established neurotoxins, and chronic exposure to these metals has been associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia. 3 , 4 In addition, imbalances in essential trace elements, such as iron (Fe), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and manganese (Mn), may impair neural function through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered neurotransmission. 5 Essential nonmetallic elements, including selenium (Se) and iodine (I), have also been associated with AD and cognitive dysfunction. 6 , 7 Collectively, these findings suggest that multidimensional serum elemental profiles may contain diagnostically relevant information for AD. However, previous studies have generally been limited by restricted elemental coverage, small sample sizes, or both.

In the present study, we conducted a retrospective cross‐sectional analysis of 874 clinically characterized adults from the Brain Aging National Cohort–Peking Union Medical College (BANC‐PUMC). Participants were classified as cognitively normal controls (NCs) or as having AD according to established diagnostic criteria. Most participants underwent standardized cognitive assessments, including the MMSE and MoCA. Serum samples were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) to quantify 20 elements, including 18 metallic or metalloid elements—magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), chromium (Cr), Mn, Fe, cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni), Cu, Zn, arsenic (As), strontium (Sr), molybdenum (Mo), Cd, tin (Sn), antimony (Sb), Hg, Pb, and bismuth (Bi)—and two essential nonmetallic elements, Se and I.

We systematically examined the associations of 20 serum elements with AD diagnosis and global cognitive performance and further characterized age‐ and sex‐related patterns within the NC and AD groups using multivariable regression models. Building on these analyses, we developed and evaluated multiple supervised machine learning models for AD classification and cognitive score prediction based on serum elemental features. Using the concentrations of 20 serum elements, we constructed classification models to distinguish AD from NC and regression models to predict individual MMSE and MoCA scores. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the first comprehensive efforts to integrate serum elemental profiling with machine learning in a Chinese cohort, providing new insights into the potential role of elemental homeostasis in dementia research. Given the retrospective cross‐sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating and indicative of feasibility rather than as evidence of clinical utility. Nevertheless, our results highlight the potential of machine learning models based on serum elemental profiles as a minimally invasive approach for AD identification and cognitive assessment.

METHODS

Study participants

Participants were recruited from the BANC‐PUMC, a longitudinal volunteer cohort of Beijing residents enrolled through the willed body donation program of the National Human Brain Bank for Development and Function (PUMC brain bank). The overall study design and baseline characteristics of this cohort have been reported previously. 8

For the present retrospective cross‐sectional analysis, we included 874 participants with available serum samples and clinical data, including 862 aged ≥50 years and 12 aged <50 years. Of these participants, 447 had a clinical diagnosis of AD, including eight individuals with familial AD, and 427 were classified as cognitively NC. MMSE data were unavailable for 170 participants, whereas MoCA data were unavailable for 207 participants. Among the 874 participants, 662 had complete MMSE and MoCA assessments and were included in cognitive score prediction analyses. The remaining participants had incomplete cognitive evaluations, including 165 participants without formal cognitive scores due to specialist clinical assessment combined with self‐ or informant‐reported symptoms, and 47 participants who completed only one cognitive assessment because of limited cooperation or clinical conditions. Serum concentrations of 20 elemental biomarkers were measured in all participants.

The diagnosis of AD was established by senior neurologists at Peking Union Medical College Hospital according to the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association criteria for probable AD. When feasible, participants underwent cognitive screening using the MMSE and/or MoCA, together with an assessment of self‐reported or informant‐reported cognitive complaints. Abnormal screening results were defined according to education‐adjusted cutoff values. For the MMSE, the cutoff values were ≤19 for illiterate participants, ≤22 for participants with primary school education, and ≤26 for those with junior high school education or higher. For the MoCA, the corresponding cutoff values were ≤14, ≤20, and ≤25, respectively. Individuals with normal screening results were classified as cognitively NC. Individuals with abnormal screening results or suspected cognitive impairment underwent further diagnostic evaluation, including comprehensive neuropsychological testing, neurological examination, routine laboratory investigations, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) to exclude other major causes of cognitive impairment. In a subset of participants with persistent diagnostic uncertainty after specialist evaluation, PET was additionally performed to provide biomarker evidence of AD‐related pathology and to increase diagnostic confidence. The final diagnosis of AD was established through the integrated clinical judgment of senior neurologists based on all available clinical, neuropsychological, imaging, and laboratory data.

All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the institutional review board of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Nos. 009‐2014, 031‐2017, and 2022125).

Elemental analysis by ICP‐MS

Standard stock solutions were diluted with sample diluent A to prepare calibration curves. Internal standardization was performed using 45Sc, 72Ge, 89Y, 115In, and 185Re, whereas analytical quality control (QC) was conducted using synthetic external quality‐control materials at low‐, medium‐, and high‐concentration levels (QC low, QC medium, and QC high, respectively). All serum samples were collected under relatively standardized conditions, including collection after an overnight fast and, whenever feasible, avoidance of sampling during acute illness. Seven multielement standard stock solutions (J0–J6), sample diluent A, the internal standard solution, and synthetic external quality‐control materials were provided for internal use by EXPECLIN Medical Technology Co. Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) and were not commercially available.

Element concentrations were quantified using ICP‐MS (PreMed 7000; EXPECLIN Medical Technology Co. Ltd.) under the following operating conditions: radiofrequency power, 1550 W; auxiliary gas flow rate, 1 L/min; nebulizer gas flow rate, 0.6 L/min; cooling gas flow rate, 14 L/min; collision gas (helium) flow rate, 0.77 L/min; and diluent gas (argon) flow rate, 0.61 L/min.

Multivariable regression analyses of serum element associations

To assess whether individual serum elements were independently associated with AD, we first performed age‐ and sex‐adjusted logistic regression (LR) analyses. The concentrations of each of the 20 elements and the Cu‐to‐Zn ratio were standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 across the full cohort. Separate LR models were then fitted with AD status (1 = AD, 0 = NC) as the dependent variable, a single standardized element as the primary predictor, and age (years) and sex (female vs. male) as covariates. For each model, odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated and reported per 1‐SD increase in the corresponding element. To account for multiple comparisons across the 20 elements and the Cu/Zn ratio, p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

To examine age‐ and sex‐related patterns, serum concentrations of the 20 elements were first standardized as z scores using the mean and SD of the full cohort, allowing regression coefficients to be interpreted in SD units. Analyses were performed separately in the NC and AD groups. Two‐sided Wald tests were used for all models. p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across the 20 elements using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure within each analysis set. Regression coefficients were visualized as heatmaps stratified by diagnostic group, with color indicating the β coefficient and asterisks denoting associations that met predefined FDR significance thresholds.

Machine learning and regression analysis

In the full cohort of 874 participants, serum concentrations of 20 elements were measured together with age, sex, and the Cu/Zn ratio, yielding a total of 23 features. Machine learning approaches were used to evaluate the ability of these features to distinguish AD from NC. An overview of the classification workflow is presented in Supplementary Figure 1.

Supervised machine learning models were developed in Python (version 3.9.12; Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA) using the pandas package (version 1.3.5; pandas development team/NumFOCUS, Austin, TX, USA), scikit‐learn (version 1.0.2; scikit‐learn developers), and joblib (version 1.1.0; joblib developers). The analysis dataset was imported from an Excel file into a pandas DataFrame. The binary outcome variable (AD vs. NC) was encoded as 0/1 using the “LabelEncoder” class (AD = 1, NC = 0). Predictor variables included serum concentrations of 20 elements (Mg, Ca, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Se, Sr, Mo, Cd, Sn, Sb, I, Hg, Pb, and Bi), the Cu/Zn ratio, and the demographic variables age and sex. These variables were extracted from all columns beginning with the fourth column of the dataset. Sex was treated as a categorical variable and converted into a binary indicator variable (female vs. male) using one‐hot encoding implemented through the “get_dummies (drop_first = True).”

The full dataset was randomly shuffled and partitioned into a training set and an internal hold‐out test set at an 80:20 ratio using the train_test_split() function in scikit‐learn (test_size = 0.20, shuffle = True, random_state = 42). A fixed pseudorandom seed of 42 was specified to ensure reproducibility. No stratification was applied during data partitioning. Eight commonly used classification algorithms were trained on the training set using their scikit‐learn implementations: LR (LogisticRegression), support vector machine (SVM) (support vector classifier), k‐nearest neighbors (KNN) (KNeighborsClassifier, k = 5), decision tree (DecisionTreeClassifier), random forest (RF) (RandomForestClassifier), gradient boosting (GB) machine (GradientBoostingClassifier), a multilayer perceptron neural network (MLPClassifier; single hidden layer with 136 neurons, max_iter = 1000, alpha = 0.001), and Gaussian naïve Bayes (GaussianNB). Unless otherwise specified, default scikit‐learn hyperparameters were used.

After model training, each fitted model was serialized and saved using the joblib library to facilitate reproducible application of the trained classifiers. For model evaluation, the saved models were reloaded and applied to the internal hold‐out test set. For each algorithm, predicted class labels and, when available, predicted probabilities of AD were generated. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), and F1 score. Precision, recall, and F1 score were calculated with AD designated as the positive class. For models that generated probabilistic outputs, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was additionally calculated using the predicted probability of the AD class. These performance metrics were used to compare the ability of different algorithms to distinguish AD from NC based on serum elemental features and demographic variables.

To further assess the robustness and stability of model performance, five‐fold stratified cross‐validation was additionally performed on the full dataset. The dataset was divided into five folds while preserving the proportion of AD and NC participants in each fold. Performance metrics were summarized as mean ± SD across the five folds.

Regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether the same 23 features could predict MMSE and MoCA scores (Supplementary Figure 2). Using the same Python environment and scikit‐learn package described earlier, regression models were developed with MMSE or MoCA score as the continuous outcome variable. Predictor variables included age, sex, serum concentrations of 20 elements, and the Cu/Zn ratio. Sex was treated as a categorical variable and converted into a binary indicator variable using one‐hot encoding with the first category dropped. For cognitive score prediction, a complete‐case dataset comprising 662 participants with available MMSE and MoCA scores and complete data for all 23 predictor variables was used. The dataset was randomly shuffled and partitioned into a training set (n = 529) and an internal hold‐out test set (n = 133) at an 80:20 ratio using the train_test_split() function in scikit‐learn (test_size = 0.20, shuffle = True, random_state = 42). The same data partition was used for both MMSE and MoCA prediction to ensure comparability across outcomes. No stratification was applied, and both MMSE and MoCA scores were modeled as continuous outcomes.

Before model fitting, all predictor variables were standardized using the “StandardScaler” function. Means and SDs were estimated from the training set and subsequently applied to both the training and test sets to prevent information leakage from the test set. Three supervised regression algorithms were trained using the standardized training data: multiple linear regression (MLR) (LinearRegression), decision tree regression (DTR) (DecisionTreeRegressor), and RF regression (RFR) (RandomForestRegressor). After model training, each fitted model was serialized and saved using the “joblib. dump” and subsequently reloaded for evaluation to ensure reproducible application of the trained models throughout the analysis. For each regression algorithm, predicted MMSE or MoCA scores were generated for the internal hold‐out test set using the standardized predictor variables. Predicted values were rounded to the nearest integer to reflect the discrete scoring structure of the MMSE and MoCA instruments. Model performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the linear association between model‐derived cognitive estimates and observed cognitive scores, while Spearman rank correlation coefficients were used to evaluate consistency in cognitive score ranking. Prediction errors were assessed using root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated to estimate the proportion of variance explained by each model. Sensitivity analyses were additionally performed using unrounded continuous predictions from the MLR models to evaluate whether rounding affected correlation estimates.

Statistical analyses

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this study was a retrospective cross‐sectional analysis of an existing cohort. All eligible participants with available serum samples and clinical data were included, resulting in a final sample of 874 participants. Analyses involving MMSE and MoCA scores included all participants with available data for the respective cognitive assessment. All sample sizes (n) and statistical tests are reported in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.1; GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) and R (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for variables with sample sizes greater than 100. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, whereas nonnormally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. Associations between serum element concentrations and cognitive scores were evaluated using linear regression models implemented with the “lm()” function in R. p values from the MMSE and MoCA association analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure. Forest plots were used to visualize regression coefficients and their 95% CIs. As a sensitivity analysis, age‐ and sex‐matched comparisons between NC and AD participants were performed using 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching based on age and sex, resulting in 324 matched participants in each group. Serum elemental differences were then re‐evaluated in the matched cohort.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 874 participants were included in this study, comprising 427 cognitively NC and 447 patients with AD. The mean age of the overall cohort was 66.8 years and did not differ significantly between the NC and AD groups (66.4 ± 9.9 vs. 67.1 ± 9.0 years; t(872) = −1.13, p = 0.260). Overall, 466 of 874 participants (53.3%) were female. The proportion of women was significantly higher in the AD group than in the NC group (255/447 [57.0%] vs. 211/427 [49.4%]; χ 2(1) = 5.11, p = 0.024). As expected, MMSE scores were significantly lower in the AD group than in the NC group (AD: n = 277, median [IQR] = 24 [19–27]; NC: n = 427, median [IQR]: 30 [30]; Mann–Whitney U = 3043.5, p < 0.001). Similarly, MoCA scores were significantly lower in the AD group than in the NC group (AD: n = 240, median [IQR] = 18.00 [14.00–20.25]; NC: n = 427, median [IQR] = 30.00 [30.00–30.00]; Mann–Whitney U = 13.5, p < 0.001) (Table 1). The AD group encompassed a broad range of cognitive impairment severity, as reflected by the wide distribution of MMSE scores.

Serum elemental differences between NC and AD groups

We first compared serum concentrations of 20 elements between NC and AD participants (Figure 1A). Compared with NC participants, participants with AD exhibited significantly higher serum concentrations of Pb, Sn, and Bi and significantly lower concentrations of Hg, Se, Fe, Co, Ca, Mn, Ni, Sb, and As. No significant between‐group differences were observed for I, Cu, Zn, Cd, Sr, Mg, Cr, and Mo. We also compared the serum Cu/Zn ratio because of its proposed relevance to systemic oxidative stress and neurodegeneration, 9 but found no significant difference between the two groups.

Because AD risk is strongly influenced by age and sex, 10 we next examined whether these associations remained after adjustment for both variables using LR models. To further evaluate potential confounding by age and sex, we performed an age‐ and sex‐matched analysis of NC and AD participants. Serum elemental differences were subsequently re‐evaluated in the matched cohort (Supplementary Figure 3). After matching, the difference in serum Ca was no longer statistically significant, suggesting that the association observed in the primary analysis was largely attributable to differences in age and sex between the groups. In contrast, serum Cu concentrations were significantly higher, Zn concentrations were significantly lower, and the Cu/Zn ratio was significantly higher in the AD group, indicating a disruption of Cu–Zn homeostasis in AD. For most other elements, the direction of the associations remained consistent with the primary analysis, although the magnitude of the differences was attenuated.

Because the clinical diagnosis of AD is intrinsically linked to cognitive impairment, we next examined the associations between serum element concentrations and global cognitive performance. Higher concentrations of Mn, Co, Ni, and Sb were associated with better MMSE performance, whereas higher concentrations of Mg, Sn, and Pb were associated with lower MMSE scores (Figure 1B). Similar patterns were observed for MoCA scores, with an additional inverse association observed for Zn (Figure 1C). Overall, these analyses suggest that disturbances in elemental homeostasis, particularly involving Mn, Co, Ni, Sn, Sb, and Pb, are associated not only with AD diagnosis but also with the severity of global cognitive impairment.

Association of age and sex with serum elemental levels

Because AD risk is strongly influenced by age and sex, we next examined the associations of these demographic factors with serum elemental profiles within each diagnostic group.

Among NC participants, age was associated with widespread variation in serum element concentrations (Figure 2A). Older NC participants exhibited lower concentrations of Ca, Fe, Hg, Mn, Ni, Sb, Sn, and Zn, but higher concentrations of Cr, indicating broad age‐related alterations in serum elemental profiles (Supplementary Table 1). In contrast, age‐related associations were substantially weaker in AD participants, with only a limited number of elements remaining significantly associated with age after FDR correction (Supplementary Table 2). These findings suggest that age exerts a strong influence on serum element concentrations in NC participants, whereas the corresponding associations are attenuated in AD.

We next examined sex‐related differences in serum element concentrations within each diagnostic group (Figure 2B). Among NC participants, females had higher age‐adjusted concentrations of Ca, Cu, and Se, but lower concentrations of Fe and Sb, than males (Supplementary Table 3). In AD participants, the sex‐related differences in Cu and Fe were largely preserved, with females again exhibiting higher Cu and lower Fe concentrations than males. In addition, female AD participants had lower concentrations of Sb and Sr than male AD participants (Supplementary Table 4). For the remaining elements, sex‐related differences were small and did not reach statistical significance in either group. Collectively, these findings indicate that serum element concentrations exhibit element‐specific sex differences, some of which are preserved in AD, whereas others seem to be altered.

Machine learning models for AD classification

To distinguish AD from NC, we evaluated eight supervised machine learning algorithms using age, sex, the Cu/Zn ratio, and serum concentrations of 20 elements as input features.

In the current internal hold‐out analysis, the RF model showed the highest apparent performance among the evaluated classifiers, achieving an AUC of 0.94, followed by GB (AUC = 0.93) and LR (AUC = 0.86). In contrast, the SVM (AUC = 0.57) and KNN (AUC = 0.54) models exhibited the poorest discriminatory performance, with KNN showing substantial misclassification (Figure 3A). Confusion matrix analysis further demonstrated that RF achieved the highest classification accuracy, correctly classifying 154 of 175 test cases, including 83 participants with AD and 71 NC participants (Figure 3B). GB also performed well, correctly classifying 80 participants with AD and 72 NC participants. By contrast, the SVM and KNN models misclassified a substantial number of participants in both groups.

A summary of model performance is presented in Figure 3C. RF achieved an accuracy of 88%, with a precision of 0.86, recall of 0.92, and F1 score of 0.89. LR and GB also performed well, achieving F1 scores of 0.82 and 0.87, respectively. In contrast, KNN exhibited the poorest performance, with an F1 score of 0.53. To evaluate the robustness of these findings, we further performed five‐fold stratified cross‐validation. The RF model consistently demonstrated strong performance, with a mean AUC of 0.94 ± 0.02 and accuracy of 0.88 ± 0.02 across the five folds (Supplementary Table 5). Overall, these results indicate that RF showed the highest apparent discrimination between AD and NC under the current modeling framework.

Exploratory prediction of MMSE and MoCA scores using serum elemental profiles

Because MMSE and MoCA assessments can be time‐consuming and require active participant cooperation, we next investigated whether serum elemental profiles could be used for exploratory prediction of MMSE and MoCA scores using regression models.

Three regression models—DTR, RFR, and MLR—were evaluated for MMSE and MoCA score prediction (Figure 4A,B). Model performance was evaluated using Pearson correlation, Spearman rank correlation, RMSE, MAE, and R2 values. For MMSE prediction, all three models showed significant correlations between model‐derived estimates and observed MMSE scores. The RFR demonstrated the strongest performance, with the highest Pearson correlation (r = 0.48, p < 0.001) and Spearman correlation (ρ = 0.59, p < 0.001), together with the lowest RMSE (3.95) and MAE (2.40) among the evaluated models. The DTR showed moderate correlations with observed MMSE scores (Pearson r = 0.35, p < 0.001; Spearman ρ = 0.49, p < 0.001), whereas MLR showed relatively weaker associations (Pearson r = 0.22, p = 0.012; Spearman ρ = 0.45, p < 0.001) (Figure 4A,C). Similar patterns were observed for MoCA prediction. The RFR achieved the strongest association with observed MoCA scores, showing Pearson r = 0.62 (p < 0.001) and Spearman ρ = 0.66 (p < 0.001), with RMSE of 5.45 and MAE of 3.67. The DTR also showed significant correlations (Pearson r = 0.57, p < 0.001; Spearman ρ = 0.65, p < 0.001), whereas MLR showed weaker correlations (Pearson r = 0.34, p < 0.001; Spearman ρ = 0.58, p < 0.001) (Figure 4B,C).

Because MMSE and MoCA are integer‐based clinical scoring systems, we further performed sensitivity analyses to determine whether rounding of MLR‐derived predictions affected correlation estimates. Correlation coefficients obtained from unrounded continuous predictions were highly consistent with those obtained from rounded predictions for both MMSE (Pearson r = 0.22 vs. 0.22; Spearman p: 0.46 vs. 0.45) and MoCA (Pearson r = 0.34 vs. 0.34; Spearman ρ: 0.59 vs. 0.58), indicating that rounding did not materially influence the correlation analyses (Supplementary Table 6).

These findings suggest that serum elemental profiles contain information related to global cognitive performance, although the performance for cognitive score prediction was more modest than that observed for AD classification.

DISCUSSION

Altered elemental homeostasis has been repeatedly reported in individuals with cognitive impairment; however, most previous studies have focused on a limited number of metals and have often been constrained by small sample sizes or heterogeneous study designs. 11 In the present study, we quantified 20 serum elements in 874 participants from the BANC‐PUMC cohort and integrated these measurements with standardized cognitive assessments and machine learning analyses. This approach enabled a relatively comprehensive evaluation of the relationships between systemic serum elemental profiles, clinically defined AD, and global cognitive performance in a large Chinese cohort.

We identified a characteristic multielement serum profile associated with AD. Among the analyzed elements, Pb showed the strongest association with AD. Although findings from previous studies have not been entirely consistent, several independent cohorts have also reported elevated Pb concentrations in AD, 12 and experimental studies have suggested that chronic low‐level Pb exposure promotes oxidative stress, synaptic dysfunction, and hippocampal neurotoxicity. 13 Our findings are also broadly consistent with previous reports implicating Sn and Sb in AD‐related elemental alterations, 11 , 14 while extending these observations by demonstrating that some of these elements are associated not only with AD status but also with cognitive performance. In parallel, the lower concentrations of Se, Co, and Mn support the possibility that reduced availability of trace elements involved in antioxidant defense, mitochondrial metabolism, and enzymatic function may contribute to neuronal vulnerability. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Not all associations with AD status were reflected in associations with cognitive performance. Se and Ca were inversely associated with AD diagnosis in multivariable models; however, neither showed a clear relationship with MMSE or MoCA scores. This pattern suggests that these elements may be more closely related to disease susceptibility or the long‐term systemic environment than to cross‐sectional variation in global cognitive function. Conversely, Mg and Zn were not independently associated with AD status after adjustment for age and sex, but higher Mg concentrations and, in the MoCA analyses, higher Zn concentrations were associated with lower cognitive scores. These findings raise the possibility that some serum elements reflect broader determinants of cognitive performance that are not specific to AD. Associations involving Cd and cognitive performance were imprecise and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Taken together, these findings suggest that serum elemental profiles may capture both AD‐related biological processes and more general influences on cognitive function.

Because age and sex are major determinants of AD risk, we also examined their associations with serum element concentrations within each diagnostic group. In NC participants, older age was associated with lower concentrations of Ca, Fe, Hg, Mn, Ni, Sb, Sn, and Zn and higher concentrations of Cr, indicating broad age‐related alterations in serum elemental profiles. In contrast, these age‐related associations were markedly attenuated in AD participants, suggesting that AD may be associated with disruption of the typical age‐related patterns of systemic elemental homeostasis. We also observed clear element‐specific sex differences. In NC participants, females had higher age‐adjusted concentrations of Ca, Cu, and Se and lower concentrations of Fe and Sb than males, and several of these differences were preserved in AD, whereas others were not. These findings emphasize the importance of carefully accounting for age and sex when interpreting associations between serum elements and AD.

Beyond the biological associations described earlier, we also evaluated the classification performance of serum elemental features. The classification models were intentionally developed using serum element concentrations together with age, sex, and the Cu/Zn ratio to explore the discriminative value of this minimally invasive and clinically accessible feature set. Although differences in individual elements are unlikely to be diagnostically sufficient, machine learning approaches can integrate multidimensional elemental information and thereby improve discriminatory performance. Among the models evaluated, RF achieved the highest apparent performance, with an accuracy of ~88%, together with high precision, recall, and F1 score, indicating that multielement serum profiles contain substantial information relevant to clinically defined AD status. These findings are broadly consistent with a growing body of studies applying machine learning to AD diagnosis, in which reported accuracies have ranged from 77% to 96.8%, depending on the modality and study design. 20 Although deep learning models based on neuroimaging data can achieve very high accuracy, they typically require expensive equipment and specialized expertise and may, in some settings, involve radiation exposure or contrast agent administration. By comparison, serum‐based models are minimally invasive and relatively inexpensive and may therefore represent a practical complementary approach for risk stratification and screening, particularly in settings where advanced neuroimaging is not readily available. In contrast, regression models based on the same feature set showed only moderate correlations with MMSE and MoCA scores, suggesting that serum elemental profiles explain only part of the variability in global cognitive performance. Taken together, these findings should be interpreted as proof‐of‐concept evidence that serum elemental features may provide complementary information within broader efforts to improve diagnostic precision in AD and support more individualized approaches to disease assessment and management. 21 , 22 Future integration with established blood‐based AD biomarkers, such as amyloid‐β, phosphorylated tau, and neurofilament light chain, together with genetic risk factors such as apolipoprotein E genotype, neuroimaging markers, and other clinical variables, may further enhance diagnostic performance and improve biological interpretability. 23

Another practical advantage of a serum element‐based approach is its potential suitability for repeated, minimally invasive assessment over time. Although the present study was cross‐sectional and did not evaluate prognostic outcomes, this characteristic raises the possibility that serum elemental models could ultimately contribute to longitudinal monitoring, risk stratification, or prediction of cognitive decline. Whether temporal changes in serum elemental profiles track disease progression or treatment response will require dedicated longitudinal investigations.

Finally, this study has several strengths. It included a relatively large cohort, quantified 20 serum elements under standardized analytical conditions, and integrated descriptive, regression‐based, and machine learning analyses within a single dataset. The study also benefited from detailed clinical phenotyping and standardized cognitive assessments in a well‐characterized brain donation cohort.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective cross‐sectional study, and causal relationships cannot be inferred. Second, MMSE and MoCA data were unavailable for a subset of participants, and this missingness may have been informative because some participants with more advanced impairment or limited cooperation were unable to complete the full cognitive evaluation. Therefore, the regression models for cognitive scores may be biased toward participants who were able to complete both assessments. Third, serum element concentrations were measured at a single time point and may not fully capture longitudinal variability. Fourth, although AD diagnosis was based on standardized clinical criteria and PET was performed in selected diagnostically uncertain cases, biomarker‐supported diagnosis was not applied systematically across the cohort, and no disease‐comparator groups were included. Therefore, the current models should not be interpreted as differential diagnostic tools for distinguishing AD from other neurodegenerative or dementing disorders. In addition, model performance was evaluated using a single internal hold‐out test set without external validation, and the reported performance metrics may therefore be sensitive to the specific data split. Although five‐fold stratified cross‐validation supported the stability of the current findings, prospective validation and independent external cohorts are needed to further establish model generalizability. Fifth, although age and sex were accounted for in the analyses, other potential confounders, including smoking status, vascular and metabolic comorbidities (e.g., hypertension and hyperlipidemia), dietary factors, and environmental exposures, were not systematically incorporated into the current modeling framework. Therefore, serum elemental profiles should not be regarded as AD‐specific biomarkers at this stage but rather as a potentially informative and clinically practical feature set whose biological specificity requires further validation. Finally, all participants were recruited from the Beijing metropolitan area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Available MRI and CT data were obtained as part of routine clinical evaluation rather than through a uniformly timed, standardized research imaging protocol. In addition, not all participants underwent high‐resolution volumetric MRI, precluding robust analyses of associations between serum elemental profiles and quantitative neuroimaging markers, such as hippocampal volume or cortical thickness. Because only a limited number of participants had died by the time of the current analysis, postmortem interval and time‐from‐death data were available for only a small subset of participants and, therefore, could not be meaningfully incorporated into the analyses. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal sampling, external validation cohorts, standardized neuroimaging protocols, and more comprehensive environmental and clinical metadata.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates that clinically defined AD is associated with a distinct pattern of serum elemental dysregulation rather than isolated alterations in individual elements. Several of these alterations remained independently associated with AD after adjustment for age and sex, and some were also associated with lower MMSE and MoCA scores. Machine learning models based on serum elemental features demonstrated promising apparent performance in distinguishing AD from NC, whereas their performance in modeling cognitive scores was more modest. Collectively, these findings support the potential of serum elemental profiles as a complementary, minimally invasive source of information for AD identification. Further validation in biomarker‐defined, longitudinal, and externally validated cohorts will be required before broader clinical application can be considered.
