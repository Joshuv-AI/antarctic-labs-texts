Accuracy of ventilation feedback devices compared with a reference standard during asynchronous ventilation with continuous chest compressions in a simulated cardiac arrest: a prospective non-randomized cross-over study
Authors: Christophe Duchatelet, Kristof Segher, Thomas Tackaert, Maxim Vanwulpen, Bert Ingels, Dimitri Muylaert, Said Hachimi-Idrissi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: OHCA, Cardiac arrest, Ventilation, Resuscitation, Ventilation feedback devices

Introduction

High-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) depends on the delivery of effective chest compressions and appropriate ventilation to maintain coronary and cerebral perfusion and gas exchange. In intubated patients, current guidelines recommend asynchronous positive-pressure ventilations during continuous chest compressions, with a controlled ventilation rate of 10/min and tidal volumes of 6–8 mL/kg.1, 2 Despite these recommendations, inappropriate ventilation during CPR remains common and has been associated with unfavourable physiological effects and outcomes.3, 4, 5, 6

To improve ventilation quality, visual real-time ventilation feedback devices (VFD) have been developed to guide providers by displaying measured ventilation rate and tidal volumes. Evidence from simulation studies and limited clinical investigations suggests that such devices may improve compliance with guideline-recommended ventilation targets.7 However, the accuracy and physiological validity of these measurements during ongoing CPR have not been adequately established, particularly during continuous chest compressions. Several researchers have raised concerns on the ability of current ventilation measurement devices to correctly calculate intra-arrest tidal volumes and ventilation rates.8, 9, 10, 11

Ventilation during continuous chest compressions is characterized by complex interactions between positive-pressure insufflations and externally applied chest compressions. We previously demonstrated that chest compressions during ventilation can induce reversed airflow and divide inspiratory flow into multiple fragmented airflow episodes.12 Those findings have later been confirmed by other research groups.13 Most ventilation feedback devices rely on flow or pressure differential signals measured at the proximal airway and bidirectional airflow within one respiratory cycle may impair the VFDs to correctly calculate tidal volumes and ventilation rates during CPR with asynchronous ventilations and continuous chest compressions.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the accuracy of ventilation parameters displayed by two ventilation feedback devices, compared to measurements obtained from a high-fidelity reference pneumotachograph in a simulated model of cardiac arrest with advanced airway during asynchronous ventilation and continuous chest compressions with presence of reversed airflow.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a prospective, simulation-based, non-randomized cross-over study designed to assess the accuracy of two commercially available ventilation feedback devices (VFDs) in measuring ventilation rate and tidal volume during simulated CPR. Device-displayed parameters are compared against a predefined reference standard to evaluate measurement agreement.

Setting

The study was conducted in the skillslab of AZ Alma, a 493 bed regional hospital near Eeklo, Flanders, Belgium. The skillslab provides a controlled simulation environment and is specifically designed to facilitate training in complex Advanced Life Support (ALS) scenarios. A high-fidelity manikin (SimMan® 3G 2012, Laerdal, Stavanger, Norway) was intubated with an 8 mm endotracheal tube (Lo-Contour™, Mallinckrodt, USA) and configured to replicate an intubated adult patient undergoing ALS with continuous chest compressions. The lung model was checked for leakages, and a preliminary comparative assessment was performed to assess the ability of the manikin model to reproduce physiologically plausible ventilation patterns during simulated cardiopulmonary resuscitation with continuous chest compressions and asynchronous ventilation.

Participants

A convenience sample of twenty ALS certified emergency department nurse practitioners with prehospital experience were recruited. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (B67020083371).

Participants were blinded to both the specific study hypothesis and the feedback screen of the tested devices.

Simulation protocol

Participants performed two trials (one per device), manually ventilating at a target of 10 breaths/min for 1 min using a 1600 mL self-inflating bag without positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) valve. During ventilation, a second participant applied continuous chest compressions at a target of 100–120/min and depth of 5–6 cm, simulating real-life ALS.

Devices were tested in a fixed sequential order (EOLifeX® followed by AccuVent®) for all participants. No randomization of device sequence was performed.

Reference standard and measurements

A purpose-built high-fidelity reference pneumotachograph (HFRP) (Sensirion, Switzerland) was placed in-line with the airway circuit and the tested device and served as the reference standard. The HFRP was based on a Sensirion flow sensor, which is factory-calibrated for accurate bidirectional airflow measurement. The sensor was operated according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Prior to each experiment, the measurement system was checked for proper function and signal stability, and zero-flow conditions were verified to ensure accurate baseline measurements. The HFRP recorded airflow signals at a sampling rate of 200 Hz. We previously reported application of this technique to measure prehospital intra-arrest ventilation flow. For a detailed description of the materials and methods of this measurement technique, we refer to our previous work.14 Raw flow data were exported and analysed offline. Tidal volumes were calculated by integrating inspiratory airflow over time. In the presence of compression-induced reversed airflow, tidal volumes were calculated as the sum of all inspiratory flow fragments within a single respiratory cycle, in accordance with previous established methodology.12, 13 (Fig. 3). Ventilation rate was determined based on identified respiratory cycles during one minute. Compression depth and rate were monitored an accelerometer (CPR stat-padz multifunction, Zoll Medical Company, Chelmsford, USA).

Airflow during a manual ventilation of the manikin with three superimposed chest compressions. Every single compression during the inspiratory phase of the ventilation produces reversed airflow. Tidal volumes were calculated as the sum of the inspiratory airflow fragments during one respiratory cycle.

To allow accurate capture of device-displayed parameters, the screens of both ventilation feedback devices were continuously recorded during each trial using a Microsoft Surface Pro 9 (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA) mounted on a fixed stand. Video recordings were obtained using OBS Studio (Open Broadcaster Software) with synchronized time stamps, facilitating subsequent offline extraction and manual verification of ventilation rate and tidal volume displayed by the devices. Ventilation data of the VFD was synchronized with the data of the HFRP offline using Microsoft Excel Data Analysis Toolpak. For EOlifeX®, expiratory tidal volume (VTe) and ventilation rate were evaluated and compared with the corresponding reference measurements. For AccuVent®, inspiratory tidal volume (VTi) and ventilation rate were evaluated and compared with the corresponding reference measurements.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the mean difference (bias) between device-displayed ventilation variables and reference measurements obtained from the HFRP for ventilation rate and tidal volume.

The secondary outcome was measurement accuracy, defined as the agreement between device-displayed and reference-derived values for tidal volume and ventilation rate. Accuracy was assessed by comparing paired device-displayed values with corresponding measurements derived from the HFRP.

Sample size

No formal sample size calculation was performed.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Continuous data are presented as mean (standard deviation; range) or median (interquartile range; range) as appropriate.

For EOlifeX®, breath-by-breath pairing with the reference standard was not feasible because the device displayed more ventilation events than were identified by the HFRP. Therefore, comparisons were performed using case-level summary measures. EOlifeX tidal volume values represent the mean of all expiratory tidal volume values displayed during each trial. Welch’s t-test was used to quantify bias as the mean differences (device minus reference) and reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). To demonstrate that the reported bias estimates were not driven by outliers, case medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) were additionally calculated For AccuVent®, where pairing of ventilation variables was possible, agreement between methods was assessed with Bland–Altman analysis using all paired breath measurements, reporting the mean bias and 95% limits of agreement. Precision of agreement was evaluated as the standard deviation of the differences and expressed as a coefficient of variation. Bland–Altman plots were inspected for systematic and proportional bias. Reliability was further assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient with 95% CI.

A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel Data Analysis Toolpak. No missing or indeterminate measurements occurred during the study.

Reporting standards

The study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT extension for simulation-based research. A completed reporting checklist is provided in the Supplementary Appendix 1.

Results

The median age of the participants was 42 years (9; 26–64), 10 of them were male. All participants (20/20) held current ALS certification. The median years of prehospital experience on the physician/nurse staffed rapid response car was 10 years (6; 2–25).

EOLifeX®

Using the EOLifeX® ventilation feedback device, each of the 20 participants performed a trial of simulated cardiac resuscitation, each manually ventilating the manikin for a duration of 1 min at a target ventilation rate of 10 breaths/minute. Mean compression depth was 5.34 cm (1.02; 4.9–6.05) and mean compression rate was 122/min (5.4; 108–132). Analysing the raw flow data obtained from the HFRP, a total number of 254 ventilations were identified. The analysis showed a mean ventilation rate of 12/min (1; 8–14) (median 12/min IQR 11–13) and a mean tidal volume of 544 mL (32; 482–604) (median 549 mL IQR 521–567). Airflow fragmentation by continuous chest compression was observed in all ventilations. Reversed airflow was 49 ml (13; 19–72) on average. The display on the ventilation feedback device showed a mean ventilation rate of 111/min (5; 99–121) (median 110/min IQR 107–114) and mean expiratory tidal volume of 104 ml (14; 68–240) (median 102 mL IQR 96–125) respectively. The mean difference (bias) between displayed and measured ventilation rate was +99 breaths/min (95% CI 96.6–101.4), indicating overestimation relative to the reference standard. Tidal volume showed a negative bias of −440 mL (95% CI −456.3 to −423.7), reflecting underestimation (Table 1).

AccuVent®

Using the AccuVent® ventilation feedback device, each of the 20 participants performed a trial of simulated cardiac resuscitation, each manually ventilating the manikin for a duration of 1 min at a target ventilation rate of 10 breaths/minute. Mean compression depth was 5.22 cm (1.05; 4.8–6.12) and mean compression rate was 115/min (5.3; 104–125). Analysing the raw flow data obtained from the HFRP, a total number of 222 ventilations were identified. The analysis showed a mean ventilation rate of 12/min (1; 8–13) and a mean tidal volume of 532 mL (35; 460–614). Airflow fragmentation by continuous chest compression was observed in all ventilations. Reversed airflow was 56 ml (15; 22–78) on average. The display on the ventilation feedback device showed a ventilation rate of 12/min (1; 8–13) and an inspiratory tidal volume of 360 ml (24; 292–398) on average respectively. The mean difference (bias) between displayed and measured tidal was −172 ml (95% CI −191.9 to −152.1), indicating underestimation relative to the reference standard (Table 1). No difference was observed for ventilation rate.

Bland–Altman analysis confirmed the substantial systematic bias for tidal volume by −172 mL. The 95% limits of agreement ranged from −195.8 mL to −148.4 mL, indicating considerable absolute disagreement between the two methods. Visual analysis of the Bland–Altman plot showed a clear proportional bias, with the magnitude of the difference increasing across the range of measured volumes. The variability of the differences was relatively small, with a standard deviation of 12.1 mL, corresponding to a coefficient of variation of 2.7%, indicating good repeatability of the measurement differences. The ICC demonstrated poor agreement, with a value of −0.79 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.74) (Fig. 4).

Blan-Altman analysis visualising the level of agreement between measurements of the AccuVent® compared to those from a high-fidelity reference pneumotachograph (HFRP) for tidal volume.

No Bland–Altman analysis was performed for AccuVent® ventilation rate, as the paired t-test could not demonstrate any systematic bias between device-displayed and reference measurements.

Discussion

In this simulation-based study of asynchronous ventilation during continuous chest compressions, in the presence of airflow fragmentation, we observed discrepancies between ventilation parameters displayed by both EOLifeX® and AccuVent® compared to measurements obtained from a reference standard. While EOLifeX® demonstrated implausible ventilation rates, both devices showed underestimation of tidal volume measurement.

Despite a true measured ventilation rate of 12 breaths/min and tidal volume of 544 ml, the EOLifeX® displayed a clinically implausible mean ventilation rate of 111 breaths/min and expiratory tidal volume of only 104 ml. This is approximately tenfold overestimation of ventilation rate corresponds closely to the applied chest compression rate, suggesting that the device interprets individual compression-induced flow fragments as separate ventilatory events. Previous work has demonstrated that chest compressions superimposed on ventilations during CPR generate bidirectional airflow within the airway during one respiratory cycle. This possibly results in display of every individual measured flow fragment, leading to a substantial overestimation of ventilation rate and a corresponding underestimation of tidal volume. Intermittent reversal and fragmentation of airflow can lead to multiple false-positive breath detections. To date, published studies on the EOLifeX® have only focused on 30:2 scenarios or simulated respiratory arrest without chest compressions, settings in which the feedback device can report clinically plausible values.8, 15

In contrast, the AccuVent® device provided a clinical plausible ventilation rates and no significant difference was observed compared to the reference standard, suggesting an algorithm capable of filtering compression-induced artifacts to identify individual ventilations. However, despite accurate respiratory rate detection, the AccuVent® significantly underestimated inspiratory tidal volumes. The mechanism underlying this systematic underestimation remains unclear, as the proprietary algorithm used by the device manufacturer is not publicly available. Furtmüller et al. suggested earlier that reversed airflow due to chest compressions may prematurely disrupt the per-breath integration of airflow used to determine tidal volumes, leading to underestimation of true intra-arrest tidal volumes.9 The clinical plausibility of the displayed ventilation parameters of the AccuVent is supported by prior studies demonstrating its feasibility and utility in both simulated and clinical cardiac arrest scenarios with advanced airway and continuous compressions and asynchronous ventilation.16, 17 However, we suggest that the results of those finding should be interpreted with caution, as our findings show clinically significant differences in displayed and true tidal volumes.

The observed differences between the two devices might be partly explained by the underlying measurement principle. The EOLifeX® device reports expiratory tidal volumes, whereas the AccuVent® reports inspiratory tidal volume. Tassart et al. have in their work, using the EOLifeX® device, shown that passive volumes induced by chest compressions or noise from inadvertent manipulations of the BVM interfere with the accurate detection of different ventilation phases, requiring post intervention advanced signal processing using custom made study specific algorithms.18

Orlob et al. recently proposed a novel algorithm designed to filter out compression-related artifacts and thereby enable a more reliable analysis of intra-arrest ventilation. While their approach represents an important methodological advancement and demonstrates promising accuracy under controlled conditions, it remains confined to experimental and academic settings. To date, such algorithms have not been integrated into commercially available feedback devices, limiting their practical applicability during real-world resuscitation. Consequently, we advise clinicians to be aware of these constraints when interpreting ventilation feedback, and further development is recommended to translate advanced signal-processing techniques into clinically usable technology.19

The preservation of near-guideline ventilation performance observed in this study may be explained by the fact that the study consisted of a short, one-minute simulation under controlled conditions. This setting does not fully reproduce the complexity of real-world cardiac arrest, where cognitive load, fatigue, time pressure, and environmental stressors may increase reliance on device feedback.

A key consideration in interpreting these findings is the use of a manikin-based simulation model. While such models allow controlled and reproducible assessment of device performance, they cannot fully replicate the complex thoracic and pulmonary mechanics of humans undergoing cardiac arrest. First, in preparation of this study, different ALS manikins were tested, as there’s substantial variability in their ability to reproduce human ventilation characteristics.20 A 2012 model of the Laerdal SimMan 3G was selected, as it proved to be able to produce reversed airflow, as no leakage was observed, and as it showed similar ventilation variables compared to real-life airway tracings of 5 out-of hospital cardiac arrest patients during continuous compressions and asynchronous ventilations. Detailed results of this preliminary comparative assessment are provided in the Supplementary Appendix 2. However, additional work would be required for comprehensive a validation of this manikin model. Secondly, the overall tidal volume and reversed airflow distribution in this study are similar to data reported in human studies regarding intra-arrest ventilation in adults.12 However, in this study, airflow fragmentation occurred in all studied ventilations, whereas in humans there is wide variation in occurrence and frequency of reversed airflow amongst different patients. Although the systematic presence of airflow fragmentation of this manikin model created exactly the setting in which we hypothesized the VFDs would show discrepancies, higher frequency of reversed and fragmented airflow may be increasing the susceptibility of the VFDs to compression artifacts and artificially driving its apparent failure.

The EOLifeX® and AccuVent® have proven its potential to increase ventilation quality in basic life support or 30:2 scenarios.7 However, our simulation study identifies a potential technical limitation of these devices when transitioned to the ALS setting with advanced airway and asynchronous ventilations with continuous chest compressions in the presence of reversed airflow.

Future directions

Further research using human, cadaveric, or advanced experimental models is needed to validate these findings and to establish the clinical impact of measurement inaccuracies. Development of feedback devices with improved algorithms to filter compression-induced flow fragmentation and validation in real-world scenarios of cardiac arrest are recommended.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a manikin-based simulation study, which inherently limits physiological realism. Second, the study included a relatively small sample size of experienced providers, resulting in homogeneous ventilation performance. Third, the short duration of the simulation scenarios may not capture changes in performance over time. Fourth, the study focused exclusively on asynchronous ventilation during continuous chest compressions and did not include data on 30:2 CPR. Fifth, analyses were based on multiple ventilation measurements per participant without explicit modelling of within-subject correlation, which may have led to underestimation of variance and inflation of statistical significance. Sixt, the study used the EOlifeX® training device rather than the EOlife® device intended for clinical use. Although both devices are believed to operate according to the same measurement principles, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings directly to the clinical EOlife® device. Finally, the transferability of these results to real-world clinical practice remains uncertain.

Conclusion

During simulated CPR on a manikin model of continuous compressions with asynchronous ventilation, in the presence of reversed, EOlifeX® displayed clinically implausible ventilation rate measurements and underestimated tidal volume. AccuVent® accurately measured ventilation rate but systematically underestimated tidal volume.
