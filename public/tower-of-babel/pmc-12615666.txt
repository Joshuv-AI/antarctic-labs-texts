Development of a LASSO dynamic prediction system for interbody cage subsidence following OLIF surgery
Authors: Jin Zhang, Hetong Li, Guofang Fang, Ziwen Zhu, Xiuwang Li, Yang Chen, Zuoxu Hou, Weida Zhuang, Yubao Liu, Jianwei Wang, Jintao Zhong, Shanrui Liu, Elvis Chun-Sing Chui, William Weijia Lu, Ling Qin, Wing-Hoi Cheung, Ke Lu, Hongxun Sang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCage subsidence after oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) frequently causes poor outcomes, yet existing predictive models lack accuracy and applicability. This study leveraged the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to efficiently identify key predictors of subsidence from quantitative CT vertebral bone quality and endplate-related factors. We analyzed 337 OLIF surgical segments (674 endplates; May 2017–May 2024), observing subsidence in 45.70%. LASSO selected intervertebral disc height correction, intraoperative endplate injury, inferior endplate morphology, and volumetric bone mineral density of the inferior vertebra/endplate as top predictors. These variables informed a multivariable logistic regression model, visualized as a digital nomogram. The model demonstrated excellent predictive performance in training and validation cohorts via ROC, precision-recall, and calibration curves. Decision curve analysis confirmed high clinical utility across risk thresholds, enabling personalized preoperative risk assessment and prevention strategy optimization for OLIF patients.Subject terms: Risk factors, Disease prevention, Geriatrics, Medical imaging, Prognosis, Computational science, Software, Translational research

Introduction

In the surgical management of lumbar degenerative disorders, oblique lateral interbody fusion (OLIF) has emerged as a noteworthy minimally invasive procedure, garnering considerable attention in recent years1. This approach leverages potential anatomical corridors to achieve indirect decompression2,3, facilitating the insertion of large-scale interbody cages4. Notably, OLIF boasts distinct advantages in minimizing disruption to the posterior spinal stabilizing structures, reducing intraoperative hemorrhage, and expediting postoperative recuperation5,6. Nonetheless, cage subsidence represents a frequently encountered complication post OLIF2,7,8. Research indicates that the long-term incidence of subsidence remains consistent regardless of the implementation of a nail-rod internal fixation system, potentially compromising various clinical outcomes8–13. Literature underscores multiple contributory factors to cage subsidence, including osteoporosis, endplate morphology, endplate degradation, cage dimensions, and excessive intervertebral space expansion9,14,15, with diminished bone mineral density (BMD) emerging as one of the primary risk factor10,16–18.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) currently stands as a widely utilized modality for assessing BMD and serves as the gold standard for osteoporosis diagnosis. Nevertheless, DXA’s accuracy is susceptible to influences like spinal degeneration, deformities, and calcification of surrounding structures in elderly individuals, leading to erroneous findings and potential osteoporosis underdiagnosis19. To address DXA’s limitations, previous investigations predominantly relied on conventional computed tomography (CT) to gauge the Hounsfield unit (HU) of vertebral cancellous bone, aiming to evaluate BMD alterations and predict cage subsidence risks20–22. While CT exhibits superior detection accuracy in severely degenerated vertebrae compared to DXA, discrepancies persist in defining HU cutoff point for osteoporosis/osteopenia diagnosis, necessitating further elucidation of universally accepted diagnostic criteria.

Thankfully, the advent of quantitative computed tomography (QCT) mitigates this issue. Leveraging high-resolution CT and specialized software, QCT enables more precise detection of volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD) in the target region. Research suggests QCT may outperform DXA in identifying bone porosity, offering enhanced diagnostic capabilities23,24. By accurately quantifying vertebral body density and endplate strength, QCT facilitates a comprehensive assessment of bone quality in segments slated for fusion, thereby informing preoperative planning and surgical decision-making. Furthermore, QCT relies on image post-processing technology, obviating the need for additional radiation exposure and associated cost increments for patients undergoing evaluation.

The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression method facilitates the compression of variable coefficients within a regression model through penalty terms, thereby fostering model sparsity and identifying feature variables with optimal predictive power25,26. Compared to conventional regression techniques, LASSO offers the advantage of simplifying models to mitigate overfitting and address multicollinearity issues, resulting in the construction of more efficient prediction models27,28. To date, no study has concurrently employed LASSO regression and nomograms to investigate the relationship between QCT-based vertebral bone quality evaluation or endplate-related factors and the incidence of cage subsidence after OLIF. This study aims to construct a predictive model using LASSO-logistic regression, which incorporates QCT-based vertebral bone quality assessment and endplate status to predict cage subsidence following OLIF. The primary objective is to evaluate the accuracy and reliability of this model, with the ultimate goal of providing guidance for preoperative planning, refining prevention strategies, and enhancing clinical outcomes.

Results

Study cohort characteristics

In this study cohort, 109 out of 214 patients (50.93%) and 154 out of 337 surgical segments (45.70%) were found to have imaging evidence of cage subsidence, with an average subsidence distance of 5.48 ± 1.67 mm. There were 127 segments (37.69%) with grade I subsidence, 24 segments (7.12%) with grade II subsidence, and 3 segments (0.89%) with grade III subsidence. The remaining 105 patients (49.07%) and 183 segments (54.30%) were categorized in the non-subsidence group, with an average subsidence distance of 2.23 ± 0.90 mm. Compared to the non-subsidence group, the subsidence group had a higher intraoperative correction value of the DH (7.27 ± 1.97 mm vs. 2.85 ± 1.67 mm, P < 0.001), more concave superior and inferior endplate morphologies, superior ESCA [159.7 (153.2–163.8)° vs. 162.5 (155.6–167.7)°, P < 0.001] and inferior ESCA [162.3 (154.8–169.3)° vs. 165.8 (162.7–170.4)°, P < 0.001], and higher rates of intraoperative endplate injury [57 cases (37.01%) vs. 27 cases (14.75%), P < 0.001]. Furthermore, the statistical significance of the differences in QCT bone density indicators between the subsidence and non-subsidence groups was more reliable. Compared to the non-subsidence group, the subsidence group had lower QCT bone mineral density values (83.72 ± 23.67 mg/cm³ vs. 107.86 ± 26.78 mg/cm³, P < 0.001), lower overall lumbar spine averages (79.48 ± 21.40 mg/cm³ vs. 104.64 ± 26.82 mg/cm³, P < 0.001), and lower vBMD values of the superior and inferior vertebrae of the surgical segment, with superior vertebrae (68.50 ± 21.12 mg/cm³ vs. 95.34 ± 27.12 mg/cm³, P < 0.001) and inferior vertebrae (75.28 ± 25.20 mg/cm³ vs. 104.03 ± 29.53 mg/cm³, P < 0.001). In addition, the vBMD values of the superior and inferior endplate of the surgical segment were lower, with superior endplates [224.3 (178.2–264.2) mg/cm3 vs. 250.4 (213.4–273.7) mg/cm3, P < 0.001] and inferior endplates [150.9 (132.2–181.5) mg/cm3 vs. 216.5 (162.9–259.8) mg/cm3, P < 0.001]. In contrast, other variables did not show significant differences between the groups, including gender, age, BMI, comorbidities, smoking and drinking history, long-term history of anti-osteoporosis and steroid drug use, preoperative diagnosis, follow-up duration, surgical fusion segments, surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative wound complications, cage length, cage height, cage positioning, SLA and LLA correction values, preoperative endplate injury, final follow-up intervertebral fusion rates, and DXA bone mineral density indicators. The inter-rater reliability was excellent: CPR, ICC 0.943 (95% CI, 0.930–0.954); SLA correction values, ICC 0.975 (95% CI, 0.953-0.985); LLA correction values, ICC 0.977 (95% CI, 0.964–0.985); DH correction value, ICC 0.924 (95% CI, 0.903–0.940); DH loss value, ICC 0.911 (95% CI, 0.891-0.928); superior endplate morphology (ESCA), ICC 0.918 (95% CI, 0.899–0.934); inferior endplate morphology (ESCA), ICC 0.939 (95% CI, 0.924–0.950); QCT bone mineral density values, ICC 0.987 (95% CI, 0.982-0.990); overall lumbar spine average, ICC 0.993 (95% CI, 0.990–0.995); superior vertebrae vBMD values of surgical segment, ICC 0.975 (95% CI, 0.969–0.979); inferior vertebrae vBMD values, ICC 0.965 (95% CI, 0.957–0.972); superior endplate vBMD values, ICC 0.961 (95% CI, 0.918–0.978); and inferior endplate vBMD values, ICC 0.984 (95% CI, 0.980–0.987).

It is worth noting that due to the large number of variable indicators included in this study, in order to ensure the stability of the prediction model, single factor analysis was conducted in advance to screen important variables and exclude strong interfering variables from the LASSO regression model. Among them, QCT bone mineral density values are highly correlated with the overall average lumbar spine value, and may indirectly affect the final outcome through the BMD of adjacent vertebrae in the surgical segment. Therefore, the above variables and indicators are not included in LASSO regression. Ultimately, the initially screened important variables served as candidate predictive factors for cage subsidence, including intraoperative endplate injury, DH correction value, morphologies of the superior and inferior endplates of the surgical segments, superior and inferior vertebrae vBMD values, and superior and inferior endplate vBMD values.

Screening key characteristic variables based on LASSO regression

All surgical segments of patients were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. The training cohort was used to develop the predictive model, while the validation cohort was used for model prediction and testing. There were no statistically significant differences in important variables included randomly in both cohorts, and baseline characteristics of the two cohorts are presented in Table 2. To prevent overfitting of the final predictive model and mitigate the impact of multicollinearity, LASSO regression was utilized to screen candidate predictive factors within the training cohort. Variables were selected based on the lambda value (lambda.1se) which was one standard error from the minimum mean squared error, identified through tenfold cross-validation, ultimately identifying five key characteristic variables with the best predictive power. These included intraoperative endplate injury, DH correction value, inferior endplate morphology of the surgical segment (ESCA), inferior vertebrae vBMD value, and inferior endplate vBMD value. The cross-validation plot and variable selection pathway for the LASSO regression model are shown in Fig. 1A, B, and the regression coefficients for each variable are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Developing predictive nomogram based on logistic regression

The predictive ability of each key characteristic variable in the training cohort was assessed through the ROC curve of univariate logistic regression. The AUC for intraoperative endplate injury was 0.620 (95% CI, 0.565–0.676); for inferior endplate morphology of the surgical segment (ESCA), the AUC was 0.625 (95% CI, 0.552–0.698); for inferior endplate vBMD value, the AUC was 0.747 (95% CI, 0.683–0.810); for DH correction value, the AUC was 0.751 (95% CI, 0.694–0.823); and for inferior vertebrae vBMD value of the surgical segment, the AUC was 0.806 (95% CI, 0.751–0.861). Overall, inferior vertebrae vBMD value and DH correction value demonstrated better predictive capabilities as individual factors, while inferior endplate vBMD value, inferior endplate morphology, and intraoperative endplate injury showed moderate predictive abilities. The ROC curve analysis of the five key characteristic variables in univariate logistic regression is shown in Fig. 1C.

The five key characteristic variables selected through LASSO regression were included in a multivariate logistic regression to further develop the model in the training cohort. The results indicated that the differences between all feature variables were statistically significant and there was no issue of multicollinearity. Notably, we no longer use P-values for variable selection but consider the five selected key characteristic variables as independent risk factors for cage subsidence. The modeling results of univariate and multivariate logistic regression in the training cohort are shown in Table 3, and results of multicollinearity testing based on the variance inflation factor are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Finally, these independent predictive factors were incorporated into a logistic regression model to develop a nomogram for predicting the risk of cage subsidence following OLIF surgery. The scores for the predictive factors included in the nomogram were summed, and a vertical line was drawn at the total score intersecting two coordinates representing the predicted cage subsidence. The intersection points correspond to the linear predictive value and the predicted probability of cage subsidence. The derivation formula and basic principles are detailed in Supplementary Note 2. As shown in Figs. 2A, 3, transforming complex mathematical models into a visual dynamic nomogram clearly illustrates how the risk of cage subsidence post-OLIF surgery gradually increases with the occurrence of intraoperative endplate injury, the increase of DH correction value, as well as decreases in inferior ESCA, inferior vertebrae, and inferior endplate vBMD value of the surgical segment.

Fitting nonlinear relationships based on restricted cubic spline

RCS curves were used to fit the relationship between each independent risk factor and clinical outcomes within a continuous range of changes. To avoid overfitting leading to a decrease in accuracy while ensuring the smoothness of curves and model fitting effectiveness, the optimal number of knots was automatically selected through a data-driven approach using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Additionally, the inflection point of the curve was set by default at the location of the maximum change in the first derivative. As shown in Fig. 2B, there exists a non-linear correlation between DH correction value and the risk of cage subsidence (P = 0.006). When the corrected value of DH during surgery exceeds 2.45 mm, the risk of cage subsidence begins to rapidly increase, while before that, the risk of subsidence is relatively stable. At this point, knots were set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of DH variation value, with the reference value set at the median. Above the inflection point of 2.45 mm, the odds ratio (OR) for DH correction value per unit increase was 3.62 (95% CI, 2.64–4.96).

As shown in Fig. 2C, there exists a non-linear correlation between the inferior endplate morphology of the surgical segment (ESCA) and the risk of cage subsidence (P = 0.022). When the inferior ESCA decreases to 160.0°, the risk of cage subsidence begins to increase rapidly, whereas before this point, the subsidence risk remains relatively stable. At this juncture, knots were set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of inferior ESCA, with the reference value set at the 20th percentile. Below the inflection point of 160.0°, the OR for inferior ESCA per standard deviation decrease was 0.76 (95% CI, 0.49-1.18).

As depicted in Fig. 2D, there exists a non-linear correlation between inferior vertebrae vBMD value and the risk of cage subsidence (P = 0.006). When the vBMD decreases to 119.0 mg/cm3, the risk of cage subsidence starts to increase rapidly. At this point, knots were set at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of vBMD, with the reference value set at the median. Below the inflection point of 119.0 mg/cm3, the OR for inferior vertebrae vBMD value per standard deviation decrease was 0.43 (95% CI, 0.32–0.57). It is noteworthy that when the vBMD value continues to decline to 60.0 mg/cm3, the risk of cage subsidence tends to level off again.

Similarly, as depicted in Fig. 2E, there exists a non-linear correlation between inferior endplate vBMD value and the risk of cage subsidence (P = 0.003). When the vBMD decreases to 204.0 mg/cm3, the risk of cage subsidence starts to increase rapidly, whereas before this point, the subsidence risk remains relatively stable. At this juncture, knots were set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of inferior endplate vBMD value, with the reference value set at the 10th percentile. Below the inflection point of 204.0 mg/cm3, the OR for inferior endplate vBMD value per standard deviation decrease was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.51–0.92).

Validation and evaluation of predictive model based on LASSO-logistic regression

The predictive ability of the final model constructed based on the training cohort was assessed using ROC curves, and the model was validated using the validation cohort. As shown in Fig. 4A, B, the AUC of the predictive model in the training cohort was 0.976 (95% CI, 0.957–0.995), with an AIC of 54.325. The optimal cutoff value was –0.405, with a sensitivity of 0.962, specificity of 0.931, positive predictive value of 0.919, negative predictive value of 0.968, and prediction accuracy of 0.945. The AUC of the predictive model in the validation cohort was 0.972 (95% CI, 0.943–0.992), with an AIC of 54.674. The optimal cutoff value was –0.405, with a sensitivity of 0.917, specificity of 0.906, positive predictive value of 0.898, negative predictive value of 0.923, and prediction accuracy of 0.911. Overall, the final model demonstrated excellent predictive ability for cage subsidence in both the training and validation cohorts, striking a good balance between goodness of fit and complexity.

PR curves were used to visualize changes in precision and recall at different thresholds for the final model. As shown in Fig. 4C, D, the AUC of the predictive model in the training cohort was 0.962, while in the validation cohort, it was 0.959. It is noteworthy that as recall increases, precision generally decreases. However, in both the training and validation cohorts, within a larger threshold range, precision gradually increases or remains constant as recall increases, indicating a good trade-off between precision and recall at different thresholds for the final predictive model.

Utilizing calibration curves to visualize the results of the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and to assess the disparity between predicted probabilities and actual observed outcomes in the model constructed based on LASSO-logistic regression. As depicted in Fig. 4E, F, in the training cohort, when the actual occurrence probability is >0.10, the calibration curve of the model approaches the ideal curve, indicating a high model prediction accuracy. Moreover, it remains effective in the validation cohort, demonstrating high consistency between predicted probabilities and actual occurrence probabilities.

DCA was employed to comprehensively evaluate the clinical utility of the predictive model. As shown in Fig. 4G, H, in the training cohort, when the risk threshold probability ranges from 0.05 to 0.85, the net benefit rate of the DCA curve significantly surpasses the two extreme curves, indicating considerable practical value and clinical applicability of the predictive model. Similarly, in the validation cohort, when the risk threshold probability ranges from 0.07 to 0.85, compared to “all segments with subsidence occurrences” and “all segments without subsidence occurrences,” the predictive model provides maximal net benefit for clinical application.

Fusion rate and clinical symptoms

At the last follow-up, the fusion rate of all surgical segments was 79.82% (269/337), and the difference between the subsidence group and non-subsidence group of the cage was not statistically significant [124 cases (80.52%) vs. 145 cases (79.23%), P = 0.770]. Compared to preoperative values, both the immediate postoperative lumbar back VAS and NRS scores decreased in both the subsidence and non-subsidence groups (P < 0.05). Although the improvement in the non-subsidence group was greater than that in the subsidence group, there was no statistically significant difference in VAS scores/NRS scores between the two groups at preoperative, immediate postoperative, and last follow-up. Clinical symptoms comparison between the two groups is shown in Table 4. In this study, among the 109 patients in the subsidence group, 31 cases (28.44%) experienced mild low back pain during follow-up, but without numbness or radiating pain in the lower limbs. Symptoms were significantly relieved after conservative treatment, and no patients underwent revision surgery. Complications related to 3D navigation during surgery, as well as cases of intraoperative and postoperative internal fixation failure, were excluded during the initial screening of this study. Among the 105 patients in the non-subsidence group, 17 cases (16.19%) had similar conditions as described above. None of the patients in this study experienced neurological symptoms or fusion segment vertebral fractures after surgery.

Discussion

OLIF has emerged as a progressively mature and clinically effective minimally invasive surgical approach29. It facilitates the reconstruction of spinal stability, restoration of intervertebral height, and indirect decompression, while also reducing surgical time, blood loss, and damage to posterior spinal tissues2,3. For suitable candidates, the Stand-alone OLIF (SA OLIF) technique further amplifies these advantages. However, cage subsidence, a common post-OLIF complication, presents a significant clinical challenge7,10,13,30. This adverse outcome, characterized by progressive narrowing of the intervertebral space, can lead to exacerbated postoperative lumbar pain, vertebral instability, and the need for revision surgery, ultimately impacting patients’ quality of life and recovery12. Hence, developing an effective model capable of predicting the risk of this complication is of paramount clinical significance and holds applicability in surgical planning and prognosis management. In this study, we included a total of 337 surgical segments and 674 endplates from 214 patients who underwent OLIF surgery, and constructed and validated a nomogram model for predicting post-OLIF cage subsidence using LASSO-logistic regression. This model comprehensively considers the vertebral bone quality and endplate condition of the operative segments, providing clinicians with a visual and accurate tool for preventing and managing the risk of cage subsidence following OLIF through early intervention. Moreover, the LASSO regression algorithm utilized in this study effectively addresses variable selection issues, selecting the most influential key features from numerous potential predictor variables25,26. This approach not only enhances the predictive accuracy of the model but also simplifies its application27,28, making it more accessible for widespread clinical use. Overall, the model developed in this study enables healthcare professionals to more intuitively and accurately assess the risk of cage subsidence in patients preoperatively, thereby implementing appropriate preventive measures to minimize its incidence. These measures may include selecting more suitable adjunct fixation methods, adjusting surgical techniques, or enhancing perioperative osteoporosis management strategies.

Previous studies5,31–33 have shown that the incidence of postoperative static implant subsidence ranges from 11 to 46.7%. In this study cohort, 109 out of 214 patients (50.93%) and 154 out of 337 surgical segments (45.70%) were found to have imaging evidence of cage subsidence, which is consistent with findings reported by Kotheeranurak et al.33 This relatively high subsidence rate may stem from different measurement methods for subsidence distance and the use of low-threshold subsidence classification criteria. We measured changes in the final follow-up intervertebral space height as the metric, rather than solely focusing on the distance from the cage embedded in the superior or inferior endplate planes. Additionally, we incorporated variables related to intraoperative endplate damage, which similarly contribute to higher subsidence rates. Studies indicate that cage subsidence may lead to aggravated postoperative pain and fusion failure, necessitating revision surgery in severe cases32. Despite the relatively high subsidence rate observed in our study, the fusion rate and clinical outcomes were similar between the sedimentation and non-sedimentation groups, which is consistent with the results reported by Jin et al.2,16 It is noteworthy that among the 154 subsided segments included in this study, only 24 exhibited grade II subsidence, and 3 exhibited grade III subsidence. The high proportion of mild subsidence may have mitigated the severe negative impact of post-fusion clinical outcomes. Therefore, there remains controversy regarding whether mild subsidence results in compromised clinical outcomes.

Postoperative cage subsidence is widely believed to be a complex process influenced by multiple factors, including patient-related, surgical, and implant-related factors34,35. However, prediction models established based on multiple linear regression in various studies are often limited by sample size and interactions among variables, and their predictive outcomes and clinical applicability require further validation. Therefore, in this study, we comprehensively analyzed factors contributing to cage subsidence and effectively employed the LASSO regression algorithm to facilitate variable selection and complexity control. This approach ultimately retained only the key feature variables most relevant to predicting the outcome, rendering the predictive model concise yet accurate. Such a method is particularly suitable for addressing clinical issues characterized by complexity and a multitude of variables. In the end, DH correction value, intraoperative endplate injury, inferior endplate morphology of the surgical segment, inferior vertebrae vBMD value, and inferior endplate vBMD value were identified as possessing optimal predictive capability.

Studies have indicated that excessive cage height or overdistraction of the intervertebral space during surgery can accelerate the occurrence of subsidence9. This may be related to the “distractive-tension band effect” formed by the stretching of surrounding ligaments and contraction of paraspinal muscles. Furthermore, positioning the cage posteriorly with a smaller contact area with the endplate also contributes to uneven local stress distribution in the fused segment18, ultimately leading to endplate fractures and cage subsidence. Additionally, overcorrection of SLA or LLA may lead to stress concentration at the posterior part of the cage where it contacts the endplate, causing localized endplate damage and collapse under load, which in severe cases can result in rotational displacement of the cage. It is noteworthy that in this study, the intraoperative DH correction values in the subsidence group were significantly higher than those in the non-subsidence group, and the RCS model fitting indicated that the risk of cage subsidence begins to increase rapidly when the change in DH exceeds 2.45 mm. However, despite the smaller cage size, more posterior placement, and greater correction magnitudes of SLA and LLA in the subsidence group compared to the non-subsidence group, these differences were not statistically significant, consistent with reports by Wu et al.9 and Chen et al.22. This may be related to differences in the study populations and sample sizes. Nevertheless, we still recommend the cautious selection of the appropriate cage size through intraoperative trialing, based on preoperative sagittal measurements, slowly restoring intervertebral disc height, and correcting SLA, to accurately place the cage across the apophyseal ring and lay it flat on the endplate.

Previous studies36,37 have indicated that the probability of intraoperative endplate injury ranges from 16.8% to 33.1%, exerting a direct impact on the fixation of implants and intervertebral fusion stability. Similarly, our study observed a comparable phenomenon, with a significantly higher incidence of intraoperative endplate injury observed in the subsidence group compared to the non-subsidence group, consistent with findings reported by Chen et al.22 It is evident that intraoperative endplate injury, due to direct contact between the bony endplate and the implant, may result in incomplete contact or loosening between the implant and the endplate, thereby facilitating gradual displacement or subsidence of the implant. Furthermore, local bone destruction and absorption resulting from endplate injury will further weaken endplate strength and support stress, increasing the risk of implant subsidence. Interestingly, at the onset of this study, we conducted a statistical analysis of pre-existing endplate damage. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the two groups [14 cases (9.09%) vs. 18 cases (9.83%), P = 0.816]. Therefore, we speculate that mild Schmorl’s nodes (with vertebral body height involvement <20%) and localized Modic changes in the bony endplate, which cause sclerosis at the margins, may prevent substantial impact on the endplate during the chronic progressive phase. According to findings by Huang et al.38, endplate morphology is also a significant predictor of post-OLIF implant subsidence, although it may not emerge as an independent risk factor in multivariable regression analysis. Results from studies by Hu et al.30 and Chen et al.22 concur, demonstrating that endplate morphology is indeed a risk factor for implant subsidence. The former found that concave endplates are more prone to implant subsidence than flat endplates, while the latter measured the endplate concavity angle and determined the optimal cutoff value to be 176.9° through analysis. In our study, we represented endplate morphology using the Endplate Shape Contour Angle (ESCA) and fitted the relationship between ESCA and the rate of implant subsidence using restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves over a continuous range. It is noteworthy that only the morphology of the inferior endplate of the operative segment was selected as a key feature variable, and the risk of implant subsidence began to increase rapidly when the ESCA of the inferior endplate decreased to 160.0°, remaining relatively stable before this threshold. This suggests that more concave endplate morphology cannot provide a larger contact area for the implant, significantly increasing the risk of subsidence. Importantly, we have for the first time discovered that this relationship is not linear; when the inferior endplate ESCA exceeds 160.0°, implant restabilization may occur instead.

BMD and osteoporosis are among the primary risk factors for implant subsidence, potentially offering higher predictive value compared to endplate injury, endplate morphology, and implant size10,16–18. Therefore, accurate preoperative assessment of overall bone density and bone quality in the segments undergoing fusion surgery is crucial for patients planning lumbar fusion procedures. In recent years, there has been increasing attention on the value of QCT in BMD assessment and early prevention of osteoporosis, with clinical application guidelines and osteoporosis diagnostic criteria established by the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) and the American College of Radiology (ACR)39. QCT avoids the impact of vertebral osteophytes, bone bridging, and calcification of the aortic wall, and surrounding structures, enabling accurate assessment of vBMD within specific regions. Research suggests its sensitivity in detecting osteoporosis may surpass that of DXA technology23,24. Studies have indicated that the metabolic activity of trabecular bone in the spinal vertebrae is approximately 8 times higher than that of cortical bone40, making detection targeting trabecular bone more sensitive to real changes in BMD. Additionally, according to research by Okano et al.41, endplate density can reliably predict vertebral fractures and postoperative implant subsidence. Thus, QCT enables comprehensive evaluation of bone quality in segments slated for fusion by precisely measuring target vertebral density and endplate strength, facilitating preoperative planning and surgical decision-making. Furthermore, since patients planning OLIF surgery typically undergo routine lumbar CT scans preoperatively, QCT, based on image post-processing technology, spares patients from additional radiation exposure and increased testing costs. However, there is currently a lack of studies utilizing QCT to assess vertebral and endplate bone quality and explore its relationship with the incidence of post-OLIF implant subsidence.

Our study utilized both DXA and QCT for BMD testing according to guideline standards. The results revealed that, in the pre-screening univariate analysis, although the subsidence group exhibited lower overall lumbar spine average T-scores compared to the non-subsidence group, these differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that DXA bone mineral density indicators may not possess robust predictive capabilities. Consistent with multiple research findings20,38,42, the repeatability and stability of DXA T-scores as predictive factors still require further validation. Conversely, the statistical significance of differences in QCT BMD indicators between the two groups was more reliable, and they were further selected as key feature variables by LASSO, including inferior vertebrae vBMD value and inferior endplate vBMD value. It is noteworthy that there is a strong correlation between QCT-based BMD measurements and overall lumbar spine average values, which were not included in the final model to ensure the stability of the predictive model.

Through fitting RCS curves, it was found that when the inferior vertebrae vBMD value of the surgical segment decreased to 119.0 mg/cm3, the risk of implant subsidence began to increase rapidly, and when the vBMD value continued to decrease to 60.0 mg/cm3, the risk of implant subsidence tended to plateau again. This contrasts with the simple linear correlation between vertebral BMD and implant subsidence risk assumed by many studies. Based on this, we reasonably speculate that this nonlinear change may be due to the fact that the bone endplate density is higher than the trabecular bone density of the vertebra. When the latter decreases to a certain extent, the risk of implant subsidence mainly depends on the strength of the endplate. However, at this point, there is an increased likelihood of direct endplate fracture and implant flip displacement, especially for endplates that are already damaged or irregularly shaped. Additionally, the segmental inferior endplate vBMD value also exhibits a nonlinear correlation with implant subsidence risk, with a significant increase in risk occurring when it decreases to 204.0 mg/cm3. Overall, the findings of the above results benefit from QCT’s sensitivity to high-turnover trabecular bone detection and selectivity in measuring specific sites, highlighting the significant value of QCT-based vertebral bone quality assessment and endplate condition in predicting complications following OLIF surgery.

In this study, we incorporated five key feature variables selected by the LASSO regression algorithm into a multivariable logistic regression to further build a model in the training cohort and develop a nomogram model to predict implant subsidence. This predictive model integrates different independent risk factors to quantify the risk of clinical outcomes, aiding healthcare providers in devising individualized treatment plans and adjusting postoperative management strategies based on specific patient risks. The visual nomogram model intuitively demonstrates that with the occurrence of intraoperative endplate injury, the increase of DH correction value, and the decrease in inferior ESCA, inferior vertebrae, and inferior endplate vBMD value of the surgical segment, the risk of implant subsidence after OLIF gradually increases. Narrower predictive probability values may stem from the nonlinear relationship between risk factors and subsidence risk, as indicated by RCS curves suggesting stable linear correlations within certain ranges for each risk factor. Overall, the predictive model constructed based on LASSO-logistic regression significantly improved the AUC from the lowest 0.620 with a single characteristic variable to 0.976 in the training cohort and 0.972 in the internal validation cohort. It exhibited excellent predictive ability for cage subsidence in both the training and validation cohorts, with a good balance between goodness of fit and complexity. Furthermore, the comprehensive evaluation of the final model indicated a good trade-off between precision and recall at different thresholds, with high consistency between predicted probabilities and actual occurrence probabilities in both the training and validation cohorts. DCA curves suggested that the predictive model demonstrated good clinical utility and interpretability at a wide range of threshold probabilities, which is crucial for achieving personalized precision medicine and enhancing the quality of clinical decision-making.

Based on these findings, when planning OLIF for lumbar degenerative diseases, we recommend utilizing QCT to assess the density of the segmental vertebrae and endplates as a routine method for preoperative bone quality assessment. Through personalized and precise prediction, we suggest selecting adjacent vertebrae with a vBMD value greater than 119.0 mg/cm3 and corresponding endplates with a vBMD value greater than 204.0 mg/cm3 for the fusion segment. Simultaneously, in the routine preoperative lumbar spine radiography for endplate morphology measurement, particular attention should be paid to endplates with an ESCA less than 160.0°, and intraoperative endplate injury and excessive intervertebral space expansion should be avoided as much as possible. For patients predicted to be at high risk of subsidence preoperatively, although studies by Wu et al.10 and He et al.13 have shown no significant difference in subsidence rates between OLIF with additional pedicle screw fixation and SA OLIF, we still recommend selecting appropriate additional fixation methods intraoperatively or reinforcing the vertebral body and endplates with bone cement injection43. Additionally, the perioperative use of anti-osteoporosis medications, such as bisphosphonates or parathyroid hormone analogs44, may have a positive effect on maintaining vertebral bone mass and improving the stability of internal fixation shortly after surgery. Overall, combining preoperative prediction with preventive strategies can effectively reduce the risk of postoperative implant subsidence and optimize patients’ clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the study cohort was based on single-center patients, and thus the developed and evaluated predictive model may not represent a broader population and may not universally represent physicians at the level of surgical techniques and management strategies. Moreover, as a real-world retrospective study, the design characteristics may have influenced the clinical outcomes during the follow-up process. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct multicenter studies or prospective cohort studies to further validate the predictive model constructed in this study. Secondly, the sample size of this study is relatively small, and it aimed to select relevant variables for screening surgical fusion segments, which may overlook potential confounding factors. Future research with larger sample sizes and external validation cohorts is needed to improve the predictive and generalization abilities of the model. Finally, while deep learning and other machine learning techniques may have greater modeling potential, their performance is constrained by data scale and characteristics. Within the sample size range of this study, the deep learning model developed in the preliminary stage did not show a significant advantage over the currently employed predictive model. Nevertheless, this study comprehensively analyzed the factors leading to implant subsidence after OLIF. More importantly, for the first time, a reliable LASSO regression algorithm was used to select key feature variables and construct an effective predictive model, confirming its strong predictive ability and wide clinical applicability. Among them, comprehensive assessment of bone quality in the planned surgical segments based on QCT significantly improved the accuracy of preoperative individualized risk assessment, thereby providing an important basis for early prevention strategies. In the future, with further research into local microanatomy and biomechanics, as well as a deeper understanding of the impact of the vertebral microenvironment and implant stability, integrating new predictive factors or exploring new biomarkers may significantly enhance the predictive performance of the nomogram model, providing a more solid scientific foundation for personalized treatment of spinal fusion surgery.

OLIF demonstrates unique advantages and good efficacy in treating degenerative lumbar diseases; however, postoperative implant subsidence is relatively common. This study developed a novel nomogram predictive model based on LASSO-logistic regression to assess the risk of implant subsidence after OLIF. The model confirmed that QCT vertebral bone quality assessment combined with endplate status analysis enables dynamic, continuous, and personalized accurate predictions. Ultimately, validation of this model demonstrated excellent predictive performance and significant clinical utility, providing guidance for preoperative planning, adjustment of preventive strategies, and optimization of clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patient data

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical and imaging data of 905 patients who underwent planned OLIF surgeries at a single institution between May 2017 and May 2024. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Hospital, Southern Medical University (Approval No.: NYSZYYEC2024K036R001) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Unique Identifying Number or Registration ID: ChiCTR2000030692). As a low-risk study in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, the same ethics committee (Shenzhen Hospital, Southern Medical University) waived the requirement for informed consent from patients. All methods were performed in compliance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The reporting of this work adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort Studies in Surgery (STROCSS) guidelines for cohort, cross-sectional, and case-control studies45.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with symptomatic lumbar degenerative diseases unresponsive to conservative treatment for three months, including discogenic lower back pain, mild to moderate lumbar spinal stenosis, grade I to II lumbar spondylolisthesis, and lumbar instability; (2) degenerative lumbar scoliosis with Cobb’s angle <30°, rotation <3°, and lateral slip <5 mm; (3) degenerative changes primarily confined to one or two lumbar segments with no or only mild degenerative changes in adjacent segments; (4) suitability for OLIF surgery with preoperative indirect decompression expected to alleviate neurological symptoms; (5) follow-up duration >3 months with complete clinical and imaging data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) radicular pain, severe disc herniation/extrusion, severe/bony lumbar spinal stenosis, grade II or higher lumbar spondylolisthesis, severe scoliosis; (2) tumor, infection, or trauma-induced lumbar instability; (3) intraoperative injection of bone cement to reinforce the screw path; (4) spontaneous fusion of facet joints/lumbar zygapophyseal joints; (5) history of abdominal, retroperitoneal, or lumbar region surgery; (6) complications related to intraoperative 3D navigation included poor screw placement, incorrect intervertebral space positioning, incorrect cage placement, and vertebral fractures necessitating secondary revision surgery; (7) intraoperative internal fixation failure, including pedicle burst and dural tear; (8) postoperative internal fixation failure, including screw loosening/breakage, cerebrospinal fluid leak, intervertebral disc infection, deep infection, incisional hematoma, and nerve root irritation symptoms.

Overall, apart from the aforementioned surgical contraindications and exclusion criteria, this study excluded 691 patients due to loss of follow-up, elective surgery abandonment, change of surgical methods, and missing clinical/imaging data. The final study cohort included 214 patients, encompassing 337 surgical segments and 674 endplates, with 63 males (29.44%) and 151 females (70.56%). Ages ranged from 42 to 90 years, with an average of 64.79 ± 10.18 years. The follow-up period ranged from 3.3 to 45.0 months, averaging 13.57 ± 9.77 months. Preoperative diagnoses included lumbar spinal stenosis (128 cases, 59.81%), discogenic lower back pain (56 cases, 26.17%), degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (17 cases, 7.94%), and degenerative lumbar scoliosis (13 cases, 6.07%). The surgical fusion involved single segments in 98 cases (45.79%), double segments in 109 cases (50.93%), and triple segments in 7 cases (3.27%). Surgical technique

All OLIF procedures were continuously performed at the same institution by a team of experienced spine surgeons following guideline standards. Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned in the right lateral decubitus position. A 4–6 cm longitudinal incision was made 5 cm anterior to the midpoint of the targeted intervertebral disc. Initially, blunt dissection was performed on the abdominal muscles. Subsequently, the retroperitoneal space was swept with the index finger to allow direct visualization and separation of nerves, blood vessels, and peritoneum. Expanders and a working sleeve were sequentially inserted to establish a surgical corridor. The annulus fibrosus was incised, intervertebral disc material and cartilaginous endplates were completely removed, and the endplates were prepared until bleeding occurred, including disruption of the contralateral annulus fibrosus. After trial fitting, an interbody cage (Clydesdale Spinal System, Medtronic Sofamor Danek, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) filled with allogeneic freeze-dried bone (Allogeneic Bone Repair Materials, Kejian Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and a resorbable bone graft substitute (Resorbable Bone Graft Substitute, NovaBone Products, LLC., Alachua, FL, USA) was positioned optimally.

If lateral plate fixation was added, appropriate-sized dual-hole plates and head/tail screws (PIVOXTM Oblique Lateral Spinal System, Medtronic Sofamor Danek, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) were inserted after trial fitting for anterolateral interbody fixation. If posterior internal fixation was combined, the abdominal incision was closed, the patient’s position was changed, and a 0.5 cm longitudinal incision was made over the targeted pedicle. A puncture needle and guidewire were inserted along the vertebral pedicle, followed by the placement of a polyaxial pedicle screw (spinal fixation system, Double Medical Technology Inc., Xiamen, Fujian, China). A pre-bent connecting rod of appropriate length was percutaneously inserted, and the nuts were tightened for pedicle screw fixation. The surgical site was repeatedly irrigated, and the incision was closed layer by layer.

Postoperatively, patients received routine antibiotics to prevent infection and oral neurotrophic medications. On the second postoperative day, patients wore a lumbar brace for protection and began ambulating as appropriate. Immediate postoperative evaluations (within one week) included anteroposterior, lateral, and dynamic lumbar X-rays and CT scans to assess the position of the cage and intervertebral fusion. Regular outpatient follow-ups reevaluated these measures and periodically recorded clinical symptom-related scoring scales.

Data collection and radiographic assessment

Retrospective collection of patient baseline clinical data and perioperative parameters included gender, age, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, long-term history of anti-osteoporosis and steroid drug use, primary diagnosis, follow-up duration, surgical fusion segments, surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative incision complications, and the size and position of the interbody cage. The placement of the cage between the vertebrae was measured using the center point ratio (CPR)46, defined as the distance from the midpoint of the cage to the posterior edge of the inferior endplate of operative segment, divided by the length of that endplate as a percentage (Fig. 6E). A CPR greater than 50% indicated an anterior placement of the cage during surgery, and vice versa. Clinical symptom assessments included preoperative, immediate postoperative, and last follow-up visual analog scale (VAS) and numeric rating scale (NRS) scores.

All patients underwent lumbar digital radiography (DR) and multi-detector computed tomography (MDCT, General Electric Healthcare, Boston, MA, USA) before surgery, immediately after, and at the last follow-up. Imaging data were collected through the institutional picture archiving and communication system (PACS), including anteroposterior, lateral, and dynamic position lumbar X-rays, and thin-layer CT scans, and were downloaded and stored in DICOM format.

The clinical outcome of the study was the occurrence of cage subsidence after OLIF, defined as the loss of disc height (DH) due to the insertion/breakthrough of the cage near the endplate plane at the last follow-up compared to the immediate postoperative position. According to the classification suggested by Marchi et al.31, the fusion segments were divided into subsidence and non-subsidence groups, categorizing cage subsidence into four grades based on the percentage of DH loss: Grade 0 (0–24% DH decrease), Grade I (25–49% DH decrease), Grade II (50–74% DH decrease), and Grade III (75–100% DH decrease). Grade 0 was considered a restabilization phase of the cage (Fig. 6A), while Grades I, II, and III were defined as subsidence17,42 (Fig. 6B–D). DH was defined as the average of the anterior and posterior heights of the target intervertebral space (Fig. 6F), the DH correction value referred to the difference between immediate postoperative and preoperative DH, and the DH loss value referred to the difference between the last follow-up and immediate postoperative DH. The segmental lordosis angle (SLA) was represented by the angle between the superior endplate of the upper vertebra and the inferior endplate of the lower vertebra of the fusion segment (Fig. 6J), and the lumbar lordosis angle (LLA) was represented by the angle between the upper endplates of L1 and S146 (Fig. 6I). SLA/LLA correction values referred to the difference between the immediate postoperative and preoperative SLA/LLA values. Apart from the clearly defined vertebrae/endplate, the one located above the cage in this study was defined as the superior vertebrae/endplate of the surgical segment, and vice versa. Endplate-related factors include morphology, density, and damage of the endplate. Endplate morphology is represented by the endplate sagittal concavity angle (ESCA)18,22, which is defined as the angle formed by the line connecting the anterior and posterior edges of the endplate to the deepest point in the sagittal view (Fig. 6F). If the endplate is irregular, the obtuse angle formed between the most convex and most concave points is determined. There is a negative correlation between ESCA and the degree of endplate concavity; a larger ESCA indicates a flatter vertebral endplate. The literature review reveals ongoing disputes regarding the naming, definition, and classification of endplate damage, with no consensus to date. For cases with severe bone endplate rupture, the nucleus pulposus embedding in the vertebral body, and diffuse Modic changes that already existed before surgery, they were excluded before inclusion in this study. In this study, intraoperative endplate injury is defined as a cortical disruption of bony continuity found immediately post-surgery (Fig. 6G). At the last follow-up, lumbar fusion was assessed using anteroposterior, lateral and dynamic position lumbar spine X-rays, considering a non-fusion status if the intervertebral mobility was greater than 5 degrees and/or displacement was greater than 3 mm47. Bone fusion was assessed using thin-layer CT, where bone trabeculae growth into and connection with adjacent endplates indicated bone fusion if the intervertebral space was blurry without a clear lucency band20,48 (Fig. 6H).

The aforementioned imaging parameters were measured using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer, Version 2023.1 (RadiAnt DICOM Viewer Software, Medixant, Poznan, Poland), primarily using plain lumbar films. In cases of dispute, multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) tools were used to assess the sagittal/coronal images of lumbar CT. All parameters were independently measured by two researchers with at least five years of radiographic training, under the supervision of an experienced spinal surgeon. Independent blinded evaluations were conducted to determine the degree of cage subsidence and the state of intervertebral fusion, with consensus reached through consultation in case of any disputes. Additionally, the inter-rater reliability was assessed and the mean values of the measurement parameters were recorded.

Bone mineral density assessment

This study utilized MDCT which operated under standard scanning conditions preset by the system. The scanning technical parameters included a voltage of 120 kV and a current of 250 mA, employing spiral scanning to acquire thin-layer volumetric data at slice thicknesses of 1.250 mm and 0.625 mm. The collimation width was set at 40.00 mm, the scan field of view at 500.00 mm, and the display field of view at 250.00 mm, with the table height initialized at 125.00 cm. The data were reconstructed using standard body reconstruction and MPR techniques. Adjustments to scanning parameters were permitted for radiological technicians based on individual patient body types to ensure image quality, alongside routine quality control checks on the CT machine, calibration phantoms, and associated workstations following the operational guidelines39. This approach adhered to clinical consensus on QCT and peripheral QCT bone density measurements for osteoporosis diagnosis, proposed by the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) in 200749, the American College of Radiology (ACR) guidelines on QCT applications from 2008 and their revised version in 201339, and recommendations from the Chinese guidelines on QCT for osteoporosis diagnosis, defining individual QCT bone density measurements as the average of trabecular bone density at the L1 and L2 vertebrae. Lumbar QCT absolute values were used for diagnosis: BMD > 120 mg/cm³ indicates normal bone mass, 80–120 mg/cm³ indicates low bone mass, and BMD < 80 mg/cm³ indicates osteoporosis. To thoroughly assess overall lumbar spine bone mass and avoid errors caused by severe local vertebral degradation or abnormalities, QCT bone mineral density measurements were conducted on the entire lumbar spine and compared with DXA, represented by the average values from L1 to L5. QCT assessments of vertebral bone quality included the density of vertebral bodies and the strength of adjacent endplates within the surgical fusion segments. Among them, the vBMD values of the superior vertebrae/endplate were indicated as those of the superior vertebrae/endplate of the surgical segment, and vice versa.

Pre-surgical imaging data were collected through the institutional PACS system, including thin-layer CT images in standard and bone windows, and stored in DICOM format. The QCT bone density parameters were all obtained through the analysis of saved volume data images using bone density computer-aided detection software, Version 1.0 (Bone’s FRAX, Bone’s Technology Limited, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). This method is grounded in the fact that the metabolic activity of spinal vertebral trabecular bone is approximately eight times that of cortical bone40, and QCT 3D technology allows for separate assessment of these, hence the delineated trabecular bone regions in QCT images are unaffected by spinal degenerative changes and calcification in surrounding tissues23,24,49,50. Using lateral lumbar positioning images to determine any vertebrae to be excluded, semi-automated analysis was performed at the QCT workstation using the region of interest (ROI) drawing tool by adjusting axial, coronal, and sagittal images to precisely select the trabecular bone ROI in the middle layer of the vertebrae. According to guideline recommendations, the ROI should include as much of the vertebral trabecular bone as possible while avoiding bilateral central endplates, peripheral cortical bone, posterior vertebral venous plexus, and areas of bone abnormality (Fig. 7A). Adjustments in the coronal and sagittal images also allowed for analysis of the bony endplate by fine-tuning the 3D ROI position and thickness to include the central endplate and apophyseal ring, avoiding areas of bone proliferation, defects, and Schmorl’s nodes (Fig. 7B). Given the body type variation in a subset of patients, researchers were allowed to manually adjust specific calibration regions for muscle and fat at the QCT workstation to perform synchronous autologous model auto-calibration. Likewise, all QCT bone density parameters were independently tested under the supervision of a spinal surgeon by two researchers trained in radiological imaging and QCT operations, with assessments of inter-rater reliability.

Furthermore, the study also employed a DXA system (Hologic Discovery CI, Hologic, Inc., Boston, MA, USA), which conducted continuous fan-beam scans under standard settings. Initial scan parameters included a tube voltage of 140 kV with a tube current mean of 5 mA during 50% duty cycles. Radiological technicians were allowed to adjust scan parameters based on individual patient differences, with routine quality control performed on the internal calibration systems and specialized phantoms according to the guidelines. According to the Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Primary Osteoporosis developed by the Osteoporosis and Bone Mineral Disease Branch of the Chinese Medical Association in 2017 and the revised version in 2022, individual DXA bone density measurements are defined as the lowest T-values in the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip. WHO recommended diagnostic standards were applied: T-score ≥ -1.0 indicates normal bone density, –2.5 < T-score < -1.0 indicates decreased bone mass, and T-score ≤ –2.5 indicates osteoporosis51. Overall lumbar spine DXA bone mineral density measurements were represented by the average T-score from L1 to L4. Preoperative DXA results, including BMD and T-score values for L1 to L4 vertebrae, the left hip joint, and the femoral neck, were collected using the workstation. It is worth noting that preoperative lumbar spine DR, CT scans, and DXA bone mineral density testing were conducted concurrently.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data visualization were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R, Version 4.2.2 (R software, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A pvalue of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In univariate analysis, categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentage), and comparisons were made using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, with normally distributed variables presented as mean ± standard deviation; comparisons between groups involved independent sample ttests. Among them, the F-test was used to evaluate the homogeneity of variance of continuous variables, and Welch’s correction was used for inter-group comparisons that do not conform to the F-distribution. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range), with comparisons made using the Mann–Whitney U test. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were used to evaluate the reliability of imaging parameters and BMD indices between evaluators, with ICC < 0.40 considered poor, 0.40–0.75 as fair, and ICC > 0.75 as good52.

Through PASS, Version 15.0 (Power Analysis and Sample Size Software, NCSS Statistical Software, Inc., UT, USA) was used for sample size estimation. The results show that to ensure sufficient statistical power for the model, the estimated sample size requirement based on specific criteria is 257 surgical segments, including 77 segments with outcome events. In this study, all surgical segments of patients were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. Variables were compared based on the results of univariate analysis. Within the training cohort, multivariate analysis was performed using the LASSO regression algorithm27,28,53, which incorporated tenfold cross-validation and introduced L1-norm regularization to obtain the feature variables where the optimal penalty parameter Lambda value was located. Based on this, selected variables were included in a multifactorial logistic regression model to construct nomograms for predicting clinical outcomes. Moreover, this study did not involve the conversion between continuous and categorical variables; instead, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to describe the nonlinear relationships between continuous feature variables and clinical outcomes. The effectiveness and reliability of the nomogram prediction model were evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), the balance of the precision-recall (PR) curve, calibration of the calibration curve, and the net benefit thresholds of decision curve analysis (DCA) to test the clinical applicability of the model.
