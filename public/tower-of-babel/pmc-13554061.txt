Natural variation in BRN1 enhances nitrogen sensitivity to improve rice nitrogen use efficiency
Authors: Huwei Sun, Yake Chen, Yibo Hu, Hanyun Wang, Wenjun Zhu, Yuqing Liu, Ningning Ren, Bin Hu, Chengcai Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen Revolution rice varieties deliver high yields but require excessive nitrogen (N) fertilizer and show diminished N responsiveness, severely reducing nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE). To dissect the molecular basis of low N sensitivity in modern cultivars, we conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for biomass response to N (BRN), a trait tightly linked to N sensitivity, using a diverse rice germplasm panel. We identified BRN1 as a key regulator of N-dependent biomass accumulation that regulates NLP3, a master transcription factor governing nitrate signaling. Under elevated N supply, the strigolactone signaling repressor D53 accumulates substantially and interacts with BRN1 to repress NLP3 transcription, thereby reducing rice N response. Notably, the high-response BRN1H allele encodes a more stable protein that alleviates D53-mediated suppression. Introgression of this allele into modern cultivars significantly enhanced N sensitivity and grain yield under both low and high N conditions. Our findings establish a D53-BRN1-NLP3 regulatory module controlling rice NUE, providing a target for rice breeding to sustain high productivity with improved resource sustainability.Subject terms: Natural variation in plants, Plant physiology

Introduction

The agricultural Green Revolution of the 1960s transformed global crop production, driving large yield gains in wheat and rice that alleviated food insecurity for a rapidly growing human population. A key driver of this success was the widespread adoption of semi-dwarf Green Revolution varieties (GR varieties)1,2, which show improved lodging resistance, a trait partly linked to their reduced sensitivity to nitrogen (N) fertilization3,4. However, this reduced N responsiveness incurs a critical trade-off: it drastically lowers N-use efficiency (NUE), forcing GR varieties to rely on excessive N fertilizer inputs to achieve maximal yield. Overreliance on N fertilizer has caused widespread resource waste and severe environmental damage, including soil acidification, water eutrophication, and greenhouse-gas emissions5. Unraveling the molecular mechanisms governing N sensitivity in modern high-yielding rice is therefore essential for improving NUE, reducing N fertilizer dependency, and advancing sustainable agriculture.

Central to the Green Revolution’s success are DELLA proteins, key repressors of gibberellin (GA) signaling. GA promotes plant growth by triggering DELLA degradation6,7. In rice, the semi-dwarf phenotype of GR varieties is primarily conferred by the mutant sd1 allele, which reduces GA biosynthesis8 and promotes accumulation of the DELLA protein SLR19. The accumulation of DELLA characteristics of GR varieties confers yield-enhancing dwarfism but also reduces N sensitivity in rice1,2,6. In recent years, a series of studies centered on the GA pathway have opened new avenues for tackling the challenge of low NUE in GR varieties9,10. SLR1 accumulation modulates N sensitivity by repressing the transcriptional activity of GRF4—a master regulator of N metabolism that globally activates N-responsive genes, thereby limiting NUE and grain yield9. Another key regulator of N-dependent growth is NGR5, which positively controls N-induced tillering. Although the GA receptor GID1 targets NGR5 for proteasomal degradation, SLR1 competitively inhibits this interaction, thereby accounting for the increased tillering in GR varieties10. Introducing superior alleles of NGR5 and GRF4 into GR varieties significantly enhances grain yield, with particularly pronounced benefits under low N supply, offering a promising breeding strategy for improving NUE9,10.

Beyond the GA pathway, genetic selection during the Green Revolution also favored a partial loss-of-function allele of HTD1 (D17), a gene involved in strigolactone (SL) biosynthesis, highlighting the coordinated selection of GA and SL signaling components to shape GR variety traits11. SLs have emerged as pivotal regulators of plant adaptation to N availability and are increasingly recognized as targets for improving crop NUE12–16. A well-documented hallmark of SL signaling in rice is the N-dependent accumulation of D53, a central repressor of SL signaling: as N supply increases, D53 protein levels rise significantly in rice roots13,17–19. This excess D53 interacts with GRF4, impairing its DNA-binding activity, and thereby reducing N utilization15. Shifting the D53-GRF4 balance towards higher GRF4 abundance enhances both NUE and grain yield, underscoring the potential of co-modulating plant development and NUE for next-generation sustainable breeding15. Identifying additional N-responsive transcription factors targeted by D53 thus represents a promising strategy to further improve N sensitivity and NUE in rice. To date, the potential of the SL pathway for enhancing crop NUE remains underexplored.

Despite their high yield potential, GR varieties require massive N inputs due to inherently low N sensitivity9. Enhancing N sensitivity to maximum NUE under reduced N fertilization is a top priority for sustainable agriculture and crop breeding. NUE is a complex trait encompassing N uptake, transportation, assimilation, remobilization, and their integration into growth and development20–32. Previous association analyses of N-responsive yield components revealed a strong correlation between tillering response to N (TRN) and NUE in rice, leading to the cloning of the N-efficient gene OsTCP1926 and the subsequent identification of a functionally analogous gene OsGATA833. Biomass accumulation is fundamental to agronomic yield and serves as an integrated readout of a crop’s N utilization capacity. The biomass response to N (BRN) precedes responses of yield components and arises at earlier developmental stages. Biomass is highly sensitive: plant biomass rapidly adjusts to changes in N supply, making BRN a foundational trait for studying NUE. Therefore, BRN provides a rapid approach for identifying N-sensitive genes, yet this approach remains underused.

Here we used a genome-wide association study (GWAS) in a genetically diverse rice panel to identify BRN1, a gene controlling BRN. BRN1 encodes a transcription factor that physically interacts with D53, thereby repressing transcription of NLP3—a core regulator of the nitrate signaling pathway. Overexpression of BRN1 or its elite allelic variation (BRN1H)—which resists D53-mediated repression—significantly increased rice biomass and grain yield under both low N (LN) and high N (HN) conditions, demonstrating that BRN1H is a key regulator of N sensitivity and NUE. By uncovering the molecular basis of low N sensitivity in GR varieties, our findings provide a rational framework for developing high-yield, high-NUE rice varieties, filling a critical gap in sustainable agricultural production.

Results

BRN1 is a positive regulator of N response in rice

Given that biomass accumulation is a direct indicator of plant N sensitivity, we first validated the impact of N supply on rice (Zhonghua 11, ZH11) growth with low N (LN, 0.1 mM NH4NO3) and high N (HN, 1 mM NH4NO3) for 12 days. Under HN conditions, rice seedling biomass increased by 31.3% compared to LN (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c), confirming that N availability directly modulates rice biomass. To identify the key genes governing N sensitivity, we selected 179 rice accessions that have a similar germination rate from the rice Mini-Core Collection34 under LN and HN conditions for 12 days. These accessions are mainly landraces that were collected from 71 countries or regions and that cover all rice subgroups, including 25 tropical japonica (TRJ), 28 temperate japonica (TEJ), 35 aus (AUS), 61 indica (IND), 3 aromatic (ARO) and 27 admixed (ADM) accessions (Fig. 1a). We evaluated the biomass response to N (BRN) under LN and HN conditions. The biomass displayed a significant positive response to increasing N from LN to HN for the 96.6% of varieties in the population, with the magnitude ranging from 0 to 2.7fold. The BRN was further calculated with the value of (HN dry weight – LN dry weight)/LN dry weight (Fig. 1b), and BRN exhibited significant variation among different varieties (Supplementary Fig. 1d); this indicated that BRN might be an ideal index for phenotyping N sensitivity to carry out a GWAS.

GWAS using a mixed linear model identified a major association peak on chromosome 11, with the top single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP; Chr11: 28880164) highly linked to BRN (Fig. 1c). Linkage disequilibrium analysis narrowed this peak to an approximately 40-kb block spanned six candidate genes (LOC_Os11g47870, LOC_Os11g47890, LOC_Os11g47900, LOC_Os11g47910, LOC_Os11g47920, and LOC_Os11g47930). Transcript profiling of these candidates revealed that LOC_Os11g47920 (designated BRN1, previously named DHD135) exhibited the most pronounced N-induced upregulation in roots (Fig. 1d). Haplotype analysis of BRN1 in the Mini-Core Collection grouped accessions into two common alleles: BRN1H (high-BRN) varieties and BRN1L (low-BRN) varieties. Accessions carrying BRN1ᴴ showed significantly higher N sensitivity than those with BRN1ᴸ (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. 1e), indicating BRN1 as the causal gene for N-modulated biomass.

BRN1 encodes a GRAS (GAl, RGA, SCR) family transcription factor. Subcellular localization and expression analysis showed BRN1 is predominantly nuclear-localized and is highly expressed in roots and leaves (Fig. 2a, b). BRN1 transcript levels were higher under HN than LN conditions (Fig. 2c, d). To validate its function, we generated three brn1 knockout (KO) lines (KO1, KO2, and KO3) via CRISPR/Cas9 and three constitutive overexpression (OE) lines (OE3, OE6, and OE7) in ZH11 (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). At the seedling stage, KO lines had significantly lower dry weight and 15N accumulation than ZH11 under HN conditions; OE lines showed higher dry weight and 15N accumulation than ZH11 under both LN and HN conditions (Fig. 2e–h). Field experiment confirmed these trends: KO lines had significantly reduced tiller number, dry weight (including grain weight), and grain yield per plant under HN conditions, while OE lines showed significant increases in both traits under LN and HN conditions (Fig. 2i–l and Supplementary Fig. 2c–f).

BRN1 controls biomass and N utilization via modulating NLP3 transcription

To elucidate BRN1-mediated regulation, we used chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) to identify its target genes. ChIP‑seq and ChIP‑qPCR results revealed that the nitrate signaling core transcription factor NIN-LIKE PROTEIN 3 (NLP3)36,37 as a potential target (Fig. 3a, b). Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assays confirmed direct binding of BRN1 to the NLP3 promoter (Fig. 3c, d). A transcriptional activation assay in rice protoplasts showed BRN1 significantly enhanced luciferase (LUC) reporter activity driven by the NLP3 promoter (Fig. 3e). qRT-PCR analysis further showed NLP3 transcript levels were higher in OE lines and lower in KO lines than in ZH11 under both LN and HN conditions (Fig. 3f). To genetically verify the regulatory relationship between BRN1 and NLP3, we crossed nlp336 and brn1 single mutants to generate brn1 nlp3 double mutants (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Subsequently, ZH11, nlp3, brn1, and brn1 nlp3 double mutant lines were grown under both LN and HN conditions for 12 days (Supplementary Fig. 3b–e) and 9 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 3f–i), respectively. The results showed that, under LN conditions, the dry weights of brn1, nlp3, and brn1 nlp3 mutants were comparable to that of ZH11. In contrast, under HN conditions, the dry weights of all three mutant genotypes were significantly lower than that of ZH11. Notably, the brn1 nlp3 double mutants displayed dry weights similar to those of the respective single mutants without further reduction (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating that BRN1 and NLP3 function in the same genetic pathway.

As NLP3 is a core transcription factor in the nitrate signaling pathway36,37, we examined N-responsive genes expression differences in the OE lines, and the results showed that the transcript levels of NLP3 and multiple NLP3 target or downstream genes, including NRT1.1B, NRT2.1, NIA1, NR2, NAR2.1, AMT2;3, and GS1.2, were significantly upregulated in the roots of OE lines relative to ZH11 plants (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Given that NLP3 enhances rice NUE and grain yield37, we performed genetic rescue by overexpressing NLP3 (35S::NLP3) in the BRN1 KO background. The KO/NLP3-OE lines exhibited significantly higher dry weight and grain yield per plant than KO plants, even exceeding ZH11 (Fig. 3g–i and Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). These results confirm that NLP3 acts downstream of BRN1 to regulate rice biomass and N utilization.

D53 represses BRN1-mediated nitrogen utilization

To gain further insight into BRN1-mediated regulation, we carried out yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening and identified D53, a strigolactone (SL) signaling repressor known to modulate rice N sensitivity and utilization12,15, as a BRN1-interacting protein. D53 accumulation under HN reduces rice N sensitivity by repressing transcription factors involved in N utilization13,15,38,39. Y2H assay showed that co-expression of D53-BD and BRN1-AD enabled yeast growth on Leu-/Trp-/His-/Ade- medium, confirming their interaction (Fig. 4a). This interaction was validated in vitro (pull-down) and in vivo (bimolecular fluorescence complementation and split firefly luciferase complementation) in tobacco leaf cells (Fig. 4b–d).

To further dissect the molecular mechanism underlying D53-dependent regulation of BRN1, we first compared biomass between NIP (Nipponbare) and d53 gain-of-function mutants (accumulating excess D53 protein) under both LN and HN conditions. Relative to NIP, d53 mutants exhibited significantly lower biomass than NIP under both conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that excess D53 represses biomass accumulation and N utilization. EMSA showed increasing D53 levels do not affect BRN1’s binding to the NLP3 promoter (Fig. 4e), but transcriptional activation assays revealed D53 suppressed BRN1-mediated activation of NLP3 (Fig. 4f). To test whether D53-induced N insensitivity is rescued by BRN1 overexpression, we generated 35S::BRN1/d53 transgenic lines (d53/OE). These lines exhibited higher dry weight, 15N accumulation, and grain yield per plant than d53 mutants, with values exceeding NIP plants under both LN and HN conditions (Fig. 4g–m and Supplementary Fig. 6). This confirms BRN1 overexpression mitigates D53-mediated N insensitivity.

Natural variation in BRN1 confers differential NUE

To identify the elite BRN1 alleles, we analyzed haplotypes in the Mini-Core Collection and confirmed two main haplotypes: BRN1H (high-BRN1) and BRN1L (low-BRN1) (Fig. 5a, b). BRN1H also located to the nucleus, bound the NLP3 promoter, and interacted with D53, which was similar to BRN1L (Supplementary Fig. 7). Sequence analysis revealed that BRN1H carries 4 unique promoter SNPs and 4 unique coding-region SNPs, all in linkage (Fig. 5a). To explain BRN differences between haplotypes, we performed both qRT-PCR expression and promoter activity assays and found no correlation between BRN1 expression/promoter activity and BRN values (Fig. 5c, d), ruling out transcriptional difference. Cell-free stability assays showed that BRN1H protein was more stable than BRN1L under both LN and HN conditions, with a larger difference under HN conditions. BRN1H was more stable than BRN1L at 60 min under LN and as early as 15 min under HN conditions (Fig. 5e), suggesting post-transcriptional regulation drives functional divergence between the two haplotypes.

Genetic complementation using proBRN1L::BRN1H or proBRN1H::BRN1H in brn1 KO lines restored dry weight and grain yield to levels exceeding ZH11, with no difference between the two constructs under LN and HN conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8). This confirmed that coding-region SNPs, not promoter SNPs, underpin functional variation in BRN1. We further assessed the function of BRN1H in the elite cultivar Xiushui134 (XS134). The proBRN1H::BRN1H/XS134 lines exhibited elevated dry weight and grain yield under both LN and HN conditions (Supplementary Fig. 9), thereby establishing that BRN1H enhanced biomass and N utilization in rice.

We also examined near-isogenic line (NIL)-BRN1H in XS134 background. NIL plants exhibited a significant increase by 15.9% and 25.7% in dry weight under both LN (low NH4NO3) and HN (high NH4NO3) conditions, respectively at seedling stage (Fig. 5f–i). The BRN of NIL plants is significantly higher than that of XS134 plants (Fig. 5j). Although NIL exhibited higher NO3− and NH4+ uptake than XS134, with a more notable difference in NO3− uptake (Fig. 5k–n). However, compared to different levels of NH4+ supply, NIL exhibits higher sensitivity to NO3− supply (Supplementary Fig. 10). Gene expression analysis revealed no significant difference in BRN1 expression in NIL compared to XS134 (Supplementary Fig. 11a), whereas NLP3 expression was significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 11b). These results indicate that NIL primarily enhances the nitrate utilization, consistent with BRN1-mediated regulation of nitrate core transcription factor NLP3.

Large-scale field trials demonstrated NIL exhibited greater plant height, dry weight, and panicle length than XS134 under both LN and HN conditions (Fig. 6a–d and Supplementary Fig. 12a–f), leading to 17.4% higher grain yield under LN and 22.9% under HN conditions (Fig. 6e–h). Harvest index did not differ significantly between XS134 and NIL under both LN and HN conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12g, h). However, grain yield response to N was significantly higher in NIL than in XS134 (Supplementary Fig. 12i), indicating that increased N application synchronously promoted grain and straw biomass accumulation in NIL plants without altering harvest index, while significantly improving biomass and grain yield response to N. NUE was also significantly higher in NIL than in XS134 under both LN and HN conditions (Fig. 6i, j). These results highlight that BRN1H has great potential for improving NUE in elite rice.

Collectively, our data support a working model (Fig. 6k): increased N supply reduces SL levels, leading to D53 accumulation. D53 interacts with BRN1 to suppress activation of NLP3 transcription, thereby reducing N sensitivity. The BRN1H allele alleviates this inhibition via increased protein stability, enhancing N responsiveness and NUE. Understanding D53-BRN1-NLP3 module provides actionable targets for breeding high-yield, high-NUE rice varieties and advancing a sustainable Green Revolution.

Discussion

Widespread application of N fertilizers has driven substantial increases in crop yields. However, as N inputs have risen, yield gains have plateaued and NUE has declined sharply. This plateau reflects the gradual loss of N sensitivity in modern crop varieties, especially GR varieties, in which excess N fails to boost productivity. Understanding how elevated N inputs reduce crop N sensitivity is essential to develop strategies that reconcile high yields with low N requirements. Three complementary approaches have emerged to improve NUE: breeding varieties with inherently high N sensitivity, optimizing fertilization regimes, and improving agricultural management. Among these, breeding solutions are uniquely scalable and sustainable, as they reduce long-term reliance on external inputs. Our study contributes to this goal by uncovering a novel regulatory module (D53-BRN1-NLP3) that controls N sensitivity in rice, providing both a mechanistic explanation for the low N responsiveness of GR varieties and actionable targets for crop improvement.

A defining feature of GR varieties is reduced N sensitivity, a trait linked to selection for semi-dwarfism and lodging resistance9,40. Recent work has highlighted the role for SLs, phytohormones that regulate plant architecture and nutrient adaptation. The SL biosynthetic gene HTD1 was widely selected during the Green Revolution, with partial loss-of-function alleles favored in GR varieties11. This link is further supported by observations that SL biosynthesis declines with increasing N supply12. Under HN conditions, reduced SL levels lead to the accumulation of the SL signaling repressor D5312,13,19, which interacts with N-responsive transcription factors (e.g., GRF4 and SPL14) to repress their activity, ultimately lowering N sensitivity and NUE15,38,39. These findings imply a potential role for SLs in modulating NUE in GR varieties.

This D53-mediated repression of N utilization pathways provides a plausible explanation for why GR varieties respond poorly to N inputs. Overcoming this constraint requires identifying additional N-related transcription factors targeted by D53, as modifying these interactions could restore N sensitivity. Our study addresses this gap by using GWAS for BRN in a diverse rice Mini-Core Collection, leading to the identification of BRN1. We show that BRN1 expression is induced by high N (particularly nitrate) and that BRN1 physically interacts with D53 (Fig. 2c, d; Fig. 4a–d). Importantly, D53 represses BRN1 transcriptional activation activity, a mechanism likely underlying reduced N sensitivity under high N conditions (Fig. 4e, f).

A key insight from our work is the functional consequence of natural variation in BRN1: the BRN1ᴴ allele encodes a more stable protein that resists D53-mediated repression, thereby enhancing N sensitivity and NUE (Fig. 5e; Fig. 6). This finding directly addresses a core limitation of modern varieties: by introgressing BRN1ᴴ into BRN1L cultivars, we can mitigate D53-induced N insensitivity, a longstanding constraint of post-Green Revolution breeding.

The NLP family proteins are well-established master regulators of nitrate signaling and N utilization41. In rice, nitrate availability modulates NLP3 activity via a sophisticated pathway: under low nitrate, SPX4 binds NLP3 and sequesters it in the cytoplasm, preventing the activation of N-responsive genes; under high nitrate, nitrate triggers NRT1.1B-mediated recruitment of NBIP1, which ubiquitinates and degrades SPX4, liberating NLP3 to translocate to the nucleus and activate N-responsive genes36. Building upon this framework, our work identifies BRN1 as a direct upstream regulator of NLP3 (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

We demonstrate that BRN1 binds the NLP3 promoter (via ChIP-seq, EMSA, and Y1H) and enhances its transcription (via protoplast activation assays), with NLP3 acting genetically downstream of BRN1 (Fig. 3a–e). This places BRN1 in a critical position within the nitrate signaling network: under high N, BRN1 amplifies NLP3-mediated N utilization, while D53 counteracts this by repressing BRN1. The BRN1ᴴ allele circumvents this inhibition through increased protein stability, permitting continuous NLP3 activation even when D53 accumulates to high levels (Fig. 5e and Fig. 6k).

Notably, NIL carrying BRN1ᴴ in the XS134 background displayed a stronger dry weight response to nitrate than to ammonium, and ¹⁵N uptake assays confirmed greater nitrate uptake (Fig. 5f–n and Supplementary Fig. 10). This specificity aligns with nitrate-inducible expression of BRN1 and highlights its role in optimizing nitrate utilization—a key pathway for NUE in aerobic rice systems. By integrating BRN1 into the known NLP3 regulatory cascade, our work expands our understanding of how plants fine-tune N responses to environmental supply.

The practical value of our findings is underscored by field trials: introgressing BRN1ᴴ into XS134 increased grain yield by ~17.4% under low N and ~22.9% under high N (Fig. 6g, h). This dual benefit—enhanced yield under both N-limited and N-replete conditions—is highly valuable, as it serves farmers in regions with variable N input capacity while reducing environmental harm in high-input systems.

Haplotype analysis of 203 Mini-Core Collection accessions revealed that BRN1H is enriched in indica (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Extended to 3,024 accessions from the 3 K Rice Genomes Project42 revealed 60.4% of indica varieties carried BRN1H, compared with only 3.8% of japonica varieties. Within indica subgroups, BRN1H frequency varied: 38.6% in indica III, 77.8% in indica II, and 86.9% in indica I (Supplementary Fig. 13b). These patterns suggest BRN1H was preferentially retained in indica during domestication. Geographically, BRN1ᴴ is highly enriched in indica varieties, presenting a clear breeding opportunity: transferring BRN1ᴴ to japonica cultivars—many of which are GR varieties with low N sensitivity—could rapidly improve their NUE without compromising yield potential.

In conclusion, our work reveals the D53-BRN1-NLP3 module as a critical regulator of N sensitivity in rice, explaining the reduced NUE in GR varieties and paving the way for breeding more sustainable crop cultivars. By leveraging natural variation in BRN1, we can develop high-yielding rice varieties that perform well under reduced N inputs—advancing a second Green Revolution centered on resource-use efficiency and environmental stewardship.

Methods

Plant materials

Rice Mini-Core Collection accessions were used as previously described34. The japonica varieties Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) and XiuShui134 (XS134) were used in this study. The brn1 knockout (KO), nlp3 and brn1 nlp3 double mutants and BRN1 overexpression (OE) lines were generated in the ZH11 background36. The d53 mutant was generated in the Nipponbare (NIP) background17. NIL-BRN1H was developed by backcrossing with XS134. proBRN1H::BRN1H/XS134 was generated in the XS134 background.

Hydroponic culture of rice

Hydroponic culture (for dry weight, GWAS, gene expression, and rice protoplast preparation) was performed in a growth chamber with 12 h light (28 °C)/12 h dark (25 °C) photoperiod, about 70% humidity and about 200 μmol m−2 s−1 photon flux density. About 48 rice seedlings were cultivated in 1 L (for 12 days) or 12 rice seedlings were cultivated in 30 L (for 8–9 weeks) Kimura B nutrient solution (standard N condition) containing macronutrients (1 mM NH4NO3 (0.5mM (NH4)2SO4, 1mM KNO3), 0.18 mM KH2PO4, 0.18 (0.09) mM K2SO4, 0.55 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 1.6 mM Na2SiO3·9H2O) and micronutrients (46.2 μM H3BO3, 0.32 μM CuSO4·5H2O, 9.14 μM MnCl2·4H2O, 0.08 μM (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, 0.76 μM ZnSO4·7H2O, and 40 μM Fe(II)-EDTA), pH 5.8. The nutrient solution was renewed daily. For different N concentrations, NH4NO3 were replaced by (NH4)2SO4 or KNO3. LN (0.2 mM N) and HN (2 mM N) were used to simulate field conditions26,31. To exclude potassium (K+) effects, the LNO3− solutions were supplemented with KCl to match K+ levels in HNO3−.

GWAS mapping

We selected 179 rice accessions with similar germination rate from the Mini-Core Collection. Agronomic traits were surveyed at rice seedling stage (about 12-day after germination) under LN and HN conditions. The biomass response to N (BRN) value was calculated as (HN dry weight–LN dry weight)/LN dry weight and used for GWAS with a previously reported SNP dataset34. SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) below 5% were filtered out, retaining 8,962,580 SNPs. GWAS was performed using GEMMA (v0.941) with a linear mixed model43.

Rice cultivation conditions

Rice seeds were sown in seedbeds and grown for 30 days, then transplanted to the field and until maturity. Plant spacing was 20 cm, with 8 plants × 6 rows per plot under LN and HN conditions. Three replicates were used for plot yield tests. Field trials were conducted at the experimental station in Lingshui (Hainan), Jinan, Hangzhou, and Guangzhou. Urea was used as the N source: 50 kg ha−1 for LN and 150 kg ha−1 for HN, applied at seedling, tillering, and heading stages. NUE was defined as grain yield per unit available N in soil and calculated as (plot yield × 100) /(planted area × N application rate)26.

15N analysis

Rice seedlings were cultivated hydroponically in modified Kimura B solution containing 1 mM NH4NO3 as the sole nitrogen source for 12 days. After 3 days of N starvation, seedlings were transferred to 0.1 mM CaSO4 for 1 min, then to a complete nutrient solution containing 15NO3− (atom% 15N: 40%) and/or 15NH4+ (atom% 15N: 40%) for 1 h. Roots were collected following a 1-min wash with 0.1 mM CaSO4, dried at 70 °C to a constant weight, ground, and analyzed by Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer26.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the roots of seedlings grown under different N regimes for 12 days. The quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) were performed as previously described44. Primers were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell-free protein degradation assay

Cell-free protein degradation assays were performed using 12-day-old ZH11 seedlings ground in liquid nitrogen. Lysates were extracted with lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 4 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and 10 mM ATP) as described45. Total protein concentration was normalized. Rice cell lysates (200 μL) were incubated with 100 ng purified GST-BRN1L or GST-BRN1H fusion protein. After incubation, proteins were extracted and analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blotting using anti-GST antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-138). GST served as a control.

ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR assay

Transgenic lines of 35S::BRN1-GFP, cultivated for 12 days under HN conditions, were used for the ChIP-seq assay. ChIP-seq was performed as described previously with minor modifications46. Briefly, approximately 2 g of root tissues were harvested and immediately cross-linked within 1% formaldehyde for 3 times and then quenched by glycine. Nuclei were isolated and sheared by sonication to obtain DNA fragments mainly ranging from 200 to 500 bp. After centrifugation, the soluble chromatin fraction was collected and incubated with Dynabeads protein A/G and anti-GFP (A-11122; Invitrogen) at 4 °C for 10 h. After incubation, the beads were sequentially washed with low-salt (150 mM NaCl in TE) and high-salt buffer (250 mM NaCl in TE) for 15 min. The washed beads were then treated with Tn5 transposase at 37 °C for 20 min and TE buffer was added to stop the reaction. Following tagmentation, the DNA was reverse-crosslinked for 12 h and purified with 50% Isopropanol. The purified DNA was amplified with standard Illumina primers and then deeply sequenced by standard protocols. The precipitated DNA was recovered and analyzed by qPCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Y1H assay

Yeast one-hybrid assay was conducted following the Yeast Protocols Handbook (Clontech) and the method previously described47. The fragment of NLP3 promoter (approximately 300 bp), was amplified and cloned into the pLacZi2μ vector containing LacZ reporter gene to generate proNLP3::LacZ. Full-length coding sequence of BRN1 was cloned into the pJG4-5 vector, which includes the B42 activation domain (AD), to generate AD-BRN1. Yeast strain EGY48 was co-transformed with pJG4-5 (AD) and proNLP3 served as a negative control. Transformants were cultured on -Trp/-Ura dropout plates supplemented with 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (X-Gal) to enable blue color development.

Y2H assay

The coding region of D53 was fused to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain of the “bait” vector pGBKT7. A cDNA library prepared from young rice inflorescences was used for yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening. Full-length and various truncated versions of BRN1 were inserted into the EcoRI/BamHI restriction sites of the “prey” vector pGADT7. Following 3 days of growth on SD-Trp/-Leu plates at 30 °C, protein interactions were examined on the selective medium SD-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade. The yeast strain containing D53-BD combined with the empty pGADT7 vector served as the negative control. All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions18.

BiFC assay

The full-length BRN1 coding sequence was cloned into pNC-BIFC-Enc (expressing the N-terminal fragment of split YFP, nYFP) to generate BRN1-nYFP construct. The full-length D53 coding sequence was inserted into the pNC-BIFC-Ecn (expressing the C-terminal fragment of split YFP, cYFP) to generate the D53-cYFP construct. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring both constructs (BRN1-nYFP and D53-cYFP) was co-infiltrated with the p19 strain into 5-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. YFP fluorescence was visualized 48–72 h post-infiltration by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica STELLARIS 5)48.

Pull-down assay

Pull-down assays were conducted as previously described49. The cDNAs of BRN1 and D53 were cloned into the pET28a-GST and pETMALc-MBP vectors to create GST-tag and MBP-tag fusions, respectively. The expression strain of Escherichia coli BL21 containing two vectors were induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at at 28 °C for 6 h. GST and MBP antibodies were employed in the western blotting.

SFLC assay

Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 containing the cDNA of the genes was incubated in Luria–Bertani medium at 28 °C for 16 h. The Agrobacterium cultures were harvested and resuspended in infiltration buffer (0.2 mM acetosyringone, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM MES, pH 5.6) to a final optical density of OD600 = 1.0. Equal volumes of the Agrobacterium cultures were mixed and maintained at room temperature for 3 h before co-infiltration into tobacco leaves. After infiltration, the plants were cultured at 23 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod for 48 h. The infiltrated leaves were sprayed with 1 mM luciferin solution (Sigma, L9504) supplemented with 0.01% Triton X-100 in darkness for 3 min, and luminescence signals were then detected using a low-light cooled CCD imaging system (NightOWL II LB983)36.

Transcriptional activity assay

For transient transcriptional activation assays, the promoter regions of target genes (approximately 2 kb upstream of ATG) were cloned upstream of the firefly luciferase (LUC) gene in the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector to generate reporter constructs50. The Renilla luciferase (REN) gene driven by the CaMV 35S promoter in the same pGreenII 0800-LUC vector served as an internal control. For effector constructs, full-length coding sequences of BRN1 and D53 were separately cloned into the pCAMBIA2300-35S vector, with the corresponding empty vector used as the effector control. Various combinations of these effector and reporter constructs were co-transfected into rice protoplasts following previously described methods51,52. After 18 h of incubation, luciferase activity was quantified as described above. Primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

EMSA assay

EMSAs were performed as previously described9, using the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20148) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNAs of BRN1 were amplified and cloned into the pGEX-4T3-GST vector (D53 was processed as in the Pull-down assay). GST, GST-D53, GST-BRN1L and GST-BRN1H proteins were purified using Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow (Cytiva). DNA probes of NLP3 were amplified and labeled with a biotin labeling kit. Primers and probe sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Data analysis

Experiments were pooled to calculate means and standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance of differences was assessed using two-sided Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test with a significance level set at p < 0.05.
