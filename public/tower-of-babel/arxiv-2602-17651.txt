Non-Trivial Zero-Knowledge Implies One-Way Functions
Suvradip Chakraborty, James Hulett, Dakshita Khurana, Kabir Tomer

Abstract
A recent breakthrough [Hirahara and Nanashima, STOC’2024] established that if 𝖭𝖯 ⊈ 𝗂𝗈𝖯 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly} , the existence of zero-knowledge (ZK) with negligible errors for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} implies the existence of one-way functions (OWFs). This work obtains a characterization of one-way functions from the worst-case complexity of zero-knowledge in the high-error regime. Assuming 𝖭𝖯 ⊈ 𝗂𝗈𝖯 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly} , we show that any non-trivial, constant-round public-coin ZK argument for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} implies the existence of OWFs, and therefore also (standard) four-message zero-knowledge arguments for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} . Here, we call a ZK argument non-trivial if the sum of its completeness, soundness and zero-knowledge errors is bounded away from 1 1 . As a special case, we also prove that non-trivial non-interactive ZK (NIZK) arguments for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} imply the existence of OWFs. Using known amplification techniques, this also provides an unconditional transformation from weak to standard NIZK proofs for all meaningful error parameters. Prior work [Chakraborty, Hulett and Khurana, CRYPTO’2025] was limited to NIZKs with constant zero-knowledge error ε 𝗓𝗄 \varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}} and soundness error ε 𝗌 \varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}} satisfying ε 𝗓𝗄 + ε 𝗌 < 1 \varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}}<1 .

Introduction

Zero-knowledge proofs are a cornerstone of modern cryptography, allowing a prover to convince a verifier about the validity of an NP statement without leaking any information about the witness.
A major research agenda, initiated by Ostrovsky and Wigderson
[
12
,
11
]
, asks: what are the minimal cryptographic assumptions needed for zero-knowledge (
ZK
) proof systems? Their celebrated results established that, assuming NP is hard on average, the existence of
ZK
proofs for NP implies the existence of one-way functions (
OWFs
), a fundamental cryptographic primitive.

More recently, Hirahara and Nanashima
[
7
]
made a notable advance by proving that even the
worst-case
hardness of
\mathsf{NP}
(specifically,
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
) suffices to construct one-way functions from zero-knowledge proofs for
\mathsf{NP}
. In fact, they only require the existence of zero-knowledge proofs for a
specific
type of language outside
\mathsf{ioP/poly}
, thereby opening up a new route for constructing one-way functions based solely on worst-case hardness.

Non-trivial
ZK
and One-way Functions.

What happens if we allow
ZK
proof systems with non-negligible and possibly large security errors?
Unfortunately, techniques of
[
12
,
11
,
7
]
that link zero-knowledge to one-way functions are critically dependent on the assumption of small errors.
As we will show, this is not merely an artifact of their analysis, but a fundamental limitation of their reductions. This state of affairs leaves open a basic gap in our understanding of the complexity of
ZK
.

Note that
ZK
proof systems with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}\geq 1
can be realized trivially

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}=1

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}

We ask:

Do non-trivial

?

Non-trivial

But if we did manage to construct them, we would not know how to leverage them to build
OWFs
—leaving unresolved questions about whether such protocols could function as a stepping stone towards one-way functions, or if they are fundamentally weaker and cannot be used to construct basic cryptographic primitives.

This Work.

Our main result is a complete characterization of the worst-case complexity of non-trivial constant round ZK.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

If
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
and non-trivial public-coin, constant round (computational)
ZK
arguments for
\mathsf{NP}
exist, then one-way functions exist.

The above result requires the assumption that the underlying ZK argument is public-coin. We also obtain a result for private-coin protocols by showing that unless auxiliary-input one-way functions exist, we can convert any constant-round private-coin protocol into a public-coin one. Thus, non-trivial constant round
ZK
protocols yield auxiliary-input
OWFs
.

Theorem 2

(Informal)
.

If
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
and non-trivial constant round (computational)
ZK
arguments for
\mathsf{NP}
exist, then auxiliary-input one-way functions exist.

We also consider the special case of non-interactive zero-knowledge (
NIZK
) arguments for
\mathsf{NP}
, where the prover sends a single proof string and both parties share a common reference string (
CRS
). We show that nontrivial
NIZKs
for
\mathsf{NP}
, together with the assumption that
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
, imply the existence of one-way functions.

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

If
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
and non-trivial (computational)
NIZK
arguments for
\mathsf{NP}
exist, then one-way functions exist.

This improves recent work
[
5
]
which obtained one-way functions from any
NIZK
with zero-knowledge error
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
and soundness error
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
satisfying

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}+2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}}<1-\frac{1}{p(|x|)}

for some polynomial
p(\cdot)
, or satisfying
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}}<1
in the case where
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
are constants and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}=o(1)
. However, the regime where
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}<1
but
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}}\geq 1
remained open until this work.

Application: Amplifying
ZK
.

Arguably one of the most natural questions about non-trivial
ZK
proofs is whether they can be
amplified
to drive down their errors to negligible, and obtain “standard”
ZK
. For
NIZKs
, this question has been studied extensively recently, and such amplification has been shown to be possible under additional assumptions such as the existence of public-key encryption
[
6
]
, and even just one-way functions
[
3
,
1
]
.

Our theorem above can be viewed as eliminating the need to make any cryptographic assumptions when amplifying
NIZK
. In more detail, if
\mathsf{NP}
is easy in the worst case then
NIZKs
for
\mathsf{NP}
exist unconditionally, whereas if
\mathsf{NP}
is hard in the worst case, then Informal Theorem
3
builds one-way functions from non-trivial
NIZKs
for
\mathsf{NP}
. The resulting one-way functions can be used, together with weak
NIZKs
, to obtain standard
NIZKs
by relying on known amplification theorems in
[
3
,
1
]
.

For technical reasons we will still need to make the extremely mild complexity-theoretic assumption (Assumption 1) that
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq(\mathsf{ioP/poly})\setminus\mathsf{BPP}
. In other words, if
\mathsf{NP}
languages are efficiently decidable infinitely often with advice, then they are efficiently decidable on all input lengths. This is an extremely mild complexity assumption (it is implied by
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
, which is already a mild worst case assumption).

Corollary 1

(Informal)
.

Under Assumption 1, non-trivial (computational)
NIZK
proofs imply standard
NIZK
proofs.

Note that the corollary above applies only to
NIZK
proofs (with statistical soundness), since we currently do not have approaches to amplify arbitrary non-trivial

NIZK
arguments from assumptions weaker than public-key encryption.

A natural next question is whether similar amplification is possible for
interactive

ZK
. Here, we note that one-way functions imply the existence of four-round
ZK
arguments
[
2
]
, yielding the following informal Corollary.

Corollary 2

(Informal)
.

Under Assumption 1, non-trivial public-coin constant-round interactive (computational)
ZK
arguments imply four-round
ZK
arguments.

Technical Overview

For simplicity throughout this overview, we will highlight our techniques assuming that the completeness error
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}=0
. Formally we work with the general case where
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}\neq 0
, but this is a straightforward extension of the analysis described in this overview.

In the following two subsections, we recall techniques used in prior works
[
11
,
5
]
to show that zero-knowledge requires one-way functions, as well as barriers that prevent these techniques from working when
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}\approx 1
. These will build intuition towards our eventual approach. Readers that are already familiar with these works may skip ahead to our techniques described in Section
2.3
.

We discuss prior ideas in the context of
non-interactive
zero-knowledge (
NIZK
), and then describe methods to close the gap in parameters in the non-interactive setting. However, we note that the more general interactive case requires the most significant new insights, discussed in Section
2.4
.

2.1
Previous Techniques

[
11
]
Techniques.

The key tool used in
[
11
]
is Universal Extrapolation (UE): informally, UE lets us “reverse sample” the randomness that would generate a particular output of an efficient process.
In more detail, given a polynomial-time sampler
\mathsf{Samp}
and an output
y
from its distribution, UE refers to the ability to efficiently and uniformly sample (up to small error) from the set of all inputs that

\mathsf{Samp}
maps to
y
.
UE is possible if and only if one-way functions do not exist
[
8
]
.

Towards a contradiction,
[
11
]
assume the non-existence of one-way functions. Then using UE, they convert a
NIZK

(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V})
and simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
for a language

into an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that decides
\mathcal{L}
. In particular,
\mathcal{A}
samples a
real

CRS
from
\mathsf{Gen}

that would lead the
simulator
to output that same
CRS

\mathcal{A}
then accepts if and only if
\mathsf{V}
would accept the proof output by
\mathsf{Sim}(x;r)
.

To argue that
\mathcal{A}
works, one has to show that it will accept
x\in\mathcal{L}
with higher probability than it accepts
x\not\in\mathcal{L}
. Consider each of these cases:

•

If
x\not\in\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{A}
can only accept if it finds a proof
relative to a real
CRS

that causes
\mathsf{V}
to accept
x

will immediately tell us that this can happen with probability at most
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
.

•

If
x\in\mathcal{L}
, note that by completeness
\mathsf{V}
will accept a real
CRS
and proof with probability 1
and so by zero-knowledge will accept a simulated
CRS
and proof with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
.

Now consider an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
that is
almost
identical to
\mathcal{A}
, except that it starts by sampling a
CRS
from
\mathsf{Sim}
instead of from
\mathsf{Gen}
. We note first that this is (up to the small statistical error of the UE) identically distributed to simply running
\mathsf{V}
on a simulated
CRS
and proof, so by the above

with probability at least

and
\mathcal{B}

Thus,

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+2\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
is noticeably smaller than 1. Since one can implement

as long as one-way functions

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+2\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<1

requires

Note that in the analysis above, we first uses the zero-knowledge property to bound the difference between real and simulated proofs (costing one
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}

Techniques.

The improvement in
[

2.3
Repetition to the Rescue

In order to improve on prior techniques, it will help to first understand
why
the above counterexample is bad for them. We note that the counterexample hinges on the fact that on
\mathsf{crs}=0
the simulator always has the
ability
to output a
Good
proof (i.e.
0
), but still chooses to output a
Bad
proof (i.e.
1
) some of the time.
Here by
Good
we mean a proof that is statistically close to a real proof, and by
Bad
we mean a proof that is far from a real proof. This causes the distribution used by
\mathcal{A}
to have
2\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
error (once due to the distance between the real and simulated
CRS
and once due to the distance between the real and simulated proofs
conditioned on
a particular
CRS
), while still keeping the real and simulated (
CRS
, proof) distributions
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
-close overall.

It turns out that this type of simulator behavior is the
only
reason prior techniques don’t work with protocols where
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}\approx 1
. In particular, for every fixing of
x\in\mathcal{L}
, if every
CRS
was either
Good
(i.e., the simulator conditioned on this
CRS

always
outputs an accepting proof for
x
) or
Bad
(i.e., the simulator conditioned on this
CRS

never
outputs an accepting proof for
x
), then the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
from
[
11
]

will work
only assuming that
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<1
! This is because on any
Bad

CRS
output by
\mathsf{Gen}
, the real prover is guaranteed to output an accepting proof whereas the simulator never does. This allows us to infer that
\mathsf{Gen}
must output a
Good

CRS
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
, and thus
\mathcal{A}
will accept
x\in\mathcal{L}
with the same probability.

Of course, we cannot in general assume that the simulator is guaranteed to always output
Good
proofs on certain
CRS
es and always output
Bad
proofs on others. But, we can hope to mimic a similar effect by sampling
many
simulated proofs for the same
CRS
and checking whether
they all
reject. Our updated definition of a
Good

CRS
is one where (with overwhelming probability)
at least one
of the simulated proofs that is sampled is accepted.
7
7

7

We note that
[
13
]

\mathsf{crs}

exists
an accepting proof relative to it. However, their analysis is limited to the negligible-error setting and doesn’t seem to naturally generalize to the interactive setting.

In particular, we fix an appropriate polynomial

•

For

11
]

.

•

For

, we call a

Very roughly, as long as

8

8

In more detail note that

We have swept some technical details under the rug, but the above ideas coarsely suffice to obtain

2.4
Generalizing to Interactive Zero Knowledge

As discussed in the introduction, our primary goal is to obtain an implication from non-trivial
honest-verifier

ZK
to
OWFs
, since this gives us the strongest possible theorem statement.
First, we note that techniques from
[
11
]
naturally extend to the interactive case, but get further from the desired result as the number of rounds increases: to get a one-way function from a
k
round protocol, they require
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+k\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<1
. Similarly, simple extensions of the
[
5
]
techniques require
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}})^{1/k}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<1
.

A natural goal is therefore to see if we can extend the techniques that gave us a tight result for
NIZKs
to the interactive setting, and more specifically, eliminate the dependence on
k
in the above constraints.

In order to generalize our techniques to the interactive setting, it will help to take a slightly different view of our proof in the non-interactive case. We can treat our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
as defining a malicious prover
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
, then simulating a run of
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
and
\mathsf{V}
to decide whether to accept
x
. In the analysis above, we treated
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
as using UE to sample many possible simulated proof strings, and outputting an accepting one if found. But note that we equally well could phrase
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
as choosing whichever sampled proof string has the maximum probability of being accepted. Phrased this way, we can now begin to extend this idea to the interactive case.

An Inefficient Prover.

We would like to define a malicious prover
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
such that our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
to decide
\mathcal{L}
can simply simulate an interaction between
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
and
\mathsf{V}
on
x
and output the result. Unlike the non-interactive case,
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
can’t immediately check if the message it is considering would lead to
\mathsf{V}
accepting or rejecting, since this decision may depend on randomness sampled at a later point in the protocol. Thus, all we can hope for is to have
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
choose the “best” message available to it, which means choosing the message that maximizes the probability that
\mathsf{V}
accepts
if we continue to apply the prover strategy
\widetilde{P}
in future rounds
. For now, we will assume that
\widetilde{P}
can (inefficiently) find such messages, and we will later see how to make
\widetilde{P}
efficient.

More formally, suppose for simplicity that we are starting from a protocol that satisfies statistical soundness and zero-knowledge. We can (recursively) define
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
that, given
x
and a partial transcript
\tau
, considers all possible next messages
m
in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}

and outputs whichever one maximizes the probability of
\mathsf{V}
accepting
x
if it interacts with
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
starting from transcript
\tau\|m
. Here, defining this success probability based
only
on the transcript and not on
\mathsf{V}
’s internal state limits us to only working with public-coin protocols.

Let
\mathcal{A}(x)
be the algorithm that runs
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
and
\mathsf{V}
on input
x
and outputs
\mathsf{V}
’s decision bit; we can now argue that
\mathcal{A}
correctly decides
\mathcal{L}
.

•

If
x\not\in\mathcal{L}
, statistical soundness tells us that no prover can convince
\mathsf{V}
with probability greater than
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
, and so in particular
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
cannot. Thus,
\mathcal{A}(x)
will accept with probability at most
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
.

•

If
x\in\mathcal{L}
, we can argue that
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
performs “almost as well as” the honest prover
\mathsf{P}
. In particular, we note that since
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
always chooses the best possible message in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
, the only way for
\mathsf{P}
to choose a better message is if it sends a message that
\mathsf{Sim}
never would. But from statistical zero-knowledge, we know that this can happen with probability at most
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
!
9
9

9

Importantly, we are applying zero-knowledge
globally
to the entire transcript instead of on a message-by-message basis. This is the key reason why we avoid the
O(k)\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
term in the analysis from
[
11
]
.
Thus, the probability that
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
convinces
\mathsf{V}
is at least
(1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
, which tells us that
\mathcal{A}
accepts with probability at least
(1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
.

As in the
NIZK
case, this immediately gives us an algorithm for
\mathcal{L}
as long as
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
is noticeably smaller than 1.

Making
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
Efficient.

So far, we required
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
to find the
best
possible next prover message in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
, which may not be efficient to compute.
This inefficiency meant that our eventual decision algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is inefficient, which is a problem.
To fix these issues, we need to modify how we define
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
so that it has a polynomial run time, but still works well enough to argue that
\mathcal{A}(x)
will accept with good probability when
x\in\mathcal{L}
.

Taking inspiration from the
NIZK
case, we can have
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
use UE to sample many possible next messages from
\mathsf{Sim}
, and simply choose the best message
among those it sampled
. While this won’t guarantee that we get the best
possible
next message, it does guarantee that (with overwhelming probability) we get a message that is “one of the best”. In particular, suppose we order all the messages in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
by the probability that
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
will convince
\mathsf{V}
if it chooses that message next. For any
\delta
, if we have
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
sample
\frac{n}{\delta}
many possible next messages, it will sample at least one in the top
\delta
fraction of this order with overwhelming probability.

There is however one catch to this strategy that only arises in the interactive case: in order to choose the best message among those sampled,
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
has to be able to compute the probability that each message will lead to it convincing
\mathsf{V}
. Note though that as long as
\mathsf{V}
is public coin (and so stateless),
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
can get a very good estimate of this probability, as
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
can simulate an interaction between itself and
\mathsf{V}
“in its own head”. A Chernoff bound says that
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
only needs to run
\poly(n,\frac{1}{\delta})
such simulations in order to (with overwhelming probability) estimate its success probability to within an additive
\delta
factor for any
\delta
. Note, however, that since
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
has to simulate itself a polynomial number of times on a transcript that is only one message longer, its run time will grow by a
multiplicative
factor of
\poly(n)
for each round. This will give us a final run time of
\poly(n)^{k}
, which constrains us to only work with protocols where
k
is a constant.

Putting this all together, we get an
efficient

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
that with overwhelming probability chooses a next message that has success probability at most
2\delta
smaller than some message in the top
\delta
fraction of messages in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
. We claim that as long as
\delta
is set to a sufficiently small inverse polynomial, our prior analysis is robust enough to work with these weaker guarantees on
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
.

In more detail, note that all we have to change in the above analysis is the argument that
\mathsf{P}
can’t pick a “better” message than
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
too commonly. Since our weaker guarantee on
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
is that it picks a message (approximately) in the top
\delta
fraction of possible messages from the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
, we just have to bound the probability that
\mathsf{P}
picks a message that is better than a
(1-\delta)
fraction of the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
.

By a union bound over each round, the probability that
\mathsf{Sim}
ever outputs such a message is at most
\delta k
, so by (statistical) zero-knowledge, the probability that a real transcript contains such a message is at most
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\delta k
. By our guarantees on
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
, we know that it loses at most
2\delta
success probability in each round compared to
\mathsf{P}
conditioned on not picking such a “too good” message. Thus, since
\mathsf{P}
will always convince
\mathsf{V}
to accept
x\in\mathcal{L}
, we have that
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
convinces
\mathsf{V}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}-3\delta k
.

Finally, in order for
\mathcal{A}
to be correct, we will need
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
to be noticeably smaller than
1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}-3\delta k
. As long as
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
is noticeably smaller than 1, we can pick an appropriate inverse-polynomial
\delta
that makes this happen.

2.4.1
Working with Computational Zero-Knowledge

Up until this point, we’ve been applying zero-knowledge with respect to a distinguisher
\mathcal{D}
that
perfectly
answers questions about the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
—in particular, one that checks if a given prover message better than the bottom
(1-\delta)
fraction of the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
.
Here recall that a message
a
is better than
b
if the probability that
\widetilde{P}
convinces
\mathsf{V}
is higher if
\widetilde{P}
chooses
a
in its next round, compared with
b
.

If we want to work with protocols that only have computational zero-knowledge, we need to come up with an efficient distinguisher that still works well enough for our proof.

Our efficient distinguisher will try to
approximate
the fraction of messages from
\mathsf{Sim}
that a given message is better than.
To do this, it will sample a large number of possible next messages from the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
and compare them to the given message. Formally, it will sample
\poly(n,\frac{1}{\delta})
such messages, and check if at most a
2\delta
fraction of them are better than the given prover message.
By a Chernoff bound, we have that if the given prover message is in fact better than a
(1-\delta)
fraction of all possible messages in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
, this check will pass with overwhelming probability.

As in the previous section, we note that while we may not be able to compute the
exact
success probability efficiently, the same estimation procedure can with overwhelming probability get us within an additive factor of
\delta
for any inverse-polynomial
\delta
. Thus, except with negligible probability, we know that our distinguisher will say “real” if
any
prover message in its input transcript is at least
2\delta
better than the bottom
(1-\delta)
fraction of messages in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
.

By a careful analysis, we can show that
that this efficient distinguisher still works for the analysis in the previous section.
Indeed, there are only two points where we had to use properties of the distinguisher: first to argue that it rarely outputs “real” on a simulated transcript, and second to argue that
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
does “almost as well” as
\mathsf{P}
conditioned on the distinguisher saying “simulated”.

•

For the former case, we claim that the efficient distinguisher says “real” on a transcript from
\mathsf{Sim}
with probability at most
2\delta k
. For any given round, we know that the prover message in the transcript was generated by
\mathsf{Sim}
, and hence comes from the same distribution as the messages the distinguisher samples. Thus, the probability that the message in the transcript is in the top
2\delta
fraction of the sampled messages is just
2\delta
, regardless of what procedure we use to compare them.

•

For the latter case, we note that the only change between this section and the last is that the efficient distinguisher may allow
\mathsf{P}
to have messages that have up to
2\delta
higher success probability than the bottom
(1-\delta)
fraction of messages in the support of
\mathsf{Sim}
. Since in the last section we already allowed
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
to output messages that have up to
2\delta

lower
probability than the top
\delta
fraction of messages, we essentially are just doubling the error from there.

In both cases, we see that setting
\delta
to be half as large as the setting of
\delta
in the previous section suffices to make the same analysis go through.

2.4.2
Working with Private Coins

The above proof critically relies on the zero-knowledge protocol we start with being public-coin so that we can efficiently simulate
\mathsf{V}
starting from an arbitrary transcript and thus estimate the probability of it accepting. However, we note that at least intuitively, we shouldn’t have to make this assumption: if
\mathsf{V}
is able to computationally hide information about its randomness in a private-coin protocol, intuitively the map from
\mathsf{V}
’s randomness to its messages is a one-way function.

We formalize this intuition in Appendix
A
, showing that either infinitely-often auxiliary-input uniform-secure one-way functions exist or every constant-round private-coin protocol can be converted into an equivalent public-coin protocol.

We note that, unlike in the case of public-coin protocols, we obtain
auxiliary-input
one-way functions. This is because in our transformation, in order to achieve soundness the verifier has to resample its randomness such that it is consistent with the full transcript. Since the verifier does not have access to the malicious prover’s code, there does not immediately seem to be a way to construct an efficient sampler such that UE on that sampler will allow us to do this. We get past this by allowing the sampler to take the transcript as auxiliary input, but this limits our result to achieving auxiliary-input one-way functions. Since this auxiliary input comes from the prover messages and not from the worst-case instance, switching to average-case hardness does not seem to improve the situation. Nonetheless, our results are sufficient to rule out information-theoretic constructions of (constant-round) non-trivial ZK even in the private-coin setting.

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{Hyb}_{i}^{2}}[\mathsf{V}(x,\tau)=0\land\overline{R_{i}}]+\frac{4}{p_{\mathsf{Est}}(n)}=\Pr_{\mathrm{Hyb}_{i}^{1}}[\mathsf{V}(x,\tau)=0\land\overline{R_{i}}]+\frac{4}{p_{\mathsf{Est}}(n)}

where the equality comes from the fact that
\tau
is sampled from the same distribution in the two hybrids. Plugging this all in, we get that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{Hyb}_{i}^{2}}[\mathsf{V}(x,\tau^{\prime})=0\land\overline{R_{i}}]\leq\Pr_{\mathrm{Hyb}_{i}^{1}}[\mathsf{V}(x,\tau)=0\land\overline{R_{i}}]+\frac{4}{p_{\mathsf{Est}}(n)}+\frac{1}{8k\cdot p(n)}+e^{-n}

Noting that
\frac{4}{p_{\mathsf{Est}}(n)}+\frac{1}{8k\cdot p(n)}+e^{-n}\leq\frac{1}{4k\cdot p(n)}
for sufficiently large
n
, we get the desired claim.
∎

∎

5.4
Putting It All Together

Proof of Theorem
8
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that ai-OWFs don’t exist, and hence
\Gamma
is infinite. We then have that Algorithm
7
satisfies the requirements of Definition
7
with respect to
a(n)=1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}(n)-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}(n)-\frac{1}{2p(n)}
and
b(n)=\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}(n)
. Indeed, Lemma
9
immediately tells us that we satisfy the first two points for infinitely many
n
. For the third point, we have that for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle a(n)-b(n)=1-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}(n)-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}(n)-\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}(n)-\frac{1}{2p(n)}\geq\frac{1}{2p(n)}

where the inequality comes from the definition of
p(n)
. Thus, we have that
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{ioBPP/poly}
, so by Remark
6
, we have that
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{ioP/poly}
. This provides our contradiction.
∎

Remark 9
.

Theorem
8
can also be phrased in the following way: if
\mathcal{L}
has a constant-round, public-coin
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
-ZK argument with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<_{n}1
, then
\mathcal{L}
has a decision-to-inversion reduction (Definition
10
). The reasoning for this is identical to that in Remark
7
.

Given the above remark, the techniques from
[
7
,
10
]
immediately allow us to improve Theorem
8
from an
auxiliary-input
one-way function to a standard one-way function.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose that every
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{NP}
has a constant-round, public-coin
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
-ZK argument with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<_{n}1
and
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
. Then OWFs exist.

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
, we know there is a language
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{NP}
(that thus has an appropriate argument) where
\mathcal{L}\not\in\mathsf{ioP/poly}
. Theorem
8
thus tells us that there exists an auxiliary-input one-way function. We can then plug this into Lemma
1
to get a new language
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{NP}
and a polynomial
m(\cdot)
such that
(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\{U_{m(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})\not\in\mathsf{ioAvgBPP/poly}
. But since
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{NP}
(and thus has an appropriate argument), Remark
9
tells us that there is a decision-to-inversion reduction for
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. Hence, Lemma
2
tells us that one-way functions exist.
∎

6
Applications to Amplification

Similar to
[
5
]
, we can plug the one-way function we get from a high-error zero-knowledge protocol into prior work in order to get (almost) unconditional amplification of these high-error protocols. Below, we separately consider the cases of non-interactive zero-knowledge and interactive zero-knowledge.

6.1
Non-Interactive Zero-Knowledge

In the non-interactive case, a recent line of works
[
6
,
4
,
3
,
1
]
has explored what assumptions are needed in order to amplify (adaptively-sound) NIZKs with high errors to have negligible errors, with
[
3
,
1
]
only requiring one-way functions in certain cases. If we instantiate these results with the one-way function we get from Corollary
3
, we get that we can amplify such NIZKs (almost) unconditionally. Formally, we recall the following theorem from
[
1
]
, rephrased slightly to fit our terminology.

Theorem 9

(
[
1
]
, Theorem 8.27)
.

Suppose OWFs exist. Then if there exists an
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
-NIZK proof with adaptive and statistical soundness for a language
\mathcal{L}
with (1)
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}
negligible and (2)
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}<_{n}1
, then there exists an
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}^{\prime})
-NIZK with adaptive and statistical soundness for
\mathcal{L}
with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}^{\prime}
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}^{\prime}
all negligible.

Combining this with Corollary
3
immediately gives us the following result.

Corollary 5
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
or
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}
. Suppose further that all of
\mathsf{NP}
has
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
-NIZKs with adaptive and statistical soundness where (1)
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}
is negligible and (2)
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}<_{n}1
. Then all of
\mathsf{NP}
has
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}^{\prime})
-NIZKs with adaptive and statistical soundness where
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}^{\prime}
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}^{\prime}
are all negligible.

Proof.

First, suppose
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}
. In this case, every
\mathcal{L}
in
\mathsf{NP}
has a trivial NIZK (
\mathsf{P}
sends nothing and
\mathsf{V}
checks the instance on their own), meaning the claim holds trivially.

Otherwise, suppose
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
. By Corollary
3
, we get that one-way functions exist; we can then use Theorem
9
to amplify the NIZK guaranteed for any
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{NP}
to have negligible errors.
∎

In the interactive case, a classic result of
[
2

Similar to the non-interactive case, we can instantiate this result using the one-way function we get from Corollary
4
in order to (almost) unconditionally “amplify” (actually, just build from scratch)

Theorem 10

(
[
2
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

Suppose OWFs exist. Then for any
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{NP}
, there exists a four-round

-ZK argument for
\mathcal{L}
with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}
all negligible.

Combining this with Corollary
4
immediately gives us the following result.

Corollary 6
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}
or
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}
. Suppose further that all of
\mathsf{NP}
has a constant-round, public-coin
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}})
-ZK argument with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}<_{n}1
. Then all of
\mathsf{NP}
has a 4-round
(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}^{\prime})
-ZK argument with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{c}}^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}^{\prime}
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{zk}}^{\prime}
all negligible.

Proof.

First, suppose
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}
. In this case, every
\mathcal{L}
in
\mathsf{NP}
has a trivial zero-knowledge protocol (
\mathsf{P}
sends nothing and
\mathsf{V}
checks the instance on their own), meaning the claim holds trivially.

Otherwise, suppose
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{ioP/poly}

Appendix A
Private to Public Coin in the Absence of ai-OWFs

Theorem 11
.

If there exists an argument system that satisfies the following properties:

•

It satisfies correctness up to
\epsilon_{c}
error.

•

It satisfies soundness up to
\epsilon_{s}
error.

•

There exists a simulator such that the output of the simulator is at most
\epsilon_{zk}
distinguishable from the transcript of an honest execution of the protocol. Note that this is weaker than honest-verifier zero-knowledge since the simulator does not simulate the entire view of the verifier, only the transcript.

•

For some constant
t\in\mathbb{N}
the first
t
verifier messages are uniformly random strings.

Then either io-uniform-ai-OWFs exist, or for every polynomial
q
, there exists an argument system that satisfies the following properties:

•

It satisfies correctness up to
\epsilon_{c}+1/q(\lambda)
error.

•

It satisfies soundness up to
\epsilon_{s}+1/q(\lambda)
error.

•

There exists a simulator such that the output of the simulator is at most
\epsilon_{zk}+1/q(\lambda)
distinguishable from the transcript of an honest execution of the protocol.

•

The first
t+1
verifier messages are uniformly random strings.

Additionally, the new prover, verifier, and simulator run in time polynomial in the original prover, verifier, and simulator runtimes.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we model the verifier
V
as sampling its first
t
messages
r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}
uniformly at random. Then, before sending the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
message,
V
samples internal randomness
r
.
For
i\geq t+1
, the
i^{\text{th}}
verifier message is computed by a deterministic polynomial-time machine
V_{i}
that takes as input the transcript
\tau
and the randomness
r
.
Additionally suppose there exist polynomials
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
such that the size of the transcript just before the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
verifier message is
p_{2}(\lambda)
and
|r|=p_{1}(\lambda)
.

Define
f(a,x)=V_{t+1}(a,x)
. If
f
is a
1/2q(\lambda)
-distributional (infinitely-often, uniform-secure) auxiliary-input one-way function we are done, since by
[
9
]
it may be amplified to construct an (infinitely-often, uniform-secure) auxiliary-input one-way function.
Suppose not, i.e., there exists a PPT machine
A
such that for all large enough
\lambda
, for any
a\in\{0,1\}^{p_{1}(\lambda)}
, and for
x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{p_{2}(\lambda)}
,

\{V_{t+1}(a,x),A(a,V_{t+1}(a,x))\}\approx_{\frac{1}{2q(\lambda)}}\{V_{t+1}(a,x),x\}.

where the approximation symbol represents statistical indistinguishability.
In particular,

\displaystyle\{V_{t+1}(a,x),x,A(a,V_{t+1}(a,x))\}\approx_{\frac{1}{q(\lambda)}}\{V_{t+1}(a,x),A(a,V_{t+1}(a,x)),x\}.

(2)

We proceed by a sequence of hybrids, where the final hybrid will represent a proof system where the first
t+1
verifier messages are uniformly random.

Hybrid 0:
Identical to the honest protocol.

Hybrid 1:

The verifier behaves identically to the honest verifier except it appends
A(\tau,V_{t+1}(\tau,r))
to its
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
message, where
\tau
is the transcript of the first
t
messages.
This appended value is ignored by all subsequent verifier and honest prover steps.

The protocol preserves correctness since the appended value is ignored by both parties. It preserves soundness since the appended value can be sampled by the malicious prover themselves and is ignored by the verifier.
The simulator for (honest-verifier) zero-knowledge can run the simulator from Hybrid 0 and then sample
A(V_{t+1}(\tau,r))
and append to the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
verifier message in the simulated transcript. Since the honest transcript distribution in Hybrid 1 can be sampled by sampling an honest transcript in Hybrid 0 and running the same sample-and-append step, switching between Hybrid 0 and Hybrid 1 preserves simulator security.

Hybrid 2:

Identical to Hybrid 1 except the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
message is computed as
V_{t+1}(\tau,r)\|r
, and all subsequent verifier steps use
A(\tau,V_{t+1}(\tau,r))
in place of
r
. In other words, after computing
V_{t+1}(\tau,r)
the roles of
r
and
A(\tau,V_{t+1}(\tau,r))
are switched.

By (
2
), swapping
A(a,V_{t+1}(\tau,r))
and
r
in the transcript after
V_{t+1}(\tau,r)
can be detected with advantage at most
\frac{1}{q(\lambda)}
, even by unbounded adversaries.
Therefore, completeness, soundness, and simulation security are all preserved upto
1/q(\lambda)
error. For simulation security we crucially use the fact that the simulator only simulates the transcript instead of the full view of
V
, since (
2
) does not hold if given access to the randomness of
A
, which is part of the view of
V
.

Hybrid 3:

Identical to Hybrid 2 except the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
message consists only of the randomness
r
.

A(a,V_{t+1}(\tau,r))
is computed before the
(t+2)^{\text{th}}
verifier message instead of before the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
message.

Since
V_{t+1}(\tau,r)
can be computed from the transcript, this change does not affect completeness, soundness, or simulation security.
The verifier now samples the first
t+1
messages uniformly at random, so we are done.
∎

Corollary 7
.

The existence of a constant-round
(\epsilon_{c},\epsilon_{s},\epsilon_{zk})
(honest-verifier) zero-knowledge argument implies either io-uniform-ai-OWFs exist, or for every polynomial
q
, there exists a public-coin constant-round
(\epsilon_{c}+1/q(\lambda),\epsilon_{s}+1/q(\lambda),\epsilon_{zk}+1/q(\lambda))
(honest-verifier) zero-knowledge argument.

Proof.

Follows from repeated application of Theorem
11
along with the following observations:

•

While there is polynomial blowup in runtime each time the theorem is applied, since there are constant rounds the final runtimes are polynomial.

•

While errors grow by an inverse polynomial additive term, we can set the to be small enough to obtain our desired final bounds.

•

While the theorem only guarantees a weakening of honest zero-knowledge, for public-coin protocols, both notions are the same. Therefore we recover honest-verifier zero-knowledge for the final protocol.

∎

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
2

Here we provide an explicit proof of Lemma
2
, noting that it is closely mirrors the proof of Lemma 3.1 in
[
10
]
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Let
(R^{(\cdot)},\{f_{x}\}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{*}},p(\cdot))
be the decision-to-inversion reduction for
\mathcal{L}
guaranteed by the statement of the lemma. We define the function
g(r_{1}\|r_{2})=\mathcal{D}(r_{1})\|f_{\mathcal{D}(r_{1})}(r_{2})
, and claim that
g
is a weak one-way function.

Indeed, suppose for the sake of contradiction that
g
is not a weak one-way function. This means that there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that inverts
g
with probability at least
0.75
on infinitely many input sizes. Using
\mathcal{A}
, we define an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
to decide
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})
as follows. On input
x
,
\mathcal{B}
first samples a random
r_{2}
and runs
\mathcal{A}(x,f_{x}(r_{2}))
. If
\mathcal{A}
fails to find a preimage of
f_{x}(r_{2})
,
\mathcal{B}
immediately halts and outputs
\perp
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{B}
outputs
R^{\mathcal{A}}(x)
.

We now claim that
\mathcal{B}
puts
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})\in\mathsf{ioAvgBPP/poly}
, which is a contradiction. Fix any
n
where
\mathcal{A}
succeeds in inverting
g
and that is sufficiently large for our guarantees on
R
to hold. To show that the first point of Definition
9
holds, we fix an
x\in\Supp(\mathcal{D}_{n})
and consider two possible cases. If
\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)
inverts
f_{x}
with probability at most
\frac{1}{p(n)}
, we have that the first step of
\mathcal{B}(x)
will cause it to output
\perp
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{p(n)}
. If instead
\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)
inverts
f_{x}
with probability at least
\frac{1}{p(n)}
, our guarantees on
R
tell us that (as long as the first step doesn’t output
\perp
),
\mathcal{B}(x)
will output
\mathcal{L}(x)
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{p(n)}
. Thus in either case, we have that
\mathcal{B}(x)\in\{\perp,\mathcal{L}(x)\}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{p(n)}
, which for sufficiently large
n
is at least
0.9
.

For the second point of Definition
9
, we note that
\mathcal{B}(x)
can only output
\perp
if
\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)
fails to invert
f_{x}
. Thus the probability of this happening over a random
x\overset{\$}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{D}_{n}
is exactly the probability that
\mathcal{A}
fails to invert a random output of
g
, which we know to be at most
0.25
.

Putting this all together, we have reached a contradiction, and so must have that
g
is in fact a weak one-way function. By Theorem
4
, this means that one-way functions exist.
∎
