Fully Dynamic Spectral Sparsification of Hypergraphs
Gramoz Goranci, Ali Momeni

Abstract
Spectral hypergraph sparsification, a natural generalization of the well-studied spectral sparsification notion on graphs, has been the subject of intensive research in recent years. In this work, we consider spectral hypergraph sparsification in the dynamic setting, where the goal is to maintain a spectral sparsifier of an undirected, weighted hypergraph subject to a sequence of hyperedge insertions and deletions. For any 0 < ε ≤ 1 0<\varepsilon\leq 1 , we give the first fully dynamic algorithm for maintaining an ( 1 ± ε ) (1\pm\varepsilon) -spectral hypergraph sparsifier of size OPEN n ​ r 3 ​ poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ n , ε − 1 CLOSE ) nr^{3}\operatorname{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n,\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right) with amortized update time OPEN r 4 ​ poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ n , ε − 1 CLOSE ) r^{4}\operatorname{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n,\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right) , where n n is the number of vertices of the underlying hypergraph and r r is an upper-bound on the rank of hyperedges. Our key contribution is to show that the spanner-based sparsification algorithm of Koutis and Xu (2016) admits a dynamic implementation in the hypergraph setting, thereby extending the dynamic spectral sparsification framework for ordinary graphs by Abraham et al. (2016).



1
Introduction

Sparsifying large graphs into smaller ones while provably retaining key graph-centric properties is a powerful algorithmic framework at the heart of scalable graph processing. A notable example is spectral sparsification, where reweighted subgraphs preserve essential spectral properties of the original graph. Rooted in the breakthrough work on ultra-fast solvers for Laplacian systems
[
ST04
]
, a series of advancements led to the development of essentially linear-time algorithms for computing spectral sparsifiers
[
SS08
,
ST11
,
BSS12
]
. Since then, these sparsifiers have been successfully applied to obtain significantly faster algorithms for a myriad of machine learning primitives, such as spectral clustering and graph partitioning
[
LGT14
,
PSZ17
,
Fen18
]
, pruning of neural network
[
Hoa+23
,
Lae23
]
, and graph learning
[
Cal+18
]
, to name a few.

In many downstream machine learning tasks, a common assumption is to endow the objects of interest with pair-wise relationships. While these relationships are naturally represented as graphs, they often fail to capture the complex interdependencies found in real-world networks, such as biological interactions or social connections
[
BCHJ04
]
. As highlighted in
[
ZHS06
]
, modeling these relationships using
hypergraphs
has a host of advantages, often leading to improved performance over graph-based methods in fundamental learning tasks like clustering, classification, and embedding. This raises the following natural question – an analogue of spectral sparsification for graphs:
Do hypergraphs admit spectral sparsifiers?
Recent research has increasingly focused on this question, leading to algorithms that achieve near-optimal sparsifier sizes
[
Kap+21
,
Kap+21a
,
OST23
,
JLS23
,
Lee23
]
.

In this work, we study spectral sparsification through the lens of an emerging and important aspect of large-scale hypergraph processing:
dynamism
. Concretely, given a hypergraph that undergoes an intermixed updates of hyperedge insertions and deletions, the goal is to efficiently maintain a sparsifier that spectrally approximates the input hypergraph under these updates. Dynamic maintenance of spectral sparsifiers is particularly relevant to real-world applications, where hypergraph data is constantly changing. Recomputing solutions to hypergraph-based optimization problems from scratch after each update is often computationally prohibitive, making efficient dynamic algorithms essential.

Our main contribution is the first fully dynamic algorithm for maintaining spectral sparsifiers of hypergraphs, with strong theoretical guarantees, as summarized in the theorem below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
,

\gamma\geq 1
be a constant, and
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
be an initially empty
n
-vertex hypergraph guaranteed to have at most
m
hyperedges of rank
at most
r
and weight ratio
\max_{i,j}w_{i}/w_{j}\leq W
throughout any sequence of hyperedge insertions and
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\gamma}}}\right)
hyperedge deletions.
Then, there is a fully dynamic algorithm that, with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lceil\log m\rceil/n^{2}}}\right)
against an oblivious adversary, maintains a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H
with an expected size of

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(nr^{3}\varepsilon^{-2}\log^{2}m\log^{5}n\log W}}\right)

and an expected amortized update time of

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{4}\varepsilon^{-2}\log^{2}m\log^{5}n\log r}}\right).

To put our result in context, note that reading the set of vertices that define a single hyperedge of rank
r
inherently requires
\Omega(r)
time, implying the update time must necessarily depend on
r
. When
r=2
, this result precisely recovers the dynamic spectral algorithm of ordinary graphs by Abraham et al.
[
Abr+16
]
. In fact, when
r
is a big constant – a setting which may be relevant to many applications, our algorithm achieves both nearly optimal sparsifier size and poly-logarithmic update time in the input parameters of the problem.

1.1
Technical Overview

The starting point of our algorithm is the now standard sampling technique that constructs a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier
{\tilde{H}}=(V,\tilde{E},\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}})
of a hypergraph
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
by sampling each hyperedge
e\in E
with probability
p_{e}
and then setting
\tilde{w}_{e}\leftarrow w_{e}/p_{e}
.
While the sampling itself can be implemented in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m}}\right)
time, the challenge lies in computing
p_{e}
for each
e\in E
efficiently.

The sampling probability
p_{e}
is typically inversely proportional to the effective resistance of hyperedge
e

[
BST19
]
. As a result, if we can upper bound the effective resistance for all hyperedges, we can avoid computing
p_{e}
for every
e\in E
.
This enables us to sample each hyperedge with constant probability
p
and then rescale its weight to
\tilde{w}_{e}\leftarrow w_{e}/p
.

Motivated by
[
Abr+16
,
OST23
]
, which both build on the work of
[
KX16
]
, our algorithm uses a set of spanners
B
to bound the effective resistance of
{H}\setminus{B}
, and then return
B
along with the sampled edges from
{H}\setminus{B}
(with constant probability
p
) as the
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H
.
More precisely, we find a sequence of
\alpha
-hyperspanners
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{t}
, where
T_{1}
is an
\alpha
-hyperspanners of
H
,
T_{2}
is an
\alpha
-hyperspanners of
H\setminus T_{1}
, and so on.
We call
B=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\cup\dots\cup T_{t}
a
t
-bundle
\alpha
-hyperspanner of
H
.
The hypergraph
B
guarantees that every hyperedge
e\in H\setminus B
has effective resistance of at most
4\alpha/rt
.
By choosing a proper value for
t
, we ensure that
p=1/4
for every hyperedge
e\in{H}\setminus{B}
.

A priori, maintaining such a nested bundle of hyperspanners in a dynamic setting is not straightforward. To tackle this, we first design a decremental algorithm that handle only hyperedge deletions. It is not hard to see that this algorithm can be generalized to a fully dynamic one at the cost of paying a
\log r
overhead in the update time, where
r
is the rank of
H
.
Next, we outline the high-level idea behind our decremental algorithm.

Assume that
\tilde{H}
contains the
t
-bundle
\alpha
-hyperspanner
{B}={T_{1}}\cup{T_{2}}\cup\dots\cup{T_{t}}
, and that a deletion of an edge from
H
has caused the removal of another hyperedge
e\in T_{j}
from
B
to maintain
B
as a
t
-bundle
\alpha
-hyperspanner.
Since
T_{j+1}
is an
\alpha
-spanner of
H\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{j}T_{i}
, if
e
is not removed from
H
, it must appears in
H\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{j}T_{i}
.
The same holds for
T_{j+2}
if
e
is not selected to be in
T_{j+1}
after the update, as
T_{j+2}
is an
\alpha
-spanner of
H\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{j+1}T_{i}
, and so on.
Thus, the deletion of
e
from
T_{j}
could potentially result in its insertion into the underlying hypergraphs used to maintain
T_{j+1},T_{j+2},\dots,T_{t}
.
This is expensive for two reasons: (1) handling the insertion of
e
into the underlying hypergraphs requires designing a fully dynamic algorithm to maintain the
\alpha
-hyperspanners, and (2) the insertion would need to be passed to
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
algorithms maintaining
T_{j+1},T_{j+2},\dots,T_{t}
.

To overcome this challenge, we ensure that
e
is removed from
T_{j}

only if
it is removed from
H
.
This property is called the
monotonicity property
, and we refer to an algorithm that supports this property as a
monotone algorithm
.
This addresses the concerns raised in the previous paragraph: (1) since the deletion of
e
from
T_{j}
does not result in the insertion of
e
into
{H}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{j}{T_{i}}
, we only need to design a decremental algorithm to maintain each
T_{i}
, and (2) the deletion does not affect the underlying hypergraphs used to maintain
T_{j+1},T_{j+2},\dots,T_{k}
.

Preliminaries

A hypergraph
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
with a vertex set
V
, a hyperedge set
E
and a weight vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{E}
defined in
E
, is a generalization of the standard definition of graphs in which each hyperedge consists of a subset of vertices.
We define
m=|E|
and
n=|V|
and usually refer to
H
as an
m
-hyperedge
n
-vertex hypergraph.
For an integer
r\geq 2
, we say that
H
is of rank
r
if
|e|\leq r
for every hyperedge
e\in E
.
We denote by
E(H)
the set of hyperedges of
H
.

For a hyperedge
e\in E
,
w_{e}
is the coordinate in
\boldsymbol{w}
corresponding to
e
.
Similarly, for a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
defined on
V
and a vertex
u\in V
,
x_{u}
is the coordinate in
\boldsymbol{x}
corresponding to
u
.

Spectral Hypergraph Sparsification.

For any input vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, the
energy
of hypergraph
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
on
\boldsymbol{x}
is defined as

Q_{H}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}\cdot\max_{u,v\in e}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{u}-x_{v}}}\right)^{2}.

We use
Q_{H}(\boldsymbol{x})
to define the notion of a spectral hypergraph sparsifier for
H
.
For sake of brevity, we refer to such sparsifier as a
spectral hypersparsifier
of
H
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
H=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
be a hypergraph and let
{\tilde{H}}=(V,\tilde{E},\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}})
be a sub-hypergraph of
H
, i.e.,
\tilde{E}\subseteq E
.
For
0<\varepsilon<1
, we say that
\tilde{H}
is a

(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H
if, for every vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
defined on
V
,

(1-\varepsilon)Q_{\tilde{H}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq Q_{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq(1+\varepsilon)Q_{\tilde{H}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2.1)

Note that for graphs (i.e., hypergraphs of rank
2
), this definition is equivalent to the definition of a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral sparsifier; for a graph
G=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
and a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, it follows that

Q_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{uv\in E}w_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{u}-x_{v}}}\right)^{2}=\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{G}\boldsymbol{x},

(2.2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{G}
is the Laplacian matrix of
G
defined as the
n\times n
matrix with non-diagonal coordinate
uv
equal to the negated weight
-w_{uv}
for every edge
uv\in E
, and diagonal coordinate
uu
equal to the weighted degree
\sum_{uv\in E}w_{uv}
of vertex
u
.

Effective Resistance

A common approach to studying the spectral properties of a graph
G=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
is to view it as an electrical network, where each edge
e\in E
acts as a resistor with resistance
r_{e}=1/w_{e}
, and each node has a potential.
Using this,
Equation

2.2
is the energy of the electrical flow when the potential vector
\boldsymbol{x}
is applied to the nodes.

The
effective resistance
R_{G}(u,v)
of a pair of vertices
u,v\in V
is defined as the potential difference between
u
and
v
when inducing one unit of current in the network.
Alternatively, it can be defined as

R_{G}(u,v)=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{u}-x_{v}}}\right)^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{G}\boldsymbol{x}},

and thus, bounding the effective resistance
R_{G}(u,v)
bounds the energy
Q_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and vice versa.

To compute
R_{G}(u,v)
in an electrical network (i.e., the graph obtained from
G
by setting
r_{e}=1/w_{e}
for every
e\in E
), we will utilize the well-known rules for combining the series and parallel resistors summarized in the following facts.

Fact 2.1
.

A series of resistors from
u
to
v
with resistances
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k}
can be replaced by a single resistor with endpoint
u
and
v
and an equivalent resistance of
\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}
.

Fact 2.2
.

A set of parallel resistors with endpoints
u
and
v
and resistances
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k}
can be replaced by a single resistor with endpoints
u
and
v
and an equivalent resistance of
1/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}1/r_{i}}}\right)
.

Since our analysis relies on computing
R_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
, in the rest of the text, we consider
1/w_{e}
as the
length
of the edge
e

Spanners and Hyperspanners.

We first discuss the concept of spanners in graphs and then consider its generalization to hypergraphs.
For a graph
G=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})

As discussed before, the length of an edge

Thus,

d_{G}(u,v)=\min_{\text{$(u,v)$-path $P$ in $G$}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{e\in P}\frac{1}{w_{e}}}}\right).

Definition 2.2

For a graph

To discuss the notion of hyperspanners, we first define the concept of hyperpaths.

Analogous to graphs, the length of the hyperpath is defined as
\sum_{i=1}^{k}1/w_{e_{i}}

We denote by

-hyperpath in

Definition 2.3
.

For a hypergraph
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
and
\alpha\geq 1
, a sub-hypergraph
{H^{\prime}}=(V,E^{\prime},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})

\alpha
-hyperspanner of

for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V
.

The Algorithm

In this section, we explain our fully dynamic algorithm for maintaining a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of a hypergraph
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
.
Following
[
BST19
]
, we describe our algorithm under the assumption that every hyperedge is of size
(r/2,r]
.
At the end of the section, we will generalize the algorithm to hypergraphs of rank
r
using the lemma below.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and suppose that there is an algorithm which, given any
m
-hyperedge
n
-vertex hypergraph
H^{\prime}
with hyperedges of size
(r/2,r]
, computes a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H^{\prime}
with size

r^{4}\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot S(m,n)

in
T(m,n,r,\varepsilon^{-1})
time, where
S
and
T
are monotone non-decreasing functions.
Then, for any
m
-hyperedge
n
-vertex hypergraph
H
of rank
r
, there is an algorithm for computing a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H
with size
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{4}\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot S(m,n)}}\right)
in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T(m,n,r,\varepsilon^{-1})\log r}}\right)
time.

The exponents of
r
and
\varepsilon
in
Lemma

3.1
are adjusted for our later use.
Before proving the lemma, we first prove the decomposability of spectral hypersparsifiers below.
This will be used in both the proof of
Lemma

3.1
and in the proofs that follow.

Lemma 3.2

(Decomposability)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
be a hypergraph, and
E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k}
be a partition of
E
.
For
1\leq i\leq k
, define
{H_{i}}=(V,E_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i})
as the sub-hypergraph of
H
induced by
E_{i}
.
Let
\tilde{H}_{i}
be a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H_{i}
.
Then, the hypergraph
{\tilde{H}}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}{\tilde{H}_{i}}
is a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
H
.

Proof.

By
Equation

2.1
, for every integer
1\leq i\leq k
and every vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(1-\varepsilon)Q_{{\tilde{H}_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq Q_{{H_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq(1+\varepsilon)Q_{{\tilde{H}_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Summing over all
i
, we have

(1-\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{{\tilde{H}_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{{H_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{{\tilde{H}_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Since
E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k}
is a partition of
E
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{{\tilde{H}_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=Q_{{\tilde{H}}}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{{H_{i}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=Q_{{H}}(\boldsymbol{x})
, thereby completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Adapted from
[
OST23
]
)
.

Let
{H}=(V,E,\boldsymbol{w})
be a hypergraph with hyperedges of size
(r/2,r]
, and let

be a
t
-bundle
\alpha
-hyperspanner of

Then, for every hyperedge

of
{H}\setminus{B}
and every pair of vertices

where

is the effective resistance of the pair
u,v
in the associated graph
{G_{H}}
of
H

Proof sketch.

Let

be a hyperedge in
{H}\setminus{B}
, let

be any pair of vertices from
e

{B}={T_{1}}\cup\dots\cup{T_{t}}

By the definition of
{B}
, for each

, there exists a hyperpath
\pi_{i}
in
T_{i}
of length at most
\alpha\cdot 1/w_{e}
that connects
u
and
v
(recall that the length of an edge

is

Thus, there are at least

hyperedge-disjoint paths connecting

and

in
H

In the associated graph

, each path

is represented as a sequence of intersecting cliques.
Since

for every

disjoint paths between each pair of vertices within the cliques, and each of these paths contain at most two edges.
Consequently, each path

represents at least

edge-disjoint parallel paths of length at most

in

We conclude that there are at least

edge-disjoint parallel paths in

of length at most

Using the parallel decomposition rule of resistances to compute the effective resistance between

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,

be a positive integer,

-hyperedge
n
-vertex hypergraph with hyperedges of size

-spectral hypersparsifier

{i_{\text{last}}}=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lceil\log\rho\rceil,\lceil\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m/m^{\star}}}\right)\rceil}}\right\},

Note that after the algorithm terminates,

-spectral hypersparsifier of

with probability at least

.
To do so, we use induction to show that, for every integer

, the inequality

Q_{{H_{{i_{\text{last}}}-p}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)^{p}Q_{{I_{p}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

holds for
{I_{p}}=\bigcup_{j={i_{\text{last}}}-p+1}^{i_{\text{last}}}{B_{j}}\cup{H_{i_{\text{last}}}}

.
We will use this later to prove that

{I_{i_{\text{last}}}}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{i_{\text{last}}}{B_{j}}\cup{H_{i_{\text{last}}}}
is a

(1\pm\varepsilon)
-spectral hypersparsifier of
{H_{0}}={H}
with probability at least

For

3.5
.
Suppose that the claim is true for

.

Q_{{H_{{i_{\text{last}}}-l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)^{l}Q_{{I_{l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

.
Since

, for every vector

\displaystyle Q_{{\tilde{H}_{{i_{\text{last}}}-l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=Q_{{B_{{i_{\text{last}}}-l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})+Q_{{H_{{i_{\text{last}}}-l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq Q_{{B_{{i_{\text{last}}}-l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)^{l}Q_{{I_{l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)^{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q_{{B_{{i_{\text{last}}}-l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})+Q_{{I_{l}}}(\boldsymbol{x})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)^{l}Q_{{I_{l+1}}}(\boldsymbol{x}),

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)

By symmetry,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon/(2k)}}\right)^{l+1}Q_{{I_{l+1}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq Q_{{H_{{i_{\text{last}}}-(l+1)}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

We now prove that

Assume that

Lemma 3.13

We first explain how we maintain the

\sigma
, and a positive integer

and the forest

in expected total time
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(mi\log n}}\right)

We now define the spanner

of

Definition A.5

(Clustering hierarchy)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a random permutation
\sigma
, and an integer
k\geq 2
, let
S_{0}\supseteq S_{1}\supseteq\dots\supseteq S_{k}
be subsets of
V
, where
S_{0}=V
and
S_{k}=\emptyset
, such that
S_{i+1}
is obtained by sampling each vertex of
S_{i}
with probability
n^{-1/k}
.

For every
1\leq i\leq k
, we define the clustering
C_{i}=C_{S_{i},\sigma,i}
on
G
with centers
S_{i}
and radius
i
.
We denote by
V_{i}
the set of all vertices covered by
C_{i}
.
i.e.,
V_{i}=\{v\in V\mid d(v,S_{i})\leq i\}
.
Moreover, we define the forest
F_{i}=F_{S,\sigma,i}
on
C_{i}
as defined before.

We denote by
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}
the clustering hierarchy on
G
.

Definition A.6

(Spanner
F
)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a random permutation
\sigma
, and an integer
k\geq 2
, let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}
be a clustering hierarchy on
G
with covered vertices
V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{k}
and forests
F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{k}
.
The spanner
F
consists of the following edges:

1.

Intra-cluster Edges: For every
1\leq i\leq k
,
F
consists of all the edges in the forest
F_{i}
defined on the clustering
C_{i}
.

2.

Inter-cluster Edges: For every
1\leq i\leq k-1
and for each
v\in V_{i}\setminus V_{i+1}
belonging to the cluster
C_{S_{i},\sigma,i}[s]
of
C_{i}
centered at
s
, if
v
has some neighbors in the cluster
C_{S_{i},\sigma,i}[s^{\prime}]
with
s^{\prime}\neq s
, then
F
consists of the edge
(v,v^{\prime})
where
v^{\prime}
is the neighbor of
v
in
C_{S_{i},\sigma,i}[s^{\prime}]
with the smallest possible ordering w.r.t.
\sigma
.

After an update in
G
, an edge in
F
may no longer correspond to items 1 and 2 above.
In such a case, we keep the edge in
F
until it is removed from
G
, while adding new edges to
F
that satisfy items 1 or 2.

Remark A.7
.

[
Abr+16
]
lets the inter-cluster edge

to be arbitrarily chosen, but we choose

to be the neighbor with the smallest possible ordering w.r.t.

Although this does not change the asymptotic analysis on the running time and the size of

Recall that every selected edge remains in

By choosing

This eliminates the need to add a new edge to

Remark A.8
.

The choices for intra-cluster and inter-cluster edges in

In this case, we have a set of parallel edges adjacent to the vertex with the least possible order.
Here, we arbitrarily choose one of these edges to be in

.
It is straightforward to see this approach does not change the guarantees compared to when
G
is simple.

To prove the update time for maintaining the spanner

Lemma A.9

(

For a vertex

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\log n}}\right)

Since a change of cluster for

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\deg(v)}}\right)

is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{v\in V}i\deg(v)\log n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(im\log n}}\right)

Since we support the monotonicity property, we keep more edges in

than the spanner of

BKS12
]
: due to the updates,

may change its parent in the forest

from

to

as follows.
If the neighbor

of

\sigma(v^{\prime\prime})<\sigma(v^{\prime})
, then

becomes the parent of

(v,v^{\prime\prime})

Lemma A.10

(
[
Abr+16
]
)
.

For a vertex
v\in V
, the expected number of times
v
changes its parent in the forest
F_{i}
is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\log n}}\right)
.

Proof.

Since parallel edges have the same endpoints, the expected number of parent changes in
G
is upper bounded by the expected number of parent changes in the underlying simple graph of
G
.
Therefore, we assume in this proof that
G
is a simple graph.

Suppose that
G
has gone under an arbitrary sequence of edge deletions.
Let
V_{j}
be the centers that had distance
j
from
v
at some point, and let
(s,v^{\prime})
be the configuration in which
v
was assigned to the cluster centered at
s
,
d(v,s)=j
, and
v^{\prime}
was the parent of
v
in
F_{i}
.

We first prove that while keeping its distance equal
j
from
S_{i}
,
v
cannot go back to the configuration
(s,v^{\prime})
after moving to another configuration due to an update.
There are two possible cases.

1.

If, after an edge deletion,
v
changes its configuration but stays in the same cluster.
i.e.,
v
changes its configuration from
(s,v^{\prime})
to
(s,v^{\prime\prime})
.
Since
v
has not changed its cluster, it follows that
d(v^{\prime},s)\leq d(v^{\prime\prime},s)=j

before
the update.
However, note that
\sigma(v^{\prime})\leq\sigma(v^{\prime\prime})
as
v^{\prime}
had chosen before
v^{\prime\prime}
to be the parent of
v
.
Thus, if
v
chooses
v^{\prime\prime}
as its parent only if
d(v,s)>j
after the update.
As
d(v,s)
is monotonic,
v
cannot come back to
(s,v^{\prime})
while keeping its distance form
S_{i}
equal to
j
.

2.

If
v
changes its configuration to
(s^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})
, where
s^{\prime}\neq s
.
Since
v
keeps its distance from
S_{i}
to be equal
j
, it follows that
d(v,s)=d(v,s^{\prime})=j

before
the update.
By construction,
\sigma(s)\leq\sigma(s^{\prime})
as
v
had been assigned to the cluster centered at
s
before the update.
However, since
v
changed its cluster,
d(v,s)>j
after the update.
As
d(v,s)
is monotonic,
v
cannot come back to
(s,v^{\prime})
while keeping its distance form
S_{i}
equal to
j
.

We now bound the expected number of times
v
changes its parent while keeping
d(v,S_{i})=j
.
We only need to compute the expected number of configurations that appear throughout any arbitrary sequence of edge deletions.
Let

be the number of configurations
v
visits while
d(v,S_{i})=j
.
Note that
k\leq n^{2}
.
Since
\sigma
is a random permutation, each configuration has an equal probability to happen first, which is equal to
1/k
.
Similarly, after visiting the
l
th configuration, the probability of moving to an unvisited configuration is
1/(k-l+1)
.
Thus, the expected number of configuration change is

\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k-l+1}=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log k}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right),

which also upper bounds the expected number of parent changes while
d(v,S_{i})=j
, as several consecutive configurations might assign

to different clusters with the same parent.

To upper bound the number of times
v
changes its parent in
F_{i}
, first note that once
d(v,S_{i})
is increased, it cannot be decreased later as
d(v,S_{i})
is monotonic.
Since in
F_{i}
,
1\leq d(v,S_{i})\leq i
and the expected number of times that
v
changes its parent while
d(v,S_{i})=j
is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)
, it follows that the number of parent changes in
F_{i}
is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\log n}}\right)
.
∎

Lemma A.11

(

-spanner of

Proof.

Suppose that

(

Proof.

is obtained by sampling each vertex of
