Fiber Post and Core Build-up in Endodontically Treated Severely Mutilated Tooth: A Case Report
Authors: Alka Gupta, Mukesh Sisodiya, Mukesh Soni, Apurva Kurhade, Vidushi Jaiswal, Shivani Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Restoring endodontically treated teeth with extensive coronal loss often necessitates the use of post and core systems, with fiber posts increasingly favored for their biomechanical compatibility with natural dentin. This case report presents the clinical use of prefabricated glass fiber posts and a resin composite core to rehabilitate a severely damaged maxillary anterior tooth. A 24-year-old male reported with trauma-induced fracture of tooth #11, leaving it unsuitable for conventional restorative approaches. The treatment plan included root canal therapy followed by placement of a fiber post, core build-up, and final restoration using a porcelain-fused-to-metal crown. Clinical procedures involved root canal preparation, appropriate fiber post selection, adhesive luting, core build-up with composite resin, and crown placement, all performed with strict adherence to bonding protocols to achieve optimal durability. The case highlights the advantages of fiber post systems, including favorable stress distribution, reduced incidence of root fractures, and improved esthetics compared to metallic posts. Potential challenges such as debonding and adhesive failure emphasize the need for precise technique. The successful outcome in this patient demonstrates that fiber post and core restorations provide a reliable, aesthetically pleasing, and biomechanically sound solution for the rehabilitation of severely compromised teeth.

INTRODUCTION

Endodontically treated teeth with significant coronal destruction are typically restored with a post and core followed by a prosthetic crown.[1] While cast post and core systems are traditionally used for their strength and fit, they can lead to fractures due to increased stress concentration and differences in Young’s modulus between dentin and metal. Prefabricated glass fiber posts have emerged as a preferable alternative, offering better biomechanical stability and reduced fracture risk.[2] Resin composite cores and glass fiber posts are increasingly used due to improvements in adhesive techniques, with the modulus of resin composites being more similar to dentin, reducing stress and preventing root fractures.[3] The larger cement layer at the post-dentin interface also helps absorb occlusal forces and reduce fractures, which are often confined to the cervical third and may be repairable.[4] Failures related to fiber posts are typically due to debonding between the post-cement interface, rather than cement-dentin bonding. Studies show that fiber post fractures are less common and have higher survival rates compared to cast post systems.[5] A clinical case is presented to highlight the benefits and technical steps of this approach over traditional methods.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 24-year-old male patient was referred to the department of Prosthodontics and crown and bridge, Government College of Dentistry, Indore, Madhya Pradesh, complaining of a fractured upper front tooth following trauma. Clinical findings reveal extensive coronal destruction of the maxillary right central incisor (tooth #11), rendering it non-restorable with conventional methods. Diagnosis was a non-vital tooth with a fractured tooth number 11 due to trauma-induced fracture. Patient consent form has been obtained before starting the procedure.

The patient’s treatment plan consists of three key steps. First, endodontic therapy will be performed to address pulpal necrosis through root canal treatment. This will be followed by a post and core restoration, where a prefabricated fiber post will be placed to rebuild the lost coronal structure, providing support for the final restoration. Lastly, a full-coverage crown, specifically a porcelain-fused-to-metal crown, will be placed as the final restoration to restore both the function and aesthetics of the tooth, ensuring long-term durability and a natural appearance.

Clinical procedure

The root canal was accessed using heated Rhein root canal pluggers, and gutta-percha was removed with a #2 Largo Peeso Reamer while preserving apical sealing. The canal was then prepared with #3 post drill. After preparation, a periapical radiograph was taken to assess the quality and determine the appropriate fiber post size, which was selected based on the anatomical dimensions. The Blue X-Post #3 was trimmed to the required length using a high-speed diamond bur and cleaned with alcohol. It was wetted with an XP BOND/Self Cure Activator bonding system, and the solvent was removed with an air syringe. The post was shielded from light until cementation.

The dentin walls of the root canal were etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 15 seconds, followed by water spraying for 20 seconds and drying with absorbent paper points. The fusion bond/light-cure bonding system was applied for 20 seconds, and excess adhesive was removed using absorbent paper points before air-blowing to evaporate the solvent.

Core-X flow material was applied directly to the fiber post surface and into the root canal and pulp chamber. The post was placed and stabilized, then light-cured for 40 seconds. Excess material was removed with a diamond bur, and a periapical radiograph confirmed the post-adaptation and root sealing.

The tooth was prepared for a porcelain-fused-to-metal crown, and a two-step putty-wash impression with polyvinyl siloxane was taken. The crown was cemented with self-adhesive resin cement (RelyX U200). Clinical, radiographic, preoperative, and postoperative images are shown in Figure 1.

DISCUSSION

Fiber post and core systems have become a cornerstone in the restoration of endodontically treated teeth, particularly when significant coronal tooth structure is compromised.[6] Their evolution reflects a shift toward materials that meet both the biomechanical and aesthetic demands of modern dentistry. Fiber posts possess a modulus of elasticity similar to that of natural dentin, which facilitates uniform stress distribution and reduces the risk of root fractures compared to rigid metal posts.[7] Additionally, the translucency of glass fiber posts makes them ideal for anterior restorations, minimizing the risk of discoloration and ensuring a natural appearance. Prefabricated fiber posts often require less tooth structure removal during placement, preserving more of the natural tooth and enhancing the overall prognosis. Furthermore, the use of fiber posts can streamline the restorative process by eliminating the need for laboratory fabrication, allowing for chairside placement and reducing treatment time. However, the success of fiber post restorations is highly dependent on proper adhesive protocols, as inadequate bonding can lead to debonding or microleakage.[8] To enhance fracture resistance, a minimum ferrule height of 2 mm is recommended; failure to achieve this increases the risk of failure. While fiber posts are less rigid than metal posts, they may not provide sufficient support in teeth with minimal remaining structure, necessitating careful case selection. Long-term studies have demonstrated survival rates of up to 94.1% over six years for teeth restored with prefabricated fiber posts, with failures generally being less catastrophic (e.g., debonding) compared to those associated with metal posts, which can lead to root fractures.[9] In recent years, the rehabilitation of grossly decayed teeth has seen significant advances, particularly in the use of fiber posts and core systems. A study by Ketaki N et al. (2025)[5] presents a case report on the rehabilitation of a severely decayed premolar using a custom-modified fiber post and zirconia crown. This approach underscores the versatility and strength of fiber posts in providing support for severely compromised teeth, while zirconia crowns offer superior aesthetics and durability.

CONCLUSION

Fiber post and core restorations have emerged as a reliable and effective solution for rehabilitating endodontically treated teeth, particularly those with significant loss of coronal structure. Their biomechanical compatibility with dentin and favorable clinical outcomes make them a preferred choice in contemporary restorative dentistry.
