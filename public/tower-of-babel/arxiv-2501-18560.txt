Bandits with Anytime Knapsacks
Eray Can Elumar, Cem Tekin, Osman Yağan

Abstract
We consider bandits with anytime knapsacks (BwAK), a novel version of the BwK problem where there is an anytime cost constraint instead of a total cost budget. This problem setting introduces additional complexities as it mandates adherence to the constraint throughout the decision-making process. We propose SUAK (Strategic Under-utilization for Anytime Knapsacks), an algorithm that utilizes upper confidence bounds to identify the optimal mixture of arms while maintaining a balance between exploration and exploitation. SUAK is an adaptive algorithm that strategically utilizes the available budget in each round in the decision-making process and skips a round when it is possible to violate the anytime cost constraint. In particular, SUAK slightly under-utilizes the available cost budget to reduce the need for skipping rounds. We show that SUAK attains the same problem-dependent regret upper bound of O ⁡ ( K ​ log ⁡ T ) O(K\log T) established in prior work under the simpler BwK framework, where K K denotes the number of arms, and T T denotes the time horizon. We also establish an instance-dependent lower bound showing that the O ⁡ ( K ​ log ⁡ T ) O(K\log T) dependence is unavoidable. Finally, we show via extensive simulations that SUAK achieves considerably higher rewards than naive adaptations of BwK algorithms that do not optimize learning under anytime cost constraints.



1
Introduction

Multi-armed bandits (MAB) is one of the fundamental problems in the field of sequential decision-making under uncertainty. In its essence, it is a problem setting where an agent must strategically allocate resources among the arms to maximize cumulative reward over time, navigating the trade-off between gathering information about uncertain arms (exploration) and exploiting known information to optimize immediate rewards (exploitation). This problem finds applications across diverse domains, including reinforcement learning
(
Intayoad et al., 2020
)
, online advertising
(
Slivkins, 2013
)
, clinical trials
(
Villar et al., 2015
)
, and resource allocation
(
Soare, 2015
)
.

The
Bandits with Knapsacks
(BwK) problem, introduced by
Badanidiyuru et al. (2013)
, is an extension of the classical multi-armed bandit problem, with the additional constraint of limited resource capacity akin to the
knapsack
problem
(
Tran-Thanh et al., 2012
)
. In this scenario, an agent is confronted with a set of arms, each associated with an
unknown
reward and cost distribution. Unlike the traditional bandit setting, selecting an arm incurs both a reward and a cost here, and the objective is to maximize the total reward while respecting the total capacity constraint of the knapsack. The BwK problem encapsulates the trade-off between exploration and exploitation while managing resource constraints, presenting a rich framework with applications such as online advertising
(
Avadhanula et al., 2021
;
Badanidiyuru et al., 2018
)
, dynamic resource allocation
(
Kumar and Kleinberg, 2022
)
, and personalized recommendations
(
Yu et al., 2016
)
.

In this paper, we consider a specific variant of this problem, which we call the bandits with anytime knapsacks (BwAK) problem, where instead of a total cost budget there is an anytime constraint on the average cost. This problem setting introduces an additional level of complexity as a mixture strategy needs to be employed to be able to pull arms with mean costs higher than the average cost budget without violating the anytime constraint.

1.1
Limitations of the State-of-the-Art

Standard BwK algorithms are designed for a
total
resource budget over the horizon. A policy can satisfy a budget
cT
even if its early consumption exceeds rate
c
, provided that its terminal consumption remains within the total budget. BwAK instead requires

S_{c}(t)\leq ct,\qquad\forall t\geq 1,

where
S_{c}(t)
is the cumulative realized cost through round
t
. Hence a policy that is feasible under the standard BwK criterion can violate the BwAK constraint at an intermediate round. At round
t
, the learner can use only the accumulated budget slack
ct-S_{c}(t)
. Existing BwK algorithms use, among other approaches, optimistic estimates of the reward–cost optimization problem, primal–dual resource prices, or allocations based on the remaining budget and horizon; these methods are reviewed in §
2.1
. None of these approaches, without an additional feasibility mechanism, guarantees the prefix-wise constraint above for every realized cost sequence
(
Badanidiyuru et al., 2013
;
Agrawal and Devanur, 2014
;
Flajolet and Jaillet, 2015
;
Li et al., 2021
)
.

A natural adaptation to overcome this is to pull the arm selected by a BwK rule only when that pull cannot violate the current prefix constraint and to pull the null arm otherwise. This enforces feasibility, but the underlying BwK rule is not designed to accumulate budget slack for future high-cost pulls. It can therefore remain close to the constraint boundary, increasing the number of null-arm pulls imposed by this feasibility check. We introduce a baseline,
Safe-Skip BwK (SS-BwK)
, in §
3.1
; it applies this feasibility check to a standard BwK decision rule. The simulations show that this direct adaptation can incur more regret from null-arm pulls than a policy that considers the anytime constraint when deciding which arm to pull.

1.2
On the Importance of Anytime Constraints

The formulation of the anytime constraint considered here has broad applications across various fields. A notable example is inventory management, where a factory produces goods at a constant rate and seeks to maximize revenue by selling to buyers in a marketplace, where bids consisting of price and order size are placed.
Our anytime constraint is especially relevant in such scenarios since having a negative inventory is not possible.
An important aspect of this constraint is that it introduces the trade-off between exploiting the available inventory, and skipping a round to accumulate more inventory in order to capture bids with higher order sizes.
This highlights the added complexity of our problem setting and underscores its broader applicability in settings beyond the standard BwK framework.

Another example is in clinical trials, where drugs or treatment plans with unknown costs, which can be side effects; and rewards, which can model treatment efficacy; need to be evaluated. This example can be modeled using the anytime constraint if participants are recruited to the trials at a constant rate per week, and are not allowed to trial more than one drug. In online advertising, an advertiser may have a daily budget limit to prevent overspending. In this context, the ‘arms’ represent different ad campaigns or strategies, each with varying costs that can be selected for the day. The reward can be modeled as the daily revenue generated from clicks or the number of users who subscribe.
One last example is in satellite systems, where solar panels generate energy and excess energy can be stored in a battery. Here,
c
can correspond to the energy generated per unit time, and arms can correspond to different tasks that need to be performed, with their rewards reflecting the importance of the tasks. The cost of an arm then represents the energy needed for the task.

Contributions

1.

SUAK Algorithm:
SUAK utilizes the upper confidence bounds to explore the optimal base that solves the
problem, and also strategically under-utilizes the available budget to limit the number of rounds that are skipped when satisfying the anytime constraint. It is also horizon-free; SUAK takes only the average cost target
c
as an input parameter, and does not need knowing
T
beforehand.

2.

Theoretical Analysis:
We provide an
O(K\log T)
instance-dependent upper bound on the regret of SUAK which shows linear dependence on the number of arms. We also show an instance-dependent regret bound of
\Omega(K\log T/\Delta)
where
\Delta
is the reward gap of an arm.

3.

Technical Novelty:
Our analysis departs from classical UCB-style proofs by exploiting the control logic of
SUAK, which pulls an arm whenever its cost confidence interval lies inside
c
and skips rounds to enforce
anytime cost constraints. This structure enables a nonstandard conditioning as the estimation error on the arm cost
gap will always be smaller than a threshold value. This also implies a lower bound on effective sample size for
arms, yielding tight multiplicative bounds, while a pull-probability lower bound on an arm couples base selections
to arm-level sampling without over-counting. Further, We jointly bound the regret due to under-budgeting and global skips by analyzing a coupled process with a negative-drift argument above a suitable barrier and Doob’s optional stopping theorem applied to a localized exponential supermartingale.

Problem Statement

We consider a
K
-armed stochastic bandit problem with the set of non-null arms
[K]
. For each arm
i\in[K]
, the reward–cost pairs
\{(r_{i}(t),\rho_{i}(t))\}_{t\geq 1}
are i.i.d. across rounds under a fixed joint distribution
P_{i}
supported on
[0,1]^{2}
, with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{i}}[r_{i}(t)]=\mu_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{P_{i}}[\rho_{i}(t)]=\rho_{i}.

(1)

The joint processes are independent across arms. We allow arbitrary dependence between reward and cost within the same arm and round. At each round
t
, the agent has the option of skipping by not pulling any of the
K
arms. We model this decision by introducing an arm with cost and reward equal to
0
, denoted by
\bot
, which is known as the
null arm
in the BwK literature. We let
\mathcal{A}:=[K]\cup\{\bot\}
denote the action set, and set
\rho_{\bot}=\mu_{\bot}=0
. Accordingly,
\bm{\rho}=[\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{K},\rho_{\bot}]^{T}
and
\bm{\mu}=[\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{K},\mu_{\bot}]^{T}
denote the mean cost vector and the mean reward vector indexed by
\mathcal{A}
, respectively. Throughout this paper, we use bold symbols to denote vectors or matrices. We use
\Delta_{m}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}x=1\}
for the probability simplex with
m
coordinates. We let
i^{**}:=\arg\max_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}
denote the arm with the highest mean reward and, for simplicity, assume this arm is unique.

Let
i(t)
be the arm pulled by the agent in round
t
,
r(t)
represent the reward received in round
t
, and
c(t)
represent the cost incurred in round
t
. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the filtration generated by the observed history, where
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
contains all information available before round
t
. The i.i.d. assumption above implies that, for every
i\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\!\left((r(t),c(t))\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\,i(t)=i\right)

\displaystyle=P_{i},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\,i(t)=i]

\displaystyle=\mu_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}[c(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\,i(t)=i]=\rho_{i},

(3)

In particular, adaptively selecting an arm using the preceding observed history does not alter the distribution of its current reward and cost observations. Note that the null arm is deterministic
(r_{\bot}(t),c_{\bot}(t))=(0,0)
. To make all action-selection quantities predictable, let

\displaystyle N_{i}(t):=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\mathds{1}\left\{i(s)=i\right\}}

(4)

denote the number of times arm
i
is pulled immediately before round
t
. Define
S_{c}(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}c(s)
and
\bar{c}(t)=S_{c}(t)/t
as the cumulative cost and the average cost incurred through round
t
. Whenever
N_{i}(t)>0
, we define the empirical reward and cost means as

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\rho_{i}(s){\mathds{1}\left\{i(s)=i\right\}}}{N_{i}(t)},\qquad\bar{\mu}_{i}(t):=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}r_{i}(s){\mathds{1}\left\{i(s)=i\right\}}}{N_{i}(t)}.

(5)

Define the realized cumulative reward through round
t
by
F(t):=\sum_{s=1}^{t}r(s)
. We assume that there is an average cost budget of
c
per round that cannot be exceeded at any round, which we refer to as the anytime cost constraint. The agent aims to maximize the expected cumulative reward received under this constraint. This objective can be
expressed as:

\displaystyle\max\ \mathbb{E}[F(t)]\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{u}c(s)}{u}\leq c,\quad\forall u\leq t.

(6)

We call this setting, which captures many practical applications as discussed in §
1.2
, the
Bandits with Anytime Knapsacks (BwAK)
problem. Ideally, regret would be measured relative to the optimal policy that knows all reward and cost distributions and maximizes expected reward while satisfying the anytime constraint. Because this policy is difficult to characterize, we instead use the following linear relaxation and define regret relative to its optimal value.

Linear Relaxation.
Following the prior work, we consider the following linear relaxation:

\displaystyle OPT_{LP}(T)=\max_{\bm{\pi}}\

\displaystyle T\cdot\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\pi}

(7)

s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}^{T}\bm{\pi}\leq c,\ \bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K+1}.

(8)

where the vector
\bm{\pi}
represents the policy which defines the fraction of time an arm will be pulled. Because the feasible region is a polytope defined by a single resource constraint and the simplex boundary, standard linear programming theory implies that an optimal basic feasible solution exists with at most two non-zero coordinates (i.e., at most two arms will have nonzero
\pi_{i}
values). We refer to the support of a basic feasible solution as a
base
and, use
\mathds{V}
exclusively for the canonical feasible LP vertices:

\displaystyle\mathds{V}:=

\displaystyle\ \bigl\{\{i\}:i\in\mathcal{A},\ \rho_{i}\leq c\bigr\}

(9)

\displaystyle\ \cup\bigl\{(h,\ell):h,\ell\in\mathcal{A},\ h\neq\ell,\ \rho_{h}>c>\rho_{\ell}\bigr\}.

(10)

Thus a two-arm base is a cost-
c
boundary vertex with its higher-cost arm listed first, while a one-arm base is a feasible single arm. Note that
\{\bot\}\in\mathds{V}
, and a support consisting of two arms whose mean costs are both strictly below
c
cannot be considered a two-arm base.

We let
\mathcal{I}^{i}:=\{\mathcal{I}\in\mathds{V}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}
denote the set of bases that include arm
i
, let
\bm{\pi}^{*}
denote an optimal basic feasible solution of (
7
), and let
r^{*}:=\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\pi}^{*}
be its optimal reward per round. We also define
\mathcal{I}^{*}:=\{i:\pi_{i}^{*}>0\}\in\mathds{V}
as the optimal base.

Let
OPT
denote the total expected reward of a dynamic policy in
T
rounds that conforms to a total budget constraint over
T
rounds as in the standard BwK literature instead of the anytime budget constraint we consider here. It was shown that
OPT_{LP}\geq OPT

(
Badanidiyuru et al., 2013
)
.

Let
REF
denote the total expected reward of a dynamic policy over
T
rounds that conforms to the average cost constraint. This constraint is stricter than the total budget constraint. This can easily be seen as satisfying the anytime constraint in the last round
T
with
c=B/T
produces the total budget constraint of
B
over
T
rounds. Hence,
OPT_{LP}\geq OPT\geq REF
. While regret could be defined as the difference between expected cumulative reward of SUAK and
REF
, we choose a stronger regret definition by defining it with respect to
OPT_{LP}
as

\displaystyle R_{T}=OPT_{LP}-\mathbb{E}\left[F(T)\right]=Tr^{*}-\mathbb{E}\left[F(T)\right]

(11)

so that our results can be compared with prior work on the total budget setting.

2.1
Related Works

In this subsection, we present some of the works that are relevant to our problem setting.

Bandits with knapsacks:

The BwK problem has been studied before and algorithms that achieve optimal problem-independent regret bounds of
O(\sqrt{K\cdot OPT})
have already been developed
(
Agrawal and Devanur, 2014
)
. However, deriving problem-dependent lower bounds that reflect the resource-learning difficulty, and developing algorithms that attain such bounds, are still open questions.
One notable work in this field is by

Flajolet and Jaillet (2015)
, in which the BwK problem is considered under three cases with
1
,
2
; and
d
constraints. For
2
constraints,
a regret bound of
O\left(\lambda^{2}K^{2}\log T/(\delta_{\min}^{3}\Delta)+K^{2}\sigma\log T/\delta_{\min}^{3}\right)
is achieved where
\delta_{\min}
is the minimum cost gap;
\sigma
is the minimum
1/\mu
value where
\mu
denotes the mean reward;
\lambda=1+2\kappa
; and
\kappa
is a constant assumed to be known
a priori
such that
|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}|\leq\kappa|\rho_{i}-\rho_{j}|
for any
i,j
. For the
d
constraint setting, a regret bound of
O(2^{K+d}\log T)
is achieved. The
2
-constraint setting is similar to our work, as our one-dimensional cost, and the anytime cost constraint add up to two constraints for the LP. Their regret bound has quadratic dependence on
K
, whereas our regret bound is linear in
K
. The lower bound in §
4.2
shows that linear dependence on
K
together with logarithmic dependence on
T
is unavoidable on some problem instances.

Another notable prior work is by
Li et al. (2021)
, where a
d
-dimensional cost vector is considered.
They propose a two-phase algorithm where the first phase pulls each arm the same number of times until all the suboptimal arms are eliminated. In the second phase, the base with the highest UCB index is chosen. Their analysis also develops LP-based decompositions of regret and uses remaining-resource LP structure; these are established tools that our proof adapts to the anytime setting rather than claims as new.
They achieve a regret bound of
O(Kd\log T/(b^{3}\Delta^{2})+d^{4}/(b^{2}\min\{\chi^{2},\Delta^{2}\}\min\{1,\sigma^{2}\}))
, where
\Delta
in their setting is defined as the gap between the reward of the optimal solution per round and the maximum reward that can be obtained per round when one arm is removed;
b
is the average cost budget per round;
\chi
is the minimum nonzero value in the optimal policy;
\sigma
is a constant related to the linear dependency between arms.

Bandits with evolving arm-specific budgets:

Combes et al. (2015)
study stochastic bandits in which each arm has its own availability budget. Their general-budget formulation permits these arm-specific budgets to increase over time and even to depend on the sample path. They introduce the B-KL-UCB algorithm which attains
O(\log T)
regret. However, this setting differs structurally from BwAK. Exhausting the budget of one arm only changes the availability of that arm, and their oracle policy greedily selects the highest-mean available arm. In BwAK, by contrast, all arms consume the same cost budget and the cumulative incurred cost must satisfy
\sum_{s=1}^{t}c(s)\leq ct
for every
t
. A high-cost arm can therefore be used only by balancing it with lower-cost or null actions, and the LP optimum may be a boundary mixture of arms on opposite sides of
c
. Hence, while their logarithmic result is important for evolving budgets, it does not directly cover the BwAK problem we consider here.

Bandits with Replenishable Knapsacks:
In this setting, cost of an arm is allowed to be negative, which allows the knapsack to be replenished. In
Bernasconi et al. (2024c)
, a more general BwK formulation with long-term constraints is considered, which stipulates the total consumption of each resource at round
T
should be less than zero up to small sublinear violations. The costs can be negative as well as positive. This is again similar to our setting as our setting can be viewed as their setting where sublinear violations are not allowed. They propose a Primal-Dual algorithm-based framework that uses the
E
XP3-SIX algorithm, and achieve
O(\sqrt{KT}\log(KT))
instance-independent regret bound. They also remark that initial
o(T)
rounds can be skipped to cover the potential violations and implement the long-term constraint as a hard constraint like in our setting. They provide an upper bound of
O(\sqrt{KT})
constraint violations in
Bernasconi et al. (2024c, Corollary 8.2)
, which suggests an upper bound of
O(\sqrt{KT})
on the number of initial rounds that would need to be skipped to achieve hard constraints. Note that this upper bound might not be tight. This would lead to
O(\sqrt{KT})
gap-independent regret in our problem setting. In our work, we focus on instance-dependent regret bound and show that we achieve
O(K\log T)
instance-dependent regret for the same problem setting. In
Bernasconi et al. (2024b)
, they improve their prior work
Bernasconi et al. (2024c)
by using a UCB-based approach which lets them provide
O(\sqrt{T})
regret in stochastic settings without assuming Slater’s condition. The upper bound on constraint violations is still
O(\sqrt{KT})
, which would lead to an upper bound of
O(\sqrt{KT})
for the instance-independent regret in our problem setting. Another notable prior work is by
Slivkins et al. (2024)
,
where packing and covering constraints, as well as negative resource consumption, are considered. Their algorithm works when the initial budget is
B=\Omega(T)
, or
B=o(T)
, compared to the prior work which mostly restricts
B
to
B=\Omega(T)
. This is similar to our setting as our setting can be reduced to theirs by implementing the budget increase of

by subtracting

from the costs of all arms including the null arm. However, their algorithm is suboptimal in our setting with
B=0
, as they remark in the discussion of
Slivkins et al. (2024, Theorem 3.6)
, that their proposed algorithm LagrangeCBwLC
is suboptimal when

.
This is as expected since Lagrange-based algorithms generally require knowing the ratio

, which goes to infinity when

.

In
Bernasconi et al. (2024a)
, there exists an arm with a negative expected cost that allows to replenish the budget. However, this work cannot be used in our setting as their work can only be used when
B=\Omega(T)
,
and they only provide instance-independent bounds of
O(\sqrt{KT})
, whereas we provide
O(K\log T)
instance-dependent bounds.

BwK with non-monotonic resource utilization:
This setting generalizes the standard BwK model by allowing each action to induce a resource-drift vector whose components may be positive, negative, or zero. In each round, the learner observes both the reward and the realized resource drift, and the available budget of each resource is updated by the corresponding drift.
Kumar and Kleinberg (2022)
allow actions to have positive, negative, or zero expected resource drift and establish logarithmic regret with respect to an LP benchmark under nondegeneracy and separation assumptions.
Their model admits a direct reduction to the single-resource BwAK setting considered in this paper. For each non-null arm
i
, set its resource drift to

d_{i}=c-\rho_{i},

set the null-arm drift to

d_{\bot}=c,

and initialize the resource budget at zero. Then, after
t
rounds, the accumulated resource budget is

\sum_{s=1}^{t}d_{i(s)}=ct-\sum_{s=1}^{t}c(s).

Therefore, requiring this budget to remain nonnegative at every round is exactly equivalent to the BwAK anytime constraint. Under the same reduction, their LP drift constraint coincides with (
7
). Consequently, their results imply logarithmic instance-dependent regret for BwAK instances satisfying their assumptions. A direct quantitative comparison with SUAK is nevertheless difficult because the two analyses are expressed in terms of different problem-dependent parameters.
Under this reduction, their minimum drift-magnitude parameter becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{drift}}^{\rm KK}=\min\{c,\delta_{\min}\}.

(12)

If the unique optimal solution assumed in their analysis is a binding two-arm base
\{h,\ell\}
with
\rho_{h}>c>\rho_{\ell}
, their support-separation parameter is

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{support}}^{\rm KK}=\min\{\pi_{h}^{*},\pi_{\ell}^{*}\}=\frac{\min\{\delta_{h},\delta_{\ell}\}}{\delta_{h}+\delta_{\ell}},

(13)

and their conditioning parameter
\sigma_{\min}^{\rm KK}
is the smallest singular value of the corresponding constraint matrix

\displaystyle D_{h\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}-\delta_{h}&\delta_{\ell}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

If, instead, the single resource constraint is nonbinding at a unique optimal singleton
i^{*}
, their slack parameter becomes

\delta_{\mathrm{slack}}^{\rm KK}=c-\rho_{i^{*}}=\delta_{i^{*}}
. These quantities enter their control margin and learning constant. They are conceptually different from SUAK’s adaptive, data-dependent under-budgeting parameter
\omega(t)
defined in (
18
).

The comparison can therefore be summarized more narrowly. Their algorithm uses the horizon
T
, assumes a unique optimal LP support, and its regret bound has squared inverse dependence on its LP gap
\Delta
. In contrast, SUAK is horizon-free: it takes
c
, but not
T
, as an input; it does not require uniqueness of the true optimal support or externally supplied gap-dependent control parameters; and its regret bound has inverse dependence on the base reward gap. These distinctions, however, do not imply uniform dominance of one approach over the other. SUAK assumes that every non-null arm is separated from the threshold
c
, and its classification and reserve terms can become large when some
\delta_{i}
is small, even if that arm is not part of the optimal base.

Conservative and safe bandits:

Conservative bandits
is a framework where an action is considered safe at round
t
if it keeps the cumulative reward up to round
t
above a given fraction of a baseline policy. In
Wu et al. (2016)
, a baseline safe arm is given, and they provide an algorithm that utilizes the UCB principle where the baseline arm is pulled if the arm chosen by the UCB principle is not safe. In
Moradipari et al. (2020)
, an anytime constraint is used instead of a constraint on cumulative reward that demands the expected reward of the pulled arm to be greater than a given threshold with high probability. They provide an algorithm that starts with a safe baseline and utilizes confidence regions to explore the other safe arms.
Safe bandits
is a similar framework to conservative bandits where in each round, the agent is required to select an arm with a given property no less than a predetermined (safe) threshold with high probability. For example, in
Hou et al. (2023)
, the agent needs to choose at most
K
items from a set of
L
items; and with probability at least
1-\delta
, the sum of variances of the selected items should not exceed a given threshold, which they call as the anytime-safe constraint. They propose a two step approach where first a set of arms is selected according to the UCB principle, and if this selection exceeds the threshold, the selected arms are split and pulled over multiple rounds. Our work is also similar in the sense that we also implement constraint checking as an additional separate step in SUAK.

Algorithms for the BwAK

Before presenting the SUAK Algorithm, to demonstrate the additional complexities of our problem formulation over the standard BwK setting, and also to serve as a baseline, we present a naive approach which makes it possible to convert any BwK algorithm to our BwAK setting.

3.1
The Naive Approach

In this subsection, we consider a naive strategy that incorporates skipping into existing BwK algorithms, and show that it can lead to large regret. In this approach, in a round
t
, we first check if it is possible to violate the anytime constraint, and skip the round if it is the case. Otherwise, we let the BwK algorithm pull an arm. For this end, we use the
One Phase
Algorithm in
Li et al. (2021)
, and add skipping such that round
t
is skipped if
S_{c}(t-1)+1>ct
. This implementation, which we call the
Safe-Skip BwK (SS-BwK)
Algorithm, is provided in Algorithm
1
.

In this algorithm, the initialization phase consists of sampling each arm once while using skips to prevent violation of the constraint. After this phase, we utilize a skipping mechanism in lines
4
–
6
, and if the round is not skipped, the algorithm proceeds to the linear program in line
8
. Here
\bm{\mu}^{U}(t)
is the UCB of arm reward at round
t
and
\bm{\rho}^{L}(t)
is the LCB of arm cost at round
t
. We write
B_{r}(t)=cT-S_{c}(t-1)
for the total budget remaining before round
t
and
H_{r}(t)=T-t+1
for the number of remaining rounds. The decision variable
\bm{x}
is therefore an allocation-count vector over the remaining horizon, not a probability vector. In addition to the remaining-budget constraint, the LP explicitly includes the time-resource constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{x}=H_{r}(t)
. This equality is without loss of generality because the null arm has zero reward and zero cost and can absorb any otherwise unused allocation. Consequently,
\tilde{x}_{i}(t)/H_{r}(t)
is already a probability distribution over arms, and SS-BwK samples the round-
t
arm from this distribution.

To show that this naive approach might suffer a large regret due to large number of skips, we run simulations on the following problem instance with
|\mathcal{A}|=4
arms (three non-null arms and the null arm) where
\mu=[0.45,0.7,0.8,0]
; and
\rho=[0.3,0.75,0.8,0]
. Except for the null arm, the arm reward (cost) values are independently sampled from Beta distributions with parameters
\alpha=10\mu,\beta=10(1-\mu)
(
\alpha=10\rho,\beta=10(1-\rho)
). The average cost budget per round is
c=0.5
. We average results from
10
simulation runs, each with
500,000
rounds.
We compare the results with results from SUAK which we propose in §
3.2
.
The simulation results are given in Figure

It can be seen that the number of skips of SS-BwK exhibits sublinear growth and it is much higher than those of SUAK, which results in higher regret compared to SUAK.
Hence, this shows that merely adding skips to a BwK algorithm and treating it as a BwAK algorithm is not a viable solution.
In view of this, in the next section, we present SUAK, an algorithm that strategically under-utilizes the available budget to reduce the number of skips needed, and achieve smaller regret.

3.2
The SUAK Algorithm

We propose an algorithm called
Strategic Under-utilization for Anytime Knapsacks
(SUAK) that utilizes upper and lower confidence bounds of the arm rewards and costs using the UCB principle to upper bound the reward that can be obtained from a particular base. SUAK also uses skipping a round to satisfy the anytime cost constraint, and targets an average cost of
c-\log t/(\omega^{2}(t)\cdot t)
to limit the number of skips, where
\omega(t)
is defined in (
18
) and depends on the LCB of the estimated minimum cost gap of arms at round
t
. The pseudo-code is provided in Algorithm
2
.

SUAK works as follows. At each round, it uses the observations collected so far to construct confidence bounds on the mean rewards and costs of the arms. It then determines whether additional exploration is needed to identify which arms have mean cost below or above the budget threshold
c
. On rounds when every current cost-confidence interval excludes
c
, SUAK constructs an optimistic LP using upper confidence bounds on rewards and lower confidence bounds on costs, and selects an arm from an optimal LP base. Throughout this process, SUAK uses strategic under-utilization of the available budget and skips rounds when necessary to ensure that the anytime cost constraint is satisfied. In particular, when operating according to an LP base, it targets an average cost of
c-\log t/(\omega^{2}(t)t)
, where
\omega(t)
is defined in (
18
), in order to build sufficient budget slack and limit the number of global skips.

Before solving the LP, SUAK first determines whether the mean cost of each non-null arm can be confidently classified as lying strictly above or below the target cost
c
. An arm
l
is
cost-uncertain
at round
t
if its broad cost confidence interval contains
c
, i.e.,

\varrho_{l}^{L}(t)\leq c\leq\varrho_{l}^{U}(t),

where

\displaystyle\varrho_{l}^{L}(t)

\displaystyle:=\bar{\rho}_{l}(t)-7\sqrt{\frac{1.5\log t}{N_{l}(t)}},

(15)

\displaystyle\varrho_{l}^{U}(t)

\displaystyle:=\bar{\rho}_{l}(t)+7\sqrt{\frac{1.5\log t}{N_{l}(t)}}.

(16)

For an unobserved non-null arm, we set

\varrho_{i}^{L}(t)=-\infty,\ \varrho_{i}^{U}(t)=+\infty,

and

\rho_{i}^{L}(t)=\mu_{i}^{L}(t)=0,\ \rho_{i}^{U}(t)=\mu_{i}^{U}(t)=1,

without evaluating any expression involving
1/N_{i}(t)
when
N_{i}(t)=0
. To separately account for the cost incurred while resolving this cost uncertainty, SUAK maintains a dedicated classification cost tracker. Let
S_{p}(t)
denote the cumulative realized cost of classification pulls through round
t
, and let
N_{p}(t)
denote the number of classification rounds, including both classification pulls and the dedicated skips used to keep the average cost of classification rounds below
c
. We initialize

S_{p}(0)=N_{p}(0)=0.

; and, skips that round in that case. Otherwise, it next checks the global anytime constraint and executes a global skip whenever

After these checks, SUAK tests for cost uncertainty. If some non-null arm has a broad cost confidence interval containing
c
, one such arm is selected for classification (any tie-braker can be used if there are multiple such arms); otherwise, SUAK proceeds to the LP step. Note that since these intervals are recomputed every round, the classification condition may become active again later.

Whenever SUAK reaches the LP step, it computes a lower confidence bound on the minimum cost gap and the corresponding under-budgeting parameter:

\displaystyle\delta_{\min}^{L}(t)

\displaystyle:=\min_{i\in[K]}\left(|\bar{\rho}_{i}(t)-c|-\sqrt{\frac{1.5\log t}{N_{i}(t)}}\right),

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{U}(t)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{proj}_{[0,1]}\left(\bar{\mu}_{i}(t)+\epsilon_{i}(t)\right),

(19)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{proj}_{[0,1]}\left(\bar{\rho}_{i}(t)-\epsilon_{i}(t)\right),

(20)

and

The corresponding upper cost bound
\rho_{i}^{U}(t)
and lower reward bound

are defined analogously.

\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\langle\bm{\mu}^{U}(t),\pi\rangle.

\mathcal{I}_{t}=\{q:\pi_{q}(t)>0\}

|\mathcal{I}_{t}|=1

t

where the term
\log t/\omega^{2}(t)

\omega(t)

clipped to the interval

[\omega(t),1-\omega(t)].

The higher-cost arm is then selected with probability

and the lower-cost arm otherwise. This clipping guarantees that, on the relevant good events, each arm in a selected two-arm base is sampled with probability
\omega(t)
. A nonzero lower bound for
\omega(t)
is established in Lemma
4.1

Note that SUAK is non-stationary as it is adaptive to the available budget at that round. This design is essential as it was shown in
Flajolet and Jaillet (2015, Lemma 2)
that a non-adaptive design suffers
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
regret even if the optimal solution
\bm{\pi}^{*}
is known unless all arms consume the same deterministic cost every round. The main intuition behind this result is that the fluctuation of the available budget around its mean at round
t
can be as high as
\Omega(1/\sqrt{t})
.

4
Analysis of SUAK

In this section we provide an instance-dependent regret analysis of
SUAK
. The main difficulty compared with a standard UCB proof is that a selected base is only partially observed: in a two-arm base, only one of the two arms is pulled in a round. In addition, the algorithm incurs regret both from explicitly classifying arms relative to the cost threshold
c
and from deliberately under-utilizing the budget. The analysis below separates these effects and shows that each contributes at most logarithmic regret.

Consistently with (
10
), each base in the regret analysis is a basic feasible solution of the LP. Thus a two-arm base
\mathcal{I}=(h,\ell)
will have
\rho_{h}>c>\rho_{\ell}
(where
\ell
may be the null arm), and its weight vector will be

\displaystyle\pi_{h}^{\mathcal{I}}:=\frac{c-\rho_{\ell}}{\rho_{h}-\rho_{\ell}},\qquad\pi_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}:=\frac{\rho_{h}-c}{\rho_{h}-\rho_{\ell}}.

(21)

For a one-arm base
\mathcal{I}=\{i\}
with
\rho_{i}\leq c
,
\pi_{i}^{\mathcal{I}}=1
will hold. We define

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{I}}:=\bm{\mu}^{T}\bm{\pi}^{\mathcal{I}}.

(22)

Then, the optimal reward will be

r^{*}=\max_{\mathcal{I}\in\mathds{V}}r_{\mathcal{I}}
. Note that for a two-arm base,
r_{\mathcal{I}}
denotes the reward of the unique mixture on the cost-
c
boundary, rather than the maximum reward over all feasible policies whose support is contained in
\mathcal{I}
. This distinction is important for the regret decomposition developed below.

Theorem 4.7

(Gap-dependent regret of

Under Assumption

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq{}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(1+\lambda^{*}+r^{*}\left(1+\frac{1}{c}\right)\right)\frac{5K}{2},

(52)

where

is an optimal dual solution in (

\lambda^{*}\leq 1/c
,
\nu^{*}\leq 1

Remark 1

(Shadow prices and reduced-cost charging)
.

A unit of unused cost is not generally worth
r^{*}/c
units of reward; the appropriate global conversion rate is the optimal dual multiplier
\lambda^{*}
. The remaining loss on a selected two-arm base is bounded directly by
G_{\mathcal{I}}=\max_{q\in\mathcal{I}}g_{q}
, regardless of SUAK’s adaptive within-base randomization. Retaining the canonical mixture weights in the optimism calculation gives
G_{\mathcal{I}}\leq 12\max_{q\in\mathcal{I}}\epsilon_{q}(t)/\delta_{q}
. Charging these reduced-cost gaps therefore preserves the
1/\Delta_{\min,i}
reward-gap dependence without an additional inverse canonical-weight factor. This argument does not require SUAK to track each base’s canonical occupation measure. Together with the linear protective drift
\eta\geq\delta_{\min}/3
, it yields the cubic cost-gap dependence below.

(Simplified instance-dependent regret bound)

,
r^{*}\leq 1
,
\lambda^{*}\leq 1/c

imply

\displaystyle R_{T}=O\!\left(\frac{K\log T}{\delta_{\min}^{3}\Delta_{\min}}+\frac{\nu^{*}K\log T}{c\delta_{\min}^{3}}+\frac{K\log T}{c\delta_{\min}^{2}}\right)+O\!\left(K\left(1+\frac{1}{c}+\frac{\nu^{*}}{c\delta_{\min}}\right)\right).

For fixed

\delta_{\min}

4.2
Instance-Dependent Lower Bound

We next apply the classical Lai–Robbins instance-dependent lower-bound argument
(
Lai and Robbins, 1985
)
to an embedded stochastic multi-armed-bandit subclass of BwAK. The subclass used below automatically satisfies the anytime constraint, so the reduction transfers the classical logarithmic lower bound directly. Consequently, the
K\log T
scaling of Theorem
4.7
cannot be improved uniformly on this subclass for fixed reward gap. In particular, the argument does not characterize the information cost of learning consumptions, identifying an optimal binding-budget mixture, or avoiding budget exhaustion, and it does not provide a matching lower bound for the inverse cost-gap factors in Theorem
4.7
.

Let
\Theta_{+}
denote the class of BwAK instances satisfying Assumption
1
. We call a feasible policy
uniformly efficient on
\Theta_{+}
if, for every instance in
\Theta_{+}
and every
a>0
, its regret satisfies
R_{T}=o(T^{a})
. All uses of uniform efficiency in this subsection refer to this class. This restriction aligns the lower bound with Theorem
4.7
; it imposes no efficiency requirement at zero-cost-gap instances. For Bernoulli parameters
p,q\in(0,1)
, define the binary relative entropy

\displaystyle\operatorname{kl}(p,q):=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}.

(68)

Theorem 4.9

(Classical-bandit subclass lower bound for BwAK)
.

Fix any
0<\rho_{0}<c
, and consider the subclass of BwAK instances in which every non-null arm has deterministic cost
\rho_{i}(t)=\rho_{0}
and Bernoulli rewards with means
\mu_{i}\in(0,1)
. Assume that the best arm is unique, let
\mu_{*}:=\max_{i}\mu_{i}
, and define
\Delta_{i}:=\mu_{*}-\mu_{i}
. Then every feasible policy uniformly efficient on
\Theta_{+}
satisfies

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{R_{T}}{\log T}\geq\sum_{i:\Delta_{i}>0}\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\operatorname{kl}(\mu_{i},\mu_{*})}.

(69)

Consequently, there exists a family of BwAK instances for which

\displaystyle R_{T}=\Omega\!\left(\frac{K\log T}{\Delta}\right),

(70)

where
\Delta
is the common reward gap of the suboptimal arms. In particular, for fixed
\Delta>0
, every uniformly efficient feasible policy incurs
\Omega(K\log T)
regret on some BwAK instance.

The proof is provided in Appendix
.7
.

Remark 2

(Scope of the lower bound)
.

Theorem
4.9
is the classical Lai–Robbins lower bound
(
Lai and Robbins, 1985
)
transferred to a BwAK subclass whose costs make the anytime constraint nonbinding. Accordingly, the matching statement is limited to the logarithmic order in
T
, linear dependence on
K
, and the standard
1/\Delta
reward-gap scaling on that embedded subclass. The theorem does not establish asymptotic optimality of SUAK’s coefficient, characterize regret on every fixed BwAK instance, or show that the higher-order dependence of Theorem
4.7
on the cost gaps
\delta_{i}
or
\delta_{\min}
is information-theoretically necessary. A converse for genuinely binding-budget instances would need to capture the information required to learn consumptions and identify an optimal mixture while preserving anytime feasibility; establishing such a lower bound remains an interesting direction for future work.

Simulations

We evaluate the empirical performance of SUAK through simulations. For comparison, we include the
Primal Dual
and
One Phase
algorithms of
Li et al. (2021)
and the
UCB Simplex
algorithm of
Flajolet and Jaillet (2015)
.
Li et al. (2021)
leave the derivation of theoretical regret guarantees for the
One Phase
algorithm as an open problem and instead analyze the related
Primal Dual
algorithm, which is expected to have weaker empirical performance. We implement their skip versions as described in §
3.1
, and refer to them by appending ’_skip’ to their names. We consider an instance with
K=8
arms with means
\mu=[0.35,0.45,0.52,0.72,0.84,0.9,0.92,0.9,0]
, cost means
\rho=[0.25,0.3,0.4,0.6,0.7,0.75,0.8,0.85,0]
, and
c=0.5
. For each non-null arm, rewards and costs are sampled independently from Beta distributions with parameters
(10\mu_{i},10(1-\mu_{i}))
and
(10\rho_{i},10(1-\rho_{i}))
, respectively. We run each simulation runs for
2.5
million rounds, and report the results averaged over
10
trials. The simulation results are given in Figure
2
. The shaded areas represent error bars with one standard deviation. The second plot in Figure
2
is a zoomed-in version of the first plot showing only SUAK and Safe-Skip BwK (SS-BwK).

Figure 2:
Plots of cumulative empirical regret (top row), number of skips (bottom left), and average empirical cost deviation
\bar{c}(t)-c
(bottom right). Null-arm pulls caused by dedicated or global skip branches are counted as skips; null-arm pulls selected within an LP base are not.

The Primal Dual Skip algorithm (PDS) performs the worst in this experiment. This is expected because PDS is designed primarily for theoretical guarantees and pulls every arm the same number of times until it identifies the optimal solution. The UCB Simplex Skip algorithm (USS) also performs poorly because it assumes knowledge of a constant
\kappa

a priori
such that
|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}|\leq\kappa|\rho_{i}-\rho_{j}|
for any
i,j
, and the confidence intervals for the arm rewards are multiplied by a factor of
\lambda=1+2\kappa
. In our simulation setting,
\lambda=9
, which increases the number of samples needed for exploration.

It can be seen that SUAK performs better compared to PDS or USS, and exceeds the performance of SS-BwK after around round
1.4\times 10^{6}
.
This is since the regret of SUAK concentrates mostly on the initial rounds. Pulls during initial rounds are used to reduce the uncertainty in the mean costs of arms, but are also helpful in determining the optimal base in the main algorithm, hence this reduces the regret of suboptimal base selections while searching for the optimal base.
After these rounds, SUAK can catch up to and eventually surpass the performance of SS-BwK due to the higher regret of SS-BwK from its high number of skips, verifying the practical utility of SUAK.

The third plot in Figure
2
shows that the number of skips grows sublinearly for all algorithms, with USS and SUAK requiring very few skips. This demonstrates the effectiveness of SUAK’s under-budgeting mechanism. Null-arm pulls caused by the dedicated classification cost tracker skip in line
7
and the global anytime skip in line
9
of Algorithm
2
are counted as skips; null-arm pulls that arise because the null arm belongs to the selected LP base are not counted as a skip. The final plot in Figure
2
is the average cost-budget gap
\bar{c}(t)-c
. Under the anytime constraint this quantity must be non-positive on every round, so negative values correspond to under-utilization of the cost budget. During the early classification-heavy rounds, the dedicated accounting keeps the average cost close to
c
. As SUAK reaches the LP step more frequently, under-budgeting lowers the average empirical cost below the target and the deviation approaches zero as
\log t/(\omega^{2}(t)t)
vanishes. The average empirical cost of other algorithms are very close to the

.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we study the bandits with anytime knapsacks (BwAK) problem, where we employ a stricter anytime cost constraint instead of a total cost budget. We provide SUAK, a novel algorithm that under-utilizes the available cost budget, utilizes skipping to limit the probability of violating the anytime constraint, and uses upper confidence bounds to balance exploration and exploitation. SUAK achieves an instance-dependent regret upper bound of
O(K\log T)
. While logarithmic regret is already known for related models, including non-monotonic resource utilization
(
Kumar and Kleinberg, 2022
)
, and evolving arm-specific budgets
(
Combes et al., 2015
)
, direct comparison of this result is not possible due to differences in the problem setting and the variables used in the regret bounds.We also use the classical Lai–Robbins lower bound
(
Lai and Robbins, 1985
)
to show that logarithmic dependence on
T
and linear dependence on
K
are unavoidable for fixed reward gap under some problem instances.
We provide simulation results to demonstrate the empirical performance of SUAK. Our work opens multiple directions for future research. One interesting future direction is to extend our results to
d
-dimensional costs. This is a challenging problem as the anytime constraint needs to be satisfied in every dimension, which can introduce additional skips. Another interesting open direction is when the rewards and costs of arms are non-stationary. In this case, satisfying the anytime cost constraint would be challenging but we conjecture it may be accomplished using a more conservative cost budget under-utilization.

96\log T/\delta_{i}^{2}
such pulls after the first observation, plus at most one initial pull when
N_{i}(t)=0

\displaystyle N_{i}^{{\rm cls},G}(T)\leq\frac{96\log T}{\delta_{i}^{2}}+1,

(82)

which proves (
38
).

Number of dedicated classification skips

We next bound the number of skips generated by the dedicated classification cost tracker.
Consider any round on which the dedicated classification-skip condition is triggered. Immediately before that skip, let

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle:=\text{number of classification pulls already executed},

(83)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle:=\text{number of dedicated classification skips already executed}.

(84)

Since the classification cost tracker is updated only on these two types of rounds,

\displaystyle N_{p}=m+s.

(85)

Moreover, every classification pull incurs a cost in
[0,1]
, while a dedicated skip incurs zero cost. Hence the cumulative cost incurred satisfies

\displaystyle S_{p}\leq m.

(86)

A dedicated skip occurs only if

\displaystyle S_{p}+1>cN_{p}.

(87)

Using
N_{p}=m+s
and (
86
),

\displaystyle c(m+s)<S_{p}+1\leq m+1.

(88)

Therefore

\displaystyle cs<(1-c)m+1,

(89)

and hence

\displaystyle s<\frac{1-c}{c}m+\frac{1}{c}.

(90)

After performing the current skip, the number of dedicated skips becomes
s+1
, so

\displaystyle s+1

\displaystyle<\frac{1-c}{c}m+\frac{1}{c}+1

(91)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{c}+\frac{1}{c}+1,

(92)

where the last inequality uses
1-c\leq 1
.

Now consider the last dedicated classification skip, if any, that occurs by time
T
. At that time, the number
m
of classification pulls already executed is no larger than the total number
M_{\rm cls}(T)
of classification pulls through horizon
T
. Applying (
92
) to this last skip gives

\displaystyle S_{\rm cls}(T)\leq\frac{M_{\rm cls}(T)}{c}+\frac{1}{c}+1.

(93)

If no dedicated classification skip occurs, the same inequality is immediate. Thus (
39
) holds pathwise.

Total number of classification pulls.

Finally, decompose the total number of classification pulls according to whether the confidence event holds:

\displaystyle M_{\rm cls}(T)=M_{\rm cls}^{G}(T)+M_{\rm cls}^{F}(T),

(94)

where
M_{\rm cls}^{G}(T)
and

M_{\rm cls}^{F}(T)
denote classification pulls on rounds satisfying

\mathcal{G}_{t}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{c}
, respectively.

Summing (
38
) over all non-null arms gives the pathwise bound

\displaystyle M_{\rm cls}^{G}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}^{{\rm cls},G}(T)

(95)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\frac{96\log T}{\delta_{i}^{2}}+1\right).

(96)

On a confidence-failure round, the algorithm can execute at most one classification pull. Therefore

\displaystyle M_{\rm cls}^{F}(T)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathds{1}\left\{\mathcal{G}_{t}^{c}\right\}}=F_{T}.

(97)

Combining (
96
) and
(
97
) yields, pathwise,

\displaystyle M_{\rm cls}(T)

\displaystyle\leq 96\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\log T}{\delta_{i}^{2}}+K+F_{T}.

(98)

Taking expectations on both sides gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{\rm cls}(T)]\leq 96\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\log T}{\delta_{i}^{2}}+K+\mathbb{E}[F_{T}],

(99)

which is (
40
) and completes the proof.

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\eta[H_{m}-(1-c)]\right)

(151)

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\log 2}{\omega_{\max}}+\eta\right)

(152)

\displaystyle\leq e^{1/2}4^{-m}\leq e\,4^{-m}.

(153)

There is at most one such threshold-induced entrance per epoch. By construction, an excursion already active at an epoch boundary retains its original entrance time and is not counted again. At the beginning of each interval created by a classification/failure restart, we likewise allow at most one initial low-slack excursion with the trivial hitting-probability bound one. This same initial-excursion convention is what absorbs the post-round-one state at the
E_{0}\to E_{1}
boundary. The explicit sum of these hitting probabilities is evaluated below. To convert these hitting-probability bounds into a bound on the number of global skips, we first bound the expected number of skips generated by an excursion conditional on its reaching the unsafe region
[0,1-c)
. We then multiply this conditional bound by the corresponding hitting probability and sum over all possible excursion entrances.

We next bound skips conditionally from any state inside an excursion. Set
V_{t}:=e^{-\eta L_{t}}
; pathwise feasibility gives
0<V_{t}\leq 1
. On an ordinary good LP action inside the low-slack region, the preceding calculation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{t}-V_{t-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 0.

(154)

A dedicated classification cost tracker skip also decreases
V_{t}
. On an actual global skip,
L_{t-1}<1-c\leq 1
, so
V_{t-1}\geq e^{-1}
. Since
\eta c\leq 1
and
1-e^{-\eta c}\geq\eta c/2
,

\displaystyle V_{t}-V_{t-1}

\displaystyle=V_{t-1}(e^{-\eta c}-1)\leq-\frac{\eta c}{2e}.

(155)

Let
\sigma_{e}
and
\kappa_{e}
denote the entrance state and terminal state of an excursion, indexed by completed rounds, and let
I_{e,t}^{\rm skip}
indicate a global skip in that excursion. By the pre-action stopping convention, every included action obeys the conditional drift inequality. Applying it after any stopping time
\theta
with
\sigma_{e}\leq\theta\leq\kappa_{e}
, summing up to
\kappa_{e}\leq T
, and conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\kappa_{e}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\theta}]-V_{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\eta c}{2e}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=\theta+1}^{\kappa_{e}}I_{e,t}^{\rm skip}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\right].

(156)

Using
V_{\kappa_{e}}\geq 0
and
V_{\theta}\leq 1
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=\theta+1}^{\kappa_{e}}I_{e,t}^{\rm skip}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\right]\leq\frac{2e}{\eta c}.

(157)

Let
\tau_{e}
be the first state time in
[\sigma_{e},\kappa_{e}]
at which
L_{\tau_{e}}<1-c
, setting
\tau_{e}=\kappa_{e}
if the unsafe region is not reached earlier. No global skip can occur before this first unsafe state; if
\tau_{e}=\kappa_{e}
, there are no global skips in the excursion. Applying (
157
) at
\theta=\tau_{e}
and then the tower property therefore yields the conditional bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{e}^{\rm skip}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{e}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2e}{\eta c}\,\Pr(\tau_{e}<\kappa_{e}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{e}}).

(158)

The hitting estimates above are conditional on the entrance history and uniform over that history. Summing (
158
) over entrances in a restart interval now gives an explicit constant. Since the excursion calculation starts at
m=1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{m}e4^{-m}=e,\qquad\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e4^{-m}=\frac{e}{3}.

(159)

Adding the interval’s possible initial excursion, whose hitting probability is bounded by one, the total hitting-probability mass within any restart interval is at most

\displaystyle 1+e+\frac{e}{3}=1+\frac{4e}{3}.

(160)

Therefore, by (
158
), conditional on the starting history of any restart interval, its expected number of global skips is at most

\displaystyle\frac{2e}{c\eta}\left(1+\frac{4e}{3}\right).

(161)

If no high-cost arm exists, every good ordinary LP action has mean slack increment at least
\delta_{\min}\geq\eta
at every slack level. Hence the same potential drift and conditional skip-count calculation apply directly to the entire interval, with no threshold or excursion decomposition. Its conditional expected skip count is at most
2e/(c\eta)
, which is smaller than (
161
).

Finally, condition successively on the histories at the starts of the intervals. The conditional bounds are uniform, and the number of intervals is at most
1+M_{\rm cls}(T)+F_{T}
. The isolated epoch
E_{0}=\{1\}
contributes at most one additional global skip. Since
c\eta<1
and
1+M_{\rm cls}(T)+F_{T}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{\rm gskip}^{\rm G}(T)]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2e}{c\eta}\left(1+\frac{4e}{3}\right)\left(1+\mathbb{E}[M_{\rm cls}(T)]+\mathbb{E}[F_{T}]\right)

(162)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+2e(1+4e/3)}{c\eta}\left(1+\mathbb{E}[M_{\rm cls}(T)]+\mathbb{E}[F_{T}]\right)

(163)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{27}{c\eta}\left(1+\mathbb{E}[M_{\rm cls}(T)]+\mathbb{E}[F_{T}]\right),

(164)

where
1+2e(1+4e/3)<27
. This proves (
50
). The simplified form follows from Lemma
4.3
and
\eta\geq\delta_{\min}/3
.
∎

.7
Proof of Theorem
4.9

Proof.

Because
\rho_{0}<c
and costs are deterministic, pulling any non-null arm in every round satisfies the anytime constraint pathwise:

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}c(s)=t\rho_{0}<ct,\qquad\forall t.

(165)

Hence the null arm is never needed for feasibility and the budget constraint of the LP relaxation is slack. The optimal LP policy therefore places all mass on the unique arm with largest reward mean
\mu_{*}
, and
r^{*}=\mu_{*}
. To avoid confusion with the pre-round count
N_{i}(t)
used elsewhere in the paper, define the horizon pull count

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{i}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathds{1}\left\{i(t)=i\right\}},\qquad i\in\mathcal{A}.

(166)

For any policy, the exact regret decomposition on this subclass is

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=T\mu_{*}-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[K]\\
j\neq i^{*}\end{subarray}}(\mu_{*}-\mu_{j})\,\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{j}(T)]+\mu_{*}\,\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{\bot}(T)]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j:\Delta_{j}>0}\Delta_{j}\,\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{j}(T)].

(167)

Thus, after discarding the nonnegative regret due to null-arm pulls, the classical Lai–Robbins change-of-measure lower-bound argument
[
Lai and Robbins, 1985
]
applies to the non-null arms.

Fix a suboptimal arm
i
. Consider an alternative instance
\theta^{\prime}
that changes only arm
i
and increases its Bernoulli reward mean from
\mu_{i}
to some
\mu_{i}^{\prime}>\mu_{*}
, while leaving all costs and all other reward distributions unchanged. Both instances belong to
\Theta_{+}
because
\delta_{\min}=c-\rho_{0}>0
in each. Let
\theta
denote the original instance. Under
\theta
, arm
i
is suboptimal, whereas under
\theta^{\prime}
it is the unique optimal arm. Since the two instances differ only in observations from arm
i
, the chain rule for relative entropy gives

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{T}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}^{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)]\,\operatorname{kl}(\mu_{i},\mu_{i}^{\prime}).

(168)

To lower-bound this information, let
A_{T}:=\{\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)<T/2\}
. Uniform efficiency under
\theta
and (
167
) imply
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)]=o(T^{a})
for every
a>0
, and therefore
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(A_{T})\to 1
. Under
\theta^{\prime}
, every action different from arm
i
—including the null arm—has a strictly positive reward gap. Hence uniform efficiency under
\theta^{\prime}
implies
\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}}[T-\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)]=o(T^{a})
for every
a>0
. Consequently, for every fixed
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}(A_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}}[T-\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)]}{T}=o(T^{a-1}).

(169)

Then,

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{T}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}^{T})\geq\operatorname{kl}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(A_{T}),\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}(A_{T})).

(170)

Since the first probability tends to one and the second is
o(T^{a-1})
, the right-hand side is at least
(1-a-o(1))\log T
. Combining this with (
168
), dividing by
\log T
, and then letting
a\downarrow 0
yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)]}{\log T}\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{kl}(\mu_{i},\mu_{i}^{\prime})}.

(171)

Letting
\mu_{i}^{\prime}\downarrow\mu_{*}
and using continuity of
\operatorname{kl}(\mu_{i},\cdot)
gives

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{i}(T)]}{\log T}\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{kl}(\mu_{i},\mu_{*})}.

(172)

Multiplying by
\Delta_{i}
, summing over suboptimal arms, and applying (
167
) proves (
69
).

For the symmetric consequence, choose one arm with mean
\mu_{*}=1/2+\Delta
and the remaining
K-1
arms with mean
1/2
, where
0<\Delta\leq 1/4
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{kl}\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\Delta\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-4\Delta^{2})=\Theta(\Delta^{2}).

(173)

Therefore each of the
K-1
suboptimal arms contributes
\Theta((\log T)/\Delta)
to the lower bound. Since
K-1=\Theta(K)
for
K\geq 2
, this yields (
70
).
∎
