Post‐COVID‐19 Public Health Challenges: Insights Into Post‐COVID Symptoms, Stigma, and Post‐Pandemic Recovery Support From the Vietnam Experience
Authors: Binh‐Minh Le Thi Nguyen, Truc Thanh Thai, Tri Nugraha Susilawati, Thanh Chi Vo, Mai Quynh Pham, Anh‐Thu Trang Thai, Nguyen Thao Dang, Nhan My Le, Thuy Anh Bui, Minh Cuong Duong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the burden of post‐COVID‐19 symptoms, social stigma, and quality of life, and to examine their interactions among individuals recovering from COVID‐19 in Vietnam.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted from March to June 2022 among individuals who had recovered from COVID‐19 within 1–3 months prior. Participants completed a self‐administered questionnaire capturing demographic and baseline characteristics, health status during infection, post‐COVID symptoms, perceived social stigma using the five‐item COVID‐19 Perceived Discrimination Scale (C‐19‐PDS), and quality of life using the EQ‐5D‐5L. Multivariable logistic regression was used to investigate the relationship between social stigma and perceived decline in quality of life.ResultsAmong 764 participants, 55.4% were female, and 45.4% were under 30 years old. Most participants (81.3%) reported at least one of 27 post‐COVID‐19 symptoms, 27.5% experienced social stigma, and 38% reported a decline in quality of life. Perceived social stigma was significantly associated with reduced quality of life (adjusted OR = 1.81; 95% CI 1.20–2.73).ConclusionsPost‐COVID symptoms and social stigma are common among COVID‐19 survivors, and social stigma significantly impacts post‐recovery quality of life. Comprehensive support from families, communities, and authorities is essential to promote full recovery and social integration.

Introduction

Chronic infectious diseases impose a dual burden of persistent physical morbidity and social adversity. Visible symptoms and persistent misconceptions regarding infectivity often precipitate labeling, social avoidance, and discrimination [1]. This intersection of biomedical pathology and psychosocial exclusion severely compromises social reintegration [2].

Similar patterns have been observed during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which has globally affected over 770 million individuals, resulting in more than 7 million deaths since 2019 [3]. While most cases achieve clinical recovery, a substantial proportion of survivors continue to experience persistent physical and psychological impairments, indicating incomplete recovery [4]. These post‐COVID‐19 symptoms are associated with significant declines in quality of life, with up to 59% of recovered individuals reporting ongoing physical or mental health challenges [4, 5, 6].

Post‐COVID symptoms can manifest as fatigue, shortness of breath, joint pain, headache, and cognitive impairments [7]. Psychological consequences, including anxiety, depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and sleep disturbances, are also common [4, 5]. Older age and greater severity of the acute infection are consistently linked to poorer post‐recovery outcomes, likely due to reduced physiological resilience and preexisting comorbidities [4, 5].

Beyond health challenges, COVID‐19 survivors often face social stigma arising from fear, anxiety, and misunderstanding about the virus and post‐COVID consequences [8]. Stigma can lead to exclusion, discrimination, and difficulties reintegrating into society, further undermining mental health and recovery [8, 9]. Studies from developing countries have reported that 50%–70% of COVID‐19 survivors experience social stigma, which is strongly associated with lower quality of life [10, 11]. Despite this evidence, the relationship between COVID‐19‐related stigma and post‐recovery quality of life remains insufficiently explored.

In Vietnam, approximately 12 million confirmed COVID‐19 cases have been reported, with around 11 million recoveries [12]. However, data on post‐COVID‐19 quality of life from these patients and its link to social stigma are limited. To support post‐pandemic health monitoring and future pandemic preparedness, this study aimed to examine post‐COVID‐19 symptoms among recovered patients in Ho Chi Minh City and investigate the prevalence of social stigma and its association with perceived decline in quality of life.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Context, and Participants

A cross‐sectional study was conducted from March to June 2022 among individuals who had recovered from COVID‐19 from 1 to 3 months prior. This period coincided with Vietnam's transition into the new normal phase, approximately 5 months after the country shifted toward adaptive living following the fourth pandemic wave [13]. Four districts of Ho Chi Minh City (Districts 5, 6, 8, and Hoc Mon) were selected using simple random sampling method. A multi‐stage sampling strategy was employed, ultimately incorporating 12 neighborhoods selected across 4 urban and suburban districts in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. These districts were part of the COVID‐19 Levels 1 and 2 risk zones identified during the fourth wave and were chosen for their high population density, ensuring adequate demographic representativeness of the study population [14, 15].

The inclusion criteria included individuals aged 18–65 years, confirmed COVID‐19 diagnosis by primary healthcare centers, negative RT‐PCR or rapid antigen test result for SARS‐CoV‐2 within 1–3 months prior to the study, and provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included self‐reported neuropsychiatric conditions, communication limitations (e.g., hearing, visual, or language impairments), SARS‐CoV‐2 reinfection at the time of the study, or acute health conditions requiring hospitalization.

Study Procedure

Participants were recruited via multi‐stage cluster sampling, involving the random selection of 12 neighborhoods. A total of 1018 eligible individuals provided written informed consent before completing a structured, self‐administered questionnaire in Vietnamese.

Measurements

A self‐administered questionnaire comprised five sections: demographic and baseline health characteristics, health status during the COVID‐19 infection, post‐COVID‐19 health status, perceived social stigma, and perceived quality of life.

Sociodemographic characteristics included age groups (18–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60, and 61–65), gender, ethnicity (Kinh or others), education level (<high school, high school, and >high school), and marital status (single, married, or widowed/separated/divorced). Socioeconomic status was classified into three categories—lower, middle, and upper—based on household income thresholds relative to the media, reflecting the capacity for basic need fulfillment and discretionary spending.

The baseline health characteristics section included the number of COVID‐19 vaccination doses received (1, 2, or 3), the presence of chronic comorbidities (0, 1, or ≥2). The health status during the COVID‐19 infection section included place of treatment (home/hospital), severity of symptoms (mild or moderate/severe), and the duration of treatment (≤7, 8–14, and >14 days).

The post‐COVID‐19 health status section included recovery time (≤4, 4–8, and 8–12 weeks) and post‐COVID‐19 symptoms. The latter was referenced according to the NICE guidelines for the management of long‐term effects of COVID‐19 [16]. For the purposes of this study, the total 27 symptoms listed by NICE were classified into 8 groups: general symptoms (yes/no), which included body aches, anxiety, fatigue, hair loss, fever, and depression; neurological symptoms (yes/no), such as tingling/numbness in limbs, dizziness, headaches, forgetfulness, lack of concentration, and sleep disturbances; ear, nose, and throat symptoms (yes/no), including ear pain, sore throat, loss of smell, loss of taste, and tinnitus; respiratory symptoms (yes/no), which involve cough and shortness of breath; cardiovascular symptoms (yes/no), such as chest pain, palpitations, and arrhythmia; gastrointestinal symptoms (yes/no), including nausea, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, and diarrhea; musculoskeletal symptoms (yes/no), such as joint pain and muscle pain; and dermatological symptoms (yes/no), including rash and dermatitis.

Perceived social stigma was evaluated using the five‐item COVID‐19 Perceived Discrimination Scale (C‐19‐PDS) [17], which assesses survivors’ experiences of stigma‐related attitudes and discriminatory behaviors. Responses are scored on a 4‐point Likert scale (ranging from 0 = “never” to 3 = “always”), where higher cumulative scores indicate a greater intensity of perceived societal stigma.

The perceived quality of life was assessed using the EQ‐5D‐5L scale, which has been standardized for the general population of Vietnam [18]. This scale includes 25 subjective assessments across five dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression (five items per domain). Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting greater impairment in daily functioning and, consequently, a poorer health‐related quality of life. For this study, any participant reporting impairment in one or more of these dimensions was categorized as having an impaired quality of life.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using Stata version 17. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are reported as means (standard deviation, SD) or medians (interquartile range, IQR), depending on their distribution. Univariable logistic regression was initially performed to examine associations between sociodemographic/baseline characteristics, clinical status, and post‐COVID‐19 outcomes (social stigma and quality of life). Variables demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.05) in univariable analysis were subsequently included in multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors and explore the association between social stigma and quality of life decline. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Study Participants and Their Associations With Perceived Quality of Life and Social Stigma

Figure 1 presents participant recruitment into this study. Of the 1018 eligible individuals, 810 completed the survey (79.6% recruitment rate). After excluding 46 incomplete responses, the final analytical sample comprised 764 participants. The cohort was predominantly female (55.4%), of Kinh ethnicity (89.0%), and aged ≤30 years (45.4%). Most participants had attained at least a high school education (79.8%), married (50.4%), and identified with the middle socioeconomic group (70.6%). Post‐recovery, 27.5% reported perceived social stigma, and 38.0% experienced a decline in quality of life (Figure 2). Multivariable analysis indicated that women (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.56–2.86) and participants aged >60 years (OR = 2.52, 95% CI: 1.28–4.95) faced a significantly higher risk of decline in quality of life. Additionally, social stigma was significantly associated with age, marital status, and socioeconomic status (all p < 0.05).

Univariate logistic regression.

Multivariable analysis (Table 1) revealed that receiving ≥3 vaccine doses was significantly protective against both reduced quality of life (OR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.13–0.77) and social stigma (OR = 0.2, 95% CI: 0.08–0.51). Conversely, moderate‐to‐severe acute symptoms were strong predictors for both outcomes (QoL decline: OR = 2.55; stigma: OR = 4.0). Although comorbidities (OR = 1.65; 95% CI: 1.11–2.45) and hospitalization (OR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.05–4.82) significantly predicted impaired post‐discharge quality of life, they did not correlate with social stigma. Conversely, treatment duration >14 days was associated with higher perceived stigma (OR = 2.72; 95% CI: 1.07–6.90) but showed no significant link to quality‐of‐life decline (Table 1).

Association Between Persistent Symptoms of COVID‐19 and Perceived Social Stigma and Reduce in Quality of Life

Overall, 81.3% of study participants reported at least one post‐COVID‐19 symptom following recovery (Supporting information Appendix 1). As shown in Table 2, all eight symptom groups were significantly associated with a decline in quality of life. With respect to perceived social stigma, significant associations were observed for seven of the eight symptom groups, general, otolaryngological, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal symptoms (all p < 0.05). Neurological symptom groups were not significantly associated with perceived social stigma (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides evidence that post‐COVID‐19 symptoms and perceived social stigma remain substantial challenges among individuals recovering from COVID‐19 in Vietnam, even during the post‐acute phase and the transition to the “new normal.” Our findings have revealed that over 80% of participants reported persistent symptoms, 27.5% experienced social stigma, and 38% reported a decline in quality of life. Notably, perceived social stigma emerged as an independent predictor of impaired quality of life, even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, vaccination status, disease severity, and persistent symptoms (Adjusted OR = 1.81; 95% CI: 1.20–2.73). These findings highlight that the impact of post‐COVID‐19 sequelae extends beyond physiological dysfunction to encompass significant psychosocial burden. Fear of discrimination and social exclusion may discourage individuals from seeking timely medical or psychological support, thereby amplifying the negative effects of persistent symptoms on daily functioning and health‐related quality of life.

Post‐COVID‐19 Symptoms

The high prevalence of post‐COVID‐19 symptoms observed in this study aligns with emerging global evidence that post‐acute sequelae of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection persist well beyond the acute infection phase [19]. Symptoms across multiple organ systems, including general, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal domains, were strongly associated with impaired quality of life [20]. These findings reinforce the concept of post‐COVID‐19 condition as a multisystem disorder that extends beyond physical recovery and interferes with daily functioning, productivity, and overall well‐being.

Our results are consistent with a systematic review and meta‐analysis of 24 studies, which reported that approximately 36% of individuals experienced a decline in quality of life following COVID‐19 recovery, with fatigue and respiratory symptoms being particularly prevalent [21]. That analysis also demonstrated substantial objective sequelae, including abnormal lung function in 39% of patients and persistent radiological abnormalities in up to 59%, such as ground‐glass opacities, septal thickening, fibrous bands, and bronchiectasis [21]. Together with our findings, this evidence underscores the substantial and sustained health burden faced by COVID‐19 survivors.

Although the proportion of participants reporting reduced quality of life in our study (38%) was lower than that reported in studies from Northwest Spain (67%) [22] and the Netherlands (72%) [23], these differences are likely explained by variations in study populations. Participants in those studies were generally older and had more severe acute COVID‐19, whereas most of our participants were younger and experienced mild disease managed at home [22, 23]. This highlights the critical role of age and disease severity in shaping post‐COVID‐19 recovery status.

Notably, neurological symptoms were prevalent but were not significantly associated with perceived social stigma. One possible explanation is that differences in how post‐COVID‐19 symptoms are perceived by others may influence stigmatizing responses. More externally observable symptoms may be more readily interpreted as signs of ongoing illness or infectivity and could, therefore, contribute to social avoidance or discrimination. However, symptom visibility and others' interpretations of symptoms were not directly measured in our study, and this explanation remains speculative [24, 25].

Social Stigma as a Determinant of Quality of Life

Stigma has unintentionally created an invisible barrier between patients and society, leading to a range of negative social consequences [26]. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, stigma emerged as one of the most significant social repercussions of infection [11]. Numerous studies worldwide have documented the prevalence of COVID‐19‐related stigma [27], with reports from developed countries indicating that up to 50% of COVID‐19 survivors experienced discrimination [11], whereas a study in India found that nearly all participants reported at least one stigma‐related experience [28]. Furthermore, a community survey in the United Kingdom revealed an extraordinarily high prevalence of stigma among individuals with post‐COVID conditions, with 95.4% reporting overall stigma, 62.7% experiencing enacted stigma, 86.4% experiencing internalized stigma, and 90.8% experiencing anticipated stigma [29]. In contrast, our study found that approximately 27.5% of individuals recovering from COVID‐19 experienced social stigma, a figure notably lower than that reported in previous studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in cultural contexts, pandemic phases, public health messaging, and the measurement tools used to assess stigma. Nevertheless, our findings confirm that stigma remains a persistent and meaningful issue even as societies transition to post‐pandemic conditions.

Crucially, we identified a statistically significant association between perceived social stigma and reduced quality of life. This relationship has been consistently observed in studies conducted in Iran [30], China [31], and Indonesia [10], where higher stigma levels were associated with poorer quality of life, increased depressive symptoms, and adverse mental health outcomes among COVID‐19 survivors. These converging findings reinforce the interpretation that stigma is not merely a social inconvenience but a key psychosocial determinant of recovery.

Stigma may also exacerbate health outcomes through multiple pathways, including social isolation, reduced access to support networks, internalized shame, and heightened psychological distress. In this way, stigma may interact with post‐COVID symptoms to create a self‐reinforcing cycle that hinders full recovery and reintegration into society [32, 33].

Implications for Public Health and Clinical Practice

Our findings demonstrate that viral clearance is an inadequate metric for clinical recovery, as the health and psychosocial sequelae of COVID‐19 persist well beyond the acute phase. The recovery needs of COVID‐19 survivors are therefore substantial and multifaceted [34]. Healthcare professionals and policy‐makers should prioritize the development of post‐COVID‐19 care strategies that enable early identification and timely management of persistent physical and mental health problems.

In parallel, community‐level interventions are needed to address stigma [27]. Educational campaigns should emphasize that persistent symptoms are not markers of infectivity, thereby dispelling fear‐driven discrimination and fostering social reintegration [5]. Clinically, systematic monitoring within the 3‐month post‐recovery window is essential to detect late‐onset complications, facilitate early intervention, and support post‐COVID‐19 recovery.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the relationship between perceived social stigma and quality of life. Although using multivariable regression models could control key observed confounders, this statistical adjustment cannot overcome the inherent limitation of our cross‐sectional design. Specifically, cross‐sectional data cannot establish temporal relationships or causality between perceived social stigma, persistent symptoms, and quality of life decline. Hence, longitudinal studies are warranted to delineate the causal pathways and long‐term dynamics among these variables. Second, although the C‐19‐PDS underwent pilot testing and forward‐backward translation to ensure content validity, it lacks formal cultural validation for the Vietnamese population, potentially introducing measurement bias. Third, reliance on self‐reported data for health status and stigma may incur recall bias. We mitigated this by limiting recruitment to a 1–3‐month post‐recovery window and using structured instruments. Third, although perceived stigma may not capture all enacted discrimination, its strong correlation with quality‐of‐life outcomes underscores its clinical relevance. Future research should prioritize longitudinal monitoring of vulnerable cohorts to strengthen societal resilience against subsequent health emergencies [35, 36]. Finally, our sample included a relatively small proportion of participants who had been hospitalized compared with those who had experienced COVID‐19 in the community. This may reflect the younger demographic and the predominance of mild infections in the community setting. However, this feature may limit the generalizability of our results to older populations or individuals with more severe acute COVID‐19 requiring hospitalization or intensive care. Therefore, caution is warranted when comparing our findings with cohorts of hospitalized or elderly patients, in whom the prevalence and severity of post‐COVID‐19 symptoms and their impact on quality of life may differ.

Conclusions

Post‐COVID‐19 symptoms are common among COVID‐19 survivors in Vietnam, and social stigma remains prevalent and significantly associated with reduced quality of life in the post‐recovery period. Addressing both physical sequelae and social stigma is essential to support full recovery and reintegration into society. Public health strategies should prioritize post‐COVID‐19 care, mental health support, stigma reduction, and community education. Lessons learned from the COVID‐19 pandemic highlight the importance of preparing health systems and societies to address not only biomedical but also the social consequences of future public health crises.
