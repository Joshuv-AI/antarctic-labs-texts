L-Theanine mitigates skin aging in rats via modulation of the AGEs-RAGE pathway, enhancement of antioxidant defense, and attenuation of inflammation
Authors: Dan Xu, Yutong Yang, Xinyu Zheng, Fan Wang, Zhongshan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the anti-aging effects of L-theanine in a D-galactose-induced aging model in rats and to elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. Aging was induced via subcutaneous administration of D-galactose, while L-theanine was delivered orally for eight weeks. Skin aging was induced in rats by daily subcutaneous injection of D-galactose. L-theanine was administered orally for eight weeks. Skin condition was assessed through by macroscopic observation and histological examination. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were conducted to measure oxidative stress parameters, inflammatory cytokines, and AGEs/RAGE expression levels. Introduction

Skin aging is a complex, multifactorial process influenced by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including metabolic alterations, oxidative stress, and inflammation. As the most readily accessible organ, the skin serves as an optimal model for elucidating aging mechanisms and evaluating anti-aging interventions1,2.

A primary contributor to aging is the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), irreversible compounds generated through the Maillard reaction between reducing sugars and amino groups of proteins or lipids. AGEs compromise the structural and mechanical integrity of the skin by modifying collagen and elastin fibers, and activate the receptor for AGEs (RAGE), initiating downstream pro-inflammatory and oxidative signaling cascades3–7. These cascades involve the activation of NF-κB and MAPK pathways, upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, and overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), collectively accelerating cellular senescence and tissue degradation8–12.

The AGEs-RAGE signaling axis has been implicated in numerous age-related pathologies, including diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases13–15. Consequently, strategies aimed at inhibiting AGEs formation or disrupting AGEs-RAGE signaling are considered promising approaches to delay aging and attenuate age-associated functional decline.

L-theanine (L-T), a non-proteinogenic amino acid abundantly present in tea leaves, has attracted considerable attention for its favorable safety profile and multifaceted bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and anti-aging effects16–20. Emerging evidence suggests that L-theanine may reduce AGEs accumulation, enhance antioxidant enzyme activities, and suppress RAGE expression in hepatic and intestinal models21–23. It has also been shown to modulate pivotal inflammatory pathways, such as NF-κB and MAPK24,25, although direct evidence within skin aging models remains limited.

In this study, we investigated the effects of L-theanine on skin aging using a D-galactose-induced rat model. We hypothesized that L-theanine would mitigate aging by reducing AGEs/RAGE accumulation and restoring oxidative and inflammatory balance. While our in vivo results supported the protective role of L-theanine, the lack of direct molecular validation remains a limitation. Therefore, future studies should employ molecular techniques (e.g., Western blot, qPCR) and in vitro cellular models such as human dermal fibroblasts to delineate the precise mechanisms. Additionally, long-term studies with extended observation periods and comprehensive dose–response designs are warranted to further define the therapeutic potential of L-theanine against skin aging.

Materials and methods

Animal model and grouping

Forty five-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (n = 40; weight: 180–220 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Guizhou Medical University. The animals were housed in standard cages under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C; 12-hour light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water, and were acclimatized for seven days prior to experimentation. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No. 2403387), and all experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

An aging model was established by daily subcutaneous injection of D-galactose (200 mg/kg) for eight consecutive weeks26. Rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8 per group): (1) Control group: subcutaneous saline + oral saline; (2) Model group: subcutaneous D-galactose + oral saline; (3) Low-dose L-theanine group: subcutaneous D-galactose + oral L-theanine (100 mg/kg); (4) Medium-dose L-theanine group: subcutaneous D-galactose + oral L-theanine (200 mg/kg); (5) High-dose L-theanine group: subcutaneous D-galactose + oral L-theanine (400 mg/kg).

Oral administration was performed once daily by intragastric gavage, two hours after subcutaneous injection (see Table 1 for group allocation).

Evaluation of skin appearance

The dorsal skin of each rat (4 × 4 cm area) was shaved weekly, and surface characteristics were documented every seven days. Standardized photographs were taken under uniform lighting and background conditions.

Macroscopic photographs were evaluated qualitatively by two independent investigators blinded to group assignments. Observations included the presence of wrinkles, pigmentation, and hair changes. Scoring systems designed for human photoaging, such as the Glogau scale27, were not applied because of their inapplicability to rodent models.

Histological analysis

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Paraffin-embedded skin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin, differentiated in acid alcohol, neutralized in ammonia, and counterstained with eosin. Sections were then dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Following deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and Masson’s Ponceau acid fuchsin, differentiated in phosphomolybdic acid, counterstained with aniline blue, and rinsed with glacial acetic acid. Sections were subsequently dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and sealed with neutral balsam.

ELISA-Based biochemical assays

Antioxidant enzyme activity

Cardiac blood was collected and centrifuged (4 °C, 3000 rpm, 10 min), and the serum was stored at −80 °C. Skin and liver tissues were rinsed in cold PBS, weighed (0.1 g), homogenized in 1 mL of extraction buffer, and sonicated on ice. Homogenates were centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), and the supernatants were collected for quantification of the following parameters: (1) Superoxide dismutase (SOD, JL22893-96T, JONLNBIO)28; (2) Catalase (CAT, RX300417R, RUIXIN BIOTECH)29; (3) Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, JL21016-96T, JONLNBIO)30; (4) Malondialdehyde (MDA, RXJW302836R, RUIXIN BIOTECH)31; (5) Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, RXWB0465-96, ABTS method).

Note

MDA was classified as a marker of lipid peroxidation rather than as an antioxidant enzyme, ensuring scientific accuracy.

Inflammatory cytokine quantification

Levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the skin, liver, and serum were quantified using ELISA kits (RUIXIN BIOTECH, China: IL-6 RX2D302196, IL-1β RX2D302066, TNF-α RX2D310636) according to the manufacturer’s instructions32,33.

Detection of ages and RAGE

Advanced glycation end products (AGEs, JL12657-96T, JONLNBIO) and the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE, JL21310-96T, JONLNBIO) were measured in the skin, liver, and serum using ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue preparation followed the procedure described in Sect. “Antioxidant enzyme activity”34.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 8, and histological quantification was conducted using ImageJ. Prior to statistical testing, data normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For normally distributed and homoscedastic data, one-way ANOVA was applied followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. For non-normal or heteroscedastic data, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons (Holm adjustment) was employed. A two-tailed α level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p-values are reported for key comparisons to enhance transparency and enable effect size estimation.

Results

L-Theanine improves skin appearance in aged rats

After eight weeks of D-galactose administration, rats in the model group displayed evident signs of skin aging, characterized by deep static wrinkles, erythema, hair thinning, and loss of coat luster. In contrast, L-theanine treatment alleviated these aging-related changes in a dose-dependent manner. Rats receiving medium and high doses exhibited smoother skin, diminished erythema, improved hair density, and brighter fur. Notably, the overall appearance of the high-dose group closely resembled that of the control group (Fig. 1).

Representative macroscopic images of dorsal skin appearance (4 × 4 cm shaved area, photographed weekly). Prior to the experiment, all rats exhibited white skin and bright coat color. After D-galactose administration, the model group developed deep static wrinkles, erythema, hair thinning, and darkened coat color, whereas L-theanine treatment alleviated these changes in a dose-dependent manner.

L-Theanine preserves skin histological architecture

Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed pronounced epidermal thinning and inflammatory cell infiltration in the model group. In contrast, L-theanine administration significantly increased epidermal thickness and reduced inflammatory infiltration relative to the model group (Fig. 2a and b). Masson’s trichrome staining further demonstrated that L-theanine preserved collagen architecture. While the model group exhibited sparse and disorganized collagen fibers, L-theanine-treated groups showed denser and more uniformly aligned fibers, with the high-dose group displaying morphology most comparable to the control group (Fig. 3a and b).

L-Theanine reduces ages and RAGE levels in Skin, Liver, and serum

ELISA results showed markedly elevated levels of AGEs and RAGE in the skin, liver, and serum of the model group, confirming the successful establishment of the aging model. L-theanine administration significantly reduced AGEs and RAGE levels in a dose-dependent manner across all examined tissues (Fig. 4a and b), suggesting its potential to attenuate glycation-associated tissue damage in vivo.

L-Theanine enhances systemic antioxidant defense

Relative to the model group, L-theanine treatment significantly decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the skin, liver, and serum (Fig. 5a). In parallel, activities of key antioxidant enzymes—including superoxide dismutase (SOD, Fig. 5b), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, Fig. 5c), and catalase (CAT, Fig. 5d)—were significantly elevated in all L-theanine-treated groups. Moreover, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) was markedly enhanced, especially in the medium- and high-dose groups (Fig. 5e). Collectively, these results indicate that L-theanine substantially strengthens systemic antioxidative defenses.

L-Theanine suppresses inflammatory cytokine production

D-galactose administration markedly increased levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the skin, liver, and serum. In contrast, L-theanine treatment significantly reduced cytokine levels in a dose-dependent manner across all examined tissues (Fig. 6a and c), demonstrating its pronounced systemic anti-inflammatory activity.

Discussion

Skin aging is a multifactorial process primarily influenced by oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs). In this study, we demonstrated that L-theanine, a naturally occurring amino acid, attenuated D-galactose-induced skin aging in rats. L-theanine administration reduced AGEs and RAGE levels, suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities across the skin, liver, and serum. These findings suggest that L-theanine may exert anti-aging effects by modulating the AGEs/RAGE axis and alleviating the associated oxidative and inflammatory responses, although causal mechanisms were not directly validated.

The accumulation of AGEs is widely recognized as a hallmark of aging, particularly in skin tissue, where protein cross-linking compromises collagen integrity and elasticity while excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation accelerates tissue damage35–37. Consistent with previous studies, our results showed markedly elevated AGEs and RAGE levels in the D-galactose model group. L-theanine supplementation reversed these changes, suggesting its potential to limit AGEs accumulation and/or promote AGEs clearance. Prior research has demonstrated that L-theanine can reduce AGEs levels and downregulate RAGE expression in hepatic tissue, possibly through restoration of redox balance and mitochondrial function38. Our findings extend these observations to cutaneous tissue and indicate broader systemic effects.

Oxidative stress is closely linked to aging. In this study, L-theanine significantly increased the activities of key antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GSH-Px) and decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels—a marker of lipid peroxidation—across multiple tissues. These improvements were dose-dependent, with the high-dose group showing the most pronounced benefits. These results align with previous studies reporting that L-theanine enhances antioxidant defenses via NRF2-mediated pathways and glutathione biosynthesis39,40. Although these pathways were not directly examined here, they remain important targets for future mechanistic validation.

Chronic inflammation is another critical contributor to skin aging, partly mediated by AGEs-RAGE interactions that activate downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways23–25,41,42. Here, L-theanine significantly reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels in all tested tissues, supporting its systemic anti-inflammatory activity. These findings are consistent with reports that L-theanine suppresses NF-κB signaling and downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokine production42–44. However, molecular endpoints such as NF-κB p65 or MAPK phosphorylation were not directly assessed, limiting mechanistic interpretation.

A key limitation of this study is the absence of in vitro validation for the proposed signaling pathways. Future studies should employ human dermal fibroblast (HDF) or keratinocyte models to determine whether L-theanine directly modulates AGEs-RAGE signaling, NF-κB activation, or MAPK phosphorylation under oxidative stress. Such models would also facilitate investigation of collagen synthesis, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) expression, and markers of cellular senescence, including p21, p53, and SA-β-gal activity.

Another limitation is that, although we observed clear dose-dependent effects during the 8-week treatment period, the long-term efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing regimen of L-theanine remain undefined. Extended-duration studies incorporating broader dose ranges, pharmacokinetic profiling, and longitudinal biomarker analysis will be essential to evaluate clinical translatability.

Finally, the interplay between skin aging and systemic metabolic organs warrants further investigation. Given that AGEs are primarily metabolized in the liver and excreted through the kidneys45,46, the decline in serum AGEs levels observed here may reflect hepatic metabolism and clearance rather than direct cutaneous effects. This underscores the need for future studies employing organ-specific interventions, such as topical delivery or tissue-targeted knockouts, to disentangle systemic versus skin-specific contributions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that L-theanine attenuates D-galactose-induced skin aging in rats, as evidenced by reduced AGEs and RAGE levels, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, and decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines. These beneficial effects were observed not only in the skin but also in the liver and serum, indicating systemic activity. The outcomes were dose-dependent, with the greatest improvements in the high-dose group.

While our findings highlight the promising anti-aging potential of L-theanine, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Future work should focus on validating its effects on key signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, MAPK) and confirming these observations in vitro using human skin cell models. Furthermore, long-term studies with extended observation periods, dose–response profiling, and pharmacokinetic evaluation will be necessary to establish optimal dosing regimens and assess clinical safety.

In conclusion, L-theanine represents a promising natural compound for mitigating skin aging through modulation of the AGEs-RAGE axis, reduction of oxidative stress, and suppression of inflammation. These findings provide a foundation for its further development as a potential functional ingredient in dermatological and anti-aging applications.
