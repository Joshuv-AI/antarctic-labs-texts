Seasonal, Regional, and Clinical Factors Associated With Manipulation Under Anesthesia Following Total Knee Arthroplasty: A PearlDiver Study
Authors: Tera A Scott, Randall R Rainwater, Louis S Steen, Simon C Mears, C Lowry Barnes, Jeffrey B Stambough, Benjamin M Stronach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a widely performed and highly effective surgical intervention for patients with advanced knee osteoarthritis, offering significant pain relief and improved mobility. However, a subset of patients experiences postoperative stiffness, which impairs overall knee function and reduces patient satisfaction. When conservative measures like physical therapy fail, manipulation under anesthesia (MUA) is often employed to improve range of motion and prevent long-term arthrofibrosis [1,2].

The incidence of MUA following TKA ranges from approximately 0.5% to 10%, and multiple patient- and surgery-related factors have been associated with increased risk [3]. Previous studies have identified that nonobese women, particularly those under 65 years and with low comorbidity burdens (ASA ≤2), are most likely to require MUA [3]. Other associations have been inconsistently reported, including keloid formation, hyperlipidemia, discharge disposition, and certain medication exposures [4,5]. Conversely, several commonly implicated comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, anxiety, depression, rheumatoid arthritis, and opioid abuse/dependence have not been found to significantly increase MUA rates following TKA [6,7]. While demographic predictors appear relatively reproducible, the biologic mechanisms underlying postoperative stiffness remain incompletely understood. From a pathophysiologic perspective, stiffness after TKA is driven by capsular fibrosis, myofibroblast activation, and dysregulated inflammatory signaling, particularly along transforming growth factor-β pathways [8,9]. Local joint conditions reflecting prior tissue injury or contracture, as well as systemic inflammatory or fibrotic diseases, may therefore plausibly influence postoperative capsular remodeling and the risk of arthrofibrosis. However, few large-scale studies have examined inflammatory and fibrotic pathologies specifically as mechanistic contributors to MUA risk.

In addition to patient-specific risk factors, little is known about the influence of environmental factors such as geography and seasonality on the incidence of MUA. Prior studies have demonstrated that healthcare delivery and surgical outcomes can vary significantly by region and time of year, influenced by differences in climate, patient medical literacy, and cultural or socioeconomic factors [[10], [11], [12]]. For instance, cold weather may limit patient mobility and adherence to physical therapy, particularly in northern regions with severe winters. Similarly, the winter holiday season can disrupt rehabilitation schedules due to clinic closures, reduced staffing, and competing personal obligations. These disruptions, combined with generally lower physical activity levels during winter months, may hinder early recovery and contribute to increased MUA rates [13]. Given that access to and engagement with rehabilitation are modifiable risk factors, seasonal and regional variation in these patterns may influence postoperative stiffness risk and the likelihood of undergoing MUA.

To address these gaps, we utilized the PearlDiver database to conduct a national-level retrospective cohort analysis examining both patient-specific and environmental risk factors for MUA following TKA. We hypothesized that (1) pre-existing inflammatory, fibrotic, and musculoskeletal conditions would be independently associated with altered risk of postoperative MUA due to their effects on capsular remodeling and tissue healing, and (2) regional and seasonal variation would be associated with differences in MUA rates, reflecting disparities in rehabilitation access and postoperative recovery patterns. Improved identification of biologically and environmentally mediated risk factors may inform personalized perioperative strategies aimed at reducing postoperative stiffness and the need for MUA.

Material and methods

In this retrospective database review, we used the PearlDiver Mariner170 dataset. The Mariner dataset includes medical and prescription claims for 170 million patients from 2010 through April 30, 2023, incorporating all payer types and containing International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Ninth and Tenth Revisions diagnosis codes and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) procedure codes to capture medical and surgical diagnoses, procedures, healthcare costs, and prescription data. This study was performed in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data guidelines [14]. Because the PearlDiver database contains deidentified information and is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, institutional review board approval was not required.

Patients who had a diagnosis code for TKA (CPT-27447) and had active medical records within 90 days post-TKA were defined (n = 1,703,497). Of these subjects, those documented as receiving MUA (CPT-27570) were selected (n = 49,594). These groups were further screened based on surgical geography (Midwest, South, Northeast, or West), time of year (winter [December-February], spring [March-May], summer [June-August], or fall [September-November]), patient demographics (age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI]), and the presence for specific comorbidities (smoking status, obesity, autoimmune disorders, musculoskeletal and orthopaedic history, fibrotic pathologies, renal, and metabolic comorbidities). Variables were selected based on prior literature demonstrating associations with fibrosis, inflammation, or functional recovery, as well as their availability within the PearlDiver database [9,15,16]. Patients were not matched for this study. The CPT, ICD-Ninth Revision, and ICD-Tenth Revision codes used to identify complications and the comorbidities used in CCI calculations are outlined in Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis was conducted using the statistical tools available within the PearlDiver database. Multivariate regression was used to assess associations between MUA and predictor variables, and chi-square analysis was performed to evaluate regional and seasonal variation, both with statistical significance set at an alpha level of 0.001 to reduce the risk of type 1 error. For continuous variables, independent t-tests were utilized to assess differences between groups, also with an alpha level of 0.001. All analyses were performed in accordance with established statistical practices to ensure the validity of the findings.

Results

The incidence of TKA has increased from 2011 (n = 118,897) to 2022 (n = 168,796) while the rate of MUA has steadily decreased from 3.12% to 2.61% over the same period (Fig. 1, slope = −0.059% per year, P < .001). Of the 1,703,407 TKA recipients, 49,594 patients (2.91%) underwent MUA within 90 days post-TKA. Patients who underwent MUA were on average 5 years younger (61.6 vs 66.3 years, P < .001), more often women (62.4 vs 64.5%, P < .001), and had a lower CCI (1.26 vs 1.51, P < .001) than their no MUA counterparts (Table 1).

A comprehensive analysis of comorbidities revealed that multiple musculoskeletal, fibrotic, autoimmune, renal, and metabolic conditions were significantly associated with variations in MUA risk. Several musculoskeletal and fibrotic conditions increased MUA rates, including patellar dislocation (relative risk [RR]: 1.81; confidence interval [CI]: 1.60-2.04), prior ligamentous knee injury (RR: 1.22; CI: 1.14-1.30), patellar tendonitis or bursitis (RR: 1.22; CI: 1.14-1.31), arthrofibrosis (RR: 1.68; CI: 1.49-1.89), and pathologic contracture (RR: 1.15; CI: 1.07-1.24), all with P < .001 (Tables 2 and 3). Conversely, a history of pathology in joints other than the operative knee was associated with a decreased risk of MUA after primary TKA. Patients with prior total hip arthroplasty (RR: 0.79; CI: 0.73-0.83; P < .001), revision total hip arthroplasty/TKA (RR: 0.61; CI: 0.53-0.69; P < .001), periprosthetic joint infection (RR: 0.40; CI: 0.28-0.57; P < .001), periprosthetic fracture (RR: 0.30; CI: 0.17-0.54; P < .001), aseptic loosening (RR: 0.69; CI: 0.55-0.85; P < .001), and pulmonary fibrosis (RR: 0.73; CI: 0.67-0.80; P < .001) were significantly associated with reduced MUA incidence (Tables 2 and 3). No significant differences were found among rates of knee instability, pathologic fracture, patellar fracture, sarcoidosis, scleroderma, and systemic sclerosis.

MUA rates were highest in the Midwest (3.22%, P < .001) and lowest in the South (2.65%, P < .001) (Fig. 2 and Table 5). Seasonally, MUA rates were higher in the winter (3.07%, P < .001) and lower in the summer (2.82%, P < .001), with no difference across spring and fall (Table 6). The day of surgery did not significantly affect MUA rates, with Monday-Thursday TKA patients (n = 1,617,509) showing a 90-day MUA rate of 2.84% compared to 2.95% for Friday cases (n = 146,821; RR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.99-1.05; P = .13).

Inter-regional analysis showed that MUA was more likely in the Midwest (RR: 1.16; CI: 1.14-1.18; P < .001) and West (RR: 1.05; CI: 1.02-1.08; P < .001), and less likely in the South (RR: 0.87; CI: 0.85-0.88; P < .001) (Table 5). No significant difference in MUA rates was seen in the Northeast (RR: 0.97; CI: 0.95-0.99; P = .01) when compared to all other regions (Table 5). Inter-seasonal analysis demonstrated increased MUA rates in winter (RR: 1.08; CI: 1.06-1.10; P < .001) and decreased rates in summer (RR: 0.96; CI: 0.94-0.98; P < .001), with no significant differences in spring (RR: 0.99; CI: 0.97-1.01; P = .26) and fall (RR: 0.98; CI: 0.96-1.00; P = .05) (Table 6). Multivariate analysis revealed that age had the strongest association with the likelihood of undergoing MUA (t = −136.1, P < .001), followed by gender and CCI. Discussion

Our study supports other regional and multi-institutional reports describing MUA is more common in younger women with lower comorbidity scores [6,17]. We identified several specific fibrotic and musculoskeletal conditions, including arthrofibrosis, patellar dislocation, ligamentous knee injury, patellar tendonitis or bursitis, and pathologic contracture, that were associated with significantly increased MUA risk. These findings align with previous studies reporting that joint-specific pathologies, particularly those involving capsular fibrosis or altered knee biomechanics, can predispose patients to postoperative stiffness and necessitate manipulation [18,19]. Together, these findings reinforce that both biological predisposition to fibrosis and preexisting structural abnormalities of the knee play a central role in postoperative motion recovery.

Younger age remained the strongest independent predictor of MUA, aligning with meta-analyses reporting that MUA patients are on average approximately 4.2 years younger than non-MUA patients [6]. The mechanisms underlying this association remain incompletely understood, though younger patients often have higher functional expectations and activity demands, which may lower both patient and surgeon thresholds for pursuing MUA when postoperative motion is limited [3]. Female sex was also independently associated with increased MUA risk, consistent with prior investigations [6,20]. Biological differences in collagen metabolism and connective tissue remodeling influenced by estrogen have been shown to alter fibroblast activity and extracellular matrix deposition, potentially increasing susceptibility to fibrotic complications [21,22]. Differences in pain perception, joint laxity, and healthcare utilization patterns between sexes may also contribute to earlier recognition and treatment of stiffness [23,24]. Lower CCI was similarly associated with increased MUA risk, which likely reflects treatment selection rather than true biological protection. Healthier patients are more likely to undergo secondary procedures due to lower anesthesia risk and greater functional recovery expectations, whereas surgeons may be more conservative in medically complex patients [20,25,26].

Analysis of knee-specific musculoskeletal and fibrotic conditions demonstrated strong associations between prior local pathology and increased MUA risk. Arthrofibrosis, characterized by excessive fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition, leads to capsular thickening and restricted motion and was among the strongest predictors [19,27]. Similarly, ligamentous injury, patellar instability, and tendon pathology may alter normal joint biomechanics, increase baseline inflammation, and disrupt soft tissue balance, predisposing patients to postoperative stiffness [26]. Pathologic contractures reflect preexisting soft tissue limitations that may persist postoperatively, consistent with prior studies linking preoperative stiffness and knee pathology to increased MUA risk [19]. Interestingly, prior arthroplasty-related conditions, including previous joint replacement, revision surgery, and periprosthetic complications, were associated with reduced MUA risk. These associations likely reflect altered patient expectations, rehabilitation experience, and surgeon decision-making thresholds rather than biologic protection against fibrosis. Patients familiar with postoperative recovery may tolerate limited motion differently, and surgeons may pursue more conservative management in medically complex or surgically experienced individuals.

Several systemic comorbidities, including renal disease, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and autoimmune conditions, were associated with lower MUA risk, likely reflecting differences in physiologic reserve, perioperative risk, and treatment selection rather than direct modulation of fibrotic pathways [20]. Patients with chronic kidney disease, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia often have altered metabolic or cardiovascular physiology, which may prompt surgeons to pursue more conservative management and limit additional procedures such as MUA [4,25,28]. Similarly, autoimmune conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, and psoriasis, are often treated with immunosuppressive medications that inhibit fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, potentially reducing capsular contracture and arthrofibrosis [29,30]. This may help explain why rheumatoid arthritis patients have been shown in prior studies to achieve greater postoperative range of motion compared to osteoarthritis patients, despite a higher overall complication risk [30,31]. However, when controlling for covariates in multivariate analysis, these associations changed, with renal disease no longer demonstrating a significant relationship with MUA risk, metabolic disorders associated with an increased likelihood of MUA, and autoimmune conditions remaining associated with a decreased risk. These shifts suggest that some apparent protective effects observed on univariate analysis may reflect confounding from patient characteristics and that underlying metabolic and inflammatory factors may play a more complex role in postoperative stiffness when other variables are accounted for.

Prior research has demonstrated that patients in the Midwest and West are more likely to receive home physical therapy as opposed to outpatient therapy post-TKA [32]. This may lead to reduced supervision, lower therapy intensity, and less consistent follow-up, all of which could contribute to an increased need for MUA. Additionally, patient and provider expectations surrounding physical function and recovery may affect interventions intensity to restore mobility. The Western region includes 19 of the 25 most active counties in the United States and has the highest proportion of assisted living communities nationally [33,34]. This is notable as both patients and providers in these regions may be more proactive in pursuing MUA to restore optimal function. The increase in MUA rates during the winter months, as observed in our study, may relate to seasonal barriers to postoperative rehabilitation. Colder temperatures and inclement weather have been associated with reduced mobility, decreased adherence to physical therapy, and delayed outpatient follow-up, all of which may contribute to postoperative stiffness [35]. Psychosocial factors such as seasonal affective disorder or decreased motivation in colder months may also play a role in reduced engagement with recovery protocols [36]. We also hypothesized that reduced weekend staffing, therapy availability, and resource access might delay early postoperative care—particularly for Friday surgeries—thereby increasing stiffness risk. However, multivariate analysis showed no higher incidence of MUA after Friday cases. This underscores the strength of current perioperative protocols and discharge planning, highlighting how standardized recovery pathways and early outpatient therapy effectively ensure consistent outcomes regardless of surgery day.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces selection bias as TKA indication was not captured. Use of administrative data limits clinical detail, omitting factors such as preoperative range of motion, surgical technique, rehabilitation protocols, pain management, or patient adherence—all of which affect MUA risk. The decision to perform MUA is subjective and varies by surgeon, introducing selection and treatment bias that limit standardized conclusions across a heterogeneous population. The database also lacks information on criteria used to offer MUA, patients who declined the procedure, and surgeon-specific thresholds, introducing further selection and treatment bias. Coding inaccuracies and unmeasured confounders such as socioeconomic status, healthcare access, practice patterns, or climate may also influence observed variation, while seasonal definitions may not be consistent across regions. Although MUA rates varied significantly across regions and seasons, the absolute differences were small (<1%) and unlikely to affect individual patient care. Outcomes were limited to 90 days, missing delayed or nonsurgical management, and the insured-only cohort limits generalizability. These limitations suggest findings should be interpreted cautiously; nonetheless, this study provides the first national-level assessment of geographic and seasonal MUA trends and identifies clinical factors associated with increased risk, offering new insight into how environmental and patient-specific factors intersect post-TKA.

Conclusions

In summary, younger age and female sex are the strongest predictors of MUA, with fibrotic and musculoskeletal conditions and lower comorbidity burden also contributing substantially. While geographic and seasonal variation reached statistical significance, their overall impact was comparatively modest. These findings highlight that patient demographics and clinical characteristics are the primary drivers of MUA risk and underscore targets for early intervention, patient counseling, and rehabilitation optimization. Future research should clarify the biological and clinical mechanisms underlying these associations to inform targeted prevention strategies and personalized rehabilitation aimed at reducing postoperative stiffness and improving outcomes.
