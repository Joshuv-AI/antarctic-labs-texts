Computations in Equivariant Gromov–Witten theory of GKM spaces
Daniel Holmes, Giosuè Muratore

Abstract
. We study equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants and quantum cohomology in GKM theory. Building on the localization formula, we prove that the resulting expression is independent of the choice of compatible connection, and provide an equivalent formulation without auxiliary choices. Motivated by this theoretical refinement, we develop a software package, 𝙶𝙺𝙼𝚝𝚘𝚘𝚕𝚜 . 𝚓𝚕 \mathtt{GKMtools.jl} , that implements the computation of equivariant GW invariants and quantum products directly from the GKM graph. We apply our framework to several geometric settings: Calabi–Yau rank two vector bundles on the projective line, where we obtain a new proof for a recent connection to Donaldson–Thomas theory of Kronecker quivers; twisted flag manifolds, which give symplectic but non-algebraic examples of GKM spaces; realizability questions for abstract GKM graphs; and classical enumerative problems involving curves in hyperplanes. These results demonstrate both the theoretical flexibility of GKM methods and the effectiveness of computational tools in exploring new phenomena.

1.
Introduction

GKM theory (after Goresky–Kottwitz–MacPherson
[
GKM98
]
) provides a combinatorial framework to study the geometry and topology of certain
T
-spaces with finitely many fixed points.
The essential idea is that the equivariant cohomology ring can be encoded in a finite graph, the
GKM graph
, together with edge-label data arising from the isotropy representations. This correspondence has become a valuable tool in equivariant symplectic geometry, algebraic geometry, and representation theory, as it translates global geometric phenomena into tractable combinatorial structures.

Beyond cohomology, GKM theory has also found applications in enumerative geometry, in particular in the study of equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants and quantum cohomology.
Localization techniques reduce the evaluation of these invariants to contributions associated to fixed loci of the torus action on the moduli space, which can themselves be expressed in terms of GKM graphs. This approach has led to explicit formulas for Gromov–Witten invariants of GKM spaces
[
LS17
,
Hir23
]
. Nevertheless, the resulting expressions are often complicated by auxiliary data, most notably the requirement to identify the compatible connection on the GKM graph induced from the geometry of the space, and by the intricate combinatorics of the resulting contributions.

The purpose of this paper is twofold. On the theoretical side, we revisit the localization formula for equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants in the GKM setting, and establish a reformulation that is independent of any choice of connection. On the computational side, we introduce a software package,
GKMtools.jl
, designed to make the theoretical machinery of GKM theory effective in explicit calculations. The package provides a general framework for working with GKM graphs, for computing equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants, and for evaluating quantum products.

Let us highlight two of our mathematical results.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
3.7
)
.

The equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants of an algebraic or Hamiltonian GKM space can be computed purely in terms of the GKM graph, without
knowledge of the compatible connection induced by the space.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
5.5
, §
5.1.3
)
.

Let
X_{k}
be the GKM variety given by the total space of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(k-1)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-k-1)
, linearized by an algebraic torus
T
acting with exactly two fixed points. Suppose
k\geq 1
.
If
X_{k}
is
equivariantly Calabi–Yau
, that is
c_{1}^{T}(T_{X_{k}})=0
, then

(1.1)

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{(-1)^{d(k+1)-1}}{d^{3}k^{2}}\binom{k^{2}d}{d}

where
\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}\in H_{2}(X_{k})
is the class of the zero section.
Moreover, the associated
BPS
numbers
n_{0,d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}(X_{k})
satisfy

n_{0,d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}(X_{k})=\frac{1}{d(k+1)}D(d,d,k+1)

where

is the diagonal Donaldson–Thomas invariant of degree
d
of the Kronecker quiver
K_{m}
.

We proved Theorem
1.2
directly using the localization formula.
Independently, a completely different proof of this result appeared in
[
vGNS25
, Theorem 3.2]
. Their approach uses degenerations of log Gromov–Witten invariants and the relation between refined DT invariants of quivers and log BPS invariants of log Calabi–Yau surfaces given by
[
Bou20
, Theorem 8.13]
.
Yet another approach would be to use the results of
[
BP08
]
(see Remark
5.1
). An example from the physics literature is
[
CGMn+07
, Equation (4.53)]
.

The contents of the paper are organized as follows. In Section
2
we review the necessary basic notions of GKM theory and describe the relations between curve classes corresponding to edges of the GKM graph (Proposition
2.34
).
In Section
3
we recall the definitions of equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants and quantum cohomology, together with the localization formula of
[
LS17
]
and
[
Hir23
]
. We then prove in Theorem
3.6
that this formula is independent of the choice of a compatible connection on the GKM graph, and provide an equivalent formulation without reference to such a choice. Section
4
introduces the package
GKMtools.jl
and explains its main functionalities. In Section
5
we present a series of applications, ranging from concrete calculations to structural results:

•

In Section
5.1
we compute equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants (with connected domain curves) of Calabi–Yau rank two vector bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, identify the cases where these invariants are rational (Lemma
5.3
), and establish explicit formulas (Theorem
5.5
). In the equivariantly Calabi–Yau case we recover in a new way a recent relation with diagonal Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the Kronecker quiver (Section
5.1.3
).

•

In Section
5.2
we give explicit examples of equivariant quantum products for twisted flag manifolds (Theorems
5.12
and
5.14
). These are Hamiltonian GKM spaces that do not admit compatible Kähler structures and hence fall strictly within the symplectic, non-algebraic category.

•

In Section

we show how equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants can be used to prove non-realizability results for certain GKM graphs, ruling out their occurrence as Hamiltonian or algebraic GKM spaces.

•

In Section
5.4
we illustrate how the GKM approach extends to classical enumerative questions, such as the enumeration of curves contained in hyperplanes.

Our results show the strength of GKM theoretic methods in Gromov–Witten theory and provide computational infrastructure to explore further examples.

We expect this combination of theoretical and algorithmic developments to serve as a useful resource for future investigations in symplectic geometry, algebraic geometry, and mathematical physics.

2.
GKM theory

In this section, we recall the necessary background on GKM theory (see
[
GZ01
,
GKZ24
]
for more details).
GKM spaces are named after the influential paper by Goresky, Kottwitz, and MacPherson
[
GKM98
]
. They provide a large class of examples for which a lot of geometry can be reduced to combinatorial properties of the attached
GKM graph
.

2.1.
GKM spaces

Throughout this paper, we will deal with two cases simultaneously: the smooth quasi-projective algebraic case and the symplectic Hamiltonian case.
This distinction reflects the fact that Gromov–Witten invariants are defined differently in these two settings. Nevertheless, our results can often be stated in some form that holds in both contexts. The central object of our paper will be a topological space
X
together with a group action of a group
T
. We call such a structure a
T
-space.

By variety we mean an integral separable complex scheme of finite type.

By
(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{r}
we mean
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{\times r}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
T
-space
X
is
algebraic GKM
if

(1)

X
is a smooth quasi-projective variety,
T=(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{r}
is a torus and the action is algebraic,

(2)

there are only finitely many fixed points, i.e.
0<|X^{T}|<\infty
, and

(3)

for each
p\in X^{T}
, the
T
-weights of
T_{p}X
are pairwise linearly independent.

Before defining the notion of
Hamiltonian GKM
spaces, we need to recall the notion of Hamiltonian group action in the context of compact real Lie groups acting on smooth manifolds.

Definition 2.2

(
[
GGK02
, §1.1]
)
.

Let
T
be a compact real torus acting smoothly on a symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
. Let
\mathfrak{t}
be the Lie algebra of
T
and
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
the dual Lie algebra.

The action is called
Hamiltonian
if it admits a
moment map
, that is, a smooth map
\mu\colon X\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}^{*}
such that for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{t}
we have
\omega(X_{\xi},\cdot)=\iota_{X_{\xi}}\omega=d\langle\mu,\xi\rangle
, where
X_{\xi}
is the vector field on
X
induced by
\xi
.

A Hamiltonian action automatically preserves the symplectic form
\omega
.
Further important properties of moment maps are recalled in Section
2.2
.

We call a
T
-space
X

Hamiltonian GKM

if
X
satisfies Definition
2.1
, replacing condition (
1
) with the following one:

(
\star
)

X
is a connected manifold with a symplectic form
\omega
and a compatible almost complex structure
J
. The action of the torus
T=(S^{1})^{\times r}
is Hamiltonian and preserves
J
.

An important consequence of this definition is the following: if
T^{\prime}\subset T
is a codimension
1
subtorus, the connected components of
X^{T^{\prime}}
are either points or
2
-spheres
[
GZ00
, §1.5]
. Often, this propriety is part of the definition of GKM manifold, see
[
GSZ12
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
X
is an algebraic GKM space, its analytification

X^{\mathrm{an}}

is naturally a Hamiltonian GKM space. It is enough to restrict the action of
T
to a maximal compact torus
(S^{1})^{\times r}\subset T
. We may prove that this action is Hamiltonian using
[
MFK94
, Examples 8.1(ii)]
. Indeed, if
X
is an open subset of a projective variety
\bar{X}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
, equivariantly embedded via
[
CG10
, Theorem 5.1.25]
, the restriction of the Fubini–Study form is a Kähler form on
X
. Taking an appropriate choice of coordinates, there exists a linearization
\rho\colon T\rightarrow U(n+1)
to the unitary group. The moment map (see
[
Kir84
, Lemma 2.5]
) is defined as

\mu(x)a=\frac{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\rho_{*}(a)\mathbf{x}}{2\pi i\,||\mathbf{x}||^{2}},

where
a\in\mathfrak{t}
and
\mathbf{x}
is a representative vector for
x\in X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

So the Hamiltonian case includes the algebraic one. We still distinguish these cases because the fundamental definitions of Gromov–Witten invariants are different in each case.

Definition 2.4
.

By
GKM space
we mean an algebraic or Hamiltonian GKM space unless otherwise stated.
By a
T
-stable
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in a GKM space we mean either a
T
-invariant 2-sphere in a Hamiltonian GKM space or a
T
-invariant (complex)
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in an algebraic GKM space.
This is compatible with the analytification construction of Remark
2.3
.

Remark 2.5
.

A GKM space has only finitely many
T
-stable
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, see
[
GH04
, Proposition 1.1]
and
[
GKZ24
, Proposition 2.3, Remark 2.4]

Remark 2.6
.

The terms
GKM space
or
GKM manifold
are often defined in more general settings, where a broader class of spaces is allowed and

[

In our setting, equivariant formality holds by

Example 2.7
.

Examples of algebraic GKM spaces include smooth projective toric varieties, and homogeneous spaces

Let

2.2.
The moment map

We briefly recollect some nice properties of the moment map, which is always present in our setting. When
X
is an algebraic GKM space, we consider the moment map of its analytification, as explained in Remark
2.3
.

For Hamiltonian actions by
T=(S^{1})^{\times r}
on a compact symplectic manifold
X
, the moment map is unique up to an additive constant and automatically
T
-invariant
[
GGK02
, Proposition 2.9]
.
By the Atiyah–Giullemin–Sternberg convexity theorem,
\mu(X)\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}
is the convex hull of
\mu(X^{T})
, the images of the fixed points
[
GGK02
, §2.2]
.
Moreover, the
1
-simplices of the boundary of
\mu(X)
are images of the
T
-invariant
2
-spheres.
Conversely, each
T
-invariant
2
-sphere gets mapped by
\mu
to a line segment whose slope is the weight of the
T
-action on the
2
-sphere
(cf. discussion of
local normal form
in
[
GGK02
, §2.2]
and
[
GKZ23
, after Remark 2.11]
).

2.3.
GKM graphs

The combinatorial device which encodes a lot of a GKM space’s geometry and topology is its
GKM graph
.

Definition 2.10

(Underlying graph)
.

Let
X
be an algebraic or Hamiltonian GKM space. Assign to it the undirected graph
G
constructed as follows.

•

The vertices of
G
correspond to
T
-stable points,

•

the flags correspond to pairs
(L,v)
where
v
is a
T
-stable point, and
L
is a
T
-invariant irreducible linear summand of
T_{X,v}
,

•

the edges correspond to pairs
(L_{1},v_{1}),(L_{2},v_{2})
of distinct flags for which there exists a
T
-stable
\mathbb{P}^{1}
(or
S^{2}
) in
X
that contains
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
and to which
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are tangent.

We denote by
V(G),E(G)
and
F(G)
, respectively, the set of vertices, edges and flags of
G
. We will usually denote an edge by
e
, and by
e=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
we mean that
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are the two vertices defining
e
.
We will write
C_{e}
for the
T
-stable
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in
X
corresponding to
e
.
By
E(G)^{\pm}
we denote the set of all flags defined by an edge. Such flags may be denoted as pairs
(e,v_{1}),(e,v_{2})
. We denote by
\overline{(e,v_{1})}
the other flag defined by
e
. Finally, we denote by
F(G)_{v}
(resp.,
E(G)_{v}
) the set of all flags (resp., edges) incident to a vertex

.

The varieties
\mathbb{P}^{1}

Remark 2.12
.

Let
X
be an algebraic GKM space. If it is projective, then all flags are in
E(G)^{\pm}
. Indeed, every flag is tangent to a
T
-stable
\mathbb{P}^{1}

[
GKZ24
, Proposition 2.3]
which has exactly two fixed points.
It defines two flags, and one edge. On the other hand, if
X
is not projective, then we may have flags not in
E(G)^{\pm}
, corresponding to
T
-invariant curves whose closure in

is an affine line. An example of such

is the total space of a vector bundle over a GKM space. When

is Hamiltonian, we replace projectivity with compactness.

Remark 2.13

(
G
is simple)
.

The graph
G
is simple, i.e. it has no multiple edges or loops, see
[
CK25
, Remark 2.7]
. This can be easily shown using the moment map (cf. Section

), which sends each
T
-stable
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to a non-degenerate line segment in
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
whose endpoints are the images of the fixed points and whose slope is a multiple of the
T
-weight of the
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Remark 2.14
.

If
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X=2n
then
G
is
n
-valent,
that is at each vertex the number of edges and number of flags sum up to
n
.

Definition 2.15

(Axial function)
.

Let
X
be an algebraic or Hamiltonian GKM space and
G
the graph in Definition
2.10
. The
axial function
of
X
,

\alpha\colon F(G)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{t}^{*},

assigns to each flag
(L,v)
the
T
-weight of
L
.

It can easily be seen that if
e=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, then
\alpha(e,v_{1})=-\alpha(e,v_{2})
.
By construction,
\alpha
takes values in the character lattice
M\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}
which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
where
r=\text{rank}(T)
.

Definition 2.16

(GKM graph)
.

The
GKM graph
of a GKM space
X
consists of the graph
G
constructed above together with the axial function
\alpha
.

Remark 2.17

(
G
is connected)
.

Note that
G
is connected because
X
is connected, see
[
CK25
, Remark 2.9]
.
This can be shown either by considering the Chang–Skjelbred Lemma or by using the moment map to connect every vertex to a vertex

locally minimizing
\langle\mu(p_{0}),\xi\rangle
for some fixed generic
\xi\in\mathfrak{t}

The number of such

is the Betti number

Hence, there is a unique such

.

The GKM graph constructed from a GKM space is therefore an abstract GKM graph in the following sense.

Definition 2.18

(Abstract GKM graph)
.

An

of dimension

and torus rank

is a connected unoriented

-valent simple graph with flags
G
with an axial function
\alpha\colon F(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{r}
satisfying

(1)

\alpha(e,v_{1})=-\alpha(e,v_{2})
for all
e=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\in E(G)
and

(2)

for all
(L,v),(L^{\prime},v)\in F(G)

and

are linearly independent.

2.4.
Equivariant cohomology

We briefly recall how to express equivariant cohomology of GKM spaces and how to perform equivariant integration using the GKM graph.
We refer to
[
Hsi75
]
,
[
Ati82
]
and
[
AF24
]
for background on equivariant cohomology and equivariant integration.

We write
H_{T}^{*}:=H_{T}^{*}(\{\mathrm{pt}\})\cong\mathbb{Z}[t_{1},\dots,t_{r}]\cong\mathbb{Z}[\mathfrak{t}]
, where
r=\mathrm{rank}(T)
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}
are the
equivariant parameters
.
Using the above isomorphism, we may think of
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}
as elements of
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
.
We then have the restriction map
H_{T}^{*}(X)\rightarrow H^{*}(X)
and the integration maps
\int_{X}^{T}\colon H_{T}^{*}(X)\rightarrow H_{T}^{*}
and
\int\colon H^{*}(X)\rightarrow H^{*}(\{pt\})
of degree
-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X
that are compatible with restriction.
The restriction map
H_{T}^{*}\rightarrow H^{*}(\{pt\})
is given by setting the equivariant parameters to zero.
For any
x\in X^{T}
we have a
T
-equivariant map
\{x\}\rightarrow X
and hence a
localization map

H_{T}^{*}(X)\rightarrow H_{T}^{*}
.
For
\gamma\in H_{T}^{*}(X)
, we write
\gamma|_{x}\in H_{T}^{*}
for the image of
\gamma
under this localization map.

The following
GKM theorem
originates from
[
GKM98
, Theorem 1.2.2]
, see also
[
GZ01
, Theorem 1.7.3]
and references therein.

Theorem 2.20
.

Let
X
be a GKM space.
Then the localization map

H_{T}^{*}(X;\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{v\in V(G)}\mathbb{Q}[\mathfrak{t}]

is injective.
Its image is given by all tuples
(f_{v})_{v\in V(G)}
such that for all edges

The following localization formula for equivariant integration is due to Atiyah–Bott
[

, Equation (3.8)]
and Berline–Vergne

]

We state it for GKM spaces although it is of course much more general.

Theorem 2.21
.

Let

be either a projective algebraic or a compact Hamiltonian GKM space, and let

be its GKM graph.
Let

correspond to

under the identification of Theorem

Then

\int_{X}^{T}f=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\frac{f_{v}}{\prod_{\epsilon\in F(G)_{v}}\alpha(\epsilon)}.

Remark 2.22
.

The right hand side of the localization formula above lives purely in

.
It therefore gives rise to many interesting rational functions in

that simplify non-trivially to a polynomial.
Geometrically, this is clear because

takes values in

.
However, the same polynomiality result still holds if the right hand side is calculated for an abstract GKM graph

and

as in Theorem

, without assuming the existence of a realizing space

, see
[

, Theorem 2.2]
.

Remark 2.23

(First Chern class)
.

Let

be a GKM space with GKM graph

.
The equivariant first Chern class

is determined by

2.5.
Connections

Given a GKM space
X
and an edge
e=\{p,q\}
, the Birkhoff–Grothendieck theorem
[
Gro57
, Théorème 2.1]
gives a splitting

T_{X}|_{C_{e}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a_{n}).

Restricting a summand to the two fixed points

determines a (complex) one-dimensional
T
-stable subspace of
T_{C_{e},p}
and
T_{C_{e},q}
, respectively.
By pairwise linear independence of the weights, those subspaces correspond precisely to flags of
G
at
p
and
q
, respectively.
Thus, one obtains a bijective map

\nabla_{(e,p)}\colon F(G)_{p}\longrightarrow F(G)_{q}.

The collection
\{\nabla_{(e,p)}:{(e,p)}\in E(G)^{\pm}\}
forms a compatible connection in the following sense.

Definition 2.24
.

A
compatible connection
on an abstract GKM graph
G
with axial function
\alpha
is a collection of bijective maps
\nabla_{(e,p)}\colon F(G)_{p}\rightarrow F(G)_{q}
for all
e=\{p,q\}\in E(G)
satisfying

(1)

\nabla_{(e,q)}=\nabla_{(e,p)}^{-1}
for all
e=\{p,q\}\in E(G)
, and

(2)

for every
(e^{\prime},p)\in E(G)_{p}
there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\alpha(\nabla_{(e,p)}(e^{\prime},p))=\alpha(e^{\prime},p)-a\alpha(e,p).

Definition 2.25

(Induced connection)
.

We will refer to the compatible connection constructed above using the Birkhoff–Grothendieck theorem as the
compatible connection on
G
induced by
X
.

Definition 2.26

(
k
-independence)
.

An abstract GKM graph
(G,\alpha)
is called
k
-independent
if for each
p\in V(G)
and any distinct
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\in F(G)_{p}
, the weights
\alpha(\epsilon_{1}),\dots,\alpha(\epsilon_{k})
are linearly independent.

Remark 2.27
.

It follows that every GKM graph is
2
-independent by definition.
If a GKM graph is
3
-independent, one verifies easily that a compatible connection is unique if it exists.
On the other hand, when 3-independence fails, there can be many compatible connections. Note that the Birkhoff–Grothendieck theorem only implies that
X
induces a compatible connection on its GKM graph. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no general way to determine the induced connection algorithmically.

2.6.
Curve classes

Another problem in calculating Gromov–Witten invariants purely in terms of the GKM graph using the localization formula is that one needs to understand the relations between the classes
[C_{e}]\in H_{2}(X)
for all

.

The following lemma is well-known to experts.
For Hamiltonian GKM spaces, a proof using Morse theory may be found in
[

, Theorem 4.5(i)]
.
For algebraic GKM spaces, a similar proof works using the Białynicki–Birula decompostion
[

, Lemma 4.1 & Theorem 4.4]

Lemma 2.32
.

Let
X
be an algebraic or Hamiltonian GKM space.
Then
H_{*}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is torsion-free.

The next Proposition gives us an explicit way of computing a complete set of relations in

among

Definition 2.33
.

Let
G
be a graph. A

is a finite sequence of flags
\{(e_{i},p_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset E(G)^{\pm}
such that

•

e_{i}=\{p_{i},q_{i}\}
for

•

q_{i}=p_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1

•

.

Proposition 2.34
.

Let
X
be either a projective algebraic or a compact Hamiltonian GKM space, and let
G
be its GKM graph.
Let
\{1_{e}\}_{e\in E(G)}
be the standard basis of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}
.
For each closed loop
\gamma=\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
in
G
, write
\epsilon_{i}=(e_{i},p_{i})
and define the linear map

\displaystyle R_{\gamma}\colon\mathfrak{t}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{E(G)}

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\alpha(\epsilon_{i}),\xi\rangle 1_{e_{i}}.

Then the kernel of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module homomorphism

\displaystyle Q\colon\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H_{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle 1_{e}

\displaystyle\longmapsto[C_{e}]

is
K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}
, where

Proof.

By the Universal Coefficients Theorem and torsion-freeness (Lemma

By equivariant formality, this is equivalent to

By applying Theorem

of linear polynomials

Since both are linear in

Note that

Then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{e_{i}}\alpha(\epsilon_{i})=0\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ for all closed loops }\{\epsilon_{i}=(e_{i},p_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}\text{ in }G.

Note that this is equivalent to

\left\langle(r_{e}),R_{\gamma}(\xi)\right\rangle=0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ for all closed loops }\gamma\text{ in }G\text{ and }\xi\in\mathfrak{t},

where

Conversely, any collection

) determines a non-empty family of elements

By Theorem

\psi([C_{e}])=\int_{C_{e}}\psi=\frac{f_{p}-f_{q}}{\alpha(e,p)}=r_{e}

for all

.
Therefore, given

\displaystyle Q((d_{e}))=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\langle(d_{e}),(r_{e})\rangle=0\hskip 14.22636pt\forall(r_{e})\in\mathbb{C}^{E(G)}\text{ satisfying \eqref{eqn:loopCondition}}.

In other words,
\text{Ker}(Q)=\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}\cap(K^{\perp})^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}\cap K

The
moreover
part follows because
R_{\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}}=R_{\gamma_{1}}+R_{\gamma_{2}}

Remark 2.36

The method derived from Proposition

Remark 2.37

(Alternative approach using Kirwan classes)

To obtain an optimal number of relations, one may use the degree two elements

-linear homogeneous basis of

[

[

(2)

Here,
a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}
are the integers associated to

\nabla_{\epsilon}
as in Definition

, that is for

\alpha(\nabla_{\epsilon}\epsilon_{i})=\alpha(\epsilon_{i})-a_{i}\alpha(\epsilon)
. It can easily be seen that

does not depend on the choice of the flag

.

The connected components of the fixed locus of
T
acting on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}(X,\beta)
correspond bijectively to decorated trees
\overrightarrow{\Gamma}
consisting of:

•

A tree
\Gamma
.

•

A map of graphs
\overrightarrow{f}\colon\Gamma\rightarrow G
, that is, a map of the sets of vertices, flags and edges of
\Gamma
and

satisfying obvious compatibility conditions.

•

A
degree map

\overrightarrow{d}\colon E(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
s.t.
\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma)}\overrightarrow{d}(e)\left[C_{\overrightarrow{f}(e)}\right]=\beta
.

•

A
marking map

\overrightarrow{s}\colon\{1,\dots,n\}\rightarrow V(\Gamma)
.

Let
\Gamma_{n}(X,\beta)
be the set of decorated trees
\overrightarrow{\Gamma}
as above.
We use the following notation.

•

Let
S_{v}:=\overrightarrow{s}^{-1}(v)
be the markings at

, and let
n_{v}:=|S_{v}|
.

•

For a flag
(e,v)\in E(\Gamma)^{\pm}
,

\alpha_{(e,v)}:=\frac{\alpha(\overrightarrow{f}(e,v))}{\overrightarrow{d}(e)}

•

Let
\text{Aut}(\overrightarrow{\Gamma})
be the automorphism group of the decorated graph
\overrightarrow{\Gamma}
.

The main result of
[
LS17
, Theorem 4.7]
, reformulated for our purposes, is:

Theorem 3.4
.

For
\gamma_{i}\in H_{T}^{*}(X)
we have

GW_{0,n}^{X,\beta}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})=\sum_{\overrightarrow{\Gamma}\in\Gamma_{n}(X,\beta)}GW_{\overrightarrow{\Gamma}}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}),

where

GW_{\overrightarrow{\Gamma}}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}):=\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Aut}(\overrightarrow{\Gamma})|}\prod_{e\in E(\Gamma)}\frac{h(\overrightarrow{f}(e),\overrightarrow{d}(e))}{\overrightarrow{d}(e)}\\
\prod_{v\in V(\Gamma)}\alpha(\overrightarrow{f}(v))^{\mathrm{val}(v)-1}\prod_{i\in S_{v}}\gamma_{i}|_{\overrightarrow{f}(v)}\prod_{e\in E(\Gamma)_{v}}\alpha_{(e,v)}^{-1}\left(\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma)_{v}}\alpha_{(e,v)}^{-1}\right)^{n_{v}+\mathrm{val}(v)-3}.

Proof.

For
v\in V(\Gamma)
and
e\in E(\Gamma)
with
v\in E
, let

\psi_{(e,v)}:=c_{1}(\mathbb{L}_{(e,v)})\in H^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,E(\Gamma)_{v}\cup S_{v}}).

where
\mathbb{L}_{(e,v)}
is the line bundle over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,E(\Gamma)_{v}\cup S_{v}}
whose fiber at a moduli point
[C_{v},x_{v}]
is the cotangent bundle of
C_{v}
at the marked point corresponding to
(e,v)
.
To obtain the desired formula from
[

, Theorem 4.7]
, set

and use

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,E(\Gamma)_{v}\cup S_{v}}}\frac{1}{\prod_{e\in E(\Gamma)_{v}}\alpha_{(e,v)}-\psi_{(e,v)}}=\prod_{e\in E(\Gamma)_{v}}\alpha_{(e,v)}^{-1}\left(\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma)_{v}}\alpha_{(e,v)}^{-1}\right)^{n_{v}+\text{val}(v)-3}

which is obtained by expressing the left hand fraction as power series in

, using

-linearity of the equivariant integral, and

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}}\psi_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\psi_{n}^{a_{n}}=\frac{(n-3)!}{a_{1}!\dots a_{n}!}

(cf.
[

]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

also holds for Gromov–Witten invariants of compact Hamiltonian GKM spaces, defined via global Kuranishi charts.

3.4.
Independence of the connection

The formula stated in Theorem
3.4
appears to depend not only on the underlying GKM graph
G
, but also on the compatible connection on
G
induced by
X
(cf. Section
2.5
).
This dependence arises from the presence of the
h
-factors in each individual summand. We may have two GKM spaces that share the same abstract GKM graph, but induce different compatible connections. Even in this case, the Gromov–Witten invariants are the same because of Theorem
3.6
. This has strong implications, because as we anticipated in Remark
2.27
, the computation of a connection induced by a space
X
could be a difficult problem. The implication of the following theorem is that we do not need to compute such a connection, because every compatible connection
leads to the same computational result.

Theorem 3.6
.

The formula in Theorem
3.4
is independent of the choice of compatible connection on the GKM graph
G
.

Proof.

Let
e=\{p,q\}
be an edge of
G
,
\epsilon=(e,p)\in E(G)^{\pm}
and
d\geq 1
.
Let
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{m-1}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}
be as in Section
3.3
.
It suffices to show that the factor

h(e,d)=\frac{(-1)^{d}d^{2d}}{(d!)^{2}\alpha(\epsilon)^{2d}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}b\left(\frac{\alpha(\epsilon)}{d},\alpha(\epsilon_{i}),da_{i}\right)

from
[
LS17
, Lemma 4.5]
does not depend on the choice of compatible connection, since this is the only point where the connection enters the formula.
By definition, we have

b(u,w,a)=\begin{cases}\prod_{j=0}^{a}(w-ju)^{-1}&a\geq 0\\
\prod_{j=1}^{-a-1}(w+ju)&a<0.\end{cases}

As noted in
[
Liu13
, Example 19]
, the factors of
b(u,w,a)
are given by expressing

(3.2)

\frac{e^{w}}{1-e^{-u}}+\frac{e^{w-au}}{1-e^{u}}

as
\sum_{j}c_{j}e^{f_{j}}
where
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f_{j}\in\mathfrak{t}^{\ast}
.
Then

b(u,v,w)=\prod_{j}f_{j}^{c_{j}}
.
Note that the
(f_{j},c_{j})
are unique by linear independence of characters.
If
\nabla_{\epsilon}(\epsilon_{i})=\epsilon_{i}^{\prime}
, then Equation (
3.2
) for
b\left(\frac{\alpha(\epsilon)}{d},\alpha(\epsilon_{i}),da_{i}\right)
is

\frac{e^{\alpha(\epsilon_{i})}}{1-e^{-\alpha(\epsilon)/d}}+\frac{e^{\alpha(\epsilon_{i}^{\prime})}}{1-e^{\alpha(\epsilon)/d}}.

Summing this over all
i\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
gives

\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{e^{\alpha(\epsilon_{i})}}{1-e^{-\alpha(\epsilon)/d}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{e^{\alpha(\epsilon_{i}^{\prime})}}{1-e^{\alpha(\epsilon)/d}}.

This expression no longer depends on the bijection

\nabla_{\epsilon}\colon\{\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{m-1}\}\longrightarrow\{\epsilon_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}\}.

As before, writing this as
\sum_{j}c_{j}e^{f_{j}}
gives

\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}b\left(\frac{\alpha(\epsilon)}{d},\alpha(\epsilon_{i}),da_{i}\right)=\prod_{j}f_{j}^{c_{j}},

which is therefore independent of the connection.
∎

3.5.
A connection-free expression.

The expression

\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}b\left(\frac{\alpha(\epsilon)}{d},\alpha(\epsilon_{i}),da_{i}\right)

can be reformulated in a fully explicit manner that does not depend on the connection.
First, we partition the sets
E(G)_{p}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
and
E(G)_{q}\setminus\{\overline{\epsilon}\}
as

E(G)_{p}\setminus\{\epsilon\}=E_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup E_{k},\hskip 28.45274ptE(G)_{q}\setminus\{\overline{\epsilon}\}=E_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\dots\sqcup E_{k}^{\prime}

where each
|E_{i}|=|E_{i}^{\prime}|>0
and for any
\tilde{\epsilon},\tilde{\epsilon}^{\prime}\in E_{i}\cup E_{i}^{\prime}
we have
\alpha(\tilde{\epsilon})-\alpha(\tilde{\epsilon}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}\cdot\alpha(\epsilon)
.
The partition is made unique up to labeling by requiring the number of parts to be minimal.
Such a partition exists as soon as the GKM graph admits a compatible connection
\nabla_{\epsilon}
at
\epsilon
, but it is independent of the choice of connection.
(Choosing a connection amounts to choosing a bijection
E_{i}\rightarrow E_{i}^{\prime}
for each
i
.)
We then have

\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}b\left(\frac{\alpha(\epsilon)}{d},\alpha(\epsilon_{i}),da_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}

where the rational functions
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
in
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}
are defined as follows.
Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.
By definition, the weights
E_{i}\cup E_{i}^{\prime}
all lie on the same line
L_{i}
in
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
with slope
\alpha(\epsilon)
.
Let
\Lambda_{i}\subset L_{i}
be the lattice
E_{i}+\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}\cdot\alpha(\epsilon)
.
Orient
L_{i}
such that
to the right
means in positive
\alpha(\epsilon)
-direction.
For any
f\in\Lambda_{i}
, define

\displaystyle\iota(f)

\displaystyle:=|\{p\in E_{i}:p\text{ is strictly to the left of }f\}|,

\displaystyle\iota^{\prime}(f)

\displaystyle:=|\{p\in E_{i}^{\prime}:p\text{ is strictly to the right of }f\}|.

B_{i}
is then defined by the (finite) product

B_{i}=\prod_{f\in\Lambda_{i}}f^{\iota(f)+\iota^{\prime}(f)-|E_{i}|}.

Theorem
3.6
and Proposition
2.34
give the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

The equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants of an
Hamiltonian or algebraic
GKM space can be computed purely in terms of the GKM graph, without knowledge of the compatible connection induced by the space.

5.1.
Calabi–Yau local models for
\mathbb{P}^{1}

A particularly beautiful scenario for computing Gromov–Witten invariants is that of Calabi–Yau threefolds.
In this case, we have

\text{vdim}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X;\beta))=n

so working with
n=0
marked points implies that
GW_{g,0}^{X,\beta}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Hence, this should naively be a
“count of genus
g
curves on
X
in curve class
\beta
”
.
It is a classical question to ask how
GW_{g,0}^{X,\beta}
relates to the actual number of such curves
[
CK99
, §7.4]
.
An important special case is to consider the contribution of an
isolated
,
rigid
, or
super-rigid
curve (see e.g.
[
Pan99
]
).

5.1.1.
Background

Using string theory, Gopakumar and Vafa arrived at the formula

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta\neq 0,g\geq 0}GW_{g,0}^{X,\beta}t^{2g-2}q^{\beta}=\sum_{\beta\neq 0,h\geq 0}n_{h,\beta}(X)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\left(2\sin\left(\frac{kt}{2}\right)\right)^{2h-2}q^{k\beta}

and conjectured that the
BPS-invariants

n_{h,\beta}(X)
defined by this equation should be integers (see
[
HKK+03
, §34]
and references therein).
Furthermore, for any
\beta
there should exists
h_{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that for all
h\geq h_{\beta}
,
n_{\beta,h}(X)=0
.
Restricting to the genus zero part gives

(5.1)

\displaystyle GW_{0,0}^{X,\beta}=\sum_{\beta=d\gamma}\frac{n_{0,\gamma}(X)}{d^{3}}.

It is tempting to interpret

n_{0,\beta}(X)
as the number of genus
0
curves in class
\beta
.
In particular, the degree
d
covers of a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in class
\beta
should contribute
1/d^{3}
to
GW_{0,0}^{X,d\beta}
.
While this interpretation is false in general (see
[
CK99
, §7.4.3]
and
[
HKK+03
, §29.1.2]
, it is correct for certain spaces like
\mathcal{O}(-1)\oplus\mathcal{O}(-1)\rightarrow\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, which is the local model for a rigid
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in a Calabi–Yau threefold (see
[
HKK+03
, §27.5]
and references therein).

Interesting local models to study in this context are rank 2 vector bundles
{L_{1}\oplus L_{2}\rightarrow C}
over a curve
C
.
In this case, Bryan and Pandharipande worked out the theory of
disconnected
equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants, where the domain of a stable map may be disconnected
[
BP08
]
.
They consider a
T
-action on the total space that is trivial on the base curve
C
.

For symplectic Calabi–Yau
6
-manifolds, the Gopakumar–Vafa conjectures were proved in
[
IP18
,
DIW21
]
.

Remark 5.1
.

In principle, one could try to deduce the results in Section
5.1
by specializing the generating function in
[
BP08
, Corollary 7.2]
appropriately, taking its logarithm, and dealing with the resulting partition combinatorics.
On the other hand, our proofs do not rely on
[
BP08
]
and are based directly on the localization formula (Theorem
3.4
).

5.1.2.
Our setting

We shall work with
T
-equivariant vector bundles
X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
Throughout, we assume that the
T
-action on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is the standard action with two fixed points and primitive weight.
Our setting differs from that considered in
[
BP08
]
because we have a non-trivial
T
-action on the base and work with
connected
Gromov–Witten invariants.
The class of the zero section will be denoted by
\mathbf{0}_{X}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
X
be the total space of the
T
-linearized rank
2

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a_{1})\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a_{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
We say that

is:

•

Calabi–Yau
if
c_{1}(T_{X})=0
,

•

equivariantly Calabi–Yau
if
c_{1}^{T}(T_{X})=0
.

Equivalently,
X
is Calabi–Yau if
\det(\Omega_{X})=0
, or if
a_{1}+a_{2}=-2
. On the other hand, equivariantly Calabi–Yau implies the Calabi–Yau property and is equivalent to the sum of all tangent weights being zero at both fixed points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

From now on, we denote by
X_{k}
for
k\geq 0
the
T
-linearized Calabi–Yau rank 2 vector bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(k-1)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-k-1)
with
T
-weights labeled as in Figure
5.1
.

Figure 5.1.

The GKM graph of

.
Here,
t_{1}
is the weight of

, and

are the weights of the fibers at
[1:0]

The weights at
[0:1]
are automatically determined by the degrees

and

We consider this as an algebraic non-projective GKM space whose GKM graph contains a flag for each

The same localization formula from Theorem
3.4
then applies to calculate equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants of

However, since

is not proper,
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X_{k};\beta)
is not necessarily proper and hence the equivariant integral is not a priori guaranteed to lie in

.
Instead, it generally lies in the fraction field of
H_{T}^{*}(\text{pt};\mathbb{Q})
, so it is a rational function in the equivariant parameters
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
.
By the Calabi–Yau threefold assumption,

is a rational function of homogeneous total degree zero (see Section

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Hence

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{(t_{1}+t_{3})(2t_{1}+t_{3})\cdots(kt_{1}+t_{3})}{t_{2}(t_{2}-t_{1})(t_{2}-2t_{1})\cdots(t_{2}-(k-1)t_{1})}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{it_{1}+t_{3}}{t_{2}-(i-1)t_{1}}.

If
k\geq 2
,
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
is a constant if and only if

t_{3}\in\{-t_{1}-t_{2},-kt_{1}+t_{2}\}.

Proof.

The expression for the Gromov–Witten invariant follows by applying the localization formula of Theorem
3.4
to the GKM graph of
X_{k}
given in Figure
5.1
.

Let
t_{3}=at_{1}+bt_{2}
. Since
X_{k}
is
2
-independent, we must have
a\neq 0\neq b
. If the expression

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(i+a)t_{1}+bt_{2}}{t_{2}-(i-1)t_{1}}

is constant then it must be equal to
b^{k}
, simply by taking
t_{1}=0
. Thus, dividing by
b^{k}
we have

\prod_{i=1}^{k}((i+a)t_{1}+bt_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(bt_{2}+b(1-i)t_{1}).

This is possible if and only if

\{i+a:1\leq i\leq k\}=\{0,-b,-2b,\ldots,(1-k)b\}.

Clearly,
-k\leq a\leq-1
and
b=\pm 1
. It can easily be seen that the only two possible solutions are
a=b=-1
and
a=-k,b=1
.
∎

Thus, for

we have the following cases in which we can hope for polynomial Gromov–Witten invariants of

:

(5.2)

\begin{cases}t_{3}=-t_{1}-t_{2}&\Longleftrightarrow\,\,\,\,c_{1}^{T}(T_{X_{k}})=0\\
t_{3}=-kt_{1}+t_{2}&\Longleftrightarrow\,\,\,\,c_{1}^{T}(T_{X_{k}})=(1-k)t_{1}+2t_{2}\\
t_{3}=-t_{1}+yt_{2}&\Longleftrightarrow\,\,\,\,c_{1}^{T}(T_{X_{1}})=(1+y)t_{2}\hskip 8.53581pt(k=1\text{, any }y\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})\end{cases}

The first case is the
equivariantly Calabi–Yau
case (see Definition
5.2
).

Lemma 5.4
.

The
T
-equivariant Gromov–Witten invariant
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
, as a meromorphic function, has no pole on
t_{1}=0
.

Proof.

Let

be the pointwise stabilizer of the zero-section
\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset X
.
Since

acts on

with primitive weight,

is a subtorus of
T
.
The inclusion

induces a map
H_{T}^{*}\rightarrow H_{T^{\prime}}^{*}

corresponding to the restriction to the hyperplane

.
Since

is still compact, one may use virtual localization to calculate the
T^{\prime}
-equivariant
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
, which is a homogeneous function of degree zero in the fraction field of
H_{T^{\prime}}^{*}
.
It is obtained by applying
H_{T}^{*}\rightarrow H_{T^{\prime}}^{*}
to the
T
-equivariant
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
.
Hence, setting

in the
T
-equivariant

must yield a well-defined element of the fraction field of

∎

Note that for

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
k>0
.

(1)

If
X_{k}
is equivariantly Calabi–Yau, i.e.
t_{3}=-t_{1}-t_{2}
, then

(5.3)

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{(-1)^{d(k+1)-1}}{d^{3}k^{2}}\binom{k^{2}d}{d}.

(2)

If
t_{3}=t_{2}-kt_{1}
, then

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{1}{d^{3}}.

(3)

If
k=1
and
t_{3}=-t_{1}+yt_{2}
for any non-zero integer
y
, then

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{y}{d^{3}}.

Proof.

Our strategy is to understand how the formula in Theorem
3.4
behave when applied to our setting. The GKM graph of
X_{k}
is shown in Figure
5.1
.
Let
e
be the edge of
X_{k}
. By Equation (
3.1
), for any
m\geq 1
we have

(5.4)

h(e,m)=\frac{(-1)^{m}m^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}t_{1}^{2m}}\cdot b(t_{1}/m,t_{2},m(k-1))\cdot b(t_{1}/m,t_{3},m(-k-1)),

where

\displaystyle b(t_{1}/m,t_{2},m(k-1))

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{m(k-1)}\left(t_{2}-\frac{j}{m}t_{1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=m}^{mk}\left(t_{2}-\left(\frac{j}{m}-1\right)t_{1}\right)^{-1},

and

(5.5)

\displaystyle b(t_{1}/m,t_{3},m(-k-1))

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m(k+1)-1}\left(t_{3}+\frac{j}{m}t_{1}\right).

In case (
5.3
), that is
t_{3}=-t_{1}-t_{2}
, an easy refactoring shows that the left hand side of Equation (
5.5
) is equal to

(-1)^{m(k+1)-1}\prod_{j=1}^{mk}\left(t_{2}-\left(\frac{j}{m}-1\right)t_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\left(t_{2}-\left(k+\frac{j}{m}-1\right)t_{1}\right).

From Equation (
5.4
) it is not difficult to deduce that

h(e,m)=\frac{(-1)^{mk-1}m^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}t_{1}^{2m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\left(t_{2}+\frac{j}{m}t_{1}\right)\left(t_{2}+\left(\frac{j}{m}-k\right)t_{1}\right).

We can compute
h(e,m)
also in the remaining two cases, arguing in the same way. The results for cases (
2
) and (
3
) are, respectively,

\displaystyle h(e,m)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m}m^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}t_{1}^{2m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\left(t_{2}+\frac{j}{m}t_{1}\right)\left(t_{2}+\left(\frac{j}{m}-k\right)t_{1}\right)

\displaystyle h(e,m)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m}m^{2m}}{(m!)^{2}t_{1}^{2m}}y\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\left(yt_{2}+\frac{j}{m}t_{1}\right)\left(yt_{2}+\left(\frac{j}{m}-1\right)t_{1}\right).

From the formulas above, we proved that, in the three cases,
h(e,m)
is the quotient of two polynomials where the denominator is a power of
t_{1}
. Moreover, we have

\alpha_{(\eta,v)}^{-1}=-\alpha_{(\eta,v_{0})}^{-1}=\frac{m}{t_{1}}\,\,\,\,\,\forall\eta=\{v_{0},v\}\in E(\Gamma),\overrightarrow{f}(v)=[1:0].

In particular, since
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
is the quotient of two polynomials of the same degree (see Section
3.1
),
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}\in\mathbb{Q}[t_{2}/t_{1}]
, that is, it
is a polynomial in
t_{2}/t_{1}
.

By Lemma
5.4
,
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
has no poles at
t_{1}=0
, hence it is constant. We can compute it by setting
t_{2}=0
,
t_{1}=1
. Let
v
be a vertex of a decorated tree. From

\displaystyle\alpha([1:0])

\displaystyle=t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}

\displaystyle\alpha([0:1])

\displaystyle=-t_{1}(t_{2}-(k-1)t_{1})(t_{3}+(k+1)t_{1}),

it follows that
\alpha(\overrightarrow{f}(v))=0
if

is mapped to
[1:0]
. The fact that
\alpha(\overrightarrow{f}(v))
appears with exponent
\mathrm{val}(v)-1
in the

formula, implies that the contribution from a decorated tree is zero when it has a vertex mapped to
[1:0]
with valency strictly greater than one. Hence the contributions to
GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
come only from star-like trees having the central vertex mapped to
[0:1]
and all other vertices mapped to
[1:0]
.

The set of all such decorated trees is in bijective correspondence with the set of partitions of
d
. We denote the partitions of
d
by
d
-vectors
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
of non-negative integers such that
\sum_{j=1}^{d}ja_{j}=d
. The bijection is realized in the following way: the partition
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
corresponds to the tree having exactly
a_{j}
edges of degree
j
, for
1\leq j\leq d
. This decorated graph has
a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{d}!
automorphisms.

The formula in Theorem
3.4
can be rewritten in terms of partitions of
d
. The contribution of each star-like graph with central vertex

is

(5.6)

\frac{1}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{d}!}\frac{\alpha([0:1])^{\mathrm{val}(v_{0})}}{\alpha([0:1])}\frac{(-d)^{\mathrm{val}(v_{0})}}{-d^{3}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(-\frac{h(e,j)}{j}\right)^{a_{j}}.

If
k=1
, then
\alpha([0:1])=0

5.6
) is non-zero only if
\mathrm{val}(v_{0})=1
. That is, the contribution comes only from the decorated graph with just one edge. This contribution is easy to compute in all three cases. So let us suppose
k\geq 2
.

From
\mathrm{val}(v_{0})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}
, in the first case we deduce that

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{-1}{d^{3}k(k-1)}\sum_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})}\frac{1}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{d}!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{(-1)^{j(k-1)}(k-1)d}{j}\binom{kj}{j}\right)^{a_{j}}.

After canceling common signs and factors, Equation (
5.3
) holds true if and only if

(5.7)

\sum_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})}\frac{1}{a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{d}!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{(k-1)d}{j}\binom{kj}{j}\right)^{a_{j}}=\frac{k-1}{k}\binom{k^{2}d}{d}.

This equation follows from Lemma
5.7
taking
t=(k-1)d
.

In case (

GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}=\frac{1}{d^{3}(1-k)}\sum_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})}\frac{1}{a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{d}!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{(1-k)d}{kj}\binom{kj}{j}\right)^{a_{j}}.

By applying Lemma
5.7
with

, we see that this expression is

. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
d
and
k
be positive integers. Let us denote by
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
any
d
-vector of non-negative integers such that
\sum_{j=1}^{d}ja_{j}=d
. Then the following formula holds in
\mathbb{Q}[t]

(5.8)

\sum_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})}\frac{1}{a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{d}!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{t}{j}\binom{kj}{j}\right)^{a_{j}}=\frac{t}{d+t}\binom{k(d+t)}{d}.

Proof.

Both sides of the equation are clearly in
\mathbb{Q}[t]
. Let us suppose that
t
is a positive integer.
Let
C_{k,j}
be the Fuss–Catalan number

C_{k,j}=\frac{1}{(k-1)j+1}\binom{kj}{j},

and let
G_{k}(x)=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}C_{k,j}x^{j}
be the generating function.
Let
F(x)
be the power series

F(x):=1+\sum_{d\geq 1}\sum_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})}\frac{1}{a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{d}!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{t}{j}\binom{kj}{j}\right)^{a_{j}}x^{d}.

Using
\exp(ab)=\exp(b)^{a}
and the product formula (see
[
Knu97
, Equation (38), 1.2.9]
),

F(x)=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{t}{j}\binom{kj}{j}x^{j}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{j}\binom{kj}{j}x^{j}\right)^{t}.

By
[
JK24
, Theorem 8]
,

G_{k}(x)=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{kj}\binom{kj}{j}x^{j}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{j}\binom{kj}{j}x^{j}\right)^{1/k}.

Hence,
F(x)=G_{k}(x)^{kt}
. Let us define

(5.9)

A_{d}(n,k):=\frac{n}{-kd+n}\binom{-kd+n}{d}.

We need to prove that for each
n\geq 1
,

(5.10)

G_{k}(x)^{n}=\sum_{d\geq 0}A_{d}(n,-k)x^{d}.

The case

\frac{n}{kd+n}\binom{kd+n}{d}=\frac{n}{(k-1)d+n}\binom{kd+n-1}{d}.

Let us suppose Equation (
5.10
) holds true for

is

\displaystyle G_{k}(x)G_{k}(x)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d}A_{j}(1,-k)A_{d-j}(n,-k)\right)x^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}A_{d}(n+1,-k)x^{d}.

In the last equation, we used
[

. Finally, by taking

. Thus Equation
5.8
holds in
\mathbb{Q}[t]
.
∎

A key step in the above proofs was to show that

Another strategy for completing this step is as follows. Try to find a proper algebraic or compact Hamiltonian GKM space

with
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}Z=3
whose GKM graph

contains an edge

such that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\cdot[C_{e}]\cap\text{span}_{\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}}\{[C_{e^{\prime}}]:e^{\prime}\in E(G)\setminus\{e\}\}=\emptyset,

(\dagger)=\begin{cases}\frac{q^{[C_{e}]}}{1-q^{[C_{e}]}}&\text{if }k=0\,\,\text{ or }\,\,t_{3}=t_{2}-kt_{1}\text{ with }k\geq 1\\
y\frac{q^{[C_{e}]}}{1-q^{[C_{e}]}}&\text{if }k=1\text{ and }t_{3}=-t_{1}+yt_{2}\\
\sum_{d\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{d(k+1)+1}}{k^{2}}\binom{k^{2}d}{d}q^{d[C_{e}]}&\text{if }k\geq 1\text{ and }t_{3}=-t_{1}-t_{2}\\
\end{cases}

and at least one of those cases applies.

Proof.

The
q^{0}
term of
x_{1}\ast_{T}x_{2}
is always
x_{1}\smile x_{2}
.
Since
e
is the only edge with
\mathcal{C}_{1}(e)=0
and we have
\mathcal{C}_{1}(e^{\prime})>0
for all other edges
e^{\prime}
, all relevant Gromov–Witten invariants can be computed over the local model
X_{k}\cong N_{C_{e}/X}\rightarrow C_{e}
in Figure
5.1
, involving only positive multiples of
[C_{e}]
, which corresponds to the class
\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}\in H_{2}(X_{k})
of the zero section.
Let
i\colon C_{e}\hookrightarrow X
be the embedding of
C_{e}
into
X
.

By the defining properties

\int_{X}^{T}(x_{1}\ast_{T}x_{2})\smile z=\sum_{\beta\in H_{2}(X)}GW_{0,3}^{X,\beta}(x_{1},x_{2},z)q^{\beta}

of
\ast_{T}
and

\int^{T}_{X}\mathrm{PD}(C_{e})\smile z=\int^{T}_{C_{e}}i^{*}z

of
\mathrm{PD}(C_{e})
, it suffices to prove the following equation.

GW_{0,3}^{X,d[C_{e}]}(x_{1},x_{2},z)=d^{3}\left(\int^{T}_{C_{e}}x_{1}\right)\left(\int^{T}_{C_{e}}x_{2}\right)\left(\int^{T}_{C_{e}}z\right)GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}

Pick
x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in H_{T}^{*}\cdot H_{T}^{\leq 2}(X)
such that
i^{*}x_{1}^{\prime}=i^{*}x_{1}
,
i^{*}x_{2}^{\prime}=i^{*}x_{2}
, and
i^{*}z^{\prime}=i^{*}z
.
This is possible because

is surjective.
Indeed, it is surjective in degree 0 by
H_{T}^{*}

has non-zero pairing with

(cf. Lemma

Since all calculations take place over the local model

, we have

By the

and the

on

GW_{0,3}^{X,d[C_{e}]}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\left(\int^{T}_{d[C_{e}]}x^{\prime}\right)\left(\int^{T}_{d[C_{e}]}y^{\prime}\right)\left(\int^{T}_{d[C_{e}]}z^{\prime}\right)GW_{0,0}^{X,d[C_{e}]}.

The proof of (

) is completed by noting that

(and similarly for

), that
\int_{d[C_{e}]}(\dots)=d\int_{C_{e}}(\dots)
, and that
GW_{0,0}^{X,d[C_{e}]}=GW_{0,0}^{X_{k},d\mathbf{0}_{X_{k}}}
by locality of the calculation.

Finally, the expression for
(\dagger)
follows from Theorem
5.5
and the sentence before it regarding the well-known case

.
Note that no other case can occur by Lemma

(cf. Proposition

∎

Remark 5.13
.

The point of introducing

As an example application of
GKMtools.jl
(see Section

Theorem 5.14
.

Let

be a twisted flag manifold and

(5.15)

p\ast p=q^{3\gamma}\left(q^{2\beta}-5q^{3\beta}+35q^{4\beta}-273q^{5\beta}+2244q^{6\beta}-19019q^{7\beta}+\dots\right)\\
+\left(q^{a\gamma+n\beta}\text{-terms with }0\leq a\leq 2\right).

Proof.

Since

is of the form

for some

and
\delta\in H_{2}^{\text{eff}}(X)

|A|+2\int_{\delta}c_{1}(T_{X})=12

By Figure

, every

\delta=b\beta+c\gamma

for some

Since
\int_{\beta}c_{1}(T_{X})=0
and
\int_{\gamma}c_{1}(T_{X})=2

\int_{\gamma}c_{1}(T_{X})=2b
and the degree condition translates into

Since

To calculate the rational coefficients of these terms for the first few

Remark 5.15
.

We have not identified a closed form for the sequence

occurring as coefficients in (

However, integrality of the terms is explained as follows.
First,

[

Thus, the construction of Gromov–Witten invariants for semipositive symplectic manifolds applies, which yields integer invariants in genus zero

Second, this construction agrees with the definition via global Kuranishi charts by

5.3.
Realizability of GKM graphs

So far, we have used GKM theory to calculate certain Gromov–Witten invariants.
Now we turn this game around and demonstrate how Gromov–Witten theory can be applied to the
realizability problem
of GKM theory.
Given an abstract GKM graph, this problem asks whether there exists a GKM space whose GKM graph is the given one.
The question has many interesting refinements by asking for specific
T
-compatible geometric structures that the GKM space should have, for example a
T
-compatible Kähler structure
[
GKZ23
, §2]
.
The twisted flag varieties of Section
5.2
provide examples of Hamiltonian GKM spaces that do not admit a compatible Kähler structure.

By Theorem
3.7
the Gromov–Witten invariants of Hamiltonian or algebraic GKM spaces are determined purely by their GKM graph.
Theorem
3.4
, Proposition
2.34
and Theorem
3.6
give an algorithm to compute them.
However, from Theorem
3.4
it is not at all obvious that the Gromov–Witten invariants calculated using this formula satisfy the Kontsevich–Manin axioms. It is not even clear a priori why they should give polynomials in the equivariant parameters rather than rational functions.
In fact, it is not true that these nice properties hold for any abstract GKM graph as demonstrated in the examples below.
We can therefore derive new necessary criteria for Hamiltonian or algebraic realizability of GKM graphs by determining when their GW invariants are well-behaved.

We only demonstrate this idea with a few examples here.
We intend to develop this further in future work.

5.3.1.
Example 1: a non-Hamiltonian 2D GKM graph

Let
G
be the 2D GKM graph given by a cycle of length 8 with weights

t_{1},t_{2},-t_{1},-t_{2},t_{1},t_{2},-t_{1},-t_{2}

in cyclic order, as depicted in Figure
5.4
.

Figure 5.4.
An abstract GKM graph not realizable by any Hamiltonian GKM space, illustrated using the method of Example
2.19
(2).
The same GKM graph is discussed in
[
GKZ24
, Example 2.44]
.

Let
\beta
be the curve class corresponding to the first plus the second edge, both with multiplicity one.
Then a calculation with
GKMtools.jl
(see Section
4
) yields

GW_{0,0}^{G,\beta}=\frac{1}{t_{1}^{2}t_{2}-t_{1}t_{2}^{2}},

and

\left(\mathrm{PD}(p_{1})\ast_{T}\mathrm{PD}(p_{1})\right)[q^{\beta}]=\left(\frac{t_{2}^{2}}{t_{1}^{2}-t_{1}t_{2}},\frac{-t_{2}}{t_{1}},\frac{-t_{2}}{t_{1}-t_{2}},0,0,0,0,0\right),

where the vector notation indicates values under localization to the fixed points in cyclic order.
Any of those rational functions failing to be polynomial in
t_{1},t_{2}
proves that
G
is not the GKM graph of a Hamiltonian or algebraic GKM space.

Remark 5.16
.

In this particular case, there are multiple other ways of deducing the same result.
First, one could prove that no moment map image of
G
could satisfy the required convexity property: there would always be a segment on the boundary of the image that is not the image of a
T
-stable
S^{2}

[
GKZ23
, §2.3]
.
Second, one could calculate the combinatorial Betti numbers and get
(b_{0},b_{2},b_{4})=(2,4,2)
, which cannot be the Betti numbers of a connected space.
The combinatorial Betti numbers match the topological Betti numbers if the underlying GKM space is Hamiltonian
[
GZ01
, Theorem 1.3.2]
, so this gives another way of showing that
G
is not realizable as a Hamiltonian GKM space.

Remark 5.17
.

As noted by
[
GKZ24
, Example 2.44]
,
G
is realizabale for a weaker notion of GKM space where we drop the symplectic assumption and the axial function is well-defined only up to sign.

G
is then realized by the equivariant connected sum of three copies of
S^{2}\times S^{2}
, each carrying the diagonal action of
T=S^{1}\times S^{1}
, with a certain choice of
T
-invariant almost complex structure.

The following proposition is an example of how our understanding of the local model

Examples that satisfy the conclusion of the proposition are given by Figure

Proposition 5.18

Let

Let

Proof.

5.4.
Enumerating curves contained in a hyperplane

Let us consider the following problem:

Question 5.20
.

Let
n,k,
and
d
be positive integers. How many rational curves of degree
d
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
pass through a given set of linear subspaces in general position, under the condition that each curve lies entirely within some
k
-dimensional linear subspace?

We show how
GKMtools.jl
answers this question.
Let us denote by
S
the flag variety

S=\{(x,h)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\times G(k+1,n+1):x\in h\},

and by
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
the projections to, respectively,
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
G(k+1,n+1)
.
Note that curve classes in
S
are given by combinations of classes
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
where
\beta
(resp.,
\beta^{\prime}
) is a line in a fiber of
\pi^{\prime}
(resp.,
\pi
), see
[
EH16
, Section 9.3.1]
. In particular,
\pi(\beta)
is the class of a line and
\pi^{\prime}(\beta)
is the class of a point.

It follows that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,m}(S,d\beta)
parametrizes stable maps to
S
such that the projection of those maps to
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a degree
d
curve, and the projection to
G(k+1,n+1)
is a point.
Since
S
is homogeneous, it admits a transitive action of an algebraic group
G
. The virtual dimension of

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,m}(S,d\beta)
is

(k+1)(n-k+d)+k+m-3.

Remark 5.21
.

In
[
MPKS19
,
BDOP25
]
, the authors consider only the case
n=3
and
k=2
, focusing on the singularities of the curves. They construct the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,m}(E/B,\beta)
of stable maps to
E
relative to a fibration
E/B
. They apply this construction to the fibration
S/G(3,4)
. Finally, using WDVV equations they find a recursive formula for the number of rational
planar
curves of degree
d
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
meeting
r
general lines and
s
general points, where
r+2s=3d+2
. In this section we give an answer to Question
5.20
for any
n
and
k
without using WDVV equations.

Let
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be pure dimensional subvarieties of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{codim}(\gamma_{i})=(k+1)(n-k+d)+k+m-3.

Using
[
FP97
, Lemma 14]
, there are elements
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset G
such that scheme theoretic intersection

(5.16)

\mathrm{ev}_{1}^{-1}(g_{1}\cdot\pi^{-1}(\gamma_{1}))\cap\cdots\cap\mathrm{ev}_{m}^{-1}(g_{m}\cdot\pi^{-1}(\gamma_{m}))

is a reduced number of points supported in the locus of automorphism-free maps with irreducible domain. Moreover, this number coincides with the Gromov–Witten invariant

(5.17)

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,m}(S,d\beta)}\mathrm{ev}_{1}^{*}(\pi^{*}(\gamma_{1}))\smile\cdots\smile\mathrm{ev}_{m}^{*}(\pi^{*}(\gamma_{m})).

The intersection in Equation (
5.16
) is obtained as an application of the Kleiman–Bertini Theorem in
[
Kle74
]
(see for example
[
Mur23
, Corollary 3.9]
and references therein for other examples of this well-know application), and it could be empty.

If
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\rightarrow S
is a stable map representing a point of Equation (
5.16
), the image of the composition
\pi\circ f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a degree
d
curve, contained in a
k
-dimensional linear subspace, and clearly meets some translate of
\gamma_{i}
for all
i
. Assuming all
\gamma_{i}
in general position, we obtain that Equation (
5.17
) is the answer to Question
5.20
.

We computed some concrete examples of this equation using
GKMtools.jl
and the results were as expected. Moreover, we computed some new enumerative number of curves; for example there are exactly
540
planar cubics in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
meeting five lines and four planes in general position. See the documentation page linked in Section
4
.

Equation (
5.17
) is zero if
d<k
. The reason, from a geometric point of view, is that in this case any irreducible rational curve of degree
d
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is contained in many
k
-dimensional linear subspaces. Thus, in order to have a finite number of stable maps, we need to impose some constraints on the subspaces containing the curves. More results on this problem may be found in
[
MP
]
.
