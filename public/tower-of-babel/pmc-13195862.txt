Development of the Asian Mediterranean Diet and its sensory acceptability in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD)
Authors: Verena M. H. Tan, Gary T. L. Chiah, Yewon Lee, Ives Y. Lim, Mark C. C. Cheah, George B. B. Goh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a growing public health concern in Asia, where dietary patterns differ significantly from those in Mediterranean regions. Although the Mediterranean Diet (MedDiet) is recommended for MASLD management, its adoption in Asia remains limited. This study aimed to develop a culturally adapted Asian Mediterranean Diet (AMED) and to evaluate its sensory acceptability among individuals with MASLD.MethodsA multi-phase methodology with a cross-sectional study design was employed, involving dietary mapping, portion standardization, recipe development, polyphenol profiling, and sensory evaluation. A 4-week AMED menu was formulated using culturally relevant ingredients and cooking methods. Nutritional composition and polyphenol content were determined. Sensory evaluation of 14 AMED dishes was conducted among 30 MASLD patients using a 7-point hedonic scale across six sensory domains.ResultsTwelve out of 14 dishes (85.7%) received favourable sensory scores (≥ 5). Texture was the strongest predictor of overall taste and acceptability (β = 0.83, p < 0.001). Polyphenol analysis revealed substantial antioxidant content across several dishes. The AMED menu met key MedDiet macronutrient targets and incorporated polyphenol-rich ingredients aligned with dietary recommendations for MASLD.ConclusionThe AMED represents a culturally adapted, nutritionally coherent, and sensory-acceptable interpretation of the MedDiet for Asian populations. These findings provide a foundation for future clinical trials and public health strategies targeting cardiometabolic diseases in Asian populations.Trial registrationNCT06321211; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06321211?term=NCT06321211&rank=1. Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [1] is the leading cause of chronic liver disease globally, affecting approximately 38% of all adults worldwide [2]. Its prevalence is rising across Asia and is projected to increase by 6–20% by 2030 [3]. In Singapore, the prevalence of MASLD is estimated to be around 40% [4].

MASLD is defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, or dyslipidemia, in the absence of significant alcohol consumption [2, 5]. While pharmacological treatment such semaglutide and resmetirom have recently been given approval from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of MASLD [6, 7], the concomitant diet and lifestyle modifications remain the cornerstone of MASLD management [8]. Recent evidence has shown that the Mediterranean diet significantly improves liver biomarkers in MASLD patients, including reductions in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), liver stiffness, and hepatic fat content [2].

The Mediterranean Diet (MedDiet) is a traditional dietary pattern originating from olive-growing regions such as Greece, Southern Italy, and Spain. It emphasizes a high intake of plant-based foods, including fruits and vegetables, legumes, and whole grains. Olive oil serves the primary fat source, contributing to a high ratio of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA). The diet al.so includes moderate consumption of fish, dairy, and wine, while limiting red and processed meats [9]. In this context, ‘moderate consumption’ reflects typical levels within the traditional MedDiet. For example, moderate wine intake in Mediterranean populations generally equates to about one standard glass per day (approximately 100 ml, or about 10–12 g of alcohol).

There is a substantial body of evidence supporting the efficacy of the MedDiet in preventing and managing chronic conditions such as metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. These conditions usually co-exist and share a pathophysiological link with MASLD [10]. Inflammation is a key driver of insulin resistance and oxidative stress [11]. The MedDiet’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, largely attributed to its high content of phytochemicals, including dietary polyphenols [12, 13] are reported to exert beneficial biological effects. Importantly, many of the proposed benefits of the MedDiet arise from the dietary pattern as a whole, its macronutrient composition, food diversity, and the synergistic effects of combining foods within the food matrix [14].

Despite its benefits, the adoption of the MedDiet in Asian populations remains limited due to cultural unfamiliarity and limited accessibility of traditional Mediterranean ingredients [15, 16], Embedding the principles of a MedDiet in non-Mediterranean populations with distinct food cultures presents a significant challenge. Recent studies from South Korea [17–19], Singapore [20], and India [21, 22] have explored culturally adapted versions of the MedDiet, showing improvements in insulin sensitivity, lipid profiles, and hepatic steatosis indices. However, heterogeneity in the definitions and interpretations of what constitutes a MedDiet intervention, may lead to deviations from core principles, limiting reproducibility and consistent health outcomes.

To date, no study has systematically mapped the nutritional composition of the traditional MedDiet and identified culturally appropriate substitutes commonly consumed in Asian diets. This study aims to establish a comprehensive Asian Mediterranean Diet (AMED) framework for clinical and public health implementation. Specifically, the objectives are to: (1) identify key dietary and food-based components in the MedDiet with evidence-based health benefits and use these to develop an AMED framework, (2) develop a 4-week AMED meal plan based on nutrient composition and food group recommendations aligned with MedDiet criteria, (3) evaluate the polyphenol content of selected recipes formulated according to AMED framework, and (4) assess the acceptability of selected AMED dishes among patients diagnosed with MASLD.

Material & methods

This study employed a multi-phase methodology with a cross-sectional study design comprising dietary mapping, recipe development, polyphenol analysis, and sensory evaluation to adapt the MedDiet for Asian populations. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06321211). Ethical approval for the sensory evaluation component was obtained from the Singapore Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (RECAS-0244) and SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (2024/2153).

Phase 1: dietary mapping

To establish an evidence-based foundation for defining the MedDiet in this study, we conducted a scoping review-style dietary mapping following PRISMA-ScR principles. Searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies published between January 2020 and October 2025 [23–29]. Search terms included controlled vocabulary and keywords related to MedDiet interventions, nutrient composition, fat quality, food-group intake, and serving sizes. Full search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori. We included randomized controlled trials, non-randomized interventions, and high-adherence cohort studies that (i) prescribed a MedDiet intervention or (ii) reported quantitative nutrient or food-group intake data relevant for defining the MedDiet. Studies were eligible if they involved adults (≥ 18 years) and reported at least one of the following: macronutrient targets, daily or weekly food-group recommendations, fat-quality specifications (e.g. MUFA: SFA ratio), or standardized portion sizes. Pediatric studies, observational studies lacking explicit MedDiet operationalization, and papers without extractable quantitative dietary data were excluded. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer. For each included study, we extracted intervention prescription values, baseline intakes, and post-intervention achieved intakes. All values were harmonized into standard units (grams, millilitres, or servings based on national dietary guidelines).

Because MedDiet studies vary in whether they report prescription or achieved intakes, extracted data were categorized as intervention prescription, baseline, or post-intervention achieved. Our operational definition and AMED fidelity criteria were based primarily on intervention prescriptions. Where these were unavailable, post-intervention achieved values were used as proxies and indicated in Supplementary Table S2. Baseline values were included only for contextual comparison.

The extracted data were synthesized to generate a consolidated nutrient and food-group profile for the traditional MedDiet. This profile, together with established conceptual frameworks such as the MedDiet Pyramid, formed the foundation for the operational definition presented earlier and guided the development of the AMED framework, by identifying which components required cultural adaptation and which nutrient thresholds needed to be preserved.

Using the nutrient profile derived from dietary mapping, food components with established health promoting effects were translated into a 4-week meal plan tailored to Asian dietary patterns. The conversion of nutrient targets into food-based recommendations was guided by the Singapore Health Promotion Board dietary guidelines. The final menu meal plan was analysed using FoodWorks [30] to ensure alignment with the target nutrient profile. Key elements of the traditional MedDiet, such as the use of herbs, spices, and minimally processed flavouring ingredients were retained, while culturally appropriate, polyphenol-rich foods were incorporated in place of alcoholic sources traditionally present in the MedDiet. Additional evidence-based clinical considerations were integrated where relevant. For example, sodium levels were aligned with clinical guidelines for hypertension prevention.

Portion sizes were standardized using Singapore Health Promotion Board references [31] and adjusted for cultural relevance. Variability in serving-size definitions across Mediterranean countries was addressed by converting all reported portions into quantifiable weights and volumes. The finalized framework therefore includes precise portion definitions, culturally appropriate substitutions for traditional Mediterranean ingredients, and additional dietary elements to reflect local eating patterns.

Framework for the Asian Mediterranean Diet (AMED)

The AMED framework applies core MedDiet principles to an Asian culinary context while maintaining alignment with established Mediterranean nutrient and food-group targets. Adaptations were made only when necessary to reflect cultural relevance, ingredient availability, and local cooking practices. Macronutrient distributions were kept within MedDiet norms, with an emphasis on monounsaturated fat as the dominant fat source. While olive oil was included where feasibly, commonly used Asian vegetable oils, such as canola or rice bran oil, were included when their use did not alter the overall fat-quality profile.

To support adequate intake of plant-based foods, recommended portions of vegetables, fruits, wholegrains and legumes were incorporated consistently across daily meals. Red and processed meat were intentionally limited and replaced with culturally familiar protein sources such as fish, soy-based products, and legumes. Although moderate wine consumption is traditionally associated with MedDiet, alcohol was excluded from AMED for cultural and clinical reasons. To preserve the antioxidant and polyphenol-rich characteristics of the traditional MedDiet, the AMED menu emphasized non-alcoholic polyphenol sources such as tomatoes, green tea, legumes, nuts, and regionally available vegetables. Polyphenol profiling confirmed that these substitutions maintained high polyphenol content across AMED dishes.

Portion sizes were adapted to Asian eating norms and standardized using the Singapore Health Promotion Board dietary guidelines to ensure both cultural feasibility and nutritional consistency. Collectively, these fidelity criteria ensured that the AMED remained conceptually and nutritionally aligned with core MedDiet principles while providing a culturally appropriate dietary model for Asian populations.

Phase 2: recipe development

A total of 168 culturally adapted recipes were developed to constitute a 4-week menu cycle aligned with the AMED framework. A sample one-week meal plan can be found in Supplementary Table S3. Recipes were designed to ensure variety across meal occasions (main meals and snacks) and were modified to meet target macronutrient and polyphenol distributions. Standardized recipes were created for each recipe to ensure consistency in preparation and nutrient content. Selected recipes underwent kitchen trials to evaluate feasibility of preparation (ingredient availability, cooking techniques, preparation time), as well as sensory appeal (palatability and acceptability, via informal testing). A final set of 14 recipes was selected for scale-up and inclusion in sensory evaluation sessions.

Phase 3: polyphenol analysis

A subset of recipes was selected for polyphenol analysis. Prepared dishes were homogenized, freeze-dried, and analysed at an external laboratory. Total polyphenol content was measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu assay, and catechin subtypes were quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography with diode-array detection (HPLC-DAD) using validated standards. Due to limitations in available polyphenol databases, comparisons were restricted to total polyphenol levels. Nonetheless, recipe development prioritized the inclusion of polyphenol-rich ingredients to enhance the nutritional profile of the adapted AMED. Phase 4: sensory evaluation

Participant recruitment

Sensory evaluation was conducted to assess the acceptability of 14 AMED dishes among individuals with MASLD. Participants were recruited from the MASLD clinic at the Singapore General Hospital from May to September 2024. Eligibility criteria included imaging-confirmed hepatic steatosis via ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging. Exclusion criteria included secondary causes of steatosis (e.g. medication-induced MASLD), unstable weight (> 5% change in preceding 3 months), use of weight-loss medications, other liver diseases (e.g. viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, cholestatic liver disease, Wilson’s disease), unstable diabetes, renal failure, pregnancy or lactation, inability to provide informed consent, sensory impairments (taste or smell), food allergies or intolerances, and self-imposed dietary restrictions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment in the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 1983) and was approved by the Singapore Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (RECAS-0244).

Study visits

Visit 1 – baseline measurement

Participants completed a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) adapted for Singaporean dietary pattern [34] and a validated 14-item Mediterranean Diet Score (MDS) [35]. The total score ranged from 0 to 14, and evaluated as ≤ 4 low, 5–9 moderate, and ≥ 10 high compliance. A health questionnaire was also administered to collect demographic data, medical history, medication use, food allergies, and lifestyle factors (e.g. physical activity, sleep). Anthropometric measurements (weight, height, BMI, waist circumference) and body composition were recorded. A stadiometer (SECA 213) was used to measure height to the nearest millimetre. A segmental body composition analyser for impedance testing (Tanita MC-180MA) was used to measure body weight, with participants weighing light clothes and without shoes. The waist circumference was taken halfway between the last rib and the iliac crest, with participants standing upright, using a measuring tape. Retrospective clinical data were extracted from medical records, including liver enzymes (alanine transaminase, aspartate aminotransferase, albumin, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, GGT), platelet count, lipid profile (total cholesterol, low-density lipoproteins, high-density lipoproteins, triglycerides). However, some of these clinical data were not available for all participants. For example, not every participant had a recent lipid profile or transient elastography result on file.

Visit 2 and 3 – sensory evaluation sessions

Participants attended two sensory evaluation sessions at SIT@Dover sensory lab between April to September 2024, evaluating seven dishes per session. Prior to each session, participants were instructed to abstain from food for two hours and avoid strong-tasting foods, smoking, and scented products.

From the approved set of AMED dishes, 14 recipes were selected for sensory evaluation based on their novelty and cultural familiarity (Table 2). Recipes were produced in collaboration with a commercial food production partner (SATS Ltd, Singapore). Each dish was prepared in a central kitchen, blast-chilled, and stored at -18 °C.

At each sensory session, dishes were reheated to 60–71 °C, using standardized methods (steaming and microwaving). Dishes were served in 50 g tasting portions with palate cleansers (plain crackers and filtered water) provided between samples. Participants evaluated each dish using a 7-point hedonic scale (1 = dislike very much, 7 = like very much) across six attributes: appearance, aroma, texture, saltiness, overall taste, and overall acceptability. A score ≥ 5 indicated positive sensory reception. Visual aids were provided to support assessment of appearance. Data were collected digitally via QR-linked forms or physical forms, with optional open-ended feedback for qualitative insights. Each session lasted approximately 60–90 min. Reimbursement was provided upon completion of both sensory sessions.

Statistical analysis

Data distributions for continuous variables were assessed using normality testing (Shapiro-Wilk tests, Q-Q plots, and visual inspection of histograms). Variables with approximately normal distributions were summarized as mean (SD), whereas non-normal distributions were summarized as median (IQR). Sensory ratings obtained from 7-point hedonic scales were treated as ordinal and therefore summarized using median (IQR) for primary analyses. A sensory score of ≥ 5 on the 7-point hedonic scale was considered indicative of positive acceptability. Differences in sensory ratings among dishes were evaluated using the Friedman test. Kendall’s W was computed as the effect-size estimate and interpreted as small (W < 0.3), moderate (0.3-0,5), or large (≥ 0.5). When the Friedman test indicated significant differences, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction were used for post-hoc comparisons.

Associations between individual sensory attributes and overall taste and acceptability were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models (Rpackage lmerTest, v3.1-3). Although hedonic scores are ordinal, mixed-effects models were used as a complementary analysis after confirming acceptable residual diagnostics. The models included dish, sex, ethnicity, age, and BMI as fixed effects, and participant identifier as a random effect to account for repeated measurements. False discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was applied to control for multiple comparisons. All analyses were conducted in R.

Sample size

Sensory studies using repeated evaluations have shown that panels in the range of 20–30 participants can yield reliable acceptability modelling when observation density is high. A previous study showed that a sample of 27 participants was sufficient to produce stable sensory-perceptual estimates under repeated measurement conditions [36]. Hence, a target sample size of 30 participants was deemed adequate to characterize acceptability patterns while remaining feasible in a clinical MASLD population. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the focus on acceptability rather than clinical endpoints, a sample size of 30 was deemed sufficient to detect meaningful trends in sensory preferences and inform future intervention studies.

Results

A total of 30 participants completed the study. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 3. Mean (SD) age of the participants was 43.9 (12.1) years, with a predominance of male participants (63.3%). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 29.7 (5.4) kg/m², and a mean waist circumference of 97.6 (19.7) cm, is consistent with obesity and central adiposity respectively. Due to retrospective data collection from medical records, complete data for some clinical parameters were not available for all participants. Among the 22 participants with available transient elastography data, the mean liver stiffness measurement was 6.85 (2.93) kPa, and the mean-controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) was 333.0 (42.4) dB/m, indicative of hepatic steatosis. Lipid profile data (N = 21) revealed borderline elevated triglycerides and low HDL-cholesterol, further supporting the presence of metabolic risk. Liver function tests (N = 26) showed elevated ALT and GGT, consistent with hepatic stress. The baseline Mediterranean Diet Score of 4.40 (1.77) indicates low adherence to Mediterranean diet in this cohort of participants.

Dietary mapping

A synthesis of nutrient composition and food group intake from MedDiet intervention studies is presented in Table 4. The dietary components across studies demonstrated a moderate-to-high fat profile, with total fat contributing 36.0% of total energy. MUFA were the predominant fat type (20.9%). Carbohydrates accounted for 45.3%, and protein contributed 18.8%. Dietary fibre intake was high (29.7 g), consistent with the plant-based emphasis of the MedDiet. Daily food group intake reflected the core components of the MedDiet. Mean consumption of wholegrains was 209.4 g/day, fruits 339.6 g/day, and vegeTable 390.6 g/day. Nuts were consumed at 22.7 g/day, and olive oil at 27.6 g/day, reinforcing the emphasis on unsaturated fats. Salt intake averaged 2812 mg/day, with several studies incorporating sodium reduction strategies for cardiovascular risk management. Dairy intake averaged 274 g/day. Weekly intakes of fish and shellfish (114.8 g), legumes (69.6 g), and moderate amounts of poultry (54 g) and red meat (94.4 g) were reported. Processed meat intake was generally limited. These findings provide a consolidated nutrient and food group profile of MedDiet interventions, serving as a reference for the development of culturally adapted AMED.

Sensory evaluation

Thirty participants completed sensory evaluation across two sessions. Of the 14 AMED dishes assessed, 12 (85.7%) achieved favourable mean scores (≥ 5.0) across key sensory attributes (Fig. 2). The mean (SD) ratings were: appearance 5.29 (1.31), aroma 5.46 (1.19), texture 5.29 (1.33), saltiness 5.29 (1.28), overall taste 5.45 (1.37), and overall acceptability 5.45 (1.38). These findings indicate broad acceptability among individuals with MASLD.

Discussion

This is the first to systematically develop an AMED framework using a multi-phase approach that included dietary mapping, recipe development, polyphenol profiling, and sensory evaluation.

Development of the AMED framework

Following a comprehensive review of key nutrients, food components, and mechanisms underlying the health benefits of the traditional MedDiet, the AMED framework was designed to reflect core MedDiet principles, while incorporating culturally appropriate adaptations for Asian populations. This demonstrates the feasibility of translating MedDiet concepts to non-Mediterranean settings, enhancing both cultural acceptability and long-term adherence.

In this study, the AMED framework was assessed primarily in terms of its nutrient composition and sensory acceptability. Without external validation against a standardized MedDiet reference or comparison with alternative dietary patterns, the potential for construct circularity remains, as the fidelity criteria were derived from the same mapping process that informed the framework. Consistent with its intended role as a developmental and feasibility study, future studies with larger, powered samples are needed to evaluate subgroup differences and determinants of sensory acceptability.

Barriers to adherence and cultural adaptation

Most MedDiet interventions have been conducted in Mediterranean populations, limiting generalizability to multi-ethnic Asian populations. Cultural dietary preferences, ingredient availability, and local culinary practices pose significant barriers to dietary adherence [38, 39]. Studies have also reported low-to-moderate adherence to the MedDiet even within Mediterranean regions, with challenges such as food cost, convenience, and cultural shifts toward Westernized diets. In Southeast Asia, these barriers are compounded by limited familiarity with the MedDiet, as reflected in the low baseline MedDiet score (4.4 out of 14) observed in this study, consistent with prior findings from Singapore [40].

The TANGO study [20] represents one of the few attempts to adapt the MedDiet for Asia. However, its primary focus was on the effects of C15:0 (pentadecanoic acid) supplementation on MASLD outcomes, rather than evaluating a culturally adapted MedDiet per se. In contrast, the AMED framework developed in this study addresses key barriers by embedding familiar ingredients and local cooking methods, while maintaining nutritional fidelity to MedDiet principles. The inclusion of polyphenol-rich foods such as tomatoes, green tea, and regional vegetables supports the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential of the diet [41, 42]. Additionally, the framework incorporates culturally tailored portion standards to address inconsistencies in serving size definitions across MedDiet studies [9].

This study fills a critical gap in the literature. While previous efforts have explored Mediterranean-like diets in Asian contexts [19, 20, 43], none have systematically mapped nutrient profiles, portion standards, and polyphenol content to develop a culturally coherent and operationally defined dietary model. The high acceptability of AMED dishes in this study suggests that aligning MedDiet principles with regional food culture may improve adherence and support chronic disease prevention in Southeast Asian populations.

In addition to ingredient selection and portion adaptation, cultural considerations also informed decisions around traditionally included components of the MedDiet, such as wine. Moderate wine consumption is traditionally part of the MedDiet and has been associated with cardiometabolic benefits [44, 45]. However, wine was excluded from the AMED menu for cultural and clinical reasons, given the generally low alcohol consumption across Southeast Asia and frequent avoidance for religious or personal reasons [44]. Polyphenols found in wine, such as resveratrol and tyrosol, are also present in non-alcoholic sources including olive oil, berries, tomatoes, soy, and cruciferous vegetables, all of which were incorporated into the AMED recipes [44, 46]. Polyphenol profiling confirmed that the antioxidant potential of AMED dishes was preserved, demonstrating that the exclusion of wine did not compromise nutritional quality or cultural relevance. However, the analysis did not assess bioavailability or physiological effects, limiting conclusions about clinical significance. Further studies are needed to evaluate the metabolic implications of polyphenol-rich AMED dishes.

Acceptability of the AMED recipes

Sensory evaluation demonstrated strong acceptability, with 85.7% of AMED dishes rated favourably for appearance, aroma, texture, taste, and overall acceptability. Among all attributes, texture emerged as the strongest predictor of overall acceptability, consistent with evidence that texture and flavour influence dietary adherence [39]. Recent studies further support the role of taste preferences in shaping dietary patterns. Individuals with higher MedDiet adherence exhibit heightened sensitivity and enjoyment of saltiness and umami, while those following Western diets tend to prefer intense sweetness and show reduced taste sensitivity [47]. A recent study found that preferences for bitter and sweet tastes were significantly associated with MedDiet adherence among young adults [48] Specifically, a higher preference for bitter flavours and foods such as olive oil, legumes, and cruciferous vegetables was positively associated with adherence, while a preference for sweet-tasting foods like pastries and red meat was inversely associated. These findings suggests that screening for individual taste sensitivity and preferences may enhance the effectiveness of culturally adapted dietary interventions. Taste sensitivity, particularly for saltiness, umami, bitterness, and sweetness, have been shown to predict dietary adherence [47, 48]. Incorporating such screening into future AMED implementation strategies may support personalised dietary recommendations and improve long-term adherence.

Potential health benefits and clinical implications

The MedDiet is associated with reduced risk of cardiometabolic diseases, including MASLD [2, 9, 41, 49, 50]. Evidence from the FLIPAN trial showed that increased adherence to the MedDiet significantly improved liver steatosis and metabolic dysfunction in MASLD patients [10]. In contrast, the MEDINA study found that while both the MedDiet and a low-fat diet led to reductions in visceral fat, the low-fat intervention achieved statistically significant improvements in intrahepatic lipid content and insulin resistance, whereas the MedDiet group showed non-significant but clinically meaningful reductions [51]. These differences may reflect variations in study designs, population characteristics, intervention fidelity, and dietary adherence. Nonetheless, a substantial and consistent body of evidence supports the MedDiet’s role in mitigating metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, conditions pathophysiologically linked to MASLD [15]. In the recent EASL-EASD-EASO guidelines, an improved diet quality, such as adopting a Mediterranean-style dietary pattern, together with other lifestyle modifications, are effective strategies to reduce liver fat and liver injury [5]. Importantly, the dietary interventions should be tailored to individual and cultural preferences to maximize long-term adherence. As this study did not assess clinical endpoints, interpretations regarding potential health effects must remain descriptive and cannot be taken as evidence of clinical efficacy. Nonetheless, the AMED’s alignment with MedDiet nutrient patterns and its high sensory acceptability suggest potential for broader application in MASLD prevention and management, consistent with expert recommendations to adapt successful dietary patterns to local food cultures for better compliance. Future trials should evaluate its effect on metabolic, hepatic, and behavioural outcomes, and compare its efficacy with other culturally adapted dietary models.

This successful cultural translation of the MedDiet into an Asian context suggests that AMED may offer comparable health benefits for chronic disease prevention and management in the Asian population. These findings underscore the value of culturally tailored dietary models and highlight the need for future research to validate AMED against MedDiet standards, compare it with other adapted diets, and assess its clinical impact through well-designed trials.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents the first systematic development and evaluation of a culturally adapted AMED in Singapore using a comprehensive, multi-phase methodology. By integrating nutrient mapping, recipe development, polyphenol profiling, and sensory evaluation, it offers a robust and transparent framework for cultural translation of the MedDiet. The detailed dietary prescriptions, including macronutrients targets, food-group recommendations, and portion standards, enhance reproducibility and support future assessments. Furthermore, the inclusion of polyphenol quantification adds objective evidence of antioxidant content, reinforcing the nutritional credibility of the AMED menu. Conducting sensory evaluation among individuals with MASLD in a real-world clinical setting further strengthens the study’s relevance, while the use of culturally familiar ingredients and cooking methods enhances feasibility and addresses known barriers to adherence in Asian populations.

The primary limitation of this study is the modest sample size for sensory evaluation, though it aligns with standard requirements for repeated-measures sensory testing of novel food products. Future research should build on these findings through randomized controlled trials assessing clinical outcomes, including metabolic and hepatic biomarkers, as well as patient-reported measures. Expanding the 4-week AMED menu and refining polyphenol databases would further enhance the framework’s culinary diversity and applicability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the MedDiet can be effectively and culturally adapted for Asian populations while maintaining its core nutritional principles. The resulting AMED framework is nutritionally coherent with MedDiet standards and was well accepted in sensory evaluation, indicating its feasibility for use in Asian settings. Although the AMED aligns with dietary patterns associated with cardiometabolic benefits in prior research, the present study did not examine clinical outcomes; thus, its potential health effects remain to be established. These findings provide a foundational basis for future intervention trials aimed at evaluating the metabolic, hepatic, and behavioural impacts of AMED, as well as its suitability for broader clinical and public health applications.
