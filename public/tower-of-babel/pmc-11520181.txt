From outdated to outstanding: an institution’s journey in reforming the dental materials science curriculum and lessons learned
Authors: Galvin Sim Siang Lin, Chan Choong Foong, Ramesh Kumaresan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Dental Materials Science (DMS) course within Malaysian undergraduate dental education has traditionally been taught in isolation, with a relatively stagnant delivery mode. Recent shifts in national dental competency standards have necessitated substantial reform in the DMS curriculum. This study aims to outline the journey of a local dental institute in developing and implementing a competency-based DMS curriculum.MethodsA faculty curriculum task force was established to oversee the curriculum reform process. The reform was guided by a “bottom-up” approach using the five-step curriculum reform model: (1). Curriculum mapping, (2). Comprehensive needs assessment, (3). Clear learning objectives and outcomes, (4). Curriculum development and implementation, and (5). Continuous evaluation and improvement.ResultsStep 1 revealed the need to reduce content repetition and improve alignment with the new competency standards. Step 2 identified the need to include advanced materials, integrate theoretical and clinical aspects, and enhance teaching methods. The new curriculum included 25 relevant topics. Step 3 established three course learning outcomes, while Step 4 integrated DMS content horizontally and vertically across the curriculum, introduced innovative teaching methods, and diversified assessment methods. Step 5 ensured ongoing curriculum monitoring, with both short-term and long-term evaluations planned.ConclusionThe curriculum reform process represents a significant advancement in dental materials science while addressing challenges and aligning the curriculum with national standards. Ongoing evaluation, adaptability, interdisciplinary collaboration, and international engagement are essential for further improvements. This journey serves as a valuable reference for educators seeking to update their DMS curricula.

Background

Dental education in Malaysia has a rich history of producing highly skilled and competent dental professionals who are committed to meeting the dynamic demands of the field. The country’s five-year undergraduate dental programs have traditionally adhered to a structured framework rooted in the principles of Bloom’s and Simpson’s taxonomies [1, 2]. These programs offer a blend of foundational and clinical courses aimed at providing students with a holistic understanding of dental sciences, spanning preclinical and clinical phases [3]. Central to this comprehensive education is the Dental Materials Science (DMS) course, a cornerstone of the preclinical phase in the country’s undergraduate dental curriculum [4]. However, it is evident that the delivery of this course has remained relatively stagnant over the years. In most Malaysian undergraduate dental programs, the DMS course is typically isolated as a stand-alone course in the first or second year (preclinical year) of the Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS) or Doctor of Dental Surgery (DDS) program. While it certainly imparts essential knowledge, its isolation from other dental specialties and exclusion from the clinical phase may restrict its practical relevance in real-world dental practice [5].

Historically, the instructional approach for the DMS course has leaned heavily on a series of lectures and tutorials [4, 6]. Although conventional teaching method, such as didactic lectures, has their merits [7], they may not fully engage students or facilitate deep comprehension of the subject matter [8]. The need for more innovative and interactive approaches to teaching DMS courses has become increasingly apparent.

In recent years, the landscape of dental education in Malaysia has undergone a significant transformation. In July 2021, the Malaysian Dental Council released the Competencies of New Dental Graduates statement, which has set forth new, higher standards for dental graduates [9]. Based on Bloom’s cognitive taxonomy (Fig. 1), these standards demand not only to “Explain” (Cognitive level: C2) but also to “Justify” Cognitive level: C4) their selection of dental materials. Additionally, graduates are expected to progress from “Mechanism” (Psychomotor level: P4) to “Complex Overt Response” (Psychomotor level: P5) based on Simpson’s psychomotor taxonomy (Fig. 1). These elevated competency requirements highlight the urgency for a comprehensive overhaul of the DMS curriculum.

Confronted with these challenges, the Faculty of Dentistry at AIMST University Malaysia has proactively undertaken a significant curriculum reform to enhance and revise its DMS curriculum. Curriculum reform can be defined as the process of evaluating and modifying the scope and structure of the curriculum to improve student learning outcomes and enhance the quality of education [10, 11]. This may involve reviewing and revising the content of the curriculum as well as considering how that knowledge is organized and presented to students, ensuring alignment with intended learning outcomes [12]. The goal of the current curriculum reform is to align it with the new Competencies of New Dental Graduates statement and the evolving landscape of dental education in Malaysia. Nevertheless, the process of implementing curriculum reforms is a formidable challenge, often requiring adjustments across multiple dimensions that may potentially clash with deeply entrenched beliefs and subjective realities within both individual and organizational spheres [13].

The present article serves as an extension of the institutional project at the Faculty of Dentistry, AIMST University Malaysia, by documenting the institution’s journey in developing and implementing a competency-based DMS curriculum. In this article, we present a curriculum reform model for the DMS curriculum, which includes five key components: (1). Curriculum mapping, (2). Comprehensive needs assessment, (3). Clear learning objectives and outcomes, (4). Curriculum development and implementation, and (5). Continuous evaluation and improvement. This model offers a structured framework that helps faculty members ensure that the DMS curriculum is well-organized, comprehensive, and aligned with educational goals and objectives, bridging the gap between outdated practices and exceptional education in dental materials science. We hope that our curriculum reform model and the lessons learned through its implementation can offer valuable guidance for dental educators seeking to revise their DMS curricula in the future.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was exempt from review by the university’s institutional review boards (Ethical approval code: AUHEC/FOD/11/10/2023).

Study design

It employed a qualitative, exploratory design to examine the curriculum reform process for the DMS curriculum at AIMST University Malaysia. The institution’s journey of DMS curriculum reform began in late 2021. A curriculum taskforce team was formed, given the trust and mandate to manage the curriculum reform. The task force members were appointed by the dean based on their expertise and institutional roles. This selection was later ratified by consensus among the deputy deans. The task force team comprised the head of the dental materials department and members of the BDS curriculum review committee, all working under the guidance of the top-level management, including the dean and deputy deans. One of the study’s authors was an active member of this committee, contributing significantly to the reform process.

Curriculum reform model

In the pursuit to transform the DMS curriculum at AIMST University Malaysia, a curriculum reform model is adopted to align the curriculum with the competency requirements outlined by the Malaysian Dental Council. The “Bottom-up” approach, as proposed by Tyack and Cuban [14], was used as a guiding framework to guide the curriculum revision and development process. This model was chosen to allow the gradual integration of new ideas, as informed by stakeholders across different levels of the educational hierarchy, including faculty members, alumni, industry representatives, and regulatory bodies like the Malaysian Dental Council. Below, we outline the methodology employed in each of the five key steps (Fig. 2), with the final step, continuous evaluation and improvement, currently underway and to be reported separately.

Step 1: curriculum mapping

Curriculum mapping of the existing DMS curriculum based on Harden’s 10-window framework was performed [15]. Subsequently, a “Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats” (SWOT) analysis was conducted to identify specific areas within the curriculum that demanded improvement and refinement [15]. In parallel, the curriculum content was compared with the Malaysian Dental Council’s new competency statements to identify gaps in the existing curriculum.

Step 2: Comprehensive needs assessment

Building on insights from the curriculum mapping, a comprehensive needs assessment was conducted to better understand the current educational landscape and gather input from key stakeholders. This phase involved engaging with departmental staff and faculty members from various dental specialties. The team also reviewed industry perspectives through stakeholders’ annual reports and sought input from alumni to understand their experiences and expectations regarding the DMS course. An extensive literature review was conducted to explore the latest advancements in dental materials.

Furthermore, experts from other local universities were invited to identify relevant topics in contemporary dental materials science. To ensure a comprehensive selection of topics, the Fuzzy Delphi method was employed, carefully considering the cognitive and psychomotor levels associated with each topic [16]. As we venturing deeper into this process, the required resources and infrastructure necessary for the successful delivery of the new curriculum were scrutinized. This included a careful examination of laboratory equipment, teaching materials, and faculty training needs.

Step 3: clear learning objectives and outcomes

In this phase, the course learning outcomes were realigned with the newly established competency statements by the Malaysian Dental Council. Insights from the previously mentioned Fuzzy Delphi consensus were utilized, which offered valuable guidance on relevant topics and appropriate competency levels. As a result, specific learning outcomes for each topic were revised to ensure their measurability and attainability.

Step 4: curriculum development and implementation

During this phase, the team bridged the gap between preclinical and clinical DMS education by restructuring the course content throughout the 5-year BDS program. This effort involved fragmenting the curriculum into components integrated across various dental specialties. Furthermore, the team revised the existing DMS curriculum blueprint to include the newly introduced lecture topics, ensuring alignment with specific learning outcomes and competency levels.

Based on the course learning outcomes, appropriate teaching and assessment methods were designed. To foster student engagement, innovative teaching methodologies were piloted and adopted, including a hybrid approach combining team-based and case-based learning (TBL-CBL) [17]. Integral to the curriculum were more hands-on laboratory exercises supplemented with constructive feedback [18], empowering students to apply theoretical knowledge and hone critical skills. Simultaneously, assessment methods were modified to better match the desired cognitive and psychomotor levels. This modification allowed students’ achievements to be accurately measured and aligned with the course learning outcomes.

A well-defined timeline was used to ensure a seamless curriculum rollout. This timeline encompassed curriculum development, resource procurement, and faculty training. Several workshops were organized to engage external experts who were representatives of the Malaysian Qualifying Agency (MQA) to provide faculty training before implementing the new DMS curriculum. The MQA is the national statutory body for accrediting academic programs. The new DMS curriculum structure underwent a meticulous review and validation process, which involved representatives from the MQA. To ensure a seamless transition, the new DMS curriculum was introduced in a few stages. Initially, a pilot phase was implemented in the last semester of the 2022 cohort. During this phase, faculty members incorporated short modules with the desired learning outcomes on recent dental materials throughout the semester alongside the proposed innovative teaching methods. To gauge student understanding and progress, small group discussions and single best answer (SBA) assessments were included.

Step 5: evaluation and continuous improvement

For the pilot study that took place at the end of the semester for the 2022 cohort, we convened an academic quality assurance meeting to deliberate on the curriculum monitoring process and address any limitations encountered. These measures paved the way for the full implementation of the new DMS curriculum in 2023. Next, both short-term and long-term evaluations are planned to continuously evaluate and improve the new DMS curriculum. In the short term, annual curriculum review meetings will be convened to analyze outcomes (e.g., assessment results, student evaluation of teaching), make required adjustments, and ensure the curriculum stays aligned with the evolving needs of dental education. In the long-term, a comprehensive evaluation will occur at the end of the first cycle of implementation in 2028. This phase will involve continuous evaluations of student performance and faculty feedback. Valuable insights will be gathered through surveys and focus group discussions with both students and faculty members at the end of the cycle.

Results

Step 1: Curriculum mapping

From the curriculum mapping exercise published earlier [15], repetition in lecture topics was identified, with content primarily focused on the cognitive level C2 and psychomotor level P4. This fell short of the desired cognitive level C4 and psychomotor level P5, as outlined in the new competency statement. Additionally, we observed an imbalance in the emphasis on psychomotor skills, an over-reliance on a single academic staff member, and limited integration with other dental specialties. Considering these findings, the team decided to address these issues by revising and eliminating repetitive content, amplifying the focus on psychomotor skills, exploring advanced pedagogical strategies, and fostering both vertical and horizontal integration in the new DMS curriculum.

Step 2: Comprehensive needs assessment

Stakeholder reports and faculty feedback highlighted various areas for improvement in the DMS curriculum, such as incorporating advanced materials and technologies, bridging theoretical concepts with clinical scenarios, introducing more interactive teaching methods, increasing hands-on experiences, and fostering collaborative team learning. Furthermore, Delphi consensus surveys helped identify relevant DMS topics, highlighting that topics like gold filling, dental amalgam, and investment materials were considered irrelevant [16]. Experts also reached a consensus that the DMS curriculum should target cognitive levels C2 to C4 and psychomotor levels P2 to P4 without the need for the higher psychomotor level, “Complex Overt Response” (P5). However, a consensus has been reached among the team members to align the psychomotor (P5) level with the national competency statement. Based on these findings, 25 topics were selected for inclusion in the new DMS curriculum (Table 1).

Step 3: Clear learning objectives and outcomes

Based on the comprehensive needs assessment and curriculum mapping exercise, we have formulated three course learning outcomes that will serve as the foundation for the new DMS curriculum: (1). Explain the classifications, compositions, properties, ideal requirements, and the performances of various dental materials, (2). Justify the selection of different dental materials based on their advantages, disadvantages, indications, and contraindications, and (3). Display the ability to mix and manipulate various dental materials independently. These course learning outcomes were used as guiding principles to align specific learning outcomes for each topic within the curriculum.

Step 4: Curriculum development and implementation

The new DMS curriculum is developed with a focus on both vertical and horizontal integration, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Table 2 displays the comparison between the existing and new DMS curricula. It can be noted that the teaching methods have evolved beyond traditional lectures and tutorials to include a rich array of interactive learning experiences. These encompass more hands-on practical sessions, small-group discussions, hybrid TBL-CBL sessions and collaborative learning in laboratory materials with students from the Bachelor of Dental Technology (BDT) program. In terms of assessment, the new DMS curriculum differs from its previous reliance on multiple-choice questions (MCQ), short-answer questions (SAQ), and the objective structured practical examination (OSPE).

To align with the elevated cognitive demands outlined in the new competency statement, SBA, single correct answer (SCA), and modified essay question (MEQ) were introduced as crucial components of the assessment process. Furthermore, the assessment methods have been expanded to incorporate seminars, capstone projects, and a variety of assignments. These additions provide a more comprehensive evaluation of student’s knowledge and skills, ensuring that they are well-prepared for the dynamic field of dental materials science. Moreover, there is a slight variation in the weighting of teaching and assessment methods when comparing the existing and new DMS curricula (Table 2). The new curriculum places greater emphasis on psychomotor aspects of course delivery. Meanwhile, the previous heavy reliance on a single summative psychomotor assessment using OSPE has been replaced with the inclusion of laboratory practical assessments as an integral part of the formative psychomotor assessment process.

Step 5: Evaluation and continuous improvement

The faculty members were positive about the inclusion of new topics into the curriculum, and modifications to the teaching and assessment methods were well-received. These changes included a shift towards incorporating higher-level critical thinking assessments, such as single-best-answer questions, in addition to basic knowledge tests. Student feedback on these changes was also positive, as reported in a prior publication [17], with over 80% of students agreeing that the hybrid TBL-CBL learning sessions enhanced their understanding of dental materials and improved their reasoning skills. The changes in teaching methods also contributed to statistically significant improvements in student academic performance, with more students achieving higher grades as compared to conventional didactic lectures during the pilot phase of the curriculum reform [19]. Encouraged by the positive feedback from both the pilot phase and academic quality assurance meeting, the full implementation of the new DMS curriculum commenced in 2023.

Discussion

The institution’s journey toward the development and implementation of a new competency-based DMS curriculum represents a significant milestone in addressing the evolving landscape of dental education. The curriculum task force recognized the need to align its curriculum with the new competency standards outlined by the Malaysian Dental Council [9], demanding a transition from a traditional teacher-centered didactic approach to a more student-centered, practical, and interactive approach. The present curriculum reform is guided by a “bottom-up” approach which has also been applied in other health profession education settings [20, 21], emphasizing the involvement and input of key stakeholders within the institution. This approach is rooted in Tyack and Cuban’s framework [14], and encourages active participation from various levels of the institution, including faculty members, department heads, and top-level management. The “bottom-up” approach ensures that the curriculum reform process is informed by the experiences, perspectives, and expertise of those directly involved in dental education.

Several core educational theories underpinned the new DMS curriculum. The curriculum taskforce team considered applying principles of andragogy or adult learning theory [22], which emphasizes self-directed learning and problem-solving. The reform aimed to tailor the new curriculum to accommodate the unique learning needs and motivations of adult dental students, thereby promoting autonomy and active engagement in the learning process. Moreover, it highlights experiential learning in the new curriculum to allow students to actively engage with dental materials in practical settings [23]. Hands-on laboratory exercises, clinical simulations, and real-world case studies were incorporated to facilitate deep learning and skills development. Collaborative learning strategies were also introduced to encourage dental students to work together in small groups to solve complex problems and share insights. Dental students can learn together with dental technology students during laboratory materials practical sessions. Such collaborative learning has been found to foster teamwork, critical thinking, and peer-to-peer knowledge exchange [24].

The incorporation of various summative and formative assessment methods into the new DMS curriculum brings substantial advantages for both educators and students. These diverse assessment techniques contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation of student’s knowledge and skills [25], aligning the curriculum with the evolving needs of dental education. The inclusion of various written assessment methods, such as SBA, SCA, and MEQ, alongside traditional formats, like MCQ and SAQ, offers educators a broader toolbox to evaluate students. This diversity allows for a more thorough examination of students’ understanding and applications of dental materials, rather than relying on a single approach that might not capture the full scope of their abilities [26]. Additionally, formative assessments like seminars and capstone projects promote active learning and critical thinking [27, 28]. By incorporating a variety of assessment methods, the curriculum can better assess students’ ability to justify their choices and meet higher cognitive levels, ensuring that graduates are prepared to address the evolving challenges of the profession.

Moreover, the decision to split psychomotor assessments into formative lab practical assessments and summative OSPE exams brings significant benefits to students. First, it would allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of students’ psychomotor skills throughout the course. Formative lab practical assessments provide continuous feedback and opportunities for improvement. It has been reported that formative assessments foster a supportive learning environment by encouraging and reinforcing students’ self-regulated learning strategies [29]. Second, the reduction of pressure associated with a single high-stakes exam can alleviate stress and anxiety among students. High-stakes exams often create an environment of immense pressure, hindering students’ ability to learn and retain information effectively [30]. Formative assessments, on the other hand, reduce this pressure and allow students to focus on learning rather than the fear of failure. Finally, aligning psychomotor assessments with the national competency statement ensures that students are well-prepared for real-world dental practice. By emphasizing these skills in the curriculum and assessing them continuously, students are better equipped to meet the rigorous demands of the profession and ensure patient safety.

The institution’s journey to implement the new competency-based DMS curriculum was not without challenges. Curriculum reform is a complex process that necessitates adjustments across various aspects of education. One of the primary challenges faced during the curriculum reform was the resistance of faculty members to change [31]. It is natural for individual faculty members to resist changes that challenge established practices and beliefs. Furthermore, a previous study identified common barriers to curriculum reform, including time constraints, instructional challenges, loss of autonomy, and resistance to change [32]. To address this, the present curriculum taskforce team emphasized the importance of aligning the curriculum with the evolving needs of dental education and industry to the faculty members. In addition, faculty development programs were conducted to prepare faculty members for the adoption of innovative teaching methods.

The DMS course traditionally followed a stand-alone structure, isolated from other dental specialties. The challenge was to integrate DMS content horizontally and vertically across the curriculum [4]. Key lessons learned from this challenge include the importance of effective communication among faculty members and the need for a clear roadmap for integration [33]. The curriculum task force team also acknowledged the value of interdisciplinary collaboration in curriculum design. It is worth highlighting that implementing a reformed curriculum necessitated resource allocation, including the procurement of laboratory equipment, teaching materials, and faculty training. Securing these resources within budgetary constraints posed a significant challenge, and careful planning is essential to ensure the effective delivery of the new curriculum. Moreover, a thorough assessment of resource needs was conducted during the needs assessment phase. The higher standards set forth by the Malaysian Dental Council posed an additional challenge in aligning the curriculum with these requirements, necessitating a revision of course learning outcomes. The lesson learned includes the importance of involving stakeholders in defining clear learning outcomes and continuously monitoring their attainment [34].

The successful implementation of the new DMS curriculum shows a transformative future in dental education at AIMST University Malaysia. The institution’s journey to implement the new curriculum is an ongoing process. This continuous evaluation process provides insights into the long-term impact of the curriculum reform. Monitoring graduates’ performance and their ability to meet the competency standards set by accrediting bodies is an ongoing endeavor. Undeniably, the new curriculum offers opportunities for research and innovation in dental materials science education. Faculty members can engage in educational research to explore the impact of innovative teaching methodologies on student learning outcomes [35], which can lead to a deeper understanding of the subject matter.

The integration of DMS content with other dental specialties will also open doors for interdisciplinary collaboration. Students from different programs, such as dental technology, can collaborate in practical sessions, enhancing their understanding and teamwork skills [35]. This collaborative approach mirrors the real-world dental practice environment. Nevertheless, the new DMS curriculum should remain adaptable to incorporate emerging materials and technologies. Regular updates and revisions are necessary to keep pace with industry advancements. The new DMS curriculum should also look beyond national boundaries and consider global best practices in dental education. Collaboration with international institutions and exposure to international standards can further enhance the quality of education provided [36].

Finally, it should be noted that the present study has limitations. Specifically, it represents the findings from a single institution’s efforts to redesign its DMS curriculum. However, the experiences and lessons learned from the present institution can serve as valuable guidance for others undertaking similar initiatives [37]. Hence, this present study constitutes a foundation for stimulating discourse on curriculum design for DMS. Conclusion

The journey from an outdated DMS curriculum to a more well-structured, competency-based model at AIMST University Malaysia marks a significant leap forward in dental education. Recognizing the limitations of the previous static and fragmented curriculum, the faculty initiated a comprehensive curriculum reform process. This transformative process was guided by a structured five-step model, including curriculum mapping, comprehensive needs assessment, defining clear learning objectives and outcomes, curriculum development and implementation, and continuous evaluation and improvement. The reformed curriculum is grounded in contemporary pedagogical principles and designed in collaboration with key stakeholders, ensuring it aligns with evolving competency standards while bridging gaps between cognitive and psychomotor skills. Despite several challenges, the successful implementation has laid the foundation for a dynamic, research-informed, and globally relevant dental materials science education at AIMST University Malaysia. Ongoing evaluation, interdisciplinary collaboration, adaptability to emerging trends, and international engagement will further enhance the curriculum’s quality and impact in the field of dental materials science education. This journey serves as a valuable reference for local and international dental educators seeking to revamp their DMS curricula.
