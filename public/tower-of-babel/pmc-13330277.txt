Psychometric evaluation of the Indonesian version of the confidence scale among primary healthcare nurses in dengue-related prevention practice
Authors: Nuzul Sri Hertanti, Nga Thi Thuy Nguyen, Do Thi Kim Chi, Yeu-Hui Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConfidence may influence nurses’ clinical practice. In dengue-endemic countries such as Indonesia, dengue infection prevention represents a core component of dengue-related practice among primary healthcare (PHC) nurses. However, validated Indonesian instruments for assessing nurses’ confidence in dengue-related prevention practice are limited. Therefore, this study aimed to translate and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Indonesian version of a Confidence Scale (C-Scale-I).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between May and August 2024 among nurses working in PHC units across Indonesia. The original C-Scale was adapted into Indonesian using a six-step cross-cultural process, including forward translation, synthesis, backward translation, expert review, pretesting, and instrument appraisal. Psychometric evaluation included face and content validity, construct validity using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), measurement invariance, known-groups validity, concurrent validity, internal consistency, and test-retest reliability.ResultsA total of 458 nurses from 85 PHC units participated. Pretesting among 30 nurses indicated acceptable face validity. Content validity was also acceptable based on evaluation by eight experts. EFA supported a single-factor structure explaining 74.13% of the total variance. CFA confirmed the one-factor model with acceptable fit indices, with factor loadings ranging from 0.79 to 0.86. Measurement invariance across education levels was supported based on ΔCFI and ΔSRMR criteria. Known-groups validity was indicated by higher C-Scale-I scores among nurses familiar with national dengue guidelines compared with those who were not. Concurrent validity showed a positive correlation with the Visual Analogue Confidence Scale. The scale had acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92; McDonald’s ω = 0.92) and test-retest reliability over a two-week interval (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.74, 95% CI 0.52–0.86).ConclusionsThe C-Scale-I demonstrates acceptable validity and reliability for assessing confidence in dengue-related prevention practice among PHC nurses. This tool may assist nursing leaders and educators in identifying confidence gaps and strengthening continuing education initiatives related to dengue prevention in PHC settings. Further studies are needed to evaluate additional psychometric properties, such as responsiveness to change, discriminant validity, and predictive validity, and to examine its applicability in other nursing populations and contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09471-6.Keywords: Confidence, Cross-cultural adaptation, Dengue, Nurse, Prevention, Primary healthcare, Psychometrics

Background

Confidence is an important psychological factor that reflects nurses’ belief in their ability to competently perform professional tasks and make appropriate clinical decisions under various circumstances [1–3]. In nursing practice, confidence has long been recognized as a key determinant of clinical performance, communication, and patient safety [4, 5]. Higher confidence has been associated with improved clinical decision-making, technical performance, and readiness for independent practice, as well as greater engagement in continuing professional development [6–8].

According to Bandura’s social cognitive theory, confidence is closely related to self-efficacy and is developed through mastery of experiences, feedback, and social modeling [1]. Although these constructs are related, they are conceptually distinct [9]. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capability to perform specific tasks required to achieve desired outcomes, whereas confidence reflects a broader perception of competence and readiness to carry out professional responsibilities [1, 9]. In medical and nursing education literature, self-efficacy is typically task-specific, while confidence is often used to describe healthcare professionals’ perceived preparedness to apply knowledge and skills in clinical practice [9].

In this study, confidence was conceptualized as nurses’ perceived readiness to perform dengue-related prevention practice, which represents a central component of dengue-related practice in primary healthcare (PHC) settings. As the primary professional responsibility of PHC nurses, dengue-related prevention practice includes community health education, promotion of vector control measures, early identification of suspected dengue cases, and coordination of case reporting and referrals [10, 11]. Although confidence reflects a broad perception of professional competence, it may vary across clinical contexts depending on task demands, practice environments, and professional experience [8, 12, 13]. Therefore, valid and reliable instruments are needed to measure nurses’ confidence across different clinical contexts and practice settings.

In dengue-endemic countries such as Indonesia, PHC nurses are at the frontline of dengue prevention, early case detection, and community health education [14]. Their confidence in performing these roles may be relevant to the effective implementation of dengue-related prevention activities. Although direct evidence on confidence in dengue-related prevention practice is limited, studies from other infectious disease contexts suggest that confidence is associated with communication skills, decision-making, and preparedness in responding to emerging infectious threats, such as COVID-19 [8]. Similarly, confidence has been associated with nursing competency and clinical performance in high-acuity settings, such as emergency and intensive care units [3].

Despite its importance, confidence in dengue-related prevention practice remains understudied. Most previous research focused on knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward dengue prevention [15, 16], with confidence only briefly included as part of self-reported attitudes or practices. For example, one survey found that while many healthcare workers felt confident in implementing preventive measures, fewer expressed confidence in their ability to accurately recognize or diagnose dengue fever [15]. However, those findings were based on single-item measures, which might not adequately reflect the intended meaning of confidence in clinical practice.

Several instruments have been developed to measure confidence in nursing practice. However, most were designed for specific clinical or disease-related contexts rather than dengue practice. For example, the Confidence in Dementia Scale (CODE) assesses hospital staff’s confidence in caring for people with dementia [17, 18]. Ogawa and Nakatani [7] developed the Professional Confidence Scale for Public Health Nurses, a 34-item instrument describing how nurses gain confidence through accumulated practice experience. That instrument provides a comprehensive understanding of confidence in community health settings. In addition, the Palliative Care Nursing Self-Competence Scale measures nurses’ perceived competence in providing palliative care across ten domains [19]. The scale was later validated among oncology nurses in the United States and was found to also reflect confidence in delivering palliative care [20]. Although these instruments provide valuable insights into confidence in specific areas of practice, their constructs are aligned with competencies required in those clinical domains, which may limit their applicability to dengue-related prevention practice in PHC settings.

The Confidence Scale (C-Scale) developed by Grundy [21] is a concise instrument designed to measure nurses’ confidence in performing physical assessments. This scale has shown acceptable validity and reliability among nursing students and staff nurses. Unlike instruments that focus on disease-specific competencies, the C-Scale measures general indicators of confidence in task performance, including certainty of performance, absence of hesitation, perceived competence, and satisfaction with one’s performance [21]. Because these indicators reflect confidence in task execution rather than disease-specific competencies, the scale can be adapted to different practice contexts by specifying the task in the item wording. In this study, the task context was defined as dengue-related prevention practice, so the scale allows assessment of nurses’ perceived confidence in performing these activities across diverse PHC settings. In addition, its brevity makes it practical for use among nurses with demanding workloads.

Cross-cultural adaptation of existing instruments allows preservation of established measurement constructs while ensuring linguistic and contextual appropriateness for the target population. Based on these considerations, the C-Scale was selected for cross-cultural adaptation to measure PHC nurses’ confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. Therefore, this study aimed to translate and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Indonesian version of the Confidence Scale (C-Scale-I) among PHC nurses.

Methods

Research design

We conducted a cross-sectional study to translate the C-Scale into Indonesian and to examine the psychometric properties of the adapted instrument. This study was part of a larger project examining dengue-related knowledge, attitudes, and confidence among PHC nurses in Indonesia.

Confidence scale (C-scale)

The original C-Scale, developed by Grundy [21], measures self-confidence related to the performance of physical assessment. It was originally tested among nursing students and staff nurses performing head-to-toe physical assessments. The instrument consists of five statements, each rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = no confidence to 5 = extremely confident). The items include: (1) I am certain that my performance is correct; (2) I feel that I perform the task without hesitation; (3) My performance would convince the observer(s) that I am competent at this task; (4) I feel sure of myself as I perform the task; and (5) I feel satisfied with my performance. The total score ranges from 5 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater confidence.

The original instrument had acceptable validity and reliability, with Cronbach’s α = 0.85 for staff nurses and test-retest reliability coefficients of 0.89 and 0.84 for 1-hour and 9-day intervals, respectively [21]. The concurrent validity was supported by significant correlations with the Confidence Visual Analogue Scale (C-VAS) and the Confidence Verbal Descriptor Scale (C-VDS), with correlation coefficients of 0.64 and 0.77, respectively [21]. In previous studies, this C-Scale was applied to various nursing skills and contexts, including urinary catheterization and nasogastric-tube feeding among nursing students [22, 23], neonatal resuscitation among neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) nurses [24], bedside practice confidence among newly hired nurses [25], and simulation implementation among nursing faculty [26].

Translation and adaptation procedure

Permission to translate the C-Scale was obtained from the original developer. The adaptation followed the six-step approach by Beaton et al. [27], ensuring conceptual and semantic comparability between the original and Indonesian versions: (1) forward translation, (2) synthesis, (3) backward translation, (4) expert committee review, (5) pretesting, and (6) final instrument appraisal. For the forward translation, two bilingual translators independently produced Indonesian translations of the original English items. One translator was a dengue researcher familiar with clinical terminology, while the other was an officer at the Ministry of Health (MOH) experienced in program implementation. The two translations (T1 and T2) were synthesized into a single reconciled version (T12) by the research team after carefully comparing wording, meaning, and contextual appropriateness between both translations. For the backward translation, two new bilingual translators, both fluent in English and Indonesian and blinded to the original instrument, independently translated the reconciled Indonesian version (T12) back to English. The back-translated versions (BT1 and BT2) were compared to the original scale to confirm conceptual accuracy. Any discrepancies identified between the back-translations and the original items were first discussed by the research team and subsequently addressed during the expert committee review to ensure conceptual equivalence.

An expert committee of eight members reviewed all versions (original, T1, T2, T12, BT1, and BT2) for semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence. The panel consisted of two clinical nurses experienced in dengue management, two nursing academics, two public health experts, one dengue officer from a district health office, and one officer from the MOH. All panel members held at least a master’s degree and had more than 10 years of professional experience in nursing or dengue prevention. Feedback from the panel was incorporated to produce a pre-final version of the instrument.

The pre-final version was pilot tested among 30 PHC nurses to assess clarity and comprehensibility. Each participant rated the items on a four-point scale, where 1 indicated “very difficult to understand” and 4 indicated “very easy to understand.” The face validity was examined by calculating the item-level face validity index (I-FVI) and scale-level face validity index (S-FVI/Ave), which respectively represent the proportion of ratings of 3 or 4 (clear) and the average of I-FVIs across items. A value of ≥ 0.80 was considered satisfactory [28]. The internal consistency and two-week test-retest reliability were also estimated for this pilot sample.

Following pilot testing, the expert committee reconvened to appraise the instrument’s content validity. Each expert rated the relevance of each item on a four-point scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant), and both the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) were calculated. Acceptable thresholds were I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 [29]. For each item, the I-CVI was determined by calculating the proportion of experts who rated the item as either 3 or 4 out of the total eight experts. To evaluate the overall content validity of the instrument, the S-CVI/Ave was calculated by averaging the I-CVI values across all items [29]. The finalized C-Scale-I was then used for the psychometric evaluation.

Psychometric testing

Research settings and participants

Convenience sampling was used for participant recruitment. Participants were nurses working in PHC units across Indonesia. Indonesia is an archipelagic country consisting of more than 17,000 islands and 38 provinces [30]. Administratively, the country is divided into provinces, which are further subdivided into regencies and districts. Each district typically has at least one PHC unit providing primary care services [31]. Therefore, PHC units are widely distributed across diverse geographical settings, including both urban and rural areas. The participating PHC units represented various geographical contexts across Indonesia.

The inclusion criteria were active employment in a PHC unit and willingness to participate in the study. Following methodological recommendations suggesting at least ten respondents per item for the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) [32] and a minimum of 300 participants for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [33], 458 nurses from 85 PHC units across 21 provinces were recruited for this study. Using separate samples for EFA and CFA is recommended to evaluate the stability of the factor structure and reduce the risk of overfitting [34, 35]. As no established guideline exists for determining the optimal sample split between EFA and CFA [36], the dataset was randomly divided using the Random Case Selection function in SPSS, with 140 responses used for the EFA and 318 for the CFA.

Instruments

The questionnaire package consisted of two sections. The first captured demographic characteristics, including age, gender, educational level (nursing diploma or bachelor’s degree and above), length of work experience in PHC, previous experience in caring for dengue patients, participation in dengue-related training, exposure to dengue topics during formal education, and familiarity with the national dengue guidelines. The second section contained the five-item C-Scale-I, which was used to measure nurses’ confidence in performing dengue-related prevention practice. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5. Higher total scores indicated greater confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. For the concurrent validity, participants also rated their overall confidence in dengue-related prevention practice on a single-item C-VAS ranging from 0 (no confidence) to 100 (highest confidence), with higher scores indicating greater confidence.

Data collection

Data were collected between May and August 2024 using online self-administered questionnaires. The survey link was distributed through professional nursing networks and social media platforms such as WhatsApp and Instagram, which are commonly used by nurses in Indonesia. Because the survey link was distributed through online networks, the total number of nurses who received the invitation was unknown. Therefore, the response rate could not be calculated. An online approach was selected to facilitate recruitment from geographically dispersed PHC units across the country. Interested nurses accessed an online information sheet describing the study objectives and procedures, and were asked to provide electronic informed consent before participation. Those who consented were automatically redirected to the main survey, which included demographic questions, the C-Scale-I, and C-VAS. To minimize duplicate responses, the survey platform restricted submissions to one entry per email address. In addition, all questionnaire items were set as required fields in the online survey to ensure that only complete responses were submitted.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0, AMOS version 26.0, and R version 4.3.3. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables reported as means and standard deviations (SDs). Prior to analysis, the dataset was examined for completeness, normality, and outliers. No missing data were observed because all items in the online questionnaire were set as required fields. The data distribution of confidence scores followed a normal distribution, as indicated by skewness and kurtosis values of close to zero (− 0.27 and 0.16, respectively). Values of skewness within ± 2 and kurtosis within ± 7 are generally considered acceptable indicators of approximate normality [37, 38].

Psychometric testing included the face validity, content validity, item analysis, construct validity (EFA and CFA), measurement invariance, known-groups validity, concurrent validity, internal consistency reliability, and test-retest reliability. The item analysis was conducted by calculating corrected item-total correlations to assess each item’s contribution to the overall scale. Corrected item-total correlations ≥ 0.30 were considered acceptable, indicating that each item contributes meaningfully to the overall scale [39, 40].

The suitability of the data for factor analysis was confirmed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. A KMO value of ≥ 0.60 and a significant Bartlett’s test (p < 0.05) indicated that the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor extraction. The EFA was conducted using principal axis factoring (PAF), which extracts factors based on shared variance among items and accounts for measurement error, making it appropriate for identifying latent constructs [41, 42]. Because the scale was theoretically expected to measure a single factor, no rotation was applied [21]. Factor retention was determined based on eigenvalues > 1.0, inspection of the scree plot, and conceptual interpretability of the factor structure. Items with a factor loading of ≥ 0.32 were retained [43, 44].

The CFA was subsequently performed on the second subset of data using maximum-likelihood (ML) estimation to evaluate the model fit of the single-factor structure derived from the EFA. The ML estimation was used for the CFA because it is commonly considered for instruments with five to seven response categories and widely used in nursing research [45]. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices, including Chi-square divided by the degrees of freedom (χ²/df < 3.0), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI > 0.90), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR < 0.08) [46].

Measurement invariance was examined using multi-group CFA in the lavaan package in R. Three levels of invariance were tested sequentially: (1) configural invariance, which examined whether the overall factor structure was the same across groups; (2) metric invariance, which constrained factor loadings to equality across groups; and (3) scalar invariance, which further constrained factor loadings and item intercepts to equality across groups [34]. Model fit was evaluated using the CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. In general, measurement invariance is assessed using ΔCFI, ΔRMSEA, and ΔSRMR across nested models. However, because RMSEA can be sensitive to low degrees of freedom, sample size, and model complexity, invariance decisions in this study were primarily based on ΔCFI and ΔSRMR [47, 48]. Values of ΔCFI ≤ 0.01, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 for metric invariance, and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 for scalar invariance were used as the main reference criteria [47]. Because one response category was empty for one item in one education group, the items were treated as approximately continuous and estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR), which is appropriate for five-point Likert items [49].

Known-groups validity was assessed by comparing the total scores of the C-Scale-I between nurses who reported being familiar with national dengue guidelines and those who were not. Nurses familiar with the guidelines were expected to have higher confidence, as greater familiarity with clinical guidelines has been associated with stronger confidence in performing related tasks [50]. An independent samples t-test was conducted. A statistically significant difference between groups was interpreted as evidence supporting known-groups validity, indicating that the instrument can distinguish between groups theoretically expected to differ in the construct of interest [35]. Concurrent validity was examined by correlating the total score of the C-Scale-I with the C-VAS. A Pearson correlation coefficient of ≥ 0.50, consistent with the criterion used in the original validation study, was considered evidence of acceptable concurrent validity [21].

The internal consistency reliability was examined using Cronbach’s α, with values between 0.70 and 0.95 considered acceptable [51]. In addition to Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s omega (ω) was calculated using the OMEGA macro for SPSS developed by Hayes and Coutts [52] to provide an additional estimate of internal consistency reliability. Values of ≥ 0.70 were considered acceptable [52]. The test-retest reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement and single-measure ICC to evaluate the stability of the scale over time [51, 53]. ICC values > 0.70 were interpreted as indicating acceptable reliability [51, 53]. In addition, the standard error of measurement (SEM) and minimal detectable change (MDC) were calculated to further support the interpretability of the scale scores. SEM estimates the extent of measurement error associated with an observed score, whereas MDC represents the smallest change required to indicate a true change beyond measurement error. SEM was calculated as SD × √(1 − ICC), and MDC was calculated as 1.96 × √2 × SEM [54]. The factor 1.96 reflects the 95% confidence level, and √2 accounts for error from two measurements [54].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Public Health, and Nursing, Universitas Gadjah Mada-Dr. Sardjito General Hospital, Indonesia (Ref. No. KE/FK/0455/EC/2024), and from the Taipei Medical University Joint Institutional Review Board, Taiwan (No. N202405011). The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki as well as relevant international and national ethical standards. All participants were provided with clear information about the study aims, procedures, potential risks and benefits, voluntary participation, compensation, confidentiality, their rights as participants, and contact details of the researchers. Electronic informed consent was obtained prior to participation in the online survey. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. To protect confidentiality, participant identities were anonymized using unique identification codes in all electronic datasets.

Results

Participant characteristics

In total, 458 PHC nurses participated, with 140 included in the EFA and 318 in the CFA (Table 1). The mean age of participants was 40.68 (SD = 10.02) years in the EFA group and 39.69 (SD = 9.27) years in the CFA group. Most participants were female and held a nursing diploma. On average, participants had approximately 14 years of work experience in PHC settings. The majority reported previous experience assessing and caring for dengue patients, and most had received dengue-related education during their formal studies. However, fewer participants had attended dengue-specific training or reported familiarity with national dengue guidelines. The two subsamples were generally comparable across most characteristics. However, the CFA subsample had a higher proportion of nurses with a bachelor’s degree or higher compared with the EFA subsample.

Face and content validity

The pretesting phase among 30 PHC nurses demonstrated that all five items were clear and understandable, with the S-FVI/Ave reaching 1.00. Similarly, the content validity evaluation by eight experts showed perfect agreement regarding the relevance of all items, with both the I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave achieving 1.00.

Item analysis

Corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.75 to 0.82, exceeding the recommended minimum acceptable value of 0.30. All items contributed meaningfully to the overall construct and were retained for subsequent analyses.

Construct validity

The dataset was suitable for the EFA (KMO = 0.88, Bartlett’s test p < 0.001). Since the scale was theoretically designed to measure a single construct, PAF without rotation was conducted. Based on eigenvalues > 1.00, scree plot inspection, and factor loadings ≥ 0.32, the EFA supported a single-factor structure, which explained 74.13% of the total variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.76 to 0.92 (Table 2). The scree plot also showed a clear break after the first factor (Fig. 1).

Measurement invariance

Measurement invariance across education levels was examined between nurses with a Diploma (n = 321) and those with a Bachelor’s degree or higher (n = 137) to determine whether the C-Scale-I performed similarly across these groups. Education level was selected as the grouping variable because the sample included PHC nurses with different educational backgrounds, and its distribution also differed significantly between the EFA and CFA samples. The configural model showed acceptable CFI and SRMR values (CFI = 0.972, TLI = 0.945, RMSEA = 0.138, SRMR = 0.024). The metric model showed CFI = 0.972, TLI = 0.960, RMSEA = 0.117, and SRMR = 0.033. Compared with the configural model, the changes in fit indices were minimal (ΔCFI = 0.000, ΔRMSEA = − 0.021, ΔSRMR = 0.009). The scalar model showed CFI = 0.973, TLI = 0.970, RMSEA = 0.101, and SRMR = 0.035. Compared with the metric model, the changes in fit indices also remained small (ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA = − 0.016, ΔSRMR = 0.002). The fit indices for each model are presented in Table 4.

Known-groups validity

Known-groups validity was examined by comparing C-Scale-I scores between nurses who reported familiarity with national dengue guidelines and those who did not. Nurses familiar with the guidelines had higher C-Scale-I scores (Mean = 20.05, SD = 3.45) than those who were not familiar (Mean = 18.47, SD = 3.52) (t = − 4.73, p < 0.001).

Concurrent validity

The concurrent validity was examined by correlating the total score of the C-Scale-I with the C-VAS. The analysis showed a positive correlation (r = 0.61, p < 0.001).

Reliability

The C-Scale-I showed acceptable internal consistency among 458 nurses, with Cronbach’s α = 0.92 and McDonald’s ω = 0.92. The test-retest reliability, assessed among 30 nurses over a two-week interval, showed a single-measures ICC of 0.74 (95% CI, 0.52–0.86), indicating acceptable stability over time. The SEM was 1.32 and the MDC was 3.66.

Discussion

The current study provides evidence of acceptable reliability and validity of the C-Scale-I for assessing PHC nurses’ confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. During the translation and adaptation process, several wording adjustments were made to contextualize the items for dengue-related prevention practice. The original C-Scale was designed to assess confidence in performing physical assessments among both nursing students and staff nurses [21]. However, experts in the present study identified that the original wording might cause confusion regarding the type of “performance” being evaluated. Therefore, the phrase “dengue infection prevention practice” was added after the term “performance” in each item to contextualize the original C-Scale to dengue-related prevention practice. For example, the original item “I am certain that my performance is correct” was revised to “I am certain that my performance in dengue infection prevention practice is correct.” The term “dengue infection” was used instead of “dengue” to align with Indonesia’s national dengue management guideline, which uses “dengue infection” in the context of prevention and control. Dengue infection prevention practice represents a central component of dengue-related practice performed by PHC nurses in Indonesia, as defined previously in the Background Sects [10, 11]. Therefore, adapting the C-Scale items to this specific practice context contextualizes the original confidence construct within the routine dengue-related roles of PHC nurses. Future applications of the C-Scale-I should be interpreted within this defined scope.

In addition, the term “observer” was clarified to improve the clarity of the item wording for participants. Since nurses’ roles related to dengue are not limited to interactions with patients but also involve providing education to families and communities and collaborating with other healthcare professionals, the term “observer” was expanded to include patients, family members, community members, colleagues, and other healthcare professionals. This modification helped ensure that the items reflected the broader professional interactions involved in dengue-related prevention practice among PHC nurses. Such contextual adaptations have been recommended in previous methodological studies to improve clarity and ensure the instrument reflects the real practice environment of the target population [27, 32]. The final wording of the adapted C-Scale-I items is provided in Table S1.

Results of the face and content validity assessments supported the clarity and relevance of all items. For face validity, PHC nurses rated the clarity of each item using a four-point scale and were also invited to provide written comments or suggestions for improvement. No indications of misunderstanding or suggested wording changes were reported by the participants. As all participants rated the items as “3” or “4,” the I-FVI and S-FVI/Ave were 1.00, indicating that the wording of the items was easy to understand. This may reflect the contextual adaptations made during the translation process, which ensured the item wording was appropriate and familiar to PHC nurses in Indonesia. For content validity, experts agreed that the adapted items were relevant for assessing nurses’ confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. The S-CVI/Ave of 1.00 obtained in this study was consistent with the Taiwanese version of the C-Scale, which achieved the same value when evaluated by seven experts [23]. This high level of agreement may be related to the concise structure of the scale, which consists of only five items with straightforward wording, making the relevance of each item easier for experts to evaluate. Although several experts in the present study suggested minor wording revisions during the review process, they still rated all items as either “3 = quite relevant/requires minor revision” or “4 = highly relevant,” resulting in an I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave of 1.00. The original and adapted versions of the C-Scale have been applied across diverse nursing populations and clinical contexts, including nursing students and practicing nurses performing various procedures such as urinary catheterization, nasogastric-tube feeding, neonatal resuscitation, and simulation-based education [22–24, 26, 55]. Those prior studies demonstrated the scale’s flexibility and applicability beyond its original context of physical assessment.

The corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.75 to 0.82, indicating that each item contributed meaningfully to the overall construct and supporting the retention of all items. Although values approaching 0.80 may be considered a possible indicator of item redundancy, item deletion is primarily recommended for items with low corrected item-total correlations (< 0.30) [39, 40]. Removing items based solely on high correlations risks diluting the conceptual coverage and validity of the scale [56], particularly for a single construct where high inter-item correlations are expected.

The EFA confirmed that all five items measured a single underlying factor, which explained 74.13% of the total variance. The high percentage of explained variance indicates that the items capture a substantial proportion of the variance in the underlying construct. The CFA further verified this one-factor structure with acceptable model fit indices. Although the chi-square test was statistically significant, this index is known to be sensitive to sample size and therefore should be interpreted together with other fit indices [46]. The CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR values indicated that the one-factor model provided an acceptable fit to the data. Although the RMSEA point estimate was within the acceptable range, its 90% confidence interval (0.02 to 0.12) was relatively wide, with a slightly elevated upper bound. This wider interval may reflect the limited degrees of freedom in the present model (df = 5), as smaller df tend to increase sampling error and result in less precise CI estimates [48]. Consequently, the upper bound of the CI may appear higher than expected even when the model fits the data well [48]. In addition, fixed cutoff values for RMSEA and its confidence interval should be applied with caution across studies, as the appropriate threshold can vary based on sample size, degrees of freedom, and model specification [57]. No correlated error terms or model modifications were applied, and the model was retained as originally specified. Each item showed strong factor loadings, indicating that the items were closely related to the underlying construct of confidence. These findings provide evidence supporting the construct validity of the C-Scale-I and indicate that the items reflect a single underlying construct representing nurses’ confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. Nevertheless, models with a small number of items may produce relatively favorable fit indices, which should be interpreted with caution [34].

Since the sample included PHC nurses with different educational backgrounds, testing measurement invariance across education levels was important to ensure the scale performs consistently across these groups. Regarding measurement invariance, although the absolute RMSEA values across all three models exceeded the suggested cutoffs, these results should be interpreted with caution. RMSEA is known to be sensitive to low degrees of freedom, sample size, and model complexity, which may lead to inflated values [47, 48]. Consequently, changes in CFI and SRMR are considered more stable criteria for evaluating measurement invariance [47]. In the present study, ΔCFI and ΔSRMR remained within the recommended thresholds, indicating acceptable scalar invariance. These results suggest that observed differences in scores between the two groups likely reflect true differences in confidence in dengue-related prevention practice rather than measurement bias.

In addition, known-groups validity was supported by the finding that nurses who reported being familiar with national dengue guidelines had significantly higher confidence scores than those who were not familiar with the guidelines. Familiarity with national dengue guidelines may increase nurses’ understanding of the recommended prevention practices, which in turn may enhance their confidence in performing these specific tasks. This finding suggests that the C-Scale-I can distinguish between groups that are theoretically expected to differ in confidence levels [35].

Furthermore, the concurrent validity analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between the C-Scale-I and the C-VAS, indicating that the two instruments assess related aspects of self-perceived confidence. This positive correlation suggests that nurses who rated themselves as more confident on the C-VAS also tended to score higher on the C-Scale-I. This finding is consistent with the original validation study, which also reported significant associations with the C-VAS [21]. While the C-VAS captures a single and general self-perceived confidence, the C-Scale-I provides a more specific evaluation of confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. However, because both instruments were self-reported and administered within the same survey session, the observed correlation may partially reflect common method variance. Future studies may further examine discriminant validity to confirm that the C-Scale-I specifically measures confidence in dengue-related prevention practice rather than other related constructs.

The internal consistency of the C-Scale-I was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.92), which was higher than the original version (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) [21]. Similar levels of reliability were reported in previous studies using the C-Scale among Taiwanese nursing students (Cronbach’s α = 0.91) [23], suggesting that the instrument may perform consistently across different populations. Because the scale measures a single construct and contains only five items, relatively high inter-item correlations may occur, which can contribute to a higher Cronbach’s α value. McDonald’s ω produced the same reliability estimate, supporting the internal consistency of the scale. In addition, the test-retest reliability over a two-week interval indicated acceptable stability. Although the test-retest reliability was evaluated in a subsample of 30 nurses, this sample size aligns with methodological recommendations suggesting that approximately 20–30 participants are generally sufficient for evaluating test-retest reliability of questionnaires [58]. These findings indicate that the instrument demonstrates acceptable internal consistency and stability. The SEM of 1.32 and MDC of 3.66 suggest that a change of at least 3.66 points in the total score may indicate a true change beyond measurement error. This finding provides preliminary support for the potential use of the C-Scale-I in monitoring changes in nurses’ confidence following educational interventions. However, future studies should confirm responsiveness before this interpretation is applied. In addition, as the SEM and MDC were estimated from a relatively small test-retest subsample of 30 PHC nurses, these values should be interpreted with caution.

Implications for nursing and health policy

The C-Scale-I provides a brief instrument that may be used to assess PHC nurses’ perceived confidence in dengue-related prevention practice. Such information may support educational planning by helping educators understand nurses’ confidence levels before participating in training or professional development activities, such as curriculum design, simulation-based training, or continuing education programs, related to dengue infection prevention. Future studies should investigate whether the instrument is sensitive to changes in confidence following educational interventions and examine whether changes in confidence scores are associated with improvements in clinical practice outcomes. At the health system level, information derived from the scale may contribute to research on nurses’ perceived readiness for dengue-related prevention practice. However, further research is required to investigate its use across different healthcare settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It followed a systematic cross-cultural adaptation process based on established methodological guidelines. The relatively large sample size allowed separate analyses for EFA and CFA, supporting the evaluation of the scale’s construct structure. The findings provide evidence of acceptable reliability and validity of the C-Scale-I. In addition, the scale consists of only five items, which makes it practical for use among nurses in busy PHC settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to cross-culturally adapt and validate the C-Scale for use among Indonesian nurses involved in dengue-related prevention practice.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, data were collected through self-reported online questionnaires, which may have introduced response bias. Nurses who were more interested in dengue or more familiar with online platforms might have been more likely to participate. In addition, because the survey was distributed through professional networks and social media platforms, the response rate could not be determined. Second, although participants were recruited from multiple sites across nearly 60% of Indonesia’s provinces, convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of the findings to all PHC nurses in Indonesia. Third, although measurement invariance across education levels was examined, discriminant validity was not assessed in the present study. In addition, because the study used a cross-sectional design, other measurement properties such as responsiveness to educational interventions, predictive validity, and stability across different outbreak periods or seasons could not be evaluated. Future studies should examine these properties to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the scale. Finally, the CVI for the scale was very high (S-CVI/Ave = 1.00). This may be related to the small number of items, as shorter instruments with clearly worded items are more likely to receive high expert ratings. In addition, the same expert panel participated in both the item review process and the content validity evaluation, which may have contributed to the high level of agreement observed. Future studies may consider involving an independent expert panel or using approaches such as Delphi techniques to further evaluate item relevance. Furthermore, as the C-Scale-I was validated specifically among PHC nurses in the context of dengue-related prevention practice in Indonesia, its applicability to other nursing populations, healthcare settings, or countries with different dengue epidemiological contexts should be examined in future studies.

Conclusions

The C-Scale-I showed acceptable validity and reliability for assessing the perceived confidence of Indonesian PHC nurses in dengue-related prevention practice. Its concise five-item format makes the instrument practical for use in PHC settings. The scale may be useful for examining PHC nurses’ perceived confidence in dengue-related prevention practice and for supporting research and educational assessment in this area. Future applications of the C-Scale-I should be interpreted within this defined scope. Further studies are needed to evaluate its performance across different nursing populations and practice settings and its applicability in other infectious disease prevention contexts.
