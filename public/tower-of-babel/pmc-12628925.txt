Epigenome-wide profiling of trisomy 18 specific DNA methylation signatures in first-trimester chorionic villi
Authors: Ju Yeon Kim, Su Ji Yang, Jin Woo Kim, Yong Jun Choi, Hyun Jung Lee, Young Ran Kim, Sang Hee Jung, Ji Hyon Jang, Nari Kim, You Jung Han, Ji Hyae Lim, Hyun Mee Ryu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrisomy 18 (T18, Edwards syndrome) is a lethal chromosomal disorder characterized by multiple congenital anomalies and high perinatal mortality. Although epigenetic alterations have been described in aneuploidy conditions, their causal role in the pathogenesis of T18 remains unclear. This study aimed to characterize genome-wide DNA methylation changes associated with T18 during early development.Material and methodsGenomic DNA was extracted from chorionic villi of five T18 and five euploid fetuses, as well as from normal maternal blood, at 11–13 weeks of gestation. High-resolution methyl-capture sequencing (MC-seq) was performed to profile DNA methylation at approximately 3.2 million CpG sites. Differentially methylated CpG sites (DMCs) and regions (DMRs) were identified. Functional and disease-association enrichment analyses were conducted using multiple bioinformatics tools.ResultsA global trend of DNA hypermethylation was observed in the chorionic villi of T18 fetuses. A total of 6,510 DMCs were identified, including 4,022 hypermethylated and 2,488 hypomethylated CpG sites. Additionally, 301 DMRs were identified, comprising 233 hypermethylated and 68 hypomethylated regions. Notably, chromosome 18, the disease-causing chromosome, contained the highest number of hypermethylated DMRs. Functional enrichment analysis of the 283 genes, including 301 DMRs, revealed significant involvement in biological processes and disease phenotypes relevant to T18, including nervous system development, anatomical structure morphogenesis, and embryonic morphogenesis (adjusted P < 0.001 for all). Among them, 76 DMRs exhibited completely inverse methylation patterns in maternal blood and were identified as potential epigenetic biomarkers for non-invasive prenatal testing of T18.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive MC-seq-based analysis of T18-specific DNA methylation patterns in first-trimester chorionic villi. These findings suggest that DNA methylation changes may represent downstream consequences of chromosomal imbalance in T18 and provide a foundation for future investigations into its pathophysiology and the development of epigenetic biomarkers for early non-invasive detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02018-4.

Background

Trisomy 18 (T18), also known as Edwards syndrome, is a rare but severe chromosomal disorder caused by the presence of an extra copy of chromosome 18. It is one of the most common chromosomal aneuploidies and is associated with severe developmental abnormalities and a broad spectrum of congenital malformations. Its prevalence ranges from 1 in 3,600 to 1 in 10,000 live births and has increased in recent decades due to advancing maternal age [1, 2]. T18 is characterized by severe growth restriction and multiple congenital anomalies involving the neurological, craniofacial, and cardiovascular systems. It is associated with a high rate of fetal loss and stillbirth, and among live births, more than 90% of affected infants die within the first six months, with fewer than 5% surviving beyond one year [2–4]. Despite its well-characterized cytogenetic etiology, the molecular mechanisms underlying the broad phenotypic spectrum of T18 remain poorly understood.

Chromosomal aneuploidies, such as T18, are known to induce widespread alterations in epigenetic regulation, particularly DNA methylation. These epigenetic changes can influence gene expression and may contribute to the developmental defects observed in aneuploidies [5–7]. Therefore, DNA methylation profiling serves as a powerful approach for understanding the downstream effects of chromosomal imbalance and for identifying disease-specific molecular signatures.

The placenta, and particularly first-trimester chorionic villi, serves as a biologically informative tissue that reflects both fetal genomic and epigenome. To date, most studies investigating DNA methylation profiles in aneuploidy have focused on Trisomy 21, the most common autosomal aneuploidy, while studies specifically targeting T18 remain limited. Moreover, previous studies analyzing methylation patterns in first-trimester placental tissues from fetuses with T18, T21, and T13 have predominantly used microarray-based platforms [8]. However, these platforms are limited by low resolution and fixed probe content, which restricts their ability to comprehensively profile methylation at single-CpG resolution. To date, no study has applied high-resolution sequencing-based approaches such as methyl-capture sequencing (MC-seq) to systematically analyze DNA methylation in first-trimester placental tissue from a T18 fetus. MC-seq offers substantial advantages over microarrays, including genome-wide CpG site coverage, quantitative accuracy, base-level resolution, and the ability to identify novel differentially methylated CpG sites (DMCs) and regions (DMRs) across diverse genomic contexts.

Current prenatal screening for T18 includes ultrasonography, maternal serum biomarkers, and invasive cytogenetic testing, all of which have inherent limitations and associated risks. Noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) using cell-free DNA (cfDNA) has transformed screening for common aneuploidies, but still faces practical constraints, including cost, workflow complexity, and the predominance of maternal cfDNA. These challenges motivate the search for placenta-derived, disease-specific methylation signals to inform future cfDNA assay development. However, tissue‑ and disease‑specific epigenetic biomarkers for T18 have not been comprehensively characterized.

In this study, we systematically characterized T18‑specific epigenetic alterations by performing high‑resolution DNA methylation profiling of first‑trimester chorionic villi using MC-seq. We identified and functionally annotated DMR-associated genes to explore their biological significance and potential contribution to T18-associated developmental abnormalities. Furthermore, we prioritized placenta-derived DMRs that display contrasting methylation in maternal peripheral blood as candidates for future cfDNA-based assay development.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and sample processing

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the CHA Bundang Medical Center (CHAMC 2018-12-062-003) and the Cheil General Hospital (CGH-IRB-2013-10). Singleton pregnant women who received antenatal care at the hospital’s Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology between June 2015 and May 2017 were enrolled after providing written informed consent. Chorionic villi samples were collected during the first trimester (11–13 weeks of gestation; Supplementary material: Table S1) and stored in liquid nitrogen until analysis. Gestational age was determined by ultrasonography.

Fetal karyotypes were determined using GTG banding. All T18 cases exhibited a complete extra copy of chromosome 18 (47, XX, +18 or 47, XY, +18), and all control samples had normal karyotypes (46, XX or 46, XY) (Supplementary material: Table S1). Maternal bloods of normal pregnant women were also collected during the first trimester (11–13 weeks of gestation; Supplementary material: Table S1). Genomic DNA was extracted from all samples using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 69504), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

High-throughput methylome profiling using MC-seq

MC-seq was performed on genomic DNA from maternal blood and placental tissues to assess genome-wide CpG methylation levels, following previously described protocols [9]. Library preparation was performed using the Agilent SureSelect Target Enrichment system for Illumina paired-end sequencing (version B.3, June 2015) with 3 μg of genomic DNA per sample. The SureSelect Human Methyl-Seq probe set was used in all cases.

DNA concentration and purity were assessed using PicoGreen and NanoDrop. DNA fragmentation was conducted using adaptive focused acoustic (AFA) technology (Covaris). Fragmented DNA was end-repaired, followed by A-tailing at the 3′ ends and ligation of Agilent adapters. Adapter-ligated products were PCR-amplified, and the final libraries were quantified by qPCR (according to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide) and quality-checked using the TapeStation DNA ScreenTape D1000 (Agilent).

For target capture, 250 ng of each library was hybridized with capture probes using hybridization buffers, blocking mixes, RNase block, and 5 μL of the SureSelect all-exon capture library, according to the Agilent SureSelect Target Enrichment protocol. Hybridization was performed at 65 °C with a heated-lid thermal cycler set at 105 °C for 24 h. Captured DNA was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the Zymo EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit. This was followed by 8 cycles of PCR to enrich adaptor-ligated fragments and an additional 6 cycles to incorporate sample-specific barcodes. The final purified products were again quantified by qPCR and evaluated using the TapeStation DNA ScreenTape D1000 (Agilent). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq™ 2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Data processing and methylation profile calling

Paired-end reads (101 bp) generated from sequencing were assessed for quality using FastQC (version 0.11.5). Before starting analysis, Trimmomatic (version 0.32) was used to remove adapter sequences and bases with base quality lower than 3 from the ends of reads. A sliding window trim method was applied to remove bases within a window size of 4 if the average quality score was below 15. Reads shorter than 36 bp after trimming were discarded to produce high-quality clean data.

The cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC hg19) using BSMAP based on the SOAP (Short Oligo Alignment program). BSMAP (version 2.90, parameter set -n 1 and -r 0) allowing only uniquely mapped reads. Mapped data (SAM file format) were sorted and indexed using SAMtools (version 1.2), and PCR duplicates were removed with Sambamba (version 0.5.9).

Methylation level was called with the methratio.py in BSMAP program [10]. The methylation ratio of every single cytosine located in the Agilent SureSelect target region satisfying higher than 10 CT count was reliable as a methylation call. For the regions covered by both ends of a read pair, only one read is used to call methylation. The resulting coverage profiles were summarized as # of C / effective CT counts for each of the three sequence contexts (CG, CHG, and CHH).

Identification of DMCs and DMRs

Methylation level at each base of CpG was normalized using median scaling. An independent t-test was performed to assess the statistical significance of methylation differences between T18 and control samples. DMCs were defined by the following criteria: absolute delta mean (|Δmean|) ≥ 0.2 (i.e., ≥ 20% difference in methylation), P-value < 0.05, and the false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 [11]. The average paired-end read mapping efficiency across all 15 samples ranged from 79.6 to 82.0%, with a mean of 81.1% (Supplementary material: Table S2). Methylation ratios were further filtered to retain only CpG sites with a minimum read coverage of ≥ 10 × to ensure consistency and comparability between samples. DMCs were then assembled into DMRs based on the following criteria: (1) a minimum length of 10 base pairs; (2) at least three DMCs with consistent methylation directionality (either all hyper- or all hypomethylated) within a region of at least 50 bp; (3) the proportion of DMCs among CpGs in the region exceeded 50% [9, 11–13].

Identification of gene network and ontologies from DMR‑associated gene lists

Functional annotation of DMR-associated genes was performed using Cytoscape (ver.3.10.1) with the ClueGO plugins. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. GO terms with an adjusted P-value (adjP) < 0.05 and at least three associated genes were considered statistically significant [9, 14]. Chromosomal distributions of DMRs were visualized using the Phenogram tool [15]. Disease association analysis was performed using ToppFun (ToppGene Suite), which integrates curated gene-disease annotations from multiple databases, including AllianceGenome, Clinical Variations, DisGeNET, the GWAS Catalog, and OMIM/MedGen. Statistical significance was determined using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control, with an adjP < 0.05. [16] Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using STRING v12.0. The minimum required interaction score was set at 0.700 (considered “high confidence”). Clustering was performed based on an epsilon parameter of 20. The PPI enrichment P-value for the generated network was provided by STRING [17]. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as averages with standard deviations, and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and visualizations related to differentially methylated results were performed using R version 3.3.1 (www.r-project.org) and SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The statistical power was calculated using post hoc analysis with G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), as described in our previous studies [18–20]. Based on the calculated effect size (Cohen’s d > 3.0), the sample size used in this study achieved over 90% power at a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed). This large effect size suggests strong group separation despite the limited sample size.

Result

Overview of MC-seq analysis

All samples were collected from pregnancies between 11 and 13 weeks of gestation (Supplementary material: Table S1). At the time of chorionic villus sampling, there were no significant differences in maternal age or gestational age between the T18 and control groups (P > 0.05 for all; Supplementary material: Table S1). DNA methylation levels at CpG sites in chorionic villus cells from fetuses with T18 and euploid controls, as well as from maternal blood, were quantified using high-resolution methyl-capture sequencing (MC-seq). Each sample yielded more than 300 million reads with high sequencing quality (Q20 and Q30 > 95%) (Supplementary material: Table S2).

On average, approximately 3.1 million CpG sites per sample were analyzed, covering regions within promoters, gene bodies, intergenic regions, and CpG islands (Supplementary material: Table S3). The assayed CpG sites represented approximately 11% of the total 28,217,449 CpG sites in the human genome (hg19) and were distributed across the following genomic features: CpG islands (CGIs; 1,597,050 CpGs), CGI shores (± 2 kb from CGIs; 810,354 CpGs), and other regions (732,656 CpGs). These CpGs were further categorized into intragenic regions (1,559,007 CpGs), including 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs), exons, introns, and 3′ UTRs, and intergenic regions (1,581,053 CpGs), which encompassed promoters (2 kb upstream of transcription start sites; 820,913 CpGs), downstream regions (1.5 kb downstream of transcription termination sites; 43,216 CpGs), and other non-annotated regions.

Based on predefined selection criteria, we identified 233 hypermethylated and 68 hypomethylated DMRs (Fig. 1; Supplementary material: Tables S4 and S5). Hyper- and hypomethylated DMRs were distributed across multiple chromosomes, with hypermethylated DMRs being substantially more numerous in all chromosomes analyzed (Fig. 1). Notably, chromosomes 2, 4, 13, 15, and 18 contained only hypermethylated DMRs (Fig. 1; Supplementary material: Table S5). Among them, chromosome 18 had the highest number of hypermethylated DMRs (Fig. 2; Supplementary material: Table S5). At the gene level, the genes with the highest number of hypermethylated DMRs were KCTD1 on chromosome 18 and TBC1D16 on chromosome 17, while the gene with the highest number of hypomethylated DMRs was TRH on chromosome 3 (Supplementary material: Table S5). In the BP category, the most significantly enriched term was regulation of Wnt signaling pathway (adjP = 0.0005). The largest number of DMR-associated genes (n = 106) was involved in anatomical structure development (adjP = 0.0007). Other enriched pathways directly related to clinical manifestations of T18 included nervous system development, anatomical structure morphogenesis, and embryonic morphogenesis (adjP < 0.01 for all). In the MF category, the most significantly enriched term was cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding (adjP < 0.0001), and the term associated with the largest number of genes (n = 44) was sequence-specific DNA binding (adjP = 0.0007). In the CC category, chromatin was the most significantly enriched term (adjP = 0.0028), and the term with the highest number of associated genes (n = 41) was chromosome (adjP = 0.0078).

Discussion

Although the cytogenetic basis of T18 is well established, the molecular mechanisms underlying its diverse clinical manifestations remain unclear. As T18 is a rare chromosomal disorder, systematic molecular studies for T18 present challenges due to the limited availability of biological samples. Previous studies suggest that the extensive phenotypic variability in T18 may result not only from gene dosage imbalance on chromosome 18 but also from broader disruptions in genome-wide regulatory networks [21]. These disruptions may involve both upstream genes located on chromosome 18 and downstream targets affected by epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation. Therefore, profiling the epigenetic alterations may provide critical insights into the developmental pathophysiology of T18.

In this study, we performed high-resolution MC-seq to systematically characterize genome-wide DNA methylation patterns specific to T18 chorionic villi. Our results demonstrate that placental DNA from T18 fetuses exhibits distinct methylation signatures that may reflect downstream consequences of chromosomal imbalance and contribute to the early pathogenesis of this lethal aneuploidy. We identified 301 DMRs, including 233 hypermethylated and 68 hypomethylated regions. Notably, hypermethylation was predominant across multiple genomic contexts and was most enriched on chromosome 18, the disease-causing chromosome.

Functional enrichment analysis of DMR-associated genes revealed significant involvement in pathways related to nervous system development, morphogenesis of anatomical structures, embryonic development, and transcriptional regulation. Body weight showed the strongest statistical enrichment among associated phenotypes, aligning with hallmark clinical features of T18, including IUGR and low birth weight. Several DMR-associated genes, including NDN, HNF1B, and MECOM, are well-known regulators of fetal growth, metabolic homeostasis, and organogenesis [22–24]. Our results suggest that methylation changes of these genes may interfere with pathways critical for proper fetal development. In addition to body weight, disease association analysis revealed significant enrichment for cardiovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, and hepatoblastoma, all of which are clinically observed in T18 [1]. Congenital heart defects are one of the most frequent anomalies in T18. Hypomethylated MECOM and hypermethylated IRX4, WNT3A, and CCND2, involved in cardiac morphogenesis and vascular development, may contribute to these cardiac anomalies [25–28]. Furthermore, cardiovascular disease-related DMRs highlight the broader involvement of vascular signaling pathways. Atrial fibrillation-associated DMRs were located in the intergenic region of ZFHX3, a gene known to be involved in atrial electrical remodeling and conduction. Over 20% of T18 patients present with tachyarrhythmias or conduction abnormalities [29], supporting the relevance of epigenetic alterations in ZFHX3. Hepatoblastoma-associated DMRs included in MIR34A, potentially reflecting an epigenetic predisposition to abnormal hepatic development and tumorigenesis, consistent with the elevated incidence of hepatoblastoma in children with chromosomal abnormalities [30, 31]. These findings suggest that DNA methylation alterations in T18 may not only disrupt normal development but also increase susceptibility to a spectrum of diseases by dysregulating multiple organ systems.

We also identified KCTD1 (chromosome 18) and TBC1D16 (chromosome 17) as the genes with the greatest number of hypermethylated DMRs, and TRH (chromosome 3) as the gene with the most hypomethylated DMRs. These genes are associated with developmental and endocrine pathways. KCTD1 is involved in craniofacial and ectodermal development [32, 33], TBC1D16 in growth factor signaling and intracellular trafficking [34, 35], and TRH in thyroid hormone regulation [36, 37]. Methylation dysregulation in these genes may contribute to craniofacial anomalies, metabolic dysregulation, and growth restriction observed in T18. Although additional studies are needed to clarify the mechanistic roles of these epigenetic alterations, our results provide a valuable foundation for further research on T18-specific DMRs during early gestation.

Furthermore, we identified a subset of 76 DMRs showing inverse methylation patterns in maternal blood, suggesting their potential as epigenetic biomarkers for NIPT. Clinically, this DMRs could facilitate the development of novel methylation-based NIPT platforms. From a clinical perspective, this DMRs could reduce psychological burden and support personalized prenatal care, especially for pregnancies at high risk of T18 due to advanced maternal age or abnormal ultrasound findings.

Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the results. Moreover, DNA methylation patterns can vary by ethnicity, geographic region, and developmental stage. Therefore, larger-scale studies with ethnically and geographically diverse cohorts are necessary to validate the identified DMRs. Second, although functional relevance was inferred from gene proximity and known biology, direct functional validation (e.g., transcriptomic or proteomic data) was not performed. no direct mechanistic validation (e.g., transcriptomic or proteomic analysis) was conducted. Third, while our study identifies promising T18-specific DMRs in placental tissue and prioritizes candidates that display contrasting methylation patterns in maternal blood, these loci remain to be validated. Future studies using adequately powered and matched sample sets, including T18 plasma and chorionic villi, will be essential to assess their noninvasive biomarker potential and clinical utility in NIPT development. Finally, biological variability due to placental mosaicism, maternal factors, or environmental influences may act as confounding variables. Therefore, future studies should aim to integrate DNA methylation profiles with gene expression and proteomic data to elucidate the molecular consequences of chromosomal imbalance in T18. Cross-validation of candidate DMRs in other fetal tissues or model systems will also be crucial to confirm their roles in disease pathogenesis.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive genome-wide characterization of DNA methylation patterns specific to T18 in first-trimester chorionic villi using high-resolution MC-seq. We identified novel, disease-specific DMRs that distinguish T18 placental tissue from euploid controls, many of which are functionally associated with genes involved in neurodevelopment, cardiac morphogenesis, fetal growth, and endocrine regulation, core features of the T18 phenotype. These findings expand our understanding of the epigenetic landscape of T18 and suggest candidate DMRs with potential for use as NIPT biomarkers. While further validation and mechanistic studies are warranted, our results provide a foundational reference for future research into the epigenomic architecture of fetal aneuploidy.
