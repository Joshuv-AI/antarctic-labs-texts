Enhanced mechanical, thermal, and reprocessing properties of dynamically vulcanized BIIR/TPU blends with carbonized collagen nanoparticles
Authors: Naiqun Yao, Tao Lin, Hanbin Wang, Xiaoyan Pang, Zhiwen Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A new class of green, sustainable and environmentally friendly bromo-isobutylene-isoprene rubber/thermoplastic polyurethane vulcanizates (BIIR/TPU TPVs) with various rubber contents were successfully developed via dynamic vulcanization. The characterization and performance enhancement of carbonized collagen nanoparticles (CCNs) with an average diameter of 0.73 ± 0.24 µm dispersed in BIIR/TPU-70 TPV have been further investigated. The results showed that CCNs in the CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV lead to improved properties due to the good filler dispersion and the improved interfacial compatibility between BIIR and TPU. Compared to pure BIIR/TPU-70 TPV, the mechanical properties showed a significant increase in tensile strength of 3.4 MPa. The reprocessability of the TPVs was also improved, with only a minor reduction in elongation at break after two reprocessing cycles. These results demonstrate that the incorporation of CCNs can effectively enhance the mechanical properties, thermal stability, stiffness and reprocessability of BIIR/TPU TPVs, making them promising materials for applications in automotive interior materials, medical stoppers and footwear materials.

Introduction

Driven by global energy conservation, emission reduction and sustainable development strategies, thermoplastic vulcanizates (TPVs) have emerged as high-performance green thermoplastic elastomers, which integrate the superior elasticity of crosslinked rubber with the outstanding recyclability and processability of thermoplastics.1,2 Compared with traditional chemically cross-linked rubber products, TPVs can be repeatedly thermoformed and recycled, which meets the growing demands of automotive, sports equipment and other industrial fields for eco-friendly and high-performance elastomers. Bromo-isobutylene-isoprene rubber/thermoplastic polyurethane (BIIR/TPU) TPVs fabricated via dynamic vulcanization (DV) combine the unique damping performance of BIIR and the outstanding mechanical strength of TPU, exhibiting tremendous application potential in high-end elastomer composites.

The comprehensive performance of BIIR/TPU TPVs is fundamentally determined by the phase morphology, the content and cross-linking degree of the rubber phase, interfacial compatibility and filler modification effect.3 It is widely acknowledged that a high rubber phase content (≥50 wt%) and complete phase inversion structure are crucial for constructing high-elasticity TPVs, enabling the cross-linked rubber phase to form a continuous and efficient elastic network in the plastic matrix.4 Nevertheless, BIIR and TPU are thermodynamically incompatible.5–7 Excessively high BIIR loading further aggravates interfacial defects and phase separation, resulting in weakened interfacial interaction, deteriorated thermal stability and degraded mechanical and elastic properties of high-rubber-content TPVs. Although compatibilizers can optimize phase morphology via molecular structure design, they are insufficient to eliminate the inherent performance limitations caused by high rubber phase proportion, restricting the development of high-performance BIIR/TPU TPVs.8–10

Filler modification is the most efficient and feasible strategy to synchronously optimize the interfacial structure, mechanical properties and thermal stability of TPVs. Conventional reinforcing fillers, including carbon black, nano-silica and clay, have been widely adopted to regulate the microstructure and performance of TPVs.11–15 However, traditional fillers suffer from irreparable defects: their production and application processes cause potential environmental risks, and the lack of surface reactive functional groups leads to poor dispersion in polymer matrix and weak interfacial bonding.16 In view of the above deficiencies, eco-friendly biomass-derived fillers have attracted growing research interest.

Chrome leather wastes are typical industrial hazardous solid wastes with huge annual output,17–19 and traditional disposal methods such as landfill and incineration will cause severe environmental pollution and secondary ecological damage.20–24 Notably, leather waste contains multi-scale collagen fibers with excellent mechanical properties and abundant hydroxyl, amino and carboxyl functional groups, which endow it with unique advantages as a biomass reinforcing filler for rubber composites.25,26 Nevertheless, residual chromium impurities and poor thermal stability of raw collagen severely limit its direct large-scale application in polymer modification.

In this study, novel carbonized collagen nanoparticles (CCNs) with rich surface active groups were fabricated via calcining collagen after removing chromium at high temperatures. After investigating the effect of the rubber contents on the properties of BIIR/TPU TPVs which were fabricated by initial blending and dynamic vulcanization, CCNs were blended with BIIR/TPU TPVs to prepare CCNs-BIIR/TPU TPVs. The results indicated that CCNs can effectively enhance the elasticity, mechanical properties and thermal properties of the CCNs-BIIR/TPU TPVs. The improvements could be attributed to the strong polymer–filler interaction. The present study provides valuable insights into the design of filler-reinforced elastomer composites, which contributes to the eco-friendly recycling of leather wastes and functional elastomer materials.

Experimental

Materials

Commercially TPU (Trademark: WHT-6430) was fabricated by Wanhua Chemical Group Co., Ltd. Liquid butyl rubber (Trademark: GM-1) was supplied by Shanghai Yuanxiang Industrial Co., Ltd; BIIR (Trademark: 2222) was manufactured by Exxon Mobil Corporation. Leather wastes were obtained from Xingye Leather Technology Co., Ltd (Jinjiang, China). Tns-(2.4-di-tert-butyl)-phosphite (antioxidant 168) was used as an antioxidant and supplied by Rianlon Corporation. TPU-g-MAH was used as an compatibilizer and obtained from Huixin Plastic Chemical Co., Ltd. Zinc oxide was supplied by Shanghai Jinhua Chemical Factory Co., Ltd. Stearic acid was obtained from PT. Dua Kuda Indonesia. Zinc stearate was obtained from Guoxing Chemical Co., Ltd. Phenolic resin (Trademark: SP1045) was manufactured by SI Group and stannous chloride were used as crosslinking agents and supplied by Tianjin Damao chemicals reagent factory.

Preparation of carbonized collagen nanoparticles (CCNs)

The chromium-containing leather scraps (20.0 g) were dispersed in a 1 wt% potassium hydroxide solution (400 ml) and underwent hydrolysis at 90 °C for 2 h. After freeze-drying, the samples were placed in a tubular furnace and carbonized at 600 °C for 2 h (N2 flow). The samples were then washed thoroughly with a large amount of distilled water until the pH of the filtrate reached neutrality. After drying, carbonized collagen nanoparticles were obtained.

Preparation of epoxidized liquid butyl rubber (EIIR)

50 g liquid butyl rubber xylene solution with a mass fraction of 20 wt%, followed by dropping the mixed solution of H2O2 and HCOOH (the molar ratio of the double bond in liquid butyl rubber : H2O2 : HCOOH is 1 : 1 : 1) and reaction at 40 °C for 5 h. Finally, EIIR was obtained by removing the solvent.

Preparation of dynamically vulcanized BIIR/TPU blends

BIIR/TPU TPVs with various rubber contents were prepared in a Hapro mixer (RM-200C, Harbin Hapro Electrical Technology Co. Ltd). BIIR, zinc oxide, stearic acid, zinc stearate, phenolic resin, stannous chloride were first mixed using an open mill. Then, TPU granules with antioxidant 168 were first added into the mixing chamber and shear-melted completely followed by incorporating of EIIR and TPU-g-MAH as compatibilizers, and as-above premixed blends were subsequently added until reaching the final stable torque. The dynamically vulcanized BIIR/TPU blends were obtained and the final products with various rubber contents were named as BIIR/TPU-70, BIIR/TPU-60 and BIIR/TPU-50 TPVs. For comparison, the dynamically vulcanized BIIR/TPU-70 blend with addition of 3 wt% CCNs were obtained and the final product was named as CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70. The compositions of BIIR/TPU-70, BIIR/TPU-60, BIIR/TPU-50 TPVs and CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 are shown in Table 1.

Characterization

The CCNs were characterized using a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (BRUKER Tensor 27, DE), and the spectrum was obtained in transmission mode between 4000 and 400 cm−1. The X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a K-ALPHA X-ray Photoelectron Spectrometer (ThermoFischer ESCALAB 250Xi, USA) equipped with Al Kα X-ray radiation (hv = 1486.6 eV). The laser Raman spectrometer (Horiba Scientific LabRAM HR Evolution, France) was used to characterize the structure, graphitization degree, and defects of samples with the following parameters: 532 nm excitation wavelength, 2 µm diameter laser spot, 1.5 mW power, 30 s integration time, and a wavenumber range of 50–3500 cm−1. An Apreo 2C instrument (Thermo Scientific Apreo 2C, USA) was performed to take the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of CCNs and BIIR/TPU TPVs. The BIIR/TPU TPVs samples were fractured and broken into two parts after quenching in the liquid nitrogen. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was carried out on DMA Q850 (TA Instruments, USA) at N2 atmosphere. The samples were tested at the frequency of 10 Hz with the heating rate of 5 °C min−1. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the TPVs was measured on a TGA analyser (STA8122, Japan) at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from 30 °C to 800 °C under N2 atmosphere with the flow rate of 120 mL min−1. Tensile tests were conducted at 25 °C on dumbbell-shaped specimens of 4 mm width, 75 mm gauge length, and 1 mm thickness using a tensile testing machine (Instron 3365, USA) with a speed of 500 mm min−1. Five specimens were used to calculate an average test value. Strain recovery tests with the extension of strain to 50% were tested. The residual strain is defined as the permanent set and the hysteresis loss at 50% deformation was calculated by subtracting the area under the force–retraction curve from the area under the force–deformation curve. For recycling experiments, the samples were cut into small pieces and hot-pressed under 10 MPa for 5 min at 180 °C, and cut into dumbbell-shaped specimens for tensile tests.

Raman spectroscopy was further employed to investigate the structural characteristics of CCNs. As illustrated in the spectral profile (Fig. 2), the CCNs display two distinct vibrational modes at 1358 cm−1 and 1591 cm−1, corresponding to the D-band (disorder-induced vibrational mode) and G-band (in-plane stretching vibration of sp2-hybridized carbon domains), respectively. The relative intensity ratio of the D-band (ID) exhibits a positive correlation with structural defect density, while the observed upshift of the G-band position (compared to the 1580 cm−1 reference peak for single-crystalline graphite) indicates lattice distortion within the carbon framework. Notably, nitrogen doping induces significant spectral broadening of both D- and G-bands, which can be attributed to localized electronic structure perturbation and symmetry breaking in the lattice structure caused by heteroatom incorporation. Quantitative analysis reveals an ID/IG intensity ratio of 1.21, demonstrating that nitrogen doping effectively reduces the overall graphitization degree through the introduction of sp3-hybridized defect sites and topological distortions.

Dynamically vulcanized BIIR/TPU blends

The torque and temperature as a function of mixing time during dynamic vulcanization of the BIIR/TPU-70 TPV is presented in Fig. 3. The torque initially increased with mixing time due to the increasing shear rate, and subsequently decreased because of the melting of TPU granules. After reaching the lowest point at about 266 s, the torque raised sharply and reach the peak due to the addition of rubber premix and the occurrence of the rapid cross-linking of rubber phase induced by the vulcanizing agent. Finally, the torque slowly declined with the completion of phase inversion. During this stage, the cross-linked BIIR phase was broken into small particles and evenly uniformly dispersed within the TPU phase. The temperature curve exhibited a continuous decline with the addition of the cold contents into the hot mixer and the blends began to melt. Subsequently, the temperature rose steadily, eventually exceeding the set temperature due to crosslinking of rubber phase and shear friction. The exhibited behavior of the dynamic vulcanization curves of above BIIR/TPU blends accorded with typical DV pattern.1

Dynamic vulcanization curves of the BIIR/TPU-70 TPV.

The morphology of TPVs affected by the complicated relationship among processing conditions, composition, crosslinking reaction, viscosity and elasticity ratios of the two components and the compatibility between plastic and rubber. If the rubber and plastic components were incompatible, as-prepared TPVs would display a rough phase structure, weak interaction at the rubber–plastic interface, and thus poor properties. Fig. 4 displays SEM images of cryo-fractured surfaces of BIIR/TPU TPVs and CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV. As shown in Fig. 4(a) and (b), the surface of the BIIR/TPU TPVs with higher TPU loading exhibited more ridges like wrinkles over the boundary. The images exhibited no remarkable voids on the fracture surfaces of the BIIR/TPU TPVs and CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV. It's worth noting that some black particles corresponding to CCNs are clearly seen in Fig. 4(d), indicating that CCNs are well dispersed in the CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV matrix. The results suggest a good compatibility and a strong interfacial interaction between BIIR and TPU phases with using EIIR and TPU-g-MAH as compatibilizers.

An investigation on the BIIR/TPU TPVs properties including elasticity, stiffness, mechanical characteristics, hardness, thermal properties and reprocessability were further conducted, and the results are presented in the Fig. 5–8 and Table 1. The elasticity of BIIR/TPU TPVs dominated by the high content and high cross-linking of the rubber phase. Tensile recovery tests were conducted on BIIR/TPU TPVs to evaluate the elasticity. Hysteresis loss and permanent set according to the tensile recovery curves are commonly employed to assess the elasticity of TPVs. It can be seen from Fig. 5 that both the permanent deformations and hysteresis losses of the BIIR/TPU TPVs increased as the BIIR content increased attributed to the larger BIIR domains dispersed in TPU matrix, which hindered sufficient recovery force after high deformation. It is worth noting that the CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV exhibited the lowest hysteresis losses and permanent deformations compared to the other BIIR/TPU TPVs meaning the outstanding elasticity. This results were attributed to the increased crosslinking density and the enhanced network structure.

The TGA and DTG curves of BIIR/TPU TPVs are shown in Fig. 6. The TGA curves of BIIR/TPU TPVs with various rubber contents and CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV varies little before 350 °C and the degradation starts at 230 °C. The obvious distinction of TGA curve is at 300–500 °C. Combining with DTG curves (Fig. 6(b)), two degradation processes of BIIR/TPU TPVs and CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV referred to BIIR and TPU phase.32,33 The degradation temperature increased to higher temperatures with the reduction of rubber contents. Compared with BIIR/TPU-70 TPV, the remarkable change is the temperature of CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV degradation, which is raised from 370 °C to 380 °C for the TPV with 3 wt% CCNs as shown in Table S1. The introduction of CCNs improves the thermal stability of BIIR/TPU TPV.

The storage modulus of the TPVs, which is directly related to the material's stiffness, shows a significant temperature dependence. Fig. 7(a) exhibits a decreasing trend of storage modulus as the temperature increases. The storage modulus of BIIR/TPU TPVs with higher rubber contents increases due to the enhanced physical crosslinking between rubber molecular chains. These crosslinking points restrict chain mobility, leading to the TPV with greater rigidity under dynamic stress and thus an elevated storage modulus. Compared to the BIIR/TPU-70 TPV without adding CCNs, the storage modulus of CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV in the temperature of −100 °C and −50 °C was significantly higher, which was attributed that CCNs acted as a rigid filler can effectively reinforce the TPVs, resulting in the enhanced stiffness. Fig. 7(b) exhibits the variation of the loss factor and the distinct peak corresponds to the glass transition temperature (Tg) of IIR phase. It could be seen that higher rubber content can cause the Tg shifting to higher temperature, and the mobility of the rubber molecular chains becomes restricted when the test temperature approaches the Tg of the rubber, leading to improve the rigidity of the material. The changes in Tg of BIIR/TPU TPVs further corroborate the aforementioned trend in the variation of the modulus.

The mechanical properties of BIIR/TPU TPVs with various BIIR contents and the addition of CCNs are depicted in Fig. 8(a) and Table 2. The BIIR/TPU TPVs with various BIIR contents exhibited impressive mechanical properties due to the strong interfacial adhesion between the BIIR and TPU phases with the contribution of compatibilizer, which enables effective stress transfer from the rigid plastic phase to the soft rubber phase. The tensile strength of the BIIR/TPU TPVs enhanced from 3.2 MPa to 4.1 MPa and the elongation at break declined from 726% to 460% with the reduction of BIIR content, respectively. BIIR/TPU-70 TPVs with the highest rubber content exhibited relatively inferior tensile strength may attributed to the increased agglomeration of crosslinked BIIR. In addition, CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV with a improved tensile strength of 3.4 MPa compared with BIIR/TPU-70 TPV. The improved mechanical properties can be ascribed to the good filler dispersion and favorable interaction between filler and rubber. Further, the hardness of the BIIR/TPU TPVs was also investigated and the results were listed in Table 1. With the content of BIIR range from 50 wt% to 70 wt%, the hardness reduced gradually, shifted from 78A to 65A with the addition of 70 wt% BIIR. Upon adding the CCNs nanoparticles into BIIR/TPU-70 TPV, the hardness of the TPV increases instead because that CCNs as a nanoparticle reinforcing agent well-dispersed within the matrix, effectively enhanced the hardness of the BIIR/TPU TPV.

Reprocessability as a key advantage of TPVs due to the growing emphasis on environmental protection and resource saving. To illustrate this point, the reprocessability of CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV has been studied and the corresponding mechanical performance results are presented in Fig. 8(b and c). Notably, the tensile stress increased slightly and the elongation at break of CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 showed slight decreases after recycling for 2 times which probably attributed to additional crosslinking and the thermal degradation of TPU during extended high-temperature processing. It indicated that CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPVs exhibited relatively good reprocessability without a significant loss of mechanical properties.

Conclusions

A novel bromo-isobutylene-isoprene rubber/thermoplastic polyurethane vulcanizates (BIIR/TPU TPVs) with various rubber contents were successfully developed via dynamic vulcanization. Further, carbonized collagen nanoparticles (CCNs) were successfully incorporated into BIIR/TPU-70 blends with good dispersion as confrimed by SEM. The mechanical properties including tensile strength and elasticity of the CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV show enhanced with tensile strength of 3.4 MPa and reduced hysteresis loss and permanent deformation. Moreover, thermal stability of CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV was also enhanced. Furthermore, the reprocessability of the CCNs-BIIR/TPU-70 TPV remained satisfactory with only a minor decrease in elongation at break after two recycling cycles, which offers potential for a wide range of industrial applications.
