Increasing paced heart rate reduces cerebral pulsatility
Authors: A J S Webb, J Thomas, O Llwyd, N Herring
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral pulsatility is a key index of cerebral haemodynamics, a central determinant of intravascular shear stress within intracranial vessels, and is associated with cerebral small vessel disease. It increases with a spontaneously falling heart rate, but the effect of altering heart rates on cerebral haemodynamics is unknown. We therefore tested whether increasing paced heart rate would reduce middle cerebral artery Gosling's pulsatility index (PI)on transcranial ultrasound, during emergency or elective implantation of a pacemaker for bradycardia. In 20 participants (14 males, mean age 78), paced heart rate was increased from 50 bpm or the individual's resting heart rate up to 100 bpm in 10 bpm increments. Cerebral pulsatility was strongly and inversely associated with heart rate, with an average reduction in PI of 0.07 per 10 beats per minute in unadjusted analyses, and 0.08 in mixed effects linear models adjusted for age, sex and cardiovascular risk factors (P < 0.001). Increasing heart rate reduced peak systolic velocity by 2.51 cm/s per 10 bpm and increased end‐diastolic velocity by 1.26 cm/s per 10 bpm, which both mediated the effect on cerebral pulsatility. However, there was no effect on mean velocity. The decrease in peak systolic middle cerebral artery velocity was linear with increasing heart rate, but end‐diastolic velocity was maximal at a heart rate of 80 bpm. Overall, cerebral pulsatility was inversely and causally associated with paced heart rate. Heart rate modification could be a potential intervention to modify cerebral pulsatility, particularly in the context of bradycardia induced by heart block.Keywords: hypertension, pacemaker, pulsatility, stroke

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral pulsatility increases with age and is tightly associated with aortic stiffness and aortic pulsatility (Webb et al., 2020, 2021) reflecting increased transmission of the aortic waveform to the brain, modified by dampening of the waveform at points of tortuosity and impedance and by the distal resistance of smaller cerebral vessels. It is highly reproducible, progresses over the age of 65 and independently predicts the risk of recurrent cerebrovascular events. However, methods to modify it independently of mean blood pressure are not clear. Cerebral pulsatility is also associated with the severity of white matter hyperintensities (Webb et al., 2012), acute lacunar infarcts, cerebral microbleeds and dilated perivascular spaces. In the ECLIPSE study a phosphodiesterase 3 inhibitor (cilostazol) (Han et al., 2013) reduced cerebral pulsatility, potentially explaining beneficial effects of cilostazol in clinical trials beyond its known antiplatelet activity (Webb, 2019). An increased pulse pressure is particularly seen in acute, severe bradycardia due to heart block, and this is therefore likely to translate into increased cerebral pulsatility.

Beat‐to‐beat cerebral pulsatility was strongly associated with reduced, concurrent heart rate, especially in older people (Webb et al., 2022). This is consistent with less reduction in diastolic flow due to a reduced length of cardiac cycle with increased heart rate, but is potentially confounded by concurrent, spontaneous changes in blood pressure. High pulsatility is therefore likely in individuals with significant bradycardia, and thus may be reduced by appropriately increasing heart rate with a pacemaker. However, the optimal heart rate to optimize cerebral haemodynamics is unclear (bhuva, 2025). However, there are very limited studies measuring the cerebral haemodynamic effects of pacemaker implantation, the effect of different pacemaker settings on cerebrovascular function, and no studies of the long‐term effects of pacemakers on vascular cognitive decline. Therefore, we tested whether increasing paced heart rate results in a reduction in cerebral pulsatility in patients undergoing acute or subacute pacemaker implantation for bradycardia.

METHODS

This study was sponsored by the University of Oxford and approved by the UK Health Research Authority and by the London Brent Research Ethics Committee (23/PR/0090). All participants provided informed consent and the research was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Participants were recruited from the John Radcliffe Hospital, Oxford between June 2023 and June 2024, prior to implantation of a pacemaker for symptomatic bradycardia. Eligible participants had evidence of bradycardia when symptomatic (<60 beats/min (bpm)) and a resting heart rate of <75 bpm when recruited, with at least one temporal bone window allowing transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD). Participants were recruited either during an acute admission for emergent pacemaker implantation for sustained bradycardia (e.g., complete heart block) or during a planned admission for intermittent symptomatic bradycardia, such as sick sinus syndrome or Mobitz Type 2 block with syncopal events or prolonged pauses on cardiac monitoring according to European Society of Cardiology guidelines (Glikson et al., 2021). People were excluded if they had recent unstable angina or myocardial infarction (within 6 months); a reduced ejection fraction (<50%); current, untreated, severe valvular heart disease; previous ventricular fibrillation or tachycardia; severe carotid stenosis (>70%); or a predisposition to intracerebral haemorrhage. Initially, the target population was 32 participants, but this was reduced to 20 participants at a pre‐planned interim analysis.

Experimental design

To determine the relationship between paced heart rate and cerebral pulsatility in de novo pacemakers, participants were tested in the cardiac catheter laboratory during pacemaker implantation, once the pacemaker was successfully implanted, usually prior to skin closure. All pacemakers were inserted under local anaesthetic without sedation. End‐tidal CO2 was recorded via nasal cannulae, and thus was dependent upon spontaneous ventilation.

Participants were consented at least 1 h prior to implantation and underwent a standardised interview to determine clinical and demographic characteristics. Arterial stiffness by carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity and aortic blood pressure were determined by applanation tonometry (Syphmocor, AtCor Medical, Naperville, IL, USA). During pacemaker implantation, physiological monitoring was established including continuous middle cerebral artery blood velocity (Doppler Box, 2 MHz probe, Diamon monitoring set, DWL Compumedics, Singen, Germany), single‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG), non‐invasive continuous, beat‐to‐beat blood pressure (Finometer Nova, FMS, Amsterdam, Netherlands) and end‐tidal carbon dioxide via nasal cannulae. Insonation of the middle cerebral artery was performed on the right side via the temporal bone window, targeting a depth of 50–55 mm, distal to an identified bifurcation consistent with division of the internal carotid artery into the middle and anterior cerebral arteries. Where non‐invasive blood pressure was unreliable, repeated brachial blood pressures were recorded. Once pacing was established, most commonly ‘DDD’ pacing with an atrio‐ventricular delay of 120 ms, then participants were monitored at their spontaneous heart rate or 50 bpm. The paced heart rate was then increased by 10 bpm increments for 40–60 s, up to a maximum heart rate of 100 bpm (e.g., 50, 60, 70, 80, 90 and 100 bpm), and then in 10 bpm decrements for the same duration. This was usually performed during skin closure, or immediately afterwards.

Analysis

Cerebral blood velocity, heart rate and blood pressure waveforms were extracted and cleaned by a combination of automated and manual processes, as described previously (Thomas, 2023; Webb et al., 2021), to derive trough, mean and peak values for each heart beat. Briefly, ectopic heart beats were automatically detected by excessively short inter‐beat intervals (≤3 SD), with linear interpolation of blood pressure and blood flow velocities for beats preceding and following the ectopic. Waveforms were visually reviewed, with linear interpolation of errors, whilst marked outliers were excluded from further analysis. The primary outcome was middle cerebral artery (MCA) pulsatility index (MCA‐PI, Gosling's pulsatility index). Secondary outcome measures included diastolic, mean and systolic blood pressure (DBP, MBP and SBP); and end‐diastolic, mean and peak systolic MCA velocities (EDV, MV and PSV).

The primary, prespecified analysis was determined as the relationship between heart rate and MCA‐PI, and with each secondary outcome, by a linear regression across all beats for each individual, then averaged between individuals. For a secondary, prespecified confirmatory analysis, mean heart rate, pulsatility and cerebral blood velocity were determined for the best 10 beats during each fixed heart rate during the heart rate escalation/de‐escalation paradigm. These beats were determined by visual inspection in each monitoring period as the latest consecutive 10 beats in the monitoring period including no artefacts in the TCD and blood pressure waveforms. Where two periods of monitoring were available at each heart rate, the resulting cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) and blood pressure (BP) values were averaged across the two periods. Associations between mean values during each monitoring period were again determined for each participant by linear regression. Thirdly, the population average during each heart rate was determined for each heart rate period, both as the average absolute value and as the proportional change to the same value for each individual at 80 bpm.

The final prespecified confirmatory analysis was carried out as mixed effect linear models with an autoregressive, autocorrelation design to allow for repeated measures within each individual, with individual participants as random effects, and heart rate as fixed effects. This was performed for both approaches to modelling the association between heart rate and each outcome, with the primary analysis using all beats individually, and secondarily using the best 10 beats in each monitoring period. This was performed to determine the contribution of heart rate to each outcome measure across the whole population, unadjusted and adjusted for clinical features (age, sex, aortic stiffness, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, aortic stiffness, etc.) to adjust for potential confounding of the relationship inducing differences between heart rate and cerebral pulsatility, particularly with age. Finally, mediation analyses were performed for the mixed‐effects models as a post hoc analysis at an individual and group level to determine whether changes in systolic or diastolic cerebral blood flow velocity had a dominant effect in mediating the change in cerebral blood velocities and cerebral pulsatility, and whether this pattern was consistent for changes in blood pressure. These mediation analyses were performed for mixed effects models with individual heart beats as fixed effects, or grouped by the estimated average during the best 10 beats during each monitoring period. All mediation analyses for competing mediators (PSV and EDV or SBP and DBP) were performed within a Structural Equation Modelling framework to define each pathway of effect (direct or via each of the two possible mediators) with the R lavaan package.

All analyses were performed combining heart rates from the period of increasing heart rate and decreasing heart rate ramp, both for individual beats and when averaged across the best 10 beats (providing two estimates for each heart rate period in each linear regression or mixed effects model).

All analyses were performed in MATLAB R2024a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using in‐house software, or in RStudio with the packages tidyverse, psych, rms, nlme, lme4, readxl, lavaan.

RESULTS

The effect of heart rate on MCA‐PI reflected a significant decrease in PSV in individual tests and on average (Table 3). Although there was not a significant effect on EDV when averaging individual relationships, due to inter‐individual variability (Figure 1), there was a significant increase in EDV when analysed with a mixed effect linear model, including after adjustment. However, this relationship was less linear, with an increase in EDV with HR below 80 bpm, but no increase, or a decrease, above this heart rate. Overall, there was a very small reduction in mean cerebral blood velocity of less than 1 cm/s per 10 bpm.

The effect of heart rate on cerebral blood velocity parameters matched the effect on blood pressure indices, with a marked reduction in pulse pressure in particular (Figure A4, Table 3), reflecting a marked reduction in systolic blood pressure (8 mmHg per 10 bpm) and increase in diastolic blood pressure, including when averaging individual results or when estimated by mixed effect models. There was an increase in diastolic blood pressure in mixed effect linear models, but there was no overall significant effect on the mean blood pressure with either analysis.

The relationships between heart rate and either cerebral pulsatility, or cerebral blood velocity or blood pressure parameters were consistent across demographic groups and cardiovascular risk factors (Table 4), although the study is underpowered to assess this. There was a weak trend to a more negative relationship between heart rate and cerebral pulsatility at older ages, and a significantly greater increase in EDV with increasing heart rate at older ages. A higher baseline absolute cerebral blood velocity also predicted a more negative relationship between heart rate and PSV and less of an increase in EDV but was not associated with a difference in the relationship with cerebral pulsatility (Table 4). There was no association between baseline clinical characteristics and blood pressure indices (Table A2), and the effect of age or absolute cerebral blood velocity on the association between heart rate and PSV or EDV was unaffected by adjustment for age, sex or baseline blood pressure (Table A3).

As expected, the effect of heart rate on cerebral pulsatility was almost entirely mediated by the effect of heart rate on peak systolic and end‐diastolic velocity, as PI is calculated from these two indices (total direct effect HR to PI = −0.01 per 10 bpm; total indirect effect = −0.07 per 10 bpm). However, there was a slightly greater effect via systolic than diastolic blood pressure when modelled together (HR–SBP–PI = −0.04; HR–DBP–PI = −0.03), with very similar results when using all heart beats individually or using the average values during each specified heart rate. Effects of heart rate on blood pressure indices mediated some of the effect on cerebral pulsatility (indirect effect = −0.04 per 10 bpm, P = 0.002; direct = −0.03, P = 0.004), with more effect of DBP than SBP (HR–DBP–PI = −0.03, P = 0.03; HR–SBP–PI = −0.02, P = 0.15).

The effects of heart rate on systolic versus diastolic cerebral blood velocity, and thus cerebral pulsatility, are also evident with assessment of the average pulse waveform in the brain at each specified heart rate (Figure 2). With a sequential increase in heart rate, there is a systematic reduction in the systolic peak across the range of heart rates tested, and particularly with a reduction in the augmentation of the pressure waveform and the anacrotic notch. In contrast, end‐diastolic velocity increases between 50 and 80 bpm, but above this level, there is no further increase in EDV, and a potential fall as heart rate increases above 80 bpm (Figures 1 and 2).

DISCUSSION

In a cohort of 20 patients requiring implantation of a pacemaker, there was a strong association between increased paced heart rate and a significant reduction in cerebral pulsatility, with an average 0.08 reduction in PI for every 10 bpm increase in heart rate. This reflected a systematic reduction in peak systolic blood velocities due to reduced augmentation, and a rise in end‐diastolic flow up to 80 bpm. However, there was very limited effect on mean cerebral blood velocity. There were consistent effects on blood pressure, with a reduction in pulse pressure and systolic pressure and an increase in diastolic pressure up to 80 bpm but limited effect upon mean blood pressures. Although effects of heart rate on cerebral blood flow are dependent on effects on aortic blood flow, the measured effects on peripheral blood pressure only partially mediated the relationship between heart rate and cerebral blood velocity.

The relationship between heart rate and cerebral pulsatility is driven by both a reduction in systolic blood flow and an increase in diastolic flow, with effects of heart rate affecting aortic blood flow which is transmitted to the brain as the principal determinant of cerebral blood flow (Webb et al., 2012). As heart rate rises, the R–R interval is reduced and the period for diastolic pressure to fall is reduced. This is enhanced in older patients with increasing aortic stiffness (Webb et al., 2012) due both to more rapid transmission of the reverse travelling wave from the periphery, and a diminished Windkessel effect in the aorta as the elasticity of the aorta is reduced. However, the mediation of the relationship between heart rate and pulsatility by systolic and diastolic blood flow velocities confirms a slightly greater effect of systolic blood flow compared to diastolic. Although pulsatility is inevitably mediated by PSV and EDV as it is calculated through these indices, this analysis confirms that the influence of heart rate is not only mediated by this mechanism, but also by secondary effects on systolic blood flow.

The limited mediation of the relationship between blood pressure and cerebral pulsatility may reflect the greater effect of heart rate on the aortic blood flow and cerebral circulation rather than on peripheral blood pressure. At the same time, there is reduced time for atrial filling and a reduced atrial ‘kick’, thus limiting the maximum systolic output and the apparent augmentation of the cardiac waveform. Once heart rate rises above 80 bpm, diastolic flow no longer rises but starts to reduce in parallel to the systolic flow, likely due to reduced filling time and an overall reduction in stroke volume.

Cerebral pulsatility is a not only a key index of cerebrovascular function, but may also have direct detrimental effects on cerebrovascular disease, and thus could reflect an increased cerebrovascular risk in individuals requiring a pacemaker due to heart block induced bradycardia. A previous study demonstrated that concurrent, beat‐to‐beat cerebral pulsatility was inversely associated with concurrent heart rate, with a stronger effect in older patients with established small vessel disease on brain imaging (Webb et al., 2022). Cerebral pulsatility was increased by treatment with a rate‐reducing beta‐blocker in 10 healthy volunteers compared to amlodipine (Webb & Rothwell, 2016) whilst cilostazol, which increases heart rate by PDE3 inhibition in the heart, modestly reduced cerebral pulsatility by 0.04 in the ECLIPSE trial of patients with a lacunar stroke (Han et al., 2013). The potential for cerebrovascular harm in block‐induced bradycardia could therefore reflect an increased risk of cerebrovascular injury and cognitive decline. Cerebral small vessel disease is the most prevalent cause of vascular cognitive impairment (Simoni et al., 2012) and is strongly associated with both cerebral pulsatility and a low end‐diastolic blood flow (Shi et al., 2020) as well as hypoperfusion of the brain in the white matter (Markus et al., 2000). However, the direction of causality is unclear without an intervention that specifically reduces cerebral pulsatility. Thus, the significant 0.08 reduction in MCA‐PI by increasing paced heart rate by 10 bpm and the reduction of people with a PI above 1, with a heart rate below 50, has potential both to test the hypothesis that pulsatility is a causative driver of cerebral small vessel disease and to test whether increased heart rate has potential as an intervention to reduce the risk of cognitive decline in patients with pacemakers.

Of the 30,000 pacemakers implanted annually in the UK (bhuva, 2025) and almost 1 million globally, the majority are implanted in people over the age of 70 for bradycardic indications such as sick sinus syndrome and atrio‐ventricular conduction system disease, as well as heart failure. These patients are therefore at an increased risk of cerebral hypoperfusion due to either intermittent bradycardia or ventricular systolic dysfunction reducing cardiac output, and thus may be at an increased risk of cerebral small vessel disease and vascular cognitive decline (Ovsenik et al., 2021). Conventionally pacemakers are programmed to a lower base rate to minimize pacing where possible and maintain battery life, although this may not be an issue in octogenarians with less than 10–15 years’ life expectancy given the battery duration of most modern pacemaker devices. Moreover, whilst a lower resting heart rate in younger patients is associated with cardiovascular fitness and lower mortality (Cole et al., 1999; Jouven et al., 2005), this is unlikely to apply to octogenarians with isolated cardiac conduction disease but no angina and otherwise normal left ventricular function. Therefore, increasing paced heart rate to more physiological levels in patients at risk of cerebrovascular injury, but with normal cardiac function, has the potential to significantly reduce the clinical burden of the disease in this high‐risk group, warranting further investigation.

This study benefitted from careful physiological testing, in a controlled environment and a highly consistent relationship between the primary exposure and the primary outcome, but there were limitations to the study. Firstly, this was a small cohort, although there was a very consistent relationship between heart rate, cerebral pulsatility and absolute cerebral blood velocity. However, the small size of the cohort means that the study is underpowered to determine whether clinical and demographic risk factors interact with these relationships, and in which groups the relationship between heart rate and cerebral pulsatility may be particularly important. Secondly, the cohort were only studied immediately after pacemaker implantation, with increased pacing rates for less than 10 min. Although this provides an advantage as it tests the effect of increased heart rate in a heart not previously paced, it is unknown whether there is physiological adaptation that could counteract the effect of increased heart rate during sustained pacing. This needs testing in further studies. Thirdly, continuous measurement of blood pressure during acute pacemaker implantation in the catheter laboratory was limited by peripheral vasoconstriction affecting reliability of measurement from the finger, limiting analysis of the effect of heart rate on the blood pressure waveform and the mediating effect of blood pressure on cerebral blood flow. Fourthly, brain imaging was not available in sufficient participants to draw any conclusions about the relationship between heart rate and cerebral pulsatility in people with established cerebrovascular disease, as suggested in previous studies.

Overall, this experimental medicine study identifies a novel potential mechanism by which a key index of cerebral blood flow is controlled. The strength of the physiological relationship is highly robust despite the small sample size, but there is a need for more detailed studies of the effect of longer duration pacing at a higher heart rate to determine whether the effect of higher paced heart rates is sustained, and whether it is associated with any detrimental effects, such as increased cardiac strain. However, if pacing at a higher rate is safe, tolerable and sustained, it provides a simple intervention that could be tested in a large population of patients. This could be tested either in people with a new pacemaker or a pacemaker already in situ, including in older patients at an increased risk of cerebrovascular disease. However, such an intervention may benefit from further analysis of the optimal paced rate, and how this may vary by patient characteristics, time of day and activity levels.

Conclusion

Cerebral pulsatility was reduced by increasing paced heart rate at the time of pacemaker implantation. This reflected a reduction in systolic cerebral blood velocity and an increase in diastolic cerebral blood velocity, up to 80 bpm with similar but partially independent effects on blood pressure. This presents a novel approach to modify cerebral pulsatility that needs testing in systematic clinical trials.
