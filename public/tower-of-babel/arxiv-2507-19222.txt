Convergence of the KMP model to the KPZ equation
Guillaume Barraquand, Francesco Casini

Abstract
. We prove that the Kipnis-Marchioro-Presutti (KMP) process converges to the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation, as time t t goes to infinity, in a properly scaled observation window shifted by t 3 / 4 t^{3/4} . Our proof is based on identifying the KMP process with a stochastic flow of kernels describing transition probabilities in a certain model of random walk in space-time random environment. This allows to apply a recent result of [ Par24 ] proving convergence of the density field of random walks in random environment to the KPZ equation in a suitably general sense.



1.
Introduction

Kipnis, Marchioro and Presutti introduced in
[
KMP82
]
a stochastic model for energy exchange in a chain of harmonic oscillators, further studied in both mathematics and physics literature. The model can be described on an arbitrary graph as follows. Vertices are assigned random energies, evolving as a Markov process: for each pair of adjacent vertices, their energies are redistributed at exponential rate
1
, uniformly over all possible redistributions that conserve the total energy.

This model, as well as variants and generalizations
[
GKRV09
,
CGGR13
,
CGRS16
]
, is often studied through the notion of Markov duality
[
JK12
,
GR25
]
. The original article
[
KMP82
]
actually proved one of the first examples of a so-called absorbing duality. Besides Markov duality, the model on a finite lattice connected to boundary reservoirs is one of the simplest prototypes of driven diffusive system in non-equilibrium statistical physics. Recently, the model received increased attention in the physics literature
[
BSM22a
,
BSM22b
,
GPR+22
,
RGIB23
,
KLD23
,
BM24
]
, in the context of macroscopic fluctuation theory (MFT). The latter is a general framework
[
BDSG+02
,
BDSG+15
]
that extends the hydrodynamic theory to predict the large deviation behaviour of many stochastic transport models. We shall also mention a new approach to characterizing the stationary measure of the model based on the so-called hidden temperature model
[
DMFG24
]
, new duality and intertwining relations
[
GRvT25
]
, as well a recent proof of the hydrodynamic limit
[
FGHS25
]
.

The KMP model shares many similarities with the Symmetric Simple Exclusion Process (SSEP). In particular, the MFT equations – a couple of nonlinear PDEs from which one can conjecturally express large deviation rate functions – are exactly the same for both models
[
ZM16
,
GPR+22
,
RGIB23
]
. This similarity does not concern only large deviations. On the graph
\mathbb{Z}
, both the SSEP starting from a step initial condition and the KMP model starting from a localized energy distribution are expected to converge to the Edwards-Wilkinson equation (additive noise stochastic heat equation). Indeed, this is proven for the SSEP
[
KL13
]

and several variants of KMP model
[
FF98
,
BRAS06
,
Yu16
]
.

The goal of this article is to show that despite the similarities between SSEP and KMP models, the scaling limit of the KMP model is richer in the sense that in the appropriate scaling window, its fluctuations converge to the multiplicative noise stochastic heat equation (
3.2
), that is the exponential of the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation (
3.1
) (Theorem
3.1
). The main idea behind our proof is that the field of energies in the KMP model should be understood as a stochastic flow that describes the probability distribution of a random walk in random environment (Proposition
2.5
). Once this identification is made, we can apply a recent result of
[
Par24
]
proving convergence to the KPZ equation for a general class of random walks in (dynamic) random environment. A special case of this result is stated below as Theorem
3.5
.

We expect that up to model-dependent constants, our main result also holds for more general redistribution mechanisms involving a greater and possibly random number of consecutive sites, with a similar proof. Further, our work also suggests Conjecture
3.2
: for any velocity
v\in(-1,1)
,
v\neq 0
, as time
t
goes to infinity, the logarithm of KMP energies around site
\lfloor vt\rfloor
converges to the Tracy Widom distribution.

Before commenting further on the connections between the KPZ equation and random walks in random environment, we should mention that a connection between the KMP model and the KPZ equation is already studied in
[
KLD23
]
. Indeed, based on MFT predictions, the large deviations in the SSEP and KMP model, or any other model with the same mobility and diffusion coefficient, should be related to a stochastic PDE describing the probability distribution of a Brownian particle in a white noise velocity field. The latter is known to be related to the Kardar Parisi-Zhang universality class
[
LDT17
,
BR20
,
BLD20
]
. More precisely,
[
KLD23
]
studied the crossover between the large deviation of the KMP model and the small time large deviation behaviour of the KPZ equation.

KPZ universality class and random walks in random environment

The KPZ equation, introduced in
[
KPZ86
]
, is an emblematic model in the KPZ class. Although it is distinct from the universal KPZ fixed point
[
MQR21
]
, it arises in general as scaling limit of models when one can rescale simultaneously time and space as well as a parameter of the system that controls the asymmetry of the model, or the strength of the noise. For example, the weakly asymmetric simple exclusion process converges to the KPZ equation
[
BG97
]
. When tuning the strength of the noise, that is scaling the temperature to infinity, the free energy of directed polymers converges to the KPZ equation
[
AKQ14
]
.

A toy model of discrete time random walk in space-time i.i.d. random environment was introduced in
[
BC17
]
. When transition probabilities are beta distributed, the model is exactly solvable, which allows to prove that the quenched probabilities of deviations of order
t
for the random walk have Tracy-Widom distributed fluctuations on the scale
t^{1/3}
. This is a signature of the KPZ universality class. A number of further results on the model were obtained in subsequent works
[
TLD15
,
TLD16
,
CG17
,
BRAS18
,
BR20
,
BR23
,
HCGCC23
,
HDCC24
,
HCC24
]
.
In the context of the present paper, the most important development is the fact that the quenched probabilities of moderate deviations of order
t^{3/4}
have fluctuations which are described by the KPZ equation. This was predicted, with increasing degree of precision, in
[
TLD16
,
LDT17
,
BLD20
]
and then proved in
[
DDP24b
]
for rather general discrete time simple random walks (see also
[
DDP24a
]
and
[
BW22
]
for similar results).

It is natural to wonder about the universality of this convergence, whether it remains true for more general non simple random walks in random environment, possibly allowing some correlations in space that vanish at large scale. In Physics references, universality was studied in
[
HDCC25
]
(see also
[
HDCC24
,
HCC24
,
DDP24b
]
). Interestingly, the scalings depend on the model: it was found in
[
Has25
]
that if the average
\mathrm{E}[X(t+1)-X(t)]
of an elementary step of the random walk
X(t)
is deterministic (with respect to the law of the environment), that is if the randomness of the environment is felt only in the higher cumulants of a random walk elementary step, the scaling of moderate deviations should be different. This prediction was confirmed in
[
Par24
]
, showing that for a general class of
\mathbb{R}
-valued random walks in random environment, moderate deviations converge to the KPZ equation on a scale that depends on some integer
p
. The correct scale of moderate deviations to consider is
n^{\frac{4p-1}{4p}}
where
p
is the smallest integer so that the
p
-th moment
\mathrm{E}[(X(t+1)-X(t))^{p}]
of an elementary step is random with respect to the law of the environment. The general convergence result of
[
Par24
]
is stated below as Theorem
3.5
in the case
p=1
, which is the only case we need. In the case
p=2
, the coefficient
7/8
can also be predicted by considering a continuous model of diffusion with space-time white noise diffusion coefficient
[
LD23
]
.

2.
KMP model as a stochastic flow

2.1.
The KMP model

We recall the definition of the KMP model on
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
KMP82
]
)
.

The KMP model
\left(\eta(t,x)_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\right)_{t\geqslant 0}
is a continuous-time Markov process defined on the state space
\Omega=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The infinitesimal generator is given by

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{L}_{x,x+1}\,,

where the action of the linear operators
\mathcal{L}_{x,x+1}
on local functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
reads

\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{L}_{x,x+1}f\right)(\eta)=\int_{0}^{1}&\left[f(\ldots,\eta(x-1),\,p(\eta(x)+\eta(x+1)),\,(1-p)(\eta(x)+\eta(x+1)),\,\eta(x+2),\ldots)\right.\\
&\left.\quad-f(\eta)\right]\frac{p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\alpha-1}}{\text{B}(\alpha,\alpha)}dp\,.\end{split}

In words, at exponential rate
1
on each bond
(x,x+1)
the total energy
\eta(x)+\eta(x+1)
is redistributed according to a
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
-distributed random variable, i.e. the couple
(\eta(x),\eta(x+1))
is replaced by

(B(\eta(x)+\eta(x+1)),(1-B)(\eta(x)+\eta(x+1)))

where
B\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
. More precisely, the process may be constructed as follows. Associate to each bond
(x,x+1)
a Poisson process
(T_{x,x+1}^{(j)})_{j\in\{0,1,\ldots\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
For each Poisson time
T=T_{x,x+1}^{(j)}
, let
B_{x,x+1}^{T}
be an independent random variable distributed as Beta
(\alpha,\alpha)
. On each bond, at each Poisson times
T
, the energies are updated by letting

(2.1)

(\eta(T,x),\eta(T,x+1))=\left(B(\eta(T^{-},x)+\eta(T^{-},x+1)),(1-B)(\eta(T^{-},x)+\eta(T^{-},x+1))\right)

where
\eta(t^{-},x)
denotes the left-limit at time
t
, i.e. the value just before the jump. The process may be constructed using a variant of Harris graphical construction
[
FF98
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the original paper
[
KMP82
]
, the KMP model was introduced with a
\text{Uniform}([0,1])
-random variable (corresponding to
\alpha=1
). It has been generalized in
[
CGGR13
]
to
B\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
, which is sometimes called generalized KMP process.

Remark 2.3
.

The KMP model can be interpreted as a system of particles with positions
(\mathcal{R}_{n}(t))
, see
[
FF98
]
and
[
GPR+22
]
. Consider the special case
\alpha=1
for simplicity. From a KMP energy configuration, we can define a particle configuration (up to a global shift) via

\eta(n,t):=\mathcal{R}_{n+1}(t)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)\,.

The particle’s dynamic is such that each particle carries a Poisson clock, and when it rings,
\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)
jumps to a location chosen uniformly in the interval
[\mathcal{R}_{n-1}(t),\mathcal{R}_{n+1}(t)]
.

2.2.
A continuous-time random walk in random environment

We now define a specific model of random walk in random environment (RWRE). The position of the random walk at time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is denoted
(X(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. The environment is made of a family of Poisson processes (indexed by bonds of
\mathbb{Z}
) and Beta variables for each Poisson time on each bond.

Definition 2.4
.

On a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, we define the following random variables: for each bond
(x,x+1)
, let
\left(T_{x,x+1}^{(j)}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
be a Poisson point process, independent for each bond.

For each bond
(x,x+1)
and each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
B_{x,x+1}^{(j)}
be an independent random variable distributed as Beta
(\alpha,\alpha)
as previously.
We denote a realization of the environment by
(\mathcal{P},\omega)\in\Omega
, where
\mathcal{P}
is a realization of the Poisson processes and
\omega
is a realization of the Beta variables.

Given
(\mathcal{P},\omega)\in\Omega
, the random walk dynamic is the following. If at time
t
,
X(t)=x
, we have to consider the first Poisson times larger than
t
associated to the bonds
(x-1,x)
and
(x,x+1)
. Let

T_{1}:=\min_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}\left\{T_{x-1,x}^{(j)}\,:\,T_{x-1,x}^{(j)}>t\right\},\qquad T_{2}:=\min_{j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}\left\{T_{x,x+1}^{(j^{\prime})}\,:\,T_{x,x+1}^{(j^{\prime})}>t\right\}\,.

Then, we have that:

•

if
T_{1}>T_{2}
, the walker remains at site
x
with probability
B_{2}
or goes to
x+1
with probability
\left(1-B_{2}\right)
, where
B_{2}
is the Beta variable associated with the Poisson time
T_{2}
;

•

if
T_{1}<T_{2}
, the walker goes to
x-1
with probability
B_{1}
or remains at site
x
with probability
\left(1-B_{1}\right)
, where
B_{1}
is the Beta variable associated with the Poisson time
T_{1}
.

In words, we may say that when the Poisson clock rings on a fixed bond, the walker goes to the left-most site of the bond with probability
B_{T_{\cdot}}
, or it goes to the right-most one with probability
1-B_{T_{\cdot}}
.
Figure
1
shows a possible path taken by the random walk.

Figure 1
.

The horizontal segments (bridges) represent the times of the Poisson point process attached to each bond. They are decorated with random variables
B\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
. The continuous red line is a possible trajectory of random walk, from
(0,x)
to
(\tau,x-1)
. The gray circles represent the location of the particle after each Poisson event.

The law of the RWRE can be described by the quenched transition kernel

(2.2)

K_{s,t}(y,x):=\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P},\omega}\left(X(t)=x|X(s)=y\right)\,.

where
\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P},\omega}(\cdot)
is the probability measure of the random walk conditioning on
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
. We sometimes use the shorthand notation
K_{t}(x,y):=K_{0,t}(x,y)
.

The dynamic of the RWRE
(X(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, yield dynamic for its transition kernel. For some
t\geqslant 0
, we consider the transition kernel
(K_{t}(y,x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
, for a particle initially located at site
y
. In the same set up as before, the updating rule consists of:

•

if
T_{1}>T_{2}

(2.3)

K_{T_{2}}(y,x)=B_{T_{2}}\left(K_{t}(y,x)+K_{t}(y,x+1)\right)\;;

•

if
T_{1}<T_{2}

(2.4)

K_{T_{1}}(y,x)=(1-B_{T_{1}})\left(K_{t}(y,x-1)+K_{t}(y,x)\right)\,.

The transition kernel
K_{s,t}(y,x)
defined in (
2.2
) is a stochastic flow of kernels,
in the sense of Definition 5.1 of
[
SSS14
]
. This means that it satisfies the conditions:

(1)

Consider
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s\leqslant t\leqslant u
, then a.s. we have that
K_{s,s}(x,y)=\delta_{x}(y)
and

K_{s,u}(y,x)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{s,t}(y,z)K_{t,u}(z,x)\,.

(2)

Consider
t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots t_{n-1}<t_{n}
, then
\left(K_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}(\cdot,\cdot)\right)_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
are all independent.

(3)

The kernels
K_{s,t}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
K_{s+u,t+u}(\cdot,\cdot)
are equal in finite dimensional distribution for each
s\leqslant t
and
u
.

2.3.
The KMP as a stochastic flow of kernels

As is probably already clear from (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), the transition kernel of the RWRE defined above evolves as the KMP energies.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\left(\eta(t,x)_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\right)_{t\geqslant 0}

be the KMP process with initial condition
\left(\eta(x,0)\right)_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
Let
\left(K_{t}(y,x)_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}\right)_{t\geqslant 0}
be the stochastic flow of kernels defined in (
2.2
). For any bounded function
\varrho_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, let

(2.5)

\varrho_{t}(x):=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\varrho_{0}(y)K_{t}(y,x).

Then, if
\varrho_{0}=\eta(0,\cdot)
we have that

\eta(t,x)\overset{(d)}{=}\varrho_{t}(x),

in the Skorokhod space
D\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{Z}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the initial condition
\eta(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}_{0}(x)
, we have

\eta(0,x)=K_{0}(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}_{0}(x)\,.

Comparing the dynamic of the KMP model (
2.1
) and those of the transition kernel (
2.3
)-(
2.4
), and assuming that the two stochastic processes share the same Poisson events and the same environment, we have
\eta(t,x)=K_{t}(0,x)
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For an arbitrary initial condition
\varrho_{0}=\eta(\cdot,0)
, defining
\varrho_{t}(x)
as (
2.5
), the Proposition follows by linearity.
∎

As stationary measures for the KMP model are well-known
[
KMP82
,
CGGR13
]
, we obtain the following.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mu_{\alpha}
be the product measure on configurations
(\eta(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that the
\eta(x)
are i.i.d. and
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha)
distributed. Then, the Markov process
\varrho_{t}
defined in (
2.5
) has a unique stationary measure (up to multiplicative constant) given by
\mu_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Given Proposition
2.5
, this is an immediate consequence of the known stationary measure for the KMP model.
∎

2.4.
k
-point motion

The description of the KMP model as a stochastic flow of transition kernels provides a new perspective on the Markov duality between the KMP model and the following model introduced in
[
KMP82
,
CGGR13
]
.

Definition 2.7

(Dual-KMP)
.

The Dual-KMP model
(\xi(t,x)_{x\in\mathbb{Z}})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a continuous-time Markov process defined on the state space
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\xi(t,x)
represents a number of particles on site
x
at time
t
. At rate
1
, on each bond
(x,x+1)
, the total number of particles
\xi(x)+\xi(x+1)
is redistributed according to a random variable
R\sim\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(n,\alpha,\alpha)
random variable with
n=\xi(x)+\xi(x+1)
, such that at every Poisson time
T
,
(\xi(T^{-},x),\xi(T^{-},x+1))
becomes

(R,R-\xi(T^{-},x),\xi(T^{-},x+1))\,.

We recall that a random variable
R\sim\text{BetaBinomial}(n,\alpha,\beta)
, if it has density given by

\mathbb{P}(R=k)=\binom{n}{k}\int_{0}^{1}p^{\alpha+k-1}(1-p)^{\beta+n-k-1}dp\,.

The KMP and the dual-KMP satisfy a Markov duality, with respect to the duality function (see
[
KMP82
,
CGGR13
]
)

D(\eta,\xi):=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}d(\eta(x),\xi(x)),\hskip 10.0ptd(\eta(x),\xi(x))=\eta(x)^{\xi(x)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\xi(x))}\,.

Namely, the following equation holds true:

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}_{\eta(0,\cdot)}\left[D(\eta(t,\cdot),\xi(0,\cdot))\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi(0,\cdot)}\left[D(\eta(0,\cdot),\xi(t,\cdot))\right]\,,

where the expectations on the right-hand-side and on the left-hand-side are taken with respect to the law of the KMP with initial condition
\eta(0,\cdot)
and of the dual KMP with initial condition
\xi(0,\cdot)
, respectively.

We observe that the redistribution of the dual particles on each bond can be seen as binomial experiments with random probability of success with law
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
. Therefore, when the Poisson events occurs, each of the particles in the bond behaves as an independent random walk in a Beta random environment.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and call
k
-point motion the joint evolution of
\left\{(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{k}(t))\right\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, where for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

X_{i}(t)
is a copy of the RWRE initially located on an arbitrary site of
\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore, we observe that the dual-KMP process can be interpreted as the
k
-point associated with the RWRE, in which the kernel of each of these walkers evolves as the KMP process (properly initialized).

The transition kernel for the
k
-points motion to go from
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
to
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is defined as

(2.7)

\mathbf{p}_{t}^{(k)}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\to(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})):=\mathbb{E}\left[K_{t}(x_{1},y_{1})\cdots K_{t}(x_{k},y_{k})\right]\,.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
be a product measure over
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, with densities

\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{\text{inv}}(x_{i})\right],

where, with a slight abuse of notation, we have used again the notation
\mathbb{E}
to denote the expectation with respect to the Gamma variables in the definition of the stationary measure
\mu_{\text{inv}}
. Then, the
k
-points motion
(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{k}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
has a unique stationary measure given by
\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The proof is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.6
.
∎

The duality relation (
2.6
) can now be re-obtained starting from reversibility and using the kernel (
2.7
) for the
k
-points motion. Consider two configuration vectors
\xi=(\xi_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(\hat{\xi}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\xi_{x}
(
\hat{\xi}_{x}
) denotes the number of particles at site
x
. We assume that both vectors contains
k
particles and we denote by
x_{1},x_{k}
and by
\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}
their location. It is convenient to denote
\mathrm{P}_{t}(\xi,\hat{\xi})=\mathbf{p}_{t}^{(k)}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\to(\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}))
. By Lemma (
2.8
), we have the detailed balance condition

\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(\xi)\mathrm{P}_{t}(\xi,\hat{\xi})=\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(\hat{\xi})\mathrm{P}_{t}(\hat{\xi},\xi)\,.

For fixed
(\eta(0,x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we denote
f(\xi)=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta(0,x)^{\xi(x)}
and we write

\sum_{\hat{\xi}}\mathrm{P}_{t}(\xi,\hat{\xi})\frac{f(\hat{\xi})}{\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(\hat{\xi})}=\frac{1}{\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(\xi)}\sum_{\hat{\xi}}\mathrm{P}_{t}(\hat{\xi},\xi)f(\hat{\xi})\,.

We analyze separately the two sides of the equation above. On the left-hand-side, we have that

\sum_{\hat{\xi}}\mathrm{P}_{t}(\xi,\hat{\xi})\frac{f(\hat{\xi})}{\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(\hat{\xi})}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi(0)}\left[\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta(0,x)^{\xi(t,x)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\xi(t,x))}\right]

On the right-hand-side we have that the quantity
\nu^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(\xi):=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\xi(x))}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
, while we have the following chain of equalities:

\begin{split}\sum_{\hat{\xi}}\mathrm{P}_{t}(\hat{\xi},\xi)f(\hat{\xi})=&\sum_{\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta(0,x)^{\hat{\xi}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[K_{t}(\hat{x}_{1},x_{1})\cdots K_{t}(\hat{x}_{k},x_{k})\right]\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\hat{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\cdots\sum_{\hat{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\eta(0,\hat{x}_{\ell})K_{t}(\hat{x}_{\ell},x_{\ell})\right]\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta(0,y)K_{t}(y,x)\right)^{\xi(x)}\right]\\
=&\mathbb{E}_{\eta(0)}\left[\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta(t,x)^{\xi(x)}\right]\end{split}

Therefore, we immediately read off the duality relation by setting
\xi(0,x)=\xi(x)
for all
x
.

3.
Main result

3.1.
Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation

The Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) is a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE), introduced in
[
KPZ86
]
, which governs the evolution of a function
h(t,x)
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. This SPDE reads

(3.1)

\partial_{t}h(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}h(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{x}h(t,x)\right)^{2}+\Xi(t,x)\,,

where
\Xi(t,x)
is a space-time white noise. We consider solutions to the KPZ equation (
3.1
) in the Cole-Hopf sense. For any fixed horizon time
T>0
and intial condition
h_{0}\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, a random function
h\in C([0,T],C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}))
is a solution to (
3.1
) if
h(t,x):=\log(\mathcal{U}(t,x))
, where
\mathcal{U}(t,x)
solves the multiplicative-noise stochastic heat equation

(3.2)

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}\mathcal{U}(t,x)+\mathcal{U}(t,x)\Xi(t,x)\,,

with initial condition
\mathcal{U}_{0}(x)=e^{h_{0}(x)}
. A function
\mathcal{U}(t,x)
is a solution to (
3.2
) with initial condition
\mathcal{U}_{0}(x)
if it satisfies

\mathcal{U}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t}(x-y)\mathcal{U}_{0}(y)dy+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t}(x-y)\mathcal{U}(s,y)\Xi(s,y)\,.

where
p_{t}(x)
denotes the heat kernel. We will consider a slightly different solution where
h_{0}
is not a continuous function but rather the so-called narrow wedge solution, that is when
h(t,x)=\log\mathcal{U}(t,x)
and
\mathcal{U}_{0}(x)=\delta_{0}(x)
. There exists a unique solution to (
3.2
) (see
[
BC95
,
Par19
]
) in the class of processes in
C((0,T],C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}))
adapted to the filtration generated by the white noise
\Xi
, and satisfying the
L^{2}
bound

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R},\,t\in(0,T]}t\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{U}(t,x)^{2}\right]<\infty\,.

3.2.
Main result

Consider the KMP process
(\eta(t,x)_{x\in\mathbb{Z}})_{t\geqslant 0}
, with initial condition
\eta(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{x=0\}}
. For
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, for
t\in[0,T]

and for
x\in N^{-1/2}\mathbb{Z}-N^{1/4}t
we introduce the density field

(3.3)

\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,x\right)=C_{N,t,x}\;\eta(tN,N^{3/4}t+N^{1/2}x)\,,

where

(3.4)

C_{N,t,x}=e^{N^{1/4}x+N^{1/2}\frac{t}{2}+\frac{t}{8}}\,.

This density field should be seen as a distribution which can be integrated against test functions
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
as

(3.5)

\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\frac{x}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\,.

We recall the definition of the Schwartz space

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\,:\,\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0},\;\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert x^{\alpha}(D^{\beta}f)(x)\rvert<\infty\right\}\,,

and denote its dual space by
\mathcal{S}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The sequence
(\mathfrak{F}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
is tight with respect to the topology
D\left([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})\right)
. Furthermore, any limit point lies in
C\left((0,T],C(\mathbb{R})\right)
and coincides with the law of the Itō solution to the multiplicative noise stochastic heat equation given by

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}\mathcal{U}(t,x)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}\mathcal{U}(t,x)\Xi(t,x)\\
\mathcal{U}(0,x)=\delta_{0}(x)\end{cases}\,.

Here,
\alpha>0
is the parameter of the
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
distribution of the random environment and
\Xi(t,x)
is the standard Gaussian space-time white noise.

Proof.

Since the KMP process can be identified with the stochastic flow of kernels (
2.2
), by Proposition
2.5
, we may apply the result of
[
Par24
]
(reported it in the case
p=1
in Theorem
3.5
of the subsequent Section
3.5
), specialized to our particular family of stochastic flows. More in detail, in Section
4
we verify that the assumptions of
[
Par24
]
are satisfied by our transition kernel (Proposition
4.1
). For completeness, in the subsequent Section
3.5
, we recall and list these hypothesis in Assumptions
1
. This yields the convergence of the field obtained by linearly interpolating the values at discrete times. In Proposition
5.1
, we prove the convergence in
D\left([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})\right)
. Finally, in Section
6
, we prove that
\gamma=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}
(Proposition
6.7
).
∎

3.3.2.
More general models of energy redistribution

We expect that, up to model dependent constants, the statement of Theorem
3.1
still holds for generalizations of the KMP model, with the same proof method.

First, one can consider a generalization of the weight’s distribution. The most minor generalization would be to choose
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha\neq\beta
random variables. This would simply induce a drift in the density profile of the associated RWRE. More generally, one could consider that the energy is redistributed on a given bond according to a random variable
B
following a more general distribution, for instance it could be a sum of Dirac masses, or a mixture of Dirac masses and a continuous part.

Second, one could consider more complicated redistribution mechanism. For example, one could redistribute the energies on a larger number
n
of consecutive sites, this number
n
could even be random. As long as the sum of energies is preserved by the redistribution rule, it would still be possible to define a stochastic flow of kernels and possibly apply Theorem
3.5
as long as some mild assumptions are satisfied. To give a concrete example, one could consider the following model: associate a Poisson process to each
n
-tuple of consecutive sites and redistribute the energies on these sites at rate
1
in the same proportions as an independent
\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))
random variable.

3.3.3.
Convergence in a better topology

In a different direction, it would be interesting to prove convergences in better topologies for the field (
3.5
). However, one cannot prove the point wise convergence for the field (
3.5
), since
\eta(t,x)
locally converges to i.i.d. Gamma distributed variables (the stationary measure) which is very irregular at large scales. However, we conjecture that replacing
\eta(t,x)
with
\sum_{y\,:\,y\geqslant x}\eta(t,y)
would instead make the field (
3.5
) converge pointwise (the fluctuations due to the stationary measure would average out in the sum). Such a result is proved in the context of the Beta RWRE which is a more tractable model
[
DDP24b
]
.

3.3.4.
Convergence of continuous time stochastic flows of kernels

Theorem
3.5
is stated for discrete time RWRE, corresponding to discrete stochastic flows of kernels. A similar result should hold for continuous time RWRE, and it would be interesting to understand how
\gamma
depends on the law of the RWRE in that case. Our arguments in Section
6
suggest that for continuous time RWRE
X(t)
on countable state space, the value of the noise strength
\gamma^{2}
in the limit should be the same as for the skeleton chain
\widetilde{X}(n)
associated to
X(t)
(If
X(t)
has rates
q_{x\to y}
, then we define the skeleton chain
\widetilde{X}
as the discrete time Markov chain with transition probabilities
p_{x\to y}=\frac{q_{x\to y}}{-q_{xx}}
).

3.4.
Notations

It is important in the proofs below to distinguish the probability measure on the environment from the law of the random walk, and to distinguish the averaged law of the random walk from the random walk conditioned on the environment. We will use the following notations:

\mathbb{P}

Probability measure of the environment.

\mathbf{P}

Path-space annealed probability of the continuous-time random walk.

\mathrm{P}

Path-space probability measure of a discrete-time symmetric random walk
(\mathcal{X}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
.

\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P},\omega}

Quenched (conditioning on the environment) path-space probability measure for the random walk. Similarly
\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P}}
(conditioning on Poisson processes only).

\mathfrak{P}

Law of a Poisson process
(N(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
with intensity
1
.

The expectations, variances and covariances associated with the probability distributions listed above will be written using the corresponding font.

A general convergence result

In

\mathbb{Z}

[

in this specific set-up.
Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

\omega\in\Omega\to\mathcal{K}^{\omega}(\cdot,\cdot)

Let

\mathcal{K}_{0,N}:=\mathcal{K}_{0,1}\cdots\mathcal{K}_{N-1,N}\,,

where
\mathcal{K}_{0,1}\mathcal{K}_{1,2}(y,x)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{K}_{0,1}(y,z)\mathcal{K}_{1,2}(z,x)
. We assume that the sequence satisfies the following hypotheses.

We introduce some notation. We define the
1

(3.8)

\mathbf{p}(x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}_{1}(0,x)\right].

Then, we introduce its moment generating function

Moreover, considering
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

and define the

\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif}}(x,y):=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y_{1}-y_{2}=y\}}\mathbf{p}^{(2)}\left((x,0)\to(y_{1},y_{2})\right)

Assumptions 1
.

We assume the following 6 statements:

HP 1)

\mathcal{K}_{0,1}(\cdot,\cdot),\mathcal{K}_{1,2}(\cdot,\cdot),\mathcal{K}_{2,3}(\cdot,\cdot)\ldots

D_{N,t,N^{-1/2}(N^{1/2}x+d_{N}t)}=\exp\left(N^{1/4}x+\frac{t}{2}N^{1/2}+\frac{t}{8}+tO(N^{-1/2})\right).

Since we are considering a convergence in

we can discard the error term

, hence the expression given in (

).

Proposition 4.1
.

The assumptions
1
are verified by the sequence of kernels
(K_{n-1,n}(x,y))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
.

Proof.

Assumptions
HP 1)
,
HP 2)
and
HP 6)
immediately follows from the definition of the sequence of kernels. Assumption
HP 3)
is a consequence of the explicit expression for the moment generating function (
4
) and of the fact that
\mathbf{p}
is the probability distribution of a continuous-time random walk at time
1
so that
m_{1}=0
and
m_{2}=1
.

To prove assumption

we simply need to prove that
\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}(0,y)
has some probability to be nonzero. Indeed, we may assume that there are no Poisson events associated with the bonds
(-1,0)
and
(1,2)
and only one at time

on the bond
(0,1)
. This happens with positive probability (with respect to the law of independent Poisson processes), and in such a case,
\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}(0,y)=2B_{T}-1

Assumption
HP 5)
is trivial if
k=1
or if
r_{1}+\ldots+r_{k}=1
. First, we prove it for

and we generalize for arbitrary
k
. Let
(X_{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
and
(X_{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
be two RWRE as in Section
2.2
, with
2
-point kernel given by (
2.7
). Assume that at
t=0
they are located at
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
respectively, with
|x_{1}-x_{2}|=2d
for some
d\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}{2}
and, without loss of generality, we assume
x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}
(indistinguishable particles). We introduce the Poisson event
\mathcal{E}_{d}
defined as follows:

\mathcal{E}_{d}:=\left\{\exists t\in[0,1]\;:\;X_{1}(t)=\lfloor\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}\rfloor\;\vee\;X_{2}(t)=\lceil\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}\rceil\right\}\,.

For
i=1,2
, for fixed
x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
r_{i}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
such that
2\leqslant r_{1}+r_{2}\leqslant 4
, we introduce
\Delta_{i}:=(X_{i}(1)-x_{i})^{r_{i}}
and we write that

\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}|\mathcal{E}_{d}\right]\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{E}_{d})+\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}|\mathcal{E}_{d}^{c}\right]\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{E}_{d}^{c})\,.

We only need to bound the first addend on the right-hand-side, since the second one coincides with the case of two independent walkers. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have that

\mathbf{E}[\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}|\mathcal{E}_{d}]\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[\Delta_{1}^{2}|\mathcal{E}_{d}]\,\mathbf{E}[\Delta_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{E}_{d}]}\,.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, we denote by
\Delta_{i}(n)
the displacement of the
i
-th walker in
n
jumps. By the fact that the there can only be a finite number of Poisson event
[0,1]
, we have obtain the bound

\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{1}^{2}|\mathcal{E}_{d}\right]=\sum_{n=\lfloor d\rfloor}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}[\Delta_{1}^{2}(n)]\frac{\mathfrak{P}(n,\mathcal{E}_{d})}{\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{E}_{d})}\leqslant C_{1}e^{-C_{2}d}\,,

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. A similar argument can be performed for
X_{2}(t)
. Moreover, let us observe that
\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}y_{j}^{r_{j}}\mathbf{p}^{\otimes 2}(\mathbf{y})
is uniformly bounded in
r_{1},r_{2}
by a positive constant. Therefore, we conclude that, for all
r_{1},r_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,3\}\,:\,2\leqslant r_{1}+r_{2}\leqslant 4
, there exist
C_{3},C_{4}>0
such that

\begin{split}&\left|\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}(y_{j}-x_{j})^{r_{j}}\mathbf{p}^{(2)}((x_{1},x_{2})\to(y_{1},y_{2}))-\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}y_{j}^{r_{j}}\mathbf{p}^{\otimes 2}(y_{j})\right|\leqslant C_{3}e^{-C_{4}|x_{1}-x_{2}|}\,.\end{split}

To extend the argument to general
k\in\{3,4\}
, we introduce the Poisson event by assuming that the walkers are labeled according to
x_{i}\leqslant x_{i+1}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
:

\mathcal{E}:=\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\bigcup_{t\in[0,1]}\left\{X_{i}(t)=\;\left\lceil\frac{x_{i-1}+x_{i}}{2}\right\rceil\right\}\cup\left\{X_{i}(t)=\left\lfloor\frac{x_{i}+x_{i+1}}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

Then, by applying repeatedly the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the result follows.
∎

We introduce the sequence of fields

\left(\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}(t,x)\right)_{N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}\subset C([0,T],\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})),

where

is obtained by linearly interpolating the filed
\mathfrak{F}_{N}(t,x)
between the points in

Applying Theorem

, we conclude that the sequence of field
\left(\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}(t,x)\right)_{N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}

is tight in

and it weakly converges to the solution
\mathcal{U}(t,x)
of (

) in the sense of Theorem
3.5
.

5.
Convergence in the Skorokhod space

Building on the convergence of the sequence of interpolated fields
\left(\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}(t,x)\right)_{N\in\mathbb{Z}{\geqslant 0}}
to the solution
\mathcal{U}(t,x)
of (
3.6
), established in the previous section, we now prove that the sequence of fields
\left(\mathfrak{F}_{N}(t,x)\right)_{N\in\mathbb{Z}{\geqslant 0}}
, introduced in (
3.5
), likewise converges in the Skorokhod space
D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The sequence of fields
\left(\mathfrak{F}_{N}(t,x)\right)_{N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
is tight in
D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))
and its finite dimensional distributions weakly converge to the finite dimensional distributions of
\mathcal{U}(t,x)
, solution of (
3.6
).

Proof.

First, we show tightness. Referring to
[
Mit83
]
, it is enough to prove tightness in
D([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for the sequence of processes
(\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle)_{N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
defined in (
3.5
). To this aim we state and prove the following Lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}
,
t,s\in[0,T]
with
s<t
, and
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, we have the estimate

(5.1)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(s,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|\right]\leqslant C(t-s)^{1/2-\epsilon}+o(N)\,.\end{split}

where
o(N)
goes to zero as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

We denote by
(\overline{X}_{N}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
the symmetric continuous-time random walk, whose transition kernel is obtained from the one of
(X_{N}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
by averaging out the
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
random variables. Then, we have that the continuous time stochastic process

\mathfrak{Z}_{t}(N^{-1/4})=\frac{e^{N^{-1/4}\overline{X}(Nt)}}{\mathbf{E}[e^{N^{-1/4}\overline{X}(Nt)}]}

is a mean-one continuous-time martingale. We will interpret this martingale as a Radon-Nikodym derivative.
Actually, it coincides with the tilting constant (
3.10
) after a suitable shift of the space variable, that is

(5.2)

D_{N,t,N^{-1/2}(\overline{X}_{N}(t)-td_{N})}=\mathfrak{Z}_{t}(N^{-1/4})\,.

Let
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and consider the path space measure for the random walk
(X_{N}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
given by
\mathrm{Q}_{N}^{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot)=\frac{d\mathrm{Q}^{\mathcal{P}}}{d\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P}}}\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot)
, where
\frac{d\mathrm{Q}^{\mathcal{P}}}{d\mathrm{P}^{\mathcal{P}}}=\mathfrak{Z}_{t}(N^{-1/4})
. Then, by a direct computation, one has that

\mathrm{Q}_{N}^{\mathcal{P}}\left(\overline{X}_{N}(Nt)\right)=d_{N}t\,.

We conclude the stochastic process
(Y_{N}(Nt))_{t\geqslant 0}
, defined as
Y_{N}(Nt):=\overline{X}_{N}(Nt)-d_{N}t
, is has zero expectation under
\mathrm{Q}_{N}^{\mathcal{P}}
.
Therefore, by using (
5.2
), which is related to the constant
C_{N,t,x}
by (
4.3
), and recalling the definition of the field
\mathfrak{F}_{N}
in (
3.5
), we have the following estimate, for some
C>0
:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(s,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|\right]=&\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathrm{Q}_{N}^{\mathcal{P}}\left(\phi\left(\frac{X_{N}(tN)-d_{N}s}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{X_{N}(sN)-d_{N}t}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)\right|\right]\\
&\leqslant C\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Q}_{N}^{\mathcal{P}}\left(\left|\frac{Y_{N}(tN)}{\sqrt{N}}-\frac{Y_{N}(sN)}{\sqrt{N}}\right|\right)\right]\,,\end{split}

where we used the Lipschitz property of
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Finally, by applying the Donsker invariance principle and by the Hölder property of the Brownian trajectories, one obtains the estimate (
5.1
).
∎

To prove tightness we apply Aldous criterion
[
Ald78
,
Bil99
]
. This consists of proving two facts.

(1)

For every
t\in[0,1]

the sequence
\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle
is tight.

(2)

For all
\epsilon>0

\begin{split}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in\mathbb{T}\\
\theta\leqslant\delta\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(\tau+\theta,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(\tau,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle|>\varepsilon\right)=0\end{split}

(1) is a consequence of the finite dimensional convergence proved below. (2) follows from Markov inequality along with the estimate (
5.1
).

To complete the proof we show the convergence of the finite dimensional distribution of the field
\mathfrak{F}(t,x)
to
\mathcal{U}(t,x)
. This is a consequence of the convergence of the interpolated field
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}
in
C((O,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))
. Let us explain the details for the one point distribution. Given the weak convergence of
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}
, it suffices to show that
for any
t\in[0,T]
,
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and for all
\epsilon>0
we have that

(5.3)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|>\epsilon\right)=0\,.

By the definition of interpolated field
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}
we have the following estimate

\begin{split}\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|\leqslant&\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}\left(\frac{k}{N},\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|\\
&+\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}\left(\frac{k+1}{N},\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|\,.\end{split}

Now, using the estimate (
5.1
) and by using the fact that
|t-\frac{k}{N}|,|t-\frac{k+1}{N}\leqslant\frac{T}{N}
we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle\mathfrak{F}_{N}\left(t,\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\bar{\mathfrak{F}}_{N}\left(\frac{k}{N},\cdot\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|\right]\leqslant o(N)\,,

so that (
5.3
) holds by Markov’s inequality.
∎

The goal of this section is to compute the value of the noise strength

Proposition 6.1

For the stochastic flow defined in Section

Proposition (

6.1.
Definition of the discrete-time process

Definition 6.2
.

On a probability space
(\Omega^{(\epsilon)},\mathcal{F}^{(\epsilon)},\mathbb{P}^{(\epsilon)})
, we define the following random variables: for each bond
(x,x+1)
, let
\left(\mathcal{I}_{x,x+1}^{(n)}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
be a Bernoulli process with parameter
\epsilon\in[0,1]
, independent for each bond.

For each bond
(x,x+1)
and each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, let
B_{x,x+1}^{(n)}
be an independent random variable distributed as Beta
(\alpha,\alpha)
.
We denote a realization of the environment by
(\text{Bin},\omega)\in\Omega^{(\epsilon)}
, where
Bin
is a realization of the Bernoulli processes and
\omega
is a realization of the Beta variables.

Given
(\text{Bin},\omega)\in\Omega^{(\epsilon)}
, the random walk dynamic is the following. If at discrete-time
n
,
X(n)^{\epsilon}=x
, the update rule is as follows:

•

If
\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x-1,x}=\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x,x+1}=1
or if
\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x-1,x}=\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x,x+1}=0
the particle does not move. These events happen with probability
\epsilon^{2}
and
1-2\epsilon(1-\epsilon)
respectively.

•

If
\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x-1,x}=1
and
\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x,x+1}=0
: the walker moves to
x+1
with probability
1-B^{(n)}_{x,x+1}
, or remains at
x
with probability
B^{(n)}_{x,x+1}
. This event happens with probability
\epsilon(1-\epsilon)
.

•

If
\mathcal{I}^{(n)}_{x-1,x}=0
and
\mathcal{I}^{n}_{x,x+1}=1
: the walker moves to
x-1
with probability
B^{(n)}_{x-1,x}
, or remains at
x
with probability
1-B^{(n)}_{x-1,x}
. This event happens with probability
\epsilon(1-\epsilon)
.

The law of the discrete time RWRE of Definition
6.2
can be described by the quenched transition kernel

K_{n-1n}^{\epsilon}(x,y):=\mathrm{P}^{Bin,\omega}(X^{\epsilon}(n)=y\,|\,X^{\epsilon}(n-1)=x)\,.

Above we have denoted by
\mathrm{P}^{Bin,\omega}
the quenched path space probability measure, by conditioning with respect to the Beta-variables and to the
\text{Binomial}(n,\epsilon)
process, obtained as a sum of
n
independent
\text{Bernoulli}(\epsilon)
variables. Again, for all
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
we denote by
K_{N}^{\epsilon}=K_{0,1}^{\epsilon}\cdots K_{N-1,N}^{\epsilon}
the quenched
N
-steps transition kernel.

We now introduce a transition kernel for the difference of two continuous time walkers, for which we derive the invariant measure. This will be useful to characterize the stationary distribution of its discrete-time counterpart. To this aim, consider two copies of the RWRE of Definition
2.4

(X_{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
,
(X_{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. We denote the annealed transition kernel for the Markov process
\left(Z(t)\right)_{t\geqslant 0}:=\left(X_{1}(t)-X_{2}(t)\right)_{t\geqslant 0}
by

\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif,t}}(z,a):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{p}^{(2)}_{t}((z,0)\to(y_{2}+a,y_{2}))\right]\,.

In what follows, we drop the subscript
t
when
t=1
(c.f. with (
3.9
)). We report the following Lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
be the product measure on configurations
x\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{\text{inv}}(x)\mu_{\text{inv}}(0)]
. Then, the Markov process
(Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, with annealed transition kernel
\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif}}^{t}(\cdot,\cdot)
, has a unique invariant measure given by
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
.

Its proof is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.6
, then is omitted.
For the rest of the paper, we consider the invariant measure
\pi_{\alpha}^{\text{inv}}
to be re-normalized as

(6.1)

\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad x\neq 0\\
\frac{(\alpha+1)}{\alpha}&\text{if}\quad x=0\end{cases}\,.

In analogy to (
4.1
), we introduce the annealed kernel for the one point as

\mathbf{p}_{n}^{\epsilon}(x):=\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[K_{n}^{\epsilon}(0,x)\right]\,.

Moreover, we introduce the annealed kernel for the discrete-time two-points motion as

(6.2)

\mathbf{p}_{n}^{(2),\epsilon}\left((x_{1},x_{2})\to(y_{1},y_{2})\right):=\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[K_{n}^{\epsilon}(x_{1},y_{1})K_{n}^{\epsilon}(x_{2},y_{2})\right]\,.

Above, we have denoted by
E_{Bin,\omega}
the expectation with respect to the environment and to the Binomial process. Furthermore, we define

(6.3)

\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif},n}^{\epsilon}(x,y):=\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{p}^{(2),\epsilon}_{\epsilon,n}\left((x,0)\to(y_{1}+y,y_{1})\right)\,.

Definition 6.4
.

We denote by
(Z^{\epsilon}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
the discrete-time Markov process with transition kernel
\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif},n}^{\epsilon}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

We observe that
\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif},n}^{\epsilon}(\cdot,\cdot)
is invariant with respect to the measure
\pi_{\alpha}^{\text{inv}}
of Lemma
6.3
. This is inherited from
\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif},t}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

6.2.
Convergence of
\gamma_{\epsilon}
to
\gamma

We rewrite in a more convenient form the noise variance
\gamma^{2}
, introduced in (
3.13
). Let

(6.4)

\displaystyle N:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-y)^{2}\mathbf{p}(x)\mathbf{p}(y)-\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}(a-z)^{2}\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif}}(z,a)\right]\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\,,

(6.5)

\displaystyle D:=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif}}(z.a)|a|-|z|\right]\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\,,

be the numerator and the denominator of the constant
\gamma^{2}
, respectively, so that
\gamma^{2}=\frac{N}{D}
.
We introduce the discrete-time counterpart of the numerator as

N^{\epsilon}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-y)^{2}\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x)\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(y)-\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}(a-z)^{2}\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif},\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(z,a)\right]\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\,.

Similarly, we introduce the discrete-time counterpart of the denominator as

D^{\epsilon}:=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\left[\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{p}_{\text{dif},\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(z,a)|a|-|z|\right]\,.

Furthermore, we define the discrete-time constant as

(6.6)

\gamma_{\epsilon}^{2}:=\frac{N^{\epsilon}}{D^{\epsilon}}\,.

Proposition 6.5
.

We have the convergence

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{\epsilon}^{2}=\gamma^{2}\,.

Proposition
6.5
comes from the fact that the discretization
X^{\epsilon}(n)
converges to the RWRE

from Definition
2.4
in a suitable sense. The following Lemma will be useful.

Lemma 6.6
.

For all
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have that

(6.7)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{(2),\epsilon}\left((x_{1},x_{2})\to(y_{1},y_{2})\right)=\mathbf{p}^{(2)}\left((x_{1},x_{2})\to(y_{1},y_{2})\right)\,.

Moreover, for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have that

(6.8)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x)=\mathbf{p}(x)\,.

Proof.

For
\epsilon\in[0,1]
, for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and
t\in[0,T]
we define

(6.9)

\mathcal{Q}^{\epsilon}_{x,x+1}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}t\rfloor}\mathcal{I}_{x,x+1}^{i}\sim\text{Binomial}(\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}t\rfloor,\epsilon)\,.

Then,
\epsilon\in[0,1]
we introduce the counting process

\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\epsilon}(t)\right)_{t\in[0,T]}:=\left(\ldots,\mathcal{Q}^{\epsilon}_{x-1,x}(t),\mathcal{Q}^{\epsilon}_{x,x+1}(t),\mathcal{Q}^{\epsilon}_{x+1,x+2}(t)\,\ldots\right)_{t\in[0,T]}

Here, for each bond,
\mathcal{Q}_{x,x+1}^{\epsilon}(t)
is an independent copy of (
6.9
) in which we have the sum of
\mathcal{I}^{i}_{x,x+1}
random variable. By using
[
Bil99
, Example 12.3]
and for all
(x,x+1)
we have that

\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\epsilon}_{x,x+1}(t)\right)_{t\in[0,T]}\xrightarrow[\epsilon\to 0]{d}(N(t))_{t\in[0,T]}

in the Skorokhod space
D([0,T],\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{\mathbb{Z}})
. Therefore, by using the definition of the kernel (

) and the law of total expectation, we have that

\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{(2),\epsilon}\left((x_{1},x_{2})\to(y_{1},y_{2})\right)=\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[K_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x_{1},y_{1})K_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x_{2},y_{2})|\bm{Q}^{\epsilon}(t)\right]\right]\,.

The mapping

\bm{Q}^{\epsilon}(t)\to\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[K_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x_{1},y_{1})K_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x_{2},y_{2})|\bm{Q}^{\epsilon}(t)\right]\right]

can be seen as continuous and bounded function of the trajectories of the stochastic process

, for

. Therefore, by Portemanteau theorem, we have the result. With the same argument one proves (

∎

Proof of Proposition
6.5
.

For the proof of this proposition, we need an intermediate lemma.

It is a direct computation to write the numerator
N^{\epsilon}
as

N^{\epsilon}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{\alpha}^{\text{inv}}(z)\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}xy\mathbf{p}^{(2),\epsilon}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}((z,0)\to(x+z,y))-\sum_{x,y}xy\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x+z)\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\,.

For fixed
z
, there is only a finite number of locations
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
for which
\mathbf{p}_{\epsilon}((z,0)\to(x+z,y))-\mathbf{p}_{\epsilon}(x)\mathbf{p}_{\epsilon}(y)
is not vanishing. Moreover, by performing a similar argument to the one done in the proof of
HP 5)
in Proposition
4.1
, we obtain that there exists an exponentially decaying and
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
function
G_{\text{Decay}}:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,\infty)
such that, uniformly in
\epsilon
,

\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}xy\mathbf{p}^{(2),\epsilon}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}((z,0)\to(x+z,y))-\sum_{x,y}xy\mathbf{p}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}^{\epsilon}(x)\mathbf{p}^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor}(y)\right)\leqslant G_{\text{Decay}}(|z|)\,.

Therefore, by Lemma
6.6
and the dominated convergence theorem, one obtains that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}N^{\epsilon}=N
. Using again Lemma
6.6
and the dominated convergence theorem one proves that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}D^{\epsilon}=D
. This follows from the fact that the addends in the summation over
z
vanish for
z
large enough.
∎

6.3.
Computation of the limit

Proposition 6.7
.

Recall
\gamma_{\epsilon}
from (
6.6
). We have

(6.10)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{\epsilon}^{2}=\frac{1}{4\alpha}\,.

Proof.

We treat the discrete-time numerator and denominator separately.
Let us start with the numerator. We re-write
N^{\epsilon}
as

(6.11)

N^{\epsilon}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\mathrm{E}_{z}^{(\epsilon)}\left[X^{\epsilon}(\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor)-z\right],\mathrm{E}_{0}^{(\epsilon)}[Y^{\epsilon}(\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor)]\right)\,.

Above, we have denoted by
\mathrm{E}^{(\epsilon)}_{z}
the quenched expectation with respect to the path space measure of the RWRE initialized at
z
.
Using the telescopic identity

(6.12)

X^{\epsilon}(\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor)-X^{\epsilon}(0)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor-1}\left(X^{\epsilon}(i+1)-X^{\epsilon}(i)\right)\,,

and using the fact that the two walkers can be correlated only through the same Bernoulli event, we have that

\begin{split}N^{\epsilon}=&\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor-1}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{\alpha}^{\text{inv}}(z)\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\mathrm{E}_{z}^{(\epsilon)}\left[X^{\epsilon}(i+1)-X^{\epsilon}(i)\right],\mathrm{E}_{0}^{(\epsilon)}\left[Y^{\epsilon}(i+1)-Y^{\epsilon}(i)\right]\right)\,.\end{split}

By using the invariance property of the measure
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
we have that

\begin{split}N^{\epsilon}=\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\mathrm{E}_{z}^{(\epsilon)}\left[X^{\epsilon}(1)-z\right],\mathrm{E}_{0}^{(\epsilon)}\left[Y^{\epsilon}(1)\right]\right)\,.\end{split}

Therefore, it is enough to compute the covariance for one step of the chain, which we re-write as
\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-z)K_{1}^{\epsilon}(z,x),\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\right)
.

If
|z|>1
, this covariance is trivially vanishing, then we consider the remaining cases.
In what follows, for the sake of notation, we denote by
\bm{\mathcal{I}}
a random vector containing the
\text{Bernoulli}(\epsilon)
variables, associated with the environment, relevant for the considered case.

Case
z=0
.
Here, we have that

\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}xK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,x),\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\right)=\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}xK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,x)\right)\,.

The relevant
\text{Bernoulli}(\epsilon)
variables are listed in
\bm{\mathcal{I}}=(\mathcal{I}_{-1,0},\mathcal{I}_{0,1})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
.
By the law of total variance we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}xK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,x)\right)\\
=&\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}xK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,x)\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right)\right]+\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}xK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,x)\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right]\right)\,.\end{split}

By using the fact that
E_{\omega}[B]=1/2
, the expectation of the second addend equals zero. For the first addend we obtain that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}xK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,x)\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right)\right]&=2\epsilon\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}(B_{0})+o(\epsilon)=\frac{\epsilon}{2(2\alpha+1)}+o(\epsilon)\,.\end{split}

Case
z=1
.
The relevant
\text{Bernoulli}(\epsilon)
variables are listed in
\bm{\mathcal{I}}:=(\mathcal{I}_{-1,0},\mathcal{I}_{0,1},\mathcal{I}_{1,2})
. By applying the law of total covariances, we have that

\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-1)K_{1}^{\epsilon}(1,x),\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\right)=\\
\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-1)K_{1}^{\epsilon}(1,x),\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right)\right]\\
+\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-1)K_{1}^{\epsilon}(1,x)\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right],\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right]\right)\,.

Since
E_{\omega}[B]=1/2
, the second addend equals zero. The first addend is of order
\epsilon
only when
\bm{\mathcal{I}}=(0,1,0)
, otherwise it is equal to zero or of order
o(\epsilon)
. Then, we write

(6.13)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{COV}^{(\epsilon)}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}(x-1)K_{1}^{\epsilon}(1,x),\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}yK_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\,\bigg|\,\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right)\right]=&\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}(B_{0})\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\\
=&\frac{\epsilon}{4(2\alpha+1)}+o(\epsilon)\,.\end{split}

Case
z=-1
.
By symmetry, one obtains again (
6.13
).

Using equation (
6.11
) for
N^{\epsilon}
and the measure
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
, written in (
6.1
), we have that

(6.14)

N^{\epsilon}=\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2(2\alpha+1)}+\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}\frac{\epsilon}{2(2\alpha+1)}\right)+o(1)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}+o(1)\,.

Let us now turn to the denominator.
By using the telescopic identity (
6.12
), the invariance of the measure
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
and by an argument similar to that done for the numerator, we obtain

(6.15)

D^{\epsilon}=\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}(z)\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathrm{E}_{z}^{\text{dif},(\epsilon)}\left[|Z^{\epsilon}(1)|-|Z^{\epsilon}(0)|\right]\right]\,.

Here,
\mathrm{E}_{z}^{\text{dif},(\epsilon)}
denotes the quenched expectation with respect to the law of the Markov chain
(Z(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
introduced in Definition
6.4
.
Therefore, to find the exact expression for the denominator it is enough to compute the annealed expectation

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathrm{E}_{z}^{\text{dif},(\epsilon)}\left[|Z^{\epsilon}(1)|\right]\right]-|z|=&\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{1}^{\epsilon}(z,a+y)|a|\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right]\right]-|z|\,.\end{split}

For
|z|>1
, the the denominator is vanishing. We then consider the remaining cases.

Case
z=0
.
Here, the relevant
\text{Bernoulli}(\epsilon)
variables are listed in
\bm{\mathcal{I}}=(\mathcal{I}_{-1,0},\mathcal{I}_{0,1})
. Then, we write

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,a+y)|a|\bigg|\mathcal{I}\right]\right]=&\left(1-4\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}(B)\right)\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\\
=&\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\,.\end{split}

Case
z=1
.
Here, the relevant
\text{Bernoulli}(\epsilon)
variables are listed in
\bm{\mathcal{I}}=(\mathcal{I}_{-1,0},\mathcal{I}_{0,1},\mathcal{I}_{1,2})
.In this case, we have contribution at most
\epsilon
only when
\mathcal{I}_{-1,0}=\mathcal{I}_{0,1}=\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=0
(that is an event with probability
1-3\epsilon+o(\epsilon)
) or when
\mathcal{I}_{-1,0}+\mathcal{I}_{0,1}+\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=1
(that are events with probability
\epsilon+o(\epsilon)
). Then, we obtain

(6.16)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{1}^{\epsilon}(0,y)\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{1}^{\epsilon}(1,a+y)|a|\bigg|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\right]\right]-1\\
=&\left(\left(1+\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}[B_{-1}]\right)+2\left(\frac{1}{4}-\mathbb{VAR}^{(\epsilon)}(B_{0})\right)+\mathbb{E}^{(\epsilon)}[2(1-B_{1})+B_{1}]\right)\epsilon-3\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\\
=&\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+1}\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\end{split}

Case
z=-1
.
By symmetry, we have again (
6.16
).

By computing the expectation with respect to the invariant measure
\pi^{\text{inv}}_{\alpha}
of equation (
6.1
), we obtain

(6.17)

\begin{split}D^{\epsilon}=&\lfloor\epsilon^{-1}\rfloor\left(\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}\epsilon+\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}\epsilon\right)+o(1)=2+o(1)\end{split}

Finally, by using (
6.14
) and (

), we have that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{\epsilon}^{2}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{N^{\epsilon}}{D^{\epsilon}}=\frac{1}{4\alpha}\,.

∎

Appendix A
Discrete-time analogues

In this section, we consider a discrete-time random walk in a i.i.d. beta-distributed random environment introduced in
[
BC17
]
. We explain its connection to a discrete time energy redistribution model in a so-called “brick-wall” geometry. Even if the redistribution rule differs from the one of the KMP, this connection has been an important source of inspiration for this work. Finally, we explain that both models may also be seen as a Haar unitary random quantum circuit, or equivalently, a toy model of directed waves in random media introduced in
[
SKR92
]
.

A.1.
The discrete-time Beta random walk in random environment

Let
(B_{n,x})_{n,x\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a collection of i.i.d.
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
distributed random variables, playing the role of the random environment. We denote by
(X(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbb{Z}
a discrete-time random walk, moving in this random environment (Beta-RWRE). As above,
\mathrm{P}
is the quenched path space measure, while
\mathbb{P}
is the probability measure over the environment. The transition probabilities for the walker read

\text{P}(X(n+1)=x+1|X(n)=x)=B_{n,x}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{P}(X(n+1)=x-1|X(n)=x)=1-B_{n,x}\,.

Let
\mathsf{p}_{n}(x):=\mathrm{P}_{0}\left(X(n)=x\right)
be the quenched
n
-steps transition probability for the walker, starting from
0
and reaching
x
, for which we adopt the convention that it is non-zero only if
n
and
x
have the same parity. The following recursion relation holds

(A.1)

\mathsf{p}_{n}(x)=\accentset{\uparrow}{\mathsf{p}}_{n}(x)+\accentset{\downarrow}{\mathsf{p}}_{n}(x)=\mathsf{p}_{n-1}(x-1)B_{n-1,x-1}+\mathsf{p}_{n-1}(x+1)\left(1-B_{n-1,x+1}\right)\,,

where we denoted

\accentset{\uparrow}{\mathsf{p}}_{n}(x)=\text{P}\left(X(n)=x,\,X(n-1)=x-1\right),\quad\accentset{\downarrow}{\mathsf{p}}_{n}(x)=\text{P}\left(X(n)=x,\,X(n-1)=x+1\right)\,.

We write equation (
A.1
) in order to compare the model with the energy exchange model and the random quantum circuit discussed below. This recursion appeared in the context of directed waves in random media
[
SKR92
]
, see also
[
CS94
,
Kar94
]
. It was used to compute
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{E}[X(n)]^{k}]
for
k=2
in in
[
SKR92
]
and
k=3,4
in
[
CS94
]
.
However, this recursion is not particularly convenient to study the model. It turns out to be much better to consider, for any set
A
,

q_{n}(x)=\mathrm{P}(X(0)\in A|X(-n)=x).

For a fixed time
n
and
A={0}
,
(q_{n}(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(p_{n}(-x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
have the same distribution. It was shown in
[
BC17
]
that the evolution of the function

u(n,x_{1},\dots,x_{k}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{n}(x_{i})\right]

is solvable by Bethe ansatz (and the solution admits a
k
-fold contour integral expression). This was used in
[
BC17
]
to prove that
\log\mathsf{p}_{n}(cn)
has
n^{1/3}
scale tracy-Widom fluctuations, putting the model in the context of the KPZ universality class.

Before discussing other related models, let us derive the stationary measure for the Beta-RWRE on a finite segment with reflecting boundaries. We recall that Beta RWRE on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
with a boundary condition was studied in
[
BR23
]
. Here we consider segment of length
N+1
, where the sites are denoted by
x\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
.

(A.2)

B_{t,x}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha_{x+1},\alpha_{x})\,\qquad\forall x\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\,,

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and for
x\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
the recurrence is given by (
A.1
), while on the boundaries we have that

\mathsf{p}_{n}(0)=B_{n-1,1}\,\mathsf{p}_{n-1}(1),\quad\mathsf{p}_{n}(N)=B_{n-1,N-1}\,\mathsf{p}_{n-1}(N-1)\,.

Therefore, we say that the boundaries are reflecting.

Lemma A.1
.

The stationary measure of the Beta-RWRE on the finite segment
\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
is giben by

(A.3)

\nu_{\text{stat}}=\bigotimes_{x=0}^{N}\nu_{\text{stat}}(x),\quad\text{where}\quad\nu_{\text{stat}}(x)\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{x}+\alpha_{x+1})\,.

Proof.

Stationarity follows from the elementary property: given the random variables

\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\sim\text{Gamma}(a+b),\;\text{independent};\quad B_{1},B_{2}\sim\text{Beta}(a,b),\;\text{independent}\,,

then

\nu_{1}B_{1}\sim\text{Gamma}(a),\;\nu_{2}(1-B_{2})\sim\text{Gamma}(b)\quad\text{independent}\,.

Independence follows form another elementary property: given two independent random variables
\nu\sim\text{Gamma}(a+b)
and
B\sim\text{Beta}(a,b)
, then

(\nu B,\nu(1-B))\sim(\text{Gamma}(a),\text{Gamma}(b))\;\text{independent}\,.

∎

A.2.
Brick-wall KMP model

We introduce the discrete-time Markov process
(\eta(n,x))_{x,n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, which we call brick-wall KMP model (BW-KMP). Any configuration at time
n\in\mathbb{Z}
belongs to the state space
[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\eta(n,x)
represents the energy at site
x
. To describe its time evolution, we consider a sequence of i.i.d.
(B_{n,x})_{n,x\in\mathbb{Z}}
, such that
B_{n,x}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
and we define the following updating rule:
When
n
is even, we redistribute the energy on bonds
(x,x+1)
with
x
even according to the rule

(A.4)

\left(\eta(n+1,x),\eta(n+1,x+1)\right)=\left(1-B_{n,x},B_{n,x}\right)\left(\eta(n,x)+\eta(n,x+1)\right).

When
n
is odd, we do the same with
x
odd.
There is a connection between the BW-KMP process and the Beta-RWRE defined in Section
A.1
.

Proposition A.2
.

Assume that, at discrete-time
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\eta(n_{0},x)+\eta(n_{0},x+1)=\mathsf{p}_{n_{0}}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n\geqslant n_{0}
, we have

(A.5)

\begin{split}&\eta(n,x)=\mathsf{p}_{n-1}(x-1)B_{n-1,x-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta(n,x+1)=\mathsf{p}_{n-1}(x+1)\left(1-B_{n-1,x+1}\right)\,.\end{split}

Proof.

The proof is done by induction. By using (
A.4
) we have that

\begin{split}\left(\eta(n_{0}+1,x),\eta(n_{0}+1,x+1)\right)=&\left(1-B_{n_{0},x},B_{n_{0},x}\right)(\eta(n_{0},x)+\eta(n_{0},x+1))\end{split}

From the assumption, it follows that
\eta(n_{0}+1,x+1)=B_{n_{0},x}\mathsf{p}_{n_{0}}(x)
. We now repeat the argument for
n_{0}+1
and
x+2
, obtaining
\eta(n_{0}+1,x+2)=(1-B_{n_{0},x+2})\mathsf{p}_{n_{0}}(x+2)
. Now we assume that, for arbitrary
n>n_{0}
, we have
\mathsf{p}_{n}(x)=\eta(n,x)+\eta(n,x+1)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then, the same argument leads to

\eta(n+1,x+1)=B_{n,x}\mathsf{p}_{n}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\eta(n+1,x+2)=(1-B_{n,x+2})\mathsf{p}_{n}(x+2)\,.

∎

A.3.
A model for directed waves in random media and random walk in random environment

Motivated by a problem of directed waves in random media,
[
SKR92
]
considered a unitary variant of the stochastic heat equation with multiplicative noise (unitary means that it preserves the
L^{2}
norm). The simplest model is the stochastic PDE

\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)=\frac{\mathbf{i}}{2}\partial_{xx}\psi(t,x)+\mathbf{i}\Xi(t,x)\psi(t,x),

but it is not well defined a priori, which was leading to some conflicting results in the litterature – see the discussion in
[
SKR92
]
. Saul, Kardar and Read argued that the directed polymer interpretation of the usual stochastic heat equation is key, and that unitary analogues should likewise be defined through a path integral. These considerations led them to the following discrete model.

We consider a wave function
\Psi_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
evolving at discrete times as follows. It is convenient to write

\Psi_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{n}^{+}(x)\\
\Psi_{n}^{-}(x)\end{pmatrix}

and imagine that the components
\Psi_{n}^{\pm}(x)
are attached to the edges adjacent to
(n,x)
in the lattice,
\psi_{n}^{+}(x)
is the component of wave function coming from above, i.e. coming to
(n-1,x+1)
, so it should be associated with the edge from
(n-1,x+1)
to
(n,x)
, and similarly for
\psi_{n}^{-}(x)
.

The time evolution is the following. Fix some
(n,x)
. It is convenient to write

\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}^{\text{in}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{n}^{\text{in},+}(x)\\
\Psi_{\text{in}}^{\text{in},-}(x)\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi^{+}_{n}(x)\\
\Psi^{-}_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}^{\text{out}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{n}^{\text{out},+}(x)\\
\Psi_{n}^{\text{out},-}(x)\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi^{+}_{n+1}(x-1)\\
\Psi^{-}_{n+1}(x+1)\end{pmatrix}\,.

Then, the time evolution of the wave function is determined by

\mathbf{\Psi}^{\text{out}}_{n}(x)=U_{n,x}\,\mathbf{\Psi}^{\text{in}}_{n}(x)\,.

where
U_{n,x}
is a Haar distributed
2\times 2
unitary matrix (and the family
U_{n,x}
is i.i.d.). Since
U_{n,x}
is unitary

\|\mathbf{\Psi}^{\text{out}}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{\Psi}^{\text{in}}\|^{2}\,.

Moreover, as argued in
[
SKR92
]
,

(A.6)

B_{n,x}:=\frac{|\Psi_{n}^{\text{out},-}(x)|^{2}}{\|\Psi_{n}^{\text{out}}(x)\|^{2}}

does not depend on
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\text{in}}
(because the Haar measure is translation invariant) and is uniformly distributed (because columns of Haar unitary matrices are uniformly distributed on the complex sphere).

Remark A.4
.

One could consider a more general model where
\Psi_{n}^{+}(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
\Psi_{n}^{+}(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
and
U_{n,x}
is Haar distributed in
U(N+M)
. The model would be very similar with
B_{n,x}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(N,M)
.

Proposition A.5
.

Given that at time
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
\lVert\Psi_{n_{0}}^{\text{out}}(x)\|^{2}=\mathsf{p}_{n_{0}}(x)
.
Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n\geqslant n_{0}
we have

\mathsf{p}_{n}(x)=\|\Psi_{n}(x)\|^{2}\,.

Proof.

Using the environment (
A.6
), the proof is analogous to the one of Proposition
A.2
.
∎

Remark A.6
.

We observe that the BW-KMP and the model described in this last subsection can be viewed as random quantum circuits, since the updating rule can be rephrased in a unitary dynamic evolving quantum states – see for instance
[
NVH18
,
FKNV23
]
.
