Probable invasive Alternaria rhinosinusitis in a previously healthy patient diagnosed with heterozygous CARD9 deficiency and combined immunodeficiency
Authors: Chelsea Morinishi, Omai B Garner, Shangxin Yang, Paul R Allyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlternaria is a dematiaceous mold emerging as a cause of invasive fungal infections in immunocompromised patients including invasive fungal rhinosinusitis. Prior case reports have described severe fungal infections in seemingly healthy patients later diagnosed with autosomal recessive CARD9 (caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 9) mutations. Here we report a case of invasive rhinosinusitis due to dematiaceous mold in a previously healthy patient with heterozygous CARD9 mutation and combined immunodeficiency cured with surgical resection and triazole therapy. The causative pathogen was most likely Alternaria species based on histopathologic findings and Next-Generation Sequencing results despite negative tissue cultures. We performed a retrospective review of all adult patients at our institution with Alternaria species identified by culture or polymerase chain reaction and found no other instances of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis. While highlighting the diagnostic dilemmas of our case, we present a comprehensive review of the available literature regarding management and clinical outcomes of dematiaceous mold infections, particularly invasive Alternaria rhinosinusitis, and the clinical implications of CARD9 deficiency.Keywords: Invasive fungal rhinosinusitis, Molecular diagnostics, Immunocompromised, Dematiaceous mold, Alternaria, CARD9 mutation

Case report

A 51-year-old woman with a history of chronic allergic rhinitis, nasal polyposis, and frequent childhood ear infections presented to our hospital with five months of worsening nasal pain and crusting. Prior to onset, she had an extensively documented, recent history of recurrent maxillary and ethmoid sinusitis for which she was treated by otolaryngology with short courses of oral antimicrobials and steroids. Due to a lack of clinical response, she underwent bilateral middle turbinectomy, uncinectomy, and polypectomy three years before presentation. Tissue histopathology at this time had demonstrated chronic sinusitis, sinonasal inflammatory polyps, and granulomatous inflammation with numerous fungal organisms highlighted by Grocott’s methenamine stain (GMS), but no antifungal treatment was initiated due to negative fungal tissue cultures. During the following three years, she developed progressively worsening nasal congestion refractory to a combination of saline rinses, fluticasone and azelastine nasal sprays, and courses of oral steroids plus antimicrobials. Five months prior to presentation, she noticed increased nasal crusting, purulent nasal drainage, and gum swelling. She presented to an outside hospital one month prior to admission and underwent biopsy of the right maxillary sinus that demonstrated chronic granulomatous inflammation with Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) and GMS staining with septate, filamentous microorganisms. There was concern for possible granulomatosis with polyangiitis, for which she was treated with another course of prednisone without antifungal therapy by the outside treating physicians. Within one month, she developed progressive disease with worsening sinus pain, intermittent epistaxis, anosmia, and a new oronasal fistula, prompting presentation to our hospital for expedited multidisciplinary care.

On arrival, physical examination was remarkable for marked gingival recession, an open hard palate defect with surrounding erythema, and bilateral crusting of the nares (Fig. 1). Nasal endoscopy revealed moderate nasal crusting in the right nasal cavity greater than the left, but no areas of necrosis. Laboratory findings showed elevated C-reactive protein of 1.5 mg/dL and a positive ANA of 1:320 but were otherwise unremarkable. Computed tomography (CT) of the face (Fig. 2) showed extensive left greater than right opacification of the maxillary, ethmoid, and frontal sinuses with oronasal fistula and erosive changes of the bilateral medial orbital walls and hard palate. Citrobacter freundii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa grew on superficial sinus cultures. The Infectious Diseases team started isavuconazole (ISAV) given its more favorable side effect profile and broad fungal activity based on the outside pathology report noted above. Cefepime was also initiated for targeted bacterial coverage.

Axial and sagittal views of the CT showing (A) complete opacification of the left maxillary sinus and (B) erosion of the hard palate with fistulization along the posterior hard and soft palate.

Sinus debridement was performed on hospital day 10, wherein septal perforation and extensive necrotic tissue were seen in the maxillary and ethmoid sinuses (Fig. 3). Tissue pathology (Fig. 4A-B) with GMS staining showed septate, acute angle branching fungal organisms with globose structures, and tissue invasion. Due to the severity of her disease and the increasing concern for invasive fungal sinusitis including Mucormycosis, liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) was added to her antifungal regimen. Broad tissue cultures were obtained and additional specimens were sent to the University of Washington for AFB, bacterial, and fungal PCR with reflex to Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS). Surgical fungal and AFB cultures ultimately did not grow. On further review of the pathology slides, globose swelling was noted along the hyphae, which increased suspicion for a dematiaceous mold. After the addition of the Fontana Masson stain to differentiate dematiaceous molds from hyaline molds, the fungal organisms stained positive (Fig. 4C).

Operative view of septal perforation and necrotic tissue of left inferior turbinate.

Pigmented, septate hyphae staining positive with Grocott’s methenamine stain (A-B) and Fontana-Masson stain (C).

L-AmB was discontinued seven days after the first debridement based on the above pathology results. The patient was discharged with ISAV 372 mg daily and 14 days of ciprofloxacin. A few weeks after discharge, tissue fungal NGS results showed Alternaria spp., Candida krusei, Botryotrichum species, Malassezia restricta, and Pichia barkeri. Candida krusei, Malassezia restricta, and Pichia barkeri were suspected to be either colonizers or contaminants due to the absence of yeast on tissue pathology. Alternaria spp. was determined to be the most probable causative pathogen rather than Botryotrichum species as the histopathologic findings (Fontana Masson positive and globose structures) were not consistent with Botryotrichum. She underwent four additional debridements in the outpatient setting given the extent of her disease. After 40 days, ISAV was switched to posaconazole (POS) 300 mg daily due to peripheral edema, which was attributed to the drug. Prior data also showed a more favorable minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for POS compared with ISAV. As she demonstrated radiographic and endoscopic improvement, posaconazole was discontinued at 10.5 months from initial debridement. She remained disease-free at 17 months and successfully underwent reconstructive palate surgery.

Early in her outpatient course, the patient was concurrently evaluated by Immunology for possible primary immunodeficiency disorder. Initial blood tests showed low absolute lymphocyte count (0.96 ×10 ³/µL). Subsequent workup included lymphocyte enumeration and immunoglobulin levels which revealed lymphopenia (CD3 636, CD4 420, and CD8 185) and panhypogammaglobulinemia (IgG 662, IgM 34, and IgA 73). Considering the increased risk for bacterial and fungal infections, Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease, chronic granulomatous disease, leukocyte adhesion deficiency, and cystic fibrosis were also considered, but workup was negative. Whole exome sequencing was then performed for genetic analysis of her combined immunodeficiency, which revealed a heterozygous variant in the CARD9 (caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 9) gene (c.723dupG). Mitogen/antigen proliferation testing revealed low T-cell response to Candida and tetanus antigens, indicating T-cell dysfunction. She initiated subcutaneous immunoglobulin injections every two weeks with normalization of IgG and IgA levels, but persistently low IgM. Her immunizations were updated accordingly, including Pneumococcus, RSV, COVID-19 and influenza.

Methods

With approval from the Institutional Review Board, a retrospective chart review was performed of all adult patients with positive sinus cultures, tissue PCR, and/or blood microbial cell-free DNA for Alternaria spp. at our institution since 2004. Invasive rhinosinusitis was defined as invasion of vasculature or extra-sinonasal involvement of tissues and bone based on pathology, imaging, and/or endoscopic visualization. Clinical characteristics were assessed including age, comorbidities (such as diabetes, hematologic malignancy, neutropenia, solid organ or stem cell transplant), radiographic and endoscopic evaluations, surgical management, antifungal selection and duration. Cure, the primary clinical outcome, was defined as pathology confirming clean surgical margins and/or resolution of symptoms. Recurrence was also assessed through review of clinical notes, imaging, and endoscopy reports. Patients with polymicrobial growth on sinus cultures and positive sinus cultures without radiographic or clinical evidence of invasive disease were excluded.

In addition, a literature review was performed to identify additional adult cases of invasive Alternaria rhinosinusitis and to evaluate for specific risk factors and treatment outcomes related to medical and surgical interventions. A search of Google Scholar and PubMed was performed with the following keywords: “Alternaria”, “invasive rhinosinusitis”, “antifungal”, “immunocompetent,” “immunocompromised,” and “treatment outcomes.” The search yielded case reports and series in English dating from 1981 to 2026. Case studies with pediatric patients, chronic allergic fungal sinusitis, non-invasive rhinosinusitis, incomplete treatment information, and non-English language were excluded.

Results

From our institution, we identified 27 sinus cultures positive for Alternaria species in adult patients. There were no qualifying cases that utilized blood microbial cell-free DNA for diagnosis. On chart review of the patients’ clinical history, imaging, and pathology results, no additional cases of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis were identified other than the case described.

Our literature review yielded 19 case reports and 1 case series of invasive Alternaria rhinosinusitis published between 1977 and 2026 (Table 1). A majority were young patients with a median age of 36 (23 – 74). Among 24 individual cases, a majority were immunocompromised (16/24 cases, 67%) with neutropenia as a frequent concurrent risk factor (13/16 cases, 81%). The degree of neutropenia was not consistently reported. Hematologic malignancy or disorders comprised 88% (14/16 cases) and stem cell transplant (SCT) recipients comprised 44% (7/16 cases) of immunocompromised patients [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Only one patient was diagnosed with a primary immunodeficiency (homozygous CARD9 deficiency) [9]. In contrast, a minority of cases (8/24 cases, 33%) lacked any identifiable risk factors for invasive fungal infection [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Alternaria spp. were identified by routine culture in all cases and tissue invasive disease was confirmed by imaging and/or histopathology. Clinical manifestations varied from a necrotic septal lesion to oronasal fistula.

Almost all cases underwent at least one surgical debridement in combination with antifungal therapy. Antifungal selection and duration varied: a majority received L-AmB monotherapy while recently published cases received L-AmB monotherapy, combination L-AmB with triazole or L-AmB followed by triazole. The triazoles used were itraconazole (ITR), voriconazole (VOR), or POS. No cases were treated with ISAV. Of two patients who were treated with medical management alone, only one achieved cure. Only one patient underwent surgical resection alone and achieved cure. While most (17/21 cases, 81%) patients who received surgical and medical management achieved cure, some required multiple debridements and L-AmB. The earliest published cases resulted in at least one relapse without long-term follow-up despite surgical resection and L-AmB; however, this may suggest an undiagnosed defect in the host response as no risk factors had been identified [15,16]. No infection-related deaths occurred.

Discussion

Our case of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis demonstrates the challenges of determining the causative pathogen when microbiologic and histopathologic workup are discordant. First, the importance of investigation with histological and culture-based methods should be emphasized. The patient underwent maxillary biopsy one month prior to her index hospitalization, which showed evidence of acute branching, septate, and filamentous organisms. It is unclear if tissue cultures were sent, but they may have facilitated more timely diagnosis and treatment. Upon subsequent admission to our hospital, empiric antifungal therapy was initiated several days prior to surgical debridement and tissue cultures, which may have reduced the diagnostic yield of cultures. The growth requirements of the offending pathogen, Alternaria, were likely not a significant factor as dematiaceous molds can grow on most standard mycological media [21].

In contrast, molecular methods including NGS offer higher sensitivity, with the ability to detect small amounts of genetic material from viable and dead fungal organisms [22]. However, this also increases the detection of contaminants or colonizing organisms. In our case, fungal NGS testing detected Alternaria spp., Candida krusei, Botryotrichum species, Malassezia restricta, and Pichia barkeri. While molds can share some morphologic characteristics, histological methods can clarify the diagnosis. Between the two molds Botryotrichum and Alternaria, the globose swellings seen along septate hyphae are more consistent with a dematiaceous mold. Like other molds, Alternaria has septate hyphae which branch at 45 °, but differs by its more fragmented appearance, globose structures, and melanized cell well identifiable with a Fontana-Masson stain [23]. Caution should be applied when interpreting the results of a positive Fontana-Masson as non-dematiaceous molds including Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Zygomycetes have also been shown to produce melanin [24]. Conversely, Botryotrichum is not known to produce melanin nor has it been described as a human pathogen, except in one case of pulmonary Botryotrichum in the setting of chronic granulomatous disease [25]. Based on the clinical context and histopathologic findings, Alternaria is suspected to be the most likely culprit pathogen, though this could not be microbiologically confirmed.

Normally found in soil, wood, and decaying plant matter, dematiaceous molds including Alternaria are significant plant pathogens, but also cause a diverse spectrum of human infections known as phaeohyphomycoses [23]. Clinical manifestations range from superficial infections including onychomycosis and keratitis to brain abscesses and disseminated disease [23]. Immunocompetent patients typically present with allergic fungal sinusitis or allergic bronchopulmonary mycosis, which are commonly due to Bipolaris and Curvularia [23]. Rarely, dematiaceous molds like Cladophialophora bantiana can cause brain abscesses in immunocompetent patients [23]. Among the immunocompromised, Alternaria most frequently causes superficial or localized cutaneous infection: in a systematic review of solid organ transplant recipients, 94% of Alternaria infections were soft tissue and skin infections [21,23,26]. Cutaneous manifestations vary from ulcerative (typically from inoculation) to painless papulonodules which may represent disseminated infection [21]. Like Bipolaris and Curvularia, Alternaria is an emerging cause of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis in solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients and patients with hematologic malignancy [21,26,27]. Based on case reports and series, a majority of adult cases with invasive Alternaria rhinosinusitis are immunocompromised with prior stem cell transplantation or acute leukemia [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8],18]. Alternaria can cause chronic invasive rhinosinusitis with more indolent destruction of the paranasal sinuses and adjacent tissues over several months [27].

According to the 2021 global guideline by the European Conference of Medical Mycology, International Society for Human and Animal Mycology, and the American Society for Microbiology, VOR monotherapy or L-AmB monotherapy or combination with triazoles or echinocandins are moderately recommended as first-line for phaeohyphomycosis overall [28]. Alternatively, a combination of POS or VOR with echinocandins are moderately recommended as other first-line agents for disseminated infection [28]. In contrast, ISAV is marginally recommended as second-line therapy due to a lack of clinical data [28]. One published case of invasive Alternaria sinusitis was successfully treated with ISAV as salvage therapy [29].

MICs can also guide treatment options, although there are no clinical breakpoints to define MIC cutoffs associated with susceptibility. For Alternaria isolates in SOT recipients, Radcliffe et al. showed more favorable MICs for POS (0.125–0.25 μg/mL) and L-AmB (0.04–2.0 μg/mL) than fluconazole (2 to > 64 μg/mL) [26]. VOR MICs were variable (0.125–4 μg/mL) [26]. No information was available for ISAV. Initially, our patient was treated with ISAV due to its more favorable side effect profile, but switched to POS based on more favorable clinical data and ISAV intolerance. After 5 surgical debridements and an extended duration of POS, our patient achieved clinical cure. Antifungal therapy should be continued until clinical, radiographic, and histopathologic clearance of infection.

Healthy patients who develop severe or invasive phaeohyphomycoses should undergo immunologic evaluation to elucidate potential primary immunodeficiency disorders. Our patient appeared healthy, but reported a history of mucosal infections in childhood and refractory pansinusitis in adulthood. To investigate primary T-cell or B-cell deficiencies and phagocytic disorders, immunologic workup initially comprised lymphocyte enumeration, immunoglobulin levels, Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease panel, neutrophil oxidative function test, and Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency panel followed by mitogen/antigen proliferation testing and whole exome sequencing. Immunologic workup revealed several primary immunodeficiencies: heterozygous CARD9 gene variant (c.723dupG) on whole exome sequencing, lymphopenia, impaired T-cell function, and panhypogammaglobulinemia, conferring defects in the innate and adaptive immune system. CARD9 or caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 9 is an adaptor protein that mediates signals from pattern recognition receptors Dectin-1, Dectin-2, and macrophage-inducible C-type lectin by stimulating cytokine release in response to fungal pathogens [9]. CARD9 deficiency is autosomal recessive, resulting in protein loss-of-function, leading to impaired neutrophil function and recruitment and killing by monocytes and macrophages [9,23]. To date, this is the only mutation with pathognomonic association with phaeohyphomycosis, but also chronic and recurrent candidiasis and dermatophytosis [23]. Compared to homozygous mutations classically found in autosomal recessive CARD9 deficiency, our patient only had one heterozygous CARD9 variant. This is consistent with prior data suggesting variable phenotypes for CARD9 deficiency or dysfunction depending on gene dosage [30]. Our experience highlights the importance of immunologic investigation to determine other infectious risks and to guide preventive and immunomodulatory strategies.

Conclusion

Accurate diagnosis of invasive fungal infections relies on a combination of histologic and culture methods. Molecular diagnostics can facilitate identification of rare fungal pathogens but may also introduce diagnostic uncertainty when multiple pathogens are detected. In our case, histologic methods and clinical context were essential to clarifying Alternaria as the most likely etiology of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis. This case also contributes to the growing literature on invasive Alternaria rhinosinusitis in the setting of combined immunodeficiency and heterozygous CARD9 deficiency, successfully treated with surgery and extended triazole therapy. Refractory sinus infections with progressive destruction of surrounding tissue should elicit a high index of suspicion for occult mold infections, including dematiaceous molds.
