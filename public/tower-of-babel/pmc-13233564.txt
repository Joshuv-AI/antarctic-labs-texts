KPT-330 alleviates osteoarthritis by inhibiting subchondral bone osteoclastogenesis via the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways
Authors: Wugui Chen, Yijie Liang, Chengzhao Liu, Guangfeng Ling, Shiqing Lei, Jiaqing Huang, Wang Lin, Chengshou Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOsteoclast-driven subchondral bone remodeling is a critical driver of osteoarthritis (OA) progression. While exportin-1 (XPO1) inhibitors demonstrate anti-osteoclastogenic potential in cancers, their therapeutic utility in OA remains unexplored. We hypothesized that selective XPO1 inhibition by KPT-330 suppresses osteoclastogenesis to ameliorate OA. In destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)-induced OA mice, KPT-330 reversed subchondral bone loss, preserved cartilage integrity, and reduced osteoclast numbers. In vitro, KPT-330 (≤50 nM) non-toxically inhibited RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. Mechanistically, KPT-330 suppressed p65 phosphorylation/nuclear translocation and attenuated MAPK signaling (p38, ERK1/2, and JNK), thereby downregulating osteoclastogenic transcription factors c-Fos and NFATc1. These findings establish XPO1 inhibition as a potential dual-action strategy targeting osteoclast-mediated bone-cartilage crosstalk in OA.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a widespread, long-term condition that primarily impacts weight-bearing joints such as the hips and knees and is characterized by pain, rigidity, and restricted movement. Although knee arthroplasty provides substantial benefits for advanced OA patients, it is associated with complications, including periprosthetic fractures, implant instability, and the financial burden of surgery.1 Consequently, advancing our understanding of OA and implementing early interventions are critical to clinical practice.2 The key pathological features of OA include the breakdown of articular cartilage, changes in subchondral bone, and the development of osteophytes. An increasing body of evidence suggests that osteoclasts play a role in the regulation of bone metabolism at the articular surface; nonetheless, the exact mechanisms are yet to be completely clarified.3,4 The exploration of the role of osteoclasts in OA and the development of targeted therapies are emerging as key research areas and may lead to promising new therapeutic options.5

Osteoclast maturation and bone resorption involve multiple complex processes, including induction, aggregation, fusion, differentiation, and bone resorption. These processes necessitate the coordinated action of numerous signaling pathways, notably the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, with nuclear protein transport serving as a critical step. Exportin-1 (XPO1/CRM1) is a well-characterized nuclear export protein that plays a significant role in the transport of various proteins, including tumor suppressor proteins (TSPs) such as p53, IKB, and p37, from the nucleus to the cytoplasm.6 Its function is particularly relevant in the context of cancer, where XPO1 is often upregulated, leading to increased export of TSPs and promotion of cancer progression. Small-molecule selective inhibitors of nuclear export (SINE) compounds, including selinexor (KPT-330) and eltanexor (KPT-8602), are currently being tested in clinical trials to determine their efficacy in treating hematologic and solid tumors.7,8,9,10

Additionally, in severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) mouse models bearing human multiple myeloma (MM) bone lesions, SINE compounds, including KPT-330 and KPT-8602, demonstrated significant anti-MM activity, inhibited bone resorption, and prolonged survival.11 Gravina et al.12 further established the efficacy of SINEs (KPT-330 and KPT-251) in mitigating bone destruction due to prostate cancer metastasis, suggesting an impact on proangiogenic and pro-osteolytic cytokine secretion and osteoclastogenesis in vitro, with minimal impact on osteoblasts and bone marrow stromal cells.11,12 Chen et al. further elucidated the anti-osteoclastic and osteoporosis-mitigating effects of the selective nuclear export inhibitor eltanexor (KPT-8602), demonstrating its impact on the nuclear accumulation of the IκBα-NF-κB p65 complex and subsequent inhibition of osteoclastogenesis in vitro.13 On the basis of these findings, XPO1 inhibitors may be considered to have significant effects on osteoclastogenesis; however, few studies have reported whether XPO1 inhibitors can serve as potential therapeutics for OA. Among SINE compounds, selinexor (KPT-330) was selected given its advanced translational status and well-characterized target engagement as an XPO1 inhibitor. We reasoned that repurposing KPT-330 to modulate osteoclast-driven subchondral remodeling could provide a pragmatic path toward disease-modifying strategies in OA, which has not been directly tested in destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)-induced OA models.

Result

Toxicity study and dose selection of intraperitoneal KPT-330

To ensure uniform drug absorption across all groups, intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of KPT-330 was employed based on established protocols. As shown in Tables S1 and S2, KPT-330 exhibited a favorable safety profile at lower doses, with dose-dependent toxicities emerging exclusively at 20 mg/kg. Body weight monitoring revealed no significant changes at 5 and 10 mg/kg throughout the 8-week observation period, whereas 20 mg/kg induced significant weight reduction from week 2 onward (p < 0.05). No significant alterations in hematological parameters (white blood cell count [WBC] and hemoglobin [HGB]) or biochemical markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], blood urea nitrogen [BUN], and creatinine [Cr]) were observed at 5 and 10 mg/kg; however, marked abnormalities were detected at 20 mg/kg (p < 0.05). These findings confirm that i.p. administration of KPT-330 possesses an adequate therapeutic safety window at doses up to 10 mg/kg based on body weight, hematologic parameters, and serum biochemistry (Tables S1 and S2).

KPT-330 ameliorates subchondral bone loss in DMM-induced OA

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of KPT-330 in OA, we surgically induced OA via DMM in the knee of 10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice. The mice were randomized to receive 5 or 10 mg/kg KPT-330 i.p. twice weekly for 8 weeks (Figure 1A). Micro-CT analysis of the tibial subchondral bone at the endpoint revealed significant bone loss in DMM + vehicle mice compared to sham controls. This was also characterized by a lower bone volume fraction (BV/TV, Figure 1D), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, Figure 1F), trabecular number (Tb.N, Figure 1G), and connectivity density (Conn.Dn, Figure 1I) (all p < 0.05). Concurrently, DMM + vehicle-treated mice showed increased osteophyte scores (Figure 1C; p < 0.05), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, Figure 1E; p > 0.05), and structure model index (SMI, Figure 1H; p < 0.05). KPT-330 ameliorates cartilage degradation in DMM-induced OA

Histological assessment of knee joints via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and safranin O/fast green staining confirmed severe articular cartilage degeneration following DMM surgery (Figure 2A). H&E staining showed reduced hyaline cartilage thickness and expansion of the calcified cartilage (CC) layer. The hyaline cartilage-to-total articular cartilage (HC/TAC) ratio was significantly lower in the DMM + vehicle group (17.50% ± 9.35%) than in sham controls (63.83% ± 9.70%). KPT-330 treatment increased this ratio to 31.67% ± 12.91% (5 mg/kg) and 47.50% ± 13.69% (10 mg/kg) (Figure 2D, p < 0.05). Safranin O/fast green staining demonstrated profound proteoglycan loss, which was attenuated by KPT-330. Consistent with this, blinded OARSI scoring (Osteoarthritis Research Society International) was markedly elevated in DMM + vehicle mice (12.83 ± 2.99) versus sham (0.33 ± 0.41) and was significantly reduced by KPT-330 treatment to 8.67 ± 2.16 and 3.50 ± 1.05 at 5 and 10 mg/kg, respectively (Figure 2C, p < 0.05). In addition to cartilage degeneration, DMM surgery induced prominent osteophyte formation at the joint margins. Quantitative IHC revealed that KPT-330 simultaneously reins in both arms of this vicious cycle: the percentage of MMP-9-positive cells increased from 15.40% ± 5.09% to 60.07% ± 9.50% (#p < 0.05) and was reduced to 29.57% ± 5.00% (low dose) and 15.43% ± 5.54% (high dose) (versus DMM, ∗p < 0.05); the percentage of IL-1β-positive cells climbed from 21.28% ± 4.38% (sham) to 56.15% ± 8.21% (DMM) and was brought down to 36.00% ± 5.00% and 21.38% ± 6.29% (#p < 0.05); the percentage of TNF-α-positive cells rose from 7.23% ± 3.49% (sham) to 47.50% ± 5.52% (DMM) and fell to 26.02% ± 5.91% (low dose) and 17.40% ± 3.31% (high dose) (versus DMM, ∗p < 0.05). IC50 shifted from 323.3 ± 18.2 nM at 24 h to 110.5 ± 9.7 nM at 96 h (Figure 4A), indicating time-dependent CRM1 inhibition. Importantly, ≤50 nM KPT-330 preserved ≥95% viability at all time points, whereas ≥100 nM evoked a sharp loss, Consequently, 0–50 nM was selected for all downstream assays. Within the non-toxic window (≤50 nM), KPT-330 imposed a concentration-dependent brake on osteoclastogenesis (Figures 4B, 4D, and 4E). After 7-day priming with M-CSF (30 ng/mL) plus RANKL (50 ng/mL), TRAP+ stained multinucleated osteoclasts covered 41.55% ± 6.87% of the culture area and a cell number of 6.75 ± 1.17 per field (n = 6). Addition of 12.5 nM KPT-330 reduced the area to 23.23% ± 6.28%, and cell number to 4.83 ± 0.75 (p < 0.05); 25 nM further lowered both parameters to 12.09 ± 5.30% and 4.17 ± 0.75 (p < 0.05); at 50 nM, osteoclast occupancy was restricted to 8.01% ± 3.43%, with only 2.83 ± 0.75 giant cells remaining (p < 0.05 versus control), translating to an 80.72% suppression of planar area and 58.07% reduction in cell number.

The functional impact of KPT-330 on osteoclastic bone resorption was quantified in parallel on bovine cortical slices and Osteo-Assay plates (Figures 4C and 4F). RANKL + M-CSF stimulation generated resorption pits covering 57.33% ± 8.87% of the plate surface, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging of cortical slices revealed pronounced Howship’s lacunae. KPT-330 dose dependently restricted the excavated area to 15.30% ± 5.17% at 25 nM and 2.00% ± 2.10% at 50 nM (p < 0.05), while markedly shrinking resorption lacunae on bone slices. Collectively, these data demonstrated that, within its non-toxic range, the compound not only suppresses osteoclast formation but also cripples their resorptive capacity.

KPT-330 suppresses the osteoclastogenic transcriptional program

Real-time qPCR showed that KPT-330 (25–50 nM) dose dependently reversed RANKL + M-CSF-driven induction of osteoclastogenic transcripts. At 50 nM, expression relative to baseline was reduced to 47% for c-Fos, 52% for NFATc1, 79% for CTSK, and 97% for TRAP (n = 3; p < 0.05 versus 0 group; Figure 5A. Time-course immunoblots showed that NFATc1 and c-Fos peaked at days 1–5 after RANKL + M-CSF induction; 50 nM KPT-330 attenuated these peaks by 68% and 61% (p < 0.05; Figure 5B) and concurrently blunted osteoclastogenic CTSK induction by 64% (p < 0.05; Figure 5C). Moreover, KPT-330 significantly suppressed IL-1β-stimulated MMP-9 upregulation by 47% (p < 0.05; Figure 5D). In vitro, RANKL + M-CSF triggered rapid p65 phosphorylation at 30 min, and 50 nM KPT-330 curtailed p-p65 abundance by 71% without altering total p65 (Figures 6C and 6D; p < 0.05). Discussion

OA is a chronic and disabling joint disorder characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, aberrant subchondral bone remodeling, and synovial inflammation. The pathogenic interplay within the osteochondral unit, particularly between cartilage and subchondral bone, is central to OA progression, making this interface a critical target for disease-modifying strategies.4,5,25 In the initial stages of OA, abnormal increases in the number and activity of subchondral osteoclasts lead to excessive bone resorption, disruption of the bone remodeling equilibrium, and consequent subchondral bone loss, marrow cavity enlargement, and reduced bone mineral density.3,13,26 While drugs like denosumab and bisphosphonates alleviate OA-related pain by inhibiting osteoclast activity and thus bone resorption,5,27 the development of safe and specific therapies remains challenging, limited by potential side effects.2,5,27 This underscores the need for agents with broader mechanisms of action for OA treatment.

Key findings and therapeutic implications

This study establishes that pharmacological inhibition of XPO1 with KPT-330 confers potent, dose-dependent protection against structural deterioration in a murine OA model. Our in vivo data demonstrate that KPT-330 treatment effectively preserved subchondral bone architecture, reversing the osteoporotic changes (reduced BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N) induced by DMM surgery (Figure 1). Concurrently, KPT-330 mitigated cartilage degradation, as evidenced by improved histological scores (OARSI), increased HC/TAC ratio, and enhanced retention of aggrecan and collagen-II (Figure 2). The therapeutic efficacy of KPT-330 stems from a dual mechanism: direct suppression of osteoclast-driven bone resorption and inhibition of osteoclast-mediated cartilage catabolism. This was substantiated by the dose-dependent reduction in subchondral osteoclast number (TRAP staining) and the coordinated downregulation of key catabolic (MMP-9) and pro-inflammatory (IL-1β and TNF-α) factors in the joint (Figure 3). Moreover, many studies have confirmed its protective effects on osteoblasts and long bones.11,12,13 This multi-target action profile positions KPT-330 beyond pure anti-resorptives, offering a more comprehensive strategy to halt the interdependent deterioration of the osteochondral unit.27,28

Targeting osteoclasts: Beyond pure anti-resorption

Pathological activation of osteoclasts is a key driver of aberrant subchondral bone remodeling in OA. However, their contribution to joint destruction extends beyond bone resorption, involving non-resorptive, secretory mechanisms that directly promote cartilage degradation, which are less understood.29,30 Our findings demonstrate that KPT-330 significantly reduces articular levels of the key proteolytic enzyme MMP-9, along with the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α.31,32 By simultaneously curtailing bone resorption and the secretion of these catabolic mediators, KPT-330 disrupts a critical arm of the bone-cartilage vicious cycle. This approach aligns with the evolving understanding that effective OA therapy must address the non-resorptive, signaling functions of osteoclasts.4,30 However, further investigations are warranted to explore the effects of KPT-330 on other factors, including inflammation, angiogenesis, and innervation.4,30

Osteoclast-chondrocyte crosstalk in OA

Subchondral bone remodeling and cartilage degeneration are intricately linked through bidirectional biochemical communication across the osteochondral interface. In this study, KPT-330 simultaneously suppressed osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and reduced articular levels of MMP-9, IL-1β, and TNF-α, suggesting interruption of this pathological crosstalk.33 Beyond their bone-resorptive function, osteoclasts actively contribute to cartilage degradation through the secretion of matrix-degrading enzymes and pro-inflammatory cytokines that diffuse into the articular cartilage.4,30,34 Recent evidence demonstrates that osteoclast-derived exosomes and extracellular vesicles can transfer bioactive molecules, including miRNAs and proteins, directly to chondrocytes, promoting catabolic gene expression and accelerating OA progression.34 For instance, miR-212-3p within osteoclast exosomes can lead to chondrocyte dysfunction by suppressing the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway.35 Understanding the role of osteoclasts in cartilage maintenance and degradation may help in identifying new therapeutic approaches for pathologies characterized by cartilage degeneration. However, direct evidence for KPT-330-mediated modulation of osteoclast-chondrocyte communication was not investigated.36 Future studies utilizing transwell co-culture systems or osteoclast-derived conditioned medium treatments will be essential to delineate cell-autonomous versus indirect effects of XPO1 inhibition on cartilage homeostasis.

Mechanistic insights: Overlapping inhibition of the MAPK-NFATc1 axis

In OA, the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways act as synergistic inflammatory drivers yet exhibit distinct regulatory roles.18,37,38 During osteoclast differentiation, NF-κB acts as the core switch, specializing in rapid and potent transcription of genes such as NFATc1, while MAPK mediates a broader and more sustained stress response by regulating the expression of osteocyte-specific genes (e.g., TRAP and CTSK), influencing osteoclast fusion, functional maturation, and factor regulation. Additionally, the hierarchical interplay between MAPK and NF-κB pathways is complex and bidirectional; for instance, MAPK can activate NF-κB, and NF-κB can regulate the expression of MAPK components. Previous studies have established the osteoclast-inhibiting effects of XPO1 inhibitors in cancer and osteoporosis.12,13,39 Mechanistically, the anti-osteoclastogenic effects of XPO1 inhibitors are attributed to their ability to block the nuclear export of the IκBα-NF-κB p65 complex, thereby trapping this inhibitory complex in the nucleus and preventing NF-κB activation. Early work by Tai et al. (2014) demonstrated that SINE compounds (KPT-185/KPT-330) suppressed NF-κB/NFATc1 activation in myeloma-associated osteolysis.11 Similarly, Chen et al. (2022) later identified that the anti-osteoclastogenic effect of KPT-8602 in osteoporosis depends on the nuclear accumulation of IκBα-p65 complex, which directly impairs RANKL-induced NF-κB signaling.13 Consistent with these established mechanisms, our study showed that KPT-330 significantly reduced p65 phosphorylation and its nuclear translocation (Figure 6C). Although we did not directly assess inhibitor of NF-κB alpha (IκBα) phosphorylation and degradation, these collective findings—supported by prior mechanistic studies—strongly suggest that KPT-330 attenuates osteoclastogenesis through XPO1-mediated interference with the canonical IκBα-NF-κB p65 pathway.

Consistent with the effects of other dual-pathway inhibitors like toddalolactone and neratinib,18,40 our study found that KPT-330 modulated both pathways. We confirmed that KPT-330 inhibited NF-κB activation, as shown by reduced p-p65 levels and impaired p65 nuclear translocation. Notably, we identified a robust inhibitory effect on the MAPK pathway, with KPT-330 attenuating RANKL-induced phosphorylation of p38, ERK, and JNK during osteoclast differentiation, leading to downstream downregulation of NFATc1 and c-Fos. NF-κB inhibition is consistent with the known XPO1-IκB axis, whereas MAPK dampening may reflect altered nucleocytoplasmic trafficking of MAPK regulators; further mechanistic work is warranted.

MAPK signaling is not only crucial for osteoclast differentiation but also has a well-established role in chondrocyte catabolism and synovial inflammation.41 In chondrocytes, MAPK activation drives the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) and matrix-degrading enzymes, fueling a catabolic state.18,37 Therefore, by concurrently dampening MAPK signaling in both osteoclasts and chondrocytes, KPT-330 effectively targets a central node in the pathogenic bone-cartilage crosstalk, positioning it as a dual-target inhibitor capable of interrupting the vicious cycle in OA.1,5,42

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identifies KPT-330 as a promising disease-modifying agent for OA. Its primary mechanism involves concurrent suppression of the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, leading to the inhibition of pathological osteoclastogenesis and a reduction in the secretion of catabolic and inflammatory factors. This dual action protects both subchondral bone and articular cartilage through a central signaling node. These findings position XPO1 inhibition as a potential therapeutic approach capable of targeting the multi-tissue pathology of OA.

Limitations of the study

Our findings support XPO1 inhibition as a potential OA therapy, but several limitations exist. First, the DMM model in young male mice limits generalizability to aging, metabolic OA, and female populations. Second, i.p. administration of KPT-330 (5–10 mg/kg) lacked formal safety validation (e.g., histopathology) and pharmacokinetic comparison to the oral route, despite showing no overt toxicity within effective ranges. Future studies should evaluate intra-articular delivery to minimize systemic exposure, enable lower doses, and clarify direct joint effects via comparative pharmacokinetics studies. Third, we focused on subchondral bone changes in the knee, with preserved architecture observed; however, systemic effects on long bone mineral density or cortical parameters remain unexplored due to micro-CT limitations (optimized for trabecular bone, precluding reliable cortical data extraction). Fourth, while IHC showed preserved aggrecan, direct evidence of reduced cleavage by MMP-13/ADAMTS5 (via neoepitope antibodies anti-ITEGE/anti-DIPEN) is needed. Finally, direct chondrocyte effects were not assessed, necessitating future co-culture and explant studies to distinguish cell-autonomous mechanisms from indirect mechanisms, and the direct mechanistic link between XPO1 inhibition and MAPK regulation also requires further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (10-week-old, 24–26 g) were housed at 22 ± 2 °under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (IACUCFJMU2025-Y-2864) and followed the ARRIVE guidelines.

Method details

Drug administration and toxicity study

The safety profile of KPT-330 was evaluated in male C57BL/6 mice (n = 6 per group) following established protocols.14,15,16 KPT-330 was formulated by dissolving in DMSO and diluting with PBS to achieve final DMSO concentrations not exceeding 10%. Animals received intraperitoneal injections of KPT-330 at a volume of 100 μL per 20 g body weight (equivalent to 5 mL/kg), delivering doses of 5, 10, or 20 mg/kg twice weekly for 8 consecutive weeks. Body weights were monitored weekly to assess treatment-related toxicity. At study termination, blood was collected terminally via cardiac puncture under isoflurane anesthesia. Hematological analysis encompassed white blood cell count (WBC) and hemoglobin (HGB) measurements. Comprehensive serum biochemistry was performed to assess hepatic function (alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)) and renal function (blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine(Cr)) using automated clinical chemistry analyzers.

DMM model and randomization

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and underwent destabilisation of the medial meniscus (DMM) on the knee as previously described. Sham-operated mice underwent capsulotomy without ligament transection. Based on the KPT-330 dosage reference from the literature and animal safety validation studies (Tables S1 and S2). DMM animals were randomly assigned (random number generator) to four groups (n = 6 each group) and receive KPT-330 injected intraperitoneally twice weekly for 8 weeks, first dose was administered on postoperative day 3: (1)Sham Group: Sham +PBS; (2)DMM group: DMM + PBS; (3)DMM low-dosage group: DMM + KPT-330 5 mg/kg; (4)DMM high-dosage group: DMM + KPT-330 10 mg/kg. Randomisation and surgical procedures were performed blinded to group allocation.

Micro-CT

Micro-CT (Bruker SkyScan 1272, USA) was used to examine knee joints at a resolution of 9 μm, with an X-ray voltage of 75 kV and a current of 46 μA. The resulting images underwent correction, analysis, and reconstruction by using the DataViewer, CTAn v1.14, and CTvox v3.0 software. The osteophyte scoring system is based on the size and maturity of osteophytes (red dashed box in Figure 1A), and is divided into four grades as previous reported.17 The following subchondral bone parameters were quantified18,19: bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular number (Tb.N, 1/mm), structure model index (SMI), and connectivity density (Conn.Dn, 1/mm3).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Mouse knee joint tissues were fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA for two weeks, embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm in the sagittal plane. Safranin-O/fast green and H&E staining were performed according to standard protocols. Cartilage degradation was graded by two blinded observers using the OARSI scoring system (0–24 scale).20 Medial tibial osteophyte scores and maturity scores were evaluated according to the grading system developed by C. Little and Muschter.21,22 Size scores, maturity scores and the sum of both scores were each averaged over the number of sections analyzed per animal and recorded. In addition, the sum of both scores is presented.

For IHC,13 endogenous peroxidase was quenched (3% H2O2, 15 min), blocked (5% goat serum, 1 h) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (see Key Resources Table, 1:200-1:1000). Signal was developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with haematoxylin. Negative controls received isotype IgG. The intensity of IHC staining was quantified using ImageJ software. Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and a color threshold was applied to select DAB-positive areas (hue: 0–25, saturation: 100–255, brightness: 0–200). The number of positively stained cells was counted in each field (40× objective), and the percentage of positively stained area within the articular cartilage region of interest was measured using ImageJ. Two blinded observers independently scored each section, and the percentage of stained area was calculated as (positive area/total area) × 100%.

Isolation and culture of BMDMs

Bone marrow cells were isolated and cultured as previous reported.13,23 Bone marrow was flushed from femora/tibiae of 7-week-old male C57BL/6 mice with α-MEM+10% FBS+ 1.0% P/S. Cells were plated at 1.0×106/cm2 in α-MEM containing 30 ng/mL M-CSF for 3 days, then lifted with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA and re-plated for experiments.

Cell activity

BMDMs were plated in 96-well plates at 1×104 cells/well. Following a 24-h incubation, the medium was replaced with α-MEM containing different concentrations of KPT-330 (ranging from 0 to 500 nM,100 μL/well). At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, the spent medium was replaced with 100 μl MTT reagent (5 mg/mL stock in PBS). After 4 h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2, protected from light), without removing medium, 0.1 mL acidified isopropanol (0.04 N HCl in isopropanol) was added to each well and mixed thoroughly by repeated pipetting. Absorbance was read within 1 h on an ELISA plate reader at 570 nm (test) and 630 nm (reference).

Osteoclast differentiation (TRAP assay)

BMDMs were seeded at 2×104 cells well in 24-well plates with 30 ng/mL M-CSF+50 ng/mL RANKL ± KPT-330 (0-50 nM) for 7 days (medium refreshed daily). Cells were fixed (4% PFA) and stained for TRAP. Multinucleated (≥3 nuclei) TRAP+ cells were counted in five fields per well.

Bone resorption assay

BMDMs (1×104) were cultured on bovine cortical bone slices and osteo assay surface 96-well plates with M-CSF+RANKL+KPT-330 (0–50 nM) for 9 days. Resorption bovine cortical bone slices were visualised with 1% toluidine blue and imaged by SEM (Hitachi S-3400N). Osteo Assay Surface plates were washed twice with PBS, incubated with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min to remove cells, rinsed with distilled water. Wells were rinsed 3 times with ultrapure water, gently pipetted to remove cell debris. To enhance contrast, 200 μL/well 1% toluidine blue was applied for 3 min, followed by three washes with ultrapure water until the rinse was clear; plates were blotted dry and left to air-dry completely. This stains resorption pits as pale-purple circular erosions against a dark mineralized background, while any residual cells stain deep purple. Images of the entire well were acquired using a light microscope with a ×40 objective. For analysis in ImageJ, the image was opened and converted to 8-bit, and the contrast was increased to further enhance the outline of the erosion rings. The Magic Wand tool was used to manually outline each erosion ring, pressing Backspace to delete the interior color for clearer outlines. Subsequently, Image-Adjust-Threshold was used to automatically select all erosion outlines. Resorbed area was calculated as (white pit area/total well area) ×100%.24 Each condition was run in triplicate wells per experiment, and three independent experiments using separate BMDMs preparations were performed.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted (RNA Prep Kit) and reverse-transcribed (TransScript). qPCR was performed with SYBR-Green on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Relative expression was calculated by 2ˆ−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as reference.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA +1% protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins (30 μg) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF, blocked (5% non-fat milk) and probed with primary antibodies (1:500-1:1000, 4 °C, overnight) followed by HRP-conjugated secondary (1:5000-1:8000, 1 h). Bands were visualised with ECL and quantified with ImageJ. To ensure the specificity of Western blotting for phosphorylated and total proteins, such as phosphorylated p65 (p-P65) and total p65, equal amounts of sample were loaded onto two separate gels. This allowed for independent probing of p-P65 and total p65. Stripping and reprobing protocols were employed to enhance specificity. Initially, membranes were probed with the p-P65 antibody, developed with ECL, and then stripped using Restore PLUS Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Scientific, 46430) for 15 min at room temperature. Following thorough washing with TBST, membranes were re-blocked and re-probed with the GAPDH antibody. GAPDH served as the loading control, and protein expression levels were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software. The integrated density of each band was normalized to GAPDH and expressed as a fold change relative to the control group.

Immunofluorescence

BMDMs on glass coverslips were fixed (4% PFA), permeabilised (0.2% Triton X-100), blocked (5% goat serum) and incubated with anti-P65 (1:200) overnight. Alexa 555 conjugated secondary (1:500) and DAPI were applied. Images were acquired on Zeiss LSM 880 and processed identically.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data distribution was tested with Shapiro-Wilk test; homogeneity of variance was assessed by Levene test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD and analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD post hoc test. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed with Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn multiple-comparison post hoc. p < 0.05 was considered significant. The statistical details of all experiments can be found in the legend. All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate (technical replicates) with cells isolated from three independent mice (biological replicates). Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 7.0 and SPSS 22.0.
