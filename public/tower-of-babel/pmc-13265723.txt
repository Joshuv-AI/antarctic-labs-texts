The ubiquitin receptor StDA1 negatively regulates aurantio-obtusin biosynthesis by mediating the degradation of the StHDR1 protein in Senna tora seeds
Authors: Shuang Liu, Xuecui Yin, Wenjing Yang, Ann Abozeid, Dongfeng Yang, Zongsuo Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aurantio-obtusin is one of the major active components in Senna tora (S. tora) seeds. Although the ubiquitin receptor DA1 regulates plant seed size, it remains unclear if StDA1 regulates the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds. In this study, StDA1 is identified as a negative regulator of aurantio-obtusin production in S. tora seeds. Antisense overexpression of StDA1 led to larger seeds in S. tora and promoted the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin. In contrast, overexpression of StDA1 resulted in a decrease in aurantio-obtusin accumulation in seeds. Moreover, using StDA1 as a bait protein, we identified as an interacting factor, StHDR1, a key enzyme in the aurantio-obtusin biosynthesis pathway. StHDR1 is able to positively regulate aurantio-obtusin accumulation in S. tora seeds, which is the opposite effect of StDA1. Through its interaction with StHDR1, StDA1 promotes StHDR1 degradation via the 26S proteasome, thereby reducing the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin. In conclusion, StDA1 is a regulator that plays a role in the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S.tora seeds by modulating the stability of the StHDR1 protein. This discovery points to the StDA1-StHDR1 complex as a key regulatory node that could potentially be targeted to enhance the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds.

Introduction

Senna tora (S. tora) is a commonly used Chinese medicinal plant with a high medicinal and nutritional value. In the food industry, S. tora seeds were adopted as an ingredient for the production of beverages and teas1; in the medical field, S. tora seeds were applied in the treatment of numerous diseases including cardiovascular diseases, psoriasis, diabetes, and dermatological disorders2–4. This pharmaceutical action is owing to the presence of anthraquinones in mature seeds5,6. However, the qualification rate of anthraquinone content in S. tora seeds in the current market was reported to be 33.3%7. Thus, with the growing demand for S. tora seeds in the food and pharmaceutical industries, there is an urgent need to increase the yield quantity and quality.

4-Hydroxy-3-methylbut-2-enyl diphosphate reductase (HDR, also known as cracking tolerance B (Lyt B) or isoprenoid synthesis H (Isp H)) catalyzes the final step of the 2-C-methyl-d-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway to synthesize isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP)8. The MEP pathway is responsible for providing common precursors (IPP and DMAPP) for the biosynthesis of various terpenoids in plants and plays an important role in regulating the biosynthetic pathways of plant secondary metabolites. Overexpression of the LeHDR was reported to enhance carotenoids in ripe tomato fruits and Arabidopsis thaliana (A. thaliana) leaf seedlings, while co-expression with the taxadiene synthase enhanced taxadiene accumulation9. Overexpression of SmHDR1 enhanced the accumulation of tanshinones in the hairy roots of Salvia miltiorrhiza (S. miltiorrhiza)10. Overexpression of AaHDR1 increased the content of artemisinin, artemisinin B, other sesquiterpenes, and monoterpenes in Artemisia annua (A. annua)11. A mutant of osisph1 was able to reduce the biosynthesis of chlorophylls and carotenoids in the leaves and seeds of rice plants12. It implies that HDR genes are conserved and important in regulating secondary metabolite accumulation in different species. In S. tora, the MEP pathway is one of the pathways for the biosynthesis of aurantio-obtusin. Although the regulation of terpenoids biosynthesis by HDR gene has been well described, the mechanism of HDR gene regulating aurantio-obtusin biosynthesis remains to be elucidated.

Aurantio-obtusin accumulates only in the S. tora seeds, so seed size is not only an important indicator to measure seed yield of S. tora seeds, but also an important condition to affect the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds. The coordinated growth of the seed coat, endosperm, and embryo is regulated by both maternal and zygotic tissues, which establish the seed size13. The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway was found to be one of the major signaling pathways controlling seed size by maternal tissues. The pathway consists of ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1), ubiquitin-coupled enzyme (E2), and ubiquitin ligase (E3)14, and the E3 ligase is used for specific recognition and combining with substrates15. In A. thaliana, DA1 is recognized as a ubiquitin receptor with two ubiquitin-interaction motif (UIM) structural domains and a Lin11/ is-1/Mec-3 (LIM) structural domain at its N-terminus, as well as a peptidase structural domain at the C-terminus16,17. The modulation of cell proliferation by the DA1 pathway is crucial in seed size regulation in the maternal system. DA1 and its homolog da1-related gene (DAR1) can negatively regulate seed size. The simultaneous deletion of DA1 and DAR1 leads to larger seeds. The E3 ligases DA2 and Enhancer of DA1 (EOD1)/Big brother (BB), as well as the deubiquitinating enzyme Ubiouitin-specific protease15 (UBP15)/Suppressor2 of DA1 (SOD2), are all able to interact with DA1 to synergistically regulate seed and organs size17–19. Genetic analyses have shown that UBP15 has opposite roles to DA2 and EOD1/BB in regulating seed and organ size13. In other species, the DA1 pathway also plays an essential role in the regulation of seed size. For instance, down-regulation of BnDA1 in Brassica napus (B. napus) increased its seed yield20; overexpression of mutated zmda1 or zmdar1 in maize increased grain yield21; and similarly, overexpression of TaDA1 reduces the size and weight of wheat grains22. However, in rice, the homolog of AtDA1, the ubiquitin-interacting motif-type ubiquitin receptor (HOMOLOG OF DA1 ON RICE CHROMOSOME 3, HDR3) positively regulates grain size by promoting spikelet shell cell division23. In conclusion, the DA1 pathway can effectively increase seed yield, but whether it affects the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in seeds needs to be further elaborated.

In our investigation, we explored the properties of the ubiquitin receptor gene StDA1 from S. tora, revealing that StDA1 suppresses plant growth, results in smaller seed sizes, and diminishes aurantio-obtusin accumulation. Further analysis demonstrated that StDA1 and StHDR1 exert antagonistic effects on aurantio-obtusin accumulation. Furthermore, StDA1 controlling aurantio-obtusin biosynthesis by affecting StHDR1 protein stability. Ultimately, our results provided new insights into the biosynthetic mechanism of aurantio-obtusin accumulation in S. tora seeds. Meanwhile, it also provides a molecular target for genetic selection of high content S. tora cultivars.

Results

Identification and expression analysis of the DA1 gene family

Aurantio-obtusin accumulates only in the S. tora seeds, so seed size is not only an important indicator to measure seed yield of S. tora seeds, but also an important condition to affect the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds. In other plant species, the DA1 gene has been validated as a core component regulating the seed-organ size network. However, its regulatory role in seed development within S. tora remains unclear. Thus, this study concentrated on DA1 protein family and identified three genes (KAF7829707.1, KAF7831023.1, and KAF7816388.1) within S. tora genome6, employing a BLASTP search with the A. thaliana DA1 protein sequence as a reference16,24. Among these, KAF7829707.1 exhibited homology with AtDA1, while the other two genes were identified as homologs of AtDAR2; notably, they lacked ubiquitin-interaction motif (UIM) domains (Fig. 1a, b). Consequently, we designated KAF7829707.1 as StDA1. Additionally, comparative protein domain analysis revealed that StDA1, along with DA1 protein sequences from various species, featured two UIM domains arranged in tandem at the N-terminus, a LIM domain in the intermediate region, and a DA1-like structural domain at the C-terminus (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1), underscoring the strong conservation of the DA1 family across different plant species. To elucidate the expression pattern of the DA1 family genes in S. tora, we used RNA-seq data downloaded from the NCBI database6 to evaluate DA1 family genes expression patterns at different stages of seed development. Compared with the other two genes, KAF7829707.1 (StDA1) expression level was lower at the late phase of S. tora seed development and was higher at the early stage of S. tora seed development (Fig. 1c). For the analysis of the subcellular localization of the StDA1 protein, the complete coding sequence (CDS) of the StDA1 was successfully cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector using gene cloning technology, enabling its fusion expression with green fluorescent protein (GFP). StDA1 negatively regulates aurantio-obtusin accumulation in S. tora seeds

The well-established function of the StDA1 gene in seed size regulation across diverse species24, and it is intrigued by the specific accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds (Supplementary Fig. 2). This led us to hypothesize that StDA1 may be involved in the regulation of aurantio-obtusin accumulation in S. tora seeds. For further investigation of this hypothesis, StDA1 was overexpressed and antisense overexpressed in S. tora plants under the control of the CAMV 35S promoter. Finally, three lines were obtained from overexpressing (StDA1-OE) and antisense overexpressing (StDA1-AE) transgenic plants were analyzed, respectively, alongside transgenic S. tora plants containing an empty vector (EV) as a control (Supplementary Fig. 3). Subsequent analysis of agronomic traits revealed distinct differences among StDA1-OE, StDA1-AE, and EV plants. Notably, StDA1-OE lines exhibited significantly reduced plant height and width compared to EV lines, whereas the difference in plant height between StDA1-AE and EV lines was less pronounced, with StDA1-AE lines showing significantly increased plant width (Fig. 2a, d, e). At the pod-bulging stage, no significant differences were observed in the number of tillers among StDA1-OE, EV, and StDA1-AE lines (Fig. 2f). However, StDA1-OE lines displayed slightly lower numbers of pods and shorter pod lengths compared to the EV strain, while StDA1-AE lines exhibited contrasting results (Fig. 2b, g, h). During the maturity stage of S. tora seeds, StDA1-OE lines exhibited a slight decrease in hundred-seed weight compared to EV lines, with negligible differences in seed length and width. Conversely, StDA1-AE lines showed slightly higher hundred-seed weight and seed length compared to EV lines, with no significant variation in seed width (Fig. 2c, i–k). Aurantio-obtusin is the main active ingredient in S. tora seeds, and it is only present in the seeds (Supplementary Fig. 2). To determine whether the StDA1 impacts the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds or not, seeds of StDA1-OE, EV, and StDA1-AE lines were collected at seed maturity stage and dried using an oven at 50 °C. To further validate these results, additional pull-down and BiFC experiments were performed. The pull-down experiments with purified His-tagged StHDR1 (StHDR1-His) with GST proteins and GST-tagged StDA1 (StDA1-GST) were performed, and the results show that StHDR1-His proteins could bind to StDA1-GST proteins, suggesting that StDA1 physically interacts with StHDR1 using in vitro (Fig. 5g). In addition, the results of BiFC analysis showed that significant YFP fluorescence on tobacco leaves with StDA1-nYFP/StHDR1-cYFP co-expression, while YFP fluorescencewas absent in the negative control (Fig. 5h), demonstrating that StDA1 can interact with StHDR1 in vivo. Notably, while StDA1 is predominantly localized in the nucleus, its interaction signals with StHDR1 are detected in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. This phenomenon can be attributed to the subcellular distribution characteristic of StHDR1—which exhibits both cytoplasmic and nuclear localization (Supplementary Fig. 5). Specifically, when StDA1 binds to StHDR1 in the cytoplasm, the resulting protein complex remains localized in the cytoplasm due to StHDR1’s cytoplasmic anchoring, thereby generating cytoplasmic interaction signals. Concurrently, StDA1 can also interact with StHDR1 within the nucleus, where the complex is retained in the nuclear compartment, producing nuclear interaction signals. Therefore, the coexistence of interaction signals in both regions is not contradictory but rather reflects the combined effects of StHDR1’s spatial flexibility and StDA1’s broad interaction capacity. Taken together, StHDR1, a vital enzyme in the aurantio-obtusin biosynthesis pathway in S. tora can interact with StDA1, suggesting that StDA1 may regulate the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds through the key enzyme StHDR1.

Furthermore, Y2H assays were performed to analyze the interaction domains of StDA1 with StHDR1. The results revealed that StDA1-LIM (containing the intermediate region LIM) interacted with StHDR1, whereas StDA1-UIMs (containing the N-terminal UIMs) and StDA1-C (containing the C-terminal region) did not exhibit interaction with StHDR1 (Supplementary Fig. 6). This suggests that the LIM domains of the StDA1 protein are crucial segments for its interaction with StHDR1.

StDA1 regulates the stability of StHDR1 protein

As it is widely acknowledged, the UIM structural domain of AtDA1 primarily interacts with ubiquitin16. Hence, StDA1 has the capacity to bind to ubiquitin. Subsequently, the full-length StDA1 sequence and a truncated variant lacking two UIM domains (StDA1delUIMs) were separately constructed as GST-tagged fusion proteins. Concurrently, ubiquitin was constructed as HA-tagged fusion proteins. While GST and StDA1delUIMs-GST failed to bind to HA-ubiquitin, StDA1-GST exhibited binding to HA-ubiquitin, indicating the necessity of the UIMs of StDA1 for ubiquitin binding (Supplementary Fig. 9). Furthermore, it was explored that the StDA1 could undergo ubiquitination modification in plants. Under the control of the 35S promoter, StDA1 was fused to GFP and expressed in tobacco. Total proteins from plants containing StDA1 and GFP (EV) were isolated and subjected to immunoprecipitation in the presence of the MG132 proteasome inhibitor and TUBE1 protein. The results revealed a significant level of ubiquitination in StDA1 plants compared to GFP plants (Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that StDA1 is subject to ubiquitination in plants. These findings collectively suggest that the StDA1 protein may function as a ubiquitin receptor.

The ubiquitin receptor DA1 is supposed to participate in the protein degradation process mediated by ubiquitin-interacting proteins16,18. Ubiquitin proteasomes often play a crucial role in regulating the degradation of various proteins. Thus, to elucidate the impact of StDA1 on the function of StHDR1, we conducted experiments wherein the recombinant protein StHDR1-His was incubated with total protein extracted from EV plants. This led to rapid degradation of the StHDR1 protein. However, co-incubation of StHDR1-His with the recombinant protein StDA1-GST facilitated the degradation of StHDR1, which was inhibited by the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 8a, b). Concurrently, we evaluated the stability of the StHDR1 protein in a transient tobacco expression system. Compared with upon transfection of the StHDR1-Flag construct into tobacco leaves alone, the abundance of StHDR1 protein decreased when the StHDR1-Flag construct was co-transfected with the StDA1-GFP construct into tobacco leaves, whereas higher levels of StHDR1 accumulation were observed following MG132 treatment (Fig. 8c, d). These results are consistent with the findings from in vitro cellular degradation experiments, indicating the essential role of StDA1 in maintaining the stability of the StHDR1 protein. Furthermore, to further investigate the influence of StDA1 on the stability of the StHDR1 protein, we assessed StHDR1 protein levels in StDA1-OE, StDA1-AE, and EV lines. Recombinant StHDR1-His protein was subjected to incubation in cell lysates of EV, StDA1-OE, and StDA1-AE transgenic plants with or without the addition of MG132, followed by quantification via immunoblotting. Compared to EV plants, StHDR1-His protein exhibited greater stability in protein extracts from StDA1-AE transgenic plants but was more prone to degradation in protein extracts from StDA1-OE transgenic plants (Fig. 8e, f). Moreover, compared to WT plants, StHDR1 transcription levels were significantly downregulated in StDA1-OE transgenic plants, while StHDR1 expression levels were significantly upregulated in StDA1-AE transgenic plants, revealing that StDA1 negatively regulates the transcriptional expression of StHDR1 (Fig. 4). Discussion

S. tora, a prevalent herb within the legume family, is extensively utilized in traditional medicine, particularly its seeds. Recent pharmacological studies have unveiled a myriad of therapeutic benefits associated with S. tora, encompassing antifertility, antihypertensive, anti-inflammatory, antifungal, anti-glycation, anti-hyperglycemic, and antibacterial properties25. The principal bioactive constituents of S. tora include anthraquinones, such as aurantio-obtusin, chrysophanol, emodin, rhein, and obtusifolin3. Notably, aurantio-obtusin stands out as a distinctive component found solely in S. tora seeds. Given the expanding utilization of S. tora seeds across various sectors, including Chinese patent medicine, clinical formulations, health products, and the food industry, the demand for these seeds continues to surge annually. However, current studies into the role of certain genes or proteins in governing the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in S. tora often rely on the hairy root genetic transformation system26. Leveraging our prior experience with the genetic transformation system of S. miltiorrhiza hairy roots27, our team has established a successful genetic transformation system for S. tora hairy roots. Furthermore, identifying MenB as a positive regulator of anthraquinone accumulation in S. tora. Yet, it was found that aurantio-obtusin was stored only in seeds based on the expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. 2), and aurantio-obtusin synthesis remains inefficient in model plants such as A. thaliana, tobacco, and wheat. Hence, the establishment of a genetic transformation system tailored specifically for S. tora plants. Our endeavors culminated in the transfection of StDA1 into germinating S. tora seeds using the vacuum-infiltration method, akin to techniques employed in A. thaliana, soybean, and Medicago sativa (M. sativa) seeds28–30. The outcomes revealed that StDA1 overexpression led to reduced pod numbers, seed size, and aurantio-obtusin accumulation, whereas StDA1 antisense overexpression resulted in increased pod numbers, seed size, and aurantio-obtusin content. While previous studies primarily attribute DA1’s regulatory role to organ and seed size16,20,22, our findings suggest a broader function for StDA1, extending to the modulation of secondary metabolite accumulation in plants. This discovery contributes significantly to our understanding of DA1’s regulatory mechanisms in plant secondary metabolite accumulation.

AtDA1 encodes a ubiquitin receptor featuring two UIMs capable of in vitro ubiquitin binding16. In S. tora, the presence of these two UIM motifs on the StDA1 sequence, essential for ubiquitin binding, alongside evidence of StDA1 ubiquitination in plants, implies its involvement in ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation of interacting partners. The expression dynamics of StDA1, relatively diminished during late seed maturation stages, contrast with the pattern observed for StHDR1. We speculate that the interaction between these two genes is likely intrinsically linked to the aurantio-obtusin accumulation. In vitro assays revealed a hastened degradation rate of StHDR1 protein in StDA1 overexpressing plants compared to antisense overexpression counterparts. From these findings, it can be inferred that StDA1 modulates aurantio-obtusin accumulation in seeds by regulating StHDR1 stability. Meanwhile, it demonstrates the mechanisms by which post-translational modifications of proteins can regulate the accumulation of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds.

In ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, pinpointing ubiquitinated targets holds paramount significance in unraveling the intricacies of plant secondary metabolite biosynthesis and plant growth and development. In poplar, the transcription factor WOX4 stands as a notable target of DA1 ubiquitination, where the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway modulates WOX4 protein stability to govern cambium cell division within the formative layer and facilitate xylem formation31. Similarly, in A. thaliana, DA1 targets the deubiquitinating enzyme UBP15 and physically interacts with and cleaves it, resulting in a reduction in its protein abundance and a regulation of seed size19. In our current investigation, HDR1 emerges as the terminal enzyme in the MEP pathway, regulating aurantio-obtusin biosynthesis in S. tora. Intriguingly, the obtained results showed that StDA1 interacts with StHDR1, facilitating the degradation of StHDR1 proteins. This finding not only presents a fresh perspective on StHDR1 regulation at the protein level but also positions StHDR1 as a newfound target for DA1-mediated ubiquitination. Additionally, the MEP pathway serves as the core biosynthetic pathway for most secondary metabolites in medicinal plants. Notably, HDR, as the terminator enzyme of this pathway, plays a pivotal role in regulating secondary metabolite biosynthesis by controlling the metabolic flux of key precursors. Based on this, the study hypothesizes that the StDA1-StHDR1 functional module may exert similar regulatory functions in the accumulation of secondary metabolites in other medicinal plants by synergistically modulating metabolic flux. This discovery not only provides a fresh perspective for elucidating the biosynthetic regulatory networks of secondary metabolites in medicinal plants but also holds significant implications for advancing targeted metabolic engineering strategies.

Ubiquitin receptors are generally available to bind to E3 ligase to collaboratively affect substrate degradation. In A. thaliana, two E3 ubiquitin ligases (BIG BROTHER (BB) and AtDA2) can synergistically regulate organ size by ubiquitinating AtDA117,18. In poplar, an E3 ligase (PagDA2) was identified by Y2H library screening, and PagDA2 can ubiquitinate PagDA1 in vitro and in vivo to affect the degradation of PagWOX4 protein31. Unfortunately, the E3 ubiquitin ligase that can interact with the ubiquitin receptor StDA1 has not yet been identified in S. tora, and the absence of this key cofactor limits our ability to resolve the complete ubiquitination regulatory pathway. Based on this, we intend to continue to explore the E3 ligase that can bind to StDA1 in subsequent studies, and thus elucidate the regulatory mechanism of ubiquitination modification in the biosynthesis of aurantio-obtusin in S. tora seeds.

Based on the aforementioned experimental findings, we have formulated a hypothesis to elucidate the mechanism underlying aurantio-obtusin regulation in S. tora through the StDA1-StHDR1 complex. In wild-type S. tora seeds, StDA1 is able to interact with StHDR1 and promote StHDR1 protein degradation, which ultimately leads to a decrease in aurantio-obtusin accumulation. In contrast, in S. tora seeds of the da1 mutant, silencing of StDA1 resulted in the inability of StDA1 to bind to StHDR1, releasing more StHDR1 protein and ultimately augmenting aurantio-obtusin accumulation (Fig. 9). To sum up, our study underscores StDA1’s direct role in regulating the protein stability of key enzymes in secondary metabolic pathways. Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The seeds of S. tora, sourced from Shaanxi Tianshili Botanical Pharmaceutical Co., underwent soaking in a thermostatic water bath at 50 °C for 5–6 h before being planted in pots and nurtured at 25 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle. After approximately one month of growth, the leaves were harvested for RNA extraction and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA. Amplification of the CDS regions of StDA1 and StHDR1 was carried out using primer sequences detailed in Supplemental Table 4. Subsequently, these genes were individually ligated to pCAMBIA1300-mCherry using ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit V2 (Vazyme, C116-01) to generate overexpression and antisense overexpression plants. Freshly sprouted S. tora seeds were subjected to Agrobacterium-mediated transformation through vacuum infiltration, and then the seeds were immersed in Agrobacterium resuspension within a centrifuge tube. The vacuum pump was used to evacuate the seeds. When the vacuum pressure reached 0.08 MPa, the vacuum was stopped and timed for 5 min, and after the timer ended, the pressure was quickly released and repeated 3 times. The transformed seeds were transplanted into soil and initially kept in darkness for 24 h before being transitioned to a 16-h light/8-h dark incubation cycle. Additionally, tobacco seedlings were grown under similar light conditions for comparative analyses.

Transcriptome data analysis

Transcriptome data (Nos. SRX7700171, SRX7700176, SRX7700182, and SRX7700189) from different stages of seed development were downloaded from the NCBI SRA database6 (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). The raw SRA files were converted to FASTQ format using the prefetch and fasterq-dump tools from the SRA Toolkit software (version 3.0.3)32. Quality control was then performed on the raw sequencing reads using fastp software (version 0.23.4) with default parameters to filter out low-quality sequences, yielding high-quality clean reads33. The coding sequences (CDS) of the S. tora genome6 were used as reference transcripts, and Salmon (version 1.10.2) was employed to perform gene expression quantification. The k-mer length was set to 31, while all other parameters remained at their default values34. This process automatically removes duplicate transcripts and corrects non-standard bases. Clean reads were further quantified via Salmon’s quasi-mapping (selective alignment) mode to obtain expression indicators34. The fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values for all genes were then calculated using the standard FPKM formula35. Ultimately, heatmaps were generated using the Metware Cloud platform (https://www.metwarebio.com/metware-cloud-platform/)36.

DNA and qRT-PCR analysis

DNA was extracted from transgenic plant samples using SteadyPure Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit Ver.2 (Accurata Biotech, AG21011), PCR amplification was performed using primers (mcherry-F/R). The amplified products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to verify the integration of the exogenous gene. Primer sequences were as detailed in Supplemental Table 4.

At the flowering stage of S. tora, roots, stems, leaves, and flowers were meticulously gathered, while seeds were harvested during the podding stage, ensuring three replicates for each tissue type. RNA extraction from these samples and transgenic plant samples was accomplished using the Steady Pure plant RNA extraction kit (Accurata Biotech, AG21019). For reverse transcription and qRT-PCR analysis, the 5X Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix (Accurata Biotech, AG11705) and SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurata Biotech, AG11701) were employed, respectively, with specific reaction conditions as described in the instruction manual. Additionally, 18S was used as housekeeping gene and primer sequences were designed via Primer3 as detailed in Supplemental Table 4. Three replicates per reaction. The qRT-PCR results were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method36.

Determination of aurantio-obtusin content

The seeds of S. tora were pulverized into a fine powder using a pulverizer. Subsequently, 0.1 g of the powder was combined with 10 ml of extraction solution (methanol: hydrochloric acid = 10:1) and allowed to soak overnight, followed by a 2-h sonication period. Afterward, the mixture underwent centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before being subjected to analysis via HPLC. The chromatographic column used in the HPLC experiment was Waters SunFire C18, the detection wavelength was 284 nm, and the column temperature was set at 30 °C. The mobile phases were acetonitrile and 0.1% phosphoric acid in water, respectively.

Mantel test heatmap correlation analysis

To evaluate the associations between seed phenotypic traits and aurantio-obtusin content in S.tora transgenic plants, a correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The Mantel test, implemented via the Advanced Mantel-Test Heatmap Plugin on the Metware Cloud Platform (https://www.metwarebio.com/metware-cloud-platform/), was employed to assess these relationships. This approach quantified the linear correlations between multivariate phenotypic datasets (e.g., seed size, weight, pod number) and aurantio-obtusin content, with results visualized as a heatmap to highlight significant associations.

Subcellular localization

The CDS of StDA1 and StHDR1 were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector, yielding StDA1/StHDR1-GFP constructs. Subsequently, considering the nuclear localization of StTCP11 in S. tora, StTCP11 was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-mCherry vector as a control35. Equal quantities of Agrobacterium carrying StDA1/StHDR1-GFP and StTCP11-mCherry constructs were combined and infiltrated into tobacco leaves. Observation of the results was conducted after a period of 2–3 days.

Screening for interacting proteins

The identification of StDA1 interacting proteins was performed through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis37,38. Initially, total proteins extracted from S. tora seeds (wild-type) were subjected to incubation with StDA1-His proteins, followed by pull-down assay to isolate StDA1-His interacting proteins. Detection of these interactors was facilitated using anti-His antibody (Abmart, M30111, diluted 1:3000). Subsequently, the gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue, and the target band was cut and sent to Wuhan Jinkairui Bioengineering Co., Ltd for mass LC-MS/MS detection. Finally, the libraries were searched and compared using Proteome Discovered 2.2 software to obtain information on peptides that interact with StDA1.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays

Fragments encoding specific protein domains along with the complete coding sequence (CDS) of StDA1 were inserted into the pGBKT7 (BD) vector, resulting in the generation of BD-StDA1, BD-StDA1-Uim, BD-StDA1-Lim, and BD-StDA1-C constructs. Concurrently, StHDR1 were incorporated into the pGADT7 (AD) vector to yield AD-StHDR1 constructs, respectively. Co-transformation of AD-StHDR1 constructs with the corresponding BD-StDA1 constructs was carried out in yeast strain Y2HGold. The transformed yeast cells were then plated on SD-Trp/-Leu and SD-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade media supplemented with 200 ng/ml AbA, followed by continuous incubation at 28 °C for 4 days.

Bimo-lecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and luciferase complementation (LCI) assays

In BiFC assay, StDA1 was cloned into pXY106 to produce StDA1-nYFP fusion proteins. StHDR1 was cloned into pXY104 to prepare StHDR1-cYFP fusion proteins. Equal amounts of StDA1-nYFP and StHDR1-cYFP Agrobacterium were co-injected into tobacco leaves and incubated for 2–3 days. YFP fluorescence in tobacco leaves was imaged using a fluorescence inverted microscope.

In LCI assay, StDA1 was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-Cluc vector to generate StDA1-Cluc fusion proteins. Similarly, StHDR1 were incorporated into the pCAMBIA1300-Nluc vector to create StHDR1-Nluc fusion proteins. Equal quantities of Agrobacterium carrying StDA1-Cluc and StHDR1-Nluc constructs were co-injected into tobacco leaves and allowed to incubate for 2–3 days. The luciferase activity was assessed using a chemiluminescence instrument, and luminescence was captured via a plant live imaging system.

Pull down assays

The StDA1 gene was cloned into the pGEX4T-1 vector, resulting in the StDA1-GST construct. Simultaneously, StHDR1 were inserted into the pCold-TF vector, yielding StHDR1-His constructs, respectively. The StDA1-GST protein was purified using ProteinIso® GST Resin (TransGen, DP201-01), while StHDR1/StTCP4.1-His proteins were purified using ProteinIso® Ni-NTA Resin (TransGen, DP101-01). Following purification, the StDA1-GST protein was incubated with StHDR1-His proteins bound to the Ni-NTA Resin in 300 μl of pull-down buffer (composed of 40 mM HEPES-KOH at pH 7.5, 0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.2% Triton X-100, and 100 μM protease inhibitor cocktail) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the retained Ni-NTA Resin was washed 3–5 times using PBS buffer and subjected to analysis using anti-His antibody (Abmart, M30111, diluted 1:3000) and anti-GST antibody (Abmart, M20007, diluted 1:5000).

Ubiquitin binding and ubiquitinylation assays

The present study has to investigate the potential interaction between DA1 and ubiquitin, and then cloned the StDA1 sequences lacking the UIM motifs (StDA1delUIMs) into the pGEX4T-1 vector, resulting in the StDA1delUIMs-GST fusion protein. Subsequently, StDA1delUIMs-GST protein was purified using ProteinIso® GST Resin. Recombinant HA-tagged ubiquitin was then combined with purified GST, StDA1-GST, or StDA1delUIMs-GST proteins bound to the GST Resin. The pull-down experiment was conducted following the aforementioned procedures and analyzed using anti-GST antibody and anti-HA antibody (Abmart, M20003, diluted 1:5000).

The coding sequences of StDA1 were amplified using primer sequences listed in Supplemental Table 4 and subsequently ligated into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector. Agrobacterium carrying GFP and StDA1-GFP constructs were then used for tobacco transformation following the previously described transgenic techniques, resulting in tobacco plants expressing GFP alone or fused with StDA1. Total proteins were extracted from the leaves of these tobacco plants using a buffer solution consisting of 10 mM Tris/HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 10% glycerol, 100 µM protease inhibitor cocktail, and 50 mM MG132. The extracted proteins were then combined with Tandem Ubiquitin Binding Entities 1 (TUBE1) protein17 (Amylet Scientific, UM401) and 50 µM MG132, followed by a 30-min incubation on ice. Ubiquitination analysis was conducted using anti-GFP antibody (Abmart, M20004, diluted 1:5000) and anti-Ubi antibody (Abmart, T55520, diluted 1:4000) as per the instructions provided with the GFP-Nanoab-Magnetic kit (Lablead, PGM025).

Cell free protein degradation assay

The cell-free protein degradation assay was adapted from previous protocols, with modifications39. In brief, total proteins were extracted from 4-month-old S. tora leaves of various genotypes using degradation buffer composed of 25 mM Tris/HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 μM protease inhibitor cocktail, 5 mM DTT, and 10 mM ATP. The protein concentration in the resulting supernatant was determined using the BCA method. Subsequently, the total protein from S. tora, recombinant StHDR1-His protein, and 50 mM MG132 were co-incubated at room temperature, and samples were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h. The level of StHDR1-His was assessed through immunoblotting using an anti-His antibody.

Immunoblotting

The StHDR1 coding sequence was inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-Flag vector, while the StDA1 coding sequence was introduced into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector, and resulting in the generation of StHDR1-Flag and StDA1-GFP constructs. To investigate whether StDA1 influences the degradation of StHDR1 protein, equal amounts of Agrobacterium carrying StHDR1-Flag and StDA1-GFP constructs were co-injected into tobacco leaves. After 2 days of incubation, 50 μM MG132 was used to inject at the same location on the leaves and incubation was continued for 12 h. Total proteins were extracted from transiently expressed tobacco leaves using an extraction buffer containing 10 mM Tris/HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 10% glycerol, and 100 µM protease inhibitor cocktail. Immunoblotting analysis using anti-GFP, anti-Flag (Abmart, M20008, diluted 1:4000), and anti-His antibodies was conducted for further investigation.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett multiple comparisons. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To ensure reproducibility of experimental results, each experiment was independently performed with at least three biological replicates, yielding consistent outcomes.
