Exploring the influence of personality and partner attachment on perinatal depression and anxiety
Authors: Tea Terzic, Polona Rus Prelog, Ales Oblak, Maja R Makovec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePerinatal depression and anxiety are prevalent complications affecting 18–26% of pregnancies worldwide. This study investigated the influence of personality traits and partner attachment styles on depressive and anxiety symptoms in Slovenian women. We compared the results longitudinally at two time points: during pregnancy and at 6 weeks postpartum.MethodsA non-clinical sample of 325 women in their third trimester and a clinical sample of 49 women diagnosed with perinatal depression and/or anxiety were assessed during pregnancy and 6 weeks postpartum. We investigated how the personality traits of women and their partner attachment styles influence their clinical symptoms and how they change after giving birth. Clinical symptoms were measured by Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) and Zung Anxiety Scale, partner attachment by Experiences in Close Relationships-Revised (ECR-R) and personality traits by Big Five Inventory (BFI).ResultsHigher neuroticism, lower openness and lower extraversion were those personality traits, which were associated with higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms in pregnant women in both clinical and non-clinical samples. Higher partner anxiety attachment scores were also associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms in women in both samples. The clinical group receiving outpatient psychiatric support showed significant improvements in depressive and anxiety symptoms after birth when compared with non-clinical group of women.ConclusionsThis study emphasizes the importance of personality traits and partner attachment style as predictors of perinatal depressive and anxiety symptoms. Prenatal screening and interventions addressing these risk factors may prevent or reduce the severity of perinatal depressive and anxiety disorders. Further research is needed to assess the efficacy of such interventions in high-risk populations.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Perinatal depression, Anxiety, Personality, Attachment, Psychiatric support

Introduction

The perinatal period begins at the onset of pregnancy and continues until one year after birth. This phase marks a significant transition in a woman’s life, encompassing numerous biological, psychological and social changes. Given the development of new life, maternal changes also affect the infant, forming a complex biopsychological network.

Perinatal depression and anxiety are among the most prevalent reproductive complications [1], affecting approximately 18–26% of pregnancies worldwide [2]. A Slovene study from 2014 revealed that 21.7% of pregnant Slovenian women presented elevated depressive symptoms, and 12.5% reported high trait anxiety [3]. These conditions are linked to negative outcomes for maternal physical and mental health, as well as for the emotional and cognitive development of infants and children during critical growth periods [4]. Antenatal depression is a significant risk factor for postnatal depression [5]. Therefore, early screening and diagnosis during pregnancy are essential to ensure timely intervention. In Slovenia, gynecologists use standardized tools (www.podn.si) to detect symptoms of depression and anxiety, thereby supporting early treatment efforts.

Expectant mothers display diverse trajectories of depressive symptoms, ranging from gradual onset to transient postpartum symptoms that subside quickly [6]. Some women recover rapidly, whereas others may experience prolonged distress lasting months or even years postpartum [6]. Previous studies have shown that the etiology of perinatal depression is complex [7, 8], with some stable characteristics, such as history of life events [9], personality [10], emotional regulation [11], coping strategies [12] and social support [13].

Personality shapes how individuals interpret experiences, emotions, and social interactions [14]. Personality traits as risk factors for perinatal depression were already studied [15–17]. Previous studies have reported that emotional stability, extraversion, and conscientiousness are protective factors against depression [6, 18]. Openness fosters adaptability in novel situations, whereas extraversion is associated with greater social support and positive affect [19]. On the other hand, dysfunctional perfectionism, avoidant personality style and neuroticism were shown to be significant risk factors for perinatal affective symptoms [16, 20].

Personality traits also play an important role in attachment dynamics and close relationships in adulthood [21]. Attachment styles, first conceptualized by Bowlby in 1973, refer to patterns of emotional regulation and behavior in close relationships. A secure attachment style is linked to relationship satisfaction [22], whereas an insecure attachment style in adults is based on less optimal experiences with early caregiving [23]. Traditionally, research on attachment styles and perinatal depression has focused on the mother‒child bond [24], but recently, this question has also been addressed in relation to the bond between partners. Partner attachment was already described as an important contextual factor for depressive and anxiety symptoms during the third trimester of pregnancy [25]. Securing attachment style between caregivers has been shown to protect against postpartum depression [26], whereas insecure attachment style is considered to be a risk factor for depressive symptoms [25]. However, the role of partner attachment during pregnancy has received relatively little research attention so far.

Identifying risk factors for perinatal depression and anxiety disorders is essential for prevention and early intervention. In our Slovenian study, we investigated how women’s personality traits and partner attachment styles are related to perinatal depression and anxiety. Furthermore, we investigated how these factors differ between non-clinical samples of women and women treated for perinatal depression or anxiety at two time points (pregnancy and 6 weeks after birth). We believe our study provides important insights into identifying more vulnerable groups of pregnant women in Slovenia at risk for perinatal depression and anxiety. To our knowledge, this is the first Central- Eastern European study to compare non-clinical and clinical sample of pregnant women analyzing affective disorders and partner attachment. Furthermore, it offers a longitudinal perspective since we analyzed women’s depressive and anxiety symptoms before and after delivery.

Materials and methods

Participants

The non-clinical sample included women in the last trimester of pregnancy who attended school for parents at the maternity hospital of the University Clinical Centre, Ljubljana, between March and September 2014. A total of 325 women in their third trimester of pregnancy were included. The non-clinical sample was not screened out for subclinical symptoms. They were asked whether they are treated with antidepressants or whether they receive outpatient psychiatric care. Only those without such support were included. The study was approved by the Republic of Slovenia National Medical Ethics Committee (NMEC) (protocol No. 92/12/13).

The clinical sample included 49 pregnant women who attended the outpatient unit at the University Psychiatric Clinic Ljubljana. The inclusion criterion was a clinical diagnosis of perinatal depression and/or anxiety. The study was approved by the Republic of Slovenia National Medical Ethics Committee (NMEC) (protocol No. 0120–559/2020/6).

All participants were aged 18 years or older, provided informed consent, and completed an anonymous self-report. The survey took approximately 30 min to complete. The first self-report was conducted during pregnancy and the second 6 weeks postpartum. Among the 49 participants in the clinical sample, 32 received antidepressants during pregnancy. Postpartum Edinburgh Postnatal Depression scale (EPDS) and Zung anxiety scores were available for 31 participants from clinical sample (63.3%).

The statistical model includes participants who participated at both time points (before and after childbirth). Continuous variables were compared via the independent samples t test. Categorical variables were compared via the chi-square test. All p values were corrected for multiple comparisons via the false discovery rate.

Instruments

The participants completed a structured questionnaire that collected demographic data, including maternal age, gestational week at the first visit and years of education.

Psychological assessments included the following:

∙ Experiences in Close Relationships-Revised (ECR-R) [27]: A 36-item scale measuring romantic attachment across two subscales: anxiety (fear of rejection) and avoidance (discomfort with intimacy).

∙ Experiences in Close Relationships-Revised (ECR-R) [27]: A 36-item scale measuring romantic attachment across two subscales: anxiety (fear of rejection) and avoidance (discomfort with intimacy).

∙ Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [28, 29]: A 10-item self-report scale for screening for depression during pregnancy and postpartum. A score ≥ 11 indicates depressive symptoms.

∙ Zung Anxiety Scale [30]: A 20-item scale evaluating symptoms of anxiety, with total scores ranging from 20 to 80.

∙ Zung Anxiety Scale [30]: A 20-item scale evaluating symptoms of anxiety, with total scores ranging from 20 to 80.

∙ Big Five Inventory (BFI) [31]: A 44-item scale assessing five personality dimensions: neuroticism, openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, and agreeableness.

Analysis

The data were analyzed via RStudio Team (2020). T tests and chi-square tests were used to evaluate baseline differences between the groups. Linear mixed models (lme4 package) were used to assess changes in depression and anxiety symptoms over time. Assumptions of normality, collinearity and homoscedasticity were tested. Due to non-normally distributed residuals, we fitted a robust linear mixed-effects (robustlmm package) model with group, time, neuroticism, attachment anxiety and their interactions with time as fixed effects and a random intercept for the participant. Listwise deletion was used for the missing data. The critical value was set at 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Demographic characteristics of both samples

The non-clinical sample included 325 pregnant women and 49 pregnant women of Caucasian origin. The mean age of the participants was 31.24 years (SD 4.45) in the non-clinical sample and 32.87 years (SD 5.02) in the clinical sample. All participants were well educated, the average number of years of education was 16.12 (SD 2.47) for the non-clinical sample and 15.59 (SD 2.71) for the clinical sample.

In our clinical sample, 23 women who received antidepressants and 8 women who did not receive antidepressants completed all the postpartum questionnaires. For the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, within subjects, the main effect was not significant (F = 0.134, p = 0.719) and interactions with antidepressants during pregnancy (F = 0.123, p = 0.731) were non-significant. For Zung, within subjects, the main effect was not significant (F = 0.587, p = 0.454), and neither were its interactions with antidepressants during pregnancy (F = 0.082, p = 0.778).

Depressive symptoms in non-clinical and clinical samples before and after childbirth

We compared depressive symptoms, measured by EPDS scores, during pregnancy and after childbirth in two groups of women: a non-clinical (healthy) group and a clinical group diagnosed with perinatal depression or anxiety. There was no significant main effect of group on depressive symptoms, b = −0.71, SE = 0.96, t = −0.74, p = 0.457, and no significant main effect of time, b = −0.03, SE = 2.5, t = −0.012, p = 0.99. However, a significant group and time interaction was found (b = −4.81, SE = 1.75, t = −2.75, p = 0.006), indicating that the change in depressive symptoms from pregnancy to postpartum differed between the groups. Specifically, while the non-clinical group showed stable or slightly increasing symptoms, the clinical group experienced a notable decrease in depression symptoms after birth (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The participants of our study, made in urban central Slovenian region, were well educated and had quite stable living conditions, which are known protective factors for perinatal depression and anxiety [32]. We compared non-clinical samples and clinical samples of pregnant women treated for perinatal depression and/or anxiety disorders. In both samples, the associations between the investigated factors were consistent. Higher neuroticism, lower openness, lower extraversion and higher attachment anxiety scores were associated with higher levels of depression and anxiety symptoms in women.

Personality traits significantly influence perinatal affective symptomatology as shown in previous studies [6, 15, 17, 33–35]. Neuroticism is a key vulnerability factor, as was also demonstrated in our Slovenian sample. Neuroticism is characterized by heightened stress reactivity and frequent negative emotional responses [36]. Women with this trait often exhibit insecurity, worry, and emotional instability, factors known to increase the risk of perinatal depression [17]. These symptoms often reflect maladaptive patterns within complex personality structures, which become particularly vulnerable during the transition to motherhood [6]. Neuroticism can also moderate the effect of stress on depression [37] and plays a central role in the onset and maintenance of depressive symptoms [38]. Depression and neuroticism also share substantial heritability [39]. Furthermore, neuroticism is also correlated with lower perceived social support and higher sensitivity to rejection [34]. Some authors have suggested that neuroticism should be routinely assessed in the clinical screening of pregnant women to enable early referral to psychological or psychiatric support [16]. Importantly, some studies linked neuroticism with suicidal ideation in women after delivery [40].

Lower extraversion and openness score reflect tendencies toward being passive, quiet and reserved and was associated with negative affect [19]. Our findings are similar to foreign studies where lower extraversion and lower openness are associated with perinatal depression and anxiety [35]. Parental personality traits, assessed before conception, can impact family dynamics, the development of child’s depressive symptoms and development [41]. Parental traits such as agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, extraversion, and openness are not just critical for the parents’ own mental health and parenting style, but also the temperamental characteristics of their offspring [35, 41].

In recent years, there has been a growing recognition of the importance of examining the mother–child attachment relationship, not only in the postpartum period but also in the prenatal period, as both are formative in shaping attachment bonds [42, 43]. The quality of the marital relationship plays a significant role in the adjustment to pregnancy but also in the bond formed with the fetus [26, 42]. Our results indicate that partner attachment anxiety is significantly associated with higher depressive scores in pregnant women. In our previous study, anxious (and not avoidant) attachment was shown to be significantly associated with higher levels of perinatal depression, so we tracked only anxious attachment in the present study [25]. The level of anxious intimate attachment in our study remained stable at both time points, before and after birth. Attachment injury is typically characterized as a moment of intense isolation and abandonment by the injured partner, which can fundamentally alter their internal working models of self and others, thus increasing attachment insecurity [26]. Attachment anxiety is characterized by heightened fear of abandonment and persistent reassurance seeking [21, 44]. Insecure attachment styles are associated with poorer mother–infant bonding and higher relational distress [22, 45]. Depression severity also increases with attachment disorganization [24]. Thus, attachment style is both a risk factor and an early intervention target for pregnant women [24]. Addressing parenting stress and bonding difficulties early on may mitigate long-term relational and emotional challenges.

A comparison of the non-clinical and clinical groups of women provided some important insights. The clinical group received outpatient psychiatric support, which included psychoeducation, cognitive behavioral therapy, perinatal group therapy and systemic interventions, some women were treated with antidepressants. The clinical group with higher levels of neuroticism, anxiety, and anxious attachment showed a relatively large reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms after birth. These findings suggest that the clinical group responded well to psychiatric support. However, the exact causality of this postpartum symptom change cannot be drawn since it is a consequence of many factors, postpartum adaptation being one of them. In our clinical sample, whether a woman took antidepressants during pregnancy did not influence the change in depressive and anxiety symptoms postpartum. These results suggest that pharmacotherapy should be integrated with psychological support tailored to individual needs and personality profiles. Good perinatal screening and accessible psychological and psychiatric support are surely important. Many psychotherapy options can be considered, such as cognitive behavioral therapy [46], brief interpersonal psychotherapy [47], or brief partner inclusive hybrid interventions [48]. There is also compelling evidence for task-sharing and telemedicine to improve access to psychotherapies for perinatal depressive and anxiety symptoms [49].

Our study has some important limitations. The attrition rates in our clinical group were high, and this limits statistical power and generalizability of results. The attrition rates in this study are consistent with prior findings in perinatal psychiatric research [50]. Dropout was more common among women who received antidepressants, which may reflect larger reductions in depressive symptoms or disengagement from services after perceived recovery. High attrition 6 weeks after birth could be also associated with new mothers’ lack of time to finish questionnaires. High attrition complicates the interpretation of results, so future studies should explore innovative follow-up methods. Furthermore, the causation of the depressive and anxiety symptom reduction in clinical group cannot be clearly established, and we cannot specify which exact treatment techniques drove the improvement.

To summarize, our study has several important preventive and clinical implications. The strength of this study is that the personality traits and anxious intimate attachment play important roles in perinatal anxiety and depression. The additional value of our study is that we studied perinatal dynamics longitudinally, before and after birth. We were able to determine the clinical robustness of neuroticism, openness, extraversion and anxious intimate attachment and the need to address these factors in the non-clinical sample for personalized screening. Simultaneously, we determined the relative flexibility of these factors with the application of comprehensive outpatient psychiatric support. The limitations of the study include the lack of data on other complex factors, which may have contributed to clinical symptoms.

Conclusion

This study underscores the importance of personality factors, especially neuroticism, lower openness, lower extraversion and anxious attachment, as predictors of perinatal depression and anxiety. Tailored psychiatric interventions addressing these risk factors may prevent or reduce the severity of perinatal depressive and anxiety disorders. Further research is needed to assess the efficacy of such interventions in high-risk populations.
