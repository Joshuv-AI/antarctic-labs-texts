Genomic and population analyses reveal asymmetric subgenome evolution and local adaptation in the polyploid Lycopus lucidus
Authors: Rongxiang Zhang, Maohui Wang, Feng Jiang, Jiao Chen, Yu Wu, Niyan Xiang, Xiaolei Yu, Yebing Yin, Yiling Wei, Tao Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyploidization and subsequent diploidization are fundamental processes in plant evolution, yet how subgenome asymmetry arising from these events shapes ecological adaptation remains poorly understood. We generate a chromosome‑level genome assembly and population genomic data for Lycopus lucidus, a polyploid medicinal plant with broad environmental adaptability across China. The genome reveals a recent whole‑genome triplication event. The three subgenomes show marked asymmetry in transposable element accumulation, gene retention, and evolutionary rates. Subgenome B has been stabilized under strong purifying selection, whereas subgenomes A and C have undergone dynamic reorganization with accelerated gene turnover, pseudogenization, and asymmetric selection pressures. Population genomic analysis of 60 individuals identifies four genetic lineages corresponding to geographic regions, with a demographic bottleneck coinciding with late Holocene cooling. Among genes under selection between lineages, 93.8% originate from subgenome C, suggesting this subgenome serves as a reservoir for adaptive variation. Genotype-environment association analyses identify 9,496 adaptive SNPs enriched in stress response, secondary metabolism, and wax biosynthesis pathways, with precipitation as the primary driver of local adaptation. Our findings demonstrate that subgenome asymmetry established during polyploid diploidization provides the genetic substrate for subsequent environmental adaptation, establishing L. lucidus as a model for understanding polyploid genome evolution and its adaptive consequences.Subject terms: Plant evolution, Evolutionary genetics

Introduction

Polyploidy, or whole-genome duplication (WGD), is a major evolutionary force in plants, profoundly shaping the genomic architecture of modern flowering plants1. By generating gene duplicates, polyploidy creates genetic redundancy that serves as raw material for functional innovation and can promote reproductive isolation, thereby facilitating speciation and evolutionary diversification2–4. However, the genomic redundancy introduced by polyploidy is often unstable. Newly formed polyploid genomes typically undergo a process termed “diploidization,” gradually reverting to a diploid-like state through mechanisms such as gene loss, chromosomal rearrangements, and subgenome differentiation1,5–7. A notable phenomenon observed in many polyploids is the asymmetry of this process, manifesting as “biased fractionation” or “subgenome dominance,” where genes from one ancestral subgenome are systematically retained and expressed at higher levels than those from the other6,8–10. Cotton (Gossypium) represents a classic model for studying asymmetric evolution in polyploid genomes, where the D subgenome exhibits significant expression dominance associated with transposable element distribution and epigenetic regulation6,11,12. A key unresolved question concerns how subgenome interactions and evolutionary trajectories shift as diploidization proceeds, and crucially, how these genomic changes translate into phenotypic novelty and ecological adaptation. Investigating polyploid systems at various stages of diploidization can help reconstruct the continuum of genomic changes and reveal the full dynamics of this process, yet most well-studied models like cotton are ancient polyploids, providing a snapshot of the endpoint rather than the ongoing process (Table 1).

Here, we address this question by studying Lycopus lucidus (Lamiaceae), a species that offers a unique opportunity to investigate the dynamic process of diploidization and its adaptive consequences. The genus Lycopus comprises approximately 14 species inhabiting temperate regions of the Eastern Hemisphere and North America, with four species native to China13. Among these, L. lucidus, known as “Di shen” in traditional Chinese medicine, is widely distributed across northern China and valued for its medicinal, culinary, and ornamental uses14–16. Phytochemical studies have identified Lycopus species as rich sources of volatile oils, terpenoids, phenolic acids, and flavonoids13, with L. lucidus characterized by high concentrations of flavonoids and essential oils17. Its primary bioactive constituents underpin applications in treating gynecological conditions, wound infections, and rheumatic arthralgia, while its edible roots are commonly consumed14,18. Notably, L. lucidus exhibits exceptional adaptability to multiple environmental stresses, including cold, heat, drought, waterlogging, and shade, across a wide geographic distribution in China. Furthermore, our preliminary analyses indicate that L. lucidus underwent a relatively recent polyploidization event, placing it at an early stage of the diploidization process, a stark contrast to the ancient polyploid models previously studied. This combination of recent polyploid origin, broad environmental adaptability, and economic importance makes L. lucidus an ideal system to investigate how early-stage subgenome asymmetry contributes to ecological adaptation.

In this study, we aimed to bridge this gap by generating a chromosome-level genome assembly for L. lucidus and coupling it with whole-genome resequencing of 60 individuals from 13 geographically isolated populations across China. Specifically, we asked: (1) Does the relatively recent polyploid L. lucidus genome exhibit signatures of subgenome asymmetry, and what are the underlying mechanisms (e.g., structural variations, gene loss, pseudogenization) characterizing its ongoing diploidization? (2) How has the L. lucidus population been structured by geography and climate, and do genes contributing to local adaptation show preferential retention in specific subgenomes, thereby linking subgenome asymmetry to adaptive evolution? By addressing these questions, we aim to elucidate the early stages of polyploid genome diploidization and its role in shaping adaptive potential, and establish a foundation for understanding how subgenome asymmetry translates into population-level adaptation.

Results

L. lucidus genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

See Supplementary Note 1 for details of genome assembly, which revealed a 1.18 Gb chromosome-level genome with three subgenomes (A, B, C) derived from an ancient whole-genome triplication (Fig. 1A, B). as shown in Supplementary Note 2, LTR insertion times differ among subgenomes, with subgenomes A and B harboring older insertions while subgenome C shows more recent transposon activity (Fig. 1C). Supplementary Note 3 presents the phylogenomic placement of L. lucidus as sister to Leonotis leonurus within Nepetoideae (Fig. 1D). Finally, Supplementary Note 3 describes the annotation of 74,127 protein-coding genes (PCGs) and 534 pseudogenes (Table 1), whose GO enrichment patterns indicate functional differentiation among the three subgenomes (Fig. 2), and documents the lineage‑specific expansion of terpene synthase (TPS) genes, particularly the TPS‑a subfamily, following polyploidization (Fig. 3A).

Three ancestral polyploidization events with distinct contributions to metabolic innovation

Despite morphological similarities among Lamiaceae species (opposite leaves, bilabiate corollas, square stems), significant evolutionary divergence exists. Phylogenetic analysis revealed L. lucidus diverged from Araliaceae approximately 102.91 Mya, consistent with Lamiaceae’s WGD history. Synteny analysis identified 7951 syntenic blocks containing 66,148 collinear genes within the L. lucidus genome. Comparative analysis with L. sibiricus revealed 2052 syntenic blocks comprising 41,399 L. lucidus genes and 15,457 L. sibiricus genes. A 1:3 gene copy number relationship for certain loci between these species supports a whole-genome triplication (WGT) event in L. lucidus following its divergence from Leonurus (Fig. 3B). Evolutionary timing analysis of duplicated genes revealed three distinct peaks in Ks distribution, corresponding to three WGD events: a WGT event shared with core eudicots (~113.37 Mya; Ks ≈ 1.93), a WGD event shared by Lamiaceae species (~59.79 Mya; Ks ≈ 1.02), and a recent WGT event endemic to L. lucidus originating from interspecies hybridization (~4.77 Mya; Ks ≈ 0.08) (Fig. 3C).

Using DupGen_finder, we identified 60,039 duplicated genes in the L. lucidus genome (Supplementary Data 1), including 45,781 WGD-type, 4272 TD-type, 2271 PD-type, 6277 TRD-type, and 1438 DSD-type genes, indicating recent WGD events primarily drove genome expansion. Among these, 4,039 expanded genes were WGD-type, 336 TD-type, 231 PD-type, 133 DSD-type, and 544 TRD-type. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed distinct metabolic specializations: WGD-derived genes showed significant enrichment in sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, linoleic acid metabolism, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 3D). TD and DSD types were predominantly involved in sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis. PD duplicates displayed enrichment in diverse plant secondary metabolites, lipid metabolic pathways (linoleic acid, arachidonic acid, α-linolenic acid metabolism), carbohydrate metabolism (starch and sucrose conversion), and terpenoid biosynthesis. TRD duplicates were primarily associated with protein degradation (ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis), terpenoid production, hormonal signaling networks, and pathogen defense mechanisms (Fig. 3D). Notably, duplicated genes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis mainly originated from WGD and PD events, contrasting with previous findings that secondary metabolic genes primarily derive from TD and PD events19,20. Evolutionary rate analysis showed most genes underwent purifying selection (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, WGD-type genes exhibited higher Ka and Ks values compared to other types, suggesting rapid recent evolution of WGD-derived duplicated genes (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 2).

Subgenome B emerges as the dominant subgenome with asymmetric evolutionary trajectories

Following WGD events, diploid species typically initiate rapid diploidization processes, including gene loss, subgenome dominance, and chromosomal rearrangements to restore gene dosage balance and maintain genomic stability. Nucleotide substitution rate analysis revealed significant differences between subgenome B and both subgenome A (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05) and subgenome C (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4). According to SubPhaser results, subgenomes A and B belong to homologous chromosome groups, with subgenome A exhibiting lower nonsynonymous mutation rates and Ka/Ks ratios compared to subgenome B (Fig. 4). This makes subgenome A more evolutionarily stable, becoming the “dominant subgenome” that retains more ancestral functional genes, while subgenome B may become the recessive subgenome, reinforcing functional asymmetry during diploidization4,21. Analysis of purifying selection revealed subgenome A contains 21,832 purifying-selected genes compared to 14,732 in subgenome B. GO enrichment analysis showed significant functional divergence (p < 0.05) in biological processes associated with purifying-selected genes between subgenomes (Fig. 4D). HYPHY analysis identified 281 genes under relaxed selection, with subgenomes A and C containing more relaxed genes (98 and 105, respectively) compared to subgenome B (68 genes) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 3). Similarly, among 300 genes under intensified selection, subgenomes A and C contained more intensified genes (96 and 132, respectively) versus subgenome B (68 genes) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 3). These findings demonstrate that L. lucidus achieved sub-functionalization through differential retention and selective pressure across subgenomes, with subgenome A showing stronger conservation of biological processes while subgenomes B and C exhibit greater enrichment of molecular functions (Fig. 4D).

Based on confirmed recent WGT event and consistent syntenic relationships among the three subgenomes, we reconstructed the karyotypic evolutionary trajectory using subgenome B (LlucB) as a foundation and Paulownia fortunei chromosomes as a reference (Supplementary Fig. 2). Comparative analyses revealed well-conserved karyotypes in both species, with a distinct 2:6 correspondence between P. fortunei and L. lucidus chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 2). P. fortunei (Paulowniaceae), having undergone no lineage-specific genome duplication events following the Lamiales-shared WGD, serves as an excellent karyotypic model for reconstructing ancestral Lamiaceae chromosomes. We ultimately reconstructed 11 ancestral Lamiaceae chromosomes (ALK) (Fig. 4H). The chromosomal composition of L. lucidus is characterized as AAB-type, suggesting origin through hybridization between an autotetraploid ancestor and a diploid progenitor (Fig. 4G). Previous research established that ancestral Lamiales species possessed 11 chromosomes, which doubled to 22 through the Lamiaceae-shared WGD (L-WGD) event22, generating the ancestral tetraploid karyotype of L. lucidus. The Lamiaceae-specific WGD event occurred approximately 60 Mya, while the recent hybrid doubling in L. lucidus took place around 4.77 Mya. This temporal pattern indicates the tetraploid ancestor maintained remarkable karyotypic stability throughout the 60–4.77 Mya period, with recent hybridization disrupting this conserved state. Analysis of sequenced Lamiaceae species (T. quinquecostatus, L. sibiricus, L. japonicus, S. barbata, and S. baicalensis) reveals these taxa experienced no lineage-specific WGD events following the L-WGD but underwent extensive chromosomal structural variations (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In summary, the ancestor of Lycopus species maintained stable chromosomal architecture after the Lamiaceae-shared WGD event without dramatic structural rearrangements.

Positive selection and convergent evolution implicate KNOX1 and LOB in underground organ development

Genes under positive selection often reflect plant adaptation to environmental pressures. To identify PSGs and REGs in L. lucidus, we compiled a dataset of 5247 orthologous genes across 12 species. Using branch-site and branch models, we detected 310 PSGs (Supplementary Data 4) and 776 REGs (Supplementary Data 5) in L. lucidus. These genes were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways including DNA replication, RNA polymerase, base excision repair, mismatch repair, nucleotide excision repair, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results corroborate the elevated nucleotide substitution rates observed across WGD events in L. lucidus subgenomes, where extensive purifying selection acts on numerous genes to eliminate deleterious mutations, ultimately driving the genome toward evolutionary equilibrium (Supplementary Fig. 1). Species across Lamiaceae, Araliaceae, and Apiaceae families exhibit varying degrees of root swelling, particularly in Araliaceae where medicinally valuable roots (e.g., P. ginseng, P. notoginseng) develop pronounced swelling. To evaluate whether convergent mechanisms underlie root swelling in these families, we first identified 109 shared PSGs among the three lineages (Supplementary Data 6). Three genes involved in sugar transport and starch/sucrose metabolism (evm13355.t1, evm50924.t1, and evm46483.t1) may contribute to starch and sucrose accumulation in swollen roots.

We employed two approaches to detect convergence. First, using the JTT-Fgene model, we compared observed convergent amino acid substitutions among L. lucidus, P. stipuleanatus, and D. carota against neutral expectations, identifying 19 genes with significant convergence (Supplementary Table 1). Second, applying the PCOC method, which considers changes in amino acid preferences rather than convergent substitutions, we detected 1049 genes showing convergent evolution (Supplementary Data 7). Intersection of both methods revealed 11 convergent evolutionary genes (CEGs), including KNOX1 (evm65044.t1) and LOB (evm56005.t1) (Table 2).

Four distinct genetic lineages corresponding to geographic regions reveal population structure in China

To investigate the genetic relationships among L. lucidus lineages, we conducted whole-genome resequencing of 60 individuals collected across China (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 2). The resequencing generated 997 Gb of data with an average depth of 12.32× (Supplementary Data 8). Through stringent variant calling and filtering, we identified 14,478,124 high-quality SNPs, approximately 3.6% of which were located in coding regions (Supplementary Table 3). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear division of the populations into two major groups: Shandong (SD) and non-Shandong (non-SD) populations (Fig. 5B). ADMIXTURE analysis at K = 2 completely separated all SD populations from other populations (Fig. 5C). Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis with 100 bootstrap replicates resolved four distinct monophyletic clades: Shandong populations (Group4, yellow), Southwest China populations (Group3, green), Hunan-Hubei populations (Group2, purple), and East China populations (Group1, red; Fig. 5C). Notably, Group1+Group2+Group3 collectively formed the non-SD cluster in PCA. Cross-validation scores from ADMIXTURE suggested K = 4 as the optimal number of ancestral populations (Fig. 5C). The non-SD populations (Group1-3 lineage) further subdivided into three distinct lineages at K = 4: Group1 lineage (southeastern China: JSSY, JSSQ, ZJHZ, HNCD, HBXG), Group2 lineage (central subtropical China: HBSZ, HNYZ), and Group3 lineage (southwestern China: CQFD, CQSZ, GZBJ). These assignments revealed a clear geographic distribution pattern, with distinct Shandong, southeastern, central, and southwestern clusters evident in PCA. The Group1 lineage showed relatively little genetic admixture from other populations, while Group2 and Group3 lineages exhibited signs of genetic introgression, potentially reflecting historical gene flow during population dispersal.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD), quantified as r², serves as a fundamental parameter for forward genetics studies in plants23. Our LD decay analysis revealed distinct patterns among population groups, with Group1 exhibiting the most rapid LD decay and Group2 demonstrating the slowest decay rate (Fig. 6). The observed genetic diversity patterns reflect evolutionary adaptation to historical environmental conditions, governed by two key factors: effective population size (Nₑ) and cumulative evolutionary pressures24. We quantified nucleotide diversity (π) and FST for all four groups. Overall, Chinese L. lucidus populations exhibit rich genetic diversity with clear geographic distribution patterns. The average nucleotide diversity of L. lucidus was 2.48 × 10⁻³, higher than cultivated crops like peach (π = 1.1 × 10⁻³)25 but lower than wild species such as grape (π = 3.5 × 10⁻³)26 and Sapindus mukorossi (π = 9.1 × 10⁻²)27, suggesting L. lucidus has relatively high genetic diversity and remains in early domestication stages. Among the four lineages, Group4 showed the highest nucleotide diversity (π = 2.55 × 10⁻³), while Group1 had the lowest (π = 1.48 × 10⁻³). High genetic divergence existed between Group1 and Group4 (FST = 0.345), whereas genetic differentiation between Group2 and Group4 was lowest (FST = 0.214, Fig. 6A). The SD lineage (π = 2.55 × 10⁻³) and non-SD lineages (Group1 + Group2 + Group3; π = 2.15 × 10⁻³) had nearly identical genetic diversity, with a genetic differentiation index lower than that among the four lineages (FST = 0.167) (Fig. 6A), suggesting possible introgression or gene flow between these lineages.

We further analyzed LD decay patterns across the four groups (Fig. 6). The physical distances at which LD decayed to half its maximum value were 51 bp, 314 bp, 71 bp, and 119 bp for each respective lineage. These values are substantially smaller than those reported in wild soybean (~27 kb), wild rice (20 kb), wild maize (22 kb), and wild European grapevines, being comparable only to domesticated grapevines (350 bp)27. Our findings are consistent with LD decay trends observed between domesticated and wild silkworm populations28. This pattern likely results from L. lucidus’s high fecundity, short generation times, and recent hybrid origin, which would significantly elevate recombination rates. When considered alongside the low nucleotide diversity and FST values, these results further support that all groups have experienced population bottlenecks of varying severity. Notably, Group1 exhibited both the fastest LD decay rate and the highest nucleotide diversity, suggesting it maintains the largest effective population size (Fig. 6B).

Late Holocene population bottleneck and striking enrichment of selected genes in subgenome C

To elucidate the evolutionary history of L. lucidus, we employed the Pairwise Sequentially Markovian Coalescent (PSMC) model29 for population history inference, calibrated using an assumed generation time of 1 year and a neutral mutation rate of 8.521 × 10⁻⁹ per year. PSMC analysis revealed that all three groups exhibited a consistent decline in effective population size (Nₑ) from approximately 0.2 Mya to a bottleneck event (with Nₑ ranging between 30,000 and 80,000) around 2 thousand years ago (kya), followed by gradual population recovery (Fig. 6C). These results indicate that the three groups share a common ancestral population that experienced at least one severe bottleneck event, potentially associated with dramatic climate fluctuations during the Quaternary glaciation, as population declines corresponded to cooling events (Fig. 6C). Warmer temperatures during the Last Glacial Maximum may have contributed to the subsequent increase in Nₑ of L. lucidus. Notably, when the three lineages reached their bottleneck around 2 kya, the Group2 lineage emerged and subsequently expanded, though its population size remained smaller than the other two groups (Fig. 6C). ADMIXTURE analysis revealed mixed ancestry in Group2 and Group3 populations. TreeMix analysis indicated genetic exchange between Group1 and Group3 (Fig. 6D). Further analysis of individual subpopulations showed that HBSZ and HNYZ from Group2 experienced significant external gene flow, while the CQSZ population from Group3 also displayed evident genetic introgression (Fig. 6D). Our results suggest that the Southeast lineage (Group1) may be the origin of L. lucidus in China.

Population selection analyses enable the identification of SNPs and genes involved in natural selection and population differentiation. Through comparative analysis of FST and XP-CLR values (top 1%) between Group4 (SD-lineage) and other groups (non-SD lineage), we identified 166 and 9 selective sweep regions containing PSGs using top 1% and 0.1% thresholds, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6). These regions encompassed 566 and 68 PSGs. Notably, among the 566 PSGs, 531 originated from subgenome C (93.8%), while 35 derived from subgenomes A and B (Supplementary Data 9). All 68 top 0.1% PSGs exclusively belonged to subgenome C (Supplementary Data 10). GO enrichment analysis of the 566 overlapping PSGs revealed 20 significant terms (p < 0.05), primarily associated with flooding stress response (D-lactate dehydrogenase activity, glycolate dehydrogenase activity, glycolate oxidase activity); rapid growth adaptation (xyloglucan:xyloglucosyl transferase activity, transferase activity, plant-type cell wall organization); and antioxidant systems (glycolate oxidase activity, oxidoreductase activity) (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 11). Lactate and glycolate metabolic processes were most prominent, with three enriched genes: evm26972.t1 (3 nonsynonymous SNPs), evm26973.t1 (6 nonsynonymous SNPs), and evm26976.t1 (3 nonsynonymous SNPs), all encoding D-lactate dehydrogenase, highlighting their critical role in adaptive evolution.

Environmental isolation dominates genetic differentiation over geographic distance

The Mantel test revealed a strong positive correlation between genetic distance and environmental distance in L. lucidus (r = 0.250, p < 0.05), confirming the critical role of environmental isolation in population genetic differentiation (Fig. 7). Environmental variable correlation analysis identified four weakly correlated variables (BIO8, BIO10, BIO13, BIO17) from the initial set of 20 variables, with all pairwise correlations below the threshold (|r| > 0.7). Isolation-by-environment (IBE) analysis showed no correlation between genetic distance and geographic distance (r = 0.195, p > 0.05), indicating that geographic distance may not be a driving factor for genetic variation (Fig. 7D, E). However, a partial Mantel test controlling for geographical distance showed no significant residual correlation between environmental distance and genetic distance (r = 0.159, p = 0.117) (Fig. 7G), suggesting that apparent environmental effects may partially depend on collinearity with geographical factors, with no detectable environmental selection pressure independent of geographical distribution.

Precipitation drives local adaptation with WGD-derived genes preferentially retained in subgenome A

Ancestral nucleotide polymorphisms may persist across evolutionary timescales through balancing selection during population differentiation, creating genomic segments exhibiting heightened variation within and among emerging subpopulations30,31. Conversely, these ancestral polymorphisms can undergo biased distribution among derived lineages under selective pressures, generating elevated genetic divergence in specific chromosomal segments. To identify regions exhibiting genetic variation patterns that deviate from genome-wide population structure, we compared π, Tajima’s D, FST, and XP-CLR statistics between the top 5% and bottom 5% windows of MDS1. We found that the top 0.5% windows exhibited significantly higher π, more positive Tajima’s D values, and lower FST and XP-CLR values compared to genomic background windows (Fig. 7), consistent with long-term balancing selection. Conversely, the bottom 5% windows showed significantly reduced π, more negative Tajima’s D, and elevated FST and XP-CLR (Fig. 7). Furthermore, we identified two directional selection regions (located at ~22.2 Mb and ~36.5 Mb on Chromosome 27) that distinguish the SD-lineage and non-SD-lineage. Gene annotation revealed that these regions are enriched for genes associated with plant immunity, development, and stress responses, including LRR (leucine-rich repeat), F-box proteins, Msr (methionine sulfoxide reductase), RPF1 (Rhizobium polar growth factor 1), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligases, and glycine-rich protein-like (GhGRPL) (Supplementary Data 12). These genes likely played critical roles in the divergence of the two lineages.

To identify genomic signatures of environmental adaptation in L. lucidus, we conducted comprehensive genotype-environment association analyses using an integrated framework combining LFMM and RDA. Based on RDA results, precipitation variables (Bio13 + Bio17) explained 46.942% of the variation, while temperature variables (Bio8 + Bio10) explained only 0.244%, indicating precipitation variation is the main driver of genetic variation. We identified 9496 putative adaptive variants (involving 5737 genes) from the overlapping results of LFMM and RDA, widely distributed across the genome (Supplementary Data 13). GO enrichment analysis showed these genes were mainly involved in defense response, signal transduction, and responses to external stress and stimuli (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 14). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to plant growth, metabolism, and abiotic stress responses, including plant-pathogen interaction, starch and sucrose metabolism, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, cutin\suberine\wax biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, and ABC transporters (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 15). These functional annotations suggest L. lucidus may regulate relevant metabolic pathways to cope with environmental changes.

To integrate insights from comparative and population-level analyses, we investigated the evolutionary origins of genes associated with local adaptation to determine the relative contributions of different duplication modes and their distribution across subgenomes. We categorized the duplication origins of genes containing core environmentally associated SNPs identified through GEA analysis. Among these candidate adaptive genes, the vast majority (3644, representing 63.52%) originated from WGD events (Fig. 8), constituting the highest proportion. This was followed by transposed duplication (TRD, 521 genes, 9.08%) and tandem duplication (TD, 336 genes, 5.86%) (Supplementary Data 16). This pattern indicates that WGD serves as the primary source of genetic material for local adaptation in L. lucidus. Furthermore, examination of the chromosomal distribution of these candidate adaptive genes revealed their asymmetric distribution across subgenomes, 1497 genes resided in subgenome A, 1188 in subgenome B, and 959 in subgenome C, demonstrating clear subgenome preference at the genomic level (Fig. 8). The same trend was observed for the remaining four gene types.

Discussion

Genes under positive selection often reflect plant adaptation to environmental pressures. We detected 310 PSGs and 776 REGs from 5247 orthologous genes across 12 species, which were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, including DNA replication, repair, and ribosome biogenesis. These results corroborate the elevated nucleotide substitution rates observed across WGD events in L. lucidus subgenomes, where extensive purifying selection acts on numerous genes to eliminate deleterious mutations, ultimately driving the genome toward evolutionary equilibrium. Species across Lamiaceae, Araliaceae, and Apiaceae exhibit varying degrees of root swelling, particularly in Araliaceae, where medicinally valuable roots (e.g., P. ginseng, P. notoginseng) develop pronounced swelling. Among 109 shared PSGs identified across these three lineages, three genes involved in sugar transport and starch/sucrose metabolism may contribute to starch and sucrose accumulation in swollen roots. Using two complementary approaches to detect convergence, we identified 11 high-confidence convergent evolutionary genes (CEGs), including KNOX1 and LOB. KNOX1 participates in regulating plant organ morphogenesis, including shoot meristem differentiation, internode formation, and ovary development31,32. LBD regulates lateral root development in plants33. Previous work in Arabidopsis demonstrated that LBD30 can form a trimeric protein complex with AtAS1, mediated by AtAS2, and influence lateral root boundary establishment by suppressing the expression of KNOX family members STM and BP34. Although Araliaceae, Lamiaceae, and Apiaceae are phylogenetically distant, they may have independently evolved the complex trait of root enlargement by altering the genetic basis of the same developmental regulatory modules (meristem activity and organ boundaries), though this requires further experimental validation.

Polyploidy represents a fundamental evolutionary mechanism in angiosperms, facilitating environmental adaptation and stress resilience while serving as a key speciation driver35–37. The genomic consequences of whole-genome duplication (WGD) events manifest through three primary mechanisms: (1) TE activation and epigenetic remodeling, (2) increased mutation rates and regulatory network rewiring, and (3) structural genome reorganization, collectively generating phenotypic novelty and evolutionary diversification38. Comparative analyses demonstrate that polyploid lineages consistently outperform their diploid progenitors in abiotic stress tolerance (salinity, cold, and drought), with polyploid frequency showing a positive latitudinal gradient reflecting their adaptive advantages in harsh environments4,38. The hexaploid common wheat (Triticum aestivum; AABBDD) exemplifies this phenomenon, having acquired superior environmental adaptability through the fusion of three divergent diploid genomes. This genomic merger confers distinct agronomic advantages, including enhanced stature, improved seed morphology, and robust stress resistance profiles37. A characteristic outcome of WGD, termed the “gigas effect,” results from increased nuclear DNA content that directly influences cellular dimensions, ultimately producing larger plant organs and tissues38. This phenomenon is well-documented in Fragaria spp., where polyploid cultivars consistently develop larger fruits than their diploid relatives39,40. Within the Lamiaceae family, L. lucidus represents a unique case as the only known species exhibiting root swelling. We postulate that this distinctive morphological trait likely originated through its lineage-specific WGD event. However, the evolutionary advantages of polyploidy are counterbalanced by significant metabolic costs associated with maintaining larger genomes. Consequently, polyploid lineages frequently undergo rediploidization, a gradual genomic streamlining process involving: (1) restoration of diploid-like chromosome pairing, (2) targeted gene loss and chromosomal rearrangement, and (3) TE-mediated structural variation21,41. These mechanisms collectively drive functional innovation through expression dosage rebalancing, redundant gene elimination, epigenetic landscape remodeling, and gene subfunctionalization/neofunctionalization, ultimately enhancing adaptive potential while establishing a reproductive isolation barrier21,38,42.

A central evolutionary process following polyploidization is “rediploidization,” where polyploid genomes gradually revert to diploid-like states through gene silencing, loss of redundant sequences, chromosomal rearrangements6, and transcriptome rewiring43. Chromosomal fusion is the primary pathway for reducing chromosome number after polyploidization, a process also termed “descending dysploidy.” It occurs through three main mechanisms: end-to-end joining (EEJ), nested chromosome fusion (NCF), and chromosomal translocation (CT)11. This phenomenon is widespread in alpine plants including Rhodiola44 (Supplementary Fig. 7), Rheum nobile45, Salix brachista46, Hippophae47, Rhododendron48, and Gentiana49. These species underwent WGD events followed by rapid rediploidization via structural variations that reduced chromosome numbers, a pattern potentially attributable to growth-defense trade-offs50. While initial polyploidization advantages provide temporary buffers, the prolonged selective pressures of high-altitude environments make large genome maintenance costly. Chromosomal fusion and redundant sequence deletion streamline genomes, with WGD-generated genetic redundancy providing raw material for adaptation while chromosomal restructuring promotes speciation through reproductive isolation4.

Polyploids are categorized into autopolyploids and allopolyploids based on origin. Allopolyploids exhibit fractionation bias through chromosomal rearrangements and gene loss, unlike autopolyploids10,51–53, though this bias varies considerably. Beyond chromosome reduction, many polyploids maintain stable counts (e.g., soybean, cotton, L. lucidus), with soybean as an autotetraploid and cotton/L. lucidus as allopolyploids. This stability is governed by “subgenome dominance”, reflecting ancestral genomic divergences53. Soybean’s autotetraploid nature results in balanced subgenomes with random gene loss53, while allopolyploid cotton shows faster evolution in subgenome A under relaxed selection9,54. Our analysis provides strong evidence supporting the subgenome dominance theory. As an allopolyploid undergoing clear subgenome differentiation following a ~4.77 mya polyploidization event, L. lucidus shows subgenomes A and C exhibiting greater evolutionary stability. HYPHY analysis revealed asymmetric selection pressures: compared to subgenome B, subgenome A contains more purifying-selected genes, while subgenomes A and C show more intensified/relaxed selection and higher pseudogene frequencies (Supplementary Data 2). These patterns indicate: (1) subgenome B has stabilized as the dominant subgenome, maintaining core functions; (2) subgenomes A and C remain in active functional reorganization; and (3) this asymmetry aligns with established diploidization models43,45. High recombination rates confirmed by LD decay analysis further support this conclusion. Building on clarified subgenome structure, a key question emerges: how will the L. lucidus genome continue evolving? While significant subgenome differentiation exists, future large-scale structural variations for chromosome reduction (“descending dysploidy”) remain uncertain. Genomic evolution relies on “plasticity” for environmental fine-tuning, often led by non-dominant subgenomes. Subgenome dominance and structural variation-driven diploidization represent complementary rather than mutually exclusive strategies, with polyploids utilizing both for genetic resource optimization against environmental challenges. Our findings support descending dysploidy as a major karyotypic restructuring mechanism in plant polyploids, consistent with chromosome number reduction as a core evolutionary trend in land plants8,22,52,55,56.

This study presents the first genomic evidence for significant divergence between Shandong (SD-lineage) and non-Shandong (non-SD-lineage) populations of L. lucidus, revealing north-south differentiation shaped by Shandong’s unique climatic regime. Despite genetic divergence, both lineages shared congruent demographic trajectories, indicating Pleistocene climatic oscillations imposed parallel selective pressures across the species’ range. Local adaptation emerges as the predominant evolutionary force structuring this divergence57. PCA and structure analyses revealed substantial divergence corresponding to fundamentally distinct climatic regimes, with Shandong’s semi-arid climate (550–700 mm precipitation; pH 7.5–8.5) selecting for different adaptive strategies than humid subtropical environments south of the Yangtze River (>1000 mm precipitation; pH 5.0–6.5)58. Through FST and XP-CLR analyses, we identified 166 selective sweep regions (top 1%) containing 566 PSGs. GO enrichment revealed 20 significant terms associated with flooding stress response, rapid growth adaptation, and antioxidant systems (Supplementary Data 11). These genomic signatures reflect adaptations to Shandong’s distinctive drought stress and high soil alkalinity, contrasting with humidity-related selection pressures in southern populations. Climate-associated genetic variant identification provides critical insights for predicting adaptive capacity under climate change59,60. Our genotype-environment association analysis identified four key bioclimatic variables, with bio17 (precipitation of driest quarter) explaining most (64.18%) observed variation. Redundancy analysis confirmed precipitation variables (Bio13 + Bio17) as primary divergence drivers (46.94% variance explained), while temperature variables (Bio8 + Bio10) showed minimal influence (0.24%), strongly suggesting water availability represents the dominant selective pressure.

Combined LFMM and RDA approaches detected 9496 environmentally associated SNPs within 5737 PCGs. GO enrichment demonstrated involvement in response to external stimuli, while KEGG enrichment indicated participation in secondary metabolic pathways, including wax and flavonoid biosynthesis. The wax layer provides protective barriers in arid regions61, while flavonoids serve as essential photoprotectants through light-absorption and antioxidant activity62. Previous studies show flavonoid accumulation enhances drought adaptation63 with FLS genes under positive selection64. Given SD-lineage habitats are more arid and high-temperature, flavonoid biosynthesis genes appear under directional selection for drought and high-light stress adaptation. Brassinosteroids (BRs) additionally regulate growth and stress responses65, modulating SOS pathway-mediated salt stress66 and improving drought tolerance67 and heat protection64. These genes likely play crucial roles in L. lucidus adaptation to drought and alkaline soils, though genetic transformation experiments are needed to validate their adaptive divergence.

Our integrated analysis reveals a hierarchical architecture linking gene duplication mechanisms, subgenome evolutionary dynamics, and environmental adaptation. The overwhelming predominance of WGD-derived genes (63.52%) among environmentally associated loci provides strong evidence that WGD served as the primary genomic reservoir for adaptive evolution, aligning with the “gene dosage balance hypothesis”68. Preferential retention of adaptive genes in subgenome A (1497 genes) compared to subgenomes B (1188) and C (959) demonstrates that the evolutionarily stable dominant subgenome has become a privileged repository for environmentally relevant genes. This subgenomic asymmetry suggests diploidization processes have choreographed the genomic geography of adaptation, with the dominant subgenome assuming disproportionate importance in environmental response. We propose a coherent model for adaptive evolution in L. lucidus: (1) WGD and PD events supplied essential genetic raw material, particularly for environmentally critical secondary metabolic pathways; (2) environmental selection pressures acted predominantly on this WGD-derived repertoire, driving accelerated evolution for adaptive functions; and (3) subgenome dominance structured these adaptive outcomes, with subgenome A preferentially retaining selected genes. This multi-level convergence strongly suggests WGD events provided genetic raw material, subgenome dominance determined genomic organization, and environmental pressures fine-tuned selective retention, ultimately constructing a sophisticated adaptive landscape in L. lucidus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

The reference genome for L. lucidus was generated from a single specimen collected from Guihua Town, Changde County, Hunan, China (28.0°N, 108.48°E). The plant was maintained under natural conditions at Wuhan University’s experimental garden to minimize environmental stress prior to high-molecular-weight DNA extraction. Morphological authentication was performed by Dr. Tao Yuan (Wuhan University). No collection permits were required as L. lucidus is neither endangered nor protected. For nucleotide diversity and population structure assessment, resequencing specimens were obtained from 13 natural populations across eight Chinese provinces: Suizhou, Hubei (HBSZ, n = 5); Xiaogan, Hubei (HBXG, n = 5); Yongzhou, Hunan (HNYZ, n = 4); Changde, Hunan (HNCD, n = 5); Shuyang, Jiangsu (JSSY, n = 5); Suqian, Jiangsu (JSSQ, n = 5); Huzhou, Zhejiang (ZJHZ, n = 5); Tai’an, Shandong (SDTA, n = 3); Linshu, Shandong (SDLS, n = 5); Heze, Shandong (SDHZ, n = 5); Fengdu, Chongqing (CQFD, n = 5); Shizhu, Chongqing (CQSZ, n = 5); and Bijie, Guizhou (GZBJ, n = 5) (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 2). Fresh leaves were sampled, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until DNA/RNA extraction. Tissues from leaves, roots, and stems were used for total RNA extraction, and qualified libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI, China). A multi-platform sequencing strategy (BGI DNBSEQ-T7, PacBio HiFi, Hi-C) was implemented for reference genome construction. DNA extraction and library preparation followed Zhang et al.69 and Yuan et al.70. BGI sequencing yielded approximately 100× clean reads. PacBio HiFi produced approximately 32× read data from one library. Hi-C generated approximately 100× clean paired-end reads (Supplementary Table 4). Individual genomic DNA samples (n = 60) underwent population resequencing on the BGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform (350 bp insert size) at an average depth of 20×. The detailed information for all samples used in this study is provided in Supplementary Data 17.

Draft genome assembly and annotation

Genome size and heterozygosity were initially estimated using second-generation sequencing data (DNBseq) subjected to k-mer analysis. The 21-mer frequency distribution was generated using Jellyfish v2.2.671 and analyzed with Genomescope272. For de novo genome assembly, PacBio HiFi reads were assembled using Hifiasm v0.16.173 with parameters “-t 60 -l 2 -n 4”. The DNBseq short reads were not incorporated into the primary assembly but were reserved for subsequent base-quality correction and validation. Hi-C reads were aligned to the assembly using BWA v0.7.17-r118874. Resulting alignments were processed with HapHiC v1.0.675 using parameters “-RE AAGCTT --quick_view”. Manual refinement was conducted using JuiceBox Assembly Tools v1.11.0876. Chromosome phasing was performed using Subphaser v1.2.677 with parameters “-k 150 -q 2 -f 2.0”, selected based on the SubPhaser documentation and benchmarking results.

Transposable elements (TEs) annotation was performed using EDTA v1.9.678 with parameters “--species others --sensitive 0 --anno 1 --threads 60 --curatedlib SINE_LINE.fa”. TE regions were subsequently masked using RepeatMasker v1.2.179. PCGs prediction integrated three complementary approaches: de novo, homology-based, and transcript evidence. De novo gene modeling utilized AUGUSTUS v3.4.080 and GeneMark-EP v4.68_lic81. Transcript assembly was performed using Trinity v2.13.282 and StringTie v2.1.383, valid transcript alignments were clustered and gene structures were iteratively optimized using PASA v2.5.3 (https://github.com/PASApipeline/PASApipeline). Homology-based annotation was conducted with GeneWise v2.4.184 using protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana, Leonurus japonicus, Leonurus sibiricus, Scutellaria baicalensis, and Scutellaria barbata. Non-redundant gene models from these three prediction strategies were consolidated using EVM v1.1.185. For non-coding RNA (ncRNA) annotation, tRNA genes were predicted using tRNAscan-SE v1.486. Other ncRNA genes, including microRNAs (miRNAs) and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), were annotated using Infernal v1.1.287 by screening against the Rfam database v14.188 with parameters “--rfam --cut_ga --nohmmonly --tblout rna.tblout --fmt 2 --clanin Rfam.clanin -o rna.cmscan --cpu 60 Rfam.cm genome.fa”. Pseudogenes were identified by comparing homologous protein sequences with the reference genome using Genewise v2.4.184. Protein functions were annotated using BLAST searches against the Pfam89, Gene Ontology (GO)90, InterPro 5.3191, KEGG92, Swiss-Prot93, and NCBI NR databases. Genome completeness was assessed with BUSCO v5.2.294.

Comparative genomic and evolutionary analyses

To clarify the evolutionary relationships of L. lucidus within the Lamiaceae family, we selected protein sequences from 42 species for comparative phylogenetic analysis and divergence time estimation (Supplementary Data 18). Additionally, to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the convergent evolution of storage roots in three phylogenetically distant families, Araliaceae, Lamiaceae, and Apiaceae, we performed convergent evolution analysis using 12 representative species. The selection deliberately included key taxa with root swelling from these families: Panax stipuleanatus (Araliaceae), L. lucidus (Lamiaceae), and Daucus carota (Apiaceae). Closely related species from the same families lacking swollen roots were included as controls, such as Coriandrum sativum (Apiaceae), Aralia elata (Araliaceae), and Scutellaria baicalensis, S. barbata, T. quinquecostatus, Leonurus japonicus, and L. sibiricus (Lamiaceae), to help identify lineage-specific genetic changes. The well-characterized model plants Arabidopsis thaliana and Vitis vinifera served as distantly related outgroups, providing both a root for phylogeny reconstruction and a broader evolutionary context.

Orthologous gene clusters were identified using OrthoFinder v2.5.295. A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on 292 single-copy genes, with species divergence times estimated using the mcmctree module in PAML96. Three fossil calibrations were applied: L. sibiricus - L. japonicus (~0.2 million years ago, Mya), Apiaceae - Araliaceae (~54.3–69 Mya), and the most recent common ancestor (MRCA, ~110–124 Mya). Gene family dynamics (expansion and contraction) were analyzed using CAFE 597, integrating gene family data, phylogenetic relationships, and divergence times. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed with ClusterProfiler v4.3.498, and results were visualized using the ChiPlot web platform99. We systematically identified and compared pseudogenes across subgenomes to determine whether the functional categories of pseudogenized genes differ significantly, thereby elucidating the role of asymmetric gene decay in shaping subgenome evolution and potential functional specialization following polyploidization. Protein sequences from four closely related species (S. baicalensis, S. barbata, L. japonicus, and L. sibiricus) were aligned to each genome using GenBlastA100 with the command: genblastA -P blast -pg tblastn -q pep.fa -t genome.fa -p T -e 1e-5 -g T -f F -a 0.5 -d 100000 -r 10 -c 0.5 -s 0 -o pep.out. The resulting alignments were used as seed target proteins and mapped to the genome using GeneWise84 to identify potential pseudogenes.

WGD, gene duplication identification, and ancestral chromosome reconstruction

To investigate whole-genome duplication (WGD) events in Lamiaceae evolution, we comprehensively analyzed gene duplication patterns in L. lucidus. Using DupGen_finder101 with L. sibiricus as the phylogenetic outgroup, we systematically classified duplicated gene pairs into five categories: whole-genome duplications (WGD), tandem duplications (TD), proximal duplications (PD), transposed duplications (TRD), and dispersed duplications (DSD). For evolutionary rate analysis, protein sequences were first converted to corresponding coding DNA sequences (CDS) using PAL2NAL v14.1102. We then computed non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution ratios for all identified gene pairs using KaKs_Calculator103 to assess selection pressures on duplicated genes. WGDI v0.6.5104 was used to identify homologs and estimate Ks values (YN00 model). Ks distributions and synteny visualization were generated using GenomeSyn105[111]. The ancestral Lamiaceae genome was reconstructed following Sun et al.104 using WGDI, with reference to methodologies described by Yuan et al.20 and Shao et al.106.

Identification of orthologs, PSGs, and REGs

Orthologs were detected using the reciprocal best hit (RBH) approach107, as implemented in previous studies108–110. From 5474 homologous genes (with L. lucidus as the foreground branch; Fig. 2), positively selected genes (PSGs) and rapidly evolving genes (REGs) were identified using the branch-site model in CodeML. REG validation additionally employed the branch model. Retained genes met dual criteria: a chi-square test p-value < 0.05 and BEB-supported site intervals spanning at least 10 amino acids. Given the importance of subterranean organ development in plant speciation and evidence that convergent evolution of these structures may facilitate adaptive radiation in ecologically similar lineages111, we investigated the genetic basis of parallel underground organ development across three distinct plant families (Araliaceae, Lamiaceae, and Apiaceae). We applied two complementary approaches to examine potential molecular mechanisms underlying “underground” organ formation in these lineages. First, raw protein sequences were aligned using PRANK112, and poorly aligned regions were filtered out using Gblocks113. The resulting alignments were converted into PAML format. Ancestral amino acid sequences at internal nodes were reconstructed using CODEML in PAML v4.9j96. Molecular-level convergent evolutionary events were identified using: (1) an amino acid substitution-based analysis with convCal108, and (2) PCOC114, which incorporates amino acid physicochemical properties (e.g., molecular size, charge, and polarity) in its detection algorithm.

Population SNP detection and filtering

To estimate nucleotide diversity (π) and population size in L. lucidus, we processed raw sequencing data from 60 individuals representing 13 populations. Initial quality filtering was performed using Fastp v0.23.2115. Cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA-MEM v0.7.874. The resulting alignments were sorted and compressed with SAMtools116, followed by duplicate removal using Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertion-deletion mutations (indels) were identified using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK)117 pipeline, including HaplotypeCaller, CombineGVCFs, and GenotypeGVCFs, with output generated in variant call format (VCF). SNP filtering applied the following thresholds: QD < 2.0, MQ < 40, FS > 60.0, SOR > 3.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. Indel filtering used: QD < 2.0 || ReadPosRankSum < −10.0 || InbreedingCoeff < −0.8||FS > 100.0||SOR > 5.0||QUAL < 30118. Only indels ≤40 bp were retained. Variants showing multiple alleles, >50% missingness, or minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.005 were removed to generate a basic variant set. For downstream analyses (phylogenetic trees, LD decay, PCA, population structure), SNPs with MAF < 0.05 were excluded to form a core dataset. Final variant annotation was performed using ANNOVAR v2015-12-14119 against the reference genome.

Population structure and LD analysis

Population genetic structure of L. lucidus was reconstructed using the whole-genome SNP dataset. PLINK v1.07120 generated eigenvalue and eigenvector matrices, visualized in R. ADMIXTURE121 assessed genetic structure across all individuals (K = 2–6). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using maximum likelihood methods implemented in FastTree122 with 1000 bootstrap replicates, using Stachys sieboldii as the outgroup, and visualized via ChiPlot [104]. Population-level genetic diversity was estimated by calculating nucleotide diversity (π) with VCFtools v0.1.16123 using parameters “–window-pi 100 kb –window-pi-step 10 kb”. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was performed using PopLDdecay v3.42124 (-MaxDist 500). Genome-wide pairwise r² values were calculated for all SNP pairs, and LD decay profiles were plotted in 1-kb bins using Plot_MultiPop.pl124.

Demographic history and introgression

We conducted population genetic analyses to reconstruct demographic and evolutionary histories. Changes in effective population size over time were estimated using the PSMC algorithm29 with optimized parameters (-N25 -t15 -r5 -p “4 + 25*2 + 4 + 6”). Reliability of inferred trajectories was assessed through 100 rounds of resampling to generate confidence intervals. Population divergence and gene flow patterns were examined using maximum-likelihood analysis in TreeMix v1.13125, incorporating a Gaussian approximation for allele frequency drift. Potential admixture events were explored by testing models with increasing complexity (allowing 1 to 4 migration edges). Phylogenetic reconstruction robustness was evaluated using 1000 bootstrap iterations to quantify branch support.

Detection of selected genes

Genomic signatures of selection were identified using two complementary approaches. Population structure assessment through principal component analysis (PCA) and phylogenetic inference using SNP data revealed two distinct lineages within L. lucidus: the SD group and non-SD populations. Using the SD lineage as reference, we performed a cross-population composite likelihood ratio (XP-CLR) scan with XP-CLR v1.1.2126, applying a sliding window approach (100 kb windows, 10 kb step size) to detect selective sweeps. Lineage-specific differentiation was quantified by computing pairwise FST values between groups using VCFtools v0.1.16123 with matching window parameters (100 kb windows, 10 kb step size). Genomic regions simultaneously ranking in the top 1% of both XP-CLR and FST analyses were designated as high-confidence selection targets. Functional annotation of candidate genes was conducted via GO enrichment analysis in ClusterProfiler v4.3.498 to elucidate their potential adaptive roles.

Identification of environment-associated adaptive variants

To detect genomic regions showing anomalous population structure potentially under selection and to identify heterogeneous correlation patterns, we adopted a window size of 100 SNPs. The Euclidean distance matrix was calculated and visualized through MDS transformation. Outlier windows corresponded to those with extreme MDS1 values (top 0.5% and bottom 0.5%). For screening local adaptive environmental variation, we acquired 19 bioclimatic variables and one topographic factor (elevation) from WorldClim v2.1 at 2.5-min (5 km) resolution127. To avoid multicollinearity among environmental factors, we computed Pearson correlation coefficients using corrplot v0.92128, retaining factors with |r | < 0.7. Subsequently, Maxent v3.4.1 modeled relationships between 20 environmental factors and distribution data from 794 L. lucidus sites to compute climatic variable contributions. Ultimately, four key uncorrelated factors were selected: mean temperature of wettest quarter (BIO8), mean temperature of warmest quarter (BIO10), precipitation of wettest month (BIO13), and precipitation of driest quarter (BIO17).

We applied two complementary genotype-environment association (GEA) approaches to identify SNPs associated with environmental variables. First, we employed a univariate latent factor mixed model (LFMM) using the LEA v3.3.2 package129, which accounts for population structure while testing allele frequency-environment correlations. Input genotypes were formatted using the ped2lfmm function, and for each environmental predictor, we ran five independent MCMC chains (5000 burn-in iterations followed by 10,000 sampling steps). Significance thresholds were determined using false discovery rate (FDR) correction via the p-values function. Second, we conducted multivariate redundancy analysis (RDA) implemented in vegan v2.5-7130 to detect SNPs correlated with composite environmental gradients. Prior to analysis, genotype data were converted from VCF format using PLINK v1.07120. Environmental predictor significance was evaluated through 999 permutations using the anova.cca function, while adaptive loci were identified via the outliers function. Only SNPs consistently identified by both LFMM and RDA were retained as high-confidence candidates. To assess the influence of geographic and environmental factors on genetic differentiation, we performed Mantel tests on matrices of (FST/1 − FST), geographic distance, and environmental distance. Correlations between genetic differentiation (FST/1 − FST) and geographic/environmental distances were determined using Mantel tests in the vegan package130. Finally, candidate SNPs were functionally annotated through GO enrichment analysis using ClusterProfiler v4.3.498 to infer potential biological roles.

Statistics and reproducibility

The significance of differences in gene evolutionary rates among different subgenomes was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, with significance levels denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. For functional enrichment analysis, GO and KEGG terms were evaluated by Fisher’s exact test combined with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons, and only terms with an adjusted P-value below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For reference genome assembly, no biological replicate was performed, as the L. lucidus genome was assembled from a single specimen. For population resequencing, a total of 60 individuals from 13 natural populations were used. Each individual plant was treated as an independent biological replicate (sample sizes per population: n = 3–5, as detailed in the “Sample collection and genome sequencing” section). For RNA-seq used in genome annotation, total RNA was extracted from leaves, roots, and stems of the same single specimen used for genome assembly. These three tissue samples were processed once each, serving as representative transcript evidence without biological replication.
