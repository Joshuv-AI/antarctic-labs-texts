Implementation climate and readiness for the THRIVE intervention in school settings: a mixed methods study
Authors: Sinko Wang, Hayley Quinones, Lisa Miller, Joan Luby, Patricia L Kohl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchools are increasingly recognized as critical settings for delivering mental health interventions. The successful implementation of school-based programs like THRIVE, a parent–child intervention, is likely influenced by the organizational context and the school’s readiness for change. This mixed methods study, part of a Type 2 hybrid implementation-effectiveness RCT, aimed to explore baseline contextual factors that may influence the implementation of THRIVE in school settings.MethodsUsing a convergent mixed methods design, we investigated the climate of schools implementing THRIVE. Twenty-five interventionists were recruited from six school districts. Quantitative data were collected using the School Implementation Climate Scale (SICS) and Organizational Readiness for Change (ORC). Individual qualitative interviews guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) were also conducted. Schools were classified as resource-rich or resource-constrained using the Social Vulnerability Index. Descriptive and bivariate statistics were performed. Deductive thematic analysis was conducted. Results were integrated through a joint display table to identify convergences and divergences.ResultsThe overall SICS score averaged 3.27 (SD = 0.66) and the overall ORC score averaged 3.79 (SD = 0.42), suggesting moderately positive school implementation climate and readiness. On average, participating schools demonstrated a strong focus on evidence-based practice (EBP) and high availability of training and resources for EBP, while providing limited incentives for EBP use (M = 2.09, SD = 0.93). They also presented a collaborative and supportive working environment with shared values but insufficient communication (M = 3.36, SD = 0.73). Qualitative findings are mostly consistent with quantitative results. Most interventionists (n = 24) believed THRIVE aligns with school values and family needs and remained optimistic despite barriers such as time commitment and negative perceptions. Compared to resource-rich schools (n = 11), resource-constrained schools (n = 14) placed less emphasis on EBP (t(23) = 2.37, p < .05) and had fewer facilitators (n = 5), but perceived greater benefits in implementing THRIVE (n = 14).ConclusionsThis study found a moderately positive implementation climate and organizational readiness for change in schools adopting the THRIVE intervention. However, limited incentives, communication challenges, and resource constraints may hinder successful implementation. The divergence between strong support for EBPs and the lack of resources suggests that implementation strategies must be tailored and flexible to promote buy-in and address structural barriers.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06139874. Background

Educational settings are a promising service-delivery platform to increase the reach of interventions to prevent child psychopathology, including those that seek to promote child socio-emotional development by enhancing supportive parenting and increasing the quality of the parent–child relationship [1–4]. However, the climate of school systems, and high demands and limitations in the role of school counselors, may present unique and complex challenges that could hinder the efficient uptake and implementation of a preventive parent–child intervention [5, 6], such as THRIVE. Considering these challenges, this study seeks to better understand the implementation climate and readiness of schools adopting THRIVE, an intervention delivered by school interventionists to parent and child dyads outside the classroom (see 1.1 THRIVE intervention).

In recent years, escalating rates of child mental and behavioral health issues have become an urgent public health concern [7, 8]. Unmet mental health needs often manifest in schools as bullying, aggression, and classroom disruptions [9], with all of these factors negatively influencing academic performance. Parent–child interventions have emerged as a critical strategy to address early child behavioral and mental health problems by enhancing parenting practices. Children who receive sensitive and responsive caregiving are less likely to develop psychopathology or experience difficulties in school [10, 11], and this association is much stronger for families with a low socioeconomic status [12].

While several empirically supported early interventions effectively improve parenting practices [13–15], there is limited service accessibility in most resource-constrained communities [16]. Traditional clinic-based models often exacerbate the service gap due to long waitlists, geographic distance, and a lack of trained professionals [17]. Therefore, school-based mental health interventions become a feasible and accessible solution to meet children’s socio-emotional and behavioral service needs [1, 2], particularly for those who exhibit problematic behaviors without meeting clinical diagnostic thresholds in early childhood [3, 4]. As such, THRIVE was developed as a potentially feasible preventive intervention for young children and their caregivers, accessible through schools as part of routine support. In this study, we further compared schools serving resource-rich and resource-constrained communities, because community-level resources may shape both the school implementation climate and family access to preventive parenting support [1, 5, 18].

THRIVE intervention

The THRIVE intervention is an 8-session preventive intervention, adapted from the validated Parent–Child Interaction Therapy-Emotional Development (PCIT-ED) model, which consists of 20 sessions [19, 20]. PCIT-ED focuses on emotion development and targets the caregiver as the child’s external emotion regulator and coach. It has shown powerful effects for children with depression but has basic elements thought to be developmentally useful for children without depression. Based on this, a shortened form of PCIT entitled “THRIVE” was developed to enhance accessibility [21]. Specifically, THRIVE targets children between the ages of 4.0 and 6.11, who do not have serious chronic medical illness or psychiatric symptoms in the clinical range. In the U.S., children typically attend preschool at ages 3–4, kindergarten at age 5, and first grade at age 6. Accordingly, THRIVE participants may be enrolled in preschools, kindergarten, or elementary schools.

Like PCIT-ED, THRIVE targets the parent–child dyad, uses a teach and coach approach and focuses on: (1) enhancing parental warmth and support (sessions 1–3), (2) teaching parent effective yet nurturing limit setting (sessions 4–6) and (3) encourages the parent to facilitate the child’s emotion development by helping them teach their child to accurately identify and regulate their own emotions (sessions 7–8) [21]. Home practice activities (i.e., five-minute practice each day) are assigned following each session.

THRIVE was also developed to be delivered by trained school-based interventionists (e.g., school social workers, counselors, and parent engagement coordinators) and, unlike PCIT-ED, THRIVE does not require a master’s-level clinician, thereby enhancing broader accessibility. To be equipped with the necessary skills, each new interventionist completed 11 h of online training as part of their onboarding. The training videos provided detailed instructions on how to deliver each THRIVE session. Interventionists also had the opportunity to review training videos and the program manual throughout implementation. Ongoing supervision was provided by a licensed social worker with experience delivering THRIVE. To further enhance accessibility, each session could be delivered either online or in person (i.e., a hybrid format), offering greater scheduling flexibility for families and interventionists.

A pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) of THRIVE was successfully completed in a high-risk school district in St. Louis, Missouri, yielding promising effects across child and parent outcomes [22]. Given the complexity of implementing a preventive parent–child intervention such as THRIVE within school settings, which requires a school interventionist to work directly with the parent–child dyad outside of the classroom, it is essential to investigate the organizational and community context that supports or hinders its adoption.

Implementation climate and readiness

A positive implementation climate and readiness (ICR), including both school implementation climate (SIC) and organizational readiness for change (ORC), is essential for school interventionists’ uptake of evidence-based practice (EBP) [23, 24]. SIC refers to the extent to which the employees’ use of EBPs is rewarded, supported, and expected by their organization [25, 26]. A positive SIC may encourage the use of EBPs and integration of training into everyday practice [27–29]. ORC is another contextual factor influencing the use of EBPs. ORC refers to the degree to which an institution is prepared to implement a new intervention [30, 31]. With an increased ORC, interventionists are more likely to navigate challenges and encourage the continued use of the intervention within the organization’s structure and among the individuals benefiting from it. To systematically examine these organizational factors, this study draws on an implementation framework to provide a comprehensive theoretical lens for understanding multilevel influences on implementation.

Theoretical framework

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) is a comprehensive theoretical framework widely applied to investigate factors influencing the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of interventions across various contexts [32, 33]. CFIR organizes implementation constructs into five interrelated domains: (1) Innovation, (2) Individual, (3) Inner Setting, (4) Outer Setting, and (5) Process. CFIR is particularly useful in identifying barriers and facilitators across domains that shape implementation outcomes and guide the development of targeted implementation strategies [34, 35]. CFIR also provides a theoretical lens for data collection, analysis, and integration to improve THRIVE implementation in educational settings (see 2 Method).

More recently, researchers have applied CFIR to educational contexts, recognizing schools as complex organizations with unique structural and cultural features that influence intervention adoption [36]. Studies have consistently found that inner setting factors, such as implementation climate, leadership support, available resources, and staff engagement, are among the most influential determinants of implementation success for school-based mental health programs [36–38]. However, barriers such as scheduling conflicts, competing academic priorities, and insufficient organizational readiness have been shown to outweigh facilitators, underscoring the need for systematic assessment of school contexts prior to intervention adoption [18, 36].

However, most studies focused on secondary school settings or broad wellness initiatives, with comparatively less attention to preventive mental health interventions targeting young children and their caregivers in elementary schools. Few studies have accounted for the broader socioeconomic context of implementation settings, such as differences between resource-rich and resource-constrained communities. To address these gaps, the present study applies the CFIR to examine ICR among schools in diverse communities adopting THRIVE.

Method

Study design

This study employed a convergent mixed methods design (Fig. 1) to investigate the implementation climate of schools adopting THRIVE. The study design allowed us to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the school implementation climate by contextualizing, triangulating, and substantiating the unique perspectives captured separately by quantitative and qualitative data [39, 40]. Both data were collected and analyzed concurrently, and the joint display table was used to compare and integrate both quantitative and qualitative findings [41].

Sample and procedure

Between April and November 2024, this study collected data from 25 school interventionists participating in a Type 2 hybrid implementation-effectiveness RCT of THRIVE, funded by the National Institute of Mental Health (RO1MH130621). Interventionists included school social workers, counselors, and parent engagement coordinators from six school districts in the St. Louis area, with a diverse range of educational settings including public, private, and federally funded institutions (i.e., charters and Head Start). The school districts were diverse, spanning urban, suburban, and low-, middle-, and high-socioeconomic-status areas.

As indicated in Tables 1, 25 interventionists had an average age of 45 years, ranging from 28 to 60 years. All interventionists were female. They reported their race/ethnicity as White (64%), Black (20%), and Other (16%). The majority had a master’s degree (n = 18, 72%). Approximately one-third of them were school social workers (n = 8), and a similar proportion were school counselors (n = 9). The rest were family service workers, parent educators, disability coordinators, etc. (n = 8, 32%). Most interventionists (88%) have worked in schools for more than three years, and more than half (56%) have been in their current role for more than three years.

Interventionists began their onboarding process by completing the baseline survey, which was distributed through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, web-based platform for collecting and managing study data [42, 43]. Then, the qualitative interview was conducted with each individual after they finished their THRIVE training, but prior to starting to administer the intervention. Both quantitative and qualitative data were collected from the same interventionists (n = 25). All interventionists signed informed consent approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board.

Quantitative measures

School implementation climate

The SIC was assessed using the School Implementation Climate Scale (SICS), a validated measure designed to capture school staff perceptions of the extent to which their school environment supports the effective implementation of EBPs [44]. The SICS comprises seven subscales: focus on EBP, educational support for EBP, recognition of EBP, reward for EBP, use of data to support EBPs, existing supports to deliver EBPs, and EBP integration. Respondents rate items on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a more supportive climate for EBP implementation. The scale has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80 across subscales) and construct validity in school-based mental health service settings [44]. In the current study sample, SICS also demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.93 for the total scale and ranging from 0.77 to 0.88 across subscales, except for the reward subscale (0.62; see supplemental materials).

Organizational readiness for change

The school’s ORC was measured using the TCU Organizational Readiness for Change Scale (ORC-D4) [45]. The ORC-D4 encompasses multiple domains that capture staff and organizational factors hypothesized to influence change processes within service settings. For this study, we focused on two domains: Staff Attributes and Organizational Climate. Each subscale includes 5–6 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions. The ORC-D4 has established evidence for both its construct validity and reliability, as demonstrated in multiple research studies [46–48]. In the current sample, ORC also demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.94 for the total scale and ranging from 0.63 to 0.94 across subscales, except for the change subscale (0.47; see supplemental materials).

Social vulnerability index

This study used the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) 2022 [49] to characterize the broader community-level vulnerability that may influence implementation success. The SVI is a composite index that ranks U.S. Census tracts (e.g., ZIP Code Tabulation Areas) according to 16 social indicators, organized into four thematic domains: (1) socioeconomic status (2), household characteristics (3), racial & ethnic minority status, and (4) housing type & transportation. Each ZIP code has a percentile ranking (0 = lowest relative vulnerability, 1 = highest relative vulnerability). We merged the SVI 2022 data with our implementation dataset using school ZIP codes. The schools where the interventionists work were categorized as resource-rich (i.e., low social vulnerability) schools (SVI < 0.5, RRS) and resource-constrained (i.e., high social vulnerability) schools (SVI > 0.5, RCS).

Qualitative interview

The same group of interventionists who completed the quantitative survey was invited to participate in the individual qualitative interview. A total of 25 individual interviews were conducted [50, 51]. Each interview was an approximately 25–30-minute, in-depth, semi-structured individual interview conducted via video conference, Zoom [52]. The research team adapted the CFIR-guided interview protocol to align with the context of a school-based intervention, ensuring that the questions captured multilevel factors across domains [53]. While the full interview covers all five CFIR domains, this study focuses only on the inner setting (i.e., school culture) and the outer setting (i.e., family needs and resources; Table 2). The interviews were conducted and audio-recorded by the author SW and later transcribed verbatim by the author HQ [54].

Data analysis and integration

Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed separately and then integrated using the joint display approach [41, 55] to provide different perspectives on the school implementation climate, while also highlighting convergence and divergence between quantitative results and qualitative findings (Fig. 1). Regarding quantitative analysis, the research team first used descriptive statistics [56] to report overall SICS and ORC and their subscales. Then, this study conducted bivariate analyses. The assumption of normality was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test [57]. Given that the normality assumption was met for all continuous variables, the independent-samples t-tests [56] were conducted to compare SICS and ORC between RRS (n = 11) and RCS (n = 14). The Mann–Whitney U tests were also conducted to verify the robustness of findings, given the small sample size [58]. All statistical analyses were conducted in Stata MP 17 [59].

Concurrently, the research team conducted deductive thematic analysis [60, 61] using Dedoose, a cloud-based qualitative data analysis platform for qualitative coding [62]. Authors SW and HQ served as the two raters under the supervision of the author PK. The qualitative codebook was initially constructed based on the CFIR codebook, using its constructs, definitions, and inclusion and exclusion criteria [63]. During the coding process, new constructs that emerged from the transcripts were added, and the codebook was expanded to include more detailed child codes to capture nuances within each construct. Before beginning formal coding, two raters and the supervisor read through all the transcripts, making note of early reflections and emerging insights. Then, they independently coded a randomly selected subset from each district and held regular discussions to refine codes, resolve discrepancies, and ensure consistent interpretation of the data until thematic saturation was reached. Once consensus was reached on the subset of the interviews and the codebook was finalized, two raters restarted the process and independently coded all interviews using the revised coding scheme. To ensure coding consistency, interrater reliability was calculated using Cohen’s κ. Values above 0.70 were considered acceptable, reflecting substantial agreement [64]. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus to ensure coding consistency.

Regarding data integration, the research team employed the joint display approach to examine convergence and divergence between quantitative results and qualitative findings [41]. Specifically, this analytic approach organized quantitative indicators alongside corresponding qualitative themes and exemplar quotations. Data triangulation across quantitative patterns from the SICS and ORC, with qualitative themes from the CFIR-guided interviews, was achieved through this side-by-side comparison, consistent with best practices for convergent mixed methods designs [39, 41, 55]. Furthermore, the team used this approach to compare ICR across schools in resource-rich and resource-constrained areas. A matrix was developed to compare the frequency of codes and the content of quotations across different resource levels, further contextualizing the findings.

Results

Descriptive results

Reported by all 25 school interventionists, the overall SICS score averaged 3.27 (SD = 0.66) on a 5-point Likert scale, indicating a moderately positive implementation climate. On average, participating schools demonstrated a strong focus on EBP (M = 3.93, SD = 0.57) and a high availability of training and resources for EBP (M = 3.96, SD = 0.82), while providing limited incentives for EBP usage (M = 2.09, SD = 0.93).

Furthermore, the average ORC score was 3.79 (SD = 0.42) on a 5-point Likert scale, indicating moderate school readiness for change. The high average score on the Staff Attributes subscale (M = 4.16, SD = 0.47) and across all its subscales (i.e., professional growth, efficacy, influence, adaptability, and satisfaction) suggested that interventionists perceive themselves as highly competent, adaptable, motivated, and emotionally well-prepared to implement the intervention. Within the Organizational Climate subscale, ‘mission’ (M = 3.72, SD = 0.66), ‘autonomy’ (M = 3.71, SD = 0.54), and ‘cohesion’ (M = 3.66, SD = 0.65) scored relatively higher than ‘communication’ (M = 3.36, SD = 0.73), suggesting a collaborative and supportive working environment with shared values but insufficient communication.

Qualitative findings

Thematic analyses revealed several key themes reported by interventionists regarding school-based implementation of the THRIVE program (Fig. 2). First, interventionists consistently described a strong culture of innovation, collaboration, and support within their schools. Second, many interventionists highlighted a perceived alignment between the goals of THRIVE, the needs of the students, and the broader mission of their schools, indicating high compatibility of the intervention with existing values and practices. Third, interventionists described multiple barriers to implementation, such as time constraints and stigma surrounding parenting programs, and noted the existing tension for change, but limited resources to support program implementation. Notably, few interventionists mentioned concrete supports or infrastructure provided by schools to facilitate implementation, underscoring gaps in readiness for implementation.

School culture

Most interventionists (n = 23) mentioned that their school values innovation, supports changes, and brings in new EBPs and strategies. When asked about adopting new strategies, one interventionist described her school as “very progressive. I feel like we’re always doing something” (Interventionist[I] 431). Most interventionists (n = 22) also described close working relationships with their colleagues, including other counselors, teachers, and principals. More than half of the interventionists (n = 14) felt trusted by school administrators. Along with the collaboration comes a strong feeling of support, as many school interventionists (n = 18) reported a supportive work environment. They could get help if needed and were also willing to help other co-workers with their workload. One interventionist elaborated that they were willing to “help one another and do things outside of their job to share the load of running the center together” (I541).

Alignment of values

Many interventionists (n = 17) commented on having shared values/goals amongst the school, often explained as wanting to come together to do whatever is best for the social-emotional and behavioral development of children. Almost all the interventionists (n = 24) expressed positive attitudes about the intervention and believed it would meet the needs of children and families in their school, sharing that “I think we certainly have parents who need these skills of THRIVE” (I641). Others (n = 22) elaborated on how THRIVE aligned with their schools’ values of promoting the social-emotional and behavioral development of children and discussed its potential benefits. By increasing social and emotional skills for children and parents, THRIVE may “help children build self-esteem, parents feel good about their parenting style” (I531), which contributes to the “relationship” at home. Furthermore, this positive impact may extend from the home environment to educational settings. “A lot of our kids don’t have skills that maybe THRIVE can teach them, which then we know … transfer(s) over into the school environment” (I411).

Implementation barriers

While interventionists held a positive outlook on THRIVE and believed it would meet the needs of children and families in their school, they also identified potential barriers to implementation in the outer setting. The two most common themes were time commitment (n = 17) and negative perceptions of parenting programs (n = 16).

Many interventionists noted that parents’ busy schedules could be a significant barrier to participation, citing factors such as work schedules, caring for other children, and various life responsibilities that create time conflicts and hinder both scheduling and engagement in the intervention. Although the hybrid delivery method offers the flexibility of meeting in person or through teleconference, interventionists still had to balance the working schedule of parents with their own, one commenting, “if I have a working parent, …, is that parent going to be able to access this in a way that’s going to be beneficial?” (I651). Others worried not only about the meeting times but also the availability of the parents to dedicate time to the intervention homework: “It becomes a challenge for them with their schedules to continue to commit to doing the five minutes of play each day” (I622).

Another concern raised by most interventionists was the difficulty of engaging their population due to stigma and negative perceptions of parenting programs. They worried that families would feel uncomfortable sharing their personal lives with the interventionists and would be closed off to feedback on their parenting for fear of being judged. One interventionist shared, “people have the mindset that what happens in our home should stay in our home and we don’t need to talk about it and we don’t need help” (I122). Another shared, “allowing someone from school to see you directly and how you are interacting with your child …makes them very vulnerable and…not open to receiving that from someone at the school” (I622).

Resources for change

When looking at the need for an intervention like THRIVE, some interventionists (n = 9) described that their communities are in need and that there is a tension for change from the current situation. Some talked about the inaccessibility of child mental health services in general, while others talked about specific families they personally knew who would benefit from THRIVE. One interventionist stated, “mental health services are so astronomically expensive, and we have so many families that just it’s not an option for them” (I421). Another remarked, “We always have a couple of kids, especially in kindergarten, who are kind of school reluctant, and parents really struggle. We’re pulling children out of a car because the parents don’t know how to get kids to do what they want them to do” (I241).

Given the existing tension for change at school, some interventionists (n = 9) also identified available resources at their schools that could aid in the implementation of the THRIVE program. The most commonly mentioned resource was the school’s ability to provide families with technology to use for the teleconference meetings, such as one who stated “we provide a Chromebook for all of our students that they can use.” (I311). Others mentioned administrators allowing them to flex hours, “I’ve gotten the go-ahead from our director of student services that I can have flexible contract hours, so … if family needs to do it at 5 pm, I can come in late that day” (I621).

In addition to available resources provided by school, many interventionists (n = 16) were also able to identify facilitators from the outer setting (i.e., families) that would help with THRIVE’s success. Most commonly, interventionists identified facilitators such as families having necessary resources (e.g., computers and reliable internet) or developing strong parent buy-in. Many believed that because the program is being offered as a resource, if a parent says yes and then starts seeing results, it will promote further engagement. One interventionist commented on this, saying, “I feel like if the parent believes it’s important and helping, then they’re going to keep tuning into the Zoom or showing up in my office” (I431).

Lastly, implementing a program through a school means that targeted children and families typically have an existing relationship with the school staff, facilitating the intervention, summarized as a school-family relationship (n = 11). Some interventionists noted that a weak school-family relationship can be a barrier: “We don’t have a lot of engagement with parents now in our schools…That’s a disadvantage” (I121). However, in schools where the relationship is strong, it can be an advantage: “I already have a strong relationship with the families…I’m already there in their kind of personal space, and I’ve built a rapport with them. So, I feel like I’ll be able to positively impact some families and also be able to implement the program effectively” (I512).

Mixed methods integration

Qualitative data from individual interviews supported most of the quantitative findings, such as a supportive work environment, close working relationships, strong collaboration, and openness to EBP. However, there are some discrepancies in quantitative and qualitative findings regarding the resources available from the school. Quantitative data showed a high availability of training and resources for EBP on average, while only a few interventionists mentioned this in their interviews. One possible explanation is the differences in their socioeconomic status, as some interventionists have particularly mentioned socioeconomic hardships related to technology and transportation. Therefore, this study further investigated the interplay of socioeconomic hardships on ICR. The following section is a comparison of major quantitative results and qualitative findings between RRS (n = 11) and RCS (n = 14) (see supplemental materials for more details).

The joint display table (Table 3) indicated that schools in areas with low social vulnerability (i.e., RRS) on average presented a slightly higher ORC score and SICS score than those in areas with high social vulnerability (i.e., RCS), even though the differences were not statistically significant (p > .05). More specifically, compared to RRS, RCS placed less emphasis on EBP (t [23] = 2.37, p < .05), presented a more stressful working environment (t [23] = -3.10, p < .01), and had a lower level of employee satisfaction (t [23] = 2.17, p < .05). However, interventionists from RCS demonstrated better resilience as they reported higher self-efficacy, influence, and adaptability, even though the differences were not statistically significant (see supplemental materials).

Supplementing and contextualizing the quantitative results, qualitative findings revealed that a higher proportion of interventionists (n = 6, 43%) in RCS mentioned the need for change in their current situation. An example of tension is that child mental health services are not affordable for families in need in their low-resource communities. Interventionists from RRS (n = 3, 27%) also discussed other tensions, such as parents’ lack of effective interaction skills with their children. Regarding implementation climate, although more interventionists from RCS discussed school resources (n = 6, 43%), such as technology support, some of them particularly emphasized the lack of resources, including a lack of mental health coordinators.

Qualitative findings also provided profound insights into outer settings between RRS and RCS, including family needs and resources. Among the emerged themes, facilitators and benefits were the two that showed large group differences. Compared to interventionists in RRS, those in RCS mentioned fewer facilitators (n = 5, 36%), but perceived greater benefits in implementing THRIVE (n = 14, 100%). They emphasized the importance and difficulty of gaining parents’ commitment and engagement, while also expecting to see significant improvements in child behavioral health, parenting skills, and parent–child interaction.

Discussion

This baseline mixed methods study offered preliminary insights into the organizational and community context influencing the implementation of THRIVE in school settings. Interpreted through CFIR, the findings suggest both facilitators and barriers across inner- and outer-setting domains. Within the inner setting, collaborative culture, value alignment, and organizational readiness emerged as potential facilitators, while limited incentives and insufficient communication posed challenges. In the outer setting, family buy-in and strong school-family relationships may support engagement, whereas time burden and stigma surrounding parenting programs may constrain it. Notably, differences between RRS and RCS revealed a tension between high individual readiness and perceived intervention value on one hand, and limited structural capacity on the other, particularly in schools serving communities with greater vulnerability.

Inner setting

Organizational culture and the perceived fit of THRIVE may contribute to its uptake in school settings. Interventionists described a strong school culture of innovation, collaboration, and support, and also emphasized the alignment among THRIVE’s goals, students’ needs, and the school’s mission. Prior research has identified similar inner-context determinants of implementation in school-based programs [5, 28, 38]. The current findings extend this work by situating these facilitators within the context of a preventive early childhood mental health intervention delivered across schools with varying resource levels.

However, interventionists also identified inadequate infrastructure (i.e., communication and incentive) for EBP usage, possibly resulting in gaps between positive organizational climate and actual EBP adoption. Existing research suggests that effective EBP implementation depends on both a positive overall organizational climate and a strong strategic climate specifically oriented toward implementation [23, 24]. These preliminary findings suggest that a positive organizational climate alone may not be sufficient to sustain implementation without corresponding incentive structures and communication infrastructure to support EBP adoption.

Outer setting

Regarding CFIR’s outer-setting domain, family-related factors (i.e., potential needs and resources) emerged as both facilitators and barriers. Similar to previous research, stigma and logistical demands may pose persistent challenges to family participation in mental health programs [6, 36], while family buy-in and positive school-family relationships have been recognized as protective factors that may contribute to high engagement [28]. The coexistence of these facilitating and constraining forces in the current study suggests that THRIVE implementation strategies may need to simultaneously build on relational strengths, such as school-family relationship while addressing structural constraints like scheduling flexibility and stigma.

Resource-level differences

Although mixed methods integration largely confirmed convergence between quantitative and qualitative findings, a notable discrepancy emerged regarding perceived resource availability, prompting further comparison between RRS and RCS. Prior research has documented that schools in resource-constrained communities face greater implementation burden due to limited infrastructure and competing demands [1, 2, 5, 6, 12, 16, 36]. The current findings reflect a similar pattern: compared to RRS, RCS placed less emphasis on EBP, reported higher workplace stress, and showed lower employee satisfaction. These inner-setting disparities were accompanied by outer-setting differences. Although interventionists in RCS perceived greater benefits of THRIVE, they identified fewer family-level facilitators to support its implementation. These findings suggest that socioeconomic context may influence both organizational capacity and family engagement with school-based interventions, thereby affecting uptake of EBPs.

Despite these structural disadvantages, interventionists in RCS reported higher resilience and a greater need for change. Research on implementation in resource-constrained settings has found that professionals often develop adaptive capacities in response to systemic constraints, yet without adequate organizational support, such resilience may lead to implementation fatigue and burnout [23, 27, 29]. Similar tensions between individual readiness and structural capacity have been documented in school-based mental health implementation research [34, 38]. These preliminary findings suggest that leveraging individual readiness in RCS may require concurrent improvement in organizational infrastructure.

The differences observed between RCS and RRS may reflect broader structural inequities. Contextual factors such as poverty, limited infrastructure, and systemic disinvestment can create disparities not only in mental health outcomes but also in the conditions necessary for successful implementation [6, 36, 38]. These preliminary findings underscore the importance of contextually tailored implementation strategies that account for structural vulnerability, rather than applying uniform approaches across diverse school settings. While the study aimed to understand, rather than improve, schools’ ICR, the findings highlight actionable strategies to foster buy-in and reduce structural barriers. For example, strengthening cross-role communication about early childhood mental health and implementation of EBPs, obtaining administrative support from school principals to allow flexible work hours for school counselors delivering THRIVE, providing incentives to encourage interventionists to offer flexible scheduling options for families, and introducing a “push-in therapy” model, where schools partner with community-based organizations to provide services in the school setting, may help address key barriers identified in this study.

Limitations

A key strength of this study is the integration of quantitative data measured using validated scales (i.e., SICS and ORC) with rich qualitative data collected through CFIR-guided interviews, enabling data triangulation and enhanced contextual interpretation of findings. The joint display analysis further revealed convergence and divergence across data sources at the construct level. Additionally, by linking data with the SVI, this study examined community-level structural barriers rarely examined in school-based implementation research. However, several limitations should be noted. The small sample size (n = 25) limited the statistical power for multivariate analyses, and the cross-sectional design captures a single time point. Subgroup comparisons between RRS (n = 11) and RCS (n = 14) schools should be interpreted as exploratory, given the small subgroup sizes. Generalizability may be limited to the St. Louis area, and only interventionist perspectives were captured. As THRIVE implementation continues, future research can draw on a larger, more diverse sample and incorporate perspectives from caregivers, children, and school administrators. Ongoing qualitative data collection at midline and endline may deepen understanding of contextual influences over time. Future analyses can also examine how baseline ICR shapes both implementation outcomes (e.g., fidelity, retention) and clinical outcomes (e.g., child mental health).

Conclusion

This study provides a preliminary understanding of the implementation climate and organizational readiness for THRIVE, a preventive early childhood mental health intervention, in school settings. Although schools generally exhibit moderately positive implementation climates and readiness for change, structural and contextual barriers exist, which may hinder the implementation of THRIVE. Disparities between schools in resource-rich and resource-constrained communities further highlight the importance of contextually tailored strategies. Despite these challenges, interventionists across settings strongly support THRIVE, particularly in resource-constrained communities with high perceived need and potential benefits. These findings underscore the critical role of school context in shaping implementation success and the need for future research to develop implementation strategies that promote the delivery of school-based mental health interventions.
