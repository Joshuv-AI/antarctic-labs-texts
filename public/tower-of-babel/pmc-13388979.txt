Unveiling gene modules at Atlas scale through hierarchical clustering of single-cell data
Authors: Feng Tang, Zhongmin Zhang, Weige Zhou, Guangpeng Li, Yang Xu, Luyi Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA major challenge in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis is recovering biologically meaningful cell ontology trees and conserved gene modules across datasets. Data integration and batch-effect correction methods have enabled effective analyses of multiple datasets but often fail to disentangle cell states in heterogeneous samples, such as cancer and the immune system. Here, we present Super Single-Cell Clustering (SuperSCC), a computational framework that utilizes machine learning models to discover cell identities and gene modules across multiple datasets without the need for data integration. Notably, SuperSCC can be implemented at both the cell lineage and cell state levels, thereby allowing the creation of hierarchies of cell programs with specific cell identities and gene modules. This information can be used to identify shared rare populations across datasets regardless of batch effects and has advantages for mapping cell labels from reference to query datasets. We used SuperSCC to perform atlas-level data analysis with more than 90 datasets and built cell state maps of complex tissues, such as the human lung, in healthy and diseased states. SuperSCC outperforms existing approaches in identifying cellular contexts, achieves higher annotation accuracy, and identifies gene modules that indicate conserved immune cell statuses in the lung microenvironment.Subject terms: Data integration, Functional clustering, Software, Computational models

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized biological research by providing insights at the individual cell level, offering a detailed view of transcriptome regulation1–3. As the number of publicly available single-cell datasets rapidly increases4,5, there is an urgent need to integrate these datasets to construct comprehensive cellular atlases, which are essential for a deeper understanding of complex biological systems. Such integrative approaches enable researchers to explore cellular hierarchies and gene expression dynamics across a range of conditions, including healthy and diseased states.

Despite advancements in scRNA-seq technologies, current data integration methods face significant challenges. A critical issue is how to integrate datasets from different sources while preserving biologically relevant variability and avoiding overcorrection of key signals6. Batch correction steps are necessary prior to downstream analyses, such as clustering and cell type annotation; however, batch correction can introduce biases and obscure biologically meaningful variations, complicating interpretation based on integrated datasets7. This traditional “merge-then-analyse” approach is also computationally demanding, as various data types must be combined into a single, large set of integrated embeddings before analyses can be performed.

In addition, standard approaches for clustering and marker gene detection often fail to capture the inherent hierarchical structures of different cell types and states. For instance, broad cellular categories, such as lymphoid cells, and more specific subpopulations, such as CD8+ T cells, exist at different levels within the cellular hierarchy. However, current tools, including Seurat8, Scanpy9, and SCMarker10, struggle to distinguish these levels accurately. These hierarchical relationships are critical for understanding functional diversity within tissues. While recent methods, such as Spectra11, have addressed some challenges by identifying gene programs without requiring batch correction based on large atlases, such methods cannot be used to construct hierarchical structures and require prior knowledge in the form of gene sets. Collectively, no existing scRNA-seq analysis tool offers cell identity and gene module hierarchy construction without relying on batch correction or prior knowledge.

To address these limitations, we developed the Super Single-cell Clustering (SuperSCC) algorithm, a computational framework that enhances scRNA-seq analysis by enabling hierarchical clustering, marker gene and gene module detection, and robust label transfer across multiple datasets without the need for batch correction. The “analyse-then-merge” strategy employed by SuperSCC involves iterative clustering and cluster merging to build cell type hierarchies for each individual dataset. Subsequently, conserved gene modules are identified across datasets and aligned based on shared gene modules at multiple levels. We applied SuperSCC to analyse large-scale datasets, including human lung tissue and immune cell atlases, across diverse conditions and diseases. Compared with the current state-of-the-art methods, SuperSCC demonstrates superior performance in identifying cellular contexts, annotating cell states, and discovering conserved gene modules. Notably, SuperSCC identified several rare and conserved immune cell states, some of which are linked to immunotherapy efficacy, underscoring its potential to reveal insights into disease biology.

Results

The workflow of SuperSCC

SuperSCC is a generalized framework for multiple tasks involving single-cell transcriptomic data, including informative feature extraction, cluster-aware clustering, gene module detection and label transfer. Within the framework, machine learning approaches are used to identify informative features that best represent the data and exhibit significant correlations with cell types or clusters (Fig. 1a). Such supervised methods can be used to identify abundant subtle differences in gene expression between different cell types, improving cell clustering and label transfer performance. Thus, SuperSCC enables cluster-aware clustering that prevents overclustering or underclustering and allows the construction of cell identity hierarchies by merging similar clusters at different levels (Fig. 1b). This cell identity and marker hierarchy enables SuperSCC to identify conserved gene modules across datasets, thereby enabling comprehensive analyses at the gene module level, benefiting atlas-level analysis involving data from different sources (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, SuperSCC can map cell labels from reference to query datasets (Fig. 1d). To run SuperSCC, a normalized cell-by-gene count matrix with or without cell or cluster labels is used as input, and a set of profiles for different tasks is output. For informative feature extraction, SuperSCC provides a small pool of genes that best represent a given dataset; these genes can be utilized for further cluster-aware clustering and label transfer. For cluster-aware clustering, SuperSCC provides broad clustering (at the cell lineage level) and fine clustering (at the cell state level) results, as well as informative genes for the corresponding identified clusters. For gene module detection, SuperSCC generates hierarchical gene sets that represent diverse biological functions. For label transfer, the predicted class and the class probabilities for the query datasets are returned.

SuperSCC performs better in the aforementioned scRNA-seq tasks than existing tools do owing to two key features. First, owing to its cluster-aware clustering process, SuperSCC overcomes clustering bias when graph-based clustering methods are implemented with an inappropriate resolution parameter12, thereby mitigating overclustering and underclustering and enabling the construction of cell ontology trees that provide a biologically meaningful cell program hierarchy. Second, such hierarchical cell program information can be further used to define conserved gene modules and hence identify shared rare populations without the need for data integration or batch effect correction across datasets, which may compromise latent biological differences. This ‘analysis-then-merge’ strategy, which involves providing a hierarchical cell program based on individual datasets and then combining datasets at the gene module level, overcomes the common weaknesses of traditional ‘merge-then-analysis’ approaches, which require data integration. To facilitate interpretation of results and comparison of gene modules, we integrated large language model (LLM) and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) functionalities into SuperSCC, which can also be used as a benchmarking tool. Finally, we used SuperSCC to perform atlas-level data analysis based on ~100 datasets containing different tissue types and disease states (Supplementary Data 1).

SuperSCC has great potential for multiple scRNA-seq analysis tasks

To validate the performance of SuperSCC, we benchmarked SuperSCC with other widely used tools in different analysis tasks. For this, eighteen datasets with expert-annotated labels were chosen from the manuscript’s dataset pool and used to calculate evaluation metrics. First, we employed an LLM to assess the performance of SuperSCC, Seurat8 and Scanpy9 in capturing informative features based on two scoring metrics, the relevant gene ratio (RGR) and the biological relevance score (BRS), for the top 20 markers (Fig. 2a). The RGR represents the proportion of genes associated with signatures of relevant cell types, whereas the BRS is a relevance score associated with cell type-specific signatures. Additionally, multiple LLMs and temperature settings were tested to generate robust results that were not biased under certain LLM settings. SuperSCC demonstrated a significant performance advantage over Seurat and Scanpy when assessed using standard LLMs (GPT-4.1-mini, Qwen-Max, and DeepSeek-V3). This superiority was robust across all temperature settings and both evaluation metrics (RGR and BRS; Fig. 2b). While the margin narrowed to an average improvement of 2–5% when using a reasoning-optimized model (GPT-5) as the evaluator, SuperSCC consistently maintained the highest overall scores (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). Furthermore, results from the smaller Qwen3-4b model mirrored those of the larger non-reasoning models (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f), suggesting that SuperSCC’s performance gains are independent of the evaluator’s size. Further analysis confirmed that the marker selection method was the primary contributor to the variance in the BRS and RGR among the groups (Fig. 2c). Significant variations were observed among the marker selection methods under certain LLMs (Fig. 2d). Notably, even though different LLMs introduced some variation in absolute scores, SuperSCC exhibited highly consistent performance across all tested models and consistently achieved the highest BRS and RGR (Fig.2c; Supplementary Fig. 2a). To assess the influence of the probabilistic sampling inherent in LLMs on scoring stability, we performed three independent replications for each query and each LLM at a fixed temperature of 0.5. Dataset-level scores were then aggregated across replications and compared among marker-selection methods. SuperSCC significantly outperformed both Scanpy and Seurat on the BRS and RGR in all evaluated settings (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These results indicate that the superior performance of SuperSCC, supported by our LLM-based evaluation system, is not affected by the LLMs or temperature settings. Moreover, SuperSCC showed better performance in capturing informative features than Seurat and Scanpy did for rare cell types, with proportions of less than 1% (Supplementary Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 2c). Finally, we repeated the above analysis using the top 10 markers identified by each method and found that SuperSCC still had the best performance, indicating that the results are not sensitive to this parameter setting (Supplementary Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 2d). To further validate the cell identity markers identified by SuperSCC, we compared the SuperSCC-derived markers for 20 common lung cell types with corresponding manually curated marker sets in the CellMarker 2.0 database13. Our analysis revealed that the top 20 markers identified by SuperSCC consistently included marker genes cataloged in CellMarker, highlighting SuperSCC’s robust capability to accurately capture cell-type-specific features (Supplementary Data 1). Furthermore, SuperSCC outperformed Seurat and Scanpy in prioritizing the most relevant markers for corresponding cell types. For instance, the CellMarker database lists 32 markers for lung dendritic cells, among which CD83 is the most frequently cited in the literature, signifying its superior relevance to dendritic cells (DCs) compared to other genes. Notably, CD83 ranked prominently among the top 20 markers identified by SuperSCC for the lung DC1 and DC2 populations (as annotated by the dataset providers). In contrast, Scanpy and Seurat prioritized less specific DC markers while failing to identify this pivotal gene.

Furthermore, we evaluated whether SuperSCC outperforms other tools in recovering broad cell ontology. To do this, we measured the similarity between the clustering results of each method and the ground-truth cell labels provided by the data generator. SuperSCC showed the best performance with 50% (9 out of 18) of the validation datasets, and its overall score was the highest for two evaluation metrics, AMI (0.735 ± 0.0297) and NMI (0.736 ± 0.0297), with increases of 5% to 32% over the scores of the comparison algorithms, including Scanpy, Seurat, Sccaf14, Scshc15, SC316, CIDR17, Sclca18 and Monocle319 (Fig. 2e). In terms of ARI and FMI, Sccaf (ARI: 0.616 ± 0.0634; FMI: 0.739 ± 0.0428) performed comparably to SuperSCC (ARI: 0.614 ± 0.0540; FMI: 0.738 ± 0.0329), with both methods’ ARI and FMI scores increasing by 8% to 34% compared to the other methods (Fig. 2e). Notably, SuperSCC outperformed Seurat and Scanpy, which were run with default settings or resolution-fine-tuning approaches, in terms of all four evaluation metrics (Fig. 2e; Supplementary Fig. 2e). Interestingly, given the clustering approach of SC3, in which k was determined by the number of M clusters searched by SuperSCC, its comprehensive performance ranked at the tier 2 level (Fig. 2e), suggesting that the SuperSCC-derived M cluster results were similar to the ground-truth cell type distribution.

Moreover, we benchmarked the performance of SuperSCC in label transfer applications through self-validation and cross-validation experiments, which are classified according to whether training and testing were performed on the same dataset. In the self-validation experiments, all the tested algorithms performed well, achieving average overall scores above 0.8 (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Notably, SuperSCC consistently performed well, with high ACC (0.931 ± 0.00860), Homog (0.881 ± 0.0102), recall (0.931 ± 0.00860), Prec (0.927 ± 0.00882), Compl (0.896 ± 0.00925), MCC (0.921 ± 0.00867), F1 (0.928 ± 0.00887) and VM (0.889 ± 0.00967) scores. Among all the tested methods, SuperSCC performed only slightly worse than scANVI20, but substantially outperformed SingleR21, SingleCellNet22, Scmap23, and, to a lesser extent, Seurat (Supplementary Fig. 2f). The detailed evaluation scores for each algorithm and scoring metric are provided in Supplementary Data 1. Similar observations were noted in the cross-validation experiments (Supplementary Fig. 2g). Although scANVI is a semi-supervised deep learning framework that typically requires GPU acceleration for efficient inference, SuperSCC demonstrates competitive performance with reasonable computational costs in a CPU-only environment. We found SuperSCC performed training and inference on datasets ranging from 2,000 to 20,000 cells, achieving completion times averaging between 1 and 20 min (Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, scANVI spent 40% more computation time for the same task than SuperSCC when GPU acceleration was disabled. These results indicate that SuperSCC achieves an effective balance between computational efficiency and accuracy.

SuperSCC can identify conserved and hierarchically organized gene modules across tissue atlases

Traditional single-cell analysis pipelines rely on data integration prior to downstream clustering, risking the loss of nuanced biological signals during batch correction7,8. SuperSCC addresses this limitation through an “analysis-then-merge” framework: first, cell identity hierarchies within individual datasets are resolved; then, the hierarchical information is used to detect conserved gene modules (GMs) across datasets; finally, the datasets are aggregated at the GM level (Fig. 1c). With its hierarchical clustering strategy, SuperSCC distinguishes broad cell identity-associated modules (M-cluster GMs; MGMs) from fine-grained state-specific modules (F-cluster GMs; FGMs) based on 99 datasets spanning 20 tissues (Fig. 3a). At its broadest resolution, SuperSCC defines 15 major gene modules (MGMs; Supplementary Data 1) representing core cellular programs (e.g., the immune response, angiogenesis), which are organized into six functional families (Fig. 3b). These MGMs are enriched in lineage-defining pathways and exhibit tissue-specific dominance; for example, immune response-related MGMs dominate in diverse tissues (54.6% of all MGMs), whereas cytoskeleton-associated MGMs uniquely characterize respiratory airway datasets (12%, Fig. 3d). At a finer resolution, 65 submodules (FGMs; Supplementary Data 1) delineate cell-state-specific programs, including biological processes absent in MGMs, such as antioxidant and stress responses (10% of FGMs) and energy synthesis (Fig. 3c). Critically, FGMs often refine MGMs: cell-cycle-related FGMs, which are prevalent in brain datasets (52%), can be used to better understand cell development-related MGMs, whereas angiogenesis-related MGMs (24.8% of all MGMs) expand into immune-linked submodules at the FGM level (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Strikingly, eight immune response-related and two angiogenesis-related MGMs were conserved in >75% of the datasets, with activity localized to the expected cell types (e.g., immune-related MGMs in T cells, angiogenesis-related MGMs in endothelial cells; Fig. 3f, g; Supplementary Fig. 5a), underscoring SuperSCC’s capacity to resolve context-independent signatures. Such gene module conservation was also observed in FGMs (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d). This hierarchical modularity, namely, broad MGMs anchoring core functions and FGMs capturing dynamic states, provides a scaffold to interrogate functional crosstalk between cellular programs.

To evaluate the functionality and generalizability of GMs derived from SuperSCC, we applied SuperSCC to a dataset comprising cells subjected to perturbations mimicking T-cell receptor activation. We assessed whether SuperSCC-derived GMs could differentiate perturbed cells from controls. The results demonstrated that SuperSCC-derived GMs effectively captured the perturbation, as indicated by significantly higher gene module scores in perturbed cells compared to control cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Additionally, we compared GMs derived from SuperSCC and Spectra. SuperSCC-derived immune response-related functional gene modules (FGMs) exhibited up to ~50% overlap with Spectra-derived immune-related GMs across various datasets (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Given that the SuperSCC-derived GMs represent conserved modules across datasets, whereas the Spectra-derived GMs represent dataset-specific modules, the substantial overlap between the results of these approaches indicates that the FGMs identified by SuperSCC capture broadly conserved biological programs rather than dataset-specific artefacts. This cross-method validation further supports the conservation and generalizability of SuperSCC-derived GMs.

Hierarchical gene modules reveal cross-compartment immune regulation

The hierarchical relationship between MGMs and FGMs enables the discovery of dynamic gene functionality across biological scales. Unlike static module-detection methods11,24,25, SuperSCC reveals how broad cellular programs bifurcate into specialized states, particularly within immune contexts (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). We demonstrate this by exploring two phenomena: stress adaptation in immune cells and immune-activation signatures in non-immune populations.

Within the immune response-related MGMs, non-immune FGMs, such as the antioxidant and stress (FGM62/FGM63) and energy synthesis (FGM54/FGM55) modules, were enriched in heat shock genes (Fig. 4a, b). These FGMs could be used to distinguish stressed immune subsets such as activated B cells (Fig. 4c), T cells, mast cells and macrophages through upregulated heat shock protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e), illustrating that SuperSCC can identify functional diversity within immune lineages. These observations are consistent with those of a previous study, in which stressed T cells were characterized by HSPA1A/HSPA1B expression26. However, the results of SuperSCC extended the stress response state beyond T cells to other immune cells and highlighted relevant heat shock genes, such as HSPB1, HSPD1, HSPH1, HSPA4 and HSPA6, which have been reported to be involved in the immune response27–29 but are less commonly treated as indicators of stressed immune cells. Strikingly, SuperSCC demonstrated greater sensitivity than did Scanpy in detecting stress immune cells with a proportion of less than 10% (Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). Further analysis showed that immune cells exhibiting stress responses, as identified by gene modules derived from SuperSCC’s “analysis-then-merge” approach, were obscured in multi-dataset analyses that relied on conventional integration workflows (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Furthermore, we examined the frequency of stress B cells (F cluster 6) under different clinical conditions in the Laughney_2020 dataset (Supplementary Fig. 8a) and found that the number of stress B cells was markedly lower in lung cancer patients receiving treatment compared to those who did not receive treatment (Fig. 4d). Interestingly, correlation analysis based on the frequency of cells in each patient revealed strong associations between F cluster 6 and F clusters 2, 4 and 30, which contained T cells and are referred to as stress B cells-associated T cells here (Fig. 4e). Further differential expression (DE) analysis comparing stress B cells-associated T cells with other T cells revealed upregulated heat shock genes, suggesting that stress B cells-associated T cells were also in stress response states (Fig. 4f). Additionally, we validated the biological functions of these FGMs with external datasets. FGM23, an immune GM containing heat shock genes, displayed significantly increased gene activity in non-responder patients after immunotherapy (Fig. 4g, h), indicating that the number of stress immune cells could serve as an indicator of immune prognosis, which is consistent with previous findings26,30.

Strikingly, angiogenesis FGMs—notably FGM32—were clustered with immune response-related FGMs (Fig. 4b), suggesting functional crosstalk. Indeed, MGM4, a pan-immune module, contained above 40% non-immune FGMs (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c), including FGM32, which shared few overlapping genes with its parent MGM (Fig. 5a). This hierarchical divergence suggests a hypothesis: FGM32 exhibits an immune-activated angiogenesis signature independent of classic endothelial markers (VWF and PECAM1). To test this, validation using the Dong_2020_1 dataset revealed two endothelial subpopulations (F clusters 14 versus 18) distinguished by FGM32 activity (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 8b). In F cluster 14, MHC class II genes (HLA-DRB1, HLA-DPB1), CD74, and IL3RA—hallmarks of antigen-presenting endothelial cells observed in inflammatory niches31,32, were uniquely upregulated (Fig. 5c; and Supplementary Fig. 8c). The strong correlation between MGM4 (immune) and FGM32 (angiogenesis) activity (Fig. 5d) further supported their functional synergy, suggesting that F cluster 14 may contain endothelial cells involved in immune responses.

In addition, cell communication analysis confirmed that immune-related endothelial cells (F cluster 14) communicated more with immune cells, particularly macrophages and B cells, than with non-immune endothelial cells (F cluster 18) (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. 8d). Collectively, this hierarchical resolution reveals rare, immune-related endothelial cells—a population obscured in traditional analyses and rarely mentioned by previous studies33,34—demonstrating how SuperSCC bridges broad modules to context-specific states, exposing latent biological insights within atlas-scale datasets. To enable users to effectively and conveniently utilize this hierarchical information, we developed a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system with a user-friendly interface (Fig. 5f). This system provides hierarchical cell labels and cluster alignments at various levels in response to user queries.

In summary, the hierarchical clustering and gene modules identified by SuperSCC provide biological insights at multiple levels. Linking broad-level patterns to finer-level details reveals the multifunctionality and dynamic transitions within cell populations, enabling the discovery of rare cell states and functional interactions among cells.

Discussion

With the rapid expansion of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets, methods that not only scale computationally but also extract biologically meaningful insights from increasingly complex data are needed. SuperSCC addresses this challenge by unifying hierarchical cell-state mapping, conserved gene module (GM) discovery, and automated biological interpretation into a single framework. Crucially, our integration-free approach avoids the pitfalls of batch correction while facilitating atlas-scale analyses that reveal cross-tissue biological programs and novel cellular states.

The hierarchical structure of SuperSCC enables thorough exploration of immune contexts, revealing immune activation even for non-immune cells. The application of SuperSCC to 99 datasets across 20 tissues revealed conserved GMs that define fundamental cellular processes. Immune response and angiogenesis emerged as pan-tissue MGMs, which were present in >70% of the datasets, whereas finer-resolution FGMs were associated with stress responses (e.g., heat shock genes HSPA1A and HSPB1) and metabolic programs in immune cells. Strikingly, hierarchical analysis linked immune response-related MGMs to non-immune FGMs, such as those involved in angiogenesis (FGM32) and antioxidant pathways (FGM62 and FGM63), revealing cross-compartment coordination. These findings illustrate how SuperSCC’s multi-level framework elucidates cellular plasticity, capturing dynamic transitions and microenvironmental crosstalk. However, while the default two-layer hierarchy behind SuperSCC provides a balance of efficiency and granularity for many datasets, it may not automatically resolve very deep hierarchies in complex systems, such as multi-tissue or developmental atlases, without user iteration, potentially requiring additional manual oversight. For datasets with known deep hierarchies or multi-tissue complexity, we recommend iterative application on subsets to extend SuperSCC beyond the default two-layer hierarchy, thereby adapting to highly heterogeneous systems. Additionally, if a major cluster from the broad layer lacks sufficient heterogeneity for subclustering, SuperSCC’s fine clustering step typically returns a single subcluster identical to the parent cluster, as its merging algorithm merges overly split clusters due to their high similarity. However, the risk of the fine layer adding minimal new information remains a concern. For this, users should evaluate the output hierarchy against domain knowledge or external references to ensure biological relevance.

Notably, SuperSCC identified GMs with direct clinical relevance. Stress response-related FGMs were conserved across inflammatory diseases and correlated with poor immunotherapy response in independent cohorts, while immune exhaustion-related modules in epithelial cells aligned with fibrotic progression. These conserved programs, which were validated across >50 datasets, highlight SuperSCC’s ability to identify actionable biological insights from noise-rich single-cell data.

We also pioneered the use of LLMs to evaluate the GMs identified by SuperSCC and benchmarked the results through comparison with other methods. The LLM facilitated the validation of the SuperSCC-derived modules, increasing the accuracy and biological relevance of the results. In the future, similar to a recent pioneering study35, we envision integrating more bioinformatics into an agent-based workflow, where text outputs such as gene names are sent to the LLM for further analysis. It is important to note, however, that LLMs exhibit inherent stochasticity, whereby the precise phrasing or details of generated outputs may vary across invocations due to factors such as sampling temperature, decoding strategies and inference policies. Nonetheless, this variability did not undermine the reliability of our findings, as SuperSCC demonstrated consistent outperformance relative to competing methods across multiple non-reasoning LLMs and experimental settings, underscoring its robustness. Regarding the scoring discrepancy between reasoning-optimized models (such as GPT-5) and models more heavily trained on factual compression and retrieval (such as DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4.1-mini, and Qwen-Max), one potential explanation is that gene set analysis and functional enrichment scoring primarily require rapid, high-fidelity factual recall rather than extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. Models optimized for efficient retrieval from vast factual knowledge bases may therefore exhibit higher accuracy in this domain compared to those that prioritize multi-step logical inference. This hypothesis remains speculative and warrants further empirical investigation, potentially through controlled experiments that disentangle factual recall from reasoning demands.

Moreover, LLMs served as an innovative and complementary validation tool in this study—enhancing interpretability and biological insights—rather than a substitute for established curated databases, with due recognition of the potential temporal lag in LLM training data relative to the most current specialized resources. While SuperSCC offers significant advances, further improvements are possible. Integrating spatial transcriptomics could provide a more detailed understanding of gene activity in tissue architectures, elucidating how gene activity varies across different spatial domains such as tumor margins36, immune cell niches37, and developmental zones38. In addition, multi-omics data, including genomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, could be incorporated to refine the module detection results by providing complementary molecular information. For instance, by combining DNA methylation data with transcriptomic profiles, the regulatory mechanisms driving gene expression changes39 could be identified, whereas proteomic data could be incorporated to validate the functional outcomes of these changes at the protein level39.

Nevertheless, SuperSCC advances single-cell analysis by integrating hierarchical clustering, cross-dataset GM discovery, and AI-driven interpretation. Its ability to identify hidden biological information—from immune-active endothelial cells to stress-primed immune states—positions it as a critical tool for studying complex systems, offering a blueprint for translating ever-growing atlas-scale data into mechanistic insights.

Methods

The SuperSCC algorithm consists of four major aspects. First, a supervised feature selection process leveraging filter-, embedded- and wrapper-based methods is implemented to capture the most informative features in the dataset; this algorithm is the key novelty of SuperSCC and is referred to as the Detector. Second, a cluster-aware clustering algorithm is built by combining a graph-based clustering strategy with Detector, resulting in highly similar clusters merging at different levels and the establishment of cell identity hierarchies. The third step involves training and modelling on the basis of a reference dataset with known cell labels and key features identified by Detector and then assigning the cell labels in the reference dataset to the query dataset by calculating the similarity of the feature loadings between the two datasets. Ultimately, gene modules were derived using an optimized Jaccard similarity-based approach to identify conserved gene sets among datasets. The details of each step are outlined below.

Detector - A supervised feature selection process

The input to Detector is a cell-by-gene matrix with an additional column containing the cell labels from the data generator or the cluster labels generated by any clustering algorithm. The initial feature selection process is performed by removing low-variance features in the dataset, and the remaining features are called seed features. The variance cut-off is the average (default) or median value of the variances across all features in the dataset. In the case of filter-based categories, the seed features are processed with a correlation filter by conducting an F test (default), chi-square (χ2) test or mutual information (MI) analysis. Thus, the features are ranked on the basis of the corresponding statistics, and features that are independent of class and thus irrelevant for classification are discarded.

With the intersection mode, for the F test or χ2 test, only significant seed features (p value < 0.05) are retained, whereas seed features with nonzero evaluation scores are processed via MI analysis. With the ensemble mode, the seed features are ranked by the specific statistic in descending order.

Then, the features are scored on the basis of importance weights obtained from model fitting. Furthermore, in the case of wrapper-based categories, the recursive feature elimination (RFE) algorithm is used to combine one of the above induction models (SVM, RF, LR) to rank features on the basis of importance weights or to remove the most nonsignificant features iteratively until the number of features reaches the expected value. Given the risk of overfitting, the RFE algorithm is implemented across different cross-validation splits, thereby enabling the expected number of selected features to be adjusted automatically. The number of selected features is equal to the number of features needed to maximize the cross-validation score. In ensemble mode, the seed features are ranked by the importance weights calculated by the induction model in descending order. In intersection mode, only the features remaining after implementing the RFE algorithm are retained.

Finally, by integrating the results from all three categories, the selected feature set is the intersection of the feature subsets from each feature category in intersection mode, and the comprehensive feature ranking is the geometric mean of the feature rankings from each feature category in ensemble mode.

Cluster-aware clustering process

This process starts with a cell-by-gene matrix, followed by applying the Leiden40 algorithm to a k nearest neighbors (KNN) graph to obtain the raw cluster (Rcluster) labels. The highly variable genes (HVGs) that best explain the variation in the dataset and are highly correlated with Rcluster are subsequently identified via Detector. To capture representative examples of each Rcluster from the HVG pool, Detector was implemented in one-vs.-rest mode (referring to SuperOVR), in which all other Rclusters excluding the interest Rcluster were treated as one large set. The product of the two similarity matrices is the score determining whether the two compared clusters should be merged. Only clusters with scores above 0.1 are merged. As there are multiple cluster pairs that meet these requirements, the two compared clusters with the highest score are merged. Afterwards, the merged cluster labels are treated as the cell label input for Detector, and this process is run recursively until no merging candidates remain. The final merged clusters are theoretically cell lineage clusters and are referred to as M clusters here. Then, for each M cluster, we repeat the above procedure with three minor changes: 1) we changed the default resolution from 1 to 0.2 when applying the Leiden algorithm since subclustering is sensitive to selected resolution; 2) the best representatives from each sub cluster, not from HVGs pool but from all features in the dataset, are selected; and 3) we merged clusters with fewer than 10 positive markers. With this approach, cell state clusters, referred to here as F clusters, are obtained and used to construct the cell identity and gene module hierarchies.

Label transfer process

This process starts with a cell-by-gene reference matrix containing known cell labels, which is processed with Detector to find the HVGs. Then, the reference matrix is filtered to keep only the HVGs. On the basis of this data subset, a grid search is executed based on the cross-validation splits to evaluate the best parameters of the different induction models. By default, the SVM model parameters (“C”, “kernel”), LR model parameters (“penalty”, “C” and “multiclass”), and RF model parameters (“n_estimators”, “criterion” and “max_features”) are included in the grid search. In other cases, customized parameters for the corresponding model found on the official scikit-learn website are used for parameter optimization. The best parameters for the induction model are used for model training based on a reference dataset. To obtain predictions based on a query dataset without cell labels, the query dataset is also filtered to retain only the HVGs found in the reference dataset. If the HVGs are not in the query dataset, those genes would be padded with zero (default) or be imputed by MAGIC40 to ensure the features in the query and reference datasets have the same dimension. Then, the features of each data point in the query dataset are mapped to the feature space used for model training to obtain the predicted labels. Briefly, for the case of an SVM model, the decision function assigns novel data points to labels corresponding to the nearest support vector. For an LR model, the decision function assigns novel data points to the labels with the highest probability after sigmoid transformation. For an RF model, the decision function assigns novel data points to the labels with the most votes from all independent decision trees.

Gene module detection

As described previously24, gene modules are defined following a Jaccard similarity-based approach. In brief, for each M cluster or F cluster across all collected datasets, we focus on those genes with log2 fold change above 1 that are exclusively expressed in the interest M/F cluster and then rank the genes by importance weights obtained from SuperOVR; we ultimately obtain a list of the top 50 genes for gene module detection. Each gene set is compared to all other gene sets to evaluate the degree of gene overlap between gene sets. Given overlapping instances of at least 10 genes, the compared two gene sets with the maximal overlapping rate are treated as the founders of a new gene module. When the number of overlapping gene sets with at least 10 genes exceeds 5, the gene set with the highest overlapping rate with founder is added to the set. To ensure that each gene module always consists of 50 members, genes other than the shared genes are ranked by importance weights, and the genes with the highest importance weights are retained. Notably, for shared genes between comparison sets, genes with higher importance weight are retained for the next round of gene module updating. This process is repeated until no satisfactory candidates remain to be added to the gene module. After creating the first gene module, the remaining gene sets are processed with the above procedure by searching for the founder of the gene module, updating the founder by adding the gene set with maximal overlap to the founder and creating a gene module list containing 50 genes according to the importance weights in the pool of gene sets, thereby forming the remaining gene modules. Notably, in this study, we used only gene modules consisting of at least three gene sets from different datasets for downstream analysis and ultimately obtained 15 MGMs and 65 FGMs.

Data curation

The details of the datasets used in this manuscript are shown in Supplementary Data 1. To select the datasets, we first generated a list of potentially relevant studies through searches in PubMed, the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), CELL x GENE and EMBL-EBI. Each dataset was examined to determine whether the raw sequencing files/count matrix could be publicly accessed; If the data could not be accessed, the dataset was removed from our search. In addition, datasets with atlas-level data and expert annotations were prioritized. Finally, we collected 99 datasets for downstream analysis. For data preprocessing, if a quality-control (QC) counts matrix was provided, we used this matrix directly; otherwise, we followed the filtering rule in the source paper of the corresponding dataset to remove low-quality cells and low-abundance features. After QC, we obtained 20,000 cells subsets with the same cell type frequency for each dataset (Python package: sklearn42 [v: 1.2.2], train_test_split) as the original subset and then performed log normalization (feature counts for each cell were divided by the total counts for that cell and multiplied by 10000, followed by applying the natural log transformation (R package: Seurat [v: 4.3.0], NormalizeData).

For benchmark datasets, we used quality control criteria (recommended by 10x Genomics: sequencing depth of >20,000 reads per cell, coverage with >70% saturation rate, and 2,000–3,000 genes detected per cell) to ensure the reliability of the ground-truth labels provided by the original data generators, thereby supporting the accuracy of evaluation metrics that depend on such labels for computation.

Running SuperSCC

The log-normalized counts matrix was used as the input to SuperSCC, and the algorithm was implemented with default settings to obtain the M (SuperSCC, global_consensus_cluster) or F clusters (SuperSCC, sub_consensus_cluster) and the corresponding representative cluster markers for each dataset. These M/F clusters and relevant representative markers (top 50) were used for downstream clustering benchmarking analyses and for building the conserved gene module hierarchy on the basis of the aforementioned gene module detection method. The Log-normalized counts matrix and cell type labels (provided by a data generator) were input into the find_markers_ovr function in SuperSCC to obtain top 10/20 markers for each cell type for each dataset, which were finally used for feature selection benchmark analyses. To execute the SuperSCC label transfer pipeline, the data were input to feature_selection function in SuperSCC to capture the most representative features, followed by model training and label prediction via the SuperSCC model_training and predict_label functions, respectively. This label transfer pipeline was run with different input data: 1) A count matrix with cell type labels was split into training and testing subsets, accounting for 70% and 30% of the original data, respectively. The training subset was used for model training, whereas label prediction was performed on the basis of the test subset; this represents the ‘self-validation’ category of experiments. 2) Alternatively, model training and label prediction were performed on the basis of separate datasets; this represents the ‘cross-validation’ category of experiments. The SuperSCC predictions were retained for label transfer benchmark analyses.

Eighteen datasets were used for abovementioned feature selection and clustering analyses (Supplementary Data 1). Nine and six datasets were used for label transfer in the self-validation and cross-validation processes, respectively. The reference dataset used for label transfer and model training in the cross-validation process was produced by randomly selecting cells from multiple datasets (Supplementary Data 1).

Regarding the minimum hardware requirements for the SuperSCC framework, 12 GB of memory and a CPU with 20 logical processors are needed.

Running scanpy and seurat

Both Scanpy and Seurat were implemented by performing default processing steps (normalization, identification of HVGs, principal component analysis (PCA), clustering and marker identification) to obtain clusters and their markers. There are two key differences in clustering between Scanpy and Seurat: 1) Seurat requires scaling data before PCA; however, Scanpy does not require data scaling. 2) In Seurat, the Louvain algorithm with a default resolution of 0.8 is used for community detection within the neighborhood graph, whereas in Scanpy, the Leiden algorithm with a default resolution of 1 is used. Clusters generated by both tools were used for benchmark clustering analyses. Similarly, Scanpy (Python package: Scanpy [v: 1.9.8], rank_genes_groups) and Seurat (R package: Seurat [v: 4.3.0], FindAllMarkers) were applied to data grouped by cell type labels (provided by the data generator) to retain the top 10/20 markers for each cell type for each dataset, followed by benchmark feature selection analyses. For anchor-based label transfer, Seurat was implemented according to the user guide provided by the developers (https://satijalab.org/seurat/articles/integration_mapping.html). The predictions were subsequently used for benchmark label transfer analyses.

Running candidate tools for benchmarking

Sccaf14 (v: 0.0.1), Scshc15 (v: 1.0.0), SC316 (v: 1.30.0), CIDR17 (v: 0.1.5), Sclca18 (v:0.0.0.9), Monocle319 (v:1.3.7), scANVI20 (v: 1.3.0), SingleR21 (v: 2.4.1), SingleCellNet22 (v: 0.1.0) and Scmap23 (v: 1.30.0) were run with default settings according to their user guides. Notably, since SC3 requires users to determine k to infer clusters, we set k to the number of M clusters detected by SuperSCC when running SC3. The clusters generated by SC3, CIDR, Scshc and Sccaf were used for benchmark clustering analyses. When scANVI, Scmap, SingleR and SingleCellNet were run, all tools were run in self-validation and cross-validation modes, similar to the SuperSCC label transfer pipeline. The predictions from scANVI, Scmap, SingleR and SingleCellNet were used for benchmark label transfer analyses.

Feature selection, clustering and label transfer benchmarking

For feature selection benchmarking, we utilized large language models (LLMs), via API calling including GPT 4.1-mini (access date: April 14, 2025), GPT 5 (access date: August 8, 2025), DeepSeek V3 (access date: December 26, 2024), Qwen-max (access date: January 25, 2025) and Qwen3-4b (access date: August 6, 2025), to assess which gene sets provided by the different algorithms (SuperSCC, Scanpy and Seurat) offer the best representation of the corresponding cell type. The ideal prompt for gene set evaluation was produced by prompt engineering and iterative refinement (Supplementary Fig. 9). An example GPT4 output after employing such a prompt for gene set evaluation was shown in Supplementary Fig. 9. The evaluation output includes two scoring metrics: the relevant gene ratio (RGR) and the biological relevance score (BRS). The former indicates the proportion of genes associated with signatures of relevant cell types to the total number of input genes, whereas the relevance score is associated with cell type-specific signatures on the basis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) collections for each gene set. Finally, we averaged the relevant gene ratio/biological relevance scores across cell types to obtain the overall score, reflecting the performance of the algorithm in capturing biologically meaningful features for each dataset. The explained variance of variables (LLM, marker selection method, temperature) was evaluated on BRS and RGR (R package: effectsize, rank_eta_squared). In addition, we calculated the trend between the biological relevance score and the cell type frequency to highlight the efficacy of each algorithm for extremely low cell type (<1%) frequency. To compare with manually curated marker sets in the CellMarker 2.0 database, the SuperSCC (find_markers_ovr function) was executed on the Habermann_202043 dataset to identify markers for each cell type provided by the data generator. These markers were then intersected with the corresponding cell type marker genes in CellMarker to assess their concordance.

For clustering benchmarking, the clusters detected by nine algorithms (SuperSCC, Scanpy, Seurat, CIDR, Scshc, Sccaf, Sclca, Monocle3 and SC3) and the ground truth cell type labels were used to calculate four scoring matrices: the adjusted mutual information (AMI), adjusted Rand index (ARI), normalized mutual information (NMI) and Fowlkes-Mallows index (FMI). For label transfer benchmarking, the labels predicted by six tools (SuperSCC, Scmap, Seurat, scANVI, SingleR, and SingleCellNet) were utilized in self-validation or cross-validation experiments, along with ground-truth cell type labels, to compute eight scoring matrices: the accuracy (ACC), homogeneity (Homog), recall, precision (Prec), completeness (Compl), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), F1 score and V measure (VM).

Gene module enrichment/conservation analysis

We used signatures from GO collections (C5.GOBP, C5.GOCC and C5.GOMF). A hypergeometric test (R package: clusterProfiler44 [v: 4.10.1], enrichGO) was applied to test whether the provided gene modules were enriched in the corresponding signatures. Signatures with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significant. Fifteen MGMs and 65 FGMs were further grouped by functional similarities into 6 and 11 hallmarks, respectively. In addition, overlapping rates for comparisons among 238 M cluster gene sets and among 631 F cluster gene sets identified by SuperSCC were calculated on the basis of the top 50 genes within 99 datasets across 20 tissue types. Furthermore, we counted the number of gene sets contributing to GMs across datasets and then summed those numbers on the basis of hallmarkers to denote the frequency of each hallmarker for specific tissue types or across tissue types.

Then, for each module, we obtained the representative cell type by identifying its contributor (clusters within a gene set associated with the module) to that module. We subsequently calculated the gene module activity score (R package: Seurat [v: 4.3.0], AddModuleScore) and counted the frequency of conservation events for which dots with high activity scores were consistent with the expected cell type across the 99 test datasets. The overall conservation frequency and conservation frequency for each tissue were used to evaluate module conservation at different levels.

Biological signal conservation benchmarking

The Sikkema_20231, Reed_202445, Suo_202246, and Laughney_202047 datasets, containing stressed immune cells as identified through SuperSCC’s “analysis-then-merge” paradigm, were integrated using Harmony48 with default settings. Subsequently, standard Scanpy clustering was performed. Cell type annotations provided by the dataset authors were used to identify clusters corresponding to T cells, B cells, mast cells, and macrophages. We then assessed whether stressed immune cells could be distinguished among clusters within each annotated immune cell type.

Connection analysis between MGMs and FGMs

To assess the connection between the MGMs and FGMs, we combined hierarchy clustering and GM information to bridge modules across different levels and functionalities. We first obtained contributor F clusters for all 65 FGMs and traced their M clusters and MGMs as follows: if F cluster A contributing to FGM1 was the child cluster of M cluster B, we examined whether M cluster B contributed to any MGMs and, if so, recorded the MGM name; otherwise, an unknown source remained. Then, for each FGM, we calculated the frequency of the different MGMs and unknown sources. Finally, pairwise MGMs and FGMs, along with frequency number, contributor M/F clusters and functional hallmarks of the corresponding modules were treated as inputs for Sankey plot visualization (Python package: Plotly49 [v: 5.22.0], Sankey).

Cell communication analysis

In dataset Dong_2020_150, the matrix before QC, containing 20,000 cells, was used for CellChat-based cell communication analysis. Since cell type annotations were not provided for this dataset, we used SuperSCC-derived GM information and classic marker gene sets to annotate 7 cell types: B cells, T cells, immune endothelial cells, nonimmune endothelial cells, malignant cells, macrophages and fibroblasts. The overexpressed ligands or receptors in one cell group and the overexpressed ligand‒receptor interactions were subsequently evaluated (R package: CellChat51 [v: 1.6.1], identifyOverExpressedGenes and identifyOverExpressedInteractions). A cell‒cell communication network was inferred by modelling the probability of each interaction and performing a permutation test (R package: CellChat, computeCommunProb), followed by filtering out cell‒cell communication with low confidence scores (R package: CellChat, filterCommunication). The communication probability at the signalling pathway level was obtained by summing the communication probabilities of all ligand–receptor interactions associated with each signalling pathway (R package: CellChat, computeCommunProbPathway). Afterwards, we aggregated the cell‒cell network by counting the number of links or summing the communication probability (R package: CellChat, aggregateNet). Finally, circle plots and bubble plots were generated to visualize the cell‒cell communication, with a focus on the MHC II signalling pathway (R package: CellChat, netVisual_circle, netVisual_bubble).

Compare SuperSCC GMs and Spectra GMs

Spectra (v: 0.1.0) was applied with default settings to identify GMs in the datasets from Sikkema_20231, Reed_202445, Suo_202246, and Laughney_202047. Subsequently, an overlap analysis was conducted to evaluate the concordance between Spectra-derived GMs and the 65 SuperSCC-derived FGMs previously described.

Validation of SuperSCC GMs via scRNA-seq dataset in perturbation

The Kartha_202252 dataset, comprising untreated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and PBMCs stimulated in vitro with interferon-γ (IFNγ), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or phorbol myristate acetate (PMA), was partitioned into sub-datasets based on experimental conditions. Subsequently, SuperSCC’s hierarchical clustering and gene module detection were performed sequentially to identify conserved gene modules (GMs) across these sub-datasets. Gene module activity scores were then calculated (R package: Seurat [v4.3.0], AddModuleScore function) using the immune-related GM derived from gene sets in the perturbation datasets.

Hierarchy clustering, Pearson correlation and differential gene expression analysis

For hierarchy clustering, the Jaccard similarity among the 65 FGMs was calculated, followed by pairwise distance computations (R package: base [v: 4.3.1], dist(method = “euclidean”)) and a final clustering step (R package: base, hclust(method = “average”)).

For Pearson correlation calculations, for dataset Dong_2020_1, we calculated activity scores for FGM32 and MGM4 and extracted both scores for cells within F cluster 14 for correlation analysis (R package: base, cor.test(method = “pearson”)). A hypothesis test was subsequently performed to determine whether there was a positive correlation between the scores (R package: base, cor.test(method = “pearson”)). A p value < 0.05 indicated that score A was positively correlated with score B. Similarly, for dataset Laughney_202047, we performed correlation analysis between F cluster 6 and the other F clusters on the basis of the patient frequency.

For differential gene expression analysis, a t test was performed to compare the gene expression between F cluster 14 and F cluster 18 in the Dong_2020_1 dataset or between stress B-associated T cells and other T cells in the Laughney_2020 dataset. Genes with FDR-corrected p value < 0.05 and |log2foldchange | > 1 were considered differentially expressed.

Retrieval augmented generation (RAG) system

To enhance the usability of SuperSCC’s hierarchical cell identity markers and gene modules, we developed a retrieval augmented generation (RAG) system that delivers hierarchical cell labels and cluster alignments at various levels in response to user queries. The RAG system is designed as a sophisticated, multi-stage pipeline built on the LangChain (v: 0.3.24) framework. It processes CSV documents containing cell type, tissue type, and SuperSCC’s hierarchical data, which are segmented and vectorized using open-source Hugging Face (v: 0.29.3) embedding models before being indexed in a Qdrant vector database (v: 1.12.2). The retrieval process employs an advanced hybrid search strategy, integrating dense vector similarity with sparse BM25 keyword matching, and supports metadata-based filtering for refined hierarchical searches.

Post-retrieval, a cross-encoder model re-ranks documents for relevance, followed by an LLM-based scorer that filters out irrelevant passages. The generation phase leverages an OpenAI-compatible large language model (LLM) to produce a comprehensive response from the curated context. The pipeline also incorporates automated query translation and rewriting before retrieval to optimize performance. For transparency and verifiability, a post-generation analysis identifies and cites the precise source segments from retrieved documents used in the response, ensuring a robust and traceable RAG workflow. Additionally, a user-friendly interface was developed using the Streamlit (v: 1.45.1) framework.

Statistical analysis

A Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test (R function: stats, kruskal.test) was performed to determine whether there were statistically significant differences among the distributions of three groups. When the Kruskal–Wallis test yielded a significant result (p < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s multiple comparison test with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment for multiple testing (R package: dunn.test, dunn.test(method = “bh”)).
