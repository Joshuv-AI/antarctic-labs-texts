A polynomial approach to Carlitz’s q q -Bernoulli numbers
Mohamed Mouzaia, Bakir FARHI

Abstract
This paper investigates q q -analogues of the classical Bernoulli polynomials and numbers. We introduce a new polynomial sequence ( B n , q ​ ( X ) ) n ∈ ℕ 0 (B_{n,q}(X))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}} , defined via the Jackson integral, and explore its connections with Carlitz’s q q -Bernoulli polynomials and numbers. Specifically, we prove that the numbers B n , q ​ ( 0 ) B_{n,q}(0) are exactly the Carlitz q q -Bernoulli numbers and that the polynomials B n , q ​ ( X ) B_{n,q}(X) are genuine q q -analogues of the classical Bernoulli polynomials. This approach leverages the Jackson integral to reformulate Carlitz’s q q -Bernoulli numbers in terms of classical polynomial structures, offering new insights into their properties.

1
Introduction and Notation

Throughout this paper, we let
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denote the sets of positive and nonnegative integers, respectively. For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we let
\mathbb{R}_{n}[X]
denote the
\mathbb{R}
-vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}[X]
consisting of polynomials of degree at most
n
. The Bernoulli polynomials and numbers, denoted
B_{n}(X)
and
B_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
), are defined via their exponential generating functions:

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}e^{Xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}(X)\frac{t^{n}}{n!},\quad\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

so that
B_{n}=B_{n}(0)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. These generating functions yield several key properties of the Bernoulli polynomials and numbers, including:

\displaystyle B_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}B_{k}X^{n-k},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\int_{X}^{X+1}B_{n}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=X^{n},

(1.2)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Notably, the second formula (
1.2
) characterizes the
n
-th Bernoulli polynomial: for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the unique real polynomial
P(X)
satisfying
\int_{X}^{X+1}P(t)\,dt=X^{n}
is
P(X)=B_{n}(X)
. As is well known, Bernoulli polynomials and numbers play a central role in number theory, combinatorics, and mathematical analysis, owing to their connections with sums of powers, zeta functions, and the Euler-Maclaurin formula
[
1
]
.

For
q>0
,
q\neq 1
, the
q
-calculus generalizes classical calculus through the parameter
q
, recovering standard results as
q\to 1
. The
q
-analogue of a number or indeterminate
X
is defined as
[X]_{q}:=\frac{q^{X}-1}{q-1}
. Fundamental concepts, introduced by Jackson
[
4
]
, are the
q
-derivative and
q
-integral of a function
f
(satisfying suitable regularity conditions), defined as:

\displaystyle D_{q}f(X)

\displaystyle:=\frac{f(qX)-f(X)}{(q-1)X},

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,d_{q}t

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(1-q)b\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n}f(q^{n}b)-(1-q)a\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n}f(q^{n}a),&\text{if }0<q<1,\\
(q-1)b\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{-n}f(q^{-n}b)-(q-1)a\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{-n}f(q^{-n}a),&\text{if }q>1,\end{cases}

for all
a,b
in a suitable domain ensuring the well-definition of the expressions. A
q
-antiderivative of a function
f
is a function
F
such that
D_{q}F=f
. The fundamental theorem of
q
-calculus
[
5
]
states that if
f
is Jackson-integrable on
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
and
F
is a
q
-antiderivative of
f
, continuous at
0
, then
\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,d_{q}t=F(b)-F(a)
.

Next, we define a real
q
-polynomial as any polynomial in
[X]_{q}
with real coefficients, equivalently a real polynomial in
q^{X}
. The degree of a
q
-polynomial is its degree as a polynomial in
[X]_{q}
(or
q^{X}
). The set of all real
q
-polynomials forms an
\mathbb{R}
-vector space with principal bases
([X]_{q}^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
(q^{kX})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the set of real
q
-polynomials of degree at most
n
is an
\mathbb{R}
-vector subspace with principal bases
([X]_{q}^{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
,
(q^{kX})_{0\leq k\leq n}
, and
(q^{kX}[X]_{q}^{n-k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
, the latter being particularly important for our purposes. Like classical polynomials, two real
q
-polynomials that coincide on an infinite subset of
\mathbb{R}
are identical (for
q>0
,
q\neq 1
). To distinguish polynomials (resp.
q
-polynomials) from numbers, we consistently indicate the indeterminate
X
for polynomials (resp.
q
-polynomials).

In
q
-calculus, some authors investigate
q
-analogues of well-known polynomial sequences, such as Bernoulli, Euler, and Chebyshev polynomials, in the form of
q
-polynomial sequences. For example, in
[
2
]
, Carlitz introduced the
q
-polynomial sequences
(\eta_{n,q}(X))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
(\beta_{n,q}(X))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, defined by:

\displaystyle\eta_{n,q}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k}{[k]_{q}}q^{kX},

(1.3)

\displaystyle\beta_{n,q}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k+1}{[k+1]_{q}}q^{kX},

(1.4)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with the convention
\frac{k}{[k]_{q}}=1
for
k=0
. Carlitz showed that the second
q
-polynomial sequence
(\beta_{n,q}(X))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
converges to the classical Bernoulli polynomial sequence as
q\to 1
, i.e.,
\lim_{q\to 1}\beta_{n,q}(X)=B_{n}(X)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. He also considered the values of
\eta_{n,q}(X)
and
\beta_{n,q}(X)
at
X=0
, respectively denoted by
\eta_{n,q}
and
\beta_{n,q}
, and proved the important formulas:

\displaystyle\eta_{n,q}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\eta_{k,q}q^{kX}[X]_{q}^{n-k},

(1.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{n,q}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k,q}q^{kX}[X]_{q}^{n-k},

(1.6)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Thus,
\beta_{n,q}
are
q
-analogues of the Bernoulli numbers
B_{n}
, and Formula (
1.6
) is a
q
-analogue of Formula (
1.1
). For instance, the first four Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers are

\beta_{0,q}=1~,~\beta_{1,q}=-\dfrac{1}{[2]_{q}}~,~\beta_{2,q}=\dfrac{q}{[2]_{q}[3]_{q}}~,~\text{ and }\beta_{3,q}=-\dfrac{q(q-1)}{[3]_{q}[4]_{q}}.

(1.7)

For further reading on Carlitz’s
q
-Bernoulli numbers and polynomials and their extensions, see, e.g.,
[
3
]
.

This paper introduces, via the Jackson integral, a new sequence of polynomials,
(B_{n,q}(X))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, and studies its relationship with Carlitz’s
q
-polynomial sequences
(\eta_{n,q}(X))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
(\beta_{n,q}(X))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Our objectives are twofold: first, to establish that
B_{n,q}(0)=\beta_{n,q}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
; second, to prove that
B_{n,q}(X)
are genuine
q
-analogues of the Bernoulli polynomials, that is,
\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(X)=B_{n}(X)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. This approach provides a novel perspective on Carlitz’s
q
-Bernoulli numbers
\beta_{n,q}
by leveraging the Jackson integral to reformulate their associated
q
-polynomial sequences in terms of classical polynomial structures.

2
The results and the proofs

Our
q
-Bernoulli polynomials

B_{n,q}(X)
are defined through the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, there exists a unique real polynomial
B_{n,q}(X)
satisfying:

\int_{X}^{qX+1}B_{n,q}(t)\,d_{q}t=(q-1)X^{n+1}+X^{n}.

(2.1)

Proof.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Comparing the degrees of the polynomials on both sides of (
2.1
), we see that if
B_{n,q}(X)
exists, it must be of degree
n
; so
B_{n,q}(X)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[X]
. To establish the existence and the uniqueness of
B_{n,q}(X)
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[X]
, consider the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector subspace
E_{n}
of
{\mathbb{R}}_{n+1}[X]
defined by:

E_{n}:=\left\{P\in{\mathbb{R}}_{n+1}[X]:~P\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\right)=0\right\}

and the linear mapping
\varphi_{n}
defined by:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{n}:~{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[X]&\longrightarrow&E_{n}\\
P&\longmapsto&\int_{X}^{qX+1}P(t)\,d_{q}t.\end{array}

We claim that
\varphi_{n}
is an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector spaces. First, we show that
\varphi_{n}
is injective. Suppose
P\in\ker{\varphi_{n}}
, so that

\varphi_{n}(P)=\int_{X}^{qX+1}P(t)\,d_{q}t=0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}.

Evaluating at
X=[k]_{q}
(
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
), we have
q[k]_{q}+1=[k+1]_{q}
, so

\int_{[k]_{q}}^{[k+1]_{q}}P(t)\,d_{q}t=0.

Summing these integrals from
k=0
to
k=N-1
for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{[N]_{q}}P(t)\,d_{q}t=0\quad(\forall N\in{\mathbb{N}}).

Let
Q
be the polynomial
q
-antiderivative of
P
such that
Q(0)=0
, i.e.,
Q(X)=\int_{0}^{X}P(t)\,d_{q}t
. Then, the above implies
Q([N]_{q})=0
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Since the points
[N]_{q}
(
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
) are pairwise distinct (because
q>0
and
q\neq 1
), it follows that
Q=0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
. Thus
P=D_{q}Q=0_{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}
. Hence
\ker{\varphi_{n}}=\{0_{{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[X]}\}
, and
\varphi_{n}
is injective. Furthermore, since
\dim{{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[X]}=\dim{E_{n}}=n+1
, then
\varphi_{n}
is an isomorphism, as we claimed it to be. Consequently, the polynomial
(q-1)X^{n+1}+X^{n}=X^{n}\left(1-(1-q)X\right)
(which clearly belongs to
E_{n}
) has a unique preimage by
\varphi_{n}
. This ensures the existence and the uniqueness of
B_{n,q}(X)
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[X]
and completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Assuming the existence of the limit
\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(t)
, which we provisionally denote by
\widetilde{B}_{n}(t)
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, by setting
q\to 1
in Formula (
2.1
), we obtain

\int_{X}^{X+1}\widetilde{B}_{n}(t)\,dt=X^{n},

which is one of the characterizing properties of the
n
th
Bernoulli polynomial
B_{n}(t)
. Thus,
\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(t)=B_{n}(t)
, justifying the terminology “
q
-Bernoulli polynomials” for the
B_{n,q}(t)
’s. However, establishing the existence of
\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(t)
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
is far from trivial. At the end of the paper, we will rigorously show (by another technique) that indeed
\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(t)=B_{n}(t)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
(see Theorem
2.11
).

Notation 2.3
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we denote by
F_{n,q}(X)
the polynomial given by

F_{n,q}(X):=\int_{0}^{X}B_{n,q}(t)\,d_{q}t,

which is a
q
-antiderivative of
B_{n,q}(X)
satisfying
F_{n,q}(0)=0
.

Examples 2.4
.

To compute the polynomials
B_{n,q}(X)
explicitly for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we express
B_{n,q}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{n,q}^{(k)}X^{k}
, where
a_{n,q}^{(k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for
k=0,1,\dots,n
. Substituting into (
2.1
) and equating coefficients of the polynomials on both sides yields a Cramer system of
(n+1)
equations in the
(n+1)
unknowns
a_{n,q}^{(k)}
(
k=0,1,\dots,n
). Solving this system gives the polynomial
B_{n,q}(X)
. For example, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{0,q}(X)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle B_{1,q}(X)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q+1}+X,

\displaystyle B_{2,q}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{(q+1)(q^{2}+q+1)}-\frac{2q+1}{q^{2}+q+1}X+X^{2}.

Our
q
-Bernoulli numbers are naturally defined as
B_{n,q}:=B_{n,q}(0)
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Thus, we have

B_{0,q}=1,\quad B_{1,q}=-\frac{1}{[2]_{q}},\quad B_{2,q}=\frac{q}{[2]_{q}[3]_{q}}.

For
n=0,1,2
, we observe that the numbers
B_{n,q}
coincide with the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers
\beta_{n,q}
(see (
1.7
)). In the following, we will prove that this holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and establish a general connection between the polynomials
B_{n,q}(X)
and the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli polynomials
\beta_{n,q}(X)
. To this end, we first introduce some preliminary results. The following proposition is taken from
[
3
]
, and for the sake of completeness, we include its proof here.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
3
, Corollary 2.13]
)
.

For all positive integers
n
and
N
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}[k]_{q}^{n-1}=\dfrac{\eta_{n,q}(N)-\eta_{n,q}}{n}.

Proof.

Let
n,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}{[k]_{q}}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}\left(\frac{q^{k}-1}{q-1}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}\left(q^{k}-1\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i}q^{ki}(-1)^{n-1-i}\quad(\text{by the binomial formula})

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-i}\binom{n-1}{i}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{(i+1)k}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-i}\binom{n-1}{i}\frac{q^{(i+1)N}-1}{q^{i+1}-1}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n-1}{i-1}\frac{q^{iN}-1}{q^{i}-1}.

Using the identities
\binom{n-1}{i-1}=\frac{i}{n}\binom{n}{i}
and
q^{i}-1=(q-1)[i]_{q}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, it follows that:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}{[k]_{q}}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}\frac{i}{[i]_{q}}\left(q^{iN}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\eta_{n,q}(N)-\eta_{n,q}(0)\right)\quad(\text{by \eqref{eq4}}),

as required.
∎

We also require the following results concerning the derivatives of certain
q
-polynomials.

Proposition 2.6
.

For all positive integer
n
, we have

\eta_{n,q}^{\prime}(X)=n\frac{\log q}{q-1}q^{X}\beta_{n-1,q}(X).

Proof.

From (
1.3
), we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle\eta_{n,q}^{\prime}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k}{[k]_{q}}\left(q^{kX}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k}{[k]_{q}}(k\log q)q^{kX}.

Using the identity
k\binom{n}{k}=n\binom{n-1}{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq n
, we rewrite the sum as

\displaystyle\eta_{n,q}^{\prime}(X)

\displaystyle=n\frac{\log q}{q-1}\cdot(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\frac{k}{[k]_{q}}q^{kX}

\displaystyle=n\frac{\log q}{q-1}\cdot(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-k-1}\binom{n-1}{k}\frac{k+1}{[k+1]_{q}}q^{(k+1)X}

\displaystyle=n\frac{\log q}{q-1}q^{X}\beta_{n-1,q}(X)\quad(\text{according to \eqref{eq5}}).

The proposition is proved.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

B_{n,q}(0)=F_{n,q}^{\prime}(0).

Proof.

Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

B_{n,q}(0)=\lim_{X\to 0}B_{n,q}(X)=\lim_{X\to 0}D_{q}F_{n,q}(X)=\lim_{X\to 0}\dfrac{F_{n,q}(qX)-F_{n,q}(X)}{(q-1)X}.

This limit is of indeterminate form
\frac{0}{0}
. Applying l’Hôpital’s rule, we get

B_{n,q}(0)=\lim_{X\to 0}\dfrac{qF_{n,q}^{\prime}(qX)-F_{n,q}^{\prime}(X)}{q-1}=F_{n,q}^{\prime}(0),

as required.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove our main results connecting the polynomials
B_{n,q}(X)
and their
q
-antiderivatives
F_{n,q}(X)
with the Carlitz
q
-polynomials
\eta_{n,q}(X)
and
\beta_{n,q}(X)
. We begin with the following key theorem.

Theorem 2.8
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

F_{n,q}\left([X]_{q}\right)=\dfrac{\eta_{n+1,q}(X)-\eta_{n+1,q}}{n+1}.

Proof.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, evaluate (
2.1
) at
X=[k]_{q}
. Since
q[k]_{q}+1=[k+1]_{q}
and
(q-1)[k]_{q}^{n+1}+[k]_{q}^{n}=q^{k}[k]_{q}^{n}
, we obtain

\int_{[k]_{q}}^{[k+1]_{q}}B_{n,q}(t)\,d_{q}t=q^{k}[k]_{q}^{n}.

Summing from
k=0
to
k=N-1
for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we get

F_{n,q}([N]_{q})=\int_{0}^{[N]_{q}}B_{n,q}(t)\,d_{q}t=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{k}[k]_{q}^{n}.

By Proposition
2.5
, this equals

F_{n,q}\left([N]_{q}\right)=\dfrac{\eta_{n+1,q}(N)-\eta_{n+1,q}}{n+1}

for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This shows that the
q
-polynomials
F_{n,q}([X]_{q})
and
\frac{\eta_{n+1,q}(X)-\eta_{n+1,q}}{n+1}
take the same values on
{\mathbb{N}}
, implying that they are identical; that is,

F_{n,q}\left([X]_{q}\right)=\dfrac{\eta_{n+1,q}(X)-\eta_{n+1,q}}{n+1},

as required.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

F_{n,q}^{\prime}\left([X]_{q}\right)=\beta_{n,q}(X).

Proof.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Differentiating the formula in Theorem
2.8
and applying Proposition
2.6
, we obtain

\frac{\log q}{q-1}q^{X}F_{n,q}^{\prime}\left([X]_{q}\right)=\frac{\log q}{q-1}q^{X}\beta_{n,q}(X),

implying the required result.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

B_{n,q}=\beta_{n,q}.

On the other words, our
q
-Bernoulli numbers coincide with the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers.

Proof.

Take
X=0
in the formula of Corollary
2.9
and use Proposition
2.7
.
∎

We conclude this paper by proving that the polynomials
B_{n,q}(X)
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
) are genuine
q
-analogues of the classical Bernoulli polynomials; that is,
B_{n,q}(X)
tends to
B_{n}(X)
as
q\to 1
.

Theorem 2.11
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(X)=B_{n}(X).

Proof.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. By (
1.6
), we have

\displaystyle\beta_{n,q}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k,q}q^{kt}[t]_{q}^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k,q}\left((q-1)[t]_{q}+1\right)^{k}[t]_{q}^{n-k}.

By identifying this with the formula in Corollary
2.9
, we derive that:

\displaystyle F_{n,q}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k,q}\left((q-1)t+1\right)^{k}t^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k,q}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(q-1)^{i}t^{i}\right)t^{n-k}\quad(\text{by the binomial formula})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{i}\beta_{k,q}(q-1)^{i}t^{n+i-k}.

Then, integrating from
0
to
X
yields (since
F_{n,q}(0)=0
):

F_{n,q}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{i}\beta_{k,q}(q-1)^{i}\dfrac{X^{n+i-k+1}}{n+i-k+1}.

Finally, Applying the
q
-derivative
D_{q}
to both sides, we obtain

B_{n,q}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{i}\beta_{k,q}(q-1)^{i}\dfrac{[n+i-k+1]_{q}}{n+i-k+1}X^{n+i-k}.

As
q\to 1
, we have
\beta_{k,q}\to B_{k}
and
[n+i-k+1]_{q}\to n+i-k+1
(for all integers
k
and
i
such that
0\leq i\leq k\leq n
); hence

\lim_{q\to 1}B_{n,q}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}B_{k}X^{n-k}=B_{n}(X)

(according to (
1.1
)). This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

From Corollary
2.9
, the connection between our polynomials
B_{n,q}(X)
and the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli polynomials
\beta_{n,q}(X)
is given by

\beta_{n,q}(X)=\left(\int_{0}^{s}B_{n,q}(t)\,d_{q}t\right)^{\prime}\bigg|_{s=[X]_{q}}

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. A similar formula for
\beta_{n,q}(X)
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
), derived directly from its definition (
1.4
), is given by:

\beta_{n,q}(X)=\left(\int_{0}^{s}\left(\dfrac{t-1}{q-1}\right)^{n}\,d_{q}t\right)^{\prime}\bigg|_{s=q^{X}}\quad(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).
