Safety outcomes of trazodone versus antipsychotics for delirium after hospital admission in adults aged 65 years and older: a nationwide cohort study using a target trial emulation framework
Authors: Chun-Ting Yang, James M Wilkins, Kevin T Pritchard, Qiaoxi Chen, Xiaojuan Liu, Dae Hyun Kim, Kueiyu Joshua Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundTrazodone has emerged as a potentially safer alternative to atypical antipsychotic medications for managing delirium-related symptoms in older adults after hospital admission. This study aimed to compare safety outcomes in older adults who were prescribed trazodone versus atypical antipsychotic medication after hospital discharge.MethodsThis population-based cohort study was done using US Medicare data from Jan 1, 2013, to Dec 31, 2022, and Optum de-identified Clinformatics Data Mart Database data from Jan 1, 2013, to Aug 31, 2025, with a target trial emulation framework. Patients aged at least 65 years without psychiatric disorders who were prescribed a new trazodone or atypical antipsychotic medication (quetiapine, risperidone, or olanzapine) prescription within 30 days of hospital discharge with a claims-based delirium diagnosis were included. We applied propensity score overlap weighting to adjust for 162 covariates. The primary outcome was all-cause rehospitalisation. Secondary outcomes included rehospitalisation for delirium, falls (including fall-related emergency department visits), pneumonia, urinary tract infections, stroke, and all-cause mortality. Treatment-outcome associations were estimated using the Fine–Gray model for non-fatal outcomes (presented as subdistribution hazard ratio [SDHR]) and the Cox model for all-cause mortality (presented as hazard ratio [HR]). Estimates from the two databases were pooled using inverse-variance weighting.Findings11 678 trazodone and 29 590 atypical antipsychotic medication users were included. Compared with antipsychotic medications, trazodone was associated with lower risks of rehospitalisation (SDHR 0⋅95 [95% CI 0⋅91–0⋅98]), rehospitalisation for delirium (SDHR 0⋅80 [0⋅75–0⋅86]), urinary tract infection (SDHR 0⋅84 [0⋅72–0⋅99]), and all-cause mortality (HR 0⋅84 [0⋅79–0⋅90]); no significant difference was observed for falls (SDHR 0⋅93 [0⋅85–1⋅02]), pneumonia (SDHR 0⋅96 [0⋅79–1⋅16]), and stroke (SDHR 0⋅92 [0⋅77–1⋅11]). The reduced risk of rehospitalisation with trazodone was consistent when compared with risperidone (SDHR 0⋅89 [0⋅84–0⋅95]), and olanzapine (SDHR 0⋅88 [0⋅82–0⋅94]) but no difference was seen with quetiapine (SDHR 0⋅97 [0⋅93–1⋅01]).InterpretationThese results revealed reduced risks of rehospitalisation and all-cause mortality associated with trazodone versus atypical antipsychotic medications. Trazodone is a potentially safer alternative to antipsychotic medications in the management of delirium-related symptoms after hospital discharge.FundingNational Institute on Aging.

Introduction

Delirium is a common and concerning clinical syndrome in adults aged 50 years or older who are admitted to hospital, with symptoms persisting until discharge and for months afterwards in up to 50% of cases.1 Clinical guidelines recommend non-pharmacological interventions as first-line management for delirium, and reserving pharmacological treatment (eg, antipsychotic medications and benzodiazepines) for cases in which de-escalation strategies fail, contributing factors of delirium have been addressed, and the neuropsychiatric disturbances cause significant distress or pose a risk of harm to the patients or others.2,3

Antipsychotic medications are among the most-used pharmacological treatments for managing hyperactive behavioural symptoms of delirium in older adults admitted to hospital, such as agitation and psychosis. However, their broad pharmacological effects are associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, including oversedation, cognitive decline, falls, pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), stroke, and increased mortality.2–4

Because of the safety concerns of antipsychotic medications, trazodone is considered as a potentially safer alternative for managing delirium-related behavioural symptoms.5,6 Trazodone shows sedating effects at low doses and antidepressant activity at high doses. However, evidence directly comparing trazodone and antipsychotic medications in older adults with delirium is scarce. To date, only one study done among long-term care residents with dementia has reported that trazodone use was associated with a lower mortality than antipsychotic medications.7 But these findings might not be generalisable to older adults, in whom delirium might be triggered by acute medical conditions (eg, infection or surgery), or by hospitalisation-related factors (eg, disrupted sleep–wake cycles).

Our study used two nationwide US databases with a trial emulation design to compare safety outcomes, including rehospitalisation, specific rehospitalisation reasons (including delirium, falls, pneumonia, UTIs, and stroke), and all-cause mortality between older adults who were prescribed trazodone versus atypical antipsychotic medication after hospital discharge.

Methods

Data source

We used two nationwide US databases, namely Medicare fee-for-service parts A, B, and D data and the Optum de-identified Clinformatics Data Mart Database (Optum CDM). Medicare is a federal programme providing medical insurance coverage for people aged 65 years and older or with a disability. Our Medicare population included all people with a dementia diagnosis and a random sample of beneficiaries without dementia that represented 16% of fee-for-service beneficiaries. The Optum CDM population was drawn from a US national cohort with commercial insurance coverage. Our study included everyone in Optum CDM who fit the study selection criteria, with no prior selection as with Medicare. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Boston, MA, USA (#2023P001110). Our study followed the STROBE reporting guideline for observational studies.

Study population

Using a target trial emulation framework,8 we emulated a hypothetical clinical trial comparing use of trazodone versus antipsychotic medications after discharge among older adults aged 65 years or older with inpatient delirium. Key components of the trial design (ie, eligibility, treatment strategies, assignment, follow-up, endpoints, causal contrast, and statistical analysis) and the approaches used to emulate each of these components are shown in the appendix (p 5). In brief, validated claims-based measures were used to identify eligible patients, study outcomes, and key study covariates such as dementia, comorbidity burden, and frailty. Propensity score methods were applied to balance baseline characteristics between treatment groups to approximate randomisation in the observational data.

Adults aged 65 years or older who were first prescribed trazodone or an atypical antipsychotic medication (quetiapine, risperidone, or olanzapine) within 30 days of hospital discharge with a delirium diagnosis9 were identified in the Medicare database from July 1, 2013, to Dec 31, 2022, or in the Optum CDM from July 1, 2013, to Aug 31, 2025. The 30-day window after discharge was applied to account for the potential stays of the patients in short-term skilled nursing facilities after discharge, where prescription data are unavailable and the average length of stay is approximately 1 month.10,11 Delirium was identified using diagnosis codes on the basis of a claims-based algorithm, which predicts delirium based on counts of delirium diagnoses, age, and dementia status (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0⋅88, 95% CI 0⋅84–0⋅91).9 Give that age (≥65 years, as an inclusion criterion) and dementia status (as a stratification factor) are already incorporated in our study design, we primarily relied on the diagnosis codes to identify delirium cases. The date of the first prescription of trazodone or antipsychotic medications was the cohort entry date.

Patients without continuous enrolment in medical and pharmacy coverage in the 180 days before the cohort entry date were excluded. We also excluded patients ever diagnosed with bipolar disorders, depression, schizophrenia, and other psychiatric disorders before the cohort entry date, those who received hospice or palliative care in the 180 days before the cohort entry date, and those who received study drugs above the prespecified maximum dose per day at cohort entry, to ensure that the trazodone and antipsychotic medications were primarily prescribed for managing delirium or behaviour disturbance following hospital admission, rather than for major depression or psychosis. The prespecified maximum doses per day were 300 mg for trazodone, 300 mg for quetiapine, 3 mg for risperidone, and 10 mg for olanzapine (cohort identification diagram and operational definitions are provided in the appendix pp 2, 33).7

Baseline covariates and study outcomes

Based on data in the 180 days preceding and including the cohort entry date, we controlled for 162 baseline covariates, including the following covariates: demographics; presence of dementia, determined by a validated claims-based algorithm with a positive predictive value of 77%;12 comorbidities, medication history, and health-care utilisation; the combined comorbidity score that has been validated for predicting mortality in older adults;13 and a claims-based frailty index that has been validated against several clinical measures of frailty (eg, gait speed, grip strength, and activity of daily living disability), dementia severity, hospital admission, and death.14–16 We also assessed whether patients were admitted to the intensive-care unit (ICU) or underwent surgical interventions within 30 days before the cohort entry date, given that these factors are clinically significant and influence delirium prognosis in patients admitted to hospital (detailed covariate lists and operational definitions are provided in the appendix pp 34–40).

The primary outcome was all-cause rehospitalisation. Secondary outcomes included rehospitalisation for delirium, falls (including fall-related emergency department visits or hospital admissions), pneumonia, UTIs, stroke, and all-cause mortality. The outcomes were ascertained based on validated algorithms (appendix p 41). Study patients were followed from 1 day after the cohort entry date to ensure temporality between exposure and outcome, until the earliest occurrence of any of the following events: study outcome, discontinuation of the index exposure (prescription gap >30 days), switching to the other study drug, exposure to antidepressants or antipsychotic medications other than the study drugs, death, disenrolment, maximum follow-up period of 180 days, or the end of the data availability.

Statistical analysis

Between-group comparisons of baseline characteristics were conducted using standardised mean differences (SMDs); an SMD less than 0⋅1 indicated an adequate covariate balance.

To address confounding and ensure comparability between study groups, we applied propensity score overlap weighting, which estimates the average treatment effect in a population with the greatest overlap in patient characteristics between study groups.17 Under this approach, the weight for each individual equals the probability of being assigned to the opposite group: propensity score for the exposure group (ie, trazodone) and propensity score for the control group (ie, antipsychotic medications). Propensity score overlap weighting has been shown to improve the precision of treatment, meaning outcome estimates compared with other propensity-score methods.18,19 We estimated the propensity score using logistic regression to model the probability of receiving trazodone versus antipsychotic medications, considering 162 baseline covariates (appendix pp 7–13).

In the propensity score-weighted cohort, subdistribution hazard ratios (SDHRs) for the association between trazodone versus antipsychotic medications on non-fatal study outcomes were estimated using Fine–Gray models to account for competing risk of death.20 Robust standard errors were used to compute 95% CIs. Cox proportional hazards model was applied to estimate the association between treatment and all-cause mortality, presenting as hazard ratio (HR). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by including an exposure-time interaction term in the model, with a p<0⋅05 indicating a violation. Risk differences (RDs) of study outcomes between study groups were estimated as the difference in cumulative incidence derived from the cumulative incidence function (CIF). 95% CIs of RDs were obtained via the bootstrap method with 200 iterations, a number chosen on the basis of our pilot data showing stable CIs beyond this point (appendix p 42). To optimize confounding adjustment, we stratified the analysis by patient dementia status, recalculating propensity score and estimating the treatment effects separately for the dementia and non-dementia subgroups. HRs and RDs across subgroups and databases were subsequently pooled using a fixed-effect meta-analysis.

Several sensitivity analyses were done. First, to address unmeasured confounding due to baseline cognitive and functional status, which is absent from claims data, we linked the Medicare population with the Minimum Data Set and the Outcome and Assessment Information Set, both federally mandated assessment tools maintained by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. We incorporated variables related to activities of daily living, behavioural and psychological symptoms, and cognitive impairment within the baseline assessment period into the propensity score model (definitions in the appendix p 40). Missing indicators were included for individuals with incomplete data. Second, we weighted the trazodone and antipsychotic medication users using high-dimensional propensity scores (hdPS) that automatically identified and adjusted for hundreds of proxy covariates that might be correlated with the unmeasured confounders for propensity score estimation from multiple data dimensions.21 Third, we did an intention-to-treat analysis without censoring patients who deviated from their assigned treatment. Fourth, we extended the maximum follow-up period to 365 days to address outcome misclassification resulting from right censoring at the end of the 180-day follow-up in the primary analysis. Fifth, we used the random-effect meta-analysis model to pool the study estimates. Sixth, to minimise potential misclassification of study drugs, we excluded patients who were rehospitalised or admitted to skilled nursing facilities between their hospital discharge for inpatient delirium and the cohort entry date. Seventh, we excluded patients with insomnia to address the concern that trazodone was more likely to be prescribed to those with pre-existing sleep disorders. Eighth, to avoid including individuals who used trazodone or antipsychotic medications on an as-needed basis, we excluded those who received study drugs at or below half of the recommended starting dose for agitation (ie, 12⋅5 mg for trazodone, 6⋅25 mg for quetiapine, 0⋅125 mg for risperidone, and 1⋅25 mg for olanzapine) at the first prescription.22,23 Lastly, we restricted the cohort to patients whose first trazodone or antipsychotic medication prescription was taken within 7 days of hospital discharge to reduce potential immortal time between hospital discharge and the cohort entry date.

Subgroup analyses were done by age (<80 years vs ≥80 years), sex (male vs female), race (White vs non-White), dementia status (with vs without dementia), and ICU stay (ever vs no ICU stay). Patients were categorised as White or non-White (including Asian, Black, Hispanic, or unknown) because of limited subgroup sizes.

All analyses were done using Aetion Substantiate (2025) with R (5.30.X) integration and SAS 9.4.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in the design and conduct of the study, data collection, data management, data analysis, data interpretation, manuscript preparation, manuscript review, or approval of the manuscript, or decision to submit for publication.

Results

11 678 trazodone users and 29 590 antipsychotic medication users were identified (table 1; appendix p 3). The overall comorbidity score and frailty index were similar between the study groups. Compared with antipsychotic medication users, trazodone users were younger, had a higher prevalence of ICU stay and sleep-related disorders, had more frequent office visits, and longer stays in hospital (table 1; appendix pp 7–9). After propensity score overlap weighting, all baseline characteristics were well balanced in the population pooled across Medicare and Optum CDM (appendix pp 7–13), in the Medicare-only population (appendix pp 14–20), and in the Optum CDM-only population (appendix pp 21–27).

Most sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with those of the primary analysis, showing a reduced risk of rehospitalisation associated with trazodone versus antipsychotic medication use (appendix p 4). In the comparison between trazodone and individual antipsychotic medications (figure), trazodone use was associated with a significantly lower risk of rehospitalisation compared to risperidone and olanzapine but the significance was not observed for quetiapine. The lower risk of rehospitalisation with trazodone versus antipsychotic medications was observed across various subgroups (figure), including adults younger than 80 years and non-White populations, those with and without dementia, and patients admitted or not to ICU. No difference in the risk of rehospitalisation between study groups was observed among adults aged 80 years and older. Significant heterogeneity in SDHR estimates was identified across age subgroups (p<0⋅01; figure). Stratified by database, the significantly lower risk of rehospitalisation associated with trazodone was observed across most subgroups in the Medicare population, but not in the Optum CDM population, possibly because of the relatively small sample size (appendix pp 31–32).

Discussion

Based on two large US national cohorts, we found that among older adults with inpatient delirium, use of trazodone after discharge was associated with a significantly lower risk of rehospitalisation than with antipsychotic medications. A similar trend was observed when comparing trazodone to risperidone and olanzapine but not quetiapine.

The observed decrease in rehospitalisation risk among trazodone users might be attributable to the significantly lower incidence of hospital admissions because of delirium and UTIs. No previous evidence has linked trazodone to the risk of hospital admissions because of delirium or UTIs. By contrast, previous studies have shown that patients treated with antipsychotic medications experienced more cognitive function decline than those who did not receive antipsychotic medications.24,25 These findings support our observation of a lower risk of delirium associated with trazodone than antipsychotic medication use. Additionally, antipsychotic medications might affect the urinary system in several ways (eg, central serotonin and dopaminergic effects, and peripheral α1-adrenergic and anticholinergic activities), which can lead to urinary retention, incontinence, and UTIs.26 A meta-analysis reported that antipsychotic medication use versus non-use, was associated with a significantly increased risk of incontinence (odds ratio 4⋅09 [95% CI 1⋅71–9⋅79]), and quetiapine was found to have higher odds of causing voiding dysfunction versus other antipsychotic medications (odds ratio 2⋅14 [1⋅41–3⋅26]).27 In line with these findings, we speculate that the significant difference in the rehospitalisation for UTI between trazodone and antipsychotic medication users observed in our study might be partially explained by the higher anticholinergic burden of antipsychotic medications than trazodone. This finding also helps explain the increased risk of delirium among antipsychotic medication users in our study, particularly given that quetiapine and olanzapine, two atypical antipsychotic medications with high anticholinergic effects,28 comprised the vast majority of the study population (25 456 [86⋅0%] of 29 590).

The lower mortality risk observed with trazodone than with antipsychotic medication use in our study is consistent with a previous study of Canadian long-term care residents, which reported a 25% reduction in all-cause mortality among trazodone versus antipsychotic medication users (HR 0⋅75 [95% CI 0⋅66–0⋅85]).7

Conducting a randomised trial of trazodone versus antipsychotic medication use in older adults with delirium is challenging, given the difficulties of recruiting and retaining older adults with frailty in trials and obtaining consent from those with active behavioural disturbance.29 In this context, observational data serve as a valuable resource to inform prescribing decisions. By using two nationwide databases with a trial emulation design, we observed lower risks of harms of trazodone over antipsychotic medications, suggesting the potential of trazodone as a safer alternative to antipsychotic medications in older adults who were discharged from the hospital after experiencing inpatient delirium. However, a reduction in rehospitalisation risk was not found among patients aged 80 years or older. Although this subgroup finding should be interpreted as exploratory, it might indicate that trazodone and antipsychotic medications pose similar risks in this older age group, as trazodone also has potential adverse effects, particularly sedation. These findings further highlight the importance of prioritising non-pharmacological approaches for managing behavioural disturbances in older adults.

When pooling results from the Medicare and Optum CDM databases, differences in patient characteristics and outcome risk profiles were observed. Despite being younger and less frail (appendix pp 21–22), the Optum CDM population had higher all-cause mortality (appendix p 30), probably reflecting differences in hospital admission patterns, as this cohort included more patients admitted for high-risk conditions such as ICU stays. Nevertheless, the confounding structure underlying the comparison between trazodone and antipsychotic medication users appeared similar across databases (ie, similar comorbidity score and frailty index but a higher prevalence of sleep disorders in trazodone users), suggesting that applying the same analytical strategy and combining the study estimates was reasonable. Furthermore, no significant heterogeneity in the relative treatment effects was detected (appendix p 30), supporting the validity of pooling results across databases.

Our study has several limitations. First, claims data do not have clinical information on delirium type (hyperactive or hypoactive), severity, and resolution. Despite considering several confounders and methodology efforts in our analysis, residual confounding related to delirium severity or behavioural disturbances might still be present. Antipsychotic medications are more likely to be prescribed for patients with more severe behavioural symptoms than trazodone. However, sensitivity analyses that incorporated additional clinical assessment variables (eg, cognitive and functional status, and behavioural and psychological symptoms) and hdPS adjustment did not attenuate the observed associations toward the null, suggesting that the clinical benefit associated with trazodone use in our study is unlikely to be fully explained by residual confounding. Second, delirium is often underdiagnosed and under-recorded in administrative claims data,30 so our study might have captured primarily severe or hyperactive delirium cases. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the relevance of our findings for those with less severe or hypoactive delirium. In addition, the accuracy of delirium coding can vary across hospitals, clinical service lines, and calendar periods (eg, during the COVID-19 era). Misclassification of delirium cases is possible; however, such misclassification is expected to be non-differential across treatment groups and would tend to attenuate rather than exaggerate the observed associations. Third, our study focused on use of trazodone or antipsychotic medications after discharge following an inpatient delirium episode. These medications might have been initiated during hospital admission or prescribed after discharge to manage ongoing behavioural disturbances. However, information on inpatient medication use was not available in the claims data, potentially introducing misclassification bias. Moreover, delirium severity is often greater during hospital admission than at discharge. Thus, the relevance of our findings to in-hospital delirium management should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, we could not access prescription data for patients admitted to short-term skilled nursing facilities, a potential transition for older adults after hospital admission. To address the potential misclassification of study drug exposure, we did a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who were rehospitalised or admitted to skilled nursing facilities between hospital discharge and the first trazodone or antipsychotic medication prescription, which yielded consistent results with the primary analysis. Fifth, although this study emulated a hypothetical clinical trial, it does not overcome limitations of data sources and was not randomised. Residual confounding remains a major limitation and certain trial components cannot be fully addressed using claims data. For example, we were unable to confirm whether patients consumed the dispensed medications. Additionally, the treatment assignment and time zero differed by 1 day since the follow-up began 1 day after cohort entry to ensure the temporal sequence between exposure and outcome. Sixth, our study period spanned the pre-COVID period, the initial COVID surge, and the period after the surge, during which hospital admission patterns and the characteristics of patients diagnosed with delirium might have differed. Therefore, the study findings might reflect a mixture of these populations, and future research is warranted to explore the potential effect of these temporal variations. Seventh, some patients might appear in both datasets if they transitioned between commercial insurance and Medicare fee for service. Although such overlap is expected to be uncommon among adults aged 65 years and older, its exact extent cannot be determined. Eighth, because of limited event numbers for the secondary outcomes, these numbers were not included in the sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Lastly, our study results might not be directly applicable to non-US health-care systems because of regional differences in practice patterns, health-care delivery, and health-care coverage.

On the basis of two US national cohorts, we found that use of trazodone after discharge was associated with lower risks of rehospitalisation and all-cause mortality than antipsychotic medications among older adults experiencing an inpatient delirium episode. Although further studies are needed to confirm these findings, our results suggest that trazodone might be a safer alternative to antipsychotic medications for managing delirium-related behaviour symptoms after hospital discharge.

Supplementary Material

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception to Nov 1, 2025, with no language restrictions, using the following search terms in all fields: “(trazodone)” AND “(antipsychotic OR quetiapine OR risperidone OR olanzapine)” AND “(hospitalization OR discharge)” AND “(older adults)” AND “(safety OR outcome)” AND “(cohort)”. This search identified six articles. Of these, two examined drug-use patterns, three analysed associations based on use versus non-use of psychotropic agents, and one was an active comparator cohort study comparing trazodone with atypical antipsychotics in long-term care residents. This latter study was the only one directly relevant to evidence regarding outcomes associated with trazodone versus antipsychotic medications in older adults aged 65 years and older. However, its findings might not be fully generalisable to older adults after discharge, who might have a different risk profile (eg, behavioural disturbances triggered by acute medical conditions or hospitalisation-related factors) than long-term care residents.

Added value of this study

In two nationwide US cohorts, trazodone use was associated with a lower risk of rehospitalisation compared with antipsychotic medications, potentially because of a reduced risk of rehospitalisation for delirium and urinary tract infections. Exploratory subgroup analyses showed this reduction across subgroups, regardless of sex, race, dementia history, or admission to intensive-care unit. However, the association was not observed in adults aged 80 years or older.

Implications of all the available evidence

Trazodone might be a safer alternative to atypical antipsychotic medications for managing delirium-related symptoms in older adults after hospital discharge. These findings support considering trazodone in clinical decision-making for patients who require pharmacological treatment following hospital admission. However, prioritising non-pharmacological approaches to manage behavioural disturbances remains essential, particularly in the oldest age subgroup.
