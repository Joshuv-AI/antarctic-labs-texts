Ocular Complications of Mpox: Evolving Understanding and Future Directions
Authors: Jack Begley, Timothy Kaftan, Helen Song, Tolulope Fashina, Caleb D Hartley, Nam Nguyen, Ian Crozier, Jean-Claude Mwanza, Steven Yeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMpox (formerly known as monkeypox), an infectious disease caused by the monkeypox virus (MPXV), has been endemic in regions of Central and Western Africa. In 2022, the global spread of the clade IIb MPXV led to a multinational outbreak, primarily affecting sexual transmission networks among men who have sex with men. Despite interventions, new cases have continued to emerge. In Africa, the spread of a novel strain of clade I MPXV, clade Ib, has prompted a Public Health Emergency of International Concern designation by the World Health Organization in August 2024. This article provides an updated overview of the epidemiology, systemic, and ocular manifestations, highlighting the clinical features, diagnostic testing, and implications relevant to ophthalmologists and eye care providers, including infection prevention and control measures. The ocular manifestations of mpox primarily involve the ocular surface and anterior segment, with presentations ranging from conjunctivitis to severe, vision-threatening keratitis and uveitis. While the 2022 to 2024 Clade IIb outbreak has shown a lower incidence of ocular involvement compared with previous outbreaks, the potential for significant visual morbidity remains. Treatment involves both systemic and topical therapies, with tecovirimat being the primary systemic option, though its efficacy and ophthalmic bioavailability remain under investigation. Ongoing surveillance and research are essential to further understand the epidemiology and ophthalmic features of mpox and, ultimately, to optimize prevention and treatment strategies for patients.Key Words: Mpox, monkeypox virus (MPXV), clade Ib, clade IIb, epidemiology, ocular manifestations, conjunctivitis, keratitis, infection prevention and control, clade 1a

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY UPDATE

Mpox (formerly termed monkeypox) is an infectious disease caused by the monkeypox virus (MPXV), a double-stranded DNA orthopoxvirus. Mpox (caused by clade I and clade II MPXV) has been endemic in Central and Western Africa, respectively, for decades. In 2022, the new global spread of clade IIb MPXV to nonendemic areas prompted urgent global attention and the declaration of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) by the World Health Organization (WHO; July 2022). In the context of this ongoing outbreak, the majority of cases were associated with sexual transmission networks in men who have sex with men. Public health interventions, including around behavioral change and vaccination, contributed to a decline in cases, leading to the lifting of the PHEIC by May 2023, though new cases continue to be reported.

However, long before the recent clade IIb mpox epidemic, increasing case numbers of clade I mpox have been reported in endemic areas over several decades, particularly in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). In part, the increase was thought to be related to the waning of cross-protective immunity afforded by smallpox vaccination.1,2 More recently, during and in the background of the clade IIb epidemic, a dramatic increase in the numbers of clade I mpox cases in DRC, including newly affected geographic areas, has occurred. Furthermore, sustained human-to-human (H2H) transmission of a newly identified clade Ib MPXV variant, including along sexual transmission networks, has recently been reported from South Kivu, DRC.3,4 The combination of the high burden of clade I mpox in DRC and the recent regional spread (to Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, and Uganda) prompted a new declaration of a global PHEIC in August 2024.

According to the WHO, 99,176 laboratory-confirmed mpox cases, 535 probable cases, and 208 deaths have been reported globally from January 1, 2022 to June 30, 2024.5 More recently, the largest proportion of cases has been detected in Africa, with 43.5% of new cases in May 2024 and 60.7% of new cases in June 2024 occurring in this region.5,6 In 2024, 99% of new mpox cases in Africa were reported in the DRC,5 where thus far, mpox is exclusively caused by clade I MPXV (associated with higher case fatality rates).3 Recent genomic surveillance in DRC suggests increases are driven both by zoonotic spillovers of clade Ia MPXV (in known endemic areas) and H2H transmission of novel clade Ib MPXV (in eastern DRC and new regional spread).7

SYSTEMIC MANIFESTATIONS AND RISK FACTORS FOR MPOX

After exposure to MPVX, the incubation period ranges from 1 to 21 days,8 although most patients develop symptoms or signs of disease within 7 to 14 days.9 Common presenting symptoms and signs include fever, lymphadenopathy, myalgias, fatigue, headache, and rash.8,9 Characteristic disseminated skin lesions classically begin on the face and spread centrifugally in a typical monomorphic progression (vesicles to pustules to umbilicated lesions to crusted scabs) over 2 to 4 weeks. However, in the context of the global clade IIb MPXV epidemic, atypical disease was frequently reported; over half of patients developed a rash before or at the same time as other symptoms and signs.8 In addition, initial skin or mucocutaneous lesions were commonly noted at likely sites of oral or anogenital initial exposure.8

Common complications of mpox include bacterial superinfections (skin and soft tissue infections, pneumonia, and sepsis), oral and conjunctival mucocutaneous lesions (leading to dysphagia/odynophagia and eye pain), severe lymphadenopathy (causing pain and oropharyngeal or respiratory compromise), and nausea/vomiting and abdominal pain. Compromised oral intake related to the above, in the setting of high insensible losses (fever, high burden of skin lesions), leads to volume depletion and/or severe dehydration. Although conjunctival involvement is common, severe ocular complications are infrequent. Particularly during the clade IIb global epidemic, anogenital complications (urethritis and proctitis associated with severe pain) have been described. Encephalitis and myocarditis are rarely described.8 Mpox caused by clade I MPXV infection is associated with higher case fatality ratios (1.4% to over 10%) compared with clade II MPXV infection (case fatality ratio: 0.1% to 3.6%).10 However, severe and fatal disease after clade IIb infection has been described in severely immunocompromised patients, most notably in untreated people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) with low CD4 counts.11 During the current clade I mpox outbreak in the DRC, 67% of cases and 78% of deaths have occurred among individuals aged 15 or younger.12

Risk factors for infection include close contact with infected humans or animals. Mouth-to-mouth, face-to-face, skin-to-skin, and mouth-to-skin contact can all result in transmission of mpox.8 People who have contact with clothing, bedding, towels, or other surfaces that have been contacted by a person with mpox are also at risk.8 In the recent 2022 to 2024 clade IIb epidemic, sexual transmission networks among men who have sex with men and sex workers have driven H2H transmission. In endemic areas in Africa, transmission has been presumed to be predominantly driven by repeated zoonotic spillovers (with some but nonsustained H2H transmission); however, sustained H2H associated with new clade Ib MPXV virus infections, including in sexual transmission networks, suggests an increasing role for H2H spread.4,7 Indeed, the high burden of cases in children younger than 15 years in the DRC suggests a complex interplay of zoonotic spillover and H2H transmission.13

OPHTHALMIC MANIFESTATIONS

In the complex disease background described previously, mpox-related ophthalmic disease is predominantly a disease of the ocular surface and anterior segment, although ocular presentations may be wide-ranging (Tables 1 and 2). A case series published recently by Finamor et al40 described the initial development of ophthalmic manifestations varying between 7 days and 47 days after the onset of skin lesions. These findings can be categorized into external and ocular manifestations. The external manifestations involve periorbital cutaneous lesions that closely resemble those found elsewhere on the body.41 The ocular manifestations include conjunctivitis, keratitis, and uveitis.41

Conjunctivitis is most commonly described, having been reported in ∼20% of cases in endemic areas during previous outbreaks, with the majority of cases affecting children.42 Both membranous43 and pseudomembranous44 conjunctivitis have been reported, often requiring repeated membrane removal. Historically, the presence of conjunctivitis has been associated with a more severe disease course.33 However, limited case series and reports during the 2022 to 2024 clade IIb epidemic have yet to identify a similar association.45–47

Mpox-related corneal disease can present with a variety of different clinical features. Geographic corneal ulcers,16,19,25,26,28,29,32,43 limbitis,16,23,28,30,31 serpiginous epithelial keratitis,22–27,48 peripheral ulcerative keratitis,18,20,21,26,29,38 stromal edema,19,26,31,38 keratic precipitates,16,17,22,24,25 and corneal perforation14–16 have all been reported during the 2022 to 2024 outbreak. Corneal involvement can also lead to bacterial superinfection, further increasing the risk for visual morbidity including corneal scarring, vision impairment, need for corneal transplant, and phthisis bulbi.17,49 Mpox-related uveitis can occur in the form of anterior uveitis,14,17,22,24–28 disciform keratitis with endothelial plaques,16,24 and scleritis.16,22,41

Nguyen et al16 reported a case of isolated ocular mpox in 2023, illustrating that skin lesions and systemic prodromal symptoms are not a prerequisite for the development of ocular disease. In their report, MPXV genomic material was isolated from the anterior chamber and detected through RNA-deep sequencing as part of an extensive workup for undifferentiated necrotizing anterior scleritis and keratitis. The patient was started on oral tecovirimat, topical trifluridine, and weekly ophthalmic betadine washes before being transitioned to intravenous (IV) tecovirimat and cidofovir 4 months later due to a worsening in vision-to-light perception only. Three weeks after the initiation of IV therapy, the ocular disease showed no improvement, and the eye remained reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (PCR) positive for MPXV with an opacified and vascularized cornea.

While the corneal manifestations of mpox can all lead to significant scarring and visual morbidity, the overall incidence rate of corneal involvement appears to be lower with clade II MPXV infections. Recent studies from France,23,37 Spain,26 and Mexico38 reported rates of keratitis of 0.34% to 1.0%, much lower than the 4% incidence of keratitis reported in 1987 during an MPXV clade I outbreak.34 However, a study from Nigeria published in 2023, which likely included primarily MPXV clade II cases, found a 6% incidence of keratitis.39,50

Ocular involvement appears to be less prevalent during the ongoing 2022 to 2024 clade IIb mpox outbreak.35,47 According to data from the WHO, as of June 30, 2024, conjunctivitis has only been reported in 0.6% of cases.5 Recent studies from Europe found rates of ocular involvement to be similarly low, at 1.0%,35 1.1%,36 and 0.8%.37 These cases were predominantly caused by clade II MPXV, suggesting a decreased rate of ocular involvement as compared with that caused by clade I MPXV infection, in which involvement at least of the conjunctiva was reported in ∼20% of cases from 2010 to 2014.33 The frequency of ocular involvement in mpox caused by newly identified MPXV clade Ib strains in the DRC is unknown and will require ongoing disease surveillance.

DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA

The diagnosis of mpox involves a combination of clinical characteristics, epidemiologic criteria, and laboratory testing. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has established case definitions to help categorize possible mpox infections.51 Epidemiologic criteria include any of the following within 21 days of illness onset: (1) having contact with a person with a similar appearing rash or who received a confirmed or probable diagnosis of mpox, (2) having intimate in-person contact with a social network experiencing mpox activity, (3) traveling outside the United States to a country with confirmed cases of mpox or where MPXV is endemic, or (4) having contact with an animal or product derived from an animal species from areas of African endemicity.

A “Suspect Case” is any patient with a new rash characteristic of mpox or someone who meets one of the epidemiologic criteria.51 A “Probable Case” is a patient with no suspicion of other recent Orthopoxvirus exposure (eg Vaccinia virus in ACAM2000 vaccinations) and demonstration of orthopoxvirus DNA by PCR, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or is positive for anti-Orthopoxvirus IgM within 4 to 56 days from rash onset.51 A “Confirmed Case” is a patient with demonstrated presence of MPXV DNA by PCR, next-generation sequencing, or culture from a clinical specimen.51

MONKEYPOX VIRUS DETECTION FROM OCULAR TISSUES

MPXV infection can be detected by direct molecular testing such as electron microscopy, sequencing, and PCR, as well as through indirect serologic antibody testing such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, immunofluorescence assay, and immunohistochemistry.52 Reverse-transcriptase PCR is the current gold standard for laboratory diagnosis of mpox due to its high sensitivity and specificity.52,53 The utility of serologic testing for the diagnosis of mpox is limited due to cross-reactivity between orthopoxviruses.52 This permits factors such as recent vaccination or infection with other orthopoxviruses to interfere with accurate testing for MPXV.52

PCR testing of conjunctival and corneal specimens can confirm the presence of MPXV. Finamor and colleagues reported a duration of ocular MPXV PCR positivity ranging from 19 to 145 days, and Raccagni et al32 isolated MPXV from a conjunctival sample 8 months after the onset of cutaneous lesions. These findings illustrate that MPXV can persist in ocular tissues long after the resolution of cutaneous manifestations.40 Among the 5 cases reported by Finamor et al,40 a cycle threshold cutoff value of 33.2 was identified for viral isolation. Positive PCR testing for MPXV occurred as early as 4 days after the onset of ocular symptoms.40 In addition to the conjunctiva and corneal surface, MPXV was detected in the aqueous humor in 1 of the 2 patients that were sampled 44 days after initial onset of disease.40 Routine ocular sampling for MPXV in African settings has so far not been reported.

CURRENT AND EMERGING TREATMENT OPTIONS

Treatment for the ocular manifestations of mpox includes both systemic and topical therapies. According to the CDC, systemic therapy should be considered for all mpox patients with ocular involvement.54 Oral tecovirimat (TPOXX) is an inhibitor of the orthopoxvirus F13L gene and is approved by the European Medicines Agency and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of mpox, though only under an expanded access indication.55 The safety and efficacy of tecovirimat for mpox, however, is unknown, and clinical trials are ongoing. Tecovirimat is commonly administered orally in 600 mg doses twice per day for 2 weeks, although shorter courses of 5 to 7 days have proven to be effective in animal models.56 While efficacy data supporting tecovirimat for the treatment of mpox is pending, it should also be acknowledged that ophthalmic bioavailability has not been well-characterized.49

Cidofovir, a viral DNA polymerase inhibitor most commonly used in the treatment of cytomegalovirus retinitis, has been shown to have activity against MPXV in animal models.57 Of note, prolonged exposure to cidofovir during the treatment of mpox (or for any indication) can lead to drug-induced uveitis.41 Brincidofovir, a viral prodrug of cidofovir, is FDA-approved for the treatment of smallpox in adults and children and has also proven to be effective against orthopoxviruses in vitro and in animal studies.57 Brincidofovir can be used in combination with tecovirimat but should be avoided in patients taking cidofovir.

IV vaccinia immune globulin (VIGIV), an FDA-approved treatment for complications of smallpox vaccination, may be considered in severe cases of mpox where the development of robust immune response is impaired. However, according to the CDC, VIGIV has no proven benefit in mpox infection, and it is unknown whether a person infected with MPXV would benefit from treatment. In addition, concerns exist regarding a possible association between VIGIV and increased corneal scarring,58 although this has not been demonstrated in subsequent studies.49,59

Topical therapies have also been explored for ocular mpox treatment. Trifluridine, an inhibitor of thymidine phosphorylase most commonly used to treat herpes simplex virus keratitis, can be considered for use in cases of mpox-associated conjunctivitis and is recommended by the CDC for the treatment of mpox-associated keratitis.54 However, several adverse ocular effects of trifluridine itself have been described, including photophobia, corneal edema, punctate keratopathy, and keratitis sicca.60 In addition, prior evidence from ocular cowpox infections suggests that the coincident use of trifluridine and topical corticosteroids should be avoided.54 Topical antibiotics and lubricants are also recommended by the CDC in patients with mpox-associated keratitis to prevent bacterial superinfection.54 Topical interferon alfa-2b could also be considered as an alternative treatment option in resource-limited settings, but further investigation is needed.27

THE ROLE OF VACCINATION IN THE PREVENTION OF OCULAR COMPLICATIONS

While safety and efficacy data for any MPXV-specific vaccine is lacking, 2 vaccines originally developed to prevent smallpox infection have been recommended for prevention, JYNNEOS (Bavarian Nordic) and ACAM2000 (Emergent Product Development Gaithersburg Inc.)61 JYNNEOS, an attenuated, nonreplicating, live virus vaccine administered subcutaneously in 2 doses, has been preferentially recommended for the current mpox outbreak, in part due to its less severe side effect profile.62 Historical data, while limited, suggests that vaccination may reduce the burden of ocular disease. For example, the rate of mpox-associated conjunctivitis in the Congo Basin from 1981 to 1986 was 30% in unvaccinated persons versus 7% in vaccinated persons.49,63 However, other studies found no significant difference in the rates of mpox-associated keratitis between the two groups.34 Further investigation is warranted to determine the relationship between vaccination status and a potential reduction in the incidence of the ocular manifestations of mpox.

INFECTION PREVENTION AND CONTROL PRECAUTIONS

Transmission of mpox can occur through contact with infected wild animals, humans, or other contaminated materials. Both MPXV clade I and clade II can spread through direct H2H contact, but clade I MPXV has not been associated with sexual transmission until recently in the DRC in 2023.3 Conjunctival viral shedding has been reported in patients with mpox-associated conjunctivitis, but it is unclear whether conjunctival viral shedding occurs in patients without conjunctivitis.49,64

For patients with mpox, the CDC recommends frequent handwashing and avoidance of eye rubbing to avoid autoinoculation of the eye. Prophylactic treatment with topical trifluridine can be considered for those at higher risk of autoinoculation, such as children under the age of 8.54 However, adverse effects of topical trifluridine can include punctate keratopathy, keratitis sicca, corneal edema, and photophobia,60 so the risks and benefits should be considered before initiation.

The CDC recommends that patients with suspected or confirmed mpox infection be placed in a single-person room. Special air handling precautions are not required unless procedures that may spread aerosolized secretions are being performed (eg, intubation and extubation). In these situations, the patient should be transferred to an airborne infection isolation room.65 The recommended personal protective equipment for health care personnel includes a gown, gloves, eye protection, and a particulate respirator equipped with N95-rated filters or higher.65

Individuals with confirmed mpox infection should maintain isolation precautions until all lesions have crusted, separated, and formed a fresh layer of skin underneath.65 Health care personnel who have been exposed to MPXV do not need to be excluded from work but should be monitored daily for signs and symptoms of mpox for 21 days.65 The Eye Bank Association of America currently recommends the exclusion of potential ocular tissue donors who, in the last 21 days before death, developed a new rash characteristic of mpox, had close contact with an individual with confirmed mpox, tested positive for orthopoxvirus infection in the absence of other known etiology or tested positive for mpox.66

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Mpox-related ocular disease, including conjunctivitis, keratitis, and vision loss, has been long described but not well-characterized in the setting of endemic mpox in Africa. Emerging and more highly resolved clinical characterization in case reports/series from the ongoing clade IIb mpox global epidemic describe predominantly ocular surface and anterior segment disease, though at lower prevalence than that described historically in endemic mpox. Nonetheless, vision-threatening complications have been similarly described. Moreover, the overall increasing burden and the recent surge in clade I mpox cases in DRC, including the new clade Ib strain associated with sustained H2H transmission, has prompted a new declaration of a PHEIC and coincident concern for vision-threatening complications. Current management of mpox-related ocular disease often involves a combination of systemic and topical therapies, but further investigation of these therapies is warranted. Infection prevention and control measures remain critical in limiting the spread of the disease in health care settings. Continued surveillance and research are essential for monitoring the evolving epidemiology of mpox, characterizing ophthalmic disease and its complications, and optimizing the prevention and treatment of both systemic and ophthalmic manifestations.
