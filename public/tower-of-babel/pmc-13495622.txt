Multiplex PCR–supported identification of Taenia saginata following proglottid passage in a long-term expatriate resident of Bali, Indonesia: A case report

Introduction

Human taeniasis is a zoonotic cestode infection that may go unrecognized because intestinal infection is often clinically silent. Three human-infecting Taenia species have been documented in Indonesia: T. asiatica in North Sumatra [1], T. saginata and T. solium in Bali [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], and T. solium in Papua [2]. Reported prevalence varies substantially across Balinese communities and study periods [3], [4], [6].

Taenia saginata is transmitted through ingestion of raw or undercooked beef containing viable cysticerci. Accurate species identification is epidemiologically important and clinically relevant in settings where T. solium also occurs, because Taenia eggs are morphologically indistinguishable [7], [8].

Passage of motile proglottids is characteristic of T. saginata infection and frequently prompts healthcare-seeking. Otherwise, infection may remain clinically silent or cause nonspecific manifestations such as abdominal discomfort, nausea, appetite or weight changes, diarrhea, flatulence, or perianal pruritus [7], [8].

Taeniasis has been reported in Bali predominantly among local communities. We describe PCR-supported T. saginata infection in a long-term expatriate resident with plausible exposure in Bali. A targeted literature search did not identify a previous report specifically describing molecular identification of T. saginata in an expatriate resident of Bali; however, publication and indexing gaps cannot be excluded. This report was prepared in accordance with the CARE (Case REport) guidelines.

Presentation of case

A 39-year-old Russian man who had resided in Bali, Indonesia, for approximately 5.5 years presented to an outpatient clinic after passing whitish, motile, ribbon-like material in his stool two days earlier. He was otherwise well and denied abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, or other gastrointestinal symptoms. He had no history of parasitic infection or chronic illness. He reported no travel outside Bali during the preceding 12 months, including to other helminth-endemic regions. He regularly consumed locally sourced beef and occasionally ate undercooked beef, providing a plausible exposure to T. saginata. No relevant family history or occupational exposure was identified.

On examination, the patient appeared well-nourished, was afebrile, and had normal vital signs. His abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no organomegaly or palpable masses. No perianal irritation or visible proglottids were present. The patient brought the expelled specimen, which he had retrieved from his stool, for examination (Fig. 1).

Gross appearance of the expelled tapeworm specimen: a whitish, flattened, ribbon-like portion of strobila composed of contiguous proglottids and measuring approximately 58 cm × 2 cm.

Gross examination showed a whitish, flattened, ribbon-like structure measuring approximately 58 cm in length and 2 cm in width. It comprised multiple contiguous proglottids and was reportedly motile when first observed. The specimen represented only part of the strobila, not an intact adult worm; no scolex was identified.

Laboratory testing showed a leukocyte count of 7620/mm³ (reference range, 4000–11,000/mm³), hemoglobin of 13.9 g/dL (13.0–17.0 g/dL), and platelet count of 193,000/mm³ (150,000–450,000/mm³). The relative eosinophil count was 4% (0–3%), corresponding to an absolute count of approximately 305 cells/mm³ ; the patient reported no allergic disease or recent allergic symptoms. Creatinine was 1.05 mg/dL (0.7–1.3 mg/dL), blood urea nitrogen was slightly elevated at 21.4 mg/dL (7–20 mg/dL), and total protein was 6.9 g/dL (6.0–8.3 g/dL). Transaminases, lipid profile, and fasting glucose were within their respective reference ranges.

Normal-saline wet-mount microscopy of stool demonstrated spherical Taenia-type eggs with a thick, radially striated embryophore. No other ova or parasites were identified. Molecular analysis was performed for species identification (Fig. 2).

Normal-saline wet mount of stool showing a spherical Taenia spp. egg, approximately 30–40 µm in diameter, with a thick, radially striated embryophore (original magnification, ×400). Individual oncospheral hooklets are not clearly resolved in this image.

Detection of Taenia species was performed using a multiplex PCR targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene. DNA was extracted from the expelled proglottids using the DNeasy Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and from fecal samples using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen). The primer sequences were Tsol F, 5′-TGTTTTCTTTGCATTTAGCTGGTGTATCA−3′; Tasi F, 5′-TTGGTTGGTGGGTTATCTGATTTAAACTTG−3′; Tsag F, 5′-TAATTGGGTTAAAATTGTGTGTACTGTGGG−3′; Taenia R, 5′-TCTCCCCAAACACACTATTGAAAACATAGC−3′; and Tsag R, 5′-TTTAAATTAAACTAAAAAACCACGGGCAGG−3′. Reactions were prepared with GoTaq (Promega) in a total volume of 20 µL. Cycling comprised 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel. Expected amplicon sizes were 414 bp for T. solium, 283 bp for T. saginata, and 594 bp for T. asiatica (Fig. 3). Amplicon sequencing and an internal amplification control were not performed.

Agarose gel electrophoresis of multiplex PCR products targeting mitochondrial cox1. M, 100-bp DNA ladder; lane 1, T. saginata positive control (283 bp); lane 2, T. asiatica positive control (594 bp); lane 3, T. solium positive control (414 bp); lane 4, distilled-water negative control; lane 5, DNA from the patient’s expelled proglottids, showing a strong T. saginata-sized amplicon; lane 6, DNA from the pretreatment fecal sample, showing a weaker amplicon at the same position; and lane 7, post-treatment fecal sample, interpreted as negative for a T. saginata-specific amplicon.

Following PCR-supported identification of T. saginata, the patient received a single 2-g oral dose of niclosamide, followed two hours later by 30 g of oral magnesium sulfate as a purgative to facilitate expulsion. The next day, he experienced transient diarrhea and mild abdominal discomfort. Diarrhea was considered most likely related to the osmotic effect of magnesium sulfate, although the cause of the abdominal discomfort could not be determined. Both symptoms resolved gradually with supportive care and hydration. At the one-month assessment, stool microscopy and multiplex PCR were negative, and the patient reported no further proglottid passage (Fig. 3). At telephone follow-up 10 months after treatment, he remained free of gastrointestinal symptoms and reported no further passage of proglottids or other helminth material. This course was consistent with a sustained clinical response but did not establish complete parasitological cure because serial post-treatment stool examinations were not performed.

Discussion

Previous epidemiological investigations have established that T. saginata remains endemic in parts of Bali, but they have primarily described local communities [3], [4], [6], [9]. A targeted literature search did not identify a published report specifically describing molecular identification of T. saginata in an expatriate resident of Bali. Because publication or indexing gaps cannot be excluded, this case should not be considered definitively the first. Its relevance lies instead in showing that long-term foreign residents may acquire taeniasis locally and may present only after noticing proglottids.

The patient had no gastrointestinal symptoms and sought care only after passing motile, whitish proglottids. This presentation is typical of clinically silent intestinal T. saginata infection: proglottid passage may be the first perceptible event, whereas other patients report nonspecific abdominal discomfort, nausea, appetite changes, diarrhea, flatulence, or perianal pruritus [7], [8].

His 5.5-year residence in Bali, absence of travel outside Bali during the preceding year, consumption of locally sourced beef, and occasional consumption of undercooked beef support possible local acquisition. Nevertheless, neither the precise source nor the timing of infection could be established.

The relative eosinophil count was slightly elevated at 4%, but the absolute count was approximately 305 cells/mm³ and therefore not elevated by commonly used absolute-count thresholds. Although the patient reported no allergic disease and stool microscopy identified no parasites other than Taenia, this minor relative elevation cannot be attributed confidently to taeniasis. Blood urea nitrogen was marginally elevated at 21.4 mg/dL, with normal creatinine and no clinical evidence of renal dysfunction; this isolated finding was considered clinically insignificant.

Adult T. saginata may reach several meters in length; therefore, the 58-cm specimen was only a portion of the strobila rather than an intact worm. Because no scolex was found, complete spontaneous expulsion could not be confirmed. A retained scolex can remain attached to the intestinal mucosa, regenerate the strobila, and resume proglottid shedding, underscoring the need for an effective cestocidal treatment and post-treatment follow-up [7].

Microscopy can establish Taenia infection but cannot distinguish T. saginata, T. solium, and T. asiatica because their eggs are morphologically similar. Molecular testing is therefore useful for species-level identification in Indonesia, where these species have overlapping or neighboring distributions. The multiplex PCR in this case used mitochondrial cox1 targets and species-specific positive controls. Contemporary reports continue to demonstrate the value of mitochondrial amplification, with sequencing where available, for human Taenia identification [1], [10].

The proglottid-derived DNA produced a stronger T. saginata-sized band than the pretreatment fecal DNA. This may reflect a higher concentration of parasite DNA in proglottid tissue; fecal amplification can also be affected by heterogeneous parasite material, sample volume and storage, extraction efficiency, and PCR inhibitors [11]. The QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit was used for fecal extraction, but no internal amplification control was included to quantify inhibition. Accordingly, the band-intensity difference in this single case should not be interpreted as proof that proglottid-based PCR is diagnostically more sensitive than stool-based PCR. In addition, the amplicon was not sequenced, and the effect of potential primer-site polymorphism in local Balinese isolates was not assessed. Species assignment therefore rests on the expected amplicon size relative to species-specific positive controls rather than sequence confirmation. PCR-based methods nevertheless complement morphology when proglottids are fragmented or expertise is limited [12].

The history of undercooked-beef consumption provides a biologically plausible route of infection. The case also has practical implications for travel and expatriate medicine: clinicians should consider taeniasis in visitors and long-term foreign residents of endemic settings who report proglottid passage or undercooked-beef exposure, even when gastrointestinal symptoms are absent. At the public-health level, persistent transmission reinforces the importance of meat inspection, safe food preparation, sanitation, and accessible diagnostic services. PCR adds species-level resolution but requires laboratory infrastructure and is less accessible and more costly than microscopy; a pragmatic local approach is to use microscopy for detection and reserve molecular testing for speciation when epidemiologically or clinically important.

A single 2-g oral dose of niclosamide is an accepted treatment for adult taeniasis [13]. Follow-up was particularly important because the scolex was not identified in the recovered specimen. The absence of further proglottid passage and negative microscopy at one month supported a clinical response, but did not by themselves prove parasitological cure. Patient education included thorough cooking of beef, avoidance of raw or undercooked beef, hand hygiene, and sanitation.

Magnesium sulfate was administered after niclosamide to accelerate intestinal evacuation and facilitate expulsion of parasite material. However, a purgative is not routinely required by current standard guidance for taeniasis. Its osmotic effect can cause diarrhea, abdominal cramping, and fluid loss; therefore, the patient’s transient diarrhea was most plausibly related to magnesium sulfate, although contributions from niclosamide or other factors could not be excluded.

The absence of further proglottid passage during 10 months of follow-up was consistent with a sustained clinical response. Nevertheless, symptom history alone cannot exclude persistent infection, and a single negative stool examination provides weaker evidence than serial post-treatment examinations. Repeated stool microscopy over follow-up would have provided stronger evidence of complete parasitological cure, particularly because the scolex was not recovered.

Although T. saginata infection is usually clinically silent or mild, rare complications include appendicitis, intestinal obstruction or perforation, and biliary involvement [14], [15], [16]. These uncommon outcomes reinforce the value of diagnosis and treatment even in patients without substantial gastrointestinal symptoms.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that T. saginata infection may be clinically silent except for proglottid passage. Multiplex PCR supported species-level identification beyond the limitations of egg morphology. Treatment with niclosamide was followed by a sustained clinical response, although complete parasitological cure could not be confirmed because serial stool examinations were not performed.
