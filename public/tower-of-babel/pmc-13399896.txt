Refractory ventricular tachycardia requiring radiofrequency ablation following surgical closure of ventricular septal defect: A rare occurrence
Authors: Sayantika Saha, Satarupa Mukherjee, Subhendu Mandal, Kuntal Roychowdhuri, Rakesh Sarkar, Manoj Kumar Daga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArrhythmia is known complication in postoperative period of congenital heart disease among which ventricular tachycardia is one of the major causes of morbidity and mortality. Here we present an unique case of eleven month old baby managed by radiofrequency ablation for refractory ventricular tachycardia in postoperative VSD closure in view of doubly committed VSD with outlet extension due to refractory hypotension and non response to medical therapy and cardioversion. Although VT is mostly managed by medical treatment conservatively in postoperative period, this patient didn’t respond to either medical treatment or cardioversion requiring RFA as bailout strategy. Radiofrequency ablation can be adopted as one of the bailout strategy for the postoperative VT storm even in pediatric population.

INTRODUCTION

Postoperative arrhythmias are a significant contributor to mortality after cardiac surgery, accounting for approximately 9.1% of deaths, with a reported prevalence of 7.3% to 48%.[1] These rhythm disturbances are observed more frequently following intracardiac repair of tetralogy of Fallot, atrioventricular canal repair, arterial switch procedures, and congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries. Although the incidence of ventricular arrhythmia is reportedly low among the postoperative arrhythmia, especially after ventricular septal defect (VSD) repair, monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) may occur as a late presentation requiring medical or catheter ablation therapy. In this report, we describe an 11-month-old infant who developed refractory (on drug) monomorphic VT following VSD repair. The arrhythmia was ultimately treated successfully with catheter ablation as a bailout strategy.

CASE REPORT

An 11-month-old infant diagnosed with a doubly committed 11-mm VSD with perimembranous extension and severe pulmonary hypertension was planned for surgical repair. The VSD margins were difficult to delineate and required both transatrial and transpulmonary exposure. Additional traction on the tricuspid valve and surrounding tissues was required to achieve complete closure of the VSD. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and was weaned smoothly from cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). The CPB time was 110 min with adequate myocardial protection, and the aortic cross-clamp time was 75 min.

Immediately after weaning from CPB, atrioventricular dissociation with a junctional rhythm with a QRS complex of right bundle branch block (RBBB) morphology and normal axis was noted. The child required minimal inotropic support postoperatively. The transesophageal echocardiography is not done regularly in our unit in postoperative patients. However, the bedside two-dimensional echocardiography revealed good biventricular function with ejection fraction around 40%–45% (although not quantified properly in view of VT) without any residual defect or any clot, vegetation or pleural or pericardial effusion. There was no mitral or tricuspid regurgitation. Approximately 6 h later, she developed a wide-QRS tachycardia [Figure 1] (RBBB with extreme right axis) associated with profound hypotension. Synchronized DC cardioversion was performed with a 15-J shock, resulting in transient rhythm control; however, the tachycardia recurred shortly thereafter. All the electrolytes were checked and corrected as per the protocol.

12-lead electrocardiogram showing ventricular tachycardia

Intravenous amiodarone, injection lignocaine, and esmolol infusion were administered sequentially without any significant change in rhythm or heart rate. Notably, the arrhythmia was incessant, and the infant’s hemodynamic status continued to deteriorate. She was transferred to the catheterization laboratory (on postoperative day 1) after 16 h of since the appearance of VT for electrophysiological (EP) study with provision for radiofrequency ablation (RFA).

The electrophysiological study revealed that ongoing wide QRS tachycardia was VT as evidence of V-A dissociation and wide QRS tachycardia with negative HV interval.Subsequently 3D electro anatomical mapping (EAM) was performed under Ensite X system (NAVX-Abbott) using single quadripolar catheter (mapping). A 5 Fr(French) nonirrigation tip ablation catheter, small curve[CORDIS] was placed in the left ventricular cavity to map the VT [Figure 2]. Afterward, the same catheter was used to ablate the fascicular VT. RFA was performed at the left posterior fascicle, where a sharp near-field electrogram preceded the onset of the QRS complex by 30 ms [Figure 3]. The rhythm converted to sinus during ablation, but reverted to VT within 2 h of shifting to the pediatric intensive care unit.

The 3D electro anatomical mapping(EAM) of VT [LAT-Local Activation Timing Map] showing the earliest point of activation marked as white area in the posteroseptal part of LV cavity

During ablation of the ongoing VT signal acquired from distal electrode [marked as His1,2] of ablation catheter shows prepotential preceding 28msec from the onset of QRS terminating the tachycardia [cycle length-240 msec] after 12 beats followed by appearance of slow ventricular rhythm [approx. 500msec cycle length] which subsequently converted into sinus rhythm after 5beats

The patient was taken back to the catheterization laboratory again, and further ablation was performed over a wider area surrounding the previous site (around the left posterior fascicle). After the second ablation, VT was noninducible, and the patient’s hemodynamics improved significantly. The ejection fraction following ablation was around 35%–40%. She was extubated on postoperative day 6 and discharged on postoperative day 13 in sinus rhythm without any neurological deficit.

At a 6-month follow-up, the patient remained in sinus rhythm with good biventricular function.

DISCUSSION

Tachyarrhythmia is one of the most important causes of mortality following surgical correction of congenital heart defects. The incidence of arrhythmias following VSD surgical closure is relatively low. Houyel et al. reported RBBB and complete atrioventricular block following VSD repair, but did not find any association with ventricular arrhythmia.[2] Doshi et al. reported focal VT in an adult 24-year-old patient who underwent surgical repair for truncus arteriosus type 1 in infancy presented with monomorphic premature ventricular contraction, which was located and managed with EP study and RFA.[3]

VT following cardiac surgery is a significant cause of mortality. Fuchs et al. reported that postoperative ventricular arrhythmias are associated with increased mortality in patients undergoing surgery for congenital heart disease.[4] In another study by Ganea et al., it was reported that arrhythmia following congenital cardiac surgery is multifactorial, and it is early identification can reduce mortality and morbidity.[5]

Polymorphic VT in the postoperative period is often related to electrolyte imbalance and a high catecholaminergic state, whereas monomorphic VT is usually attributable to re-entry mechanism involving scarred myocardial tissue. Monomorphic VT is rare immediately after VSD surgical closure, as scar formation typically takes time. However, in our patient, the possible mechanism of VT is postulated to be related to automatism or trigger activity of the conduction system (left bundle fascicle), which is presumably altered with regards to EP property in the context of patch repair in the vicinity of the said His Purkinje system.

Intravenous antiarrhythmic drugs such as amiodarone, procainamide, and lignocaine can be effective in managing monomorphic VT in hemodynamically stable patients. In this given case, the VT was refractory to medical therapy. Therefore, RFA guided by 3D mapping was considered a bailout strategy for restoring hemodynamic stability.

Tachyarrhythmia may impair diastolic filling and reduce cardiac output, leading to myocardial ischemia and hypotension or shock. Costello et al. reported a case of VT in a neonate following arterial switch surgery, which was terminated by RFA after 7 weeks of the surgery.[6] Kerr et al. demonstrated that early postoperative EP studies and catheter ablation reduce morbidity and mortality in patients with congenital heart disease who develop arrhythmias within 12 months of surgery.[7]

In our patient, antiarrhythmic therapy was followed by radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) in view of drug-refractory VT. The breakthrough of treatment in this particular case lies in taking a quick decision to perform EP study and RFCA in such a young child, considering the limitation of hardware issues, periprocedural complications like valvular injury and tamponade, etc. While RFCA has been reported in patients developing VT years after VSD closure due to surgical scarring, its use in the immediate postoperative period following VSD repair is rare. In this case, early bailout RFCA proved to be lifesaving.

CONCLUSION

This is likely one of the rare case reports in pediatric cardiology and cardiac surgery describing successful RFCA in an 11-month-old infant immediately following VSD closure, with excellent outcomes. 3D mapping-assisted catheter ablation can significantly improve survival in patients with drug-resistant postoperative tachyarrhythmias.
