Predictive power of parametric temperature based topological indices with applications in structure property modeling of anti tuberculosis drugs
Authors: Sakander Hayat, Seham J. F. Alanazi, Melaku Berhe Belay, Shaohui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis remains a serious global health issue, with increasing resistance to existing drugs creating an urgent need for new treatment strategies. This study applies cheminformatics and quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) modeling to support anti-tuberculosis drug discovery. Specifically, it focuses on two parametric temperature-based topological indices, which are graph-theoretic molecular descriptors derived from chemical structure. These indices are evaluated through discrete optimization, statistical and computational analysis to determine their predictive accuracy for key physicochemical properties such as enthalpy of formation and boiling point. The study uses 22 benzenoid hydrocarbons as test cases to assess the performance of the proposed indices across various parameter values. Optimal parameter values are identified for which the indices show the strongest correlation with experimental data. The optimized descriptors are then applied to model physicochemical properties of 13 commonly used anti-tuberculosis drugs. High correlation coefficients demonstrate the strong predictive power of the indices in estimating molecular behavior. The results suggest that these temperature-based descriptors can serve as effective tools for QSPR modeling and drug development, offering a bridge between molecular topology and practical pharmaceutical applications.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), an airborne infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily affects the lungs but has the potential to spread throughout the body. It is estimated that nearly one-third of the world’s population has been infected with the TB bacterium1. While the majority of these infections are latent and asymptomatic, a subset of individuals—particularly those with weakened immune systems, such as HIV-positive patients—may develop active TB disease2. Men are statistically more likely to contract active TB than women, as reported in various studies. Although TB is treatable, there remains no vaccine that reliably prevents the disease in adults3. Without the development of more effective vaccines, the complete eradication of tuberculosis remains unlikely4. In response, the pharmaceutical sector is intensifying efforts to enhance drug development methods. One key approach involves using molecular informatics and Quantitative Structure–Property Relationship (QSPR) models to address emerging infections and treat novel syndromes or genetically driven disorders.

Molecular informatics is an interdisciplinary field that leverages computational and data-driven techniques to analyze and predict the properties and behavior of chemical compounds. Central to this field are QSAR (Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship) and QSPR (Quantitative Structure–Property Relationship) models5, which establish mathematical relationships between molecular structures and their biological activities or physicochemical properties. A crucial component of QSAR/QSPR modeling is the use of molecular6 descriptors—quantitative representations of molecular characteristics7 such as topology, geometry, electronic distribution, and hydrophobicity. These descriptors serve as inputs to statistical or machine learning models, enabling the prediction of compound behavior and guiding drug design8, materials discovery9, and environmental risk assessment. For graph-theoretic applications in computer science and allied areas, we refer the reader to10–13.

A widely employed approach for assessing the performance of molecular graph-based descriptors involves comparative testing, which utilizes selected reference molecules in conjunction with their known chemical attributes. For example, Gutman and Tošović14 investigated the predictive capacity of degree-related descriptors in modeling the physicochemical behavior of octane. Building upon this, Malik et al.15 extended the analysis to a broader class of compounds, namely benzenoid hydrocarbons (BHs). Subsequently, Hayat et al.16,17 further advanced this research by examining how these descriptors relate to the quantum mechanical and thermodynamic characteristics of BHs. More recently, the scope of this research has expanded to encompass temperature-reliant molecular descriptors, enabling the prediction of thermodynamic18, quantum-theoretical19, and physicochemical20 features of benzenoid systems.

To evaluate the effectiveness of a specific category of molecular graph-based descriptors, it is standard practice to perform comparative analyses using an appropriate selection of test molecules and their associated physicochemical properties. In a foundational study, Gutman and Tošović14 assessed the predictive capability of degree-based molecular descriptors by examining their correlation with the physicochemical characteristics of isomeric octanes, a representative class of alkanes. Materials and methods

A benzenoid system, which is a connected planar graph without cut vertices, can naturally represent any benzenoid hydrocarbon. Each interior region is surrounded by a regular hexagon having sides of one unit in length. Benzenoid hydrocarbons are a class of aromatic compounds that contain one or more benzene rings in their structure. They can be classified into saturated (e.g., cyclohexyl derivatives), partially saturated (e.g., dihydronaphthalene), and fully aromatic types (e.g., benzene, naphthalene, anthracene), depending on the degree of hydrogenation of the rings. Fully aromatic benzenoids exhibit delocalized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}-electron systems and follow Hückel’s rule, which imparts significant stability and unique chemical reactivity.

The term inlets in a benzenoid system, as outlined in Ref.27, are relevant to this study. Consider a benzenoid system E having p hexagons and v vertices. For a path \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_1 - p_2 - \cdots - p_{\ell +1}$$\end{document} of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(2\le \ell \in \mathbb {N})$$\end{document} inside E, the corresponding degree sequence is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(d_{p_1}, d_{p_2}, \ldots , d_{p_{\ell +1}})$$\end{document}. Based on this sequence, different types of inlets are identified: a fjord follows the degree sequence (2,3,3,3,3,2), cove has (2,3,3,3,2), bay has (2,3,3,2), and fissure follows (2,3,2). Results and discussion

A high level of predictive accuracy for the boiling point and enthalpy of formation of lower benzenoid hydrocarbons (BHs) is achieved by employing the first parametric temperature index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^1_{\alpha }$$\end{document} and the second parametric temperature index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^2_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, evaluated over a range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} values.

To begin with, the analytical determination of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^1_{\alpha }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^2_{\alpha }$$\end{document} for the 22 benzenoid hydrocarbons (BHs) listed in Tables 1 and 2 is carried out using the procedure outlined in “Materials and methods” section. Specifically, the formulae presented in (5) and (6) are employed to compute the exact values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^1_{\alpha }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T^2_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, respectively. Comparison with traditional topological descriptors

Table 4 summarizes the performance of different classes of topological descriptors in predicting physicochemical properties of benzenoid hydrocarbons. Optimized parametric temperature indices in QSPR modeling of anti-tuberculosis drugs

Though TB is generally treated using antibiotic regimens, certain cases, especially those involving drug-resistant strains or severe pulmonary complications, may necessitate surgical intervention1. TB continues to be one of the leading causes of death from infectious diseases worldwide30. Since the 2000 Cape Town Declaration, there has been a significant increase in research and development of anti-tuberculosis therapies, driven by the goal of producing less toxic, more effective treatments with shorter durations31. Commonly prescribed TB medications include amikacin, bedaquiline, clofazimine, imipenem-cilastatin, linezolid, moxifloxacin, terizidone, ethambutol, ethionamide, isoniazid, levofloxacin, p-aminosalicylic acid, and pyrazinamide32.
