Exact, non-singular black holes from a phantom DBI Field as primordial dark matter
Tausif Parvez, S. Shankaranarayanan

Abstract
We present the first exact, non-singular black hole solution in General Relativity sourced by a Dirac-Born-Infeld (DBI) scalar field. Crucially, the solution is exclusively supported by the phantom branch of the DBI action , dynamically replacing the central singularity with a regular core. The solution is asymptotically flat, possesses non-trivial scalar hair, and replaces the central singularity with a regular 2-sphere. The mechanism for singularity resolution is a dynamical kinetic stiffness — analogous to shear thickening in non-Newtonian fluids — which also explains the evasion of classical no-hair theorems. We show these black holes evaporate to a stable, non-singular, extremal Planck-scale relic. This provides a robust mechanism to evade standard evaporation constraints, opening a vast, previously forbidden mass window for light Primordial Black Holes to constitute dark matter. The model is testable via distinctive gravitational-wave signatures from its scalar hair.

It is widely understood that resolving these singularities requires a repulsive component to counteract the standard attractive nature of gravity at high curvatures

;

This leads to a crucial question: Is the requirement of exotic matter an unavoidable neccessity for singularity resolution, or does the issue lie deeper?
We argue that the fundamental limitation lies in two related aspects of standard matter formulations. First,
the standard formulation of matter itself, where actions are built from “canonical” Lagrangians that depend

From this perspective, the central singularity is a pathology of pushing an ineffective, low-energy theory — the canonical kinetic term — beyond its regime of validity. To show this explicitly, we consider a scalar field

The canonical kinetic term is merely the leading-order operator, valid only when field gradients are small,

Simon 1990

The central singularity of a BH can therefore be viewed as a direct consequence of extrapolating a low-energy effective theory (the canonical scalar) into a regime where its basic assumptions are violated. To understand the fate of gravitational collapse, one must specify the UV-completion of the scalar field theory. A guiding principle for such modifications comes from Born-Infeld non-linear electrodynamics, which was introduced to resolve the infinite self-energy of the point electron

We therefore take the logical step of applying this well-motivated UV-completion to the strong-gravity environment of a BH interior, where controlling gradients is paramount.
However, our analysis reveals a second, more subtle limitation:

While fundamental phantom fields are problematic, their appearance as an

The resulting spacetime is asymptotically flat, evades the classical no-hair theorems by possessing non-trivial scalar hair, and features a regular 2-sphere of finite area at its core — providing a plausible resolution of the central singularity.

Conclusions and Implications:

The discovery of a stable, regular BH sourced by an effective phantom DBI field has important implications across multiple frontiers of physics.
First, our analysis demonstrates that
kinetic regularization
—where non-linear field self-interactions generate a repulsive force at high gradients — provides a robust, well-motivated principle for singularity resolution. This addresses the fundamental limitation of canonical field theories, which fail catastrophically in the large-strain regime of gravitational collapse. Furthermore, we have shown that singularity resolution is not guaranteed by an arbitrary non-linear completion;
it necessarily requires the specific structure of the phantom DBI action

(\epsilon=1)
. Unlike models requiring
ad hoc
exotic matter
Ayon-Beato and Garcia 1998
or speculative quantum gravity

Appendix E
Energy Condition: Sign of
\mathbf{T^{t}_{t}}

A critical check for any classical solution is its adherence to fundamental energy conditions. The Weak Energy Condition (WEC) states that the energy density
\rho
must be non-negative for any timelike observer. For our static, spherically symmetric spacetime, the energy density measured by an observer at rest is
\rho=-T^{t}_{t}
. We therefore investigate the sign of

to determine where, if at all, the WEC is satisfied.

From the field equations (
23

T^{t}_{t}=\epsilon\Lambda^{4}\bigg(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{2X}{\Lambda^{4}}}\bigg)~.

(51)

Using

, this becomes:

T^{t}_{t}=\epsilon\Lambda^{4}\bigg(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{f(r)(\phi^{\prime}(r))^{2}}{\Lambda^{4}}}\bigg)~.

(52)

From Eq. (
52
), we see that
T^{t}_{t}>0
(and thus
\rho<0
for
\epsilon=1
) whenever the term
f(r)(\phi^{\prime}(r))^{2}>0
. To determine the sign of this term, we use our solution for

from Eq. (
36

f(r)(\phi^{\prime}(r))^{2}=f(r)\left(\frac{2a^{2}f(r)}{\epsilon B\kappa^{2}(r^{2}+a^{2})}\right)^{2}=\frac{4a^{4}(f(r))^{3}}{\epsilon^{2}B^{2}\kappa^{4}(r^{2}+a^{2})^{2}}~.

(53)

This confirms that the sign of

is identical to the sign of
f(r)
. Therefore, the energy density

is negative wherever

is positive. Note that asymptotically

(and

vanishes at infinity. This leads to the following conclusions for the three mass regimes:

•

Horizon-less Object (

for all

In this section, we analyze the thermodynamic properties of the non-extremal black hole solution (

, where classical approximations are most secure. We derive the corrections to the standard Schwarzschild results by expanding our solution in powers of

First, we expand the metric function

from Eq. (
29

\tan^{-1}(x)=x-x^{3}/3+x^{5}/5-\dots

\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)=\frac{a}{r}-\frac{a^{3}}{3r^{3}}+\frac{a^{5}}{5r^{7}}-\mathcal{O}\left(a^{7}\right)~.

(54)

We substitute this into (

\displaystyle f(r)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{3GM}{a}\bigg[\frac{r}{a}-\frac{a^{2}+r^{2}}{a^{2}}\left(\frac{a}{r}-\frac{a^{3}}{3r^{3}}+\dots\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2GM}{r}+\frac{2a^{2}GM}{5r^{3}}+\mathcal{O}(a^{4})~.

(55)

This confirms that our solution is the Schwarzschild metric plus a small

correction term.

F.1
Horizon Radius

To calculate the Hawking temperature, we must first find the precise location of the event horizon
r_{h}
by solving
f(r_{h})=0
.

1-\frac{2GM}{r_{h}}+\frac{2GMa^{2}}{5r^{3}_{h}}\approx 0~.

(56)

We assume a perturbative ansatz

, where
\delta
is a small correction of order

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2GM}{2GM+\delta}+\frac{2GMa^{2}}{5(2GM+\delta)^{3}}\approx 0

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\delta}{2GM}\approx-\frac{a^{2}}{20G^{2}M^{2}}~~{\rm or}~~\delta=-\frac{a^{2}}{10GM}~.

(57)

The corrected horizon radius is therefore slightly smaller than the Schwarzschild radius:

r_{h}\approx 2GM-\frac{a^{2}}{10GM}~.

(58)

F.2
Hawking Temperature

The Hawking temperature is defined by the surface gravity
\kappa_{s}
at the horizon:

T_{H}=\frac{\kappa_{s}}{2\pi}=\frac{f^{\prime}(r_{h})}{4\pi}~.

(59)

First, we compute the derivative of the expanded metric function:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{d}{dr}\left(1-2GMr^{-1}+\frac{2GMa^{2}}{5}r^{-3}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{2GM}{r^{2}}-\frac{6GMa^{2}}{5r^{4}}~.

(61)

Now, we evaluate this at the corrected horizon

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(r_{h})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2GM}{(2GM-\frac{a^{2}}{10GM})^{2}}-\frac{6GMa^{2}}{5(2GM)^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2GM}+\frac{a^{2}}{20G^{3}M^{3}}-\frac{3a^{2}}{40G^{3}M^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2GM}\left(1-\frac{a^{2}}{20G^{2}M^{2}}\right)~.

(62)

The Hawking temperature is therefore:

\displaystyle T_{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}f^{\prime}(r_{h})=\frac{1}{8\pi GM}\left(1-\frac{a^{2}}{20(GM)^{2}}\right)

(63)

\displaystyle T_{H}

\displaystyle=T_{S}\left(1-\frac{a^{2}}{20(GM)^{2}}\right)~.

(64)

where
T_{S}=1/(8\pi GM)
is the standard Schwarzschild temperature. This shows our black hole is slightly cooler than a Schwarzschild black hole of the same mass.

The evaporation rate (Power,

(65)

where

is the Schwarzschild area.
The evaporation rate is then:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\propto A_{h}(T_{H})^{4}

\displaystyle\propto\bigg[A_{S}\left(1+\frac{3a^{2}}{20(GM)^{2}}\right)\bigg]\bigg[T^{4}_{S}\left(1-\frac{a^{2}}{20(GM)^{2}}\right)^{4}\bigg]

(66)

where

.

Appendix G
Phantom field from Non-linear sigma model

In modern cosmology and high-energy physics, complex scalar field Lagrangians, such as the Dirac-Born-Infeld (DBI) or k-essence models, are frequently employed
Armendariz-Picon et al. 1999

A common example of this is a Non-Linear Sigma Model (NLSM), which describes the dynamics of Goldstone bosons arising from spontaneous symmetry breaking
Callan et al. 1969
;
Weinberg 1979
. In these models, one can ‘‘integrate out” heavy or auxiliary fields to find the effective action for the remaining light, dynamical fields. This process can generate highly non-trivial kinetic structures
4
4

4

We thank Susobhan Mandal for making this point to us.
.

Here, we demonstrate how a simple two-field NLSM, with one dynamical field (
\phi

\chi

\phi
that is non-canonical and exhibits phantom behavior.

We begin with the starting Lagrangian for the two scalar fields
\phi
and
\chi
. The field
\phi
has a canonical kinetic term
X=-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi
, while
\chi
is an auxiliary field with no kinetic term:

\mathcal{L}(\phi,\chi)=\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)\chi^{a}+\chi^{b}

(67)

where
a
and
b
are arbitrary powers, and
\Lambda
is the energy scale of the theory.

Since
\chi
is non-dynamical, its equation of motion is a simple algebraic constraint. We find it by varying the Lagrangian with respect to
\chi
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\chi}=a\chi^{a-1}\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)+b\chi^{b-1}=0

(68)

We can solve this constraint equation for
\chi
in terms of
X
:

\displaystyle\chi^{a-b}

\displaystyle=-\frac{b}{a}\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)^{-1}

(69)

This gives the “on-shell” solution for the
\chi
field:

\chi(X)=\left[-\frac{b}{a}\right]^{\frac{1}{a-b}}\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{a-b}}

(70)

Now, we “integrate out”
\chi
by substituting this solution back into the original Lagrangian to obtain the effective Lagrangian for
\phi
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}(\phi)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)\chi^{a}+\chi^{b}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)\chi^{a}

(71)

Substituting the expression for
\chi^{a}=(\chi(X))^{a}
from Eq. (
70
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b-a}{b}\right)\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)\left(\left[-\frac{b}{a}\right]^{\frac{1}{a-b}}\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{a-b}}\right)^{a}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b-a}{b}\right)\left[-\frac{b}{a}\right]^{\frac{a}{a-b}}\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)^{1-\frac{a}{a-b}}

\displaystyle=C(a,b)\cdot\left(1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)^{-\frac{b}{a-b}}

(72)

where
C(a,b)
is a constant that depends only on
a
and
b
.

The Phantom Field Case

Choosing
b=-a
we get:

-\frac{b}{a-b}=\frac{1}{2};\quad C(a,-a)=2

(73)

Therefore, the effective Lagrangian for
\phi
is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}=2\sqrt{1+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}}

(74)

Note that this non-canonical action was generated purely from the internal dynamics of the two-field model. Thus, our action Eq. (
2
) can be written as:

S=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\bigg[\frac{R}{2\kappa^{2}}+\epsilon\Lambda^{4}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}\right)\bigg]~,

(75)

The physical nature of this effective field is revealed in the low-energy limit, i.e., for small field gradients
X\ll\Lambda^{4}
. We can Taylor expand the Lagrangian using
\sqrt{1+z}\approx 1+z/2+\mathcal{O}(z^{2})
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}\approx 2\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}\right)=2+\frac{X}{\Lambda^{4}}

(76)

The constant ‘2’ acts as a cosmological constant and can be ignored. The kinetic part of the Lagrangian is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}}\approx+\frac{1}{\Lambda^{4}}X=-\frac{1}{2\Lambda^{4}}g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi

(77)

A standard canonical scalar field has a Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\text{canonical}}=X
. A field with a “wrong-sign” kinetic term,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}}\propto-X
, is known as a
phantom field

Arkani-Hamed et al. 2004
.

Thus, this simple NLSM model, upon integrating out the auxiliary field
\chi
, dynamically generates an effective theory for
\phi
that is both non-canonical and phantom-like.
