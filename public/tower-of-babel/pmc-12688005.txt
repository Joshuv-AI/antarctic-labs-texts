Comparison of nutritional status between adults with and without disabilities: a population-based cross-sectional study in South Korea
Authors: Jong Eun Park, Tae Hui Jang, So Young Kim, Jong Hyock Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESThere has been a notable lack of effort to evaluate the nutritional status of persons with disabilities objectively and identify their nutritional challenges. This study aimed to assess whether nutritional inadequacies and imbalances are more pronounced in persons with disabilities compared to those without, using data from the 6th Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2013).SUBJECTS/METHODSThe participants were classified into 387 persons with disabilities and 4,909 without disabilities. The 15 types of disabilities were categorized into 5 groups, and disability severity was classified as severe or mild. Nutrient intake and nutritional status were assessed using 24-h dietary recall data.RESULTSCompared to persons without disabilities, those with disabilities had significantly lower nutrient adequacy ratios for 8 of 10 nutrients, the exceptions being vitamins B1 and C (all P < 0.05). The likelihood of low overall nutrient adequacy based on the mean adequacy ratio was significantly higher among persons with disabilities (odds ratio [OR], 1.50; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.11–2.04), with particularly elevated odds for those with severe, visual, and internal organ disabilities. Persons with severe disabilities had higher odds of consuming a diet with low nutrient density (OR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.05–2.40) and of experiencing nutritional deficiency (OR, 2.21; 95% CI, 1.02–4.75) than those without disabilities. Additionally, the odds of consuming a high-carbohydrate diet, with > 70% of daily energy intake from carbohydrates, was 3.63-fold higher (95% CI, 1.40–9.42) among persons with mental disabilities.CONCLUSIONPersons with disabilities faced significant nutritional inadequacies and imbalances compared to those without disabilities. Targeted nutrition interventions and disability-inclusive nutrition policies and practices are urgently needed to reduce these disparities.

INTRODUCTION

An estimated 1.3 billion people, constituting 16% of the global population, currently live with a significant disability, a number that is steadily increasing due to the rising prevalence of noncommunicable diseases and the global trend of longer life expectancy [1]. The situation in South Korea reflects these global trends. As of the end of 2023, the number of registered persons with disabilities in South Korea was approximately 2.64 million, accounting for 5.1% of the total population—a substantial increase from 3.0% in 2003. Of these registered individuals, approximately 37.0% are classified as having severe disabilities. Additionally, among persons with disabilities, the proportion aged ≥ 65 yrs increased from 37.1% in 2010 to 53.9% in 2023 [2].

As a result, the growing size of the disabled population, along with the increasing proportion of persons with disabilities living with chronic and age-related conditions, is intensifying the demand for tailored health policies and services that can address their complex needs. However, many persons with disabilities continue to face significant health disparities and inequities [13]. For instance, persons with disabilities are twice as likely to develop conditions such as stroke, diabetes, depression, asthma, obesity, or poor oral health, and some die up to 20 yrs earlier than those without disabilities [1]. Overall, persons with disabilities fare significantly worse across a wide range of health indicators and social determinants of health than their nondisabled counterparts [3]. Furthermore, they are often marginalized from, or have limited access to, public health policies and interventions designed to address major risk factors such as tobacco use, poor diet, and alcohol consumption [45].

The Global Burden of Disease study has shown that a suboptimal diet is responsible for more deaths and disability-adjusted life years globally than any other risk factor, including tobacco smoking [6]. Despite this, current nutrition policies and dietary interventions rarely target persons with disabilities or involve them in the development process. Since 2012, the Korean government has implemented the Master Plan for National Nutrition Management every 5 yrs to enhance public nutrition and health and to improve overall quality of life by implementing systematic national nutrition policies [7]. However, it was only recently that these plans began to formally address the need for nutritional management of persons with disabilities.

Persons with disabilities face various nutritional challenges—including feeding difficulties, swallowing disorders, inappropriate food choices, inadequate dietary intake that fails to meet reference intakes, and household food insecurity. These factors contribute to the high prevalences of malnutrition and compromised immune function within this population, and a significant proportion of persons with disabilities develop obesity or sarcopenia [8910]. In South Korea, persons with disabilities experience an elevated level of food insecurity [11] and are disproportionately represented at both extremes of the weight spectrum, with higher rates of obesity, severe obesity, and underweight compared to those without disabilities [12]. Despite these observations, there has been a lack of comprehensive nutritional surveys and dietary data specific to this population in South Korea. Furthermore, systematic efforts to evaluate the nutritional status of persons with disabilities objectively and to identify the distinct nutritional challenges they face have been sparse.

This study aimed to assess whether nutritional deficiencies and imbalances were more severe among persons with disabilities by comparing their nutritional status with that of persons without disabilities, and to identify vulnerable disability subgroups (by disability severity and type) using data from the 2013 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES).

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This study used data from the KNHANES, a nationally representative survey of the non-institutionalized Korean population conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). The cross-sectional survey generates nationwide statistics regarding health status, health behaviors, and food and nutrient consumption among the Korean population, in accordance with Article 16 of the National Health Promotion Act [13]. The analysis focused on the health interview and nutrition survey data from the first year (2013) of the sixth KNHANES, which is the only national dataset that enables direct comparisons of dietary survey data between populations in South Korea with and without disabilities.

The study population was limited to adults ≥ 19 yrs of age who completed the 24-h dietary recall and could verify the presence or absence of a disability. In total, 5,396 individuals with and without disabilities were included. After excluding pregnant women and those with extreme total daily energy intake values (< 500 or > 6,000 kcal; n = 100), the analysis involved 5,296 participants.

The participants were classified into 387 persons with disabilities and 4,909 persons without disabilities based on their disability registration status, type of disability, and disability grade. The 15 types of disabilities defined in the Act on Welfare of Persons with Disabilities [14] were grouped into 5 categories: external physical disabilities (disabilities of the extremities, disabilities due to brain injury, and facial deformity); visual disabilities; hearing, speech, and language disabilities; mental disabilities (intellectual disabilities, disabilities due to autism, and mental health disorders); and internal organ dysfunctions (renal failure, heart disease, liver disease, respiratory problems, ostomy, and epilepsy). Disability grades of 1–6 were divided into 2 severity levels: severe disability (grades 1–3) and mild disability (grades 4–6). Since KNHANES targets the non-institutionalized civilian population, persons with disabilities residing in institutional settings were not included in this study. In addition, KNHANES records only the primary disability type; therefore, persons with multiple (co-occurring) disabilities could not be identified.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, using only depersonalized information from the KNHANES dataset, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the KDCA (reference number: 2013-07CON-03-4C). All participants provided written informed consent prior to study participation.

Daily nutrient intake

The analysis of daily nutrient intake was based on data collected by a 24-h dietary recall method, which documented the types and quantities of all foods consumed by the participants on the day preceding the survey. The analysis focused on the average daily intakes of energy and of 14 nutrients. To control for individual differences in total energy intake, nutrient intake was standardized as intake per 1,000 kcal of energy (nutrient density). This approach minimizes the impact of variations in overall energy consumption on the assessment of nutrient intake. Additionally, the contributions to energy of macronutrients—carbohydrates, proteins, and fats—were calculated to assess whether the macronutrient distribution aligned with the acceptable macronutrient distribution range (AMDR) for adults, as recommended by the 2015 Dietary Reference Intakes for Koreans (KDRIs) [15].

Assessment of nutrient adequacy ratio (NAR) and index of nutritional quality (INQ)

The NAR and mean adequacy ratio (MAR) were analyzed for 10 key nutrients to assess nutrient intake adequacy [16]. To assess the adequacy of nutrient intake relative to established standards, the intakes of energy, protein, vitamin A, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, niacin, vitamin C, calcium, phosphorus, and iron were compared to the recommended nutrient intake (RNI) by sex and age group, as specified in the 2015 KDRIs [15]. For each individual, the NAR was calculated as the intake of a given nutrient divided by its RNI (NAR = Individual Daily Nutrient Intake ÷ RNI of Each Nutrient). For energy, when a specific RNI was not provided, the estimated energy requirement (EER) by sex and age group was used. Theoretically, the NAR values could be < 1, > 1, or 1. However, to prevent a nutrient with a high NAR from compensating for a nutrient with a low NAR, the NAR values for each nutrient were capped at 1; i.e., 1 was the upper limit. The MAR, which reflects the adequacy of overall nutrient intake or diet, was defined as the average of the NARs. It was calculated as the sum of NARs for each nutrient divided by the number of all involved nutrients (MAR = Sum of NARs for All Evaluated Nutrients ÷ Number of Nutrients). A value of 1 for both NAR and MAR indicates that the intake meets the requirement.

The INQ is a measure of nutritional quality based on nutrient density [17]. The INQ values of 9 nutrients (excluding energy) were defined as the ratio of observed nutrient density (intake per 1,000 kcal) to the recommended density (age- and sex-specific RNI per 1,000 kcal). Thus, an INQ value ≥ 1.0 denotes adequate nutrient density relative to energy intake. INQ values were summarized as an “INQ score,” defined as the number of nutrients with an INQ value < 1.0; higher scores indicate poorer overall nutrient density.

The overall assessment of nutritional status was based on several indicators established in previous studies. Nutritional deficiency was defined as energy intake < 75% of the EER together with intakes of vitamin A, vitamin B2, calcium, and iron that were below the estimated average requirement (EAR) for each nutrient [18]. A high-carbohydrate diet was defined as one in which > 70% of daily energy intake was derived from carbohydrates [1920]. Overall nutrient adequacy, measured by the MAR, was categorized into tertiles; participants in the lowest tertile were classified as having low overall nutrient adequacy. INQ scores—defined as the number of nutrients with an INQ value < 1.0—were likewise categorized into tertiles; participants in the highest tertile were classified as consuming a diet with low nutrient density.

Covariate assessment

The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants were age, sex, household type (single-person household), marital status (married, widowed/divorced/separated, unmarried), education level (elementary school graduate or lower, middle-school graduate, high-school graduate, college graduate or higher), occupation type (unemployed, white-collar worker, blue-collar worker, service/sales worker and other), household income (quartile), and residential area (metropolitan cities, small and medium-sized cities, rural areas). Household income was equivalized by dividing total household income (KRW) by the square root of household size (i.e., Equivalized Income = Total Household Income/√Household Size). Equivalized income was then categorized into quartiles within each survey period. Health behavior variables included smoking status (non-smoker, former smoker, current smoker) and drinking behavior. The frequency of binge drinking, defined as consuming 7 or more drinks (5 or more for women) on a single occasion over the past year, was categorized as non-drinker or never binge drinker, once a month or less, and once a week or more frequently. Morbidity from major chronic diseases (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, and cancer) was summarized as the number of physician-diagnosed conditions (none, 1, ≥ 2, or unknown).

Statistical analysis

Because the KNHANES data were collected using a stratified, multistage sampling design, this study employed a complex sample data analysis method that used stratification variables, cluster variables, and weight variables as design elements. All estimates were expressed as weighted percentages (or means) with SEs to ensure representativeness of the Korean population. Statistical significance for differences in general characteristics and nutrient intake between persons with and without disabilities was determined using the PROC SURVEYFREQ procedure for categorical variables and the PROC SURVEYREG procedure for continuous variables. Additionally, differences in nutrient intake were examined according to disability type (non-disabled persons and the 5 categories of disabilities) and severity (non-disabled persons and the 2 levels of disability severity). Post hoc analysis using Dunnett’s test was conducted to identify groups that showed significant differences in the variables of interest compared to persons without disabilities.

To determine the association between overall nutritional status, dietary quality, and disability, multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted using the PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC procedure to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The logistic regression models were systematically adjusted for the following confounding factors: age, sex, single-person household status, marital status, education level, smoking status, binge-drinking frequency, household income, and chronic disease status. The data were statistically processed and analyzed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with the threshold for statistical significance set at a P-value < 0.05.

RESULTS

General characteristics of persons with and without disabilities

The sociodemographic characteristics and health behaviors of the persons with and without disabilities are presented in Table 1. Among the 387 persons with disabilities, 67.2% had external physical disabilities, followed by hearing, speech, and language disabilities (9.74%), visual disabilities (9.18%), mental disabilities (6.87%), and internal organ dysfunctions (5.92%). Approximately 34.36% of the persons with disabilities had severe disabilities (grades 1–3), and 62.43% had mild disabilities (grades 4–6). Compared to persons without disabilities, those with disabilities included a significantly higher proportion of men, elderly individuals, single-person households, and those who were widowed, divorced, or separated. They were also more likely to be unemployed or work in blue-collar jobs and had lower education levels and household incomes (all P < 0.001). Conversely, health behaviors such as current smoking and binge drinking were less prevalent among persons with disabilities than among those without disabilities (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, persons with disabilities exhibited a higher prevalence of chronic diseases, particularly multimorbidity (≥ 2 conditions) (P < 0.001).

The respective energy composition ratios for carbohydrates, protein, and fat were 68.18:14.14:17.67% for persons without disabilities, and 70.13:13.21:16.48% for persons with disabilities. Notably, the proportion of energy derived from carbohydrates was significantly higher among persons with disabilities (P = 0.008), whereas the proportions derived from protein (P < 0.001) and fat (P = 0.045) were significantly lower. Persons with mental disabilities had the highest proportion of energy from carbohydrates (78.05%; P < 0.001) and the lowest proportions from protein (10.63%; P < 0.001) and fat (11.31%; P = 0.003). The proportion of persons consuming carbohydrate and fat within the AMDR recommended by the KDRIs was higher among persons without disabilities than among those with disabilities (both P < 0.001). Persons with disabilities were more likely than those without disabilities to consume carbohydrates above the AMDR across all disability severity levels and types, whereas fat intake below the AMDR was more frequent among persons with disabilities. Conversely, the proportion of individuals meeting the AMDR for protein was greater among persons with disabilities (P = 0.035).

Nutrient intake per 1,000 kcal of energy intake varied significantly by disability status, severity, and type. The density of carbohydrate intake was higher among persons with disabilities compared with those without disabilities (P = 0.002), and the difference was particularly pronounced for those with severe disabilities (P = 0.003) or mental disabilities (P < 0.001). Protein intake density was significantly lower among persons with disabilities compared with those without disabilities (P = 0.005), particularly among persons with severe disabilities (P = 0.007). By disability type, both protein (P < 0.001) and fat (P = 0.035) intake densities were lowest among persons with mental disabilities, whereas those with visual disabilities had higher intake densities than those without disabilities. Additionally, the intake densities of micronutrients, including vitamin A, vitamin B2, niacin, calcium, phosphorus, iron, and sodium, varied significantly by disability status, severity, and type. Calcium intake density was significantly lower among persons with mild disabilities than among persons without disabilities, whereas it was highest among those with severe disabilities (P = 0.049).

NARs and INQs of key nutrients

The NARs for energy (P = 0.019), protein (P = 0.004), vitamin B2 (P = 0.003), niacin (P < 0.001), calcium (P = 0.007), and phosphorus (P = 0.021) were significantly lower in persons with compared to those without disabilities (Table 3). Analysis by disability severity revealed that energy, protein, phosphorus, and iron intakes were significantly lower among persons with severe disabilities compared to those without disabilities. Conversely, vitamin B2 and niacin intakes were significantly lower among the persons with severe and mild disabilities. The NARs of vitamin A, B2, niacin, and calcium varied by disability type. However, the NARs of vitamin B1 and C did not differ significantly by disability status, type, or severity. In the tertile analysis of the MAR, persons with disabilities were more likely than those without disabilities to be classified in the lowest tertile (indicating a low level of overall nutrient adequacy) across all levels of disability severity and types (all P < 0.001).

Prevalence of inadequate dietary outcomes and associations with disability status, severity, and type

The prevalence of nutritional deficiency did not differ significantly between persons with and without disabilities (6.35% vs. 7.05%; P = 0.664) (Table 4). In multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for potential confounders, persons with severe disabilities had higher odds of nutritional deficiency than those without disabilities (OR, 2.21; 95% CI, 1.09–4.97), whereas persons with visual disabilities had lower odds (OR, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.01–0.82). For mental disabilities, the association was strong in the age- and sex-adjusted model, but attenuated and was no longer statistically significant after further adjustment for covariates.

DISCUSSION

Nutrition and disability are closely related; malnutrition or a deterioration in nutritional status directly causes or contributes to disability [21]. However, causality can also operate in the opposite direction—disability can lead to nutritional vulnerabilities, including inadequate or excessive consumption of nutrients. In our previous research, we demonstrated that households that include persons with disabilities experience substantially higher food insecurity than households without disabilities [11]. Building on that work, the present study examined individual-level nutrient intakes among persons with disabilities to investigate these issues in greater depth. This cross-sectional study, using data from a nationally representative survey in Korea, is one of the few studies to assess systematically the nutritional status of persons with disabilities using DRIs, an issue that remains underexplored in Korea.

Nutrient intake data were collected via 24-h dietary recall conducted by trained interviewers [13]. Given the challenges and relative rarity of structured dietary interviews with persons with disabilities—particularly those with visual, auditory, speech, and developmental impairments—the scholarly significance and potential contributions of this study are considerable. Disability-related variables included registration status, severity, and type, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the nutritional status of persons with disabilities across subgroups defined by disability severity and type. The Korea National Disability Registration System (KNDRS) was established to provide social welfare benefits based on predefined criteria for disability registration and objective medical assessments using a disability grading system [14]. The robust definition of disability status in this study was based on those who reported having registered with the KNDRS. This study illuminates nutritional vulnerabilities among persons with disabilities, a historically understudied area, and highlights priority areas for improvement.

Although there were differences across disability severities and types, persons with disabilities had lower NARs for 8 of the 10 assessed nutrients, including energy, protein, and key vitamins and minerals, compared to those without disabilities. The likelihood of low overall nutrient adequacy (lowest-tertile MAR) was also significantly higher among persons with disabilities than among those without disabilities, and was particularly elevated among those with severe, visual, or internal organ disabilities. Compared to those without disabilities, fewer persons with disabilities met the DRI-recommended AMDR for carbohydrates and fat; exceeding the carbohydrate AMDR was more common, whereas fat intake below the AMDR was more prevalent in the disability group.

Internationally, nutritional issues among persons with disabilities have been recognized as a serious public-health concern [102223]. In a nationally representative sample of the United States population, persons with disabilities were less likely to meet the recommended intakes of saturated fat, fiber, vitamin A, vitamin C, calcium, and potassium than were those without disabilities [10]. Among older adults with limitations in the activities of daily living and social participation due to health problems or physical or mental disabilities, NARs for 10 nutrients and MAR were significantly lower than those in the control group [23]. Persons with disabilities were also less likely to meet established recommendations for key food groups, including fruits, vegetables, and whole grains [24], and had lower Healthy Eating Index scores than those without disabilities [25]. Although relatively few studies have examined the nutritional status of persons with disabilities, several have documented serious nutritional problems in this population, aligning with the patterns observed in our study.

We not only characterized the major nutritional problems affecting persons with disabilities but also identified priority subgroups with heightened vulnerability who require comprehensive assessment and immediate intervention, needs that have been inadequately addressed in prior studies. This pattern appears to worsen with increasing disability severity. Persons with severe disabilities were more likely to have low overall nutrient adequacy and other adverse nutritional outcomes, including the consumption of low-nutrient-density diets and nutritional deficiency. However, calcium intake per 1,000 kcal was higher among persons with severe disabilities than among persons without disabilities or with mild disabilities, likely reflecting a denominator effect driven by their lower total energy intake.

Persons with severe disabilities may be particularly susceptible to malnutrition and metabolic disorders owing to limitations in daily physical activity and multiple eating-related challenges. Dysphagia and other oral-motor impairments become more prevalent and severe as functional limitations worsen, increasing the risk of inadequate intake, reduced dietary diversity, and avoidance of harder-to-chew, nutrient-dense foods (e.g., meats, raw vegetables, and whole grains) [2627]. Certain disabilities can also lead to metabolic and physiological changes that impair nutrient absorption and utilization, sometimes necessitating clinically indicated special diets. Intensive treatments and polypharmacy commonly required in this population may induce dysgeusia and xerostomia, thereby diminishing appetite, altering food preferences, and resulting in deficiencies in specific nutrients [28].

Persons with mental disabilities had higher odds of macronutrient imbalance, characterized by an energy distribution skewed toward carbohydrates. Macronutrient energy distribution (carbohydrates, fat, and protein) is a key indicator of nutritional balance, and imbalances are frequently reported among persons with disabilities [2223]. In this study, the mental-disabilities category encompassed intellectual disability, autism, and mental-health disorders. Several factors may have contributed to the observed pattern. Psychotropic medications commonly prescribed for mental-health conditions can increase appetite and cravings for sweet or carbohydrate-rich foods [29303132]. Food selectivity and sensory sensitivities frequently reported in autism spectrum disorder have been associated with greater intake of refined, energy-dense, carbohydrate-rich items (e.g., white bread, pizza, cakes, cookies, and ice cream) and reduced dietary variety [33]. In addition, cognitive impairments and intellectual disabilities may hinder comprehension of nutrition information, meal planning, and food preparation [10], thereby increasing reliance on convenient, ultra-processed products that are typically high in refined grains and added sugars.

Epidemiologic evidence indicates that a high carbohydrate intake is associated with an increased risk of mortality [34]. Such dietary patterns are also linked to cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, particularly when carbohydrates are derived primarily from refined grains and added sugars [3536]. Although our study did not permit direct assessment of carbohydrate quality, future studies using detailed dietary data—including information on carbohydrate sources (e.g., refined vs. whole grains) and intakes of added and free sugars—are warranted to elucidate these pathways and to inform targeted nutrition support for persons with disabilities.

In our study, the composite outcome of “nutritional deficiency”—defined as energy intake < 75% of the EER and intakes of vitamin A, vitamin B2, calcium, and iron below the EAR—was less common among persons with visual disabilities than among other disability groups. Although not shown in the table, additional analysis indicated that, compared with individuals without disabilities, those with visual impairments had a higher proportion meeting 2 to 4 of the deficiency criteria among the 5 nutrients used to define nutritional deficiency. However, only 0.83% of individuals with visual impairments met all 5 criteria, whereas 7.05% of individuals without disabilities did. This result likely contributed to the lower odds of meeting the criteria for the composite nutritional deficiency outcome among persons with visual impairments compared with those without disabilities.

Nevertheless, these results should not be interpreted as evidence of no nutritional risk among persons with visual disabilities. Analyses based on the MAR, a summary measure of overall nutrient adequacy, indicated higher odds of low overall adequacy among persons with visual disabilities compared to those without disabilities. This provides evidence that nutrient intake among persons with visual impairments cannot be considered adequate. This observation also highlights the strong need for future studies with larger sample sizes to evaluate more accurately the nutritional challenges faced by this population.

Suboptimal dietary practices among persons with disabilities can lead to a variety of health complications. For instance, these issues contribute to the higher prevalence of malnutrition, underweight, and obesity in this population [812]. Persons with disabilities are also at greater risk of nutrition-related chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension [13]. Nutritional factors have a substantial effect on self-perceived health, functional status, rehabilitation outcomes, and overall quality of life [837]. For persons with disabilities, adherence to a healthy, nutrient-dense diet is important because it may help manage underlying conditions and support functional capacity.

Persons with disabilities face complex and multifaceted barriers to achieving optimal nutritional status and diet quality. Physical, cognitive, and financial barriers can hinder access to nutritious foods. Many experience mobility limitations or lack transportation, making it difficult to reach grocery stores or markets that offer healthy options [910]. Additionally, kitchens and food preparation spaces are often not designed to accommodate those using wheelchairs or other mobility aids, further restricting their ability to prepare meals independently [38]. Psychological and cognitive barriers, including depression, anxiety, and cognitive impairments, can substantially influence eating behaviors and dietary adherence. Persons with cognitive and psychological disabilities may exhibit inconsistent eating patterns, food selectivity, and behavioral eating challenges [1039].

Financial constraints, including unemployment, low income, and additional costs associated with managing a disability—such as for medical care, transportation, personal assistance, and adaptive equipment—can exacerbate these challenges. These financial burdens reduce the disposable income available for food purchases, potentially forcing individuals to rely on cheaper, less nutritious options that fit within their limited budget [1011].

Despite these challenges, nutrition policies and interventions often give insufficient consideration to this population, and targeted programs and tailored education remain limited. Moreover, gaps persist in financial assistance programs aimed at improving access to nutritious foods. At the systems level, maintaining disability identifiers in national nutrition surveys is essential to enable ongoing surveillance and program evaluation.

This study had several limitations. First, owing to its cross-sectional design, we cannot infer temporal or causal relationships between disability status and nutrient inadequacy. Second, our analyses focused on intakes of individual nutrients and overall nutrient adequacy relative to dietary guidelines. Although this approach is informative for assessing whether recommendations for particular nutrients are met, it has important limitations. Because individuals typically consume foods as components of meals or dishes rather than as discrete nutrients, this perspective may overlook dietary balance, variety, and synergistic interactions among food components. Consequently, further research using dietary-pattern approaches is warranted to examine how persons with disabilities combine foods within the context of their overall diet and how these patterns relate to health outcomes. Third, our sample of persons with disabilities may not be fully representative of the current disability population. We could not use the most recent KNHANES cycles because disability information was not consistently available. In addition, because KNHANES samples only the noninstitutionalized civilian population, persons with disabilities residing in institutional settings were not included. KNHANES also records only the primary disability type, precluding identification of multiple disabilities. As a result, potential nutritional disadvantages among those with multiple disabilities could not be assessed and may have been underestimated. Although we used the best available data to assess nutritional status objectively, these data may not reflect current conditions, underscoring the urgent need for an ongoing national survey to monitor nutrition in this population. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured or uncontrolled factors may remain, and our findings may not be generalizable to all disability groups or to populations in other countries where the distribution of disability types may differ.

Despite these limitations, this study assessed individual nutrient intake as well as overall nutrient adequacy and balance among persons with disabilities compared with those without disabilities, encompassing 15 legally prescribed types and severities of disabilities in Korea. While several previous studies have focused on particular types of disabilities, such as physical or developmental disabilities, few have made direct comparisons with those without disabilities. Additionally, much of the existing literature comprises small-scale, facility-based studies—such as those conducted in residential facilities or day-care centers for persons with disabilities—and has not been conducted using nationally representative samples.

Our findings indicate that persons with disabilities were more likely to have inadequate or imbalanced diets compared with those without disabilities. Overall nutrient adequacy relative to DRIs was lower in this population. Moreover, persons with severe disabilities were more likely to consume low-nutrient-density diets and to meet criteria for nutritional deficiency. Persons with mental disabilities exhibited a marked tendency toward a high carbohydrate intake, indicating macronutrient imbalance. These results highlight the urgent need for targeted nutritional interventions and policies to reduce these disparities and promote healthier dietary practices among persons with disabilities. At the systems level, inclusion of disability identifiers in national nutrition surveys should be maintained to enable ongoing surveillance and program evaluation. Disability-inclusive nutrition policy and practice are crucial to improving health, preventing secondary conditions, and ensuring the equitable inclusion of persons with disabilities in public health initiatives.
