We admitted a 72-year-old woman to the internal medicine service. She had weight loss of 18 kg over 2 years, decreased oral intake, and recent onset of anasarca, with a history of seronegative inflammatory arthritis. Her musculoskeletal symptoms began 4 years prior with migratory arthralgias and morning stiffness, initially affecting the right metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints, followed by wrist tenderness, right elbow swelling, and bilateral knee pain. Shortly after the symptoms started, a rheumatologist started the patient on methotrexate and later prescribed disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and biological agents, including leflunomide, hydroxychloroquine, abatacept, and tocilizumab. However, her arthritic symptoms did not improve and after 3 years, she discontinued all DMARDs and biologic agents. She continued to experience gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (anorexia, early satiety, and generalized abdominal pain), persistent weight loss, and fatigue. She denied diarrhea, vomiting, or change in bowel movements. Her worsening GI symptoms were attributed to her antirheumatic medications.

When admitted to hospital, the patient was cachectic and reported exertional dyspnea. She had grade 4 bilateral lower-extremity edema extending to the abdominal wall, no ascites, normal heart sounds with a faint systolic murmur, and a normal jugular venous pressure. She had no joint or neurologic abnormalities and no peripheral stigmata of infective endocarditis or chronic liver disease.

Blood tests showed hypoalbuminemia of 13 (normal 30 to 45) g/L, a normal 24-hour urine albumin level, and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 66.2 (normal < 8) mg/L. Abdominal computed tomography showed focal thickening of the right hemicolon. We performed esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy to rule out gastritis and colitis; we noted duodenitis (Figure 1A), but the rest of the examinations were normal. Three separate sets of blood samples cultured during the first 7 days of admission were negative. We ordered transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) to investigate for right-sided heart failure as a potential cause of anasarca and pedal edema. This suggested vegetations on the mitral valve. We ordered transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), which revealed vegetations on the anterior leaflet of the mitral and the left coronary cusp of the aortic valve (Figure 2). The aortic valve demonstrated moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation while the mitral valve was functioning normally. These findings were consistent with culture-negative endocarditis. We tested for agents known to cause blood culture–negative endocarditis, sending serologies for Brucella spp., Bartonella spp., Coxiella spp., and Mycoplasma pneumoniae, as well as Legionella urinary antigen testing and blood Mycobacterial culture. All were negative.

We consulted the infectious disease service, and they obtained some additional history; the patient had developed a profound diarrheal illness with watery diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal cramping that lasted for 3 to 4 days while living in Guatemala 5 years before presentation. Her diarrhea completely subsided, with no residual symptoms during the remainder of her stay. Upon her return to Canada, she recalled migratory arthritis within a few months but did not have any GI symptoms until 2 to 3 years later. Although treatment for her presumed arthritis had little-to-no benefit on her joint and muscle pains, methotrexate worsened her GI symptoms, and she continued to have weight loss with elevated inflammatory markers.

Based on this constellation of findings, we suspected tropical sprue or, more likely, Whipple disease. We requested additional pathological testing on previously taken EGD biopsies from the patient’s stomach and duodenum, including Gram staining, Grocott methenamine silver stain (GMS), Ziehl–Neelson (ZN), Warthin–Starry, and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining.

These biopsies were reviewed by another GI pathologist who confirmed the presence of PAS-positive macrophage infiltrates in the duodenal mucosa, with a dense lamina propria in all biopsies, pathognomic for Whipple disease (Figure 3). Examination of PAS-stained biopsies at high magnification revealed abundant macrophages laden with PAS-positive, rod-shaped inclusions, classic for Tropheryma whipplei infection (Figure 3C). The sample from the stomach mucosa also tested positive for T. whipplei by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Because of the patient’s long history of inflammatory arthritis unresponsive to treatment, weight loss with anasarca, culture-negative endocarditis, and duodenal biopsy findings, our multidisciplinary team — including internal medicine, pathology, and infectious disease specialists — diagnosed Whipple disease. We initiated induction therapy with intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone (2 g daily) for 4 weeks, followed by oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) and hydroxychloroquine (300 mg twice daily). Our patient was ultimately switched to oral trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMPSMX) (160 mg/800 mg, twice daily) because of her GI intolerance to doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine.

After 1 year of treatment with TMP-SMX, the patient reported complete resolution of pedal edema and anasarca, weight gain of 18 kg (from 41 kg to 59 kg), excellent appetite, and complete recovery from joint pain. Her serum albumin level had increased from 14 g/L to 33 g/L and her CRP was 4.3 mg/L. A follow-up TTE showed no evidence of residual aortic or mitral valve vegetations. A repeat EGD showed complete resolution of duodenitis (Figure 1B), and biopsies were negative for T. whipplei by PCR.

Discussion

Whipple disease is an uncommon multisystem infection caused by T. whipplei, a gram-positive bacillus transmitted through the fecal–oral route and found in soil and sewage. Whipple disease commonly presents with chronic migratory arthralgias, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss but can present as a severe multisystem disease, including culture-negative endocarditis or meningoencephalitis (Box 1). First visualized in 1992 using electron microscopy, T. whipplei stains strongly with PAS and is acid-fast negative, a finding that distinguishes it from other intracellular pathogens.5 The incidence is estimated at 1 to 3 cases per million people, with fewer than 1000 cases reported worldwide, with a higher prevalence among White men from Europe and North America for reasons that remain unclear.5,6 Given this background, we speculate that our patient’s infection began during the severe gastroenteritis she experienced in Guatemala, which directly preceded disease onset and is consistent with higher T. whipplei carriage rates reported in lower-income countries and its presence in sewage.5–7

Our patient’s case exemplifies the many presentations of Whipple disease and the reasons it is so often misdiagnosed. She initially developed migratory, seronegative inflammatory arthritis — one of the most common early manifestations of Whipple disease — which can precede gastrointestinal symptoms by 3 to 5 years. Our patient’s joint pain persisted for nearly 3 years and did not improve despite use of multiple DMARDs and biologic agents. This is consistent with reports of Whipple disease–associated arthritis being mistaken for rheumatoid arthritis or spondyloarthritis. 5,8 Immunosuppressive therapy can worsen T. whipplei infection, as may have occurred in our patient, who noted worsened GI symptoms and weight loss during methotrexate treatment.

Tropheryma whipplei is among the more common causative organisms of culture-negative endocarditis.6,9 Whipple disease endocarditis is often associated with arthropathy and may occur without preceding intestinal symptoms.6 It is notable that our patient had no clear features of endocarditis on clinical examination. Although TTE results raised suspicion of vegetations, TEE confirmed them on the aortic and mitral valves, consistent with previous reports identifying the aortic valve as the most commonly involved.6 This reinforces the role of TEE in patients with suspected culture-negative endocarditis, particularly when risk factors such as unexplained inflammatory arthritis and weight loss are present.

Gastrointestinal involvement eventually provided the diagnostic breakthrough for our patient. Although her first endoscopy revealed only mild duodenitis, further analysis of biopsies with targeted stains (PAS, Warthin–Starry, GMS, ZN) and T. whipplei–specific PCR confirmed the diagnosis. Multiple small bowel biopsies (duodenum and proximal jejunum) and molecular testing are recommended in patients in whom Whipple disease is suspected given patchy mucosal involvement that may not be obvious upon initial endoscopic visualization.1 Because T. whipplei can be detected in the stool of fewer than 1% to 5% of those in high-income countries and in as many as 40% of asymptomatic people in low-income countries, tissue diagnosis, typically from a gastrointestinal biopsy, is essential to distinguish true infection from colonization.2,6,10 Identification of PAS-positive macrophage infiltrates laden with rod-shaped inclusions remain a classic histopathologic hallmark of Whipple disease.1,6

Treatment for Whipple disease consists of prolonged antimicrobial therapy that penetrates the central nervous system (CNS) because of the risk of latent CNS infection. Until recently, this has involved 2 to 4 weeks of IV ceftriaxone, penicillin G, or meropenem, followed by at least 12 months of oral TMP–SMX.3 We started our patient on ceftriaxone induction followed by doxycycline–hydroxychloroquine maintenance therapy. Recently, results of a randomized controlled trial suggest that an all-oral regimen of doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine may be noninferior to a regimen including IV ceftriaxone induction.4 It is important to note that the presence or absence of Whipple endocarditis does not substantially alter treatment choices.3,4,6

Whipple disease may masquerade as malignancy, autoimmune disease, or idiopathic inflammatory bowel disease. Our patient’s trajectory highlights how overlapping clinical syndromes can obscure the underlying infectious cause. Prompt recognition and multidisciplinary collaboration are key to avoiding prolonged morbidity and ensuring complete recovery.6
