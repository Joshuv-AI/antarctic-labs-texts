Distribution Parameter Actor-Critic: Shifting the Agent-Environment Boundary for Diverse Action Spaces
Jiamin He, A. Rupam Mahmood, Martha White, University of Alberta, Canada CIFAR AI Chair {jiamin12, armahmood, whitem}@ualberta.ca

Abstract
We introduce a novel reinforcement learning (RL) framework that treats distribution parameters as actions, redefining the boundary between agent and environment. This reparameterization makes the new action space continuous, regardless of the original action type (discrete, continuous, mixed, etc.). Under this new parameterization, we develop a generalized deterministic policy gradient estimator, Distribution Parameter Policy Gradient (DPPG), which has lower variance than the gradient in the original action space. Although learning the critic over distribution parameters poses new challenges, we introduce interpolated critic learning (ICL), a simple yet effective strategy to enhance learning, supported by insights from bandit settings. Building on TD3, a strong baseline for continuous control, we propose a practical DPPG-based actor-critic algorithm, Distribution Parameter Actor-Critic (DPAC). Empirically, DPAC outperforms TD3 in MuJoCo continuous control tasks from OpenAI Gym and DeepMind Control Suite, and demonstrates competitive performance on the same environments with discretized action spaces.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms are commonly categorized into value-based and policy-based methods. Value-based methods, such as Q-learning
(
Watkins & Dayan 1992
)
and its variants like DQN
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
, are particularly effective in discrete action spaces due to the feasibility of enumerating and comparing action values. In contrast, policy-based methods are typically used for continuous actions, though they can be used for both discrete and continuous action spaces
(
Williams 1992
;
Sutton et al. 1999
)
.

Policy-based methods are typically built around the policy gradient theorem
(
Sutton et al. 1999
)
, with different approaches to estimate this gradient. The likelihood-ratio (LR) estimator can be applied to arbitrary action distributions, including discrete ones. In continuous action spaces, one can alternatively compute gradients via the action-value function (the critic), leveraging its differentiability with respect to actions. This idea underlies the deterministic policy gradient (DPG) algorithms
(
Silver et al. 2014
)
and the use of the reparameterization (RP) trick for stochastic policies
(
Heess et al. 2015
;
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
. These approaches can produce lower-variance gradient estimates by backpropagating through the critic and the policy
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
.

Despite the flexibility of policy gradient methods, current algorithms remain tightly coupled to the structure of the action space. In particular, different estimators and architectures are often required for discrete versus continuous actions, making it difficult to design unified algorithms that generalize across domains. Although the LR estimator is always applicable, it often requires different critic architectures for different action spaces and carefully designed baselines to manage high variance, especially in continuous or high dimensional action spaces.

In this paper, we introduce the
parameter-as-action framework
, an alternative to the classical RL formulation that treats
distribution parameters
as actions. For a Gaussian policy, for example, the distribution parameters are the mean and variance, and for a softmax policy, the distribution parameters are the probability values. The RL agent outputs these distribution parameters to the environment, and the sampling of the action is now part of the stochastic transition in the environment. Distribution parameters are typically continuous, even if the actions are discrete, mixed or structured. By shifting this agent-environment boundary, therefore, we can develop one continuous-action algorithm for a diverse class of action spaces.

We first propose the
Distribution Parameter Policy Gradient
(DPPG) estimator, and prove it has lower variance than the corresponding update in the original action space. This reduction in variance can increase the bias, because the critic can be harder to learn. We develop an augmentation approach, called
interpolated critic learning
(ICL), to improve this critic learning. We then introduce a deep RL algorithm based on TD3
(
Fujimoto et al. 2018
)
, called
Distribution Parameter Actor-Critic
(DPAC), that incorporates the DPPG estimator and ICL. We evaluate DPAC empirically to assess the viability of this new framework. Although our goal is not surpassing the performance of existing algorithms, DPAC outperforms TD3 on
20
MuJoCo continuous control tasks from OpenAI Gym and DeepMind Control Suite, and achieves competitive performance on the same environments with discretized action spaces—without hyperparameter tuning.
We also provide targeted experiments to understand the bias-variance trade-off in DPAC, and show the utility of ICL for improving critic learning.

Problem formulation

We consider a Markov decision process (MDP)
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,d_{0},r,\gamma\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition function,
d_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial state distribution,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{R})
is the reward function, and
\gamma
is the discount factor. Here,
\Delta(\mathcal{X})
denotes the set of distributions over a set
\mathcal{X}
. In this paper, we consider
\mathcal{A}
to be either discrete or continuous.

1

Note that the framework and methods proposed in this paper also apply to other complex types of action spaces. We focus on discrete and continuous action spaces in our presentation for simplicity.
We use
\pi(a|s)
to represent the probability of taking action
a\in\mathcal{A}
under state
s\in\mathcal{S}
for policy
\pi
.
The goal of the agent is to find a policy
\pi
under which the below objective is maximized:

\displaystyle J(\pi)

\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim d_{0},A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|S_{t}),S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})}\big[\gamma^{t}R_{t+1}\big]=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\gamma^{t}R_{t+1}\right],

(1)

where the second formula uses simplified notation that we follow in the rest of the paper. The
(state-)value function
and
action-value function
of the policy are defined as follows:

\displaystyle v_{\pi}(s)\doteq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\gamma^{t}R_{t+1}|S_{0}=s\right],\quad q_{\pi}(s,a)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[R_{1}+\gamma v_{\pi}(S_{1})|S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a\right].

(2)

In this paper, we consider actor-critic methods that learns a parameterized policy, denoted by
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
, and a parameterized action-value function, denoted by
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
. Given a transition
\langle S_{t},A_{t},R_{t+1},S_{t+1}\rangle
,
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
is usually learned using temporal-difference (TD) learning:

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}+\alpha\left(R_{t+1}+\gamma Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A_{t})\right)\nabla Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A_{t}),

(3)

where
\alpha
is the step size, and
A_{t+1}
is sampled from the current policy:
A_{t+1}\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t+1})
.

The policy is typically optimized using a surrogate of
Equation

1
:

\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathbb{E}_{S_{t}\sim d,A_{t}\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A_{t})\right],

(4)

where
d\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is some distribution over states. Below we outline three typical estimators for the gradient of this objective.

The likelihood-ratio (LR) policy gradient estimator
uses

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A)
, where
A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})
.
Since the LR estimator suffers from high variance, it is often used with the value function as a baseline:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})(Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A)-V(S_{t})),

(5)

where
V(S_{t})
could either be parameterized and learned or be calculated analytically from
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
when the action space is discrete and low dimensional.

The deterministic policy gradient (DPG) estimator

(
Silver et al. 2014
)
is used when the action space is continuous and the policy is deterministic (
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
), and uses
the gradient of
Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}
with respect to the action:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPG}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{A}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A)|_{A={\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}.

(6)

The reparameterization (RP) policy gradient estimator

(
Heess et al. 2015
;
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
can be used if the policy can be reparameterized (i.e.,
A=g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})
,
\epsilon\sim p(\cdot)
, where
p(\cdot)
is a prior distribution):

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{A}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A)|_{A=g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})}.

(7)

3
Parameters-as-actions framework

Figure 1:

Comparison between the classical reinforcement learning (RL) framework and the proposed parameters-as-actions framework.

In the classical RL setting (col 1), the agent’s policy
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
consists of
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
, which produces the
distribution parameters
, and a sampling function
f
that returns an action given these parameters.
In the parameters-as-actions framework (col 2), the sampling function
f
is considered part of the environment, and the agent outputs the distribution parameters
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})
as its action.
This shift redefines the interface between agent and environment, potentially simplifying learning and enabling new algorithmic perspectives.

The action space is typically defined by the environment designer based on domain-specific knowledge. Depending on the problem, it may be more natural to model the action space as either discrete or continuous. In both cases, the agent’s policy at a given state
s
can often be interpreted as first producing distribution parameters
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)
, followed by sampling an action
A\sim f(\cdot|{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s))
from the resulting distribution. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{U}
as the part of the policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
that maps states to distribution parameters, and by
f(\cdot|{u})
the distribution over actions defined by parameters
{u}\in\mathcal{U}
.

In the classical RL framework, both
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
and
f
are considered part of the agent, as in the left of
Figure

1
. In this work, we introduce the
parameters-as-actions framework
:
the agent outputs distribution parameters

{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)
as its action, while the sampling process
A\sim f(\cdot|{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s))
is treated as part of the environment, depicted on the right in
Figure

1
.

This reformulation leads to a new MDP in which the action space is the parameter space
\mathcal{U}
. The reward and transition functions in this MDP become:

\displaystyle{\bar{p}}(s^{\prime}|s,{u})

\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f(a|{u})p(s^{\prime}|s,a),\quad\text{or}\quad{\bar{p}}(s^{\prime}|s,{u})\doteq\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(a|{u})p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\,da,

(8)

\displaystyle{\bar{r}}(s,{u})

\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f(a|{u})r(s,a),\quad\text{or}\quad{\bar{r}}(s,{u})\doteq\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(a|{u})r(s,a)\,da,

(9)

depending on whether the original action space
\mathcal{A}
is discrete or continuous, respectively.

This gives rise to the
parameter-space MDP

\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{U},{\bar{p}},d_{0},{\bar{r}},\gamma\rangle
. As in classical RL, we can define the corresponding value functions, which are connected to their classical counterparts:

\displaystyle{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s)\doteq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\pi}}\left[\gamma^{t}R_{t+1}|S_{0}=s\right],\quad{\bar{q}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s,{u})\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\pi}}\left[R_{1}+\gamma{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(S_{1})|S_{0}=s,U_{0}={u}\right].

(10)

Assumption 3.1
.

The set
\mathcal{U}
is compact. Moreover, when
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{A}
is continuous, the corresponding set is also assumed to be compact.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under
3.1
,
{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s)=v_{\pi}(s)
and
{\bar{q}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s,{u})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}[q_{\pi}(s,A)]
.

The proofs of
Proposition

3.2
and all other theoretical results are presented in
Appendix

A
.

The main advantage of this framework is that it transforms the original action space into a continuous parameter space
\mathcal{U}
, regardless of whether the underlying action space
\mathcal{A}
is discrete, continuous, or structured. This unification allows us to develop generic RL algorithms that operate over a continuous transformed action space, enabling a single framework to accommodate a wide variety of settings, including discrete-continuous hybrid action spaces
(
Masson et al. 2016
)
. For example, we can apply DPG methods even in discrete action domains, where they were not previously inapplicable. We explore this direction in detail in
Sections

4
and
5
.

4
Distribution parameter policy gradient algorithms

In this section, we introduce the
Distribution Parameter Policy Gradient
(DPPG), a generalization of DPG
for the parameters-as-actions framework. We show this estimator has lower variance, and then present a practical DPPG algorithm for deep RL based on TD3.

4.1
Distribution parameter policy gradient estimator

DPPG is the application of DPG to the parameter-space MDP. We need to slightly modify the assumptions to reason about both the distribution parameter space and the original action space.

Assumption 4.1
.

The functions
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)
,
f(a|u)
, and their derivatives are continuous with respect to the variables
{u}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
. Moreover, when
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{A}
is continuous, the functions
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)
,
d_{0}(s)
,
r(s,a)
,
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)
,
f(a|u)
, and their derivatives are also continuous with respect to
s
,
s^{\prime}
, or
a
, respectively.

Theorem 4.2

(Distribution parameter policy gradient theorem)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
4.1
, the gradient of the objective function
J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\pi}}\left[\gamma^{t}R_{t+1}\right]
with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
can be expressed as

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)\nabla_{{u}}{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s,{u})|_{{u}={\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)}\right],

where
d_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(s)\doteq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}[\gamma^{t}\mathbb{I}(S_{t}=s)]
is the (discounted) occupancy measure under
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
.

The resulting gradient estimator of the surrogate objective
\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathbb{E}_{S_{t}\sim d}\left[{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))\right]
is

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})},

(11)

where
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
is a learned parameterized critic function. Note that the DPPG estimator shares the same mathematical form as the DPG estimator (see
Equation

6
). However, the roles of the components differ: In DPPG, the policy
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
outputs distribution parameters rather than a single action, and the critic estimates the expected return over the entire action distribution, rather than for a specific action.

In fact, DPPG is a strict generalization of DPG. When the policy is restricted to be deterministic, the distribution parameters effectively become the action, and the parameter-space critic reduces to the classical action-value critic.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{A}
and
f(\cdot|u)
is the Dirac delta distribution centered at
u
, then
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
are equivalent to
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
and
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
, respectively. Consequently, the DPPG gradient estimator becomes equivalent to DPG:

\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPG}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t}).

Moreover, DPG’s theoretical analysis can also be extended to the parameter-as-action framework. In
Appendix

A
, we generalize the convergence analysis of DPG to DPPG, establishing a theoretical guarantee that holds for MDPs with arbitrary action space types.

4.2
Comparison to other estimators for stochastic policies

We now compare the proposed DPPG estimator with classical stochastic policy gradient (PG) methods, highlighting its variance and bias characteristics across action spaces.

DPPG can be seen as the conditional expectation of both the LR (see
Equation

5
) and RP (see
Equation

7
) estimators. This leads to strictly lower variance.

Proposition 4.4
.

Assume
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}=q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)
and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}={\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
. Then,

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right]
. Further, if the expectation of the action-conditioned variance is greater than zero, then

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right)<\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right)
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume
\mathcal{A}
is continuous,
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}=q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)
, and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}={\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
. Then,

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right]
. Further, if the expectation of the noise-induced variance is greater than zero, then

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right)<\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right)
.

In discrete action spaces, the LR estimator typically requires carefully designed baselines to manage high variance, especially as dimensionality increases. While biased alternatives like the straight-through (ST) estimator
(
Bengio et al. 2013
)
or continuous relaxations
(
Jang et al. 2016
;
Maddison et al. 2016
)
exist, they sacrifice unbiasedness even when using a perfect critic. DPPG avoids this trade-off, providing the first unbiased RP-style estimator with low variance in the discrete setting.

In continuous action spaces, DPG offers zero variance but assumes fixed stochasticity (i.e., no learnable exploration). RP estimators allow for learning the stochastic parameters but exhibit higher variance. DPPG offers the best of both worlds: it permits learning all policy parameters including those for stochasticity while retaining the zero-variance property per state.

With access only to samples from the evaluation policy, the standard update estimates values accurately at the target policy but fails to generalize. In contrast, the ICL update learns a critic that captures curvature useful for policy optimization.

Another direction to reduce variance is
expected policy gradient

(
Ciosek & Whiteson 2018
;
Allen et al. 2017
, EPG;)
.
The idea is to integrate (or sum) over actions, yielding zero-variance gradients conditioned on a state:

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{EPG}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{A_{t}\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A_{t})\right]
.
However, this estimator is only practical in low-dimensional discrete action spaces
(
Allen et al. 2017
)
or in special cases within continuous settings—such as Gaussian policies with quadratic critics
(
Ciosek & Whiteson 2020
)
. In contrast, our estimator
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
generalizes to a wider range of settings, including high-dimensional discrete, general continuous, and even hybrid action spaces.

Despite its lower variance, DPPG may suffer from increased bias due to the increased complexity of the critic’s input space. For discrete actions, the critic
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
inputs a vector of probabilities corresponding to discrete outcomes.
For continuous actions, with Gaussian policies, the critic
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
inputs both the mean and standard deviation. This increased input dimensionality makes it harder to approximate the true value function, and if the critic is inaccurate, the overall benefit of lower gradient variance may be diminished—an effect we examine empirically in
Section

5.3
.

4.3
Interpolated parameter-space critic learning

In this section, we propose a method to improve learning the parameter-space critic
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
.
Similar to
Equation

3
, the standard TD update for
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
is

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}+\alpha\left(R_{t+1}+\gamma{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t+1},{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(S_{t+1}))-{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},U_{t})\right)\nabla{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U_{t}).

(12)

This update, however, does not make use of the sampled action
A_{t}
, and its relationship to the outcome state and reward.
One direction to leverage this knowledge is to recognize that the transition can also be used to update the value at alternative parameters
\hat{U}_{t}
.
This is possible because the action
A_{t}
could have been sampled from distributions parameterized by many other
\hat{U}_{t}
. As a result, the value at
\hat{U}_{t}
can be learned off-policy.

What, then, should we choose for
\hat{U}_{t}
? To answer this, we ask:
what properties should the critic have to support effective policy optimization in parameter space?
Our answer is that the critic should provide informative gradient directions that guide the policy toward optimality. For MDPs, there always exists a deterministic optimal policy
(
Puterman 2014
)
. Therefore, we assume the existence of some
U_{A^{*}_{t}}\in\mathcal{U}
, a deterministic distribution corresponding to the optimal action
A^{*}_{t}
for state
S_{t}
. Ideally, the critic should exhibit curvature that points toward such optimal parameters
U^{*}_{t}
.

One candidate for
\hat{U}_{t}
is
U_{A_{t}}
, the deterministic distribution parameters associated with the sampled action
A_{t}
.
However, merely learning accurate values at
U_{A_{t}}
does not ensure that the critic has smooth curvature from
U_{t}
toward these high-value points. To encourage the critic to generalize better and provide smoother gradients, we propose using a linearly interpolated point between
U_{t}
and
U_{A_{t}}
:

\hat{U}_{t}=\omega_{t}U_{t}+(1-\omega_{t})U_{A_{t}},\quad\omega_{t}\sim\uniform[0,1].

(13)

The critic is then trained to predict the value at
\hat{U}_{t}
using the following update:

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}+\alpha\left(R_{t+1}+\gamma{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t+1},{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(S_{t+1}))-{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},\hat{U_{t}})\right)\nabla{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},\hat{U_{t}}).

(14)

We refer to this approach as
interpolated critic learning
(ICL).

To further provide intuition on ICL, we conduct a policy evaluation experiment in bandit problems, shown in
Figure

2
(column 1).

Figure

2
(column 3) and (column 4) show the learned critic functions using the standard update in
Equation

12
and the ICL update in
Equation

14
, respectively. The critic learned by ICL has more informative curvature. In the continuous action case, the learned critic is sufficient to identify the optimal distribution parameters.
More experiment details can be found in
Section

B.2
.

4.4
Distribution parameter actor-critic

Since the DPPG estimator is derived from DPG, we base our practical algorithm on TD3
(
Fujimoto et al. 2018
)
, a strong DPG-based off-policy actor-critic algorithm for continuous control. We replace the classical actor and critic with their parameter-space counterparts and use the DPPG gradient estimator (
Equation

11
) and the ICL critic loss (
Equation

14
) to update them, respectively. We omit the actor target network, as it does not improve performance (see
Section

B.4

6
Conclusions

We introduced the
parameters-as-actions framework
, redefining the agent-environment boundary to treat distribution parameters as actions. We showed that the policy gradient update has theoretically lower variance, and developed a practical deep RL algorithm called
Distribution Parameter Actor-Critic
(DPAC) based on this estimator and an improved critic learning update, ICL, tailored to this new setting. We showed better or comparable performance to TD3 across 20 environments.

This reframing allowed us to develop a continuous action algorithm that applies to diverse underlying action types: discrete, continuous, or otherwise structured. A key next step is test the algorithm in mixed spaces, and further exploit this reframing for new algorithmic avenues, including model-based methods, hierarchical control, or novel hybrid approaches.
There are also key open questions around critic learning in this new framework. More advanced strategies for training the parameter-space critic could be explored, including off-policy updates at diverse regions of the parameter space or using a learned action-value function
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(s,a)
to guide updates of
{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}^{\prime}}(s,{u})
. This will also open up new questions about convergence properties for these new variants.

A.1
Proofs of theoretical results in Section
3

3.1
.

The set
\mathcal{U}
is compact. Moreover, when
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{A}
is continuous, the corresponding set is also assumed to be compact.

Proposition

3.2
.

Under
3.1
,
{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s)=v_{\pi}(s)
and
{\bar{q}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s,{u})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}[q_{\pi}(s,A)]
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be the policy in the original MDP that first maps
s
to
{u}={\bar{\pi}}(s)
and then samples
A\sim f(\cdot|{u})
.
The state-value function
{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s)
in the parameter-space MDP is defined as:

{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\pi}}\left[\gamma^{k}{\bar{r}}(S_{k},U_{k})\mid S_{0}=s\right],

where
U_{k}={\bar{\pi}}(S_{k})
.
From
Equation

8
,
{\bar{r}}(s,{u})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}[r(s,A)]
.
Also, the transition
{\bar{p}}(s^{\prime}|s,{u})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}[p(s^{\prime}|s,A)]
.
Consider a trajectory
S_{0},U_{0},S_{1},U_{1},\dots
in the parameter-space MDP. This corresponds to a trajectory
S_{0},A_{0},S_{1},A_{1},\dots
in the original MDP where
A_{k}\sim f(\cdot|U_{k})
.
The expected reward at time
k
in the parameter-space MDP, given
S_{k}
and
U_{k}={\bar{\pi}}(S_{k})
, is
{\bar{r}}(S_{k},{\bar{\pi}}(S_{k}))=\mathbb{E}_{A_{k}\sim f(\cdot|{\bar{\pi}}(S_{k}))}[r(S_{k},A_{k})]
.
The dynamics are also equivalent in expectation:
\mathbb{E}[S_{k+1}|S_{k},U_{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim{\bar{p}}(\cdot|S_{k},U_{k})}[S^{\prime}]=\mathbb{E}_{A_{k}\sim f(\cdot|U_{k})}[\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim p(\cdot|S_{k},A_{k})}[S^{\prime}]]
.
Thus, the sequence of states and expected rewards generated under
{\bar{\pi}}
in the parameter-space MDP is identical in distribution to the sequence of states and rewards under
\pi
in the original MDP.
Therefore,
{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s)=v_{\pi}(s)
.

For the action-value function
{\bar{q}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s,{u})
:

\displaystyle{\bar{q}}_{\bar{\pi}}(s,{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\pi}}\left[{\bar{r}}(S_{0},U_{0})+\gamma{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(S_{1})\mid S_{0}=s,U_{0}={u}\right]

\displaystyle={\bar{r}}(s,{u})+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{S_{1}\sim{\bar{p}}(\cdot|s,{u})}[{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}(S_{1})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}[r(s,A)]+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{S_{1}\sim p(\cdot|s,A)}[v_{\pi}(S_{1})]\right]\quad(\text{using }{\bar{v}}_{\bar{\pi}}=v_{\pi})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}\left[r(s,A)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{S_{1}\sim p(\cdot|s,A)}[v_{\pi}(S_{1})]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R_{1}+\gamma v_{\pi}(S_{1})|S_{0}=s,A_{0}=A]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|{u})}[q_{\pi}(s,A)].

The compactness assumption in
3.1
along with continuity from
4.1
ensures these expectations and value functions are well-defined.
∎

Theorem

4.2
(Distribution parameter policy gradient theorem)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
4.1
, the gradient of the objective function
J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\pi}}\left[\gamma^{t}R_{t+1}\right]
with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
can be expressed as

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)\nabla_{{u}}{\bar{Q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s,{u})|_{{u}={\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)}\right],

where
d_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(s)\doteq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}[\gamma^{t}\mathbb{I}(S_{t}=s)]
is the (discounted) occupancy measure under
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
.

Proof.

This theorem results from applying the deterministic policy gradient (DPG) theorem to the parameter-space MDP
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{U},{\bar{p}},d_{0},{\bar{r}},\gamma\rangle
, where
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{U}
acts as a deterministic policy. The objective function is
J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim d_{0}}[{\bar{v}}_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(S_{0})]
.

Following the DPG theorem derivation (
Silver et al. 2014
, Theorem 1), for a general deterministic policy
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
, the policy gradient is:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}J(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)^{\top}\nabla_{a}q_{\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s,a)\big|_{a=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)}\right].

In our context:

•

The policy in the parameter-space MDP is
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)
.

•

The action space is
\mathcal{U}
, and actions are denoted by
{u}
.

•

The critic
{\bar{q}}_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(s,{u})
is the action-value function in this parameter-space MDP.

•

The state distribution
d_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(s)
is the discounted state occupancy measure under policy
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
.

Assumptions
3.1
and
4.1
ensure that
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)
and
{\bar{q}}_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(s,{u})
are appropriately differentiable and that the interchange of expectation and differentiation is valid.
Substituting
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
for
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
and
{\bar{q}}_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}
for
q_{\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
yields the theorem’s result:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)^{\top}\nabla_{{u}}{\bar{q}}_{{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}(s,{u})|_{{u}={{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(s)}\right].

The notation
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)^{\top}\nabla_{{u}}{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in the theorem statement implies the appropriate vector or matrix product. If
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s)
is an
m\times k
Jacobian,
\nabla_{{u}}{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
is an
m\times 1
vector, and the product
(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(s))^{\top}\nabla_{{u}}{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
results in the
k\times 1
gradient vector for
J({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})
.
∎

Proposition

4.3
.

If
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{A}
and
f(\cdot\mid u)
is the Dirac delta distribution centered at
u
, then
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
are equivalent to
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
and
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
, respectively. Consequently, the DPPG gradient estimator becomes equivalent to DPG:

\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPG}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t}).

Proof.

The DPPG gradient estimator is given by
Equation

11
:

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}{\bar{Q}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}.

Given the conditions:

1.

\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{A}
: The parameter space is the action space.

2.

f(\cdot|u)=\delta(\cdot-u)
: Sampling
A\sim f(\cdot|u)
yields
A=u
.

Under these conditions,
{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})
outputs parameters
U\in\mathcal{U}
, which are directly actions in
\mathcal{A}
. Thus, we can write
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})={{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})
, where
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})\in\mathcal{A}
.

Next, consider the parameter-space value function
{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)
. From Proposition
3.2
,
{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|U)}[q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},A)]
.
Since
f(A|U)=\delta(A-U)
, the expectation becomes
q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)
. So,
{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)=q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)
, where
U\in\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{A}
.

This means the parameter-space critic
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)
is estimating the action-value function
q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)
. Thus, we can write
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)=Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)
, where
U\in\mathcal{A}
.

Substituting these equivalences into the DPPG gradient estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{A}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A)|_{A={\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}.

This is precisely the DPG gradient estimator (
Equation

6
). Thus,

\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}^{\text{DPG}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
.
∎

Proposition

4.4
.

Assume
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}=q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)
and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}={\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
. Then,

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right].

Further, if the expectation of the action-conditioned variance is greater than zero, then

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right)<\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right).

Proof.

Proposition
3.2
states
{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},U)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|U)}[q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t},A)]
. Given
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}={\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
and
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}=q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
, this becomes
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|U)}[Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)]
. Note that
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)
and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)
are distinct critic functions. The use of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
for both signifies that they are learned approximators. In the context of this proof, we can think of
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
as separate approximators, each utilizing a corresponding subset of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}
.

Starting with the DPPG estimator (assuming continuous
\mathcal{A}
; discrete case is analogous with sums):

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|U)}[Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)]|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\left.\left(\nabla_{U}\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(A|U)Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA\right)\right|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\left.\left(\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{U}f(A|U)Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA\right)\right|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{U}f(A|U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}f(A|U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}f(A|{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA.

The differentiability under the integral sign is justified by
4.1
. The last line follows from the chain rule, where
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}f(A|{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}f(A|U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}
.

Using
{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})=f(A|{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))
and the log-derivative trick, we can express the DPPG estimator as:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t}){\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\,dA

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\right].

The LR estimator is
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})(Q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}}(S_{t},A)-V(S_{t}))
.
Its expectation is
\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right]
. The term involving the baseline
V(S_{t})
vanishes in expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})V(S_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=V(S_{t})\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=V(S_{t})\int_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})\,dA

\displaystyle=V(S_{t})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\int_{\mathcal{A}}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})\,dA=V(S_{t})\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(1)=0.

Thus,
\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}\log{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(A|S_{t})Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)\right]
.
This shows
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(\cdot|S_{t})}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right]
.

For variance reduction, let
X=\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)
and
Y=\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
. We have
Y=\mathbb{E}[X|S_{t},{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})]
(expectation over
A
).
By the law of total variance:
\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{V}(X|S_{t},{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))]+\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{E}[X|S_{t},{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})])
.
This translates to

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right)=\mathbb{E}_{S_{t}}\left[\mathbb{V}_{A}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\middle|S_{t}\right)\right]+\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right).

If
\mathbb{E}_{S_{t}}\left[\mathbb{V}_{A}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\middle|S_{t}\right)\right]>0
(i.e., the action-conditioned variance is positive on average), then
\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right)<\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{LR}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},A)\right)
.
∎

Proposition

4.5
.

Assume
\mathcal{A}
is continuous,
Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}=q_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)
, and
{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}={\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
in
\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
. Then,

\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right].

Further, if the expectation of the noise-induced variance is greater than zero, then

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right)<\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right).

Proof.

For the RP estimator, the action is generated as
A=g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})
, where
\epsilon\sim p(\cdot)
. For consistency with DPPG notation, we can write
A=g(\epsilon;U)
, where
U={{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})\in\mathcal{U}
represents all relevant learnable distribution parameters. Thus, the distribution
f(\cdot|U)
of the random variable
A
is induced by
g(\epsilon;U)
with
\epsilon\sim p(\cdot)
.

Similar to the proof of
Proposition

4.4
, given the critics are the corresponding true action-value functions, we have:

\displaystyle{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim f(\cdot|U)}[Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)]=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;{{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})))\right],

where we use a change of variables to express the expectation in terms of the noise
\epsilon
.

Now, we can express the DPPG gradient as:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}{\bar{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},U)|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}[Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;U))]|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[\nabla_{U}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;U))|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;U))|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})))\right].

The differentiability under the integral sign is justified by
4.1
. The last line follows from the chain rule, where
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})))=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{U}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;U))|_{U={\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})}
.

On the other hand, the RP gradient is:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})^{\top}\nabla_{A}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)|_{A=g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))^{\top}\nabla_{A}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)|_{A=g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))),

where we use the chain rule again in the last equation:
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t})))=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))^{\top}\nabla_{A}Q_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}}(S_{t},A)|_{A=g(\epsilon;{\bar{\pi}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}(S_{t}))}
.
Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim p}\left[\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right].

The variance reduction argument is similar to that in Proposition
4.4
. Let
X=\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)
and
Y=\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})
. We have
Y=\mathbb{E}[X|S_{t},\epsilon]
(expectation over
\epsilon
).
By the law of total variance:
\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{V}(X|S_{t},\epsilon)]+\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{E}[X|S_{t},\epsilon])
.
This translates to

\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right)=\mathbb{E}_{S_{t}}\left[\mathbb{V}_{\epsilon}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\middle|S_{t}\right)\right]+\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right).

If
\mathbb{E}_{S_{t}}\left[\mathbb{V}_{\epsilon}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\middle|S_{t}\right)\right]>0
(i.e., the noise-induced variance is positive on average), then
\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{DPPG}}\hat{J}({{\bar{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t})\right)<\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\text{RP}}\hat{J}({\pi_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}};S_{t},\epsilon)\right)
.
∎

(
Xiong et al. 2022

. We assume an on-policy linear function approximation setting and use TD learning to learn the critic. See

Assumption A.1
.

For any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist positive constants
{L_{{\bar{\pi}}}},L_{\phi}
and
\lambda_{\Phi}
, such that
(1)
\|{{\bar{\pi}}_{\theta_{1}}(s)}-{{\bar{\pi}}_{\theta_{2}}(s)}\|\leq{L_{{\bar{\pi}}}}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|,\forall s\in\mathcal{S}

(2)

(3) the matrix

{\sigma}_{\min}({\Psi}_{\theta})\geq\lambda_{{\Psi}}
.

Assumption A.2
.

For any
{u}_{1},{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exist positive constants
L_{\bar{p}},L_{\bar{r}}
, such that
(1) the parameter-space transition kernel satisfies
|{\bar{p}}(s^{\prime}|s,{u}_{1})-{\bar{p}}(s^{\prime}|s,{u}_{2})|\leq L_{\bar{p}}\|{u}_{1}-{u}_{2}\|,\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}

(2) the parameter-space reward function satisfies
|{\bar{r}}(s,{u}_{1})-{\bar{r}}(s,{u}_{2})|\leq L_{\bar{r}}\|{u}_{1}-{u}_{2}\|,\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Assumption A.3
.

For any
{u}_{1},{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a positive constant
L_{\bar{q}}
, such that
\|\nabla_{u}{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\theta}}(s,{u}_{1})\!-\!\nabla_{u}{\bar{q}}_{{\bar{\pi}}_{\theta}}(s,{u}_{2})\|\leq L_{\bar{q}}\|{u}_{1}-{u}_{2}\|,\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d},s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Assumption A.4
.

The feature function
\phi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\|\phi(\cdot,\cdot)\|\leq C_{\phi}
for some positive constant
C_{\phi}
. In addition, we define
A=\mathbb{E}_{d_{\theta}}\left[\phi(x)(\gamma\phi(x^{\prime})-\phi(x))^{\top}\right]
and

, and assume that
A
and
D
are non-singular. We further assume that the absolute value of the eigenvalues of
A
are uniformly lower bounded, i.e.,
|\sigma(A)|\geq\lambda_{A}
for some positive constant
\lambda_{A}
.

Proposition A.5

(Compatible function approximation)
.

A function estimator
{\bar{Q}}_{w}(s,{u})
is compatible with a policy
{\bar{\pi}}_{\theta}
, i.e.,
\nabla J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}{\bar{\pi}}_{\theta}(s)\nabla_{u}{\bar{Q}}_{w}(s,{u})|_{{u}={\bar{\pi}}_{\theta}(s)}\right]

2.

minimizes the mean square error
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}\left[\xi(s;\theta,w)^{\top}\xi(s;\theta,w)\right]

Remark A.8
.

B.1
Policy parameterization and action sampling

When the action space is multidimensional, we treat each dimension independently. For simplicity, our exposition will focus on the unidimensional case in the remaining of the paper.

Discrete action spaces

We use the categorical policy parameterization:
A\sim f(\cdot|[p_{1},\cdots,p_{N}]^{\top})
, where
f(x|[p_{1},\cdots,p_{N}]^{\top})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\mathbb{I}(x=i)}
is the probability mass function for the categorical distribution.
For DPAC, we choose the probability vector
{u}=[p_{1},\cdots,p_{N}]^{\top}
as the distribution parameters. We define the distribution parameters corresponding to an action
A
to be the one-hot vector
U_{A}=\onehot(A)
.

Continuous action spaces

Assume the action space is
[a_{\min},a_{\max}]
. We use the Gaussian policy parameterization that is used in TD3:
A=\clip(\mu+\epsilon,a_{\min},a_{\max}),\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)
. Same as TD3, we restrict the mean
\mu
to be within
[a_{\min},a_{\max}]
using a squashing function:

\mu=\frac{{u}_{\mu}+1}{2}(a_{\max}-a_{\min})+a_{\min},\quad{u}_{\mu}=\tanh(\text{logit}_{\mu}),

where
\text{logit}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
is the actor network’s output for
\mu
.
While TD3 uses a fixed
\sigma_{\text{TD3}}=0.1*\frac{a_{\max}-a_{\min}}{2}
, we allow the learnable standard deviation to be within a range
\sigma\in[\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}]
:

\log\sigma=\frac{{u}_{\sigma}+1}{2}*(\log\sigma_{\max}-\log\sigma_{\min})+\log\sigma_{\min},\quad{u}_{\sigma}=\tanh(\text{logit}_{\sigma}),

where
\text{logit}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}
is the actor network’s output for
\sigma
. For AC-RP, the reparameterization function is
g_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\epsilon;S_{t})=\clip(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(S_{t})+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}(S_{t})\epsilon,a_{\min},a_{\max}),\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. For DPAC, we choose the distribution parameters to be
{u}=[{u}_{\mu},{u}_{\sigma}]^{\top}\in[-1,1]^{2}
so that the parameter space is consistent across the mean and standard deviation dimensions.
Since we lower bound the standard deviation space to encourage exploration, we define the distribution parameters corresponding to an action
A
to be
U_{A}=[\frac{2A}{a_{\max}-a_{\min}},-1]^{\top}
to approximate the Dirac delta distribution, which corresponds to
\mu=A
and
\sigma=\sigma_{\min}
.

B.2
Policy evaluation in bandits

K-Armed Bandit

We use a K-armed bandit with
K=3
and a deterministic reward function:

r(a_{1})=0,\quad r(a_{2})=0.5,\quad r(a_{3})=1.

Bimodal Continuous Bandit

We use a continuous bandit with a bimodal reward function that is deterministic. Specifically, the reward function is the normalized summation of two Gaussians’ density functions whose standard deviations are both
0.5
and whose means are
-1
and
1
, respectively:

r(a)=e^{-\frac{(a+1)^{2}}{0.5}}+e^{-\frac{(a-1)^{2}}{0.5}}.

We restrict the action space to be
[a_{\min},a_{\max}]=[-2,2]
. The standard standard deviation is constrained to
[\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}]=[e^{-3},e]
.

Critic network architecture

To be consistent with the RL settings, we use the same critic network architecture as those in
Sections

B.4
and
B.5
. Specifically, we use a two-layer MLP network with the concatenated state and action vector as input. We reduce the hidden size from
256
to
16
and use a dummy state vector with a value of
1
.

Experimental details

We keep the policy evaluation (PE) policy fixed and update the parameter-space critic function for
2000
steps using either
Equation

12
or
Equation

14
. In K-Armed Bandit, the PE policy is
\bar{\pi}_{\text{PE}}=u_{\text{PE}}=[1/3,1/3,1/3]
; in Bimodal Continuous Bandit, the PE policy is
\bar{\pi}_{\text{PE}}=u_{\text{PE}}=[0,0.5]
(corresponding to
\mu=0
and
\log\sigma=0.0
). The hyperparameters are the same as those of DPAC in
Table

3
, except that the batch size is
32
, and the actor is kept fixed to the corresponding PE policy.
