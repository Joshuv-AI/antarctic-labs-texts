Inferring asymptomatic carriers of antimicrobial-resistant organisms in hospitals using genomic, microbiological and patient mobility data
Authors: Sen Pei, Dwayne Seeram, Seth Blumberg, Bo Shopsin, Anne-Catrin Uhlemann, Jeffrey Shaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsymptomatic carriers of antimicrobial-resistant organisms (AMROs) can unwittingly transmit these pathogens in hospitals, contributing to the burden of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). Surveillance in hospitals can involve different types of observations; however, a framework to coherently synthesize these datasets to identify AMRO carriers is lacking. Here, we develop a new inference framework combining a data-driven mechanistic transmission model and multimodal observations from clinical cultures, electronic health records, patient mobility, and genomic data. Using extensive simulated outbreaks, we validate the inference framework for AMROs with various levels of community importation and hospital transmission and evaluate the utility of different combinations of data sources. Inference results show that using multimodal observations consistently improves the accuracy in identifying AMRO carriers. We apply the inference framework to carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) at an urban quaternary care hospital in New York City, United States and find that the addition of even sparsely sampled genome sequence data to patient characteristics supports more accurate identification of CRKP carriers. Model simulations suggest that inference-guided targeted isolation leads to a greater reduction of AMRO burdens compared to alternative, heuristic approaches. Thus, the synergistic effect of utilizing multimodal observations for estimating AMRO carriage risk may inform improved interventions in hospital settings.Subject terms: Bacterial infection, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Epidemiology

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an urgent threat to human health. In 2019, 5 million deaths were associated with AMR globally1. Existing and emerging antimicrobial-resistant organisms (AMROs) are leading causes of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and impose heavy burdens on healthcare systems2. To reduce HAI burdens, accurate measurement of AMR transmission in healthcare facilities is needed. However, this task remains challenging due to frequent asymptomatic carriage of AMROs. Many AMROs can colonize people without causing disease for long periods of time3–5, during which these agents can spread unnoticed to other patients, healthcare workers, and even the general community6–9.

Different types of data can be collected to understand AMR transmission in healthcare systems. Clinical cultures from patients serve as a routine data source for AMR surveillance; personal health information in electronic health records (EHR) has been linked to clinical culture results to identify risk factors for AMR infection10–15; and patient contact data have supported modeling studies to understand AMRO transmission16–24. In research settings, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has been recently used in conjunction with epidemiological data to identify transmission events25–30. However, these surveillance efforts are constrained by their reliance on clinical cultures that originate from patients with confirmed or suspected infection. As a result, they capture only a fraction of the total population of colonized individuals, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of AMRO carriage and transmission. To address this limitation, we need methods to infer the presence of missing colonization intermediates and to model transmission dynamics beyond culture-confirmed infections.

Data-driven mathematical models have been used to study the transmission dynamics and control of AMROs in hospital settings31–44. Despite much effort, assimilating multimodal and sparsely observed data into a unified modeling framework to identify asymptomatic AMRO carriers remains an unmet challenge. In this study, we developed a new computational framework to synergistically use clinical culture results, WGS, and patient characteristics to infer individual-level AMRO carriage probabilities. We applied the framework to carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), an urgent threat in healthcare facilities with heavy disease and economic burdens45. CRKP belongs to the family of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), which can rapidly spread resistance between bacteria species through sharing mobile genetic elements46.

Globally, the prevalence of CRKP colonization in healthcare facilities varies by location and ranges from 0.13% to 22%47. However, hospitals do not routinely screen for CRKP carriage, and surveillance relies on clinical cultures from patients, overlooking asymptomatic colonizers. To address this limitation, we leveraged real-world hospitalization records spanning 5 years at an urban quaternary care hospital to develop an individual-level dynamic model informed by a time-varying patient-to-patient contact network to simulate CRKP transmission. We then applied a suite of statistical and mathematical techniques to assimilate multimodal observations, including genome sequences, clinical culture results, and patient characteristics, into this model, allowing us to estimate patient-level CRKP carriage probabilities despite the inherent data sparsity. Using both synthetic outbreaks and real-world data, we demonstrated that the synergistic use of different types of observations augmented the utility of these data sources and supported more accurate identification of carriers. We further performed data-driven simulations to evaluate target isolations informed by the inference results. Compared to heuristic approaches based on length of stay, risk factors, and contact tracing, the inference-guided identification led to greater reduction of colonization.

Results

Description of CRKP surveillance data

In this study, we used hospitalization records, patient characteristics, clinical culture results, and WGS data of CRKP from an urban quaternary care hospital. This dataset contains admission, discharge and transfer records providing information on the rooms where patients stayed or visited on each day. Patient characteristics were derived from the hospital EHR system, including 24 variables covering demographics, medical conditions, medical procedures, hospitalization history, antibiotic use, and other potential risk factors (Supplementary Information). Clinical culture results for samples that grew CRKP include the date and site of culture collection (blood, respiratory tract, urine, wound). A patient was defined CRKP positive if at least one sample collected from any of four body sites was positive for CRKP. We focused on a 5-year period from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2016, for which a subset of CRKP isolates were available for whole genome sequencing. During this period, 25,373 culture tests were performed for CRKP, among which 411 positive patients were discovered. 220 (53.5%) CRKP positive isolates were available and sequenced48,49. The hospitalization records for this 5-year period contained 193,909 distinct patients in 110 rooms. Positive CRKP isolates were identified in 67 rooms. Use of these datasets in this study was approved by Columbia IRB AAAT3037. Individual patient consent was waived for this retrospective data analysis.

Modeling AMRO transmission

AMRO transmission in hospitals is shaped by heterogenous contacts between individuals. To represent individual variation in contacts, we constructed a time-varying contact network using real-world hospitalization records (Methods). Specifically, patients staying in the same room on the same day were connected in the contact network, as direct patient-to-patient contact and shared healthcare workers (HCWs, e.g., nurses and doctors) within the room may facilitate transmission between patients. This contact network was updated daily, following the patient flow of new admissions, discharges, and transfers within the hospital. An example network comprised of all contacts over a 7-day period (Fig. 1a) shows that patient contacts formed several densely connected clusters. The largest contact cluster centered around patients with longer lengths of stay (light blue color) and larger numbers of contacts (large node size). We analyzed the hospitalization data from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2016 and observed heterogeneous distributions of patient length of stay (LOS) (Fig. 1b) and contact durations between patients (Fig. 1c). This complex spatiotemporal contact pattern was represented in the time-varying contact network.

Building upon this contact network, we developed a stochastic agent-based model (ABM) to simulate AMRO transmission, incorporating both within-hospital transmission and importation from outside the hospital (Fig. 1d). In this model, patients were either susceptible (S) or colonized with CRKP (C). Each new admission was colonized with an importation probability specific to each patient, estimated using patient characteristics from EHR data (Methods). Within the hospital, a susceptible patient could be colonized with a transmission probability if linked to a carrier in the contact network. Colonized patients returned to the susceptible state at a spontaneous decolonization rate. The model further considered heterogeneous testing effort across rooms (Supplementary Fig. S1) and imperfect test performance (Methods, Supplementary Information). Because cultures may not be collected from the body sites where CRKP is present, false negative results may be obtained. To represent this phenomenon and imperfect test performance, we defined an effective sensitivity as the probability of testing positive given a culture is taken from a colonized patient, reflecting the compound effect of imperfect assay sensitivity and incomplete sampling of positive patients. This ABM was integrated stochastically to represent the uncertainty in transmission dynamics. Transmission dynamics were controlled by three parameters - the mean importation rate for new admissions γ, the transmission rate in the hospital β, and the spontaneous decolonization rate α.

Due to the stochastic nature of the ABM, different realizations of simulations produced distinct outcomes. To enable inference of the likelihood of colonization given observed data, we described the evolution of individual-level colonization probabilities using a network model, informed by the contact network and epidemiological parameters (Methods). This network model served as a substrate to assimilate different types of observations and was used to infer colonization probabilities for patients without direct observations on colonization status.

The inference framework

We aim to assimilate information from hospitalization records, patient characteristics (e.g., pre-existing health conditions, previous medical procedures, hospitalization history prior to admission, etc.), clinical culture results, and WGS data (Fig. 2, Methods). To synergistically use multimodal observations, we developed an inference framework with three steps: 1) Use a variety of methods to independently estimate individual colonization probabilities from different data sources. 2) Synthesize those estimates from different observation types into single posterior estimates. 3) Incorporation into the network model for further inference of colonization probabilities of unobserved patients.

The contact information in (4) hospitalization records was implicitly represented in this network model.

We first harmonized different types of observations to a uniform format – estimates of individual colonization probabilities. Culture results can be used to estimate colonization probabilities with the consideration of test performance (Supplementary Information). Patient characteristics from EHR can be converted to individual colonization probabilities upon admission using a risk factor-based logistic regression model (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Fig. S2). WGS data can identify potential transmission events using genetic relatedness, measured by SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism) distance. If WGS data suggest a transmission between two patients, intuitively, other patients on the network path connecting these two patients should have higher colonization probabilities as the transmission may have occurred following that path. A network-based method was developed to estimate the colonization probabilities of patients on the network paths between genetically related patients (Methods, Supplementary Information). This method augmented the utility of a small number of WGS samples to a much larger patient population.

While we converted data from multiple sources into estimates of colonization probabilities, the majority of patients still had no direct observation of their AMRO carriage status (e.g., clinical culture results). To address this data sparsity issue and synthesize estimates derived from different data sources, we used a sequential individual-level inference (SILI) framework41,50, an efficient Bayesian algorithm using ensemble message passing, to estimate colonization probabilities of patients without observations. Specifically, the SILI algorithm employed a network model that describes the evolution of patient colonization probabilities in a hospital. Individual colonization probabilities of patients in the network model were updated sequentially at each time step, during which information from sparse observations was propagated to reach patients without observations (Methods, Supplementary Fig. S3). This update was performed repeatedly until the most recent observation time. The estimated colonization probability for each patient at a given time was set as the posterior value after model update. We have previously employed the SILI algorithm to identify asymptomatic carriers of MRSA in 66 Swedish hospitals using only culture test data41.

Validation using simulated outbreaks

We validated the inference framework using model-simulated outbreaks, for which the ground-truth colonization status of each patient is available. Using the ABM, we simulated an outbreak during a 50-week period using real-world patient movement data. Model parameters were set to generate an outbreak with a similar magnitude of the number of observed positive cultures as seen in real-world CRKP data (Methods, Supplementary Fig. S4). We generated synthetic observations of culture test results with the consideration of heterogeneous testing and imperfect test performance. In addition, we generated a set of observed transmission events among positive patients, mimicking the information derived from WGS data (Methods). In total, 131 positive cultures were observed during the 50-week period (99 positive cultures reported during the same period in real-world data) and 14 transmission events were identified among positive patients.

To compare the utility of different types of observations, we performed inference using different combinations of data sources: patient characteristics (EHR), clinical culture results (Test), and transmission events identified by WGS data (Seq). We started by using patient characteristics in the EHR system alone, predicting the colonization probability of each patient using a risk factor-based logistic regression model and information available prior to admission (Supplementary Information). This statistical model served as a benchmark for model performance. We then ran the inference algorithm using (1) Test, (2) Test and Seq, (3) Test and EHR, and (4) Test, EHR and Seq. Inference performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, for which a better method has a larger area under the curve (AUC). Note, the algorithm using clinical culture results (Test) alone is the original SILI algorithm developed in our previous work41.

We examined the inference results for patients hospitalized on day 300 of the simulation (Fig. 3a). The inference algorithm, regardless of the data sources used, outperformed the benchmark using the statistical model. Adding EHR data to Test data provided a marginal improvement. However, using Seq data in addition to Test data did not improve the inference, possibly due to the small number of identified transmission events (14 transmission events among 47,249 unique patients hospitalized during the 50-week period). Using Test, EHR, and Seq data collectively in the inference produced the best performance, especially among patients with estimated higher colonization probabilities. Analysis on other days of the simulation (day 40, 100 and 150) resulted in similar findings (Supplementary Fig. S5).

To better understand the added benefit of using Seq data, we evaluated the inference for the subset of 113 patients on the network paths connecting genetically related patients (Fig. 3b). Specifically, we focused on their colonization states on their last day on these network paths. We observed a stronger improvement in inference using Seq data among this subgroup of patients. We additionally included the neighbors of these patients in the contact network and found that the improvement persisted within this larger group of 4310 patients (Fig. 3c). This analysis indicates that inclusion of Seq data improves inference for patient-days that are spatiotemporally close to identified transmission events.

As interventions in hospital settings should target high-risk carriers, we compared the accuracy of identifying colonized patients among top-ranking patients, as selected using different approaches. Each day, we ranked the colonization probabilities of hospitalized patients estimated using different data sources and computed the fraction of colonized patients among the 50 top-ranking patients. The synergistic use of Test, EHR, and Seq data achieved a higher accuracy over time (Fig. 3d), especially during the latter half of the simulation period.

To explore the generalizability of the inference framework to other AMROs with distinct transmission dynamics, we validated the inference algorithm using additional synthetic outbreaks with various transmission rates and importation intensities (Supplementary Figs. S6–S12). These scenarios include different importation rates (0.03–0.2 per admission), transmission rates (0.004–0.01 per contact per day), and effective sensitivities (10–30%). The pathogen prevalence among hospitalized patients ranges from around 20% to 60%. Our findings remained robust across these scenarios. Additionally, the ranking of estimated colonization probabilities was robust to biases in the estimated transmission and importation parameters (Methods, Supplementary Fig. S13).

Inference of real-world CRKP colonization

We applied the inference to real-world CRKP surveillance data collected from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2016, including clinical culture results and WGS data. To estimate transmission and importation rates used in the inference, we calibrated the ABM to the number of positive patients confirmed every 4 weeks, fixing the effective sensitivity parameter for clinical cultures (Methods, Supplementary Fig. S14). During this period, 411 positive patients were identified using clinical cultures, among which 220 isolates were sequenced (see data details in Methods). 130 (59.1%) isolates belong to the dominant sequence type ST258. After filtering low-quality sequences, we analyzed 124 ST258 sequences (Methods). To find genetically related isolates, we first selected the 125 pairs of isolates with a SNP distance within 15 (Supplementary Fig. S15, note one isolate may appear in multiple pairs) and then performed an exhaustive search of the contact network during the 5-year period to exclude patient pairs without epidemiological linkage. We found that only 53 pairs of patients were connected via chains of person-to-person contact (including multi-step contact chains with intermediate patients). In running the inference, we used these high-confidence transmission events supported by both genomic relatedness and history of contacts in the hospital to estimate the colonization probabilities of patients on potential contact chains.

Different from synthetic outbreaks, the ground truth of patients’ colonization status is unknown for most hospitalized patients. As a result, we cannot use ROC curves to evaluate the inference performance. To circumvent this difficulty, we generated out-of-sample predictions of colonization probabilities for all patients and examined the relative ranking of confirmed positive patients among concurrently hospitalized patients. For an accurate inference algorithm, the confirmed patients should be ranked higher among other hospitalized patients. Specifically, for each positive patient whose sample was collected after week 52 (starting from January 1, 2012), we ran the inference algorithm using clinical culture results and WGS data available until 1 week prior to the sample collection week. For instance, if a positive patient i was sampled in week T, we used positive cultures and sequences collected until week T−1. We ran the inference during a 52-week period prior to the sample collection week, from week T−52 to week T−1. Then we ran the master equations from week T−1 to week T and predicted the colonization probabilities of patients hospitalized during week T. The relative ranking of patient i was computed as the percentile of i’s colonization probability among all patients staying in the hospital on the date of sample collection. To evaluate the utility of different data sources in inference, we performed the inference using different combinations of datasets: 1) Culture test results only (Test); 2) Culture test results and EHR data (Test and EHR); 3) Culture test results, EHR data, and high-confidence transmission events identified using sequence data (Test, EHR, and Seq). We additionally used three other methods to rank patients’ colonization risk: 4) A risk factor-based logistic regression model (EHR, Supplementary Materials); 5) Length of stay in the prior 52 weeks (LOS); 6) The number of total person–day contacts with positive patients in the prior 52 weeks (Contact tracing).

We compared the fraction of 411 confirmed positive patients ranked within a given percentile using different approaches, focusing on high-risk patients within the top 20% (Fig. 4a). For the inference using only clinical culture results, 23.8% positive patients were ranked in the top 5% among hospitalized patients on the day of testing, and 28.5% positive patients were ranked within the top 10%. The inclusion of EHR data substantially improved the inference – 29.0% positive patients were ranked within the top 5% and 38.4% positive patients were ranked within the top 10%. The additional inclusion of sequence data marginally improved the performance – 30.2% positive patients were ranked within the top 5% and 40.6% positive patients were ranked within the top 10%. This marginal improvement may be due to the small number of sequences compared to the total number of patients in the hospital. At the 5th percentile, the inference algorithm outperformed all three heuristic approaches. The results were robust to the assumed effective sensitivity of clinical culture tests in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. S16).

Modeling targeted isolation to reduce CRKP burdens

We performed simulations to evaluate the potential outcomes of targeted isolation informed by different approaches for identifying colonized patients. Specifically, we used the estimated parameters calibrated to the real-world CRKP data and ran simulations of the ABM starting from January 1, 2012. On the first day of week 53, we performed inference to rank the colonization probabilities of hospitalized patients using confirmed positive patients reported within the last 52 weeks. We then generated nowcasts of patient colonization probabilities on each day of the following 4 weeks, when new hospitalization information (new admissions, discharges, and transfers) became available each day. On the first day of each of the following 4 weeks, we selected the top 1% of patients (10–13 patients depending on the number hospitalized patients) with higher colonization probabilities and isolated these patients (i.e., removed all contacts with other patients during that week). This procedure (inference every 4 weeks and targeted isolation on the first day of each of the following 4 weeks) was repeated every 4 weeks until December 31, 2016. In the inference, we only used data from clinical culture results and patient characteristics in EHR, acknowledging that WGS data are limited and may not be available in real time. We also used the three alternative approaches (LOS, EHR, and Contact tracing) to identify potential colonization patients. A baseline model without any isolation was also run. To represent the stochastic dynamics of the ABM, 100 independent simulations were performed for each approach. We obtained the distributions of confirmed positive patients (observed using real-world culture test records and imperfect sensitivity) and total colonization (patient-days) in the hospital from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2016.

The targeted isolation informed by the developed inference algorithm led to the largest reduction of confirmed positive patients and total colonization compared with the baseline without interventions. On average, the inference-guided targeted isolation reduced 15.9% of confirmed positive patients (Fig. 5a) and 14.8% of total colonization (Fig. 5b), outperforming all other approaches (one-sided Wilcoxon rank sum tests, p < 0.001). Another set of simulations were performed in which the top 5% patients were screened. The reduction of confirmed positive patients and total colonization further increased for all approaches. However, the inference-guided targeted isolation remains to achieve the best performance (p < 0.001), reducing 28.0% of confirmed cases (Fig. 5c) and 22.8% of total colonization (Fig. 5d), respectively.

Discussion

Asymptomatic colonization of AMROs imposes great challenges to infection control in healthcare systems. Current surveillance efforts in hospitals focus on clinical infections, thus missing many asymptomatic carriers who can silently spread pathogens. While clinical cultures are collected for infection diagnosis, and genome sequencing can be used to identify transmission events, these datasets have limited utility for identifying asymptomatic carriers. In this study, we developed an inference framework that jointly uses multimodal observations, including patient mobility, patient characteristics, clinical culture results, and genomic sequence data, to estimate patient’s colonization risk. Our approach harmonizes information from different datasets and then synthesizes it into a network model using a Bayesian approach that propagates information to patients without observations on AMRO carriage. Applying this framework to CRKP data demonstrates the synergistic effect of utilizing multimodal observations for estimating AMRO carriage risk. Notably, our findings suggest that even sparsely collected WGS data can improve the inference for a subset of patients who are close to identified transmission paths.

Due to resource constraints, WGS data are often limited to research studies or small cluster outbreak investigations. Even within research settings, sequencing efforts typically rely on clinical cultures, since screening for carriage and sequencing isolates are costly. The lack of comprehensive evaluation for carrier status or environmental reservoirs can lead to missing information on intermediate transmission hosts and reservoirs. This may lead to genetically related isolates with unknown epidemiological linkage as well as isolate pairs with higher SNP distances (e.g., between 10 and 30) that are linked in incompletely resolved transmission chains. The inference framework developed here provides a potential solution by helping to resolve these incomplete transmission chains and guide targeted surveillance sampling in combination with WGS. By optimizing sampling strategies, this approach could both enhance the cost-effectiveness of genomic surveillance and improve the detection of transmission pathways.

In the validation of the inference algorithm and numerical control experiments, we selected several heuristic baseline metrics to identify AMRO carriers, such as LOS and the number of contacts with positive patients. These metrics were selected due to the convenience of their computation and implementation in clinical settings. Future studies can develop more sophisticated and effective centrality metrics for time-evolving contact networks to identify AMRO carriers and spreaders51.

The validation of the inference algorithm using real-world CRKP data may be affected by the sample collection bias. For instance, the performance of contact tracing for the subset of patients close to WGS samples increased substantially compared to its performance for all positive patients in Fig. 4a. This is possibly due to the tendency to collect samples from the close contacts of positive patients in clinical practices, creating a biased validation dataset favoring contact tracing. In addition, given that the model was built using observed data on colonization, it is also likely to generate bias towards existing screening practices such as screening of close contacts, surveillance of immunocompromised patients, etc.

The inference results for CRKP underscore the challenges in identifying asymptomatic spreaders. Even using all data sources, only 54.0% of lab-confirmed positive patients were ranked within top 20% (Fig. 4a). This is partly due to the inherent uncertainty in CRKP carriage, high false-negative rates, and the biased, incomplete observation of positive patients. Additionally, 31.3% of hospitalized patients have no historical medical records upon admission, creating a cold start problem for carriage risk estimation. The problem is further exacerbated by the sparse clinical culture data – on average, only 13 (~1%) clinical cultures were tested each day among 1000–1300 hospitalized patients. How data richness affects inference performance can be explored using model simulations in future works.

While we applied the inference framework to CRKP in a single hospital, this approach may be generalized to other AMROs with distinct community prevalence and hospital transmissibility, as indicated by the experiments on a variety of synthetic outbreaks (Supplementary Figs. S6–S12). Our findings also suggest that even sparsely collected WGS data can improve the inference for a large group of patients who are close to identified transmission paths. This implies that the inference framework may be applicable to hospitals with limited sequencing capacity. For pathogens with a very high prevalence in hospitals, while the inference system or other heuristic approaches may support carriage risk prediction, exhaustive case identification and isolation for AMRO control may not be practical due to a lack of resources to effectively isolate a large number of colonized patients. In this case, universal control measures (e.g., improved hand hygiene) may be more effective than targeted control. The threshold of pathogen prevalence for switching control strategies needs to be further studied.

Some study limitations should be noted. First, the model simplified several processes of AMR transmission. We did not explicitly model the mobility of HCWs and potential environmental reservoirs due to a lack of such data; however, transmission mediated by HCWs or environmental reservoirs was partly accounted for in the co-location contact network. Future works may develop a bipartite graph to represent the contact network between HCWs and patients, in which vertices are divided into two disjoint sets (in this case, patients and HCWs), should this dataset become available. Second, we assumed a uniform transmission rate across hospital rooms, neglecting transmission heterogeneity in different locations. However, sensitivity analyses showed that the inference was robust to mis-specified transmission rates (Supplementary Fig. S10) and variations in individual transmissibility50. Third, we did not model symptom development for CRKP infections, which would trigger culture tests in clinical practices. We addressed this limitation by collecting clinical cultures from patients who were tested in the real-world data to ensure that the observation model captured the heterogeneity in testing efforts across rooms. Lastly, there are several data limitations in the EHR datasets. Many variables have been pre-processed and dichotomized. Therefore, we can only use dichotomous data for these variables in the regression model. Additionally, we do not have access to prior known colonization with other AMRO. Given the well-reported plasmid and transposon transfer of resistance between Enterobacterales, knowing such information may better inform the CRKP colonization risk.

Our study showed the potential utility of the inference framework in reducing AMR burdens. Simulations of targeted isolation suggest that focusing on a small subset of patients with high colonization probabilities identified by the inference framework may support cost-effective interventions to reduce AMR infections. Future studies using this inference framework may also address other critical questions to reduce AMR transmission in the hospital – elucidating whether asymptomatic carriers are as transmissible as symptomatic infections; determining whether certain locations are more favorable for transmission; and evaluating the clinical significance of asymptomatic carriage.

Methods

ABM and the observation model

The ABM has three parameters controlling transmission dynamics: the mean importation rate for new admissions γ, the transmission rate β, and the spontaneous decolonization rate α. The transmission rate quantifies the probability of AMRO transmission between patients staying in the same room through direct patient-to-patient contact or HCW-medicated indirect contact. To keep the model parsimonious, we assumed all rooms shared the same transmission rate. The decolonization rate was estimated by fitting an exponential distribution to published empirical data recording CRKP colonization status of patients after discharge5. We estimated the mean duration of CRKP carriage was 6 months and fixed α = 1/180 per day in the model.

The ABM incorporated individual variation in colonization probability upon admission. Previous studies have linked a number of risk factors to CRKP colonization risk10–13. We estimated the relative colonization probability for each patient using a logistic regression model trained with patient characteristics and CRKP culture results obtained prior to January 1, 2012 (Supplementary Information). These relative colonization probabilities were then rescaled to match the mean importation rate γ to set the initial colonization probabilities for patients upon their first admissions. For patients who were admitted multiple times, we only set the colonization probability for their first admissions; after discharge, colonized patients could recover following the decolonization rate until re-admitted, allowing re-introduction of AMROs from previously hospitalized patients. We did not consider AMRO acquisition outside the hospital for re-admitted patients due to a lack of empirical data on community transmission.

Culture tests that yielded positive CRKP cultures were heterogeneously distributed across rooms (Supplementary Fig. S1). To model this observation process, in the ABM, we observed the patients who were tested in the real-world on their dates of sample collection. To model imperfect test results, we defined an effective sensitivity parameter ρ as the probability of testing positive given a culture is taken from a colonized patient, capturing the compound effect of imperfect assay sensitivity and incomplete sampling of positive patients (missing colonized body sites in sample collection). We estimated the plausible range of ρ to be 5–30% using published CRKP prevalence47, observed CRKP positive samples, and model simulations under different transmission scenarios (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Table S1). In a liver transplant cohort from the same hospital, for every CRE-infected patient, approximately 7 others are colonized with CRE26. In synthetic outbreaks, we set ρ=10%. For the inference using real-world CRKP data, we performed sensitivity analyses using ρ=10% and 15%.

Data harmonization

We converted clinical culture results for CRKP to colonization probabilities with the consideration of effective sensitivity and test sensitivity and specificity (Supplementary Information). To convert patient characteristics to individual colonization probability, we trained a risk factor-based logistic regression model using culture results obtained prior to January 1, 2012 (Supplementary Information). The logistic regression model used 24 variables spanning demography, medical conditions, procedures, antibiotic use, hospitalization history, and source of patient admission (other hospitals, homecare, and emergency rooms). We identified several key predictors for CRKP carriage, including diabetes mellitus, previous dialysis (any time prior to the admission), previous use of urinary catheter (any time prior to the admission), antibiotic uses during previous hospitalizations, and transferring from other hospitals (Supplementary Fig. S2). This model was used to estimate the relative colonization probability for each patient upon first admission during the study period, using only historical personal medical information collected prior to the admission date. For patients without prior records in the EHR system, the relative colonization probabilities were set as the intercept of the logistic regression model.

WGS data can be used to measure genetic relatedness to identify potential transmission events. This information can be used to derive the colonization probabilities of patients on the network paths connecting two genetically related patients. For each pair of patients with a SNP distance below a threshold, we first applied breadth-first search (BFS) to find all possible paths (up to a certain steps) in the time-varying contact network between the two patients. In the search of possible paths, we considered both directions of transmission. Due to the temporal order of contacts in the network, transmission following a certain direction may not be possible if there were not consecutive contact chains along that direction. For the real-world CRKP WGS data, the majority of genetically related patient pairs only have one possible transmission direction given the contact history.

We developed a Bayesian algorithm to synthesize estimates of colonization probabilities derived from different types of data. (1) and (2) were updated sequentially at each time step until the most recent observation time. A pseudo-code for the full inference algorithm and technical details are provided in Supplementary Information. Data analysis was performed using MATLAB R2021a.

Inference for synthetic outbreaks

We generated a synthetic outbreak in 50 weeks using the ABM informed by real-world hospitalization data starting from January 1, 2012. We first used personal medical information and the logistic regression model to generate the colonization probability for each patient upon first admission, perturbed by a Gaussian random noise (zero mean with a standard deviation of 5% of current value). Colonization status of each patient upon admission was randomly assigned using this probability. We then ran the ABM stochastically to obtain the colonization status for each patient over time. To generate culture test results, we observed the same group of patients as in the real-world testing records at their sample collection dates and applied the effective sensitivity – that is, if a colonized patient was observed, the test returned positive with a probability ρ; otherwise, the test returned negative. In model simulation, we did not explicitly model WGS data. Instead, we assumed that a transmission event between two positive patents can be identified by WGS if there existed a transmission chain between them in the simulated outbreak (i.e., if the two observed positive patients belong to the same transmission cluster). To generate an outbreak with a similar scale as in the real-world data, we set the model parameters as follows: mean importation rate γ=0.05 per admission; transmission rate β=0.004 per contact per day; decolonization rate α=1/180 per day; and effective sensitivity ρ=10% (combining the compound effect of not sampling the correct colonization site and assay performance). The statistics of the simulated outbreak are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. In the inference algorithm, we used 100 ensemble members to represent the empirical distribution of colonization probabilities and updated model states every week. We used the true model parameters β and γ in the inference, assuming these parameters can be estimated through model fitting. We further validated the inference framework using additional synthetic outbreaks with distinct epidemiological features (Supplementary Figs. S6–S12). To test the sensitivity of performance to model parameters, we ran inference using inaccurate parameters: (1) a 20% overestimation of β and a 20% underestimation of γ; (2) a 20% underestimation of β and a 20% overestimation of γ. The inference performance remained similar (Supplementary Fig. S13), indicating some degree of parameter inaccuracy may not substantially change the ranking of colonization probabilities.

Whole genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis

220 CRKP isolates collected from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2016 were available for whole genome sequencing or already sequenced as part of prior genomic surveillance of multidrug-resistant isolates collected at our medical center. We included 115 previously sequenced isolates (PMC1161058048, PMC720140854, PMC611744226). Sequences for the 105 newly sequenced isolates are deposited in the short read archive under BioProject number PRJNA1262416. All 105 isolates were sequenced on Illumina’s NextSeq 2000. All isolates underwent Illumina sequencing as previously described55. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from bacterial broth cultures using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit or QIAamp 96 QIAcube HT DNA isolation Kit (Qiagen). Index-tagged whole-genome libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep workflow and sequenced on a MiSeq system (Illumina). Reads were first mapped against the PubMLST56 and PlasmidFinder57 databases to determine the isolate MLST. Among the 220 isolates, 130 (59.1%) isolates belong to ST258. Other STs include ST307, ST17, ST392 and ST3359. An SNP analysis was performed. Prior to this analysis, low-quality sequences were filtered based on the following criteria: 1) Filtered out fastq N bases; 2) Excluded fastqs with a low MLST depth (<10); 3) Exclude isolates with high SNPs; 4) Excluded isolates with high ambiguities (~>100,000); 5) Include only isolates with aligned reads (≥4,440,000). These criteria allowed for sufficient coverage (alignment) of whole genome sequences in all retained STs. The SNP distance between pairs of isolates was computed for each ST. In this analysis, we focused on ST258, the majority of CRKP isolates, as few isolates in other STs had pairwise SNP distance below 15. After filtering, we ended up 124 ST258 isolates. The cumulative distribution of pairwise SNP distance is shown in Supplementary Fig. S12. To find genetically related patients, we filtered the patient pairs with a SNP distance no larger than 15. In total, 91 patient pairs among 59 distinct patients were identified within a SNP distance of 15. Note, one patient may appear in multiple pairs with other patients in the same transmission cluster.

Model calibration to real-world data

To perform the inference, model parameters such as the transmission rate and the mean importation rate are needed. We fit the transmission model to the real-world data on positive patients confirmed from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2016 to estimate these parameters. In the observation model, we sampled the patients as in the real-world records to reflect the highly heterogeneous testing efforts across rooms and considered imperfect testing with high false negative rates, assuming a 10% effective sensitivity. The number of positive patients was aggregated to a 4-week period to alleviate the issue of data sparsity in parameter fitting. As model parameters may change over time, we sequentially estimated the transmission rate and the mean importation rate in every 24-week period, assuming these parameters were stable within each period. We applied an iterated filtering framework58 coupled with the ensemble adjustment Kalman filter59 to estimate parameters. This approach has been previously used to estimate the importation rate and transmission rate of MRSA in 66 Swedish hospitals38,41, as well as infer the underreporting and basic reproductive numbers of early COVID-19 spread in China and the US60–63. The estimated transmission rates and the mean importation rates in 24-week periods are shown in Supplementary Fig. S14a. We ran model simulations using the ABM and estimated parameters for 100 independent realizations. The simulated confirmed positive numbers captured the real-world data (Supplementary Fig. S14b). As a sensitivity analysis, we also estimated model parameters using an effective sensitivity of 15% and performed inference for real-world CRKP data using the estimated parameters. The result ranking of observed carriers remained similar (Supplementary Fig. S16).
