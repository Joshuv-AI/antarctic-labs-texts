Abstract
. How does messaging about about large language models (LLMs) in public discourse influence the way people think about and interact with these models? To answer this question, we randomly assigned participants ( N = 470 N=470 ) to watch a short informational video presenting LLMs as either machines, tools, or companions—or to watch no video. We then assessed how strongly they believed LLMs to possess various mental capacities, such as the ability have intentions or remember things. We found that participants who watched the companion video reported believing that LLMs more fully possessed these capacities than did participants in other groups. In a follow-up study ( N = 604 N=604 ), we replicated these findings and found nuanced effects on how these videos impact people’s reliance on LLM-generated responses when seeking out factual information. Together, these studies highlight the impact of messaging about AI—beyond technical advances in AI—to generate broad societal impact.



1.
Introduction

As artificial intelligence (AI) technology advances, many competing narratives about AI have emerged.
Some narratives focus on their applications as
tools
to enhance productivity
(
Dell’Acqua et al. 2023
;
Weber et al. 2024a
;
Grammarly [n. d.]
;
OpenAI 2025a
)
, others on their role as
machines
to push scientific and computational frontiers
(
Jumper et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Shethiya 2024
;
Stöffelbauer 2023
;
Haritonova 2024
)
, and still others on their potential as
companions
that support social well-being
(
Stade et al. 2024
;
Demszky et al. 2023
;
Koulouri et al. 2022
;
rep [n. d.]
;
Roose 2024
)
.
However, it remains unclear how different messaging about AI shapes the beliefs people hold about what these systems
are
—what underlying capacities they possess
(
Newman and Knobe 2019
;
Gelman 2003
;
Weisman et al. 2017
;
Thellman et al. 2022
;
Colombatto and Fleming 2024
;
Colombatto et al. 2025
)
.
It might be the case that people are more inclined to believe that AI systems possess fundamentally human capacities when they encounter messaging that encourages them to construe AI in a particular way—for example, as social partners rather than as productivity-enhancing tools
(
Dennett 1989
;
Shanahan et al. 2023
)
.
Because these beliefs can have downstream effects on how people use and rely on AI systems
(
Colombatto et al. 2025
;
Hindennach et al. 2024
;
Thellman et al. 2022
)
, it is important to study how different messaging about AI in public communication can influence people’s beliefs.

A common approach taken in prior work is to conduct large surveys to characterize the public’s beliefs about AI and attitudes towards these technologies
(
Liao et al. 2024
;
Weber et al. 2024b
;
Colombatto and Fleming 2024
;
Kim et al. 2023
;
Brauner et al. 2023
;
Kelley et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2024a
)
.
However, such observational studies do not address the question of what
factors
give rise to those beliefs and attitudes.
To fill this need, other studies use experimental approaches to investigate how specific decisions during the design process of an AI system impact the way users subsequently interact with it and the attitudes they have towards it

(
Kim et al. 2024
;
Bansal et al. 2019
;
Buçinca et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Yin et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
.
Understanding the causal impact of these design decisions on user experience is valuable for technology developers, but these are not the only factors that impact how people think and feel about AI. Many people learn about AI systems from
how other people talk about AI
(
Do et al. 2024
;
Ju and Stewart 2024
)
.
Thus there is an important gap in our current understanding: what is the causal impact of
messaging
about AI on people’s beliefs about and attitudes towards these technologies?
Understanding the impact of different types of messaging about AI is important because effective public communication about science and technology has potential to enact real-world change
(
Ranney and Clark 2016
;
Ballew et al. 2025
;
Kotcher et al. 2021
)
.

Here we study the causal impact of different types of messaging about large language models (LLMs), a particularly prominent type of AI system, on people’s beliefs about LLMs—specifically on their beliefs about the mental capacities that LLMs might possess, such as the ability to have intentions, remember things, or feel tired.
We first conducted a pre-registered between-subjects experiment (
N=470
) where participants were randomly assigned to either watch one of three brief informational videos about LLMs or to watch no video.
One video provided an accessible introduction to how LLMs work by explaining the text-generation task they are usually trained to perform (
machine
condition).
Another video focused on what LLMs could be used for, providing guidance on how to use them for everyday tasks (
tool
condition).
The last video suggested that LLMs could be understood as social partners, capable of genuinely connecting with human users (
companion
condition).
After watching their assigned video, which the
no-video
participants skipped, all participants reported the degree to which they believed LLMs possessed 40 different mental capacities and reported their attitudes towards LLMs, such as trust in LLM outputs and confidence in using LLMs.
We found that participants who were assigned to watch the video presenting LLMs as
companions
judged LLMs to possess more fully developed cognitive and emotional capacities than participants in the other conditions. On the other hand, videos presenting LLMs as machines or tools influenced people’s attitudes towards LLMs but not their beliefs about how much LLMs possessed mental capacities.

After observing that brief exposure to certain messaging could influence people’s
beliefs
about and
attitudes
towards LLMs, we conducted a follow-up experiment (
N=604
) nine months later to explore how this intervention might affect people’s
behavior
when using LLMs to perform a specific task, such as obtaining information.
People are increasingly using LLMs instead of traditional search engines to search for factual information
(
Sharma et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Zhai 2024
;
Ghodratnama and Zakershahrak 2023
;
Schlicht et al. 2025
)
, but LLMs are not always reliable for such retrieval tasks since they often produce incorrect or internally inconsistent responses
(
McCoy et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2024
;
Zhai 2024
;
Ghodratnama and Zakershahrak 2023
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
.
While
Kim et al. 2025
has examined the impact of inaccurate and/or inconsistent outputs on how readily people rely on LLM-generated responses to answer factual questions , it is not yet known how
messaging
about LLMs interacts with those factors to further shape how people use them.

In this follow-up with a new group of participants, we explored whether messaging about LLMs could shape how people use LLM responses to answer factual questions.
These participants were again randomly assigned to one of the four video conditions then they answered a series of binary factual questions with access to pre-generated responses from a fictitious LLM system named “Theta,” similar to
Kim et al. 2025
.
Theta’s responses were systematically controlled such that some were incorrect or contained inconsistencies.
All participants also completed a subset of the mental capacity attribution survey from the initial study.

In addition to replicating the effects of messaging about LLMs on people’s beliefs about LLM mental capacities, this experiment also uncovered nuanced effects on how people relied on LLM outputs to answer factual questions.
We did not find that participants’ overall reliance on Theta’s responses to be influenced by the videos; however, when Theta’s responses were
inconsistent
, participants who watched videos presenting LLMs as
machines
relied
less
on Theta than did other participants.
These results suggest that while messaging presenting LLMs as machines might help people be somewhat more vigilant when using LLM responses, the degree to which people rely on LLM outputs largely seems to be robust to different messaging about LLMs.

Together, our studies offer three main contributions.
First, we fill an important gap in human-AI interaction research to understand the role that messaging about LLMs can have on people’s beliefs about LLMs and their reliance on LLM-generated responses to seek out factual information.
Second, our findings establish that messaging about LLMs (implemented here as short videos) has reliable and repeatable impacts on people’s beliefs about the mental capacities LLMs may possess while also shaping the attitudes people report to hold towards LLMs.
Lastly, we demonstrate nuanced findings that people’s reliance on LLM-generated responses when answering factual questions to be largely unaffected by messaging about LLMs, except when the LLM responses contain inconsistencies.
As LLMs, and other types of AI technology, become increasingly prevalent in the media, workplace, and daily life, our findings demonstrate that different messaging about AI can shape what people believe and can have nuanced effects on how they will use AI technology.
This bears implications on the media, organizations developing AI products, and individual experts to carefully consider how to effectively and ethically communicate about AI to the general public.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Public communication about AI

It is important that members of the general public understand the implications of advances in AI systems.
However, it can be challenging for people to achieve this understanding when faced with competing narratives about AI, ranging from more pessimistic to more optimistic perspectives on the overall impact of AI on society
(
Cave and Dihal 2019
;
Sartori and Bocca 2023
;
Cave et al. 2019
;
Appio et al. 2025
)
.
Thus simply disseminating more information about AI might be insufficient for helping people to reconcile these different perspectives.
Prior work investigating strategies for public communication about science and technology has explored tailoring information to individuals’ needs
(
Druckman et al. 2025
;
Fischhoff 2013
;
Fischhoff 2019
)
;
other work has focused on using anthropomorphic language to describe non-human entities in more human-like and thus more relatable ways
(
McGellin et al. 2021
;
Wood 2019
;
Jahic Pettersson et al. 2020
;
Pizza and Kelemen 2025
)
.

However, adopting these strategies into the context of public communication about AI might introduce novel risks. People already tend to ascribe human-like traits, such as intelligence, to AI systems
(
Kim and Im 2023
;
Lee et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
, so further presenting AI as having agency and/or being capable of forming intentions can lead people to develop miscalibrated expectations about an AI system’s behavior or even overly strong emotional attachments to a particular system
(
Cave et al. 2018
;
Salles et al. 2020
;
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Watson 2019
;
Thellman et al. 2022
;
Maples et al. 2024
)
.
While some existing work has sought to understand how members of the general public think about AI
(
Denia 2025
;
Cave and Dihal 2019
;
Sartori and Bocca 2023
)
, including how various metaphors for or descriptions about specific AI systems might influence people’s expectations about them
(
Pataranutaporn et al. 2023
;
Bansal et al. 2019
;
Kloft et al. 2024
;
Noh and Lee 2025
)
, there is a gap in understanding the causal impact of messaging about AI systems
in general
on people’s beliefs about the underlying nature of AI technology.
Our work aims to fill this gap by examining how different messaging about AI influences the fundamental beliefs people hold about AI, like what mental capacities AI systems might possess.

It can be generally challenging to obtain reports that reflect people’s true beliefs

but Human-AI interaction (HAI) researchers circumvent this problem by asking people to share metaphors about about AI

or report their beliefs about the mental capacities AI can possess, such as having intentions, remembering things, or feeling tired

;

More recent work has started to focus on LLMs and chatbots as they are becoming increasingly common

2.3.
Reliance on AI Systems

This led us to conduct an additional experiment to explore whether messaging could

3.
Study 1: Characterizing the Impact of Messaging on Beliefs about LLM Mental Capacities

The main research question motivating our work is:
How do different ways of presenting LLMs—as machines, tools, or companions—affect people’s beliefs about the mental capacities that LLMs might possess?

3.1.
Methods

To explore this question, we conducted a behavioral experiment (N=470) where participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: to watch a video presenting LLMs as machines, tools, or companions, or to watch no video (Fig.
1
).
Then all participants took a survey measuring primarily their attributions of mental capacities to LLMs and secondarily their attitudes towards LLMs, such as trust in LLM outputs or overall feelings towards LLMs.
This experiment was approved by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) and pre-registered with AsPredicted (
https://aspredicted.org/vgdm-gjrm.pdf
).
In this section, we describe our experimental design and procedure, approach to participant recruitment, and statistical analysis methods.

3.1.1.
Developing Videos with Different Messaging About LLMs

We designed three informational videos that exemplified different and common forms of messaging about LLMs.
The
machine
video described how LLMs generate text by predicting the next most probable word, using an illustrated example; the
tool
video highlighted applications that LLMs could be used for, such as summarizing text and assisting in brainstorming tasks; the
companion
video suggested that LLMs could be used like social partners, potentially able to connect with human users.
All videos were less than 5 minutes long, and their content was informed by both current messaging about AI in the media as well as an influential framework for describing a complex system’s behavior at three varying levels of abstraction
(
Dennett 1989
;
Thompson 2009
)
.
While the
machines
video presented LLMs in mechanistic terms, focusing on how these systems work, the
tools
video instead portrayed LLMs in primarily functional terms, focusing on what these systems were designed to do
(
Lombrozo 2012
;
Lombrozo and Carey 2006
)
.
The
companions
video presented LLMs as being more human-like, by focusing on their ability to emulate conversational interactions between people.

Each video contained one distinctive content section, but otherwise all videos shared the same introduction, conclusion, and primer introducing how LLMs learn from data.
To ensure consistency in the style, we used similar animations, graphics, and the same narrator for all videos.
In the survey, we presented each video in three parts for maximal engagement; participants had to stay on each screen for the duration of each part and manually click to move onto the next section.
For further details, links to the videos, and the full scripts, see Appendix Sec.
A
.

3.1.2.
Measuring Beliefs About & Attitudes Towards LLMs

Regardless of whether a participant watched a video or not, all participants completed a survey which where they reported how much they attributed various mental capacities to LLMs, such as the ability to have intentions, remember things, or feel tired (see Fig.
2
for the full list).
Following prior work, we measured mental capacity attribution using Likert scale ratings
(
Thellman et al. 2022
;
Eddy et al. 1993
;
Miraglia et al. 2023
;
Weisman et al. 2017
;
Colombatto and Fleming 2024
;
Colombatto et al. 2025
)
on a scale from one to seven, and our final set of capacities were compiled from two relevant prior works
(
Weisman et al. 2017
;
Colombatto and Fleming 2024
)
. Using a small-scale qualitative pilot study, we identified 40 capacities that spanned a wide variety of relevant characteristics, while minimizing redundancy (see Appendix Sec.
B.1
for details).
To additionally explore the extent to which these video interventions impacted other aspects of how people think and feel about LLMs, we also asked participants to answer five additional questions probing a range of attitudes towards LLMs using single 7-point Likert scales for the human-likeness of, trust in, confidence in using, confidence in programming, and overall feelings towards LLMs.

3.1.3.
Data Collection

We recruited participants in November 2024 using Prolific, an online research platform.
We targeted U.S.-based adults without specialized knowledge or experience with AI or computer science with a standard sample.
We used both Prolific’s pre-screening filters for education, employment, and programming experience and custom filters regarding computer science experience and self-reported knowledge of AI technology (see Appendix Sec.
B.2
for filtering questions and Appendix Sec.
B.4
for participant demographics).
Participants provided informed consent in accordance with the IRB, and were compensated $15 per hour for their participation.

Our target minimum sample size was
90
per condition (
360
total), based on a power analysis conducted using G*Power
(
Erdfelder et al. 1996
)
, with
alpha=0.05
,
power=0.9
,
Cohen^{\prime}s\ d=0.5
for a two-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test. In total, we recruited 489 participants, then, following our pre-registered exclusion criteria, we excluded 19 participants who failed our attention check (see Appendix Sec.
B.3.3
), spent less than 80 seconds on the mental capacity attribution survey, or spent a median time of less than 1 second on each item of the survey. After applying these exclusions, we retained data from a total of 470 participants for further analysis (
3.9\%
exclusion rate): 118 watched no video, 116 watched the video presenting LLMs as machines, 116 as tools, and 117 as companions.

3.1.4.
Analysis Procedure

The primary goal of our statistical analyses was to determine how the videos presenting LLMs differently influenced the degree to which participants attributed various mental capacities to LLMs.
We first categorized the mental capacities using exploratory factor analysis as a data-driven method to structure to our data (see Appendix Sec.
B.5.2
for factor analysis details).
Then, according to our pre-registered analysis plan, we fit the following linear mixed-effects regression model
1
1

1

B.5.1
on the data:
rating
\sim
video_condition * category + (1 | p_id)
.
In this model,

video_condition

no_video

We also employed a similar analysis strategy to assess the impact of these videos on the survey questions probing attitudes towards LLMs.

To test for the reliability of the effect of any predictor variable, we report the results of quantitative model comparison between linear mixed-effects regression models with and without that variable, summarized using standard test statistics
\chi^{2}
or

,

.
We use these regression models also to report quantitative estimates of key outcome variables, namely estimated marginal means, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (i.e.,

Results

4.
Study 2: Investigating How Messaging Impacts Reliance on LLMs for Obtaining Information

We explored this question in a follow-up experiment nine months later.
Our goal was to understand how our findings from Study 1 connected to how people relied on LLMs, specifically when trying to obtain factual information.
Further, conducting this experiment allowed us to explore how our findings hold over time—as AI development progresses quickly.

4.1.
Methods

This experiment followed a similar design to Study 1, but participants completed a series of
factual question-answering (QA) tasks
using an LLM system in addition reporting attributions of mental capacities to LLMs.
In short, after being randomly assigned to watch one of the videos presenting LLMs as machines, tools, or companions or to watch no video, participants completed eight factual QA tasks with pre-generated responses from a fictitious LLM system named Theta.
Participants also reported their attributions of a fixed subset of 10 mental capacities out of the full set of 40 (see Appendix Sec.
C.1
).
To account for order effects, we randomized and counterbalanced the order of the factual QA tasks and the mental capacity survey.
In this section, we focus on describing the factual QA task and measurement of participants’ reliance on LLMs. We refer the reader back to Sec.
3.1
for details on the experimental manipulation and the survey about mental capacities.
This follow-up experiment was also IRB-approved, and we pre-registered the design and primary analyses with AsPredicted (
https://aspredicted.org/wq9j-sdvq.pdf
).

4.1.1.
Factual Question-Answering Task

Our factual question
-
answering task is based on the study design of
Kim et al. 2025
.
Each task involved determining the correct answer to a difficult, binary factual question with access to a response from a fictitious LLM system named Theta.
After seeing the question and Theta’s response, participants were asked to select the correct answer to the question, report their confidence in their answer, rate Theta’s response justification quality and actionability, and optionally submit a follow-up question to Theta (see Fig.
3
for an example).
While presenting participants with controlled LLM responses may limit our understanding of how participants would use real-world LLM systems with free-form interaction, we adopt this method from prior work
(
Kim et al. 2025
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Si et al. 2024
)
as a valid approach to study people’s behaviors with LLM responses while avoiding unwanted noise from an LLM’s stochasticity and sensitivity to wordings.

While
Kim et al. 2025
manipulated the presence of explanations, the presence of source links, and the answer correctness of the LLM’s responses, in this work, we manipulated the answer correctness and the logical consistency of Theta’s responses to understand the impact of messaging on how people use
unreliable
LLM responses to answer difficult factual questions. Hence, there are four response types (
correct
-
consistent
,
correct
-
inconsistent
,

incorrect
-
consistent
, and
incorrect
-
inconsistent
), and participants saw two of each type.
All LLM responses were generated using GPT-4o
(
Hurst et al. 2024
)
either by
Kim et al. 2025
or our research team directly, approximated 100 words, and followed similar writing styles.
For the full list of questions, all explanations, and further prompting details, see Appendix Sec.
C.2
. We also discuss the limitations and implications of our study design in Sec.
5.1
.

4.1.2.
Measuring Reliance & Related Variables

Following prior work
(
Kim et al. 2025
;
Buçinca et al. 2021
;
Cao and Huang 2022
;
Lai and Tan 2019
;
Lu and Yin 2021
)
, we measured reliance as whether the participant’s submitted answer agreed with Theta’s answer (
TRUE
if the answers matched and
FALSE
otherwise).
Additionally, we used two behavioral measures—whether participants asked a follow-up question and the time they spent on the task—and three self-reported measures on a scale of one to seven: participants’ confidence in their answer, rating of Theta’s response justification quality, and actionability.
See Fig.
3
for exact question wordings of the self-report measures.
Lastly, we also recorded participants’ answer accuracy on each question.

4.1.3.
Data Collection

We recruited participants (
N=604
) in August 2025 using Prolific.
Similar to Study 1, we aimed to recruit participants who were representative of the general public in the U.S. without extensive technical knowledge or experience using LLMs.
We used similar Prolific screening questions, but additionally screened participants based on self-reported LLM usage rather than self-reported general knowledge about AI (see Appendix Sec.
C.3
for screening details).
Participants provided informed consent and were compensated at a rate of $15 per hour.

We aimed to recruit 150 participants per video condition post-exclusions, totaling 600 participants.
This number was calculated to obtain a similar number of observations for each response type in each video condition as
Kim et al. 2025
.
Our pre-registered exclusion criteria were intended to identify participants who did not engage with the task, which we assessed using a post-task attention check (see Appendix Sec.
C.3
for sample size and exclusion details).
We initially recruited a total of 611 participants, and after applying our exclusion criteria, we retained data from 604 participants (
1.15\%
exclusion rate).
152 participants were in the no-video baseline, 152 in the
machine
video condition, 150 in the
tool
video condition, and 150 in the
companion
video condition.

4.1.4.
Analysis Procedure

Our analyses focused on three key questions.
First, we asked how strongly our video intervention from Study 1 generalizes to a new sample of participants.
Second, we explored whether the magnitude of this effect changes when people are presented with LLM-generated responses as part of a behavioral task.
Third, we assessed the impact of our video messaging intervention on how readily people relied on LLM-generated outputs to answer factual questions, as measured by how often people’s answers agreed with the LLM’s answer.

First, to analyze whether the effects of the videos differ between the two experiments conducted nine months apart, we jointly considered mental capacity attributions for the 10 mental capacities that were surveyed from all participants in both experiments (
N=1,074
).
Then, we fit the following linear mixed-effects regression model:
rating
\sim
time * video_condition * category + qa_task * video_condition * category + (1 | p_id)
2
2

2

We intentionally did not include the interaction between
time * qa_task
because these variables are linearly dependent and the interactions led to rank deficiencies
.
Here
time
was a binary variable where the reference level
PAST
indicated the data was from November 2024 and
PRESENT
indicated the data was from August 2025,

category
denoted whether the mental capacity was from the
cognitive
(reference) or
emotional

and
qa_task
was a binary variable with a reference level of
FALSE

TRUE

Similarly to earlier analyses,
video_condition
was a four-level categorical variable with
no_video
as the reference and
p_id
represents the participant ID.

Next, we sought to investigate how completing factual QA tasks with LLM responses affects people’s attributions of mental capacities to LLMs, in addition to the different types of messaging.
Focusing on the most recently collected data, we compared the attributions of mental capacities between participants who reported their attributions after the videos but before completing tasks with Theta and participants who reported them after both the videos and the tasks.
To avoid excessive model complexity, we fit the following linear mixed-effects regression model for cognitive and emotional mental capacities separately:
rating
\sim
video_condition * qa_task + (1 | p_id) + (1 | capacity)

Here

This was pre-registered as an exploratory analysis, but the exact methodology was not specified then.

Lastly, our main pre-registered analysis aimed to understand how different messaging about LLMs affects people’s reliance on LLM-generated responses to answer factual questions.

Here

We employed a similar analysis strategy for

and the secondary measures of reliance (e.g.,

Additionally, we conducted an exploratory analysis probing whether participants’ reliance was correlated with their attributions of mental capacities which we describe and report in Appendix Sec.

Our statistical modeling follows a similar approach outlined in Sec.

4.2.
Results

In this section, we report our findings with respect to the three questions presented in Sec.
4.1.4
.
First, we observed that the effect of messaging on people’s beliefs generalized to a new sample of participants: messaging about LLMs as companions robustly led participants to report increased attributions of mental capacities to LLMs.
Second, we found that using responses from our fictitious LLM Theta to complete tasks further influenced how participants attributed
cognitive
capacities to LLMs, moderating the effect of the videos.
Lastly, our main analysis demonstrated that how participants relied on Theta was largely unaffected by which video they watched.
However, when Theta’s responses were
inconsistent
, participants who watched the video that presented LLMs as
machines
tended to rely on Theta less, measured by whether participants’ submitted answer agreed with Theta’s answer.
These results suggest a complex relationship between messaging and people’s reliance on LLM responses to obtain factual information.

4.2.1.
Replicated Effect of Messaging on Attributing Mental Capacities to LLMs

Given the rapidly changing landscape around AI, it is conceivable that a new sample of participants recruited nine months later might not respond to our messaging intervention in the same way.
However, we instead found that our initial findings successfully generalized to this new sample of participants.

4.2.3.
Impact of Messaging on Reliance on LLM to Obtain Factual Information

While our results so far demonstrate that the effect of messaging on people’s beliefs about the nature of LLMs is robust to a changing AI landscape and that using LLM responses to complete tasks can further moderate these beliefs, we turn to our primary analysis of this study:
whether messaging can influence how people rely on responses from LLMs to answer factual questions

4.2.4.
Summary

In Study 2, we first replicated the findings from Study 1 nine months after the original data collection, suggesting the effects of different messaging on beliefs are robust to the fast-paced AI landscape.
Our second analysis demonstrated that completing tasks with Theta’s responses after watching the videos could moderate the effects of the video messages on people’s beliefs about the mental capacities LLMs may possess.
Lastly, we found that participants’ reliance on Theta was largely unaffected by the video they watched, but we observed that messaging presenting LLMs as machines reduced participants’ reliance when they saw
inconsistent
LLM responses.
This suggests a complex relationship between how messaging framing AI as
machines
can foster increased skepticism in unreliable situations.
Taken together, these results contextualize our main findings by providing insight on how messaging, mental capacities, and using LLM responses to answer factual questions may all relate to one another.

5.
Discussion

This work aims to understand how different messaging about AI can influence people’s beliefs and behaviors around AI.
Concretely, we explored how messaging about LLMs, a popular form of AI systems, affected people’s beliefs about the mental capacities LLMs may possess, such as the ability to have intentions or know things.
We found that presenting LLMs as companions led participants to be more likely to believe LLMs can possess a broad array of mental capacities, and we demonstrated that these results are robust amid a quickly changing AI landscape.
We further explored how the effects of messaging may influence how people rely on a (potentially unreliable) LLM when seeking factual information.
Here we found that presenting LLMs as machines decreased participants’ reliance on inconsistent LLM responses, suggesting that messaging about LLMs as machines can help foster higher skepticism in unreliable situations.
All in all, our study demonstrates that messaging about AI matters: how we present AI to people has the potential to impact both what people believe about the fundamental nature of AI and how they use the technology.

5.1.
Limitations and Future Work

However, we acknowledge a few limitations of our studies. In this work, we did not further consider complexities of the real world such as the effect of conflicting information, variably reliable AI systems, and significant time delays.
First, in the real world, people can encounter many potentially contradictory messages simultaneously, and in these situations, it is currently unclear which types of messaging would prevail.
Second, while we designed Theta to present equal proportions of
correct
-
consistent
,
correct
-
inconsistent
,
incorrect
-
consistent
, and
incorrect-incon
-
sistent
responses to conduct systematic analyses,
it is possible that the videos would have a different impact on reliance if Theta had fewer (or more) inconsistencies in its responses.
Lastly, we did not investigate how long the effects of different messaging would persist before they wear off and to what extent would consistent exposure to certain messaging have cumulative effects in shaping people’s beliefs, attitudes, and reliance on AI.
Our work begins to understand how public communication about AI influences what people believe about AI, but we do not yet fully understand its complexity in the real world.

To encourage future work to study the ways messaging about AI can affect both people’s beliefs and behaviors, we discuss two potential directions to explore: higher stakes situations and reliance on LLM systems for social matters.
While our study questions were simple and the stakes were low, people are using AI in more complex settings with higher stakes, such as writing official reports
(
Pinto et al. 2024
;
Michelet and Breitinger 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Phelps and Ashley 2022
)
or obtaining medical advice
(
Shekar et al. 2025
;
Mendel et al. 2024
;
Lautrup et al. 2023
)
.
In these more complex situations when correctness is crucial, different messaging about AI might have a more pronounced effect due to the uncertainty of the situation.
Further, the joint impact of messaging and using LLM responses in higher stakes situations may demonstrate different effect sizes and directions on people’s beliefs than what we observed here, given its dependency on task and context.
Second, while in Study 2 we focused on people’s reliance on LLMs when seeking factual information, it is also becoming increasingly common for people to use LLMs for social and emotional support to the extent of replacing human relationships
(
Koulouri et al. 2022
;
Xygkou et al. 2023
;
Kirk et al. 2025
;
Maples et al. 2024
;
Laestadius et al. 2024
;
Fang et al. 2025
;
Zimmerman et al. 2024
;
Pentina et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024b
)
—despite the potential consequences
(
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Zimmerman et al. 2024
;
Salles et al. 2020
;
Cave and Dihal 2021
)
.
It is important to understand how different messaging about LLMs may influence how people use LLMs for personal matters, where beliefs and emotions may play a larger role, in order to circumvent undesired outcomes.

5.2.
Implications

Finally, we discuss implications of our findings regarding anthropomorphism and public communication about AI.

5.2.1.
Implications of Increasingly Believing AI can Possess Mental Capacities on Anthropomorphism

Believing AI can possess mental capacities can be considered a form of anthropomorphism: attributing human-like qualities to a non-human entity
(
Epley et al. 2007
;
Kim and Im 2023
;
Miraglia et al. 2023
)
.
While anthropomorphic language is often used to make complex concepts more understandable
(
Jahic Pettersson et al. 2020
;
Pizza and Kelemen 2025
)
, in the context of AI technology, this can have unintended consequences;
anthropomorphizing AI technology can lead people to miscalibrate their expectations, and in more extreme circumstances, can lead to to potential dangers such as increased emotional dependence on the systems, manipulation of users, and the general dehumanization of people
(
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Cave and Dihal 2021
;
Zimmerman et al. 2024
;
Friedman and Kahn Jr 1992
;
Watson 2019
;
Lee and Kim 2025
)
.
Thus, although it may seem like messaging that presents LLMs as companions leads people to believe LLMs possess only
slightly
more fully developed mental capacities relative to other forms of messaging, it is still significant because of its connection to anthropomorphizing AI technology and should not be taken lightly.

5.2.2.
Implications on Public Communication and Education about AI

Clear public communication about AI is important to foster appropriate expectations of these systems and reduce anthropomorphizing AI
(
Cave et al. 2018
)
, and our study demonstrates different messages about AI can have nuanced effects on people’s beliefs about and reliance on LLM responses.
While anthropomorphic language may be more natural to use, we encourage communicating about AI using less metaphorical terms, which can further inflate beliefs about AI
(
Watson 2019
)
.
We acknowledge that it is often challenging to convey sufficient technical information about AI in limited amounts of time and at appropriate levels of abstraction.
However, informational videos, like our
machine
and
tool
videos, might offer a solution, giving people bite-sized and engaging content that can influence their beliefs via
education
, rather than exaggerated claims.
Short yet impactful means of “edu-tainment”
(
Aksakal 2015
)
can be informational and engaging for adults who may not have the time, resources, and access to the typical interventions designed to foster AI literacy
(
Ng et al. 2021
;
Casal-Otero et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2021
)
.
Thus, our study not only demonstrates how messaging about AI as machines, tools, or companions can influence people’s beliefs and reliance, but it also connects to a growing number of works encouraging short informational videos as a means of public education about AI
(
Cao et al. 2025
;
Rezazade Mehrizi et al. 2023
)
.

5.3.
Conclusion

In summary, our work begins to address the role messaging about AI can have on both people’s beliefs about AI systems and on how people choose to rely on them in obtaining factual information.
On one hand, we found that messaging about LLMs as companions can have robust impacts on people’s beliefs about the nature of these systems—specifically increasing the degree to which LLMs can possess various mental capacities.
On the other hand, people’s decisions to rely on AI is more nuanced: messaging about LLMs as machines can reduce reliance of inconsistent LLM responses, but in general people’s reliance is robust to different messaging.
Taken together, our findings highlight the potential for
discourse about
AI—beyond technical advances in AI—to generate broad societal impact.

LLMs as Machines Script

Narrator:

“Since the introduction of ChatGPT at the end of 2022, there has been tremendous increase in popularity and interest in large language models, also known as LLMs, which are the technology behind ChatGPT and similar products.
These LLMs have begun to have a huge impact because of the way that they generate text by modeling language statistics.

Earlier chatbots were hard-coded to output text following strict rules, like the ones shown here.

On the other hand, current LLMs have learned from countless conversations, essays, and various forms of writing to generate coherent text.

Common examples of modern LLMs include OpenAI’s GPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude.
Although these LLMs differ from one another, they also share many commonalities. Let’s spend some time learning about how LLMs work.

So, when LLMs generate text, they actually perform “next word prediction”. When given a text input, they simply predict what word comes next. To make longer responses, the predicted word is added to the input, and the new input is fed into the LLM. This is repeated until generation stops.

To predict each word, LLMs model the statistics of language.

LLM word prediction can be broken down into two stages: context understanding: using a mechanism called attention, and word selection: which is based on probability.
The intuition behind attention is that it helps the LLM “pay attention” to context clues of the text input (such as word meanings or parts of speech) that hint at what comes next.

For example, consider the phrase “My favorite summer activity is going to the…”. What would come next and how did you decide that?
Perhaps you “paid attention” to the positive sentiment behind the word “favorite”, to the word “to” indicating the next word may be a location, and the meaning of “summer” to narrow down likely locations.
Attention in LLMs works similarly. The input text will undergo many attention operations, each focusing on a different clue.

At the end, the LLM will assign a probability to every possible word in its vocabulary.
The final step is selecting the predicted word. While simply selecting the word with the highest probability would be the most straightforward, in practice, LLMs typically perform sampling: which is simply after assigning probabilities, choose one of the top most probable words. In our example, the LLM may select “beach” or “waterpark” or “mountains”. Because of sampling, LLMs can output diverse responses, even to the same text input.

In order for LLMs to predict words accurately, a lot of data is required. LLMs, like most AI technologies, learn via repeated exposure to many examples.
In this process, LLMs are tasked with predicting the next word of an input and can compare their prediction to the true next word.

From small amounts of data, this is ineffective, but large quantities of data allows the LLM to effectively predict sequences of words.
Modern LLMs require hundreds of gigabytes of text data, if not more. For humans, reading this much text would take thousands of years.

In conclusion, LLMs are computer programs that are increasingly transforming our society, business, and daily lives. As they become more prevalent, it becomes increasingly important to know how they work so we can use them more safely and effectively.”

LLMs as Tools Script

Narrator:

“Since the introduction of ChatGPT at the end of 2022, there has been a tremendous increase in the popularity and interest in large language models, also known as LLMs, which are the technology behind ChatGPT and similar products.
These LLMs have begun to have a huge impact because of the way they can be used to accomplish lots of different tasks much more quickly.

Earlier chatbots followed strict rules because they were designed to perform specific functions in a narrow range of contexts, such as customer service bots.

On the other hand, current LLMs have learned from countless conversations, essays, and various forms of writing, resulting in a versatile tool for many different applications.

Common examples of modern LLMs include: OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude.
Although these LLMs differ from one another, they share many commonalities. Let’s spend some time learning about and how to use LLMs.

In order for LLMs to become such versatile tools, a lot of data is required. LLMs, like most AI technologies, learn via repeated exposure to many examples.
In this process, LLMs pick up on the statistical patterns in written language, including formatting and content.

From small amounts of data, this is ineffective, but large quantities of data allows the LLMs to effectively learn patterns.
Modern LLMs require hundreds of gigabytes of text data, if not more. For humans, reading this much text would take thousands of years.

LLMs have many use cases such as generating creative material. For example, stories, poems, and songs. They can also be used for summarizing text, rewording or rewriting text with different styles, and question and answering tasks.

Specifically with chat interfaces, LLMs can be useful for role-playing exercises, such as interview preparation or iterative brainstorming tasks.

In industry, LLMs are also being used for tasks such as building customer service bots, analyzing complex health records, performing sentiment analysis of customer reviews, transcribing audio files, developing personalized educational materials, and much, much more.

Here are some recommendations to get useful responses from LLMs:
First, be specific in your text input and include relevant keywords, examples, and instructions when applicable. For example, if a user wants activity recommendations in Paris, ‘tell me about Paris’ is a vague input. And if the input is vague, typically the output will be vague as well. A better input would be ‘tell me about the top tourist attractions in Paris’ which results in a more detailed response.

Second, if you are unsatisfied with the output, you can simply ask again or reword the input and try again. For example, a user may ask an LLM for hobby recommendations and the LLM may suggest running, painting, and cooking. And if she asks again, it may give new activities, like biking, reading, and kayaking! LLM text generation incorporates randomness which allows for this diversity of responses.

Lastly, when using LLMs via a chat interface, you can refer to previous messages in the conversation. This may be helpful when you want to iteratively refine an LLM output. In this example, the user wants to write an email to his boss, but the initial output starting with ‘Hi Michael!’ is too casual. He provides additional instructions to make it more formal, and the LLM changes its response to start with ‘Dear Michael, I hope this message finds you well”. Much better already!

In conclusion, LLMs are tools that are increasingly transforming our society, business, and daily lives. As they become more prevalent, it becomes increasingly important to know how to use them to obtain reliable information.”

Linear Regression

C.3.
Participant Recruitment, Sample Size, and Exclusions

The only difference in our participant recruitment process for the follow-up study was selecting individuals with little LLM experience based on their usage frequency, as opposed to their self-reported knowledge about AI.
Self-reported knowledge does not have clear delinations between options. For example, what constitutes as “some basic knowledge” as opposed to “moderate knowledge”?
Filtering participants by their usage frequency is a more objective decision criteria. Specifically, in addition to the pre-screening questions in Prolific, we asked participants:

(1)

Did you obtain a degree, are you pursuing a degree, or do you work in an area related to computer science?

•

Yes

•

No

(2)

How often do you use large language models (LLMs) and LLM-infused applications such as ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude?

•

Never

•

Rarely, about 1-3 times a month

•

Occasionally, about 1-2 times a week

•

Often, more than 2 times a week

•

Always, about once or more a day

The first question is the same as the main study and the second replaced the self-reported knowledge screening question. If participants responded “Yes” to the first question or “Often” or “Always” to the second, they were screened out of our study and were provided a pro-rated compensation for their time.

Our target sample size (
150
per condition;
600
total) was based on prior work
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
, as they used a similar set of dependent variables.
We aimed to collect a similar number of responses for each of Theta’s explanation type for each of the four video conditions.
Specifically,
Kim et al. 2025
aimed to recruit 300 participants, where each participant encounters each LLM explanation type one time. Since our participants encountered each of Theta’s explanation type twice, we needed half the number of participants per condition, leading us to aim for
150
participants per condition after exclusions.

Our pre-registered exclusion criteria aimed at excluding participants who clearly demonstrated low effort on the task. Originally, participants who met one of the four criteria were going to be omitted: (1) spend less than 5 minutes on the factual question-answering task, (2) spend a median time on task questions of less than 20 seconds, (3) achieve less than 80% accuracy on the post-task attention check where participants are shown a list of questions and asked to indicate which ones they were asked during the tasks, and (4) provide clearly irrelevant or off-topic responses to the open-ended questions.

However, after our initial pilot, using these criteria excluded approximately 20% of the participants. We used the overall time requirement of 5 minutes because it was used in prior work for a similar task
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
.
However, upon discussion with the authors of
Kim et al. 2025
, we learned this was 5 minutes from start to end, when we originally interpreted it as 5 minutes for answering the eight questions.
In further discussion, we decided that the more important criteria was participants paying attention in the task, so we decided to relax the time requirement to exclude participants who spend less than 2 minutes on the task.
Importantly, we made this update
before
data for the full study was collected.
