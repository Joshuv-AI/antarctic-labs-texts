Do Readers Simulate the Color of Inferred Objects? Influence of Sentence Typicality and Context Position
Authors: Masato Terai, Junko Yamashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Embodied cognition assumes that language processing involves sensory, motor, and affective representations. However, further empirical research is required to reveal how embodied representations are used in language processing. This study investigated whether readers could represent the color of an object and whether the color was influenced by context. Thirty-five native English speakers performed a semantic Stroop task in which a sentence was presented (e.g., Joe was excited to see a bear in the woods/at the North Pole) to them. They were then presented with a word from the sentence (bear) in colored font. A series of linear mixed-effects modeling was performed to determine the reaction times under the following three-color conditions: typical (brown), atypical (white: polar bear), and an unrelated color (green). The results showed that regardless of the sentence (implying a typical brown bear or atypical white bear), typical colors received significantly faster reaction times than atypical and unrelated colors. These results were also observed when participants read context phrases (in the woods/at the North Pole) before the keywords. The findings thus suggest that readers represent typical colors of objects, regardless of context.

Introduction

The embodied cognition view assumes that humans not only understand the lexical meaning of a word but also evoke their accumulated experiences (e.g., seeing, touching, and eating the objects) associated with the word, which is called simulation (e.g., Barsalou, 1999). Simulations are defined as “the reenactment of perceptual, motor, and introspective states acquired during the experience with the world, body, and mind” (Barsalou, 2008, p. 618). For instance, readers mentally represent an image of the dog and its owner when they read the sentence, “John takes his dog for a walk.” The simulation also includes the color and orientation of the dog and is updated as readers proceed with the sentences in the text (Barsalou, 1999). Thus, simulation is not static but dynamic; readers immediately update what they represent in texts based on the following contexts (e.g., Sato et al., 2013). When a context implies a change in object states, readers represent both the states: before and after the change (e.g., Horchak & Garrido, 2021). These findings suggest that nonlinguistic information such as an object’s shape and color is involved in understanding the language, and humans dynamically modify what they represent based on the contexts.

This study investigates object color, which plays an important role in human perception and cognition (e.g., Bramão et al., 2011; Elliot & Maier, 2014). Previous studies have suggested that color plays a role comparable to that of shape (e.g., de Koning et al., 2017; Zwaan & Pecher, 2012). However, research on the dynamic nature of simulation has predominantly focused on object shape (e.g., Hindy et al., 2012; Horchak & Garrido, 2021, 2024; Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013; Solomon et al., 2015), leaving the color relatively underexplored research area. Extending the basic question whether readers activate object-color information during sentence comprehension, this study further examines the activation of typical and atypical colors of an object and the effect of context position by manipulating the typicality of object color and the position of contextual information.

Literature Review

Embodiment Approach in Language Processing

Amodal theory assumes that cognitive representations are not inherently perceptual (e.g., Fodor, 1975) and maintains that language comprehension depends on abstract and amodal systems that are arbitrarily mapped onto their referents. The amodal theory has remained dominant in the study of language comprehension since the onset of the Cognitive Revolution in the 1950s (e.g., Horchak et al., 2014). However, the amodal view has been increasingly criticized (Barsalou, 1999; Barsalou et al., 2008). A common criticism is the symbol grounding problem (Harnad, 1990; see Searle’s [1980 ] Chinese Room Argument for the original proposal of the criticism). The symbol grounding problem is a situation in which symbols that are not grounded (connected to the real-world objects) have no meaning. Suppose an individual does not understand Chinese but visits China; learning Chinese only with a Chinese–Chinese dictionary would be impossible (Harnad, 1990). However, a purely symbolic model of the mind assumes this kind of learning for human cognition (Harnad, 1990). Therefore, as opposed to the purely symbolic view, the meaningless symbols need to be connected to the world to have their meaning.

More recent theories have also challenged the amodal view and proposed that cognitive representations are grounded in modalities. This approach, called the embodied theory of cognition, suggests that symbols are grounded in their references to the environment (e.g., Horchak et al., 2014). The embodied theory assumes that humans create simulations to represent the meaning of texts (e.g., Barsalou, 1999, 2008; Barsalou et al., 2008).

A growing body of research on human cognition has adopted an embodied cognition approach (e.g., Bergen et al., 2007; Connell, 2005, 2007; Connell & Lynott, 2011; de Koning et al., 2017; Garofalo & Riggio, 2022; Hoeben Mannaert et al., 2017; Pecher et al., 2009; Richter & Zwaan, 2009; Rommers et al., 2013; Stanfield & Zwaan, 2001; Yaxley & Zwaan, 2007; Zwaan et al., 2002). In particular, the visual properties of embodied representations, such as object shape and color, have attracted considerable research attention. Because of the focus of the present study, the review in this section concentrates on studies on color.

The findings on the simulation of object color during language comprehension have been mixed. Connell (2007) is one of the initial influential studies in this field that was replicated by multiple studies. Connell used a sentence–picture verification task in which participants first read a sentence that implied a particular color for an object and were then presented with a picture of the object mentioned in the sentence. Participants were asked to verify whether the pictured object had been mentioned in the preceding sentence. Counterintuitively, the results showed that mean reaction times were shorter when the color of the pictured object mismatched, rather than matched, the color implied by the sentence. For example, participants responded faster to a picture of a red steak (i.e., a raw steak) after reading “John looked at the steak on his plate” than after reading “John looked at the steak in the butcher’s window.” The red steak mismatched the brown color (i.e., a cooked steak) implied by the former sentence but matched the red color implied by the latter. Connell argued that the color information activated during sentence comprehension interfered with participants’ attempts to ignore color and identify the pictured object primarily by its shape, resulting in slower responses in the match condition. He further proposed that color may be represented differently from more stable object properties such as shape and orientation.

However, subsequent studies have reported a facilitation effect in matched-color conditions. Zwaan and Pecher (2012) examined activation of three visual properties (object orientation, shape, and color) with a sentence–picture verification task. Their study replicated previous experiments examining each of these properties, including Connell (2007). Contrary to Connell (2007), they found a matching advantage for all visual properties, with shorter reaction times in the match conditions than in the mismatch conditions. They provided strong evidence for matching effects for both color and shape and somewhat weaker, although still strong, evidence for orientation. Hoeben Mannaert et al. (2017) also conducted a replication of Connell (2007) and found a matching advantage. Further evidence for the importance of object color simulation among other visual traits was provided by de Koning et al. (2017), who reported that color had the strongest matching advantage, followed by shape and size.

Activation of Canonical and Contextually Implied Information During Sentence Processing

Another important research topic in embodied cognition approaches in language comprehension is multiple visual traits simulation, that is, whether canonical visual knowledge and contextually specified visual information are simultaneously accessible (e.g., Connell & Lynott, 2009; Hindy et al., 2012; Horchak & Garrido, 2021, 2024; Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013; Solomon et al., 2015). Results of previous studies are mixed. Connell and Lynott (2009) is a critical study in the domain of color activation. They investigated whether sentence comprehension involves the activation of color, and whether both canonical (i.g., typical) and contextually implied colors can remain accessible during sentence comprehension. In their study, native English speakers performed a semantic Stroop task wherein they were first presented with a sentence (e.g., Joe was excited to see a bear in the woods). After reading the sentence, they were presented with a target word (bear) colored in brown, white, or yellow. The participants named the color of the target word as quickly and accurately as they could. It was hypothesized that if a reader activated the color of the word, the response to the ink matching the color of the target word was faster than that in the mismatch condition. Two factors were examined: sentence types implying either the typical (e.g., Joe was excited to see a bear in the woods) or atypical (e.g., Joe was excited to see a bear at the North Pole) color of the object, and the typicality of ink color—the typical (brown), atypical (white), or unrelated color (yellow) in the case of bear. Although there was no significant interaction between the typicality of the sentence and the ink color, the response pattern suggested an effect of correspondence between the contextually implied color and the target ink color. In other words, when a sentence implied the typical color of the object, the participants responded faster to the word with the typical color. Interestingly, in the atypical condition, response times were similar for typical and atypical words, but they were much faster than for unrelated colors. In addition to supporting the activation of color representation during the processing of sentences, the authors concluded that even if the sentence implied an atypical color, readers activated both typical and atypical colors concurrently. However, their conclusion should be taken with caution because the effects relevant to this interpretation did not reach the conventional threshold for statistical significance (p > .05). The proposed coexistence of multiple color representations therefore requires reexamination in a study with sufficient statistical power.

Related evidence comes from studies examining object shapes. Unlike the color research, a unique feature of shape studies is the manipulation of a change in an object’s physical state (normal or deformed shapes).Shape studies generally used a sentence-picture verification task in which the experimental sentences differed in either implying object-shape change or not (e.g., The woman dropped/chose the ice cream) (Kang et al., 2020). Two scenarios are proposed regarding the multiple simulation of object shapes: a constant and a competing scenario (Horchak & Garrido, 2021). The former predicts access only to the consequences of an action, whereas the latter predicts access to both the object’s initial canonical state and its end non-canonical state. Findings of the availability of multiple shape representations are inconclusive with some supporting a competing scenario (Kang et al., 2020; Horchak & Garrido, 2021), and others not supporting it (Sato et al., 2013).

Taken together, previous studies supported the embodied accounts of cognition by showing that readers activate the visual properties of described objects, such as color and shape, during language comprehension. However, evidence for concurrent activation of multiple representations of an object remains limited, and thus further investigation is necessary. As we reviewed above, this question of concurrent activation of multiple traits of an object has been examined in a slightly different manner between color and shape studies. Whereas shape studies manipulated object-state changes in their experimental sentences (whether canonical or deformed shapes are concurrently available), color studies did not do so in their experimental sentences. Therefore, the constant and competing scenarios, which are primarily proposed to account for shape activations, may not be transferred directly to color processing. Although interesting accounts, we apply these accounts cautiously to characterize whether contextually implied atypical color information overrides canonical object-color knowledge or remains accessible alongside it.

Present Study

The present study examined the activation of object-color following Connell and Lynott (2009), but expanded two points. First, this study ran a power analysis to conduct research under sufficient statistical power. Connell and Lynott (2009) argued for the simulation of multiple colors based on the interaction between sentence and word typicality. However, the interaction only indicated a moderate suggestion (p = .057). This suggestive result might be attributable to the insufficient statistical power in their study. By securing adequate power through a power analysis, this study examined whether our findings replicate and reinforce Connell and Lynott’s (2009) suggestive result.

Second, the present study examined the role of context position, addressing the question whether color activation is affected by context position. In Connell and Lynott (2009), 16 of the 20 experimental sentences were post-context whereby a keyword was placed before the phrase that implied context (Joe was excited to see a bear at the North Pole). Previous studies on shapes have shown that readers activate only typical shapes when no change is implied in the context (e.g., Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013). Under such post-context condition, both typical and atypical colors may be simulated according to the competing scenario explanation (Horchak & Garrido, 2021): the participants read the keyword (bear) and immediately simulated an image of a typical color (brown bear); subsequently they read the context phrase (at the North Pole) and then simulated the atypical color with the initially simulated typical color not completely overwritten. This might explain why the reaction times for typical and atypical colors were similar under atypical sentence conditions in Connell and Lynott (2009). However, if context influences color simulation, providing context before a keyword under atypical sentence conditions might result in the constant scenario (Horchak & Garrido, 2021). Namely, when the participants read the context phrase (at the North Pole) before the keyword (bear), they simulate a white polar bear (atypical color of a bear) only, inhibiting the simulation of typical brown bear. By comparing the pre- and post-context conditions, this study examines the context effect on color simulation. Accordingly, the study proposes the following hypotheses:

The number of participants was determined by a power analysis using the samplesize_mixed function of the sjstats package version 0.18.2 (Lüdecke, 2022) for R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023). This function provides an approximate sample size for linear mixed models by conducting a standard power analysis and adjusting the result for the design effect in a two-level design. To protect against data loss, we recruited a larger sample of participants than indicated by the power analysis: 37 native English speakers. Two participants were excluded from the study: one who did not speak English as their first language, and another whose data were not recorded owing to a technical problem. Thus, data from 35 participants (15 women, 19 men, and 1 other) were analyzed. The results of the background questionnaire showed that 22 participants were Americans, followed by British (n = 6), Australians (n = 4), and Canadians (n = 3). Experimental Sentences

Item Building

The experimental sentences were constructed using the following steps. First, we created item pools by borrowing from previous studies (e.g., Connell & Lynott, 2009) and creating new items. Second, we conducted word- and sentence-level typicality rating tasks to validate the typicality of the colors. Participants were 26 participants who did not participate in the main study. In the word-level typicality rating task, participants were asked to evaluate which colors corresponded to typical, atypical, and unrelated colors. For example, the word bear was presented in three color words: brown, white, and green. The participants simply clicked on the radio button to determine the color that corresponded to the typicality in their mind. There were no restrictions on the choice. For instance, they could select the same box for all colors without selecting the other two boxes (e.g., they can click white for all typical, atypical, and unrelated colors).

In the sentence-level typicality rating task, a test sentence was presented with two images and four forced-choice alternatives: best matched by the first picture, best matched by the second picture, matched by both pictures equally, and matched by neither picture. One image represented the typical color of the keyword (e.g., a brown bear), and the other represented the atypical color of the object (e.g., a white bear). Participants were asked to choose one option regarding the match between the sentence and pictures. There were two versions of the task to balance the typicality of the sentences. Each task consisted of half of the sentences implying the typical color of the object and the other half implying the atypical color. Each participant worked on only one of the two versions. Both word- and sentence-level tasks were conducted using Google Forms without time restrictions.

We chose the experimental materials based on the following criteria. For the word-level typicality rating task, words that received less than 50% agreement for typical, atypical, or unrelated colors were eliminated. For the sentence-level typicality rating task, if a sentence showed less than 25% agreement in the typical or atypical condition, the items were excluded. Although these criteria may sound lenient, these are the criteria for the first screening step. We checked the typicality of words and sentences for the main study participants by implementing word-and sentence-level typicality rating tasks in the experiment. Fifteen nouns remained after the first screening (apple, ball, bear, cake, chameleon, cloud, horse, kiwi, leaf, onion, plum, popcorn, steak, strawberry, and tomato).

Target and Filler Sentences

For each of the 15 nouns, six sentence–ink-color conditions were created by crossing two levels of sentence typicality, typical and atypical, with three ink-color conditions, typical, atypical, and unrelated. This yielded 90 sentence–ink-color sets in total: 15 nouns × 6 conditions. Each set was then prepared in two context-position versions, pre-context and post-context, resulting in 180 target sentences. For example, for the noun bear, the typical context was expressed either before the target noun, as in “In the woods, Joe was excited to see a bear,” or after it, as in “Joe was excited to see a bear in the woods.” The atypical context was similarly expressed either before the target noun, as in “At the North Pole, Joe was excited to see a bear,” or after it, as in “Joe was excited to see a bear at the North Pole.” In both sentence types, the target noun was presented in a typical ink color, an atypical ink color, or an unrelated ink color (Table 1).

An equal number of filler sentences were created. There were only two conditions for the filler sentences: pre-context (e.g., To go to Kyoto, his parents took an airplane) and post-context (e.g., His parents took an airplane to go to Kyoto). The colored words were not identical to the target conditions. This was created to measure whether participants focused on the sentences. Procedure

The experiment was conducted either online using Zoom videoconferencing (Zoom Video Communications, Inc. San Jose, California, USA) or face-to-face owing to restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Those who used Zoom (n = 31 in the final data set) were asked to continue sharing their screens throughout the experiment. This allowed the first author to observe how the participants worked on the tasks. The Stroop task was created using the Hot Soup Processor version 3.5.1. software (http://hsp.tv/). In Connell and Lynott (2009), participants verbally named the colors. However, verbal responses make it difficult to measure speech onset. Thus, in this study, the participants responded using a keyboard. Although the magnitude of the Stroop effect was greater in the naming task, it was also observed in manual tasks (e.g., Augustinova et al., 2019; see Parris et al., 2022 for review of the response mode).

The experiment began with the practice of the semantic Stroop task wherein participants practiced pressing the key for 40 trials (10 trials for each color) to memorize the color-key correspondence. The practice session also included the practice of the semantic Stroop task. The details of the task are explained, as given below.

After completing the practice session, the participants proceeded to the main session. In the semantic Stroop task (Fig. 1), first, a plus sign was displayed on the far left of the screen for one second to help the participants focus on the screen. Second, a sentence in Arial font was displayed in 30-point black on a light gray background. The participants pressed the space bar to move on to the next sentence after understanding its meaning. Third, a plus sign was displayed at the center of the screen for 500 milliseconds. Fourth, a colored word was displayed in either brown (132, 75, 0 in RGB), white (255, 255, 255 in RGB), green (0, 128, 0 in RGB), or red (255, 0, 0 in RGB) in Arial in a 50-point font on a light gray background. Although Connell and Lynott (2009) changed the RGB rate depending on the item, this study kept the RGB rate identical to avoid the effect of color darkness. The participants were asked to respond to the color of the ink by pressing the keys as quickly and accurately as possible, and response latency was recorded. For the filler items, a comprehension task was entailed after the color assessment. The background was changed to bright orange (255, 222, and 173 in RGB) to indicate changes in the task. A comprehension question was presented, and participants pressed the enter key if the meaning of the sentence matched the sentence they had read before the color judgment and the tab key if the sentences did not match. Between trials, a screen with a table of color-key combinations was displayed, and the participants were instructed, on a white background, that they could pause if necessary.

Reaction times were recorded using custom software developed for this study. In both face-to-face and remote testing environments, all participants ran the software on a Windows operating system. To empirically assess the temporal precision of the software environment, we conducted a post hoc benchmark test involving 1,000 consecutive timing loops with a nominal 1 - ms interval on the same computer used for face-to-face data collection. The recorded intervals showed high temporal stability, with a mean interval of 1.66 ms (SD = 0.57, Mdn = 2.00 ms, Range = 0.00–7.00 ms). Given that reaction times in the Stroop task in this study ranged from 313 to 1,679 ms after outlier exclusion, the average deviation from the nominal 1 - ms interval was 0.66 ms. This deviation was minimal relative to the observed reaction time range. These results suggest that the program’s internal timer provided sufficient millisecond-level precision for measuring reaction time differences across conditions.

The main session was divided into two sub-sessions. The pre- and post-context conditions were separated because the learners might have noticed the differences between them. The first set contained critical and filler items in the pre- and post-context conditions, respectively. The second set contained critical and filler items in the post- and pre-context conditions, respectively. The order of the sets was counterbalanced between participants to avoid the order effect. To avoid making the participants aware of the differences between the pre- and post-context materials, an unrelated task was inserted between the two sets, wherein a sentence was presented (e.g., President of the United States lives in White House), and the participants were asked to insert the article “the” wherever they believed it necessary. There was a total of 91 sentences.

After the semantic Stroop task, the participants completed the word-level typicality rating task to confirm the typicality of the target words. All keywords used in the semantic Stroop task were presented with color words that reflect each objects’ typicality: typical, atypical, and unrelated (e.g., a brown bear, a white bear, a green bear). The participants were asked to rate the typicality of the color-word association on a six-point Likert scale (1: never comes to mind, 6: comes to mind first). Immediately thereafter, participants completed sentence-level typicality rating tasks to verify whether the typicality of the image in a sentence matched what the authors had determined. Although this task was identical to that used in the process of the item building (see Sect. 3.2.1 Item Building), each participant performed the two versions of the tasks, and the order of the tasks was counterbalanced. Both word- and sentence-level typicality rating tasks had no time constraints, and the participants answered the questions using Google Forms. The order of the presentations was randomized using Google Forms. At the end of the experiment, the participants answered a background questionnaire.

Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023). Before analyzing reaction time data, we excluded incorrect responses, filler items, and all data from participants with overall accuracy of the Stroop task less than 80% and with an overall accuracy of less than 50% for the comprehension question. Reaction times that deviated by more than ± 3 median absolute deviations (Leys et al., 2013) from the median were also excluded. This data treatment deleted 9.21% data of the target item, and the remaining 5,638 observations were analyzed.

After the treatment, a series of linear mixed-effects modeling was performed using lme4 package 1.1–37 (Bates et al. 2015b). The log-transformed reaction times of the Stroop task were used as dependent variables. The reaction times were log-transformed as the lognormal distribution for the best probabilistic distributions among Weibull, gamma, lognormal, and normal (e.g., Kusanagi, 2017) using the fitdist function of fitdistrplus package 1.2-6 (Delignette-Muller & Dutang, 2015). As lmer function does not have a log-normal distribution in its family argument, we fit the log-transformed data by setting normal to the family argument. The independent variables were sentence typicality, word typicality, context position, and interaction of the three. The number of trials and the scaled reading time for each sentence were included as possible covariates. The reading time for each sentence was measured from the presentation of the sentence to the time when the participants pressed the space bar. All categorical variables were repeat-coded to compare neighboring factor levels with the function contr.sdif the MASS package 7.3–60 (Venables & Ripley, 2002).

The best model structure was determined using the following two steps. First, we compared the models with different possible covariates: reading time and presentation order. The null model was compared to the model that included either or both possible covariates. The model with presentation order and scaled sentence reading time had the lowest AIC values. Subsequently, the best random structure was selected using the rePCA function of lme4 package 1.1–37 (Bates et al. 2015b). Following Bates et al. (2015a), the rePCA function was applied to the maximum random effects model with random structure correlation and no correlation parameters. After checking the number of dimensions, one model term was removed at a time to investigate whether it significantly increased the goodness-of-fit. The procedure was repeated as long as the goodness-of-fit increased significantly. Finally, the goodness-of-fit was compared between the models with and without correlation parameters. Correlation parameters were included if they significantly increased the goodness-of-fit.

The variance inflation factors (VIF) were reviewed using the check_collinearity function of performance package 0.16.0 (Lüdecke et al., 2021) to determine whether there were any multicollinearity issues once the final model was established. The analysis confirmed that the final models had no multicollinearity problems (the threshold was 5). Details of the analysis, R codes, and data for the analysis are provided in the OSF project (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/S5DY7).

Results

Word- and Sentence-Level Rating Tasks

First, the results of the word- and sentence-level rating tasks were reviewed before analyzing the reaction times in the semantic Stroop task to ensure that the typicality assigned to the items matches the typicality that the participants had in mind and, if necessary, make adjustments. For the word-level task, there was no problem in 14 out of 15 target words. We changed the item coding for one item (onion) because the scores for the unrelated onion color (red) (M = 3.77, SD = 1.82) were higher than those for the typical onion color (brown) (M = 3.49, SD = 1.88) (i.e., for the participants, red was more typical than brown for the surface color of an onion). Based on the results, the typical color of the onions changed to red, and the unrelated color changed to brown. For the sentence-level rating task, there was no discrepancy between the original typicality assignment and the participants’ ratings.

Semantic Stroop Task

Overall accuracy of the semantic Stroop task was 97.6%. Descriptive statistics showed that in the pre-context condition, the reaction times to atypical–typical conditions were the fastest, followed by typical–typical conditions. The atypical–unrelated condition showed the shortest reaction times. The descriptive statistics of the post-context condition showed a similar trend: typical words elicited faster reaction times regardless of sentence typicality. In addition, the atypical–unrelated condition was the slowest. Under all conditions, the post-context position showed faster reaction times than the pre-context position. Overall, Hypothesis 1 (readers activate the object color) was partially supported. Reaction times for typical color words were faster than those for other colors under both typical and atypical sentence conditions. The results did not support the hypothesis in a strict sense that reaction times should be faster whenever the target color matched the color implied by the sentence. Rather, they suggested that readers activated information about the object’s typical color even when the sentence implied an atypical color. However, Hypothesis 2 (the position of context phrases changes the simulation) was not supported. Contrary to this prediction, the descriptive statistics showed that, even in the pre-context condition, responses were faster to typical colors than to atypical colors when the sentences implied atypical colors.

Discussion

The current study investigated whether readers activate the color of inferred objects considering the influence of sentence typicality and context position using a semantic Stroop task. In this task, participants read sentences implying either a typical or atypical object color and then answered the ink color of a target word. Faster reaction times to ink colors matching the implied object color were taken as evidence for color activation. By considering the context position, we examined whether readers activated both typical and atypical colors when reading sentences implying atypical colors. The results showed that, regardless of the implied typicality (typical or atypical) and the context position (pre or post), readers responded faster to typical color words than to atypical and unrelated colors. Therefore, our findings demonstrated that readers activate the typical color of objects when they read sentences, regardless of sentence typicality and context position.

Both the current study and Connell and Lynott (2009) examined the activation of object color in typical and atypical sentence conditions. Both studies identified main effect of word typicality supporting the activation of typical colors. However, the two studies differ in the activation of atypical colors. Despite the experiment being designed with sufficient statistical power, this study did not find evidence for the activation of atypical object colors as argued by Connell and Lynott (2009). Since Connell and Lynott’s (2009) interpretation of p-value is not conventionally strong, it seems plausible to maintain that the interaction between sentence typicality and word color typicality is not significant. Thus, readers activate the typical color of an object regardless of the typicality implied by a sentence.

This study also considered the influence of context position to examine the multiple activation of target embodied knowledge claimed in previous studies. Previous studies demonstrated that readers activate the typical object shape without providing a sentence (context) (e.g., Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013). Based on this finding, it can be assumed that readers may only activate what the sentence implies if context is presented before the keyword. This is because providing context may inhibit activating irrelevant object. However, even in the pre-context condition, responses were faster to typical than to atypical colors when the sentences implied atypical colors. This result did not provide evidence that readers activate the atypical color of objects (bear in white) regardless of context position. This finding is inconsistent with previous studies showing that readers activated visual traits of objects along with the subsequent information (e.g., Horchak & Garrido, 2021; Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013).

These different results might be due to the difference between this study and previous studies in the change implied in the sentences. The object’s state changed substantially in the experimental sentences in the previous studies on shape (e.g., The woman dropped the ice cream). By contrast, it did not change significantly in the current study (e.g., Joe was excited to see a bear at the North Pole). The former sentence suggests changes in shape, whereas the latter only adds contextual information (e.g., in the woods/at the North Pole) to indicate the object-colors. This difference in the degree of change in the target object may have led to different results between studies on shape (Horchak & Garrido, 2021; Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013) and color (Connell & Lynott, 2009). Thus, the results do not rule out the constant and competing scenarios; rather, they suggest that these scenarios may not apply to the activation of object color in contexts that merely specify the object’s color without implying a color change. In addition, the lack of evidence that readers activate atypical colors even when the pre-context conditions are atypical suggests the robustness of activating typical colors. Activating atypical colors may be difficult without conscious effort to imagine the objects. The results may change if the explicit direction such as “imagine you are at the North Pole” is included before the Stroop task.

Task differences should also be noted. Previous shape studies used sentence-picture verification tasks (e.g., Horchak & Garrido, 2021; Kang et al., 2020; Sato et al., 2013), whereas the current study used Stroop task. Thus, future studies can investigate the effect of task differences.

One limitation of this study is that it did not consider the influence of specific color (red) and the interpretation should be more cautious. A post-hoc analysis of the filler items was conducted to investigate the influence of specific colors. The combination of colors and words was irrelevant for the filler items (e.g., banana-RED and butter-BROWN); therefore, it was possible to examine the influence of color, excluding the influence of word typicality. The results showed that readers responded much faster to red than to other colors (red: M = 789.72 ms, SD = 269.82; brown: M = 836.90 ms, SD = 281.12; green: M = 890.09 ms, SD = 285.50; white: M = 868.83 ms, SD = 300.11). It is unlikely that the reaction times for red were the fastest because red was assigned to keys typed with the right hand (the majority of participants [32 out of 35] were right-handed). This is because the reaction times for green, which were also assigned to the keys typed with the right hand, were much slower than those for red. Interestingly, items whose typical color was red tended to receive high typicality scores on word-level typicality tasks (e.g., apple, tomato, strawberry). At this point, it is difficult to determine the effects of the specific color (red) and its relationship with typicality. Additionally, the same statistical model was run on the data without items whose typical color is red (apple, tomato, strawberry, and plum). The Stroop effect was not observed in the new dataset (typical–unrelated: Estimate = -0.02 [-0.05, 0.01], SE = 0.01, t = -1.52, p = .130; atypical–typical: Estimate = 0.02 [-0.01, 0.05], SE = 0.01, t = 1.62, p = .108). The interaction between colors, language, and cognition has attracted the attention of researchers (e.g., Forbes & Plunkett, 2023), which can also be considered a topic for future research.

In conclusion, this study supported the embodied view of human language processing. The results of this experiment suggested that readers activate typical colors of an object. However, the study did not find evidence of activating objects’ multiple colors when a sentence indicated the atypical color of an object. Thus far, multiple activation can only be supported for the shape information based on previous studies (e.g., Horchak & Garrido, 2021; Kang et al., 2020). Future studies should explore whether readers can activate implied atypical colors by considering task differences and the effects of specific colors.
