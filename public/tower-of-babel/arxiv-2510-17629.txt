Abstract
. This paper studies the clustering behavior of weakly interacting diffusions under the influence of sufficiently localized attractive interaction potentials on the one-dimensional torus. We describe how this clustering behavior is closely related to the presence of discontinuous phase transitions in the mean-field PDE. For local attractive interactions, we employ a new variant of the strict Riesz rearrangement inequality to prove that all global minimizers of the free energy are either uniform or single-cluster states, in the sense that they are symmetrically decreasing. We analyze different timescales for the particle system and the mean-field (McKean–Vlasov) PDE, arguing that while the particle system can exhibit coarsening by both coalescence and diffusive mass exchange between clusters, the clusters in the mean-field PDE are unable to move and coarsening occurs via the mass exchange of clusters. By introducing a new model for this mass exchange, we argue that the PDE exhibits dynamical metastability. We conclude by presenting careful numerical experiments that demonstrate the validity of our model.



1.
Introduction

Interacting particle systems and their mean-field limits appear in many applications ranging from physics to mathematical models for swarming, flocking, opinion dynamics, and collective behavior. They are also used in the development of algorithms for sampling and optimization. Often, the emergence of collective behavior, e.g. consensus formation
[
HK02
]
or synchronization
[
Kur81
]
, can be interpreted as a (first- or second-order) phase transition from a disordered to an ordered state; see
[
CGPS20
]
and the references therein, in particular
[
CP10
]
.

Noise-driven interacting particle systems can exhibit metastable states that persist over long time scales. As examples, we mention the formation of clusters in opinion models
[
GPY17
]
as well as in mathematical models for transformers
[
GLPR25
,
GKPR24
,
BPA24
]
; see also
[
SS25
, Section 6.1]
. Noise-induced metastability in interacting particle systems is a manifestation of the presence of discontinuous phase transitions
[
Pen+84
,
GS20
]
. It is expected that, near a transition point, the mean-field description is no longer valid for arbitrarily long times
[
DGPS23
]
and that the effect of noise, through the Dean–Kawasaki SPDE
[
Kaw73
,
Dea96
]
, must be taken into account to capture metastable states accurately
[
Weh+25
]
. The primary objective of this work is to provide a rigorous mathematical understanding of the emergence of clusters in weakly interacting diffusions exhibiting first-order phase transitions and to derive a model for the dynamics of these clusters.

In
[
PNT90
]
, the authors justify that the mean-field description for Hamiltonian systems is inadequate to describe all regimes in the Newtonian dynamics of particles interacting via a pairwise attractive Gaussian potential.
They observed that for sufficiently strong interactions, the initially diffused particles form clusters that eventually collapse to form a single cluster (or, as the authors of
[
PNT90
]
refer to it, the condensed phase). Although the mean-field Vlasov equation describes the final cluster state, which is a stationary state of the mean-field PDE, it is unable to explain the transient dynamics that leads to the formation of the condensed phase. We quote this main conclusion of theirs below:

We find that the condensed phase is a stationary solution of the Vlasov equation, but the Vlasov dynamics cannot describe the collapse.

The goal of this paper is to study a similar phenomenology in the setting of weakly interacting diffusions with localised attractive interaction potentials.
The thermodynamic limit in our setting is the so-called McKean–Vlasov equation, and our main conclusion reads as follows:

Although the single-cluster state is a stationary solution of the mean-field McKean-Vlasov PDE, coalescence of clusters is not captured by the mean-field dynamics.

It turns out that the weakly interacting diffusion model studied in this paper exhibits very rich and interesting dynamics.
While the interacting particle model in
[
PNT90
]
is deterministic and the entropy in the corresponding mean-field Vlasov equation is conserved, the model of interacting diffusions considered in this paper implies that the corresponding mean-field equation is a diffusion-aggregation equation that exhibits coarsening through mass exchange between clusters. This mass exchange leads to
dynamic metastability
of the mean-field dynamics, which is related to the notion of slow motion of gradient flows studied in
[
OR07
]
.

We comment on some more general relations to similar effects observed in related models. First, we note that our identification of the two time scales for tunneling and coalescence is similar to the classical theory of
Ostwald ripening
: a configuration of near-equilibrium liquid droplets sitting on a precursor film that wets the entire substrate can coarsen in time by two different mechanisms: collapse or collision of droplets
[
GORS09
]
. Here, the phenomenological model is a gradient flow of Cahn–Hilliard type, where the dynamics is entirely deterministic. The authors provide a reduced model for the position and volume of single droplets in
[
GORS09
, Section 4]
, which also allows them to study the crossover between the timescales of migration (leading to collapse) and ripening (through coalescence).
The relevant time scales can be identified using suitable energies, in this case related to surface tension, and it is possible to derive a differential inequality for them. This approach was pioneered in
[
KO02
]
and later refined in
[
CNO06
,
ORS06
,
BOS11
,
OSS13
]
.

In the physical literature, a study of diffusive aggregating particles can be found in
[
PTA14
]
, where the observed behavior is studied on the PDE level. There, Ostwald ripening is related to volume modes induced by the escape of particles from clusters due to random fluctuations. However, a crucial difference from our study is that the translational modes causing movement and joining of clusters are again deterministically caused by long-range aggregation forces between the clusters, whereas in our model, the interaction potential has finite interaction range and clusters can move only due to the Brownian motions.

We also comment on the link between the problem studied in our paper and Kramers’ problem and its generalizations. Indeed, the mass leakage between clusters that occurs in the model that we derive in this paper is a generalization of Kramers’ problem pioneered in
[
Kra40
]
, where a Brownian particle is confined in a double-well potential and the timescale is related to the energy barrier of the system. Our generalization requires considering potentials with multiple timescales, which in addition change dynamically as a result of the mass exchange. In recent years, several different techniques for studying metastability in time-independent potentials have been developed, using both stochastic
[
Mat94
,
OV05
,
Ber13
,
MS14
,
BH15
]
and analytic
[
PSV10
,
HN11
,
ET16
,
HN19
]
methods. Our setting can be viewed as a combination of these, together with the analysis of Langevin-type dynamics for time-dependent potentials in the context of Brownian ratchets, e.g.,
[
KK02
]
.

Finally, we discuss briefly the relevance of the Dean–Kawasaki SPDE for the study of the dynamics of clusters. This SPDE was used to study the statistics of the clusters
[
Weh+25
]
; see also
[
Del+16
]
for some earlier numerical experiments. The Dean–Kawasaki SPDE can be used, in principle, to study noise-induced metastability for our model by applying an infinite-dimensional extension of the Freidlin–Wentzell theory
[
FW84
]
, developed by Dawson and Gärtner
[
DG89
]
. However, for the analysis of the relevant timescales in the models studied in this paper, we need to understand the combined effect of noise-induced cluster coalescence and of dynamical metastability due to slow motion for gradient flows
[
OR07
]
. Understanding the relationship between the different timescales induced by the different stochastic and deterministic effects is one of the main challenges that we address in this work.

1.1.
Setup of the model

We consider the interacting particle system given by the system of
N
stochastic differential equations

(1.1)

{\rm d}{X}_{t}^{i}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla W_{\gamma,\ell}\big({X_{t}^{i}-X_{t}^{j}}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}B_{t}^{i},\qquad\text{for }i=1,\dots,N

on the one-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}\simeq[0,1)
. Here,
\gamma>0
is the interaction strength,
\ell>0
is the interaction range and
(B_{t}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent standard Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}
.
For some fixed even function
w:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the interaction potential
W_{\gamma,\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

(1.2)

\displaystyle W_{\gamma,\ell}(x):=\gamma\ell w\left(\frac{x}{\ell}\right)\qquad\text{for }\lvert x\rvert<\frac{1}{2},

and extended
1
-periodically to
\mathbb{R}
. We are interested in locally attractive interactions.
The results of our paper are valid under the following assumption on
w
:

Assumption 1.1
.

The function
w:\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,0]
satisfies:

(i)

w
is an even and Lipschitz continuous function which is symmetrically non-decreasing, that is,
w
is non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
.

(ii)

w
has compact support, there exists some
s_{w}\in[0,\infty)
such that
\supp w=[-s_{w},s_{w}]
and
w
is bounded from above by
0
.

(iii)

w
is
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of
0
and
w
attains its global minimum at
0
with
w^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
;

Typical examples include the Hegselmann–Krause family
[
GPY17
]
, where
w
is such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x)=\phi_{0}(\lvert x\rvert)x,\qquad\text{with }\phi_{0}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)\text{ having compact support.}

The simplest example for this is

(1.4)

\displaystyle w(x)=\frac{1}{2}({x}^{2}-1)\chi_{[0,1]}(\lvert x\rvert),

that is,
\phi_{0}(r)=\chi_{[0,1]}(r)
in (
1.3
).
We are especially interested in the
strong local
interaction regime, i.e.,
\gamma\gg 1
and
\ell\ll 1
is small.

If
w
is even, we can write (
1.1
) in the form

(1.5)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=-\nabla\mathbf{W}_{\gamma,\ell}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},

where
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{N})
,

\mathbf{W}_{\gamma,\ell}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}W_{\gamma,\ell}(x_{j}-x_{k})
is
N
times the microscopic interaction energy
and
\mathbf{B}_{t}=(B_{t}^{1},\dots,B_{t}^{N})
.

The mean-field limit

If the initial positions
X^{1}_{0},\dots,X^{N}_{0}
are sampled i.i.d. from some
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with sufficiently many moments and if
w
is sufficiently regular (say Lipschitz continuous for instance), then in the limit as the number of particles
N
goes to infinity, the empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}
at time
t
converges
[
Szn91
,
CD22
,
CD22a
]
to
\rho_{t}
, where
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves the mean-field McKean–Vlasov PDE

(1.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\left(\nabla W_{\gamma,\ell}*\rho_{t}\right)\rho_{t}\right),

where
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{T}
.
The PDE (
1.6
) may be seen as a
W_{2}
-gradient flow
[
CMV03
]
for the free energy

(1.7)

\mathcal{F}_{\gamma,\ell}(\rho)=\int_{[0,1]}\rho(x)\log\rho(x)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{[0,1]^{2}}W_{\gamma,\ell}\left(x-y\right)\rho(x)\rho(y)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}y.

The free energy acts as a Lyapunov functional for the evolution (
1.6
):

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathcal{F}_{\gamma,\ell}(\rho_{t})=-\int_{[0,1]}\rho_{t}\big\lvert\nabla\big(\log\rho_{t}+W_{\gamma,\ell}*\rho_{t}\big)\big\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\leq 0.

1.2.
Summary of the effects

In this work, we study the behaviour of various competing phenomena that are present in the interacting particle system (
1.1
) and the McKean–Vlasov equation (
1.6
) and their associated timescales. We summarize these effects below:

\ell=0.1
and

for

(E1)

Formation of clusters:
The particles, although initially spread out, quickly form multiple clusters driven by the attractive nature of the interaction potential (
Fig.

1.1
, left).
Typically, this is the shortest timescale in the system. The mean-field PDE (
1.6
) exhibits a similar phenomenon (
Fig.

1.1
, right).

(E2)

Coalescence of clusters:
The clusters themselves perform Brownian motions and so can collide and merge into each other (
Fig.

1.2
). This phenomenon is absent in the mean-field system since its associated timescale grows linearly with the number of particles.

(E3)

Leakage of mass and dynamical metastability:
Once the clusters are formed, individual particles can leak from one cluster to another driven by excursions of the Brownian motions which drive the individual particles. This phenomenon is present both in the particle system and mean-field limit. The associated timescale is of the form
e^{\gamma\ell\Delta m}
, where
\Delta=-\inf w
and
m
is the cluster’s macroscopic mass, that is the number of particles in the cluster divided by
N
.
Fig.

1.3
shows an example for the mass exchange between clusters if the PDE is started initially with three clusters with different masses. Since the timescale for mass leakage
e^{\gamma\ell\Delta m}
depends exponentially on
m
, the mass exchange among clusters leads to dynamical metastability in the PDE
[
OR07
]
: A multi-cluster state can remain stable over a long period of time until it suddenly collapses to a single-cluster state, see
Figs.

1.3
and
1.4
for examples.

(E4)

Microscopic reversibility:
Finally, once a single-cluster state has been formed it is always possible for it to spontaneously dissolve due to the effect of the noise. This is a large
N
large deviation event of Dawson–Gärtner-type
[
DG89
]
and occurs at a timescale of order
e^{N\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}}
, where
\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}
is the free energy gap of the limiting free energy. Since it is driven by noise and the timescale grows exponentially in the number of particles, this phenomenon is present only in the particle system and not the mean-field system.

In what follows, we will analyse each of these phenomena individually and study the dependence of the associated timescale on the parameters of the system, i.e. the strength of the interaction

1.3.
Our contributions

A similar model was already studied in
[
GPY17
]
for the particular case of the Hegselmann-Krause interaction potential; it was shown heuristically that if
\gamma
is sufficiently large, the microscopic system exhibits initial clustering (effect
Item

(E1)
).
Subsequently, the authors explain how the clusters move like coalescing heavy Brownian motions (effect
Item

(E2)
). See also the numerical experiments and statistical analysis of the clusters in
[
Weh+25
]
.
Our contributions are as follows.

•

Generalizing the heuristics by
[
GPY17
]
on the initial formation of clusters, we determine the timescale of initial clustering for the mean-field PDE as well as for the particle system under more general initial conditions than
[
GPY17
]
.

•

We prove for a large family of compactly supported potentials
w
such as (
1.4
) that global minimizers of the free energy (
1.7
) are either the uniform state or single-cluster states in the sense that they are symmetrically decreasing with respect to their center of mass. The latter is true beyond the phase transition, that is for
\gamma>\gamma_{c}
, in which case global minimizers are non-trivial, that is, different from the uniform state.
The argument is based on a new variant of the strict Riesz rearrangement inequality which holds true for interaction kernels whose monotony is only locally strict (in contrast to the globally strictly decreasing kernels considered in
[
Bec93
]
) and can therefore be applied to certain compactly supported interaction potentials (see also
[
Sha25
, Chapter 5]
for related results).

•

By extending the description from
[
GPY17
]
for the coalescence of clusters, we introduce a new model to approximately describe both the coalescence of clusters
Item

(E2)
and the mass exchange between clusters
Item

(E3)
due to the excursions of single particles.
In particular, we argue that the clusters in the mean-field PDE are unable to move and that the PDE cannot describe the merging of clusters, except for situations where two clusters are within the range of the interaction potential.
Therefore, apart from such cases, coarsening in the PDE occurs only through mass exchange.

•

Based on our coarse-grained model for the mass exchange, we argue and show numerically that the PDE exhibits dynamic metastability: A multi-cluster state can remain stable for a long period of time of order
e^{\gamma\ell\Delta m}
until one of its clusters suddenly vanishes.

•

Besides the timescales for
Item

(E1)
,
Item

(E2)
(which is of order
N
) and
Item

(E3)
(which is of order
e^{\gamma\ell\Delta m}
), we also describe the timescale of microscopic reversibility
Item

(E4)
.
In the mean-field limit
N\to\infty
, the timescale for coalescence and microscopic reversibility diverge to infinity, leaving the timescale for
Item

(E1)
and
Item

(E3)
as the ones which determine the behaviour of the PDE.

•

We identify certain scaling regimes in which we conjecture the system (
1.1
) to converge in a suitable limit towards the Modified Massive Arratia flow
[
Kon17
,
KR19
]
.

We can summarise our observations by considering the empirical measure
\mu_{t}^{N}
of the interacting particle system which can be formally interpreted as the solution of the following interacting Dean–Kawasaki equation:

(1.9)

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}^{N}=\Delta\mu_{t}^{N}+\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}^{N}\nabla W_{\gamma,\ell}*\mu_{t}^{N})+\sqrt{2}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\cdot\left(\sqrt[\small]{\cramped{\mu_{t}^{N}}}\xi\right)\,,

where
\xi
is vector-valued space-time white noise. The above SPDE has the structure of a slow gradient flow (corresponding to the deterministic part of the above equation) perturbed by a random process (corresponding to the transport noise), making the SPDE a reversible Markov process with respect to the lifted Gibbs measure of the
N
-particle system. The competing timescales and the effects
Items

(E1)
,
(E2)
,
(E3)
and
(E4)
can then be interpreted as arising from the different terms in the SPDE.

The initial clustering
Item

(E1)
is driven by the short-time linear instability of constant state under the influence of the deterministic part of the equation. The coalescence of clusters
Item

(E2)
is produced by the noise term driving the motion of clusters. Indeed, one can check that
\sqrt{2}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\cdot\left(\sqrt[\small]{\cramped{\mu_{t}^{N}}}\xi\right)
has quadratic variation of order
N^{-1}t
thus producing a linear timescale in
N
. The mass exchange
Item

(E3)
is produced by the slow motion of the deterministic part (the slow gradient flow) and arises from the competition between the Laplacian and the interaction term. The timescale for microscopic reversibility
Item

(E4)
can also be explained as the small-noise (large
N
) large deviation timescale of the above SPDE which is exponentially large in
N
.

Background: Initial clustering after

Linearizing the PDE (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\rho}_{\rm pert}=\partial_{x}^{2}{\rho}_{\rm pert}+\partial_{x}\bigl(\rho_{\rm unif}W^{\prime}_{\gamma,\ell}*{\rho}_{\rm pert}\bigr).

The Fourier coefficients

for
k\in\mathcal{K}:=2\pi\mathbb{Z}
satisfy the system of linear ODEs

(2.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(t,k)=\bigg[i\rho_{\rm unif}k\int_{[0,1]}W^{\prime}_{\gamma,\ell}(x)e^{-ikx}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x-k^{2}\bigg]{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(t,k).

Hence, we can read-off the growth rates of the Fourier modes as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\psi(k)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Re}\left(\rho_{\rm unif}k\int_{[0,1]}W^{\prime}_{\gamma,\ell}(x)e^{-ikx}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x-k^{2}\right)=-k^{2}\left(\hat{W}_{\gamma,\ell}(k)+1\right),

where

(2.4)

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{\gamma,\ell}(k)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}W_{\gamma,\ell}(x)\cos(kx)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=\gamma\ell\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}w\left(\frac{x}{\ell}\right)\cos(kx)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

(2.5)

\displaystyle=-\gamma\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\prime}\left(\frac{x}{\ell}\right)\frac{\sin(kx)}{k}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

are the Fourier modes of the interaction potential
W_{\gamma,\ell}
. By the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma, the growth rates
\psi(k)
are bounded from above for
k\in\mathcal{K}
and are therefore maximized at some
k_{\rm max}=\argmax_{k\in\mathcal{K},k\geq 0}\psi(k)
. The sign of the maximal growth rate
\psi(k_{\rm max})
determines the stability of (
2.1
):
Viewing
\ell>0
as fixed and small and
\gamma>0
as bifurcation parameter, the minimal
\gamma>0
such that one of the growth rates
\psi(k)
becomes positive is denoted by
\gamma_{\sharp}
, called the
point of linear stability
. It is given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\sharp}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{W}_{\gamma=1,\ell}(k)}\,,

see also
[
CP10
]
, where
\gamma_{\sharp}>0
is true for sufficiently small
\ell>0
since for those
\ell
it holds that
\widehat{W}_{1,\ell}(1)=\ell\int_{\supp w}w(x)\cos(\ell x)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x<0
.

In case
\gamma<\gamma_{\sharp}
, we have
\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\psi(k)<0
, the linearized system (
2.1
) is stable and small perturbations of the uniform state
\rho_{\rm unif}
will converge to the uniform state
\rho_{\rm unif}
, see
[
CGPS20
]
for exact results directly on the level of the nonlinear PDE (
1.6
).

On the other hand, if
\gamma>\gamma_{\sharp}
, then

\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\psi(k)>0
, the system (
2.1
) is unstable and clustering occurs by means of the dominant Fourier mode
k_{\rm max}
with maximal growth rate. The estimated number of initial clusters is determined by
k_{\rm max}
and given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle n_{\rm clusters}=\frac{k_{\rm max}}{2\pi}\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular, the inter-cluster distance is given by
d_{\rm clusters}=1/n_{\rm clusters}=2\pi/k_{\rm max}
. Clustering in the linearized PDE (
2.1
) with a number of
n_{\rm clusters}
occurs whenever the initial condition
{\rho}_{\rm pert}(0,{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}{\bullet}})
is sufficiently close to a constant function and
{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(0,k_{\rm max})=\overline{{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(0,-k_{\rm max})}\neq 0
. Let us assume such an initial condition for (
2.1
). Then, since the dominant modes
{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(t,\pm k_{\rm max})
grow exponentially as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lvert{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(t,\pm k_{\rm max})\rvert=e^{\psi(\pm k_{\rm max})t}\lvert{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(0,\pm k_{\rm max})\rvert\qquad\text{for $t>0$},

the initial clustering time for (
2.1
) is of the order

(2.9)

\displaystyle t_{\rm clustering}\asymp\frac{-\log\left(\lvert{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(0,k_{\rm max})\rvert\right)}{\psi(k_{\rm max})}.

As we will argue in the following, initial clustering in the regime
\gamma>\gamma_{\sharp}
occurs both for the PDE (
1.6
) when started from a perturbation of the uniform state which has a non-zero
k_{\rm max}
-Fourier mode and for the particle system.

2.2.
Initial clustering in the PDE

The previous section shows that for
\gamma>\gamma_{\sharp}
, starting the PDE from a small perturbation
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])\cap L^{2}([0,1])
of the uniform state
\rho_{\rm unif}\equiv 1
such that
{\hat{\rho}}_{\rm pert}(0,k_{\rm max})\neq 0
yields initial clustering with the same number of clusters
n_{\rm clusters}
as the particle system. This is also observed numerically, see (

(2.10)

\displaystyle t_{\rm clustering}\asymp\frac{-\log\left(\left\lvert\widehat{\rho_{0}}(k_{\rm max})\right\rvert\right)}{\psi(k_{\rm max})}.

2.3.
Initial clustering in the particle system

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])\cap L^{2}([0,1])
be a probability measure which is close to or equal to the uniform distribution
\rho_{\rm unif}
and assume that the initial positions of the particles (
1.1
) are sampled i.i.d. from
\rho_{0}
. By the central limit theorem, the fluctuations

(2.11)

{\rho}_{\rm pert}^{N}(0,x)=\sqrt{N}\Bigg[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{j}_{0}}-\rho_{0}\Bigg]

converge to a Gaussian-valued measure
\mu
, that is, for each
h\in C_{b}([0,1])
, the real-valued random variable
\int_{[0,1]}h(x)\mu({\rm d}x)
is
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}(h))
-distributed, where
\sigma^{2}(h)=\int\bigl(h(x)-\int h(y)\rho_{0}({\rm d}y)\bigr)^{2}\rho_{0}({\rm d}x)
. Writing the initial empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{j}_{0}}
in the form

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mu^{N}_{0}\approx\rho_{\rm unif}+(\rho_{0}-\rho_{\rm unif})+\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{N}},

we find from (
2.9
) that the initial clustering time is of the order

(2.13)

\displaystyle t_{\rm clustering}\asymp\frac{-\log\big(\big\lvert\widehat{\rho_{0}}(k_{\rm max})+\frac{\hat{\mu}(k_{\rm max})}{\sqrt{N}}\big\rvert\big)}{\psi(k_{\rm max})},

where
\hat{f}(k)=\int_{[0,1]}f(x)e^{-ikx}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
denotes the
k
-th Fourier mode of
f
.

For the initial clustering timescale in the particle system, this implies the following: If the particles are sampled i.i.d. from the uniform distribution
\rho_{\rm unif}
, then the initial clustering time is of the order

(2.14)

\displaystyle t_{\rm clustering}\asymp\frac{\log N}{\psi(k_{\rm max})}.

This was already described in
[
GPY17
]
and is in line with the fact that
\rho_{\rm unif}
is a stationary state for the mean-field PDE (
1.6
), so that when starting (
1.6
) at
\rho_{\rm unif}
, no clustering occurs.

On the other hand, if the particles are sampled i.i.d. from a distribution
\rho_{1}
which is close to
\rho_{\rm unif}
, but
\widehat{\rho_{1}}(k_{\rm max})\neq 0
(in particular,
\rho_{1}\neq\rho_{\rm unif}
), then the initial clustering time is of the same order (
2.10
) as for the limiting PDE (
1.6
).

2.4.
Explicit number of initial clusters for the Hegselmann–Krause prototype

For
w
as defined in (
1.4
), we have for all
\ell\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
k\in\mathcal{K}
that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}W_{\gamma=1,\ell}(x)\cos(kx)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=\frac{2\cos(k\ell)}{k^{2}}-\frac{2\sin(k\ell)}{k^{3}\ell}.

One can show that this is minimized for
k=2\pi
which is the smallest positive element in
\mathcal{K}
.
Taylor’s formula around
\ell=0
yields
\widehat{W}_{1,\ell}(k)=\int_{[0,1]}W_{\gamma=1,\ell}(x)\cos(kx)\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=-\frac{2}{3}\ell^{2}+\frac{1}{12}k^{2}\ell^{4}+\dots
, so that

(2.16)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\sharp}\sim\frac{3}{2\ell^{2}}\qquad\text{as }\ell\to 0.

In order to determine the number of initial clusters,
we note that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\psi(k)=-k^{2}+2\gamma\frac{\sin(k\ell)}{k\ell}-2\gamma\cos(k\ell).

Viewing
\psi
as a function on
(0,\infty)
, we have that for large enough
\gamma
,
\psi(k)
attains its maximum on
(0,\infty)
in the interval
[0,\frac{2\pi}{\ell}]
.
The derivative is given by

(2.18)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(k)

\displaystyle=-2k+\frac{2\gamma}{\ell}\left(\frac{\ell\cos(\ell k)}{k}-\frac{\sin(\ell k)}{k^{2}}\right)+2\gamma\ell\sin(k\ell)

Setting
\psi^{\prime}(k)=0
leads to the equation

(2.19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\ell}\left(\frac{\ell\cos(\ell k)}{k^{2}}-\frac{\sin(\ell k)}{k^{3}}\right)+\ell\frac{\sin(k\ell)}{k}

As
\gamma\to\infty
, the limit
y:=\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}{\ell k_{\rm max}(\gamma)}
solves the transcendental equation

(2.20)

\displaystyle 0=y\cos y-\sin y+y^{2}\sin y\Leftrightarrow\tan y=\frac{y}{1-y^{2}}\,.

The smallest positive solution of this equation is at
y_{\ast}\approx 2.74
, so that

(2.21)

\displaystyle k_{\rm max}\sim\frac{y_{\ast}}{\ell}\qquad\text{as }\gamma\to\infty.

In particular, the number of initial clusters is given by

(2.22)

\displaystyle n_{\rm clusters}\sim\frac{y_{\ast}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\ell}\approx 0.44\frac{1}{\ell}\qquad\text{as }\gamma\to\infty\,.

3.
Stationary and metastable states

Having analysed the initial formation of clusters in the previous section, we now want to understand the stationary states for the mean-field PDE (
1.6
). While the particle system (
1.1
) has a unique stationary state given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\rho^{N}_{\infty}=\frac{e^{-\mathbf{W}_{\gamma,\ell}}}{Z_{N}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{N}),\qquad\text{where $Z_{N}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{N}}e^{-\mathbf{W}_{\gamma,\ell}(x^{1},\dots,x^{N})}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x^{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x^{N}$,}

the PDE (

) exhibits phase transitions and can have multiple stationary states which are characterized by
[

, see also
[

]
. In particular, a state

(3.2)

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{Z(\rho)}e^{-W_{\gamma,\ell}\ast\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}),\qquad\text{where }Z(\rho):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-(W_{\gamma,\ell}\ast\rho)(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x.

Note that the uniform state
\rho_{\rm unif}
is always stationary. As proven in
[
CGPS20
, Theorem 2.3]
, under reasonable assumptions on the interaction potential, all steady states are smooth and strictly positive.
In addition, any fixed point of (
3.2
) is a critical point of the free energy

from (
1.7
).

Simulations suggest that for generic initial conditions, the PDE converges to a single-cluster state as

unless
\gamma
is small enough, in which case the PDE converges to the uniform state
\rho_{0}
. This is in line with the fact that the mean-field PDE has a discontinuous phase transition at some
\gamma_{c}
, so that for
\gamma>\gamma_{c}
, there exist global minimizers of the free energy which are non-uniform (see
Section

3.1
below for a proof). Moreover, in
Theorem

3.3
we prove that all global minimizers are either the uniform state or single-cluster states in the sense that they are symmetrically decreasing. The argument generalizes the strict version of the Riesz rearrangement inequality from
[
Bec93
]
to compactly supported kernels, crucially using the analyticity of stationary states which we show in
Lemma

3.7
.
A heuristic argument as well as numerical results as explained in
Section

3.3
suggest that for large
\gamma
, the global minimizer of the free energy is in fact approximately (a circular version of) a Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}=\frac{\ell}{\gamma w^{\prime\prime}(0)}
.

3.2.
Non-trivial global minimizers of the free energy are symmetrically decreasing

In this section, we prove that global minimizers are either uniform or single-cluster states in the sense that they are translates of their symmetric decreasing rearrangement. For a measurable function
f:S^{n}\to[0,\infty)
, we denote by
f^{\ast}
the symmetric decreasing rearrangement of
f
.
That is, if
\overrightarrow{n}=(1,0,\dots,0)\in S^{n}
and
d:S^{n}\times S^{n}\to[0,\infty)
denotes the geodesic distance on
S^{n}
, then
f^{\ast}
is the (up to sets of measure zero) unique measurable function
f^{\ast}:S^{n}\to[0,\infty)
which depends only on
d({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}{\bullet}},\overrightarrow{n})
, is non-increasing in
d({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}{\bullet}},\overrightarrow{n})
and whose level sets have the same measures as the level sets of
f
, that is,
\{x\in S^{n}:f(x)>t\}
and
\{x\in S^{n}:f^{\ast}(x)>t\}
have the same mass for all
t>0
.

Theorem 3.3

(Global minimizers of the free energy are symmetrically decreasing)
.

Assume that
w
satisfies
1.1
and is additionally

on
(-s_{w},s_{w})

\gamma>0
and
\ell\in(0,1/(2s_{w}))

Let
\rho
be a global minimizer of the free energy (
1.7
).
Then there exists a translation
T:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
such that
\rho=\rho^{\ast}\circ T
.

The above

provides a non-trivial result if we know that the global minimizer is non-trivial, that is different from the uniform state

\rho(x)\rho(y)+\rho(\sigma x)\rho(\sigma y)
does not change. Assume that
\rho(x)\geq\rho(\sigma x)
and
\rho(y)<\rho(\sigma y)
. Then, the integrand increases by

(3.15)

\rho(x)\rho(\sigma y)+\rho(\sigma x)\rho(y)-\rho(x)\rho(y)-\rho(\sigma x)\rho(\sigma y)\\
=\big(\rho(\sigma x)-\rho(x)\big)\big(\rho(y)-\rho(\sigma y)\big)\geq 0.

The same holds true in case
\rho(x)<\rho(\sigma x)
and
\rho(y)\geq\rho(\sigma y)
.
We obtain

(3.16)

\begin{multlined}I(\rho^{\sigma})-I(\rho)={}\int_{M^{+}\times M^{+}}\max\Big\{\big(\rho(\sigma x)-\rho(x)\big)\big(\rho(y)-\rho(\sigma y)\big),0\Big\}\,\cdot\\
\Big\{K(d(x,y))-K(d(x,\sigma y))\Big\}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}y.\end{multlined}

From
(\rho^{\sigma})^{\ast}=\rho^{\ast}
and Riesz’ rearrangement inequality
Theorem

3.5
, it follows that
I(\rho)\leq I(\rho^{\sigma})\leq I((\rho^{\sigma})^{\ast})=I(\rho^{\ast})
.
Since
\rho
is a minimizer of
I
, it follows that
I(\rho^{\sigma})=I(\rho)
and the integral on the right-hand side of (
3.16
) is zero.
Since
d(x,\sigma y)<d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in M^{+}
and
K
is a decreasing function, the integrand in (
3.16
) is non-negative everywhere, therefore it is zero everywhere. Moreover, if
x,y\in M^{+}
with
d(x,y)<\delta
, we have from

d(x,y)<d(x,\sigma y)
and (
3.5
) that

(3.17)

\displaystyle K(d(x,y))>K(\min\{\delta,d(x,\sigma y)\})\geq K(d(x,\sigma y)),

so we obtain in particular

(3.18)

\displaystyle\big(\rho(x)-\rho(\sigma x)\big)\big(\rho(y)-\rho(\sigma y)\big)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }x,y\in M^{+}\quad\text{with }d(x,y)<\delta.

The idea is now that (
3.18
) implies that the function
\rho-\rho\circ\sigma
cannot change sign within balls of radius
\delta
. Since
\rho
is analytic, this will imply that
\rho-\rho\circ\sigma
cannot change sign globally on
M^{+}
.

To make this precise, define
A:=\{x\in M^{+}:\rho(x)<\rho(\sigma x)\}
and
B:=\{y\in M^{+}:\rho(y)>\rho(\sigma y)\}
.
If
A
is empty, then
\rho\geq\rho\circ\sigma
on
M^{+}
which implies
\rho=\rho^{\sigma}
. On the other hand, if
B
is empty, then
\rho\leq\rho\circ\sigma
on
M^{+}
which implies
\rho=\rho^{\sigma}\circ\sigma
. Below, we will show that it is not possible for
A
and
B
to be both non-empty. Together with (
3.8
), this implies
Theorem

3.3
.

Assume now that both
A
and
B
are non-empty.
If
x\in A
and
y\in B
, then
d(x,y)\geq\delta
by (
3.18
).
Let
x_{0}\in\overline{A}
and
y_{0}\in\overline{B}
such that
\inf\left\{d(x,y):x\in A,y\in B\right\}=d(x_{0},y_{0})
. Then,
d(x_{0},y_{0})\geq\delta
and the intersection of the open balls
B(x_{0},d(x_{0},y_{0}))
and
B(y_{0},d(x_{0},y_{0}))
is a subset of
\{\rho=\rho\circ\sigma\}
. In particular,
\{\rho=\rho\circ\sigma\}
contains an open ball. By the identity theorem for real analytic functions, we then have
\rho=\rho\circ\sigma
on
M^{+}
, which implies that
A=B=\emptyset
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Proof.

1.1

By partial integration, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho^{(n)}=\sum\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\cdot m_{2}!\cdots m_{n}!}\frac{\rho}{Z(\rho)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{-(W_{\gamma,\ell}\ast\rho)^{(j)}}{j!}\right)^{m_{j}}.

\displaystyle\big\lVert\rho^{(n)}\big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\cdot m_{2}!\cdots m_{n}!}\frac{\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{\infty}}{Z(\rho)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\left\lVert W_{\gamma,\ell}\ast\rho^{(j)}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{m_{j}}}{j!^{m_{j}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\cdot m_{2}!\cdots m_{n}!}\frac{\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{\infty}}{Z(\rho)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{C^{jm_{j}}(j-1)!^{m_{j}}}{j!^{m_{j}}}

(3.33)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{\infty}}{Z(\rho)}C^{n}\mathbf{Y}_{n}\left(0!,1!,\dots,(n-1)!\right),

where the

(3.34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=\sum\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\cdot m_{2}!\cdots m_{n}!}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{x_{j}^{m_{j}}}{j!^{m_{j}}}.

4.2.
The extended Markovian dynamics with mass exchange

We now extend the model of the Markovian dynamics of the clusters by allowing the clusters to exchange mass.
Under

We assume that at time

K(t)
clusters with centers
X^{(1)}<\dots<X^{(K(t))}
and masses
m^{(1)},\dots,m^{(K(t))}\in(0,1)
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{K(t)}m^{(j)}=1

The positions and the masses of the clusters behave as follows:

(4.8a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}X^{(j)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{Nm^{(j)}}}{\rm d}W^{(j)}_{t},

(4.8b)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}m^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\phi^{(r)}_{j-1}m^{(j-1)}+\phi^{(l)}_{j+1}m^{(j+1)}-\left(\phi^{(r)}_{j}+\phi^{(l)}_{j}\right)m^{(j)},

for

(4.9)

\displaystyle\phi^{(r)}_{j}:=\phi^{(r)}(m^{(j)},X^{(j-1)},X^{(j)},X^{(j+1)}),\quad\phi^{(l)}_{j}:=\phi^{(l)}(m^{(j)},X^{(j-1)},X^{(j)},X^{(j+1)}),

where we define

\displaystyle\phi^{(r)}(m,x_{l},x,x_{r})=\frac{1}{d_{\rm dir}(x,x_{r})-2s_{w}\ell}\sqrt{\frac{e\gamma w^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2\pi\ell}}\sqrt{m}e^{-\gamma\ell\Delta m}

(4.11)

\displaystyle\phi^{(l)}(m,x_{l},x,x_{r})=\frac{1}{d_{\rm dir}(x_{l},x)-2s_{w}\ell}\sqrt{\frac{e\gamma w^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2\pi\ell}}\sqrt{m}e^{-\gamma\ell\Delta m}

(4.12)

\displaystyle d_{\rm dir}(x_{l},x_{r}):=\begin{cases}x_{r}-x_{l}&\text{if }x_{r}\geq x_{l},\\

Throughout this paper, we will write

•

Cluster dissolution:

If the mass of a cluster approaches zero (this will happen if the cluster mass has fallen below the critical mass

This process continues until only one cluster remains.

As the derivation of the model (

4.4.
Limitations of the reduced model (
4.8
)

Observe that the model is only a valid approximate description of the PDE with a multi-clustered initial state if
X^{(j+1)}-X^{(j)}\gg\ell s_{w}
, that is, the initial centers of the clusters are sufficiently far apart, since otherwise deterministic merging phenomena.

Moreover, the model (
4.8b
) is in general not a good approximation of the dissolution of clusters. Once one of the clusters has a mass smaller than the critical mass
m_{\rm crit}
(see
Section

3.5
), then the dynamics of the particles of this cluster are not described by the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck approximation from
[
GPY17
]
and the cluster shape is in general not well approximated by (
3.39
). Moreover, in this situation, not only the behavior of
w
around zero but its general shape determines the behavior and the exact evolution of mass. In particular, the description of the ODE (
4.8b
) is in general only valid until the first dissolution of a cluster.

4.6.
Modeling the mass exchange via jumps of particles

Based on the waiting times for particles from one cluster to join adjacent clusters, we can write down a continuous-time stochastic Markov process for the mass exchange of the clusters which features jumps of particles from one cluster to another. Assuming again that the particles are arranged in
K(t)\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
clusters at time

with masses

and cluster centers

(4.29)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(N,K(t)):=\left\{\left(M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(K(t))}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{(K(t))}\colon M^{(1)}+\dots+M^{(K(t))}=N\right\},

where

is the total number of particles as before.

For

, we denote that two clusters
i
and

are neighbors on

by writing

(4.30)

\displaystyle i\sim_{K(t)}j\qquad\vcentcolon\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\lvert i-j\rvert=1\text{ or }(i,j)\in\{(1,K(t)),(K(t),1)\}\,.

We say that the state
\mathbf{\widetilde{M}}=\big(\widetilde{M}^{(1)},\dots,\widetilde{M}^{(K(t))}\big)\in\mathfrak{M}(N,K(t))
arises from the state
\mathbf{M}=\big(M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(K(t))}\big)\in\mathfrak{M}(N,K(t))

by a jump of a particle from cluster

to cluster

if

,
\widetilde{M}^{(j)}=M^{(j)}+1
and
\widetilde{M}^{(k)}=M^{(k)}
for all

\displaystyle Q\left(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{M}\right):=\sum_{j=1}^{K(t)}M^{(j)}\left(\phi_{j}^{(r)}+\phi_{j}^{(l)}\right)\left(\frac{M^{(j)}}{N},X^{(j-1)},X^{(j)},X^{(j+1)}\right)

5.
Discussion and outlook

In this paper, the dynamics of weakly interacting diffusions in an attractive localized interaction potential was studied. It was shown, through a combination of heuristics, rigorous analysis, and careful numerical experiments, that the system exhibits very rich dynamical behavior that the mean-field description cannot capture. In particular, we identified several timescales and dynamical phenomena: formation of clusters, merging/coalescence, mass transfer between clusters, and microscopic reversibility, which we summarize as follows:

•

Initial clustering:
The timescale
t_{\rm clustering}
in (
2.9
) for the onset of clustering is determined solely by the activation of the dominant unstable mode of the linearization of the mean-field PDE (
2.1
).
As shown in
Section

2.1
, in the case of the mean-field PDE and for a generic initial datum
\rho_{0}\neq\rho_{\rm unif}
, the clustering time
t_{\rm clustering}
is of order one. The same applies to the particle system when its initial positions are sampled i.i.d. from a generic probability measure
\rho_{0}\neq\rho_{\rm unif}
. However, if the initial positions of the particles are sampled i.i.d. from the uniform measure
\rho_{\rm unif}
, then the timescale for clustering is of order
t_{\rm clustering}\asymp\log N
.

•

Coalescence:
The merging of clusters only happens in the particle system on the timescale
t_{\rm coalesence}
of order
N
as argued in
Section

4.1
.

•

Dynamic metastability:
Each cluster of macroscopic mass
m\in(m_{\rm crit},1]
is metastable on a timescale
t_{\rm metastab}\asymp e^{\gamma\ell\Delta m}
independent of
N
as implied by the proposed extended model in
Section

4.2
.

•

Microscopic reversibility:
The finite particle system (
1.1
) is ergodic due to the presence of Brownian motion for each particle. However, the presence of a discontinuous phase transition (see
Corollary

3.2
) implies that the free energy (
1.7
) has a critical point. Since the clustered state is the global minimizer, its energy barrier
\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}>0
to this critical point determines the timescale of microscopic reversibility
t_{\rm reversibility}\asymp e^{N\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}}
(see
[
GS20
, Section 5]
).

Consequently, we obtain the following behaviour for the particle model (
1.1
): As long as
\log N\ll\gamma\ell\Delta
, the movements of clusters (
4.8a
) and (
4.34a
) dominates the mass exchange (
4.8b
) and (
4.34b
). In this case, the clusters will merge before the mass exchange has a significant effect. On the contrary, for
\log N\gg\gamma\ell\Delta
, the mass exchange dominates the cluster movements.

The identification of the timescales obtained in this paper raises several interesting questions that we plan to address in future work. The rigorous proof of dynamical metastability of the mean-field PDE, in the sense of Otto–Reznikoff, is still open. Furthermore, the rigorous justification of the limit to the massive Arratia flow in the appropriate scaling regime is a challenging question. A precise form of the conjecture can be found in
Appendix

A
. Finally, the rigorous analysis of similar questions for the kinetic Langevin dynamics, in particular in the weak friction regime, is also of interest.

Appendix A
Convergence towards the Modified
Massive Arratia flow

As explained above, in the regime
\gamma\gg 1
and
\ell\ll 1
, once clusters have arisen, the system (
1.1
) behaves essentially as a system of coalescing heavy Brownian motions. It is therefore natural to expect that appropriate rescalings of (
1.1
) converge in the large particle limit to the Massive Modified Arratia Flow
[
KR19
]
, which is an random element
\{y(u,t),\ u\in[0,1],\ t\in[0,T]\}
in the Skorohod space
D([0,1],C[0,T])
characterized by the following set of axioms.

Theorem A.1

(Massive Arratia flow
[
Kon17
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

There exists an random element
\{y(u,t),\ u\in[0,1],\ t\in[0,T]\}
in the Skorohod space
D([0,1],C[0,T])
such that

(C1)

for all
u\in[0,1]
, the process
y(u,\cdot)
is a continuous square integrable martingale with respect to the filtration

(A.1)

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(y(u,s),\ u\in[0,1],\ s\leq t),\quad t\in[0,T];

(C2)

for all
u\in[0,1]
,
y(u,0)=u
;

(C3)

for all
u<v
from
[0,1]
and
t\in[0,T]
,
y(u,t)\leq y(v,t)
;

(C4)

for all
u\in[0,1]
, the quadratic variation has the form

\langle y(u,\cdot)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{ds}{m(u,s)},

where
m(u,t)=\mathcal{L}^{1}\{v:\ \exists s\leq t\ y(v,s)=y(u,s)\}
,
t\in[0,T]
;

(C5)

for all
u,v\in[0,1]
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\langle y(u,\cdot),y(v,\cdot)\rangle_{t\wedge\tau_{u,v}}=0,

where
\tau_{u,v}=\inf\{t:\ y(u,t)=y(v,t)\}\wedge T
.

Based on (
1.1
), we define the random variables
Z^{N}_{\gamma,\ell}
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{[0,1]\times[0,\infty)}
by

(A.2)

\displaystyle Z^{N}_{\gamma,\ell}(u,t):=X^{\lceil Nu\rceil}_{t}.

Comparing (
4.8a
) with the axioms for the MMA
Theorem

A.1
and with the construction of the MMA by means of the finite particle system from
[
Kon17
, Section 2]
, we see that we have to rescale the noise in (
1.1
) by a factor of
\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{2}}
, or equivalently, rescale time by a factor of
\frac{N}{2}
. Having this in mind, we formulate the following conjecture:

Conjecture
.

Let
(\gamma_{n})
,
(\ell_{n})
be sequences in
(0,\infty)
and
(N_{n})
be a sequence in
N^{+}
such that

(A.3)

\displaystyle N_{n}\to\infty,

\displaystyle\ell_{n}\downarrow 0,

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{n}\ell_{n}}{\log N_{n}}\gg 1.

Then we have

(A.4)

\displaystyle Z^{N_{n}}_{\gamma_{n},\ell_{n}}\left(u,\tfrac{N_{n}t}{2}\right)\to y(u,t)\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty

in a suitable sense.

The condition
\gamma_{n}\ell_{n}\to\infty
in (
A.3
) ensures that clusters are asymptotically stable as
n\to\infty
, while

\ell\downarrow 0
ensures that clusters merge in the limit only when they have exactly the same position. Finally, we want the coalescence dynamics to dominate over the mass exchange in the model (
4.8
) which explains the condition
\frac{\gamma_{n}\ell_{N}}{\log N_{n}}\gg 1
.

Remark A.2
.

The scaling in (
A.3
) is related to the moderately interacting diffusions considered by Oelschläger
[
Oel85
]
, but his regime is
\gamma_{n}\ell_{n}\asymp 1
, while in our regime
\gamma_{n}
grows much faster.
In the direction of more localized attractive scalings, the work
[
CGK18
]
provides a first result. The authors consider the regime, in which the interaction becomes an attractive Dirac
W_{\gamma_{n},\ell_{n}}\rightharpoonup-\delta_{0}
. The derived mean-field limit has besides the linear diffusion a quadratic
backward
porous medium. The system is only well-posedness for initial data close to the uniform state and larger states show a blow-up like a reverse Barenblatt solution. In this sense, the mean-field limit can show the initial cluster, but the dynamic of clusters cannot be observed in the mean-field limit in agreement with our result.
