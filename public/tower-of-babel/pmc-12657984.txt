AUPA: weakly supervised approach for streamlining breast cancer diagnostic workflow by WSI histological type classification for efficient IHC triage
Authors: M. J. Garcia-Gonzalez, Jhon Comba Miranda, Jose Luis Rey Sanjurjo, Laura Valeria Perez-Herrera, Blanca Cimadevila Alvarez, Xabier Echeberria-Barrio, Francesco Zola, Ivan Macia Oliver, Valery Naranjo Ornedo, Karen Lopez-Linares Roman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In routine breast cancer diagnostics, pathologists often review each case twice—first to determine the need for immunohistochemical (IHC) stains, and a second time to issue the final diagnosis—creating significant workload and delays. We present an artificial intelligence-based system designed to streamline this process by distinguishing invasive carcinoma from fibroadenoma and in situ lesions in Whole Slide Images of H&E-stained breast biopsies, enabling automatic IHC stain requests for these lesions, while abstaining on non-target or low-confidence cases. The system leverages a weakly supervised method, trained directly on final diagnostic labels without the need for manual annotations. It achieves over 91% sensitivity and specificity across histological types in internal validation and shows strong generalizability in two external pilot studies. In a real-world setting, the system could determine if IHC stains should be ordered (sensitivity and specificity > 96%) and which stains (sensitivity and specificity > 81%). Compared to both expert pathologists and state-of-the-art models, our model performs competitively. Designed for real-world deployment, it is fully integrated into the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard. In validation data, it could have saved up to 43 h of pathologists’ time. Our model represents a scalable solution for more efficient diagnostic BC workflows.

Introduction

According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases in 2022, surpassing lung cancer in global incidence for the first time1. As cancer rates continue to rise, so do the demands on diagnostic services. In particular, pathology departments face growing workloads due to the increasing complexity of tumor characterization, all while maintaining strict turnaround time targets. However, evidence from healthcare systems such as England and the United States shows growing backlogs and a projected shortage of pathologists, highlighting an urgent need for tools to support histopathological workflows2,3.

The standard diagnostic workflow for BC begins with a physical exam or routine screening mammography, followed by tissue sampling via biopsy for histopathological evaluation if malignancy is suspected. After processing, a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained section is examined under a microscope by a pathologist to confirm malignancy and assess tumor characteristics. In many cases, additional immunohistochemical (IHC) staining is requested to provide biomarker information that informs prognosis and treatment strategies4.

In this context, the digitization of pathology laboratories is transforming the field. Digital Pathology (DP)—the process of converting physical histological slides into high-resolution digital images, known as Whole Slide Images (WSIs)—enables new ways of storage, management, and analysis. These digital formats unlock the potential for remote consultations, standardized reporting, and advanced computational analysis5. An auspicious development is the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into DP workflows, which have produced models capable of supporting pathologists by streamlining repetitive tasks, reducing diagnostic variability, and ultimately contributing to faster and more accurate diagnoses. Several AI tools already support pathologists in specific tasks such as mitosis detection6, cancer grading7, or TIL scoring8. Other research efforts extend AI capabilities further into areas like protein expression quantification9,10, mutation analysis11 and predictions of clinical outcomes such as prognosis12 and recurrence13. Among existing solutions, there are also tools designed for BC histological typing14,15—closely related to our approach—but many of these rely on small or public datasets and lack robust external validation, limiting their generalizability in clinical settings. In contrast, the work of J. Sandbank et al.16 developed and rigorously validated a deep learning-based quality control algorithm for BC assessment. Their system is designed to flag discrepancies between the algorithm’s output and the pathologist’s diagnosis, effectively serving as a second read within the diagnostic workflow.

Distinctly, our work focuses on an earlier, often overlooked step: the triage of histopathological samples. We propose a deep learning model that analyzes H&E-stained WSIs to classify them as tumor or non-tumor, and further to distinguish between in situ and invasive carcinoma. This allows for the automatic initiation of IHC requests, based solely on the H&E slide —reducing the need for repeated reviews by the pathologist and streamlining the path from biopsy to report. To our knowledge, no existing solution has targeted this specific workflow step in BC diagnostics; a comparable approach has only recently been explored in prostate cancer17.

We validated our model through internal evaluation and two independent pilot studies. The first (Pilot study I) tested model performance in a real-world setting, while the second (Pilot study II) aimed to understand the generalization capabilities when applied to data from a different laboratory.

The initial groundwork for this work was laid during the CADIA project—an applied research initiative aimed at introducing AI and digital pathology into the Galician public health system. Although this specific model was developed independently of CADIA, the project provided valuable clinical insights, data and infrastructural support that shaped its direction. Furthermore, Pilot Study I was developed in a hospital from the Galician public health system.

This paper describes the model development process and the results achieved, with a comprehensive analysis of the clinical validations performed and the key lessons learned. Additionally, we propose a methodology to properly assess potential biases that may affect the AI system concerning the origin of the data.

Methods

The diagnostic workflow for BC typically involves two stages: (1) an initial review of the H&E-stained tissue sample to identify the histological type and decide whether additional stains are needed, and (2) a second, integrated assessment that includes all available information to produce the final diagnostic report. Our approach streamlines this process by using an AI model to analyze H&E-stained WSIs and automatically determine the histological type. This enables the automated ordering of IHC stains, allowing pathologists to access all necessary data in a single step and potentially bypass the preliminary review. We simulated this digital workflow in two validation pilots to assess the model’s performance and its potential impact on diagnostic efficiency. Datasets

We used three datasets in our work: a development dataset to train the AI model and perform internal validation, and two additional datasets to perform Pilot Studies I and II.

Development dataset

Retrospective data were extracted from the archives of six different pathology laboratories within the Galician health system. A total of 1,930 H&E-stained diagnostic breast biopsies were selected considering the most common histological types: fibroadenoma (FD), invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS). Of these, only five cases of LCIS were identified and were excluded due to their low prevalence, which made them unsuitable for deep learning model training. The class of each sample was assigned based on the SNOMED CT code derived from pathology reports reflecting diagnoses integrating H&E-stained slides, relevant IHC staining, and clinical data. Although expert pathologists’ annotations were collected throughout the project, they were not used for model training because the institutional pathological diagnosis was considered the gold standard due to its comprehensive clinical context. Samples were stained in the participating laboratories using similar, though not identical, protocols, so differences are to be expected. As is common practice, slides often included multiple rows of tissue sections, and only the diagnostically relevant tissue section was digitized—according to pathologists’ pen marks—using a VENTANA DP 200 slide scanner (Roche Diagnostics) at 40x magnification.

The digitized images were pseudo-anonymized by relabeling the patient IDs with new project-specific IDs and removing all patient-related information. DICOM was chosen as the digital file format to ensure interoperability with the hospitals’ Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) and Laboratory Information Systems (LIS). Custom DICOM tags were manually added to encode the histological classification according to the SNOMED CT standard, which served as the ground truth for model development. To align with the model’s output format, samples were regrouped into fibroadenoma, ductal carcinoma (including all DCIS), and invasive carcinoma (including ILC and IDC).

This dataset was used to train and internally validate the algorithm. A stratified subset of 8 cases per class and laboratory (168 in total) was set aside as the internal validation set (10% of the data), while the remaining 1,757 samples formed the training set. All data were stored in DICOM format within a dedicated PACS. The class distribution is detailed in Table 2.

Pilot study I dataset

To evaluate the model in a real-world clinical setting that follows similar protocols to the development phase, we conducted a pilot study using a retrospective set of 130 anonymized H&E-stained digital biopsies. These WSIs, stored in DICOM format, were obtained from a hospital that had also contributed samples to the development dataset. However, none of the biopsies used in this pilot were part of the model’s training or internal validation sets.

All slides were digitized using the same scanner used during model development, ensuring consistency in image quality and acquisition protocols. To determine the appropriate sample size for the pilot study, we calculated the minimum number of cases required to achieve statistical significance, allowing for a maximum error of 0.05 based on expected performance variance, resulting in a requirement of at least 28 samples per class. The specific class distribution for this dataset is shown in Table 2.

Pilot study II dataset

The second pilot study assessed the model’s ability to generalize to an external cohort from an independent pathology laboratory not involved in development. This dataset included 85 anonymized H&E-stained biopsies in DICOM-WSI format, acquired using the same scanning hardware to minimize variability due to image acquisition. Unlike Pilot Study I, none of the data sources for this cohort overlapped with the training or validation sets, nor were they from institutions involved in model development. This setup allowed for a more rigorous test of the model’s robustness across institutional differences. The specific class distribution for this dataset is also shown in Table 2.

Multiple instance learning for breast cancer classification

To automate IHC stain requests and eliminate the need for an initial comprehensive review of H&E slides, we developed a model called AUPA, designed to classify the invasiveness of breast tissue samples. This classification distinguishes between the most common benign lesion, fibroadenomas, and the two main types of carcinomas: in situ and invasive carcinomas. In general, benign samples do not require further IHC analysis, whereas both in situ and invasive cases do. The model’s outcomes could directly inform whether and which IHC stains should be ordered.

Given the objective and the characteristics of our data, we adopted a weakly supervised deep learning approach based on Multiple Instance Learning (MIL). This decision was driven by two factors: (1) the large size of WSIs, and (2) the availability of slide-level diagnostic labels (e.g., SNOMED-CT codes), without detailed region-level annotations. MIL is well suited to this setting because it allows the model to learn from weak, slide-level labels while still capturing local patterns within the tissue.

In the MIL framework, each WSI is divided into smaller patches (instances), from which features are extracted and aggregated to predict the slide-level label (bag). This structure enables end-to-end training, allowing the model to learn features that are most informative for distinguishing between fibroadenoma, in situ, and invasive samples—without the need for patch-level supervision. This is important in histopathology, where slides are inherently heterogeneous, often containing a mixture of healthy tissue and multiple lesion types. MIL naturally accounts for this, as it assumes that while individual instances may not reflect the slide label, a single positive instance can be sufficient for a positive slide-level classification. This aligns well with clinical practice, where focal tumor regions can define the diagnostic category of the entire sample.

To reflect clinical practice more accurately, we framed the problem as a multi-task classification problem: the model predicts all histological types present at sample level, and the final classification is determined by the most aggressive type detected. To this end, the designed architecture is composed of two main components trained jointly: a feature extractor and a classifier, with an aggregation layer in between to combine patch-level features into a single slide-level representation. The feature extractor is based on a pre-trained DenseNet-121, followed by two fully connected layers, which transforms the output vectors into a fixed size representation per tile. Aggregation is performed using a max-pooling operation applied across all tiles of each sample. Finally, the classifier consists of three parallel branches, one for each target class, consisting of three sequential fully connected layers activated with ELU. The purpose of this classifier is that each branch predicts the probability that the input sample belongs to that particular class, allowing the system to return an “unknown” class if all probabilities are lower than 0.5.

Training details

During training, each WSI was divided into patches of 1024 × 1024 pixels at 20× magnification, a resolution found to be optimal both experimentally and in clinical practice. For each training epoch, 250 patches were randomly sampled per slide. This increased training time but acted as a regularization mechanism to reduce overfitting. Input patches were z-score normalized using the mean and standard deviation of the training dataset. To address class imbalance, the dataset was resampled during training to prevent the model from bias toward the majority class.

Training was performed with a batch size of one, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-5 and categorical cross-entropy as the loss function. The model was implemented in TensorFlow and trained on a single NVIDIA Tesla V100 SXM2 GPU (32GB).

We followed a 5-fold cross-validation strategy to train and evaluate the model, and conducted a statistical analysis of performance metrics, including sensitivity and specificity, which allowed us to ensure robustness of the results. Once all hyperparameters and training configurations were selected, we trained a final model using the entire training dataset, yielding the final AUPA Model. We used this model to validate our approach with the internal validation dataset and pilot study datasets.

External validation studies

The AI model was evaluated on two blinded validation sets (Pilot study I and Pilot study II datasets), both independent from the data used during training and internal validation. These studies were conducted in clinical settings and managed by local teams responsible for the complete workflow: from sample collection and digitization to storage in a dedicated PACS, model execution, and result integration. Model outputs were saved as an additional instance within the DICOM files, using the DICOM-SR format to ensure compatibility with existing hospital IT systems. In this deployment context, the model returned one of the possible outputs: fibroadenoma, carcinoma in situ, invasive carcinoma, “unknown”, or processing error.

Final analysis was carried out collaboratively with health system stakeholders. In addition to standard quantitative performance metrics, the review focused on the extraction of valuable information regarding the implementation of the digital workflow in the pathology laboratories.

Results

Development results

Table 3 presents the mean and standard deviation of model performance for each histological type, obtained through 5-fold cross-validation using the training dataset during the development phase. For fibroadenoma, the model achieved a specificity of 0.99 and sensitivity of 0.95. For carcinoma in situ, specificity was 0.96 and sensitivity 0.88. In the case of invasive carcinoma, the model reached a specificity of 0.95 and a sensitivity of 0.93.

To further assess model performance, we used the internal validation set, which includes 168 samples evenly distributed across histological types and laboratories. The results, summarized in Table 3, confirmed high performance across all classes: fibroadenoma detection achieved the highest scores across all metrics. For carcinoma in situ, the model achieved a specificity of 0.97 and a sensitivity of 0.94. The prediction of invasive carcinoma reached a specificity of 0.98 and a sensitivity of 0.91. To evaluate whether the model’s performance was consistent across data sources, we analyzed the results by laboratory, as shown in Table 3. The results revealed a statistically significant deviation in the model’s behavior for samples originating from Lab5, which could be suggestive of a bias related to data origin.

To explore this further, we followed the described validation methodology. First, to assess overall dissimilarity in pixel intensity distributions between populations, we computed the pairwise Euclidean distance between normalized histograms. This metric captures absolute bin-wise differences while ensuring comparability across populations of different scales. The analysis revealed that Lab2 and Lab3 were the most similar (distance = 0.027), whereas Lab1 showed the highest dissimilarity, with an average Euclidean distance of 0.368, which may indicate variability in staining, affecting image appearance. Next, we examined the model’s internal behavior. For the feature extractor, we projected the latent space of the features into two dimensions using t-SNE. Figure 2 shows the resulting 2D space, colored by tumor class (A.) and data origin (B.), allowing visual inspection of potential clustering effects. When looking at the subdivision performed according to histological type, the classes are clearly distinguished as expected; however, when subdivided accordingly to data origin, it appeared unstructured, failing to distinguish regions related to the origin of the data. This fact allows us to conclude that the feature extractor encodes the samples independently of the data origin, thereby excluding the potential existence of bias through this factor. Finally, to assess the classifier, we analyzed the “impact” of each neuron on the final prediction, effectively capturing the internal activation patterns of the model. The median impact per neuron is plotted, grouped by subpopulation (class or laboratory), along with a boxplot showing its distribution (Fig. 3A.). According to the normality and variances of the population, the Kruskal-Wallis method was used to find statistically significant differences between groups and found that there were significant differences between subtypes (p-value = 0.0477), as opposed to laboratories (p-value = 0.8164). We then plotted the median impacts for the top 50 impacting neurons (Fig. 3B.), and a heatmap for the impact of all samples for these neurons (Fig. 3C.), and discovered that when we looked at the classes, the neurons behaved differently for the fibroadenoma samples, but were the same across all laboratories. The results showed that classifier internal behavior varied meaningfully between histological types (as expected) but remained consistent across data origins, supporting the conclusion that while visual differences exist between laboratories, they do not significantly affect the classifier’s decision-making process, discarding the existence of potential biases.

Analysis of expert annotations

As part of the dataset collection, 12 pathologists were invited to manually annotate representative regions within each WSI, without access to any other clinical or diagnostic information. Each image was independently reviewed by three expert pathologists, who identified relevant areas in a manner that simulated the initial H&E review. For each region, they assigned a histological type: FD, DCIS, LCIS, ILC or IDC. The most invasive type was chosen as the final annotation for the case. These labels were then consolidated into the same three categories used by the AI model: fibroadenoma, in situ carcinoma, and invasive carcinoma. Additionally, pathologists graded tubule formation and nuclear pleomorphism on a 0–3 scale, which was used to train a separate AI model for predicting morphological features20.

To combine the assessments from the three pathologists, we implemented two consensus strategies: (1) strict consensus, where all three agreed on the histological type, and (2) majority voting, where the most frequently assigned label was selected. Of the 1,925 total samples, 100 lacked a complete set of annotations because one or more pathologists opted not to annotate the case—typically due to technical issues or because the sample did not meet predefined inclusion criteria. For 77 samples and 9 samples a strict consensus and majority voting consensus, respectively, could not be established i.e. each annotator assigned a different tissue type. These samples were excluded from the comparative performance analysis.

Although these annotations were not originally intended to measure inter-observer variability, they provided a valuable opportunity to compare expert assessments with AUPA’s predictions on the same IHC triage task. It is important to note that the model was trained using final diagnostic labels derived from the full diagnostic process—also including IHC and clinical context— while the pathologists’ assessments were based solely on H&E images. Additionally, most of the participating pathologists had limited experience with digital workflows or annotation tools.

To quantify the agreement between the expert annotations and the reference diagnostic labels, we calculated performance metrics using both aggregation strategies: strict consensus and majority vote. As shown in Table 3, both AUPA and the expert pathologists showed comparable performance in distinguishing between fibroadenoma and invasive cases. However, the model showed superior performance for the intermediate class, in situ carcinoma, suggesting that AUPA may provide valuable support in pathology workflows, particularly in borderline or ambiguous cases where consistent classification is challenging.

External validation results

After the pilot studies were completed, we received the 130 and 85 digital samples corresponding to Pilot Study I and Pilot Study II, respectively, for performance analysis. Each sample included the ground truth diagnosis—embedded in a private DICOM tag at the time of digitization and a DICOM-SR instance containing the tissue type predicted by the model.

Pilot study I

In the initial validation on the Pilot Study I dataset, the model predicted seven out of 130 samples as inconclusive, and two additional samples could not be processed due to errors. For the remaining 121 cases, performance metrics were as follows: for the fibroadenoma class, the model achieved a specificity of 0.97 and sensitivity of 0.94; for carcinoma in situ, specificity was 0.91 and sensitivity dropped to 0.54; and for invasive carcinoma, specificity and sensitivity were 0.86 and 0.81, respectively. These results are shown in Table 3 and reflect a noticeable drop in performance—particularly in the classification of carcinoma in situ—compared to the internal validation results.

To investigate the source of this decline, we performed a series of analyses comparing the Pilot Study I samples to those in the development and internal validation datasets. These included histogram comparisons, principal component analysis projections, and visual inspection of the slides. The analysis revealed that several samples contained artefacts. Common issues—highlighted in red in Fig. 4A—included pen marks, color and scanning artefacts, air bubbles, blurred regions, embedded text labels, and black borders from slide mounting.

Unlike the development dataset, where slides were cleaned and only diagnostically relevant regions were digitized, the Pilot Study I slides were scanned in full without any prior quality control. To better align the evaluation with the original digitization conditions, we manually curated the dataset by separating each WSI into clean, artefact-free regions. As shown in Fig. 4B, each region was then processed independently, and the final prediction for the sample was defined as the most aggressive label among all predictions. This refined evaluation led to improved results. The number of inconclusive cases dropped from seven to three, and the two previously misprocessed slides were correctly classified. For the remaining 125 samples, the model correctly predicted 115 cases, demonstrating a substantial improvement in performance when artefact-related noise was removed. Updated metrics for this curated evaluation are also provided in Table 3.

Pilot study II

Unlike Pilot Study I, the 85 samples included in the Pilot Study II dataset were digitized following a strict protocol to ensure that physical slides were clean and free of artefacts. After an initial visual quality check, only 2 samples were excluded due to poor image quality. For the remaining 83 samples, the model achieved a specificity of 0.98 and a sensitivity of 0.93 in predicting the fibroadenoma class. For carcinoma in situ, the model reached a specificity of 0.82 and a sensitivity of 0.93. Finally, for the invasive carcinoma class, specificity was 1.00 and sensitivity was 0.71. The results are gathered in Table 3.

Potential use of model predictions to guide IHC requests

When translating these results to the real-world scenario for which AUPA is designed—automatically requesting IHC stains to avoid the need for an initial review—the model can impact the clinical workflow in two key ways: (1) determining whether IHC stains are needed—i.e., distinguishing between fibroadenoma and in situ and invasive cases—and (2) identifying which specific stains would be appropriate—i.e., distinguishing between in situ and invasive carcinoma cases. Performance was evaluated by aggregating predictions across all validation datasets.

Table 4 summarizes these results. AUPA demonstrates strong performance in deciding whether IHC staining is required, achieving sensitivity and specificity above 96%. In selecting the appropriate stains based on tumor subtype (in situ vs. invasive), it reaches sensitivity and specificity above 81% for both classes. To contextualize these results, we also evaluated expert pathologists’ performance, using the described manual annotations and the same classification settings. As shown in Table 4, AUPA performs comparably to the experts in distinguishing fibroadenomas from carcinoma cases and exceeds their performance in identifying in situ carcinoma.

Discussion

In this work, we present the development and thorough validation of AUPA, an AI algorithm that accurately classifies cases as fibroadenoma, in situ, or invasive carcinoma in H&E-stained breast biopsies in DICOM WSI format. By enabling automatic IHC requests based on the initial H&E slide, it could potentially eliminate the need for an initial review solely to decide on further staining, allowing pathologists to issue a final diagnosis in a single step, significantly reducing diagnostic turnaround times and easing the burden on clinicians. To our knowledge, this is the first AI solution specifically designed to this end.

Despite its lighter supervision, the model performed exceptionally well in internal validation, with all metrics above 91% across all histological types. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of MIL in digital pathology and the robustness of our approach. Moreover, our extensive validation methodology showed that variations in staining or image appearance between laboratories did not bias the model. The performance differences observed are more likely due to demographic factors or laboratory protocols, rather than the origin of the data itself.

Two independent external pilot studies were conducted. Pilot Study I was designed to evaluate AUPA’s performance in a setting closer to the real world. The results highlight the importance of quality control in the digitization process—artefacts in the images led to reduced model performance and could complicate routine practice in a fully digital environment. Pilot Study II focused on assessing AUPA’s generalizability, testing the model on an external dataset coming from another hospital with different demographics and users involved in the study. The results demonstrate AUPA’s strong performance and its potential for deployment in varied clinical environments.

Compared to the state-of-the-art, the approach by Sandbank et al.16 reported 93.57% and 95.51% for sensitivity and specificity predicting invasive vs. non-invasive, and 93.79% and 93.20% in the in situ vs. benign scenario. They performed slightly better in the prior task, in which AUPA achieved 86.36% and 82.81% of sensitivity and specificity. However, their model relied on exhaustive manual region-level annotations, a process difficult to scale in a clinical setting. In contrast, AUPA achieves competitive performance and even outperforms the prior model in the in situ vs. fibroadenoma classification (100% sensitivity and 96.43% specificity), using only the final diagnostic labels. This minimal clinical intervention highlights AUPA’s strong potential for scalable and practical integration into routine pathology workflows.

As part of our investigation into the model’s potential to support workflow optimization, we explored whether AUPA’s predictions could be reframed to assist in key IHC-related decision points: determining whether IHC staining is necessary and identifying which type of staining might be most appropriate. When evaluated under these alternative groupings, the model maintained strong performance. Notably, it performed comparably to expert pathologists in distinguishing fibroadenoma from in situ and invasive samples and outperformed them in identifying in situ carcinoma. These results support the conclusion that integrating AUPA into routine clinical practice could significantly streamline the diagnostic workflow.

Previous studies17 analyzed the differences in review time for pathologists in the traditional diagnostic workflow versus the time it would take to make the final diagnosis with all necessary stains directly available and found that they saved 11 min per case with the second strategy. We have calculated the estimated time that AUPA could have saved for the pathology laboratories for samples in all validation datasets for the IS and IV classes included in the study (a total of 268 samples); in this setting, the system could have saved approximately 43 h of pathologist’s work. If the system fails in the prediction for samples that are IS or IV by classifying them as FD, the case would have followed the standard procedure, so the pathologist would have had to manually request the IHC stains. If the system were to misclassify an FD, it would result in a waste of resources due to unnecessary IHC staining and a delay in the diagnosis. According to the results, the likelihood for the latter scenario is minimal.

An additional key aspect of this work is its emphasis on interoperability with hospital IT systems. AUPA operates entirely within the DICOM standard by reading DICOM WSIs from the PACS and writing the output as a new instance of the study in DICOM SR. Ensuring interoperability is a key challenge for real-world adoption of DP. To our knowledge, AUPA is the first AI-based pathology solution to implement this level of DICOM-based workflow integration, which is increasingly recognized as the optimal format for clinical interoperability21. Beyond this technical integration, AUPA is designed to support clinical decision-making by providing the histological classification, which constitutes actionable information for IHC ordering. A key strength of the model is its flexibility: rather than imposing fixed IHC protocols, it allows each institution to interpret the output in accordance with their local guidelines. This makes AUPA suitable for deployment across a variety of clinical contexts without requiring model retraining or structural changes.

Despite its strong performance and promising results, AUPA has some limitations to be addressed in future work. Although the model was trained and validated on multicenter data, all samples were digitized using the same slide scanner; to assess generalizability across imaging hardware, we plan to test, retrain, and validate the model on data acquired with different scanners. Another current limitation is the lack of visual explanations, which can be important for clinician trust and adoption. Future versions of AUPA will incorporate explainability methods to identify the tissue regions that influenced the model’s decision, improving clinical interpretability. In addition, the model’s behavior regarding the ‘unknown’ endpoint has not been explicitly validated. However, it is expected as an outcome when confidence is low or when the slide falls outside the intended decision point. We plan to test the system on biopsies containing diagnostic categories that were not included in the current study. If necessary, future versions of AUPA will include representative examples of these categories during training to improve robustness and ensure the appropriate handling of out-of-scope cases. Finally, we plan to conduct a pilot study integrating AUPA with the LIS, enabling real-time deployment and evaluation within routine diagnostic workflows.
