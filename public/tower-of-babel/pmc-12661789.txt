CD209a regulates metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease development through macrophage STAT3 signaling pathway
Authors: Yishu Chen, Jiaming Zhou, Chenxi Tang, Pengwei Zhu, Zixin Xu, Xin Song, Jie Zhang, Li Cen, Hang Zeng, Yini Ke, Youming Li, Yi Chen, Jinghua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a growing global health concern, with macrophage-driven inflammation playing a critical role in its progression. CD209a, a C-type lectin receptor, has been implicated in immune regulation, but its function in MASLD remains unclear. This study investigates the role of CD209a in MASLD pathogenesis, focusing on its effects on macrophage-mediated inflammation.MethodsWe analyzed CD209a expression in liver tissues from patients with MASLD and diet-induced MASLD mouse models. CD209a knockout (Cd209a−/−) mice were generated, and bone marrow transplantation was conducted to assess the impact of CD209a deficiency on hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and insulin resistance. Macrophage depletion was performed to determine whether the impact is mediated by liver macrophages. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was utilized to explore macrophage phenotypic changes and underlying signaling pathways.ResultsCD209a expression was significantly upregulated in livers of patients and mice with MASLD. Cd209a−/− mice exhibited aggravated hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and insulin resistance compared with wild-type controls (P < 0.05). Macrophage depletion abolished these effects, confirming a macrophage-dependent mechanism. Single-cell RNA-seq revealed that CD209a deficiency caused a shift toward a proinflammatory macrophage phenotype, with STAT3 signaling identified as a potential mediator.ConclusionsCD209a plays a protective role in MASLD. Loss of CD209a promotes macrophage-driven inflammation through STAT3 signaling and exacerbates MASLD severity. CD209a may serve as a potential therapeutic target for MASLD management, warranting further translational research.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00824-5.

Background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is now the most common chronic liver disease worldwide, posing a significant burden on healthcare systems and markedly impairing health-related quality of life [1, 2]. MASLD represents a broad continuum of liver pathology, from simple hepatic steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), progressive fibrosis, cirrhosis, and ultimately MASLD-related hepatocellular carcinoma [3, 4]. It is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis along with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, underscoring its strong association with metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance [5]. Beyond liver-related complications, MASLD is also a key risk factor for major adverse cardiovascular events, which constitute the leading cause of mortality among affected adults [6–9]. Given its rising prevalence and systemic impact, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving MASLD is urgently needed.

Inflammation and immune cell activation play pivotal roles in the progression of MASLD, particularly in the transition from simple steatosis to MASH. Liver macrophages are considered early responders in the development of steatohepatitis, promoting disease progression through the secretion of proinflammatory mediators [10–12]. Notably, the depletion of liver macrophages at the onset of steatohepatitis effectively mitigates hepatic inflammation and attenuates the severity of liver injury, underscoring the critical role of macrophages in MASH [13].

CD209a is a C-type lectin predominantly expressed on dendritic cells and monocytes/macrophages and plays a pivotal role in pathogen recognition and internalization. It can recognize a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, and parasites, and is implicated in the immune evasion mechanisms of pathogens such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-1 and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [14]. Moreover, CD209a-expressing suppressive macrophages are recognized as crucial mediators of immunological tolerance [15]. In atherosclerosis, CD209a can interact with macrophage toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) to mediate the inflammatory response induced by oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) [16]. These findings collectively highlight the role of CD209a in immunological response and inflammation. As a receptor, CD209a interacts with multiple ligands. We have previously identified that one of its ligands, leukocyte cell-derived chemotaxin 2 (LECT2), promotes the development of hepatic steatosis. LECT2 overexpression in mice exacerbated hepatic steatosis and inflammation induced by a high-fat diet via the activation of the STAT1 pathway [17]. In contrast, however, Pilling et al. reported that another ligand of CD209a, 1866, mitigated high-fat-diet-induced hepatic steatosis and inflammation [18]. These conflicting results led us to explore further into the role of CD209a in MASLD. Building upon its specific localization within the liver cell niche, we investigated the underlying mechanisms through which CD209a may influence the development and progression of MASLD.

Methods

Human samples

This study included a total of ten human liver tissue samples (six from patients with MASLD and four from non-MASLD controls), which were randomly selected from the liver biopsy samples obtained at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. MASLD was diagnosed on the basis of the latest international consensus criteria outlined in A multisociety Delphi consensus statement on new fatty liver disease nomenclature, which require the presence of hepatic steatosis along with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor [5]. All liver tissue samples from patients used in this study were obtained with approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval no. [2025B] IIT Ethics Approval No. 0413).

Mice and treatment

Wild-type (Cd209a+/+) mice and CD209a knockout (Cd209a−/−) mice were generated by interbreeding Cd209a+/− mice, which were purchased from Saiye (Suzhou) Biotechnology. The targeted region for knockout encompassed exons 2–6 of the Cd209a gene. All animals were housed in the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences with a 12-h light/dark cycle in a specific pathogen-free environment (24–26 °C, relative humidity 50–60%). Mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly allocated to the experiment groups and fed either a standard chow diet (SCD) provided by the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences (Hangzhou, China) or a high-fat diet (60% fat, D12492; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) for 12 weeks or a methionine–choline-deficient diet (A02082002BR; Research Diets) for 4 weeks with ad libitum access to food. For macrophage depletion, mice were intraperitoneally injected with a dose of 200 μL clodronate liposomes (Liposoma BV) once weekly. For STAT3 inhibition, mice received intraperitoneal injection of stattic (MedChemExpress, 10 mg/kg) three times per week. All animal experiments were performed according to guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval no. 2021999).

Bone marrow transplantation

In brief, a lethal dose of whole-body irradiation (8 Gy) was administered to recipient mice. We then harvested the bone marrow cells via collecting and flushing the cavities of femurs and tibias isolated from donor mice using 1–1.5 mL RPMI 1640 medium. Bone marrow cells were then passed through 70 μm cell strainers (Falcon, 352340) to obtain single-cell suspensions, followed by centrifugation and resuspension in RPMI 1640 medium prior to transplantation. Subsequently, 6 h later, irradiated recipient mice were administered 1 × 107 donor bone marrow cells via tail vein injection to form chimeric mice.

Measurement of biochemical indices and metabolic analysis

Hepatic triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels were measured using commercially available kits (Beijing Pulilai Gene Technology) following the manufacturer’s instructions and were adjusted on the basis of the total protein content. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) concentrations were measured with a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT)

For the glucose tolerance test (GTT), mice underwent a 16-h fasting period before receiving an intraperitoneal injection of glucose (Sigma-Aldrich) at a dose of 1 mg/g body weight. For the insulin tolerance test (ITT), mice were fasted for 6 h and then administered an intraperitoneal injection of insulin at a dose of 0.75 U/kg. Blood glucose levels were monitored using a glucometer (LifeScan) at baseline and at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post injection.

Histological, immunohistochemical, and immunofluorescent analyses

Liver sections were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence analysis. For IHC, anti-DC-SIGN/CD209 (65740, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti-F4/80 (70076, Cell Signaling Technology) were used. For immunofluorescence, paraffin-embedded tissues were incubated with anti-DC-SIGN (ab218419, Abcam) and anti-CD68 (ab213363, Abcam), followed by Alexa Fluor 488- and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (from Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. For Oil Red O staining, frozen liver sections were processed according to standard procedures. Quantification of positively stained areas in IHC and immunofluorescence was performed using the ImageJ software, and results are expressed as the percentage of stained area relative to the total tissue area.

Isolation and flow cytometry analysis of hepatic nonparenchymal cells

Liver tissues were finely minced and suspended with 5 mL digestion buffer (low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium [DMEM] with 2% fetal bovine serum [FBS], 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, and 10 μg/mL DNase). Then, the tubes were placed on a shaker at 150–170 rpm at 37 °C for 45–60 min. The cells were run through a 70 μm filter and then centrifuged at 500g for 5 min. The precipitates were resuspended in 36% Percoll and then centrifuged again at 500g for 5 min. The upper layer of cells was removed, and the remaining red blood cells in the precipitates were lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer. Cells were stained with fixable viability dye (564406, BD Pharmingen) and then incubated with Fc block (553141, BD Pharmingen) before staining with CD45 (564279, BD Pharmingen); F4/80 (565410, BD Pharmingen); CD11b (557396, BD Pharmingen); Ly6G (740953, BD Pharmingen); NK1.1 (564143, BD Pharmingen); CD11c (565452, BD Pharmingen); B220 (612838, BD Pharmingen); CD3e (564378, BD Pharmingen); CD8a (562315, BD Pharmingen); CD24 (563450, BD Pharmingen); CD172a (742205, BD Pharmingen); MHC-II (752163, BD Pharmingen); PDCA-1 (17-3172-82, Thermo); and XCR-1 (130-111-374, Miltenyi). Finally, flow cytometry was performed using the standard settings on a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer, and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Treestar, Ashland, OR, USA).

Western blot analysis

Total proteins from liver tissues were extracted using RIPA buffer (FD008) supplemented with protease inhibitors (FD1001) and phosphatase inhibitors (FD1002) from FUDE. Total proteins from epididymal white adipose tissues were extracted with an adipose tissue protein extraction kit with protein degreasing filter columns (EX1130, Solarbio). Extracted proteins were quantified using a BCA kit (P0011, Beyotime). Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) followed by transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Inc., Darmstadt, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), followed by incubation overnight with the following primary antibodies. Anti-phospho-AKT (Ser473), anti-AKT, anti-phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705), and anti-STAT3 were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Anti-GAPDH and Anti-β-ACTIN were purchased from Proteintech. Bolts were further incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit (BL052A) or goat anti-mouse (BL05A) from Biosharp. Proteins were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (FD8020, FUDE).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using the TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), and 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed using the Evo M-MLV Kit (Accurate Biotechnology). Quantitative PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa) and gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1). The relative expression of target genes was normalized to β-actin and calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data analyses

Liver tissues from HFD-fed Cd209a+/+ and Cd209a−/− mice were harvested, washed, and digested in 0.35% collagenase IV, 2 mg/mL papain, and 120 U/mL DNase I at 37 °C for 20 min with shaking. The resulting suspension was filtered, centrifuged, and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (MACS 130-094-183). Dead cells were removed using the Miltenyi® Dead Cell Removal Kit (MACS 130-090-101), and the final cell pellet was resuspended in 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (0.04% bovine serum albumin [BSA]). Cell viability (> 85%) was confirmed via trypan blue exclusion, and CD45+ cells were sorted using a BD FACS ARIA II Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). Single-cell suspensions were processed using the Chromium 10 × Genomics Single-Cell 3′ Kit (v3) to capture 5000 cells per sample. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at a minimum depth of 20,000 reads per cell. Sequencing data were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ format using Illumina bcl2fastq software (version 2.20). The Cell Ranger pipeline (version 6.1.1) was used for sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and single-cell 3′ gene counting, with alignment to the Ensembl GRCh38/GRCm38 reference genome. A total of 10,930 single cells from three Cd209a+/+ and three Cd209a−/− mice were processed. After quality control (QC) filtering, 9784 cells were retained for downstream analyses. Cells with fewer than 500 or more than 5000 detected genes, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts below 500, or mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 25% were excluded. Data were analyzed using Seurat (version 3.1.1) for dimensional reduction, clustering, and visualization. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) on normalized expression values (LogNormalize method in Seurat). The top ten principal components (PCs) were used for clustering and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization in two-dimensional space. Clusters were identified using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph-based clustering method. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test via the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. Default thresholds were applied, including expression in > 10% of cells within a cluster and an average log fold change greater than 0.25.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was evaluated using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test, or two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons, as appropriate. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All authors had access to the study data and had reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Results

Hepatic CD209a expression is significantly upregulated in patients with MASLD and mouse models

To investigate whether CD209a is involved in MASLD, we collected paraffin-embedded liver specimens from patients with MASLD (n = 6) and non-MASLD controls (n = 4). Histological analysis of the liver tissues from patients with MASLD revealed characteristic features of macrovesicular steatosis. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated a significant upregulation of CD209 expression compared with controls (Fig. 1a, b). We further assessed CD209a expression in MASLD mouse models. Both a high-fat diet (HFD) for 12 weeks (Fig. 1c–e) and a methionine–choline-deficient (MCD) diet for 4 weeks (Fig. 1f–h) led to elevated CD209a expression relative to their respective standard chow diet (SCD) groups, as evidenced by quantitative (q)PCR and immunohistochemical staining. To delineate the cellular localization of CD209a, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE129516), which includes liver nonparenchymal cells (NPCs) from healthy and MASH mice. Through t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) clustering analysis, we identified a total of 19 cell clusters (cluster 0–18) and annotated cell types on the basis of the gene expression profiles of each cluster (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Cd209a-expressing cells were predominantly localized in two clusters, cluster 8 and cluster 14, which correspond to macrophages and dendritic cells, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Notably, Cd209a expression was significantly upregulated in cluster 8 (macrophages), while no significant change was observed in cluster 14 (dendritic cells) (Supplementary Fig. S1C). To further validate our findings, we performed immunofluorescent co-localization of CD209 and the macrophage marker CD68 in human liver samples. The results demonstrated clear co-localization of CD209 with macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Deletion of CD209a exacerbates HFD-induced hepatic steatosis and obesity in mice

To further investigate the role of CD209a in MASLD, we generated global CD209a knockout (Cd209a−/−) mice and harvested liver and bone marrow tissues for analysis. As expected, Cd209a mRNA levels were barely detectable in the liver (Supplementary Fig. S2A), liver NPCs (Supplementary Fig. S2B), and bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. S2C) of Cd209a−/− mice.

After 12 weeks on a high-fat diet, Cd209a−/− mice exhibited significantly greater body weight and liver weight compared with their Cd209a+/+ littermates (Fig. 2a–c). The absence of CD209a exacerbated hepatic steatosis, as indicated by elevated liver triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels, and increased lipid droplet accumulation as shown by H&E and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2d–g). Similar results were also observed in MCD-fed mice; Cd209a−/− mice showed more severe hepatic steatosis than Cd209a+/+ littermates (Supplementary Fig. S3A–F). In addition, serum TG and TC levels were significantly higher in Cd209a−/− mice compared with Cd209a+/+ mice under HFD conditions (Fig. 2h, i). CD209a reconstitution by bone marrow transplantation counteracts hepatic steatosis induced by CD209a deletion

On the basis of the observations above, we then infused lethally irradiated Cd209a−/− mice with bone marrow cells from either Cd209a−/− (control) or Cd209a+/+ mice (Fig. 5h) and provided an HFD diet. Mice reconstituted with Cd209a+/+ bone marrow cells exhibited less body weight gain than those transplanted with Cd209a−/− bone marrow cells (Fig. 5i). In addition, these mice showed lower hepatic TG and TC levels (Fig. 5j, k), which was further supported by H&E and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 5l). These findings suggest that bone marrow reconstitution with CD209a mitigates the exacerbated hepatic steatosis resulting from CD209a deletion. Moreover, mice receiving Cd209a+/+ bone marrow transplantation showed lower blood glucose levels at 15 and 90 min post-glucose injection, with a significantly decreased AUC, indicating that CD209a reconstitution in the bone marrow alleviates the exacerbated glucose intolerance resulting from CD209a deletion (Fig. 5m, n).

CD209a deficiency contributes to a significant increase in hepatic macrophages rather than dendritic cells

Discussion

In this study, we found that hepatic CD209a expression was significantly upregulated in MASLD. CD209a deficiency markedly exacerbated obesity, hepatic steatosis and inflammation, adipose dysfunction, and systemic insulin resistance induced by a high-fat diet, potentially through activating the STAT3 pathway and shifting macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype.

CD209a is a C-type lectin receptor located on the surface of dendritic cells and macrophages. While the role of CD209a in pathogen-induced immune response has been extensively studied, recent research suggests that it may also be involved in metabolic diseases. Intriguingly, CD209a interacts with multiple ligands, and these ligands can have distinct effects in the context of MASLD [17, 18]. However, the role of CD209a itself in MASLD has not yet been studied. We first observed a significant increase in hepatic CD209a expression in patients with MASLD and mouse models. To confirm whether CD209a plays a regulatory role in MASLD, we generated CD209a knockout mice and found intensified MASLD manifestations in these mice. Hepatic lipid deposition, hepatocyte injury markers, and proinflammatory cytokines were markedly increased in CD209a knockout mice compared with their wild-type counterparts, suggesting a protective role of CD209a against hepatic steatosis and inflammation.

Furthermore, CD209a knockout mice exhibited exacerbated glucose intolerance and insulin resistance. Protein kinase B, also known as AKT, is a pivotal signaling protein involved in insulin-stimulated glucose transport and glycogen synthesis [22, 23]. AKT undergoes two critical phosphorylation steps during its activation, with phosphorylation at serine 473 (Ser473) being indispensable for its complete activation and subsequent downstream signaling. The phosphorylation level of AKT Ser473 can reflect the responsiveness of tissues to insulin [23, 24]. In our study, insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 was significantly blunted in both liver and epididymal white adipose tissue of CD209a knockout mice compared with wild-type controls, indicating that CD209a deficiency disrupts insulin signaling in both liver and peripheral metabolic tissues.

Notably, CD209a knockout mice exhibited greater body weight gain and increased white adipose tissue mass. Histological analysis of epididymal white adipose tissue revealed more pronounced adipocyte hypertrophy and a higher density of crown-like structures (CLS), a hallmark of adipose tissue inflammation [25–28]. Macrophages within CLS contribute to oxidative stress and NF-κB activation, driving the production of proinflammatory cytokines [29]. In line with this, CD209a knockout mice showed significantly elevated expression of inflammatory genes such as Mcp1 and Tnf in adipose tissue. This amplified inflammation can disrupt adipose endocrine function, leading to increased lipolysis and adipokine imbalance [30]. These changes enhance free fatty acid flux to the liver, stimulate hepatic lipogenesis, suppress β-oxidation, and activate liver macrophages via circulating inflammatory mediators, thus reinforcing an “adipose–liver crosstalk” [31–33]. This mechanism aligns with clinical findings that link visceral adipose inflammation to MASLD progression [33]. Thus, our results suggest that CD209a deficiency aggravates MASLD, partially through exacerbated adipose dysfunction.

To elucidate how CD209a deficiency exacerbates MASLD, we examined its effects on hepatic dendritic cells and monocytes/macrophages, cell types that predominantly express CD209a. Under MASLD conditions, CD209a expression was markedly elevated in hepatic macrophages instead of dendritic cells. Flow cytometry showed that while a high-fat diet reshaped the hepatic dendritic cell compartment, CD209a deficiency did not further alter this population, suggesting dendritic cells are not major contributors to the phenotype. In contrast, CD209a deletion led to a further increase in hepatic macrophage accumulation. To confirm the involvement of macrophages in our phenotype, we depleted them using clodronate liposomes [34]. This intervention abolished the exacerbation of hepatic steatosis and inflammation observed in CD209a-deficient mice, indicating that the regulatory role of CD209a in MASLD is macrophage-dependent.

The mechanism by which CD209a modulates macrophages remains unclear. Liver macrophages exhibit significant heterogeneity, with distinct subpopulations having been reported to play different roles in metabolic processes [35]. In our study, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that macrophages with high CD209a expression were primarily associated with antigen presentation, cell adhesion, and immune regulation, whereas those with low CD209a expression were mainly involved in inflammatory responses, chemotaxis, and immune cell migration. Notably, CD209a knockout led to a marked increase in Ly6c-high recruited macrophages, a subset characterized by proinflammatory properties, suggesting that the absence of CD209a drives macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype. In contrast, the proportion of antigen-presenting macrophages was significantly reduced. In the liver, antigen-presenting macrophages play a key role in maintaining tissue homeostasis and immune tolerance [36]. Their depletion may contribute to disruption of the hepatic immune microenvironment, thereby accelerating the onset and progression of MASLD.

Meanwhile, we observed that CD209a knockout enhanced STAT3 expression in hepatic Cd209a-highly expressed macrophages, and pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 mitigated hepatic steatosis and inflammation in CD209a knockout mice. Overactivation of the STAT3 pathway in macrophages is known to trigger inflammation, while inhibition of STAT3 can block macrophage inflammasome activation and relieve inflammation [37]. These findings suggest that CD209a protects against MASLD potentially through its regulatory effect on macrophage STAT3 signaling. However, it remains unclear whether CD209a directly regulates STAT3 activation. Given that STAT3 can be phosphorylated downstream of multiple cytokines elevated under inflammatory and adipose-dysregulated conditions, the observed STAT3 hyperactivation in CD209a knockout mice may be secondary to systemic inflammation rather than direct receptor-level modulation. Still, as a membrane-bound C-type lectin receptor, CD209a may influence STAT3 activity via intermediate signaling adaptors or by modulating cytokine receptor complexes such as IL-6R/gp120 [38]. Future studies will be needed to dissect these possibilities.

CD209a deletion aggravates MASLD by modulating macrophage function, which involves complex interactions with hepatocytes and other liver-resident cell types. Activated macrophages secrete proinflammatory cytokines that impair insulin signaling and promote lipogenesis in hepatocytes [39]. In our study, CD209a deficiency led to increased hepatic macrophage infiltration and upregulation of inflammatory mediators, including Tnf, Mcp1, and Ccl3, accompanied by elevated expression of Cd36 and lipogenic enzymes Acaca and Scd1, suggesting enhanced lipid influx and synthesis. Beyond their impact on hepatocytes, macrophages can also promote fibrosis by releasing TGF-β and PDGF to activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), and amplify inflammation via neutrophil recruitment and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, consistent with the increased hepatic MPO staining observed in CD209a-deficient mice (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B) [39]. Although no overt fibrosis was detected in either genotype under the current 12-week HFD model (Supplementary Fig. S4C), prolonged feeding or alternative diet-induced models may be required to determine whether CD209a influences macrophage–HSC interaction during advanced stages of MASLD. Together, these findings highlight the pivotal role of macrophages in driving MASLD pathogenesis and underscore CD209a as a key modulator of hepatic immune–metabolic interactions.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although our findings suggest that CD209a protects against MASLD by suppressing macrophage-induced inflammation through inhibition of the STAT3 pathway, the causality of this relationship remains unproven. The increased STAT3 activation observed may be a downstream effect of enhanced inflammation rather than a direct consequence of CD209a loss. As a membrane receptor, CD209a likely interacts with upstream modulators of STAT3, which warrants further investigation. Second, the pathogenesis of MASLD is orchestrated through complex interactions among multiple hepatic cell types. In the present study, we did not delve into the direct interactions between macrophages and other liver-resident cells. Thus, the downstream mechanisms by which CD209a deficiency-induced macrophage reprogramming influences the hepatic microenvironment and contributes to MASLD progression remain to be fully elucidated. Third, we observed significant adipose tissue dysfunction and inflammation in our study. Given the critical role of adipose tissue dysfunction in MASLD pathogenesis, it is plausible that the hepatic manifestations observed in CD209a-deficient mice may be, at least in part, secondary to alterations in adipose tissue homeostasis. Future studies should aim to delineate how macrophage CD209a deficiency-mediated adipose tissue dysfunction contributes to the onset and progression of MASLD.

Conclusions

CD209a plays a protective role in MASLD. Loss of CD209a promotes macrophage-driven inflammation through STAT3 signaling and exacerbates MASLD severity. CD209a may serve as a potential therapeutic target for MASLD management, warranting further translational research.
