Magnetic and pneumatic actuation of polymeric microneedles for plasmonic hot-spot engineering and molecular sensing
Authors: Ayfer Aytac Arslan, Emrecan Yildiz, Meryem Beyza Avci, Arif E Cetin, Gokhan Demirel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoft actuators offer dynamic control of plasmonic particle gaps for hot-spot engineering, yet their potential remains underexplored. Here, we demonstrate two polymeric microneedle platforms designed for precise hot-spot generation and molecular sensing. The first employs magnetic actuation, embedding Fe particles into microneedles to enable reversible deformation under magnetic fields, with stable performance over 1000 cycles. Decorating these structures with silver nanoparticles yields strong surface-enhanced Raman scattering amplification. The second utilizes pneumatic actuation with polydimethylsiloxane microneedles, providing greater flexibility and tip contact area under negative pressure. When functionalized with gold nanoparticles, the microneedles achieve ~3.0-fold higher Raman signals than magnetic counterparts. To demonstrate multifunctionality, we integrate independent vacuum sources and a robotic arm for cargo transport and selective molecular detection. These dynamic microneedle platforms overcome limitations of conventional approaches and offer programmable, tunable, and scalable solutions for plasmon-enhanced sensing in diverse real-world applications.Subject terms: Polymers, Actuators, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Soft actuators have revolutionized nanotechnology and biomedical engineering by providing microscale motion control, offering unparalleled flexibility, adaptability, and biocompatibility1–6. These systems, which can be actuated by various stimuli such as chemical stimuli7,8, acoustic waves9,10, magnetic fields11,12, pneumatic pressure13,14, or electrical signals15,16 have found widespread applications in areas ranging from targeted drug delivery17–19 to minimally invasive surgery20–22. When integrated with sensing functionalities, soft actuators can further extend their utility to real-time monitoring and detection of chemical and biological analytes, making them ideal platforms for next-generation sensing technologies. Among the available detection methods, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) has emerged as a powerful tool for detecting trace amounts of analytes due to its exceptional sensitivity and molecular specificity23–25. SERS relies on the amplification of Raman signals via the generation of localized electromagnetic fields, known as “hot-spots”, which are formed when plasmonic nanostructures are brought into close proximity26–28. However, the practical SERS applications are often constrained by challenges in controlling the spatial distribution, density, and reproducibility of these hot-spots, as well as the need for robust and scalable platforms to facilitate precise molecular detection.

In recent years, the use of soft actuators in the field of plasmonic engineering has emerged as a significant opportunity. To this end, we developed a soft actuator platform based on pre-strained polystyrene (PS) materials29. By employing LED lights of different colors, the folding of PS films could be controlled, allowing for the manipulation of the distance between plasmonic particles. However, several challenges arose, including the irreversible folding of PS structures, the formation of hot-spots in small and static areas under light exposure, and analyte degradation due to heat generated by the light source. To address these issues, in a subsequent study, we designed external magnetic field-controlled soft actuators using silicon-based resin and magnetic iron particles30. Through magnetic field control and design optimization, we demonstrated that plasmonic hot-spots could be dynamically generated over large areas. Additionally, the formation of hot-spots could be further manipulated by varying the strength of the magnetic field. However, both light- and magnetic field-based soft actuator platforms face limitations, particularly in real-world applications, due to design constraints and their inability to facilitate multiplexed sample analysis. This highlights the need for more versatile and scalable approaches to overcome these challenges and expand the applicability of soft actuators in plasmonic engineering.

In recent years, polymeric microneedles have gained significant attention as versatile platforms for diverse applications, including drug delivery31,32, biosensing33, and tissue engineering34,35. Their favorable mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and ease of fabrication make them ideal candidates for integrating with plasmonic nanostructures, enabling the creation of tunable SERS-active devices. By combining the deformability and processability of polymeric materials with the electromagnetic properties of plasmonic nanoparticles, it becomes feasible to design microneedle-based systems that dynamically modulate hot-spot formation, thus enhancing molecular sensing capabilities.

Building upon the potential of soft actuator-integrated sensing, this work presents two distinct designs of polymeric microneedle platforms for programmable hot-spot generation and SERS-based molecular detection. The first design utilizes magnetic actuation, whereby microneedles embedded with magnetic particles respond to an external magnetic field to modulate the interparticle spacing between plasmonic structures. This approach allows on-demand control of hot-spot formation, enabling enhanced Raman signal amplification. The second design employs pneumatic (vacuum-powered) actuation, leveraging the high elasticity of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) to create microneedle platforms capable of larger-area deformation and contact areas and more efficient hot-spot generation under negative pressure. Both designs were functionalized with silver or gold plasmonic nanoparticles to enable SERS activity, and their performance was systematically evaluated in terms of actuation efficiency, hot-spot formation, and Raman signal enhancement. These soft actuator-integrated microneedle platforms offer distinct and complementary advantages, e.g., magnetic actuation provides compact, high-gradient control, while pneumatic actuation delivers superior scalability and user-friendliness. Together, they establish a powerful and adaptable framework for plasmonic hot-spot engineering. Importantly, both systems are designed for direct and localized molecular detection, addressing the limitations of our previous studies. Unlike previous works, the microneedle geometry offers minimally invasive sampling, development of flexible, reconfigurable, and highly sensitive SERS platforms suitable for real-world biomedical and chemical sensing applications.

Results

Fabrication of magnetic and pneumatic microneedle platforms

Figure 1A illustrates the schematic representation of the fabrication processes for magnetic and vacuum-powered microneedle platforms developed using a template-assisted molding and stamping approach. A commercially available microneedle device featuring 140 medical-grade stainless-steel needles served as a stamp for the fabrication, and a polymeric cover lid for the metallic needles was used as a mold. Prior to fabrication, the polymeric mold was coated with a thin layer of lubricant to facilitate the removal of polymeric microneedles. For the magnetic microneedle platforms (Fig. 1B), a mixture of Ecoflex (00-30) and magnetic Fe particles (average diameter ~5.0 µm) was prepared by mechanical stirring and subsequently poured into the mold. The metallic microneedle stamp was immersed in the mold, ensuring the mixture fully encapsulated the needles. The assembly was cured in an oven at 70 °C, after which the magnetic microneedle platforms were carefully demolded. In contrast, for the vacuum-powered (pneumatic) platforms (Fig. 1C), microneedles were fabricated using only polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184) without adding magnetic particles. The PDMS precursor and curing agent were mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio, degassed, and then poured into the mold using the same stamping approach. The samples were cured under identical conditions and demolded to yield flexible microneedles suitable for pneumatic actuation. Optical imaging of the fabricated microneedles confirmed the successful replication of the mold architecture with no observable damage for both (Fig. 1D) Ecoflex/Fe magnetic and (Fig. 1E) PDMS microneedle platforms. Both platforms retained consistent geometrical fidelity to the original mold. The microneedles exhibited a center-to-center spacing of approximately 2 mm, as observed in the optical image (Fig. 1D). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that each microneedle had a height of ~1500 µm and an inner diameter of ~220 µm (Fig. 1D).

Magnetic actuation performance and simulation analysis

A series of experiments was conducted to systematically evaluate the magnetic actuation behavior of the fabricated Ecoflex/Fe magnetic microneedle platforms. In the first experimental set, the influence of magnetic field strength on the actuation performance was investigated. Permanent neodymium iron boron (NdFeB) magnets were employed to generate magnetic fields ranging from 61.7 to 235 mT. The magnets were positioned beneath the platform, aligned parallel to the longitudinal axis of the microneedles. The weight fraction of magnetic Fe particles in the composite mixture was maintained at 80% (w/w) throughout these experiments. Figure 2A illustrates the snapshot optical images of the actuation response for the magnetic microneedle platforms, and Fig. 2C shows the closure ratio in the presence of varying magnetic field strengths at 80% (w/w). At 61.7 mT, no measurable actuation was observed. However, at 114.3 mT, the platforms exhibited a closure ratio of 0.66 ± 0.033%. Incremental increases in the magnetic field strength to 140 mT and 187 mT resulted in closure ratios of 2.03 ± 0.11% and 64.45 ± 3.22%, respectively. Notably, at the maximum magnetic field strength of 235 mT, the platforms achieved complete actuation with a closure ratio of 100%. This robust performance confirmed the feasibility of utilizing our platforms for hot-spot engineering applications.

In the second experimental set, the weight fraction of magnetic Fe particles in the composite mixture was systematically varied from 10% to 80% (w/w). The actuation performance of the platforms was analyzed in the presence of a constant magnetic field strength of 235 mT. Figure 2B illustrates the snapshot optical images of the actuation response for the magnetic microneedle platforms, and Fig. 2D shows the closure ratio in the presence of varying composite mixtures at 235 mT. The platforms containing 10% (w/w) magnetic particles achieved a closure ratio of 5.66 ± 0.29%. Increasing the magnetic particle content to 20% (w/w) resulted in a significant ~6-fold enhancement in actuation performance, yielding a closure ratio of 29.57 ± 1.48%. Further increasing the magnetic particle content to 40% and 60% (w/w) led to closure ratios of 38.15 ± 1.91% and 47.22 ± 2.36%, respectively. However, complete closure was not observed until the magnetic particle content reached to 80% (w/w), where the platforms demonstrated full actuation with a 100% closure ratio (Supplementary Movie 1). To assess the reversibility and durability of the magnetic actuation, the platforms were subjected to cyclic testing under a 235 mT magnetic field, with alternating on/off intervals of 3 seconds each. Over 1000 cycles, the platforms exhibited consistent actuation performance without significant degradation, demonstrating exceptional reversibility and mechanical stability (Supplementary Fig. 1). This robust cyclic performance underscores the potential of these platforms as reliable soft actuator systems for applications requiring repeated and precise actuation. To further enhance their responsiveness and energy efficiency, strategic design modifications could be considered. Modifying key parameters, such as reducing the microneedle height, decreasing wall thickness, increasing taper sharpness, or incorporating stress concentrators near the tip region, could significantly lower actuation thresholds without compromising structural integrity. These design refinements would enable more efficient and responsive actuation at a lower magnetic field, which could be particularly desirable for wearable or minimally invasive biomedical devices.

To complement the experimental results and gain further insights into the magnetic actuation mechanisms, two simulation protocols were implemented to replicate and extend the experimental observations (Please see the Methods section for details). In the first protocol, the microneedle was modeled as a composite containing 80% (w/w) iron particles dispersed in Ecoflex. The remanent flux density of the magnet was adjusted to simulate uniform magnetic field strengths of 61.7 mT, 114.3 mT, 140 mT, 187 mT, and 235 mT across the microneedle region. A magnetostatic solver was employed to calculate the steady-state magnetic flux distribution and the resulting magnetic body force density. In the second protocol, the magnetic field was held constant at 235 mT, while the microneedle’s magnetic material properties were modified to reflect composites containing 10%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% (w/w) iron particles. Here, in order to simulate the magnetic behavior of iron particle-embedded microneedles without explicitly modeling individual particles, which necessitate high memory requirements, we adopted a homogenized material approximation. Instead of resolving the spatial distribution of each nanoparticle within the polymer matrix, we assigned bulk material properties based on an effective medium theory derived from a reference material composed of 100% iron. Magnetic properties were scaled according to the relative volume or mass fraction of iron within the composite. Specifically, the relative magnetic permeability (μr) assigned to each composition was estimated by proportionally reducing the permeability of bulk iron, assuming a random, non-interacting particle distribution.

Figure 2E shows the magnetic field and the corresponding magnetic force distribution acting on the microneedle structure under a magnetic field strength of 187 mT and a composite concentration of 80% (w/w) Fe particles. The left panel displays the magnetic flux density (B) distribution, with denser field lines and higher intensity values localized near the lower portion of the microneedle, directly above the magnet. This vertical gradient in B reflects a strong directional magnetic influence aligned with the axis of the microneedle, generating a substantial magnetic force within the structure. The right panel of Fig. 2E depicts the resulting volumetric magnetic forces as red vector arrows distributed throughout the microneedle body. These arrows represent the body force density derived from the interaction of the magnetization vector with the local field gradient. Notably, the red arrows are longer and more concentrated along the inner surfaces of the microneedle tips, indicating higher inward-directed forces in these regions. This asymmetry is consistent with the bending and closing behavior observed experimentally, where the microneedle tips deflect inward toward each other. The central schematic further clarifies this observation by showing a simplified representation of the force imbalance, e.g., the magnetic force on the inner face of each microneedle (Finner, red arrows) is significantly greater than that on the outer face (Fouter, blue arrows). This force differential drives the microneedles to bend inward, enabling tip closure under the magnetic field. The schematic explains how this net inward force results in mechanical distortion and successful actuation.

In addition to the magnetic body forces acting along the microneedle axis, the mechanical structure of the microneedle itself also plays a critical role in determining the deformation behavior. Specifically, the tip region of the microneedle exhibits lower mechanical stiffness compared to the base due to its tapering geometry and reduced cross-sectional area. This inherent structural compliance at the tip makes it more susceptible to bending under the influence of magnetic forces. In contrast, the base of the microneedle is physically anchored to the substrate, providing a rigid boundary condition that restricts displacement and prevents deformation near the root. As a result, the observed deformation is localized at the tip, leading to the inward bending and closure behavior consistently seen in both experiments and simulations. This combined effect of magnetic force gradients and geometric stiffness variation contributes to the asymmetric collapse profile and reinforces the functional reliability of tip-based actuation for plasmonic hot-spot generation.

A direct comparison between experimental and simulation results in Fig. 2C, D reveal a strong correlation across both varying magnetic field strengths and magnetic particle compositions. In Fig. 2C, both the experimental data and simulation outputs show a nonlinear increase in closure ratio as the magnetic field increased from 60 mT to 235 mT at a fixed Fe particle content of 80% (w/w). The simulation slightly overestimates closure in the intermediate range (140–187 mT), which may result from the idealized magnetic homogeneity assumed in the computational model compared to the slight material inhomogeneities present in the real composites. Nonetheless, both trends converge at high fields, predicting complete microneedle closure at 235 mT. This agreement confirms that the simulated magnetic body forces accurately capture the cumulative deformation behavior seen experimentally. Similarly, in Fig. 2D, simulations conducted at a constant magnetic field of 235 mT effectively reproduce the closure behavior of microneedles with varying magnetic particle contents. Both experimental and simulated results demonstrate a monotonic increase in actuation with higher Fe loading, though again, the simulations tend to slightly overpredict deformation at lower particle contents (e.g., 10–20% w/w). This discrepancy likely arises from the simplified linear scaling of relative permeability in the homogenized material approximation, which may not fully capture percolation thresholds or interparticle magnetic interactions. Despite this, the overall fidelity between simulated and experimental closure ratios across all conditions validates the modeling approach and underscores its utility for predictive actuator design. These comparisons affirm the relevance of magnetostatic simulations not only for understanding but also for engineering soft microneedle systems tailored to specific actuation thresholds.

Magnetic hot-spot engineering

Subsequently, studies on the control and manipulation of plasmonic hot-spot formation were conducted using magnetic polymeric microneedle platforms. A critical factor in hot-spot formation is the precise tuning of the interparticle distance between plasmonic structures26,30. To achieve this, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) with an average diameter ranging from approximately 15 nm to 95 nm were synthesized in situ on the top of the microneedle surfaces. When the magnetic microneedle platforms were actuated under a magnetic field of 235 mT, they transitioned into a closed configuration, reducing the distance between the plasmonic structures at the microneedle tips. This reduced spacing leads to enhanced electromagnetic coupling, thereby maximizing the intensity of localized electromagnetic fields and facilitating hot-spot formation. For these evaluations, microneedles were fabricated using a composite mixture containing 80% (w/w) magnetic Fe particles, and all magnetic actuation experiments were performed under a constant magnetic field strength of 235 mT. Prior to actuation, plasmonic Ag nanoparticles were deposited onto the microneedle surfaces via an in situ synthesis approach. This was achieved by first coating the microneedle tips with a polydopamine (PDA) layer, whose functional groups (catechol, amine, and imine) enable the controlled formation of metallic nanoparticles without requiring additional reducing agents or seed particles36,37. This process leverages the inherent reducing and stabilizing properties of PDA. The PDA layer was formed through the oxidative polymerization of dopamine, and its thickness was controlled to ~10 nm, a value previously optimized in our studies for the uniform formation of AgNPs on surfaces29,30,38. Following PDA deposition, the AgNPs were grown with varying surface densities by modulating the metallic salt reduction time between 3 and 24 hours. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the fabricated platforms are presented in Fig. 3A, B. For a retention time of 24 h, the nanoparticle density on the surface was quantified as ~3.1 × 109 nanoparticles/cm2 with an inter-particle distance of 40.44 ± 17.63 nm. The AgNP-functionalized platforms were further characterized via UV-Vis spectroscopy. The colloidal AgNPs with a diameter of ~15 nm exhibit a characteristic absorbance maximum at ~400 nm. However, upon in situ AgNP formation onto the platforms for the retention times of 3 h, 12 h, and 24 h, the absorbance peak gradually redshifted to 429 nm, 431 nm, and 442 nm, respectively (Fig. 3C). These spectral shifts are indicative of plasmonic coupling effects, which arise from the controlled reduction in the interparticle distance achieved through the manipulation of nanoparticle growth duration on the platform surfaces. To validate the deposition of AgNPs on PDA coated microneedles, we also characterized the samples using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The XPS spectra of the platforms confirm the successful formation of AgNPs on the PDA deposited Ecoflex surfaces. The multi-step surface modification approach we used in our investigations, combining PDA coating and time-controlled in situ growth of AgNPs, has proven effective in ensuring uniform, reproducible, and scalable nanoparticle functionalization, as supported by both imaging and spectroscopic data. Given the intended biomedical use of these functionalized platforms, biocompatibility becomes a key prerequisite. In our recent study, we investigated the biocompatibility of similar magnetic platforms fabricated using Ecoflex embedded with iron microparticles11. The results indicated a statistically significant enhancement in cell proliferation, clearly demonstrating the non-toxic and biocompatible nature of the magnetic Ecoflex platforms.

The hot-spot formation properties and molecular sensing capabilities of the magnetic microneedle platforms were evaluated using surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS). Methylene blue (MB) was employed as a Raman reporter molecule for these experiments. To this end, an aqueous MB solution (1.0 µL, 1.0 × 10−3 M) was deposited onto the AgNP-decorated microneedles. Figure 3D illustrates the solvent evaporation process of the deposited MB solution on a microneedle tip at room temperature (25 °C). As can be seen from the time-dependent snapshot images, the evaporation of the solvent of the deposited MB droplet was completed within 300 seconds (Supplementary Movies 2 and 3). Afterward, the Raman spectrum of MB was first obtained from the magnetic microneedles in the absence of a magnetic field (blue spectrum in Fig. 3E) using a Raman laser excitation wavelength of 785 nm. This wavelength was chosen to avoid electronic transitions in the analyte MB molecule39,40. For the open-form of magnetic microneedles, characteristic peaks including C–N–C skeletal peak at 443 cm−1, C–N stretching peak at 1171 cm−1, asymmetric/symmetric C–H ring stretching peak at 1387 cm−1, and C–C ring stretching peak at 1611 cm−1 were clearly observed. It is important to note that due to the high Raman cross-section of MB, no low-intensity characteristic peaks from the underlying PDA-coated polymeric platform were detected. In the presence of a magnetic field, the tip of the microneedle platform actuates, e.g., bringing the edges into close proximity and forming hot-spots at the contact points (closed-form). This configuration resulted in enhanced Raman signals for all characteristic MB peaks. Compared to the open-form, for the closed form (red spectrum in Fig. 3E), the Raman signal intensities at 443 cm−1 and 1611 cm−1 increased by ~1.8-fold and ~1.4-fold, respectively. This enhancement is attributed to the increased physical contact area between the microneedle tips under a magnetic field, facilitating the aggregation of MB molecules and intensifying the local electromagnetic field in the hot-spot region30. In the absence of the magnetic field (open-form), near-field enhancements are confined to small regions between plasmonic nanoparticles. However, when a magnetic field is applied, hot-spot generation extends across a broader area at the joint points of the microneedle tips. Here, all Raman measurements were performed under identical experimental conditions (e.g., illumination area and Raman configurations) for both open- and closed-form platforms. To evaluate reproducibility quantitatively, we have collected more than 50 independent Raman spectra for both the open (unactuated) and closed (actuated) states of the platforms (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4 and Supplementary Table 1). The relative standard deviation (RSD) in the open state remains very low (1.30% and 1.52%), and even after actuation, the variation stays within a statistically acceptable range. To further investigate hot-spot distribution, over 1000 Raman spectra were collected from MB-deposited microneedle surfaces and analyzed via 3D Raman mapping (Fig. 3F). The mapping results revealed a gradual increase in the Raman signals of MB at 443 cm−1 and 1611 cm−1 in closed-form compared to open-form microneedles, confirming hot-spot generation at the joint points in the presence of the magnetic field.

Pneumatic actuation performance and simulation analysis

The second design of the polymeric microneedle actuator developed in this work was fabricated using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) without incorporating magnetic Fe particles. While magnetic actuation provides a straightforward and simple method for controlling polymeric microneedle motion and enabling hot-spot generation, as demonstrated here, several limitations hinder its practicality for real-world applications. These include the lack of area-selective actuation for multiplex sensing, limited workspace, higher system cost, and the necessity for materials with high magnetic particle content. In contrast, pneumatic or vacuum-powered actuation offers several advantages, such as simplified actuation mechanisms, spatially selective motion control, and ease of fabrication for soft actuators. To leverage these benefits, we designed polymeric microneedles that can be dynamically actuated by applying negative pressure (Fig. 4A). This approach enhances the physical contact area along the longitudinal axis, thereby facilitating stronger plasmonic coupling and more efficient electromagnetic field enhancement, enabling highly effective hot-spot formations. Figure 4B, C shows the SEM images of the fabricated PDMS microneedles. Similar to the magnetic microneedles, each PDMS microneedle has a height of ~1500 µm and an inner diameter of ~200 µm. Snapshots of the actuation performance of the fabricated platforms under a negative pressure of ~40 kPa show a deformation behavior comparable to that of the magnetic one (Fig. 4D). Supplementary Movie 4 shows the vacuum-powered actuation of PDMS microneedles under 40 kPa negative pressure, forming a larger contact area under the negative pressure. This expanded contact region increases the probability of hot-spot formation and analyte interaction. This simple control difference provides the PDMS microneedle platforms with enhanced flexibility and control over hot-spot formations.

Finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted using the SolidWorks Simulation module to model the mechanical response of the microneedle under vacuum conditions (Fig. 4E). The microneedle geometry was parametrically designed and discretized using a high-density tetrahedral mesh to capture local deformations accurately. The material was defined as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), where the material properties were obtained from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology - 6.777 J/2.751 J Material Properties Database (See Supplementary Table 2 for the key material parameters of PDMS for the FEA simulation). To replicate boundary constraints representative of the experimental setup, fixed geometric restraints were applied to the bottom surface and lateral base perimeter of the microneedle. These fixtures prevented translational and rotational motion, mimicking attachment to a rigid substrate. A pressure load of 40 kPa was applied uniformly on the internal surface of the microneedle to simulate the vacuum-induced negative pressure. This boundary condition was configured as a static external pressure directed inward along the surface normal vectors.

The simulation output focused on the von Mises stress and total displacement distributions across the microneedle body. Notably, the highest stress concentrations and deformations were localized at the tip region, corresponding to the collapse behavior observed experimentally. Here, the resulting force exerted on the microneedle tip was maximum, e.g., the pressure was calculated as 3.11 × 105 N/m2, which drove localized tip deformation and collapse, consistent with experimental observations. Interestingly, instead of collapsing symmetrically inward, the microneedle structure exhibited an off-center buckling deformation. This asymmetric collapse was primarily attributed to minor structural deviations introduced during the fabrication process. In particular, one side of the microneedle remained mechanically stiffer than the other due to uneven layer thicknesses or residual stresses during molding and curing. As a result, the collapse behavior was biased in one direction rather than forming a uniformly closed tip (Supplementary Movies 5 and 6 for the cross-sectional and top views of the asymmetric collapse behavior of microneedles).

This snap-through behavior reflects a form of geometry-induced bistability, where the microneedle tip undergoes a rapid transition between stable configurations under vacuum pressure. Although a linear elastic model was used in our simulations, bistable behavior was effectively captured through geometry-driven mechanical instability. The conical, hollow geometry of the microneedle introduces a stiffness gradient along its length, making the tip region more compliant. This design, combined with the mechanical properties of PDMS, particularly its compressive strength (7.0 MPa), yield strength (1.0 MPa), and tensile strength (3.0 MPa). These parameters define the pressure range at which bistable deformation occurs, enabling snap-through without permanent damage.

While such deformation may initially appear as a structural inconsistency, it can actually enhance sensing performance by promoting tighter and more localized tip-to-tip contact, which facilitates the formation of confined plasmonic nanogaps. This controlled asymmetry contributes to the generation of intense and spatially defined hot-spots, ultimately benefiting SERS signal enhancement and local analyte detection. For comparison, simulation results for an ideal symmetric microneedle structure are also provided in Supplementary Fig. 5. These results illustrate a uniform, axisymmetric collapse profile, in which the microneedle tip deforms evenly inward without preferential directionality. While this configuration ensures structural symmetry, it results in a broader contact area and less concentrated stress localization, which may lead to wider but weaker plasmonic coupling zones. In contrast, the asymmetric collapse observed in fabricated devices creates tighter, more spatially confined contact points, favoring localized electromagnetic field enhancement and more efficient hot-spot formation. This comparison supports the functional advantage of controlled asymmetry in real-world microneedle platforms. These results provide mechanical validation for the microneedle’s structural stability under operational actuation conditions and demonstrate its suitability for vacuum-assisted biomedical manipulation.

It is important to note that the microneedle geometries used in this study were derived from commercially available microneedle array molds, which inherently featured relatively tall profiles and wide base-to-tip openings. These structural characteristics, while beneficial for ensuring consistent fabrication and mechanical robustness, have a direct impact on the actuation thresholds required for functional deformation. Specifically, the tall height and broad conical shape contribute to increased bending stiffness and compressive resistance, thereby requiring stronger external inputs, e.g., either higher magnetic field strengths (up to 235 mT) or greater vacuum pressures (up to 40 kPa), to induce full collapse and closure at the microneedle tip. This tradeoff becomes particularly significant in applications where energy efficiency, portability, or actuation precision is critical. From a mechanical perspective, these geometric features introduce a higher moment of inertia and reduce stress concentration near the tip, leading to a more distributed deformation response. While this can improve structural durability and fabrication yield, it also limits the responsiveness and sensitivity of the actuation mechanism. To overcome these limitations in future device iterations, several geometric optimization strategies could be employed. For instance, reducing the microneedle height, sharpening the taper angle, decreasing wall thickness, or introducing localized thinning near the tip region could collectively lower the mechanical stiffness and shift the collapse threshold to lower input energies. Such refinements would enable more efficient tip closure under milder actuation conditions, improve SERS signal consistency by ensuring reproducible gap formation, and enhance compatibility with low-power actuation setups for wearable or implantable biomedical platforms. In this context, the current geometry serves as a robust proof-of-concept architecture, but also provides a baseline for exploring performance tradeoffs between mechanical stability and actuation sensitivity.

Similar to the magnetic microneedle platform, in addition to the mechanical influence of the applied pressure, the inherent compliance gradient of the PDMS microneedles also contributes to the deformation pattern. The tip region, due to its smaller diameter and thinner wall thickness, has lower mechanical stiffness than the base. As the base is anchored to a rigid support, most of the deformation concentrates at the free-standing tip, reinforcing the observed collapse direction and magnitude.

Afterward, the PDMS microneedle platforms were functionalized with colloidal gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) with a diameter of ~20 nm to assess their hot-spot generation performances under negative pressure. The spherical AuNPs were first synthesized through the Turkevich method. To achieve a uniform and stable AuNP layer, a ~ 10 nm PDA coating was first applied to the PDMS microneedle platforms. Based on our prior findings, the platforms were immersed in an aqueous AuNP solution for 24 hours. Figure 4F, G shows the SEM image of an AuNP-decorated PDMS microneedle. The AuNP density on the needle surface was calculated to be ~2.16 × 1010 nanoparticles/cm2 with an inter-particle spacing of 23.14 ± 14.31 nm, indicating a highly favorable morphology for hot-spot generation upon applying negative pressure. Compared to as-synthesized AuNPs in solution (λmax = 519 nm, blue curve), we observed a localized surface plasmon absorption maximum at 601 nm for the AuNP-decorated platforms (red curve), most likely due to the formation of a dense AuNP film on the platforms (Fig. 4H). To validate the deposition of AuNPs on PDA coated PDMS platforms, we also characterized the samples using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 6). The XPS spectra of the platforms confirm the successful deposition of AuNPs on the PDA modified PDMS surfaces.

The hot-spot generation performance of the PDMS microneedle platforms was then assessed, similar to magnetic platforms, in the presence and absence of negative pressure. As shown in Fig. 5A, with the application of negative pressure (~40 kPa), the AuNP decorated microneedles exhibited a SERS activity with only moderate Raman signal enhancement. In contrast, under negative pressure (Fig. 5B), a substantial increase in Raman signal was observed for the characteristic peaks. Specifically, the peak intensities were increased by ~2.2-fold for 443 cm−1 and ~1.8-fold for 1611 cm−1 compared to the unactuated state. Consistent with the design goals of this platform, the PDMS microneedles demonstrated approximately 3.0-fold greater Raman enhancement under vacuum actuation compared to the magnetically actuated microneedles. Even though the Raman signal enhancement in the closed-form magnetic microneedle platform is lower, the primary objective is to spatially confine the regions of enhanced signal, which improves local light-matter interaction and enables precise molecular detection at targeted junctions.

To gain further insight into the difference between the magnetic and vacuum-powered microneedle actuations on hot-spot formation performance, 3D Raman mapping was carried out by collecting over 1000 spectra from the contact region of the PDMS platform with and without negative pressure. As shown in Fig. 5C, D, following vacuum-powered actuation, hot-spot generation was observed over an elliptical contact area approximately 2.1 times larger than that of the magnetic platform. Further 3D Raman spatial mapping at 443 cm−1 and 1611 cm−1 revealed that hot-spot generations extended across a depth of ∼600–700 µm along the z-axis, significantly larger than the ∼300–400 µm depth observed for the magnetically actuated platforms (Fig. 3E). These findings clearly demonstrate that the current PDMS microneedle design, combined with vacuum-powered actuation, achieves not only greater Raman signal enhancement but also enables a larger hot-spot volume, thereby improving the sensitivity and spatial coverage for SERS-based molecular sensing.

To gain further insight into the physical origin of Raman signal enhancement observed in the open- and closed-form microneedle platforms, finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations were performed (Ansys Lumerical Inc.). In the simulations, an unpolarized light source was applied by independently simulating x- and y-polarized components and combining the results, as implemented by the simulation software. The light was incidentally normal to the nanoparticle surfaces. In the simulation cell, perfectly matched layer (PML) boundary conditions were applied along all axes to prevent artificial reflections at the simulation boundaries. A uniform mesh size of 1 nm was used to ensure high spatial resolution and to accurately capture the near-field electromagnetic interactions between closely spaced nanoparticles. It is important to note that the embedded Fe microparticles (average diameter ~5 µm) used in the magnetic platforms were not included in the FDTD simulations, as their optical dimensions are in the mid-infrared range and do not exhibit resonant behavior at the 785 nm excitation wavelength used in our SERS experiments. Their size, spectral mismatch, and encapsulation within the Ecoflex matrix render their contribution to near-field plasmonic coupling negligible.

As shown in Fig. 6, the electromagnetic behavior of randomly distributed gold nanoparticles was modeled within experimentally representative domains corresponding to the open and closed tip configurations. A binary mask image was employed to define the spatial boundaries of each configuration to replicate nanoparticle placement accurately within confined hot-spot regions. The binary map, extracted from experimental images, defined allowed deposition zones, with each white pixel representing a permissible region for nanoparticle placement. The pixel coordinates were scaled into real-world units (nanometers) to ensure dimensional fidelity in the simulation. Nanoparticles were modeled as gold spheres with a fixed diameter of dp = 20 nm. To account for realistic interparticle variability, a random gap spacing, e.g., g∈[1,3]nm, was uniformly sampled for each placement attempt. The minimum allowed center-to-center distance between two particles was thus defined as Dmin=dp+g. A k-d tree–based rejection algorithm was implemented in Python to enforce non-overlapping placement, ensuring statistical randomness while maintaining physical constraints. To enforce this exclusion condition, we implemented a spatial rejection algorithm using a k-d tree proximity search. Each candidate position (ri) extracted from the mask was evaluated by computing its Euclidean distance to all previously accepted particle centers (rj), and was retained only if the following condition held, e.g., minj∣∣ri−rj∣∣≥Dmin(i), where Dmin(i) is the particle-specific minimum spacing requirement including the randomly selected gap. This procedure ensured a statistically randomized yet physically constrained nanoparticle distribution within the valid region, avoiding overlap and clustering. Once the placement of particles was finalized, each nanoparticle was geometrically modeled as a triangulated sphere using icospherical mesh generation. These meshes were individually translated to their designated coordinates and then combined into a single surface model. The final 3D geometry was exported in STL format for applications such as electromagnetic, optical, or mechanical simulation. All modeling procedures were implemented in Python 3.11.5.

The total simulation area was limited to 6 µm × 6 µm to optimize memory usage while maintaining spatial resolution representative of localized hot-spot regions. Notably, the transmission response (Fig. 6A, left panel) calculated within this limited domain exhibited strong agreement with experimental measurements, as evidenced by the spectral overlap between the simulated (gray) and experimental (red) far-field behavior of the nanoparticle geometry. This close correspondence validates the relevance and accuracy of the simulation scale and nanoparticle model used and further supports the reliability of our FDTD approach in capturing the optical behavior responsible for Raman signal enhancement observed in both open- and closed-form microneedle platforms.

Figure 6C presents the simulated electric field distribution maps for the open- and closed-form microneedle configurations. The open-form structure exhibited significantly higher localized electric field maxima, particularly at isolated interparticle junctions. This behavior directly correlates with the elevated Raman intensities observed in the experimental 3D mapping, confirming that the open-form configuration enables stronger near-field coupling and enhanced SERS response. In contrast, the closed-form geometry showed a broader and more uniform field distribution due to increased contact area at the microneedle tips. However, the local field intensities were comparatively lower, which aligns with the moderate Raman enhancements observed experimentally. Similar to the Raman mapping results, although the near-field enhancements observed in the closed-form magnetic microneedle platform were lower than that of the open-form, the primary objective of this configuration is to spatially confine regions of enhanced electromagnetic fields. This spatial confinement promotes improved local light-matter interaction and enables precise molecular detection at targeted microneedle junctions. These simulation results emphasize the distinct electromagnetic behaviors of the two actuation modes. While the closed-form enables spatially confined and reproducible hot-spot generation, the open-form supports localized electromagnetic amplification, which is critical for achieving high-sensitivity molecular detection. Together, these complementary characteristics highlight the dual-mode sensing potential of the microneedle platform, enabling tunable SERS performance based on the actuation state.

Pneumatic cargo handling and molecular detection

To demonstrate the practical utility of our platform, we performed a proof-of-concept experiment in which vacuum-powered PDMS microneedle arrays were used to achieve selective cargo capture and release through controlled pneumatic actuation. Pneumatic systems were specifically chosen for this demonstration due to their superior hot-spot generation efficiency, broader SERS enhancement capabilities, and greater practicality in real-world implementations. Unlike magnetic actuation, pneumatic control enables precise spatial and temporal modulation without relying on external magnetic fields, which can pose integration challenges in compact or portable biosensing devices. This makes pneumatic actuation particularly advantageous for the development of scalable and versatile analytical platforms. To this end, following their design and fabrication, we integrated two independent negative pressure sources into microneedles. A programmable robotic arm was employed to facilitate platform movement. Polystyrene (PS) beads of two different colors were used as target cargo. In the first phase of the experiment, the robotic arm positioned the actuator platform at the target location, where selective cargo (yellow bead) was captured via negative pressure. Subsequently, the second cargo (red bead) was also captured by the microneedle platform. The beads were selectively released once the platform reached the target point. This sequence of capture, transfer, and release events is illustrated in Fig. 7A (See Supplementary Movie 7 for these sequenced events). In the second part of the demonstration, two paper substrates (~5 mm in diameter) impregnated with Raman-active MB and R6G dyes (~0.1 M) served as targets for selective molecular detection using SERS analysis. The AuNP-decorated PDMS microneedles first captured the paper targets, facilitating the transfer of target molecules onto the needle surfaces through swapping. Negative pressure was then applied to complete platform actuation, enhancing the formation of plasmonic hot spots. Subsequently, the microneedle platform was positioned at the SERS detection zone, where spectral results confirmed the successful detection of both target molecules. The Raman spectra in Fig. 7B clearly show the characteristic peaks for both MB (left panel) and R6G (right panel), validating the dual-analyte detection capability of the system. The collected SERS spectra reveal that both targets could be selectively and successfully analyzed using the fabricated microneedle platforms.

Discussion

In this work, we developed two different designs of polymeric microneedle platforms for programmable hot-spot formation and molecular sensing applications. The first design utilized magnetically actuated microneedles, where Fe particle-embedded microneedles demonstrated precise control over hot-spot generation under an external magnetic field. These platforms exhibited robust actuation performance, achieving a 100% closure ratio at a magnetic field strength of 235 mT, and maintained very good reversibility and durability over 1000 actuation cycles. The integration of plasmonic silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) onto the microneedles enabled significant Raman signal enhancements upon magnetic actuation. The second design employed vacuum-powered pneumatic actuation using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microneedles without magnetic particles. This approach addressed several limitations of magnetic actuation, such as the need for high magnetic particle content and restricted workspace, while offering simplified fabrication and area-specific control. The PDMS microneedles demonstrated very good flexibility and formed larger contact areas under negative pressure, resulting in more efficient hot-spot formation. Functionalization with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) further improved SERS performance with a 2.2-fold increase in Raman signal intensity observed under vacuum actuation compared to the unactuated state. 3D Raman mapping revealed that vacuum-powered actuation generated hot-spots over a wider spatial volume, making these platforms highly effective for SERS-based molecular sensing. Finally, a proof-of-concept experiment was performed. The integration of independent negative pressure sources and a robotic arm enabled precise control over cargo manipulation and molecular detection, demonstrating the multifunctional utility of these platforms for real-world applications. In our study, both pneumatically actuated PDMS-based platforms and magnetically actuated Ecoflex-based platforms demonstrated rapid response behavior, with actuation times ranging from approximately 0.5 to 2.0 seconds, depending on the applied pressure or magnetic field intensity. These fast response times were consistently observed during platform operation and were particularly evident in the proof-of-concept demonstrations, such as cargo transfer and sample collection tasks. In summary, both magnetic and vacuum-powered microneedle platforms offer unique advantages for hot-spot engineering and molecular sensing (Supplementary Table 3). While magnetic actuation provides compact actuation with high field gradients, vacuum-powered actuation offers superior scalability, spatial resolution, and user-friendliness. These findings pave the way for the development of advanced soft actuator systems with applications in biomedical sensing, environmental monitoring, and beyond. Future direction will focus on multiplexed target detection, system integration with portable readout units, and deployment in field-ready diagnostic tools.

Methods

Fabrication of the platforms and hot-spot analysis

Dermastamp® 140 DRS microneedle device (Larcoboleno, South Korea) was used as a mold for the microneedle fabrication. A detailed illustration of the fabrication process is presented in Fig. 1. The commercially available microneedle device features 140 medical-grade stainless steel needles, with adjustable needle depth ranging from 0.2 to 3.0 mm, controlled via a roller stick. The device includes a tightly fitted plastic lid that secures the needle bundle and enables needle height adjustment. The holes in the lower section of the plastic lid, where the needles are inserted, have a diameter of 750 μm, while those in the upper section, where the needles exit, measure 400 μm in diameter. Each needle has a diameter of 150 μm, a length of 5 mm, and a spacing of 1.58 mm between adjacent needles. Prior to fabrication, the polymeric mold was coated with a thin layer of lubricant (Mann Ease Release 200, Smooth-on Inc.) to facilitate the removal of polymeric microneedles. For the magnetic microneedle platforms, a mixture of Ecoflex (00-30, Smooth-on Inc.) and magnetic Fe particles (average diameter ~5.0 µm, Nanokar, Türkiye) was prepared by mechanical stirring and subsequently poured into the mold. The selection of Ecoflex for the magnetic microneedle platforms was based on their well-documented mechanical properties, fabrication versatility, and suitability for flexible and microneedle-based applications. The metallic microneedle stamp was immersed in the mold, ensuring the mixture fully encapsulated the needles. The assembly was cured in an oven at 70 °C, after which the magnetic microneedle platforms were carefully demolded. All magnetic actuation experiments were carried out using permanent magnets (B = 60–235 mT).

For the vacuum-powered (pneumatic) platforms, microneedles were fabricated using only polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184) without adding magnetic particles. In our study, PDMS was chosen specifically for its higher elastic modulus and structural resilience, ensuring dimensional stability under repeated negative pressure actuation. The PDMS precursor and curing agent were mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio, degassed, and then poured into the mold using the same stamping approach. The samples were cured under identical conditions and demolded to yield flexible microneedles suitable for pneumatic actuation. The actuating performance of all samples was recorded by a Canon EOS 650D camera. The closure ratios were also calculated via IMAGEJ software.

The hot-spot formation properties of the platforms were analyzed using a high-resolution confocal Raman spectrometer (Jasco NRS-4500, Japan) equipped with a 785 nm excitation laser and an operational spectral range of 200–2000 cm−1. Unless stated otherwise, all Raman spectra were acquired under consistent experimental settings: a ×20 microscope objective, a laser spot size of 3 μm, laser power of 75 mW, and an acquisition time of 30 seconds. In a typical procedure, 1.0 μL of an aqueous analyte solution (methylene blue, MB) with a concentration of 10−3 M was applied to the needle surface and left to dry at room temperature under a fume hood. Raman spectra were then recorded from a minimum of 10 different locations across the dried sample surface.

For the proof-of-concept demonstration, the selective capture and release functionality was implemented by integrating two independently controlled vacuum lines into the PDMS microneedle platform. Each vacuum line was connected to a programmable controller capable of modulating negative pressure at the individual channels (~40 kPa). During the cargo manipulation process (Fig. 7A), negative pressure was applied selectively to the target microneedle, generating localized suction at the tip and enabling the capture of polystyrene beads. Once transported to the desired location, the vacuum was deactivated to allow passive release via the elastic recovery of the PDMS structure. This procedure was repeated sequentially for a second bead. The entire platform was mounted on a programmable robotic arm to ensure accurate spatial positioning during pick-and-place operations.

In the second demonstration (Fig. 7B), the same pneumatic setup was used to capture analyte-loaded substrates and bring them into contact with AuNP-decorated PDMS microneedle surfaces. Upon contact, a vacuum was applied to induce microneedle deformation, enhancing hot-spot generation for surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS). In this configuration, the pneumatic actuation system served a dual function, enabling both physical manipulation of targets and mechanical activation of the microneedle array.

Simulations

Electromagnetic simulations were conducted using ANSYS Maxwell 3D Design. The microneedle geometry was imported as a high-resolution STL mesh, and a permanent magnet was modeled to match the experimental setup. The simulations employed the magnetostatic approximation, governed by Maxwell’s equations under steady-state conditions. The magnetic field H and magnetic flux density B were related by B=μH, where μ is the material’s magnetic permeability. In the absence of free currents, the governing condition, e.g., ∇×H=0 holds, and the constitutive relation simplifies to B=μrμ0H, with μr as the relative permeability and μ0 the vacuum permeability. The volumetric magnetic force acting on the structure was derived from the Maxwell stress tensor or approximated using Fm=(M⋅∇)B, where M is the magnetization vector. The simulation outputs, including magnetic flux lines, field vectors, and magnetic force distributions, were used to quantify the deformation trends and closure ratios across different field strengths and material compositions.

To model the composite behavior of Fe particle–embedded microneedles, we employed a homogenized material approximation, in which the effective magnetic properties (e.g., relative permeability) were scaled based on the weight fraction of iron. While we acknowledge that this approach simplifies the underlying non-linearities and ignores explicit particle-particle magnetic interactions, it enables computationally tractable simulations of the full microneedle geometry. This approach is widely used in the modeling of magnetoelastic composites, particularly when the goal is to estimate macroscopic force distributions rather than capture fine-scale particle dynamics. Importantly, the simulated closure ratios and deformation profiles using this method showed strong agreement with experimental results across a range of field strengths and Fe concentrations (Fig. 2C, D), validating the practical accuracy of this approximation. This modeling framework thus provides a reliable and computationally efficient tool for predicting actuation behavior in magnetically responsive microneedle systems and supports future design optimization under varying compositional and geometric parameters.
