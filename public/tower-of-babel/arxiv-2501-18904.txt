Finite-size scaling of Lee-Yang zeros and its application to the 3-state Potts model and heavy-quark QCD
Kazuyuki Kanaya

Abstract
We propose a new general method to study critical points (CP) using the finite-size scaling of Lee-Yang zeros (LYZ) [ 1 ] . We first study the LYZ in the three-dimensional Ising model on finite lattices. We show that the ratios of multiple LYZ (Lee-Yang-zero ratios: LYZR) have useful scaling properties similar to the Binder cumulants, providing us with a novel method to study CP. In numerical simulations of the Ising model, we confirm that this method works well. We then apply the method to analyze the CP in the three-dimensional three-state Potts model and finite-temperature QCD in heavy-quark region, which are believed to belong to the same universality class as the Ising model. In these models, the partition function at complex parameters can be evaluated by the reweighting method, which allows us to determine the LYZ by varying coupling parameters continuously around the CP. We demonstrate that the LYZR method is powerful in determining the location of the CP in these models.

Introduction

Clarifying the QCD phase diagram as a function of temperature
T
and baryon chemical potential
\mu_{B}
is one of the most interesting subjects in physics.
Many researchers are paying attention to the QCD critical point (QCD-CP) because if such a CP exists, thermodynamic properties in its vicinity receive tight constraints through the universal scaling laws.
Monte Carlo simulations of QCD at non-vanishing
\mu_{B}
, however, are plagued by the sign problem,
and a precise determination of the location of QCD-CP is not yet achieved.

Recently, a novel approach for determining the QCD-CP has been proposed based on the Lee-Yang edge singularity (LYES)
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,

,

,
10
,

,
12
,

,
14

15
]
, which is the end point of the distribution of the Lee-Yang zeros (LYZ) in the infinite-volume limit.
In Refs.
[
14
,
12
]
, the LYZ on the complex baryon chemical potential have been investigated using the QCD simulations at imaginary chemical potential and the Padé approximation. Then, identifying the first LYZ with the LYES in the infinite-volume limit, the scaling relation of the LYES has been applied to extract the location of the CP.
Although this method will work well when the system volume is sufficiently large,
influence of finite-size effects arising from the difference between the first LYZ and LYES should be evaluated carefully in practical simulations.

In this report, we develop a new method for studying CP using the finite-size scaling (FSS) properties of LYZ
[
1
]
.
We first study the three-dimensional Ising model at finite volumes and show that ratios of multiple LYZ (Lee-Yang zero ratios: LYZR) have a useful FSS property similar to that of the Binder cumulant
[
16
]
,
providing us with an alternative method, the LYZR method, to study the CP.
We also discuss the extension of the method for studying CP in general systems in the same universality class.
We numerically show that the method works well in the three-dimensional three-state Potts model and QCD in heavy-quark region.

2
Finite-size scaling of LYZ in Ising model

2.1
Lee-Yang-zero ratio

Let us start with the three-dimensional Ising model with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(h)=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}-h\sum_{i}s_{i},

(1)

on the square lattice of size
L^{3}
with the external magnetic field
h
, where
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}
represents the summation over all pairs of adjacent sites.
In the infinite-volume limit
L\to\infty
, the Ising model is known to have a CP at temperature
T=T_{c}
and
h=0
, where the critical temperature
T_{c}=4.51152322(1)
has been determined precisely in Monte Carlo simulations
[
17
]
. In what follows, we set
J=1
to make
T
and
h
dimensionless.

We denote the partition function at finite
L
and the reduced temperature
t=(T-T_{c})/T_{c}
as
Z(t,h,L^{-1})={\rm Tr}e^{-H/T}
.
The LYZ are the zeros of
Z(t,h,L^{-1})
on the complex-
h
plane for real
t
.
It is known that the LYZ are located on the imaginary axis with
\textrm{Re}h=0

[
18
]
.
Because the Ising model has the
Z(2)
symmetry,

Z(t,-h,L^{-1})=Z(t,h,L^{-1})
,
the LYZ in this model
have a reflection symmetry:
if
h=h_{0}
is a LYZ,
h=-h_{0}
is also a LYZ.
We denote the LYZ with
\textrm{Im}h>0
as
h=h^{(n)}_{\textrm{LY}}(t,L)
, where
n=1,2,\cdots
is labeled such that
\textrm{Im}h^{(n)}_{\textrm{LY}}
increases with increasing
n
.
Since
Z(t,h,L^{-1})
is a regular function of
t
and
h
for finite
L
,
h^{(n)}_{\textrm{LY}}(t,L)
are also regular in
t
for finite
L
.

According to the FSS,
Z(t,h,L^{-1})
in the vicinity of the CP is represented by the scaling function
\tilde{Z}
as

\displaystyle Z(t,h,L^{-1})=\tilde{Z}(L^{y_{t}}t,L^{y_{h}}h),

(2)

for sufficiently large
L
,
where the scaling dimensions are
y_{t}\simeq 1.588
and
y_{h}\simeq 2.482
for the three-dimensional Ising model
[
17
]
. Since the LYZ are zeros of Eq. (
2
), they obey

\displaystyle h_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(t,L)=L^{-y_{h}}\,\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(L^{y_{t}}t),

(3)

with
\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{t})
satisfying
\tilde{Z}(\tilde{t},\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{t}))=0
.

At
t<0
, corresponding to the discontinuity of the first-order phase transition at
h=0
, the LYZ are distributed densely around
h=0
in the infinite-volume limit
[
18
]
.
In fact, it is easily shown that the LYZ are aligned on the imaginary
h
axis with an equal separation as

\displaystyle h_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(t,L)=a(t)(2n-1)/L^{3}\;\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\;0\quad(t<0,\;n:\textrm{finite}),

(4)

for sufficiently large
L
, where
a(t)
is a pure-imaginary function
[
19
]
.

At
t>0
, since
\lim_{L\to\infty}Z(t,h,L^{-1})
is a regular function at
h=0
, the distributions of LYZ for
L\to\infty
must terminate at pure-imaginary points
h=\pm h_{\rm LYES}(t)
away from the real axis of
h
, which are called the LYES
[
2
]
. This implies that

\displaystyle h_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(t,L)\;\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\;h_{\rm LYES}(t)\quad(t>0,\;n:\textrm{finite}).

(5)

Since the right-hand side of Eq. (
5
) does not depend on
L
, only possible asymptotic behavior of
{\tilde{h}}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}({\tilde{t}})
for
\tilde{t}\to\infty
is
{\tilde{h}}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}({\tilde{t}})\propto{\tilde{t}}^{y_{h}/y_{t}}
, which yields
h_{\rm LYES}(t)\propto t^{y_{h}/y_{t}}

[
20
]
.

R_{nm}(t,L)

Now we focus on the ratio of two LYZ at the same
L

[
1
]
,

\displaystyle R_{nm}(t,L)=\frac{h_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(t,L)}{h_{\rm LY}^{(m)}(t,L)}=\frac{\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(L^{y_{t}}t)}{\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(m)}(L^{y_{t}}t)}.

(6)

From Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), one easily finds that Eq. (
6
) behaves in the
L\to\infty
limit as

\displaystyle R_{nm}(t)\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\begin{cases}\frac{2n-1}{2m-1}&(t<0)\\
1&(t>0)\end{cases}\qquad(\mbox{finite}~n,m),

(7)

as shown by the red step function in Fig.
1
(a). Equation (
7
) suggests that the behavior of LYZR (
6
) changes rapidly at
t=0
. This behavior may be useful in locating the CP in numerical simulations.

To gain a better insight into the behavior of Eq. (
6
) around
t=0
, we Taylor expand
\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{t})
at
\tilde{t}=0
as

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{t})=i\big(X_{n}+Y_{n}\tilde{t}+{\cal O}(\tilde{t}\,^{2})\big),

(8)

with real coefficients
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
, which is allowed from the regularity of
\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{t})
.
Substituting this into Eq. (
6
), one obtains

\displaystyle R_{nm}(t,L)=r_{nm}+c_{nm}L^{y_{t}}t+{\cal O}(t^{2}),

(9)

with
r_{nm}=X_{n}/X_{m}
and
c_{nm}=r_{nm}\,(Y_{n}/X_{n}-Y_{m}/X_{m})
.
Equation (
9
) means that
R_{nm}(0,L)=r_{nm}
is independent of
L
, while the slope at
t=0
scales as
L^{y_{t}}
. In other words,
R_{nm}(t,L)
for different
L
intersect at the CP at
t=0
, as schematically shown in Fig.
1
(a). The properties (
7
) and (
9
) are similar to the Binder cumulants
[
16
]
. They thus provide us with an alternative method to determine the CP from the intersection point in numerical simulations.

3
LYZR method for CP in general systems

3.1
LYZR in general systems

Next, we extend the previous argument to deal with CP in general systems that belong to the same universality class as the Ising model.
For clarity of presentation, we first consider the three-dimensional three-state Potts model with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal H}(\tau,\xi)}{T}=-\tau\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\delta_{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}-\xi\sum_{i}\delta_{\sigma_{i},1},

(10)

on the simple cubic lattice of size
L^{3}
with the periodic boundary conditions.
Here,
\sigma_{i}
takes three states
\sigma_{i}=1,2,3
.
The schematic phase diagram of this model is shown in Fig.
1
(b).
At
\xi=0
, this model has a first-order phase transition associated with the spontaneous breaking of the
Z(3)
symmetry.
This first-order phase transition is terminated at a CP at
(\tau,\xi)=(\tau_{\rm c},\xi_{\rm c})
as depicted in Fig.
1
(b).
This CP belongs to the Ising universality class.
In the vicinity of the CP and for sufficiently large
L
, the partition function
{\cal Z}(\tau,\xi,L^{-1})
of the Potts model is related to the partition function
Z(t,h,L^{-1})
of the Ising model by the linear transformation of the coupling parameters

\displaystyle{\cal Z}\left(\tau,\xi,L^{-1}\right)\propto Z\left(\check{t}(\tau,\xi),\check{h}(\tau,\xi),L^{-1}\right),

(11)

with

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\check{t}\\
\check{h}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tau-\tau_{\rm c}\\
\xi-\xi_{\rm c}\end{pmatrix}\equiv A\begin{pmatrix}\delta\tau\\
\delta\xi\end{pmatrix},

(12)

where the
t
axis encoded onto the
\tau
–
\xi
plane should be parallel to the first-order phase-transition line at the CP as shown in Fig.
1
(b)
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
.

We define the LYZ of the Potts model as zero points of
{\cal Z}\left(\tau,\xi,L^{-1}\right)
for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and
denote them with
{\rm Im}\xi>0
by
\xi=\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)
, where the definition of the label
n
is the same as before.
From Eq. (
11
), we find that

\displaystyle\check{h}\big(\tau,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)\big)=L^{-y_{h}}\,\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}\big(L^{y_{t}}\check{t}(\tau,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L))\big),

(13)

where
\tilde{h}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}
is defined in Eq. (
3
).
Then, by combining Eqs. (
8
), (
12
) and (
13
) one finds
[
1
]

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)=\xi_{\rm c}-\frac{a_{21}}{a_{22}}\delta\tau+{\cal O}(L^{2\bar{y}}),

(14)

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)=\frac{X_{n}}{a_{22}}L^{-y_{h}}+\frac{Y_{n}\det A}{a_{22}^{2}}L^{\bar{y}}\delta\tau+{\cal O}(L^{2\bar{y}}),

(15)

with
\bar{y}=y_{t}-y_{h}<0
.
Equations (
3
) and (
15
) imply that
{\rm Im}\,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)\propto\delta\tau^{y_{h}/y_{t}}
for
L\to\infty
and
\delta\tau\to 0

[
3
]
.
Finite-size corrections to these results can be estimated by explicitly calculating higher-order terms omitted in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
).

To introduce the LYZR for this case,
we consider the ratios of
{\rm Im}\,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)
.
By expanding them by
\delta\tau
and
L^{-1}
, one obtains
[
1
]

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{nm}(\tau,L)=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Im}\,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)}{{\rm Im}\,\xi_{\rm LY}^{(m)}(\tau,L)}=\big(r_{nm}+C_{nm}L^{y_{t}}\delta\tau+{\cal O}(\delta\tau^{2})\big)\times\big(1+D_{nm}L^{2\bar{y}}+{\cal O}(L^{4\bar{y}})\big),

(16)

where
C_{nm}=c_{nm}\det A/a_{22}
and
D_{nm}=-(Y_{n}^{2}-Y_{m}^{2})a_{12}^{2}/a_{22}^{2}
. For
L\to\infty
, Eq. (
16
) is dominated by the first bracket on the far-right-hand side.
This means that the intersection point of Eq. (
16
) converges to the CP for
L\to\infty
as in the Ising model.
Note that
r_{nm}=\lim_{L\to\infty}{\cal R}_{nm}(\tau_{\rm c},L)
is the same as
r_{nm}
of Eq. (
9
), i.e. the value of
{\cal R}_{nm}(\tau,L)
at the intersection point is universal.
However, for finite
L
, the second bracket in Eq. (
16
) gives rise to a deviation unless
a_{12}=0
.

Comparing Eq. (
16
) with the corresponding relation for the Binder cumulants
[
16
]
, one finds that the leading finite-
L
correction term
D_{nm}L^{2\bar{y}}
in the LYZR decreases faster than that of the Binder cumulants as
L
increases
[
1
]
.
This would be an advantage of the LYZR method.

These arguments are applicable to any system that belongs to the same universality class.
In the following, we examine Eq. (
16
) numerically in the Potts model and QCD in the heavy-quark region.

3.3
Heavy-Quark QCD

We now turn to QCD in the heavy-quark region. In the heavy-quark limit corresponding to pure gauge
SU(3)
Yang-Mills theory, the confined and deconfined phases are separated by the first-order phase transition.
The first-order transition
weakens as the quark masses decrease
and eventually terminates at a CP, which has been investigated by the Binder-cumulant method in Refs.
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

Now, let us adopt the LYZR method for a determination of
this CP. For simplicity, we focus on the case of degenerate
2
-flavor (
N_{f}=2
) QCD, although generalization to nondegenerate and/or many flavor cases is straightforward.
We perform the analysis on gauge configurations generated in Ref.
[
29
]
on four-dimensional Euclidian lattices with temporal lattice size
N_{t}=4
and aspect ratios
LT=N_{\textrm{s}}/N_{\textrm{t}}=8
, 9, 10, and 12.
These configurations are generated at the leading-order heavy-quark QCD action of the hopping-parameter expansion (HPE)
[
29
,
30
]
, which reads

\displaystyle S_{g+\textrm{LO}}=-6N_{\textrm{t}}N_{\textrm{s}}^{3}\beta\hat{P}-2N_{f}N_{c}\kappa^{4}(48N_{\textrm{t}}N_{\textrm{s}}^{3}\hat{P}+32N_{\textrm{s}}^{3}\hat{\Omega}_{R}),

(17)

for
N_{t}=4
with
N_{c}=3
, where
\beta
is the inverse gauge coupling and
\kappa=1/(2am_{q}+4)
is the hopping parameter with the bare quark mass
m_{q}
.
Here,
\hat{P}
and
\hat{\Omega}
are the plaquette and Polyakov-loop operators normalized such that they become unity in the weak-coupling limit.
In Ref.
[
29
]
,

configurations have been generated for three simulation points around the CP
by the heat-bath algorithm with over-relaxation, working quite efficiently on parallel computers.
We then incorporate the next-to-leading order contributions of the HPE exactly using the reweighting method
[
29
]
.
The effects of yet higher order contributions were shown to be small around the CP for
N_{t}=4

[
31
]
.
The CP of this system has been investigated using the Binder-cumulant method in Ref.
[
29
]
.

4
Summary

In this study, we proposed a general method, the LYZR method, to locate the critical point (CP) from the numerical simulations performed on finite-size systems using the ratios of LYZ.
This method is based on finite-size scaling of LYZ similarly to the conventional Binder-cumulant method.
For studies in general systems, the LYZR method is superior to the Binder-cumulant method in faster suppression of the operator-mixing effects at large system sizes.
To test the LYZR method, we performed Monte Carlo simulations in three models; the three-dimensional Ising model, the three-dimensional three-state Potts model, and finite-temperature QCD in the heavy-quark region, which have a CP in the same universality class. These results confirm the validity of the LYZR method in practical numerical analyses.
