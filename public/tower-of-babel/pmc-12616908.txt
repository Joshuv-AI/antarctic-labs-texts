Citizen science data reveals widespread sexual dichromatism in the European fire salamander (Salamandra salamandra)
Authors: Max Mühlenhaupt, Rosalie Hey, Michelle Starp, Nils Anthes, Paul Bachhausen, Thomas Bamann, Sabrina Bleidißel, Eike Bovensmann, Jürgen Braunsdorf, J Maximilian Dehling, Saskia Ebert, Hannes Egle, Karolin Egle, Simeon Egle, Heidi Enderlein, Maximilian Fischer, Lara Gemeinhardt, Sean G Grond, Ricarda D Gundert, Oliver Krüger, Lorenz Laux, Pia Oswald, Xenia Schlindwein, Manuela Schmidt, Laura Schulte, Ronny Schwalbe, Theresa Stipp, Michael Veith, Frederik Wietbrok, Barbara A Caspers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConspicuous color patterns are traditionally believed to advertise the toxicity of prey to potential predators. However, many aposematic species show drastic variation in coloration, indicating the possibility of other functions of coloration such as intraspecific communication. To study these other functions, we can investigate the influence of intrinsic (e.g., sex) and external factors (e.g., climate) on color variation. We used the aposematic European fire salamander (Salamandra salamandra) to study drivers of variation in the yellow-to-black ratio of the dorsal coloration based on citizen science data available in a new online database.ResultsOur results suggest a widespread sexual dichromatism in fire salamanders in Germany with males displaying a larger yellow-to-black ratio, i.e. are more yellow, than females. This dichromatism persisted even after correcting for a sex-difference in body shape that accounts for some of the variation in the yellow-to-black ratio. Among six investigated putative drivers of this color variation, the proportion of the aposematic yellow coloration increased with latitude but showed no association with other environmental variables such as temperature or the productivity of the habitat.ConclusionsIntegrating citizen science data in this study enabled a comparison of fire salamander populations across large parts of their distribution and highlights widespread sexual dichromatism. Future studies should further investigate potential mechanisms of mate choice in fire salamanders and other selective forces on coloration such as differential predation pressure between both sexes or the role in crypsis. Lastly, multi-national studies supported by citizen science data could fully unravel the extent of color variation in this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02461-0.Keywords: Aposematism, Amphibians, Coloration, Image analysis

Background

Color is among the most diverse traits in the animal kingdom. Different abiotic and biotic factors affect the coloration of an animal both via evolutionary changes and over the lifespan of an individual [1–4]. For centuries, coloration has attracted ecologists and evolutionary biologists aiming to study the drivers of color differences [1, 2]. However, since studying drivers of variation in animal coloration in the wild requires sampling across large spatial scales in order to recognize generalizable patterns [2], studies using a representative portion or the entire area of distribution of the focal species are scarce. Instead, in many cases, studies involve only single populations or a very small fraction of a species’ entire distribution but observed patterns are generalized to the species level. For example, two studies involving sampling of the black-and-yellow European fire salamander (Salamandra salamandra) that were limited to smaller geographic areas found sexual dichromatism in this species (i.e., males displayed a higher proportion of yellow than females) [5, 6] and this pattern was then generalized to the entire species (e.g [7]), without any further investigation. Larger scale studies require more resources, such as money, equipment and workforce, making them unfeasible for small research groups. However, harnessing the data available in social media or on citizen science platforms can drastically increase our sampling effort and thereby improve the generalizability of the conclusions from our studies [8–10].

Collecting data on animal coloration throughout a species’ range of distribution or at least large fractions of its distribution could provide deeper insights into potential drivers of variation. One such driver of variation in coloration is predation, especially if the coloration in focus is always on display such as dorsal or lateral coloration [11]. Both predator and prey can avoid being seen through background matching or disruptive coloration [12]. However, in other cases, animals feature highly conspicuous colors and patterns. If this form of coloration is paired with an underlying defense such as toxicity or spines, this antipredator strategy is termed “aposematism” [13, 14] and can be very effective at preventing predator attacks, once the association between the conspicuous coloration and an underlying defense has been established in the predator [15]. In addition to avoiding predation, dorsal and lateral coloration can also play an important role in thermoregulation and protection from harmful UV-radiation. By increasing the density of melanin, the skin of an animal becomes darker, increasing its ability to absorb radiation and thereby warming up the body surface temperature while protecting the skin from UV-damage [16, 17]. This thermoregulative strategy, termed thermal melanism, can be adaptive for ectotherms inhabiting cooler habitats by aiding in body temperature regulation [17, 18]. Indeed, a cline in melanism along gradients of temperature or its proxies (e.g., geographic latitude or elevation) has already been reported in several taxa [10, 19–21].

In addition to natural selection, sexual selection can also play an important role in the evolution of animal color [22–24]. For example, a costly coloration can be selected through mate choice [24–26]. Costly coloration involves energetically costly pigments or highly conspicuous color patterns that increase the likelihood of detection by predators, thus constituting an honest signal of mate quality [24]. Furthermore, if coloration is heritable, mating with a partner that displays costly and therefore attractive coloration can result in the production of attractive offspring. Thus, highly contrasting and elaborate color patterns can evolve in one or both sexes, depending on the mating system (i.e., if only one of the sexes (e.g., females) is choosy, contrasting and elaborate color patterns should evolve in the opposite sex (e.g., males) [27]). In aposematic species, in which coloration is traditionally believed to be linked to predator avoidance only (see above), it has been hypothesized that sexual selection via mate choice could play an important role in maintaining or even enhancing aposematic coloration [4, 28]. Once the link between conspicuousness and prey unprofitability has been established in the predator, the strength of selection by the predator on coloration could weaken. Consequently, sexual selection could take over the role of the main selective agent and induce a sexual dichromatism in the intensity or pattern of the aposematic coloration. In fact, evidence for the role of sexual selection in shaping aposematic coloration is accumulating [4, 27–30]. In aposematic amphibians in particular, it has been hypothesized that conspicuousness can act as a quality signal and can thus be selected by the choosy sex [5, 6, 31, 32].

The coloration of an animal can also change within the lifespan of an individual through phenotypic plasticity, either during development [3, 7] or, in some species, within seconds or minutes as a fast response to environmental stimuli [33]. Common drivers of color plasticity are changes in the environment, such as seasonal changes that challenge individuals to exhibit drastic changes in color to match their phenotype to the environment [34–36]. In times of rapid environmental changes caused by human activity, the extent to which plasticity in coloration can enable organisms to adapt to changes in the environment and whether plasticity can buffer a phenotype-environment-mismatch are open questions [35–38].

The European fire salamander (hereafter referred to as “fire salamander”) represents an excellent study organism for drivers of color variation, as postmetamorphic individuals have a conspicuous yellow-on-black coloration that is easily quantifiable due to the strong contrast between the two colors [39]. While the coloration of fire salamanders is considered aposematic given the potent toxic substances produced in specialized glands on the skin [40, 41], surprisingly high inter- and intrapopulation variation exists in the dorsal proportion of yellow in these salamanders [5, 6, 42–47]. There is strong evidence for the heritability of color-associated traits [7, 47], and yellow coloration is energetically costly for an individual [40, 46]. Given the role of the yellow warning coloration in preventing predator attacks [40] and the costliness of chemical defenses [25, 48], it is assumed that an individual with a more potent toxic arsenal, will advertise its defensive capabilities by expressing a higher proportion of yellow [6, 49]. However, the proportion of yellow color does not correlate with the potency of the toxins produced by an individual [6, 7, 44, 50]. Therefore, further studies concerning the emergence and maintenance of color variation in fire salamanders are needed to unravel the causes of the remarkable variation in this species.

Previous studies have demonstrated that conditions during the larval period strongly influence postmetamorphic coloration [7, 40, 46]. For example, Caspers et al. [40] reported that fire salamander larvae raised under poor nutritional conditions had lower proportions of yellow on their back after metamorphosis, compared to individuals raised under rich nutritional conditions during the larval stage, indicating that yellow coloration is energetically costly to produce.

Another interesting hypothesis suggests that the proportion of yellow color could also be mediated by environmental temperatures, resulting in more black and less yellow color in cooler environments as a consequence of thermal melanism [42, 50, 51]. However, to our knowledge, no study has found empirical evidence supporting the existence of “thermal melanism” in fire salamanders [44, 46]. Instead, Barzaghi et al. [46] reported an association between habitat productivity quantified as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI, an indicator of photosynthetic activity and green biomass) and the hue in the yellow color of fire salamanders, but not the proportion of dorsal yellow color. The authors argued that this relationship could indicate that greater resource availability during the larval stage could provide more dietary carotenoids that are needed for the expression of the yellow color and influence the hue of the yellow color during metamorphosis [44, 47, 52].

Two studies on fire salamanders in six populations in Slovakia and three populations in Germany reported a sexual dichromatism with males having a greater dorsal proportion of yellow than females [5, 6], but no such pattern was found in 25 Italian populations [46] (see also [43, 44], who did not find sex-specific differences in the dorsal color patterns of Polish and Spanish fire salamanders). However, to date, no study has considered the confounding effect of body shape, which could also drive sexual differences in the dorsal yellow proportion of fire salamanders. Fire salamanders in Central Europe are usually mostly black on their lateral sides [41, 42], and an individual with a wider body shape (e.g., due to pregnancy) will therefore have a larger proportion of black in dorsal view, as the abdomen is more stretched out and a larger proportion of the sides is visible (Fig. 1).

Both individuals were photographed in the same night in the Botanical Garden Bielefeld (BGBI), a study location. Females are usually much wider in body shape, especially when pregnant [41, 42] and, as the lateral side of a fire salamander is usually mostly black, females might have a lower proportion of yellow when viewed from above despite having the same relative amount of pigment cells responsible for the yellow coloration

In this study, we used images of fire salamanders from the recently developed web-based software Amphibian and Reptile Wildbook (ARW [53]), which applies AI-based capture-recapture analysis via pattern recognition. In the ARW, scientists and citizen scientists can upload photos of fire salamanders for population analyses (e.g [54, 55]). Using images of fire salamanders uploaded to the ARW, we studied variation in the fire salamander dorsal yellow-to-black ratio (YB ratio, as calculated by dividing the number of yellow pixels by the number of black pixels in a fire salamander image, more details are provided in the Methods section) on a large regional scale across wide swaths of the natural distribution of fire salamanders in Germany (Fig. 2). Using 30 pictures (depicting 15 males and 15 females) per location at 20 different locations, we tested for sex-specific differences in the YB ratio on the entire body viewed from above (i.e., dorsal), as well as on the head only. We used coloration on the head only, as well as on the entire body, as we expected that entire-body coloration and head coloration will be strongly correlated, but that head coloration is not influenced by sex- and season-specific differences in body shape (i.e., females are wider in body shape due to pregnancy and in winter and early spring fire salamanders and females in particular are usually wider in body shape due to a higher body condition) that could confound our results regarding a potential sexual dichromatism (see above and Fig. 1). Furthermore, we tested for associations between the YB ratio and between-site variation in six environmental variables that have been shown to drive animal coloration in previous studies (e.g [10, 19, 21, 46]). The environmental variables consisted of elevation, average temperature within the period of adult fire salamander peak activity, temperature change over the last 50 years, NDVI, and geographic latitude and longitude. We studied both average temperature and temperature change, as the former represents the current thermic conditions under which the individual developed and thus would affect coloration via developmental plasticity [3], whereas the latter represents how the thermic conditions changed in recent times and thus could represent an important selective agent leading to adaptive evolution [35, 38].

We expected that (1) male fire salamanders show higher YB ratios both on their entire back (body YB ratio) and on their head (head YB ratio) only than females. We also expected (2) no sex-difference in the relationship between body YB ratio and head YB ratio, indicating the absence of a body shape effect on these ratios. Based on the results from testing hypothesis 1) and 2), we planned to select either the body YB ratio or the head YB ratio to study the relationship between fire salamander coloration and environmental location-specific factors.

Methods

All methods were carried out in accordance with our preregistration (link to preregistration: https://osf.io/92x8w), however, the statistical analysis deviated from our preregistration as justified in the Statistical analysis section.

Sampling and processing of images

Using the Mapped Results function in the ARW, we mapped fire salamander encounters color coded according to sex. Next, we identified locations with enough fire salamander encounters (i.e., a minimum of 15 males and 15 females). There had to be a distance of at least 5 km between locations [46] to ensure no regular gene flow between populations [56, 57]. If a suitable fire salamander population within a location was identified, we requested permission to include the fire salamander images in our analysis and invited the ARW-user to actively participate in this study. If the users accepted, for each population, we randomly selected and downloaded 15 images of male and female fire salamanders, respectively, assuring that each fulfilled minimum quality criteria [39]. In brief, acceptable images had a sufficient resolution to delineate color patterns and body parts, showed the entire dorsal side of the individual, and were taken as top-view with an approximately 90° angle to the fire salamander. Images failing at least one of these criteria were discarded, and replaced by another randomly selected image from the map. Applying this process, 598 images from 20 locations (15 images per sex per location, for one location, we were only able to obtain 13 images of females, Table 1; Fig. 2) were downloaded. All photos included in this study were taken between 2017 and 2023 and depicted fire salamanders with a total length >13 cm (as determined by the person taking the photo) as this size threshold is widely accepted to distinguish sub-adult from adult fire salamanders [41].

Following the instructions provided by Sanchez et al. [39], the fire salamander body, excluding the legs, was cropped out and saved as a new image. Additionally, the head was cropped out and saved as a separate image to compare overall body coloration with head coloration (Fig. 3). For this step, the cropping-tool in GIMP (GNU Image Manipulation Program) was used. The number of yellow and black pixels on the fire salamander image was then automatically quantified using the Python script provided in Sanchez et al. [39] and we calculated the YB ratio from these values by dividing the number of yellow pixels by the number of black pixels. Due to the highly contrasting skin coloration of fire salamanders consisting of black and yellow [42], the Python script is usually highly accurate in recognizing black and yellow skin as has been shown by comparing it to another software (NeuroCheck 6.1) [39]. Issues arise when fire salamanders show different poses in the images. Therefore, the legs of the salamanders were cropped and only photos showing the entire back of an animal were included in this study (Fig. 3). All image processing and analyses were conducted by two researchers (RH and MS) who were blinded to the sex and location of the fire salamander in each picture.

Environmental data collection

For each population, a centroid was determined as the approximate center of the fire salamander encounters. Using the GPS coordinates of the centroid for each population, the elevation above sea level (in m) was obtained via the free web tool “Elevation Finder” (https://www.freemaptools.com/elevation-finder.htm). The average temperature (in °C) for each location was calculated on the basis of the centroids via a fine-scale (0.25° × 0.25°) temperature dataset of daily temperatures dating back to 1950 [58]. We only considered temperatures during the period of fire salamander activity between the beginning of March and the end of October, as fire salamanders in Germany hibernate during the winter in underground shelters that likely constitute constant-temperature environments [41]. Furthermore, the average temperature was calculated from 2000 to 2015, as this timeframe likely covers the time the individuals were born and matured (an average age of 7 to 9 years was reported for fire salamanders in different countries in Europe, however, a maximum age of 25 years has also been reported in the literature [41, 59]). Thus, this value represents the average temperature to which the fire salamanders were exposed during ontogeny and adult life. Similarly, the average temperature between 1950 and 1965 was calculated from the same dataset [58] and then subtracted from the average temperature from 2000 to 2015 to approximate how much a specific location changed in average temperature over those 50 years. While all locations experienced an increase in temperature (Table 1), between-site variation in this temperature change could be an important driver of evolutionary change in color through its effect on previous generations of fire salamanders. NDVI data were obtained via the fine-scale (0.05° × 0.05°) MODIS vegetation index dataset (https://www.cen.uni-hamburg.de/en/icdc/data/land/modis-vegetationindex.html). The NDVI per location was also averaged for March to October in the period of 2000 to 2015.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out in R version 4.2.2 [60]. Prior to any analyses, we visually checked our data for unexplainable outliers following the protocol outlined in [61] (i.e., we used the dotchart function provide in R base [60]). We did not find any unexplainable outliers and proceeded as follows. All figures presented in the results section were created using the R package ggplot2 [62].

We used linear mixed effects models (LMMs) to study variation in body YB ratio and head YB ratio. All LMMs were fitted using the function lmer from the lme4 package [63]. We log10-transformed the body YB ratio and head YB ratio for all models to ensure that the assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity and normality of our models were met. We confirmed these model assumptions visually by plotting diagnostic model plots for each model using the function check_model in the performance package [64]. Furthermore, we ensured that our models did not include any strongly confounded variables (i.e., variation inflation factor < 10) using check_model in the performance package [64]. All model diagnostic plots of the final models are provided in the supplementary information (Supplementary Fig. 1). Investigating the model diagnostic plots, we noticed heavy tails of the Q-Q-plots. However, these heavy tails do not represent issues that could affect any model inferences given that the “Posterior Predictive Check”- and “Influential Observations”- plots do not indicate any significant problems such as systematic discrepancies between the real and simulated data or influential observations (see also [64] and references therein). For each final model, we provide the conditional R² as an indication of the proportion of variance explained by the model calculated by using the function model_performance provided by the performance R-package [64] (Supplementary Table 1). Finally, each final model was compared to the null model (i.e., random effect structure and intercept only model) using the anova function in the R-package stats [60] to ensure that the final model provided more explanatory power than the null model (Supplementary Table 2).

To test for a sexual dichromatism in body YB ratio and head YB ratio, we used separate LMMs for each ratio and integrated a fixed effect of sex and initially included a random intercept of population and a random slope for sex. To find the most suitable random effect structure for these models we used the function ranova provided by the lmerTest R-package [65]. In the model including body YB ratio, the random slope and random intercept remained in the final model as the model fit was not improved by removing the random slope (P = 0.07, AICrandom intercept random slope = -93.21, AICrandom intercept = -91.92). The final model including head YB ratio as the dependent variable only included the random intercept for population (P = 0.61, AICrandom intercept random slope = 45.51, AICrandom intercept = 42.51).

To examine the relationship of head YB ratio with body YB ratio and to study if this relationship is sex-dependent, we used a LMM that included the log10-transformed head YB ratio as dependent variable. As fixed effects, the model included the log10-transformed body YB ratio that was centered around its mean to improve interpretability of the model results [66] as well as sex and their interaction. Any sex-differences in slopes would indicate an effect of sex-specific differences in body shape on the body YB ratio and a significant intercept would additionally indicate differences in the mean head YB ratio compared to the mean body YB ratio. Initially, this model included a random intercept for population, a random slope of sex by population, as well as a random slope of the body YB ratio by population to control for population-specific differences in the head YB ratio as well as population specific differences in the sexual dichromatism of the head YB ratio and population specific differences in the relationship of the body YB ratio with head YB ratio. The model including a random intercept of population and a random slope for the body YB ratio for each population as well as the initial model provided the best fit (all P < 0.01) but the two models were not significantly better when compared with each other (AICinitial model = -591.19, AICrandom intercept of population and random slope of body B/Y ratio model = -592.20, P = 0.32). Therefore, we picked the simpler model (i.e., the model including a random intercept of population and a random slope of the body YB ratio) for the final analysis.

Using the model described above, we found evidence for sex-specific differences in the relationship between the head YB ratio and the body YB ratio that are likely a consequence of sex-specific differences in body shape (for more details see the results). Therefore, in accordance with our preregistration, we used the head YB ratio to study the role of environmental variables on fire salamander yellow-to-black ratio. The model initially included all six environmental variables (i.e., elevation above sea level, average temperature, change in temperature, NDVI, latitude and longitude) that were centered around their means [66] as well as sex and the interactions of each environmental variable with sex. Additionally, elevation was divided by 100 to transform it to a similar scale as the other environmental variables. We included all environmental variables in one model to reduce the number of tests. Furthermore, the environmental variables were not strongly correlated (tested using Spearman’s correlation tests in ggpairs in the GGally package [67], all Spearman correlation coefficients ρ < 0.690, Supplementary Fig. 2) and there were no issues of collinearity (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The model also included a random intercept of population and a random slope of sex by population. We applied a step-wise model reduction using the function step provided in the R-package lmerTest [65] to find the most suitable model. We used the function emtrends provided in the R-package emmeans [68] to test if the slopes for male YB ratios differed significantly from zero if the model summary indicated a significant interaction of sex with a specific environmental variable.

Results

Sexual dichromatism

Male fire salamanders had both, a higher body YB ratio as well as a higher head YB ratio (i.e., higher proportion of yellow on the entire back and on the head only) than females (Fig. 4; Table 2). Head YB ratio and body YB ratio were highly positively associated (Fig. 5; Table 3). However, the slopes differed between the sexes, with males showing a greater increase than females (Fig. 5; Table 3, see Supplementary Fig. 3 for regression plots by sex and population). Overall, fire salamanders of both sexes had a higher head YB ratio than body YB ratio (i.e., higher proportion of yellow on the head than on the body, Tables 1 and 3, see Supplementary Fig. 3 for population differences). For further analyses, we continue with the head YB ratio.

Environmental variables associated with head YB ratio

The final model investigating environmental associations with head YB ratio retained the fixed effects of sex, change in temperature, NDVI, latitude, longitude. The model also included the interactions of sex with change in temperature, NDVI, and longitude (Table 4). Discussion

Widespread sexual dichromatism in fire salamanders in Germany

Using a semi-automated analysis of photos of fire salamanders across Germany taken by scientists and citizen scientists, we show that males have a higher proportion of yellow on their entire dorsal surface as well as on their head only compared to females. While body YB ratio and head YB ratio were strongly positively correlated, this relationship was stronger (i.e., a steeper slope) in males than in females. This indicates that a female with a high head YB ratio (i.e., high proportion of yellow on her head) has a lower body YB ratio (i.e., lower proportion of yellow on her body) than a male fire salamander with an equal head YB ratio (Fig. 5). Most fire salamanders in Germany have only very little yellow coloration on their sides, and females usually have a wider body shape than males (due to pregnancy [41, 42]). Therefore, the more stretched out bodies of females make the sides appear more visible from above, reducing the proportion of yellow when viewed from above. This sexual dimorphism in body shape likely influenced the sexual dichromatism in body coloration observed in this study (Table 2) and in previous studies [5, 6]. The head coloration is less affected by changes in body shape due to pregnancy or feeding status and therefore, comparing the head coloration between sexes is less biased. Since the yellow proportion on the head is also higher in males than in females, however, the difference in body shape can only partly explain the observed sexual dichromatism. Nevertheless, we advise caution for researchers aiming to study sex-specific differences in dorsal coloration (specifically: of the abdomen) of fire salamanders in the future as differences in body shape will likely need to be accounted for when comparing females with males.

Our finding of a sexual dichromatism, with males being more yellow than females, is in line with previous studies in Germany [6] and Slovakia [5]; in which, however, fewer populations were tested (three and six populations, respectively). In contrast, two studies investigating sexual dichromatism in fire salamanders in northern Spain and northern Italy, respectively, did not find differences in coloration between males and females [44, 46]. Therefore, our study, which used data from 20 locations, spanning the largest area of the natural distribution of fire salamanders so far, provides evidence of widespread (i.e., occurring in multiple populations in Germany but with varying strength) sexual dichromatism in this species. One potential cause of this sexual dichromatism is sexual selection driven by mate choice, whereby female fire salamanders prefer to mate with yellower males. Fire salamanders are capable of discriminating yellow from other colors even under low light conditions [69]. Therefore, color-based mate choice is theoretically possible in this species. When raised under rich food conditions as larvae, postmetamorphic fire salamanders have a higher proportion of yellow than individuals raised under poor food conditions as larvae, indicating that yellow coloration is likely energetically costly for fire salamanders [40]. However, this difference in yellow proportion disappeared with time when the postmetamorphic fire salamanders were maintained under identical nutritional conditions, which could indicate that the maintenance of the pigments responsible for yellow coloration is also costly [40]. Therefore, the proportion of yellow coloration in fire salamanders could act as a signal of a potential mate’s quality, especially when considering that more yellow coloration is more effective at deterring predators [40]. However, it is important to note that more yellow coloration does not indicate higher levels of toxicity in fire salamanders [6, 7, 44, 50]. In the spadefoot toad Scaphiopus couchii, larger males and males with a better body condition have a more dissimilar body coloration to females [70]. Furthermore, clay models that mimic these males in coloration were also preferred by females over clay models of males that resembled females in color and dorsal pattern [70]. Similarly, natterjack toads (Epidalea calamita) are sexually dichromatic, and their coloration is correlated with age, body size, hindlimb length, and sprint speed [71]. Although no correlation between color and toxicity has been detected in fire salamanders [6, 7, 44, 50], other factors, such as body condition, age or performance capacity, might be identifiable for females via coloration [72]. Male fire salamanders have been reported to prefer sites that are linked to female activity [73] and are frequently observed in an upright position referred to as a “presenter pose” [69, 74]. This pose could provide females with the opportunity to examine a male’s coloration and body condition and could therefore play an important role in visual communication during mate choice and is likely not related to anti-predator behavior in contrast to the “unken reflex” [74]. Future studies could either directly (e.g [70]), or indirectly (e.g [75]), investigate the role of fire salamander coloration in mate choice and whether other traits correlate with a fire salamander’s coloration, which would indicate whether color acts as a quality signal. Furthermore, the body parts visible for a female should be further investigated (i.e., “taking the perspective” of a female fire salamander).

Alternatively, this sexual dichromatism could be related to differential selection pressure (e.g., by predators) for both sexes in fire salamanders. As described above, male fire salamanders are often seen in “presenter pose” with their bodies raised and their forelimbs extended, staying motionless for up to several minutes [69, 74]. This behavior is believed to play a role in mate attraction [74] as the dorsal pattern is more visible for conspecifics when the body of the male is raised, but it could also expose the male to a greater threat of predation (by e.g., Natrix natrix snakes and birds of the order Strigiformes; [41, 46]). Furthermore, males are more often recaptured than females during regular population monitorings in one of the study sites (Sean Grond, Masterthesis), which could indicate higher surface activity of males - another reason why males could be exposed to a greater threat of predation [76]. Similarly, the development of fire salamander larvae in a female’s body might necessitate greater metabolic activity and therefore, the need for higher temperatures, which could be supported by a greater proportion of black in females, i.e., “sex-dependent thermal melanism”. For example, Fedorka et al. [77] reported that males of the ground cricket Allonemobius socius had darker cuticles than females did, which could be the result of additional sex-specific selection due to temperature-dependent display behavior.

Differences in the yellow proportion of body and head

An interesting result from our investigations is that fire salamanders have higher proportions of yellow on their head than on their overall dorsal surface. Caspers et al. (2020) used models of fire salamanders with varying proportions of yellow to show that models with higher proportions of yellow received fewer attacks from potential predators [40]. Since the head of a fire salamander is an irreplaceable body part necessary for the survival of an individual, protecting this body part might prove especially important compared with, e.g., the tail (fire salamanders with missing tails are frequently observed (Mühlenhaupt, personal observations)). Using coloration to focus or divert predator attacks on specific body parts is a well-known strategy in the animal kingdom [11]. For example, Watson et al. [78] used clay models of lizards with three sections—head, body and tail—that were either black or blue and reported that models with blue sections received the majority of attacks by birds on their blue sections rather than on the black sections. This is especially interesting given that lizards of multiple families have evolved blue tails that can be autotomized [79]. Thereby, attacks could be guided toward the body part that can be sacrificed (i.e., is nonvital). In fire salamanders, yellow coloration could act in the opposite way and divert predator attacks from the head as the overall proportion of yellow has already been shown to reduce predator attacks on fire salamander models [40]. Furthermore, fire salamanders have prominent toxin glands, called parotoid glands, on the sides of their heads that are in almost every case at least partly yellow [41, 42], and the yellow coloration highlights the dark glandular pores. Advertising these toxin glands might be an effective strategy for avoiding predator attacks as fire salamanders have been reported to engage in a defensive posture by lowering their heads and exposing their parotoid glands towards a predator [41, 48]. Similarly, in natterjack toads (E. calamita) that also have parotoid glands, plasticine models with larger and more conspicuously colored parotoid glands received fewer predator attacks [80].

The head coloration might also play an especially important role in mate choice of fire salamanders. For example, Ancillotto et al. [81] showed that males of the two species of Salamandrina express more conspicuous gular coloration than females. When females are nearby, this coloration is displayed in a stereotyped throat hyperextension while being in a similar “presenter pose” to fire salamanders. Therefore, the head coloration of fire salamanders might play a similarly important role in mate choice. Especially the gular coloration of fire salamanders merits further investigation given that the throat is especially visible during the “presenter pose” (see also [69]).

The influence of environmental factors on fire salamander coloration

Several other studies have already attempted to find environmental relationships with the yellow proportion in fire salamander coloration. For example, Burgon et al. (2020) found no relationship between elevation and the frequency of color morphs that vary in yellow proportion and value in S. s. bernadezi in northern Spain [44]. Similarly, Barzaghi et al. (2022) found no correlation between the proportion of yellow with NDVI, elevation or predator richness in fire salamanders of northern Italy that likely represent a hybrid of S. s. salamandra and S. s. gigliolii [42, 46]. In our study, we tested for environmental relationships with yellow proportion in fire salamanders from the widest area of fire salamander distribution studied so far and with individuals belonging to both the S. s. terrestris lineage (in western Germany) and the hybrid lineage of S. s. salamandra and S. s. terrestris (in eastern Germany) [42, 82]. We found evidence for variation in the yellow proportion of fire salamanders along their north-to-south distribution in Germany. Given that in our study, fire salamander populations from northern Germany to southwestern Germany were included, previous studies might have failed to find similar clines due to a smaller sampling area. Interestingly, the south-north cline in yellow proportion can be explained by variation due to underlying environmental factors such as variation in temperature or habitat productivity (i.e., NDVI), as these variables were not significantly associated with the head YB ratios. Possible causes of this variation could be differences in yellow proportion between genetic lineages across Germany or simply genetic drift. For example, it is well known that S. s. salamandra and S. s. terrestris and their hybrids differ in dorsal color pattern [42, 43, 82] as S. s. salamandra is commonly associated with a spotted dorsal pattern whereas S. s. terrestris is associated with a striped pattern. It is very likely that the color patterns also affect the yellow proportion (e.g., a striped pattern will result in a higher proportion of yellow compared to a spotted pattern, Mühlenhaupt et al., unpublished data). However, these differences between lineages do not ultimately explain the variation in yellow proportion. A potential explanation for this variation could be differences in mate choice behavior between the lineages. Clearly, further research spanning larger geographic areas and including more lineages of the widely distributed and highly variable fire salamander is needed. This research could also indicate whether the widespread sexual dichromatism that we have identified in fire salamanders in Germany is also present in other lineages outside Germany (e.g., in the fire salamander diversity hotspot on the Iberian peninsula [42]). Integrating citizen science approaches and even using data available through apps such as iNaturalist or social media can make such a task feasible [8].

Although the putative role of thermal melanism in fire salamanders has been suggested multiple times [42, 50, 51], neither average temperature nor temperature change showed significant associations with the yellow proportion of the fire salamanders studied here. While comparing the fully black alpine salamander (Salamandra atra) with the European fire salamander might lead to this conclusion as alpine salamanders inhabit higher elevated habitats with lower average temperatures [42], the intraspecific variation in yellow proportion of fire salamanders could not be explained through differences or changes in temperature in this or former studies [44, 46]. While we were able to cover a span of 3 °C in average temperature (Table 1), we were still far from covering the full extent of climatic variation that this species encounters in its wide range across Europe. Furthermore, other mechanisms such as behavioral adaptations could play a more important role in thermal regulation of fire salamanders. For example, fire salamanders in Mediterranean areas such as parts of the Iberian peninsula show drastically different activity patterns (i.e., activity during winter) compared to fire salamanders in temperate climates (i.e., hibernation during winter) [41, 42]. Furthermore, in some regions fire salamanders show diurnal activity whereas in other areas, they are almost exclusively nocturnal [41, 83]. Therefore, future studies could also consider differences in activity patterns across large geographic areas.

We have included temperature change as there was observed variation in how much the average temperatures have increased in our study locations and as from other studies it is clear that global warming will have a strong impact on the coloration of ectotherms [35, 37, 38, 84]. However, in our study we did not find evidence for an effect of warming on the yellow proportion of fire salamanders. Again, behavioral adaptations such as shifts in phenology (e.g., increased importance of winter and decreased importance of summer for fire salamanders in Germany) might mitigate the changes in temperature as for other sympatric amphibian species shifts in phenology with milder winters have already been shown [85, 86]. An alternative approach to the one we have used here would be to compare the coloration of fire salamanders, preferably from multiple sites, across time (e.g., by using photos and/or specimen from biological collections) to correlate the change in temperature over a span of several decades with the proportion of yellow.

Further alternative explanations of the variation in yellow proportion should also be considered. In other aposematic amphibians such as poison dart frogs (Dendrobatidae), a role of coloration in camouflage has already been shown [87, 88]. Locality-dependent differences in background could be associated with differences in coloration of fire salamanders in these locations. While the “greenness” of the habitat (i.e., NDVI) did not show an association with the yellow proportion, other features of the habitat could reduce the detectability of specific color morphs. A possible role of fire salamander coloration in crypsis has already been proposed [48, 89] but remains to be thoroughly tested.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence for a widespread sexual dichromatism of fire salamanders in Germany through the use of citizen science data. Furthermore, we find evidence for variation in the coloration of the head and the body indicating as of yet underappreciated involvement in different biological functions. Future studies investigating differences in dorsal coloration of fire salamanders should consider differences in body shape between females and males as these will likely affect the dorsal proportion of yellow. The increase in yellow proportion with latitude that we have found could be the result of differences between lineages of fire salamanders in Germany or genetic drift but the underlying mechanisms are yet to be uncovered. Clearly, more large-scale comparisons, integrating multiple lineages across diverse habitats of fire salamanders, are necessary to better understand the drivers of inter- and intrapopulation as well as intraindividual variation in fire salamander coloration. Continued implementation of citizen science approaches will be extremely helpful for facilitating those types of extensive studies in the future.
