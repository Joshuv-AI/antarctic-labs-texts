Reply
Authors: Hendrianus Hendrianus, Jun Hwan Cho, Hoyoun Won, Young-Hoon Jeong, Sang-Wook Kim
Published: 2026

We thank Dr Useini for the thoughtful commentary and for further advancing the discussion on treatment selection for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). As TAVR continues to expand into younger and lower-risk populations, optimizing patient selection has become increasingly important.

Age and surgical risk remain the foundation of contemporary decision-making and provide a robust framework for identifying appropriate candidates for intervention.1 We share Dr Useini's view that these factors alone cannot fully capture the complexity of treatment selection. However, rather than viewing current risk models as inadequate, we believe they provide an essential foundation for individualized decision-making. Contemporary surgical risk scores were developed to estimate operative risk rather than the comparative benefit of TAVR vs SAVR. Consequently, while they offer valuable prognostic information, they may be insufficient in isolation to identify which patients are most likely to derive greater long-term benefit from one treatment strategy over the other. Future treatment selection should therefore build upon, rather than replace, established risk assessment by integrating additional patient characteristics that influence both procedural and long-term outcomes.

Our findings complement this evolving perspective. Rather than identifying a universally superior treatment strategy, we observed that the relative effectiveness of TAVR and SAVR varied according to sex, baseline surgical risk, and follow-up duration. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as supporting a treatment strategy based on any single characteristic. Instead, they underscore the heterogeneity of treatment effects and suggest that the relative benefits of TAVR and SAVR differ across clinically relevant patient subgroups.2

The growing emphasis on longer-term outcomes further broadens this discussion. While recent evidence has raised important questions regarding the durability of TAVR benefits and suggested potential late advantages with SAVR in some populations,3,4 these findings should be interpreted within the context of evolving valve technologies, differences in patient selection, valve platform, procedural era, and duration of follow-up. At the same time, the less-invasive nature of TAVR—including minimalist transfemoral implantation under local anesthesia5—and its favorable early recovery profile remain important advantages. As life expectancy after valve intervention continues to increase, balancing these early procedural benefits with lifetime management considerations will become increasingly important.

Building on this discussion, we propose a conceptual framework for precision aortic valve therapy (Figure 1). Rather than redefining current practice, this framework illustrates how contemporary decision-making may evolve from a predominantly age-based and surgical risk–based paradigm toward a multidimensional assessment incorporating sex, frailty, anatomical complexity, comorbidity burden, life expectancy, valve durability, and patient preferences. We believe this approach complements multidisciplinary heart team assessment and reflects the growing recognition that the relative benefits of TAVR and SAVR vary across patient subgroups and over time.

Toward Precision Aortic Valve Therapy

Proposed conceptual framework illustrating the evolution of treatment selection from a traditional age-based and surgical risk–based approach to a multidimensional strategy integrating patient-specific characteristics. As evidence continues to evolve, the relative benefits of TAVR and SAVR appear to vary across patient subgroups and over time, supporting individualized treatment selection. SAVR = surgical aortic valve replacement; TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Ultimately, advancing precision aortic valve therapy will require validated patient-level prediction models that integrate clinical, anatomical, procedural, and longitudinal data to estimate individualized treatment benefit. Such models could complement existing risk assessment and refine patient selection beyond population-based recommendations, supporting more personalized treatment decisions between TAVR and SAVR.
