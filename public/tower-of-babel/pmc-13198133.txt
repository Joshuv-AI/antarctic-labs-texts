Acute ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction Secondary to Neoplastic Infiltration of the Left Anterior Descending Artery
Authors: Andre M Nicolau, Lucas Oliveira, Filippe Filippini, Jose C Nicolau, Carlos M Campos, Roberto Rocha Giraldez, Vera Aiello, Fabio Sandoli de Brito, Alexandre Abizaid, Henrique B Ribeiro
Published: 2026

History of Presentation

A 69-year-old woman was transferred from a referring hospital with 8 days of new-onset Canadian Cardiovascular Society class III angina, associated with worsening chest pain over the preceding 16 hours. On admission, she was hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 132/76 mm Hg, heart rate of 86 beats/min, and oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. Cardiac auscultation revealed no murmurs, and lung examination was unremarkable. No interarm blood pressure difference was noted.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of breast cancer treated with surgery and chemotherapy in 2002. Additional comorbidities included former tobacco use and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease without evidence of acute exacerbation at presentation (no wheezing or increased work of breathing).

Investigation

Given acute chest pain and cardiovascular risk factors, acute coronary syndrome was the leading diagnosis. The initial 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated significant convex ST-segment elevation in the anterior leads (V1-V6), consistent with anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (Figure 1).

Electrocardiogram Showing ST-Segment Elevation in the Anterior Leads

Initial 12-lead electrocardiogram shows significant convex ST-segment elevation in the anterior (V1-V6) leads, consistent with anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

The patient was transferred emergently for primary percutaneous coronary intervention. Coronary angiography revealed approximately 90% stenosis of the distal third of the lateral anterior descending coronary artery (LAD), characterized by long, smooth narrowing, with involvement of diagonal branches and without evidence of diffuse atherosclerotic disease (Figure 2, Video 1). Given the angiographic appearance, alternative mechanisms were considered, including spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD). The findings were most consistent with type 2B SCAD. Markedly reduced anterior wall motion was noted angiographically. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40% with anterior wall akinesia.

Coronary Angiography

Coronary angiography caudal and cranial (B) views show a long distal segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery diffusely narrowed (white arrows), with involvement of diagonal branches (black arrows).

Management

After careful assessment of hemodynamic stability, the suspected diagnosis of SCAD, and the fact that involvement was limited to the distal LAD and small branches, a conservative medical management strategy was selected. Although intracoronary imaging could have provided additional diagnostic clarity, it was deferred due to the suspected diagnosis of SCAD and the associated risk of iatrogenic vessel injury. To further clarify the underlying mechanism, computed tomography angiography was performed and demonstrated a large anterior mediastinal mass infiltrating the anterior wall of the left ventricle, the LAD, 2 diagonal branches, and adjacent costal arches (Figure 3).

(A and B) Computed tomography angiography shows neoplastic invasion of the anterior wall of the left ventricle (white arrows in A) and left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD), 2 diagonal branches, and costal arches.

Histopathologic analysis from a transthoracic biopsy revealed squamous cell carcinoma of unknown primary origin. Following multidisciplinary discussions with an oncologist and the patient's family, goals of care were transitioned to symptom-directed palliative management. The patient died 3 months later.

Discussion

ST-segment elevation on a 12-lead electrocardiogram in a patient presenting with acute chest pain is a finding that typically mandates immediate evaluation for reperfusion therapy, as the most common underlying mechanism is acute coronary occlusion due to atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion. Prompt recognition of ST-segment elevation and electrocardiographic patterns suggestive of acute coronary occlusion is essential to enable rapid diagnosis and timely activation of the catheterization laboratory.1 In this case, the patient presented with cardiovascular risk factors, acute chest pain, and anterior ST-segment elevation, appropriately prompting emergent coronary angiography. However, the angiographic findings—long, smooth narrowing of the distal LAD without diffuse atherosclerotic disease—were atypical for plaque rupture and instead suggested a nonatherothrombotic mechanism.2

SCAD manifests as STEMI in approximately 20% to 50% of cases, and its recognition is critical because management strategies differ substantially from those used in atherosclerotic myocardial infarction. Although intracoronary imaging may aid diagnosis, it carries procedural risks, particularly in vessels that may be dissected. Accordingly, coronary computed tomography angiography was selected as a noninvasive alternative.3

Unexpectedly, coronary computed tomography angiography revealed an anterior mediastinal malignancy infiltrating the left ventricular anterior wall and epicardial coronary arteries, providing a unifying explanation for the electrocardiographic findings, regional wall motion abnormalities, and the apparent SCAD-like angiographic narrowing. Given the patient's oncologic history, concern arose for malignancy recurrence vs a new primary tumor.

A transthoracic biopsy was performed and revealed squamous cell carcinoma of unknown primary origin. Following multidisciplinary discussions including an oncologist and the patient's family, care was redirected toward symptom-directed (palliative) management, and the patient died 3 months later.

Patients with cancer face an increased risk of coronary artery disease, intensified by a proinflammatory and prothrombotic state induced by the cancer and the cardiovascular toxicity of cancer therapy, in addition to traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Moreover, thoracic malignancies may compromise coronary flow through extrinsic compression or direct invasion. Both primary and metastatic tumors and both cardiac and extracardiac masses may compress on any coronary artery. Small epicardial branches have been more frequently involved, although left main and proximal left anterior descending artery involvement has been reported. Patients may present with both STEMI and non-STEMI and may be found with both angiographically significant and nonsignificant stenoses.4,5

This case underscores the value of considering alternative mechanisms of myocardial infarction even in the setting of typical ST-segment elevation and the importance of multimodality imaging in ST-segment elevation, as cross-sectional techniques may identify extrinsic or infiltrative causes that are not evident on coronary angiography. Infiltrative malignancy is a rare but important cause of STEMI, and multimodality imaging is key when acute coronary syndrome features are atypical or discordant with angiography.

Conclusions

Although acute ST-segment elevation classically indicates atherothrombotic coronary occlusion and mandates urgent reperfusion evaluation, rare alternative mechanisms exist. This case illustrates STEMI secondary to direct malignant invasion of the LAD by an anterior mediastinal squamous cell carcinoma, producing a classic pattern on the electrocardiogram. Early use of multimodality imaging can clarify discordant clinical, electrocardiographic, and angiographic findings and uncover nonatherosclerotic etiologies, enabling appropriate management and prognostication.
