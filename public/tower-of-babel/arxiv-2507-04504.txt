Unveiling the Potential of Diffusion Large Language Model in Controllable Generation
Zhen Xiong, Yujun Cai, Zhecheng Li, Yiwei Wang

Abstract
Diffusion models, originally developed for image generation, have emerged as a promising alternative to autoregressive large language models (LLMs). We present a theoretical analysis comparing autoregressive and masked diffusion LLMs, revealing that the intrinsic bidirectional attention mechanism of diffusion LLMs (dLLMs) enables superior context modeling and generation controllability. However, existing dLLM applications face significant challenges in controllable generation: the native multi-step denoising process exhibits high sensitivity to sequence length, elevated hallucination rates, and prohibitive inference costs without specialized optimizations. To address these limitations, we propose S elf-adaptive S chema S caffolding ( S 3 S^{3} ), a novel framework that enables dLLMs to generate structured outputs (e.g., JSON) while maintaining semantic fidelity and accelerating inference. Our approach injects the target schema structure into the output context, reducing unnecessary computation while improving controllability. Extensive experiments demonstrate that S 3 S^{3} achieves substantial improvements: 65% increase in structural adherence, 48% enhancement in content fidelity, and 17% reduction in hallucination rates compared to baseline. These results establish both theoretical foundations and practical pathways for deploying diffusion models in controllable text generation tasks. Code and data will be publicly released.

Introduction

Diffusion-based large language models (dLLMs) offer an alternative to traditional autoregressive (AR) generation by iteratively denoising corrupted inputs, enabling bidirectional context modeling and parallel token generation
(
Arriola et al. 2025
;
Nie et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
. Recent studies show that dLLMs can match or outperform AR models in instruction following, in-context learning, and reasoning tasks
(
Gong et al. 2024
;
You et al. 2025
)
, while also offering enhanced controllability and faster inference
(
Huang & Tang 2025
;
Labs et al. 2025
)
. Architectures such as LLaDA and DiffuLLaMA demonstrate that diffusion-based decoding scales well with model size and can be trained from scratch or adapted from AR LLMs
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Gong et al. 2024
)
. Meanwhile, hybrid models like CtrlDiff and commercial releases such as Mercury Coder push diffusion LLMs closer to practical deployment, achieving state-of-the-art speed–quality trade-offs
(
Huang & Tang 2025
;
Labs et al. 2025
)
. Multimodal extensions like LLaDA-V further confirm the flexibility of the diffusion paradigm
(
You et al. 2025
)
.

Structured output generation is a fundamental task in natural language processing where models must produce responses that adhere to predefined formats, schemas, or constraints rather than free-form text.
Unlike conventional text generation that prioritizes fluency and coherence, structured output generation requires models to satisfy both linguistic quality and structural validity simultaneously.
Structured output generation is critical for numerous real-world applications where reliability and interoperability are paramount. For examples, in enterprise systems, APIs require responses in specific JSON formats to enable seamless integration between services. Data extraction applications need outputs formatted as structured records for downstream processing. Educational platforms require mathematical expressions in standardized notation for automated evaluation.
Overall, Failure to maintain structural integrity can result in system failures, data corruption, or costly manual intervention.

However, structured output generation presents unique challenges that distinguish it from general text generation. Beyond predicting next-token to maintain a semantical coherence, models must simultaneously preserve global structural integrity throughout the generation process, for example, ensuring that opening brackets have corresponding closures, required fields are populated, and data types conform to specifications.
To address these challenges, researchers tried to integrate grammar-based Finite State Automata (FSA) system together with constrained decoding strategies to enforce LLM to enforce structural compliance during generation
(
Koo et al. 2024
)
.
However, the inherent stochasticity of LLMs can lead to dead-end states where no valid continuations exist, necessitating remedial techniques such as best-of-N sampling or backtracking mechanisms.
In parallel, leveraging the enhanced instruction-following capabilities of modern LLMs, practitioners have developed sophisticated prompt engineering techniques to guide models toward structurally compliant outputs.
Despite these advances, manual prompt crafting for diverse structural specifications remains labor-intensive and yields inconsistent results across different domains and complexity levels.
While prompt engineering serves as a practical interim solution, it lacks the systematic reliability required for production deployment scenarios.

To better understand these challenges, we analyze the language models underlying most existing approaches and identify a fundamental limitation that may impede effective structured generation. Since autoregressive LLMs are trained using next-token prediction, they cannot anticipate future tokens as generation prior or revise previously generated content. While this sequential generation paradigm suffices for free-form question answering and open-ended dialogue, it presents significant constraints for structured output tasks.
Our key insight is that autoregressive models cannot effectively leverage structural specifications as informative priors to guide the generation process. The sequential nature of autoregressive decoding prevents models from globally optimizing for structural coherence, often leading to locally valid but globally inconsistent outputs. This limitation motivates our exploration of non-autoregressive alternatives, specifically diffusion-based large language models (dLLMs).
Unlike their autoregressive counterparts, dLLMs employ iterative denoising processes that enable global context awareness and multi-step refinement. Our preliminary experiments demonstrate that dLLMs can achieve high-quality structured outputs with significantly fewer generation steps, substantially reducing inference latency while improving structural compliance. This efficiency gain, combined with their ability to incorporate structural constraints throughout the generation process, positions dLLMs as a promising solution for reliable structured output generation.

We introduce a novel training-free schema scaffolding framework and its self-adaptive variant (S3) to address the limitations of existing approaches. Our comprehensive evaluation protocol demonstrates that these methods yield significant gains in structural adherence, content fidelity, and faithfulness. Together, our work establishes both a rigorous foundation and practical solutions for deploying diffusion LLMs in structured generation tasks.

Autoregressive Large Language Models

Autoregressive (AR) Large Language Models (LLMs) have seen rapid progress starting from 2023, exemplified by OpenAI GPT-series models

;
OpenAI et al. 2024
)
, Anthropic’s Claude

Architectural improvements such as extended context windows

, madality integration

(

Gemini Team, Google 2025

These advances have driven state-of-the-art performance on broad benchmarks (e.g., MMLU

Zhou et al. 2023

and AIME
(

), yet even the strongest LLMs still grapple with well-known limitations such as hallucinated facts, exposure difficulties in controllability.
These persistent weaknesses underscore the need for alternative generative paradigms and improved control mechanisms beyond the conventional autoregressive approach.

Diffusion-based Language Models

Diffusion-based generative models have been extended to discrete language domains as a strong alternative to standard autoregressive text generation.
(
Li et al. 2023
)

Key algorithmic advances include discrete score-based diffusion processes that adapt continuous noising token sequences, improved noise schedules for text, and faster sampling methods that substantially reduce the number of denoising steps.
Several new diffusion-driven de-masking language models have been introduced, LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
and Dream
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
have demonstrated the promising potential of large diffusion language model and its strong instruction following capabilities.
Nonetheless, these diffusion-based large models often require multi-step denoising steps to ensure high-quality output, which marginally weakened their innate parallel generation advantage and decreased their overall inference speed.
(
Israel et al. 2025
)

In this work, we focus on a specific task, structured output, that the diffusion-based language model can better utilize its architectural advantages and propose a new inference pipeline to speed up the inference speed to maximize its utility in real-world applications.

Structured Output

Generating formally structured outputs (e.g., code, JSON, XML, or tables) has prompted specialized autoregessive methods to ensure well-formedness and accuracy.
One approach is grammar-constrained decoding, which guides generation using a predefined formal grammar or schema so that only syntactically valid strings (for instance, JSON conforming to a schema or code respecting a language grammar) can be produced. These deocding algorithms prune or adjust the model’s next-token probabilities to enforce compliance with context-free grammars
(
Geng et al. 2023
)
or type systems
(
Mündler et al. 2025
)
, without requiring task-specific fine-tuning.
Another line of work structures the generation process itself: planning-based or two-staged strategies first predict an intermediate representation (such as an abstract syntax tree or logical form) and then convert it into the final output, helping maintain the required format and semantics.
(
Wang et al. 2025
)

However, most of the previous works largely rely on autoregressive LLMs for their strong language modeling capabilities and outstanding instruction following ability as few-shot learners
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
.
Noticing that diffusion-based language models are weakly explored in this area, in this paper, we not only analyzed the potential of the non-autoregressive language model’s capability of structured output and further proposed a systematic evaluation system to quantify the performance the generation quality of structured output spanning from structure compliance, content fidelity, and hallucination rate.

3
Preliminary and Notations

3.1
Auto-regressive Language Modeling

The fundamental objective of modern autoregressive large language models (LLMs) during pre-training is next-token prediction over large-scale text corpora.
During inference, instruction-tuned
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2022
)
LLMs generate responses
A
to user queries
Q
by sampling from the learned conditional distribution. The generation probability can be decomposed as:

\log P_{\theta}(A|Q)=\sum_{t=1}^{|A|}\log P_{\theta}(a_{t}|a_{<t},Q)

where
A=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{|A|})
represents the generated response sequence,
a_{t}
denotes the token at position
t
,
a_{<t}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1})
represents all preceding tokens,
|A|
is the length of the response sequence, and
\theta
denotes the learned parameters.

Due to the sequential, specifically left-to-right nature of autoregressive generation, inferencing requires
O(|A|)
forward passes, which imposes two fundamental limitations: models cannot access future token information during generation, and previously generated tokens cannot be revised. These constraints may potentially limit the performance on complex reasoning tasks that would benefit from lookahead/lookaround planning or iterative refinement.

3.2
Diffusion Language Modeling

Diffusion models
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Nichol & Dhariwal 2021
)
, originally developed for continuous image generation tasks, have recently been adapted to discrete language modeling
(
Austin et al. 2021
;
Nie et al. 2025
;
Ye et al. 2025
)
. Diffusion-based language models consist of two key processes: a forward noising process and a reverse denoising process.

Given a tokenized sequence
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, the forward process progressively corrupts the sequence by masking tokens, producing increasingly noisy sequences
\mathbf{x}_{t}
for
t\in[0,1]
. As
t
approaches
1
, more tokens are masked until
x_{1}
becomes a fully masked sequence. The reverse process trains a neural network to predict the original tokens at masked position within
x_{t}
for
t\in(0,1]
. The learning objective can be formulated as:

\min_{\phi}-\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{0},x_{t}}\left[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{M}]\log p_{\phi}(x_{0}^{i}|x_{t})\right]

where
\mathbf{M}
denotes the mask token,
L
is the sequence length, and
\phi
represents the model parameters. We refer to this class of diffusion-based language models as Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) throughout this paper.

MDMs can be instruction-tuned by concatenating (
\oplus
) user instruction
Q
as fixed prefix to the masked target sequence
\mathbf{A}_{t}
(
t=1
, all masked initially), enabling conditioned generation. During inference, the model denoises masked tokens in
A_{t}
:

\log P_{\phi}(Q\oplus A)=\sum_{i=1}^{|A|}\mathbf{1}[a_{i}=\mathbf{M}]\log P_{\phi}(a_{i}|Q\oplus A_{t})

Compared with AR models, the multi-step generation process can potentially be extended for iterative token edition and refinement
(
Havasi et al. 2025
)
. More interestingly, the directionaly attention capability of MDMs can largely improve its global context awareness and even
future
token planning. In this paper, we exploit MDM’s superior global awareness and explore its potential for structured output.

4
Methods

Despite the theoretical advantages of MDMs, particularly their global attention mechanism that enables comprehensive context understanding and coherent structural content generation, achieving high-quality structured outputs from instruction-tuned MDMs remains non-trivial.
Our preliminary experiments reveal that direct prompting approaches yield suboptimal results, indicating the need for more sophisticated methodologies.
In this section, we first establish a rigorous theoretical framework for structured output generation (Section
4.1
) that provides the foundation for our approach.
Subsequently, we introduce our training-free pipeline,
schema scaffolding
(Section
4.2
), which significantly enhances structured output quality across multiple evaluation dimensions while reducing computational overhead during inference with MDMs.

4.1
Task Formalization

Formally, we define structured output generation task as follows: given a user query
Q
, and a structural specification
S
, the objective is to generate a response
A
that satisfies both the semantic requirements of
Q
and the structural constraints defined by
S
.
The structural specification
S
can take various forms, including but not limited to: schema-based structures, format constraints, compositional structures, and domain-specific formats.
The task can be mathematically formulated as a constrained optimization problem:

A^{*}=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}(S)}P_{\text{LM}}(A|Q,S)

where
\mathcal{A}(S)
represents the space of all valid outputs conforming to structure
S
and
P_{\text{LM}}
is a conditional langugage model.
In practice, searching entire
\mathcal{A}(S)
is intractable due to the complexity of the token space.

4.2
Schema Scaffolding

A key advantage of MDMs over autoregressive models lies in their generation mechanism: rather than conditioning on previously generated tokens, MDMs condition on the entire sequence context, enabling them to anticipate future token dependencies.
Building on this insight, we propose
schema scaffolding
, a training-free approach that explicitly incorporates structural constraints by pre-populating the generation context with structural templates.

The core idea of this approach is to transform unconstrained generation into a structured
fill-in-the-blank
task: In detail, our method operates by parsing the structural specification
S
to identify invariant structural elements (e.g., brackets, delimiters, field names) and replacing variable content positions with mask tokens
\mathbf{M}
.
This creates a structural scaffold
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}
that constrains the model’s generation space while preserving semantic flexibility.

Formally, we reformulate the structured generation objective as:

\displaystyle A^{*}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}(S)}P_{\text{LM}}(A|Q,S)

\displaystyle\approx\arg\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}\in\mathcal{SC}}P_{\phi}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}|Q)

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}\in\mathcal{SC}}\sum_{a_{i}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}}\mathbf{1}[a_{i}=\mathbf{M}]\log P_{\phi}(a_{i}|Q,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}})

Where
\mathcal{SC}\subset\mathcal{A}(S)
represents the constrained subspace of outputs sharing the structural template derived from
S
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}^{(\setminus i)}
denotes the scaffold with all non-masked tokens fixed except position
i
.

4.3
Self-Adaptive Schema Scaffolding

While the schema scaffolding strategy constrains MDM generation for improved structural compliance, it introduces another challenge:
how to determine the appropriate number of mask tokens for each variable content position?

Although we can build structured fill-in-the-blank templates in advance, predicting the required length for each variable field remains problematic without prior knowledge of the target content.

A naive approach involves allocating sufficient mask tokens to each variable position, expecting the model to dynamically utilize only the necessary subset.
However, our preliminary experiments show that MDMs exhibit sensitivity to sequence length, with longer scaffolds potentially altering generation behavior rather than enabling selective mask token usage.
This length sensitivity can lead to suboptimal outputs where the model either under-utilizes available positions or hallucinates content to fill unnecessary slots.

An alternative approach involves introducing specialized padding tokens through fine-tuning. While conceptually appealing, this strategy violates our training-free objective and risks introducing dataset-specific biases that may degrade model performance on unseen domains.

Nonetheless, the idea of using a special token as a placeholder enlightened us to propose an improved method that leverages the semantic token
null
as an out-of-vocabulary padding mechanism without requiring additional training. Our method guides MDMs to naturally adopt
null
tokens for representing absent or variable-length content.

Formally, we extend our scaffolding framework to incorporate adaptive length management:

\displaystyle A^{*}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}(S)}P_{\text{LM}}(A|Q,S)

\displaystyle\approx\arg\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}\in\mathcal{SC}}P_{\phi}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}|Q)

\displaystyle\approx\arg\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}\in\mathcal{SC}}\sum_{a_{i}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}}}\mathbf{1}[a_{i}=\mathbf{M}]\log P_{\phi}(a_{i}|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}Q^{+}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A_{s}})

where
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}Q^{+}}
represents our augmented prompt which guide the model to adopt
null
tokens to indicate absent values.

Empirically, this approach transforms the fixed-length scaffolding problem into an adaptive generation task where MDMs can naturally handle variable-length fields and missing values. Further experimental results demonstrate that Self-Adaptive Schema Scaffolding significantly improves overall structured output quality, particularly in scenarios involving optional fields or variable-length content.

As our approach is entirely training-free, no additional parameter updates or parameter-efficient fine-tuning procedures are applied.

5.2
Evaluation Framework

Given the unique characteristics of structured output generation, we argue that traditional accuracy-based metrics alone are inadequate for capturing the full spectrum of model performance. To address this, we propose a comprehensive evaluation framework that assesses outputs across three key dimensions:
Structural Adherence
,
Content Fidelity
, and
Faithfulness
. This multifaceted approach enables a more nuanced and reliable assessment of a model’s ability to generate coherent, informative, and trustworthy structured texts.

Structural Adherence
measures how well-generated outputs conform to the target schema. Specifically, we define:

Structure Validity (SV)
: the proportion of outputs that are syntactically valid and parse without errors, capturing basic structural correctness.

Field Completeness (FC)
: the percentage of required fields that are correctly populated, indicating whether the model includes all mandatory schema components.

Schema Compliance (SC)
: the strictest structural metric, measuring the proportion of outputs that fully adhere to the predefined schema, including correct data types, value constraints, and nested structures.

Content Fidelity
evaluates the semantic accuracy of information within structurally valid outputs. We consider:

Precision/Recall (FP/FR)
: precision and recall scores computed over individual field types, providing a detailed view of model behavior across content categories.

F1 Score (F1)
: the harmonic mean of field-level precision and recall, computed using both exact match and fuzzy match strategies to accommodate minor textual variations that preserve semantic meaning.

Faithfulness
assesses the degree to which generated content remains grounded in the source input, which is especially critical in extraction settings.

Hallucination Rate (HR)
: the proportion of output fields that include information not present in or not reasonably inferable from the source text, directly reflecting the model’s factual consistency.

Together, these metrics support systematic comparisons across models and highlight specific areas for targeted improvement in structured output generation.

5.3
Main Results

5.3.1
Structural Adherence

We begin by evaluating structural adherence using our proposed schema scaffolding methods compared to the baseline of direct prompting (Fig.
1
). Results show that the diffusion language model struggles to generate outputs that satisfy the structural constraints specified in the prompt. Even with 16 denoising steps, the baseline achieves less than 65% across all three structural metrics:
Schema Validity
,
Field Completeness
, and
Schema Compliance
.

In contrast, both the vanilla and self-adaptive schema scaffolding approaches enable the model to produce highly structured outputs that almost perfectly satisfy all structural requirements, even with as few as 8 denoising steps. Notably, performance saturates near-perfect levels as early as 16 steps.

This improvement is particularly meaningful because inference time in diffusion models scales linearly with the number of denoising steps. Our approach thus not only boosts structural adherence but also reduces generation latency. Since structural adherence directly reflects the lower bar practical usability of structured output generation systems, experimental results demonstrate that schema scaffolding significantly enhances the utility of diffusion-based language models for structured output.

Content Fidelity

We next evaluate content fidelity across different methods using field-level
Precision
,
Recall
, and

(Fig.

). Interestingly, increasing the number of denoising steps does not consistently lead to better content accuracy. In some cases, performance slightly degrades with more steps. Among all the methods, vanilla schema scaffolding consistently outperforms the baseline in recall and F1 score, indicating improved coverage of relevant content. However, its precision is notably lower than the baseline across all denoising settings. This suggests that while the model generates more fields, it may also hallucinate or over-generate tokens in an attempt to fill in remaining slots, especially when the content length varies significantly across examples.

In contrast, our self-adaptive schema scaffolding approach achieves the best performance across all three metrics. By allowing the model to adaptively insert a
null
token to pad surplus slots, this method effectively prevents over-generation and improves robustness. Despite its simplicity, this mechanism yields notable improvements in both precision and recall, thereby boosting overall content fidelity.

5.3.3
Faithfulness

Finally, we assess the faithfulness of model outputs by measuring the
Hallucination Rate
, defined as the proportion of fields containing content not grounded in the input source text (Fig.
2
). This metric is particularly critical for structured generation tasks where factual consistency is essential.

While schema scaffolding significantly improves structural adherence and content fidelity, it also leads to a higher hallucination rate compared to the baseline. We hypothesize that this is due to the diffusion model’s sensitivity to output length: when prompted with a rigid schema and fix-length content, the model tends to fabricate plausible-sounding but ungrounded tokens to fill all required slots.

In contrast, our improved self-adaptive schema scaffolding method maintains much lower hallucination rates across all denoising steps. By dynamically inserting
null
tokens as placeholders for missing or unsupported fields, the model is able to avoid over-generation and remain grounded in the source text. Empirically, our method improves the factual reliability of structured outputs without sacrificing structural or semantic quality.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we compare conventional autoregressive models with diffusion-based large language models (dLLMs) and identify the latter’s architectural advantage in bidirectional attention mechanisms for enhanced contextual understanding and comprehensive language modeling. However, current research and applications fail to fully exploit dLLMs’ potential. To address this gap, we first propose a novel schema scaffolding method and then an improvsed self-adaptive version (
S^{3}
) that guide dLLMs to adaptively generate fully controllable structured output. We also introduce a comprehensive multidimensional evaluation framework to assess controllability performance. Our experiments demonstrate that the proposed method achieves superior structural adherence, content fidelity, and reduced hallucination rates compared to direct prompting, effectively leveraging dLLMs’ inherent contextual understanding and language modeling capabilities for controllable generation.
