Association between changes in working status and frailty across different types of employment: a longitudinal study in Korea, the world’s most rapidly ageing nation
Authors: Su-Min Park, Ah Jung Ko, Min Jeong Joo, Eun-Cheol Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFrailty, a critical health indicator of ageing, poses significant public health challenges for individuals and society. This study analysed the longitudinal association between changes in employment status and frailty among middle-aged and older adults in Korea across different employment types. It also examined how employment transitions impact frailty components, particularly weakness, isolation and exhaustion.DesignA longitudinal study.SettingA nationwide community-based longitudinal study using data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging from 2006 to 2022.ParticipantsIndividuals aged 45 years or older residing in private households across Korea (excluding Jeju Island) enrolled in Wave 1 (2006). For consistency, only participants with at least four observations during the entire study period were retained. Those with fewer than four observations (n=48 228) were excluded, amounting to a final of 6122 participants, of whom 2718 were men and 3404 were women. Furthermore, for the subgroup analysis of employment types, participants not classified as regular, temporary, daily, self-employed or unpaid family workers were excluded.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was frailty assessed using the Korean Frailty Instrument. Secondary outcomes were the individual frailty components, including weakness, isolation and exhaustion.ResultsThe study included 6122 participants (2718 men and 3404 women). Frailty was observed in 3.9% of men and 8.5% of women. Returning to or maintaining employment was significantly associated with reduced frailty risk between both genders (male: Unemployed → Working OR 0.61, Working → Working OR 0.59; female: Unemployed → Working OR 0.68, Working → Working OR 0.77). Employment type-based analysis revealed that transitioning to permanent employment has the strongest protective effect on men (Unemployed → Permanent Employment OR 0.25).ConclusionsReturning to or maintaining employment was associated with a lower risk of frailty. Promoting stable employment environments and encouraging labour force participation among middle-aged and older adults may contribute to better health outcomes in ageing societies.

Background

Frailty is a key aspect of the ageing process and is associated with the natural ageing process in humans. It reflects an increased vulnerability to dramatic and sudden health changes triggered by factors such as infections, medications or environmental shifts.1 2 Although there is no universally agreed definition of frailty, it is commonly characterised as a state of diminished resilience to adverse health outcomes or external stressors. In the clinical context, frailty often alludes to older adults at the highest risk of adverse outcomes, including falls, disability, hospitalisation and the need for long-term care.

Frailty is not merely a natural consequence of ageing; it also poses significant public health challenges that extend beyond individual health and societal well-being.3 Frail older adults are at the highest risk of falls, disability and hospitalisation, leading to reduced quality of life, excessive healthcare utilisation and increased societal costs. Globally, approximately 11% of older adults are estimated to be frail, with relatively high prevalence rates in countries experiencing rapid ageing.4 By 2050, the global population aged 60 years or older is expected to nearly double to 2.1 billion, further exacerbating the impact of frailty.3 5 6 Despite this, many nations remain unprepared for the rapid growth of ageing populations. Frailty significantly affects physical, mental and social functioning, aligning with the WHO’s recognition of frailty as a critical threat to health and well-being.7 Beyond individual health, frailty places a substantial caregiving burden on families, increases physical and psychological stress in informal caregivers and imposes economic strain. At the societal level, frailty contributes to financial pressure on healthcare and social welfare systems, increases early mortality rates and reduces labour productivity.

Changes in employment status are recognised as important determinants of health outcomes.8 9 Previous studies have suggested that stable employment plays a protective role in maintaining physical and mental health.10 11 However, research directly examining the relationship between employment transition and frailty remains limited. While frailty reflects physical and psychological vulnerability, it is also closely linked to adverse health indicators, such as falls, disability, hospitalisation and increased mortality. Moreover, the relationship between employment transitions and frailty may differ depending on the employment type, such as regular employment, non-regular employment, self-employment and unpaid family work.12

In this study, we investigated the longitudinal association between changes in employment status and frailty across diverse employment types among middle-aged and older adults in Korea. Specifically, we examined employment transitions and their impact on frailty components, including weakness, isolation and exhaustion, with a focus on the effects of employment type.

Methods

Data

Data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Korean adults aged 45 years and older, spanning Wave 1 (2006) to Wave 9 (2022), was used. Participants were selected using a multistage, stratified probability sampling design based on enumeration districts from the 2005 Population and Housing Census. The baseline survey was conducted through computer-assisted personal interviewing, a standardised face-to-face interview method administered by trained interviewers and the response rate for Wave 1 was 70.7% (13). The KLoSA, initiated in 2006 by the Korea Labour Institute, is a large-scale research project designed to provide fundamental data on age-related policies and systems.

The KLoSA, initiated in 2006 by the Korea Labour Institute, is a large-scale research project designed to provide fundamental data on age-related policies and systems. The survey targeted individuals aged 45 years or older residing in private households across Korea (excluding Jeju Island). Since 2006, biennial surveys have been conducted using consistent core items in even-numbered years, whereas specific thematic surveys not covered in the core items have been conducted in odd-numbered years.

The initial KLoSA panel consisted of 10 254 participants sampled from a population aged 45 years or older (born in or before 1962). An additional 920 participants born between 1962 and 1963 were included in 2014 to replace the sample.

Study population

This study used longitudinal panel data from the KLoSA, including only individuals initially enrolled in Wave 1 (2006). All participants included after 2014 were excluded to maintain consistency in the longitudinal design. Specifically, the number of excluded participants in Waves 5, 6, 7 and 8 was 920, 890, 830 and 767, respectively.

Based on repeated biennial observations from 2006 to 2022 (Waves 1–9), a total of 55 781 person-wave observations were accumulated from the original baseline panel. Participants with missing information required to construct the frailty measure, including grip strength and group activity participation, were excluded. In addition, person-wave observations with missing household income information (‘unknown’ or ‘no response’) were excluded. After handling missing data, 54 851 person-wave observations remained available for analysis.

Furthermore, 1226 person-wave observations with missing employment-type information were excluded. After handling missing data, 53 625 person-wave observations from 10 302 participants remained available for analysis. To account for the repeated-measures nature of the data and to ensure a stable assessment of within-person changes over time, only participants with at least four observations during the study period were retained. As a result, the final analytical sample consisted of 6122 participants.

Variables

Frailty, the dependent variable, lacks a universally established definition, resulting in diverse measurement tools.2 13–16 We employed a frailty assessment tool widely used in Korean research that determines frailty based on weakness, isolation and exhaustion.17 We employed the Korean Frailty Instrument, a multidimensional frailty assessment tool developed and validated for use among Korean older adults.18 19 As employment status may affect physical, social and psychological domains, a multidimensional frailty measure was considered more appropriate than a single physical indicator such as grip strength.

Weakness was evaluated using grip strength measured two times for each hand (left and right), with the highest value among the four measurements recorded. Thresholds for frailty were defined as ≤24 kg for men and ≤15 kg for women, and participants meeting these criteria were assigned 1 point. Isolation was determined based on participants’ non-participation in group activities such as social, religious or cultural; sports or leisure; civic or social; interest or political; volunteer or learning group activities. Participants who reported no participation in any of these activities were classified as socially isolated and were assigned 1 point. Exhaustion was measured using two items from the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10): “Did you feel that everything you did was an effort during the past week?” and “Did you feel that you could not get going during the past week?” Participants who answered ‘3) Often’ or ‘4) Always’ to either question were considered to be experiencing exhaustion and were assigned 1 point. The composite frailty score ranged from 0 to 3 points. Participants scoring 0–1 point were categorised as non-frail, whereas those scoring 2–3 points were classified as frail.

Employment status, the independent variable, was assessed using the question: “Are you currently working for income? This includes working for a company, running your own business, or helping with family or relatives’ work.” Participants answered ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Those who answered ‘Yes’ were further categorised into the following subgroups for analysis: permanent employment, temporary/precarious employment and self-employment or unpaid family work.

Permanent employment comprised workers with an indefinite contract or a contract lasting a year or more. Temporary/precarious employment included temporary workers (those with a contract duration between 1 month and 1 year) and daily workers (those with a contract duration of less than 1 month). Finally, self-employment or unpaid family work included self-employed individuals and unpaid family workers.

This study controlled for potential confounders, including sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education, marital status, household income and area of residence), health-related factors (body mass index, chronic disorders, physical activity, unhealthy behaviours such as smoking and alcohol consumption and perceived health status) and previous frailty status. In addition, perceived health status was assessed using the question, “How would you rate your overall health status?” Responses were categorised as good, fair or poor, with higher categories indicating poorer perceived health.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Statistical analysis

χ2 tests and t-tests were used to compare covariates and examine the association between changes in employment status and frailty. Generalised estimating equations (GEEs) were used to evaluate the longitudinal association between changes in employment status and frailty. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the effects of employment type and examine the impact of employment transitions on the three frailty components. The results were reported as ORs with 95% CIs. All statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS software (V.9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, US).

Online supplemental table 1 highlights the association between employment status transitions and frailty within independent subgroups adjusted for the presence of chronic disorders. These results indicated that returning to or maintaining employment is critical for vulnerable groups. For men with chronic disorders, the transition from Unemployed → Working had an OR of 0.54 (95% CI 0.36 to 0.83), whereas for women, the same transition exhibited an OR of 0.59 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.82).

Online supplemental table 2 analyses the association between employment status transitions and frailty across different types of employment. Among men, transitioning to permanent employment showed the strongest protective effect against frailty (Unemployed → Permanent Employment, OR 0.25; 95% CI 0.13 to 0.51). For women, the protective effects of the transition to permanent employment or temporary/precarious employment were less pronounced than those for men. However, the association between frailty and self-employment or unpaid family work was strong in both genders. Women maintaining self-employment or unpaid family work had an OR of 0.64 (95% CI 0.53 to 0.79).

Online supplemental table 3 provides the ORs for frailty components based on changes in employment status. Returning to or maintaining employment was associated with relatively low odds of weakness and exhaustion between both genders. However, a gender-based difference was observed in isolation, where women showed an increased risk (isolation, OR 1.11; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.25).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the relationship between employment status transitions and frailty among middle-aged and older adults in Korea, focusing on diverse employment types using longitudinal panel data from the KLoSA. The findings revealed that maintaining or returning to employment was associated with reduced frailty compared with remaining unemployed. Both men and women showed significantly lower odds of frailty when employment was maintained or regained. Among men, returning to permanent employment was associated with the lowest odds of frailty, whereas among women, maintaining self-employment or unpaid family work was strongly associated with lower odds of frailty.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous longitudinal studies using the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing and the Korean Longitudinal Study of Employment and Aging, which have reported that continued labour force participation is associated with better health outcomes among older adults.20 21 However, these studies also suggest that poorer health status may increase the likelihood of labour market withdrawal. Therefore, the observed association between employment transitions and frailty may reflect both the influence of employment on health and the influence of health status on labour market participation.

In Korea, employment serves not only as a means of economic activity but also as a key contributor to individual identity and social belonging.22 The Korean labour market is characterised by a dual structure with distinct categories of regular, irregular, self-employment and unpaid family work, shaped by rapid economic growth and labour market segmentation.23 24 These employment types significantly influence health outcomes, particularly ageing-related conditions such as frailty.

Previous studies have seldom directly examined the relationship between employment status transitions and frailty; alternatively, they have focused on indirect health indicators.25 26 For instance, earlier studies have found that transitioning from employment to unemployment increases the risk of decreased grip strength, emphasising the importance of muscle-strengthening interventions to prevent sarcopenia.27 Additionally, a study involving community-dwelling older adults in Taiwan found that employment is associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms, suggesting that maintaining employment may help prevent depression in later life.28 Unlike previous studies that used single health indicators, such as grip strength, this study used a multidimensional frailty construct incorporating weakness, isolation and exhaustion. Furthermore, this study systematically evaluated the impact of employment transitions on frailty by distinguishing between permanent employment, temporary/precarious employment and self-employment or unpaid family work.29 The longitudinal approach emphasises the dynamic interplay between ageing and employment transitions over time.

Employment contributes to economic stability and reinforces social roles and connections. Previous studies have shown that returning to or maintaining employment supports social networks and social support, which protects against frailty-related health decline.20 In collectivist cultures such as Korea, employment helps individuals secure their social positions and fulfil societal roles, thereby reducing social isolation. Employment transition also influences psychological health. Unemployment is associated with increased depression, stress and anxiety, all of which can exacerbate frailty.30 31 Conversely, returning to or maintaining employment enhances psychological well-being and contributes to improved mental health, particularly in the exhaustion component of frailty. Furthermore, employment status directly influences physical activity levels because individuals who maintain or regain employment are more likely to engage in regular and structured activities, potentially mitigating their physical weakness.

Taken together, changes in employment status differentially affect the multidimensional components of frailty. The observed gender-based differences in employment types and frailty reflect the interaction of gender, cultural context and labour market structure. For men, returning to permanent employment provides the strongest protection against frailty, likely due to the economic stability, social status and identity offered in Korean society.32 33 For women, maintaining self-employment or unpaid family work has significant psychosocial benefits, reflecting their role in family-centred societal structures.34 35 Unlike in men, women’s frailty risk was less influenced by returning to permanent employment, suggesting that social connections and everyday roles provided by self-employment or unpaid family work contribute more to reducing frailty.

This study had several limitations. First, frailty as a multidimensional concept was measured using specific survey items and thresholds, which may have affected the results if the definitions or measurements varied across studies. In addition, frailty was assessed using the Korean Frailty Instrument, a multidimensional measure that includes social isolation and exhaustion in addition to physical weakness. Consequently, this instrument may identify a somewhat different group of frail individuals compared with other commonly used measures, such as the Fried Frailty Phenotype, which emphasise different frailty domains. Therefore, caution is warranted when comparing our findings with studies using alternative frailty definitions. Because the Korean Frailty Instrument incorporates social isolation and exhaustion in addition to physical weakness, the observed associations may partly reflect the influence of employment on social participation and psychosocial well-being, as well as physical functioning. Second, while employment status was categorised into permanent employment, temporary/precarious employment and self-employment or unpaid family work, detailed employment characteristics, such as job quality and working hours, were not captured. Third, although the longitudinal design allowed for the consideration of temporal changes, the observational nature of the study limited causal inference. In addition, chronic conditions were adjusted as binary variables, and information regarding disease severity, progression, functional decline or multimorbidity burden was not available. These factors may influence both employment transitions and frailty and could therefore have resulted in residual confounding. Furthermore, reverse causation cannot be excluded, as worsening health may lead individuals to withdraw from paid employment before the onset of frailty. Fourth, to ensure a stable assessment of within-person changes over time, only participants with at least four observations during the study period were included in the final analysis. Consequently, individuals lost to follow-up or with fewer repeated observations were excluded. These participants may have differed systematically from those retained in the analytical sample, potentially introducing selection bias. Finally, this study used KLoSA data; this data focused on middle-aged and older adults in Korea, limiting the generalisability of the findings to younger populations and other cultural contexts.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It provides a nuanced analysis by incorporating diverse employment types, thereby enhancing its originality. The use of longitudinal panel data enabled us to consider temporal changes and the dynamic interplay between ageing and employment transitions. Additionally, this study reflects Korea’s unique labour market and cultural characteristics, offering valuable insights into the relationship between employment types and health outcomes from a novel perspective.

Conclusions

This study identified a longitudinal association between changes in employment status and frailty across various employment types among middle-aged and older adults in South Korea. The findings suggest that employment transitions can play a critical role in reducing the risk of frailty, a key health indicator of ageing, particularly in the context of the global increase in ageing populations. Creating a stable employment environment for older adults is essential, and policies and programmes that encourage labour force participation among middle-aged and older adults may help mitigate health risks in ageing societies.

Future research should explore the generalisability of our findings using longitudinal studies in diverse cultural contexts. Moreover, investigations into the multidimensional aspects of employment, such as job quality, working hours and job satisfaction, and their impact on health outcomes are needed. These studies should integrate micro- and macro-level perspectives to deepen our understanding of the relationship between employment and health indicators.
