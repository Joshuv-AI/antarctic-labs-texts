Survival benefit of chemoradiotherapy in limited-stage mantle cell lymphoma: a National Cancer Database analysis∗
Authors: Domitilla Baccon, Jihao Zhou, Suheil Albert Atallah-Yunes, Jacqueline F Wang, Steven R Hwang, Jonas Paludo, Javier L Munoz, Madiha Iqbal, William G Breen, Scott C Lester, Ivana N Micallef, Jithma P Abeykoon, Robin R Klebig, Allison C Rosenthal, Muhamad Alhaj Moustafa, Julio C Chavez, Stephen M Ansell, Thomas M Habermann, Thomas E Witzig, Grzegorz S Nowakowski, Yucai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Combined systemic chemoradiotherapy improves survival over either alone in stage I/II MCL in a national database study.

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an uncommon subtype of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), accounting for ∼3% to 10% of adult-onset NHLs in Western countries. It is characterized by marked heterogeneity in behavior, with clinical presentations ranging from indolent disease to highly aggressive forms.1, 2, 3 Most patients present with advanced-stage disease, whereas only 5% to 15% have limited-stage involvement at diagnosis.4, 5, 6, 7 The underrepresentation of early-stage presentations in MCL clinical trials has hindered the development of standardized treatment guidelines for this population. Consequently, management of limited-stage MCL is often extrapolated from strategies used for advanced-stage MCL or adapted from treatment principles established for other limited-stage NHL subtypes.6,8, 9, 10

Historically, treatment options for limited-stage MCL have included radiotherapy (RT) alone, systemic chemoimmunotherapy, or a combination of both.8,9,11, 12, 13, 14 Retrospective institutional and registry studies suggested the importance of RT,9,13,14 whereas additional studies indicate that systemic relapse remains an ongoing concern.8,12 The role of combined-modality therapy, incorporating systemic therapy with involved-site RT, has been less clear; however, a few studies suggest it may confer superior overall survival (OS) compared with either modality alone.9,13

Over the past decade, the therapeutic landscape for MCL has evolved substantially.15, 16, 17 Findings from the StiL NHL1 trial, which demonstrated superior progression-free survival (PFS) and improved tolerability of bendamustine-rituximab (BR) compared with R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, Oncovin [vincristine], and prednisone) in patients with indolent NHL and MCL, established BR as the preferred frontline regimen for transplant-ineligible patients.18 These findings were further supported by the BRIGHT trial, which confirmed the statistical noninferiority of BR vs R-CHOP/R-CVP (rituximab, and cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisone) across indolent NHL and MCL subtypes, reinforcing BR as a standard first-line option for MCL.19 Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKis), such as ibrutinib, which demonstrated high response rates in relapsed/refractory MCL in the pivotal PCYC-1104 trial,20 have since been widely adopted in the relapsed setting. More recently, second-generation BTKis, such as acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, have demonstrated comparable or improved efficacy with enhanced tolerability in relapsed/refractory MCL, further expanding targeted options in this setting.21, 22, 23 The impact of recent advances in systemic and targeted therapies, applied in both frontline and relapsed settings, on survival outcomes according to the initial treatment strategy in limited-stage MCL remains uncertain.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large population-based analysis using the National Cancer Database (NCDB) to evaluate patterns of care and OS in patients with stage I/II MCL, focusing on treatment modality rather than specific systemic regimens (which are not available in NCDB). We compared outcomes across 3 treatment strategies: chemotherapy plus RT (Chemo+RT), chemotherapy alone, and RT alone, and examined differences by stage and treatment era to clarify the role of RT in the context of evolving systemic therapies.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted a retrospective study using the NCDB, a hospital-based registry jointly maintained by the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society.24 The NCDB captures ∼70% of the newly diagnosed cancer cases in the United States from >1500 Commission on Cancer–accredited institutions.24 All data are deidentified and exempt from institutional review board oversight.

Cohort selection and treatment groups

For this study, we queried the NCDB to identify patients aged ≥18 years diagnosed with Ann Arbor stage I/II MCL between 2004 and 2015 (Ann Arbor stage data were not yet available for those diagnosed in 2016-2020 at the time of this analysis, which limited the study period to 2004-2015). Patients with stage I/II disease and available treatment data were grouped into 3 groups based on initial therapy: chemotherapy alone, RT alone, or Chemo+RT. To examine potential temporal shifts in practice patterns and outcomes after the introduction of BR and early availability of BTKis, patients were further stratified by era of diagnosis: era 1 (2004-2009) and era 2 (2010-2015).

Variables and statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Group comparisons were performed using χ2 or the Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables. OS was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death or last follow-up. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to identify factors associated with OS. Covariates included age group, sex, Ann Arbor stage, Charlson-Deyo comorbidity scores, treatment modality, and treatment era. Two-sided P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 18.0 and BlueSky Statistics version 10.3.4.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Of the 35 135 patients diagnosed with MCL, 3836 (11.7%) had stage I/II disease, and 2428 had frontline treatment information available and were included in this analysis (Figure 1). To assess the potential impact of missing treatment information, we compared patients included in the analytic cohort with those excluded because treatment data were unavailable (supplemental Table 1 and supplemental Figure 1). Compared with included patients, excluded patients were older (median age, 73.0 years; IQR, 64.0-81.0 vs 68.5 years; IQR, 59.0-77.0; P < .001), more frequently female (49.1% vs 29.5%; P = .001), had a higher prevalence of Charlson-Deyo score of ≥2 (7.1% vs 4.7%; P = .002), and were more likely to have stage I disease (61.6% vs 47.8%; P < .001). Excluded patients also had a shorter median OS (5.3 vs 8.1 years; supplemental Figure 1).

Patient selection from the NCDB.

Among the patients with frontline treatment information (n = 2428), 1161 (47.8%) had stage I disease, and 1267 (52.2%) had stage II disease. The median age at diagnosis was 68.5 years (IQR, 59.0-77.0), and 1711 (70.5%) were male. Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the 2 eras (Table 1), although Charlson-Deyo scores of ≥2 were more frequent in era 2 than era 1 (5.6% vs 3.7%; P = .031). Treatment included RT alone in 240 patients (9.9%), chemotherapy alone in 1792 (73.8%), and Chemo+RT in 396 patients (16.3%). The treatment modality distribution was similar between the 2 eras.

Baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by treatment era and treatment categories

Era 1 includes patients diagnosed between 2004 and 2009; era 2 includes patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2015.

In era 1 (2004-2009), the RT-alone group included more older patients (aged ≥80 years) and females compared with other treatment groups (P < .001 and P = .002, respectively). RT-alone recipients were also more likely to have stage I disease (75.0%; P < .001) and fewer comorbidities, with 100% having a Charlson-Deyo score of 0 to 1 (P = .020). In era 2 (2010-2015), patients in the RT-alone group were older and had more stage I disease (P = .042 and P < .001, respectively). However, no significant differences were observed between treatment groups with respect to comorbidity or sex (Table 1).

OS

After a median follow-up of 9.8 years, the median OS for the entire cohort was 8.1 years (95% confidence interval [CI], 7.6-8.7; Figure 2A). When stratified by treatment modality, median OS was longest for Chemo+RT (11.2 years; 95% CI, 9.7-12.7), followed by RT alone (8.0 years; 95% CI, 6.7-9.4) and chemotherapy alone (7.4 years; 95% CI, 6.7-8.1; P < .001; Figure 2B).

(C) OS of the Ann Arbor stage I population stratified by treatment category (Chemo+RT vs RT alone vs chemotherapy alone). Among patients with stage I disease (n = 1161), those receiving Chemo+RT (n = 213) had a median OS of 13.8 years (95% CI, 10.7-16.9) compared with 9.3 years (95% CI, 7.0-11.6) with RT alone (n = 171) and 8.0 years (95% CI, 6.9-9.0) with chemotherapy alone (n = 777; P < .001; Figure 2C). In patients with stage II disease (n = 1267), Chemo+RT (n = 183) was associated with a median OS of 9.8 years (95% CI, 7.7-11.9) compared with 6.9 years (95% CI, 6.1-7.8) for chemotherapy alone (n = 1015) and 5.4 years (95% CI, 4.3-6.4) for RT alone (n = 69; P < .001; Figure 2D).

Era-stratified survival outcomes

Among patients diagnosed with stage I disease in era 1, Chemo+RT (n = 111) was associated with a median OS of 10.8 years (95% CI, 7.21-14.29) compared with 9.5 years (95% CI, 7.06-11.99) with RT alone (n = 78) and 7.8 years (95% CI, 5.92-9.57) with chemotherapy alone (n = 368; P = .045; Figure 3A). In era 2, the median OS with Chemo+RT (n = 102) was not reached compared with 8.7 years (95% CI, could not be estimated) with RT alone (n = 93) and 8.0 years (95% CI, 7.09-8.81) with chemotherapy alone (n = 409; P < .001; Figure 3B).

(B) OS in Ann Arbor stage I population stratified by treatment category in era 2 (2010-2015). (C) OS in Ann Arbor stage II population stratified by treatment category in era 1 (2004-2009). (D) OS in Ann Arbor stage II population stratified by treatment category in era 2 (2010-2015).

For patients with stage II disease in era 1, survival differences between treatment groups did not reach statistical significance, possibly because of smaller sample sizes. Median OS was 9.0 years (95% CI, 5.9-12.2) with Chemo+RT (n = 95), 7.1 years (95% CI, 5.7-8.4) with chemotherapy alone (n = 447), and 5.2 years (95% CI, 2.1-8.2) with RT alone (n = 26; P = .093; Figure 3C). However, in era 2, Chemo+RT (n = 88) was associated with improved OS (median not reached) compared with chemotherapy alone (n = 568; median, 6.8 years; 95% CI, 5.6-8.1) and RT alone (n = 43; median, 5.4 years; 95% CI, 4.4-6.4; P=.026; Figure 3D).

Prognostic factors

Univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of OS (Table 2). In univariate analysis, treatment modality was associated with survival outcomes, with worse OS observed in patients receiving chemotherapy alone (hazard ratio [HR], 1.49; 95% CI, 1.27-1.75; P < .001) or RT alone (HR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.16-1.81; P = .001) compared with those receiving Chemo+RT. Older age, Charlson-Deyo score ≥2, stage II disease, and treatment at nonacademic centers were associated with inferior OS. Era of diagnosis, sex, race, and ethnicity were not associated with OS.

Univariate and multivariable Cox regression analysis for clinical factors associated with OS

In multivariable analysis, treatment with chemotherapy alone (HR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.27-1.77; P < .001) or RT alone (HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.05-1.67; P = .017) remained associated with worse OS compared with Chemo+RT. Older age, Charlson-Deyo score ≥2, stage II disease, and treatment at nonacademic centers were also independently associated with inferior OS. Era of diagnosis, sex, race, and ethnicity were similarly not associated with OS in multivariable analysis.

Discussion

Our NCDB-based study represents, to our knowledge, the largest real-world analysis of limited-stage MCL, a rare clinical entity lacking randomized clinical trial data to guide treatment. We compared outcomes of combined chemotherapy and RT vs either modality alone, stratifying by stage and era of diagnosis (2004-2009 vs 2010-2015). Chemo+RT was associated with superior OS compared with chemotherapy or RT alone, and this benefit persisted after multivariable adjustment for age, comorbidity, and stage. The OS difference was clinically meaningful, with a difference in median OS exceeding several years, suggesting that combined systemic and local therapy is the most effective approach for limited-stage MCL. However, it is important to note that our study cannot conclude whether the addition of radiation is the actual cause of improved survival outcomes, given unmeasured potential confounding factors including high-risk disease features and lack of granular details of both frontline and subsequent therapy regimens. It is also important to note that patients excluded because treatment information was unavailable differed clinically from those included in the analytic cohort. Specifically, excluded patients were older, had a greater comorbidity burden, and experienced shorter OS. The significantly older age of the excluded cohort likely contributed to the observed difference in OS. These findings suggest that the analyzed cohort may have represented a healthier subset of patients with limited-stage MCL and raise the possibility that selection bias contributed to the observed associations.

Several potential mechanisms may underlie the observed association between combined-modality therapy and improved OS in patients with limited-stage MCL in our study. One possible explanation is improved initial disease control with the addition of RT to systemic therapy, which may reduce early locoregional persistence or relapse, and potentially delay subsequent systemic progression in carefully selected patients. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of likely residual confounding, including treatment selection bias and unmeasured differences in underlying disease biology among patients selected for combined-modality therapy. In addition, differences in the post–frontline-therapy disease course and access to effective salvage treatments may also have contributed to the observed survival differences. The lack of data on PFS, relapse patterns, and subsequent lines of therapy in the NCDB further limits the ability to assess how postprogression outcomes influenced OS, precluding a full attribution of survival differences to initial treatment strategy alone. Importantly, MCL is generally considered incurable; therefore, these results should not be interpreted as evidence that combined-modality therapy increases cure rates in this setting.

Combining chemoimmunotherapy with RT is biologically and clinically justified. RT provides durable local control in this radiosensitive disease, whereas chemoimmunotherapy addresses potential systemic involvement or relapse risk. In contrast, RT alone carries a risk of systemic relapse.8,12 These concepts are supported by prior institutional and registry studies and are consistent with some of our observations. An earlier NCDB analysis from 1998 to 2012 showed that combined therapy was associated with a 35% lower mortality risk after propensity adjustment during an era that preceded BTKis and before bendamustine was widely adopted.13 Leitch et al reported improved 5-year PFS (68% vs 11%) and a trend toward better 6-year OS (71% vs 25%) with RT-containing regimens.9 A Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) analysis of 657 patients demonstrated a significant survival benefit with RT, increasing median OS from 66 to 103 months and reducing mortality risk by 23%.14 Bernard et al similarly reported excellent local control with combined therapy, although systemic relapse predominated.8 In contrast, the International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group multicenter analysis of 179 patients found no OS difference across the 3 treatment modalities. Notably, 79% of the relapses occurred outside the initial involved site, reinforcing that RT alone is insufficient for long-term disease control.12

Despite this demonstrated association with improved outcome, Chemo+RT was administered to only 16% of the patients in our study, highlighting substantial underuse potentially attributable to toxicity concerns, logistical barriers, institutional variability, and clinician preference. Additionally, RT alone was used in a meaningful proportion of patients with stage II disease, suggesting that treatment selection may have been strongly influenced by factors such as age and comorbidity rather than stage-based principles. Although PFS is not captured by NCDB, the large OS difference suggests that omitting chemotherapy in eligible patients represents a missed opportunity for durable disease control. However, treatment should remain individualized. Older age (HR of 5.35 for aged ≥80 years) and higher comorbidity burden (HR of 2.00 for Charlson-Deyo score ≥2) were strong adverse prognostic factors and possibly influenced the lower use of combined therapy in these subgroups. RT alone achieved a median OS approaching 8 years in our study, supporting its role for frail patients or those who are unable to tolerate chemotherapy. This is further supported by findings from a recent Swedish population-based cohort, in which RT of ≥24 Gy in stage I MCL yielded a 5-year OS of >85%, with no survival advantage seen in patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy alone after adjusting for age and MCL International Prognostic Index. Notably, even lower doses of RT (<24 Gy) produced high response rates and acceptable survival, suggesting that reduced-dose RT may be a viable option in select cases.11 Future studies should address the outcomes of integrating RT with less toxic systemic approaches, such as newer BTKi-based strategies, as well as developing risk-adapted frameworks for patients unfit for chemoimmunotherapy.

Several limitations beyond the retrospective nature of this study should be acknowledged. NCDB does not capture key clinical and biologic details, including MCL International Prognostic Index, Ki-67, histologic variants, tumor size, details on extranodal involvement, chemotherapy regimens, or the use of rituximab maintenance therapy. Data on novel agents such as BTKis are also lacking. Therefore, era stratification served only as a surrogate for the adoption of BR and newer targeted therapies rather than confirming actual exposure. The use of BR in limited-stage disease, with or without RT, cannot be confirmed in our study because of the lack of detailed chemotherapy regimen data. In addition, although the first BTKi, ibrutinib, received regulatory approval in 2013 (during era 2 in our study), and BTKis would have been available to a subset of patients diagnosed in both eras during the follow-up period of this study, we are unable to ascertain differences in BTKi exposure between patients diagnosed in the 2 eras in our study. Treatment-specific information, including RT dose, field, number of chemotherapy cycles, and the timing of systemic therapy relative to RT, is not recorded. In addition, the NCDB captures only the initial course of therapy and does not include information on subsequent treatments, such as salvage chemoimmunotherapy, transplant, targeted therapies, etc. It also does not have information on treatment sequencing. Therefore, we were unable to assess the impact of subsequent therapy on outcomes or determine whether patients initially treated with RT alone later received systemic therapy. In addition, the NCDB does not capture the specific imaging modalities used for staging, such as computed tomography vs positron emission tomography/computed tomography. Furthermore, the NCDB does not include staging workup details such as bone marrow biopsy or gastrointestinal evaluation, which are critical in confirming limited-stage disease in MCL. The database also lacks information on PFS, cause-specific mortality, and relapse patterns, limiting assessment of disease control and introducing a potential risk of overestimating the benefits of combined-modality therapy with respect to OS. In addition, patients excluded because of unavailable treatment information were older and had a greater comorbidity burden and shorter OS than those included in the analytic cohort, raising the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding. Despite all these aforementioned limitations, this study provides valuable population-level evidence supporting the use of combined-modality therapy in limited-stage MCL.

In conclusion, within the limitations of the NCDB, this study provides important population-level evidence that combined systemic therapy and RT are associated with improved survival in limited-stage MCL. Even in the contemporary era of improved systemic therapy, addition of RT continues to confer durable benefit. We recommend that combined systemic therapy and RT be considered for appropriately selected patients, whereas RT alone remains a reasonable option for those who are unfit for chemotherapy. Prospective studies are needed to define the optimal integration of RT with current systemic regimens and to determine whether novel strategies such as incorporation of newer-generation BTKis could further enhance outcomes when combined with RT or potentially allow for omission of RT in patients with limited-stage MCL.
