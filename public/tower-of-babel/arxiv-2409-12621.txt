The maximality of T T in Thompson’s group V V Thanks: MSC (2020): 20E28; 20E32; 20F65; 20F05. Keywords: Thompson Groups, Maximal Subgroups, Infinite Simple Groups
J. Belk, C. Bleak, M. Quick, and R. Skipper

Abstract
. We show that R. Thompson’s group T T is a maximal subgroup of the group V V . The argument provides examples of foundational calculations which arise when expressing elements of V V as products of transpositions of basic clopen sets in Cantor space ℭ \mathfrak{C} .



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we prove the foundational result that Thompson’s group
T
is a maximal subgroup of Thompson’s group
V
. We were surprised to learn that this result has not previously appeared in the literature. In our view, Higman’s famous quote
[
6
]

applies:

This paper can, I think, best be considered as a sort of five finger exercise. In it we polish the techniques from a corner of group theory…

where our corner of group theory explores the connection between the dynamics of an action and corresponding algebraic structures.

We shall work with Richard Thompson’s group
V
viewed as a group of homeomorphisms of Cantor space
\mathfrak{C}=\{0,1\}^{\omega}
. We view points of Cantor space as infinite sequences
w=x_{1}x_{2}\dots
where each
x_{i}
is a letter from the alphabet
\{0,1\}
. We recommend the paper
[
5
]
as the standard introductory reference to the Thompson groups, where, in particular, the subgroup
T
of
V
is defined as consisting of those transformations that can be viewed as maps on the circle. The reader may also find it convenient to consult the paper
[
3
]
for the permutation notation that we employ here. In particular, in the latter source, it is described how the transpositions of basic clopen subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
(that we term “swaps”) generate the group
V
.

An important theme throughout the study of groups is to describe their maximal subgroups. In
[
1
]
, we described technology to construct many examples of maximal subgroups of Thompson’s group
V
, and we asserted that
T
is a maximal subgroup of
V
that does not arise from this construction. We subsequently learned that the maximality of
T
in
V
was not a known result.

Note that Thompson’s group
F
is maximal in
T
. Indeed, since
F
is the stabiliser of
0
in the circle and
T
acts 2-transitively on the orbit of
0
in the circle,
F
has only two double cosets in
T
. No similar statement holds for
T
in
V
. Indeed, it is not hard to show that the identity, the swap
(\hskip 1.0pt00\;01\hskip 1.0pt)
, and the swap
(\hskip 1.0pt00\;10\hskip 1.0pt)

lie in different double cosets of
T
in
V
. (See Section
2
for the definition of swaps.)

Section
2
contains the preliminaries needed and describes how we handle the prefix substitutions in
V
. The proof of the theorem is conducted in Section
3
.

2.
Preliminaries

We shall write
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\ast}
for the set of finite words in the alphabet
\{0,1\}
and refer to the elements of
\Omega
as
addresses
. If
\alpha\in\Omega
, then this determines a
cone

\alpha\mathfrak{C}
, which is the basic clopen subset of Cantor space
\mathfrak{C}
consisting of those elements with prefix
\alpha
:

\alpha\mathfrak{C}=\{\,\alpha w:w\in\mathfrak{C}\,\}.

We shall refer to
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
as the
cone with address
\alpha
or, more simply, the
cone at
\alpha
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are addresses in
\Omega
, we write
\alpha\preccurlyeq\beta
when
\alpha
is a prefix of
\beta
. We also write
\alpha\perp\beta
when both
\alpha\not\preccurlyeq\beta
and
\beta\not\preccurlyeq\alpha
and, in this case, we say that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
incomparable
. We shall also make use of the
lexicographic order
on addresses. We write
\alpha<\beta
when
\alpha=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{r}
is earlier than
\beta=b_{1}b_{2}\dots b_{s}
in this order; that is, when, for some
k
,
a_{1}=b_{1}
, …,
a_{k-1}=b_{k-1}
,
a_{k}=0
and
b_{k}=1
. There is similarly a lexicographic order
<
defined upon Cantor space.

Let
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\alpha_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{C}
and
\beta_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\beta_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\beta_{n}\mathfrak{C}
be two collections of disjoint cones that each form a partition of
\mathfrak{C}
. We may define a map
g\colon\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}
by
\alpha_{i}w\mapsto\beta_{i}w
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
n
and all
w\in\mathfrak{C}
. We call such a map a
prefix substitution map
and Thompson’s group
V
may be defined as the group of all prefix substitution maps with composition as binary operation. We may describe this prefix substitution map
g
by a
paired tree diagram
, or
tree-pair
,
(D,R,\sigma)
where
D
and
R
are finite binary rooted trees with
n
leaves and
\sigma
is a permutation of the points in
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. The leaves of the domain tree
D
correspond to the addresses
\alpha_{i}
and are labelled
1
,
2
, …
n
from left-to-right, while the leaves of the range tree
R
correspond to the addresses
\beta_{j}
and are labelled
1\sigma
,
2\sigma
, …,
n\sigma
from left-to-right in such a way that if
\alpha_{i}
corresponds to the leaf with some label
k
then
\beta_{i}
is also labelled
k
. Note that if
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
, …,
\alpha_{n}
are the addresses of the leaves of the rooted tree
D
appearing from left-to-right in its diagram then, in the lexicographic order,
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\dots<\alpha_{n}
.

An element
g\in V
has finite order if and only if we can represent it by a tree-pair
(D,D,\sigma)
where the domain and range trees are the same. The permutation
\sigma
then represents a permutation of the leaves of
D
. (See
[
2
,
4
]
for different proofs of this.) We shall call such elements
permutations
in
V
.

In our argument, we make particular use certain prefix substitutions that we call
swaps
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are incomparable addresses (so the cones
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
and
\beta\mathfrak{C}
are disjoint), we define the prefix substitution
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
by

p\cdot(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)=\begin{cases}p&\text{if $p\notin\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C}$,}\\
\beta w&\text{if $p=\alpha w$ for some $w\in\mathfrak{C}$,}\\
\alpha w&\text{if $p=\beta w$ for some $w\in\mathfrak{C}$.}\end{cases}

This swap is an element of
V
that has the effect of interchanging the cones
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
and
\beta\mathfrak{C}
. We think of them as analogous to the familiar transpositions in symmetric groups. It was observed in
[
3
]
that the swaps form a convenient generating set for
V
and this leads to a “Coxeter-type” infinite presentation for
V
. As in
[
3
]
, we extend this “permutation of disjoint cones” notation in the natural fashion for finite order elements of
V
as products of disjoint cycles of cones, allowing us to write an element such as
(00\;110\;010\;101)\,(011\;111)
hopefully without inducing confusion in the reader. Note that
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)=(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha 0\;\beta 0\hskip 1.0pt)\,(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha 1\;\beta 1\hskip 1.0pt)
whenever
\alpha
and
\beta
are addresses with
\alpha\perp\beta
. Through this observation, it follows that every element of
V
can be written as an even number of swaps, hinting towards the simplicity of
V
.

Finally, if
g\in V
, we define the
support
of
g
to be the set

\operatorname{Supt}(g):=\{\,p\in\mathfrak{C}:pg\neq p\,\}

and note that this is always a clopen subset of
\mathfrak{C}
. If
\operatorname{Supt}(g)\neq\mathfrak{C}
, then we say that the map
g
is of
small support
.

3.
The argument

We noted in our preliminaries that an element
g\in V
is of finite order is represented by a tree-pair
(D,D,\sigma)
. For such a permutation
g
, we shall say that
g
admits an interleaved representation
if the tree
D
can be chosen so that if
\alpha
is any leaf of
D
representing a cone
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
that is moved by
g
, then the left-neighbour and right-neighbour leaves of
\alpha
(in the natural circular order on the leaves of
D
) both represent cones that are fixed (necessarily pointwise) by
g
. In this case, we also call
g
an
interleaved permutation
. We also use the term
interleaved swap
for a swap
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
that is an interleaved permutation. Within our argument, we shall use interleaved permutations within various products to build other permutations.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
with
\alpha\perp\beta
. The formula

(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)=(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha 0\;\beta 0\hskip 1.0pt)\,(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha 1\;\beta 1\hskip 1.0pt)

expresses the swap
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
as a product of two swaps of small support, both of which are interleaved permutations.

Given a finite rooted binary tree
A
, we shall say that a sequence
(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of leaves of
A
is
interleaved
if every distinct pair
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
of leaves are not neighbours in the natural left-to-right order on the leaves of
A
. We will say the sequence is
ordered
if, for every such pair of indices with
i<j
, the leaf
\alpha_{i}
is to the left of the leaf
\alpha_{j}
(that is,
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{j}
in the lexicographic order on addresses).

We leave the following lemma to the reader. The proof only requires techniques which appear in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(The Interleaved Shaping Lemma)
.

Let
(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
and
(\beta_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
be ordered, interleaved sequences of
k
leaves on some finite rooted binary trees
A
and
B
respectively. Then there are finite rooted binary trees
D
and
R
with the same number of leaves and a bijection
\tau
from the leaves of
D
to the leaves of
R
so that the tree-pair
(D,R,\tau)
represents an element
g\in T
such that, for each index
i
,
\alpha_{i}
is a leaf of
D
,
\beta_{i}
is a leaf of
R
, and
\alpha_{i}\tau=\beta_{i}
.

Corollary 3.3
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
with
\alpha\perp\beta
such the swap
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
is an interleaved permutation, then there exists
g\in T
such that
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)^{g}=(\hskip 1.0pt00\;10\hskip 1.0pt)
. In particular, any two interleaved swaps in
V
are conjugate to each other using an element of
T
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
is a swap of small support then
{\langle}\,(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt),T\,{\rangle}=V
.

Proof.

Let
(D,D,\sigma)
be a tree-pair representing
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
with
\alpha
and
\beta
being leaves in the tree
D
. As
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
is a swap of small support the tree
D
has at least three leaves. If necessary, replace
D
by a new tree
D
with at least four leaves by splitting a leaf of the original tree
D
that represents a cone not in the support of
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
.

If it is the case that
\alpha
and
\beta
are adjacent leaves of
D
, then conjugate
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
by an element
g\in T
with representative tree-pair
(D,D,\rho)
where
\rho
is a cyclic rotation of the leaves of
D
so that
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)^{g}=(\hskip 1.0pt\beta\;\gamma\hskip 1.0pt)
where the leaf
\gamma
represents the cone
\gamma\mathfrak{C}
that is disjoint from the support of
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
and, by our assumption that
D
has at least four leaves,
\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C}\cup\gamma\mathfrak{C}\neq\mathfrak{C}
. We now compute that

(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)^{(\hskip 1.0pt\beta\;\gamma\hskip 1.0pt)}=(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\gamma\hskip 1.0pt).

Either
(\hskip 1.0pt\beta\;\gamma\hskip 1.0pt)
or
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\gamma\hskip 1.0pt)
is an interleaved swap. We may therefore assume that
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
was an interleaved swap in the first place.

The subgroup generated by
(\hskip 1.0pt\alpha\;\beta\hskip 1.0pt)
and
T
then contains all interleaved swaps by Corollary
3.3
and hence all swaps by Example
3.1
. Therefore this subgroup equals
V
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Note that in the above lemma we cannot remove the stated restriction to a swap of small support since
(\hskip 1.0pt0\;1\hskip 1.0pt)
is actually an element of
T
and
T
is a proper subgroup of
V
.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
(\alpha_{1}\;\alpha_{2}\;\alpha_{3})
is a
3
-cycle of small support then

\langle(\alpha_{1}\;\alpha_{2}\;\alpha_{3}),T\rangle=V.

Proof.

First we may assume (via conjugation arguments similar to those used in Lemma
3.4
) that
(\alpha_{1}\;\alpha_{2}\;\alpha_{3})
is an interleaved permutation. Then employing Lemma
3.2
we may find an element
t\in T
whose definition includes the prefix substitutions
\alpha_{1}\mapsto 00
,
\alpha_{2}\mapsto 100
and
\alpha_{3}\mapsto 1100
. Then

(\alpha_{1}\;\alpha_{2}\;\alpha_{3})^{t}=(00\;100\;1100).

It therefore remains to show that the subgroup
H=\langle(00\;100\;1100),T\rangle
is equal to
V
.

Firstly, there is an element
x\in T
such that

(00\;100\;1100)^{x}=(00\;100\;1110)

so
H
contains

(\hskip 1.0pt00\;1110\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt100\;1100\hskip 1.0pt)=(00\;100\;1100)(00\;100\;1110).

Now conjugate this product of disjoint swaps by
(00\;100\;1100)
to see that
H
also contains

\bigl((\hskip 1.0pt00\;1110\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt100\;1100\hskip 1.0pt)\bigr)^{(00\;100\;1100)}=(\hskip 1.0pt00\;1100\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt100\;1110\hskip 1.0pt).

Observe that this is an interleaved permutation and that the addresses corresponding to its support are
00<100<1100<1110
in the lexicographic order. Hence, by use of Lemma
3.2
, there are elements
y,z\in T
such that

\bigl((\hskip 1.0pt00\;1100\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt100\;1110\hskip 1.0pt)\bigr)^{y}=(\hskip 1.0pt000\;1000\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt010\;1010\hskip 1.0pt)

and

\bigl((\hskip 1.0pt00\;1100\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt100\;1110\hskip 1.0pt)\bigr)^{z}=(\hskip 1.0pt001\;1001\hskip 1.0pt)(\hskip 1.0pt011\;1011\hskip 1.0pt).

The subgroup
H
therefore contains the product of these last two elements, namely the swap
(\hskip 1.0pt0\;10\hskip 1.0pt)
. Hence
(\hskip 1.0pt0\;10\hskip 1.0pt)\in\langle(\alpha_{1}\;\alpha_{2}\;\alpha_{3}),T\rangle
, and so by Lemma
3.4
we deduce
\langle(\alpha_{1}\;\alpha_{2}\;\alpha_{3}),T\rangle=V
.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Thompson’s group
T
is a maximal subgroup of Thompson’s group
V
.

Proof.

Let
a\in V\backslash T
. Representing
a
by a tree-pair with some domain tree and some range tree, there is an element
b
of
T
that takes the range tree of
a
to the domain tree while returning at least one image cone to its original location. The product
c:=ab
is then a permutation in
V
which fixes at least one cone and which is not in
T
. Through conjugation by an element of
T
we may assume that
c
fixes the cone at
0
and that there is a tree-pair
(D,D,\sigma)
representing
c
so that
0
and
10
are leaves of
D
and that the cone
10\mathfrak{C}
is moved to some other cone to the right of
10\mathfrak{C}
by
c
(that is, to a cone
11\beta\mathfrak{C}
for some
\beta
, where
11\beta
is also a leaf of
D
).

Let us write
c
as a product of its disjoint cycles of cones as follows:

c=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(\alpha_{i1}\;\alpha_{i2}\;\cdots\;\alpha_{ik_{i}})

where we may assume
\alpha_{11}=10
and that all addresses
\alpha_{ij}
represent leaves of the tree
D
and so in particular have
1
as prefix.
(We refer to this as a cycle decomposition of the permutation
c
. As is usual, we omit any cycles of length one.)

Let
\alpha_{ij}
be the address appearing in the cycle decomposition of
c
that is rightmost as a leaf in the tree
D
among such addresses. (Thus the cone
\alpha_{ij}\mathfrak{C}
is moved by
c
to a cone
\alpha_{ik}\mathfrak{C}
that is to the left of
\alpha_{ij}\mathfrak{C}
; that is,
\alpha_{ik}<\alpha_{ij}
in the lexicographic order on addresses.) Further, let
\gamma
be the next leaf of
D
to the right of
\alpha_{ij}
in the cyclic order on the leaves of
D
, so necessarily
c
fixes the cone
\gamma\mathfrak{C}
pointwise. (If it were the case that
\alpha_{ij}
is the address of the rightmost leaf of the tree
D
, then
\gamma=0
, the leftmost leaf of
D
.) Now let
d
be an element of
T
with tree pair
(D,R,\tau)
where the tree
R
has both
10
and
11
as leaves and where
d
takes
\alpha_{ij}\mathfrak{C}
to the cone
10\mathfrak{C}
and
\gamma\mathfrak{C}
to the cone
11\mathfrak{C}
.

The conjugate element
e:=c^{d}
is then an element of
V
which is a permutation on the leaves of the tree
R
, which fixes the points in the cone
11\mathfrak{C}
and has a cycle decomposition with the address
10
as an entry in one of the cycles. Consequently, all other addresses in this cycle decomposition have
0
as prefix.

From this construction a straightforward computation shows the commutator
[c,e]
is the
3
-cycle
f:=(\beta_{1}\;\beta_{2}\;\beta_{3})
with
\beta_{1}=10
,
\beta_{2}
equal to the image of
10
under
e
and
\beta_{3}
equal to the image of
10
under
c
. In particular,
\beta_{2}
has
0
as prefix and
\beta_{3}
has
1
as prefix. Consequently, if
f
were to have full support, then necessarily
\beta_{2}=0
and
\beta_{3}=11
. Since
c
fixes the points in the cone
0\mathfrak{C}
, this forces
c=(\hskip 1.0pt10\;11\hskip 1.0pt)
. Then
V=\langle c,T\rangle
by Lemma
3.4
and
V=\langle a,T\rangle
follows.

Let us suppose then that
f
does not have full support. In this case,
V=\langle f,T\rangle
by Lemma
3.6
. Since
f\in\langle a,T\rangle
, we conclude
V=\langle a,T\rangle
. This shows that
T
is indeed a maximal subgroup of
V
.
∎
