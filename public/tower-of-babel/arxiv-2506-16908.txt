Magnus Methods for Stochastic Delay-Differential Equations Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 8, 2026.
Mitchell T. Griggs, Kevin Burrage, Pamela M. Burrage

Abstract
This paper introduces Magnus-based methods for solving stochastic delay-differential equations (SDDEs). We construct Magnus–Euler–Maruyama (MEM) and Magnus–Milstein (MM) schemes by combining stochastic Magnus integrators with Taylor methods for SDDEs. These schemes are applied incrementally between multiples of the delay times. We present proofs of their convergence orders and demonstrate these rates through numerical examples and error graphs. Among the examples, we apply the MEM and MM schemes to both linear and nonlinear problems. We also apply the MEM scheme to a stochastic partial delay-differential equation (SPDDE), comparing its performance with the traditional Euler–Maruyama (EM) method. Under fine spatial discretization, the MEM scheme remains numerically stable while the EM method becomes unstable, yielding a significant computational advantage.

1.1
Motivation

This paper focuses on the SDDE
eq.

1

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left[D\frac{\partial^{2}U(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}+C_{U}(t,x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

(2)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+cU(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}W^{c}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1),

\displaystyle\vphantom{\int}U(t,0)

\displaystyle=U(t,1)=0,\quad U(t,x)=T_{0}(x),\quad x\in[0,1],

is a scaling constant. The stochastic process

-Wiener process, as defined by Lord, Powell, and Shardlow
[
24
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left[D\frac{\partial^{2}U(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}+C_{U}(t-\tau,x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

(3)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+cU(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}W^{c}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1),

\displaystyle\vphantom{\int}U(t,0)

\displaystyle=U(t,1)=0,\quad U(t,x)=T_{0}(x),\quad(t,x)\in[-\tau,0]\times[0,1],

with the temperature held fixed on
[-\tau,0]
. To simulate a solution of
eq.

3

[
40
]
, where we select a spatial step size of

for some

, and then approximate the solution

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=0\,\mathrm{d}t,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[D\frac{U_{j-1}(t)+U_{j+1}(t)-2U_{j}(t)}{(\Delta x)^{2}}+C_{U}(t-\tau,x_{j})\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{cU_{j}(t)}{\sqrt{\Delta x}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{j}^{c}(t),

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{d}(t)

\displaystyle=0\,\mathrm{d}t,

for

(U_{0}(t),\ldots,U_{d}(t))=(T_{0}(x_{0}),\ldots,T_{0}(x_{d}))
when

. We denote by

the cross section
(W^{c}(t,x_{j}))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
of

.

The simplest method for simulating
eq.

4
is the Euler–Maruyama scheme, but that is unfortunately restricted by the need for a small step size in time. If we ignore the cooling function and noise (supposing
C_{U}=0
and
c=0
), then applying the EM method (which reduces to the regular Euler scheme) to the system
eq.

4
requires a temporal step size

that satisfies the Neumann stability condition

2D\,h<(\Delta x)^{2}.

(5)

Details of this requirement may be found in the doctoral thesis by Hayman
[
13
]
. When a fine spatial discretization is required, the requirement that

decreases quadratically with

requires significant computation time for the Euler implementation. The Magnus schemes that we introduce in this paper are not hindered by this condition.

n=-p,\ldots,N
.

Generally, if
Y
has MS OoC
q
then it also has strong OoC
q
(see
[
32
, page 5]
), which can be shown with the Hölder inequality (for probability spaces). Another measurement of the accuracy for a numerical scheme is its weak error, which is the error in the moments of (smooth functions of) the scheme, but we do not study these errors in this paper. However, we do employ the following result by Milstein
[
31
]
, to prove the convergence orders for our schemes. This result relates the weak and mean-square errors of each single (local) step of a numerical scheme to its total (global) convergence order. That is, this result relates local errors of
Y_{n+1}
, conditional on the value of
Y_{n}
, to the global error in
Y
. Yang, Burrage, Komori, Burrage, and Ding
[
46
]
also use this result to prove the orders of convergence for their Magnus schemes
eq.

16
and
eq.

17
. We later restate this result for SDDEs, before we then derive the MS OoC for each of our schemes.

Theorem 5
.

Let
Y=(Y_{n})_{n=0,1,\ldots,N}
be a numerical scheme with fixed step size
h=h_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
, approximating the solution
X=(X(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
of

\mathrm{d}X(t)=f(t,X(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t,X(t))\,\mathrm{d}W_{j}(t),\quad t\in[0,T],

where
f
and each
g_{j}
are spatially Lipschitz with linear-growth bound. Furthermore, suppose
X
is adapted,
X(0)
is independent of the Wiener processes, and
\mathbb{E}[\left|X(0)\right|^{2}]<\infty
. If there is a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[Y_{n+1}|Y_{n}=x]-\mathbb{E}[X(t_{n+1})|X(t_{n})=x]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant M\left(1+\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}h^{q_{1}}\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{n+1}-X(t_{n+1})\right|^{2}|Y_{n}=X(t_{n})=x\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant M\left(1+\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}h^{q_{2}},

for any
n=0,1,\ldots,N-1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
q_{2}\geqslant 1/2
and
q_{1}\geqslant q_{2}+1/2
, then

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}|Y_{0}=X(0)\right]\right)^{1/2}\leqslant M\left(1+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X(0)\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}h^{q_{2}-1/2}

(20)

holds for every
n=0,1,\ldots,N
.

To construct Magnus–Taylor schemes for SDDEs, we combine Magnus methods for the linear homogeneous part with stochastic Taylor schemes for the remaining nonlinear terms, applying them piecewise on Bellman intervals between delay multiples.

2.2
Combined schemes

Suppose
(t_{n})_{n=-p,\ldots,N}
is a time mesh that includes times
t_{p_{k}}=\tau_{k}
, for every
k=1,\ldots,K
, where
t_{p}=\tau
. In order to combine the Taylor and Magnus schemes for the SDDE
eq.

1
, we solve the equation between the multiples of the delay times. That is, we list the delay multiples
\tau_{1},2\tau_{1},\ldots,N_{1}\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2},2\tau_{2},\ldots,N_{2}\tau_{2}
,
\ldots,

\tau_{K},2\tau_{K},\ldots,N_{K}\tau_{K}
, where

N_{k}=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\tau_{k}\geqslant T\},\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

sort these

numbers into ascending order, label these ordered delay multiples
\sigma_{1},\allowbreak\sigma_{2},\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak\sigma_{N_{\mathrm{total}}}

eq.

1
on the
Bellman intervals

[0,\sigma_{1}],\allowbreak[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}],\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak[\sigma_{{N_{\mathrm{total}}}-1},\sigma_{N_{\mathrm{total}}}]

T=\sigma_{N_{\mathrm{total}}}
(or else we may assume an additional delay
\tau_{K+1}=T
is included, and then remove any delay multiples above

).

We give further details of this derivation in the next section, but to define the Magnus–Taylor schemes for
eq.

1
, we apply Magnus approximations to the linear homogeneous part

\mathrm{d}X_{H}(t)=A_{0}X_{H}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}X_{H}(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{j}(t),

(21)

and we apply stochastic Taylor approximations to the inhomogeneous equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{R}(t)

\displaystyle=f(t,X_{R}(t),X_{R}(t-\tau_{1}),\ldots,X_{R}(t-\tau_{K}))\,\mathrm{d}t

(22)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t,X_{R}(t),X_{R}(t-\tau_{1}),\ldots,X_{R}(t-\tau_{K}))\,\mathrm{d}W_{j}(t).

The MEM scheme is produced by numerically solving
eq.

21
with the first-order Magnus approximation
eq.

16
while the SDDE EM scheme
eq.

9
is applied to
eq.

22
. Similarly, the MM scheme approximates
eq.

21
with the second-order Magnus scheme
eq.

17
while applying the SDDE Milstein scheme
eq.

10
to
eq.

22
. These schemes are applied iteratively on each Bellman interval, to produce the following numerical schemes.

Definition 6
.

Suppose
X=(X(t))_{t\in[-\tau,T]}
is the unique strong solution of
eq.

1
, being approximated by the numerical scheme
Y=(Y_{n})_{n=-p,\ldots,N}
, with
Y_{n}=Y(t_{n})=\phi(t_{n})
when

. For each

, define

Y_{n}^{\tau_{k}}=Y(t_{n}-\tau_{k})

for

the
Magnus–Euler–Maruyama (MEM)
numerical approximation of

1

and this is then used to evaluate

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=M_{n}^{[1]}(t_{n},t_{n+1})\cdot\bigg\{

\displaystyle Y_{n}+\tilde{f}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\,h_{n}

(23)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\,\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1})\bigg\},

for
n=0,\ldots,N-1
. Similarly,

is the
Magnus–Milstein (MM)
numerical approximation if it is given by finding the one-step, second-order Magnus approximation

\displaystyle M_{n}^{[2]}(t_{n},t_{n+1})=\exp\bigg(

\displaystyle\bigg(A_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}^{2}\bigg)\,h_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}\,\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}[A_{i},A_{j}](I_{ji}(t_{n},t_{n+1})-I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1}))\bigg),

finding the one-step, second-order Taylor approximation

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{n}=\bigg\{Y_{n}

\displaystyle+\tilde{f}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\,h_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\,\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\vphantom{\sum}\right.\nabla_{x}g_{j}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\cdot\left[A_{i}Y_{n}+g_{i}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\quad}\hskip 71.13188pt-A_{i}g_{j}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})\left.\vphantom{\sum}\right)\,I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}(t_{n}\geqslant\tau_{k})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigg(\nabla_{x_{\tau_{k}}}g_{j}(t_{n},Y_{n},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K}})

\displaystyle\phantom{+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}(t_{n}\geqslant\tau_{k})}\!\!\cdot\left[A_{i}Y_{n}^{\tau_{k}}+g_{i}(t_{n}^{\tau_{k}},Y_{n}^{\tau_{k}},Y_{n}^{\tau_{1},\tau_{k}},\ldots,Y_{n}^{\tau_{K},\tau_{k}})\right]\bigg)\,I_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}(t_{n},t_{n+1})\bigg\},

and then using this to evaluate

Y_{n+1}=M_{n}^{[2]}(t_{n},t_{n+1})\cdot\tilde{Y}_{n},

(24)

for each
n=0,\ldots,N-1
.

3
Derivation of schemes

Here we derive the MEM and MM schemes, primarily focusing on the MM scheme as the MEM scheme is simpler to express. We give this derivation directly, applying numerous inequalities to bound the schemes. We then retain only the terms whose mean-square expectations are multiples of the step size
h
or
h^{1/2}
, while omitting all higher-order terms. In doing this, we aim to present an intuitive derivation of the MEM and MM schemes. An alternative, concise derivation for the case of no delays is available in Yang, Burrage, Komori, Burrage, and Ding
[
46
]
, who use the Itô chain rule (also known as integration by parts for Itô SDEs).

We consider equation
eq.

1
on each of the Bellman intervals
[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}],[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}],\ldots,\allowbreak[\sigma_{{N_{\mathrm{total}}}-1},\sigma_{N_{\mathrm{total}}}]
, where
\sigma_{0}=0
. On each fixed interval
[\sigma_{b},\sigma_{b+1}]
(for some
b=0,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{total}}}-1
), we apply the decomposition by Yang et al.
[
46
]
, writing the solution
X
as the product
X(t)=X_{\mathrm{H}}(t)X_{\mathrm{R}}(t)
, for solution
X_{\mathrm{H}}
of the homogeneous
d\times d
matrix SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{\mathrm{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{0}X_{\mathrm{H}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}X_{\mathrm{H}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{j}(t),\quad t\in[\sigma_{b},\sigma_{b+1}],

(25)

where
X_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma_{b})=I_{d}
.
Equation

25
is
eq.

13
applied on the present Bellman interval rather than on
[0,T]
. In the interval
[\sigma_{b},\sigma_{b+1}]
, the past solution has been determined from
eq.

1
on prior Bellman intervals. Letting
\phi_{\sigma_{b}}:[0,\sigma_{b}]\to(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}
denote the collection of the

\phi_{\sigma_{b}}(s)=(X(s-\tau_{1}),\ldots,X(s-\tau_{K}))
, it follows from

is given by

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=X(\sigma_{b})+\int_{\sigma_{b}}^{t}X_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}(s)\tilde{f}(s,X(s),\phi_{\sigma_{b}}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

(26)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\sigma_{b}}^{t}X_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}(s)g_{j}(s,X(s),\phi_{\sigma_{b}}(s))\,\mathrm{d}W_{j}(s),\quad t\in[\sigma_{b},\sigma_{b+1}].

3.1
Magnus approximation for homogeneous part

Burrage and Burrage
[

derive Magnus expansions for Stratonovich SDEs, while Kamm, Pagliarani, and Pascucci
[

extend this to the Itô setting. We use these expansions to express

\displaystyle\Omega(\sigma_{b},t)

\displaystyle=\bigg(A_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}^{2}\bigg)\,(t-\sigma_{b})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}\,\Delta W_{j}(\sigma_{b},t)

(27)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}[A_{i},A_{j}](I_{ji}(\sigma_{b},t)-I_{ij}(\sigma_{b},t))+\cdots.

In defining the MEM scheme,

27

\Omega^{[1]}(\sigma_{b},t)=\bigg(A_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}^{2}\bigg)\,(t-\sigma_{b})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}\,\Delta W_{j}(\sigma_{b},t),

(28)

while in the MM scheme,

27
is approximated by

\displaystyle\Omega^{[2]}(\sigma_{b},t)

\displaystyle=\bigg(A_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}^{2}\bigg)\,(t-\sigma_{b})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}\,\Delta W_{j}(\sigma_{b},t)

(29)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}[A_{i},A_{j}](I_{ji}(\sigma_{b},t)-I_{ij}(\sigma_{b},t)).

All unseen terms of

-order terms of

by Kamm et al.
[

]
. We also note that

Theorem 7

3.4
Convergence analysis

3.5
Simulating second-degree Itô integrals

In order to simulate the numerical schemes
eq.

10
and
eq.

24
, we require methods to approximate the integrals
eq.

11
. We first consider the non-delayed integral
I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
, and investigate the delayed case, below. In the case
i=j
, we apply the well-known identity

I_{jj}(t_{n},t_{n+1})=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1}))^{2}-h_{n}\right],

simulating
I_{jj}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
with the value
\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
. When
i\neq j
, then
I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
is more complicated to express. One method to simulate
I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
, developed by Kloeden, Platen, and Wright
[
18
]
, consists of expanding the inner process as a Fourier series, truncated to finitely many terms. Wiktorsson
[
45
]
improves this Fourier expansion by giving expression for the remainder term, rather than truncating the Fourier series. An improvement on this remainder term is given by Mrongowius and Rößler
[
36
]
. Alternative expansions are given by Kuznetsov
[
21
,
22
]
, using different Fourier coefficients.

One simple method to approximate
I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
is the trapezium method by Milstein
[
31
]
(later called the trapezoidal method by Milstein and Tretyakov
[
32
]
). This method approximates
\tilde{I}_{ij}\approx I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
with

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{F_{n}-1}\frac{\Delta W_{i}\left(t_{n}^{(l)},t_{n}^{(l+1)}\right)\,\Delta W_{j}\left(t_{n}^{(l)},t_{n}^{(l+1)}\right)}{2}

(37)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{F_{n}-1}\Delta W_{i}\left(t_{n}^{(l)},t_{n}^{(l+1)}\right)\,\Delta W_{j}\left(t_{n}^{(l+1)},t_{n}^{(F_{n})}\right),

dividing
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
into
F_{n}
subintervals of common length, with intermittent time points
t_{n}^{(l)}=t_{n}+lh/F_{n}
for
l=0,1,\ldots,F_{n}
.

Remark 12
.

Removing the first summation from the approximation
eq.

37
produces the rectangle approximation, which is the simplest approximation of
I_{ij}(t_{n},\!t_{n+1})
that maintains the theoretical OoC (1) of the Milstein scheme, as described in
[
32
,
Theorem 1.5.4
]
.

In the case of the delayed integral, the problem of expressing
I_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
is similar to expressing
I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
, but with the delayed process values
W_{i}^{\tau_{k}}(u)=W_{i}(u-\tau_{k})
replacing
W_{i}(u)
, for
u\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
. Immediately,
W_{i}^{\tau_{k}}
and
W_{j}
are independent if
i\neq j
. However, when
i=j
, if the step sizes of the numerical scheme are no larger than the smallest delay value, then the processes
W_{j}^{\tau_{k}}
and
W_{j}
are independent on the intervals
[t_{n}-\tau_{k},t_{n+1}-\tau_{k}]
and
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
. That is, by simulating the schemes with the condition

\max_{n=0,\ldots,N}\{h_{n}\}<\min_{k=1,\ldots,K}\{\tau_{k}\},

then we may treat every instance of
I_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
similarly to
I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
, and use similar approximation methods for both.

In this paper, we simulate the double integrals
eq.

11
by approximating both
\tilde{I}_{ij}\approx I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
with
eq.

37
and
\tilde{I}_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}\approx I_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}(t_{n},t_{n+1})
with

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{F_{n}-1}\frac{\Delta W_{i}^{\tau_{k}}\left(t_{n}^{(l)},t_{n}^{(l+1)}\right)\,\Delta W_{j}\left(t_{n}^{(l)},t_{n}^{(l+1)}\right)}{2}

(38)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{F_{n}-1}\Delta W_{i}^{\tau_{k}}\left(t_{n}^{(l)},t_{n}^{(l+1)}\right)\,\Delta W_{j}\left(t_{n}^{(l+1)},t_{n}^{(F_{n})}\right),\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

where
\Delta W_{i}^{\tau_{k}}(s,t)=W_{i}(t-\tau_{k})-W_{i}(s-\tau_{k})
denotes a delayed Wiener increment, for
i=1,\ldots,m
. As we noted in
Remark
12
, simulating the integrals
eq.

11
with
eq.

37
and
eq.

38
maintains the theoretical OoC of the Milstein scheme
eq.

10
. A direct proof that this Milstein SDDE scheme has OoC
1
is also given by Cao, Zhang, and Karniadakis
[
9
]
. The requirement to simulate the Wiener processes at the intermediate time points
t_{n}^{(l)}
becomes computationally cumbersome as the number
m
of independent Wiener processes increases. Unfortunately, however, using more efficient expressions for the integrals reduces the OoC of the Milstein scheme. For example, Hofmann and Müller-Gronbach
[
15
]
simulate
eq.

10
with the approximations

\displaystyle I_{ij}(t_{n},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\Delta W_{i}(t_{n},t_{n+1})\,\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1})}{2}\quad\textrm{and}

(39)

\displaystyle I_{ij}^{\tau_{k}}(t_{n},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\Delta W_{i}^{\tau_{k}}(t_{n},t_{n+1})\,\Delta W_{j}(t_{n},t_{n+1})}{2},

which results in a numerical scheme of OoC
1/2
. This loss of accuracy, using the Riemann approximations
eq.

39
, is caused by the unbounded variation of the inner process
\int_{t_{n}}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{i}(u)=W_{i}(s)-W_{i}(t_{n})
.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced Magnus numerical schemes to simulate SDDE solutions. We constructed these schemes by extending existing SDE Magnus techniques to include constant time delays. A key motivation for this extension was the simulation of SPDDEs such as the stochastic heat equation with delayed cooling
eq.

3
. This contrasts with the traditional Euler–Maruyama scheme,
which is unsuitable for simulating
eq.

3
due to the restrictive stability condition
eq.

5
.

We presented the Magnus schemes in
section

2
, alongside the regular Taylor (EM and Milstein) schemes. We produced our Magnus schemes in
section

3
, by extending the construction of SDE Magnus schemes by Yang et al.
[
46
]
to the case of SDDEs. This involved decomposing the solution
X
of the semilinear SDDE
eq.

1
into the product
X=X_{H}X_{R}
, where
X_{H}
solves the homogeneous linear SDE
eq.

25
, and
X_{R}
satisfies the inhomogeneous integral equation
eq.

26
. We then applied Magnus expansions to approximate
X_{H}
, and stochastic Taylor expansions to approximate
X_{R}
. In
section

4
, we demonstrated that the proposed schemes achieve their theoretical orders of convergence across a range of examples, including linear and semilinear SDDEs, along with an SPDDE model
eq.

3
.

Our Magnus schemes find purpose in specific situations, such as the SPDDE problem in
Example
16
. In low-dimensional problems (
Examples
13
,
14
,
15
), their accuracy is comparable to the Taylor schemes, but they incur greater computational cost due to the matrix exponential evaluations. Consequently, when both approaches are stable and applicable, Taylor methods are typically more efficient. However, as shown in
Example
16
, the Magnus methods remain stable and accurate even when the Taylor methods fail, such as in simulations requiring large spatial dimension or coarse temporal discretization.

Future research directions include a more detailed comparison of computational costs between Magnus and Taylor schemes, particularly as spatial dimension increases. For example, in
Example
16
, when the stability condition
eq.

5
is satisfied using a sufficiently small time step
h
, it would be valuable to determine whether Magnus schemes remain computationally feasible. For larger
h
, the Magnus methods offer a practical means to simulate SPDDEs with fine spatial discretization. Although we considered only one SPDDE model here, the approach could be extended to more general stochastic parabolic PDEs with delays. The computation times of the Magnus methods for SPDDEs will also improve as methods for simulating matrix exponentials improve, whether it be from development of analytic techniques or improvements in computer processor speeds.
