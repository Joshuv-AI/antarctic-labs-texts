Piceatannol-3′-O-β-d-glucopyranoside mitigates myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by inhibiting ferroptosis through the regulation of NDUFS1 lactylation via metabolic reprogramming
Authors: Genhao Fan, Chidao Zhang, Wenqing Zhang, Xuli Ji, Menglin Liu, Peng Chen, Boyong Qiu, Yankun Song, Min Zhang, Teng Ma, Zhengwei Dong, Zuoying Xing, Yuhong Huang, Mingjun Zhu, Yongxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) is a key factor affecting the prognosis of myocardial infarction patients. Currently, there remains a lack of specific drugs targeting MIRI, and it is unclear whether Piceatannol-3′-O-β-d-glucopyranoside (PG) improves MIRI through metabolic reprogramming. Therefore, this study takes a clinically oriented approach, using multi-omics technologies to investigate how PG regulates metabolic reprogramming to improve MIRI. The study focuses particularly on PG's regulation of lactate and lactylation. Results indicate that PG enhances LVEF and LVFS, reduces cTnI and CK-MB levels, decreases ROS, lactate, and LDH levels, and increases ATP expression, thereby improving cardiac function. Metabolomics results based on clinical serum samples indicate that PG can reduce lactate and pyruvate levels. The results of research conducted on animal subjects suggest that exogenous lactate supplementation has the capacity to attenuate the cardioprotective effects of PG. PG intervention significantly suppressed the expression of PDK4, MCT1, and ACSL4 proteins while enhancing GPX4 protein expression, inhibiting lipid peroxidation, and improving mitochondrial structure and function. However, the overexpression of PDK4 led to a diminution of the PG-mediated enhancement in MIRI. Overexpression of NDUFS1 K170 lactylation similarly impaired the PG-mediated improvement of MIRI. Therefore, we conclude that PG improves MIRI by inhibiting NDUFS1 K170 lactylation through metabolic reprogramming regulation. The present study provides novel targets and therapeutic agents for MIRI intervention, while also offering fresh insights into the pathogenesis of MIRI.Keywords: Piceatannol-3′-O-β-d-glucopyranoside, Lactylation, MIRI, Ferroptosis, Metabolic reprogramming

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is one of the most common types of cardiovascular disease, with its incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise [1]. Myocardial infarction is the most severe form of IHD and the primary cause of mortality, typically resulting from coronary artery occlusion that interrupts blood flow, leading to myocardial ischemia and necrosis [2,3]. Currently, reperfusion therapy has become the gold standard for AMI treatment by restoring coronary blood flow as early as possible, playing a crucial role in preventing cardiac function deterioration and improving clinical outcomes. However, the accompanying myocardial injury significantly impacts patient prognosis. Applying cardioprotective strategies concurrently with reperfusion therapy can extend the timeframe for the early salvage of unaffected cardiac tissue [4,5]. The pathological mechanisms of MIRI include ferroptosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, calcium overload, and endothelial dysfunction [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. MIRI is a key factor in the prognosis for patients with myocardial infarction. Currently, there is a lack of specific drugs and treatment methods that target MIRI. Preventing MIRI to improve patient prognosis is a critical issue in clinical practice today. There is an urgent need for new drugs to complement current reperfusion therapies and inhibit MIRI [10].

Cardiac metabolism relies on multiple energy substrates to maintain normal physiological function [11]. During ischemia-reperfusion, conventional oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is suppressed and glycolysis becomes the primary energy source. The resulting pyruvate does not enter the mitochondria for oxidation, but instead undergoes conversion into lactate by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). This leads to an accumulation of lactate within the cell [12,13]. The excessive accumulation of lactate can lead to mitochondrial and cellular calcium overload, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction [14]. There is increasing evidence to suggest that lactylation plays a key role in the pathological process of MIRI [15]. Initial research focused primarily on histone modifications at key sites, such as H3K9, H3K18, H4K5, H4K8 and H4K12 [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, with technological advancements, lactylation studies have expanded beyond histones [21]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms of lactate and lactylation mediated by lactate in MIRI remain unclear.

PG is one of the primary active constituents extracted from the dried roots or rhizomes of Rheum lhasaense A. J. Li & P. K. Hsiao, belonging to the diphenyl ethylene glycoside class of compounds. Its molecular formula is C20H22O9, with a molecular weight of 406.1. The substance under scrutiny manifests as white spherical crystals, exhibits poor solubility in water, and demonstrates high solubility in organic solvents such as ethanol and acetone. Its melting point ranges from 218 to 222 °C [22]. It is evident that PG plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, as evidenced by its ability to inhibit a number of crucial biological processes, including oxidative stress, apoptosis, ferroptosis and inflammation. It has been demonstrated in previous studies that PG has the capacity to mitigate polymyxin-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage by activating the NRF2/HO-1 signalling pathway, thereby inhibiting neuronal apoptosis [23]. Our preliminary research indicates that PG reduces cerebral infarction volume and improves neurological function by modulating the NMDAR1/NRF2/BACH1/ACSL4 signaling pathway. This mechanism suppresses excitotoxicity and ferroptosis, thereby mitigating cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [24]. However, it remains unclear whether PG improves MIRI through metabolic reprogramming. Therefore, this study takes a clinically oriented approach, using multi-omics technologies to investigate how PG regulates metabolic reprogramming to improve MIRI. The study focuses particularly on PG's regulation of lactate and lactylation.

Materials and methods

Experimental drug

Piceatannol-3′-O-β-d-glucopyranoside (HY–N2237, MCE) and Ferrostatin-1 (HY-100579, MCE) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Sodium lactate (71718, Sigma) was purchased from Merck KGaA.

Constructing the I/R model

Adult male C57BL/6J mouse aged 8 weeks, weighing 25 ± 3 g, were provided by Speifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mouse were housed at the Experimental Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Chinese Medicine. Animals were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, temperatures maintained at 21–25 °C, and humidity levels between 40 and 60%. They had free access to food and water. All animal experiment procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the use and care of animals. Approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Chinese Medicine (YFYDW2025037). The intraperitoneal administration of 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) is required to induce anaesthesia in the mouse. The initial step in the procedure is to position the mouse in a supine position on the operating table. The surgical site must then be disinfected and shaved. A longitudinal incision should be made at the left margin of the sternum, between the third and fourth intercostal spaces. The subsequent step involves the meticulous dissection of the thoracic wall tissues using hemostatic forceps, which should be performed layer by layer, thus ensuring the complete exposure of the heart. Evert the heart. Utilising 7-0 ligature, the heart should be ligated 1-2 mm below the base of the left atrial appendage. Following a period of 30 min of ischemia, the ligature should be released. Following reperfusion of the heart for a period of 24 h, the heart and blood should be collected for subsequent studies.

Animal experiment grouping and administration

The effect of PG on mouse MIRI

The study subjects were divided into the following groups: Sham, I/R, I/R + PG (3 mg/kg/d, 6 mg/kg/d, 12 mg/kg/d), and I/R + Ferrostatin-1 (1 mg/kg/d). PG and Ferrostatin-1 were administered via tail vein injection concurrently with reperfusion. The remaining groups received an equal volume of saline solution.

Lactate weakens PG's effect on improving MIRI

The study subjects were divided into the following groups: Sham, I/R, I/R + PG (12 mg/kg/d), and I/R + Lac (1 g/kg/d). PG was administered via tail vein injection concurrently with reperfusion, while Lac was administered via tail vein injection 15 min prior to reperfusion. The remaining groups received an equal volume of saline solution.

Cell culture

HL-1 cells (STM-CL-6052, Stemrecell) are mouse cardiomyocytes purchased from Stemrecell. After HL-1 was revived, DMEM high-sugar medium (PM150210, Procell) containing 10% FBS (164220, Procell) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PB180120, Procell) were added and the cells were placed in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 saturated humidity for incubation.

Constructing the OGD/R model

Transfer cells to sugar-free medium and place them in an anaerobic chamber. Introduce a gas mixture containing 95% N2 and 5% CO2 at a flow rate of 1 L/min for 10 min. After purging the chamber completely, seal the gas inlet and outlet ports. Incubate the chamber in a 37 °C constant-temperature incubator for 6 h. After oxygen and glucose deprivation, carefully discard the glucose-free medium. Wash once with PBS, then add complete medium. Reoxygenate and re-glucose for 12 h in a cell culture incubator. Collect cells for subsequent relevant assays.

Selection of PG dosage concentration

The effects of different PG concentrations on the HL-1 cell OGD/R model were observed. Groups included: control, OGD/R, and OGD/R + PG (5 μM, 10 μM, 20 μM, 40 μM, 80 μM, 160 μM, 320 μM). The control group had its medium replaced. while the remaining groups had their medium completely replaced with sugar-free medium and placed in an anoxic chamber to establish the OGD/R model.

Cell experiment grouping and administration

Effects of PG on cellular OGD/R model

The study subjects were divided into three groups: control, OGD/R and OGD/R + PG. The OGD/R model was created by inducing oxygen and glucose deprivation for 6 h, followed by reoxygenation and rehydration for 12 h. Drugs were administered during reperfusion. Both the control and model groups received an equal volume of solvent.

Exogenous lactate supplementation attenuates the effect of PG in improving the OGD/R model

The study subjects were divided into four groups: control group, OGD/R group, OGD/R + PG group, and OGD/R + PG + Lac (10 mM) group. The OGD/R model was created by inducing oxygen and glucose deprivation for 6 h, followed by reoxygenation and rehydration for 12 h. PG and Lac were administered during reperfusion for 12 h. The control and model groups received the same volume of solvent.

Echocardiogram

Mouse were anesthetized with isoflurane (R510-22-10, RWD) gas, followed by shaving of the chest hair. A short-axis M-mode image was obtained at the level of the left ventricular papillary muscles. Analyze the anterior wall thickness, left ventricular diameter, and posterior wall thickness at three consecutive end-systolic and end-diastolic phases, calculate the mean values, and measure the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS).

TTC/Evans blue staining

After reperfusion, 1 % Evans blue dye (E8010, Solarbio) was administrated into the heart cavity via the ascending aorta. The heart was subsequently frozen at −80°Cfor 20 min and cut into 2 mm sections. Place the slices in 2% TTC (G3005, Solarbio) for staining, then incubate at 37 °C for 20 min. Next, fix the tissue sections in 4% paraformaldehyde (P1110, Solarbio) for 24 h. The infarct area was quantified using Image J software.

ELISA

Both mouse cTnI (EM1466, Fine Test) and creatine kinase MB isoenzyme (EM0929, Fine Test) were purchased from Fine Test. All assays were performed according to the manufacturers' instructions.

Transmission electron microscope

Using a scalpel, cut the ischaemic myocardial region into small pieces, each approximately 0.5–1 mm3 in volume and no more than 2 mm in length. Add 2.5% glutaraldehyde dropwise and store at 4 °C overnight. Then, fix in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution for 2 h. Treat in 0.1 mol/L carboxylate buffer solution for 1 h, then wash the tissue with PBS buffer solution. Dehydrate, embed, section, stain and image using a TEM (JEM-2000EX).

Detection of reactive oxygen species in tissue sections

Thaw frozen sections at room temperature and blot dry. Use a histochemistry pen to draw circles around the tissue. Add drops of autofluorescence quencher (P0122, Beyotime) within the circles. Incubate at room temperature for 5 min, then rinse with pure water for 10 min. Then add ROS staining solution (G1746, Servicebio) dropwise, incubate at 37 °C in a dark incubator for 30 min, and wash three times with PBS for 5 min each. Add DAPI staining solution (C1006, Beyotime), incubate at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. Wash three times with PBS for 5 min each. Cover with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium (P0126, Beyotime). Observe staining using a slide scanner (Panoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH).

LDH activity assay

Prepare nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) standard solution and WST-8 colorimetric working solution according to the LDH activity assay kit. Add 50 μL of WST-8 working solution to each well containing an appropriate amount of serum. Immediately measure the absorbance at 450 nm. Subsequently, incubate at 37 °C for 30 min and measure the absorbance again. Calculate the difference between the two absorbance values and determine the LDH activity based on the standard curve.

Lactate testing

Take an appropriate amount of tissue or cells, then add 100 μL of sample preparation solution. Place it in a low-temperature grinder and grind it. Centrifuge at 12,000 g for 5 min and collect the supernatant. Transfer 50 μL of the sample to a 96-well plate. Add 50 μL of WST-8 colour development working solution to each well and mix thoroughly. Incubate at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Measure the absorbance at 450 nm. Establish a standard curve and calculate the lactate concentration in the sample.

Detect ATP

Take an appropriate amount of tissue or cells and add 100 μL of sample preparation solution. Place the mixture in a low-temperature grinder to homogenise it. Centrifuge at 12,000 g for 5 min, then collect the supernatant. Add 100 μL of the ATP assay working solution to the detection well and leave it to stand at room temperature for 5 min. Add 20 μL of the sample or standard to the detection well. Mix thoroughly and measure the relative light unit (RLU) value using a chemiluminescence reader. Calculate the ATP concentration based on the standard curve.

CCK-8 assay for cell viability detection

Set up a blank control group. After treatment, replace the culture medium. Add 10 μL of CCK-8 solution to each well. Incubate in the incubator for 30 min. Finally, measure the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Calculate and analyze the data.

Detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species

Cells were co-incubated with CM-H2DCFDA (10μΜ, S0035S, Beyotime) and MitoSO™ Red (10μΜ, S0061S, Beyotime) in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min and washed 3 times with PBS. Fluorescent photographs were then taken with Fluorescence Microscope (DMI3006, Leica), and 3 fields of view were randomly selected and statistically analyzed using Image J software.

Mitochondrial activity assay

Cells were co-incubated with Mito-Tracker Deep Red FM (200 nM, C1032, Beyotime) in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min and washed 3 times with PBS. Fluorescent photographs were then taken with Fluorescence Microscope (DMI3006, Leica), and 3 fields of view were randomly selected and statistically analyzed using Image J software.

BODIPY 581/591C11 for detecting lipid peroxidation levels

Cells were co-incubated with BODIPY 581/591C11 (2μΜ, S0043S, Beyotime) in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min and washed 3 times with PBS. Fluorescent photographs were then taken with Fluorescence Microscope (DMI3006, Leica), and 3 fields of view were randomly selected and statistically analyzed using Image J software.

RhoNox-6 detects ferrous ion levels

Cells were co-incubated with RhoNox-6 (2μΜ, S1070S, Beyotime) in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min and washed 3 times with PBS. Fluorescent photographs were then taken with Fluorescence Microscope (DMI3006, Leica), and 3 fields of view were randomly selected and statistically analyzed using Image J software.

Western blot analysis

Following cell or tissue processing, cell lysis was achieved through the utilisation of RIPA buffer (containing 1% protease and 1% phosphatase inhibitors) (R0010, Solarbio). This step was undertaken to facilitate the release of proteins. Protein quantification was performed using the BCA assay kit. The same amounts of protein were separated using SDS-PAGE gel (CW0022 M, Kangwei Century) and transferred on PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% skim-milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies for rabbit PDK4 (12949-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit MCT1 (20139-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit GPX4 (30388-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit ACSL4 (22401-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit NDUFS1(12444-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit β-actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech), and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 60 min. The immunoreactive signals were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (WBKLS0500, Millipore). The target proteins were visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Metabolomics testing

Source of human serum samples

The human serum samples utilized in this study were obtained from the “Multiple-Dose Tolerability and Pharmacokinetic Study” element of the randomised, blinded, dose-escalation, placebo-controlled Phase I clinical trial that was conducted to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic characteristics of PG for injection following single and multiple administrations in healthy subjects. This trial was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No.: 2021-034-01). Human serum samples were collected as whole blood specimens 60 min prior to dosing on Day 1 and 23 h after needle removal on Day 14. The samples were subjected to a centrifugal process at a speed of 1500g for a duration of 10 min at a temperature of 4 °C. Thereafter, the upper layer of the samples, known as the ‘supernatant’, was collected. The serum was stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer set at −80 °C. Should further information regarding the protocol of the trial be required, reference should be made to the Clinical Trial Registry and Information Disclosure Platform (Registration Number: CTR20202022).

The detection of lactylation proteomics

Tissue samples were ground into powder using liquid nitrogen. Approximately 20 mg of powder were resuspended in 200 μL lysis buffer 4% SDS (436143, Sigma)100 mM DTT (D0632, Sigma), 150 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0). Undissolved cellular debris were removed by centrifugation at 16000g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (ab102536, Abcam). The proteins were transferred into 1.5 mL Nanosep tube and centrifuge for 20min at 14000g at 20 °C to remove the DTT. Then 100 μL IAA in UA buffer (8 M Urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0) as added to block reduced cysteine residues and the samples were incubated for 20 min in darkness. Then, add 200 μL buffer ammonium bicarbonate (100 mM) to the beads in the filter cartridge, centrifuge for 15 min at 14000g at 25 °C. Finally, added 50-100 μL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer and incubated at 37 °C for 20h. The peptides were harvested by centrifugation, then acidified by 1% FA and subsequently dried by a refrigerated CentriVap concentrator. Finally, the tryptic digested peptides were desalted with C18 stage tip. LC-MS/MS were performed on a Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer coupled with Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides from each sample were loaded into a column (50 cm Low-Load μPAC™ Neo HPLC Column, Thermo Scientific) at a flow rate of 2.2 μL/min. The DIA method consisted of a survey scan from 380 to 980 m/z at resolution 240000 with AGC target of 500% and 5 ms injection time. The DIA MS/MS scans were acquired by Astral from 150 to 2000 m/z with 2 m/z isolation window and with AGC target of 500% and 3 ms injection time. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.1. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, independent samples t-test or paired t-test was used. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Following a significant ANOVA result, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey's multiple comparisons test. The threshold of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

PG inhibits MIRI by reducing lactate levels

The chemical structural formula of PG is shown in Fig. 1A. We established an I/R mouse model to observe the protective effects of PG. The results showed that the PG intervention significantly improved MIRI compared with the I/R group. This was manifested as a reduction in infarct size (Fig. 1E and F), an enhancement in LVEF and LVFS (Fig. 1B–D), a decrease in cTnI and CK-MB (Fig. 1G–H), a reduction in ROS (Fig. 1I and J), lactate (Fig. 1L) and LDH (Fig. 1K) levels, and an increase in ATP expression levels (Fig. 1N). We employed HL-1 cells to establish an OGD/R model to observe the protective effects of PG. Results indicate that PG suppresses lactate levels (Fig. 1M) and increases ATP expression levels (Fig. 1O). In addition, we observed the effects of PG on mitochondrial structure. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that compared with the I/R group, the PG group exhibited reduced mitochondrial cristae disruption and fragmentation, suppressed membrane density increase, and significantly improved mitochondrial structure (Fig. 1P–Q). In summary, these findings suggest that the protective effect of PG on MIRI may be associated with the suppression of lactate levels and the improvement of mitochondrial structure.

PG reduces lactate levels in subjects' serum by regulating metabolic reprogramming

We have preliminarily established that PG can improve MIRI, potentially through inhibiting lactate production and enhancing mitochondrial function. However, whether this holds true in humans remains to be seen. We collected serum samples from Phase I clinical trial subjects before and after PG administration for metabolomic analysis, investigating whether PG confers cardioprotective effects by modulating metabolic reprogramming. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) methods are supervised statistical methods that maximize differences between groups by pre-determined groupings, remove information irrelevant to sample classification, differentiate the metabolic profiles of samples, and look for differential metabolites before and after drug administration (Fig. 2A). Based on the results of the OPLS-DA model, metabolites with a variable importance in projection (VIP) value greater than 1 were selected. The reliability of metabolite association was assessed using correlation coefficients, and through multivariate statistical analysis, 181 differentially expressed metabolites were ultimately identified (Fig. 2B). By combining univariate statistical analysis with intergroup functional connectivity (FC), 92 differentially expressed metabolites were identified (Fig. 2C). The intersection of differentially expressed metabolites identified through univariate and multivariate statistical analyses yielded 83 relatively reliable differentially expressed metabolites (Fig. 2D). A heatmap was generated based on these potential differentially expressed metabolites (Fig. 2E). KEGG analysis results indicate that the metabolic pathways of alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, as well as glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, are closely associated with lactate metabolism (Fig. 2F). Glycolysis is the direct source of lactate production. Some of the pyruvate generated by glycolysis is reduced to lactate, while the remainder undergoes transamination to form alanine. In absolute quantification of differential metabolites, lactate and pyruvate were both significantly suppressed following administration (Fig. 2G–H). In summary, PG can regulate glycolytic metabolic reprogramming to reduce lactate levels in the subjects' serum.

Exogenous lactate supplementation attenuates the effect of PG in improving MIRI

The experimental results, both in vitro and in vivo, indicate that PG has the capacity to inhibit lactate production. The findings of the metabolomics study demonstrate that PG exerts a regulatory influence over glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, thereby reducing lactate levels in the serum of subjects. Therefore, this section will examine whether the effect of PG on improving MIRI is diminished when lactate is added exogenously. Results showed that compared with the PG group, the lactate-supplemented group exhibited significantly reduced LVEF and LVFS (Fig. 3A–C), elevated cTnI and CK-MB (Fig. 3D–E), further expanded the infarct area (Fig. 3F–G), markedly increased ROS production (Fig. 3H–I), exacerbated mitochondrial cristae disruption and fragmentation (Fig. 3J–K), elevated lactate and LDH levels (Fig. 3L–M), and reduced ATP levels (Fig. 3N). Additionally, an in vitro experiment was conducted to establish an OGD/R model to observe whether exogenous lactate could also attenuate the effect of PG in enhancing ATP production. Results showed that exogenous lactate supplementation inhibited ATP production compared to the PG group (Fig. 3O). The results indicate that exogenous lactate supplementation attenuates the effect of PG in improving MIRI.

In vivo experimental results indicate that PG inhibits ferroptosis by suppressing lactate accumulation through PDK4 inhibition

Bioinformatics analysis indicates that PDK4 is a potential target, and molecular docking results demonstrate strong binding affinity between PDK4 and PG. SPR analysis demonstrated that PG binds to recombinant PDK4 protein in a dose-dependent manner, yielding an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 5.39 μM, confirming a direct biophysical interaction (Supplementary File, Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). PDK4 is widely distributed throughout tissues, plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial function, and is closely associated with cardiovascular diseases [25]. PDK4 has been shown to phosphorylate serine residues on pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH) subunits, resulting in their inactivation and, consequently, the inhibition of PDH function. This process has been shown to reduce the entry of pyruvate into the mitochondria for the purpose of oxidation, thereby indirectly promoting the conversion of pyruvate to lactate [26]. Excessive lactate accumulation has been demonstrated to result in mitochondrial dysfunction. Therefore, inhibiting PDK4 can improve MIRI. Western blot analysis revealed that PG intervention significantly suppressed the expression of PDK4, MCT1, and ACSL4 proteins while increasing GPX4 protein expression compared to the I/R group (Fig. 4A–E). Additionally, immunofluorescence results demonstrated that PG intervention significantly suppressed PDK4 protein expression (Fig. 4F–G). These findings indicate that PG inhibits ferroptosis by regulating PDK4, MCT1, ACSL4, and GPX4. However, compared with the PG group, exogenous lactate supplementation promoted the expression of PDK4, MCT1, and ACSL4 proteins while suppressing GPX4 protein expression (Fig. 4H–L). This indicates that exogenous lactate supplementation weakened PG's regulatory effect on PDK4, MCT1, ACSL4, and GPX4, thereby increasing cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis.

In vitro experiments suggest that PG inhibits ferroptosis by preventing the accumulation of lactate through the inhibition of PDK4

CCK8 results indicate that PG enhances cell survival in the HL-1 cell OGD/R model (Supplementary File, Fig. 4). In vitro and in vivo experimental results are consistent. Western blot analysis revealed that PG intervention significantly suppressed the expression of PDK4, MCT1, and ACSL4 proteins while increasing GPX4 protein expression compared to the OGD/R group (Fig. 5A–E). In addition, we utilized fluorescent probes to observe the regulatory effect of PG on ferroptosis in the HL-1 cell OGD/R model. Results showed that compared with the OGD/R group, PG intervention significantly suppressed intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 5F–G) and mitochondrial ROS levels (Fig. 5J–K), enhanced mitochondrial function (Fig. 5H–I), reduced ferrous ion content (Fig. 5N–O), and inhibited intracellular lipid peroxidation levels (Fig. 5L–M). In order to verify the hypothesis that exogenous lactate supplementation can attenuate the effects of PG, lactate was added to cells and its impact on the expression levels of relevant proteins was observed. The results demonstrated that, in comparison with the PG group, exogenous lactate supplementation led to an enhancement in the expression of PDK4, MCT1, and ACSL4 proteins, whilst concurrently suppressing GPX4 protein expression (Fig. 5P–T). This finding suggests that exogenous lactate supplementation has the capacity to attenuate the regulatory influence of PG on PDK4, MCT1, ACSL4, and GPX4.

Overexpression of PDK4 attenuates PG inhibition of ferroptosis

In order to further validate the hypothesis that PG exerts its effects through PDK4, a reverse validation was performed using lentiviral overexpression of PDK4 to observe its impact on ferroptosis-related markers. Western blot analysis revealed that compared with the LV-Ctrl group, the LV-PDK4 group exhibited significantly increased expression levels of PDK4 and ACSL4 proteins, while GPX4 protein expression was significantly suppressed (Fig. 6A–D). The results of the study demonstrate that the overexpression of PDK4 enhances the sensitivity of HL-1 cells to ferroptosis. Next, we investigated whether overexpression of PDK4 could diminish the therapeutic efficacy of PG. Western blot analysis revealed that, in comparison with the LV-Ctrl + OGD/R + PG group, the LV-PDK4+OGD/R + PG group exhibited significantly increased PDK4 and ACSL4 protein expression levels, whilst GPX4 protein expression was significantly suppressed (Fig. 6E–H). Additionally, we used fluorescent probes to observe whether PDK4 overexpression could attenuate the inhibitory effect of PG on ferroptosis-related markers. The results demonstrated that, in comparison with the LV-Ctrl + OGD/R + PG group, the LV-PDK4+OGD/R + PG group exhibited significantly increased intracellular and mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 6I–L), markedly reduced mitochondrial function (Fig. 6M–N), further exacerbated lipid peroxidation (Fig. 6O–P), and significantly elevated ferrous ion levels (Fig. 6Q–R). Therefore, we conclude that overexpression of PDK4 weakens the role of PG in inhibiting ferroptosis.

PG inhibits NDUFS1 K170 lactylation

Lactate regulates cellular functions by influencing protein lysine lactylation (Kla). Therefore, we performed lactylation proteomics analysis on myocardial tissue from Sham, I/R, and PG groups (Fig. 7A). The number of Kla sites in each protein is shown in Fig. 7B. We analyzed the relationship between the amino acids surrounding the identified Kla sites and all human background sequences using the iceLogo tool. As shown in Fig. 7C, lactylation sites exhibit significant sequence preference. Differential analysis revealed that compared with the I/R group, 23 lactylation-modified peptides were downregulated and 27 were upregulated in the Sham group. Compared with the I/R group, 61 lactylation-modified peptides were downregulated and 45 were upregulated in the PG group (Fig. 7D–E). GSEA pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins revealed significant enrichment of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway (Fig. 7F), suggesting that PG may exert its effects by regulating oxidative phosphorylation. Through protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis for scoring and screening key proteins, we identified NDUFS1 as a hub protein among known Kla proteins (Fig. 7G–I). Mitochondrial Complex I comprise 45 subunits, and 7 of the 14 core subunits are encoded by nuclear genes. NDUFS1, the largest core subunit encoded by nuclear genes, is a part of the eight iron-sulfur chains and is responsible for the oxidation of NADH. NDUFS1 is involved in the process of oxidative phosphorylation, which supplies ATP to cardiomyocytes and maintains the structural and functional integrity of mitochondria [27]. Lactylation proteomics results revealed that the NDUFS1 K170 site was significantly downregulated in the PG group compared to the I/R group (Fig. 7J). NDUFS1 K170 was found to be highly conserved among different species (Fig. 7K). The above results indicate that PG can inhibit the lactylation of NDUFS1 K170.

PG improves cardiac function by downregulating lactylation of NDUFS1 K170

Lactylation proteomics results indicate that PG inhibits NDUFS1 K170 lactylation. We validated the results of lactylation proteomics by collecting myocardial tissues and using the pan-antibody to detect the total protein lactylation levels. Results showed that lactylation levels were significantly increased in the I/R group, and were significantly suppressed after PG loading (Fig. 8A). Previous studies have shown that mutating lysine (K) to threonine (T) can simulate lactylation and that mutating lysine (K) to arginine (R) can simulate delactylation [28,29]. Next, we used adeno-associated virus to generate NDUFS1 mutant mouse. The results showed that, compared with the I/R + EV group, the I/R + NDUFS1WT group exhibited significantly higher levels of LVEF and LVFS, as well as increased ATP expression in mouse. Meanwhile, cTnI and CK-MB levels were markedly reduced and lactate and LDH levels were significantly suppressed. However, compared with the I/R + NDUFS1WT group, the aforementioned relevant indicators significantly deteriorated in the I/R + NDUFS1K170T group (Fig. 8B–I), indicating that NDUFS1 K170 lactylation exacerbates myocardial injury. Next, we compare the lactylation and delactylation groups. The results showed that, compared with the I/R + NDUFS1K170T group, the I/R + NDUFS1K170R group exhibited significantly higher levels of LVEF, LVFS and ATP expression, as well as markedly lower levels of cTnI, CK-MB, lactate and LDH (Fig. 8J–Q). These results suggest that NDUFS1 K170 delactylation can mitigate myocardial injury. Finally, we examined whether lactylation would diminish the pharmacological efficacy of PG. Results showed that compared with the I/R + PG + NDUFS1WT group, the aforementioned indicators further deteriorated in the I/R + PG + NDUFS1K170T group, with the cardioprotective effect of PG significantly attenuated (Fig. 8R–Y). In summary, PG suppresses NDUFS1 K170 lactylation to inhibit myocardial injury.

PG maintains the stability of NDUFS1 protein and inhibits ferroptosis

Previous studies have demonstrated that lactylation can influence the stability of the protein itself [29]. Therefore, we first used Western blot to detect the expression levels of the NDUFS1 protein. The results demonstrated that NDUFS1 protein expression levels were significantly suppressed in the I/R group, but markedly increased following PG administration. However, the increase in NDUFS1 protein expression induced by PG was found to be inhibited by exogenously added lactate (Fig. 9A-B). Furthermore, as exogenous lactate levels increased, NDUFS1 protein expression levels progressively decreased, indicating a negative correlation between NDUFS1 protein expression and lactate levels (Fig. 9C-D). Exogenous lactate supplementation further increased total protein lactylation levels (Fig. 9E). In order to ascertain whether lactylation of NDUFS1 K170 impacts the expression levels of ferroptosis-related proteins, Western blot analysis was employed to detect relevant markers. The results demonstrated that, in comparison with the I/R + EV group, the I/R + NDUFS1WT group exhibited significantly elevated levels of NDUFS1 and GPX4, whilst ACSL4 was significantly suppressed. However, compared with the I/R + NDUFS1WT group, the I/R + NDUFS1K170T group exhibited significantly suppressed NDUFS1 and GPX4 expression, while ACSL4 expression was significantly elevated (Fig. 9F-I). Overexpression of NDUFS1 suppresses ferroptosis, while NDUFS1 K170 lactylation increases cardiomyocyte susceptibility to ferroptosis. In comparison with the I/R + NDUFS1K170T group, the I/R + NDUFS1K170R group demonstrated a marked increase in NDUFS1 and GPX4 expression, accompanied by a substantial suppression of ACSL4 expression (Fig. 9J-M). This finding suggests that NDUFS1 K170 delactylation exerts a regulatory effect on ferroptosis. In comparison with the I/R + PG + NDUFS1WT group, the NDUFS1 and GPX4 expression levels were significantly suppressed, while ACSL4 expression levels were significantly increased in the I/R + PG + NDUFS1K170T group. This finding suggests that NDUFS1 K170 lactylation may have a role in the attenuation of PG's function in the inhibition of ferroptosis. The results of the present study demonstrate that PG improves cardiac function by inhibiting NDUFS1 K170 lactylation and maintaining NDUFS1 protein stability.

Discussion

The present study employs multi-omics integrated analysis to explore the communicative functions between metabolic reprogramming and post-translational modifications. It provides new perspectives for investigating the mechanisms underlying MIRI intervention and identifies effective Chinese herbal monomers for therapeutic application. Our previous research indicates that PG can suppress ferroptosis and improve brain ischemia-reperfusion injury [23]. This study explores the potential of PG to improve MIRI, based on its inhibitory effect on ferroptosis. Firstly, an I/R mouse model was established in order to observe the protective effects of PG against myocardial injury. The results demonstrated that PG improved LVEF and LVFS, reduced cTnI and CK-MB levels, decreased ROS, lactate, and LDH levels, enhanced ATP expression, and improved mitochondrial structure and function, thereby inhibiting myocardial injury. Metabolomics results based on clinical serum samples indicate that PG can reduce lactate and pyruvate levels. Utilising exogenous lactate supplementation for reverse validation, the results indicated that exogenous lactate supplementation diminished the cardioprotective effects of PG.

Previous studies have demonstrated that targeting PDK4 is central to regulating lactate and lactylation [30]. Phosphorylation of serine residues on the PDH subunits by PDK4 inactivates them, thereby inhibiting PDH function, reducing the entry of pyruvate into the mitochondria for oxidation, and indirectly promoting the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, with excessive lactate accumulation leading to mitochondrial dysfunction [31]. Inhibition of PDK4 has been demonstrated to reduce infarct size in MIRI mouse models, improve LVEF, enhance pyruvate utilisation, and reduce markers of the DNA damage response [25]. However, in some cancer cells, inhibiting PDK4 can induce ferroptosis, which appears to be contradictory, primarily because different cell types exhibit fundamental differences in their energy metabolism patterns [32,33]. Our experimental results demonstrate that PG intervention significantly suppresses the expression of the PDK4, MCT1 and ACSL4 proteins, while enhancing the expression of the GPX4 protein. This inhibits lipid peroxidation and improves mitochondrial structure and function. However, PDK4 overexpression attenuates the ameliorative effects of PG on MIRI. Next, we generated an animal model with the NDUFS1 K170 site mutation. Results showed that inhibiting NDUFS1 K170 lactylation improved MIRI, and PG suppressed NDUFS1 K170 lactylation to improve MIRI. Therefore, PG improves MIRI by inhibiting NDUFS1 K170 lactylation through metabolic reprogramming regulation. Our study provides novel therapeutic targets for MIRI intervention and offers fresh insights into its pathogenesis.

The mechanism of ferroptosis involves complex interactions between iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and dysfunction of the antioxidant system [34]. Acetyl-CoA synthase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) is an enzyme that plays a role in fatty acid metabolism. It is recognised as a specific biomarker and driver of ferroptosis. The upregulation of ACSL4 increases the content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in phospholipids, which are susceptible to oxidative reactions that lead to ferroptosis. Research suggests that reducing ACSL4 expression could mitigate MIRI. [35]. Dysfunction of the antioxidant system is a key factor in ferroptosis during MIRI, particularly due to the suppression of the System Xc-/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant defense system, leading to excessive accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides. The System Xc is composed of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2) [36]. Inhibiting System Xc-decreases GPX4 expression, thereby reducing antioxidant capacity and increasing the susceptibility of cardiac myocytes to ferroptosis [37]. Therefore, inhibiting ferroptosis is an effective way to intervene in MIRI. The results of this study suggest that PG can inhibit lipid peroxidation and suppress the expression of the ACSL4 protein. It can also increase the expression of the GPX4 protein, improve mitochondrial structure and function, and thereby inhibit ferroptosis and ameliorate MIRI.

The imbalance in mitochondrial energy metabolism homeostasis caused by excessive lactate accumulation is a driving factor in the occurrence and development of ferroptosis in MIRI. Cardiac metabolism relies on multiple energy substrates to maintain normal physiological function. During ischemia-reperfusion, conventional OXPHOS is suppressed and glycolysis becomes the primary energy source. The resulting pyruvate does not enter the mitochondria for oxidation, but instead undergoes conversion into lactate by LDH. This leads to an accumulation of lactate within the cell [12,13]. The excessive accumulation of lactate can lead to mitochondrial and cellular calcium overload and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction [14]. Mitochondria are the hub of cellular energy metabolism. Dysfunction of these organelles generates excessive ROS, which can lead to ferroptosis induced by lipid peroxidation [38,39]. PDK4 is widely distributed throughout tissues, plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial function, and is closely associated with cardiovascular disease [40]. PDK4 phosphorylates serine residues on the PDH subunits, thereby inactivating them and inhibiting PDH function. This reduces the entry of pyruvate into the mitochondria for oxidation and indirectly promotes its conversion to lactate. Excessive lactate accumulation can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction [31]. Silent PDK4 reduces infarct size in MIRI mouse, improves LVEF, enhances pyruvate utilisation, and thereby improves cardiac function [25]. Therefore, targeting PDK4 is central to regulating lactate and lactylation. The results of this study indicate that PG suppresses PDK4 protein expression, thereby reducing lactate accumulation and improving MIRI. However, overexpression of PDK4 attenuates the effect of PG in improving MIRI.

There is increasing evidence to suggest that lactylation plays a key role in the pathological process of MIRI [15]. As the largest core subunit of mitochondrial complex I, NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit S1 (NDUFS1) serves as a pivotal nodal protein linking energy metabolism and redox balance. Disruptions to its function can lead to impaired mitochondrial function [41]. The heart's ability to contract and relax requires a continuous supply of ATP, which is produced through the process of oxidative phosphorylation that occurs within mitochondria [42,43]. Mitochondrial complex I is the initial enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and the largest enzyme in this chain. It is responsible for transferring electrons from reduced NADH to coenzyme Q10, and this process is coupled with the transport of protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space [44]. Mitochondrial complex I in mammals comprises 45 subunits. Among the 14 core subunits, 7 are encoded by nuclear genes [44]. NDUFS1, the largest core subunit encoded by nuclear genes, is a part of the eight iron-sulfur chains and is responsible for the oxidation of NADH [45,46]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NDUFS1 expression is reduced in the hearts of heart failure patients and myocardial infarction mouse. Cardiac-specific overexpression of NDUFS1 improves myocardial injury by alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis [27]. However, reduced NDUFS1 may lead to mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes [47]. The results of this study indicate that during MIRI, increased NDUFS1 K170 lactylation leads to reduced NDUFS1 protein expression, thereby exacerbating myocardial injury. However, PG intervention can inhibit NDUFS1 K170 lactylation and enhance the stability of the NDUFS1 protein. However, the scientific mechanisms by which lactate specifically drives NDUFS1 K170 lactylation, and how NDUFS1 K170 lactylation reduces NDUFS1 protein expression, remain unclear and require further in-depth investigation in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of this study indicate that PG suppresses ferroptosis and improves MIRI by inhibiting PDK4, reducing lactate accumulation, suppressing NDUFS1 K170 lactylation, and enhancing NDUFS1 protein stability.
