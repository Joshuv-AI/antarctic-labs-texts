High heat exposure and climate anxiety among rural women in Tamil Nadu: health and social impacts
Authors: Gayathri KG, Rekha S, Vidhya Venugopal, Vijayalakshmi P, Krishnan S
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtreme heat events pose a significant threat to human health, affecting both physical and mental well-being. Women in rural areas suffer from heat exposure due to their gender-specific responsibilities and restricted cooling access, which elevates their climate-related psychological distress. This study examines the intersection between heat exposure, physiological strain, and heat-related mental health burdens among rural women in Tamil Nadu, India.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between April and August 2025 across 15 rural villages in Tamil Nadu, India, among 350 rural women. Ambient temperature was quantified using Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) monitors (QuestTemp 3 M) during the peak hours. Data was collected using a structured, modified HOTHAPS questionnaire that covered demographics, heat exposure patterns, self-reported heat-related illnesses (HRIs), and the social impacts of heat. Psychological stress due to heat exposures was evaluated using a culturally adapted Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale (HEAS), a multi-domain tool that measures rumination, affective responses, behavioral changes, and personal impact concerns. Multivariable logistic regression was performed in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 16.0 to examine associations between heat exposure and health outcomes, adjusting for age, education, income, and the availability of cooling resources.ResultsParticipants (mean age 34.5 ± 7.5 years) experienced an average daily WBGT of 30.4 ± 2.3 °C, with 85% women exposed above the WBGT (≥ 28 °C). 75% of women reported HRIs, primarily fatigue (60%), heat rash (57%), and muscle cramps (45%). Exposure to high heat (WBGT ≥ 28.0 °C) increased the risk of HRI (OR: 10.5; 95% CI: 5.7–19.1). 80% of them reported moderate-to-severe heat-related psychological distress, with 34% reporting severe levels. Eco-anxiety was significantly higher among those with extreme heat exposure (OR: 6.3, 95% CI: 2.9–14.1), and this exposure independently predicted impaired household functioning, including reduced caregiving capacity (aOR: 3.1, 95% CI: 1.8–5.4).ConclusionsRural women in Tamil Nadu experience substantial heat-related physical and psychological health burdens. The high prevalence of HRIs and eco-anxiety levels among women underscores urgent needs for gender-sensitive heat adaptation strategies integrating both physical protection and mental health support in climate resilience planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27397-y.Keywords: Climate health, Heat stress, Eco-anxiety, Rural women, Gender vulnerability, Mental health

Background

Climate change has emerged as the most critical public health challenge of the 21st century, as extreme heat events are becoming more frequent, intense, and prolonged worldwide [1]. Rising temperatures pose a significant threat to human health, affecting both physiological functioning and mental well-being [2]. Among various climate hazards, extreme heat was recognized as the leading cause for morbidity and mortality, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3, 4]. India is more prone to heat stress due to its tropical climate, high population density, and large rural workforce, with climate projections showing a rise in both extreme heat days and heatwave events in the upcoming decades [3].

Various studies have analysed the health effects of heat exposure based on physical symptoms such as heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and dehydration [5]. Emerging research evidence proves that heat exposure can decrease work performance and even potentially cause chronic illnesses such as renal impairment and cardiovascular issues [4, 6]. In addition to the effects on physical health, exposure to heat also significantly influences mental health. Higher temperatures are substantially associated with increased psychological distress, anxiety, mood disorders, sleep disturbances, and worsening of pre-existing mental health conditions [7]. Despite this growing evidence, heat-related mental health outcomes remain underexplored, particularly in rural and low-resource settings.

In recent days, the term eco-anxiety has increased recognition as a prominent psychological response to climate change. It is broadly defined as persistent worry, fear, helplessness, and emotional distress arising from awareness of environmental degradation and anticipated climate-related threats [8]. While eco-anxiety is not considered a mental health issue, it is an emotional burden that may affect various aspects and factors related to functioning and overall well-being [9]. Much existing research on eco-anxiety has focused on adolescents, young adults, and urban populations in high-income countries, where climate concerns are often mediated through media exposure and future-oriented perceptions [10]. In contrast, eco-anxiety among rural populations in LMICs is not a concern about the future, but a reaction towards the degraded present, which links lived experiences of immediate climate stressors, such as extreme heat, water scarcity, health impacts, and livelihood insecurity [11].

Women in rural India form a particularly vulnerable social category in the context of climate-related heat stress [12]. Their gender roles often position them at the center of domestic labor, caregiving, agricultural work, and informal employment, resulting in prolonged exposure to heat across indoor and outdoor environments [13]. Activities such as cooking, childcare, water collection, and farm labour were frequently performed under high thermal stress, without rest or hydration [14]. Structural gender inequalities, including restricted access to medical care, limited financial resources, and lack of power in decision-making exacerbates women’s vulnerability to climate-related health risks [15]. Structural poverty limits their ability to live in thermally efficient homes, which plays a critical role in shaping heat exposure in rural settings [16]. In India, most rural houses are built with materials that retain heat well and are poorly ventilated, resulting in indoor temperatures higher than outdoor temperatures for weeks during the hottest summer months [17]. Studies have shown that such housing conditions provide minimal thermal comfort, particularly for women who spend substantial time indoors performing domestic work [18]. As a result, they experience continuous heat exposure throughout the day, contributing not only to physical discomfort and illness but also to psychological stress and emotional exhaustion [19].

Existing studies have focused mainly on occupational heat exposure or urban populations, with limited focus on objective heat exposure measurements and psychosocial outcomes. Moreover, the social dimensions of eco-anxiety, such as its impact on family relationships, social participation, and caregiving responsibilities has less attention, particularly in gendered perspectives [7]. These social disruptions are important to examine in collectivist societies, where family functioning and social support play major roles in individual well-being. Coping and adaptation strategies to manage heat stress constitute another underexplored area of research [20]. The short-term, individual adaptations advocated for rural women include increased water consumption, reduced physical activity, staying in the shade, and using cooling devices. While these strategies may offer temporary relief, they may not be sufficient to address chronic heat exposure and may place additional economic and emotional burdens on households [21]. Furthermore, the extent to which such coping strategies mitigate psychological distress, including eco-anxiety, remains unclear [10]. This study aims to examine the association between climate-related heat exposure and eco-anxiety among rural women in Tamil Nadu, India. The primary research question guiding this study is: What is the association between climate-related heat exposure and eco-anxiety, heat-related illness, and social functioning among rural women in Tamil Nadu, India. The specific objectives are, (1) to quantify environmental heat exposure using objective WBGT measurements; (2) to assess the prevalence of self-reported heat-related illnesses and eco-anxiety; and (3) to examine associations between high heat exposure and physical, psychological, and social outcomes after adjusting for socio-demographic confounders. By focusing on rural women, an underrepresented yet highly vulnerable group, the growing literature on climate change and mental health provides evidence to inform gender-sensitive heat adaptation and mental health interventions.

Methods

Study design and study population

This cross-sectional study examined heat-related eco-anxiety among rural women in Tamil Nadu, India, across 15 villages in the Thiruvallur, Kanchipuram, and Ranipet districts. The three districts selected purposively based on: their classification as heat-prone districts in Tamil Nadu according to India Meteorological Department records, the presence of a predominantly rural, female agricultural and informal workforce, and existing research relationships and community access facilitated through SRIHER. Within each district, blocks were selected using simple random sampling, and villages within blocks.

The sample size was estimated using the formula, [N = (Z2) * P* (1 − p)/d2] where N = sample size, Z = statistic corresponding to the level of confidence, P = expected prevalence, and d = precision [22]. To calculate the sample size, the following assumptions were made: Z=alpha value for 95% confidence (1.96), P=prevalence (50%, i.e., 0.5) [23] and d=precision 0.05, N = ~ 384. Based on the estimate, the sample size was 384; hence, we selected 350 women for this study. ‘Although the calculated sample size was 384, 350 women were successfully enrolled, representing an approximate response rate of 91%, after accounting for participant refusals and incomplete questionnaires.

The data was collected during the peak summer months (April-August, 2025) to capture maximum ambient temperature and its associated physical and psychological impacts. A total of 350 women aged 18–45 years who are permanent residents of the selected villages were included in the study. Women aged 45 years or older were excluded to avoid confounding from the menopausal transition and associated hormonal fluctuations. Women on psychiatric medications and Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) were excluded from this study. Participants included both working (130), primarily engaged in heat-intensive occupations such as brick kilns, stone quarries, agriculture, and construction, and non-working women (220). Following formal oral approval from village administrative heads and assistance from local community facilitators, eligible women were enrolled in each village until the required sample size was achieved, in accordance with the selection criteria. Ethical clearance (Ref No: NI/24/JUN/95/108) for conducting the research was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (SRIHER), and the research strictly adhered to the Helsinki Declaration of Human Research. Participants were informed about the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, the confidentiality of their responses, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their representative prior to data collection.

Assessment of environmental and physiological heat stress

A portable Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) environmental heat-stress monitor (Ques-Temp 34; QUEST Technologies, USA) was used for quantitative environmental measurements. The device has an accuracy of ± 0.5 °C at temperatures ranging from 0 to 120 °C for dry bulb temperature, and ± 5% for relative humidity. As per American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) recommendations, the measurements were taken after the instrument was allowed to stabilize for at least 15 min at each measurement location. WBGT measurements were taken at three indoor locations (living room, kitchen, and bedroom) and one outdoor location (immediately outside the house). At each location, three readings were recorded at 15-minute intervals after instrument stabilization. The arithmetic mean of all indoor and outdoor WBGT readings was calculated to represent each participant’s mean daily heat exposure. Indoor air temperature and WBGT measurements were taken at a height of approximately 1.1 m (chest height, consistent with standing work) for all indoor measurements in the living room, kitchen, and bedroom, following standard ACGIH and ISO protocols for occupational WBGT measurement. Participants performing moderate workloads are classified as “exposed” when WBGT ≥ 28.0 °C, and those performing heavy workloads are classified as “exposed” when WBGT ≥ 27.5 °C. These thresholds represent the limits at which physiological heat strain becomes unacceptable for un-acclimatized workers performing continuous work. Physiological indicators of heat strain, Core Body Temperature (CBT), were measured using an infrared thermometer (Ross max 500 HA).

Perception data on physiological and psychological heat strain symptoms

A validated and modified High Occupational Temperature: Health and Productivity Suppression (HOTHAPS) questionnaire was used to collect information on participants’ occupations, socioeconomic characteristics, perceptions of heat stress, and impacts on their health, heat-strain symptoms, and social impacts. The questionnaire covered symptoms of HRIs, such as excessive thirst, sweating, headaches, dizziness, vomiting, and fatigue; access to drinking water; and coping strategies in response to heat exposure pertaining to that season. Eco-anxiety levels were assessed using the validated Hogg Eco-anxiety scale (HEAS-13) [24]. Formal permission was obtained from the author for using the HEAS scale in this study. HEAS is a 13-item scale, which has four dimensions: affective symptoms, rumination, behavioural symptoms, and anxiety about personal impact. All four subscales are directly applicable to women experiencing sustained occupational and domestic heat stress, as the lived experience of heat in this population is directly associated with climate change-related environmental deterioration. Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Almost all the time), with a total score of 39. Severity levels were measured as mild (0–13), moderate (14–26), or severe (27–39) [25]. Trained researchers administered the structured questionnaire through face-to-face interviews in participants’ homes or workplaces. Metabolic rate was assessed by the interviewer through a structured job task discussion and observation, using predefined ACGIH classifications. All field staff received standardized training prior to data collection to ensure consistency in metabolic rate assignment.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for demographics, heat exposure variables, work profile, health outcomes, social life impacts, and coping strategies, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables summarized using means and standard deviations. Chi-square tests were conducted to compare baseline characteristics and psychological impacts between the exposed and less-exposed participant groups. The crude odds ratio (COR) and adjusted odds ratio (aOR) after adjusting for the potential confounders (age, education, employment, house ownership, and any existing illness). SPSS-licensed statistical software version 16, ‘R’ software was used for statistical analysis, and box plots, and Microsoft Excel was used for the graphs.

Results

405 women were screened, and 14% were excluded based on the selection criteria. Among the 350 women, 295 (84%) were heat-exposed, and 55 (16%) were less-exposed. Table 1 summarizes participants’ socio-demographic, household, occupational, and heat exposure characteristics. The study population’s mean age was about 34.5 ± 7.5 years, and the mean daily WBGT was 30.4 ± 2.3 °C, with 84% of women exposed above the recommended threshold (≥ 27.5 °C). The age distribution was similar in both groups, with women aged 31–45 years predominantly in both the exposed (63%) and less exposed (64%) groups. The majority of the women were married (85%). Among the heat-exposed women, educational attainment varied significantly; one-fifth were illiterate, while more than one-quarter had completed graduate-level education. 78% of the heat-exposed women resided in their own houses, 83% in pucca houses, and 9% reported inadequate ventilation. Employment status was similar between groups, with about 60% of women employed indicating a balanced socio-demographic profile across exposure categories.

Heat stress profiles

Among 350 women, majority of participants were engaged in moderate (53%) and heavy (33%) work, with only 14% in the light category, and that WBGT levels exceeded the ACGIH TLVs for both these dominant metabolic rate categories across all three districts. 84% of the participants were exposed to heat levels exceeding the recommended TLVs. The attributed WBGT exposure across the study area showed consistently elevated heat stress in all districts (Fig. 1) for both heavy and moderate work. The highest WBGT levels were recorded in Ranipet, with a wider interquartile range and extreme values near 40 °C, indicating greater variability and intensity of heat exposure, while Thiruvallur had a higher median WBGT than Kanchipuram, suggesting differences in the ambient heat burden at the district level.

Attributed WBGT exposures in the study settings, district-wise

The environmental analysis shows sustained, high-intensity heat exposure across indoor and outdoor settings (Supplementary Table 1). The Outdoor WBGT ranged from 25.6 °C to 37.1 °C (30.6 ± 2.4 °C), while that of the indoor environments ranged from 27.2 °C to 39.4 °C (30.8 ± 2.7 °C). The median values for both indoor and outdoor environments exceeded the ACGIH-recommended values for moderate and heavy work. Outdoors, the WBGT was higher, and its variability was more pronounced, a few extreme values coming close to 38–40 °C. Even the indoor environments were exposed to temperatures beyond the safe limits. This suggests that households experienced limited thermal comfort from ambient temperatures, highlighting the persistence of heat stress across locations within a domestic setting.

Self-reported heat-related illnesses and eco-anxiety level

80% of the rural women (n = 280) reported experiencing at least one HRI symptom during the study period. Among the HRI symptoms, most of the women reported heat rash (57%), heat fatigue (60%), muscle cramps (45%), dehydration (59%), headaches (72%), and urogenital problems (55%) due to exposure to heat (Fig. 2).

The distribution of eco-anxiety severity was assessed using the Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale (Fig. 3). The pie chart illustrates the proportion of women reporting mild, moderate, and severe eco-anxiety. Nearly half of the participants reported moderate eco-anxiety (46%), while 34% experienced severe eco-anxiety, and 20% reported mild eco-anxiety. Overall, 80% of women experienced moderate-to-severe eco-anxiety, which highlights the serious psychological stress imparted by climate-related heat exposure in rural regions on the mental well-being of the population.

Heat exposure and social determinants of eco-anxiety

Table 2 shows that moderate eco-anxiety was the most prevalent among 350 women. Nearly half of the participants (46%) experienced moderate eco-anxiety, while 34% reached severe thresholds. Combined, 80% of the women suffered from moderate-to-severe eco-anxiety. There were no significant differences in eco-anxiety levels between age groups, where women aged 18–30 and 31–45 had similar anxiety profiles. However, several socio-demographic factors revealed significant associations. Employment status was a significant predictor of eco-anxiety (p = 0.001), with working women showing a higher prevalence of mild and moderate anxiety (50%). In non-working, the prevalence of the severe level is high (46%). Residence type exhibited a borderline association (p = 0.06), where renters showed more severe eco-anxiety than homeowners. Women experiencing at least one HRI had considerably greater eco-anxiety levels (p = 0.005). Resource strain was also a predictor of severe eco-anxiety (p < 0.001). Participants who spent more time and money on heat coping experienced higher anxieties, including severe eco-anxiety.

Association between eco-anxiety and demographic characteristics

Perceived heat stress and eco-anxiety

The association between heat exposure and health outcomes showed that women exposed to heat levels exceeding the TLV had a higher risk of both physical and psychological adverse outcomes than those in the less-exposed group (Table 3). High heat exposure was strongly associated with self-reported HRIs, with exposed women having more than ten-fold higher odds of reporting HRIs (COR = 10.5; 95% CI: 5.7–19.1) and increased odds of experiencing moderate-to-severe eco-anxiety (COR = 9.3; 95% CI: 5.1–17.0) even after adjusting for potential confounders (age, education, employment status, house ownership, and existing illness) (aOR = 12.3; 95% CI: 6.2–24.2). Stratified analysis by anxiety severity further indicated significantly higher odds of moderate eco-anxiety among heat-exposed women (COR = 4.7; 95% CI: 2.4–8.9), while severe eco-anxiety was independently associated with high heat exposure after confounding adjustments (aOR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.1–3.7).

Elevated eco-anxiety and its impact on social life

The heightened eco-anxiety resulting from heat exposure negatively affected the women’s (N = 350) social lives. The most prevalent social consequences reported were family conflict (88%) and impaired social interactions (85%). 70% of the participants reported difficulty performing household chores, decreased participation in social events (52%), and 65% had impaired caregiving responsibilities (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study provides preliminary evidence on the combined physiological, psychological, and social aspects of heat exposure caused by climate-related heat stress among rural women in Tamil Nadu. The results demonstrate that rural women were exposed to chronic heat stress that exceeded the recommended TLVs both indoors and outdoors. The lack of thermal comfort highlights the structural vulnerabilities to climate-heat and temperature variabilities. Such persistent heat exposure in household settings has already been experienced in different parts of India [4, 26], which directly contributes to the high prevalence of HRIs. Such observations have been made earlier by researchers who found that women engaged in domestic and informal economic activities experience heat exposure with inadequate rest and hydration, making them particularly vulnerable to heat-related morbidity [27, 28]. Beyond common HRI symptoms such as headaches, dehydration, feelings of fatigue, and heat rashes, the prevalence of heat-related urogenital issues (55%) highlights a critical gender-specific health risk linked to dehydration and limited sanitation access in resource-constrained settings [29].

Beyond physical health outcomes, the majority of the women experienced moderate-severe eco-anxiety, reflecting persistent worry, fear, and distress related to extreme heat. While eco-anxiety is well-studied in contexts involving younger generations or urban communities, often from developed economies [10], our study finds that rural women are directly linked to their daily struggles for their health and survival, rather than to abstract climate ideas. The strong correlation between heat exposure and above TLV and both HRIs and eco-anxiety was significantly strong after adjusting for confounders related to socio-demographic factors, emphasizing the priority role of heat as an environmental factor influencing mental health outcomes. The aOR for moderate to severe eco-anxiety was higher than the corresponding COR, indicating the presence of negative confounding. This suggests that certain socio-demographic factors, such as education, employment status, or housing characteristics, may have suppressed the true magnitude of the association between heat exposure and eco-anxiety in unadjusted analyses. Once these protective or buffering factors were accounted for, the independent effect of chronic heat exposure on eco-anxiety emerged as even stronger. Women exposed to high heat had significantly higher odds of reporting moderate to severe eco-anxiety, suggesting that heat stress can be perceived and experienced as mental distress through pathways of physical and psychological depletion, loss of control, and anticipation of future deterioration by climate change-affected individuals [30].

Eco-anxiety was higher in non-working women, educated people, and unmarried people. Non-working women who spend more time on housework and childcare may notice rising indoor temperatures and fewer opportunities to avoid heat stress, which may increase anxiety [31]. Feminist climate research emphasises gender and power in perceptions of climate stress and vulnerability [15]. Women exposed to high heat significantly had higher odds of reporting eco-anxiety (moderate to severe type). Heat stress can be perceived and experienced as mental distress through pathways of physical and psychological depletion, loss of control, and anticipation of future deterioration among individuals affected by climate change-related factors [7] and linked to social and economic issues [32]. The study also reveals that high levels of eco-anxiety have practical implications for women’s social relationships. Family conflicts (88%), problems with social interactions, fewer social gatherings, and impaired caregiving (65%) suggest that heat-psychological strain affects the social structures that collectivist cultures rely on for resilience. The coping strategies employed by women to tackle heat are often limited to short-term, individual-level behaviors such as staying hydrated, using fans or coolers, staying indoors, reducing physical activity, and taking frequent showers [33]. Although these strategies can offer some immediate relief for individuals affected by heat-related concerns and stressors, they do not target or address the underlying factors related to heat exposure. The non-significant association between ownership of cooling machinery and the severity of eco-anxiety suggests that stressors for individuals may not be mitigated by cooling machinery alone. On the contrary, eco-anxiety may be caused by long-term exposure to heat, increased energy consumption, and economic uncertainties related to a warming climate [10].

Overall, the results highlight that eco-anxiety among women living in rural areas is a context-dependent phenomenon influenced by their surroundings, the type of work, social roles, and their lack of capacity to adapt. Eco-anxiety in rural areas should be addressed through integrated solutions that include heat-reduction measures, social protection, gender-sensitive policies, and mental health support as part of climate adaptation. Preliminary findings of this study can inform heat adaptation policies and future research. Future research would use a larger sample from diverse geographic locations to better represent the population and employ advanced statistical models to estimate the true implications of climate- and heat-related anxiety. There is an urgent need to integrate mental health aspects into regional heat action plans and climate adaptation policies, which requires a broad approach. This should prioritize local approaches to cooling and heat mitigation, targeted education efforts for women, improved housing design and layout for ventilation, and adequate access to cooling spaces to help minimize the growing psychological impact of rising temperatures.

Strengths & limitations

This is the first study to examine the relationship between heat stress and psychological health among rural women in South India. The strength of this project is that it incorporates both objective environmental data and self-reported physical, psychological, and social outcomes, thereby enhancing the quality of the psychological data collected. In addition, this study’s focus on rural women provides a more comprehensive understanding of how eco-anxiety can affect communities and households and adds to the body of literature that has historically excluded rural women from climate and mental health research. Furthermore, the research included the HEAS-13, a validated instrument for measuring eco-anxiety, adding further validity to the psychological data collected, and included information on the impact of social life, which provides a better understanding of the community-level impact of eco-anxiety. As the research was conducted in selected districts of Tamil Nadu, the generalisability of the findings to other geographical regions or cultural contexts may be limited. The identified coping strategies were described without evaluating their effectiveness or long-term sustainability for protecting vulnerable populations in a warming climate. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing definitive causal pathways between heat exposure and the onset of eco-anxiety.

Conclusions

The study demonstrates that rural women in Tamil Nadu are exposed to sustained and unsafe heat levels, both indoors and outdoors, leading to a high burden of heat-related illnesses and moderate-to-severe eco-anxiety. Heat exposure emerged as a primary determinant of both physical and psychological health, with exposed women having a 12-fold increased risk of psychological distress that significantly disrupts their social and family life. Our findings highlight eco-anxiety as an important but under-recognized consequence of climate-induced heat stress among rural women, requiring the urgent integration of mental health and gender-sensitive strategies into regional Heat Action Plans.
