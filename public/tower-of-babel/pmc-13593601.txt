Genome-wide exploration of coat color–associated loci in Hokkaido Native Horses, with a novel locus implicated in the roan phenotype
Authors: Riku TANAKA, Honoka KASUGA, Masahito KAWAI, Kouichi SHIRAI, Tomoko AMANO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenes and loci regulating horse coat color have been investigated in many breeds, but their associations with the 16 coat colors of the Hokkaido Native Horse (HKD) remain unknown. HKDs were classified according to coat-color groups based on pedigree-recorded coat color, and genome-wide association (GWA) analyses were performed. Except for gray and roan, a clear single signal corresponding to the known locus was obtained for each coat color, suggesting that the reported loci are the primary causes of these coat colors. Because gray and roan are difficult to distinguish visually and may therefore have been misclassified, these two phenotypes were re-examined by PCR analysis of the gray-associated locus, and the GWA analyses were repeated. A single significant signal corresponding to the known gray-associated locus was detected for gray in the re-analysis. For roan, two signals were again detected: a signal near the known locus on chromosome 3 and a novel signal on chromosome 8, although only the latter reached statistical significance, suggesting a more complex genetic basis. Among genes with known functions, PIK3C3, which is involved in autophagy, a process associated with melanosome degradation and pigment lightening, was the closest to the chromosome 8 signal. At the chromosome 3 lead SNP, all horses with the GG genotype were roan, but roan and non-roan horses were mixed in the other genotype classes at this SNP and in the genotype classes at the chromosome 8 lead SNP, indicating that genotype-based identification of roan horses is possible only for a subset of animals.

In the Hokkaido Native Horse (HKD), 16 coat colors (double dilute, gray, palomino, and buckskin; six basic colors, bay, dark bay, chestnut, dark chestnut, black, and dark brown; and six corresponding roan colors, bay roan, dark bay roan, chestnut roan, dark chestnut roan, black roan, and dark brown roan) were recognized in the most recent studbook population [13]. However, the number of registered animals has been declining; although more than 2,000 horses were registered in 1999, the number decreased year by year to only 1,056 in 2024, according to data from the Japan Equine Affairs Association (https://www.bajikyo.or.jp/pdf/06_shiyoutoususuii2.pdf). As the population size continues to shrink, the diversity of coat colors present within the HKD population could be lost in the future. In order to conserve coat colors, therefore, it will be important not only to maintain individual horses expressing the target phenotype but also to assess whether causal alleles are retained in the population through carriers.

Many studies have investigated the genetic control of coat color across horse breeds, and multiple causal genes and variants have been reported [7, 8, 11, 16,17,18, 21, 22, 26]. A similar genetic mechanism is generally thought to control coat color among equine breeds, and practical experience suggests that the genetic control of coat color in the HKD is similar to that in other breeds [1]. However, no study has examined to what extent HKD coat colors can be explained by the genetic basis reported in other breeds.

In the HKD, roan is common and accounts for approximately half of the population, making it one of the most important coat-color traits in this breed [24]. Several KIT regions are reportedly associated with roan color in other breeds; however, these associations are not always equally strong across breeds, indicating the need to examine the genomic regions associated with the roan phenotype specifically in the HKD [11, 18, 26].

Accordingly, case and non-case groups were established for each coat-color phenotype based on the reported relationships among coat-color loci in other breeds, and genome-wide association (GWA) analyses were performed to assess the extent to which these loci explain coat-color variation in HKD horses. These analyses provide a first step toward assessing the retention of such coat-color loci in the HKD population.

Materials and Methods

Animals and genotyping

DNA samples were obtained from 328 HKD horses between 2000 and 2023. Among them, 55 samples were obtained from mane hair, and 273 were obtained from blood. Of the 55 mane hair samples, 42 were collected at a private farm with permission of the animal owners and the Hokkaido Native Horse Conservation Association. The remaining 13 samples were collected at the Hokkaido Research Organization, Agricultural Research Department Animal Research Center. Mane hair sampling was considered a minimally invasive procedure and did not require approval by relevant Experimental Animal Committees (Rakuno Gakuen University and Hokkaido Research Organization, Agricultural Research Department Animal Research Center). The 273 blood samples were collected at the Field Science Center for the Northern Biosphere, Hokkaido University, in accordance with the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Committee of Hokkaido University (23-0002). Sampling of mane hairs and subsequent DNA extraction were performed as follows: 30 mane hairs were sampled from each animal; the portion 1 cm from the hair root was cut and collected. DNA was extracted from the collected hairs using an Isohair DNA extraction kit (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). Sampling of blood and DNA extraction were performed as follows: 3 ml of blood was collected from the jugular vein, and DNA was extracted from the collected blood using the phenol-chloroform method. All DNA samples were genotyped using the 70K Illumina Equine Genotyping BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Genomic positions of SNPs were based on the EquCab2.0 horse reference genome assembly.

The HKD horse breed is characterized by a uniform coat color over the body, without white markings. Sixteen coat colors were described in the most recent (1997–2018) HKD herd recorded in the stud book published in 2018, as follows: double dilute, gray, palomino, buckskin, six basic colors (black, dark brown, dark bay, bay, dark chestnut, and chestnut), and six roan colors corresponding to the six basic colors (black roan, dark brown roan, dark bay roan, bay roan, dark chestnut roan, and chestnut roan) [13]. Our samples covered all of the coat colors except dark brown and dark brown roan due to the small proportion (<1%) of dark brown and dark brown roan animals in the recently described HKD herd [13]. The number of HKD horses in each coat-color category was as follows: double dilute, 13; gray, 20; buckskin, 29; palomino, 44; bay, 21; chestnut, 12; black, 3; bay roan, 58; chestnut roan, 70; black roan, 46; dark chestnut, 2; dark bay, 1; dark chestnut roan, 5; and dark bay roan, 4 (total of 328 horses).

Quality control of genotype data

A total of 74,131 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were genotyped in the 328 HKD horses examined in this study, and SNPs successfully genotyped in >90% of the horses (i.e., in at least 296 horses) were retained. This threshold was set to reduce the effect of missing genotypes while retaining sufficient markers for the GWA analyses, consistent with previous equine GWA studies [12, 19, 23]. Subsequently, SNPs on the sex chromosomes and those with a minor allele frequency of <0.05 were excluded. As a result, 38,749 SNPs remained, and the mean (± SD) proportion of failed genotype calls per animal was 0.4 ± 1.6%.

For each coat-color phenotype, animals were classified into case and non-case groups according to the known epistatic hierarchy among previously reported causal genes and variants affecting coat color. The previously reported genetic basis underlying this classification is briefly summarized below. Homozygous mutations in SLC45A2 (MATP) produce the double dilute phenotype and mask the effects of other coat-color mutations. Heterozygous mutations in SLC45A2 produce palomino or buckskin, depending on the basic coat-color genotype of the horse [16]. At the STX17 locus, horses carrying at least one gray-associated G allele (G/g or G/G), corresponding to a 4.6-kb duplication in intron 6, become gray regardless of the genotypes at other coat-color loci, except in double dilutes [22]. Although the identity of the causal variant for roan in the KIT region has not been fully clarified, roan is generally regarded as a dominant trait, and the roan phenotype is expressed in both heterozygotes and homozygotes for the responsible allele, except in double dilutes and gray horses [7, 8, 11, 18, 22, 26]. To our knowledge, no previous studies have clarified whether palomino and buckskin are epistatic to roan; however, in this study, they were treated as phenotypes preceding roan, based on empirical coat-color inheritance patterns observed in HKD horses. In the absence of the effects of the aforementioned mutations in SLC45A2, STX17, and KIT, coat color is determined by the basic coat-color loci. At the MC1R locus, horses homozygous for the recessive chestnut e allele show the chestnut phenotype [17]. In horses with at least one E allele (E/e or E/E), homozygous mutation in ASIP produces a black coat color, whereas horses without homozygous ASIP mutation show the bay phenotype [21]. GWA analyses were performed using TASSEL 5 and a mixed linear model (MLM) with correction for relatedness [4, 27]. Specifically, a kinship matrix (K) was calculated in TASSEL 5 using the centered IBS option, and the resulting kinship file was incorporated into the MLM. The model used for the GWA analyses was as follows:y = Xβ + Sα + Zu + e,where y represents the vector of phenotypic observations; Xβ represents fixed effects other than marker effects, which in this study consisted only of the intercept; Sα represents the effect of the SNP being tested; Zu represents the random polygenic effect, in which u ~ N (0, Kσu^2); and e represents the residual error with e ~ N (0, Iσe^2). The significance of marker-trait associations was evaluated based on the P-values obtained from the MLM. The MLM used here can also be applied to binary phenotypes [6]. Marker-trait associations were considered significant at P<1.29 × 10−6 after Bonferroni correction (0.05/38,749).

Detection of the gray-associated G allele at STX17

The coat colors gray and roan are both formed by the inclusion of white hairs with hairs of the basic coat color. Therefore, gray and roan can be difficult to distinguish visually, and the recording of these coat colors in studbooks based on visual classification may not always correspond to the horse’s genetic background [2]. Indeed, such discrepancies have also been reported in Arabian horses [2]. Accordingly, similar confusion is considered likely in HKD horses, which may have affected the GWA results for gray and roan coat colors. In our study, the presence of the G allele was therefore examined using PCR in all 20 gray horses (Table 1; Analysis 2) and in 155 of the 183 roan horses (Table 1; Analysis 3) for which DNA was available among the samples used in the present GWA analyses. Briefly, PCR was performed using the primer pair Grey-F (5′-GCTCATTAGAAATGCAAAATCTCAG-3′) and Grey-R (5′-AGTAATCAGAGGTTCTCCTTTGAGC-3′), which was designed to encompass the regions flanking the duplication junction so that amplification would occur only when the duplication (gray allele) was present [10]. PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, and the presence of a 1,085-bp band was interpreted as indicative of gray, because the gray phenotype is expressed in both homozygous and heterozygous horses carrying the gray allele. This assay can distinguish gray from non-gray horses, but it cannot distinguish homozygous from heterozygous gray horses [10].

As a result, Analysis 2, which was designed to detect loci associated with gray (Table 1), was repeated after excluding two G allele–lacking horses from the gray group (20 animals) and excluding 17 horses carrying the G allele from the original non-case group (295 animals). Likewise, Analysis 3, which was designed to detect loci associated with roan (Table 1), was repeated after excluding 17 horses carrying the G allele from the original roan group of 183 animals, which included 28 horses whose STX17 genotype could not be analyzed because DNA samples were unavailable. Both corrected GWA analyses were performed using the same method as described above.

The adequacy of all 10 GWA analyses, including Analyses 1–8 and the two additional analyses performed after correction of the STX17 genotype, was assessed using quantile-quantile plots, which showed no marked deviation from the expected distribution.

Discussion

To evaluate the extent to which previously reported coat-color loci explain coat-color phenotypes in HKD horses, we classified the HKD population according to epistatic hierarchy and dominance relationships between coat-color genes and loci reported in other breeds and performed GWA analyses of each target coat-color phenotype.

In the GWA analyses of all target coat-color phenotypes except gray and roan, a single major SNP cluster was detected for each coat-color phenotype, and each cluster was located near a previously reported responsible gene or locus (Fig. 1; Tables 2 and 3). These results suggest that the major coat-color genes reported in other horse populations also explain much of the variation in the double dilute, palomino, buckskin, chestnut, bay, and black phenotypes in HKD horses.

For gray coat color, re-analysis after correction based on PCR examination of the gray-associated locus in STX17 showed that the main signal was retained, whereas additional signals, such as those on chromosomes 1, 23, and 31, remained significant but were markedly weaker than in the initial analysis (Fig. 2A). These results suggest that visual misclassification between gray and roan contributed to the additional signals observed in the initial analysis and that the known gray-associated locus in STX17 is associated with the gray phenotype in HKD horses. For roan, even after correcting the phenotype classification based on the STX17 locus, the overall pattern of the detected signals did not change markedly from the initial analysis (Fig. 2B). Because gray is epistatic to roan [25], some of the individuals removed from the roan group by this correction may have been genetically gray but also carried variants involved in roan. Given that roan horses accounted for 183 of 328 individuals (55.8%) in the present population, it is plausible that a substantial proportion of the removed gray individuals also carried roan-related genetic factors. This suggests that the roan-associated pattern observed in the initial analysis largely reflected the genetic factors involved in roan.

In this study, the strongest roan-associated signal was detected on chromosome 8, with the lead SNP located at 60,064,433 bp (Table 3). This region has not previously been reported as a roan-associated locus in horses. The closest to this SNP was the uncharacterized locus LOC106780925 (59,938,040–59,976,046 bp), and the second closest was phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase catalytic subunit type 3 (PIK3C3; 61,542,278–61,694,460 bp), which is involved in the autophagic machinery responsible for degrading intracellular proteins and organelles [5, 14]. Skin and hair color are determined by melanin synthesized in melanocytes and stored in melanosomes (membrane-bound organelles within melanocytes), which are subsequently transferred to keratinocytes forming the hair shaft [3, 9]. However, autophagy can also target melanosomes [15]; indeed, induction of autophagy in melanocytes reportedly promotes melanosome degradation, thereby lightening cellular pigmentation [15]. Furthermore, comparative studies of keratinocytes derived from humans with darker (African American) or lighter (Caucasian) skin showed that Caucasian-derived cells exhibit higher autophagic activity [20]. Given that PIK3C3 plays a key role in autophagy [5, 14], variations in this gene may contribute to the formation of the roan coat color in HKD horses, which is characterized by the intermixing of white hairs. However, because only 21 of the 221 horses included in the analysis had the K genotype at the lead SNP on chromosome 8, with the other 200 having the T genotype (Fig. 3), the observed association may have been affected by the chance distribution of a small number of basic-colored horses.

The second strongest roan-associated signal was detected on chromosome 3, with the lead SNP located at 78,947,404 bp (Table 3). This signal was located near KIT, a gene within a region that has been repeatedly reported to be associated with the roan phenotype in horses [11, 18, 26]. However, the associated variants, including exon 19, c.2613C>T [18], intron 17, g.79,543,439 A>G [11], and multiple SNPs within the KIT region (positions 79,538,738–79,548,356; EquCab3.0) [26], are not consistent across studies. This supports the possibility that, although KIT is the primary locus underlying roan coat color, the haplotypes responsible for the roan phenotype are not uniform but instead consist of multiple distinct KIT haplotypes. In fact, Everts et al. identified three KIT haplotypes associated with the roan phenotype, which together explained approximately 95% of roan color in American Quarter Horses and 50–60% of roan color in other breeds [7, 8]. Based on these findings, it is plausible that roan HKD horses would not share a single KIT haplotype but rather would carry different ones. This is consistent with our finding of a weak association (below the significance threshold) with roan near KIT on chromosome 3 (Figs. 1C and 2B).

Regarding genotype-based identification of the roan phenotype using the detected lead SNPs, we examined the correspondence between genotype and phenotype for each lead SNP. Although no statistically significant association with the roan phenotype was observed for the lead SNP on chromosome 3 (Figs. 1C and 2B), all horses with the G genotype at this SNP were roan (34/34, 100.0%; Fig. 3A). In contrast, the proportion of roan horses was 89.0% for the R genotype (105/118) and 62.9% for the A genotype (44/70), indicating that only the G genotype showed complete correspondence with the roan phenotype in the present dataset. However, because only 34 horses had the G genotype, whether this genotype can be used to reliably identify roan horses should be examined further in a larger population. At the lead SNP on chromosome 8, T genotype horses showed a higher proportion of roan than horses with the K genotype, but the correspondence between genotype and phenotype was incomplete, as both roan and basic-colored horses were present in each genotype class (Fig. 3B). Accordingly, reliable genotype-based identification of roan horses based on the lead SNP on chromosome 8 alone would likely be difficult (Fig. 3B). When the lead SNPs on chromosomes 3 and 8 were considered together, a higher proportion of roan horses was observed in combinations carrying the T genotype at the chromosome 8 lead SNP within the R and A genotype backgrounds at the chromosome 3 lead SNP (Table 4 and Fig. 3C). However, the combined genotypes did not completely distinguish roan from basic-colored horses.

Roan color is generally considered to be inherited in a dominant pattern [2], which has been confirmed empirically by HKD breeders. In the present study, all horses with the G genotype at the lead SNP on chromosome 3 were roan, consistent with a dominant inheritance pattern (Fig. 3A). However, this relationship was not complete, as some horses with the R genotype showed a basic-color phenotype, whereas some individuals with the A genotype were roan (Fig. 3A). Regarding the chromosome 8 lead SNP, the T genotype showed a higher proportion of roan horses than the K genotype (Table 4 and Fig. 3B), and this tendency was also observed in horses with the R or A genotype at the chromosome 3 lead SNP: RT (101/110, 91.8%) vs. RK (3/7, 42.9%) and AT (42/58, 72.4%) vs. AK (2/12, 16.7%; Table 4 and Fig. 3C). Moreover, the number of animals with the T genotype was approximately ten times higher than that with the K genotype (200 vs. 21 animals; Table 4). In this situation, even horses with the R or A genotype at the chromosome 3 lead SNP may frequently show the roan phenotype due to the high frequency of the T genotype at the chromosome 8 lead SNP, which may make the trait appear to follow a dominant mode of inheritance overall, even when one or more roan-associated KIT haplotypes do not by themselves fully determine the phenotype.

In summary, the present GWA analyses suggest that coat-color phenotypes in HKD horses are largely explained by major genes and loci previously reported in other horse populations. For the roan phenotype, a novel significant associated signal was identified on chromosome 8, whereas a suggestive but non-significant signal was detected in the KIT region on chromosome 3. Considered together with previous reports, these findings suggest that roan color in HKD horses is better explained by a more complex genetic architecture involving both multiple KIT haplotypes and an additional associated locus on chromosome 8, rather than by a simple single-locus dominant model.
