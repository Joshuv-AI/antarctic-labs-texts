One-step in vivo assembly of a 63 kb-long biosynthetic gene cluster via multiple recombination in Aspergillus oryzae
Authors: Koichi Tamano, Haruka Takayama, Ikuko Kozone, Yukiko Abe, Akio Kanda, Kei Kudo, Hikaru Suenaga, Kazuo Shin-ya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMass production of numerous secondary plant and microbial metabolites is crucial, given their value as pharmaceutical agents, dietary supplements, and pesticides. For microbial strain, mass production generally involves improving the native producer strain to enhance overall productivity via spontaneous mutagenesis or genetic modifications. Alternatively, productivity can be enhanced through heterologous production, in which the biosynthetic genes for a secondary metabolite are expressed in a more suitable strain. However, as these biosynthetic genes commonly exist as long clusters, often exceeding several tens of kilobases (kb), their handling is labor-intensive and time-consuming, requiring multiple rounds of genetic cloning and introduction into the host. Therefore, methods enabling efficient transfer of biosynthetic genes into another microorganism in a single step of transformation without the need to clone long gene clusters have been strongly desired. Such an approach has been explored in filamentous fungi, however the maximum gene size sufficiently transferred with the approach thus far is only approximately 20 kb. In this study, we transferred 63 kb pairs of DNA encoding a secondary metabolite-biosynthetic genes into the chromosome of Aspergillus oryzae, a filamentous fungus, using a single-step transformation approach based on multiple homologous recombination events. This study expands the potential of using A. oryzae as a host for efficient heterologous metabolite production. The results serve as a useful reference, providing insights, such as the DNA fragment number and assembled cluster length in host cells, for the cases where heterologous production of a secondary metabolite proves desirable in filamentous fungi.Key points• One-step transfer of 24 DNA fragments to A. oryzae and the in vivo ordered assembly.• The assembled 63 kb DNA region enabled heterologous secondary metabolite production.• The transfer method adopted in this study may be applied to other filamentous fungi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13845-7.

Introduction

Secondary metabolites serve as valuable agents in various applications, including pharmaceuticals, dietary supplements, pesticides, and pigments (Keller 2019). For example, penicillin, cephalosporin, cyclosporin, and statin are well-known pharmaceuticals (Katz and Baltz 2016), and their mass production has consequently been the subject of extensive research (Tamano and Yoshimi 2021). Mass production is achieved through various techniques, including chemical synthesis as a substitute for biological production of the target compound. However, the intricate structures of many secondary metabolites render chemical synthesis difficult. Consequently, the production of a target compound is generally enhanced by the introduction of random mutations or genetic modifications into the original host strain (Jeyachandran et al. 2023). For example, penicillin production has been successfully increased through random mutagenesis, as well as optimizing culture conditions (van den Berg 2011; McLean et al. 2015; Fierro et al. 2022). However, these techniques occasionally face challenges in enhancing the production yield of other secondary metabolites (Tamano and Yoshimi 2021).

Heterologous production is an alternative method for enhancing secondary metabolite production (Alberti et al. 2017; Vassaux et al. 2019; Ding and Ye 2023). This technique involves transferring the biosynthetic genes of the target secondary metabolites into a certain microbial host that is more suitable for their production (Anyaogu and Mortensen 2015; Dao et al. 2021; Chiang et al. 2023).

However, these biosynthetic genes are generally considerably long, ranging from tens to hundreds of kilobases (kb) (Osbourn 2010; Keller 2019). Transferring such a long DNA region to other microbial hosts is labor-intensive and time-consuming, requiring repeated cloning of the regions (Vassaux et al. 2019). Moreover, replacing the native promoters of the biosynthetic genes with constitutive promoters from highly expressed genes of a heterologous host requires repetitive cloning of partial DNA regions, making the process more complicated and time-consuming. Therefore, the transfer of large DNA fragments into heterologous host cells is hindered by numerous limitations.

Successful transfer of a large DNA fragment into heterologous host cells in a single transformation, without the need for cloning, would facilitate the rapid development of a heterologous production strain. Notably, such a technique has been established in budding yeast. Specifically, a 592-kb circular genomic DNA was successfully reconstructed by introducing 25 partial DNA fragments (17–34 kb) containing 80–360 base pairs (bp) overlapping homology tails at both ends into yeast cells (Gibson et al. 2008). Co-transformation with these partial regions enabled yeast cells to incorporate and assemble them into a large circular DNA through DNA recombination. Similar to budding yeast, in filamentous fungi, partial DNA regions can be concatenated via a single transformation step. However, only up to six partial DNA regions have been reported to date (Jarczynska et al. 2022), and the resulting concatenated long DNA regions are up to approximately 20 kb (Kirchgaessner et al. 2023).

In the present study, we aimed to develop a simple and rapid system to construct a heterologous secondary metabolite production strain in the filamentous fungus, Aspergillus oryzae. To this end, we introduced a 63-kb DNA region responsible for the biosynthesis of a secondary metabolite into A. oryzae cells. We achieved this without cloning and through a single co-transformation of 24 DNA fragments, allowing multiple partial DNA regions to be concatenated in the cells. This entire region enabled A. oryzae cells to heterologously produce a secondary metabolite. Overall, our results indicate that long biosynthetic gene clusters of secondary metabolites can be efficiently assembled in A. oryzae cells within a short time, requiring minimal labor and no specialized techniques.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and culture media

The ΔL6 (RIB40 ΔligD::ptrA) and ΔL6ΔP1 strains (RIB40 ΔligD::ptrA ΔpyrG) were constructed from the wild-type strain RIB40 of A. oryzae, referring to previously described genomic information (Machida et al. 2005). The ΔL6ΔP1 strain was designed as a host for the introduction of secondary metabolite biosynthetic genes from other species. Subsequently, the biosynthetic genes of KK-1, a cyclic peptide compound produced by the filamentous fungus Curvularia clavata BAUA-2787 (Yoshimi et al. 2018) were introduced into the ΔL6ΔP1 strain for heterologous expression, and the introduced A. oryzae mutants were termed KO strains. These KO strains were maintained on Czapek–Dox (CD) minimal agar medium (Tamano et al. 2007). The ΔL6 strain constructed from RIB40 was maintained on CD agar medium supplemented with 0.1 µg/mL pyrithiamine hydrobromide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to provide selection pressure (Kubodera et al. 2000). The ΔL6ΔP1 strain constructed from the ΔL6 strain was maintained on CD agar medium supplemented with 5 mM uridine, 10 mM uracil, and 1 mg/mL 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA). All A. oryzae strains were incubated on agar media at 30℃ for 5–7 days until spore formation.

Preparation of DNA fragments to be introduced into A. oryzae for heterologous production of KK-1

We selected the biosynthetic gene cluster of a secondary metabolite, KK-1, harbored by C. clavata BAUA-2787, as the long DNA fragment to be introduced into A. oryzae. It was because KK-1 was the only case satisfying the conditions of the long biosynthetic gene cluster (approximately 75 kb in the original host), and successful heterologous production reported in A. oryzae. DNA fragments enabling A. oryzae to heterologously produce KK-1 were generated via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the chromosomal DNA of the C. clavata BAUA-2787 strain or artificially synthesized DNA as a template. The sequences of the primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. KOD-PLUS DNA polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) was used to prepare the DNA fragments. The amplified DNA fragments were purified via gel extraction using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Transformation and co-transformation of A. oryzae

Protoplasts of A. oryzae were prepared from hyphae cultured in CD liquid medium (when pyrithiamine hydrobromide was used) or in trypticase peptone-dextrose medium by the regular protocol (Tamano et al. 2007). Approximately 3–5 × 107 protoplasts were used for transformation (i.e. transformation with a single DNA fragment), whereas 1–3 × 108 protoplasts were used for co-transformation (i.e. transformation with multiple DNA fragments). The DNA fragments prepared for introducing the KK-1 biosynthetic genes were mixed at an equal molar ratio. The mixture (approximately 100 µg of DNA) was applied to the A. oryzae protoplasts for co-transformation. Transformants and co-transformants were generated on CD agar in the absence or presence of pyrithiamine, and were subjected to single-spore isolation by plating on CD agar three times, followed by PCR screening to identify positive clones.

Southern hybridization

Aliquots (8 μg) of genomic DNA were digested with each restriction enzyme (SacI, KpnI, SphI, EcoRI, EcoRV, and PvuII), fractionated on 0.6% agarose gel, and transferred onto a Nytran SuperCharge membrane (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA). Hybridization and signal detection were performed with a digoxigenin (DIG) system, according to the manufacturers’ protocol (GE Healthcare). Briefly, DIG-labeled probes (about 900–1000 bases long) were prepared using a PCR DIG Probe Synthesis Kit (Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany) with a primer pair for Southern hybridization (Supplementary Table S1), followed by agarose gel extraction. The probes were hybridized to the DNA fragments bound to the membrane and signals were detected via chemiluminescence arising from the anti-DIG Fab-fragment-alkaline phosphatase conjugate (Roche Applied Science) using the CDP-Star Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare).

Detection and quantification of KK-1 produced by A. oryzae heterologous expression mutants

C. clavata BAUA-2787 and A. oryzae mutant strains with heterologous expression of KK-1 biosynthetic genes were cultivated in 15 mL of potato dextrose medium (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) at 27 °C for 2 days. Aliquots of the culture were transferred to rice-based R solid medium containing 3 g of brown rice, 10 mg of yeast extract (BD Biosciences), 3 mg of sodium tartarate, 3 mg of K2HPO4 (Kawahara et al. 2012) and 100 mL of YPD liquid medium containing 1 g of yeast extract (BD Biosciences), 2 g of Hipolypepton (Nihon pharmaceutical, Osaka, Japan), and 2 g of dextrose (Kanto Chemical, Tokyo, Japan), respectively. After stationary cultivation in rice-based R medium at 27 °C for 14 days, the entire culture components were extracted three times with 50 mL of acetone. The combined acetone extracts were concentrated in vacuo, and the remaining aqueous residue was extracted three times with 100 mL of ethyl acetate. Subsequently, the ethyl acetate layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and evaporated in vacuo to yield the residue containing KK-1. In parallel, after cultivation in YPD medium on a rotary shaker at 27 °C for 5 days, the aqueous residue was similarly extracted with an equal volume of ethyl acetate as performed for R medium culture. The ethyl acetate layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and evaporated in vacuo to yield the concentrate containing KK-1. The resulting extracts were dissolved in an appropriate volume of dimethyl sulfoxide and analyzed by LC/MS. Specifically, analytical ultra-high performance liquid chromatography and high-resolution electrospray ionization MS (positive mode) were performed using an ACQUITY UPLC System (Waters, Framingham, MA, USA) in conjunction with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 1.7 µm column (2.1 i.d. × 100 mm; Waters), an ACQUITY UPLC photodiode array eλ detector (Waters), and a Xevo G2 time-of-flight system (Waters). The isocratic elution was performed using 70% aqueous acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid; the flow rate was 0.3 mL min−1.

To purify KK-1, collected residues were then fractionated using normal-phase medium-pressure liquid chromatography (Sfär Silica HC D, Biotage, Uppsala, Sweden) with stepwise gradient systems of n-hexane–ethanol (0 and 20% ethanol), followed by chloroform–methanol (0, 2, and 5% methanol). The obtained target fraction (5% methanol) was subjected to preparative HPLC on a CAPCELL PAK C18 column (20.0 i.d. × 250 mm; Osaka soda, Osaka, Japan) with 90% aqueous methanol containing 0.1% formic acid to yield pure KK-1. A standard curve for quantification of KK-1 was prepared by triplicate injection of 1 μL of purified KK-1 at concentrations of 25, 13, 6.3, 3.1, 1.6, and 0.78 mg L−1.

Results

Construction of the A. oryzae host strain for heterologous expression of secondary metabolite biosynthetic genes

We constructed a host strain of A. oryzae to introduce a long DNA region encompassing the biosynthetic genes of a secondary metabolite. We engineered this strain to be defective in the ability of non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) (Chang et al. 2017) and carry a uracil auxotrophy.

First, to impair the NHEJ ability, we knocked out the ligD gene, which is responsible for NHEJ in A. oryzae (Mizutani et al. 2008; Nakamura et al. 2017). We prepared a DNA fragment for the knockout by concatenating the promoter of ligD, expression cassette of the pyrithiamine-resistant ptrA gene, and terminator of ligD, in this order, using fusion PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1). We then transformed the A. oryzae RIB40 strain with the DNA fragment, resulting in pyrithiamine-resistant transformants. After selecting several transformant colonies, we subjected them to single-spore isolation and clone check using PCR, resulting in the ligD knockout strain, which we termed ΔL6 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Subsequently, to confer the uracil auxotrophy, thus enabling the use of pyrG gene as a selectable marker, we knocked out the native pyrG gene in the ΔL6 genome. To this end, we designed a DNA fragment, in which the pyrG promoter and terminator sequences were concatenated into a single DNA fragment using fusion PCR (Supplementary Fig. S2). We then transformed the ΔL6 strain with the DNA fragment, resulting in uracil-auxotrophic 5-FOA-resistant transformant colonies. We selected several transformant colonies, and subjected them to single-spore isolation and clone check using PCR, resulting in the pyrG knockout ΔL6 strain, which was named ΔL6ΔP1 (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Preparation of DNA fragments for the introduction of the KK-1 biosynthetic genes into A. oryzae via a single co-transformation step

In a previous study, the KK-1 biosynthetic genes were introduced into A. oryzae through three successive transformations using partial DNA fragments, followed by marker recycling, ultimately enabling the A. oryzae strain to produce KK-1 (Yoshimi et al. 2018). The introduced DNA regions were prepared by cloning PCR-amplified DNA fragments. Therefore, if our proposed system could successfully enable the introduction of a long DNA fragment into A. oryzae via a single-step co-transformation, heterologous KK-1 production could be achieved in a substantially shorter period.

Of the ten KK-1 biosynthetic genes, eight are essential for the heterologous production of KK-1 in A. oryzae: kk1A, kk1B, kk1C, kk1D, kk1G, kk1H, kk1I, and kk1J (Yoshimi et al. 2018; Yamaguchi et al. 2023). Therefore, we selected these eight genes for introduction into A. oryzae. We also selected A. oryzae constitutive promoters for the expression of these genes referring to previous reported cases (Kitamoto et al. 1998; Nakajima et al. 2000; Sakai et al. 2012; Jin et al. 2021), and maintained the native terminators of the biosynthetic genes, different from the previous case that a promoter of the amylase gene amyB from A. oryzae was used for expression of all the 8 genes (Yoshimi et al. 2018). We designed the long DNA region to carry the expression cassettes of each gene (a hybrid of the constitutive promoter of A. oryzae, coding region, and terminator of each gene), a pyrG selectable marker, and 1 kb-long homologous regions to sC upstream locus flanking both ends for integration into the host chromosome. The upstream of the sC locus, which encodes an ATP-sulfurylase and is responsible for sulfate assimilation (Buxton et al. 1989; Yamada et al. 1997), was selected as a target site for integration. Because sC functions efficiently in CD minimal medium for utilizing magnesium sulfate, the expression level around sC was supposed to be relatively high. In addition, for maintaining the sC expression under the original promoter, we considered the 1-kb-long upstream site desirable for integration. However, the in vitro preparation of such a long DNA fragment as a single construct in amounts sufficient for transformation is both labor-intensive and time-consuming. This is because repeated cloning of segments of the long region is required using electrophoresis and PCR, and the resulting long DNA fragment cannot be easily fractionated or isolated using the standard molecular biology techniques. Thus, we attempted to introduce the long DNA region into the chromosome of A. oryzae via a single-step co-transformation, which would enable the partial DNA regions to be concatenated and integrated into A. oryzae chromosome through multiple homologous recombination events in vivo. Using this concept, we divided the long DNA region into 2–5 kb partial DNAs and prepared approximately 1 kb overlapping sequences at both ends of each fragment for homologous recombination. Specifically, we divided the long DNA region into 24 short DNA fragments (Figs. 1 and 2).Fig. 2Multiple recombination of the 24 DNA fragments occurred in A. oryzae after the introduction by co-transformation. One-step introduction of the KK-1 biosynthetic gene cluster into A. oryzae

We mixed these 24 short DNA fragments at an equivalent molecular ratio and applied the mixture to the A. oryzae ΔL6ΔP1 protoplasts for co-transformation. We expected the 24 short DNA fragments to be incorporated into the protoplasts, concatenated into a single long DNA and integrated into the chromosome through multiple homologous recombination events (Fig. 2). After co-transformation, we observed more than 50 single colonies of co-transformants on 20 agar plates. Subsequently, we selected 42 single colonies and subjected their spores to single spore isolation three times, followed by chromosomal DNA isolation and PCR validation (Fig. 4). In PCR validation, the regions around the 1 kb homologous ends for chromosomal integration or the regions covering the two DNA fragments to be concatenated by homologous recombination were attempted for amplification. If DNA fragments specific to these regions were amplified, the precise integration to the 1 kb-upstream site of sC locus and the correct concatenation of the introduced 24 DNA fragments could be clarified. KK-1 heterologous production by the A. oryzae recombinants

Among the 13 positive clones obtained from A. oryzae mutants with heterologous expression of the KK-1 biosynthetic genes, we randomly selected 9 clones (KO-8, 11, 16, 18, 26, 27, 29, 30, and 33) and cultured them in R and YPD media. To analyze KK-1 biosynthesis, we subjected the extracted components to HPLC–MS. As a positive control, we cultured the C. clavata BAUA-2787 under the same conditions and analyzed the extracted components in the same manner. Our results indicated that all nine transformants produced KK-1 (Fig. 6), suggesting that the biosynthetic gene cluster was successfully reconstituted within the host genome and functioned properly. The peak specifically existing in both A. oryzae mutants and BAUA-2787 were observed in HPLC (Fig. 6a), and the MS analysis of the compound corresponding to the peak showed the spectra specific to KK-1 (m/z for [M + H]+ = 1112.6602 (calculated), 1112.6608 (found, + 0.5 ppm); m/z for [M + Na]+ = 1134.6422 (calculated), 1134.6438 (found, + 1.4 ppm)) (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. S4). Among the clones analyzed, the KO-16 strain exhibited the highest production (Fig. 6), with yields of 0.80 mg/L in the YPD medium and 0.26 mg/L in the R medium (Table 1). In contrast, the KO-26 strain exhibited the lowest production, with levels of 0.01 mg/L in YPD and 0 mg/L in R, nearly equivalent to the non-production levels. The production yield differed so much between mutant clones. The positive control C. clavata BAUA-2787 strain produced KK-1 at 7.16 mg/L in YPD medium and 38.5 mg/L in R medium. Discussion

In this study, we developed a system of constructing a heterologous production strain of a secondary metabolite in A. oryzae in a rapid and efficient manner. We could assemble 63 kb DNA fragment harboring the KK-1 biosynthetic genes under the constitutive promoters and integrate it into the host chromosome via a single step of co-transformation, without any cloning. We subsequently confirmed that the constructed A. oryzae mutant strain can heterologously produce KK-1.

Several studies have explored techniques capable of enhancing secondary metabolite production, similar to our approach. Among them, one study especially documented successful concatenation of 6 DNA fragments with 50 bp homologous ends in vivo using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system, which formed a circular DNA in the wild-type Aspergillus nidulans following co-transformation (Jarczynska et al. 2022). Another study indicated that 5 DNA fragments, each with approximately 1 kb-long homologous ends, can be concatenated in vivo and integrated into a specific locus on the chromosome of Aspergillus niger with NHEJ-deficient background through homologous recombination after co-transformation (Kirchgaessner et al. 2023). It enabled the introduction of an approximately 20 kb-long DNA fragment into the chromosome without using genome editing technique via a single co-transformation step. Compared to these previous studies, we achieved the assembly of 24 short DNA fragments, which is four times that previously reported. We also constructed a 63 kb DNA fragment through assembly, three times longer than that reported in previous studies, by this approach. In our study, the length of homologous DNA overlap was approximately 1 kb rather than 50 bp and the host strain lacked the NHEJ ability, which likely explains why such a large number of DNA fragments (24) could be concatenated without using genome editing technique. This approach enhances both the precision and efficiency of homologous recombination, resulting in numerous positive clones containing all target genes. Furthermore, by enabling a greater number of DNA fragments to be used for the co-transformation of A. oryzae, our study expands the conventional technique of gene targeting through multiple homologous recombination. We also replaced the promoters of the biosynthetic genes with the constitutive promoters of A. oryzae, demonstrating the feasibility of achieving and controlling heterologous expression of secondary metabolites by optimizing biosynthetic gene expression through the strategic replacement of promoters. Overall, our study provides innovative and useful insights for expanding the potential for introducing long DNA fragments through a single co-transformation step, without the need for cloning.

In addition, only at the pyrG selectable marker, homology overlap was prepared at 548 bp. Due to the 899 bp length of the pyrG coding region and also for removing the possibility of erroneous mutant generation using the full length of pyrG in one short DNA fragment, we selected the scheme of splitting pyrG into two partial fragments. By these two partial fragments (former part and latter part) being concatenated in A. oryzae, the complete form of pyrG is constructed and enabled to function as a selectable marker. In spite of the 548 bp-long overlap only at pyrG, multiple DNA concatenations were attained at high efficiency in A. oryzae through homologous recombination in this study. Thus, if all homology overlaps were set at approximately 500 bp, the mutant having KK-1 biosynthetic genes could be constructed at relatively high efficiency. In fact, in A. niger, the correlation of the length of homology overlap and the efficiency of homologous recombination has been evaluated, showing that homologous recombination surpassed NHEJ when homology overlap was set at more than 400 bp in the NHEJ-intact strain (Jarczynska et al. 2022). Moreover, homologous recombination occurred in over 90% transformants when homology overlap was set at more than 100 bp in the NHEJ-defective strain. We used the NHEJ-defective strain of A. oryzae, in this study. Thus, a 100 bp-long overlap would work for constructing the multiple recombination mutant. However, as homology overlap is shortened, the possibility of generating the erroneous mutants by incorrect DNA concatenation increases. Taken together, even though the NHEJ-defective strain is used, a homology overlap of more than 400 bp length would be the safer choice to confidently acquire the multiple recombination mutant.

In the present study, the KK-1 production yield (maximally 0.80 mg/L for 2 days in YPD) of the A. oryzae mutants was approximately one-hundredth to one-tenth that of the original production strain (7.16 mg/L for 2 days in YPD). In the previous study (Yoshii et al., 2018), the yield was reported as approximate 4 mg/L (4 days in YPM medium) and copy numbers of some introduced genes as two or more. Thus, the yield taken in the present study was one-fifth of the previous data. The fact of lower yield in the present study appears to be a challenge for mass production by heterologous production strains, such as A. oryzae mutants. This limited production yield can be attributed to several reasons including the varieties and lengths of promoters used for expression, culture conditions (e.g. medium and period), copy numbers of introduced genes, chromosomal locus used for gene integration, and occurrence of spontaneous mutations in DNA construction using PCR. This challenge may be addressed by the introduction of multiple copies of the biosynthetic genes at several chromosomal loci. In fact, several loci named HS were reported to be useful for efficient heterologous gene expression in A. oryzae (Liu et al. 2019). This copy number increase can be achieved by replacing only two DNA fragments with fragments homologous to other chromosomal loci. In other words, the replacement of only two fragments for homologous recombination with host genome, combined with pyrG marker recycling as performed in ΔL6ΔP1 strain construction, is sufficient to introduce multiple copies of biosynthetic genes. Another solution is to obtain a large number of positive clones to eliminate low-production strains generated by spontaneous mutations by PCR. The generation of many clones increases the likelihood of obtaining mutant strains that retain high production without introducing significant mutations.

We used A. oryzae as a host for assembling a long DNA segment by concatenating multiple small DNA fragments via successive homologous recombination events in a single-step co-transformation. Notably, such an assembly is also considered possible in other filamentous fungi, as the ability of homologous recombination commonly exists in filamentous fungi. Therefore, our approach for introducing a long DNA region into a fungal chromosome as performed may be applied to other filamentous fungi, such as A. niger and A. nidulans, suitable as hosts for the heterologous production of target metabolites.

In a previous study, three transformation steps were performed after individually cloning parts of a long DNA region into a certain vector to enable KK-1 heterologous production by the constructed A. oryzae mutant (Yoshimi et al. 2018). One cycle of genetic modification, which comprises steps of transformation, single-spore isolation, and marker recycling in A. oryzae, is estimated to require approximately 50 days. Therefore, approximately 130 days (marker recycling was omitted at the last cycle) should have been required to construct the A. oryzae strain capable of KK-1 heterologous production. In addition to the time required to complete these three cycles, DNA cloning also takes additional time before transformation. In contrast, our technique omits cloning, which shorten the time required to construct the KK-1 heterologous production mutant of A. oryzae. Moreover, our single-step co-transformation approach was sufficient for constructing the production mutant, further shortening the construction period. As our approach does not require marker recycling, it also eliminates the period to construct a production mutant. In addition, the efficiency of obtaining positive clones was relatively high at approximately 30%. Therefore, our approach, which involves only one co-transformation and a subsequent single-spore isolation step, enables the introduction of a large DNA region into the chromosome of A. oryzae within approximately 30 days. This period is approximately the one-fourth of that required by standard DNA introduction. Therefore, our technique enables both the rapid construction of heterologous production mutant and the rapid generation of modified mutants through replacement of expression promoters and/or additional introduction into other loci. An advantage of this approach is that it allows the construction of both original and engineered heterologous production mutants rapidly and surely for a target secondary metabolite in filamentous fungi.

In the present study, 12 out of the 24 DNA fragments, each with fused structures as containing different gene components, were prepared using artificial DNA synthesis, although fusion PCR can also be used to generate such DNA fragments. Artificial DNA synthesis services can reduce both the labor and time for heterologous production strain construction. Although this technique increases costs, preparing all DNA fragments through artificial synthesis would decrease the labor as well as eliminate necessity of acquiring the genomic DNA of the original production strain for constructing the heterologous production mutant, and in turn, enhance the overall efficiency of strain construction. Furthermore, using this approach, artificial DNA synthesis allows for both the mutagenesis-assisted functional modification of the biosynthetic genes and the addition of other biosynthetic genes without difficulties. Through such attempts, both shifting bioproduct structure and generating a novel bioproduct are considered feasible. This approach in the present study can provide diverse applications and rapid achievement in the secondary metabolite production, desired to be utilized widely and frequently in this field hereafter.
