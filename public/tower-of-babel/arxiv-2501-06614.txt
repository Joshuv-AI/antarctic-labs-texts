Comparative Analysis of Perturbed f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) Gravity and Perturbed Rastall Gravity Models in Describing Cosmic Evolution from Early to Late Universe Relative to the Λ \Lambda CDM Model
Muhammad Yarahmadi 1 . Amin Salehi 1 . Hadis Mousavi 1

Abstract
This study conducts a meticulous examination of the cosmological implications inherent in Rastall gravity and f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity models, assessing their efficacy across distinct cosmic epochs, from early universe structure formation to late-time acceleration. In the initial stages, both models exhibit commendable compatibility with observed features of structure formation, aligning with the established Λ \Lambda CDM model. The derived Jeans’ wavenumbers for each model support their viability. However, as the cosmic timeline progresses into the late universe, a discernible disparity surfaces. Utilizing the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method, we reconstruct the deceleration parameter ( q ) (q) and identify Deceleration - Acceleration redshift transition values. For f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity, our results align closely with previous studies, emphasizing its superior ability to elucidate the recent cosmic acceleration. In contrast, Rastall gravity exhibits distinct redshift transition values. Our rigorous analysis underscores the prowess of f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity in capturing the observed cosmic acceleration, positioning it as a compelling alternative to the conventional Λ \Lambda CDM model. The discernible shifts observed in the peaks of the CMB power spectrum and evolution of deceleration parameter (q) for both f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity and Rastall gravity models in the Early and Late universe, in relation to the Λ \Lambda CDM model, provide compelling evidence supporting the proposition that these alternative gravity models can account for the anisotropy of the universe without invoking the need for dark energy.

These equations consist of nonlinear second-order differential equations with numerous variables, for which analytical solutions are generally unattainable except in the simplest cases. Consequently, only numerical analyses can be performed.

To facilitate this numerical approach, we convert the second-order differential equations into first-order equations by introducing new variables. There are several advantages to this transformation. One of the primary reasons is that first-order systems are more straightforward to solve numerically. Additionally, this conversion allows for the exploration of the system’s behavior in phase space.

Phase planes are particularly valuable for visualizing the system’s dynamics, especially in oscillatory systems, where phase paths may spiral towards zero, ”spiral out” towards infinity, or attain neutrally stable configurations known as centers. By analyzing these phase paths, we can assess whether the dynamics of a system are stable.

Likelihood Function

Likelihood Function:

where

MCMC Execution:
The MCMC algorithm was run to generate a large number of random samples (chains) from the parameter space, exploring the posterior distribution for

Analysis of MCMC Chains

•

Burn-in and Convergence:
The initial portion of the MCMC chains (burn-in period) was discarded to eliminate dependency on the initial conditions. Convergence of the chains was checked using criteria such as the Gelman-Rubin statistic.

•

Posterior Distribution:
The remaining samples represent the posterior distribution from which the mean, median, and confidence intervals for

Combining Results from Different Data Sets

Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) Effect
: The ISW effect is a phenomenon that occurs when photons from the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) travel through time-evolving gravitational potential wells in the universe. As the universe expands, these potential wells change, leading to a net gain or loss in the energy of CMB photons. This effect is particularly significant in the context of dark energy and large-scale structure.

There are two types of ISW effects:

•

Early ISW Effect
: Occurs during the radiation-dominated era and the early matter-dominated era when the potential wells are evolving.

•

Late ISW Effect
: Occurs in the current epoch, driven by the accelerated expansion of the universe due to dark energy. As dark energy causes the universe to expand more rapidly, the gravitational potential wells decay, leading to an energy gain in the CMB photons as they exit these wells.

The ISW effect is an important probe for studying the large-scale structure of the universe and provides indirect evidence for the presence of dark energy. It can be observed through correlations between the CMB and large-scale structures, such as galaxies and clusters
Sachs1967

Kofman1985

The ISW effect is manifested in the CMB temperature anisotropy caused by the gravitational potential wells that photons traverse during their journey from the last scattering surface to the observer. In the context of early

Step-by-Step Derivation: From Christoffel Symbols to the Perturbed Metric

Let’s break down the process of moving from the calculation of Christoffel symbols to deriving the perturbed FRW metric.

Compute the Christoffel Symbols (Step 1)

The Christoffel symbols, which describe the connection coefficients of the metric, are calculated using the following formula:

\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\lambda\sigma}\left(\partial_{\mu}g_{\sigma\nu}+\partial_{\nu}g_{\sigma\mu}-\partial_{\sigma}g_{\mu\nu}\right)

(104)

Here,
g_{\mu\nu}
is the perturbed metric that includes the scalar potentials
\phi
and
\psi
. The perturbed metric is given by:

g_{00}=a^{2}(\eta)(1+2\phi),\quad g_{ij}=-a^{2}(\eta)(1-2\psi)\delta_{ij}

(105)

We will now compute the Christoffel symbols step by step.

Example: Compute
\Gamma^{0}_{00}

First, let’s compute
\Gamma^{0}_{00}
. This component involves the time-time part of the metric
g_{00}
:

\Gamma^{0}_{00}=\frac{1}{2}g^{00}\partial_{\eta}g_{00}

(106)

To find this, we need the inverse of
g_{00}
, which is:

g^{00}=\frac{1}{a^{2}(\eta)(1+2\phi)}

(107)

Next, calculate the partial derivative of
g_{00}
with respect to
\eta
:

\partial_{\eta}g_{00}=\partial_{\eta}\left(a^{2}(\eta)(1+2\phi)\right)=2a(\eta)\dot{a}(\eta)(1+2\phi)+a^{2}(\eta)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}

(108)

Now, substitute this into the expression for
\Gamma^{0}_{00}
:

\Gamma^{0}_{00}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{a^{2}(\eta)(1+2\phi)}\left(2a(\eta)\dot{a}(\eta)(1+2\phi)+a^{2}(\eta)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}\right)

(109)

Simplifying this expression:

\Gamma^{0}_{00}=\frac{\dot{a}(\eta)}{a(\eta)}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}

(110)

Compute Other Christoffel Symbols

Similarly, other components like
\Gamma^{0}_{0i}
,
\Gamma^{i}_{00}
,
\Gamma^{i}_{j0}
, and
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
can be computed. For example:

•

For
\Gamma^{0}_{0i}
:

\Gamma^{0}_{0i}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{i}}

(111)

•

For
\Gamma^{i}_{00}
:

\Gamma^{i}_{00}=-\frac{1}{a^{2}(\eta)}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{i}}

(112)

•

For
\Gamma^{i}_{j0}
:

\Gamma^{i}_{j0}=\frac{\dot{a}(\eta)}{a(\eta)}\delta^{i}_{j}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x^{j}}

(113)

Compute the Ricci Tensor (Step 2)

The Ricci tensor
R_{\mu\nu}
is calculated using the Christoffel symbols:

R_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\lambda}\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\lambda}+\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\lambda\rho}\Gamma^{\rho}_{\mu\nu}-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\rho}\Gamma^{\rho}_{\nu\lambda}

(114)

Let’s compute some components of the Ricci tensor:

Compute
R_{00}

For
R_{00}
:

R_{00}=\partial_{\eta}\Gamma^{0}_{00}-\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Gamma^{i}_{00}+\Gamma^{0}_{00}\Gamma^{0}_{00}-\sum_{i}\Gamma^{i}_{00}\Gamma^{0}_{i}

(115)

Substitute the previously calculated Christoffel symbols:

R_{00}=\partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}\right)-\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\left(-\frac{1}{a^{2}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{i}}\right)

(116)

Simplifying further:

R_{00}=\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}+\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\eta^{2}}-\frac{\nabla^{2}\phi}{a^{2}}

(117)

Compute
R_{ij}

For the spatial components
R_{ij}
:

R_{ij}=\partial_{\eta}\Gamma^{0}_{ij}-\partial_{i}\Gamma^{0}_{j}-\partial_{j}\Gamma^{0}_{i}+\Gamma^{0}_{00}\Gamma^{0}_{ij}-\Gamma^{k}_{i0}\Gamma^{0}_{jk}+\text{other terms}

(118)

The calculation for
R_{ij}
is more involved but follows a similar substitution and simplification process.

Einstein Tensor and Perturbed Einstein Equations

Once the Ricci tensor
R_{\mu\nu}
is calculated, the Einstein tensor
G_{\mu\nu}
is found using:

G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R

(119)

Where
R=g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}
is the Ricci scalar. The perturbed Einstein equations then relate
G_{\mu\nu}
to the stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
via:

G_{\mu\nu}=8\pi GT_{\mu\nu}

(120)

This step involves solving for the perturbations
\phi
and
\psi
by equating the components of
G_{\mu\nu}
to the corresponding components of
T_{\mu\nu}
.

Conclusion: Deriving the Perturbed Metric

Through these steps, we’ve detailed how to go from the perturbed metric to the Christoffel symbols, then to the Ricci tensor, and finally to the Einstein tensor. The perturbed Einstein equations govern the dynamics of the scalar perturbations
\phi
and
\psi
, leading to equations like the Poisson equation, which links these perturbations to matter density.

The final perturbed metric is:

ds^{2}=a^{2}(\eta)\left[(1+2\phi)d\eta^{2}-(1-2\psi)\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}\right]

(121)
