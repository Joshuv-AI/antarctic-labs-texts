Incompressible Euler limit from the Boltzmann equation with Maxwell reflection boundary condition in the half-space
Ning Jiang, Chao Wang, Yulong Wu, Zhifei Zhang

Abstract
. In this paper, we rigorously justify the incompressible Euler limit of the Boltzmann equation with general Maxwell reflection boundary condition in the half-space. The accommodation coefficient α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in(0,1] is assumed to be O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) . Our construction of solutions includes the interior fluid part and Knudsen-Prandtl coupled boundary layers. The corresponding solutions to the nonlinear Euler and nonlinear Prandtl systems are taken to be shear flows. Due to the presence of the nonlinear Prandtl layer, the remainder equation loses one order normal derivative. The key technical novelty lies in employing the full conservation laws to convert this loss of the normal derivative into the loss of tangential spatial derivative, avoiding any loss of regularity in time. By working within an analytic L 2 ​ - ​ L ∞ L^{2}\mbox{-}L^{\infty} framework, we establish the uniform estimate on the remainder equations, thus justify the validity of the incompressible Euler limit from the Boltzmann equation for the shear flow case. Keywords. Incompressible Euler limit; Boltzmann equation; Incompressible Prandtl equation; Maxwell reflection boundary condition; Accommodation coefficients; Hilbert expansion. AMS subject classifications. 35B25; 35F20; 35Q20; 76N15; 82C40



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Boltzmann equation with Maxwell boundary condition

The Boltzmann equation in the incompressible Euler scaling is given by:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon\partial_{t}F_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}F_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{q}}B(F_{\varepsilon},F_{\varepsilon})\quad\qquad{\text{in}}\ \mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\[4.2679pt]
F_{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)=F_{\varepsilon}^{in}(x,v)\geq 0\qquad\ \,\qquad{\text{in}}\ \Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.\end{array}\right.

Here, the dimensionless number
\varepsilon>0
is the Storuhal number, and
\varepsilon^{q}
with
q>1
is the Knudsen number. The function
F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\geq 0
represents the number density of particles, where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the velocity and
x\in\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3};x_{3}>0\}
denotes the position in the half-space. For the setting up of the scaled equation (
1.1
) and its relation to the incompressible Euler equations, see
[
3
]
and
[
37
]
.

The scaled Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) is imposed with the Maxwell reflection boundary condition

(1.2)

\gamma_{-}F_{\varepsilon}=(1-\alpha)L\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}+\alpha K\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Sigma_{-}\,.

For the derivation from the dimensional Boltzmann equation to the scaled form (
1.1
), the readers can find the details in the books
[
40
,
41
]
, or papers
[
3
,
4
]
. This paper aims to rigorously justify the incompressible Euler limit of the Boltzmann equation.

The Boltzmann collision operator is defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle B(F_{1},F_{2})(v)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|v-v_{1}|^{\gamma}F_{1}(v_{1}^{\prime})F_{2}(v^{\prime})b(\theta)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{1}

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|v-v_{1}|^{\gamma}F_{1}(v_{1})F_{2}(v)b(\theta)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{1}\,,

where
v_{1}^{\prime}=v_{1}+[(v-v_{1})\cdot\omega]\omega
,
v^{\prime}=v_{1}+[(v-v_{1})\cdot\omega]\omega
,
\cos\theta=(v-v_{1})\cdot\omega/|v-v_{1}|
,
0<b(\theta)\leq C|\cos\theta|
, and
-3<\gamma\leq 1
. Such collision operators cover the full range of cutoff collision kernels. The accommodation coefficient
\alpha\in[0,1]
describes how much the molecules accommodate the state of the wall. The special case
\alpha=0
corresponds to specular reflection, while
\alpha=1
refers to complete diffusion. In this paper, we consider the case where
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\alpha=O(1)
. We remark that the specular and almost specular cases are much easier, as the Prandtl layer is weak and linear (see
[
26
,
22
,
28
]
).

We denote
n=(0,0,-1)
by the outward normal of
\Omega
. Let
\Sigma:=\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the phase space boundary of
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The phase boundary
\Sigma
can be split by outgoing boundary
\Sigma_{+}
, incoming boundary
\Sigma_{-}
, and grazing boundary
\Sigma_{0}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{+}=\{(x,v):x\in\partial\Omega\,,v\cdot n=-v_{3}>0\}\,,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{-}=\{(x,v):x\in\partial\Omega\,,v\cdot n=-v_{3}<0\}\,,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}=\{(x,v):x\in\partial\Omega\,,v\cdot n=-v_{3}=0\}\,.

Let
\gamma_{\pm}F=\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{\pm}}F
. The specular-reflection
L\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}
and the diffuse-reflection part
K\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}
in (
1.2
) are

(1.4)

\displaystyle L\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v-2[(v-u_{w})\cdot n]n)\,,

\displaystyle K\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tfrac{2\pi}{\theta_{w}(t,\bar{x})}}M_{w}(t,\bar{x},v)\int_{v\cdot n>0}\gamma_{+}F_{\varepsilon}(v\cdot n)\mathrm{d}v\,,

respectively, where
M_{w}(t,\bar{x},v)
is the local Maxwellian distribution function corresponding to the wall (boundary) with the form

(1.5)

\displaystyle M_{w}(t,\bar{x},v)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\theta_{w}(t,\bar{x}))^{3/2}}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{-\frac{|v-u_{w}(t,\bar{x})|^{2}}{2\theta_{w}(t,\bar{x})}\Big\}\,.

The quantities
u_{w},\theta_{w}
are, respectively, the velocity and temperature of the boundary. It is worth mentioning that the boundary condition (
1.2
) with (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) implies that there is no instantaneous flow across the boundary:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(v-u_{w})\cdot nF_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}v=0\,.

For simplicity, we assume
u_{w}=0,\theta_{w}=1
throughout this paper. The moving and non-isothermal boundary (i.e.
u_{w}\neq 0
and
\theta_{w}
is non-constant) case will be involved more fruitful phenomena, and will be considered in a separate paper.

1.2.
Historical remarks.

The hydrodynamic limit of the Boltzmann equation is a classical problem. Among numerous references, we mention several standard books that provide comprehensive introductions to the Boltzmann equation and its fluid dynamic limits, including
[
9
,
8
,
38
]
. Comparing to the extensive studies for the other fluid limits (such as incompressible Navier-Stokes, compressible Navier-Stokes and Euler equations), rigorous results on the incompressible Euler limit are much fewer. One of the main reasons is that in the incompressible Euler scaling, the available energy estimates for the scaled Boltzmann equation is not enough to derive the incompressible Euler equations directly. For the domains with boundary, the situation is even worse, since this limiting process includes the inviscid limit from the incompressible Navier-Stokes to Euler. It is well-known that in the limit, there arises the nonlinear Prandtl equations which are not well-understood yet, in particular for the classical solutions. Furthermore, when the Boltzmann equation is endowed with boundary conditions (Maxwell reflection or incoming boundaries), the kinetic boundary layers (or Knudsen layer) is also generated. Except some simple cases (for example, specular reflection boundary), the kinetic Knudsen layers and the fluid Prandtl layers are strongly coupled in the leading order of the expansion. In other words, the boundary layers appears as the strong boundary layers. This usually makes the problem much more challenging.

For the case that there is no boundary, an early contribution by De Masi, Esposito, and Lebowitz
[
11
]
established the incompressible Euler and Navier-Stokes limits on the torus via the Hilbert expansion method in the framework of classical solutions. After the DiPerna-Lions
[
12
]
, Saint-Raymond
[
37
,
38
]
made important contributions on this problem in the class of renormalized solutions. She used the
H
-
theorem and relative entropy method to prove the convergence from the fluctuations (no temperature) of the renormalized solutions of the Boltzmann equation towards the very weak (but very general) solutions of the Euler equation constructed by Lions
[
36
]
called dissipative solutions. Thus it provided for the first time a non-conditional result. It is also shown the weak-strong stability, i.e. if the Euler equation admits a smooth solution, then the convergence is strong.

More recently, there are two major progress on the incompressible Euler limit with boundary in the framework of analytic solutions. Jang and Kim
[
27
]
(and
[
7
]
) rigorously justified that under the incompressible Euler scaling, the asymptotics of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations with
O(\varepsilon^{\kappa})
(here
\varepsilon^{\kappa}=\frac{Knudsen\hskip 8.19447pt\!\!\!\!number}{Mach\hskip 8.19447pt\!\!\!\!number}
) dissipation from Boltzmann equation endowed the complete diffuse reflection boundary condition in the domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, corresponding to accommodation coefficient
\alpha=1
. Thus, Prandtl layer was not involved in this process. As a consequence, the incompressible Euler limit follows as a byproduct of the inviscid limit. In
[
35
]
, Kim and Nguyen justified the incompressible Euler and incompressible Prandtl limits for shear flows without passing through the Navier-Stokes equations. This work is the first to rigorously derive the nonlinear incompressible Prandtl equations directly from the Boltzmann equation. Their perspective was more on the fluid dynamic side: the Prandtl layer was obtained from the Navier-Stokes system with small dissipation, rather than being derived directly from the Boltzmann equation. As a result, the Knudsen layer was not visible in their framework. They constructed a local Maxwellian consisting of an Euler profile plus a Prandtl layer, which approximates the solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations. To ensure control over the remainder, they required analyticity in time and tangential variables
(t,x_{1},x_{2})
. We also mention the work
[
7
]
, which addressed the incompressible Euler limit in the presence of heat convection. All three of the above-mentioned works considered the fully diffuse reflection boundary condition (i.e.
\alpha=1
) and focused on the regime where the Knudsen number scales as
\varepsilon^{q}
with
q
very close to
1
.

In
[
26
]
, the incompressible Euler limit from the Boltzmann equation under the specular reflection boundary condition (i.e.
\alpha=0
) is justified also by employing the expansion method. For this simple boundary condition, the Prandtl layers are linear, and appear in the higher orders as weak boundary layers. The justification could be done in the framework of classical solutions.

For related works on the incompressible and compressible Navier-Stokes and compressible Euler limits, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
32
,
22
,
28
,
33
,
34
]
and references therein. We omit further details on these well-known results, as the focus of the present paper is to justify the incompressible Euler limit. We only point out the key difference between the (compressible and incompressible) Navier-Stokes and Euler limits from the point view of boundary layers. In fluid limits, once the Maxwellian determined by the limiting (or asympotic) fluid equations can not satisfy the boundary condition endowed on the original Boltzmann equation, the Knudsen layer would be needed. For the Knudsen boundary layer equations, the number of the solvability conditions depend on the speed of the boundary. In particular, when the boundary is not moving vertically, the number of solvability conditions is four. For the detailed information, see
[
2
,
10
,
13
,
14
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
33
,
34
,
30
,
31
,
43
]
. For the compressible and incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (including temperature), the number of boundary conditions is four. In theses cases, Knudsen layers are enough to match the expansions. In other words, the boundary layer from fluid side, i.e. the Prandtl layers are not needed. However, it is well-known that for the Euler equations (both compressible and incompressible), only one boundary condition is needed (for simplicity, we assume the boundary is not moving vertically). Thus, the number of the boundary condition provided from the solvability of the Knudsen layers does not match with the number of the boundary conditions the fluid equation needed. So for the Euler limits, both Knudsen and Prandtl layers are needed, and furthermore, they are coupled. This is the reason that usually, the limits to Euler equations are harder than those to the Navier-Stokes limits. It is only discussed for the steady boundary cases above. When the boundary is moving, the situations will be even more complex. Another issue needed to be remarked is that the boundary layer are strong or weak (i.e. in the leading order or the higher order in the expansion). This depends essentially on the types of the boundary conditions of the originally kinetic equations (Maxwell reflection or incoming boundaries, and the orders of the accommodation coefficients ) and the types of the fluid equations (nonlinear or linear, Navier-Stokes or Euler, compressible or incompressible, etc.).

In the current paper, we aim to prove the incompressible Euler limit (for the shear flow case) from the Boltzmann equation with general Maxwell reflection boundary condition, i.e. the accommodation coefficient
\alpha\in(0,1]
. We employ the classical Hilbert expansion method in the framework of analytic solutions. The key novelty is that our expansion includes the complete coupled Knudsen and Prandtl boundary layers. Furthermore, in the incompressible Euler scaling, the Prandtl boundary layers appear strongly. This coupling in the leading order bring the strong singular behavior in the remainder equation. The treatment of this difficulty will be explained in subsection 1.5.

1.4.
Main result

In this paper, we employ the Hilbert expansion method to rigorously justify the incompressible Euler limit of the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) with the general Maxwell boundary condition (
1.2
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We consider the scaling regime where the Mach number is equal to the Strouhal number. Then, according to the classical von Karman relation, the Reynolds number is given by the ratio of the Mach number to the Knudsen number, i.e.,

\mathrm{Re}=\tfrac{\mathrm{Ma}}{\mathrm{Kn}}=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{q-1}}\,.

The thickness of the Prandtl layer is
O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}}})=O(\varepsilon^{\frac{q-1}{2}})
(see
[
39
]
for instance) and the thickness of the Knudsen layer is
O(\mathrm{Kn})=O(\varepsilon^{q})
(see
[
40
,
41
]
). We choose
q=2
in (
1.1
) for simplicity of the ansatz. For the case of general
q>1
, the formal expansion will be more complex. But the analysis of the corresponding boundary layer equations will be basically the same. An outline of the formal derivation for the general
q>1
will be provided in Section
5
. As shown in Section
2
below, when
q=2
, our truncated ansatz of solution to the Boltzmann equation takes the form

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=

\displaystyle\mu+\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{13}\sqrt{\mu}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g_{k}(t,x,v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{b}_{k}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{bb}_{k})(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}},v)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{8}g_{R,\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\,.

Here,
\mu
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}
are defined in (
1.8
) and (
1.15
), respectively. The functions
g_{k}
,

, and

correspond to the interior solution, the Prandtl boundary layer, and the Knudsen boundary layer, respectively, while

denotes the remainder term.

For the initial data of the Boltzmann equation,

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)=

\displaystyle\mu+\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{13}\sqrt{\mu}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g_{k}^{in}(x,v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{b,in}_{k}(\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{bb,in}_{k})(\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}},v)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{8}g^{in}_{R,\varepsilon}(x,v)\,.

Recall that the accommodation coefficient
\alpha\in(0,1]
is assumed to be
O(1)
. Refer to (
1.6
) for the definition of the analytic norms. We now present the result on the incompressible Euler limit for the shear flow.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
-3<\gamma\leq 1
and
q=2
. Assume that
\alpha\in(0,1]
with
\alpha=O(1)
. Let
g_{k}(t,x,v),g^{b}_{k}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},v)
and
g^{bb}_{k}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}},v)
be constructed as in Proposition
3.1
. In particular,

g_{0}=(\rho_{0}+v\cdot u_{0}+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta_{0})\sqrt{\mu}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\!g^{b}_{0}=(\rho^{b}_{0}+v\cdot u^{b}_{0}+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta^{b}_{0})\sqrt{\mu}\,,

where
(\rho_{0},u_{0},\theta_{0})
and
(\rho^{b}_{0},u^{b}_{0},\theta^{b}_{0})
are the solutions to the incompressible Euler system (
2.15
) and the incompressible Prandtl system (
2.47
) for the shear flow, respectively. There is a small constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that if for
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

\|g_{R,\varepsilon}(0)\|_{X}+\varepsilon^{3}\|h_{R,\varepsilon}(0)\|_{Y}<\infty\,,

where
h_{R,\varepsilon}
is given by (
1.24
) with
\beta\geq\tfrac{9-2\gamma}{2}
, then there exist a time
T>0
such that the scaled Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) with Maxwell reflection boundary condition (
1.2
) and well-prepared initial data (
1.26
) admits a unique solution over the time interval
t\in[0,T]
with the expanded form

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=

\displaystyle\mu+\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{13}\sqrt{\mu}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g_{k}(t,x,v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{b}_{k}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},v)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{bb}_{k})(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}},v)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{8}g_{R,\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\geq 0\,,

where the remainder
g_{R,\varepsilon}
satisfies

\|g_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{X}^{2}+\varepsilon^{6}\|h_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{Y}^{2}\leq C_{T}<\infty\,.

Remark 1.1
.

Theorem
1.1
indicates that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{\varepsilon}}}\big\{F_{\varepsilon}-\mu-\varepsilon(\rho_{0}+v\cdot u_{0}+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta_{0})\mu-\varepsilon(\rho^{b}_{0}+v\cdot u^{b}_{0}+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta^{b}_{0})\mu\big\}\|_{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}\rightarrow 0\,.

This justifies the incompressible Euler limit for the shear flow from the Boltzmann equation in the half-space.

Remark 1.2
.

For general
q>1
, a formal derivation will be outlined in Section
5
. By truncating the ansatz at a suitable order, the same method as in Section
4
can be applied to obtain uniform estimates for the remainder term, thereby justifying the incompressible Euler limit for general
q>1
. While the computations are slightly more involved, they remain analogous to the case
q=2
. For simplicity, we focus primarily on the case
q=2
in this paper.

1.5.
Sketch of ideas and novelties

We employ the Hilbert expansion method to construct an approximate solution to the scaled Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) under the incompressible Euler scaling. Due to the Maxwell reflection boundary condition, coupled viscous-kinetic boundary layers (i.e. Prandtl and Knudsen layers) arise. When
q=2
, the thickness of the Prandtl layer is
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, while that of the Knudsen layer is
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Motivated by Caflisch
[
6
]
, we truncate the Hilbert expansion at a suitable order. As a result, the expansion takes the form

F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\mu+\sum_{k=0}^{13}\sqrt{\mu}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g_{k}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{b}_{k}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{bb}_{k})+\sqrt{\mu}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{8}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}\,,

with the remainder equation

(1.27)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}v\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}\mathcal{L}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2}\Gamma(\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon},\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}[\Gamma(\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon},g_{0}+g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0}+g^{b}_{0},\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon})]+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,,

where and hereafter s.o.t. refers to some other terms that are straightforward to be controlled. Here,
g_{0}=(\rho_{0}+u_{0}\cdot v+\theta_{0}\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2})\sqrt{\mu}
, where
(\rho_{0},u_{0},\theta_{0})
solves the incompressible Euler system (
2.15
), and
g^{b}_{0}=(\rho^{b}_{0}+u^{b}_{0}\cdot v+\theta^{b}_{0}\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2})\sqrt{\mu}
, where
(\rho^{b}_{0},u^{b}_{0},\theta^{b}_{0})
solves the incompressible Prandtl system (
2.47
). To obtain the uniform estimate on the remainder equation (
1.27
), the most difficult term is

\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}[\Gamma(\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon},g_{0}+g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0}+g^{b}_{0},\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon})]\,.

We remark that this singular term does not appear in the previous approaches. In
[
27
]
, there is no Prandtl layer term
g^{b}_{0}
since they worked in incompressible Navier-Stokes asymptotics. In
[
26
]
, the specular reflection was considered. For this boundary condition, the linear Prandtl layer appears in higher order.

This singular term is treated in the following way: by performing the
L^{2}
estimate on (
1.27
), we obtain

\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}[\Gamma(\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon},g_{0}+g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0}+g^{b}_{0},\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon})]\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}\leq\tfrac{\delta}{\varepsilon^{3}}\|\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}+\tfrac{C_{\delta}}{\varepsilon}\|\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{\nu}\|g_{0}+g^{b}_{0}\|_{\nu}^{2}\,.

Thanks to the coercivity of the linearized collision operator
\mathcal{L}
, the first term on the right-hand side can be absorbed into the left-hand side by choosing
\delta
sufficiently small. However, the second is of order
O(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon})
and thus singular. It is also emphasized that for different values of
q>1
, this singularity behaves like
O(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{q-1}})
. Motivated by
[
11
]
, we introduce
(\rho_{\varepsilon},u_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})=(1+\varepsilon(\rho_{0}+\rho^{b}_{0}),\varepsilon(u_{0}+u^{b}_{0}),1+\varepsilon(\theta_{0}+\theta^{b}_{0}))
and the local Maxwellian

\mu_{\varepsilon}(v)=\tfrac{\rho_{\varepsilon}}{(2\pi\theta_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{2}}}e^{-\frac{|v-u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\theta_{\varepsilon}}}\,.

By introducing the collision operators
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
and
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
around
\mu_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that

\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon})\sim\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}\mathcal{L}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}[\Gamma(\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon},g_{0}+g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0}+g^{b}_{0},\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon})]+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,.

This implies that the singular term can be absorbed into
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
. Defining
g_{R,\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}}\tilde{g}_{R,\varepsilon}
, the equation for
g_{R,\varepsilon}
is then expressed as follows

(1.28)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{R,\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{R,\varepsilon}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}g_{R,\varepsilon}+\tfrac{(\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\cdot\nabla_{x}){\mu_{\varepsilon}}}{2\mu_{\varepsilon}}g_{R,\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(g_{R,\varepsilon},g_{R,\varepsilon})+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,.

However, due to the presence of the Prandtl layer, the normal derivatives
\partial_{3}(\rho^{b}_{0},u^{b}_{0},\theta^{b}_{0})
exhibit singular behavior of order
O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}})
. Consequently, the term
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{\varepsilon}\sim O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}})
. The
L^{2}
estimate indicates that

\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tfrac{(\partial_{t}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}v\cdot\nabla_{x})\mu_{\varepsilon}}{2\mu_{\varepsilon}}g_{R,\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}v=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}b_{3}b_{i}+\partial_{3}(\rho_{\varepsilon}+\theta_{\varepsilon})b_{3}(a+c)+\partial_{3}\theta_{\varepsilon}b_{3}c\mathrm{d}x+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,,

where
(a,b,c)
are the macroscopic quantities of
g_{R,\varepsilon}
. The conservation laws of the Boltzmann equation imply that

(1.29)

\begin{cases}&\varepsilon\partial_{t}a+\sqrt{\theta_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{x}\cdot b=\textrm{s.o.t.}\,,\\
&\varepsilon\partial_{t}b+\sqrt{\theta_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{x}(a+c)=-\nabla_{x}\cdot(u_{\varepsilon}\otimes b)-\nabla_{x}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{A}(v_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}g\mathrm{d}v+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,,\\
&\varepsilon\partial_{t}c+\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{\theta_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{x}\cdot b=-\tfrac{2}{3}\nabla_{x}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{B}(v_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}g\mathrm{d}v+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,.\\
\end{cases}

We explain below our major new idea, which is based on the key observation that the singularity can be transferred into a loss of tangential derivatives. Importantly, this observation does not entail any loss of the time derivative. Hence, the solution is required to be analytic only in the tangential spatial variables, not in time. This represents a significant improvement compared to
[
35
]
. In what follows, we only consider the term
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}b_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x\ (i=1,2)
. Noticing that
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x_{3}\partial_{3}(\rho_{\varepsilon},u_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})=O(1)
and
b_{3}(t,\bar{x},0)=0
, we have by
\eqref{idea_conservation}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}b_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}\partial_{3}b_{3}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}(\varepsilon\partial_{t}a+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}b_{j})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x+\textrm{s.o.t.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}(\varepsilon\partial_{t}a)\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}x_{3}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\tfrac{\int_{0}^{x_{3}}(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}b_{j})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}}{x_{3}}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x+\textrm{s.o.t.}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}(\varepsilon\partial_{t}a)\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x+C\sum_{j=1}^{2}\|\partial_{j}b_{j}\|_{2}\|b_{i}\|_{2}+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,,

where we used the Hardy inequality. The second term loss one order tangential derivative. To get rid of the loss of time derivative , we use the equality
\partial_{t}fg=-\partial_{t}gf+\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(fg)
and
\eqref{idea_conservation}_{2}
to derive

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}(\varepsilon\partial_{t}a)\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}(\varepsilon a)\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}\partial_{t}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x+\textrm{s.o.t.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}a\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}(-\partial_{i}(a+c)-\mathrm{div}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{A}(v_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}g\mathrm{d}v)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|a\|_{2}\|\partial_{i}(a+c)\|_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\|a\|_{2}\|\partial_{j}\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{\varepsilon}g_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}a\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}(-\partial_{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{A}(v_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}g\mathrm{d}v)\mathrm{d}x+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,,

where the Hardy inequality has been used again. By performing integration by parts, it follows that

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}\int_{0}^{x_{3}}a\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{3}(-\partial_{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{A}(v_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}g_{R,\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}v)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}a(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{A}(v_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}g_{R,\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}v)\mathrm{d}x\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\|a\|_{2}\|\mathcal{P}^{\perp}g_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}\,,

which can be controlled since
\|\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{\varepsilon}g_{R,\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{\nu}=O(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}})
. As a result,

\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{3}u_{\varepsilon,i}b_{3}b_{i}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|g_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{2}(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\|\partial_{j}g_{R,\varepsilon}\|_{2})+\textrm{s.o.t.}\,.

This implies a loss of one order in tangential spatial derivatives. Thus, the energy estimates can be closed by working within analytic function spaces. Moreover, the nonlinear term
\varepsilon^{2}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(g_{R,\varepsilon},g_{R,\varepsilon})
can be estimated using the standard
L^{2}\mbox{-}L^{\infty}
framework developed in
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.

Finally, we make some remarks on the non-shear flow case. In this setting, the above analysis remains valid in principle. Because of the non-commutativity the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
and tangential derivatives, some further new estimates on the corresponding commutator will be needed. In particular, the term
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}[\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon},\partial_{i}]g
is highly singular. For simplicity, we only focus on the shear flow in the current paper. The incompressible Euler limit for general non-shear flow case will be treated in a separate paper.

1.6.
Organization of this paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present a formal analysis, where we derive the incompressible Euler and Prandtl systems, along with the corresponding asymptotic expansions. Section
3
is devoted to establishing uniform estimates for the solutions of the constructed expansions. In Section
4
, we obtain uniform estimates for the remainder term within the analytic
L^{2}\mbox{-}L^{\infty}
framework. Finally, in Section
5
, we outline the formal analysis for the case of a general scaling parameter
q>1
.

2.
Hilbert expansion

In this section, we employ the Hilbert expansion method to formally derive the incompressible Euler system from the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, under the scaling parameter
q=2
. This derivation is based on analyzing the fluctuations of the distribution function
F_{\varepsilon}
around a global Maxwellian. More precisely, we take

(2.1)

F_{\varepsilon}=\mu+\varepsilon\sqrt{\mu}g_{\varepsilon}\,,

where
\mu
is defined in (
1.8
). From plugging (
2.1
) into (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), we obtain

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\varepsilon\partial_{t}g_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{\varepsilon}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathcal{L}g_{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\Gamma(g_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})\quad\text{in}\quad\!\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\
g_{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)=g^{in}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)\quad\text{in}\quad\!\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\end{cases}

with the Maxwell reflection boundary condition

(2.3)

\gamma_{-}g_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=(1-\alpha)g_{\varepsilon}(t,x,R_{x}v)+\alpha P_{\gamma}g(t,x,v)\,,

where we used the notation
R_{x}v=v-2(v\cdot n)n=(\bar{v},-v_{3})
, and

(2.4)

P_{\gamma}g(t,x,v)=\sqrt{2\pi\mu(v)}\int_{v^{\prime}\cdot n>0}(v^{\prime}\cdot n)\sqrt{\mu(v^{\prime})}g(t,x,v^{\prime})\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}.

As will be explained below, our expansion has the form

(2.5)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}\big\{g_{k}(t,x,v)+g_{k}^{b}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},v)\big\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 2}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g_{k}^{bb}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}},v)\,,

where
g_{k}(t,x,v)
,
g_{k}^{b}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},v)
and
g_{k}^{bb}(t,\bar{x},\tfrac{x_{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}},v)
refer to the interior part, the Prandtl layer part, and the Knudsen layer part, respectively.

2.1.
Interior expansion

By the famous von Karman relation, the Reynolds number equal to the ratio of the Mach number to the Knudsen number, i.e.,

\mathrm{Re}=\tfrac{\mathrm{Ma}}{\mathrm{Kn}}=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,.

The thickness of the Prandtl layer is
O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}}})=O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, and the thickness of the Knudsen layer is
O(\mathrm{Kn})=O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Therefore, our expansion in interior takes the form

(2.6)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\thicksim\sum_{k\geq 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g_{k}(t,x,v)\,.

Plugging (
2.6
) into (
2.2
) and collecting the same orders,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-4}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g_{0}=0\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-3}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g_{1}=0\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-2}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g_{2}=\Gamma(g_{0},g_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-1}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g_{3}=\Gamma(g_{0},g_{1})+\Gamma(g_{1},g_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{0}:

\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{0}+\mathcal{L}g_{4}=\Gamma(g_{0},g_{2})+\Gamma(g_{2},g_{0})+\Gamma(g_{1},g_{1})\,,

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{k-2}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{k}+\mathcal{L}g_{k+4}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k+2\,,\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\Gamma(g_{i},g_{j})\,.

We denote

\mathcal{P}g_{k}=\big(\rho_{k}+u_{k}\cdot v+\theta_{k}\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\big)\sqrt{\mu}:=(\sqrt{\mu},v\sqrt{\mu},\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\sqrt{\mu})\cdot U_{k}\,,

where
(\rho_{k},u_{k},\theta_{k})
represent the macroscopic density, bulk velocity and temperature, respectively. Then the equation of
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-4})
and
H
-theorem implies that
g_{0}
must be an infinitesimal Maxwellian:

(2.8)

g_{0}(t,x,v)=I_{0}(U_{0}):=\big(\rho_{0}(t,x)+v\cdot u_{0}(t,x)+(\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2})\theta_{0}(t,x)\big)\sqrt{\mu}\,,

where
U_{0}=(\rho_{0},u_{0},\theta_{0})
(
U_{1},U^{b}_{1},\cdots
, that appears later are defined in the same way).

The contribution of
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-3})
gives

(2.9)

g_{1}(t,x,v)=I_{0}(U_{1}):=\big(\rho_{1}(t,x)+v\cdot u_{1}(t,x)+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta_{1}(t,x)\big)\sqrt{\mu}\,.

The contribution of
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-2})
gives

(2.10)

g_{2}=I_{0}(U_{2})+I_{1}(U_{0}):=\big(\rho_{2}(t,x)+v\cdot u_{2}(t,x)+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta_{2}(t,x)\big)\sqrt{\mu}+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathcal{A}:u_{0}\otimes u_{0}+2\mathcal{B}\cdot u_{0}\theta_{0}+C\theta_{0}^{2}\big)\,,

where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}
are the Burnett functions defined in subsection
1.3
and we have used the fact that (see
[
3
]
)

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\Gamma(\mathcal{P}f,\mathcal{P}g)+\Gamma(\mathcal{P}g,\mathcal{P}f)\}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\{\tfrac{\mathcal{P}f\cdot\mathcal{P}g}{\sqrt{\mu}}\}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\perp}\big\{\big(\rho_{f}+v\cdot u_{f}+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta_{f}\big)\big(\rho_{g}+v\cdot u_{g}+\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\theta_{g}\big)\sqrt{\mu}\big\}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:u_{f}\otimes u_{g}+\mathcal{B}\cdot(u_{f}\theta_{g}+u_{f}\theta_{g})+\mathcal{C}\theta_{f}\theta_{g}\,.

Here,
(\rho_{f},u_{f},\theta_{f})
represent the macroscopic quantities of the function
f
.

The contribution of
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-1})
gives

(2.12)

g_{3}=I_{0}(U_{3})+\big(\mathcal{A}:u_{0}\otimes u_{1}+\mathcal{B}\cdot(u_{0}\theta_{1}+u_{1}\theta_{0})+\mathcal{C}\theta_{0}\theta_{1}\big)

The solvability condition of
O(1)
is

v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{0}\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\,.

By simple calculations and using (
2.8
),

v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{0}=(1,v,\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2})\sqrt{\mu}\mathscr{A}U_{0}+\mathcal{A}:\nabla_{x}u_{0}+\mathcal{B}\cdot\nabla_{x}\theta_{0}\,,

where
\mathscr{A}
is the acoustic operator defined by

(2.13)

\mathscr{A}\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
u\\
\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{div}u\\
\nabla_{x}(\rho+\theta)\\
\tfrac{2}{3}\operatorname{div}u\end{pmatrix}\,.

Since
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
, we must have

(2.14)

\operatorname{div}u_{0}=0\,,\quad\nabla_{x}(\rho_{0}+\theta_{0})=0\,.

This represents the incompressibility condition and the Boussinesq relation. Integrability requires that
\rho_{0}+\theta_{0}\equiv 0
(for cases in a bounded domain, the Boussinesq relation should be considered alongside the conservation law of the Boltzmann equation to determine the value of
\rho_{0}+\theta_{0}
, see
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
).

The solvability condition of
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{2})
is

\partial_{t}g_{0}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{2}\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\,.

By simple calculations, we have

(2.15)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{0}+\operatorname{div}u_{2}=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}u_{0}+\nabla_{x}(\rho_{2}+\theta_{2})+\operatorname{div}(u_{0}\otimes u_{0})-\tfrac{1}{3}\nabla_{x}|u_{0}|^{2}=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}\theta_{0}+\tfrac{2}{3}\operatorname{div}u_{2}+\tfrac{5}{3}\operatorname{div}(u_{0}\theta_{0})=0\,.\end{cases}

By a simple cancellation and using (
2.14
), we know that
(\rho_{0},u_{0},\theta_{0})
satisfy the incompressible Euler system:

(2.16)

\begin{cases}\rho_{0}+\theta_{0}=0\,,\\
\operatorname{div}u_{0}=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}u_{0}+u_{0}\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{0}+\nabla_{x}p_{0}=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}\theta_{0}+u_{0}\cdot\nabla\theta_{0}=0\,,\end{cases}

where

(2.17)

p_{0}(t,x)=(\rho_{2}+\theta_{2})-\tfrac{1}{3}|u_{0}|^{2}

is the pressure. Moreover, we can obtain the incompressibility condition and Boussinesq relation for
(u_{2},\theta_{2})
from (
2.15
):

(2.18)

\operatorname{div}u_{2}=-\partial_{t}\rho_{0}\,,\quad\nabla_{x}(\rho_{2}+\theta_{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}|u_{0}|^{2})=0\,.

Next, we derive the system for
(\rho_{k},u_{k},\theta_{k})\ (k\geq 1)
. Indeed, from (
2.7
), we have

(2.19)

\mathcal{L}g_{k+2}=-\partial_{t}g_{k-4}-v\cdot\nabla g_{k-2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k\,,\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\Gamma(g_{i},g_{j})\\
=\Gamma(\mathcal{P}g_{k},g_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0},\mathcal{P}g_{k})+F_{k-1}\,,

where

(2.20)

F_{k-1}=-\partial_{t}g_{k-4}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{k-2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k\,,\\
i,j\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Gamma(g_{i},g_{j})+\Gamma(\mathcal{P}^{\perp}g_{k},g_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0},\mathcal{P}^{\perp}g_{k})

depends only on
(\rho_{j},u_{j},\theta_{j})\ (j\leq k-1)
. Multiplying (

) with
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k+2})
by
\sqrt{\mu},v\sqrt{\mu},\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\sqrt{\mu}
, one has

(2.21)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{k}+\operatorname{div}u_{k+2}=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}u_{k}+\operatorname{div}(u_{k}\otimes u_{0}+u_{0}\otimes u_{k})+\nabla_{x}(\rho_{k+2}+\theta_{k+2})-\tfrac{2}{3}\nabla_{x}(u_{0}\cdot u_{k})=\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\,,\\
\tfrac{3}{2}\partial_{t}\theta_{k}+\operatorname{div}u_{k+2}+\tfrac{5}{2}\operatorname{div}(u_{k}\theta_{0}+u_{0}\theta_{k})=\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\,,\end{cases}

where

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}^{-1}F_{k-1}\mathrm{d}v\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{L}^{-1}F_{k-1}\mathrm{d}v\,.

Once
(\rho_{k},u_{k},\theta_{k})
are determined, the incompressibility and the Boussinesq relation of
(\rho_{k+2},\\
u_{k+2},\theta_{k+2})
can be derived. Together with (
2.21
) for
k+2
,
(\rho_{k+2},u_{k+2},\theta_{k+2})
can be completely solved, inductively.

We further impose the initial data on
(\rho_{k},u_{k},\theta_{k})
(this can be realized by setting the special form of the initial data of the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
)):

(2.23)

\displaystyle(\rho_{0},u_{0},\theta_{0})(0,x)=(\rho_{0}^{in},u^{in}_{0},\theta_{0}^{in})(x_{3})\,,\quad(\rho_{k},u_{k},\theta_{k})(0,x)=(\rho_{k}^{in},u^{in}_{k},\theta_{k}^{in})(x)\,,\quad k=1,2\cdots\,.

This choice implies that the leading-order term corresponds to a shear flow.

2.2.
Viscous boundary layer expansion

Obviously,
g_{0}
in (
2.8
) matches the Maxwell reflection boundary condition (
1.2
) if and only if

u_{0}^{0}=0\,,\quad\theta_{0}^{0}=0\,.

We recall that the superscript
0
indicates the value on the boundary. However, the constraints above are too many as the boundary conditions for the incompressible Euler system. Therefore, the boundary layer correction is naturally needed. Since the thickness of the viscous layer is
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, the expansion inside the viscous layer has the form

(2.24)

\displaystyle g^{b}_{\varepsilon}(t,\bar{x},\zeta,v)\thicksim\sum_{k\geq 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}g^{b}_{k}(t,\bar{x},\zeta,v)\,,

where
\zeta=\tfrac{x_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
is the scaled normal coordinate. Throughout our paper, the far-field condition is always assumed:

(2.25)

\displaystyle g^{b}_{k}(t,\bar{x},\zeta,v)\to 0\,,\quad\textrm{as}\quad\!\zeta\to+\infty\,.

Plugging
g_{\varepsilon}+g^{b}_{\varepsilon}
into the Boltzmann equation (
2.2
) and rearrange by the order of
\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-4}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g^{b}_{0}=0\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-3}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g^{b}_{1}=0\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-2}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g^{b}_{2}=\Gamma(g_{0}^{0},g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g_{0}^{b},g^{0}_{0})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{0},g^{b}_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-1}:\quad

\displaystyle v_{3}\cdot\partial_{\zeta}g^{b}_{0}+\mathcal{L}g^{b}_{3}=\big[\Gamma(g^{b}_{0},g^{b}_{1}+g^{0}_{1})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{1}+g^{0}_{1},g^{b}_{0})\big]+\big[\Gamma(g^{0}_{0},g^{b}_{1})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{1},g^{0}_{0})\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\zeta\big[\Gamma(g_{0}^{(1)},g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{0},g_{0}^{(1)})\big]\,,

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots

(2.26)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{k}:\quad

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g^{b}_{k-2}+\bar{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bar{x}}g^{b}_{k}+v_{3}\cdot\partial_{\zeta}g^{b}_{k+1}+\mathcal{L}g^{b}_{k+4}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k+2\,,\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\Gamma(g^{b}_{i},g^{b}_{j})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k+2\,,\\
i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\Big[\Gamma(g^{0}_{i},g^{b}_{j})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{j},g^{0}_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+l+j=k+2\,,\\
1\leq l\leq N\,,i,j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tfrac{\zeta^{l}}{l!}\Big[\Gamma(g^{(l)}_{i},g^{b}_{j})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{j},g^{(l)}_{i})\Big]\,,

where the Taylor expansion at
x_{3}=0
is used:

\displaystyle g_{i}=g_{i}^{0}+\sum_{1\leq l\leq N}\tfrac{\zeta^{l}}{l!}g_{i}^{(l)}+\tfrac{\zeta^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\widetilde{g}_{i}^{(N+1)}\,.

Here, the number
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
will be chosen later. We define

(2.27)

\displaystyle p^{b}_{k}=\rho^{b}_{k}+\theta^{b}_{k}\,.

Similar to the analysis in the interior, we have

(2.28)

g^{b}_{0}=B(U^{b}_{0}):=\big(\rho^{b}_{0}+u^{b}_{0}\cdot v+\theta^{b}_{0}\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\big)\sqrt{\mu}\,,

(2.29)

g^{b}_{1}=B(U^{b}_{1}):=\big(\rho^{b}_{1}+u^{b}_{1}\cdot v+\theta^{b}_{1}\tfrac{|v|^{2}-3}{2}\big)\sqrt{\mu}\,,

(2.30)

\displaystyle g^{b}_{2}=

\displaystyle B(U^{b}_{2})+\big(\mathcal{A}:u^{0}_{0}\otimes u^{b}_{0}+\mathcal{B}(u^{0}_{0}\theta^{b}_{0}+u^{b}_{0}\theta^{0}_{0})+\mathcal{C}\theta^{0}_{0}\theta^{b}_{1}\big)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathcal{A}:u^{b}_{0}\otimes u^{b}_{0}+2\mathcal{B}u^{b}_{0}\theta^{b}_{0}+\mathcal{C}(\theta^{b}_{0})^{2}\big)\,.

The contribution of
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}^{-1})
gives

(2.31)

v_{3}\partial_{\zeta}g^{b}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\,.

That is

(2.32)

\partial_{\zeta}(\rho^{b}_{0}+\theta^{b}_{0})v_{3}\sqrt{\mu}+\partial_{\zeta}u^{b}_{0,3}\tfrac{|v|^{2}}{3}\sqrt{\mu}+\partial_{\zeta}\theta^{b}_{0}\mathcal{B}_{3}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathcal{A}_{3j}\partial_{\zeta}u^{b}_{0,j}\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\,,

which further implies

(2.33)

\partial_{\zeta}(\rho^{b}_{0}+\theta^{b}_{0})=0\,,\partial_{\zeta}u^{b}_{0,3}=0\,.

Together with the far-field condition (
2.25

(2.34)

p^{b}_{0}(t,\bar{x},\zeta)=(\rho^{b}_{0}+\theta^{b}_{0})(t,\bar{x},\zeta)\equiv 0\,,\quad u^{b}_{0,3}(t,\bar{x},\zeta)\equiv 0\,.

Moreover, we obtain that

(2.35)

\displaystyle g^{b}_{3}=

\displaystyle B(U^{b}_{3})+\mathcal{L}^{-1}\big\{-\mathcal{P}^{\perp}(v_{3}\partial_{\zeta}g^{b}_{0})+\big[\Gamma(g^{b}_{0},g^{b}_{1}+g^{0}_{1})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{1}+g^{0}_{1},g^{b}_{0})\big]+\big[\Gamma(g^{0}_{0},g^{b}_{1})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{1},g^{0}_{0})\big]

\displaystyle+\zeta\big[\Gamma(g_{0}^{(1)},g^{b}_{0})+\Gamma(g^{b}_{0},g_{0}^{(1)})\big]\big\}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(U^{b}_{3})-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{3j}\partial_{\zeta}u^{b}_{0,j}-\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{3}\partial_{\zeta}\theta^{b}_{0}

\displaystyle+\big(\mathcal{A}:u^{b}_{0}\otimes(u^{b}_{1}+u^{0}_{1}+\zeta u^{(1)}_{0})+\mathcal{B}(u^{b}_{0}(\theta^{b}_{1}+\theta^{0}_{1}+\zeta\theta^{(1)}_{0})+\theta^{b}_{0}(u^{b}_{1}+u^{0}_{1}+\zeta u^{(1)}_{0}))

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}\theta^{b}_{0}(\theta^{b}_{1}+\theta^{0}_{1}+\zeta\theta^{(1)}_{0})\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\mathcal{A}:u^{0}_{0}\otimes u^{b}_{1}+\mathcal{B}(u^{0}_{0}\theta^{b}_{1}+u^{b}_{1}\theta^{0}_{0})+\mathcal{C}\theta^{0}_{0}\theta^{b}_{1}\big)\,,

where

are defined in (

2.4.

5.
Formal derivation for general
q>1

We consider the Boltzmann equation under the general scaling parameter
q>1
.

(5.1)

\varepsilon\partial_{t}F_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}F_{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{q}}B(F_{\varepsilon},F_{\varepsilon})\,.

We take
F_{\varepsilon}=\mu+\varepsilon\sqrt{\mu}g_{\varepsilon}
, and the equation fo
g_{\varepsilon}
is

(5.2)

\varepsilon\partial_{t}g_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{\varepsilon}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{q}}\mathcal{L}g=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{q-1}}\Gamma(g_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})\,.

The formal Hilbert expansion of
g_{\varepsilon}
must account for all powers of
\varepsilon
appearing in the equation. Specifically, the asymptotic structure is determined by the characteristic thicknesses of the boundary layers: the Prandtl layer has thickness
O(\varepsilon^{\frac{q-1}{2}})
, and the Knudsen layer has thickness
O(\varepsilon^{q})
. To consistently capture these multiple scales, we propose the following multi-scale ansatz:

(5.3)

g_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{m,n,j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{m+\frac{q-1}{2}n+qj}G_{mnj}\,,

where
G_{mnj}=g_{mnj}+g^{b}_{mnj}+g^{bb}_{mnj}
, representing the interior solution, the Prandtl boundary layer, and the kinetic (Knudsen) boundary layer, respectively. It is crucial to observe that

\varepsilon^{qj}=\varepsilon^{\frac{q-1}{2}\cdot 2j+j}\,,

which can be absorbed into the indices
m
and
n
. Thus, the ansatz simplifies to

(5.4)

g_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}\varepsilon^{m+\frac{q-1}{2}n}G_{mn}\,.

We distinguish three cases according to the arithmetic nature of
q
.

Case 1.
q\in\mathbb{Z},q\geq 2
.

In this case,
\frac{q-1}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
. If
q
is odd, then
\frac{q-1}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and the expansion takes the standard Hilbert-type form:

(5.5)

g_{\varepsilon}=G_{00}+\varepsilon G_{01}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{02}+\cdots\,.

If
q
is even, then
\frac{q-1}{2}\notin\mathbb{Z}
, and the expansion includes fractional powers of
\varepsilon
:

(5.6)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle G_{00}+\varepsilon G_{01}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{02}+\cdots

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{\frac{q-1}{2}}\{G_{10}+\varepsilon G_{11}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{12}+\cdots\}\,.

In particular, the scaling
q=2
corresponds to the case considered in this paper, and the expansion structure coincides with the form above.

Case 2.
q\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},q=\tfrac{b}{a}>1,\gcd(a,b)=1
.

We further divide this case into two subcases depending on the parity of
b-a

•

b-a
is even. The exponent
\tfrac{q-1}{2}=\tfrac{b-a}{2a}
is rational, and the expansion terminates at a finite level:

(5.7)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle G_{00}+\varepsilon G_{01}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{02}+\cdots

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{\frac{q-1}{2}}\{G_{10}+\varepsilon G_{11}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{12}+\cdots\}

\displaystyle+\cdots

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{\frac{(a-1)(q-1)}{2}}\{G_{(a-1)0}+\varepsilon G_{(a-1)1}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{(a-1)2}+\cdots\}\,.

•

b-a
is odd. To preserve integrality of all exponents, the expansion must extend further, up to order

Case 3:
q\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q},q>1
.

When
q
is irrational, the exponent
\frac{q-1}{2}
is also irrational, and the powers of
\varepsilon
appearing in the expansion cannot be expressed as finite linear combinations of a fixed finite set of exponents with non-negative integer coefficients.
As a result, the expansion has infinitely many rows of different fractional orders:

(5.9)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle G_{00}+\varepsilon G_{01}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{02}+\cdots

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{\frac{q-1}{2}}\{G_{10}+\varepsilon G_{11}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{12}+\cdots\}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{\frac{2(q-1)}{2}}\{G_{20}+\varepsilon G_{21}+\varepsilon^{2}G_{22}+\cdots\}

\displaystyle+\cdots\,.

This expansion involves infinitely many layers, each associated with a distinct irrational power of
\varepsilon
, reflecting the non-periodic structure of the scales in this setting. Although there are infinitely many rows in this case due to the irrational nature of the scaling, we will truncate the ansatz at a suitable order in both rows and columns. The resulting truncated ansatz thus contains only finitely many terms.

Once the multi-scale ansatz is constructed, one can proceed with the formal derivation of the fluid-dynamic equations. This is achieved by substituting the expansion into the kinetic equation and collecting terms of the same order in
\varepsilon
. In this way, we recover the governing equations for the interior solution, the Prandtl boundary layer, and the Knudsen layer, following a procedure analogous to the classical Hilbert expansion described in Section
2
. By truncating the ansatz at a suitable order and applying a similar technical method as in Section
4
, we can justify the incompressible Euler limit for general
q>1
.
