The interaction between the RNA aptamer Dap1 and DasR mirrors DNA operator site recognition in GntR repressors
Authors: Marharyta O Makarova, Jan Hartmann, Lisa-Marie Maurer, Jannik Schweden, Michael-Paul Vockenhuber, Sigrid Weiler, Claudia Egerer-Sieber, Martin T Stiebritz, Beatrix Suess, Yves A Muller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA aptamers targeting bacterial repressors show significant promise in synthetic biology. We determined the crystal structure of the SELEX-derived aptamer Dap1 in complex with its target, the GntR/HutC repressor family member DasR. In the complex, two monomers of Dap1 individually sequester the two DNA-binding domains (DBDs) of the dimeric repressor. The DBDs of DasR interact with Dap1 in a highly similar fashion to what is observed in GntR–dsDNA complexes. This also holds true at the level of single amino acids, and identical arginine residues play crucial roles in both RNA and DNA recognition. The structure shows how DasR binding to Dap1 competes with DasR binding to its operator dre sites and, hence, how Dap1 is able to induce DasR-controlled gene transcription in vivo. A structure-informed Dap1 aptamer pool randomized at only four positions allowed for the identification of the novel aptamer Dap1m1 that binds and de-represses the DasR homologous protein NagR instead of DasR. This shows that the complex structure can serve as a blueprint for the design of novel aptamer–protein interaction pairs, opening up new venues for the generation of aptamers directed against other GntR/HutC family members and possibly also against other repressor classes.

Introduction

In vitro selected RNA aptamers directed against specific protein targets show considerable therapeutic potential but also significant promise in the field of synthetic biology [1–3]. In synthetic biology, they allow for the design of genetic devices and circuits for manipulating cellular processes at the level of transcription, splicing, and translation via various input signals [4–6].

Traditionally, bacterial repressors like LacI, AraC, or TetR are used extensively for the conditional control of gene expression in various model organisms. The most prominent example is the Tet-on/Tet-off system that makes use of the bacterial repressor TetR in combination with the tetO operator sequence [7]. It functions as a switch that, in the off-state, represses transcription, while the administration of an inducer such as tetracycline or doxycycline leads to the on-state and the induction of gene expression. Inducers dislocate TetR from the tetO operator, thereby allowing access to the promoter region. The regulation of multiple genes can be achieved by combining the Tet-on/Tet-off system with additional bacterial repressor systems, such as, for example, the cumate gene-switch system, which relies on the bacterial repressor CymR, the cuo operator, and the inducer cumate [8, 9].

Moreover, it has been shown that the range of applications of these systems can be expanded and further fine-tuned through the use of in vitro selected RNA aptamers that also target bacterial repressors such as TetR [4–6, 10]. In combination with small-molecule inducers, RNA aptamers allow for the construction of logical gates such that the transcription of a single gene can be controlled by multiple signals. Recently, an aptamer has been developed that targets the bacterial repressor DasR from Streptomyces coelicolor, similarly to the targeting of TetR by the TetR-binding aptamer K1 [11]. The DasR-binding aptamer 1 (Dap1, previously also termed B1m15 [11]) was obtained by the SELEX approach (systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment) using DasR as a target. Dap1 binds to DasR and interferes with DasR binding to the dre (DasR responsive element) site, thereby switching on gene expression [11, 12].

DasR is a member of the GntR/HutC family of bacterial repressors, with the latter family being a branch of the very densely populated GntR superfamily (Pfam family: PF00392) [13, 14]. DasR from S. coelicolor forms a homodimer, and each monomer consists of a DNA-binding domain (DBD, residues 1–88) and an effector-binding domain (EBD, residues 89–254) [15]. The EBD also contains the dimerization interface of DasR. All GntR repressors share a similar DBD structure, which consists of a winged-helix-turn-helix domain (wHTH domain) and interacts with double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) via a shared DNA-binding mode [16]. However, the various GntR branches differ in the architecture of the EBD, which, in the case of DasR and the GntR/HutC family, consists of a chorismate lyase-type UTRA domain [13, 16, 17].

In the present study, we experimentally determined the structure of the complex formed between Dap1 and DasR. We present a model for how the aptamer is able to dislocate DasR from its dre site and thereby induce gene expression. We show that the binding determinants of DasR for recognizing either dsDNA or Dap1 are highly similar. We also explore the extent to which the DasR–Dap1 complex structure can be used as a blueprint for the design of aptamers directed against other GntR/HutC repressors. Structure predictions with modern AI techniques, such as AlphaFold, yield models that bear little resemblance to the experimentally determined structure. We reckon that this is likely caused by the limited number of RNA–protein complex structures that are available for training AI-based programs.

Material and methods

Protein production and purification

Full-length wild-type DasR protein from S. coelicolor and mutant variants were produced by transforming Escherichia coli BL21 (DE) pREP4::groESL cells with pET15b-derived plasmids harbouring the dasR gene (residues 1–254, UniProtKB Q9K492) or variants thereof and an N-terminal hexa-His-tag [18]. The tag can be cleaved off by either TEV protease (plasmid pBT_639) or thrombin (pBT_242). DasR point mutant variants were produced by mutating the plasmid pBT_639 using PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis protocols.

The DasR protein and mutant variants used for biochemical characterization and ITC measurements were produced as follows. E. coli cells were transformed with the plasmid pBT_639 (or variants thereof) and inoculated into TB medium (Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) supplemented with 200 µg/mL ampicillin, 50 µg/mL kanamycin, and an auto-induction stock providing 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.05% glucose, and 0.2% lactose [19]. Initial cell growth occurred at 33°C for 4 h before lowering the temperature to 18°C and shaking overnight for protein production. The cell suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet was stored at −80°C until further usage. For protein purification, the cell pellet was resuspended in phosphate buffer supplemented with 1 M NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, 0.5 mM AEBSF, and 0.5 mM EDTA at pH 8. After cell disruption via sonification, the protein solution was purified using two Ni2+-affinity chromatography steps, followed by a size exclusion chromatography, if necessary. A TEV cleavage was performed between the first and second Ni2+-affinity chromatography step. The purity of fractions was monitored via SDS-PAGE (acrylamide/bis-acrylamide ratio = 37.5/1). After final purification, pure fractions were pooled, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C in phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) containing 200 mM NaCl until further usage.

The DasR protein used for crystallization was purified slightly differently using plasmid pBT_242. In this case, the His-tag was cleaved off using thrombin. The cells were grown at 37°C in TB auto-induction medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 10 μg/mL kanamycin as selection markers [19]. After 2 h, the temperature was lowered to 20°C, and the bacteria were grown for 48 h to the stationary phase. After cell harvesting, the cells were disrupted by sonication, the supernatant loaded onto a His Trap FF crude column (Cytiva, Munich, Germany), and the protein eluted via a linear gradient of 20–500 mM imidazole. The fractions containing the DasR protein were pooled and dialysed against 30 mM potassium phosphate, 0.1 mM EDTA, 200 mM NaCl, and pH 7.2. Thrombin digestion was performed for 20 h concomitantly with the dialysis and stopped upon addition of 2 mM AEBSF. The digested protein was loaded on a Heparin Sepharose column (Cytiva) and eluted via a linear gradient of 200 to 1000 mM NaCl. Pure DasR fractions were identified using SDS-PAGE, pooled, and concentrated. Aliquots were flash frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Full-length wild-type NagR protein (previously also termed YvoA) from Bacillus subtilis was produced and purified as described earlier [20, 21]. The NagR variant M49T, which was generated by mutating the wild-type nagR-encoding plasmid, was produced similarly to the DasR mutants, as described above. In short, E.coli BL21 (DE) pREP4::groESL cells were transformed with a pBT15b-derived plasmid harbouring the mutated nagR gene (residues 1–243, UniProtKB O34817), together with an N-terminal His-tag and a TEV protease cleavage site. Cells were grown for 3 h at 37°C in TB medium containing 200 µg/mL ampicillin and 50 µg/mL kanamycin as selection markers and an auto-induction stock containing 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.05% glucose, and 0.2% lactose. The temperature was lowered to 18°C and incubated overnight. Cell suspensions were centrifuged, and the pellet was stored at −80°C. For protein purification, the cell pellet was resuspended, disrupted by sonication, and the supernatant purified via Ni2+-affinity chromatography followed by a TEV protease digestion step and a second affinity chromatography step to remove the His-Tag. A subsequent size exclusion chromatography step yielded pure NagR protein, as monitored via SDS-Page. Fractions were pooled, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C in phosphate buffer pH 8.0.

In vitro transcription and purification of RNA aptamers

RNA was transcribed in vitro using a HindIII-linearized plasmid as a template. The plasmid codes for the T7 promoter followed by the aptamer sequence and a self-cleaving HDV ribozyme to ensure homogeneous 3′-ends (for the full plasmid sequence see Supplementary Information). In-vitro transcription was performed at 37°C overnight in a total volume of 10 ml containing 20 mM magnesium acetate, 200 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 20 mM DTT, 2 mM spermidine, 0.2 mg/ml linearized plasmid, 4 mM of each NTP, and 7.5 µg/ml of T7 polymerase (homemade). After transcription, precipitated pyrophosphate was pelleted by centrifugation, and 10% (v/v) EDTA (0.5 mM, pH 8.0) was added to the supernatant. After precipitation with 100% ethanol and a subsequent washing step with 70% ethanol, a denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (8% PAA, 8 M urea) was performed. The aptamer RNA was detected via UV shadowing, cut out, and eluted from the gel in 300 mM sodium acetate, pH 6.5, at 4°C overnight. To remove the remaining PAA, the supernatant was filtered using a 0.45 µm filter (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany), and again the RNA was precipitated and washed using ethanol as described above. Finally, the RNA was dissolved in ddH2O and stored at −20°C. RNA molarity was determined by spectrophotometric measurement using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity was analysed by gel electrophoresis.

Biochemical characterization of aptamer–repressor interactions

Complex formation between protein and aptamer variants was investigated in vitro using analytical gel filtration (AGF) chromatography. Prior to complex formation, the aptamers were snap-folded upon incubation for 1 min at 95°C, followed by 10 min on ice, and diluted with AGF buffer (30 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl). Protein variants were mixed with snap-folded aptamers at a molar ratio of 1.0:1.3 (protein monomer:aptamer), and incubated for 1 h at 25°C while gently shaking. 30 µl samples of 65 µM aptamer and/or 50 µM protein were applied to a Superdex 200 5/150 GL chromatography column (Cytiva) equilibrated with AGF buffer.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was used to determine the binding affinities of the aptamer Dap1 to DasR and NagR variants. The snap-folded aptamers were diluted with ITC buffer (50 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4, 200 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), and aliquots of protein solutions were buffer-exchanged into ITC buffer using Vivaspin Turbo 4 concentrators (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany, molecular weight cut-off 10 kDa). In the ITC measurements, the protein variants were titrated into the aptamer solution (45–70 µM protein variants into 7–12 µM aptamer). Blank titrations were performed by titrating each protein variant into the ITC buffer only. Each measurement consisted of 25 incremental titration steps (1 × 5.14 µl, 24 × 10 µl) interspaced by 360 s time intervals at 25°C and 150 rpm stirring rate using degassed solutions. Measurements were recorded in triplicate. A Standard Volume Nano ITC with a 24K gold cell was used (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA), and data were processed with the software NanoAnalyze (v3.11.0, TA Instruments).

In vivo screening and measurement of aptamer variants

A pool with the Dap1 aptamer sequence fully randomized at four positions (pPool_Dap1_4) was cloned into the high copy plasmid pJBL2807 via GoldenGate cloning as previously described [22]. The aptamer pool was expressed under the control of the strong Ptac promoter, rendering aptamer synthesis inducible upon addition of IPTG. The aptamers are stabilized by the classical T7 terminator at their 3′ end and an additional stem-loop at their 5′ end [11].

A low copy number reporter plasmid, psfGFP.NagR.nagO was constructed analog to psfGFP.DasR.dasO [11]. Thereby, the weak synthetic promoter J23112 was used to drive NagR expression [23]. The NagR binding site (nagO, cagctggtctagatcactag) was integrated directly upstream of the −10 and −35 boxes (GATACT and TTGACA) of the generic superfolder GFP promoter to ensure repression of GFP expression upon NagR binding. In case an IPTG-induced aptamer prevents NagR from binding its operator DNA, gfp expression is induced [11]. The screening system is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

E. coli DH5α psfGFP.NagR.nagO cells were transformed with pPool_Dap1_4 via electroporation and plated on selective LB plates containing 1 mM IPTG. After incubation overnight, fluorescent colonies were picked using a fluorescence binocular. The colonies were inoculated in 96-well deep-well plates containing 500 µl selective LB medium and cultivated overnight at 37°C and 1000 rpm in a plate shaker. The next day, each plate was used to inoculate 96-well plates, one containing 1 ml LB medium with and one w/o 1 mM IPTG. Following incubation overnight as before, 40 µl of each culture was resuspended in 160 µl PBS and used to measure the fluorescence/oD600 of each sample in a microplate plate reader (excitation wavelength 475 nm and emission wavelength 510 nm). Promising candidates were inoculated in triplicate for a second round of fluorescence measurement in 24-well plates with 1 ml selective LB medium and measured as described above. E. coli cells carrying the reporter plasmid as well as the empty pool plasmid were used as a negative control in each measurement. In this strain, NagR is always expressed, leading to maximal repression of gfp. A similar combination was used for a positive control. Here, a derivative of the reporter plasmid missing the nagR gene was used, leading to constant gfp expression. Each measurement was repeated at least once. Aptamer sequences that show regulatory activity were identified by Sanger sequencing.

Dap1 mutants were ordered as oligonucleotides and cloned into the vector pJBL2807 via GoldenGate cloning and confirmed via Sanger sequencing. Chemocompetent E. coli DH5α cells containing psfGFP.DasR.dasO were transformed with assembled plasmids and plated on selective LB agar plates. One colony was selected from each mutant for measurement. They were inoculated in test tubes containing 4 mL selective LB and incubated overnight at 37°C and 130 rpm. The splitting and measuring were performed as described above.

DasR-mutants were created using primers that introduce the point mutations into the template plasmid psfGFP.DasR.dasO via PCR. The linearized plasmids were reassembled by 5′-phosphorylation and ligation. The circularized plasmids were then transformed into chemocompetent E. coli DH5α cells. These cells were restored to a competent state and transformed with pPool_Dap1. The measurement was carried out as described above with the Dap1-mutants. For these measurements, the following plasmids were used as controls in combination with an empty pool plasmid psfGFP.dasO (positive control) and psfGFP.dasO.DasR (negative control).

DasR-RNA aptamer complex purification and cocrystallization

Dap1 was snap-folded upon incubation for 1 min at 95°C, followed by 10 min on ice. DasR and Dap1 were mixed in a 1.00:1.15 molar ratio (protein monomer:aptamer) and incubated on ice for 1 h. The sample was loaded onto a Superdex 200 16/60 SEC column (Cytiva), and the column was equilibrated with a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, and 200 mM NaCl. DasR-Dap1-containing fractions were pooled, and the protein solution was concentrated to 9 mg/ml in SEC buffer. Initial crystals were obtained using the Hampton Research Index Screen (Hampton Research, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA) and the sitting drop technique. Thin crystal plates derived from condition 96 of the screen (0.15 M potassium bromide, 30% w/v polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 2000) were optimized using the Hampton Research Additive Screen, and upon mixing 30 µl of each additive solution with 300 µl of Index Screen condition 96. This was done in a sitting drop plate with a protein-to-reservoir solution ratio of 0.4:0.2 µl. The best crystals were obtained with condition 61 of the Additive Screen (30% v/v ethylene glycol). Crystals were further optimized in hanging drops using 700 µl of Index Screen condition 96 mixed with 70 µl of Additive Screen condition 61 as a reservoir solution. Well-diffracting crystals grew at 19°C in drops consisting of 2 µl protein solution, 1 µl reservoir, and 0.15 µl water. The crystals were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen directly from the drop.

Crystal structure determination

A 2.0 Å resolution diffraction data set was collected from crystals of DasR in complex with Dap1 at the EMBL MX beamline P14 of the DESY synchrotron Hamburg. Data were processed with the program XDS [24]. The crystals diffracted highly anisotropically. Therefore, the web server STARANISO was used to generate an ellipsoidally truncated dataset for refinement (http://staraniso.globalphasing.org/cgi-bin/staraniso.cgi). While the diffraction is limited to only 3.0 Å resolution in one direction, the dataset extends to 2.0 Å resolution in a plane oriented perpendicular to the first direction. Initial phases were obtained with the molecular replacement technique with program PHENIX_MRAGE using the previously determined structure of full-length DasR as a search model (PDB entry code 4ZS8) [15, 25, 26]. Molecular replacement calculations performed with an entire DasR dimer yielded LLG and TFZ scores of 360 and 17, respectively. Inspection of the initial electron density maps revealed parts of the bound RNA aptamers, and that dimeric DasR interacts with two distinct RNA aptamer molecules. This observation allowed for real-space averaging of the electron density maps and significantly facilitated manual model building in the program COOT [27]. The structure was built to completion by alternating between manual model building steps and automated refinement with the program PHENIX [28]. NCS restraints were applied throughout the refinement. Crystallographic data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Table 1.

Computational analysis and structure predictions

Structures were compared and superimposed using either program LSQKAB from the CCP4 program suite or the US align web server [30, 31]. The latter server was used to compare the experimental structure to models generated with the program AlphaFold3 [32]. AlphaFold3 was executed via the AlphaFold3 webserver (https://alphafoldserver.com/). Contact surface areas were calculated with the program AREAIMOL [30]. Side chain-packing calculations were performed with the latest version of the program MUMBO available from the software repository Gitlab (https://gitlab.com/group_muller/mumbo_software3) [33]. Program OpenMM was used for energy minimization calculations and Langevin molecular dynamics simulations [34]. All 3D structure illustrations were produced with the program Pymol (Version 1.3, https://www.pymol.org/).

Results

Crystal structure of the DasR-RNA aptamer complex

The crystal structure of DasR in complex with the 32-nucleotide-long RNA aptamer Dap1 was determined at 2.0 Å resolution (Table 1). The structure displays a 2:2 stoichiometry with each of the two aptamer molecules interacting separately with the two DNA-binding domains of the DasR dimer (Fig. 1A). Of the two aptamers present in the crystallographic asymmetric unit, one aptamer (chain C in PDB entry 9I0V) is more clearly defined by its electron density than the second aptamer. Therefore, the following description focuses on this aptamer and its interface with DasR. This notwithstanding, the two Dap1 and DasR copies in the asymmetric unit display high structural similarities, and all non-hydrogen atoms can be pairwise superimposed with r.m.s. deviation of 0.61 and 0.45 Å, respectively.

Dap1 adopts a distinctive stem-loop-like structure in the complex (Fig. 1B). Of the 32 nucleotides of the aptamer, 22 are involved in canonical Watson-Crick base pairings. The majority of these pairings (10 out of 11) are formed by G-C base pairs (Fig. 1C). Next to a six-base-pair-long closing stem that adopts a canonical A-form double helix, the structure contains a non-canonical central region and a non-canonical apical heptaloop. The latter two regions are separated from each other by additional canonical base pairs.

In the central region, nucleotides C7 and A26 form a non-canonical interaction via the Watson-Crick edges of both nucleobases (Fig. 1D) [35]. Nucleotides C8, A10, and U25 form a unique base triplet (Fig. 1E). In this triplet, the N6 amino group of A10 bridges between the O2 carbonyl oxygen atoms of C8 and U25. No direct interactions occur between C8 and U25. Possibly, the C7-A26 base pair and C8-A10-U25 base triplet help to position via stacking interactions the following canonical base pairs G9-C24 and G11-C23 for optimal interactions with arginine residues from DasR (see below).

Of note is also a highly distorted and twisted pseudo-canonical interaction between U13 and A18 in the apical loop region (Fig. 1C). While U13 forms a single canonical H-bond with A18, a second H-bond is formed between atom O2 of U13 and atom N4 of C16, which stacks against A18 (Fig. 1F). Of all nucleotides, only three nucleotides, A12, C17, and U20, are not involved in any nucleobase–nucleobase interactions.

Previous research has shown that DasR and other members of the GntR/HutC family display multiple conformations [15, 21, 36]. They differ in the positioning of the DBDs in relation to the EBDs. In the DNA-bound state, the DBDs are juxtaposed next to each other and positioned in a ‘downwards’ orientation. In contrast, in the effector-bound induced and DNA-binding incompetent state, the DBDs are positioned in an ‘upwards’ orientation that precludes DNA binding through steric hindrance (Supplementary Fig. S2). In the DasR–Dap1 complex, no significant conformational changes are detectable at the level of the individual DBDs and EBDs of DasR in comparison to previous observations [15]. At the same time, the DBDs of DasR appear to be locked in the ‘upwards’ orientation, which according to the current model for the allosteric regulation of GntR/HutC-like repressors corresponds to the DNA-binding incompetent conformation (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S2).

The DasR–Dap1 interaction

The interface area covers ca. 910 Å2, and a total of 16 nucleotides from Dap1 contribute to the interaction with DasR. These nucleotides stretch from the apical loop to the central region of Dap1. Although each Dap1 molecule contacts both chains of the DasR dimer, the DasR–Dap1 interaction is dominated by a primary binding epitope that is formed between the aptamer and the DBD of a single DasR chain (Fig. 1A). A total of 33 amino acids from the DBD participate in this epitope, and their surface contributions account for about 88% of the total interface area. The remaining 12% are contributed by a secondary binding epitope that involves five residues displayed from the EBD domain of the second DasR monomer.

In the primary Dap1-binding epitope, the side chains of six amino acids form direct hydrogen bonds with nucleobases. These interactions are therefore considered to play prime roles with regard to the specificity of the aptamer–DasR interaction. Thus, the side chains from three arginine residues, Arg48, Arg58, and Arg62, contact three guanine nucleobases from regular G-C base pairs via the Hoogsteen edges of the guanine nucleobases (Fig. 2A–D). Additional hydrogen bonds are formed between the side chains of Ser57, Thr59, and Asp55 and the nucleobases of G19, G21, and U20, respectively (Fig. 2E–F). U20 is not involved in any base pairing. Instead, atom O2 of U20 forms a hydrogen bond with the carboxylate group of Asp55. In addition to these intermolecular hydrogen bonds, the amid group of the side chain of Gln63 participates in a stacking interaction with the pyrimidine ring of U13 (Fig. 2D).

In the secondary contact area, an additional interaction formed between Dap1 and the EBD of the second DasR monomer might contribute indirectly to the specificity of the Dap1-DasR interaction. The main chain of residue Gly213’ forms a Van-der-Waals contact with the nucleobase of A12, and the closest intermolecular distance (3.6 Å) occurs between the Cα atom of Gly213’ and atom N1 of A12. Structure modelling shows that a substitution of Gly213’ against any other amino acid would result in a steric clash between the side chain of the newly introduced amino acid and A12 (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Mutagenesis analysis of the DasR–Dap1 interaction

We performed a mutagenesis analysis in order to probe and validate the importance of individual side chain–nucleobase interactions in the structure of the DasR–Dap1complex. We first determined the binding affinities of Dap1 to wild-type DasR as well as to seven DasR mutant variants in which individual amino acids were substituted with alanine. The affinities were investigated via analytical gel filtration chromatography (AGF) and ITC measurements (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4). The activity of two mutants was also tested in vivo (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S5) [11].

Binding of DasR wild-type and DasR mutant variants to RNA aptamer Dap1 was investigated via AGF and ITC. (A) AGF chromatogram of DasR wild-type, (B) mutant variant DasR-R62A, and (C) DasR-T59A. (D) ITC measurements showing the titration of wild-type DasR into a Dap1 solution, (E) DasR-R62A into Dap1, and (F) DasR-T59A into Dap1. In panels (A) to (C), the elution profiles of the aptamer sample by itself are shown as a grey broken line, the protein by itself as a grey line, and the protein-aptamer mixture as a continuous black line. Molecular weight estimates were derived from calibration runs.

Wild-type DasR binds Dap1 with a KD = 15 nM. Formation of the complex is enthalpy-driven as apparent from a negative ΔH value and a positive -TΔS (Table 2). When substituting any of the three arginine residues (Arg48, Arg58, and Arg62) with alanine, binding of Dap1 to DasR is abolished as apparent in both AGF experiments and in an ITC measurement conducted exemplarily for variant DasR-R62A. As judged from the setup and experimental detection limits, we estimate that the affinities of the variants DasR-R48A, -R58A, and –R62A are worse than 1 μM (Table 2). Variant DasR-R62A was also tested in vivo (Supplementary Fig. S5). The data show that variant DasR-R62A only partially represses GFP expression in the absence of Dap1, likely due to poor dsDNA binding. In the presence of Dap1, GFP expression is not increased, which is consistent with the loss of Dap1-binding affinity of DasR-R62A.

In contrast to the arginine substitutions, variants where either residues Asp55, Ser57, Thr59, or Gln63 were substituted against alanine, retain binding affinity towards Dap1 as probed in AGF experiments (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4). Variant DasR-T59A was also probed via ITC, and its activity tested in vivo (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S5). DasR-T59A displays an about 10-fold reduced binding affinity for Dap1 in comparison to wild-type DasR (KD = 133 versus 15 nM, Table 2). In vivo data suggest that DasR-T59A retains its operator DNA binding affinity since DasR-T59A almost fully represses GFP expression in the absence of Dap1. In the presence of Dap1, GFP expression is only very slightly increased. This shows that a reduction in binding affinity from 15 nM to 133 nM suffices to prevent Dap1 from displacing DasR-T59A from its operator binding site in vivo.

These binding experiments show that of all amino acids that directly interact via their side chains with the nucleobases of Dap1, three arginine residues (Arg48, Arg58, and Arg62) appear to constitute primary determinants of the Dap1-DasR binding affinity. At the same time, however, reductions in binding affinity as low as 10-fold considerably affect the in vivo activity of Dap1 toward DasR.

To probe the contribution of individual nucleobases to the DasR–Dap1 interaction, we generated a total of 14 isosteric and non-isosteric Dap1 substitutions and investigated whether these variants retained binding to DasR in vivo (Fig. 4). In isosteric substitutions, the base pairing between adjacent nucleobases is retained, whereas base pairings are disrupted in non-isosteric substitutions [35]. In five variants, we isosterically switched G-C base pairs to C-G. We observe that all five variants (G9C-C24G, G11C-C23G, G14C-C22G, C15G-G21C, C16G-G19C) are non-functional, which indicates that the base pairs not only have a structural role but are also important for DasR binding (Fig. 4). In three of the C-G switch variants (G9C-C24G, G11C-C23G, and G14C-C22G), the prominent interactions that are formed between guanine nucleobases and arginine residues from DasR (Arg48, Arg58, and Arg62) are expected to be disrupted (Fig. 2B–D). This is in agreement with the fact that mutation of any of these arginine residues also abolishes binding in vitro. The two additional C-G switch variants (C15G-G21C, C16G-G19C) also display impaired DasR binding in vivo. Also in this case, the guanine nucleobases directly contact specific DasR residues, i.e. Thr59 and Ser57 (Fig. 2E). In the case of Thr59, this is in agreement with the in vivo data for mutant variant DasR-T59A (see above).

We also probed the non-canonical base pair C7-A26 and isosterically replaced the base pair with U7-G26 (Fig. 1D) [35]. C7-A26 does not directly interact with DasR, and the U7-G26 Dap1 variant remains fully functional, as expected. This is also the case for the isosteric replacement of the C8-A10-U25 base triplet with a U8-A10-C25 triplet, which also remains fully functional (Figs 1E and 4). By contrast, a non-isosteric variant U8-G10-C25 loses its ability to interact with DasR. However, the base triplet does not directly interact with DasR. Hence, we conclude that the triplet is either important for the overall structural stability of Dap1 or, at a local level, it is important for the proper positioning of neighboring base pair G9-C24 that interacts directly with Arg48 of DasR. We further isosterically substituted the U13-A18 base pair against A13-U18. U13-A18 forms a distorted canonical interaction in Dap1 that is likely to become disrupted in the A13-U18 variant (Fig. 1F). In agreement with this, we observe a pronounced reduction in the activity of this variant in vivo.

In addition, we generated five single substitutions, namely variants A12G, A12U, C17U, U20A, and U20G (Fig. 4). These variants probed the three nucleotide positions that are not involved in any base pairings in the Dap1 structure (Fig. 1C). We observe a reduced activity for variants A12G and U20G, but not for variants A12U and U20A. Positions 12 and 20 participate in DasR binding contacts, and the data suggest that these contacts can be disrupted depending on the nature of the substituted nucleotide. In contrast, position 17 does not contact DasR, and hence, we don’t observe any change in activity in variant C17U.

The mutational analysis fully corroborates the crystal structure of the DasR–Dap1 complex. While non-isosteric substitutions generally weaken the DasR–Dap1 interaction, isosteric substitutions do not impair DasR-Dap1 binding unless the nucleobases directly interact with DasR residues. The mutational analysis further highlights the pivotal role of the interactions formed between the guanine residues G9, G11, and G14 of Dap1 and arginine residues Arg48, Arg58, and Arg62 from DasR.

The nucleotide-binding determinants of DasR are nearly identical for RNA aptamer and DNA operator binding

DasR and the homologous repressor NagR from B. subtilis are among the best studied members of the GntR/HutC subfamily of the GntR repressor family [15, 21, 36]. In the case of NagR, crystal structures have revealed in great detail how NagR binds to its dsDNA operator site [36]. Although experimental structural data on DNA binding are missing in the case of DasR, it appears highly likely that DasR and NagR interact with dsDNA in a similar fashion since (i) the sequence identity between the DBDs of NagR and DasR is as high as 47.1% (70 compared residues) and (ii) all GntR bacterial repressors, independently of the subclasses they belong to, appear to share an identical DNA-binding mode (Fig. 5A) [16, 36]. In this binding mode, the two DBDs of the dimeric repressors bind side-by-side to a contiguous major groove segment with helices α3 and α3′ juxtaposed (Fig. 5B). At the same time, the two wing motifs present in the two DBDs reach out into the two minor groove segments that border the DNA major groove segment (Fig. 5B).

Residues involved in side chain-nucleobase contacts in RNA binding in DasR and DNA binding in NagR are highlighted by coloured boxes. Residues with changes in solvent accessibility areas greater than 5 Å2 upon RNA/DNA binding are marked with coloured horizontal lines. Sequence identities are highlighted with grey boxes. (B) Binding of NagR to dsDNA as observed in PDB entry 4U0Y [26, 36]. (C) Binding of the DBD of DasR to the aptamer Dap1.

The binding of DasR to Dap1 surprisingly mirrors the DNA binding of GntR repressors, albeit some distinct differences also exist. Thus, the two DBDs of dimeric DasR do not bind as a pair to a single double-stranded polynucleotide segment, but each DBD interacts individually with a single Dap1 aptamer molecule. Also, RNA cannot adopt the canonical B form that is preferred in dsDNA segments, and hence the corresponding major and minor grooves differ considerably in shape between RNA and DNA. However, DasR binds predominantly to the loop-like structure of Dap1 (nucleotides 7 to 26, Fig. 1C). This segment contains only a limited number of canonical base pairs and thereby allows for significant distortions in the RNA backbone structure. As a result, we observe a considerable concordance between the modes of binding of DasR to Dap1 and NagR to dsDNA. In both the RNA and DNA-binding mode, most nucleotide contacts originate from helices α2 and α3 of DasR and NagR, whereas helix α1 is relegated to the periphery of the interface (Fig. 5B–C). In both cases, helices α2 and α3 interact with RNA and DNA via the major groove of the polynucleotide, and the N-terminus of α3 points towards the polynucleotide backbone. In addition, the wing motif arches over the nucleotide backbone to reach into the minor groove in both cases. Taken together, we observe that the DBDs of DasR interact with the RNA aptamer Dap1 in a highly similar fashion to the DBDs of GntR repressors with DNA.

The similarities between RNA and DNA binding are not limited to the general architecture of the complexes but also extend to key amino acid–nucleotide interactions. In the case of DasR, Arg48, Arg58, and Arg62 represent key determinants for aptamer recognition (see above). These three residues are also conserved in NagR (Arg38, Arg48, and Arg52; NagR numbering, Fig. 5A). The crystal structure of NagR in complex with DNA shows that Arg38 and Arg48 of NagR bind to the Hoogsteen edge of guanines similarly to the binding of the corresponding arginine residues in the DasR–Dap1 complex (Fig. 5B–C). Arg38 and Arg48 of NagR are among the only three residues that directly contact nucleobases via their side chains in the NagR–DNA complex [36]. This also holds true for GntR repressors from other subfamilies. In FadR, for example, two arginine residues displayed again from identical positions are key determinants for DNA recognition [37, 38]. Thus, for both RNA and DNA binding, arginine residues displayed from helices α2 and α3 appear to be of utmost importance.

Switching the binding specificity of Dap1 from DasR to NagR – a protein approach

The high sequence identity between DasR and NagR prompts the question of whether Dap1 also binds to NagR. Gel filtration experiments show that this is not the case (Fig. 6A). In order to understand this shortfall, we generated two atomic models for a hypothetical NagR–Dap1 complex. A first model was obtained by superimposing the coordinates of the experimental structure of the DBD of NagR onto the DBD of DasR in the DasR–Dap1 complex [36]. This superposition highlights the homology between the DBDs of NagR and DasR since the DBD of NagR (molecule B in PDB entry 4U0Y) can be superimposed onto the DBD of DasR in the DasR–Dap1 complex (molecule A) with an r.m.s. deviation of 1.65 Å (63 common Cα positions).

Position Met49 in the homologous repressor NagR precludes binding to aptamer Dap1. (A) Gel filtration chromatography showing that Dap1 does not bind to NagR wild-type. (B) Computational model of the anticipated steric clashes between NagR wild-type and Dap1. (C) Gel filtration elution profile showing that Dap1 binds to mutant variant NagR-M49T. In panels (A) and (C), the elution profile of the aptamer by itself is shown as a grey broken line, the protein by itself as a grey line, and the protein-aptamer mixture as a black line. Molecular weight estimates were derived from calibration runs.

A second NagR-Dap1 complex model was obtained using homology modelling and by substituting all amino acids that differ between NagR and DasR to NagR residues in the DasR–Dap1 complex with the program SwissModel [39]. Individual interactions between NagR and Dap1 were investigated further by performing side chain-repacking calculations with program MUMBO and by substituting individual NagR residues back to the corresponding DasR residues [33]. In order to relax the rigid geometry imposed by the crystallographic template used for homology modelling, we also performed energy minimization calculations followed by Langevin molecular dynamics simulations with the program OpenMM [34].

The two models suggest that a putative clash between the side chain of Met49 of NagR and nucleobases U13, C15, C16, and G21 of Dap1 precludes binding of NagR to Dap1 (Fig. 6B). This clash cannot be relieved through energy minimization or molecular dynamics calculations. In DasR, Met49 of NagR is replaced by a threonine residue (Thr59) displayed from helix α3, and its side chain directly contacts the nucleobase G21 (Fig. 2E and 5A). To test the hypothesis that Met49 of NagR prevents Dap1 binding, we generated variant NagR-M49T. Indeed, this variant is able to bind to Dap1, albeit with a reduced affinity of KD = 162 nM (versus 15 nM for wild-type DasR) (Table 2, Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S6).

These results suggest that DasR, NagR, and possibly other GntR/HutC family members not only share highly similar DNA-binding modes but that this family of repressors could interact with synthetic RNA aptamers in a fashion that is similar to the binding of DasR to Dap1. If this holds true, then the DasR–Dap1 complex structure could serve as a starting point for the design of aptamers directed against numerous other GntR/HutC family members.

Switching the binding specificity of Dap1 from DasR to NagR - an RNA approach

Encouraged by the observation that a single amino acid substitution in NagR enables variant NagR-M49T to bind to Dap1, we explored whether, vice versa, a limited set of substitutions in Dap1 would allow binding of Dap1 variants to wild-type NagR. In order to achieve this, we closely inspected the DasR–Dap1 complex structure and the NagR-Dap1 models generated above. As a result, we designed a Dap1 aptamer pool randomized at four defined positions [12, 15, 20, 21, Fig. 7A). Positions 15 and 21 form a regular C-G Watson-Crick base pair, and the bases clash with Met49 in the NagR-Dap1 model (Fig. 6B). Since it was unclear whether any substitutions at positions 15 and 21 would be able to alleviate this clash, positions 12 and 20 were also randomized with the intention to compensate for any remaining unfavourable interactions via novel and improved protein-aptamer contacts at the periphery of the central contact patch. Adenine at position 12 is the only nucleobase in Dap1 that contacts the EBD of DasR (Supplementary Fig. S3). Substitutions at this position might therefore lead to additional and improved contacts with NagR. Similarly, uracil at position 20 interacts with Asp55 in DasR (Fig. 2F). Since Asp55 is replaced by glycine in NagR, a favourable substitution at position 20 might yield new NagR-binding contacts.

We performed an in vivo screening of the aptamer pool in order to identify those aptamers that bind to NagR. The screening system is based on a reporter gene assay and its design closely follows the one used to identify Dap1 [11]. In this assay, aptamer binding to NagR disrupts NagR binding to its DNA operator and thereby derepresses the transcription of the reporter protein GFP.

A total of about 3000 colonies were screened for GFP fluorescence. The majority of the colonies (99%) did not show any fluorescence, meaning that the aptamer mutants they expressed are not able to interfere with the DNA binding of NagR in vivo. Only one candidate showed an IPTG-dependent significant increase of fluorescence, which we termed Dap1m1. Sanger sequencing revealed that only three of the four randomized nucleotides were substituted (A12C, U20G, G21A). Furthermore, one additional exchange at position 9 (G9C) was acquired (Fig. 7B). The G9C substitution is expected to disrupt the GC base pair formed between positions 9 and 24 in Dap1 and thereby further open up the upper part of the internal loop structure. In addition, the A at position 12 was exchanged for C. Interestingly, position 15 remains C, allowing for a possible base pairing now with position 20 (U20G) instead of the previous pairing with position 21 since G21 is mutated to A in Dap1m1 (G21A, Fig. 7B). The dynamic range of induction of NagR by the new variant Dap1m1 is similar to the induction of DasR by Dap1 (Fig. 7C). Importantly, while Dap1 is able to induce DasR but not NagR-controlled gene expression, the new mutant Dap1m1 behaves exactly the opposite by inducing NagR but no longer DasR.

We stepwise mutated the four positions back to the original Dap1 sequence to characterize the importance of the individual exchanges (Fig. 7C). Whereas the back mutation C12A (Dap1m3) and A21G (Dap1m5) lead to a complete loss of function, the other two mutations, C9G (Dap1m2) and G20U (Dap1m4), cause a loss of specificity and are able to induce both NagR and DasR.

A detailed understanding of the switch in specificity and selectivity of Dap1 and Dap1m1 will have to await an experimental structure determination of the NagR–Dap1m1 complex. This notwithstanding, it is well possible that the alternative base-pairing pattern between positions 15 and 20 and 21 alleviates the clash caused between Met49 in NagR and Dap1. However, why these changes at the same time abolish the binding of Dap1m1 to DasR remains presently unclear. All in all, our experiments clearly show that the exchange of only 4 bases suffices to switch the specificity of the aptamer from DasR towards NagR.

AlphaFold3 falls short in predicting the DasR–Dap1 complex structure

AlphaFold3 models were generated to investigate whether the structure of Dap1 in complex with DasR could have been anticipated with modern AI programs [32]. We first explored models with the experimentally observed stoichiometry as input i.e., consisting of two DasR and two Dap1 chains (2:2 stoichiometry) (Fig. 8A). The five AlphaFold3-generated models are overall very similar and recapitulate general expectations for how bacterial repressors interact with polynucleotides. However, they digress significantly from the experimentally determined structure. In 4 out of 5 AlphaFold3 models, the two Dap1 molecules of the 2:2 complex are proposed to form an extended dsRNA duplex to which dimeric DasR binds via its DBDs. In one model, a pseudo-extended dsRNA duplex is formed. The models further propose that DasR binding induces a kink in the nucleotide duplex (Fig. 8A). However, in the experimentally determined structure, no duplex RNA is formed since the Dap1 molecules are monomeric and each Dap1 molecule individually interacts with one DBD of dimeric DasR (Fig. 1A).

(B) Prediction of the complex structure with an input stoichiometry of 1:1. (C) Details of the RNA-protein interface in the predicted 1:1 complex. (D) Prediction of the structure of Dap1 by itself. (E) Superposition of the predicted and experimentally determined structure (in magenta) of Dap1. In all panels, the AlphaFold3 models are coloured according to predicted LDDT values (pLDDT). High pLDDT values are coloured in dark blue and low pLDDT values in yellow and orange [32, 40]. Because of the shortcomings of the 2:2 predictions, we explored whether a 1:1 input stoichiometry would yield more realistic models (Fig. 8B). In the 1:1 models, Dap1 is predicted to form a stem loop-like structure stabilized via intermolecular base pairs and resembling the overall fold of the experimentally determined structure of Dap1 (Fig. 1B). In four out of the five models, DasR contacts Dap1 via its DBDs, whereas in one prediction, the interaction is proposed to take place via the EBD domain (Supplementary Fig. S7A-B). In the DBD-contacting models, the DBDs are oriented such that helices α2 and α3 interact with the aptamer via a major groove segment. At the same time, the wing motive of the DBD reaches out towards the minor groove (Fig. 8B). This complex organization resembles the experimentally determined DasR–Dap1 complex structure and reflects the topological arrangement typically seen in winged-helix-turn-helix-type repressors interacting with dsDNA. However, in these models, none of the side chain–nucleobase interactions that were identified as defining the specificity of the DasR–Dap1 complex are correctly predicted. None of the arginines (Arg48, Arg52, and Arg62) that are crucial for the formation of the complex contacts any of the nucleobases (Fig. 8C). The shortcomings of both the 1:1 and 2:2 models are reflected in low pLDDT values of the nucleotides and in the block-like patterns of the pAE plots (Fig. 8A–B and Supplementary Fig. S7C–D).

In a last calculation, we investigated how well Alphafold3 is able to predict the structure of Dap1 by itself (Fig. 8D–E). Here also, the predicted models do not account for key features of the experimentally determined structure. Although the flipping out of nucleobases U20 and C17 is anticipated correctly, additionally flipped-out nucleotide A12 is missing (Fig. 1B–C). Moreover, various registration shifts occur in the base pairings, and the base triplet formed by nucleobases C8, A10, and U25 is absent in the predicted structures.

As a control, we also predicted the structure of another repressor–RNA aptamer complex, namely that of TetR in complex with the aptamer K1 with Alphafold3 [10]. In this case, the predictions reliably reproduce the structure of the complex (Supplementary Fig. S7E-F). Please note that this structure has been publicly available since May 2020 (PDB Entry 6SY4) and hence, was part of the AlphaFold3 training set [32]. Thus, AlphaFold3 appears to reproduce training set entries such as the TetR-K1 aptamer complex with high fidelity, while failing at anticipating reliable novel insight into the structure of the DasR–Dap1 complex that is described for the first time in the present study.

Discussion

The DasR–Dap1 complex displays a unique topology not seen in structures of GntR repressors in complex with dsDNA operator sites. Instead of forming an extended nucleotide duplex, single Dap1 molecules bind individually to the two DBDs of DasR. At the same time, however, numerous DNA-binding features characteristic of the GntR repressor family are retained in the DasR–Dap1 complex. This holds true for the orientation of the DBDs with respect to DNA/RNA, the participation of residues from helices α2 and α3 in major groove contacts, and the arching of the wing motif into the polynucleotide minor groove. Arginine residues that constitute important DNA-binding determinants in GntR/HutC repressors are also of crucial importance for DasR–Dap1 complex formation. They participate in key nucleobase interactions, and mutating any of these arginine residues to alanine abolishes DasR binding to Dap1.

A number of AI-based programs allow for the prediction of RNA–protein complex structures, which raises the question of whether it is still necessary to determine the structure of such complexes experimentally [32, 41, 42]. To answer this question, we generated a number of different predictions with AlphaFold3 and compared these to the crystal structure of the DasR–Dap1 complex. Since many AI-based prediction tools use similar algorithms, we limited our investigation to AlphaFold3. We observed that AlphaFold3 fails at predicting the structure of the DasR–Dap1 complex. In its predictions, AlpfaFold3 appears to primarily recapitulate general expectations for how bacterial repressors interact with polynucleotides. Thus, it forces the RNA into a duplex structure and infers an overall complex topology that is reminiscent of how dimeric bacterial repressors typically bind to DNA. When experimenting with different stoichiometries, substantially different predictions are obtained with a 1:1 complex displaying the closest overall resemblance to the experimentally determined structure. However, the 1:1 complex also exhibits numerous shortcomings, such as an erroneous protein-RNA interface, base pair mismatches, and the absence of any non-Watson-Crick interactions. From this, we conclude that the detailed structure-function relationships derived in the present study would not have been possible without an experimental structure determination.

The aptamer Dap1 has been derived from a naïve pool of RNA molecules via the SELEX procedure and via its ability to bind to immobilized DasR [11]. Dap1 was then further optimized using a reporter gene assay. In this assay, the aptamer is tested for its ability to dislocate DasR from its operator site and to induce the transcription of the gfp reporter gene. The structure provides a detailed description of how Dap1 achieves this. In contrast to small molecule inducers, the aptamer does not bind to the EBDs of the repressor [15]. Therefore, Dap1 does not rely on allosteric rearrangements that couple inducer binding to the EBDs to DNA binding to the DBDs as typically seen in bacterial repressors [15]. Instead, Dap1 appears to be able to either detach DasR from DNA or prevent its attachment to DNA via a direct competition between RNA and DNA binding to DasR. However, instead of a mere spatial overlap of the DNA and Dap1-binding epitope of DasR, we observe that many fine details of the protein–nucleotide interactions are conserved and shared between dsDNA and Dap1 binding. The fact that these similarities are not a direct consequence of the selection procedure suggests that the DBDs of GntR repressors might encode for an inherently preferred polynucleotide binding mode.

The Dap1–DasR complex mirrors the interaction of GntR repressors with dsDNA. However, dsDNA preferentially adopts B-DNA secondary structure. Therefore, the question arises as to what extent the tertiary structure of Dap1 mimics the structure of B-DNA. Because of steric hindrance caused by the presence of the 2′-OH group, dsRNA is not able to adopt the canonical B-form preferred by dsDNA. Instead, dsRNA adopts the A-form in which, among others, the base pair inclinations and the shapes and accessibilities of the minor and major grooves differ considerably from those observed in B-DNA. In bacterial RNA polymerase in complex with 6S RNA, it was observed that the 6S RNA is able to mimic the structure of the B-form DNA by adjusting the minor and major widths to those observed in B-form DNA [43]. In the Dap1–DasR complex, DasR binds at the boundary between the stem and loop of the stem-loop structure, and major and minor groove widths cannot be easily quantified. A more detailed analysis will be needed to investigate the extent to which the numerous non-canonical base pairings and stacking interactions in Dap1 cause local distortions that make the Dap1 molecule more B-like and thereby facilitate the interaction with DasR.

In vivo data clearly show that Dap1 specifically induces DasR but not NagR (Fig. 7C). At first glance, it seems surprising that Dap1 is so specific for DasR since DasR and NagR share high sequence identity (47.1%), and since the SELEX evolution of Dap1 did not include any negative selection step against NagR [11]. However, high binding specificity of aptamers has already been evident for small-molecule-binding aptamers. The tetracycline-binding aptamer, for example, can only bind tetracycline but not doxycycline [44]. Other examples are aptamers that differentiate between caffeine and theophylline or neomycin and paromomycin, respectively; in each case, the ligands differ only in a single chemical moiety [45–47].

We further explored whether the structure of Dap1-DasR could be used as a blueprint for the generation of novel aptamer–protein interaction pairs. This proved successful in terms of both changes to the protein ligand and the aptamer. Thus, we were able to identify Met49 as the major obstacle for the binding of Dap1 to NagR, and indeed, a mutant variant, in which Met49 of NagR is substituted against the corresponding residue of DasR (NagR-M49T), readily interacts with Dap1 (Fig. 6C). With regard to the aptamer, we identified a novel aptamer, termed Dap1m1, that now induces the cognate repressor NagR but no longer the original repressor DasR. The design of Dap1m1 was informed by the structure of the Dap1–DasR complex, and Dap1m1 was derived from an aptamer library in which as few as 4 nucleotide positions were randomized (library with a theoretical size of only 256, Fig. 7). At the same time, a single reverse mutation in Dap1m1 weakens the specificity to such an extent that both repressors NagR and DasR are equally recognized. These findings have far-reaching implications. It now seems very likely that the DasR–Dap1 complex structure can be used as a blueprint for the development of a variety of novel aptamers that target other members of the GntR/HutC or even the entire GntR family of bacterial repressors.

However, the most exciting question remains whether it is possible to use a similar approach to switch the specificity of aptamers between repressors belonging to different classes. Is it, for example, possible to modify the TetR-binding aptamer K1 so that it also binds to GntR-family repressors and vice versa, to modify Dap1 to also bind TetR-like repressors? K1 displays a considerably different binding behaviour since a single K1 molecule sequesters both DBDs of the dimeric repressor [10]. If this proves successful, then this would provide us with a whole range of new aptamer-repressor pairs in the future. These aptamers could be used to build RNA-based genetic circuits, but also for RNA-mediated conditional gene expression, such as the control of mRNA splicing or polyadenylation [4, 5].
