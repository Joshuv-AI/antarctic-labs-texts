Laser-induced transient opacity in helium nanodroplets probed by single-shot coherent diffraction
Julian C. Schäfer-Zimmermann, Tom von Scheven, Katharina Kolatzki, Björn Kruse, Bruno Langbehn, Thomas Möller, Nils Monserud, Mario Sauppe, Bernd Schütte, Björn Senfftleben, Rico Mayro P. Tanyag, Anatoli Ulmer, Thomas Fennel, Marc J.J. Vrakking, Arnaud Rouzée, Daniela Rupp

Abstract
Single-shot coherent diffractive imaging (CDI) with intense short-wavelength light pulses enables the structural characterization of individual nanoparticles in free flight with high spatial and temporal resolution. Conventional CDI assumes that the target object exhibits a linear scattering response and static electronic properties. Here, we extend this approach to investigate transient laser-driven modifications of the electronic structure in individual nanoparticles, imprinted in their time-resolved diffraction patterns. In the presence of a near-infrared laser pulse, we observe a pronounced reduction in the diffraction signal from helium nanodroplets when probed with ultrashort extreme ultraviolet (XUV) pulses. This effect is attributed to a light-field-induced modification of the electronic structure of the droplets, which substantially increases their XUV absorption. Our results demonstrate that single-particle diffraction can capture ultrafast light-driven electron dynamics in nanoscale systems. This paves the way for the spatiotemporal tracking of reversible changes in the electronic properties of nanoscale structures with potential applications in ultrafast X-ray optics, materials science, and all-optical signal processing.

88

4
Modeling

Our model of the delay-dependent diffraction response of laser-dressed helium nanodroplets is leaning on the similarity of the electronic structure of atomic and bulk helium in the absence of an external laser field. The absorption, reflection, and refractive index of liquid helium in the XUV range are documented in the literature
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
. They mimic the equivalent properties of atomic helium, exhibiting dominant resonance features at the
1s^{2}\rightarrow 1snp
(n=2,3,…) transitions, with small blue shifts and broadening of the narrow transitions into bands due to van der Waals interactions in the liquid. Additionally, in nanodroplets, small contributions from optically forbidden transitions appear, such as
1s^{2}\rightarrow 1s2s
, as these transitions become allowed at the surface
[
94
]
.

We performed ab initio time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) calculations for isolated helium atoms, dressed by an NIR field and simultaneously probed by an XUV "kick" (see Supplementary Note 5). This approach, known as the TDSE projection or kick-response method
[
95
,
96
]
, allowed us to evaluate the atomic dipole moment across the relevant spectral range. Then, in order to include effects of the droplet environment, an artificial decoherence time
[
95
,
74
]
of
8\text{\,}\mathrm{fs}
was introduced to account for dephasing effects in the bulk liquid, such as collisional broadening, by multiplying the dipole moment with a Hann window function before Fourier transformation. The aforementioned size of the window function was determined by fitting the width of the
1s2p
transition at zero NIR field strength to the literature values for liquid helium
[
93
,
92
]
. Using the simulated dynamical polarizability, an effective refractive index
1-\delta+i\beta
was derived for bulk helium in the presence of the NIR field by means of the Clausius-Mossotti relation. More details on the simulation approach can be found in Supplementary Note 5.

a) and b). The absorption coefficient

\delta

3

5
Discussion and Conclusion

The simulated diffraction signal reproduces well the measured time-dependent integrated detector signal at low scattering angle with a drop of the diffracted intensity of about
30\%
(Fig.
3
e). This is remarkable since our model is expected to
give correct trends, but not necessarily quantitative agreement. For example, the calculations do not account for the known blue shifts of absorption features in the droplet of up to 0.5 eV compared to the atomic transitions
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
(see Supplementary Note 5.4. for further discussion of calculated and literature curves). Furthermore, the kick-response approach doesn’t include changes in state occupations during the XUV pulse. When calculating the diffraction of a later part of the XUV pulse, excitation and ionization within the droplet during the leading edge of the pulse, caused by the XUV or the combined XUV and NIR fields, are neglected. These effects will also modify the diffraction response over the course of the pulse. However, full simulations using an XUV pulse train at representative fluences indicate that at the current intensities the associated modifications should be minor (see Supplementary Note 7). Also, spectral reshaping
[
98
]
of the light propagating through the droplet is not accounted for in our model.

With these limitations in mind, the close agreement between calculation and measurement encourages us to derive a basic picture of the physical mechanisms underlying the observed reduction in diffraction signal brightness from the model. For this, we analyze the evolution of the effective refractive index maps at the photon energies corresponding to the two dominant harmonics, namely the 13th and 15th, in the XUV spectrum.

At laser intensities below
1\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
, the calculated absorption map in Fig.
3
a) shows two prominent features related to absorption corresponding to the
1s2p
(around 21.4 eV) and
1s3p
(around 23.2 eV) dipole-allowed transitions, similar to the absorption of atomic helium. With increasing NIR intensity, these bands shift in energy due to the AC Stark effect (the
1s2p
band is red-shifted and the
1s3p
band blue-shifted, respectively). We note that sub-cycle features from interfering two-photon quantum paths
[
98
]
cannot be resolved in our experiment. Around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
, a new absorption band appears above the
1s2p
feature which subsequently shifts upwards to around 22 eV. Comparison with previous studies
[
96
]
suggests that this feature corresponds to a superposition of several light-induced states (LIS), namely
2s+
,
3s-
and
3d-
(describing the population of
1s2s
by the absorption of the difference of one XUV and one NIR photon and
1s3s
and
1s3d
by the absorption of the sum of one XUV and one NIR photon). In bulk liquid, these transitions appear to merge into one broad band.

Analog assignments can be made for the general features in the
\delta
-map (refraction), displayed in Fig.
3
b). They are connected to the
\beta
-map landscape via the Kramers-Kronig relations, leading to Fano-like structures in the refraction map which are characteristically shifted in energy compared to the corresponding absorption maxima.

Focusing on specific photon energies, at the energy corresponding to the 13th harmonic (Fig.
3
c)) our model predicts that the absorption coefficient
\beta
increases significantly in the presence of the dressing laser field - by an order of magnitude compared to the field-free case - reaching a value above 0.05 at intensities above
10\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
. This increase is attributed to the progressive shift of the
1s2p
absorption band into this energy range as the intensity rises. In contrast, the refraction coefficient
\delta
remains at a nearly constant, high value across all tested intensities at this photon energy, varying only slightly between -0.1 and -0.14. The droplets therefore remain strongly refracting at this photon energy, regardless of the NIR intensity.

At the photon energy corresponding to the 15th harmonic, a different behavior is observed. In the field-free case, the absorption coefficient
\beta
is relatively high (0.05) due to the proximity of the
1s3p
absorption band of helium. As the intensity increases,
\beta
initially decreases, reaching a minimum of 0.02 at
9\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
, as the
1s3p
band shifts out of this spectral region. At even higher NIR intensities, the absorption increases again due to the emergence of several light-induced states, with a corresponding absorption shoulder that gradually enters the spectral region corresponding to the 15th harmonic. In contrast to the 13th harmonic, the absolute values of the refraction coefficient
\delta
at the position of the 15th harmonic are significantly smaller across all NIR intensities, approaching and crossing zero twice in the range between 4 and
8\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
.

To understand how the evolving optical properties translate into the observed changes in the diffraction response, it is useful to consider several limiting cases for scattering from a spherical particle (see also Supplementary Note 8 for details and visualizations).

When absorption is high (case 1), the particle behaves effectively as an opaque aperture - a condition that depends on the particle size (see Supplementary Fig. 12). In this regime, the scattering becomes largely insensitive to changes in the refractive index
\delta
, since light cannot penetrate the object sufficiently for refraction to influence the diffraction pattern. Only when absorption is low can light propagate through the particle, allowing variations in
\delta
to significantly affect scattering. In the case of low absorption but high refraction (case 2) - the equivalent of a glass sphere in the visible range - the particle exhibits a particularly strong diffraction response. Under these conditions, the scattering intensity can exceed that of an opaque sphere of the same size by up to a factor of four (see Supplementary Note 8). Finally, when both absorption and refraction are low (case 3), the scattering strength is minimal, resulting in a weak diffraction signal. This scenario is typically for the X-ray spectral region.

The calculated detector integral
D
at the photon energy of the 13th harmonic (blue solid curve in Fig.
3
c)) shows that the droplets exhibits strong scattering at low NIR intensities, thereby dominating the overall diffraction pattern
[
19
]
. This behavior corresponds to the regime of a "glass sphere", i.e. low absorption and high refraction (case 2), where the particle produces an enhanced diffraction response. However, as the NIR intensity increases, the absorption coefficient
\beta
rises above 0.02 at around
5\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
, shifting the system towards the high-absorption regime. In this regime, the particle begins to act more like an opaque aperture (case 1) and further changes in the refraction index have little effect on the scattering. As a result, the contribution of the 13th harmonic to the diffraction pattern drops by approximately a factor of two and then levels off.

In contrast, at the photon energy of the 15th harmonic, the droplet exhibits low refraction values (
\delta
) across all tested NIR intensities (gray solid curve in Fig.
3
d)). As a result, the absorption coefficient
\beta
becomes the dominant factor influencing the integrated detector signal (red solid curve) at this wavelength. At low intensities, the high absorption (
\beta>
0.05) places the system into case 1, where the particle effectively behaves like an opaque aperture. In this regime, changes in
\delta
have little effect, and the contribution of the 15th harmonic to the overall scattering intensity remains low. As the intensity increases,
\beta
decreases while
\delta
at first remains close to zero, driving the system towards case 3, where both absorption and refraction are low. Then, a moderate increase in
\delta
around
5\text{\,}\mathrm{TW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
allows a stronger diffraction response, and consequently, the scattering signal rises and eventually surpasses its initial value at this intensity before it drops back towards the previous level when
\beta
rises again. Throughout the tested intensities, the 15th harmonic consistently contributes less to the total diffracted intensity than the 13th harmonic, as evidenced by the direct comparison of their respective contribution to the integrated detector signal (red and blue curves in Fig. 3 c) and d)).

We can therefore attribute the pronounced reduction in scattering intensity observed experimentally in the presence of the dressing laser field to the significant increase in the absorption coefficient at the photon energy corresponding to the 13th harmonic. This increase is caused by the NIR-induced AC-Stark shift of the
1s2p
absorption band, and is therefore directly related to the change of the electronic structure of the droplet in the dressed laser field.

6
Summary and outlook

In this work we demonstrated a CDI-based experimental approach for characterizing the nonlinear transient electronic response of free-flying nano-samples to laser fields, giving access to both the real and imaginary parts of their refractive indices. We observed that during irradiation with a non-ionizing NIR pulse, isolated helium nanodroplets exhibit a substantial decrease in XUV scattering strength at photon energies in the vicinity of
20\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
. We propose an explanation for our findings based on laser dressing of the helium droplets’ electronic structure, leading to AC Stark shifts of the atomic-like absorption bands and the occurrence of light induced state-type structures. The observed time-dependent drop of the diffraction signal of about
30\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
on a femtosecond timescale is reproduced by Mie simulations using effective, NIR laser intensity-dependent refractive indices that were computed from
ab initio
TDSE calculations fitted to liquid helium data. In this work, the dominating effect of the NIR field to the diffraction signal from the helium droplets is an increasing absorption at the photon energy of the strongest harmonic, where at zero field, the droplet exhibits enhanced scattering due to strong refraction and weak absorption. However, conceptually both directions are feasible, transiently switching the diffraction response "on" and "off" with a laser, by choosing photon energy and material properties accordingly. On the simulation side, our approach could be refined in future work via an improved description that properly includes atomic interactions in the superfluid liquid and resolves bulk and surface contributions to the response in order to retrieve quantitative transient refractive index dynamics.

Our observations highlight the sensitivity of single-particle CDI to ultrafast changes in the electronic structure of nanoparticles. In this regard, our work establishes CDI as a method sensitive to transient laser-induced electron dynamics in matter that is generally also able to provide spatio-temporal resolution, ultimately down to tens of nanometers and hundreds of attoseconds or less.

5
Details of the simulation approach

The basis of our model are Mie scattering calculations to predict XUV scattering images of the He droplets as a function of refractive index. In the presence of the strong IR field, the latter is described as an IR-intensity-dependent effective quantity, which is extracted from atomic quantum simulations. Therefore, the atomic XUV response to impulsive excitation in the presence of a dressing IR field is simulated by solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}\psi(t)

(2)

for He in single-active electron (SAE) approximation and in atomic units. From the resulting dipole signal, the dynamical polarizability
\alpha(\omega)
in the XUV range is extracted via appropriate filtering and translated to a refractive index using the Clausius-Mossotti relation in order to compute the Mie scattering.

5.1
Effective refractive index

In order to extract optical properties from the TDSE simulations, we employ the well-known kick response method. Therefore, a short, nearly delta-like field impulse
E_{\mathrm{kick}}(t)=-\hat{E}_{\mathrm{kick}}\delta(t)
is employed as an excitation, implemented via a step in the time-dependent vector potential
A_{\mathrm{kick}}(t)=\hat{E}_{\mathrm{kick}}\Theta(t)
, where
\Theta(t)
is the Heavyside function. This excitation corresponds to a white spectrum with equal spectral intensity at all frequencies, including the relevant XUV range. The resulting temporal evolution of the dipole moment
d(t)
for excitation along the z-axis is obtained from spin-free TDSE calculations
[
105
]
using the SAE
[
106
]
and the dipole approximation
[
107
]
with the Hamiltonian in velocity gauge of the form
[
105
,
108
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{2}+V(r)}_{\text{Static part}}+\underbrace{\mathbf{A}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}}_{\text{Interaction part}}

(3)

where
\mathbf{A}(t)=A_{\mathrm{kick}}(t)\mathbf{e}_{z}+A_{\mathrm{NIR}}(t)\mathbf{e}_{z}
is the vector potential,
V(r)
is the atomic pseudopotential and
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
is the momentum operator. The pseudopotential is constructed following Ref.
[
108
]
, where the doubly charged He nucleus is assumed to be statically screened by the frozen (and analytically known) ground state 1s electron density from Hydrogen-like Helium

\displaystyle V(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r}-\left(2+\frac{1}{r}\right)e^{-4r}.

(4)

Note that a delta-like potential placed at the origin is added to match the ionization potential. The electronic ground state of the second electron using this pseudopotential defines the initial state of the simulation. The Gaussian NIR pulse is described as

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{NIR}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T}\exp(- \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{t - t_p}{T} \right)^2)\cos(\omega(t-t_p)),

(5)

where
\omega
is the angular frequency of the pulse,
T
is the length of the Gaussian envelope, and is connected to the FWHM via
T_{\mathrm{FWHM}}=2\sqrt{2\ln(2)}T
and
t_{p}
is the time of the center of the pulse.
From the solution of the TDSE using the kick excitation and the optional additional NIR pulse, the time-dependent dipole moment in the SAE approximation is calculated from

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{SAE}}(t)

\displaystyle=-e\matrixelement{\psi(r,t)}{\hat{z}}{\psi(r,t)}.

(6)

Note that this atomic response simulation predicts a dipole response containing all dipole-allowed excitations weighted according to their oscillator strength without any dephasing due to the fully coherent description. Direct Fourier transformation would yield the spectral polarizability with (unphysically) sharp atomic transition lines. To mimic the condensed phase response of Helium, we employ a mask function to the dipole signal using the Hann window

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{mask}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos(\frac{2 \pi t}{\tau- 1})\right),\ 0\leq t\leq\tau-1\ ,

(7)

temporally centered at the kick. A Hann window width of
8\text{ fs}
is used to induce a broadening of the response that matches the experimentally observed width of
1s2p
-absorption line in liquid Helium. The Fourier transformation of the masked dipole response yields the sought effective dynamic polarizability of the He atom in the medium via

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\ \frac{\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{mask}}(\omega)}{\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{kick}}(\omega)}

(8)

where the prefactor reflects the fact that the SAE description provides only the response of one of the ground state electrons. Note that the interpretation as an effective, IR-induced optical response is only justified outside the NIR spectrum. Finally, the effective refractive index of the medium follows from the Clausius Mossotti relation as

\displaystyle n(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\frac{3N\alpha(\omega)}{3\varepsilon_{0}-N\alpha(\omega)}}=1-\delta+i\beta

(9)

where
\delta
and
\beta
describe the refraction and the absorption of the (dressed) Helium and
N
is the number density. This procedure is employed to calculate the refractive index systematically as a function of the IR intensity. To remove the spurious artifacts of seemingly negative absorption due to wave mixing, the refractive index has to be averaged over the carrier envelope phase of the NIR pulse. The remaining regions with slightly negative absorption are clamped.

5.2
Mie simulations

Standard Mie simulations
[
109
,
110
]
are employed to predict the diffraction images from the refractive indices obtained by the TDSE simulations.
Mie’s solution provides an analytic result for the scattering of an incident plane electric wave at a sphere. Knowing the sphere’s refractive index and size parameter, which, in turn, is determined by the sphere’s size and the incident plane wave’s wavelength, we can evaluate the scattered electric field at any desired position. In order to align with the experimental configuration, we evaluate the scattered field on a two-dimensional real space detector plane, i.e.
z=\text{const.}
and
x,\ y
limited by the detector’s maximal scattering angle of
45^{\circ}
.

Applying the detector mask discussed in Fig. 7
b
) onto the calculated Mie-scattering signal enables the calculation of the normalized detector signal in Figs. 3
c
) and
d
) of the main manuscript.
Note that due to the normalization of the detector signal on the XUV-only signal, the exact distance of the detector to the sphere becomes irrelevant. Thus, only the mask-limited maximal scattering angle influences the final detector signal.

5.3
Calculating the time-dependent, averaged and integrated detector signal

To imitate the experimental XUV pulse, we perform multiple Mie scattering simulations for the XUV photon energies displayed in Fig. 1
a
) of the main manuscript. These lead to detector signals, which we weight by the corresponding intensity of the experimental XUV spectrum.

Additionally, the NIR intensity-dependent detector signal is converted to a delay-dependent signal by calculating the weighted averages of NIR intensities during the delay-dependent overlap of the NIR and an experimental-like XUV pulse.
Therefore, we model the envelope of an NIR pulse at a delay
\tau
and calculate the weighted average of this NIR pulse intensity over all times
t
, where the envelope of the NIR pulse and the zero-centered experimental XUV pulse overlap, using the XUV pulse as the averaging weight.
Intuitively, this corresponds to the number of NIR photons in the temporal overlap between the XUV and NIR pulse:

I_{\text{Eff},\,\tau}=\sum_{t}\frac{I_{\text{XUV},\,\tau_{0}}\left(t\right)I_{\text{NIR},\,\tau}\left(t\right)}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}I_{\text{XUV}}\left(t^{\prime}\right)},

(10)

where
I_{\text{Eff},\,\tau}
is the effective NIR pulse intensity at delay
\tau
and
I_{\text{XUV},\,\tau_{0}}\left(t\right)
being the intensity at time
t
of the modeled envelope of the experimental XUV pulse. Correspondingly,
I_{\text{NIR},\,\tau}\left(t\right)
is the intensity of the NIR envelope modeled after the experimental NIR pulse at time
t
and whose maximum is placed at delay
\tau
.
Using equation
10
, we calculate effective NIR pulse intensities for all experimental delays and, with that, the time-dependent integrated detector signal. To account for the temporal jitter between the XUV and NIR pulses
[
103
]
, we convolve the time-dependent detector signal with a Gaussian envelope of
14\ \mathrm{fs}
length.

Finally, we use the experimental helium droplet size distribution
[
20
]
in order to obtain the weighted average of the detector signal, incorporating a statistical mix of sphere sizes. This allows us to gather the time-dependent, averaged, and integrated detector signal shown in Fig. 3
e
) of the main manuscript.
