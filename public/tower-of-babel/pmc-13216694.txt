Decoding the Mitochondrial Translation-Stress Axis
Authors: Sharon Zeldin, Junichi Sadoshima
Published: 2026

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a progressive and often refractory disorder with a multifactorial and complex etiology.1 Elucidating the common molecular mechanisms underlying cardiac dysfunction in patients with DCM is essential for advancing diagnosis and therapeutic development. However, identifying novel pathogenic mechanisms and establishing their functional relevance, particularly in human systems, has proven challenging. Consequently, molecular therapies that specifically target disease-promoting pathways in human DCM have yet to be developed.

In this issue of JACC Basic to Translational Science, Chen et al2 investigated the pathogenesis of DCM through a combination of systems biology in humans and functional validation in mice. TARS2 Dysregulation in Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM)

In dilated cardiomyopathy hearts, TARS2 plays an important role in mediating increases in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial dysfunction, efferocytosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. Because TARS2 is involved in the translation of mtDNA-encoded proteins, its dysregulation leads to mitonuclear imbalance, which serves as a driver of mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive mitochondrial ROS accumulation. Up-regulation of SNCA may also contribute to mitonuclear imbalance by inhibiting the translocation of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins. Although these responses may activate the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPR) to restore mitochondrial homeostasis, its contribution to DCM progression remains to be clarified. Leveraging machine learning–based gene prioritization, the authors identified a robust 3-gene signature (TARS2, NOX4, and SNCA) from high-dimensional transcriptomic data derived from human DCM cohorts. Pseudotime trajectory analysis using the Slingshot algorithm across 432 human heart samples further demonstrated a progressive increase in TARS2 expression and mitochondrial oxidative stress scores as cardiomyocytes transition toward a pathological state. Complementary Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis provided evidence that genetically predicted higher TARS2 expression is causally linked to increased DCM risk and impaired cardiac function. Together, these convergent lines of evidence strongly implicate TARS2 as a key driver in the pathogenesis of DCM.

The study’s major strengths lie in the integration of multiple analytical modalities, which minimizes the biases inherent to any single algorithm and enhances the reliability of candidate selection. The addition of MR analysis together with gain- and loss-of-function studies in mice not only enhanced the functional significance of the authors’ findings but also provided a premise for considering therapy targeting TARS2.

TARS2 is involved in the translation of mtDNA-encoded proteins that are essential components of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), which drives oxidative phosphorylation. Because the mitochondrial ETC is composed of proteins encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, dysregulation of TARS2 may lead to a state of mitonuclear imbalance, thereby impairing the assembly of mitochondrial respiratory supercomplexes.3 Such disruption can result in mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, efferocytosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, all of which are key pathological features of DCM. Previous studies have shown that the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mtUPR), an adaptive cellular response to mitonuclear imbalance, is activated during heart failure,4 further supporting a potential causal role for TARS2 in disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis and triggering mtUPR in DCM hearts. The concurrent identification of α-synuclein (SNCA), a protein known to inhibit the mitochondrial import of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins, as a critical mediator of DCM in this study further underscores the potential importance of mitonuclear imbalance in DCM pathogenesis. It should be noted, however, that both TARS2 and SNCA possess multiple noncanonical functions beyond the regulation of mitochondrial protein homeostasis, including protein quality control mechanisms5; therefore, caution should be exercised in attributing the authors’ findings solely to mitonuclear imbalance as the underlying molecular mechanism of DCM.

Although Chen et al2 provide compelling evidence supporting a causal role for TARS2 in the pathogenesis of DCM, the possibility that TARS2 dysregulation is a consequence rather than a direct cause of DCM cannot be excluded. Likewise, it remains possible that TARS2 up-regulation occurs predominantly during the chronic phase of DCM and, therefore, may not represent the initiating mechanism of cardiac dysfunction. This limitation is particularly relevant because the human data may not permit clear assessment of the temporal relationship between TARS2 expression and the onset and progression of DCM.

In addition, it remains unclear whether the gain-of-function experiments in mice were conducted with careful consideration of physiological TARS2 expression levels. Genetic manipulation of TARS2 could itself inadvertently induce mitonuclear imbalance, particularly if excessive overexpression alone disrupts mitochondrial proteostasis.

Future investigations should include longitudinal studies to define the timing of TARS2 up-regulation and its relationship to disease progression, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cardiac remodeling. Furthermore, because TARS2 is an intracellular molecule, it would be valuable to establish alternative biomarkers that more conveniently reflect TARS2 activity or abundance in cardiomyocytes from patients with DCM. For example, activation of the mitochondrial mtUPR induces the secretion of signaling factors known as mitokines, including FGF21 and GDF15,3 which are released from stressed mitochondria to communicate with distant organs and help restore metabolic homeostasis. Exploration of such circulating markers and their mechanistic links to TARS2 dysregulation represents an important direction for future study.

Regardless of these potential caveats, the identification of a molecule that regulates mitonuclear balance as a critical mediator of DCM is highly intriguing. Mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiomyocyte death are hallmark features of heart failure, and this study raises the important possibility that dysregulated translation of mitochondria-encoded proteins contributes directly to the mechanisms underlying mitochondrial failure in DCM. Because mitonuclear imbalance is known to trigger the mtUPR as an adaptive mechanism, including activation of protective processes such as autophagy,3 it will be important to determine whether DCM is accompanied by mtUPR activation in humans and, if so, how this response influences disease progression. For example, the magnitude and duration of mtUPR activation may modulate the trajectory of DCM in patients, potentially distinguishing adaptive compensation from maladaptive remodeling.

The authors further propose that TARS2 may be therapeutically targeted with small molecules for the treatment of DCM. Such an approach, however, should proceed with caution. Simple correction of TARS2 expression may not necessarily resolve mitonuclear imbalance, particularly if the pathological effects of TARS2 dysregulation are mediated primarily through altered translation of mitochondrial proteins rather than through TARS2 abundance itself. Indeed, excessive correction could potentially exacerbate a stoichiometric imbalance between nuclear- and mitochondrial-encoded ETC components. If mitonuclear imbalance is truly the central pathogenic mechanism, strategies aimed at enhancing endogenous mtUPR signaling or reinforcing mitochondrial quality-control pathways may better support the restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis.

In summary, the investigation by Chen et al2 provides a robust systems biology framework that positions TARS2 as a critical node in the pathogenesis of DCM, linking mitochondrial protein synthesis to maladaptive oxidative stress and cardiomyocyte loss. By integrating multi-omic analyses with MR and functional validation, the authors move beyond simple association to propose a dynamic mechanistic model of disease progression. Nevertheless, translation of these findings into clinical practice requires careful consideration. The highly delicate nature of mitonuclear stoichiometry suggests that therapeutic modulation of TARS2 must be precisely calibrated to avoid inducing secondary mitochondrial dysfunction reminiscent of primary mitochondrial disease. Future studies should therefore focus on defining the therapeutic window of TARS2 activity and clarifying how its noncanonical roles, including potential involvement in mTORC1 signaling, contribute to ventricular remodeling. Ultimately, while TARS2 represents a compelling new therapeutic target, its utility in a preventive or curative strategy will depend on our ability to navigate the complex trade-offs among mitochondrial proteostasis, cardiac adaptation, and systemic metabolic health.
