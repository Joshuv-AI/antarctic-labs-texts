Direct Synthesis of Exfoliated Carbon Nitride Mediated by Sodiated Cellulose Nanocrystals for Photocatalytic and Adsorbent Applications
Authors: Narendra Chaulagain, Kazi M. Alam, Ehsan Shahini, John C. Garcia, Md Masud Rana, Riley W. Hooper, Diganta Sarkar, Damini Vrushabendrakumar, Harshitha Rajashekhar, Navneet Kumar, Alkiviathes Meldrum, Vladimir K. Michaelis, Tian Tang, Karthik Shankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs an evolving 2D organic semiconductor, graphitic carbon nitride has drawn scientific research interest for applications extending from photo/electrocatalysis to adsorbents and biosensing. Herein, a few atomic‐layer‐thick carbon nitride nanosheets (CNNS) were synthesized using an in situ exfoliation technique by introducing sodiated sulfate hydrolyzed cellulose nanocrystals into the polymerization precursor mixture. The directly exfoliated CNNS possessed a much higher concentration of unpaired electron spins than bulk carbon nitride (BCN), as evidenced by EPR spectroscopy. Solid‐state NMR, and XPS confirmed the incorporation of sodium in the carbon nitride framework. Introducing Na dopants inhibited the stacking and aggregation of carbon nitride nanosheets, widening the bandgap of a material. When compared to BCN, it was discovered that the bandgap of the exfoliated carbon nitride (NaCNC‐CNNS) increased from 2.76 to 2.90 eV, along with a fourfold increase in specific surface area and tenfold increase in pore volume. NaCNC‐CNNS exhibited a significant advantage in photogenerated charge carrier separation and transfer compared to BCN. Given its higher PEC water splitting performance and higher aqueous dye adsorption efficiency, the NaCNC‐CNNS structure appears to be more suitable as a photoelectrocatalyst and adsorbent than BCN. Molecular dynamics simulations and analyses confirmed that the NaCNC‐CNNS structure is more exposed, which facilitates the interaction of solvent molecules with nanosheets and promotes their intercalating action.

Introduction

Due to its sp2‐hybridized graphenic framework, excellent thermomechanical and photochemical stability, earth abundance of constituent elements and widespread availability of precursor compounds, graphitic carbon nitride (g‐CN) has demonstrated significant potential in applications ranging from photo/electrocatalysis and adsorbents to surface passivators and carrier transport layers in solar cells [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. An appealing aspect of g‐CN is that, unlike other 2D materials such as graphene, MoS2, Mxene, and black phosphorus, it is an organic polymer [7]. This allows for relatively simple modification of its structure, chemical composition and electronic properties through heteroatom doping and polycondensation of different monomers [8, 9, 10]. In addition to the typical C3N4, other recently identified variants of carbon nitride include C2N, C3N2, C4N3 and C3N5 [11].

Several crucial issues must be addressed before carbon nitride can undergo widespread adoption as a technologically important material. A major challenge consists of improving the quantum efficiency in light harvesting applications by reducing charge carrier recombination. Carbon nitride contains structural defects that arise due to stacking of the layers, leading to a low specific surface area and a high intersheet recombination rate of photogenerated charges [12]. Another challenge, which has received less attention, involves forming uniform, solution‐processed films with optimal morphology and structure. Due to the limited solubility in isolated solvents, carbon nitride‐based thin film devices are challenging to fabricate. It is possible to exfoliate the layers of bulk g‐CN into nanosheets due to its layered structure held together by weak van der Waals forces [13]. Nanomaterials with few sheets of g‐CN have been produced using exfoliation techniques such as liquid phase exfoliation, thermal etching exfoliation, ultrasonic‐assisted exfoliation, chemical blowing, and chemical exfoliation (with strong acids) [14, 15, 16]. Bulk g‐CN (three‐dimensional) can be processed into nanosheets (two‐dimensional) [17] and then into a variety of morphologies, including nanorods/nanotubes [18] and quantum dots [19, 20], which have applications in energy‐harvesting and industrial applications. Despite numerous efforts, challenges still exist in obtaining a high yield, controlled shape, and ultrathin nanosheets [21, 22]. In particular, the efficacy of exfoliating carbon nitride nanosheets (CNNS) remains low, for example, for a typical liquid exfoliation, a lengthy sonication period of up to 16 h is required to acquire CNNS with a concentration of just 0.15 mg/mL [23]. Molecular dynamics and other simulations offer some hope that these issues can be overcome; for example, they predict that mixed solvents consisting of polar/non‐polar and aliphatic/aromatic components should exhibit enhanced solubility for carbon nitrides and form more stable colloidal dispersions [24, 25].

In this work, we emphasize the novelty of our approach by highlighting the unique integration of sodiated sulfate cellulose nanocrystals (NaCNC) into the polymerization precursor of carbon nitride, which significantly enhances the porosity and structural properties. A unique aspect of this work is the novel in situ exfoliation technique, resulting in high‐quality carbon nitride nanosheets with significantly enhanced surface area, reduced defect density and improved optoelectronic properties. Unlike traditional methods that primarily focus on inorganic precursors, our strategy leverages the thermal degradation of organic materials to create a porous architecture through the generation of gases during the high‐temperature synthesis. This method not only avoids the drawbacks of traditional exfoliation techniques (very long sonication procedure, incomplete transformation into nanosheets for liquid exfoliation in organic solvents, undesirable chemical reactions and poor control of lateral size of nanosheets for inorganic acid exfoliation) but also provides valuable insights into the influence of Na+ on the material's performance, a perspective that has rarely been reported in literature.

During the manufacturing of cellulose nanocrystals, the cellulose precursor is initially treated with concentrated sulfuric acid to establish sulfate groups on the cellulose chains [26]. This reaction converts cellulose into cellulose sulfate [27], which improves its solubility in water [28]. The cellulose sulfate is then hydrolyzed using a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. The Na+ ions from NaOH interact with the sulfate groups, resulting in the substitution of Na+ for protons in the sulfate moieties [26, 29, 30]. During this hydrolysis, cellulose chains are broken down into smaller nanocrystals, which are stabilized through the presence of the sulfate groups [29]. The incorporation of Na+ occurs as the sulfate groups are modified, leading to the formation of sodiated cellulose nanocrystals. These sodiated sulfate hydrolyzed cellulose nanocrystals are then introduced during the synthesis of polymeric carbon nitride. Figure 1 illustrates the details of synthesizing the in situ exfoliated porous carbon nitride nanosheets (NaCNC‐CNNS). The obtained NaCNC‐CNNS suspension clearly showed the Tyndall effect (Figure 1e), confirming that the obtained polymeric carbon nitride is monodisperse and ultrathin [31]. The CNC‐assisted carbon nitride moreover has a different visual color from bulk carbon nitride, as shown by macroscopic photographs (Figure S2).

Results and Discussions

Morphology, Structure and Composition

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to assess the morphology of the polymeric g‐CN. The TEM images of NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure 2b–d) show two‐dimensional thin polymeric sheets. The nanosheet exhibits a reasonable yet homogenous contrast, consistent with their desired ultrathin and uniform thickness (Figure 2d). Furthermore, the flexibility of the exfoliated carbon nitride nanosheets allowed for the appearance of folded and overlapping structures in the image [32]. The morphology of the exfoliated material (NaCNC‐CNNS) is drastically different from that of the raw counterparts (Figure 2a). The energy dispersive X‐ray (EDX) and elemental mapping analysis demonstrate the existence of C and N elements in carbon nitride. The existence of Na intercalated between the carbon nitride nanosheets is evidenced in EDX and elemental mapping (Figure S3).

TEM images of (a) BCN, and (b–d) NaCNC‐CNNS.

Solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy offers comprehensive atomic‐level information on the molecular structure. To examine the structural variations in BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples, 13C{1H} cross‐polarization magic‐angle spinning (CPMAS) NMR experiments were conducted on BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure 3a), with characteristic spectra revealing two main resonances. The resonance with a center‐of‐gravity shift (δcgs) at 156 ppm is assigned to the CN3 units of heptazine, while the δcgs = 165 ppm is from the amine‐bearing carbons (CN2(NHx), where x = 1 or 2). These observed results agree with previous reports for melamine‐based carbon nitride [33, 34, 35]. The 23Na MAS NMR spectra of NaCNC and NaCNC‐CNNS samples are shown in Figure 3b. Comparing the 23Na MAS NMR spectra for NaCNC‐CNNS and NaCNC, reveals a broadening of the central transition, from a full‐width‐half‐maximum (FWHM) of 7 ppm (0.9 kHz) for NaCNC to 33 ppm (4.4 kHz) for NaCNC‐CNNS, a nearly fivefold increase. This broadening indicates a change in the Na chemical environment, whereby the electric field gradient around the sodium cation is now found in a more strained environment, resulting in a larger quadrupolar interaction. The as‐received NaCNC precursor was severely hydrolyzed, as evidenced by 1H NMR (1H NMR shows a single signal that is assigned to water, see Figure S4), suggesting that the sodium cations in NaCNC‐CNNS are hydrated to a lesser extent since the nanosheets effectively shield the positive charge of intercalated Na cations in the sodiated g‐CN. This hydration modifies the Na chemical environment and may minimize the spherical distortion of the coordination environment of Na (e.g., Na (H2O)6 polyhedral) or assist in Na+ motion that could partially attenuate the quadrupolar interaction resulting in a narrowing of the central transition (δcgs = −7 ppm; FWHM = 7 ppm) [36, 37, 38]. In contrast, the NaCNC‐CNNS material undergoes a heat treatment at 500°C followed by a subsequent washing and drying step overnight. The annealing and drying process removes excess water and aids in permeating Na into the polymeric carbon nitride. We propose that the Na interacts with the polymeric carbon nitride, leading to the NMR resonance to shift and broaden (δcgs = −9 ppm; FWHM = 33 ppm). The broadening is due to the quadrupolar nature of 23Na (nuclear spin, I = 3/2), resulting in a slight increase in the sodium quadrupolar coupling constant from reduced Na dynamics or a reduction of Na chemical environment symmetry upon intercalation between the nanosheets, causing an associated second‐order quadrupolar broadening of the central transition. Figure S4 illustrates the 1H MAS NMR spectra for the as‐synthesized BCN, NaCNC and NaCNC‐CNNS samples. The NaCNC shows a single resonance near 4 ppm that is attributed to water. For the BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples, two distinct resonances are observed, where the resonance with chemical shifts between 3 and 4 ppm is assigned to residual water (Hw) in the structure (after the annealing and drying steps). The second resonance with the chemical shift between 8 and 9 ppm is assigned to amine functional groups (Ha). The most noticeable difference between the 1H MAS NMR spectra is the change in Hw intensity between NaCNC, BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS, where Hw is the only resonance in NaCNC. NaCNC‐CNNS exhibits both Ha and Hw resonances, suggesting that Na cations intercalated in the CNNS are partially hydrated (vide supra). BCN shows the lowest peak intensity for Hw, likely due to the absence of sodiation for this material. The increase in the Hw signal for NaCNC‐CNNS vs. BCN could also imply the presence of an increased number of amine groups due to the severe disintegration of the CN skeleton during the material synthesis [39] and the ability of Na+ to assist in charge balancing the carbon nitride. As a consequence of the above, the NaCNC‐CNNS structure may be better suited for water adsorption than the BCN sample; this possibility is discussed in more detail in the aqueous dye adsorption section (below).

In order to reveal the chemical valence state and surface chemical composition of the BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed. XPS survey spectra for BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure S5) shows the presence of all the expected constituent elements. Additional peaks appear due to the indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate. The core‐level high resolution (HR) spectra of BCN in N1s region were deconvoluted into two peaks at binding energies of 398.8 and 400.7 eV (Figure 4a). The lower binding energy peak is associated with secondary nitrogen (pyridinic nitrogen) in C = N‐C, while the higher binding energy region in N‐(C)3 represents quaternary nitrogen [10, 40]. These two peaks in NaCNC‐CNNS appeared at binding energies of 398.25 and 399.9 eV (Figure 4b). The HRXPS spectra of BCN in C1s region were deconvoluted into three peak components at binding energies (BE) of 284.8, 287.6, and 289.85 eV which correspond to C−C, C−N−C and N = CN2 type carbons (Figure 4b). The sp3 carbon in C−N−C and the sp2 carbon in N = C−N2 are part of the basic g‐C3N4 skeleton, while the sp3 carbon in C‐C is indicative of carboxylate groups and turbostratic/adventitious carbon [40, 41]. All three peaks are visible in NaCNC‐CNNS too at 284.8, 287.8, and 288.8 eV (Figure 4b). While the first two peaks’ BE did not change, the highest BE peak downshifted by 1 eV. Such a remarkable reduction of BE implies a strong interaction of C atoms of the heptazine moiety with the intercalated Na, where C atoms gained electrons from Na by virtue of their higher electronegativity [42]. Both N1s peaks have downshifted in BE. The reduction of BE indicates a strong interaction of N atoms of carbon nitride scaffold with the intercalated Na atoms, where N atoms possibly gained electrons from Na, as a consequence of having higher electronegativity than Na atoms [42]. The core‐level HRXPS spectra in Na1s region were deconvolved into two peak components at binding energies of 1071.65 eV and 1073.1 eV for NaCNC‐CNNS sample (Figure 4c). The lower energy peak corresponds to sodium state (Na+), which was also found in our earlier report on zinc phthalocyanine‐conjugated NaCNC [43]. The appearance of the higher energy shoulder peak implies a possible ionic bond formation of liberated Na atoms with N and/or C atoms of carbon nitride's heptazine moiety since N and C atoms have much higher electronegativity. This is in agreement with HRXPS analysis of C1s and N1s discussed earlier in this section. Such intercalated Na further weakens interlayer Van der Waals forces and results in the formation of nanosheets of carbon nitride with a quantum confinement‐induced wider electronic bandgap than bulk carbon nitride. A faster charge separation has been predicted in NaCNC‐CNNS than BCN, according to recent DFT‐based theoretical calculations [44]. Therefore, while the HRXPS data confirms the preservation of the basic graphitic carbon nitride framework in both the carbon nitrides, the Na atoms in NaCNC‐CNNS interact strongly with both C and N atoms of the heptazine framework.

Core level HR‐XPS spectra of BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS in (a) C 1s region, (b) N 1s region, (c) Na 1s region, and (d) normalized EELS spectra of BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS showing C K‐edge and N K‐edge loss.

In order to elucidate the bonding nature of C and N atoms in the carbon nitride frameworks of BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples, we employed electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). C K‐edge and N K‐edge EELS spectra were resolved for both BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples (Figure 4d). The C K‐edge shows a peak at ∼284 eV corresponding to the 1s‐π* electronic transition of sp 2 hybridized carbons, and another broader peak centered at ∼294 eV that corresponds to the 1s‐σ* electronic transition in the heptazine units [45, 46, 47, 48]. The N K‐edge shows a low energy peak at ∼397 eV corresponding to 1s‐π* electronic transition of sp 2 hybridized nitrogen, and a broader high energy peak centered at ∼407.5 eV indicative of the 1s‐σ* electronic transition [45, 46, 47, 48]. No noticeable difference in the C K‐edge and N K‐edge peak positions and features were observed in the two synthesized materials. In both carbon nitrides, the characteristic peaks of O K‐edge between 535 and 550 eV were absent (Figure 4d) [46], which indicates the complete evaporation of O from the NaCNC precursor during the calcination step of NaCNC‐CNNS formation. Additionally, there was neither a monotonous enhancement of intensity nor any sort of broad feature above ∼310 eV in the C K‐edge spectra of either sample (Figure 4), which indicates the absence of both amorphous phase as well as crystalline graphite phase from the NaCNC precursor [49, 50]. Hence, EELS data confirms the preservation of the basic carbon nitride framework in both BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples without any detectable unwanted impurities.

Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the two carbon nitrides were examined (Figure 5a) to gain insight into potential changes in the chemical structure. The FTIR spectrum of NaCNC‐CNNS was similar to that of bulk CN with a strong peak at 810 cm−1 (tri‐s‐triazine ring sextant out‐of‐plane bending) and stretching vibration modes at 1200–1650 cm−1 (CN heterocycles). This finding indicated that mixing cellulose nanocrystals with melamine during the carbon nitride synthesis did not alter the fundamental structure of tri‐s‐triazine. Thus, the standard CN molecular structure was preserved in NaCNC‐CNNS due to the π‐π interaction. Also, the peaks at 2800−3500 cm−1 which are ascribed to the stretching vibration of O−H and N−H, were clearly observed and are more prominent in NaCNC‐CNNS (agreeing with the 1H NMR results, see above). This phenomenon is produced by captured H2O molecules and uncondensed amino groups. The FTIR spectrum for the bare NaCNCs shows that the distinctive vibrational characteristics of CNC (Figure S6) are in agreement with the literature [51, 52]. Almost all these peaks disappeared in the CNC‐treated carbon nitride system. This shows that the cellulose nanocrystals were utilized or pyrolyzed in entirety during the synthesis of exfoliated carbon nitride systems. The vibrational characteristics of the samples were investigated through Raman spectroscopy for the BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure 5b). In the Raman spectrum of BCN, the G‐band frequency focused at 1560 cm−1 equates to the symmetrical E2g vibrational mode in a graphite‐like compound containing sp2 trigonal bonded carbon nitride with a low volumetric proportion of sp3 ‐bonded atoms. On the other hand, the D‐band frequency, which has its center at 1220 cm−1, is caused by restrictions in the graphitic domain size brought by grain boundaries or flaws such as substitutional N atoms, sp3 carbon tetrahedral bonding, or other contaminants [53]. The D and G‐bands in NaCNC‐CNNS appeared at 1198 and 1590 cm−1, respectively. Thus, in CNC‐assisted carbon nitride, the D band has shifted to a lower wavenumber, while the G band has shifted to a higher wavenumber, compared to bulk carbon nitride. Both these shifts are indicative of a greatly reduced layer thickness in NaCNC‐CNNS compared to BCN, and support our contention of directly exfoliated carbon nitride nanosheets in the case of NaCNC‐CNNS [54]. Raman spectra of BCN (Figure 5b) and AICNC‐BCN (Figure S7d) demonstrated additional prominent peaks due to the heptazine framework at 485, 698, and 705 cm−1 (heptazine ring breathing modes). Figure S7 shows the optoelectronic properties of the AICNC‐BCN sample, whose features are similar to those of BCN. This clear difference shows the importance of sodium atoms present in sodiated cellulose nanocrystals during the exfoliation of carbon nitride.

(a) FTIR spectra, (b) Raman spectra, (c) N2 adsorption‐desorption isotherm, (d) pore size distribution of BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS, respectively.

Introducing cellulose nanocrystals (CNC) during the synthesis of carbon nitride (CN) significantly influences the formation of a porous structure. When CNC is incorporated into the carbon nitride matrix and subjected to high temperature, it undergoes thermal degradation, leading to the combustion of CNC and the generation of fumes (Figure S10a,b). This process is crucial as it creates voids and pores within the final structure. The high surface area and porosity observed in the resultant NaCNC‐CNNS can be attributed to significant gas evolution during synthesis [55]. The released gas facilitates the formation of a porous structure, creating spaces that remain after combustion. The N2 adsorption‐desorption isotherms and the relevant pore size distribution curves for each sample indicate distinct type IV isotherms (Figure 5c,d), and at higher relative pressures (0.9 to 1.0), there was an exceptionally high adsorption capacity that suggested the presence of macropores and mesopores [55]. The specific surface area of NaCNC‐CNNS is 36.05 m2 g−1, more than four times that of BCN (8.19 m2 g−1). Accordingly, the total pore volume of NaCNC‐CNNS (0.486 cm3 g−1) was more than ten times that of BCN (0.043 cm3 g−1). The high surface area of NaCNC‐CNNS is likely due to the elevated gas evolution from the melamine‐cellulose nanocrystal precursor. The Barret‐Joyner‐Halenda method (BJH) was used to examine the pore size distribution (PSD) (Figure 5d). The NaCNC‐CNNS sample showed a distinct peak at ∼28–29 nm (the pore distribution is wider with higher probabilities for larger pores compared to BCN), and it is most likely that the interlayer galleries and carbon vacancies are responsible for these holes in the nanosheets. A much larger number of reactant molecules could be adsorbed because of the higher density of reactive sites created by the increased specific surface area and total pore volume [56].

To further confirm the formation of 2D layered carbon nitride, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed. The typical AFM images of the exfoliated carbon nitride nanosheets (NaCNC‐CNNS) showed a lateral size of these sheets ranging from tens of nanometers to several micrometers (Figure 6 and S9). The thickness analysis of the nanosheets by AFM reveals an average thickness of approximately 4–5 nm, possibly encompassing 10 atomic layers. This further confirms that the carbon nitride was successfully exfoliated during the material synthesis.

Optoelectronic Properties of the Intercalated Carbon Nitride Nanosheets

UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS UV–vis) and photoluminescence (PL) spectra were used to examine the optical characteristics of exfoliated carbon nitride. With a blue shift of 20 nm in comparison to bulk CN, the semiconductor absorption edge of NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure 7a) was preserved. This blue shift can be attributed to a reduction in conjugation length and a strong quantum confinement effect due to the ultrathin structure of g‐C3N4 nanosheets. The data showed that the bandgap of exfoliated carbon nitride increased from 2.76 to 2.90 eV compared to bulk carbon nitride (Figure 7a). The blue‐shifted absorption edge in NaCNC‐CNNS is accompanied by the emergence of an absorption tail from ∼450 to 750 nm.

To study the effect of the distinct morphology, higher surface area, and enhanced light‐harvesting capability on the photocatalytic performance, we examined the recombination rate of photogenerated electron−hole pairs and charge transport pathway from the center to the surface. Photoluminescence spectroscopy is usually applied to investigate the separation rate of photogenerated electron−hole pairs, in which a higher PL intensity correlates to a higher recombination rate. Compared to BCN, the CNC‐assisted carbon nitrides displayed a much weaker PL intensity under an excitation wavelength at 365 nm, implying a low radiative recombination rate of photogenerated carriers. The emission peak of the bulk carbon nitride is located at around 470 nm, while the emission peak position is blue‐shifted to 455 nm in the case of NaCNC‐CNNS carbon nitride (Figure 7b). This 15 nm blue shift is in good agreement with the larger bandgap of exfoliated carbon nitride.

The effective masses of electrons and light holes in carbon nitride have been determined to be 0.024–0.027 and 0.04–0.05 me, respectively through density functional theory (DFT) computations [57, 58]. For mild electric fields, the effective mass of carriers in nitrogen‐doped holey graphene, a more realistic analog to practical carbon nitride nanosheets containing charged defects and other voltage barriers, has been reported to be 0.05 m e [59]. Therefore, we assumed a carrier effective mass of 0.05 m e while aiming to quantify the effect of quantum confinement in NaCNC‐CNNS. The relative permittivity (εr) of most organic semiconductors is between 2.2 and 4. We assumed εr value of 4 for the carbon nitride nanosheets identical to the fullerenic semiconductor PC61BM [60] due to the similar sp2‐hybridized framework and π‐conjugation. To estimate the bandgap enlargement (ΔEg ) in directly exfoliated carbon nitride nanosheets (NaCNC‐CNNS), we used the expression derived for the quantum confinement‐induced increase in the bandgap of graphene quantum dots (GQDs) [61]: (1) ΔEg=3ℏ2π2m∗d2−1.78e22πε0εrd where ħ is reduced Planck's constant, e is the elementary charge, m* is the carrier effective mass, ε 0 is the permittivity of free space, ε r is the relative permittivity and d is the size of the nanosheet (determined to be ∼5 nm from AFM and TEM imaging). Substituting these values into Equation (1) enables us to estimate a bandgap enlargement of 0.13 eV which closely matches the bandgap widening of 0.14 eV in NaCNC‐CNNS corresponding to the experimental blueshift in the absorption edge shown in Figure 7a.

The structural change due to intercalation of Na+ ions into the carbon nitride is supported by XRD patterns (Figure 7c). The (002) reflection for BCN relating to the characteristic interlayer stacking structure occurred at a 2θ value of 27.55° and was shifted to 27.19° for NaCNC‐CNNS, indicating an expansion of the interlayer distance between the g‐C3N4 nanosheets [62]. This peak was noticeably diminished in NaCNC‐assisted carbon nitride and displayed a less periodic texture, which implies that carbon nitride was successfully exfoliated and still contained a graphitic carbon nitride network [16]. The broad and weak intensity of the (002) reflection indicates the loss of ordered crystalline structure in the end product, and the amorphous morphology is further strengthened by the high‐resolution TEM (HRTEM) images which invariably lacked any lattice fringes (Figure 2).

Continuous wave (CW) EPR experiments were performed on BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples at room temperature to assess the presence of localized paramagnetic electron species (Figure 7d). BCN, having a single electron on the carbon atom of the heptazine ring, displayed a Voigt lineshape with an isotropic g‐factor of 2.0089 [63, 64], while the NaCNC‐CNNS exhibited a rhombic g‐tensor (gx = 2.0125, gy = 2.0091, and gz = 2.0046). Anisotropy of the g‐tensor emerged in the EPR spectrum with principal values scattered by a notable strain, suggesting the presence of radicals with distinct electronic structures (Table S1) [65, 66]. The presence of Na+ appears to increase the electron‐rich system in polymeric CN, and therefore, these electron‐rich sites can potentially act as radicals [67]. The formation of such CN radicals can create structural defects or disorders, leading to an increased density of active reaction sites [68]. In line with the possibility of radicals with varying electron spin delocalization, exchange, and dipole‐dipole coupling to adjacent radicals, the radicals often exhibit rapid relaxation and a wide variety of relaxation durations. Depending on how the NaCNC‐CNNS species is oriented in relation to the neighboring molecular or crystal structure, it “perceives” different electronic environments or interactions, thereby generating a rhombic g‐tensor. In contrast to BCN, the EPR spin intensity of the NaCNC‐CNNS sample is significantly higher, suggesting a higher concentration of unpaired electrons.

Carbon nitride is extremely fluorescent in the range between 480 and 600 nm upon excitation with blue light [9, 69, 70]. Both the BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS materials were fluorescent, with emission spectra maxima at 490 and 470 nm, respectively. The BCN fluorescence was considerably brighter for samples prepared at equal concentrations in deionized water, which we attribute to higher PL intensity owing to strong radiative recombination mentioned previously (Figure 5b and Figure S8). The fluorescence lifetimes showed a characteristic decay over a period of several tens of nanoseconds (Figure 8c,d). The decay data were fit with a biexponential function in agreement with much of the previous literature on related materials systems [71, 72], although the biexponential model may be considered as a “model of convenience” with a relatively small number of free variables. Although there is no a‐priori reason to assume a biexponential model, it fits the data well, with quite small uncertainties in the associated parameters (Table S2). The mean lifetimes were thus found to be 1.76 and 1.46 ns for BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS, respectively, in the FLIM experiments (excited with a 100‐ns 470‐nm pulsed LED). In standard fluorescence decay measurements using a 405‐nm 25‐picosecond pulsed laser, the lifetimes were found to be 4.05 and 2.94 ns for BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS, respectively, which follows the same trend but with significantly longer lifetimes than observed in the FLIM measurements. This difference is likely due to the much longer pulse width and/or the longer wavelength used in the FLIM experiments, although it is difficult to speculate on the mechanism for this difference. The shorter fluorescence lifetime of the NaCNC‐CNNS is consistent with previous work that showed the decay rate of carbon nitride as a function of increasing interlayer stacking distance [73, 74].

We performed transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements on bare carbon nitride (BCN) and exfoliated carbon nitride (CNNS) samples to evaluate the separation of photogenerated charges. The film fabrication for TPV measurement was initiated by first depositing a 50 nm thick layer of TiO2 on FTO:glass substrate using atomic layer deposition (ALD) technique, ensuring uniformity and precise control over thickness. Next, carbon nitride was deposited via spin coating at 600 rpm for 1 min, utilizing a 10 mg mL−1 carbon nitride solution prepared in α‐terpineol. After spin coating, the films were dried on a hot plate at 200°C to remove any residual solvent and enhance the adhesion of the carbon nitride layer. Illumination of carbon nitride by visible light creates photogenerated electron‐hole pairs, typically followed by rapid electron injection into TiO2 due to the higher lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of carbon nitride being higher than the conduction band edge of TiO2 [10]. During the TPV measurements, we observed a slow rise in photovoltage for both samples (Figure S11a,b). Notably, the amplitude of the generated photovoltage was similar for NaCNC‐CNNS compared to BCN under identical conditions (light intensity, pulse length, film thickness), likely due to a comparable number of photogenerated charge carriers in both films. The photovoltage decay was faster in NaCNC‐CNNS, with average lifetimes calculated to be 15.32 ms for BCN and 7.51 ms for NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure S11c) (fitting parameters are in Table S4). The faster component of the biexponential decay was 10.37 ms for BCN and 2.19 ms for NaCNC‐CNNS, which we attribute to faster interfacial electron‐hole recombination at the interface of titania and carbon nitride. Such a higher rate of interfacial recombination could have two potential origins. On the one hand, confinement of photogenerated holes in individual nanosheets of NaCNC‐CNNS vs. delocalization of holes over the BCN film facilitates accelerated recombination of holes in NaCNC‐CNNS with electrons in TiO2. On the other hand, the faster decay in NaCNC‐CNNS may suggest a reduction in trap states [75]. Qualitatively, the rising pattern of photovoltage during light illumination displayed marked differences, possibly influenced by the presence of defect states in BCN that hinder charge carrier mobility. This finding aligns with TRPL and FLIM experimental data, reinforcing the trend, and further supporting the correlation between charge carrier dynamics and material structure.

We further performed electrical measurements on metal/organic/metal sandwiched devices to study the trap/defect states. Figure S12 shows the typical nonlinear behavior and hysteresis in the J‐V curve in BCN‐based thin film with a high trap density. These defects create localized energy states within the bandgap of BCN such that when an electric field is applied, charge carriers become trapped at these defect sites [76], facilitating non‐radiative recombination of charge carriers at defect sites rather than contributing to current flow. On the other hand, marginal hysteresis in the J‐V curve for NaCNC‐CNNS‐based thin film was recorded (Figure S12), providing the clearest indication of a reduction in trap states. By minimizing hysteresis, NaCNC‐CNNS can operate with greater efficiency, reducing wasted energy and improving the performance of the energy conversion systems.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation and Analysis

The calculated molecular structures of BCN (g‐C3N4 “without Na+”) and NaCNC‐CNNS (g‐C3N4 “with Na+”) nanosheets after 10 ns of NpT simulation indicated considerable aggregation of BCN nanosheets while the NaCNC‐CNNS nanosheets are more dispersed (Figure 9a,b). The dispersed configuration of NaCNC‐CNNS nanosheets can be partly attributed to the net charge (−9) of each sheet, which introduces electrostatic repulsion among the sheets. In addition, the presence of Na+ ions between the NaCNC‐CNNS nanosheets is visible, which increases the distance between the sheets and reduces their van der Waals (vdW) interactions. In other words, the Na+ ions serve as intercalators that assist in stabilizing the dispersion of NaCNC‐CNNS nanosheets. Moreover, the structure of NaCNC‐CNNS nanosheets became more curved and distorted when Na+ was present, whereas without Na+ the BCN sheets were more rigid and planar.

Snapshots of (a) “with Na+” and (b) g‐C3N4 nanosheets “without Na+” after 10 ns of NpT simulation. Color scheme: blue, N; gray, C; white, H; purple, Na+. (c) The histogram of the COM distance between any two nanosheets.

Several quantitative analyses were conducted to further confirm the visual observations. These data are collected from the last 5 ns of the 10 ns NpT simulation. Figure 9c displays the histograms of the center of mass (COM) distance between any two of the ten nanosheets. The results show that the majority of the “with Na+” nanosheets are separated at a distance of 1.50–1.75 nm, while the majority of the g‐C3N4 “without Na+” nanosheets are separated at a smaller distance of 1.20–1.40 nm. The first non‐zero value of the histogram occurs at approximately 0.75 nm for “with Na+” and 0.40 nm for g‐C3N4 “without Na+”. The latter value (0.4 nm) is close to the interlayer stacking distance in g‐CN materials (∼0.35 nm [77]), which indicates that two neighboring g‐C3N4 nanosheets can form stacking with their COMs slightly shifted along the lateral (parallel to the sheets) direction [78, 79]. Such a compact stacking is not observed for the “with Na+” sheets.

Table S3 shows the radius of gyration Rg of the structure formed by the 10 nanosheets. A 20% increase is observed for “with Na+” compared to g‐C3N4 “without Na+,” further confirming the loosened structure in the case of “with Na+.” The three principal radii of gyration (Rg) i are also shown in Table S3, where i = x, y, z indicate the three principal directions. For “with Na+,” the three principal radii of gyration have close values, suggesting that the structure formed by the 10 nanosheets resembles a sphere (i.e., isotropic) [80]. For g‐C3N4 “without Na+” however, one of the principal radii of gyration (0.68 nm) is much smaller than the other two (0.91 nm and 0.96 nm), which indicates that the structure formed by the 10 g‐C3N4 nanosheets “without Na+” is anisotropic and resembles a short cylinder where the dimension in the thickness direction is smaller than the in‐plane dimension. This is consistent with the visual observation in Figure 9a and the earlier analysis, which shows that g‐C3N4 “without Na+” is more prone to aggregation via stacking. Finally, the surface area of the structure in each system was estimated by solvating it with TIP4P/2005 water molecules [81] and calculating the solvent accessible surface area (SASA). As shown in Table S3, SASA of “with Na+” is 58% higher than that of g‐C3N4 “without Na+,” implying that the “with Na+” structure is more exposed, and Na+ facilitates the interaction of hydrophilic solvent molecules with the nanosheets and promote their intercalating action.

Photoelectrochemical Performance of Carbon Nitride Thin Films

The photoelectrochemical properties of BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples were studied using a three‐electrode configuration in 1 M KOH electrolyte using carbon nitride films on FTO (fluorine‐doped tin oxide) as the photoanode, Pt as the counter‐electrode and Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. To summarize, powdered carbon nitride was first dispersed in α‐terpineol to create a homogenous supramolecular paste. This paste was deposited onto FTO substrates via drop casting, forming a film. Finally, the mixture was heated to 250°C for 2 h on a hot plate. A Surlyn spacer was applied to cover the sample photoanode, exposing a small window to the solution to eradicate edge effects. The photocurrent measurements were accomplished using a Zahner Zennium workstation while irradiating the photoelectrode from the front side of the device. The visible light was generated by an AM1.5G 1 sun solar stimulator, and experiments were also carried out in the presence of ultraviolet‐filtered visible light.

The flat‐band potential (VFB) and carrier concentration (Nd) of the photoanode were approximately estimated by the Mott‐Schottky plot with an AC frequency of 1 kHz in the dark. The equation applied is as follows [82, 83]: (2) Nd=2e0∈∈0d1Csc2dV−1 where Nd is carrier concentration, e0, ɛ, ɛ0 are the electron charge of 1.6 × 10−19 C, the dielectric constant of carbon nitride (= 8) [84, 85], and the permittivity of free space (= 8.86 × 10−14 F cm−1), respectively; Csc is capacitance, V is the applied potential. On the other hand, the depletion layer width Wsc is accountable for charge separation, which is provided by Equation (3) [86]. (3) Wsc=2∈∈0eNdVapp−VFB where Vapp is the applied bias potential, and the VFB is the flat band potential.

In the absence of light, the open circuit voltage (Voc) of the NaCNC‐CNNS sample was around −0.71 V versus Ag/AgCl, which is higher than BCN (−0.44 V vs Ag/AgCl) (Figure 10a). When the Voc for the NaCNC‐CNNS and BCN samples are compared, a notable negative shift of −0.27 V was observed. A significant negative shift like this suggests that the NaCNC‐CNNS sample has a greater equilibrium electron depletion [10]. The NaCNC‐CNNS samples also showed the highest shifts (∼0.16 V) in the photocurrent onset potential under light illumination and an increase in photocurrent, which signifies a drop in recombination of electron‐hole pairs in the sample [10]. It is also noteworthy that the NaCNC‐CNNS sample has a larger dark current density than the BCN sample. This could be due to the origin of shallow trap states. Significantly, the strain‐induced trap states can localize excitons, where photogenerated electrons are trapped [87]. Consequently, the shallow trapped electrons can leap to the conduction band without photoexcitation, hence augmenting the dark current [88].

(a) Semilogarithmic photoelectrochemical J‐V plots of BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS, compared under dark conditions, (b) J‐V plots under AM1.5 G one sun illumination and UV filtered AM1.5 G one sun illumination, (c) Transient photocurrent responses of photoanodes in 1 M KOH aqueous solution under 1 sun solar illumination, (d) Mott‐Schottky plots without illumination at 0.5 M Na2SO4 in the voltage range of −1 to +1 V under dark condition at 1 kHz frequency.

With the same experimental condition in the dark, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves or J‐V curves exhibited that the currents at the NaCNC‐CNNS electrode were multiple folds higher than that of the BCN electrode at 0.6 V vs Ag/AgCl (Figure 10b). Under 1 sun and UV filtered 1 sun illumination, the photocurrent responses of NaCNC‐CNNS are 22 and 12 µA cm−2 at 0.6 V vs. Ag/AgCl, respectively, roughly three times that of a pristine BCN‐based photoanode (8 µA cm−2), indicating the strong involvement of Na+ ions in the intercalation of 2D nanosheets and improvement in the water oxidation process. The improved bulk conductivity caused by the delocalization of π‐bond is mostly responsible for the increased current density [89, 90]. Other significant characteristics that may contribute to a higher PEC performance include its larger specific surface area, absorption tail and dispersibility, which facilitates film formation [25, 91, 92]. As previously indicated, the N and C atoms in the bulk carbon nitride layer display covalent bonds; yet, the g‐C3N4 structure has weak van der Waals forces. To overcome this problem, the intervention of Na+ ions was utilized as an aid to break the weak force of attraction. As the stacked nanosheets are separated from one another, the photogenerated carriers within the 2D nanosheet get localized. As the photogenerated carriers in the carbon nitrides are confined within the 2D nanosheets, which inhibit recombination, NaCNC‐CNNS/FTO exhibits improved PEC activity. Figure 10c shows the J‐t curves of the BCN‐based photoanode and NaCNC‐CNNS‐based photoanodes at 0.6 V versus the Ag/AgCl. It also demonstrates the photostability of the photoanodes in the KOH electrolyte. Photocurrent measurement under solar illumination with a UV cutoff filter reveals that more than 50% of the photocurrent density is contributed by visible photons, which proves that the NaCNC‐CNNS gently satisfies critical parameters, such as excellent charge carrier separation and a broad range of visible light absorption.

4 reasons contribute to the 2.6‐fold enhancement of the PEC performance. First, the wider electronic bandgap (2.9 eV) of NaCNC‐CNNS compared to BCN (2.76 eV) ensures a larger thermodynamic driving force for the water splitting reaction. Second, NaCNC‐CNNS has a slightly deeper highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level compared to BCN which corresponds to a higher oxidizing power of photogenerated holes in NaCNC‐CNNS. Third, the intercalated Na+ ions generate an electrocatalytic enhancement of water splitting kinetics as demonstrated by L. Ren et al. [93] and Z. Wang et al. [94] in previous studies. Last, the four times higher surface area of NaCNC‐CNNS compared to BCN results in a higher density of active reaction sites available for photoinduced charge transfer to the electrolyte.

The Mott‐Schottky plots of the NaCNC‐CNNS and BCN‐based photoanodes showed a positive slope, indicating both materials are of n‐type (Figure 10d). When the linear portion of the Mott‐Schottky curve is considered, it can be concluded that these x‐axis intercepts from each photoanode form the VFB. The Nd and VFB of both electrodes can be calculated by equation (1). The Nd in NaCNC‐CNNS is estimated to be 4.01 × 1020 cm−3, which is nearly an order of magnitude larger than BCN (4.9 × 1019 cm−3), and is responsible for the relatively high conductivity and efficient electron transfer. On the other hand, the negative shift of the flat band potential from −0.55 to −0.62 V vs Ag/AgCl suggests that NaCNC‐CNNS photoanode is more efficient for charge transfer from NaCNC‐CNNS to the FTO substrate. A negative shift in VFB can attract more electrolyte ions to the surface of a photoanode, effectively increasing the effective surface area available for the charge transfer mechanism [95]. The calculated values of Wsc are 4.56 and 16.4 nm for BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples, respectively. The wide depletion layer of NaCNC‐CNNS results in a low charge recombination probability [96]. As a result, the NaCNC‐CNNS photoanode demonstrated superior PEC performance due to low carrier recombination, higher specific surface area, and its suitable electronic band structure.

Dye Adsorption, Kinetics and Its Photocatalytic Degradation

Methylene blue (MB) was employed as a reference dye in a dye adsorption experiment involving the synthesized NaCNC‐CNNS and BCN materials. Because of its superior absorption of visible light, methylene blue, a staining dye that is extensively used in the paper, textile, and leather industries, serves as an excellent case study of a colored water contaminant. It reduces light penetration in aqueous environments, which negatively impacts underwater plants and animals. The entire research on dye adsorption was done in the dark and at room temperature. MB is a well‐known cationic dye possessing a positive charge, and on the other hand, the surface of NaCNC‐CNNS material has an electron‐rich character, as confirmed by EPR spectroscopy. Therefore, electrostatic interactions between the positively charged MB molecule and negatively charged NaCNC‐CNNS are likely responsible for the instantaneous adsorption. To further confirm the negative charge on the surface of the NaCNC‐CNNS and BCN, a zeta potential measurement was performed, which depicts an average surface charge of −38.2 mV for NaCNC‐CNNS and −19.3 mV for BCN [97, 98], proving the electron‐rich surface of NaCNC‐CNNS (Figure 11a). This higher negative zeta potential enhances the electrostatic attraction between the exfoliated carbon nitride and positive charge carrying cationic dyes, facilitating more effective adsorption of MB dye onto the surface of the exfoliated carbon nitride.

UV–vis spectra of the samples were acquired to identify the concentration of MB solutions following dye adsorption studies. MB has a weak shoulder peak at roughly 614 nm and a strong peak at 664 nm. Immediately after the NaCNC‐CNNS powder was added to the MB solution, the color of the solution quickly changed from dark blue to light, indicating that the MB dye had adhered rapidly to the surface of the NaCNC‐CNNS and the MB‐adsorbed NaCNC‐CNNS settled at the bottom of the vial during centrifugation (Figure 11b). Our findings indicate that the higher surface area and negatively charged surface of NaCNC‐CNNS are responsible for its better adsorption ability. It is also evident that radical sites introduce additional active sites on the surface of carbon nitride, significantly contributing to the dye adsorption capacity [68].

Numerous features, including adsorption capacity, adsorption constants, and the linear regression correlation coefficient, were examined and compared with BCN in order to quantify the improved dye adsorption ability of NaCNC‐CNNS. A standard solution of 5 ppm MB in 15 mL DI water was prepared and poured into a beaker along with 30 mg of the adsorbents to carry out the adsorption kinetic studies. The solution was kept under vigorous stirring. As the adsorption process proceeded exceptionally fast initially, the samples were collected after 1 min, 5 min, 10 min, and 20 min, after which the time interval of sample collection was increased to 20 min and the collection was completed at 80 min. To remove solid precipitates, the obtained samples were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm, and UV–vis spectroscopy was used to examine the supernatant. BCN was also employed in the same conditions to compare the dye adsorption performance. We used Equation 4 to compute the adsorption capacity of the BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS samples. (5) qt=qe1−e−kt (6) qt=kqe2t1+kqet where qe is the quantity of dye adsorbed after achieving equilibrium, qt is the amount adsorbed at time t, k is the pseudo‐first order or pseudo‐second order adsorption rate constant. The findings of using pseudo‐first order and pseudo‐second order kinetics are demonstrated in Table 1, and Figure 11c,d. The kinetics studies showed that the synthesized NaCNC‐CNNS sample has a higher adsorption capacity, even if it takes a little longer time to reach equilibrium. The analysis of the provided data for the samples BCN and NaCNC‐CNNS indicates that both samples exhibit pseudo‐second‐order kinetics [47, 99]. This conclusion is supported by the higher R2 values for the pseudo‐second‐order model compared to the pseudo‐first‐order model. Additionally, the equilibrium adsorption capacities (qe) are slightly higher for the pseudo‐second‐order model, with values of 2.90 mg g−1 for BCN and 5.79 mg g−1 for NaCNC‐CNNS. This substantial difference indicates that NaCNC‐CNNS has a greater capacity to adsorb the dye compared to BCN, suggesting that the structural or compositional characteristics of NaCNC‐CNNS provide more active sites or favorable interactions for adsorption, potentially due to enhanced surface area and porosity that facilitate better interaction with the dye molecules. These findings suggest that the adsorption process is more likely controlled by interactions between the adsorbate and the adsorbent rather than solely by the concentration difference of the adsorbate, highlighting the importance of surface interactions in the adsorption mechanism for both carbon nitride catalysts.

To investigate the photocatalytic activities of the NaCNC‐CNNS photocatalyst, two dyes were selected: methylene blue (MB), and rhodamine B (RhB) (Figure S13a,b). The results demonstrated that the NaCNC‐CNNS photocatalysts effectively degraded all three dyes when exposed to AM1.5G 1 Sun solar illumination. Among these, MB was specifically chosen for further analysis to study the dye degradation mechanism, as it does not undergo self‐degradation like RhB [52]. The focus on MB allows for a deeper understanding of the photocatalytic process, degradation mechanism and its efficiency in degrading organic pollutants.

The degradation performance of MB dye using the NaCNC‐CNNS photocatalyst was significantly enhanced under AM1.5G 1 Sun irradiation compared to UV‐filtered AM1.5G 1 Sun (Figure S13a,c). This improved performance can be attributed to the photocatalyst's wider bandgap, which allows for more effective absorption of higher‐energy UV photons. The presence of UV radiation generates a greater number of electron‐hole pairs, leading to increased formation of reactive species that efficiently degrade the MB dye. In contrast, the UV‐filtered conditions result in fewer charge carriers, reducing the photocatalytic efficiency and thus the rate of degradation.

Scavenger tests are commonly performed in dye degradation studies to gain insights into the reactive species involved in the degradation process. The primary pathway involves the formation of strong oxidative reactants such as hydroxyl radical by photogenerated electrons in LUMO of carbon nitride. A dye cation radical is produced by following the N‐deethylation route and the photogenerated holes engage with the MB cation radical, eventually removing one alkyl group [70]. As a result of this chemical transformation, a noticeable blue shift occurs in the absorption spectrum, shifting from 664 to 650 nm after 80 min of photodegradation (Figure S13a). To understand more about the mechanisms of MB degradation, we conducted various scavenger tests including silver nitrate (AgNO3), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and isopropyl Alcohol (IPA) (Figure S13d). The photocatalytic activity improved drastically after the addition of EDTA and AgNO3; however, it decreased in the presence of IPA (OH• scavenger). This clearly shows that there is a huge amount of photogenerated electrons and holes recombination and thus the degradation reaction is improved when one is scavenged.

Conclusions

We successfully employed a thermal polycondensation synthesis process to produce in situ exfoliated carbon nitride. The exfoliated carbon nitride differs in chemical composition, structure, surface area and optoelectronic properties from bulk carbon nitride. We have shown that, despite the small sodium concentration, solid‐state NMR, XPS and PXRD support the presence of intercalated sodium in the exfoliated carbon nitride. Na+ was incorporated into the carbon nitride framework leading to the broadening of 23Na NMR signal. Through strong interactions with C and N atoms in the sp2‐hybridized heptazine framework, Na transfers its valence electron to polymeric carbon nitride, rendering it electron‐rich and enabling it to act as a radical. Quantum confinement widens the bandgap of NaCNC‐CNNS by 0.14 eV while the electron‐rich system produces an absorption tail that extends to 750 nm. The exfoliated nanosheets have a donor density that is nine times that of bulk carbon nitride. The unique formation of carbon nitride radicals resulted in an anisotropic EPR signal. According to BET analysis, the BCN had a specific surface area of 8.19 m2 g−1, while the NaCNC‐CNNS had a greater specific surface area of 36.05 m2 g−1. PEC performance showed that the photocurrent response on exfoliated NaCNC‐CNNS was roughly three times greater, with a value of 22 µA cm−2 under AM1.5G 1 sun solar irradiation. Under visible light irradiation, the exfoliated carbon nitride showed remarkable superiority in photogenerated charge transfer and separation, hence reinforcing the PEC performance. Due to higher surface area, porosity and negatively charged surface, the NaCNC‐CNNS material displayed superior dye adsorption performance for methylene blue removal, reaching ∼80% adsorption‐desorption equilibria within 1 min. Molecular dynamics modeling demonstrated better dispersity and less aggregation of carbon nitride in the presence of Na+ compared to the case without Na+. The simulations also confirmed a larger specific surface area of the nanosheets created by the presence of intercalated Na+. The distinctive methodology adopted provides a novel approach to advancing the design and modification of photocatalytic material.
