Optimization-Free Patch Attack on Stereo Depth Estimation
Hangcheng Liu, Xu Kuang, Xingshuo Han, Xingwan Wu, Haoran Ou, Shangwei Guo, Xingyi Huang, Tao Xiang, Tianwei Zhang

Abstract
Stereo Depth Estimation (SDE) is a core technique for scene perception in vision-based systems, particularly in autonomous driving. Recent studies have revealed that SDE models are vulnerable to optimization-based adversarial attacks. However, these attacks are often confined to impractical settings, such as separately perturbing stereo views, restricted to the digital level, and static scenes, rendering them ineffective in real-world scenarios. A fundamental question remains: how can we design physically deployable, scene-adaptive, and transferable attacks against SDE models under realistic constraints? We make two key contributions to address the above question. First, we construct a unified attack framework that extends optimization-based attack techniques to four key stages of stereo matching, i.e., feature extraction, cost-volume construction, cost aggregation, and disparity regression. Leveraging this framework, we perform a comprehensive stage-wise evaluation of adversarial patches across 9 mainstream SDE models under realistic constraints (e.g., photometric consistency). Our evaluations show that optimization-based patches consistently suffer from poor transferability. Yet, a subset of patches exhibiting partial transferability leads to a valuable insight: patterns may contribute more to transferable adversarial attacks. Inspired by this, we introduce PatchHunter , the first optimization-free adversarial patch attack against SDE. PatchHunter formulates patch generation as a search task over a structured space of visual patterns designed to disrupt key SDE assumptions. Guided by reinforcement learning policy, PatchHunter efficiently navigates this large space to discover effective and transferable adversarial patterns. We evaluate PatchHunter across three levels, i.e., autonomous driving dataset, high-fidelity simulator, and real-world deployment, to comprehensively assess its effectiveness, transferability, and practicality. On the KITTI dataset, PatchHunter achieves superior attack effectiveness over optimization-based methods, while significantly improving transferability under black-box scenarios. Further evaluations in the CARLA simulator and on a vehicle equipped with industrial-grade stereo cameras highlight that PatchHunter is resilient to physical-world variations. Even under challenging conditions, such as low lighting, PatchHunter can achieve a D1-all error (ranging from 0 to 1) exceeding 0.4, whereas optimization-based attacks yield results close to 0.

Motivated by these questions, our work delivers the following novel contributions:

(1) We construct the first optimization-based adversarial attack framework that systematically generates adversarial patches by targeting the four key stages of stereo matching.

This stage-wise attack design is motivated by the insight that the stereo matching module in SDE involves four interdependent stages: feature extraction, cost volume construction, cost aggregation, and disparity generation, that collectively determine the final depth output (Figure

We comprehensively evaluate different attacks originating from this framework on the KITTI dataset

1) Optimization-based attacks struggle to achieve high transferability under practical constraints such as photometric consistency

Among the 72 source-target model pairs formed from 9 SDE models, adversarial patches generated at the feature extraction, cost volume construction, cost aggregation, and disparity regression stages show poor transferability in 85% (61 pairs), 92% (66 pairs), 100% (72 pairs) and 100% (72 pairs) of the cases, respectively (Table

2) Repetitive textures offer a promising path to improving transferability.

We find that transferable patches share a common trait: visually repetitive textures across different regions.
This pattern exploits a fundamental architecture-independent weakness in SDE: the reliance on local matching consistency. Repetitive textures naturally introduce multiple similar candidate matches along the epipolar line, which increases correspondence ambiguity and leads to a noisier disparity distribution, ultimately making such a pattern more transferable than model-specific adversarial textures.
Guided by this insight, we revise our framework: instead of optimizing a full patch, we optimize a small texture block and tile it to create explicit self-similar textures. Surprisingly, this simple change significantly improves transferability across all four stages (Figure
5
). Although 19%–29% source-target model pairs remain non-transferable, the results underscore a promising direction: enhancing attack transferability can be achieved by leveraging structural vulnerabilities in SDE.

(2) We propose the first optimization-free adversarial attack framework, named
PatchHunter
, which efficiently searches for adversarial patterns within a predefined space to attack SDE.

These patterns directly determine the visual appearance of the adversarial patch, without requiring any additional texture optimization or gradient-based fine-tuning. To achieve this, we first construct a Pattern Primitive Dictionary (PPD) that encodes 9 basic visual attributes, including patch shape, hollowness, block repetition, brightness, color, texture type, frequency, orientation, and blur, each with the potential to compromise SDE performance. Due to the combinatorial nature of these primitives and their multiple discrete choices, the PPD defines a large and semantically meaningful pattern space with approximately 44,000 unique configurations. To sufficiently identify effective and transferable patterns from such a large space, we design a reinforcement learning (RL)–based search algorithm that operates without accessing the victim SDE model’s gradients. Such a strategy treats pattern selection as a structured multi-dimensional decision-making problem. It is well-suited for high-dimensional, discrete, and non-differentiable optimization tasks, where strong solutions are discovered through reward-guided exploration.

Using
PatchHunter
, we identify a set of adversarial patterns for all tested SDE models on the KITTI under two practical black-box settings, both of which require no knowledge about the model’s internal details. The first one is the
interactive
setting, where the adversary sends input to the model and observes the output. This is practical, evidenced by a real-world case where a hacker successfully accessed the input–output behavior of a Tesla vehicle’s depth perception module
[
19
]
.
PatchHunter
discovers adversarial patterns for the target model by leveraging only input-output feedback. The second one is the more challenging
non-interactive
setting, where the adversary cannot access any aspect of the target model.
PatchHunter
identifies adversarial patterns from a different surrogate model. We obtain two outcomes.

1) Adversarial patches generated by

PatchHunter

exhibit clear superiority over optimization-based patches on attack effectiveness and transferability.
In the interactive setting, our patches achieve effectiveness that matches or surpasses the best results reported by optimization-based methods under white-box conditions, where they need to access the details of target models, e.g., parameters, architectures. In the non-interactive setting,
PatchHunter
successfully breaks source-to-target pairs that optimization-based methods consistently fail to attack, effectively raising the upper bound on transferability. Moreover, all evaluations are performed on KITTI images unseen during the search phase, demonstrating the discovered patterns’ strong generalization to new, unseen scenes.

2) The shared characteristics across all discovered patterns reveal an important adversarial configuration: structured hollowness, block repetition, and high texture clarity.
These three visual attributes consistently emerge across all effective adversarial configurations independently discovered for different victim models. This convergence suggests the existence of a model-agnostic, transferable adversarial structure, which underpins the strong transferability observed in all discovered patterns. Further ablation studies show that independently altering any of these attributes leads to a noticeable drop in attack performance, reinforcing the importance of this configuration. We attribute its effectiveness to the distinct, complementary, and mutually reinforcing disruptive effects introduced by each component.

II
Stereo Depth Estimation

SDE is fundamentally based on two key principles:
stereo matching
and
triangulation
. Stereo-matching involves identifying the 2D projections of the same 3D point in both the left and right images, producing a disparity map. Triangulation computes the depth of the corresponding 3D point to the stereo camera using the disparity between the matched left and right projections.

Stereo matching.
Mainstream methods generally adopt a four-stage scheme, as illustrated in Figure
1
.
❶
Feature extraction
. The stereo images are first processed independently through a feature extractor to produce corresponding feature maps. Early methods employ residual networks
[
20
]
to construct the feature extractor, while later approaches introduce expanded receptive fields
[
21
]
and multi-scale pyramid modules
[
11
]
to enhance contextual understanding. ❷
Cost volume construction
. This stage estimates coarse matching cost between the left and right images across a predefined disparity range. Typical strategies include feature correlation
[
22
]
, concatenation
[
23
]
, or a hybrid of both
[
24
]
, resulting in either a 3D or 4D cost volume. The cost volume is a central concept in stereo matching as it encapsulates the matching hypotheses across disparity candidates for all pixel locations. For example, the disparity probability distribution for each spatial location can be obtained by applying softmax along the disparity dimension of a 3D cost volume (the shape is
B\times C^{S2}\times H^{S2}\times W^{S2}
, corresponding to the batch size, disparity level, height, and width). However, the initial cost volume is typically noisy and unreliable, necessitating further refinement. ❸
Cost aggregation
. To suppress noise in disparity distributions, this stage integrates local and global information across the initial cost volume. Most methods employ 3D convolutional networks
[
23
,
11
]
to regularize cost volumes across spatial and disparity dimensions. More recent methods explore efficient alternatives, such as guided aggregation
[
25
]
and adaptive aggregation
[
26
]
. ❹
Disparity generation
. The final disparity map is typically obtained by transforming the refined cost volume into a probability distribution via softmax over the disparity axis, followed by a soft-weighted average. Yet, predictions can still be inaccurate in occlusion regions, fine structures, or textureless areas. To address this, many methods incorporate refinement modules that leverage high-resolution features or edge-aware guidance to correct local errors
[
22
,
27
]
.

Evaluation Metrics.

We use two metrics to evaluate the performance of attacks on SDE models. The first is D1-all, a widely used metric in SDE studies
[
14
,
15
]
. Defined by the KITTI benchmark

, D1-all measures the percentage of pixels with disparity errors exceeding both 3 pixels and 5% of the ground truth, which are considered to be erroneously estimated by the model. It is computed as:

\begin{split}\text{D1-all}&=\frac{1}{\left\|\Omega\right\|}\sum_{[h,w]\in\Omega}l[h,w],\\
l[h,w]&=\left\{\begin{matrix}1&\delta[h,w]>\max\{3,0.05\cdot\mathbf{d}[h,w]\},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{matrix}\right.\\
\delta[h,w]&=\left|\mathbf{\tilde{d}}[h,w]-\mathbf{d}[h,w]\right|,\end{split}

(2)

where
\mathbf{d}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{d}}
represent the ground-truth and predicted disparity maps, respectively.
[h,w]
are specific height and width coordinates within the map, respectively,
\Omega
denotes the set of all pixel coordinates, and
\|\Omega\|
is its size. Since we focus on adversarial patches’ effectiveness,
\Omega
is limited to pixels within the patch. D1-all (bounded between 0 and 1) reflects the pixel-level Attack Success Rate (ASR). Higher values indicate stronger attacks.

We also define a transferability metric: the proportion of cross-model evaluations where D1-all satisfies a specific condition. It can be formally represented as:

\mathcal{P}(\circ,\tau)=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{M}|(|\mathbf{M}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F_{s},F_{t}\in\mathbf{M}\\
F_{s}\neq F_{t}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{I}(\text{D1-all}_{F_{s}\rightarrow F_{t}}\circ\tau),

(3)

where
\circ
is a placeholder for a relational operator (
<
or
>
),
\tau
represents a specific threshold,
\mathbf{M}
denotes the set of SDE models,
|\mathbf{M}|
is the set size,
F_{s}
is the surrogate model,
F_{t}
is the target model,
\text{D1-all}_{F_{s}\rightarrow F_{t}}
is the D1-all error when a patch generated for
F_{s}
is applied to
F_{t}

\mathbb{I}
is an indicator function, which outputs 1 if the condition is true, and 0 otherwise.

A smaller

indicates stronger worst-case transferability under threshold
\tau

\mathcal{P}(>,\tau)

\tau

III
Optimization-based Attack Framework

As mentioned in Section
I
, existing SDE attacks
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
typically rely on impractical assumptions,
Therefore, none of them can be simultaneously (i) physically deployable under unconstrained outdoor conditions, (ii) resilient to dynamic scenes, and (iii) transferable across diverse SDE backbones and camera baselines. This naturally raises a critical question:
“Are optimization-based attacks truly practical, even with advanced enhancements?”

To answer this, we construct the first
U
niversal
S
tereo-
C
onstrained Optimization-based Attack Framework (USC) against SDE. It not only covers existing attack strategies but also explores novel attack surfaces in SDE.
Specifically, existing attacks have primarily focused on attacking feature extraction and disparity generation (stage ❶ and ❹), while overlooking the two equally critical intermediate stages in the stereo matching pipeline: cost volume construction and cost aggregation (stage ❷ and ❸). The cost volume encodes the initial disparity distribution by computing matching costs across potential disparities, whereas cost aggregation serves as a crucial bridge between raw matching costs and high-quality disparity estimates by context modeling and spatial regularization. These two stages, though largely overlooked, expose distinct vulnerabilities and represent promising attack surfaces. Therefore, our framework is explicitly designed to cover all four key stages of stereo matching, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of optimization-based attack strategies in the context of SDE.

I. Attacking feature extraction.
Inspired by
[
16
]
, attacks targeting the feature extraction stage aim to reduce the similarity between the patch’s feature representations in the left and right views. The objective function is defined as:

\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\theta_{s}}=\frac{1}{\|\Omega\|}\sum_{[h,w]\in\Omega}Cosine(F^{L}[h,w-\mathbf{d_{s}}[h,w]],F^{R}[h,w]),

(6)

where
F^{E}
and
F^{R}
denote the features of the left and right inputs, respectively.
Cosine
means the cosine similarity between two feature vectors.
\mathbf{d_{s}}
represents the scaled results of
\mathbf{d}
, used to align coordinates due to the reduced spatial resolution of feature maps.

II. Attacking cost volume construction.
Since no prior work has explored this stage, we design a new attack that injects critical noise into the cost volume. This is motivated by the fact that the accuracy of the disparity distribution in the initial cost volume
V
directly influences subsequent refinement steps such as disparity smoothing. We define the objective function as:

\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\theta_{s}}=\frac{1}{\|\Omega\|}\sum_{[h,w]\in\Omega}JSD(softmax(\mathbf{V}[h,w]),\mathcal{M}),

(7)

where
JSD
is the Jensen-Shannon Divergence,
softmax(\mathbf{V}[h,w])
represents the normalized disparity distribution at location
[h,w]
, and
\mathcal{M}
is a synthetic distribution with explicitly multi-modal structure (e.g., sine wave). The design of this multi-modal distribution is motivated by two factors. First, ideal disparity distributions concentrate most of their probability mass around the true disparity. Enforcing a multi-modal structure disperses this energy, reducing the dominance of the correct match and increasing ambiguity. Second, Garg et al.
[
28
]
showed that multi-modal disparity may lead to significant disparity drift.

III. Attacking cost aggregation.
Since both the initial and aggregated cost volumes represent per-pixel disparity distributions, manipulating their distributional structure can be achieved using the same divergence-based objective. Therefore, we reuse the objective function from the cost volume construction stage to attack cost aggregation, encouraging the aggregated volume
\mathbf{V^{\prime}}
to retain a multi-modal structure:

\mathcal{L}^{3}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{\|\Omega\|}\sum_{[h,w]\in\Omega}JSD(softmax(\mathbf{V^{\prime}}[h,w]),\mathcal{M}).

IV
Methodology

Inspired by the above findings, we present a new search-based attack methodology against SDE,
PatchHunter
. It aims to discover highly effective and transferable adversarial patches that are solely defined by basic visual patterns (e.g., colors, texture types) without any optimization.

IV-A

Threat model

We adopt a widely used and realistic threat scenario explored in prior work
[
37
]
, in which an attacker physically places a printable adversarial patch in the environment to disrupt the SDE function in autonomous driving scenarios.

Attack goal.
Our goal is to make the SDE model’s predictions within the patch region deviate significantly from the ground truth, similar to the objectives of prior works
[
16
,
15
]
. The greater the deviation, the more effective the attack, as it increases the likelihood of inducing severe depth perception errors, potentially leading to safety-critical failures.

Adversarial capabilities.
We consider two practical black-box attack settings.

(1) Interactive attack.
The adversary has no knowledge of the victim SDE model’s internal information, but can query the model with his input and observe the corresponding output. This assumption is realistic in autonomous driving scenarios, where attackers may exploit interface-level vulnerabilities or debugging channels to intercept perception data. For example, in a documented incident in 2021, a hacker successfully extracted the input-output data stream from a Tesla vehicle’s depth estimation module
[
19
]
. This suggests that an adversary could leverage an identical vehicle to probe the model’s input-output behavior, and then execute the pattern search based on the feedback.

(2) Non-interactive attack.
In this more restrictive setting, the adversary has no access to the victim model’s inputs, outputs, or internals mechanisms. Thus, it demands adversarial patterns with high transferability to succeed across unseen models.

IV-B

Challenges

Identifying effective patch patterns under black-box settings, especially those consistently transferred across SDE models, poses two main challenges. (1) It remains unclear which patterns are truly effective and transferable. While prior studies
[
22
,
7
]
and our preliminary experiments suggest that certain patterns, such as low-texture or repetitive textures, may improve transferability, it is unknown whether other types of patterns could achieve similar or even better performance. To explore this possibility, we need a rich and structured pattern space that enables systematic exploration.
(2) As the pattern space grows larger, efficient pattern search becomes increasingly challenging. On the one hand, brute-force search is inefficient and lacks scalability. On the other hand, manual tuning is unreliable due to the complex and often nonlinear interactions among pattern attributes. These limitations necessitate an automated and scalable approach for identifying effective and transferable patterns.

Overview

Figure

PatchHunter
. To address the first challenge, we define a Pattern Primitive Dictionary (PPD) that organizes diverse low-level texture patterns into different composable primitives (Table
I
). PPD consists of 9 basic pattern primitives that define common visual attributes such as patch shape, color, and texture type. These primitives are designed to be composable, and each includes one or more specific options derived from commonly used configurations, which may challenge SDE models from multiple perspectives. This compositional design results in a large and expressive pattern space. To address the second challenge, we design a Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based search method that learns a policy network with multi-head to explore the structured pattern space defined by PPD. RL is well-suited for high-dimensional, discrete, and non-differentiable optimization tasks, which can discover effective patterns through reward-guided exploration. The overall searching process is implemented using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

38

, which offers improved training stability and sample efficiency.

IV-D

Pattern Primitive Dictionary Construction

PPD defines our pattern space, structured into four semantic categories and nine basic pattern primitives (Table
I
). Each primitive captures a distinct visual attribute and enables flexible pattern composition.
The design of PPD and the specific options within each primitive are empirically motivated, drawing from prior findings
[
39
,
11
,
40
]
, commonly observed visual characteristics, and our targeted analysis of weaknesses in stereo matching pipelines. In the following, we describe each primitive, along with its potential to challenge SDE models.

\bullet

Geometric attribute.
Geometric primitives are designed to disrupt the spatial structural assumptions commonly leveraged by stereo matching, including geometric consistency, uniqueness, and completeness.
①
Patch shape
defines the global silhouette (e.g., rectangles or circles), affecting perceived object boundaries. ②
Hollowness
controls whether structured cutout regions appear within the patch and determines their scale. These cutouts fragment the spatial continuity and challenge the model’s ability to perform disparity smoothing. ③
Block repetition
determines whether identical texture blocks are tiled across the patch. If enabled, it introduces visually repetitive patterns at the patch level, even when the texture within each block is irregular. Its significant potential in enhancing transferability has been confirmed in earlier experiments.

\bullet

Photometric attribute.
④
Brightness
patches the luminance at three levels (high, normal, and low), where extreme luminance may reduce local contrast and hinder feature extraction. ⑤
Color
defines color composition using high- or low-contrast pairs. High contrast (e.g., black and white) creates strong edges and false structures, while low contrast (e.g., gray–white) suppresses saliency, leading to low-texture ambiguity.

\bullet

Texture attribute.
⑥
Texture type
determines the internal structure: (a) solid or gradient textures lack distinctive features; (b) regular textures (e.g., stripes, checkerboards) introduce global texture similarity, inducing ambiguity in stereo matching like block repetition; (c) Irregular textures (e.g., random noise) typically exhibit high-frequency and incoherent structures, which may overly activate early-stage features in CNN-based models, leading to a noisy and unstable cost volume.
⑦
Frequency
controls the density of texture distribution within the patch. High-frequency settings may enhance the effects of regular or repetitive textures, while low-frequency settings can exacerbate the ambiguity associated with low-texture regions. ⑧
Orientation
specifies the dominant direction (e.g., horizontal, vertical, diagonal) of texture variation, i.e., the direction along which visual intensity changes occur. For example, a vertically striped pattern results in horizontal texture variation. This design choice is motivated by the two patches in the first column of Figure
4
, which exhibit similar stripe-like patterns with different orientations and result in different transferability in Figure
2
(a). This observation suggests that texture orientation can have a substantial impact on the effectiveness and transferability of attacks.

\bullet

Degradation attribute.
⑨
Blur
controls the sharpness of texture boundaries by applying different types of blurring operations to the patch. A heavy blur reduces feature clarity, thereby having the potential to impair the localization and matching of local features. Conversely, a sharp and well-defined texture boundary can amplify the perceptual salience of other disruptive attributes, such as repeated textures, thereby jointly increasing the adversarial impact.

It is important to note that our PPD is
extendable.

It currently consists of nine basic pattern primitives, which already define a large and expressive space. After removing perceptually redundant configurations (e.g., the combination of different frequencies with solid color), PPD still yields nearly
44,000
unique and semantically meaningful pattern configurations, sufficient to produce effective adversarial patterns that outperform optimization-based patches in both transferability and real-world robustness (we have demonstrated it in Section
V
).
Moreover, users can easily incorporate additional custom patterns into the space by defining new primitives or extending existing ones, making the framework flexible for broader deployment scenarios.

IV-E

Reinforcement Learning-based Search

To efficiently explore the large combinatorial space defined by PPD, we formulate the pattern selection process as a reinforcement learning (RL) task. Unlike traditional gradient-based methods that optimize pixel values, our approach treats pattern generation as a discrete decision process: the agent selects one option from each pattern primitive to compose a complete adversarial configuration. This formulation enables black-box search without requiring gradient access or model internals, making it well-suited for real-world attack settings. Specifically, our RL framework consists of two main components: an
environment
that simulates the pattern search process and provides feedback, and an
agent
that learns to generate effective configurations.

IV-E
1
Environment

The environment encapsulates the full pattern configuration space and provides a structured interface for patch generation and evaluation. It is formalized by the RL tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})
, representing the state, action, and reward, respectively.

State.

In our task, we define the state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
at time step
t
as the pattern configuration selected in the previous step, i.e.,
s_{t}=a_{t-1}
. This design allows the agent to condition its current decision on its most recent output, enabling a form of short-term memory that encourages diverse exploration and prevents repetitive sampling.

Action.

To enable policy-based exploration over the pattern space, we define the agent’s action
a_{t}
at time
t
as the selection of a complete pattern configuration. Specifically, given a set of
N
pattern primitives
{\mathcal{O}_{1},\mathcal{O}_{2},...,\mathcal{O}_{N}}
, where each
\mathcal{O}_{i}={o_{i}^{1},o_{i}^{2},...,o_{i}^{K_{i}}}
is a finite option set with cardinality
K_{i}
, the action space is defined as:

\mathcal{A}=\{(o_{1}^{k_{1}},o_{2}^{k_{2}},\cdots,o_{N}^{k_{N}})\,|\,o_{i}^{k_{i}}\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\}.

(10)

Each
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
corresponds to a specific combination of visual primitives and is used to generate a candidate patch.

Reward.

Upon taking an action (pattern configuration), the environment generates an adversarial patch (patch render), applies it onto randomly sampled stereo images (patch deployment), and evaluates the performance degradation of SDE, quantified by D1-all. The D1-all score serves as the reward:
r_{t}=\text{D1-all}(a_{t})
, indicating the attack effectiveness of the current configuration. Since the evaluation only requires access to the model’s inputs and outputs, it supports the interactive setting. For the more restricted non-interactive setting, a surrogate model or model ensembles can be used to estimate the reward and enhance transferability.

IV-E
2
Agent

The agent uses a policy network to select actions based on the current state:
\pi_{\theta_{P}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
, where
\theta_{P}
denotes all learnable parameters of the policy.
We implement the policy network as a multi-head (e.g., 9 heads) multi-layer perceptron (MLP), where each head corresponds to one pattern primitive and outputs a categorical distribution over its discrete options. During inference, we apply greedy decoding by selecting the most probable option from each head to construct the complete action vector. This design enables the agent to generate a full pattern configuration in a single forward pass. We just follow the standard PPO framework
[
38
]
to train our policy net, using the clipped surrogate objective and normalized advantage for stable and efficient learning. In our experiments, the policy network converges within just 800 environment interaction steps (Figure
16
), each corresponding to a complete observation–action–reward cycle, remarkably efficient given the large pattern space of nearly 44,000 unique configurations. This efficiency stems from two key factors: (i) the highly structured pattern space defined by our PPD, which consists of semantically meaningful pattern options that have the potential to challenge various assumptions of SDE, and (ii) the simple yet effective design of our reinforcement learning framework, which enables stable and fast convergence.

②
Sharp textures (non-blur) are essential to activating self-similarity effects.

Surprisingly, reducing the patch to one-third yields higher D1-all than the large size. We hypothesize two contributing factors:
(i) Small patches may blend into the background, offering fewer matching cues and leading to larger disparity errors, especially at long ranges where they occupy fewer pixels.
(ii) Since D1-all is computed only within the patch, smaller patches shrink the evaluation area, so even a few corrupted pixels can inflate the score.

Hollow scale.
We adopt the high-density hollow pattern as the default configuration, as prior experiments consistently favor this setting for stronger attack performance. To further validate its effect, we compare it with a large-and-sparse variant (see the last column in Figure
19
), both composed of solid white blocks. As shown in Table
V
, both patterns are effective, but the small-and-dense one performs better, likely because it induces sharper local disparity changes and more thoroughly disrupts the smoothness prior, which is a key assumption in SDE.

Related attacks on MDE.

There are more works focusing on the adversarial attacks against MDE models. Similar to the efforts on SDE
[
15
,
17
]
, these MDE attacks are also optimization-based, relying on either global perturbation optimization
[
45
]
or localized texture optimization
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
to distort depth predictions. In this work, we shift the focus to attacking SDE models, which are generally considered more robust and accurate due to their geometric consistency and reliance on binocular
[
12
,
13
]
. Hence, attacking SDE is much more challenging than MDE.

Defense discussion.
Current research on protecting depth estimation from adversarial attacks remains limited and primarily targets MDE tasks. These efforts mainly fall into two categories: adversarial training
[
49
,
50
]
and patch detection
[
49
,
51
]
. For example, Cheng et al.
[
50
]
proposed an adversarial training framework to enhance the robustness of self-supervised MDE models. Krishna et al.
[
51
]
detected adversarial patches by exploiting their unnatural high-frequency artifacts, which produce sharp gradient contrasts with the surrounding image content. The detected regions are then removed and reconstructed using inpainting techniques to restore the benign appearance of the original scene. Hu and Okatani
[
49
]
combined adversarial training with saliency-based patch localization and masking.

However, these defenses are ineffective against our pattern-based attack. First, adversarial training improves robustness through a two-loop min-max process, where the inner loop generates
worst-case
perturbations for the current model, and the outer loop updates the model to resist them. Our attack, based on a structured search over discrete patterns, is incompatible with this framework because it requires hundreds of environment interactions (e.g., 800), which makes it too inefficient for adversarial training. Integrating such a slow search into the inner loop would significantly hinder model convergence and render the overall training process impractical. On the other hand, including static, pre-discovered patterns in training violates the goal of dynamic robustness and leads to overfitting, without covering the full pattern space. Second, patch detection methods typically rely on the distinct visual characteristics of adversarial patches, such as high-frequency artifacts and unnatural textures. In contrast, our patches are constructed from simple and natural textures, such as stripes, gradients, or even solid colors, that closely resemble benign patterns commonly found in real-world environments. This fundamental difference in visual appearance leads to a domain gap, rendering adversarial-texture-specific detectors ineffective against our attacks.

These observations suggest that existing defense techniques, though effective in other contexts, are ill-suited for protecting SDE models against pattern-based attacks. Designing principled defenses tailored to this unique threat remains an open challenge. We will dedicate future efforts to developing robust and practical solutions in this space.

Comparison with active sensor spoofing.
DoubleStar
[
52
]
proposes the only known active spoofing attack targeting SDE. It disrupts stereo matching by projecting asymmetric light into both cameras, creating false correspondences that violate epipolar constraints. Despite its effectiveness, DoubleStar suffers from several limitations. It requires real-time lighting projection, often involving human presence or external infrastructure. The attack is also highly sensitive to ambient lighting conditions and only works reliably in low-light environments. Contrastively,
our method adopts a completely different setting
. We design a passive patch-based attack that uses printable patches placed in the physical environment, which are naturally captured by the stereo cameras without requiring any active intervention.

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we conduct an in-depth investigation into the adversarial robustness of SDE under realistic constraints. We first present a unified framework to evaluate conventional optimization-based attacks across four stages of stereo matching, revealing their limited transferability despite access to gradients. Motivated by the discovery that structural vulnerabilities play a more critical role than fine-grained texture perturbations, we propose
PatchHunter
, the first optimization-free adversarial patch search method targeting SDE models. It formulates patch generation as a search task over interpretable visual patterns using reinforcement learning. Extensive experiments across the KITTI dataset, CARLA simulator, and real-world driving scenarios demonstrate that
PatchHunter
achieves strong effectiveness and transferability in black-box settings, significantly outperforming existing optimization-based approaches.

(2) Policy network.
We adopt a
lightweight yet effective
network architecture based on a multi-head multilayer perceptron (MLP). The model consists of a shared bottom module and multiple independent heads. The shared module includes one embedding layer followed by two fully connected (FC) layers. Each categorical option of a pattern primitive is first embedded into a 32-dimensional vector, resulting in a 9
\times
32 input (with 9 denoting the number of primitives). This is then transformed into a 9
\times
128 representation through the FC layers. Each head corresponds to a specific pattern primitive and contains two FC layers that map the flattened 1152-dimensional feature to a probability distribution over its candidate options. Despite its simplicity, this architecture proves highly effective in practice. As shown in Figure
16
, the agent rapidly converges to strong adversarial pattern configurations with significantly improved rewards, validating the effectiveness of our network design.

(3) PPO training.
In each epoch, we sample a trajectory by allowing the agent to interact with the environment for 16 time steps under the current policy. That trajectory consists of 16 state-action-reward tuples
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t})
, one for each time step
t
.
After that, we directly approximate the advantage function by normalizing the raw rewards collected within each epoch:

A_{t}=\frac{r_{t}-\bar{r}}{\sigma_{r}+\epsilon_{1}},

(11)

where
A_{t}
denotes the estimated advantage of taking action
a_{t}
in state
s_{t}
under the current policy,
\bar{r}
and
\sigma_{r}
are the mean and standard deviation of the rewards from the current epoch, and
\epsilon_{1}
is a small constant (e.g.,
1e^{-7}
). This normalization acts as a baseline and helps reduce the variance of policy gradient estimates. After that, we follow the standard PPO training procedure by optimizing the
\theta_{P}
using the clipped surrogate objective:

\mathcal{L}(\theta_{P})=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\min\left(r_{t}(\theta_{P})A_{t},\ \text{clip}(r_{t}(\theta_{P}),1-\epsilon_{2},1+\epsilon_{2})A_{t}\right)\right],

(12)

where
r_{t}(\theta_{P})=\frac{\pi_{\theta_{P}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{P\text{old}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}
is the probability ratio between the current and old policy. The clip function applies an element-wise constraint on
r_{t}(\theta_{P})
, forcing each value to lie within the interval
[1-\epsilon_{2},\ 1+\epsilon_{2}]
.
\epsilon_{2}
(typically set to 0.1 or 0.2) controls the allowed deviation between the new and old policies, acting as a trust region to prevent overly aggressive updates. In experiments, we train the policy for up to 50 epochs using a learning rate of 0.001. As shown in Figure
16
, the agent’s reward increases rapidly and stabilizes within 400 environment steps, demonstrating the high efficiency of our PPO-based search. Notably, compared to the low reward observed in the early training phase, where the policy behaves almost randomly, the learned policy consistently discovers stronger pattern configurations as training progresses.
This highlights the necessity of policy learning, as purely random exploration fails to identify effective combinations within such a large discrete space.

End-Point Error (EPE).
EPE is another popular metric in SDE studies
[
14
,
15
,
16
]

\begin{split}\text{EPE}&=\frac{1}{\left\|\Omega\right\|}\sum_{[h,w]\in\Omega}\delta[h,w],\\
\delta[h,w]&=\left|\mathbf{\tilde{d}}[h,w]-\mathbf{d}[h,w]\right|.\end{split}

(13)

We include EPE as a complementary metric to characterize the scale of disparity deviations, providing additional insights beyond the threshold-based D1-all metric. In Figure
17
(a) and Figure
17
(c), the optimization-based USC attack yields generally low EPE values in black-box settings, as indicated by many off-diagonal cells with values below 1. Such minimal perturbations to disparity predictions are unlikely to affect SDE performance in practice. In Figure
17
(b), incorporating repetitive patterns improves B-USC’s overall performance, with many off-diagonal cells exceeding 1. However, it still fails to generalize across all transfer scenarios, as a considerable number of cells remain below the 1-pixel threshold. In contrast, our pattern-driven
PatchHunter

PatchHunter
in achieving both high attack effectiveness and strong transferability.
