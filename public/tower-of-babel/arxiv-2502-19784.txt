NaijaNLP: A Survey of Nigerian Low-Resource Languages
Isa Inuwa-Dutse

Abstract
. With over 500 languages in Nigeria, three languages - Hausa, Yorùbá and Igbo spoken by more than 175 million people, account for about 65% of the languages. However, these languages are classed as low-resource due to insufficient digital resources to support tasks in computational linguistics. While several research efforts and initiatives have been presented, a coherent understanding of the state of classic Natural Language Processing (NLP) spanning grammatical formalisation to linguistic resources that support models development is lacking. This study presents the first comprehensive review of the state of affairs in NLP research across the three major Nigerian languages (NaijaNLP). We quantitatively assess the available linguistic resources and identify key challenges. Of the 293 reviewed studies, 27.6 % 27.6\% contributed new linguistic resources. This finding highlights a strong reliance on repurposing existing data rather than creating new resources. Also, language-specific challenges, such as morphological analysis and effective representation of diacritics, remain under-explored. To advance NaijaNLP and LR-NLP more broadly, we echo the need for more collaborative efforts in resource enrichment, comprehensive annotation, and increased community support.

1.
Introduction

Of the 7,170 distinct languages spoken globally
(
Ethnologue, 2026
)
, over 90% are classified as low-resource (LR) from a computational stand.
While there is no universal consensus on what constitutes a low-resource language
(
Nigatu et al., 2024
)
, a language is generally considered low-resource if it suffers from limited parallel source-target data
(
Ortega et al., 2021
)
.
In other words, low-resource languages (LRLs) are languages for which statistical methods cannot be directly applied due to insufficient linguistic resources
(
Magueresse et al., 2020
)
.
Essentially, the term low-resource encompasses a broad spectrum of resource conditions and application domains
(
Duong, 2017
;
Hedderich et al., 2020
;
Yazar et al., 2023
;
Avetisyan and Broneske, 2023
;
Krasadakis et al., 2024
;
Maddu and Sanapala, 2024
;
Masethe et al., 2024
)
.
Numerous studies have explored low-resource natural language processing (LR-NLP) across diverse domains, covering misinformation detection
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
, security
(
Teze and Nazaruka, 2024
)
, neural machine translation
(
Andrabi et al., 2021
;
Shi et al., 2022
)
, cyberbullying detection
(
Mahmud et al., 2023
)
, and sentiment analysis
(
Ghafoor et al., 2021
;
Mabokela et al., 2022
;
Girija et al., 2023
;
Hughes et al., 2023
;
Yusuf et al., 2024
;
Raychawdhary et al., 2025
)
.
A common denominator across these tasks is that LRLs suffer from limited linguistic resources leading to suboptimal performance in NLP downstream applications
(
Joshi et al., 2020
)
.
Low-resource languages are often treated as a homogeneous group, overlooking key variations in morphology, diacritics, data availability, linguistic tools and computational support. The binary classification of differentiating languages as merely low-resource or high-resource has been a dominant approach in NLP research
(
Hedderich et al., 2020
;
Yazar et al., 2023
;
Krasadakis et al., 2024
;
Maddu and Sanapala, 2024
;
Masethe et al., 2024
)
.
However, we argue that this dichotomous stance may impede progress in language enrichment as it fails to account for the unique challenges faced by individual or regional language groups. Instead, we advocate for a more nuanced approach that examines specific linguistic and regional contexts, identifies their distinct challenges and develops tailored solutions. Against this backdrop, our work is intended to provide a comprehensive review and assessment of NLP for Nigerian languages, particularly the three major languages (Hausa, Yorùbá, and Igbo), collectively referred to as NaijaNLP.

1.1.1.
Scope

This study investigates the traditional or classic NLP landscape for NaijaNLP, with focus on linguistic resources and foundational methodologies. Therefore, our primary objective is to examine and document early NLP approaches, linguistic resources, and tools that have been developed to address the unique challenges faced by the three major Nigerian languages. While generative AI and related techniques are transforming NLP, they fall outside the scope of this work due to the need to first establish the early foundational progress in the classic NLP which is lacking for NaijaNLP. Instead, we offer limited coverage of applicable embedding techniques and pre-trained models pertinent to the review. To this end, we explore the NLP landscape of Nigerian languages focusing on the three major languages to understand their state and offer targeted recommendations that can facilitate the development of LR-NLP for these languages and beyond.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

In addition to high-impact publications, the selection criteria include relevant academic theses and non-seminal publications originating locally. We surmise that excluding these NaijaNLP sources could result in the omission of linguistic nuances, valuable resources and insights beneficial to the research community. Specifically, studies are excluded if: (1) they do not cover or pertain to NaijaNLP, i.e. the study falls outside the scope of the three major Nigerian languages as outlined earlier (2) they focus on languages with substantial resources, unless they explicitly address the targeted languages.
We perform a title and abstract screening to identify studies that align with the guiding research questions. This is further reinforced by a thorough final review of the selected papers.

3.
Literature Review

As a communication system, natural language comprises of structured sequences of sounds, symbols, or gestures that convey meaning in various forms. From a formalisation standpoint, languages are composed of structured strings of lexical categories (pre-terminals), forming well-defined sentences that facilitate statistical analysis of their structure
(
Culy, 1996
;
Günther and Rinaldi, 2022
)
. Progress in Machine Learning (ML) and the broader field of
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has provided powerful tools for processing languages through NLP techniques. However, the effectiveness of NLP models depends on the availability of extensive and relevant linguistic resources, leading to a fundamental divide between high-resource and low-resource languages. High-resource languages (HRLs), such as English, French, and Spanish, benefit from sufficient digital resources and large-scale initiatives that facilitate access to standardised multilingual text resources in compatible formats
(
Graff and Finch, 1994
;
Cho, 2014
)
. These resources, sourced from governmental bodies, international organisations, news agencies, and publishers, play a crucial role in propelling various NLP applications.
Efforts to enrich and digitise LRLs, such as transducing texts
(

)
, resource enrichment
(
Adegbola, 2009
)
, and digitising indigenous poetry
(
UCLA, [n. d.]
)
are essential for their integration into computational frameworks.

3.2.
Language Particularities

Languages exhibit distinct structural, phonetic, and orthographic characteristics that influence how they are written, pronounced, and processed. These particularities manifest in diverse orthographic systems comprising of alphabets, abjads, syllabaries, and logograms, which shape idea expression, word formation and written representation. Additionally, phonetic variations, such as differences in phonemes, stress patterns, and tonal systems, impact how languages are spoken and understood. Recognising and integrating these linguistic particularities and nuances are crucial for developing effective NLP tools that accurately represent the unique feature of each language
(
Asubiaro, 2015
;
Adebara and Abdul-Mageed, 2022
;
Odoje, 2016
)
.
Several Nigerian languages are tonal and employ characters beyond the standard Latin alphabet, rendering them structurally distinct from English and other high-resource languages. Consequently, failure to adequately account for these particularities will hinder the effective adaptation of existing NLP tools that have been developed primarily for English and other HRLs in the context of LRLs. Addressing this challenge, relevant studies have explored the linguistic diversity and specific needs of Nigeria’s major languages
(
Amuda et al., 2010
;
Oyinloye and Odejobi, 2015
;
Abdulkareem and Effiong, 2016
;
Adewumi et al., 2020
;
Adebara and Abdul-Mageed, 2022
)
. This section expands on the existing studies on the linguistic particularities, especially morphological analysis and diacritic restoration.

3.2.1.
Morphological Analysis

Morphological analysis is concerned with examining word structure and formation, and has been documented in past studies
(
Iheanetu and Oha, 2017
;
Iheanetu and Oha, 2019
;
Chidiebere et al., 2020
;
Henry et al., 2020
;
Frohmann et al., 2024
)
. To uncover deeper linguistic patterns,
Ochu (2019)
leveraged concordance line strategies to extract multiword expressions in Igbo language. For effective representation of Igbo text,
Chidiebere et al. (2020)
examined collocation patterns, word order, compounding, and lexical ambiguity.
In the context of Yorùbá language,
Oyinloye and Odejobi (2015)
developed a computational system (VerbMorpher) for the morphological analysis of standard Yorùbá verbs; the system incorporates linguistic resources applicable for spell-checking, syntax analysis, and dictionary functionalities.
To enhance the effectiveness of morphological analysis,
Adegbola (2016)
employed unsupervised induction approach to infer morphological rules for standard Yorùbá. Unlike conventional methods that often rely on word-segment frequency, the approach in
(
Adegbola, 2016
)
is pattern-based that utilised word-pattern frequency. Although the study alluded to lexicons collected for the development of a Yorùbá speech synthesiser project, information about the data is lacking. For data curation and quality improvement,
Adewole et al. (2017)
applied a token validation system for automated corpus collection from online sources. The validation system is crucial in retaining key terms in the collection.
Addressing structural complexities of Yorùbá in translation,
Adebara et al. (2021)
detailed a strategy for handling language-specific morphological marking, particularly in cases where English features or cues are contextually inferred in Yorùbá. Motivated by the lack of machine-readable sense inventory for ambiguous Yorùbá words,
Adegoke-Elijah et al. (2023)
proposed a disambiguation component for a Yorùbá-English machine translation system.
Akinwonmi (2024)
introduced a declarative rule-based algorithm to address syllabification errors in Yorùbá, especially for consonant-vowel-nasal and diphthong-vowel-nasal structures.

3.2.2.
Diacritic Restoration

All the languages in NaijaNLP use diacritic marks to convey how a letter should be pronounced, however, the low-resource nature of the languages mean such marks are limited or missing. Thus, diacritic restoration involves adding or correcting diacritical marks such as accents, umlauts (two dots), and tildes associated with textual data. This restoration process is essential for accurate handling of historical texts and user-generated content where diacritics may have been omitted
(
Orife et al., 2020
;
Adewumi et al., 2020
)
. Given that many African languages utilise Latin-based scripts with diacritical marks,
Scannell (2011)
propose a strategy and open-source package for automatic unicodification that converts ASCII text into its proper unicode form across multiple languages.
In addressing orthographic and tonal diacritics, relevant studies leveraged n-gram models trained on the Igbo Bible corpus for word-level diacritic restoration
(
Ezeani et al., 2016
;
Ezeani et al., 2017
)
, and embedding models for text similarity computation
(
Ezeani et al., 2018b
;
Ezeani et al., 2018a
)
. Similarly, relevant studies documented optimal strategies for integrating diacritics and their effects on model performance for Yorùbá NLP tasks
(
De Pauw et al., 2007
;
Túnjí and Bí, 2011
;
Asahiah et al., 2017
;
Orife, 2018
;
Alabi et al., 2020
;
Ogheneruemu, 2022
)
. Recognising the impact of omitting tonal information in performance degradation,
Adegbola and Odilinye (2012)
assessed various machine learning models for handling tone-mark representation in Yorùbá text processing.
For resource enhancement,
Adewumi et al. (2020)
introduced new analogy sets to aid evaluation and,
Adewole et al. (2020)
applied a rule-based framework to resolve the omission of tone or syllable in Yorùbá text.
Asahiah et al. (2023)
developed a spell-checking and correction system that explicitly accounts for diacritic presence. In addressing diacritic usage and pronunciation challenges,
Abdulkareem and Effiong (2016)
implemented a system (YorCALL) that is aimed at enhancing user proficiency in correctly applying diacritics in Yorùbá.
The above studies on diacritic restoration, while beneficial in providing valuable insights and a range of methodology, they contributed limited linguistic resources, especially usable and accessible benchmark datasets.

3.2.3.
Numerical System

The challenges stemming from the lack of or limited standardised linguistic resources extend beyond textual to numerical data. A key area of interest is assessing whether NaijaNLP possesses the necessary linguistic resources, such as well-defined lexicons, for developing their numerical systems through published research. The earliest attempt at analysing Hausa numerals for machine processing dates back to the Automatic Text Comprehension project that surveyed and examined challenges related to converting spoken numerals into decimal representations
(
Sigurd, 1980
)
.
The project led to the development of an automated rule-based systems capable of bidirectional conversion between mathematical representations and their linguistic counterparts.
Similarly,
Rhoda (2017)
developed a number-text conversion system for Igbo, contributing to the broader goal of numerical processing in LRLs.
In a related effort,
Mustapha et al. (2023)
formulated an algorithm for converting numerical figures into Hausa words, while
Agbeyangi et al. (2016)
introduced a numeral translation system for English-Yorùbá.
Beyond numerical conversion,
Ajao et al. (2022)
developed a Yorùbá handwritten character recognition to enhance effective linguistic digitisation.
Despite these developments, research on numerical systems in NaijaNLP remains limited, highlighting the need for further studies and resource development that will ensure accurate numerical representation and processing across the languages.

3.3.
Linguistic Resources and Datasets

The development of relevant tools, resources, and techniques is essential for enabling various NLP downstream tasks and facilitating wider language coverage for low-resource NLP (LR-NLP) applications
(
Berthold, 2009
)
. On the other hand, insufficient linguistic resources and pre-processing tools presents a major challenge for LRLs when diacritic and morphological features must be considered.
To address these challenges, several initiatives, resources, and tools have been developed to support and enrich LRLs. A case in point is the low-resource Human Language Technology (LoReHLT) project, which evaluates the DARPA

9

The LORELEI initiative includes both Hausa and Yorùbá, providing researchers with resource packages comprising of monolingual and parallel texts, a bilingual dictionary, text annotated with part-of-speech (POS) tags, named-entity-recognition (NER), and noun-phase-chunking annotations. Furthermore, language processing tools such as segmenters and morphological analysers, aimed at improving NLP capabilities for these languages have been included.

In analysing the available tools and resources for NaijaNLP, we focus on relevant research areas that closely align with the LORELEI research initiative
(
Christianson et al., 2018

Zandam et al. (2023)
contributed a dataset of threatening lexicons from Twitter, and
Adam et al. (2024)
curated a collection of offensive terms in Hausa languages.
Awwalu et al. (2021)
constructed a useful tagset for part-of-speech tagging in Hausa, while
Salifou and Naroua (2014)
implemented a spell-checking and correction tool for Hausa, which is accessible via LyTexEditor and includes an extension or add-on for
OpenOffice.org
.

Igbo Dataset

Several studies have reported useful datasets to facilitate computational tasks in Igbo language.

(
Babatunde et al., 2026b
)
contributed a large scale annotated dataset consisting of 750,000 user expressions and responses for developing Chatbot systems in Igbo language. Native speakers and linguistic experts have been consulted in developing the corpus.

Chioma and Nnadozie (2023)
compiled a comprehensive set of Igbo words and their meanings, while
Nganga and Achebe (2020)
is focused on designing a methodological framework for creating digital dialectal dictionary that relies on a corpus of spoken-word and oral traditions in Igbo. In a different study,
Ajao et al. (2023)
implemented an RNN-based handwritten character recognition system for Igbo using data from native speakers.

Onuora et al. (2024)
and
Ana et al. (2024)
reported the development of a collection of hate speech content, however, information about the data was not reported. Moreover, datasets for detecting cyberbullying and related content in Igbo and Pidgin English have been discussed in
(
Okoloegbo et al., 2024
;
Okoloegbo et al., 2022
)
, however, access to such datasets is not publicly available. To aid with named-entity recognition,
Onyemaechi and Ojiako (2023)
presented a database of 158 Chi-prefixed Igbo personal names, categorised into praise, thanksgiving, testimony, prayer, and declarative groups.

).

Yoruba Datasets

For the Yorùbá language, researchers have contributed a range of linguistic resources and strategies to support various downstream applications
(
Orife, 2018
;
Ahia et al., 2024

Fagbolu et al. (2015)

Ademusire and Ninan (2023)

Afolabi and Wahab (2013)

Iyanda (2015)
compiled a Yorùbá corpus for speech generation, sourced from textbooks, newspapers, and online materials.
Similarly,
Gutkin et al. (2020)
contributed a speech synthesis dataset made of over 4 hours of 48 kHz recordings in Yorùbá, while
Emmanuel (2021)
contributed annotated prosodic read-speech syllabic data, extracted from fictional books and online scriptures.
To further standardise Yorùbá linguistic resources,
Oluwatoyin and Opeyemi (2018)
introduced YoTEx (Yoruba Text Lexicon), a repository that learns from English text to enhance Yorùbá text processing.
Okediya et al. (2019)
utilised various online resources consisting of books, blogs, social websites, and Yorùbá dictionaries, to construct a general-purpose lexical ontology for Yorùbá NLP applications.

3.3.4.
Multilingual Datasets

To address the challenges of LRLs and mitigate reliance on costly annotation, several initiatives and strategies have contributed to low-resource multilingual datasets development
(
Kolak and Resnik, 2005
;
Varab and Schluter, 2021
;
Fan et al., 2021
;
Hedderich, 2022
;
Godslove and Nayak, 2024
)
.

Adelani et al. (2023)
contributed one of the largest dataset and resources for news classification (MasakhaNEWS) in 16 African languages.
In a related effort,
(
Adelani et al., 2021
)
curated a large-scale, publicly available dataset for named-entity recognition (NER) across ten African languages. Moreover,
Adelani (2022)
introduced a sizeable human-annotated datasets for NER and machine translation across 21 African languages.
To support the provision of high-quality human-annotated cross-lingual information retrieval resources for African languages,
Adeyemi et al. (2024)
presented a publicly available test collection for cross-lingual information retrieval (CIRAL) consisting of English queries with passages in Hausa, Somali, Swahili, and Yorùbá languages.
Motivated by the need to factor in Nigeria’s linguistic and cultural diversity in news coverage,
(
Ogunrinde and Yusuf, 2026
)
curated a collection 10,000 Nigerian news articles in English, Nigerian Pidgin, Yoruba, Hausa, and Igbo languages.

Muhammad et al. (2022)
contributed another large-scale human-annotated Twitter sentiment dataset for Hausa, Igbo, Nigerian Pidgin, and Yorùbá. The collection consists of approximately 30,000 annotated tweets with code-mixed examples.

Ogunleye et al. (2023)
utilised 346,000 Nigerian banking-related tweets to develop SentiLeye, a lexicon-based sentiment analysis dataset for Nigerian Pidgin.
Multilingual machine translation is another area that is witnessing active contributions.
Adewumi et al. (2023)
present useful resources for developing dialogue agents in Swahili, Wolof, Hausa, Nigerian Pidgin, Kinyarwanda, and Yorùbá languages.

Ajagbe (2024)
utilised six antenatal clinics to develop a multilingual Nigerian speech dataset in English, Yorùbá, and Pidgin.

Amuda et al. (2010)
curated an audio-visual dataset (UISpeech) of Nigerian-accented English, recorded from native speakers of Hausa, Igbo, Yorùbá, Tiv, and Fulfulde.

Aremu et al. (2024)
developed NaijaRC, a multi-choice reading comprehension dataset for Hausa, Igbo, and Yorùbá.
Recognising the need to address language-related flaws in the benchmark (FLORES) for evaluating NLP performance in low-resource languages
(
Goyal et al., 2022
)
,
Abdulmumin et al. (2024)
engaged with native speakers to identify and correct inaccurate and inconsistent cases pertaining to Hausa, Northern Sotho (Sepedi), Xitsonga, and isiZulu languages.

Relevant studies have contributed to the development of useful corpora to tackle the spread of hateful and offensive content
(
Ferroggiaro, 2018
;
Tonneau et al., 2024
;
Muhammad et al., 2025a
)
. Essentially,
Tonneau et al. (2024)
presented the first annotated data for detecting hateful content, and
Muhammad et al. (2025a)
developed a multilingual corpus of annotated hate speech and abusive language (AfriHate) for 15 African languages.
Aliyu et al. (2024)
introduced a similar collection of hate speech in the 3 major languages, and
Udeze and Voigt (2026)
examined how a combination of Nigerian Pidgin and indigenous languages are strategically utilised in propagating offensive content.
In addition to contributing a new collection based on Instagram comments, the work in
(
Udeze and Voigt, 2026
)
utilised the existing NaijaHate corpus
(
Tonneau et al., 2024
)
to investigate the use and proliferation of offensive language in Nigerian Pidgin.
For semantic and contextual alignment with low-resource languages,
Oduwole et al. (2026)
curated a multilingual collection of image-based datasets (Flickr8k) to enable image captioning in 20 African languages (including Igbo, Hausa, Yoruba).

The datasets and strategies in Tables
4
and
5
serve as essential resources for advancing NaijaNLP. The aspect of resource creation has accelerated since 2020 (Figure
2
), which we largely attribute to the increasing community initiatives such as Masakhane
(
Adelani, 2022
)
, cross-lingual benchmarks
(
Adelani et al., 2021
;
Muhammad et al., 2022
;
Adelani et al., 2023
;
Adelani et al., 2024
;
Muhammad et al., 2025a
)
, and funding bodies that encourage collaborative research. Table

summarises the quality ratings across the 81 surveyed datasets spanning Hausa, Yorùbá, Igbo, and multilingual resources. While annotation (63%), validation (62%), and publicly accessible data (51%) are reported in most studies, inter-annotator agreement is relatively low (36%). This points to the need for improved and standardised data development process. Essentially, we recommend adoption of the strategies detailed in datasets for datasets
(
Gebru et al., 2021

to be adhered to for submissions that introduce new resources for NaijaNLP.

Building on existing corpora, various linguistic tools have been developed to enrich and ensure the accurate representation of LRL. ;

;
Idris et al., 2022
;
Ekpenyong et al., 2022
;
Adewumi, 2022b
;
Omotayo et al., 2024
)
. Other related studies include automatic spell checkers and correctors for Yorùbá
(
Oluwaseyi et al., 2024
)
and Hausa
(
Mijinguini, 2003
;

3.4.1.
Part-of-Speech Tagging and Named Entity Recognition

Depending on its context and definition, each word in a text belongs to a grammatical category, such as noun, verb, adjective, adverb and preposition. Thus, Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging facilitates the structural analysis of sentences by assigning words to their corresponding grammatical groups. Similarly, some words in a text may refer to specific entities such as people, organisations, locations, and dates. Thus, Named Entity Recognition (NER) identifies and classifies such entities accordingly. To that end, several studies have explored both POS tagging and NER related to NaijaNLP and beyond
(

3.5.
Downstream Tasks

In NLP, downstream tasks such as text classification, sentiment analysis, machine translation, and speech recognition rely fundamentally on rich annotated datasets and effective computational models. In this section, we review the existing downstream tasks and applicable resources that have been developed for Hausa, Igbo, and Yorùbá languages.

3.5.1.
Text Classification and Summarisation

Text classification (TC) involves assigning textual data to predefined classes based on a similarity or dissimilarity metric informed by a range of methodologies from rule-based approaches to deep learning models.

3.5.2.
Machine Translation and Speech Recognition

Machine Translation (MT) focuses on the automatic translation of text or speech from one language to another, and it has evolved from rule-based to transformer-based systems. Effective MT systems are heavily dependent on large volumes of parallel data to generate accurate translations
(
Mahata et al., 2020
)
.
In addressing the needs of low-resource languages,
Akinfaderin (2020)

Tresner-Kirsch et al. (2023)
propose a system for intent recognition in user messages directed to a chatbot (askNivi), which is designed to facilitate discussions on health-related topics in Hausa, Hindi, and Swahili. In another useful approach,
Brugnone et al. (2024)
leverage the wisdom of the crowd and unsupervised learning strategies to identify subpopulations based on value-laden statements extracted from Hausa narratives related to maternal and child healthcare. Responding to the need for on-demand translation,
Watt et al. (2023)
present an approach for deploying MT on the edge, thereby enabling an embedded system for English–Hausa translation.

For performance improvement,
Hatami et al. (2024)
utilise a multimodal strategy integrating visual and textual information for translation across language pairs consisting of English, Hindi, Malayalam, Bengali, and Hausa.

Robertson and Díaz (2022)
propose strategies centred around transparency to reduce translation error for chat agents in LRLs.

Maryann et al. (2022)
and
Usip et al. (2023)
develop MT systems for English–Igbo using both rule-based and corpus-based approaches.
To lessen the impact of harmful English-Igbo translation errors emanating from non-compositional multiword expressions and polysemy,
Ohuoba et al. (2024)
present diagnostic tests involving high-stake domains.

Adegbola et al. (2011)
explore a useful strategy for localising the MS Vista operating system by translating relevant English lexicons into Yorùbá terminologies.

Safiriyu et al. (2015)
develop a rule-based system to address tone changes in Yorùbá verbs and
Fagbolu et al. (2016)
design an accessible MT system for English–Yorùbá translation via mobile and web platforms.

Eludiora and Odejobi (2016)
develop an English–Yorùbá translator and
Sunday et al. (2020)
investigate a multi-layer hybrid approach that combines data-driven and rule-based methods.
Finally,
Timothy et al. (2024)
focus on mitigating challenges such as vanishing gradients, translation accuracy, and computational efficiency in English–Yorùbá MT systems.
Although MT is one of the most widely utilised NLP applications, its performance on LRL pairs remains suboptimal compared to that of HRL
(
Robertson and Díaz, 2022
)

Future research on MT should emphasise the development of more accessible richer parallel data to train models that can effectively capture complex linguistic patterns, manage long-range dependencies, accommodate domain-specific terminology, and preserve cultural nuances for NaijaNLP.

4.2.
Challenges and Future Prospects for NaijaNLP

In this section, we discuss the challenges hindering NaijaNLP from fully leveraging the state-of-the-art NLP advances and recommend mitigation strategies.

4.2.1.
Limited Foundational Frameworks and Data Processing Challenges

Computational grammar and knowledge representation are foundational to the development of digital language resources because the offer structured frameworks for language processing
(
Crysmann, 2012
)
.
The linguistic particularities and diversity of Nigerian languages present significant challenges for standard NLP techniques because these languages feature unique orthographic systems, pronunciation patterns, and syntactic structures, including the use of diacritics and tonal variations.
Additionally, complex morphological structures, such as rich affixation and non-concatenative morphology, further complicate processing. The frequent omission or misapplication of diacritics, as well as issues like mis-syllabification and elision, emphasise the need for specialised linguistic resources tailored to Nigerian Languages. However, studies in these areas remain sparse within NaijaNLP
(
Abdoulaye, 1992
;
Culy, 1996
;
Berthold, 2009
;
Ann, 2002
;
Blasi et al., 2017
;
Eze, 1997
;
Aina and Táíwò, 2021
;
Nkechi and Usip, 2022
;
Aina and Akinwale, 2023
)
.

4.2.2.
Mitigation Strategies and Future Prospects

Noting the aforementioned limitations, this section discusses strategies to mitigate challenges facing NaijaNLP and explores future prospects for enhancing the NLP tools.

1. Repurposing LLMs to Suit LRLs

Pretrained models act as crucial starting points for fine-tuning on tasks such as text classification, machine translation, question answering, text generation, and summarisation. Together, embeddings and pretrained models underpin both language understanding and generation. However, base embeddings and language generation models have HRL-centric training data and evaluation protocols, which led researchers to resort to transfer learning and task-adaptive fine-tuning strategies
(
Gururangan et al., 2020
;
Muller et al., 2020
;
Alabi et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Oladipo et al., 2024
;
Abdou Mohamed et al., 2024a
)

Moreover, benchmark studies often overlook the complexities inherent in NaijaNLP tasks
(
Adewumi, 2022a
;
Srivastava et al., 2022
;
Dossou et al., 2022
;
Chowdhery et al., 2023
;
Ojo et al., 2023
;
Liu and Yin, 2023
;
Lawal et al., 2024
;
Orok et al., 2024
;
Remy et al., 2024
)
.
In response to this limitation,
Yousuf et al. (2024)
propose a contrastive learning strategy to better align embeddings by optimising semantic distances between similar and dissimilar sentences.
To address out-of-distribution scenarios,
Jiang et al. (2023)
propose a non-parametric approach that combines compression algorithms with k-nearest-neighbour classifiers, using compressor-based distance metrics
(
Fadaee et al., 2017
)
.
To alleviate for resource limitation,
Ogueji et al. (2021)
develop a multilingual language model (AfriBerta) trained on 11 low-resource languages. Models such as AfriBERTa demonstrate that small but high quality data approach based on similar languages has the potential of superior performance compared to joint training on large datasets with high-resource languages
(
Conneau, 2019
;
Ogueji et al., 2021

.
Despite such efforts, studies specifically addressing embeddings for low-resource Nigerian languages remain limited
(

;

;

)

5.
Conclusion

Low-resource NLP continues to confront enduring challenges that require targeted, and innovative strategies. This study offered a comprehensive review of the state of NLP in Nigeria, focusing on its three major languages - Hausa, Igbo, and Yorùbá (collectively termed NaijaNLP). By examining the unique linguistic characteristics and resource constraints of these languages, our review provides actionable recommendations to advance and sustain NaijaNLP within the broader realm of low-resource NLP (LR-NLP) research.
Despite the numerical predominance of LRLs, languages within NaijaNLP are often treated as a homogeneous group. We surmise that this practice obscures significant variations in resource availability and quality and may impede targeted technological development. To that effect, we argue for a more nuanced, language-specific approach to address the unique challenges faced by individual languages. The prevalent reliance on HRL-based models further highlights the need to invest in bespoke, indigenous models.
Essentially, our investigation spans available linguistic resources, language particularities, computational tools, and collaborative initiatives, revealing significant progress alongside challenges. These challenges underscore the need for greater investment in high-quality data, robust computational tools, and enhanced community collaboration to fully harness the potential of LRLs in NLP applications. To address these challenges, we recommend:

①

Emphasising the critical role of formal grammar and linguistic analysis in developing reusable linguistic resources.

②

Prioritising the collection and annotation of larger, more diverse datasets that capture language particularities such as morphological and dialectal variations.

③

Investing in the creation of models tailored to the unique needs of each language, including the development of large language models built from scratch using indigenous data, rather than relying solely on models repurposed from high-resource languages.

④

Fostering collaborative research efforts and community engagement to ensure the sustained development of NLP resources for LRLs.

In summary, advancing NaijaNLP demands dedicated efforts to overcome linguistic, resource, and sociopolitical challenges. By prioritising language-specific approaches and fostering robust collaborative research, we can pave the way for more inclusive and effective NLP solutions for low-resource languages.
