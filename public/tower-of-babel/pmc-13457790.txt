Comparison of Palliative Care Access Between Patients With Liver Cirrhosis and Hepatocellular Carcinoma: Nationwide Population-Based Study
Authors: Jung Sil Jang, Si Nae Oh, Haeyong Pak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn August 2017, Republic of Korea (South Korea) expanded its national hospice and palliative care program to cover several serious non-cancer illnesses, including liver cirrhosis (LC). We aimed to examine the access of palliative care services among adults who died of LC compared with those who died of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsWe conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study using National Health Insurance Service data, including adults who died of LC or HCC between 2018 and 2022. Associations between cause of death (LC vs. HCC) and advance care planning (ACP) completion and receipt of hospice and palliative care services were estimated using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThe final analytic cohort comprised 44,787 patients died from LC (71% women; mean age, 66 years) and 58,685 patients died from HCC (67% women; mean age, 70 years). Patients with LC had significantly lower rates of ACP completion (23.1 vs. 31.2%; adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.765; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.723–0.809; P < 0.001) and hospice and palliative care service utilization (3.7% vs. 9.1%; aOR, 0.558; 95% CI, 0.499–0.623; P < 0.001) compared to those with HCC.ConclusionDespite nationwide coverage of hospice and palliative care services, LC decedents remained less likely than HCC decedents to complete ACP or receive hospice and palliative-care services in Korea.

INTRODUCTION

Liver cirrhosis (LC) is a life-limiting chronic illness with a median survival ranging from 2 years in decompensated disease to 12 years in compensated disease.1 Even when compensated, patients experience substantial physical and psychological symptom burdens, and their quality of life in advanced disease mirrors that of patients with advanced cancer.2

Palliative care aims to improve the quality of life of patients with cancer and advanced non-cancer chronic illnesses through comprehensive symptom management, functional support, advance care planning (ACP) and goals-of-care discussions, prognostication, and caregiver support.3 In populations without cirrhosis, palliative care has been shown to enhance patient quality of life, reduce the aggressiveness of end-of-life interventions, lower healthcare costs, improve communication among clinicians, and yield better caregiver outcomes.4

Over the past decade, a growing body of evidence has shown that early, proactive palliative care benefits patients with liver disease. A prospective study introducing multidisciplinary early palliative care during liver transplant evaluation demonstrated improved symptom burden and reduced depressive symptoms.5 In surgical intensive care unit (ICU), integration of palliative care as a structured family-support program—prognosis and preference review plus an interdisciplinary meeting within 72 hours—prompted earlier goals-of-care discussions, higher do-not-resuscitate use, and a three-day shorter ICU stay without increasing short-term mortality.6 Among ambulatory patients with LC, specialized palliative care nursing delivering psychosocial support, patient and caregiver education, and interdisciplinary care coordination improved patient and caregiver satisfaction and quality of life.7

Current American Gastroenterological Association guidelines recommend palliative care throughout the LC trajectory, regardless of transplant eligibility.8 Yet among patients with LC, ACP uptake remains low, symptom management is suboptimal, and referrals to specialty palliative care or hospice services are infrequent and late.9 In Republic of Korea (South Korea), the Act on Hospice and Palliative Care and Decisions on Life-Sustaining Treatment for Patients at the End of Life was amended in August 2017 to encompass non-cancer terminal conditions, including LC alongside chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and acquired immune deficiency syndrome.1011 How these policies have affected real-world practice for LC, especially in comparison with established cancer care pathways, remains unclear.

The objective of this study was to examine the access of ACP or hospice and palliative care services among adults who died of LC compared with those who died of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) using the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) databases.

METHODS

Data sources

This study utilized NHIS databases from 2018 to 2022. The NHIS is the single, non-profit health insurer in Korea, providing universal health coverage to the entire population. It maintains a comprehensive computerized database containing healthcare-related information, including inpatient and outpatient reimbursement claims, drug prescriptions, diagnoses, procedures, and treatments for each patient. Upon request, the NHIS provides researchers with a customized database containing de-identified information relevant to the study population.12 The validity of the NHIS database has been documented in previous study.13

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from NHIS database in Korea. The study population comprised adults who died from LC (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] codes: K74, K70.3, K70.2) and HCC (ICD-10 code: C22.0) between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2022. Patients with both LC and HCC were classified into the HCC decedent group. Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 19 years and those with incomplete data.

Covariates

We obtained the following demographic and clinical characteristic of the participants. Demographic factors included gender, age, insurance premium quintiles, and residential areas (metropolitan, urban, or rural). Clinical characteristic included the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and prior healthcare utilization. The CCI, a validated predictive tool for mortality based on comorbid conditions, was categorized into lower (1–2) and higher (≥ 3) scores. Prior healthcare utilization included emergency department visits (yes or no) and hospitalizations (yes or no) during the 12-month period preceding the final six months of life.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were the ACP completion and the receipt of hospice and palliative care services. ACP completion was defined as the documentation of advanced directives identified using claim codes for planning, managing, or consulting on decisions regarding the withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment, at any time prior to death (Supplementary Table 1). Receipt of hospice and palliative care services was identified as any utilization of inpatient, consultative, or home-based palliative care services at any time before death (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to measure participants characteristics and rate of ACP completion and hospice and palliative care services receipt. Between-group differences were assessed using t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. The association between cause of death (LC vs. HCC) and ACP completion or hospice and palliative care services receipt was estimated using multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for covariates. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National Health Insurance Ilsan Hospital, with a waiver for informed consent due to the anonymized nature of the NHIS database (IRB No. NHIMC 2023-02-011). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

A total of 44,787 patients who died of LC and 58,665 patients who died of HCC were identified and included in the final analytic cohort (Fig. 1). The mean (standard deviation [SD]) age of the LC and HCC groups were 70.4 (SD, 12.9) and 66.5 (SD, 14.2) years, respectively. Females accounted for 70.7% of the LC group and 66.6% of the HCC group (Table 1).

Annual trends in ACP and hospice and palliative care services receipt

Fig. 2 shows the annual trends in ACP completion and receipt of hospice and palliative care services among patients who died of LC and HCC in Korea between 2018 and 2022. Overall, the proportion of patients who completed ACP was higher in the HCC group (39.9%) compared to the cirrhosis group (25.0%). Yearly data consistently demonstrated this disparity, beginning in 2018 with 20.5% in the HCC group versus 15.3% in the cirrhosis group. Both groups reached peak registration rates in 2020, at 37.1% for HCC patients and 26.2% for cirrhosis patients. Although ACP rates slightly decreased after 2020, HCC patients consistently maintained higher rates compared to LC patients.

In terms of hospice and palliative care services utilization, patients with HCC again showed consistently higher engagement (34.7%) compared to those with LC (15.6%). Annual analysis revealed that in 2018, the utilization rate was higher among HCC patients (6.1%) compared to LC patients (2.0%). The highest hospice and palliative care usage were recorded in 2019 for HCC patients (10.6%), while LC patients peaked at 3.6% in the same year. Despite fluctuations over subsequent years, utilization remained significantly higher among HCC patients throughout the entire study period.

Association between cause of death (LC vs. HCC) and ACP completion or hospice and palliative care services receipt

Death from LC was significantly associated with a lower ACP completion compared to death from HCC (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.765; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.723–0.809; P < 0.001). Similarly, the utilization of hospice and palliative care services was significantly lower among patients who died of LC compared to those of HCC (aOR, 0.558; 95% CI, 0.499–0.623; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide, population-based cohort study of 106,496 decedents from LC or HCC, we observed substantial disparities in ACP completion and receipt of hospice and palliative care services. Compared with those who died from HCC, individuals who died from LC had significantly lower rates of ACP completion and hospice and palliative care utilization.

Our findings indicating a tendency toward lower palliative care provision align with previous studies in non-malignant chronic conditions compared to malignant conditions.14151617 This pattern is also evident in liver disease: in a United States nationwide retrospective cohort study with hospitalized patients with LC between 2006 and 2012, referrals increased from 0.97% to 7.1% but remained below the increase among cancer patients during the same period (2.9% to 11.9%).18 In a recent English national cohort of 29,669 patients, those with HCC were more likely than those with chronic liver disease without HCC to receive inpatient specialist palliative care (47.8% vs. 18.3%; aOR, 3.74; 95% CI, 3.52–3.97) and were more likely to die in hospice (16.8% vs. 2.4%; aOR, 8.23; 95% CI, 7.33–7.24).19 A cross-sectional survey of members from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases have reported that patients with LC often face significant barriers to accessing palliative care and ACP discussions, leading to infrequent and late referrals.20 Notably, our cohort showed even lower rates of palliative care utilization (HCC, 17.6%; LC, 3.7%) than those reported in prior international studies.1920

Several underlying factors likely contribute to these observed disparities. Firstly, clinician perceptions regarding the prognosis and disease trajectory significantly influence ACP and referral patterns to palliative care.21 Healthcare providers often perceive cancer prognoses as more predictable and rapidly progressive, prompting ACP and timely referrals to palliative care services.22 In contrast, LC is frequently viewed as a slowly progressing chronic disease characterized by intermittent acute episodes, complicating prognosis estimation and delaying ACP and palliative care referrals.23 Additionally, clinicians may lack specific training or guidelines for chronic non-malignant diseases, contributing to inconsistent and late-stage referrals.20 Enhancing clinician education through standardized guidelines and training programs specifically addressing the prognostic ambiguity and chronic disease management of LC could mitigate these barriers.22

Secondly, patient and family perceptions significantly impact ACP and palliative care utilization.16 ACP and Palliative care is often mistakenly associated exclusively with cancer or perceived as indicative of imminent death, particularly within chronic disease populations.24 Patients and families with LC might not recognize the applicability or potential benefits of early ACP and palliative interventions.14 Addressing these misconceptions through public education campaigns and clear communication by healthcare professionals regarding the broad applicability and objectives of palliative care could improve patient acceptance and engagement.21

Thirdly, socioeconomic disparities further complicate access to ACP and palliative care services, particularly evident among LC patients.16 Our findings demonstrated that LC patients were disproportionately represented in lower socioeconomic categories, such as Medicaid recipients, suggesting financial barriers or limited healthcare access.25 Patients in lower socioeconomic groups frequently encounter challenges including limited access to specialty healthcare providers, lack of transportation, and reduced health literacy, all of which impede early engagement with palliative services.24 Targeted policy interventions aimed at reducing economic and logistical barriers, such as improved reimbursement policies for palliative care consultations and enhanced accessibility in underserved regions, are crucial.16

Another critical issue is the limited availability of specialized palliative care tailored to chronic non-cancer diseases.26 While cancer-focused palliative care services have expanded significantly, similar services for chronic conditions including LC remain relatively underdeveloped.23 Investing in specialized training programs and expanding dedicated palliative care teams with expertise in managing chronic liver conditions can help bridge this gap.22 Healthcare systems should prioritize developing chronic disease-specific palliative care models, facilitating better physical and psychosocial symptom management, goals of care discussions, and ACP for LC patients.2728 Despite policy expansions aimed at bridging these gaps, significant barriers to effective implementation remain.29 In Korea, accessibility and availability of hospice and palliative care services for non-cancer patients remain markedly limited. Currently, out of 119 specialized hospice and palliative care institutions nationwide, only 39 provide consultative or home-based hospice services for non-cancer patients, highlighting substantial infrastructural deficiencies and limited professional capacity dedicated to non-malignant diseases.11

Strengths of this study include its nationwide population-based design, ensuring generalizability across diverse clinical settings.30 Leveraging the comprehensive NHIS database allowed detailed assessment of real-world practice patterns, reflecting actual clinical care beyond controlled research environments.31

This study also has several limitations. First, because we relied on administrative claims data, we lacked detailed clinical information—such as the etiologies of LC, Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score, Child-Pugh class, ascites or encephalopathy severity, and patient-reported symptom burden—limiting our ability to perform stratified analyses by etiology or adjust for disease severity and symptomatology. Second, unmeasured confounders may have influenced both referral and uptake. In our cohort, patients who died of LC were younger and more socioeconomically disadvantaged than those who died of HCC, leaving residual confounding despite adjustment. Third, outcomes were identified using billing codes, misclassification is possible. Finally, the study period (2018–2022) overlaps with the COVID-19 pandemic, which likely disrupted service availability and care patterns in ways not fully captured by claims.

In conclusion, we found significant disparities in ACP documentation and hospice and palliative care utilization between LC and HCC decedents. Multifaceted strategies—spanning clinician education, patient and family engagement, reduction of socioeconomic barriers, and expansion of specialized services—are urgently needed to ensure equitable, timely access and to improve quality of life and outcomes for people with advanced liver disease.
