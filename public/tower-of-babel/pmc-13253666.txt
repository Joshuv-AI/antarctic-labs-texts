Advances in ecotoxicological research using planarians: a comprehensive review
Authors: Shouhui Pan, Chongtao Guo, Hai Zhang, Hao Cen, Quan Zhang, Fei Dai, Congyong Li, Qing Yang, Zhouqiong Zhang, Wang Yin, Renju Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlanarians, distinguished by their extraordinary regenerative capacity and ecological role as benthic predators, have emerged as a transformative model in ecotoxicology. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of research on the effects of diverse classes of pollutants, including metals, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, personal care products, nanomaterials, and a dedicated category of “other pollutants,” namely biotoxins and physical stressors, on these organisms. We introduce a novel conceptual framework of “regenerative toxicology,” which focuses on how contaminants impair an organism’s innate self-repair mechanisms. Our analysis reveals a convergent toxicity pattern: despite differing initial targets, most pollutants induced oxidative stress, which triggered cellular responses, including apoptosis, ultimately disrupting neoblast function and leading to failures in regeneration, behavior, and reproduction. A key advancement of this study is its emphasis on ecological realism and complex exposure scenarios. We critically evaluate the sensitivity of planarians to real-world environmental samples and highlight the significant implications of combined toxicity, such as the “Trojan horse” effect, in which microplastics increase the bioavailability of co-pollutants such as metals. This review further integrates methodological innovations, from multi-endpoint behavioral assays to multi-omics and AI-driven predictive modelling, while underscoring the urgent need for standardization in culture and testing methods. By bridging molecular mechanisms with individual and potential population-level outcomes, the planarian model offers a unique, cost-effective platform for assessing the sublethal and long-term hazards of environmental pollution. Introduction

Ecosystems are crucial for sustaining global biodiversity and supporting human development. However, they face severe threats from diverse chemical pollutants, such as heavy metals, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, personal care products (PPCPs), nanomaterials, and microplastics (Grimm et al. 2008). Originating from industrial, agricultural, and domestic sources, these contaminants persist in the environment and pose toxicity risks to organisms. Moreover, they can bioaccumulate along the food chain, ultimately threatening ecosystem integrity and public health (Saxena 2025). Consequently, there is an urgent need in environmental science and toxicology to develop sensitive and reliable biological monitoring models and toxicity assessment systems for early warnings and in-depth analyses of the ecotoxicological effects of pollutants.

Planarians, representatives of the phylum Platyhelminthes, are renowned for their distinctive biological features and have garnered increasing attention in ecotoxicological studies because of their widespread distribution, sensitivity to environmental changes, and remarkable regenerative capabilities, which are driven by adult stem cells (Sánchez Alvarado 2006; Sluys and Riutort 2018; Vila-Farré and Rink 2018). This makes them valuable tools for investigating tissue repair disorders, developmental toxicity, and pollutant-induced neurotoxicity. Research in this area has expanded rapidly, encompassing various pollutants, refining toxicity assessments, and innovating new methodologies. However, existing studies have predominantly focused on model species in freshwater habitats, such as Schmidtea mediterranea, Girardia tigrina, and Dugesia japonica. Limited exploration has been conducted on planarians and flatworms inhabiting ecosystems such as soil, caves, and litter, which have lost their regenerative abilities (Cseh 2017; Prasniski and Leal-Zanchet 2009; Zuo et al. 2025). Understanding the response of these non-aquatic, non-regenerative species to pollutants may differ significantly from that of freshwater planarians, offering potential as biological indicators in specific environments.

Existing reviews have primarily focused on specific aspects of this topic. For example, Wu and Li (Wu and Li 2018) provided a comprehensive summary of the advantages of planarian models and their applications in the study of traditional pollutants. Poirier et al. (Poirier et al. 2019) discussed the potential of planarians as emerging toxicological research models. Ireland and Collins (Ireland and Collins 2022) concentrated on neurotoxicology and high-throughput behavioral screening. Xie et al. (Xie et al. 2025) attempted to link the toxicity mechanism of pollutants to planarians using the adverse outcome pathway framework (AOP). While these works have their individual emphases, some neglect attention to emerging and “atypical” pollutants (e.g., biological toxins, physical factors, salt stress, and psychotropic drugs). Others lack a standardized analytical framework encompassing "pollutant exposure—molecular events—cellular responses—individual phenotypes—ecological implications."

This review addresses the gaps in the aforementioned studies by systematically analyzing the advances in planarian ecotoxicology. It uniquely categorizes “other types of pollutants” (e.g., biopesticides, biological toxins, chemical mutagens, physical factors, salts, and neuroactive substances) as standalone topics for the first time. All pollutant categories were examined within the unified logical framework of “acute/chronic toxicity–behavioral/physiological effects–regenerative/developmental toxicity–molecular/cellular mechanisms,” providing comprehensive and comparable insights. This study goes beyond describing phenomena to explore the specific molecular events triggered by various pollutants, illustrating their convergence at the central themes of “oxidative stress” and “stem cell function inhibition.” This convergence leads to aberrant individual behavior and regeneration failure, outlining a toxic pattern characterized by “common pathways and characteristic mechanisms.” Through a meticulous evaluation of extensive literature, this review critiques the current research limitations in addressing complex pollution, prolonged exposure, environmental relevance, and method standardization. It also outlines future development priorities, including multi-omics integration, chronic multigenerational effects, microbiome interactions, and artificial intelligence-supported predictive modelling.

The review concludes that the planarian model, known for its robust regenerative capabilities and comprehensive integrated responses, has expanded the scope of toxicological research to explore the concept of “impairment of an organism’s self-repair capacity,” introducing the novel framework of “regenerative toxicology.” This model bridges the gap between conventional acute toxicity assessments and intricate in vivo studies, offering a unique approach to elucidate the potential effects of contaminants on ecosystem health and sustainability. By delineating a clear causal pathway of “stem cell disruption → aberrant organ regrowth → impaired individual function,” the planarian model serves as an invaluable tool. This review aims to present a structured overview of this evolving research domain and establish a robust foundation for advancing the broader application of planarian models in environmental risk assessment and mitigation.

The subsequent sections of this article elaborate on the unique advantages of planarians over traditional models, such as zebrafish, water fleas, and nematodes, as well as the application cases of planarians. A systematic review was then conducted on the effects and mechanisms of metal ions, pesticides, PPCPs, particulate matter, surfactants, endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), industrial chemicals and organic pollutants (ICOPs), and other types of pollutants on planarians by category. Next, key topics, including the comparison of the toxicity characteristics of various pollutants, methodological innovations, the application of omics technology, and the standardization of culture conditions, are discussed. Finally, this article summarizes the progress and challenges of current methodologies, addresses the limitations of existing research, and outlines future research directions to enhance the broader utilization of planarian models in environmental risk assessment and pollution management. Literature retrieval methods and selection criteria

A systematic approach was employed to identify, screen, and analyze the relevant literature to comprehensively reflect the research progress in planarian ecotoxicology. The scope and selection criteria of the reviewed research papers were clearly defined using the following methods.

Search method

Peer-reviewed literature published until January 2026 was gathered by searching prominent scientific databases, including the Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, and PubMed. The search approach integrated keywords associated with model organisms (e.g., “planarian,” “Dugesia,” and “freshwater flatworm”) and terms linked to toxicity and pollutants (e.g., “toxicity,” “ecotoxicity,” “pollutant,” and “heavy metal”). The search strategy involved a comprehensive query encompassing toxicity and pollutant-related terms (e.g., “toxic,” “ecotoxicity,” “pollutant,” “heavy metal,” “pesticide,” “nanomaterials,” “emerging pollutants”) in conjunction with terms such as “Schmidtea,” “Girardia,” and “freshwater flatworm”. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search results.

Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Literature studies meeting the following criteria were included: (1) use of planarian species (primarily freshwater but also terrestrial/marine species) as test subjects; (2) exposure to chemical pollutants (excluding natural stressors); (3) presentation of quantitative toxicology endpoints (e.g., medium lethal concentration (LC50), behavior, biochemistry, gene expression, regeneration, reproduction, and DNA damage); and (4) publication in English. Opinion pieces and studies without experimental data were excluded; however, their references were screened for relevant original research.

Data extraction and comprehensive analysis

Essential data were extracted into standardized spreadsheets, including pollutant categories, types, planarian species, acute toxicity (LC50), sub-individual biomarkers (cellular and molecular levels), individual-level endpoint indicators (physiological and behavioral levels), toxicological pathways, most sensitive endpoint indicators, evidence strength, and references. Quantitative data, particularly LC50 values, were organized for comparison (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). The comprehensive analysis focuses on identifying patterns (e.g., the most sensitive endpoint for a specific pollutant type), analyzing differences (species or methodological variations), and integrating biological findings to develop a mechanistic narrative. Unique advantages of planarian models in ecotoxicology research

Core advantage: powerful global and precise regeneration capability

Compared with other model organisms, planarians offer distinct advantages in toxicological research because of their high regenerative capacity (Ireland et al. 2020). Unlike zebrafish (Li et al. 2023; Braga et al. 2024) and water fleas (Kim et al. 2015, 2026), whose repair processes are limited, planarians can regenerate complex organs, such as the brain and eyes, from completely severed segments. This unique ability allows planarians to effectively demonstrate how pollutants can interfere with fundamental biological processes, such as cell fate determination and tissue pattern reconstruction. In contrast, models involving nematodes (Hughes and Hessel 2024) and rotifers (Moreira et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020) lack the capacity for macroscopic organ regeneration, limiting their research scope. The field of “regenerative toxicology,” enabled by the planarian model, investigates how environmental stress impacts the self-repair capabilities of organisms, which is essential for evaluating the ecological implications of the long-term and subtle effects of pollutants.

Unique depth and integration of toxicity endpoint metrics

Owing to the robust foundation in regenerative biology, the assessment of toxicity to planarians has established a comprehensive continuum spanning from molecular to ecological levels. Specifically, at the molecular/cellular level, the effects of pollutants on neoblasts can be directly observed through proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation-specific gene expression, and protein function. At the tissue/organ level, observable pathological changes or morphological abnormalities in organs, such as the brain and intestines, during regeneration provide insights. Furthermore, at the individual/behavioral level, the toxic effects on planarians include regeneration failure, morphological abnormalities, and disruptions in feeding and movement behaviors. This sequential progression of “stem cell disruption → abnormal organ regeneration → individual functional impairment” offers a coherent and explicit pathway for elucidating the underlying mechanisms. In contrast, the endpoints in many conventional aquatic models (e.g., Artemia spp. (Nunes et al. 2006; Serrão and Marques-Santos 2023) and water fleas) are relatively conspicuous and disparate, leading to weaker mechanistic correlations.

The planarian model achieves an excellent balance between the “availability of genetic manipulation tools” and “ecological representativeness”

In the spectrum of model organisms, there are “laboratory-type” models such as Caenorhabditis elegans (Queirós et al. 2019), which have robust genetic tools but limited ecological relevance, and “environmental indicator” organisms such as Chironomidae larvae (Corbi et al. 2019; Brovini et al. 2023) and water fleas, which exhibit strong ecological relevance but lack genetic tools. Planarians constitute a promising middle ground, with increasingly refined genetic manipulation tools, including genome editing, RNA interference (RNAi), and Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) technologies. Although planarians do not reach the level of nematodes and zebrafish as gold-standard systems, they offer adequate support for targeted gene-function studies, surpassing most ecological indicator organisms. This capability allows for mechanistic investigations, rather than mere observations. Ecologically, planarians play a crucial role as benthic predators/scavengers at the sediment‒water interface, offering direct insights into the assessment of sediment pollutants and their movement in aquatic food chains. This characteristic compensates for the limitations of nematodes (soil organisms) and zebrafish (inhabiting water layers) in representing specific habitats.

The universal advantages of ethics and cost

Planarians, being invertebrates, offer the advantages of minimal cultivation expenses, modest spatial demands, and exemptions from ethical oversight (Xie et al. 2025). These attributes facilitate the execution of extensive and enduring exposure investigations, particularly those investigating the enduring effects of dilute pollutants. Consequently, various research establishments can readily implement and sustain this model, thereby enhancing research accessibility and standardization. However, ethical limitations and breeding expenses associated with zebrafish have somewhat constrained their widespread utilization (Li et al. 2023).

In conclusion, the primary advantage of planarian models in ecotoxicology research stems from their innovative integration of the fundamental biological process of “stem cell-mediated regeneration” with the practical requirements of ecotoxicology. The planarian model does not aim to supplant the established role of zebrafish in vertebrate physiological toxicology or water fleas in acute toxicity screening. Instead, it serves to address a crucial gap by focusing on evaluating the prolonged effects of pollutants on the self-repair and tissue homeostasis capabilities of organisms, provided that specific genetic tools and ecological representativeness are available. Therefore, in the assessment of ecological risks related to developmental toxicity, tissue damage repair, and sediment interface contamination, planarians provide a distinct perspective, elucidate clear mechanisms, and offer a cost-effective platform for research. They represent a unique research model that combines depth and practicality in the contemporary ecotoxicology toolkit. This approach highlights the practical utility of planarians as rapid, sensitive, and comprehensive biological indicators. Metal ions

Metal ions present a significant threat to planarians, resulting in acute mortality and chronic functional impairments, and constitute a prevalent category of pollutants in ecotoxicology studies. The hierarchy of their toxicity levels generally follows the order of Hg2+ > Cd2+ > Cu2+ > Cr3+ > Pb2+ > Zn2+ > Fe3+ > Al3+, subject to variations influenced by the metal’s chemical form (ionic state vs. nanoscale state), species susceptibility, and environmental conditions (e.g., water hardness). The principal toxicity mechanisms involve the induction of oxidative stress and DNA damage, disruption of essential metal homeostasis, suppression of crucial enzyme activities (e.g., acetylcholinesterase (AChE)), and interference with the stem cell signalling network, culminating in aberrant behavior, aplastic disorders, and individual death.

Acute and chronic toxicity

The acute toxicity of Cr⁶⁺ to G. tigrina varies considerably among studies (Preza and Smith 2001; Ribeiro and Umbuzeiro 2014). The 48-h LC50 of Hg2+ for D. japonica is 0.48 mg/L, whereas that of Pb2+ is 182.02 mg/L (Liu et al. 2021), indicating a nearly 400-fold difference in metal ion specificity. Variability in the LC50 values of a single metal, such as Cd2+ (ranging from 1.25 mg/L to 79.1 μM ≈ 14.5 mg/L), across different studies (Plusquin et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012a) primarily stems from variations in the water matrix composition, notably hardness and pH. In environments with elevated hardness levels, Ca2+ and Mg2+ compete with Cd2+ for binding sites in biological systems, substantially mitigating its toxicity. Prolonged exposure to sublethal concentrations of toxic metals, such as Fe3+ and Cd2+, does not result in mortality but frequently triggers growth impediments, diminished food consumption, and continual upregulation of metallothionein (MT) (Plusquin et al. 2012; Ding et al. 2019).

Behavioural and physiological effects

Planarians often exhibit dose-dependent reductions in movement speed, feeding suppression, and disrupted phototaxis following exposure to metal ions. Even at sublethal levels, Hg2+ and Cd2+ rapidly impair locomotor function. These metal ions can indirectly hinder AChE activity, causing the accumulation of acetylcholine and eliciting a multifaceted behavioral pattern characterized by initial excitement, followed by prolonged inhibition (Plusquin et al. 2012). This mechanism contrasts with the direct and potent AChE inhibition induced by organophosphorus pesticides, indicating that the neurotoxic effects of heavy metals may involve diverse indirect pathways.

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Planarians exhibit high sensitivity to heavy metals during the regeneration phase. For example, Cd2+ and Cr3+ notably impede head regrowth and induce abnormalities in eyespots (Ma et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2021). Furthermore, heavy metals disrupt neoblast activity (Knakievicz and Ferreira 2008; Kovačević et al. 2009). Cd2+ can induce tumorigenesis and affect stem cell proliferation and differentiation, leading to delayed regeneration and increased mortality (Voura et al. 2017). Cu2+ and Zn2+ exposure causes oxidative stress and DNA damage, significantly inhibiting head regeneration and motility, with regenerating planarians being more sensitive than adult individuals (Wu and Li 2017; Majid et al. 2022). With respect to reproduction, metal exposure frequently leads to reduced fertility (decreased larval numbers), potentially because of energy diversion toward detoxification and stress responses (Schaly and Braguini 2024b). Toxicity mechanisms often involve oxidative stress, apoptosis, stem cell dysfunction, and neurodevelopmental disruption (Voura et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2020; Majid et al. 2022).

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

The primary toxicity mechanism of heavy metals involves the induction of oxidative stress through the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). While physiological ROS levels play regulatory roles in planarian regeneration, heavy metal ions typically stimulate ROS production beyond homeostatic thresholds and disrupt the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), glutathione transferase (GST), and glutathione/glutathione disulfide (GSH/GSSG) equilibrium, resulting in lipid peroxidation (increased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels) (Guecheva et al. 2003; Plusquin et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012a; Zhang et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2023b; Schaly and Braguini 2024b; Li et al. 2025). DNA damage is another crucial aspect of heavy metal toxicity. Comet experiments have verified that Cu2+ can trigger DNA strand breakage (Guecheva et al. 2001). Ce3+ can cause oxidative DNA damage (elevated 8-OHdG levels) and an imbalance between apoptosis and proliferation by upregulating the expression of pro-apoptotic genes (e.g., p53) and downregulating the expression of stem cell markers (e.g., piwiA), consequently depleting the reservoir of new bud cells (Xie et al. 2023b).

Summary and outlook

Metal ions are a significant pollutant category in planarian ecotoxicological research. However, the current research has several shortcomings: (1) Environmental relevance is lacking, as most LC50 data surpass the measured environmental concentration (MEC), with limited exploration of sublethal chronic effects at MEC levels. (2) Mixed toxicity gaps exist, with little understanding of metal‒metal (e.g., Cd‒Cu) or metal‒organic compound interactions in natural environments. (3) Mechanistic understanding is limited, with insufficient analysis of metal absorption, distribution, morphological changes, and detoxification pathways in planarians, including the role of plant-chelating peptides. (4) Data comparability concerns arise from the absence of standardized planarian strains, breeding conditions, and water quality, which hinders the direct integration and comparison of research findings.

Pesticides

Pesticides represent a crucial aspect of planarian ecotoxicology and include organophosphorus compounds (e.g., chlorpyrifos and methyl parathion) (Amaya-Chávez et al. 2009; Ireland et al. 2022; Lares et al. 2023; Saraiva et al. 2024), neonicotinoids (e.g., imidacloprid and thiamethoxam) (Barbosa et al. 2022; Gravato et al. 2024; De Oliveira et al. 2025), pyrethroids (e.g., cypermethrin) (Espinoza-Navarro et al. 2016), herbicides (e.g., glyphosate and its metabolites AMPA, norflurazon, and paraquat) (Horvat et al. 2005; Zhang et al. 2020; Saraiva et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023; Fuselier et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2023; Collins et al. 2024), fungicides (e.g., difenoconazole and cyproconazole) (Saraiva et al. 2018; Dornelas et al. 2022), and biopesticides. The mechanisms through which pesticides affect planarians primarily involve AChE inhibition, oxidative stress induction, neurotransmitter interference, and stem cell proliferation inhibition. Notably, the toxicity of commercial pesticide formulations is often markedly greater than that of their active ingredients alone, with adjuvants playing significant roles. Additionally, as nontarget organisms, planarians are affected to some degree by biopesticides, affecting their overall survival. For instance, two commercial biopesticide products, Bac-Control® derived from the Bacillus suyunkinensis Kustagia variant (Btk) and Boveril® from Beauveria bassiana (Bb), have been observed to impede the mobility, feeding, regeneration, and reproductive capabilities of G. tigrina, posing a potential threat to the long-term viability of planarians (Dornelas et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2022).

Acute and chronic toxicity

The 24-h LC50 of chlorpyrifos (CPF) for D. japonica was 961 µM, whereas that for S. mediterranea was 1118 µM, indicating notable interspecific variations (Rejo et al. 2023). Despite both being neonicotinoid insecticides, the LC50 of imidacloprid for G. tigrina (94.29 mg/L) (De Oliveira et al. 2025) significantly exceeds that of thiamethoxam (478.6 mg/L) (Gravato et al. 2024), implying a substantial difference in toxicity due to differences in chemical structure. G. tigrina is more sensitive to chronic sublethal exposure to difenoconazole, with low concentrations (≥ 1.16 mg a.i./L) inhibiting its reproduction (96 h-LC50 = 47.5 mg a.i./L) (Dornelas et al. 2022).

Behavioural and physiological effects

The impact of pesticides on planarian behavior reflects their neurotoxic properties. Difenoconazole at sublethal concentrations (9.28–18.56 mg a.i./L) notably decreases planarian mobility (Dornelas et al. 2022). The commercial herbicide Roundup® induces sublethal behavioral changes at 31.6 μM, resulting in dose-dependent reductions in motor function, feeding efficiency, and regeneration capacity (Córdova López et al. 2019). Fipronil at 6.25 mg/L reduces planarian movement speed (Dos Reis et al. 2022). Organophosphorus and carbamate pesticides impede AChE function, causing acetylcholine buildup in the synaptic cleft and leading to excessive neuromuscular junction excitation.

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Regenerated planarians are more sensitive to pesticides than intact planarians. Aminomethylphosphonic acid (AMPA), a glyphosate metabolite, selectively hinders tail regeneration in G. tigrina, demonstrating targeted tissue toxicity (Zhang et al. 2020). Difenoconazole delays regeneration and disrupts sexual reproduction in G. tigrina, reducing fertility rates by 72% at concentrations well below the LC50, underscoring its potent developmental and reproductive toxicity (Dornelas et al. 2022). Cypermethrin notably affects the reproductive system of D. gonocephala, modifying its seminiferous tubule size and cell structure while increasing its CD133-positive stem cell count (Espinoza-Navarro et al. 2016).

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

Pesticides induce toxicity in planarians primarily through neurotoxicity, metabolic stress, and oxidative damage. Organophosphorus/carbamate pesticides typically exert toxicity by inhibiting AChE activity (Li 2019). CPF can be activated or detoxified by cytochrome P450 (CYP450) and carboxylate esterases (CarE) (Lares et al. 2023). Glyphosate leads to oxidative stress by altering the expression of gpx and hsp70 (Zhang et al. 2020).

Toxicity and environmental correlation analysis

In ecotoxicology research, risk assessment involves integrating the LC50 values of pollutants with actual environmental concentrations. For instance, CPF concentrations typically range from 0.01 to 1 μg/L in surface water (Pereira et al. 2017). With a 72-h LC50 of 622.8 μg a.i./L for G. tigrina (Saraiva et al. 2024), the risk quotient (RQ = MEC/LC50) ranged from 1.6 × 10–5 to 1.6 × 10–3, which was consistently less than 1, indicating a relatively low risk of acute death. However, the sublethal effect concentration of CPF (LOEC = 3.88 μg a.i./L) approached the environmental concentration, resulting in RQ values of 0.003 to 0.26, highlighting the significance of considering sublethal ecological risks. Moreover, glyphosate concentrations in water can reach 10–100 μg/L (Damiani et al. 2023), with an LC50 of 128 mg/L for D. japonica over 96 h (Zhang et al. 2023), yielding RQ values of 7.8 × 10–5 to 7.8 × 10–4. Nevertheless, the toxicity of its commercial formulation, Roundup®, is notably greater and often underestimates the actual risk because of the prevalent presence of the formulation at environmental concentrations.

Analysis of the correlations between the efficacy series and exposure risk

The toxicity ranking based on the LC50 (CPF > difenoconazole > imidacloprid > glyphosate > thiamethoxam) does not directly reflect the actual ecological risk. For example, although thiamethoxam has a lower LC50 (478.6 mg/L) (Gravato et al. 2024) than glyphosate (128 mg/L) (Zhang et al. 2020), the prevalence and detection frequency of neonicotinoid pesticides in the environment exceed those of glyphosate. Their toxicity to nontarget insects, including planarian food sources, is notably potent, potentially indirectly impacting planarian populations through the food chain. Therefore, a comprehensive risk classification should consider the LC50, environmental concentration, usage volume, and ecological impact: CPF (high toxicity × medium concentration) > glyphosate formulations (medium toxicity × high concentration) > imidacloprid (medium toxicity × high detection rate) > difenoconazole (medium toxicity × low concentration) > thiamethoxam (low toxicity × high concentration). This risk categorization, which is based on actual exposure risks, closely aligns with the requirements of ecological risk assessment.

Summary and outlook

In conclusion, despite numerous studies investigating the toxicological effects of various pesticides on planarians, the current research has several limitations. (1) The toxicity of formulations is underestimated, with a predominant focus on individual active ingredients while disregarding the harmful effects of adjuvants in commercial products, resulting in an environmental risk assessment that is lower than the actual risk. (2) The persistence of metabolites is often disregarded, leading to a lack of evaluation of the toxicity of pesticide byproducts. For example, although AMPA is less toxic than glyphosate, it is more environmentally persistent. (3) There is a lack of understanding regarding the effects of combined exposure, with inadequate exploration of the synergistic or antagonistic outcomes of concurrent pesticide exposure (e.g., organophosphorus and neonicotinoid mixtures). (4) The effects of pesticides on the planarian microbiome remain unexplored, despite the significant influence of the intestinal microbiota on pesticide metabolism and toxicity. (5) The reliance on freshwater planarian models in research, while many pesticides are predominantly used in agricultural ecosystems, poses limitations to ecological models. Variations between aquatic and terrestrial environments may result in a disparity between toxic reactions and actual soil conditions. Future efforts should focus on expanding soil biological models and conducting cross-ecosystem comparative studies to comprehensively evaluate the potential repercussions of pesticides on soil ecosystems.

Pharmaceutical and personal care products

PPCPs are considered emerging pollutants and include drugs for Alzheimer's disease (e.g., donepezil, tacrine, and galantamine) (Bezerra Da Silva et al. 2016), antidepressants (e.g., fluoxetine and sertraline) (Mazzitelli et al. 2018; Ofoegbu et al. 2019b; Bayingana et al. 2023), beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol, metoprolol, and acebutolol) (AminiTabrizi et al. 2019), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., aspirin) (Liang et al. 2022), and personal care product ingredients (e.g., triclosan, ultraviolet filters, parabens, and preservatives) (Li 2012a; Van Huizen et al. 2017; Rolo et al. 2026). PPCPs can induce significant sublethal effects on planarians at environmental concentrations ranging from nanograms per liter to micrograms per liter. The toxicity of these substances is largely influenced by the interplay between their pharmacological properties and conserved targets in planarians. Neuroactive drugs, such as fluoxetine, demonstrate remarkable toxicity, which is attributed to the high conservation of their target sites (serotonin transporters) in planarians (Ofoegbu et al. 2019b).

Acute and chronic toxicity

The acute toxicity of various PPCPs to planarians varies significantly. For example, the 96-h LC50 of fluoxetine can be as low as 160 μg/L (Ofoegbu et al. 2019b), whereas certain beta-blockers, such as metoprolol, may have LC50 values as high as 711 mg/L (AminiTabrizi et al. 2019). Increased acute toxicity frequently results in pronounced regenerative inhibition and abnormal behavior in planarians after prolonged exposure.

Comparison of different outcomes

The toxicity of various PPCPs differs significantly, with the toxicity ranking within the same drug category being closely linked to their molecular structure. For instance, among beta-blockers, propranolol, with a higher lipid solubility (log Kow = 3.48), is 6.4 times more toxic than metoprolol, which has a higher water solubility (log Kow = 1.88) (Fraysse and Garric 2005). Additionally, the LC50 of fluoxetine (log Kow = 4.65) (Carter et al. 2016) was 160 μg/L, whereas the LC50 of propranolol was 111 mg/L, indicating a nearly 700-fold difference. This disparity suggests that, in addition to lipid solubility, target site conservation is crucial in determining toxicity. Fluoxetine functions by inhibiting serotonin reuptake, and because the nervous system of planarians contains serotonergic neurons, they are highly susceptible to this drug (Ofoegbu et al. 2019a). The variation in toxicity based on the mode of action underscores the importance of considering target homology across species and drug-receptor interactions, in addition to physicochemical parameters, when assessing the ecological risks of PPCPs.

Behavioural and physiological effects

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), such as fluoxetine and sertraline, can induce intricate behavioral changes (Bayingana et al. 2023). At low levels, they can increase exploratory activity, whereas at elevated levels, they can suppress locomotion and feeding. Following a 48-h exposure to 25 μM simvastatin, the survival rate of G. tigrina plummeted to 25%, accompanied by notable changes in the motor behavior metrics.

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Aspirin significantly inhibits stem cell proliferation by blocking the D. japonica STAT3/SOX2/OCT4 signalling pathway, leading to the cessation of blastema regeneration (Liang et al. 2022). Prolonged exposure to fluoxetine effectively suppresses the development of S. mediterranea cocoons, highlighting its detrimental impact on reproductive capacity (Ofoegbu et al. 2019b).

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

The toxic effects of PPCPs on planarians include interference with the neurotransmitter system, inhibition of stem cell pathways, and induction of oxidative stress and endocrine disruptions at the molecular and cellular levels (Li 2012a; Van Huizen et al. 2017; Yin et al. 2022; Ireland et al. 2025; Feix et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2025; Rolo et al. 2026).

Summary and outlook

Research on the toxicological effects of PPCPs on planarians is still in its early stages and requires further investigation in several key areas. (1) Existing studies have been limited in scope and have focused primarily on a select few neuroactive drugs while neglecting a wide range of prescription medications (e.g., antitumor drugs and immunosuppressants) and emerging personal care product ingredients (e.g., nanosurfactants and microplastic carriers). (2) Research on the combined effects of PPCP mixtures, such as those found in hospital wastewater samples, is limited. (3) Comprehensive research on the reproductive and intergenerational effects of PPCPs on planarians is lacking. (4) The mechanisms underlying the combined effects of PPCPs and microplastics remain unclear. Future studies should expand the range of PPCPs investigated, establish a mechanism-based high-throughput screening platform, conduct long-term studies on low-dose exposure and multigenerational effects, and evaluate the ecological risks associated with PPCP-microplastic composite pollution.

Particulate matter

Particulate matter consists of minute solid particles generated either artificially or through degradation. It is characterized by its small size (typically ranging from nanometers to millimeters), environmental persistence, and potentially intricate effects on ecosystems and human health (Vojnits et al. 2025). Microplastics (e.g., polystyrene (PS), polyethylene (PE), and polyurethane (PU)) (Gambino et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2022; Barbosa et al. 2025) and engineered nanomaterials (e.g., titanium dioxide (TiO2), zinc oxide (ZnO), cerium oxide (CeO2), graphene oxide (GO), silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), carbon quantum dots (CQDs), and cadmium telluride quantum dots (CdTe QDs)) (Ermakov et al. 2019; Bijnens et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2023b, a; Kang et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025) are encompassed within particulate matter pollution. The toxicity mechanisms of particulate matter in planarians include physical harm, oxidative stress, inflammatory reactions, and stem cell function inhibition. The toxicity of particulate matter is notably influenced by its physicochemical attributes, such as particle size, morphology, surface charge, and aggregation status. Nanoscale particles (size < 100 nm) generally exhibit greater toxicity than micrometer-scale particles, and surface modifications of particles (e.g., oxidation level and coatings) significantly impact their bioavailability and toxicity.

Acute and chronic toxicity

Although the acute toxicity of particulate matter is relatively low, it should not be overlooked. For instance, the 72-h LC50 of polystyrene microplastics (PS-MPs) for D. japonica is 332 mg/L (Zhang et al. 2025), and the 168-h LC50 of GO for regenerated D. japonica is 51.8 mg/L (Xie et al. 2023a).

Comparison of different outcomes

The toxicity of various nanomaterials varies significantly, and the differences are not solely dictated by their chemical composition. For instance, 50 mg/L TiO2 nanoparticles did not induce acute mortality but caused delayed neural development and epidermal structure damage (Leynen et al. 2024). In contrast, ZnO nanoparticles (n-ZnO) at the same concentration displayed acute toxicity (LC50 = 0.145 mM ≈ 12 mg/L) (Li et al. 2025), likely because of the release of Zn2+ ions from ZnO, whereas TiO2 primarily operates in its particle form. Furthermore, the aggregation status of nanoparticles is crucial for their toxicity. Iron oxide nanoparticles (IONs) tend to aggregate in culture media, reducing their bioavailability, with no fatalities observed even at 1 mg/mL (Tran 2019). Conversely, well-dispersed AgNPs exhibited notable toxicity at 10 μg/mL (Bijnens et al. 2021). These conflicting outcomes underscore the necessity of considering nanomaterial stability, aggregation dynamics, and dissolution behavior when assessing their toxicity. A straightforward dose–response relationship may not be sufficient to accurately predict environmental risks.

Behavioural and physiological effects

Microplastics and nanomaterials impact planarian behavior by reducing motility, hindering feeding, and altering phototropism. D. japonica consuming PS-MPs results in the filling of the intestinal tract, leading to a false sense of fullness, further suppression of feeding, and indirect effects on motor energy (Gao et al. 2022). TiO2 induces abnormal behaviors, such as “dragging" and lateral lying in S. mediterranea, possibly linked to sensory cilia or epidermal cell dysfunction (Leynen et al. 2024).

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Particulate matter inhibits the regenerative capacity of planarians, representing a highly sensitive toxicity endpoint. Exposure to GO diminishes the regenerated blastema area in D. japonica by 67.9% and causes severe damage to the neural fiber network (Xie et al. 2023a). n-CeO2 notably suppresses stem cell activity, decreases central nervous system collateral branches, and induces neurodevelopmental abnormalities (Xie et al. 2023b).

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

The adverse effects of particulate matter on planarians include oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, physical interference, inflammatory responses, and disruption of genes associated with neural development and the cell cycle.

Analysis of the correlations between stem cell signalling pathways and regenerative polarity

Nanomaterials can disrupt the polarity of regeneration by interfering with specific stem cell signalling pathways, leading to abnormal regeneration directions and structures. This mechanism involves various levels: GO induces an intracellular redox imbalance, activating the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway (Xie et al. 2023a). TiO2 nanoparticles penetrate the epidermis, stimulate mucus secretion, promote particle aggregation, and inhibit nerve development (Leynen et al. 2024). NPs interfere with cell cycle regulatory genes and stem cell markers, blocking the transition of stem cells from the G1 phase to the S phase (Leynen et al. 2024). The effects of various nanomaterials on regeneration polarity vary. GO predominantly influences anterior‒posterior axis polarity, leading to dual-head or tail-end phenotypes (Xie et al. 2023a). In contrast, TiO2 primarily influences the dorsal–ventral axis pattern, resulting in dorsal neural structural abnormalities (Leynen et al. 2024). This specificity may be associated with the distribution of nanoparticles within the organism and the selectivity of the target organs.

Exploration of the cocktail effect and combined toxicity

In natural environments, microplastics frequently coexist with various pollutants, including heavy metals, pesticides, and persistent organic pollutants, resulting in complex pollution matrices (Zhang et al. 2022). Research has indicated that compared with individual exposure, combined exposure to PS-MPs and perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) significantly increases the toxicity to D. japonica (Huang et al. 2024). This is evidenced by exacerbated ocular point aplastic disorder, increased DNA damage, and neuro-morphological alterations, such as the absence of lateral nerves and brain segments. The synergistic effect may arise from the ability of microplastics to function as “Trojan horse” carriers, adsorb PFOS, and facilitate its transport into planarians, thereby increasing its bioavailability in the organisms. The surface of microplastics can develop an “eco-corona," which includes metal and metal oxide nanoparticles that induce changes in ROS levels and oxidative stress enzyme levels (Queiroz and De Torresi 2025). For example, PS-MPs act as carriers for Pb2+, enhancing toxicity to freshwater planarians through a “Trojan horse” effect (Xie et al. 2024). This leads to reduced stem cell numbers, diminished regenerative capacity, oxidative damage, and disrupted energy metabolism.

Summary and outlook

Particulate matter, a prevalent form of environmental pollution, presents several challenges in toxicological studies involving planarians. (1) There is a notable emphasis on polystyrene in microplastic research, neglecting other common plastics, such as polyethylene, polypropylene, and polyvinyl chloride. (2) Chronic toxicity data are scarce, particularly regarding prolonged low-dose exposure and the chronic effects of nanomaterials. (3) There is a lack of clarity regarding in vivo processes, such as the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of particulate matter, in planarians. (4) Combined pollution assessments are lacking, notably the absence of toxicity evaluations for the combined effects of microplastics and nanomaterials (e.g., nanoplastic release from microplastics). Future research should broaden the scope of particulate matter variants, conduct extended exposure and multigenerational studies, employ isotope labelling or fluorescence techniques to monitor the fate of particulate matter in planarians, and assess the influence of microplastic aging, weathering, and biofilm formation on toxicity. Furthermore, systematic evaluations of combined toxicities, such as microplastic–metal, microplastic–pesticide, and microplastic–PPCP combinations, are recommended. Exploring the “Trojan horse” effect of microplastics as carriers of pollutants and establishing an ecological risk prediction model based on mixed toxicity coefficients are also crucial for future research.

Surfactants

Surfactants, amphiphilic molecules comprising hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, are utilized extensively in various industrial and domestic applications, such as cleaning, emulsification, and dispersion, owing to their ability to significantly lower the liquid surface tension (Liang et al. 2024). In planarian ecotoxicology research, surfactants under scrutiny include anionic variations (sodium dodecyl sulfate, SDS; sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate, LAS; PFOS; perfluorooctanoic acid, PFOA) (Li 2008; Yuan et al. 2013, 2015, 2017, 2018; Foguth et al. 2020; Feng et al. 2023), cationic variants (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide, CTAB; benzalkonium chloride, BKC; cetylpyridinium chloride, CPC) (Li 2008, 2012b), nonionic forms (4-nonylphenol, NP; octylphenol polyoxyethylene ether, OPE; Triton X-100) (Li 2008; Hagstrom et al. 2015), and amphoteric ionic types (betaine) (Li 2012b). The mechanisms through which surfactants elicit toxicity involve disruption of cell membrane integrity, inhibition of AChE activity, and induction of oxidative stress.

Acute and chronic toxicity

The acute toxicity of surfactants depends on the structure–activity relationship, particularly in planarians, where toxicity is closely linked to the charge of the surfactant. Typically, the toxicity hierarchy follows a pattern in which cationic surfactants (e.g., CTAB) are more toxic than anionic surfactants (e.g., SDS), which are more toxic than nonionic surfactants (e.g., Triton X-100). Notably, the toxicity of CTAB increased substantially over time, indicating a cumulative effect (Li 2008).

Comparison of different outcomes

The toxicity of surfactants is intricately linked to their carbon chain lengths and polar head groups. Cationic surfactants exhibit maximum toxicity with carbon chain lengths of 12–16 atoms (e.g., CTAB with 16 carbons; CPC also shows high toxicity), whereas chain lengths that are either too long or too short result in reduced toxicity (Li 2008, 2012b). For anionic surfactants, the toxicity of PFOA (an 8-carbon perfluorochain with a carboxylic acid group) (48-h LC50 = 536 mg/L) is significantly lower than that of PFOS (a perfluorosulfonic acid with a sulfonate group) (48-h LC50 = 25 mg/L), indicating that the polar head group has a substantial influence on toxicity. This structure–toxicity relationship implies that the hydrophobic–hydrophilic balance (HLB) of surfactants could serve as a crucial parameter for predicting their ecological risks (Phaodee and Weston 2023). Notably, certain surfactants (e.g., Triton X-100) induce planaria movement at low concentrations, resulting in a “hormesis” effect. This non-monotonic dose‒response relationship adds complexity to risk assessment.

Behavioural and physiological effects

Surfactants influence planarian behavior by inhibiting movement, reducing feeding, and suppressing cholinesterase (ChE) activity. Cationic surfactants, including SDS, NP, LAS, BKC, and CTAB, notably inhibit ChE activity (Li 2008, 2012b). PFOS and PFOA affect planarian motility and membrane-binding enzyme activity (Yuan et al. 2015, 2018; Foguth et al. 2020).

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Research on the effects of surfactants on planarian regeneration remains relatively scarce. Triton X-100 at sublethal concentrations delays blastema and eyespot regeneration in D. japonica (Hagstrom et al. 2015). Surfactants may influence regeneration by disrupting the mucus layer on the surface of the regenerating bud and inhibiting stem cell proliferation.

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

Surfactants primarily exert toxicity by disrupting neural conduction, triggering oxidative stress, and compromising cell membrane integrity. Additionally, cationic surfactants have a high affinity for negatively charged cell surfaces, including cellular and mitochondrial membranes, resulting in membrane potential destabilization.

Summary and outlook

Surfactants, commonly used in industry and daily life, present several shortcomings in toxicological research on planarians: (1) The chronic toxicity effects are unclear, with a focus on acute toxicity rather than long-term low-dose exposure and chronic effects; (2) Research on the effects of mixtures is insufficient, particularly in assessing the toxicity of surfactant mixtures such as those found in detergent formulations; (3) The interaction mechanism between surfactants and other pollutants (e.g., heavy metals, pesticides) remains unresolved; and (4) The reproductive effects of surfactants on planarians are largely unexplored. Future studies should include chronic exposure experiments to evaluate the endocrine-disrupting potential and reproductive toxicity of surfactants, investigate their role as pollutant enhancers in complex pollution scenarios, and develop toxicity prediction models based on structure‒activity relationships (quantitative structure‒activity relationships, QSAR).

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs)

EDCs are exogenous substances that disrupt normal endocrine system function in organisms (Chen et al. 2019). Examples include bisphenol A (BPA), bisphenol F (BPF), bisphenol analogues such as bisguaiacol (BG), natural and synthetic estrogens such as 17β-estradiol (E2), 17α-ethinylestradiol (EE2), and diethylstilbestrol (DES), androgens such as testosterone and 17α-methyltestosterone (17α-MT) and antiandrogens such as flutamide (Li 2013, 2015; Morris et al. 2022). EDCs affect planarian development, regeneration, and reproduction by mimicking or interfering with endogenous hormone signals. Despite the relatively simple nature of the endocrine system in planarians, the presence of conserved hormone signalling pathways, such as those involving estrogen and androgen receptors, renders them susceptible to EDCs.

Acute and chronic toxicity

The acute toxicity of EDCs to planarians is generally low, with an LC50 typically in the mg/L range. However, the MEC of these substances, which ranged from nanograms per liter to micrograms per liter, approached the LOEC value associated with regeneration interference, indicating a significant chronic risk (Li 2013).

Discussion on environmental concentrations and risk quotients

The concentration of EDCs in water bodies typically ranges from nanograms per liter (ng/L) to micrograms per liter (μg/L). For example, the concentration of BPA usually falls between 0.01 and 1 μg/L, while EE2 levels range from 0.1 to 10 ng/L (Swansborough et al. 2025). Based on the 168-h LC50 values for D. japonica (BPA: 1.25 mg/L; EE2: 1.65 mg/L), the associated RQ was notably low (BPA: 8 × 10–6 to 8 × 10–4; EE2:6 × 10–8 to 6 × 10–6), suggesting a negligible risk of acute mortality. However, the ratio of the sublethal effect concentration of EDCs (e.g., the lowest observed effect concentration (LOEC) of BPA on regeneration at approximately 0.1 mg/L) to the environmental concentration ranges from 100 to 10,000 (Li 2015). Given the prolonged exposure and bioaccumulation tendencies of EDCs, chronic ecological risks should not be overlooked. Furthermore, the combined effects of EDCs (e.g., the simultaneous presence of multiple estrogens) may lead to synergistic outcomes, potentially increasing the actual risk beyond that of individual compounds.

Behavioural and physiological effects

Research on the effects of EDCs on planarian behavior and physiology is relatively scarce. Existing studies have only speculated that EDCs may influence planarian behavior and physiology by disrupting neuroendocrine systems, such as the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒gonadal axis (Li 2013; Morris et al. 2022).

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

The most sensitive toxicity endpoint of EDCs in planarians is their impact on regeneration and development. Among BPA, DES, EE2, and E2, all delayed head regeneration, with DES exhibiting the greatest efficacy (LC50 = 0.52 mg/L after 168 h) (Li 2015). EDCs can disrupt regenerative polarity and organ formation by interfering with crucial developmental signalling pathways, such as the Wnt/β-catenin and Hedgehog signalling pathways. BPA, along with the bisphenol analogues BPF and BG, influences neural development during head regeneration, indicating that the bisphenol structure serves as a critical pharmacophore that hinders normal development.

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

EDCs disrupt hormone receptor-mediated signalling pathways at the molecular level. Planarians possess conserved homologues of estrogen receptors (ERs) and androgen receptors (ARs). By binding to these receptors, EDCs can either activate or inhibit the downstream gene expression (Morris et al. 2022).

Summary and outlook

As threats to global public health and environmental protection, EDCs present the following shortcomings in toxicological studies on planarians: (1) Research on the underlying mechanisms remains relatively weak: Inadequate exploration of the molecular composition and signalling pathways of planarians' endocrine system hinders a mechanistic understanding; (2) Limited focus on EDC categories such as thyroid disruptors and glucocorticoid disruptors; (3) Insufficient investigation into the reproductive and intergenerational effects of EDCs on planarians; (4) Obscured understanding of the combined pollution effects of EDCs and microplastics, where microplastics adsorb EDCs and serve as carriers. Future research should aim to characterize planarian hormone receptors at the molecular level, develop a high-throughput screening platform for EDCs, perform multigenerational exposure studies to assess genetic and epigenetic effects, and evaluate the ecological risks associated with complex pollution from microplastics and EDCs.

Industrial chemicals and organic pollutants (ICOPs)

In planarian ecotoxicological research, ICOPs such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) such as pyrene, benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P), and phenanthrene (Simão et al. 2020, 2021a, b, 2022); ionic liquids such as 1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide ([C8mim]Br) and 1-decyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide ([C10mim]Br) (Zhang et al. 2016a, b, 2018, 2019a); plasticizers such as dibutyl phthalate (DBP) (Wu et al. 2024); and flame retardants such as polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) (Yang et al. 2022) and organophosphorus flame retardants (OPFRs) (Zhang et al. 2019b), tributyltin (Ofoegbu et al. 2016), acrylamide (Hagstrom et al. 2015), and peroxyacetic acid (PAA) (Rodrigues Macêdo et al. 2019); and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (Zhang et al. 2013) have been investigated. The toxic mechanisms of these pollutants include DNA damage, oxidative stress, neurotoxicity and endocrine disruption.

Acute and chronic toxicity

The 96-h LC50 of pyrene to G. tigrina was 830 μg/L, with no mortality observed at concentrations up to 4500 μg/L for pyrene and B[a]P (Simão et al. 2020). These findings highlight the substantial variations in the structural toxicity of PAHs toward G. tigrina. The toxicity levels of different compounds toward planarians differed significantly. For example, the 96-h LC50 of PBDE-47 against D. japonica is 3.989 mg/L (Yang et al. 2022), whereas PFOA has a 96-h LC50 of 39.35 mg/L (Yuan et al. 2015).

Comparison of different outcomes

The toxicity of PAHs may be associated with their molecular size and number of rings, which exhibit nonlinear relationships. For instance, phenanthrene (3-ring) displays acute toxicity to planarians, with an LC50 of 830 μg/L over 96 h, whereas pyrene (4-ring) and B[a]P (5-ring) do not induce mortality, even at higher concentrations (4500 μg/L) (Simão et al. 2020). This atypical behavior could be attributed to the remarkably low water solubility of high-molecular-weight PAHs (e.g., B[a]P with a water solubility of only 3.8 μg/L), which constrains their bioavailability. Nevertheless, upon entering planarians through the food chain (e.g., by consuming B[a]P-exposed Chironomidae larvae), B[a]P can trigger behavioral abnormalities, such as burrowing, indicating that trophic transfer is a significant ecological risk pathway for PAHs (Simão et al. 2022). This exposure route-dependent toxicity underscores the necessity of considering the environmental fate and bioavailability of hydrophobic organic pollutants (log Kow > 5) when evaluating their risks. Relying solely on aqueous phase concentrations for LC50 estimation may lead to an underestimation of the actual risk.

Behavioural and physiological effects

Exposure to DBP results in a reduction in the total distance moved, average velocity, and acceleration of planarians, which is attributed to a decrease in ciliary count, shortened individual length, and muscle tissue impairment (Wu et al. 2024). PAH exposure affects the predation behavior and activity of planarians (Simão et al. 2020, 2021b). Acrylamide and DMF influence the movement patterns and regenerative potential of planarians (Zhang et al. 2013; Hagstrom et al. 2015).

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Exposure to DBP induces aberrant brain development in planarians, disrupts abdominal nerve cord connections, suppresses the expression of glial-associated gene (pc2), and reduces the expression of the 3C11 protein (Wu et al. 2024). PBDE-47 and PBDE-209 disrupt homeostasis and regeneration by inducing abnormal DNA damage, proliferation, and apoptosis (Yang et al. 2022). Acrylamide impedes eye-point regeneration (Hagstrom et al. 2015). The ionic liquid [C8mim]Br affects the mRNA expression of the DjCuZnSOD gene, resulting in DNA damage, as evidenced by decreased genomic template stability (GTS) in RAPD analysis (Zhang et al. 2019a).

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

Industrial chemicals operate via various molecular mechanisms. PAHs generate electrophilic epoxy intermediates through metabolic activation, forming adducts with DNA, which result in genotoxic effects (Simão et al. 2020). DBP acts as an endocrine disruptor by interfering with hormone signal transduction (Wu et al. 2024). Ionic liquids trigger oxidative stress and cause DNA strand breakage (Zhang et al. 2016b). PBDEs disrupt thyroid hormone signalling, a crucial developmental regulatory pathway, to impact neural development (Yang et al. 2022).

Summary and outlook

Toxicological studies on planarians exposed to industrial chemicals and organic pollutants have several shortcomings: (1) Limited coverage of chemical types, omitting data on numerous emerging industrial chemicals such as alternatives to perfluorinated compounds and new flame retardants; (2) Inadequate exploration of metabolic mechanisms, particularly regarding the involvement of the CYP450 enzyme system in the metabolic activation and detoxification of industrial chemicals; (3) Poor investigations into the combined toxicity of industrial chemical mixtures, such as PAH mixtures and flame retardant mixtures; and (4) Insufficient long-term toxicity data, especially concerning the chronic effects of industrial chemicals on planarians from prolonged low-dose exposures. Future research should broaden the range of screened industrial chemicals, utilize metabolomics to identify active metabolites, assess the combined toxicity of mixtures, and develop toxicity prediction models based on QSAR principles.

Other pollutants

Other pollutants studied in planarian ecotoxicology include biological toxins, such as microcystin and cyanopeptide toxins (Piontek et al. 2023; Jin et al. 2024), sanguinarine (Balestrini et al. 2017), sterigmatocystin (ST) (Piontek 2010), and Solanum viarum fruit extract (Schaly and Braguini 2024a), chemical mutagens like methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) and cyclophosphamide (CP) (Knakievicz et al. 2008; Hagstrom et al. 2015; Wouters et al. 2020), and tobacter-specific nitrosamine (4-(methylnitrosamino)−1-(3-pyridyl)−1-butanone, NNK) (Wu et al. 2012b), physical stressors including X-rays, gamma rays, high temperature, and extreme pH (Knakievicz et al. 2008; De Oliveira et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2025), salts such as sodium chloride (NaCl) (Ofoegbu et al. 2019a; Dornelas et al. 2021), antibiotics like gentamicin (Rejo et al. 2023; Mathiron et al. 2023), neuroactive substances like L-tryptophan and caffeine (Martins Castro and Luciano Braguini 2025), biomedical compounds like 3-methyladenine (Kang et al. 2021) and ammonium nitrogen (Alonso and Camargo 2011, 2015). These pollutants have garnered increasing research interest because of their diverse effects on planarian survival, behavior, regeneration, and physiological homeostasis. They operate through various mechanisms, including the induction of oxidative stress, DNA damage, disruption of stem cell function, osmotic pressure imbalance, and modulation of crucial signalling pathways.

Acute and chronic toxicity

Significant variations exist in the acute toxicity of different pollutants. For instance, the biological toxin microcystin-leucine-arginine (MC-LR) exhibited a 96-h LC50 of 3.557 mg/L for D. japonica, indicating high acute toxicity (Jin et al. 2024). MMS showed a 24-h LC50 of 2.0 µM and a 96-h LC50 of 1.4 µM for G. tigrina, suggesting cumulative toxicity over time (Knakievicz et al. 2008). The acute toxicity of NaCl varies across species, with 96-h LC50 values of 6.79 g/L for G. tigrina and 7.55 g/L for S. mediterranea (Ofoegbu et al. 2019a), highlighting the interspecific differences in salt tolerance. Gentamicin exhibited low acute toxicity, as it did not induce significant mortality in planarians at a concentration of 50 mg/L for 24 h (Rejo et al. 2023). Conversely, prolonged exposure (30 days) of Polycelis felina to ammonia (nonionic NH₃) at 0.05 mg/L resulted in a mortality rate exceeding 60%, underscoring its high chronic toxicity (Alonso and Camargo 2011). While current research predominantly focuses on acute exposure, data on long-term low-dose chronic toxicity remain limited.

Behavioural and physiological effects

This type of pollutant can induce aberrant key behaviors and physiological functions in planarians, affecting their movement, feeding, and reproduction. Salinity stress from NaCl results in osmotic imbalance and epidermal damage, notably by reducing the range of planarian movement and feeding speed (Ofoegbu et al. 2019a). Following exposure to MC-LR, planarians exhibit epidermal degeneration, body dissolution, abnormal pigmentation (albinism), and suppressed growth (Jin et al. 2024). The natural alkaloid sanguinarine markedly hinders the feeding behavior (Balestrini et al. 2017). Neuroactive contaminants, such as L-tryptophan and caffeine, can disrupt fertility and reproductive ability, thereby affecting head regeneration (Martins Castro and Luciano Braguini 2025). Ammonia exposure causes a progressive decrease in locomotor activity, with incomplete behavioral restoration upon intermittent exposure (Alonso and Camargo 2015). With respect to physical factors, X-ray irradiation indirectly influences regeneration and body recovery by impeding stem cell function, despite not directly evaluating behavior (Hu et al. 2025). Behavioural toxicity endpoints of this pollutant type are typically more responsive than mortality endpoints, making them suitable for sub-lethal toxicity evaluation.

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

This pollutant significantly inhibits the planarian regeneration process by impeding stem cell proliferation, reducing the number of H3P+ cells, and suppressing the expression of differentiation-related genes, such as piwiA, prog1, and agat1. Consequently, it hinders blastema growth and delays regeneration. Sanguinarine disrupts bioelectrical signals by upregulating Dj-H+, K+-ATPase α expression, leading to abnormal anterior regeneration, visual system defects, and anomalous pharyngeal morphology (Balestrini et al. 2017). The chemical mutagen MMS induces DNA damage and micronucleus formation, resulting in delayed bud formation, eyespot regeneration, and auricular processes (Knakievicz et al. 2008). NaCl hinders wound healing and cell differentiation, causing delayed regeneration, blastema degeneration, and developmental malformations (Shah et al. 2020). The autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) induces midbody ulcers, delays regeneration, and disrupts blastema development by inhibiting autophagy (Kang et al. 2021). Collectively, these findings suggest that this pollutant can disrupt the planarian stem cell system and regenerative regulatory network through various pathways.

Molecular and cellular mechanisms

The mechanisms of toxicity associated with this pollutant are intricate and varied, primarily involving oxidative stress, DNA damage, apoptosis, interference with signalling pathways, and organelle dysfunction. MC-LR induces ROS accumulation, disturbances in the antioxidant system (fluctuations in the activity of enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and GPX), DNA damage, and lipid peroxidation, consequently activating caspase-dependent apoptosis and ultimately impeding the function of stem cells (Jin et al. 2024). Sanguinarine modulates gene expression through apoptosis induction, differentiation interference, and effects on ion pumps and matrix metalloproteinase activity, leading to the dysregulation of regeneration (Balestrini et al. 2017). NaCl induces osmotic pressure imbalance and oxidative stress, promoting energy redistribution for osmotic regulation, resulting in inadequate energy for cell mobility and repair (Ofoegbu et al. 2019a; Shah et al. 2020). X-ray irradiation triggers the ATM/ATR (Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutated‌/ATM and Rad3-related‌) signalling pathway, initiating extensive phosphorylation cascades that affect DNA repair, the cell cycle, apoptosis, and autophagy (Ishida et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2025). Ammonia penetrates cells via passive diffusion, disrupting ion regulation and energy metabolism, and causing harm to the epithelial and muscle layers (Alonso and Camargo 2011, 2015).

Summary and outlook

Toxicological studies of other pollutants in planarians have elucidated their involvement in oxidative stress, DNA damage, interference with stem cell function, and disruption of signalling pathways, underscoring the significance of planarians as sensitive models in ecotoxicology and regenerative toxicology research. Nevertheless, the current research has several shortcomings: (1) Limited research scope, primarily focusing on acute toxicity without adequately exploring the effects of prolonged low-dose exposure on reproduction, behavioral patterns, cognitive functions, and transgenerational effects; (2) Inadequate consideration of transformation products and synergistic effects, with insufficient investigations into the environmental transformation products of biological toxins and the combined toxic mechanisms of pollutants and natural stressors such as salinity, temperature, and pH; (3) Superficial analysis of molecular mechanisms, often relying on assumptions rather than direct evidence of molecular interactions and dynamic processes; (4) A lack of environmental realism, as single-pollutant exposure in laboratory settings fails to capture the intricate pollution and bioecological dynamics of natural aquatic environments. Future research should conduct chronic multigenerational exposure experiments and systematically analyze toxicity networks by integrating multi-omics technologies, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. The interaction effects of coexisting pollutants in the real environment should be considered. Moreover, efforts should be made to promote the standardization of planarian behavior and genotoxicity detection methods in ecotoxicology. These actions provide a more scientific basis for the formulation of water quality benchmarks, ecological risk assessments, and environmental pollutant control.

Common and distinct toxic effects of various pollutants on planarians

Commonality of toxic effects

Although the abovementioned pollutants in planarians differ in their chemical structure, source, and environmental behavior, they induce toxic effects that share common features across categories. These common features mirror the conserved stress response pathways and functional strategies employed by organisms when faced with chemical stress.

At the sub-individual level, oxidative stress is frequently reported as a key molecular event associated with toxic effects, with nearly all pollutant types capable of promoting the overproduction of ROS, resulting in a disruption of redox balance. Despite varying initial targets, these pollutants commonly culminate in oxidative stress, as evidenced by alterations in the activity of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GPX, and GST), diminished levels of reduced GSH, and the accumulation of lipid peroxidation byproducts (MDA). These findings underscore the pivotal role of the antioxidant defense system in planarians as the principal and delicate molecular interface that reacts to environmental pollutants. Notably, ROS also plays a physiological role in planarian regeneration, acting as signalling molecules that initiate and regulate neoblast proliferation and tissue patterning under normal conditions. However, most pollutants induce excessive or sustained ROS accumulation, overwhelming the antioxidant defense system and leading to oxidative damage and disruption of cellular homeostasis. This distinction between physiological ROS signalling and pollutant-induced oxidative stress should be carefully considered when interpreting toxicological outcomes. Additionally, stem cell function is often considered a critical toxicity endpoint. The ability of planarians to regenerate and maintain homeostasis relies heavily on the presence of neoblasts. The pollutants discussed in this analysis directly or indirectly disrupt stem cell kinetics, leading to reduced levels of proliferation markers (e.g., H3P+) and downregulation of the expression of stem cell marker genes (piwiA and PCNA). The hindrance or reduction of stem cell reservoirs forms the central link connecting molecular harm to impaired individual regeneration and irregularities in tissue repair, highlighting the distinctive utility of planarians as toxicology models.

At the individual level, toxic effects, behavior, and regeneration are common sensitive endpoints. A decrease in planarian mobility, indicated by a reduced locomotion velocity (pLMV), and hindered regeneration, characterized by inhibited blastema growth and delayed eyespot/auricular regeneration, are typical early toxic responses to most pollutants. These endpoints encompass various physiological processes, including nerve conduction, muscle function, cell proliferation, and differentiation, and exhibit strong ecological correlations that affect foraging, escape responses, and damage repair. In addition, energy distribution imbalance represents a potential physiological consequence. When exposed to pollutants, planarians must allocate more energy to stress responses, such as the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes and repair of DNA damage, leading to diminished energy reserves for growth, regeneration, and reproduction. This disparity in energy distribution may serve as a shared physiological mechanism through which diverse pollutants ultimately reduce individual adaptability.

Notably, for many pollutant categories, direct causal evidence linking oxidative stress to specific phenotypic outcomes remains limited, and much of the current understanding is based on correlative observations (e.g., concomitant changes in antioxidant enzyme activities and behavioral endpoints).

Variability in toxic effects

Despite the commonalities mentioned above, various pollutants differ significantly in their modes of action, key targets, toxicity kinetics, and ecological risk characteristics because of their distinct chemical properties and molecular interaction mechanisms.

Rapid-acting acute pollutants, such as metal ions (e.g., Cu2+, Hg2+), cationic surfactants, and nanoparticles (e.g., n-CeO2), are known for their rapid and severe toxic effects, often resulting in high mortality rates among planarians within h to days. These effects are attributed to acute oxidation bursts or damage to membrane structures owing to their high reactivity. Conversely, chronic cumulative pollutants, including industrial organic substances (e.g., PFOS and BPA), certain drugs, and endocrine disruptors, exhibit gradual and persistent toxicity, potentially impairing regeneration and reproduction under prolonged low-dose exposure. The concern with these pollutants lies in their ability to threaten the population sustainability.

The main molecular targets and initial mechanisms of these toxic effects can be categorized as follows: specific enzyme inhibition, where pesticides (especially organophosphorus pesticides) disrupt nerve conduction by covalently inhibiting AChE activity, and nonspecific inhibition of membrane-binding enzymes (such as ChE and ATPase) by surfactants, leading to membrane structure destruction. Direct damage to genetic material caused by industrial chemicals (e.g., B[a]P), nanomaterials, and radiation results in DNA strand breaks or admixture formation with prominent genotoxic effects. Interference with signalling pathways by PPCPs (e.g., aspirin and ibrutinib) and endocrine disruptors, which primarily disrupt conserved signalling pathways (such as the STAT3 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3) and estrogen receptor pathways), affects cell fate determination and tissue pattern formation. Physical barrier disruption, where particulate matter (nanomaterials) may damage tissue integrity by physically blocking and penetrating cell membranes, in addition to chemical effects.

Toxicity sensitivity endpoints in ecology can be categorized based on their correlation with toxicity. Substances such as pesticides, surfactants, and neuroactive drugs exhibit significant neurobehavioral toxicity, swiftly impacting motor function and directly endangering individual survival by affecting foraging and predator evasion behaviors. Nanoparticles, heavy metals, and endocrine disruptors exhibit pronounced regenerative toxicity, strongly inhibiting the regrowth process and impairing damage repair and asexual reproduction within populations. Endocrine disruptors and specific pesticides, such as difenoconazole, cause notable reproductive toxicity, markedly reducing cocoon production and hatching, even at concentrations below those that cause behavioral or regenerative effects, thereby posing a direct threat to population sustainability. The biological effects of metal ions are strongly influenced by their chemical form (valence state and solubility) and environmental conditions (pH and water hardness). Nanomaterial toxicity is closely related to characteristics such as size, surface modifications, and transformations within organisms (e.g., Ce2+ release from n-CeO2). The toxicity of organic pollutants is often modulated by the balance between metabolic activation and detoxification processes (e.g., planarians exhibit CYP450-mediated activation capability).

Sources of variability in planarian toxicity studies

The considerable variation in the reported toxicity values across studies reflects the influence of multiple experimental and biological factors. Understanding these sources of variability is essential for data interpretation and cross-study comparisons. The key factors are as follows:

1. Species-specific differences. Different planarian species (e.g., D. japonica, G. tigrina, and S. mediterranea) exhibit different sensitivities to the same pollutant, likely because of variations in detoxification enzyme activities, neoblast population dynamics, or cuticle permeability. For example, compared with S. mediterranea, D. japonica generally shows greater tolerance to metals, and compared with asexual strains, sexual strains often display different reproductive responses.

2. Water quality parameters. Water hardness, pH, and dissolved organic matter significantly affect the bioavailability and toxicity of metals. Hard water reduces free metal ion concentrations through complexation with Ca2+ and Mg2+, thereby mitigating their apparent toxicity (e.g., Cd2+ toxicity decreases with increasing hardness). pH influences metal speciation (hydroxide precipitation at alkaline pH) and organic pollutant ionization states, thereby altering bioavailability.

3. Exposure conditions. Variations in exposure duration, temperature, light conditions, and feeding status can substantially affect the toxicity outcomes. Regenerating planarians typically exhibit higher sensitivity than intact adults do because of increased metabolic demands and active neoblast proliferation. Temperature affects both pollutant toxicity and the metabolic rate of planarians.

4. Pollutant characteristics. Chemical speciation, particle size (for nanomaterials), and formulation composition (for pesticides) significantly influence toxicity. Commercial pesticide formulations often exhibit higher toxicity than the active ingredients alone because of adjuvant effects. Nanomaterial toxicity depends on the size, surface charge, and aggregation state. For metals, ionic, nanoparticle, and microparticle forms (e.g., Zn2+ vs. n‑ZnO vs. μ‑ZnO) induce different toxicity mechanisms and potencies.

5. Endpoint selection. Mortality (LC50) is often less sensitive than behavioral or regenerative endpoints. Studies focusing only on acute lethality may underestimate ecological risks.

Summary and outlook

In conclusion, the toxic reactions of planarians to various pollutants demonstrate a pattern of “common pathways and characteristic mechanisms.” Oxidative stress and stem cell dysfunction are commonly reported across pollutant types; however, their mechanistic roles as central toxicity nodes require further validation through functional studies.

This pattern has significant ecotoxicological implications. When a single pollutant is assessed, the most sensitive endpoint should be selected based on the category characteristics. For example, pesticides primarily affect behavior and AChE activity, whereas endocrine disruptors target reproduction and developmental morphologies. When dealing with complex pollution, it is crucial to consider the potential superposition or synergistic effects of various pollutants on oxidative stress and stem cell inhibition pathways, regardless of their distinct initial mechanisms of action. When extrapolating findings from laboratory studies to natural populations, variations in the persistence, bioaccumulation, and exposure patterns of different pollutants in the environment must be considered. Chronic, low-dose exposure to endocrine-disrupting and regenerative pollutants may present a less apparent yet significant threat to the long-term stability of populations.

The significance of planarian models lies in their ability to reveal a seamless sequence of events spanning from molecular stress to ecological function decline within a unified living system. Subsequent studies should systematically contrast the distinct and common points of various pollutant types in this sequence and develop quantitative association models to improve the ability to predict population-level ecological risk based on molecular biomarkers. Methodological innovations and progress in establishing a standardized system for ecotoxicological research using planarians

Planarians are known for their robust regenerative abilities and primitive nervous system. Their key advantage lies in bridging the gap between high-throughput in vitro screening and traditional in vivo mammalian experiments, thereby facilitating the implementation of the “3R” principle (replacement, reduction, and refinement). The extent and diversity of model applications depend on the methodological reliability, standardization levels, and technical support. Initiatives such as the Tox21 program (Isaacs et al. 2022) and regulations such as REACH (Registration, Evaluation, Authorization, and Restriction of Chemicals) (Armstrong et al. 2021) have propelled advancements in planarian toxicology research by fostering innovative methodologies, standardization efforts, and specialized instrumentation.

Innovative approach: from multi-endpoint phenotypic screening to computational predictive modelling

Conventional toxicological assessments primarily focus on a single endpoint, such as mortality rate, movement defects, or regenerative damage, limiting the comprehensive depiction of the toxicity of chemical substances. Recent research approaches have transitioned from singular fatality rate evaluations to multifaceted toxicity assessment systems encompassing behavior, regeneration, morphology, and neural structures. A notable advancement is the introduction of a five-step semiautomated screening platform, enabling simultaneous evaluation of lethality, behavior, regeneration, brain structure, and thermal chemotactic responses in planarians (Hagstrom et al. 2015). This innovation significantly enhances the sensitivity and inclusivity of toxicity evaluations. In the realm of behavioral science, sophisticated dynamic behavior classification algorithms, such as the double Gaussian model for fitting velocity distributions, have been employed to quantify non-stimulating behaviors such as “resting,” “gliding,” and “swimming,” facilitating high-throughput and detailed behavioral toxicological analyses (Hagstrom et al. 2015).

The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) technology has ushered in a new era in planarian toxicology research. Mikael et al. pioneered the utilization of machine learning for predicting planarian regeneration morphology (Li and Liu 2024). They developed random forest and deep neural network models based on 1,716 experimental data points from the PlanformDB database, signifying a shift in planarian toxicology toward an “AI-enhanced” predictive science framework. Hossain and Roy introduced quantitative structure‒toxicity relationship (QSTR) modelling and established a quantitative structure–toxicity-toxicity relationship (QSTTR) model encompassing planarians–water fleas and planarians–fish, offering a valuable tool for interspecies risk evaluation (Hossain and Roy 2018). Wang et al. implemented a 3D-QSAR model for screening benzophenone ultraviolet absorbers, devised the “comprehensive effect value”, and successfully pursued a multi-objective collaborative optimization design approach (Xiao and Li 2022).

New standards established: advancing experimental reproducibility and data comparability

Advancements in methodology necessitate standardization. Recent studies have focused on developing reproducible experimental protocols for planarian toxicology, particularly for behavioral assessments. The essential parameters were as follows:

Species screening and standardization: Rejo et al. recommended D. japonica over S. mediterranea for high-throughput screening because of its superior performance in various behavioral paradigms and greater stability in long-term small-volume culture (Rejo et al. 2023).

Summary and outlook

In conclusion, a comprehensive methodological framework encompassing multidimensional phenotypic screening, computational prediction models, standardized conditions, and automated equipment has been developed for the ecotoxicology of planarians. These advancements confer distinct advantages for the rapid screening of environmental pollutants, evaluation of developmental neurotoxicity, and prioritization of chemical safety.

However, this model encounters several challenges. First, the flux bottleneck persists, and its competitiveness against smaller biological models, such as nematodes, is constrained by the experimental cycle and manual operations. Additionally, the complexity of the nervous system is restricted, as its rudimentary central nervous system may not adequately replicate the intricate neural behavior and cellular mechanisms of mammals. Moreover, a gap exists between computation and experimentation, with most predictive models lacking experimental validation and featuring descriptors that are weakly linked to biological mechanisms. Finally, the widespread adoption of standardized methods in resource-constrained laboratories is impeded by equipment reliance and cost.

The high-throughput nature and systematic attributes of omics data have enhanced our understanding of conventional toxicity pathways and revealed novel multitier toxicity mechanisms. Microbe-mediated indirect toxicity pathways

Bijnens et al. reported that while pollutants such as AgNPs and microplastics did not directly induce noticeable mortality or notable pathological alterations in S. mediterranea, they could significantly disrupt the microbiome equilibrium of planarians (Bijnens et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2024). This disruption includes a decreased abundance of specific bacterial communities and alterations in the community structure. Perturbed microbial balance has been linked to variations in host health status, such as oxidative stress and metabolic irregularities, indicating that the microbiome could function as a sensitive biomarker for the initial impact of pollutants. Furthermore, it may enhance or moderate long-term toxicity via the “microbiota-host” axis of the host.

Regulatory pathways for epigenetic and post-translational modification

Phosphorylation proteomics research by Hu et al. revealed significant alterations in the phosphorylation of crucial kinases and transcription factors (e.g., CCAR1 and CAMK) associated with the DNA damage response following X-ray exposure in D. japonica (Hu et al. 2025). These findings intricately connect the physical and chemical impacts of radiation (ionization, free radicals) with cellular fate (apoptosis, autophagy) and the depletion of stem cell functionality, elucidating a novel mechanism through which deleterious agents induce impaired regenerative capacity via post-translational modification pathways.

Host defense and compensatory regulatory network

Transcriptome analysis revealed that D. japonica can trigger gene networks associated with immune response, detoxification metabolism, energy supply, and DNA repair under glyphosate stress (Zhang et al. 2023). This highlights the intricate physiological responses employed by organisms to mitigate and restore damage through coordinated multi-pathway mechanisms. The breakdown of the repair network due to prolonged exposure is directly correlated with the resulting morphological anomalies and tissue injury.

Differentiated mechanisms dependent on pollutant forms

Studies combining multi-omics approaches have revealed significant differences in the toxicity mechanisms of n-ZnO, microparticles, and Zn2+ ions in D. japonica. n-ZnO predominantly generates protein coronas, leading to stable expression, mitochondrial impairment, and disruptions in energy metabolism. Conversely, μ-ZnO induced physical membrane damage and necrosis through particle accumulation. In contrast, Zn2+ ions penetrate cells directly, inducing oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress (Li et al. 2025). This mechanistic differentiation provides a molecular foundation for the precise evaluation of environmental hazards associated with diverse pollutants.

Effective strategies for linking omics data to phenotypic endpoints

Successful omics research goes beyond merely describing molecular changes; it establishes causal or strong associative links between molecular events and macroscopic phenotypes through the following strategies:

Temporal and dose-dependent correlation

By altering the exposure duration and concentration gradient, disruptions in the microbial community preceded the noticeable tissue damage. Additionally, alterations in the expression of certain genes/proteins coincided with or preceded changes in traits, such as reduced regenerative ability and aberrant movement patterns, suggesting potential causal or predictive associations.

Gene pathway enrichment combined with functional validation

Omics data were enriched and analyzed via pathways such as GO and KEGG, categorizing differential molecules into distinct biological pathways (e.g., fatty acid metabolism and DNA Damage Response (DDR) pathways). Following this, key targets underwent functional validation through methods such as RNAi, pharmacological inhibition, or overexpression (e.g., the five phosphorylated proteins validated in this study), confirming their involvement in specific phenotypes (e.g., regeneration inhibition and DNA repair defects).

Identification of key biomarkers

Omics research has identified several crucial microbial taxa (e.g., Curvibacter) and sites of gene or protein modifications that respond to specific pollutants. These molecular indicators not only serve as early indicators of toxicity but also exhibit a strong correlation with the extent of phenotypic harm, enabling quantitative analysis.

Summary and outlook

In conclusion, omics technology has been effectively utilized in planarian ecotoxicology research to systematically elucidate the intricate network mechanisms through which pollutants disrupt microecosystems, impede gene expression and protein function, and ultimately induce phenotypic harm. This study provides a comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of the toxicity mechanism by intricately linking high-throughput omics data with phenotypic endpoints at various levels, including temporal, dosage, pathway, and functional data.

Despite this, the utilization of omics techniques for investigating the ecotoxicology of planarians can be expanded in several ways: (1) Spatiotemporal omics studies should be performed to examine the dynamic spatial distribution of toxic effects in various tissues and throughout the regeneration process of planarians. (2) Enhancing the integration of metagenomics and metabolomics to comprehensively elucidate the interaction network involving “pollutants–microorganisms–host metabolism.” (3) Explore the long-term regenerative toxicity mechanism in depth, leverage the robust regenerative capabilities of planarians, and employ omics technology to investigate the enduring and transgenerational effects of pollutants on tissue regenerative capacity. These advancements will improve the predictive capabilities and practical utility of omics technologies in ecological risk assessment and the exploration of toxicological mechanisms.

The impact of planarian culture conditions on the comparability of toxicity data

Effects of the water composition used for cultivation/testing on the comparison of toxicity data

Various types of water utilized in planarian toxicology research include artificial freshwater (Wu and Li 2017), Montjuic medium (Yin et al. 2022), ASTM hard water (López et al. 2021), standard culture water (e.g., Hagstrom's formula) (Hagstrom et al. 2015), and modified Kanatani water (Mori et al. 2019). These water variants exhibit notable differences in their chemical composition (e.g., hardness, ionic composition, pH, and dissolved organic matter), potentially compromising the comparability of toxicity data.

Effects of water hardness on metal ion toxicity

Water hardness, primarily dictated by Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations, significantly influences the toxicity of heavy metals. According to the free ion activity model (FIAM), higher water hardness corresponds to lower free metal ion concentrations and reduced toxicity. For example, the Cu2+ LC50 is lower in soft water (hardness 200 mg/L as CaCO3) than in hard water. In various investigations, the LC50 of Cu2+ in D. japonica ranged from 3.46 mg/L to 8.70 mg/L, with differences possibly linked to variations in water hardness (Zhang et al. 2014; Wu and Li 2017). Furthermore, different metals exhibit varying sensitivities to hardness: Cd2+ and Zn2+ toxicity are markedly influenced by hardness (Plusquin et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012a; Wu and Li 2017; Li et al. 2025), whereas Hg2+ and methylmercury toxicity are less dependent on hardness, likely because of distinct metal complexation chemistry and bioavailability (Ireland et al. 2025).

Effects of pH on pollutant forms and toxicity

pH significantly influences the solubility, hydrolysis, and complexation of metal ions. In alkaline environments (pH > 7.5), metals such as Cu2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+ tend to precipitate as hydroxides or carbonates, decreasing the levels of free ions and their bioavailability (Cadmus et al. 2018). For example, compared with Fe3+ ions, Fe3+ forms Fe(OH)3 precipitates at a neutral pH, significantly decreasing its bioavailability under acidic conditions and thereby reducing its toxicity in water at pH 7–8 (Ding et al. 2019). Regarding organic pollutants, pH plays a crucial role in their ionization and hydrophobicity. Under acidic conditions (pH > 8), these substances ionize into an ionic form, leading to enhanced water solubility and decreased bioavailability. This pH-dependent behavior complicates the comparison of toxicity data across different studies.

The impact of dissolved organic matter (DOM)

Dissolved organic matter (DOM), including humic and fulvic acids, present in natural aquatic systems can form complexes with metals and hydrophobic organic contaminants, thereby influencing their bioavailability. Humic acid can form stable complexes with metals, thereby decreasing the concentration of free metal ions. Additionally, humic acid can interact with hydrophobic organic pollutants, increasing their solubility in water and decreasing their potential for bioaccumulation. In laboratory settings, studies often utilize deionized water or synthetic media, which lack natural DOM. This absence may result in the overestimation of toxicity data concerning metals because of the absence of complexation protection offered by DOM or the underestimation of data related to hydrophobic organic compounds because of the lack of solubilization effects from the DOM.

Ion composition interference for specific pollutants

Certain ions in the culture medium may compete with contaminants for binding sites or influence their absorption. For example, the levels of Na+ and K+ in the culture medium could impact the uptake of heavy metals by planarians because of shared ion channels and transporters. In the case of neurotoxic pollutants such as organophosphorus pesticides and AChE inhibitors, the presence of Ca2+ in the culture medium may modulate the excitability of the neuromuscular junction, consequently modifying the behavioral toxicity outcome.

Standardization requirements and recommendations

Enhancing the comparability and repeatability of planarian toxicological data requires (1) providing detailed reports of water quality parameters (hardness, Ca2+/Mg2+ concentration, pH, conductivity, and dissolved oxygen) in published research; (2) prioritizing the use of standardized water quality (such as ASTM hard water or ISO standard water) for acute toxicity testing; (3) considering the influence of water quality on specific pollutants in research design and conducting water quality correction when necessary; (4) investigating the correlation between water quality and toxicity, establishing correction factors, and facilitating the conversion of data under different water quality conditions; and (5) reporting the free ion concentration (which is calculated using chemical equilibrium models such as WHAM (Windermere humic aqueous model) (Kim et al. 2015) or Visual MINTEQ (Khalidy and Santos 2021)) instead of the total concentration in metal toxicity studies. Standardized and transparent reporting is essential for integrating planarian toxicological data on a broader scale, thereby establishing a reliable foundation for ecological risk assessment.

Future perspectives

Future research should prioritize (1) promoting the standardization of culture and testing conditions to improve data comparability; (2) advancing mechanism analysis and toxicity prediction through multiomics and artificial intelligence technologies; (3) conducting long-term low-dose compound exposure and intergenerational effects studies; and (4) integrating standardized planarian testing plans into environmental risk assessment frameworks.

Conclusion

This review systematically synthesizes the research advancements of planarian models in the field of ecotoxicology, highlighting their fundamental importance as a distinctive tool for toxicological studies. The key findings are as follows:

Planarians, known for their remarkable regenerative capacity driven by adult stem cells, have broadened the conventional scope of toxicological assessment to encompass the concept of impairing the self-repair capabilities of organisms, introducing a novel investigative framework of “regenerative toxicology.” The sequential progression of “stem cell disruption → abnormal organ regeneration → functional defects in the individual” offers unique insights into the enduring and subtle environmental hazards posed by contaminants.

Various pollutants, including metals, pesticides, PPCPs, particulate matter, and EDCs, induce oxidative stress in planarians. This oxidative insult subsequently triggers cellular stress responses, including apoptosis and DNA damage repair pathways, ultimately compromising the stem cell function and regenerative capacity. Nonetheless, distinct variations exist in the primary molecular targets, action kinetics, and ecological endpoints that are most susceptible to different pollutants (e.g., neurobehavior, regeneration, or reproduction), highlighting a “common pathway” in the response pattern of the “characteristic mechanism.”

The planarian model has demonstrated its utility as a rapid and comprehensive biosensor in various empirical studies involving environmental media and wastewater toxicity. It has been shown to sensitively detect complex mixtures and sublethal effects, addressing the limitations of chemical analysis in the early warning of ecological toxicity.

In conclusion, the planarian model has emerged as an essential tool in modern ecotoxicology because of its distinctive regenerative perspective, capacity to integrate multiple endpoints, and practical accessibility. This model offers robust support for gaining profound insights and issuing early alerts regarding the risks that pollutants pose to ecosystem well-being and population viability.
