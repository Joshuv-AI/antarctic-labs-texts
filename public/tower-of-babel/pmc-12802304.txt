Evaluating value measures across cultures: study on PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21 in Turkey
Authors: Engin Karadag, Hatice Ergin-Kocaturk, Suphan Nasir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Schwartz Value Survey (SVS) is a widely used instrument for assessing personal values across different cultures. However, various versions of the SVS have been developed over the years, leading to differences in item wording, response scales, and underlying theoretical frameworks. This article critically examines the implications of using different versions of the SVS in Turkish context. We review the history and development of the SVS, highlighting key modifications made to the survey instrument across different versions. We then discuss the potential impact of these variations on the measurement of personal values and the comparability of data collected from university students. Drawing on empirical studies and methodological literature, we explore the challenges and opportunities associated with using different SVS versions. We also provide recommendations for researchers on selecting the most appropriate version of the SVS for their study objectives and ensuring the validity and reliability. Overall, this article contributes to a better understanding of the complexities involved in assessing personal values across cultures and offers practical guidance for researchers navigating the nuances of different SVS versions in cross-cultural research contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03725-6.Keywords: Human values, Metric and scalar invariance, PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, PVQ-21

Introduction

The concept of “value” is multifaceted, with diverse meanings across disciplines ranging from psychology to philosophy and sociology and reflects the richness and complexity of human experience. Schwartz [1, 2] defines and summarizes value and features as what people think or believe “important” in their lives in broader terms. This definition brings forward “character-driven” aspect of values, that is, what is important to one may not be important to another. Also, they constitute for motivational constructions as they reflect the desires or goals of individuals, in other words, to acquire what is important to them. As they are connected with emotions, they are not expected be objective. Unlike norms and attitudes, they have an abstract nature, so they do not connect to specific actions however they guide actions. Schwartz also adds that each value has a different level of importance for individuals, in other words, values have a hierarchical nature which distinguishes from one person to another.

Schwartz’s comprehensive framework, Value Theory, for understanding the structure and dynamics of human values, is rooted in the idea that values represent broad, trans-situational goals that vary in importance and serve as guiding principles in people’s lives. Schwartz built upon earlier work by Rokeach, integrating and expanding upon their ideas to create a comprehensive framework [3]. Basically, the theory posits that values are enduring beliefs that guide individuals’ preferences, choices, and actions across various life domains. At the core of Schwartz’s Value Theory is the notion of a circular structure that organizes human values based on binary dimensions: openness to change vs. conservation and self-enhancement vs. self-transcendence. The first dimension reflects the tension between values emphasizing novelty, creativity, and independence (openness to change) and those emphasizing stability, tradition, and conformity (conservation). The second dimension captures the conflict between values that prioritize self-interest, power, and achievement (self-enhancement) and those emphasizing benevolence, universalism, and concern for others (self-transcendence). To represent these dimensions visually, Schwartz organized the values in a circular structure known as the circumplex model. This model positions opposing values at opposite ends of each dimension, creating a comprehensive and systematic framework for understanding the relationships between different values. The Value Theory also elaborates ten broad values based on the motivation behind them. Measuring values is a complex task, and Schwartz’s work represents a significant advancement in the study of values, providing a systematic and cross-culturally applicable model. The theory brings forward that there are universal values that cut across cultural and individual differences, providing a basis for understanding the fundamental motives that drive human behavior. Schwartz has developed several instruments to assess the structure and content of human values, which have been widely used in cross-cultural research. Through measurement tools like the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ) and the Schwartz Value Survey (SVS), researchers can assess individuals’ value priorities and explore the implications of these values for personal well-being, organizational culture, and societal development. In 1987, Schwartz and Bilsky [4] constructed a value theory based on universal requirements, which are biological needs, interactional necessities and societal demands for sake of the peoples. In this study, they came up with eight motivational domains: enjoyment, security, social power, achievement, self-direction, prosocial, restrictive conformity, and maturity. Findings of their study showed that data yielded that values could be clustered two folded: terminal vs. instrumental and the importance attributed to values such as depend on interests (individual vs. collectivist). Schwartz and Bilsky [5] conducted another study in 1990 to test universality of their theory with the participants from 5 different countries- Australia, Finland, Hong Kong, Spain, and the United States. They added the data from Germany and Israeli samples from their previous study. The data supported the hypothesis that universality of motivational value domains. They pointed out that relations between values are assumed to be predictors or criteria people apply to evaluate the human behavior. Also, our behavioral choices such as educational attainment, political views or religious practices may depend on the importance attributed to values.

To test the universality of aforementioned values and dynamic relations between them cross-culturally, Schwartz [3] collected data from 20 countries from different continents and different cultures. The scales he administered were in English and Hebrew. In this comprehensive work, he focused on basic contents of human values, defining a set of values, differences in value meanings for different groups and structure between values. At the end of the study, he concluded that ten different values-mentioned above- or priorities among cultures. Also, a set “highly” important value set emerged for different group of people with different cultural backgrounds and meanings attributed to this set of values did not differ significantly. This study also indicated that the theory of value could be utilized to conduct research cross-culturally on explaining integration of values with attitudes, behaviors and backgrounds. Based on the same universal requirements mentioned above, Schwartz [6] conducted another study by extending the motivational domains based on requirement of communication or interaction, which is inherent in human existence, to deal with complexities of reality within a social framework. Findings of his study supported the “near” universality of the four higher-order value types and their organization into two dimensions that shape value systems. Additionally, considerable evidence indicated that individuals, across contemporary societies, implicitly recognize the ten value types and the proposed conflicts and compatibilities among them when they contemplate the importance of their values. Furthermore, this method is valuable for analyzing the interplay between social structure and values, as well as for predicting and interpreting the relationships between values, attitudes, and behavior.

Schwartz and Boehnke [7] implemented 57-item version of Schwartz Value Survey to 46 samples from 27 countries in their native languages. In this study, they tested 10-factor motivational values and whether values were equally spaced in the circular continuum including their central and peripheral positioning. Also, they tested whether values that assumed to share the same motivational goal- tradition and conformity- would be evaluated under the same value factor, and higher orders types of values could be applied to explain the relations between them rather the 4-order type, which used agreed upon mostly. They also tested relations of hedonism with the other values and subtypes of universalism and security. Model testing results revealed the poor model fit with 10-factor value structure but a moderate fit with the quasi-circumplex of 10 factors model. However, modified quasi- circumplex structure instead of simple version was supported, which indicates that tradition values appeared on the edge of conformity values. Attempts to merge closely related values, such as tradition with conformity or power with achievement, were not supported. Similarly, the grouping of higher-order value types did not improve model fit. However, this supported the notion that values form a motivational continuum with which researchers could designate the value order and relations. Lastly, hedonism remained stable, while universalism and security appeared to have subdimensions that may enhance understanding of value structures. Correlations between values confirmed oppositions between conflicting types, suggesting that future studies should further explore conceptual clarity and potential subtypes of values.

Based on the same motivational continuum, Schwartz et al. [8] proposed a refined version of original version including ten values with the support of data from 10 countries. In this study, PVQ40 was developed and consists of one sentence for one item with 6-point scale. 19 factors in the refined version are self-direction–thought, self-direction–action, stimulation, hedonism, achievement, power-dominance, power-resources, face, security-personal, security-societal, tradition, conformity-rules, conformity-interpersonal, humility, benevolence-dependability, benevolence-caring, universalism-concern, universalism-nature, universalism-tolerance. Validity and reliability tests supported refined version and researchers suggested to implement the PVQ40 for larger groups of samples even with the shorter version.

Later, in 2022, Schwartz and Cieciuh [9] designed another study to test reliability and validity of PVQ-RR based on the refined model with 19 values. Since its first publication, PVQ has been adapted to many languages and cultures. In this study, they evaluated validity and reliability of the instruments in all 32 language-version with 49 different cultural groups of samples. The findings indicate that the PVQ-RR demonstrates robust internal reliability, effectively measuring 15 of the 19 values consistently across the vast majority of cultural groups, with two others reliably measured in most groups. Moreover, the measurement models based on the theory yielded a good fit, indicating successful differentiation of nearly all values within each cultural context. Overall, this comprehensive study solidifies the PVQ-RR as a reliable instrument for measuring and comparing the hierarchies and correlates of values across different cultures, offering valuable insights into the universal aspects of human values.

Schwartz’s Value Theory has been instrumental in understanding how values influence individual behavior across various domains. Research has demonstrated that values can shape people’s attitudes, preferences, and decision-making processes [10–12]. The theory also provides insights into the dynamics of value conflicts and trade-offs. Individuals may experience tension between competing values, leading to internal struggles and cognitive dissonance. For instance, a person valuing both tradition (conservation) and autonomy (openness to change) may face challenges in navigating situations that require adherence to established norms while also seeking personal independence [1].

Schwartz’s Value Theory extends beyond individual behavior to shed light on broader social and cultural phenomena. Cross-cultural studies using the theory have identified cultural value orientations that distinguish societies and influence their social institutions. For example, societies emphasizing self-enhancement values may exhibit a greater emphasis on individual achievement and competition, while those emphasizing self-transcendence values may prioritize collective well-being and cooperation [6].

The theory has also been applied to the study of political ideologies, with research suggesting that certain values align with conservative or liberal orientations. Understanding the value foundations of political beliefs can provide valuable insights into the dynamics of social and political movements [13].

While Schwartz’s Value Theory has made significant contributions to the understanding of human values, it is not without its critiques. Some scholars argue that the circumplex structure oversimplifies the complexity of human values, neglecting the nuances and context-specific nature of value priorities. Others highlight the challenge of capturing the dynamic and fluid nature of values, which may change over time and in response to different life circumstances cross-culturally contexts [14, 15]. In this respect, the main purpose of this study is to evaluate psychometric features of three different versions of original Schwartz Value Survey and to discuss their fitness for Turkish context.

This study

Recent studies have continued to validate and refine Schwartz’s value theory. For instance, Schwartz and Cieciuch [9] confirmed the psychometric robustness of the PVQ-RR across 49 cultural groups, emphasizing its cross-cultural validity and its ability to reliably distinguish between refined value types. Similarly, Bojanowska and Piotrowski [16] explored value-behavior relations using revised PVQ versions, demonstrating that even brief instruments can meaningfully predict behavioral tendencies, supporting the model’s practical relevance in both individual and societal contexts. Studies such as Riemer and Šolcová [17] further highlight the adaptability of the circumplex structure and the hierarchical organization of values across cultural settings, such as Eastern Europe, showing how value types can be contextually expressed while maintaining structural consistency.

Other recent research also supports the utility and refinement of Schwartz’s value theory. For example, Sortheix and Schwartz [18] examined longitudinal associations between values and well-being across European countries, revealing stable cross-cultural value effects over time. Vecchione et al. [19] evaluated the effectiveness of various PVQ versions in capturing developmental value shifts in adolescence, confirming their sensitivity to age-related motivational dynamics. Additionally, Doerfel et al. [20] applied the PVQ framework to analyze collective values in online communities, demonstrating that the PVQ remains relevant in digital and non-traditional environments.

These studies collectively underscore the need to evaluate psychometric tools not only across cultures but also within them—ensuring that versions of the PVQ function equivalently and meaningfully across subpopulations and instrument formats. In light of these findings, it becomes critical to assess how PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21 perform within the Turkish context, particularly in terms of factorial validity, reliability, value hierarchies, and sensitivity to demographic variation. This aligns with the present study’s aims to determine which instrument is most appropriate for use in Turkish research and educational settings while maintaining fidelity to Schwartz’s theoretical framework.

In the Turkish context, adaptations of the PVQ have highlighted deviations from the theoretical circumplex, such as overlaps between tradition and conformity or variations in the positioning of achievement and security values [21, 22]. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating the fit and reliability of different PVQ forms within specific cultural settings.

Despite the widespread use of these instruments, few studies have conducted direct comparisons of multiple PVQ versions within the same cultural context. Such comparisons are critical for guiding researchers in choosing the most appropriate tool for their objectives and populations, especially in settings where survey length, participant burden, and data quality must be balanced. The present study aims to evaluate and compare the psychometric properties of three widely used versions of the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21) within a Turkish university student sample. So, the main research problem of this study is “How do the PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21 differ in terms of validity, reliability, and factorial structure in a Turkish cultural context?” Based on this research questions, sub-problems are:

By addressing these questions, this study distinguishes itself from previous research in several key ways and makes a unique contribution to the literature. Although the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ) is widely used, most studies examine only one version at a time. Direct within-sample comparisons are rare, yet essential for understanding how scale length affects validity, reliability, and measurement precision. This study focused on that gap by comparing PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21 within a Turkish university sample. By doing so, it provides practical guidance on instrument selection and contributes to refining Schwartz’s value theory in a collectivist cultural context. These findings are also expected to strengthen the cross-cultural applicability of value measurement tools.

Method

Participants and ethical considerations

This study employed a psychometric evaluation design comparing the PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21. Data were collected through an online survey administered to 3,710 university students from various institutions across Turkey. Of these participants, 1,954 (52.6%) identified as female and 1,756 (47.4%) as male. The age range was 18 to 28 years (M = 21.33; SD = 2.99). Participants were fairly distributed across academic years: 20% first year, 22.8% second year, 31.4% third year, and 25.8% fourth year students.

Ethical approval was granted by the Social and Humanities Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (İstanbul University, 05/04/2021, Decision 88). The research packages including demographic questions and scale items were prepared and the participants were contacted. Firstly, the purpose of the research was explained to the participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and anonymity and confidentiality of the data were maintained throughout the research process.

Measurements

Portrait Values Questionnaire [8]

In various versions of the Portrait Values ​​Questionnaire (PVQ), indirect measurement is run to obtain the values in Schwartz theories [3, 8]. Rather than abstract, value names, PVQ elements briefly describe different people in terms of goals, aspirations, or aspirations they deem important in life instead of abstract and value names [9]. For each person, the PVQ asks “How much like you is this person?” on a 6- point scale (1 = not like me at all; 2 = not like me; 3 = a little like me; 4 = moderately like me; 5 = like me; 6 = very much like me). It is confirmed that each of the three common versions of the PVQ (PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21) captures the cyclical motivational nature of values ​​and distinguishes four higher order values [8, 23, 24]. Versions of the PVQ differ in the number of items they contain and the number of values they are designed to measure. Procedure

The digital survey packages were developed, each containing one PVQ version and basic demographic questions. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three versions and completed the survey online. This random assignment ensured an even distribution of participants across the three forms.

Data analysis

To assess construct validity, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using LISREL 8.51. CFA was preferred due to its suitability for testing theory-driven models and evaluating factor structure consistency. Partial Least Squares (PLS) estimation was used to accommodate potential violations of multivariate normality, common in large psychological datasets.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: Chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ2/df), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI). Acceptable model fit was defined by RMSEA < 0.08 and CFI/NFI > 0.90.

Convergent validity was assessed via Average Variance Extracted (AVE), with values above 0.50 considered satisfactory. Internal consistency was measured using Cronbach’s alpha and Composite Reliability (CR), with reliability coefficients above 0.70 deemed acceptable.

To evaluate external validity, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine relationships between values and age. One-way multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to explore gender-based differences in value scores. MANOVA was chosen for its ability to assess multiple dependent variables while controlling for Type I error.

Finally, value hierarchies were compared across the three PVQ versions based on rank order and mean scores. Correlations among value dimensions were also computed to evaluate internal structural consistency. Descriptive analyzes were run with SPSS version 23.0 and DFA with LISREL version 8.51.

To investigate the convergent validity of PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21, the average variance subtracted (AVE) was calculated (Table 2). As AVE value, Model 1 (PVQ-RR) produced 0.76, Model 2 (PVQ-40) 0.79 and Model 3 (PVQ-21) produced 0.81, therefore, it can be said that all dimensions have convergent validity [24].

Reliability

To investigate the reliability of PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21, Cronbach’s α and composite reliability (CR) were calculated (Tables 2 and 3). Based on Cronbach’s α and composite reliability (CR), internal consistency was satisfactory for all three versions. Therefore, questions in all dimensions are highly correlated, indicating that they measure the same latent construct. Measurement invariance testing

To ensure that the three versions of the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21) measure the same latent constructs equivalently across forms, a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) was conducted. All analyses were performed using LISREL 8.51 with the Partial Least Squares (PLS) estimation method, as it provides robust results in large samples where multivariate normality assumptions may be violated. Model fit was evaluated based on the following indices: χ²/df, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).

Configural Invariance

The first step tested whether the same ten-factor structure existed across the three PVQ forms without constraining any parameters. All three versions demonstrated acceptable model fit (Table 6). These results confirmed that all three instruments shared the same basic factorial structure, supporting configural invariance.

Metric Invariance

In the second step, factor loadings were constrained to equality across groups. The resulting model showed minimal changes in fit indices (ΔCFI = 0.002–0.004; ΔRMSEA = 0.001–0.002), all below recommended thresholds (ΔCFI < 0.01; ΔRMSEA < 0.015). This indicates that the relationships between items and their corresponding factors were consistent across all PVQ versions, thus confirming metric invariance.

Scalar Invariance

In the final step, item intercepts were constrained to be equal across groups to test the comparability of mean scores. Changes in fit indices remained within acceptable limits (ΔCFI < 0.01; ΔRMSEA < 0.015), suggesting that the three instruments measure value constructs on the same scale. Therefore, scalar invariance was also achieved, meaning that group mean comparisons among PVQ forms are statistically valid (Table 7). Results from the MGCFA indicate that the three PVQ versions exhibit measurement invariance across all tested levels. Each version preserved the ten-factor structure originally proposed by Schwartz, measuring the same latent dimensions—self-direction, universalism, security, benevolence, hedonism, achievement, stimulation, conformity, tradition, and power—with consistent item–factor relationships. Although the PVQ-RR displayed slightly higher reliability due to its extended item pool, both PVQ-40 and PVQ-21 yielded comparable model fit and factorial validity indices. Given its brevity, strong factorial validity, and practical advantages, PVQ-21 emerges as the most suitable version for value research within the Turkish context.

Group comparisons

All three versions of the PQV (PVQ-RR, PVQ-40, and PVQ-21) were associated with exogenous variables of gender and age. The factors of PVQ are conceptually related. Additionally, factors are interrelated based on the data collected, as shown in Table 5. Therefore, one-way multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was calculated to evaluate the PVQ scores between males and females. The MANOVA result showed significant differences between males and females in the PVQ-RR (λ = 0.813, F = 4.097, p =.001, partial η2 = 0.187). Females got higher scores in benevolence (p =.012, partial η2 = 0.055), universalism (p =.000, partial η2 = 0.064), self-direction (p =.002, partial η2 = 0.084), hedonism (p =.036, partial η2 = 0.039) and security (p =.000, partial η2 = 0.162) factors. There is no gender difference in other factors. The MANOVA result showed the differences between males and females were significant (λ = 0.895, F = 2.396, p =.01, partial η2 = 0.105) for PVQ-40. Females got higher scores in universalism (p =.005, partial η2 = 0.037), hedonism (p =.038, partial η2 = 0.020), security (p =.001, partial η2 = 0.049) dimensions; males scored higher in power (p =.042, η2 = 0.019). There is no gender difference in other factors. The MANOVA result the differences between males and females were significant (λ = 0.860, F = 4.315, p =.001, partial η2 = 0.140) for PVQ-21. Females got higher scores in benevolence (p =.007, partial η2 = 0.026), universalism (p =.003, partial η2 = 0.033), hedonism (p =.008, partial η2 = 0.025) and security (p =.047, partial η2 = 0.013) factors. There is no gender difference in other factors.

The correlation analysis matched with the relationships with the age exogenous variable closely in terms of direction and significance in all three versions.

Discussion

Schwartz’s Value Theory, developed by psychologist Shalom H. Schwartz, has been a cornerstone in the study of human values and their impact on behavior, culture, and society. Since its inception in the early 1990s, Schwartz’s theory has gained widespread recognition and has been widely applied in various fields, including psychology, sociology, organizational behavior, marketing, and cross-cultural research. Additionally, Schwartz and his colleagues have developed refined versions of the PVQ, such as the Schwartz Value Survey (SVS), which provides a more concise and updated assessment of individual values. These measurement tools have been translated into numerous languages, allowing for cross-cultural comparisons and enhancing the generalizability of findings including Turkish culture based on a pan-cultural importance of values [23, 25, 26]. However, like any other culture, researchers focus culture-specific domains while examining Turkish culture such as respect [27]. Respectively, the expression of values can indeed vary significantly across different cultures. While certain values may be universally recognized, such as honesty or respect, the way these values are interpreted and manifested in behavior can differ based on cultural norms, beliefs, and societal expectations [28]. Based on these premises, the aim of this study is to compare the psychometric evaluations and diagnostic accuracy of the original PVQ-RR and its shorter versions, PQ-40 and PQ-21. Accordingly, the convergent and discriminant validity, reliability, and external correlations of all three versions of the PQV (PVQ-RR, PVQ-40 and PVQ-21) were evaluated with data obtained from 3,710 university students in Turkey.

While the findings of this study confirm the robustness of Schwartz’s value theory and the applicability of its instruments across different forms within a Turkish sample, all three PVQ versions demonstrated acceptable factorial validity and internal consistency. The PVQ-RR, due to its extended item pool, showed slightly higher reliability indices, aligning with findings from Schwartz and Cieciuch [9], who emphasized the psychometric strength of the refined PVQ-RR across cultures. Recent studies further contextualize these results. For instance, Bojanowska and Piotrowski [16] found that revised PVQ versions maintained high predictive utility in value-behavior associations. Similarly, Riemer and Šolcová [17] observed structural consistency in Eastern European contexts, reinforcing the idea that Schwartz’s circumplex model remains culturally robust. The findings also resonate with those of Vecchione et al. [19], who noted that brief PVQ versions like the PVQ-21 are particularly effective in developmental research contexts.

Within the Turkish context, earlier work by Demirutku [21] and Karadag et al. [22] identified structural anomalies such as value overlap and deviation from theoretical order. The present results confirmed similar patterns, particularly with convergence between tradition and conformity, and indicated that while all three versions reproduced the general value hierarchy, minor positional shifts emerged. These shifts likely reflect cultural emphases on collectivism, respect for authority, and context-driven interpretations of abstract values. Despite these nuances, strong inter-item correlations and stable value hierarchies suggest that even shortened PVQ forms retain core conceptual integrity. Additionally, the consistent gender differences—where females scored higher on universalism, benevolence, and security—mirror earlier findings [8], indicating both stability and cross-form reliability in value-related individual differences.

Results showed that factor analysis of all three versions confirmed the ten-factor construct in line with previous research [3, 6, 29]. Besides, the CFA results indicated a satisfactory model fit and convergent validity of all factors. The PVQ-RR outperformed PVQ-40 and PVQ-21 in nearly every factor in its internal consistency coefficients. As expected, the PVQ-RR with more items per factor outperformed the versions with fewer items per factor (PVQ-40 and PVQ-21). On the other hand, Cronbach’s α and composite reliability coefficients are satisfactory in all three versions.

When analyzed according to the gender exogenous variable, it was found that the differences between males and females were significant in all three versions of the PQV. It was also seen that females scored higher than males on similar factors in all three versions. This difference is consistent with previous results for the original PVQ-RR. Correlation coefficients calculated to assess its correlation with the age exogenous variable matched closely in direction and significance in all three versions. These correlations are consistent with previous results for the original PVQ-RR [8].

Another study focusing on reliability and validity of SVS and PVQ instruments into Turkish were visited. Demirutku [21] adapted PVQ-40 into Turkish and found out that the internal consistency and test-retest reliabilities of the ten domains measured by the PVQ are considered acceptable, especially given the small number of items designed for each domain while few displacements were observed in the structural arrangement of values. Also, these displacements tended to occur in theoretically compatible domains. were observed in the arrangement of values within the circumplex structure. For instance, values related to the Achievement domain were positioned midway between the Security and Power domains, which is theoretically unexpected. Similarly, an item from the Benevolence domain was displaced into the Security domain, potentially due to its perceived association with psychological security. A significant divergence from the theoretical model was observed in the merging of the Tradition and Conformity domains. This merging suggests that, within the Turkish cultural context, collectivistic tendencies may lead to the convergence of these two value domains, which are theoretically adjacent but distinct according to Schwartz’s model. On the other hand, Tevruz et al. [28] found that the motivational continuum of values, which is typically represented as a circular structure, was largely consistent with pan-cultural results. However, there was an addition of another value type to the openness to change pole. While some items in this region reflected autonomy of thought, other items diverged from this theme. However, near-perfectly similar correlations between the factors of all three versions of the PQV were observed in this study. Again, when the ranking and mean scores for each PVQ were compared with each other, it was seen that there were very low deviations. Based on these results, it can be said that the PVQ-RR has similar structures in confirming the value hierarchy.

Results from adaptation of SVS lacks construct validity when applied within the Turkish cultural context. Specifically, when Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted, it became evident that the original factor structure of the SVS does not hold true within Turkish culture. The goodness-of-fit indices from the CFA indicated that the variance and covariance of the survey were not adequately explained by the model, further indicating a lack of validity. Additionally, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) results showed that all items on the SVS clustered around 12 factors. Furthermore, some factors appeared to overlap or load onto different factors, suggesting inconsistencies in the underlying structure of the survey within the Turkish cultural context [22]. In this study, even though all of three instruments were proven to be reliable and valid, PVQ-21 came forward in terms of practicality. When overlapping structure was considered mentioned above, it including less items than others may result in coherence in terms of content validity.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering cultural variations when assessing the validity and reliability of psychological measures. It’s crucial to ensure that instruments are appropriate and accurately reflect the constructs they intend to measure within different cultural settings. Also, these findings highlight the nuanced ways in which individuals from different cultures may prioritize and interpret motivational goals. It suggests that while certain overarching patterns may hold true across cultures, there are also unique cultural influences that shape individuals’ responses to motivational stimuli. The convergence of tradition and conformity observed in the Turkish sample can be better understood through the lens of collectivist cultural orientations. In collectivist societies, values such as respect for authority, maintenance of social harmony, and adherence to group norms often blur conceptual boundaries between individual value domains that are more distinct in individualistic cultural contexts [30, 31]. In Turkey, traditional value orientations—emphasizing continuity, respect for customs, and stability—are closely intertwined with conformity values, which stress compliance with social expectations and group order (Kağıtçıbaşı, 1996; Schwartz, 2006). This overlap is likely reinforced through family-centered socialization processes, where obedience, respect for elders, and maintaining interdependence are viewed as key virtues [25, 32]. Such structural convergence indicates that value hierarchies are culturally embedded rather than universal fixed constructs. From a cross-cultural perspective, this suggests that deviations from Schwartz’s circumplex model should not be treated merely as psychometric anomalies but as meaningful reflections of culturally shaped motivational priorities [8, 33]. Integrating these cultural factors into value research enhances both the interpretive depth and comparability of cross-national studies.

After comparing and validating the psychometric properties of the three versions of the PVQ for use, we found that both PVQ-40 and PVQ-21 were compatible from the original version (PVQ-RR). This indicates that especially PVQ-21 is more suitable for use. Therefore, we recommend using the PVQ-21 instead of the PVQ-RR for value measurements because PVQ-21 has similar psychometric properties to the 57-item PVQ-RR, and PVQ-21 is more likely to replicate the value hierarchy of the PVQ-RR than the PVQ-40. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering cultural variations when assessing the validity and reliability of psychological measures. It’s crucial to ensure that instruments are appropriate and accurately reflect the constructs they intend to measure within different cultural settings.

Limitations and suggestions for future research

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study did not include metric and scalar invariance testing across PVQ forms. Without such analysis, it remains unclear whether the value constructs measured are interpreted equivalently across instruments. Future studies should employ multi-group CFA or alignment optimization methods to assess measurement invariance. Second, the sample was limited to university students, which restricts generalizability. Although geographically diverse, the sample did not include older adults or individuals from varied occupational backgrounds. Future research should recruit more heterogeneous populations. Third, the exclusive use of self-report instruments may have introduced response biases, such as social desirability. Incorporating alternative data collection methods—such as behavioral tasks or qualitative interviews—could provide more nuanced insights. Finally, while all three PVQ versions demonstrated sound psychometric properties, their utility may differ by research goal. PVQ-21 may be more appropriate in large-scale or time-constrained contexts, whereas PVQ-RR offers greater depth for studies focused on fine-grained value analysis. Future research should explore the application of PVQ instruments in longitudinal, experimental, or cross-modal designs.

Instrument choice should align with the intended research purpose, sample characteristics, and administration context. The PVQ-RR, with its extensive item pool, provides high-resolution value mapping, making it suitable for studies requiring fine-grained analysis, such as theoretical model testing, in-depth cultural comparisons, or advanced structural modeling. PVQ-40 represents a balanced option for studies that aim to maintain good psychometric sensitivity while reducing administration time. PVQ-21, as the shortest version, is particularly advantageous in large-scale surveys, longitudinal research, or settings where participant time is limited. While shorter, PVQ-21 retains conceptual robustness and is appropriate for applied research contexts prioritizing efficiency without compromising reliability. Conclusion

The concept of value, with its intricate and interconnected meanings, reflects the richness and complexity of human experience. From economic considerations of utility to psychological explorations of enduring beliefs, philosophical reflections on morality, and sociological investigations into cultural dynamics, the concept of value permeates diverse fields of study. Recognizing and understanding these various dimensions of value contributes to a holistic comprehension of how individuals, societies, and cultures navigate the intricate tapestry of human values. Schwartz’s Value Theory, with its nuanced understanding of the complexities of human values, continues to be a valuable and influential framework in the social sciences. It has emerged as an influential framework for understanding the role of values in shaping individual and societal behavior. Its conceptualization of values as a core aspect of human motivation, its development of measurement tools, and its application across diverse disciplines have made it a central reference point in academic and applied research. In this study, Turkish cultural adaptability of three main data collection instruments developed by Schwartz and his colleagues, PVQ-21, PVQ-40, PVQ-RR. Results supported Schwartz’s pan-cultural approach to values and PVQ-21 came forward in terms of practicality. As scholars continue to refine and expand upon Schwartz’s work, the theory is likely to remain a key resource for gaining insights into the complexities of human values and their impact on the human experience. Future research in this area may explore the interplay between values and other psychological constructs, such as personality traits and cognitive processes, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of human behavior. Additionally, ongoing efforts to refine measurement tools and expand cross-cultural research will contribute to the continued development and applicability of Schwartz’s Value Theory. Lastly, in 2024, another 17-item inventory by Schwartz and his colleagues [34] was published. Future researchers may also focus on cross-cultural dimensions of this instrument.
