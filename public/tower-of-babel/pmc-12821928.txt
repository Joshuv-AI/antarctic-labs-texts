Pineal region hemangioblastoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Bo Tan, Yuyuan Li, Xingrong Zhang, Bin Yang, Yaohui Huang, Xiaohong Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPineal region hemangioblastoma(PR-HB) is an exceptionally rare vascular tumor, often misdiagnosed due to overlap with more common pineal lesions. Accurate identification is essential for safe surgical management.Case presentationWe report a 54-year-old woman presenting with acute dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. MRI revealed a vividly enhancing pineal mass with prominent flow voids causing obstructive hydrocephalus. The tumor was removed via a left paramedian supracerebellar infratentorial approach, achieving gross-total resection. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed hemangioblastoma (inhibin-α positive, PAX8 negative). The patient recovered uneventfully, and no recurrence was observed at 12-month follow-up.DiscussionAlthough extremely rare, pineal hemangioblastoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of hypervascular pineal masses. Review of previously published cases demonstrates consistent radiological features, diagnostic pitfalls, surgical considerations, and the importance of distinguishing hemangioblastoma from metastatic renal cell carcinoma.ConclusionPineal hemangioblastoma is a surgically curable entity. Recognition of its imaging and immunohistochemical characteristics facilitates safe resection and accurate diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03435-1.

Introduction

Hemangioblastomas (HBs) are rare, benign, WHO grade I vascular tumors accounting for approximately 1–2.5% of intracranial neoplasms, primarily affecting the cerebellum, brainstem, or spinal cord [1]. Roughly 20–30% are associated with von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease [2]. Pineal region tumors represent less than 1% of central nervous system neoplasms and are predominantly germ cell tumors (GCTs), pineal parenchymal tumors, and metastases [3–7]. Hemangioblastomas originating in the pineal region are exceedingly rare, with only a small number of cases reported to date.

Because pineal hemangioblastomas share radiological and histopathological features with other clear-cell tumors—particularly metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC)—preoperative misdiagnosis is common [8]. Inaccurate identification may increase surgical risk given the tumor’s pronounced vascularity [9, 10]. Recognition of characteristic imaging findings and appropriate immunohistochemical markers is therefore essential for accurate diagnosis and safe operative planning.

In this report, we describe a sporadic pineal region hemangioblastoma treated at our institution and place our findings in the context of previously published cases [5, 6, 11]. By summarizing key diagnostic considerations, radiological features, surgical nuances, and immunohistochemical distinctions, this case highlights important clinical lessons relevant to the management of this extremely rare entity [12, 13].

Case presentation

A 54-year-old female presented with acute-onset dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. MRI revealed a 3.0 × 2.8 cm pineal mass that was isointense on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2, with prominent serpiginous flow voids and intense homogeneous contrast enhancement. Discussion

Literature review

Methodology of literature review on PR-HB

Due to the extreme rarity of pineal region hemangioblastomas (PR-HB), fewer than 10 cases have been reported in the literature. To further contextualize our case, we conducted a descriptive review of previously published reports. We reviewed studies indexed in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, published before July 1, 2025. The following search terms were used: “pineal hemangioblastoma,” “PR-HB,” “pineal tumor hemangioblastoma,” and “von Hippel–Lindau pineal.”

Inclusion Criteria: (1)Histologically confirmed pineal hemangioblastoma.(2)Case reports, case series, or cohort studies.(3)Studies published in English or with an English abstract.

Exclusion Criteria: (1)Ambiguous tumor location.(2)Non-pineal hemangioblastomas.(3)Duplicated data across studies.

The review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, although a meta-analysis was not feasible due to the limited number of cases. Variables extracted from the included studies included patient demographics, VHL status, clinical symptoms, radiological characteristics, surgical approach, immunohistochemistry (IHC), postoperative complications, and outcomes.

Data screening and extraction were performed independently by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Summary of published PR-HB cases

Pineal region hemangioblastomas (PR-HBs) are exceedingly rare, with only six histologically confirmed cases, including ours, reported in the literature [3–7, 14]. Despite their WHO grade I benign classification, these tumors pose significant diagnostic and surgical challenges due to their deep-seated location and marked vascularity [15, 16]. Our institutional case and literature review highlight important clinical, radiographic, surgical, immunohistochemical, and molecular characteristics that aid in the recognition and management of this entity [17, 18].

Radiological pitfalls and differential diagnosis

MRI plays a pivotal role in the preoperative identification of PR-HBs [19]. The most striking and consistent imaging feature is the presence of serpiginous intratumoral flow voids, indicative of the tumor’s rich vascular network [20]. On contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images, these lesions typically demonstrate homogeneous or slightly heterogeneous enhancement with minimal surrounding edema [15, 21].This distinctive feature helps distinguish PR-HBs from other pineal tumors.

However, the radiological appearance of PR-HBs can overlap with more common pineal tumors such as germinomas, pineoblastomas, and metastatic RCC [8]. Germinomas are often isointense on T1, hyperintense on T2, and show uniform enhancement, but they frequently occur in younger males and may be associated with elevated serum β-HCG or PLAP [19]. Pineoblastomas tend to be more aggressive and infiltrative, often presenting with restricted diffusion and cystic components [21]. RCC metastases pose a particular diagnostic challenge due to their hypervascularity and clear cell histology, and misdiagnosis may lead to inappropriate systemic investigations or treatment delays [8]. Histopathological and immunohistochemical distinction

Given the histologic overlap between hemangioblastomas and metastatic RCC, especially in clear cell-rich tumors, immunohistochemistry (IHC) is essential for diagnostic accuracy [22, 23]. In all reported cases, including ours, PR-HBs consistently demonstrated positivity for inhibin-α, neuron-specific enolase (NSE), and CD34 (vascular channels), and were uniformly negative for PAX8, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), cytokeratin, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) [24–26]. The absence of PAX8 is particularly important in excluding RCC, as it is a sensitive marker for renal origin [26]. Thus, a focused IHC panel should be employed whenever a vascular pineal tumor is encountered [23].

Surgical approaches and technical considerations

Gross-total resection (GTR) is the gold standard for treatment and was successfully achieved in all resected cases in our review [27]. The supracerebellar infratentorial (SCIT) approach was the most commonly employed due to its direct trajectory to the pineal region and avoidance of cortical transgression [27–29]. However, the choice of surgical corridor should be tailored based on tumor size, extension, and venous anatomy [30–32].

In addition to the conventional SCIT route, several minimal-access variations to the pineal region have been proposed in recent years to further minimize cortical retraction and venous injury. Feigl and Kuzmin described these alternative minimally invasive approaches and their anatomic feasibility for pineal tumors [33], which broaden the surgical armamentarium for this deep-seated region.Hemangioblastomas are among the most vascular tumors encountered in neurosurgery [9]. Intraoperative bleeding can be brisk and obscuring, requiring meticulous microsurgical dissection and early control of feeding vessels [10, 34]. Molecular and genetic considerations

Approximately 50% of PR-HBs in our review were associated with von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease [6, 36]. VHL-related hemangioblastomas often present earlier and may be multifocal or recurrent [7, 37]. Therefore, all patients with PR-HB should undergo VHL genetic testing and screening for systemic lesions such as retinal angiomas, renal cysts, or pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors [37].

On a molecular level, VHL inactivation leads to upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α, HIF-2α) and subsequent overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), contributing to the tumor’s vascular nature [38]. Belzutifan, a selective HIF-2α inhibitor recently approved for VHL-associated tumors, has demonstrated efficacy in reducing HB burden in unresectable or multifocal disease [39]. Although its role in sporadic PR-HB remains investigational, future studies may explore its use in high-risk or surgically challenging cases [40].This targeted approach could potentially benefit patients with multifocal lesions or those where surgery may not be feasible due to the tumor’s location or vascularity.

Comparative analysis of VHL-associated vs. sporadic pineal hemangioblastomas

Among the six cases of PR-HBs reviewed, three cases were associated with von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease and three were sporadic. The mean age of patients with VHL-associated PR-HBs was 38.3 years (range 30–45 years), while sporadic cases had a higher mean age of 44.7 years (range 35–54 years). VHL-related hemangioblastomas tend to present earlier in life and may be associated with multifocal tumors or recurrence, whereas sporadic cases generally present as isolated tumors with no recurrence at short-to-medium-term follow-up (mean 12.3 months). In our review, none of the VHL-associated cases experienced recurrence, although longer follow-up periods are needed for definitive conclusions regarding recurrence patterns in VHL cases. Sporadic tumors have been associated with excellent postoperative outcomes, with all cases achieving gross-total resection (GTR) and no recurrence reported.

Limitations and future directions

This study is limited by its small sample size and retrospective nature. The rarity of PR-HB precludes robust statistical analysis, and publication bias may lead to overrepresentation of successfully resected cases. Furthermore, the absence of uniform imaging protocols and follow-up duration across reports limits comparative assessment.

Future multi-institutional registries or collaborations are needed to aggregate data on PR-HBs, including molecular profiling, intraoperative strategies, and long-term outcomes. Integration of artificial intelligence-based radiomics may also assist in non-invasive differentiation of vascular pineal lesions.

Management of hydrocephalus

In patients with PR-HBs, obstructive hydrocephalus is a common preoperative finding, as seen in all reported cases, including our own. Hydrocephalus occurs due to the tumor’s location obstructing the cerebral aqueduct or fourth ventricle. In our institutional case, hydrocephalus was resolved postoperatively following gross-total resection (GTR), and preoperative CSF diversion was not required.

The decision to forgo preoperative CSF diversion is based on several considerations. First, obstructive hydrocephalus caused by a pineal region tumor often improves significantly after tumor resection, as demonstrated in the majority of published cases. Second, performing preoperative CSF diversion—such as ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunting or external ventricular drainage (EVD)—may increase operative risks, including infection, shunt malfunction, and increased venous congestion, particularly when dealing with highly vascular tumors like hemangioblastomas. This management approach aligns with several reports in the literature where preoperative CSF diversion was not necessary, as resection of the tumor typically resolves the obstructive hydrocephalus without significant postoperative complications.

Conclusion

Although extremely rare, hemangioblastoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of solid, enhancing pineal masses with prominent flow voids. Familiarity with its radiologic, histologic, and immunophenotypic features allows for preoperative suspicion and safe resection. Gross-total resection is curative in sporadic cases, and novel therapies such as belzutifan may benefit VHL patients.
