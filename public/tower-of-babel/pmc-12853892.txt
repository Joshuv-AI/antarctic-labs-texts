The diagnostic dilemma of idiopathic granulomatous mastitis with an emphasis on histopathologic findings
Authors: Kiana Anousha, Behnaz Jahanbin, Farid Azmoudeh Ardalan, Vahid Soleimani, Maryam Azizi, Amin Rezvani

Background

Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM) is a rare, benign inflammatory breast disease with a vague clinical course and poorly understood pathogenesis. This entity often occurs in parous women with a history of breastfeeding, though it can affect men and women of all age groups [1, 2]. Considering IGM has multiple facets, precise evaluation of clinical, radiological, and histological findings is necessary for an accurate diagnosis. This becomes especially important due to the possibility of a lower recurrence rate with early diagnosis [3].

Neither the clinical presentations nor the radiologic manifestations are consistent among the patients. Clinically, IGM can have various presentations, including palpable breast mass, pain, erythema, skin fistula, and nipple discharge [4]. In radiologic studies, findings may vary from increased vascularity and thickened skin to an irregularly shaped mass or axillary lymphadenopathy [5]. Moreover, the clinical and radiological features can overlap with a wide range of benign and malignant breast conditions, posing a great diagnostic challenge. Hence, histopathological findings remain the established cornerstone for the accurate diagnosis and exclusion of other breast pathologies [6, 7].

The distinctive microscopic feature of IGM is the presence of a non-necrotizing lobulocentric granuloma, accompanied by variable numbers of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells; however, typical histopathologic features may not be evident in some cases due to the severity of inflammation [8]. The presence of vacuolated, empty spaces rimmed by neutrophils, along with granulomatous inflammation, has recently been considered a distinct subtype of IGM, correlated with Corynebacterium spp. infection, termed “Cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis“(CNGM) [9]. Though research on the etiologic and morphologic overlap between IGM and CNGM is ongoing.

Finally, there is no validated treatment modality for IGM, and the efficacy of therapeutic options is controversial, with variable recurrence rates as high as 50% [10, 11].

Given the prolonged and tiring course of IGM, there is no room for misdiagnosis, as it only adds to the burden of the disease. Therefore, we aim to retrospectively assess histologically confirmed cases of IGM over a 1-year period to review and discuss the histologic findings of this entity. We hope our study sheds light on the diverse histopathological presentations of IGM, given the limited data in the literature on this topic.

Methods and materials

We retrospectively searched the histopathologic database of the Cancer Institute at Imam Hospital for IGM cases diagnosed between April 2021 and March 2022. Patients’ age, gender, clinical presentation, Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) score, and history of lactation and pregnancy were obtained from documented medical files. The sampling method was also noted. All H&E-stained slides were evaluated for histopathologic features, including the following parameters. The pattern of inflammation was classified as lobulocentric, lobular-spared, or confluent. Lobulocentric inflammation is defined as inflammation that is clearly and predominantly around the lobular units. In the mixed pattern, inflammation in the stroma was also notable, in addition to the lobular concentration. In confluent form, the severity of inflammation was too high, and the detection of lobular units was not easy due to possible destruction. Presence of granulomas was evaluated with descriptive features, such as well-formed or poorly defined. The presence or absence of multinucleated giant cells, as well as their type, was noted. The types of inflammatory cells, with a focus on neutrophils, were defined. Fibrosis was categorized as either mild or moderate to severe. Granulation tissue, empty space, fat necrosis, and necrosis were also assessed. Finally, we considered cases of a granulomatous mastitis that fulfilled the following histologic criteria: lobulocentric inflammation with granuloma formation and presence of Neutrophils as a component of inflammatory cells [12]. Infections, systemic granulomatous diseases, and neoplasms were ruled out by complete clinical assessment. Results

During the study period, 40 cases of IGM were identified and reviewed. All patients were female, aged 22 to 64, with a mean age of 35.86 years. The majority of patients were in the 30–39 age group (n = 23, 57.5%). Right breast involvement was the dominant site of involvement (21 cases (52.5%)). The most common clinical manifestation was palpable mass (21 cases (52.5%), which was associated with nipple retraction, pain, and collection in two (0.09%), two (0.09%), and one case (0.0.4), respectively. Inflammatory symptoms, including pain, swelling, erythema, and thickened skin, were detected in 9 cases (22.5%). Fistula, abscess formation, and collection were each present in one case (2.5%), each accompanied by inflammatory symptoms. Three cases (7.5%) were asymptomatic and underwent biopsy due to sonographic findings. 30% of patients had a history of breastfeeding over the past 5 years, and one patient (2.5%) had a history of prolactinoma.

The BI-RADS score was reported in 31 of the cases. BI-RADS 4a was the most reported radiologic score, followed by 4b, 3, 5, and 4c.

The clinical impression of GM was made prior to biopsy in 25 cases (62.5%). On the other hand, carcinoma was suspected initially in two patients (5%).

Core needle biopsy and vacuum-assisted biopsy were performed for diagnosis in 33(82.5%) and three patients (7.5%), respectively, while four patients (10%) underwent surgery. Lists of collected demographic and clinical data are summarized in Table 2.

Discussion

Initially described by Kessler and Wolloch in 1972, IGM is a destructive inflammatory breast disease mainly affecting women with a history of at least one pregnancy in recent years [13, 14]. The incidence of IGM has recently been increasing, especially in Asia and Mediterranean countries, which signifies the importance of improving our understanding of this entity [15]. As a chronic inflammatory breast disease with a high recurrence rate, IGM can cause breast deformity and be a physical and psychological burden to the affected women, especially considering the relatively young population of the patients [16]. In line with the literature, about 80% of our patients were in the 30–50 age group in the current study, and only two women were over 50.

Various factors have been hypothesized to be correlated with IGM, including hormonal alterations, gestation, breastfeeding, hyperprolactinemia, microbiological agents, smoking, and α1-antitrypsin deficiency [17]. There is no consensus regarding the definite cause of IGM; however, a localized inflammatory autoimmune reaction against extravasated ductal secretions has been postulated to play a role in the disease development [18]. This secretion is probably due to a deviation in the estrogen-progesterone ratio or hyperprolactinemia, which explains the high rate of the disease occurrence within a few years of pregnancy [19]. Furthermore, non-caseating granulomatous lesions have been correlated with drug-induced hyperprolactinemia in previous studies [20]. Also, a high prolactin level has been demonstrated as a recurrence factor for IGM [21]. This was also reflected in the current study, in which the history of breastfeeding in recent years was present in approximately 30% of the cases. In addition, a history of high prolactin levels was present in nearly 2.5% of the cases. Recently, local infection with Corynebacterium spp. has been proposed as a possible etiologic factor, but the definite correlation with this lipophilic Gram-positive bacillus remains unknown [1].

The disease primarily affects one breast in any quadrant, but the involvement of the upper outer quadrant is slightly more common [22]. Right and left localization were both dominant in different studies, so no frequent laterality is determined for IGM [23]. Over half of our patients came with the right breast lesion. Also, bilateral involvement as a breast mass was present in one case that had a history of breastfeeding in the past year.

Although IGM is a diagnosis of exclusion, the correlation among clinical, radiological, and histological features remains the core of the diagnosis. The most common clinical presentation is a unilateral, painful, usually firm breast lump with variable size [24]. Considering the inflammatory nature of the disease, IGM can also cause skin erythema, skin thickness, abscess formation, fistula, axillary lymphadenopathy, nipple retraction, and rarely peau d’orange [25]. We also observed the same trend in our cases. The most common complaint was a palpable breast mass in over half of our patients, which was occasionally associated with other symptoms such as nipple retraction and mastalgia. Symptoms of mastitis, including swelling, skin erythema, skin thickening, and edema, were the second most common presentation. Clinical features of chronicity, such as fistula, were only observed in 7.5% of the patients.

Radiologic studies may vary depending on the severity of the inflammation; however, the most frequently observed mammographic and sonographic findings are a nonspecific asymmetric density and a hypoechoic mass lesion, respectively [26]. Even though some radiologic features are in favor of a benign condition, many findings are indistinguishable from those seen in breast cancer, leading to unnecessary invasive interventions [27]. Our cases were classified according to BI-RADS score, most commonly as 4 (suspicious for malignancy). Similarly, suspicious BI-RADS scores were reported in over 50% of IGM cases in previous studies [28]. It is worth noting that BI-RADS classification did not show significant correlation with radiological preliminary and pathological diagnosis in previous studies [29].

This vast spectrum of clinical and radiological presentations is highly mistaken for a variety of breast diseases. Therefore, adequate sampling and precise histomorphologic evaluation are essential to avoid misdiagnosis.

Histologically, IGM is characterized by a lobulocentric, non-necrotizing, granulomatous inflammatory process, usually accompanied by neutrophilic microabscesses, multinucleated giant cells, and acute and chronic inflammatory cells, which sometimes surround an empty cystic space. Aggregation of epithelioid histiocytes is essential for diagnosis, whether or not it forms a well-defined granuloma [30]. Consistent with the literature, a well-formed granuloma was detected in 82.5% of our cases, and histiocyte aggregation was poorly defined in only 15% of cases (Figs. 1 and 2). Moreover, granulomas may not be included in the core needle biopsy specimen in up to 15% of cases, which explains the absence of granulomas in one of our cases [31].

Besides histiocytes, lymphocytes, plasma cells, multinucleated giant cells, neutrophil aggregates, and eosinophil infiltration are found within the inflammation [6]. We observed prominent neutrophilic aggregates in over 92% of our cases, and a scattered neutrophil infiltrate in the remaining cases (Fig. 3). On the other hand, eosinophilic infiltration was detected in only 32.5% of cases. This aligns with prior studies on IGM, which highlight neutrophils as the predominant inflammatory cells [32].

Typically, the inflammation is centered on breast lobules, though occasionally the confluent inflammatory process may obscure the lobular architecture, resulting in vanishing the lobulocentric nature of the disease [33]. Despite limited studies on the various patterns of inflammation in IGM, the results are also in agreement with the notion that the lack of lobulocentricity does not exclude IGM [31, 34]. Lobulocentricity was identified in nearly half of our patients. In contrast, the other half showed confluent (22.5%) or mixed (32.5%) patterns of inflammation, underscoring once again the importance of lobular involvement in diagnosis, even if it is not evident at first glance.

Both Langhans-type and foreign body-type giant cells have been detected in IGM, but the literature leans towards the former, even though it has been commonly associated with tuberculosis mastitis [6, 35–37]. In contrast to most previous reports, foreign body-type giant cells were found exclusively in 15 of our cases (37.5%) and were mixed with Langhans-type giant cells in 14 cases (35%). Pure Langhans-type giant cells were present in only 5 cases (12.5%), and no giant cells were detected in 6 cases (15%).

In addition, the degree of fibrosis, fat necrosis, and necrosis was occasionally seen, though the mentioned parameters seem to have poor interobserver concordance among the authors. These findings are more reflective of secondary changes in breast tissue due to inflammation rather than IGM [38].

Cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis (CNGM) is the new trend in the spectrum of granulomatous breast conditions, defined by the presence of cystic empty spaces within some granulomas and believed to be a subtype of IGM by many authors [35, 39]. The empty spaces are considered dissolved lipid vacuoles surrounded by variable numbers of neutrophils, histiocytes, and lymphocytes, and may contain scant lipophilic, rod-shaped, Gram-positive bacilli of the Corynebacterium genus [35]. Considering the fastidious nature of the organism and its sparsity within the vacuoles, Gram stains and microbial cultures are often negative, resulting in a high rate of underdiagnosis [40]. On the other hand, early stages of CNGM may present only with granulomatous inflammation, without the classic “empty spaces” [35], which adds weight to the linked etiopathology of IGM and CNGM. In this study, empty spaces were present in nearly 70% of the cases. The rim of inflammatory cells was variable, with the neutrophils being the most commonly observed, followed by mixed inflammatory cells and histiocytes, respectively.

It should be considered that microscopic features of IGM may overlap with a variety of diseases, including infective mastitis, vasculitis, post-partum duct ectasia, and systemic autoimmune granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis; therefore, the presence of atypical features for IGM should be taken seriously, and other causes of mastitis must be initially ruled out [30]. Malignant conditions may cause inflammation and granulomatous reaction in the adjacent breast parenchyma. Moreover, synchronous occurrence of IGM and breast carcinoma has been reported [41, 42]. For that reason, any sign of atypia must bear a possible malignant disease in mind. Another salient differential diagnosis is tuberculosis mastitis, especially in developing countries.

In comparison with IGM, granulomatous inflammation in tuberculosis is more pronounced around ductal structures, in addition to more evident areas of necrosis and eosinophilic infiltration [43]. Significant dilation of terminal ducts, along with periductal infiltration of lymphoplasma cells and the presence of lipid-laden histiocytes, is in favor of mammary duct ectasia [44]. Granulomatous mastitis could be associated with systemic granulomatous diseases such as Crohn’s disease, granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA), eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA), sarcoidosis, etc. Crohn’s disease is histologically characterized by granulomatous mastitis with marked eosinophilic infiltration. The presence of naked granulomas with no necrosis or vasculitis is suggestive of sarcoidosis [45, 46]. Vasculitis and extensive fibrinoid necrosis are consistent with systemic vasculitis with breast involvement [47]. Further physical examination, serologic investigation, and attention to the type of targeted vessels must be considered to facilitate diagnosis [48].

Despite being self-limited in a few cases, IGM has a prolonged clinical course with poor response to therapy and a high rate of recurrence [49]. There is no consensus on the optimal treatment protocol for IGM. Available therapeutic options, including conservative and surgical strategies, are used either separately or in combination, though the efficacy of these interventions remains debated [50]. Due to differences in clinical presentation and disease severity, the ideal treatment should be planned individually for each patient [51].

Conclusion

Although IGM has a benign nature, it can cause severe destruction to the breast parenchyma and cause long-term consequences. Clinical presentation and radiologic features can be both helpful and misleading. As a result, diagnosis relies heavily on histomorphologic findings. Taking account of characteristic features is as important as unusual ones for avoiding pitfalls. The standardized treatment plan is controversial, and the recurrence rate is high even with surgical excision, causing unsatisfactory outcomes.
