Treatment Patterns and Clinical Outcomes in Youth With Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder and Autism Spectrum Disorder
Authors: Raman Baweja, Daniel A. Waschbusch, Narpinder Malhi, Margaret D. Weiss, Michael Murray, James G. Waxmonsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe co-occurrence of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is common and associated with greater clinical complexity. This study evaluated real-world treatment patterns and outcomes among children and adolescents with ADHD, comparing those with and without comorbid ASD.MethodA retrospective comparative cohort study analyzed electronic health records from the TriNetX Research Network, including 869,510 children and adolescents aged 3 to 18 years with ADHD, of whom 155,543 (17.9%) had ASD. Outcomes included treatment patterns and clinical outcomes. Analyses used propensity score matching and Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for sociodemographic and psychiatric factors, estimating relative risks (RRs), hazard ratios (HRs), and 95% confidence intervals over 1 year.ResultsChildren and adolescents with ADHD+ASD had higher psychiatric comorbidity and health care use than those without ASD. They received fewer CNS stimulant prescriptions (RR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.81-0.83) but a higher proportion of non-stimulant prescriptions (RR = 1.52, 95% CI = 1.50-1.54) compared with those without ASD. Slightly higher ADHD prescribing occurred among Hispanic youth (RR = 1.03) and preschoolers (RR = 1.23) with ASD compared to those without ASD. Within the ADHD+ASD cohort, CNS stimulants were associated with the most favorable outcomes, including reduced hospitalization, emergency visits, suicidal behaviors, and subsequent antipsychotic/mood stabilizer initiation (adjusted HRs 0.33-0.83). Atomoxetine showed relatively favorable outcomes among non-stimulants.ConclusionComorbid ASD in children and adolescents with ADHD increases clinical burden and shifts treatment from CNS stimulants to non-stimulants and other psychotropics. Despite lower use, CNS stimulants are associated with improved outcomes, underscoring the need for prospective studies examining the risk-to-benefit ratio in this population.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders in youth, with a global prevalence of approximately 6% and a male-to-female ratio of about 2:1.1 Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is also highly prevalent, affecting 1% to 2% of children globally, with a male predominance of approximately 3:1 to 4:1.2

The co-occurrence of ADHD and ASD is common. Approximately 30% to 40% of children with ASD meet criteria for ADHD,3 and about 21% of youth with ADHD meet criteria for ASD.4 This bidirectional relationship likely reflects shared genetic liability and overlapping neurobiological mechanisms, particularly involving executive dysfunction, attention regulation, and social–cognitive processing.5,6 ADHD-related impulsivity and hyperactivity may compound social communication difficulties inherent to ASD, whereas ASD-related rigidity and sensory sensitivities may exacerbate attentional and regulatory challenges associated with ADHD.5,7

Youth with co-occurring ADHD and ASD experience higher rates of psychiatric comorbidities, more frequent emergency department visits and hospitalizations, and increased use of psychotropic medications, including antipsychotics and mood stabilizers, compared to youth with either disorder alone.8, 9, 10 Both ADHD and ASD independently increase risk for suicidal ideation/attempts; however, the combined impact of these co-occurring conditions on suicidality remains insufficiently studied.11, 12, 13

The presence of ASD in youth with ADHD may also complicate treatment planning. Central nervous system (CNS) stimulants, including methylphenidate and amphetamines, are first-line, evidence-based treatments for ADHD and demonstrate large effect sizes in reducing core ADHD symptoms in youth without ASD.14,15 In youth with comorbid ASD, controlled trials have documented the capacity of stimulants to reduce ADHD symptoms and benefit co-occurring irritability and aggression, however; effect sizes are smaller compared with youth with ADHD alone.16, 17, 18 Lower tolerability, including increased rates of adverse emotional effects, and overall discontinuation, is observed in ASD populations, requiring cautious prescribing.18,19 These reactions may reflect ASD-associated regulatory vulnerabilities rather than isolated medication-specific adverse effects.19

Non-stimulant medications, including atomoxetine and guanfacine, demonstrate moderate-to-large effects on ADHD symptoms in ASD and generally have acceptable tolerability.16,20, 21, 22 In randomized controlled trials of atomoxetine in youth with ADHD and ASD, the effect size was large (Cohen d ≈ 0.8), comparable to stimulants in other studies.21,22 Tolerability was generally better, and adverse events were similar to those reported in trials of youth with ADHD without ASD.21, 22, 23

Most studies to date have focused primarily on ADHD symptom change as the main outcome, an approach that may have limited relevance for individuals with more complex clinical presentations. Prior research has also relied heavily on unsystematic spontaneous self-reports to assess tolerability. In contrast, little is known about whether ASD comorbidity alters the risk of hospitalizations, emergency visits, suicidality, or the need for more intensive adjunctive medications (eg, mood stabilizers and antipsychotics), or whether stimulant versus non-stimulant therapies differentially affect these outcomes. To date, no large-scale studies have systematically compared treatment patterns or clinical outcomes between youth with ADHD+ASD and those with ADHD alone.

Substantial variability exists in real-world prescribing patterns of ADHD medications by age, sex, race, and ethnicity among youth with ADHD, as well as among those with ASD.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Among youth with ADHD, stimulants are more frequently prescribed to older children and adolescents, whereas α-2 agonists are more commonly used in preschool-aged children.24,25 In youth with ASD, overall psychotropic prescribing generally increases with age, whereas ADHD medication use may decline in older adolescents.26 It remains unclear whether these demographic factors influence ADHD prescribing differently in youth with versus without ASD.

The current study aims to address these gaps by examining a large, nationally representative cohort of children and adolescents with ADHD, comparing those with and without comorbid ASD. Specifically, we evaluate the following: (1) associations of ASD diagnosis with medication treatment patterns; (2) differences in prescription patterns across age, sex, race, and ethnicity; and (3) associations between ADHD medications and clinical outcomes, including hospitalizations, emergency visits, suicidal ideation/attempts, and subsequent antipsychotic or mood stabilizer use. A deeper understanding of these patterns is essential for optimizing care strategies, reducing disparities, and informing evidence-based treatment.

Method

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study used data from TriNetX, LLC (“TriNetX”), a global health research network providing access to electronic health records (EHRs) from health care organizations (HCOs) worldwide. The dataset was extracted in October 2025 from the TriNetX Research Network, which includes EHRs for approximately 151 million patients across 114 HCOs, of whom around 21 million are aged 3 to 18 years. The dataset comprises data from outpatient, specialty, inpatient, and emergency departments, including laboratory results, medication prescriptions, procedures, and diagnoses. Additional details regarding the TriNetX network structure, data standardization processes, and quality control measures are provided in the Supplemental Material (Supplement 1. Data Source and Network Description, available online). This study involved a secondary analysis of de-identified data, the study was deemed exempt from institutional review board review, and patient consent was not required.

Study Sample

The study included 869,510 children and adolescents aged 3 to 18 years with a diagnosis of ADHD (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM F90]) between 2011 and 2025. Among them, 155,543 (17.9%) also had a diagnosis of ASD (F84) and were classified as the “ADHD with ASD” group (ADHD+ASD cohort). The remaining 713,967 youth had ADHD without ASD and were classified as the “ADHD without ASD” group (ADHD-ASD cohort).

The sample was geographically diverse: 21.4% from the northeastern, 27.5% from the midwestern, 34.8% from the southern, and 11.2% from the western region of the United States; 5.3% from outside the United States; and 1.3% from unknown locations. Approximately 54% of HCOs were in academic settings, whereas 46% were in non-academic settings, with a mean follow-up duration of 2.9 ± 2.8 years. Further details are provided in the Supplement 2, available online, and the Appendix, available online, includes diagnostic and procedural codes used. Medication and service use were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes.

Variables

Dependent Variables (Outcomes)

The primary dependent variables were ADHD treatment patterns assessed over 1 year, and clinical outcomes associated with medication exposure following the index event.

Clinical outcomes included suicidal ideation/attempts, hospitalizations for any reason, emergency department visits, and subsequent initiation of mood stabilizers and antipsychotics, which are commonly relevant in individuals with ADHD and ASD. Mood stabilizers considered in this study included lithium, valproate, oxcarbazepine, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine.29

Independent Variables

For ADHD treatment patterns, the independent variable was ASD status at the index event, categorized as ADHD with ASD versus ADHD without ASD.

For clinical outcomes, the independent variables were prescriptions of ADHD medications within the past year, classified as either CNS stimulants or non-stimulants. CNS stimulants included methylphenidate and amphetamines, whereas non-stimulants included atomoxetine, viloxazine, guanfacine, and clonidine. Non-stimulants were further subdivided into α-2 agonists (clonidine and guanfacine) and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (NRIs) (atomoxetine and viloxazine).

Index events for clinical outcome analyses were defined by the comparison of interest (eg, CNS stimulant vs non-stimulant prescriptions). This study was designed as a comparative effectiveness analysis of ADHD medications in the ADHD+ASD cohort. Treatment exposure categories were kept mutually exclusive throughout the study period. Patients with combined treatment, defined as receipt of both stimulant and non-stimulant ADHD prescriptions within 30 days of each other, were excluded to ensure clear attribution of outcomes. Baseline characteristics of all groups are provided in Table S1, available online.

Covariates

Covariates included demographic characteristics (age at index, sex, and race/ethnicity as documented in the EHR) and psychiatric comorbidities, including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, psychotic disorders, substance use disorders, sleep and eating disorders, intellectual disability, tic disorders, and disruptive behavior disorders. All covariates were measured prior to the relevant index event.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in November 2025 using the TriNetX secure analytics platform. Descriptive analyses summarized baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and corresponding p values were calculated to compare demographic characteristics, psychiatric comorbidities, and health care use among youth with ADHD who had a co-occurring diagnosis of ASD and those without ASD.

Treatment Outcomes

Propensity Score Matching

To address baseline imbalances, propensity score matching was conducted using the TriNetX built-in platform. Matching variables included sociodemographic characteristics and psychiatric comorbidities. Greedy nearest neighbor matching with a 0.1-SD caliper was applied to achieve 1:1 matching without replacement. This approach enhanced interpretability, prevented overrepresentation, and allowed balanced comparisons of prescription patterns.30 Details of the matched sample sizes and the balance diagnostics are provided in Supplement 3, available online.

Longitudinal Analyses

Relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals and absolute risk differences were estimated to assess time-dependent outcomes (prescription patterns) 1 year after the index event. Participants with prescriptions initiated before the index event were included to evaluate continuation versus new initiation of ADHD medications during follow-up. In an additional sensitivity analysis, the dataset was restricted to incident prescriptions by excluding individuals whose prescriptions began before and extended beyond the index event. This sensitivity analysis ensured that only newly initiated medication episodes were analyzed.

Clonidine is frequently prescribed off-label for pediatric sleep problems31 and is also used to treat both ADHD and ASD.32,33 A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding patients with documented sleep disorders to minimize confounding by indication.

To further explore demographic variation, stratified longitudinal analyses were conducted by sex, age group (3-5 years, 6-11 years, and 12-18 years), and racial/ethnic group (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic; White vs Black). These subgroup analyses examined whether treatment patterns and outcomes differed by demographic characteristics.

Clinical Outcomes in ADHD With ASD Cohort

Cox Proportional Hazards Models

Cox proportional hazards models were used within the ADHD+ASD cohort to evaluate time-to-event outcomes over the 1-year follow-up, with clinical outcomes defined as the event of interest. Patients were censored at the time of the event, loss to follow-up, or at the end of the 1-year observation period. Models were adjusted for sociodemographic variables and psychiatric comorbidities to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs). Covariates included age (continuous), sex (male = 1), race (White = 1), ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino = 1), and psychiatric comorbidities (present = 1); reference categories were non-male, non-White, non-Hispanic/Latino, and absence of comorbidities. p Values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. The robustness of these findings was further evaluated in sensitivity analyses described below.

Sensitivity Analyses

Intellectual disability subgroup: To assess the impact of comorbid intellectual disability (ID), as that diagnosis may link to antipsychotic use and service outcomes, sensitivity analyses stratified the ADHD+ASD cohort by ID status (with vs without documented ID). Models were adjusted for the same sociodemographic characteristics and psychiatric comorbidities as in the primary analysis.

Medication Class Comparisons

This sensitivity analysis evaluated clinical outcomes within specific ADHD medication classes (eg, methylphenidate vs amphetamines; guanfacine vs clonidine; α-2 agonists vs NRIs). Models were adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics and psychiatric comorbidities, as well as for co-prescribed ADHD medications from other classes (eg, adjusting for non-stimulants when comparing methylphenidate with amphetamines). This approach accounted for concurrent treatment exposure and enabled more precise estimation of class-specific associations with clinical outcomes.

Sustained Medication Exposure and Acute Clinical Outcomes

This sensitivity analysis required patients to have at least 2 prescriptions for either a CNS stimulant or a non-stimulant over a 6-month period, with outcomes assessed during the subsequent 6 months. This ensured that exposure reflected recurrent pharmacotherapy rather than a single prescription, while accommodating typical medication gaps during school breaks (for stimulants) and longer prescription durations (90 days for non-stimulants).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

In a cohort of 869,510 children and adolescents with ADHD, 155,543 (17.9%) had a co-occurring diagnosis of ASD. Overall, male youth comprised the majority of the cohort (67.9%). The most common psychiatric comorbidities were anxiety disorders (37.6%), mood disorders (18.0%), and disruptive behavior disorders (17.0%) (Table 1). Compared with ADHD-only youth, those with ADHD+ASD were slightly younger (mean age 8.3 vs 8.6 years, SMD = 0.106) and more likely to be male (77.5% vs 65.8%, SMD = 0.260). Non-stimulant medications comprised a greater percentage of total ADHD medications in the ADHD+ASD cohort, with overall rates of prescriptions being higher in the ASD group (26.4% vs 17.3%; RR = 1.52, 95% CI = 1.50-1.54), including α-2 agonists, atomoxetine, and viloxazine. Sex- and Age-Stratified Analyses

Prescription patterns were generally consistent across sexes and age groups, with a few notable differences, as discussed below.

Sex Differences

Female youth with ASD received slightly lower rates of overall pharmacologic treatment compared with male youth with ASD (Table 2). CNS stimulants were less frequently prescribed in female youth in the ADHD+ASD cohort compared with female youth in the ADHD–ASD cohort (RR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.76-0.80), with a slightly more pronounced difference in female youth than in male youth (RR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.82-0.84). Likewise, the relative increase in non-stimulant prescriptions was higher in male youth in the ADHD+ASD cohort (RR = 1.54, 95% CI = 1.52-1.57) than in female youth (RR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.42-1.51). Atomoxetine prescribing in female youth remained unchanged across cohorts.

Age Differences

Among preschoolers (ages 3-5 years), overall ADHD medication prescriptions were higher in ADHD+ASD (RR = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.15-1.32) compared to ADHD–ASD, although CNS stimulant use did not differ (Table S2, available online). No significant differences were observed in ADHD medication or atomoxetine prescriptions among children aged 6 to 11 years.

Combined Age and Sex Differences

Among male youth aged 6 to 11 years, ADHD medication prescribing rates were comparable across cohorts, whereas female youth in the same age group had lower prescriptions in ADHD+ASD youth (RR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.91-0.96) compared with ADHD–ASD female youth (Table S2, available online). Among adolescents (aged 12-18 years), atomoxetine prescriptions were higher in male youth with ADHD+ASD (RR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.10-1.23) compared with ADHD–ASD male youth, with no differences observed among female youth.

Race and Ethnicity Analyses

When examining prescription rates, overall rates for all medication classes were generally lower for racial and ethnic minority youth compared with White youth, with 2 notable exceptions: (1) clonidine prescriptions were more common among Black youth across both cohorts, and (2) antipsychotic prescriptions were higher in the ADHD–ASD cohort for both Black and Hispanic youth compared to their White and non-Hispanic counterparts (Table S3, available online).

In contrast, when examining prescription patterns (relative differences between ADHD–ASD and ADHD+ASD within each racial group), patterns were largely consistent across racial and ethnic groups and mirrored the primary analysis. The only exception was among Hispanic youth, who had slightly higher ADHD medication prescribing in the ADHD+ASD cohort compared with ADHD–ASD youth (RR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01-1.06), whereas no significant differences were observed among Black youth.

Results were consistent with the primary analysis in youth with and without ID (Table S4, available online), except for suicidal ideation/attempts, for which no significant difference was observed between CNS stimulants and non-stimulants among youth with ADHD, ASD, and comorbid ID.

Within-class comparisons, adjusted for co-prescribed ADHD medications, largely confirmed the primary findings (Table S5, available online). Discussion

This large-scale, retrospective analysis of more than 850,000 children and adolescents provides novel insight into how comorbid ASD alters the clinical trajectory of youth with ADHD. A diagnosis of ASD in children and adolescents with ADHD was associated with greater clinical complexity, including higher psychiatric comorbidity and increased health care use. Significant differences in treatment patterns were observed between those with and without ASD. In preschoolers, ASD was associated with higher likelihood of being prescribed ADHD and other psychotropic medications. The diagnosis of ASD in youth with ADHD shifted treatment patterns from CNS stimulants to non-stimulants, despite stimulants being associated with lower risks of adverse clinical outcomes. Despite the relatively higher use of α-2 agonists, NRI agents, especially atomoxetine, were associated with better clinical outcomes among non-stimulant treatments. These findings highlight the complex clinical needs of this population and carry important implications for assessment, treatment planning, and engagement with the health care system.

The presence of an ASD diagnosis influenced treatment patterns in youth with ADHD. Although CNS stimulants were the most prescribed medications overall, they were less frequently used in youth with ASD, for both methylphenidate and amphetamine, even though rates of all other medication classes increased with an ASD diagnosis. Specifically, new CNS stimulant prescriptions declined by 12% following ASD diagnosis. These shifts may reflect concerns about reduced tolerability and worsening irritability compared with ADHD youth without ASD.18,19 Methylphenidate remained the most commonly prescribed stimulant in both groups, consistent with evidence supporting its favorable response profile in ASD. Meta-analyses indicate larger and more consistent effects of methylphenidate on hyperactivity16 and improvements in irritability,34 with long-term studies suggesting greater effectiveness among children with higher intellectual functioning.35

Despite longstanding concerns about stimulant use in ASD populations, particularly adverse emotional and behavioral responses,18 our findings suggest that CNS stimulants, especially methylphenidate, are associated with more favorable outcomes than non-stimulants in youth with ADHD and ASD. These findings persisted in sensitivity analyses requiring youth with ADHD and ASD to have at least 2 stimulant prescriptions. This aligns with prior evidence of long-term benefits in children with higher intellectual functioning, including improvements in irritability.34,35 In our analysis, stimulant use was associated with lower risks of hospitalization, emergency department visits, suicidal ideation/attempts, and subsequent prescriptions of antipsychotics and mood stabilizers. These results are consistent with emerging population-based ADHD research,36,37 although prior work has not specifically examined youth with comorbid ASD. Taken together, these findings support the applicability of current ADHD practice guidelines, favoring initiation with stimulants, even in youth with comorbid ASD.14

There was also a notable shift toward non-stimulant medications (a 9% increase), among youth with ADHD who received an ASD diagnosis. This shift may reflect clinician perceptions of better tolerability compared with stimulants in ADHD with ASD, a trend supported by existing evidence.16,20,38 Although effect sizes measuring treatment impacts are generally smaller for non-stimulants than for stimulants in youth with ADHD overall,15 stimulant-related improvements appear attenuated in ADHD youth with ASD compared with those without ASD.16,18 In contrast, albeit limited by fewer total studies, available evidence for non-stimulants does not suggest a meaningful decline in efficacy for youth with ASD. For example, a meta-analysis found that atomoxetine produced modest and consistent improvements across core ADHD domains in ASD, with effects typically stronger on parent-reported than on teacher-reported measures.16 Guanfacine also appears to yield substantial ADHD symptom reductions in youth with ASD, with treatment effects comparable to those observed in ADHD without ASD.16,39

Despite NRIs being prescribed much less than α-2 agonists in youth with ADHD and ASD, NRIs were associated with more favorable long-term outcomes across multiple outcomes, including suicidal ideation/attempts, subsequent antipsychotic or mood stabilizer prescriptions, inpatient hospitalization, and emergency department visits. Recent evidence suggests that atomoxetine may provide clinical benefits comparable to those observed in methylphenidate trials in youth with ASD, while demonstrating fewer adverse events and generally better tolerability.38 Therefore, when CNS stimulants are not tolerated, atomoxetine could be considered a viable treatment alternative.16,20,38 However, little is known about the response rates to non-stimulants in youth with ASD who do not respond to adequate and tolerable doses of CNS stimulants. In youth with ADHD more broadly, about 43% of stimulant non-responders ultimately respond to atomoxetine.40 There is also evidence that α-2 agonists may be efficacious in youth with suboptimal responses to stimulants,41 suggesting that either agent could be beneficial even if CNS stimulants are ineffective.

Among children and adolescents with ADHD, co-occurring ASD was associated with a 1-year absolute increase in antipsychotic (7%), antidepressant (4.5%), and mood stabilizer (2%) use. Given the high comorbidity of mood and anxiety disorders, elevated prescribing of antidepressants is not surprising. However, more controlled studies examining their efficacy and tolerability are needed. Antipsychotics such as risperidone and aripiprazole are US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved for treating irritability associated with ASD. Antipsychotics have the highest effect sizes for reducing irritability/aggression in ASD17,42; however, they are associated with long-term morbidity and mortality, particularly at higher doses.43 Similarly, mood stabilizers have more concerning adverse effect profiles than ADHD medications.44 Evidence also supports that ADHD medications have moderate effect sizes for reducing both irritability and core ADHD symptoms, with stronger evidence for stimulants compared with non-stimulants.17,42,45 In this analysis, CNS stimulants were associated with reduced subsequent use of antipsychotics and mood stabilizers compared to non-stimulants. Although cautious prescribing of antipsychotics is appropriate for some ASD youth, our findings suggest that stimulants may be both safe and potentially protective in this population when carefully monitored.

These findings highlight a potential clinical paradox: although stimulants were associated with more favorable outcomes across domains, youth with ADHD and ASD were less likely to receive them and more likely to be treated with non-stimulants. This pattern suggests possible underuse of stimulants, likely driven by concerns about tolerability, particularly irritability and behavioral adverse effects. Although such concerns are understandable, they may lead to avoidance of stimulant trials and inadvertently limit access to treatments with more favorable downstream outcomes. Increased use of α-2 agonists may reflect their perceived tolerability and utility for comorbid symptoms (eg, sleep disturbance), contributing to their adoption as early treatment options despite limited comparative effectiveness data. These findings underscore the need for a more balanced, individualized approach in which stimulants are not prematurely excluded and, when they are not tolerated, atomoxetine is considered as a viable stimulant alternative.

For preschoolers, ADHD treatment guidelines recommend behavioral interventions as first-line treatment, with pharmacotherapy reserved for refractory symptoms.14,33 Preschoolers with both ADHD and ASD showed an overall increase in ADHD medication prescriptions. Given the additional impairment associated with ASD, clinicians may consider initiating ADHD treatment earlier in this population. Again, methylphenidate was prescribed more often than amphetamine, aligned with evidence supporting its use in preschoolers.33,46 However, most medication increase was driven by nonstimulants versus stimulants, possibly because both young age and comorbid ASD diagnoses are associated with reduced stimulant tolerability.18,47 In a smaller retrospective study (n = 497) of preschoolers with ADHD, CNS stimulants were associated with better response rates than α-2 agonists for children aged 4 to 5 years, regardless of ASD status, although stimulants were associated with more frequent side effects.24

All medication classes, versus only ADHD agents, were prescribed more often in preschoolers with ASD compared with those without ASD, despite the limited data supporting these alternative medication classes in patients under the age of 6 years. The higher rate of non-ADHD medication prescriptions in preschoolers with ADHD and ASD highlights the need for earlier behavioral interventions. As most psychotropic medication prescriptions originate in primary care,48 these results also suggest the need to better integrate behavioral health resources for preschoolers into primary care settings to ensure that parents have access to psychosocial services when they are facing decisions about initiating psychotropic medication.

Although racial and ethnic minority youth showed consistently lower use of pharmacologic treatments overall, treatment patterns following an ASD diagnosis varied across groups. Among Black youth, ADHD medication use remained relatively stable, whereas in Hispanic youth it demonstrated a more pronounced rise, compared with White and non-Hispanic youth respectively. Results are more pronounced for nonstimulants than stimulants, as the largest relative increase in nonstimulant prescriptions occurred among Black youth, followed by Hispanic youth with ADHD and ASD. The additional functional and behavioral challenges associated with ASD may heighten parent or clinician willingness to initiate or escalate medication treatment for youth with ADHD and ASD versus ADHD alone. Prior studies have found that families of Hispanic ethnicity often prioritize functional impairments when making treatment decisions for ADHD.29,49 Furthermore, variations in non-stimulant prescribing across racial and ethnic groups may reflect differing expectations about medication benefits or concerns about side effects.49 Even so, broad disparities in access and overall treatment engagement persist, highlighting the need to address systemic barriers such as provider bias, cultural mistrust, and structural inequities within the health care system.50 Social determinants of health, including poverty and neighborhood disadvantage, likely drive these disparities and may independently influence both medication initiation and treatment outcomes. These factors were not captured in the current dataset, and represent an important direction for future health equity research.

Despite the strengths of a large sample size and robust analytic methods, this study has several limitations inherent to retrospective analyses of EHR data. Although prescribing records reflect treatment decisions, they do not capture actual medication adherence, treatment response, or symptom severity, and causal inferences cannot be drawn from observational data. Clinician intent, psychosocial factors influencing treatment selection, and patient-level characteristics such as income and education were not available, nor were social determinants of health such as housing stability, food security, and neighborhood resources—factors that shape both access to care and the real-world impact of pharmacologic treatment, particularly among minoritized youth. This study was designed as a comparative effectiveness analysis requiring mutually exclusive treatment groups to enable clearer attribution of outcomes to specific medication classes. Accordingly, patients receiving combined treatment were excluded from the outcomes analysis; these individuals likely represent more clinically complex and severely impaired cases,51 and the findings may not generalize to them. However, because most youth with ADHD do not appear to receive combined treatment, the findings for the stimulant-only and non–stimulant-only groups remain relevant to a meaningful segment of the treated ADHD+ASD population. Selection bias is also possible, as individuals with higher functioning or less severe ASD symptoms may have been more likely to receive specific medications. Despite using propensity score matching and Cox models, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including symptom severity, family context, and socioeconomic status, likely persists. Propensity score matching balances recorded diagnostic characteristics but cannot account for baseline clinical severity or functional impairment—key drivers of medication selection in ASD. Findings are limited to health care encounters captured in TriNetX and may not generalize to youth receiving services outside of participating HCOs. Because the dataset includes a substantial proportion of academic medical centers, the sample may overrepresent specialized, resource-rich settings, thereby limiting generalizability to community or rural pediatric practices with less access to multidisciplinary and subspecialty care. In addition, TriNetX includes only billed medical care and does not capture school-based supports, early-intervention services, or other non-billable behavioral therapies central to ASD treatment. Thus, the observed increase in medication prescribing among preschool-aged children with ASD should be interpreted in the context of incomplete data on non-pharmacologic services. Although sensitivity analyses excluding youth with intellectual disability yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis, the dataset lacked granular clinical measures (eg, intelligence quotient [IQ] and adaptive functioning), precluding stratified analyses by impairment level. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized measures of symptom severity and related clinical features are needed to better characterize treatment patterns, particularly among clinically complex patients, including those receiving combined treatment.

To conclude, youth with comorbid ADHD and ASD represent a clinically complex population, characterized by elevated psychiatric burden and increased use of mental health services. This study demonstrates that ASD comorbidity significantly influences ADHD treatment trajectories, with a shift away from CNS stimulants toward non-stimulants and other psychotropic medications. Preschool-aged children with ADHD and ASD were also more likely to receive ADHD medications and other psychotropics. Among youth with ADHD and ASD, stimulant use was associated with the most favorable clinical outcomes, including fewer hospitalizations, emergency visits, and suicidal behaviors, as well as reduced subsequent initiation of mood stabilizers and antipsychotics. Among non-stimulants, atomoxetine demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes, making it a viable alternative when stimulants are not tolerated. These findings underscore the importance of individualized, carefully monitored treatment strategies that account for comorbid ASD when treating youth with ADHD. Future prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether youth with ADHD and ASD are more likely to experience the longer-term outcomes observed here, versus the acute challenges reported in short-term trials, and to inform evidence-based best practices that optimize care for this clinically complex population.
