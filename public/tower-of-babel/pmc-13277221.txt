Integrated evaluation and screening of salt-tolerant wheat germplasm and indices
Authors: Wenjing Xu, Xu Liu, Zheng Zhang, Jiantang Zhu, Ran Han, Xiaolu Wang, Kai Wang, Guang Qi, Qingqi Fan, Jianjun Liu, Cheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuccessful evaluation and screening of salt-tolerant wheat germplasm at different stages are of great importance for breeding resilient crops. In the present study, we tested 30 wheat entries out of 417 American accessions screened in a previous study. Fifteen physiological and biochemical indices were investigated at the seedling stage, and eight agronomic indices at the adult stage. Principal component analysis and correlation analysis found superoxide dismutase activity, soluble protein, and potassium levels to be related to high salt tolerance at the seedling stage. Hierarchical clustering analysis identified seven accessions exhibiting high salt tolerance at both the seedling and adult stages. A Random Forest model was constructed to predict salt-resilient entries at the seedling stage. Transcriptome and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR analyses comparing salt tolerant and sensitive accessions revealed that genes and signaling pathways related to the redox system, MAPK, and plant pathogen interactions contributed to salt resistance. Our results provide basic materials for the breeding of new salt-tolerant wheat varieties, indices to screen for salt-resilient cultivars, and insights into salt-tolerance mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08889-w.Keywords: Wheat, Salt stress, Integrated investigation, Salt-tolerant accessions, Salt tolerance-related indices

Introduction

Soil salinity is a key type of abiotic stress that contributes to limited crop productivity worldwide, particularly in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) – a staple food crop for much of the global population [1]. Approximately 7% of the world’s total land area is impacted by soil salinity, including 20–30% of the 230 million hectares of irrigated land [2, 3]. Excess salt in the soil disturbs ion homeostasis, reduces water uptake, and generates oxidative stress, all of which impair plant growth and development [4]. Identifying and developing salt-tolerant cultivars is therefore a key strategy for ensuring stable wheat production in salt-affected areas [5, 6].

Physiological and biochemical indices are widely used to characterize the mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in plants. Measurements such as the levels or balance of ions (eg, Na⁺, K⁺, and K⁺/Na⁺ ratio), the activity of antioxidant enzymes [eg, superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT)], and osmotic adjustment (eg, proline, soluble proteins, and soluble sugars) can reflect how seedlings are adapting to salt-induced stress [1, 7]. These indices can provide insights into the biochemical and physiological mechanisms of tolerance, and serve as practical markers for screening large germplasm collections. However, their relative contribution to salt tolerance in wheat remains poorly defined, and there is limited consensus on which traits are most useful for reliable selection.

Integrative analytical approaches can help address this gap. Multivariate analyses, such as principal component analysis (PCA) [8] and correlation analysis (CA) [9] can enable the identification of indices that are most closely associated with salt tolerance, facilitating the comprehensive evaluation of complex physiological responses. Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) provides a reliable approach to sorting wheat entries into various salt-tolerance categories [10]. In addition, machine learning models such as Random Forest (RF) provide powerful predictive tools by modeling the relationship between physiological indices and cultivar performance under stress [11]. Furthermore, RNA-seq and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis can reveal the key genes and signaling pathways that highly associated with salt tolerance [12]. Combining these approaches offers the potential not only to identify the most informative traits, but also to construct predictive models that classify wheat accessions into tolerance groups with a high degree of accuracy.

Herein, a panel of 30 wheat accessions were systematically evaluated based on different stages of their life cycle. Salt-tolerant wheat accessions and relevant physiological and biochemical indices were explored via PCA, CA, and HCA, and potential salt-tolerance mechanisms were identified by RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analyses. Moreover, an RF-based predictive model was developed and used to integrate salt-tolerance indices for the classification of cultivars into categories of high, medium, and low tolerance. Together, these approaches offer a robust framework for screening wheat for salt tolerance, as well as providing valuable germplasms and tools for future breeding and genetic improvement of wheat crops.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The seeds of 30 wheat accessions (Table S1), which were selected for high germination rate under salt stress following an initial screen of 417 wheat accessions [13], were obtained from Dr. Harold Bockelman (Small Grains and Potato Germplasm Research, Aberdeen, Idaho, USA). Two additional cultivars (Qingmai 6 [14] and Jimai 229 [15]; kept in the Crop Research Institute, Shandong Academy of Agricultural Science, Jinan, Shandong, China) were selected the as the salt-tolerant and vulnerable controls, respectively.

Experimental design

Wheat seedling experiments were designed as follows: 100 seeds of each of the 32 accessions were subjected to sterilization and germination. After germination, the seeds were transferred to Hoagland solution in a culture box (96-well plate, 125 mm × 85 mm × 110 mm) for 5 days for leaf and root development, and then to fresh Hoagland solution with or without 150 mM NaCl (salt and control treatments) for a week when tolerant and sensitive controls exhibited the most phenotypical differences, after which wheat seedling samples were collected for measurements of shoot length, root length, fresh weight, and physiology indices, as well as RNA-seq analysis.

Two separate nurseries were utilized for field trials. Within the first nursery (affiliated to Dezhou Academy of Agricultural Science, Dezhou, Shandong, China), plants were grown in two patches: one treated with 0.3% salt, and one control patch without salt treatment (37.36, 116.35; hereafter referred to as the “Dezhou salt field” and “Dezhou control field”). The second nursery was a field with high-salinity soil in Kenli district, Dongying (Shandong, China; 37.5475, 118.7844; hereafter referred to as the “Dongying salt field”). A row of each of cv. Qingmai 6 (tolerant control) and cv. Jimai 229 (sensitive control) plants was cultivated in every 10 rows. The complete set of accessions and controls was planted in triplicate in each field. The Dezhou control field was used as a control for both saline fields. The sowing dates were November 7 and 8, 2024, respectively, for Dezhou and Dongying.

Seedling physical characteristics

Measurements taken from the seedlings included the leaf length (distance from the crown to the leaf tip) and the root length (distance from the crown to the root tip), measured using a ruler. Fresh weight was measured using an electronic balance. A total of 10 seedlings of each accession were sampled for 10 replicates of measurements.

Physiological parameters

Chlorophyll, sodium and potassium ions, and soluble protein content were all determined following previously reported protocols [16]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content, as well as CAT and SOD activities were measured using kits from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Proline, H2O2, and soluble sugar content, as well as POD activity were measured using kits from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three seedlings were pooled together for each measurement, and this was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to a salt tolerance index (STI) calculation (STI = Trait value under salt conditions/Trait value under control conditions). Prism 10 (https://www.graphpad.com/) was used for data processing and visualization. Statistical significance between groups was determined using a paired t-test, with a threshold of p < 0.05. Differences among groups of accessions were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

PCA of indices

To identify the key indices contributing to salt tolerance, PCA was performed on the STI values of all measured traits (Table S1) using the FactoMineR and factoextra packages in R v4.3.3 (https://cran.r-project.org/src/base/R-4/R-4.3.3.tar.gz). Prior to analysis, data were centered and scaled to remove unit effects, and the dataset was used for all subsequent analyses. The eigenvalues, variance explained by each principal component, and loading scores were extracted. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were plotted using biplots, in which accessions were grouped based on their relative positions.

CA of indices

A CA was performed to assess relationships among physiological and biochemical indices, and to identify traits most strongly associated with salt tolerance. Pair-wise correlations among all indices were calculated using the cor_matrix function in R. Spearman’s rank correlation was employed for traits whose data deviated from normality. A correlation heatmap was generated using the corrplot (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/corrplot/index.html) and pheatmap (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html) packages in R, displaying both the correlation coefficients and significance levels. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Bonferroni method. A “Comprehensive” analysis based on PCA ranking values was included to illustrate the correlation between each index and salt tolerance.

HCA

HCA was conducted to categorize the wheat accessions into groups of high, medium, and low salt tolerance. A Euclidean distance matrix was computed from the STI dataset, and clustering was performed using the complete linkage method in the stats::hclust function in R. The output was visualized as a heatmap with dendrogram branches, simultaneously displaying similarity patterns among indices (rows) and accessions (columns). The optimal number of clusters was determined based on dendrogram inspection and biological relevance, and the clusters revealed the three salt tolerance categories.

RF modeling of salt tolerance

To confirm the physiological and biochemical parameters most strongly associated with salt tolerance, and to develop a predictive model for classification, the RF algorithm was applied using the RandomForest package [17] in R. The STI values of 15 physiological and biochemical traits were used as predictor variables, and the HCA-derived salt tolerance categories (high, medium, and low) were used as the response variable. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets, and model hyperparameters were optimized with tenfold cross-validation. The RF model was constructed using 1,000 decision trees (ntree = 1,000), and variable importance was evaluated using the Mean Decrease in Gini. Model robustness was evaluated using the out-of-bag error rate as an internal cross-validation metric, and performance was assessed by calculating the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) using the pROC package [18] to evaluate the reliability of classification into the three salt tolerance groups. The ROC analysis was performed using a one-vs-rest (OvR) multiclass classification approach.

RNA-seq analysis

Leaf samples from seedlings of the top three salt-tolerant and -sensitive accessions, according to the PCA, were collected for RNA-seq analysis. Leaf samples were collected from both control and salt treatment for all selected accessions. Three plants were pooled for each reading, and the experiment was repeated in triplicate for each cultivar. RNA-seq library construction, trimming of raw reads, mapping of unique reads onto the wheat genome reference, transcript calculations, and analysis of differentially expressed genes were all carried out by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), according to previously reported methods [19]. Differential expression analysis was performed by pairwise comparisons between individual tolerant and sensitive accessions to identify genotype-specific responses.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 50-mg (fresh weight) of the samples used in the RNA-seq analysis, using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (www.qiagen.com) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Contaminating genomic DNA was removed with RNase-free DNase I (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.; Nanjing, China). RNA was quantified using the Nanodrop™ 2000 device (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA), and reverse transcribed to first-strand cDNA using the HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). Gene-specific forward and reverse PCR primers targeting select significantly differentially expressed genes identified from the RNA-seq analysis were designed using QuantPrime (https://quantprime.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/main.php) and are listed in Table S2. qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [20]. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Evaluation of adult-stage traits in the field

Six plants of each accession were grown in 1.5-m rows at 20-cm intervals for the purpose of assessing and comparing plant height, tiller number, flag leaf length and width, spike length, spikelet number, seeds per main spike, 1,000-kernel weight, and survival rate. The cor.test function in R was used to test the correlation among the STIs of eight agronomic traits from plants grown in the Dezhou and Dongying fields. A broad sense heritability (H2) of the eight traits was estimated based on a linear mixed-effects model fitted using the lme4 package in R. Variance components were extracted using the VarCorr function. H2 was calculated according to σG2​/(σG2 + σE2​), where σG2​ represents genetic variance and σE2​ represents environmental variance [21].

Results

Comparative analysis of traits in salt-treated and control seedlings

Among the 32 tested wheat accessions, significant variation was observed across all 15 measured physiological and biochemical indices under salt-stress versus control conditions (Fig. 1). Salt treatment generally had a negative impact on growth- and photosynthesis-related traits, including root length, shoot length, total chlorophyll content and fresh plant weight, while leading to increases in stress-responsive factors such as soluble protein, POD, SOD, proline and MDA. Notably, for each measured parameter, the data from the salt-affected accessions covered a broad range of values, suggesting a range of tolerance levels to salinity.

PCA of parameters contributing to salt tolerance

To identify the major contributors to phenotypic variation under salt stress, PCA was performed on the STIs of all measured traits at the seedling stage. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together accounted for 54.6% of the total variance (Fig. 2A). Traits such as soluble protein, H2O2 content, SOD activity and potassium content showed the highest absolute loading values within PC1, whereas proline content and fresh plant weight contributed strongly to PC2 (Table 1). The biplot revealed that the wheat accessions were distributed widely along the PC1 axis, reflecting differences in their physiological responses to salt stress. It also revealed a separation of accessions along the PC1 axis, with tolerant accessions clustering towards the negative side, and sensitive accessions clustering towards the positive side (Fig. 2B), suggesting that indices contributing to PC1 are particularly important for distinguishing levels of salt tolerance. The three most and least salt-tolerant accessions according to the PCA ranking (PI 486141, CItr 17,940, and PI 338921; and PI 497986, PI 557017, and PI 557537, respectively, excluding controls; Table 2) were subjected to RNA-seq analysis.

Relationships among indices by CA

To calculate any correlation among the tested indices, Spearman’s CA was used. Coefficients of variation ranged from –78% to 98% across traits, indicating a broad phenotypic spectrum suitable for discriminating tolerance levels (Fig. 3; Table 3). Fresh plant weight was significantly negatively correlated with sodium content (r = –0.78, p < 0.01) and potassium/sodium ratio (r = –0.72, p < 0.01). On the other hand, SOD activity and potassium content were strongly positively correlated (r = 0.98, p < 0.01). The “Comprehensive” calculation revealed that salt tolerance was significantly positively correlated with soluble protein content (r = 0.96, p < 0.01), SOD activity (r = 0.91, p < 0.01), potassium content (r = 0.87, p < 0.01), soluble sugar content (r = 0.83, p < 0.01) and H2O2 content (r = 0.82, p < 0.01). These results suggest that osmotic substrate accumulation, ion homeostasis, and enhanced antioxidant defense contribute to mitigating salt-induced damage. The positive correlation of the above five factors with salt tolerance implies that they could be useful markers in screening for tolerant wheat cultivars.

Accession classification by HCA

HCA was used to classify the 30 wheat accessions into three distinct groups according to salt tolerance. Accessions with similar response profiles clustered together, as reflected by the branching structure of the heatmap (Fig. 4). A total of 13, 10, and seven accessions in the seedling stage were categorized as high, medium, and low salt tolerance, respectively, indicating that the physiological indices can be used to effectively classify accessions (Fig. 4; Table 4). The dendrogram was in agreement with the PCA results, supporting the robustness of these groupings. For plants in the adult stage grown in the Dezhou saline field, HCA identified 22 accessions as high, nine as medium, and one as low salt tolerance, while in the Dongying saline field, 10 accessions were categorized as high, 16 as medium, and six as low salt tolerance (Table 4).

Predictive modeling of salt tolerance

The constructed RF model demonstrated good predictive performance, achieving an overall accuracy of 77.8% and a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.667, indicating substantial model reliability (Fig. 5A). Discrimination of the low-tolerance cohort was absolute (sensitivity = 1.0; specificity = 1.0). For the high-tolerance group, while the model maintained perfect specificity (1.0), the sensitivity (0.50) reflected a partial overlap with the “medium” tolerance class, as visualized in the curves of the ROC analysis, which yielded an average AUC of 0.963 for all tolerance classes (Fig. 5B). The out-of-bag error rate stabilized at 8.7%, suggesting that the model was trained well with adequate generalization ability. The results were in alignment with those of the aforementioned analyses. To measure the contribution of each predictor, the variable importance was calculated by Mean Decrease Gini (Fig. 5C). The results revealed that the potassium/sodium ratio, potassium content, SOD activity, and soluble protein content were strongly associated with the model’s classification performance.

Substantial variation was observed for all eight parameters measured in wheat accessions grown in saline fields compared with those grown in a field with normal soil conditions (Fig. S2). Discussion

Screening for salt tolerance in wheat germplasm based on the different life stages

Stress due to salinity is a major hindrance to wheat production worldwide, and breeding salt-tolerant wheat varieties is crucial for sustaining crop yields in saline environments. For this purpose, precise evaluation of wheat germplasms is a priority. The seedling stage is particularly important for salt-tolerance screening, as it represents the earliest phase of the established plant. Salt-related stress at this stage directly impacts germination, root and shoot growth, and physiological stability. Early exposure to a high-salt environment can cause increased seedling mortality rates, reduced overall health, and long-term yield decline [22–24]. Moreover, responses observed at the seedling stage often serve as predictive indicators of later-stage performance under saline conditions [25–27]. While early seedling screening provides a rapid and cost-effective approach for identifying potentially salt-tolerant wheat accessions, salt tolerance is a complex, developmentally regulated trait that can vary across growth stages. Therefore, evaluation at the adult stage under saline field conditions is also essential to confirm any salt tolerance identified at the seedling stage. In addition, adult-stage evaluation directly reflects the agronomic potential of wheat genotypes in saline environments, providing a more reliable basis for breeding and selection. Thus, evaluating wheat accessions at both seedling and adult stages provides a reliable and approach for differentiating between tolerant and sensitive genotypes.

In the present study, the implementation of HCA on a set of 15 physiological and biochemical indices measured at the seedling stage led to the identification of 13 salt-tolerant wheat accessions out of 30 accessions investigated. To confirm whether they were salt tolerant, the accessions were cultivated in the Dezhou and Dongying saline fields, and eight agronomic traits were measured. HCA of these traits identified 22 salt-tolerant accessions from the Dezhou field, and 10 from the Dongying field. This discrepancy may due to high salt intensity and coastal saline-alkali soil texture in Dongying salt field. However, broad heritability between the traits of plants grown in the two saline fields was low, and correlation was non-significant. The low heritability values observed for the majority of traits indicate that phenotypic variation between the saline fields was largely driven by environmental effects rather than genetic differences among accessions. This is consistent with previous findings that field-based assessment of salt tolerance is strongly influenced by the heterogeneity of soil salinity, as well as other abiotic stresses such as alkalinity, drought, and nutrient imbalance [2, 28]. Consequently, it is difficult to accurately distinguish genotypic salt tolerance solely based on field-based performance, particularly when environmental stresses and their levels vary across sites.

Although the overall trait consistency between the tested environments was low, traits with higher relative heritability (such as flag leaf width and tiller number) could still provide some information about genotypic salt tolerance. However, reliable classification of salt-tolerant wheat accessions requires a combination of data from field and controlled-environment experiments. In the present study, bringing together the observations of physiological indices at the seedling stage with adult-stage field performance enabled the identification of accessions exhibiting both strong inherent tolerance as well as field adaptability. Such integrative approaches are crucial for selecting stable and high-performing wheat genotypes for saline environments [4, 29]. Herein, accessions PI 483456 and PI 486141 were classified as high salt tolerance at the seedling stage and in the Dezhou and Dongying saline fields; accessions CItr 17,940, PI 542979, PI 298213, PI 486142, and PI 486143 exhibited high salt tolerance at the seedling stage and in one of the tested salt fields (they revealed medium tolerance in the other salt field). These seven accessions showed potential for use in specific saline environments and have never previously been reported as salt tolerant before. The discovery of these previously unrecorded tolerant genotypes expands the available gene pool for wheat breeding programs.

Practical implications for screening and breeding

In this study, we adopted an integrative analytical framework combining multivariate statistical analyses, machine learning, and transcriptomics to comprehensively identify salt-tolerant wheat germplasm and key physiological and molecular determinants of salt tolerance. This integrated framework provides a comprehensive and robust assessment of wheat cultivars.

PCA and CA were performed initially, to reduce data dimensionality and explore relationships among physiological and biochemical parameters, enabling the identification of key traits contributing to phenotypic variation under salt stress. From these two analyses, osmotic adjustments (such as soluble protein and sugar levels), antioxidant defenses (including H2O2 content and SOD activity), and ion homeostasis (eg, potassium content) were found to be key factors in screening wheat entries at seedling stage for large-scale screening of salt tolerant wheat germplasms. A high K⁺/Na⁺ ratio indicates a plant's superior ability to either exclude Na⁺ at the root interface or sequester it into vacuoles, thereby protecting the metabolic machinery in the cytosol [2]. SOD acts as the first line of defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during environmental stress to prevent lipid peroxidation of cell membranes and protect DNA and proteins from oxidative damage [30]. Soluble proteins act as molecular chaperones, stabilizing the structure of other functional proteins and membrane lipids during dehydration [31]. These traits are all considered as hallmarks of salinity tolerance. Building on PCA and CA, HCA grouped the tested accessions into three classes of tolerance based on their overall phenotypic profiles. Notably, this classification was independently supported by the RF model, which not only confirmed the grouping patterns but also ranked the indices by their contribution to salt-tolerance prediction. Finally, RNA-seq analysis of representative tolerant and sensitive accessions identified differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways associated with antioxidant defense, plant pathogen interaction, and stress signaling, offering mechanistic insights into salt tolerance. The enriched GO terms for antioxidant activity, peroxidase activity, and hydrogen peroxide metabolism correspond well with the higher SOD activity observed in salt-tolerant wheat. These pathways enhance ROS scavenging and reduce oxidative damage under salt stress [30]. The MAPK signalling pathway regulates stress signal transduction and modulates ion transporters, which directly contributes to the maintenance of a high K⁺/Na⁺ ratio and potassium content, key indicators of ion homeostasis and salt tolerance [32]. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and the plant-pathogen interaction pathway also contributes via shared stress-response signals [33]. Therefore, the RNA-seq results and the previous physiological traits in this study were mutually confirmed. Together, this multi-method strategy minimized the risk of bias from any single analytical approach and enhanced confidence in the identified tolerant entries. Such an integrated workflow provides a rigorous framework for salt-tolerance screening and may be broadly applicable to other evaluations of abiotic stresses in wheat and related crops. The present study provides both a methodological reference and a set of valuable germplasm resources for improvement of wheat production in saline environments.
