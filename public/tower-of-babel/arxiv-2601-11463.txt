Abstract

Introduction

The study of the isometric and isomorphic properties of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces is an active and well-developed topic within the theory of Banach spaces. Concerning the isometric theory, by the Banach-Stone theorem,
\mathcal{C}(K)
and
\mathcal{C}(L)
are linearly isometric if and only if the compact spaces
K
and
L
are homeomorphic. Moreover, by the Mazurkiewicz-Sierpinski theorem, for every infinite countable compact space
K
there is a unique countable ordinal
\alpha
and unique natural number
n
such that
K
is homeomorphic to
[0,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n]
. In order to shorten the notation, in what follows given an ordinal
\alpha
we write
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
instead of
\mathcal{C}([0,\alpha])
. Concerning the isomorphic theory, in 1960 Bessaga and Pełczyński
[
4
]
proved that for any infinite countable compact space
K
there exists a unique ordinal
\xi<\omega_{1}
such that
\mathcal{C}(K)
is isomorphic to

\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\omega^{\xi}})
and in 1966 Miljutin
[
23
]
proved that whenever
K
is an uncountable compact metric space, then
\mathcal{C}(K)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
, which completed isomorphic classification of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces for
K
metrizable compacta. We refer, for example, to
[
1
, Chapter 4]
or
[
14
, Section 2.6]
for more information and additional historical background. Both qualitative and quantitative variants of the results mentioned above were considered by many researchers, especially during the last 20 years, some of which are mentioned below.

In the present work, we consider the following strengthening of the notion of an isomorphism: we say that a bounded linear operator
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
is
positive embedding
(resp.
positive isomorphism
) if
T
is an isomorphic embedding (resp. surjective isomorphism) with
T\geq 0
(that is,
Tf\geq 0
whenever
f\geq 0
). Let us emphasize that we do not assume positive isomorphism
T
satisfies
T^{-1}\geq 0
, because by the well-known Kaplansky’s theorem
[
19
]
, whenever there exists a surjective isomorphism
T
between
\mathcal{C}(K)
and
\mathcal{C}(L)
spaces such that both
T\geq 0
and
T^{-1}\geq 0
, then
T
is actually an isometry. We shall sometimes write (one-sided) isomorphic embedding and (one-sided) positive isomorphism in order to emphasize that we do not assume
T^{-1}\geq 0
. Our first main result could be understood as a qualitative improvement of the result by Bessaga and Pełczyński mentioned above. The symbol
\height(K)
used below denotes the height of the compact space
K
, see Section
1
for a precise definition.

Theorem A
.

Let
K
and
L
be infinite countable compact spaces. The following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a positive isomorphism from
C(K)
onto
C(L)
.

(ii)

C(K)
is isomorphic to
C(L)
and
\height(K)\leq\height(L)
.

Moreover, for any compact spaces
K
and
L
, if there exists a positive embedding
T:C(K)\to C(L)
, then
\height(K)\leq\height(L)
.

A notable consequence of Theorem
A
is that given infinite countable compacta
K
and
L
, the Banach spaces
\mathcal{C}(K)
and
\mathcal{C}(L)
are isomorphic if and only if there exists a surjective isomorphism
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
such that either
T\geq 0
or
T^{-1}\geq 0
. A second notable consequence is the following (the proof follows from Theorem
A
and certain earlier results, see Section
2
for more details).

Corollary A
.

Let
K
and
L
be compact spaces, and
K
is metrizable. Then there exists a positive embedding of
\mathcal{C}(K)
into
\mathcal{C}(L)
if and only if
ht(K)\leq ht(L)
.

Moreover, some parts of our argument produce quantitative estimates that seem to be new even in the classical context. Recall that given compact spaces
K
and
L
we define the
Banach-Mazur distance
between
\mathcal{C}(K)
and
\mathcal{C}(L)
, denoted
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(K),\mathcal{C}(L))
, as the infimum of the numbers
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|
over all surjective isomorphisms
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
. Similarly, we let
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(K),\mathcal{C}(L))
to be the infimum of the numbers
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|
over all one-sided positive isomorphisms
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
. Notice that the quantity
d_{BM}^{+}
is not symmetric as by Theorem
A
we have, for example,
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{2}))<\infty
while
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{2}),\mathcal{C}(\omega))=\infty
. Once we obtain an estimate of
d_{BM}
or
d_{BM}^{+}
, it is natural to ask whether this estimate is in fact optimal. The following summarizes some of the most interesting quantitative estimates we were able to obtain.

Theorem B
.

Let
\alpha
be a countable nonzero ordinal and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

We have

d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n))\leq d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n))\leq 2+\sqrt{5},

and

2n-1\leq d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}))\leq n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

(ii)

If
\alpha=\omega^{\beta}
for some ordinal
\beta
, then

d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha n}))=d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha n}))=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

Let us now discuss the relevance of our Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
to some recent results and formulate open problems we were not able to solve. There are several existing works, where the authors consider various morphisms between
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces, with the primary interest being improvements of the Banach-Stone theorem. By the Milgram’s theorem
[
22
]
(resp. Kaplansky’s theorem
[
19
]
, resp. Jarosz’s theorem
[
15
]
), if there exists a bijection
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
such that both
T
and
T^{-1}
preserve multiplication (resp. pointwise ordering, resp. disjointness of supports), then
K
and
L
are homeomorphic. There is a recent attempt to find a common generalization of those results by Kania and Rmoutil using the notion of compatibility isomorphism, see
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
. Another direction in generalizing the Banach–Stone theorem is provided by results of Dutrieux and Kalton
[
6
]
, and of Galego and da Silva
[
8
]
, who showed that if the distance (Gromov–Hausdorff, Kadets, Lipschitz, or even coarse) between
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces is small, then the corresponding Banach spaces are linearly isometric. Positive isometric embeddings between general
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces were studied also in
[
25
]
. Our Theorem
A
shows that a one-sided variant of the Kaplansky’s theorem yields an isomorphic characterization of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces for
K
is an infinite countable compact space. This suggests further directions of a potential research as one could try to consider one-sided variants of some of the other results mentioned above. The most pressing open problem for us is, whether we can extend our Theorem
A
to uncountable compact metric spaces, that is, whether one could improve Miljutin’s theorem in the following sense.

Question 0.1
.

Let
K
be an uncountable compact metric space. Is it true that there exists an isomorphism
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}([0,1])
such that either
T\geq 0
or
T^{-1}\geq 0
?

Theorem
B
is very much related to the recent efforts of finding the Banach-Mazur distance between various
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces. This is motivated by the problem of Bessaga and Pełczyński
[
4
]
, posed in 1960, in which they asked for the exact value of the Banach-Mazur distance between
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{C}(\beta)
for ordinals
\alpha,\beta
. Pełczyński even raised a conjecture in 1968
[
24
, Problem 28]
as to whether this distance must always be an integer. In 1970 Gordon
[
13
]
proved that the distance between non-isometric
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces with
K
countable has to be at least
3
and
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))=3
. Since then, the problem remained basically untouched until 2013, when Galego with Candido
[
5
]
proved (among other results) that
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot n))\leq 2+\sqrt{5}
and
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{n}))\leq n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
for every
n\geq 2
. In 2025, Malec and Piasecki
[
21
]
proved that equality actually holds for the latter, and we have
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{n}))=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
. Our Theorem
B
improves and extends those results (we note that proofs required a number of new techniques and ideas and we refer the interested reader to Remark
2.8
, Remark
2.18
and Remark
3.2
for more details).

We should mention that the issue of estimating the value of
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\alpha),\mathcal{C}(\beta))
for various ordinals
\alpha,\beta
was undertaken also in the recent paper by Somaglia and the last author of this paper, see
[
27
]
. We find it quite interesting that despite the efforts of several researchers the problem of finding the exact value of
D_{3}:=d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 3))
remains open. In 2024 Gargent and Piasecki
[
9
]
proved
D_{3}\in(3.23,3.88)
, disproving the conjecture by Pełczyński from 1968 mentioned above, and in very recent preprint Korpalski and Plebanek
[
20
]
found even a more tight and more precise estimate which gives in particular the rough estimate
D_{3}\in(3.53,3.88)
.

Similarly, despite a considerable effort we were not able to find the exact value of
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))
and
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2),\mathcal{C}(\omega))
. The best estimates we have are
3\leq d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))\leq 2+\sqrt{3}
(see Proposition
3.4
) and
3\leq d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2),\mathcal{C}(\omega))\leq 2+\sqrt{5}
(see Theorem
B
). This motivates the following question.

Question 0.2
.

What are the values of
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))
and
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2),\mathcal{C}(\omega))
?

Our estimates also do not give answer to the question of whether the distances
d_{BM}
and
d_{BM}^{+}
are substantially different. We suspect the answer to the following question is positive, for instance for
\alpha=\omega
and
\beta=\omega\cdot 2
, but we were not able to find a proof.

Question 0.3
.

Are there countable ordinals
\omega\leq\alpha<\beta
such that
d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\alpha),\mathcal{C}(\beta))<\min\{d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\alpha),\mathcal{C}(\beta)),d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\beta),\mathcal{C}(\alpha))\}
?

1.
Preliminaries

All topological spaces are tacitly assumed to be Hausdorff. If
K
is a topological space and
\alpha
is an ordinal, by
K^{(\alpha)}
we denote the standard
Cantor-Bendixson derivative of
K
of order
\alpha
, that is, we have
K^{(0)}=K
,

K^{(1)}=\{x\in K:\,x\text{ is an accumulation point of }K\},

and for each ordinal
\alpha
,
K^{(\alpha+1)}=(K^{(\alpha)})^{(1)}
, and
K^{(\alpha)}=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}K^{(\beta)}
, if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. We note that it is easy to check that if for each open set
U\subseteq K
and an ordinal
\alpha
, it holds
U^{(\alpha)}=U\cap K^{(\alpha)}
, when
U
is considered as a topological space with the inherited topology, a fact that we will quite frequently use.

A compact space
K
is called
scattered
if there exists an ordinal
\alpha
such that
K^{(\alpha)}=\emptyset
. The
height
of a scattered compact space
K
, denoted as
\height(K)
, is defined as the smallest ordinal
\alpha
such that
K^{(\alpha)}=\emptyset
. It follows easily by compactness that
\height(K)
is a successor ordinal for each compact space
K
. The existence of a homeomorphism of compact spaces
K,L
is denoted by
K\simeq L
.

We also recall that each countable compact space is scattered, and moreover, the homeomorphism classes of such spaces are determined by their heights and the cardinality of the last nonempty derivative, by the Mazurkiewicz-Sierpinski classification (see e.g.
[
28
, Theorem 8.6.10]
). Also, it is a standard fact, which can be easily proved by transfinite induction, that for each ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denoting
K=[1,\omega^{\alpha}n]
, it holds

K^{(\alpha)}=\{\omega^{\alpha}i:\,i=1,\ldots,n\},

hence
\height(K)=\alpha+1
and
\left|K^{(\height(K)-1)}\right|=n
. Together with the aforementioned classification, it follows that each countable compact space is homeomorphic to the ordinal interval
[1,\omega^{\alpha}n]
for some countable ordinal
\alpha
and natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Next, if
K
is compact,
\mathcal{C}(K)
stands as usual for the Banach space of continuous real-valued functions on
K
endowed with the supremum norm.

2.
The classification

The main purpose of this section is to prove our main classification results.

2.1.
Preservation of height

We begin by establishing the fundamental rigidity of the topological height under positive embeddings.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
K
and
L
be compact spaces. If there exists a positive embedding of
\mathcal{C}(K)
into
\mathcal{C}(L)
, then
\height(K)\leq\height(L)
.

We start with a few auxiliary results.
Given a compact space
K
and a family
\mathcal{U}
of nonempty subsets of
K
, the
order
of
\mathcal{U}
is the smallest integer
k
(if it exists) such that each point of
K
lies in at most
k
sets from
\mathcal{U}
. The family
\mathcal{U}
is
point-bounded
if it has order
k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K,L
be compact spaces and
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
be a positive embedding. Assume that
\mathcal{U}
is a point-bounded system of open subsets of
K
, and for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
f_{U}\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
be a norm-
1
function which vanishes outside of the set
U
. Then for each
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
, the family
\{y\in L:\,Tf_{U}(y)\geq\varepsilon\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}

is a point-bounded system of compact sets that have nonempty interiors.

Proof.

The sets forming the system are clearly compact. To show that they have nonempty interiors it is enough to show that for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
and for each
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
, the set
\{y\in L:Tf_{U}(y)>\varepsilon\}
is nonempty.
This however follows from the elementary estimates

\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}\leq\left\|Tf_{U}\right\|=\sup\{\left|Tf_{U}(y)\right|:\,y\in L\}=\sup\{Tf_{U}(y):\,y\in L\},

since
f_{U}
is a positive function of norm
1
and the embedding
T
is positive.

To proceed further, let
n
denote the order of the system
\mathcal{U}
and assume that
k\in\mathbb{N}
is such that there exists a point
y\in L
and sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}
from
\mathcal{U}
such that
Tf_{U_{i}}(y)\geq\varepsilon
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
. Then, we have

k\varepsilon\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}Tf_{U_{i}}(y)\leq\left\|T\right\|\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{U_{i}}\right\|\leq\left\|T\right\|n.

Hence
k\leq\frac{\left\|T\right\|n}{\varepsilon}
, as desired.
∎

Now, we collect several general properties of positive embeddings of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces related to the Cantor-Bendixson derivatives of
K
, which are also used in Section
3
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
K
and
L
be compact spaces and
T:\mathcal{C}(K)\to\mathcal{C}(L)
be a positive embedding.

(a)

Assume
U\subseteq K
is open and
S\subseteq L
is compact with
\height(S)<\height(U)
. Then for each
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
, there exists a norm-
1
function
f
supported on
U
such that
\left\|Tf|_{S}\right\|<\varepsilon
.

(b)

For each
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
and any function
f\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
, if the set
\{x\in K:f(x)=1\}
contains an open set
U
with
U^{(\alpha)}\neq\emptyset
, then

\{y\in L:Tf(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

We first prove
(a)
by contradiction. Suppose that for every norm-
1
function
f\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
supported on
U
, we have
\left\|Tf_{|S}\right\|\geq\varepsilon
.
We claim that for every ordinal
\alpha
, if a function
g\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
is supported on
U
and satisfies
g=1
on an open set
V\subseteq U
with
V^{(\alpha)}\neq\emptyset
, then

\{y\in S:\,Tg(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}\neq\emptyset.

If this claim holds, then taking
\alpha=\height(S)
yields a contradiction, as
S^{(\height(S))}=\emptyset
by definition.

We proceed by transfinite induction on
\alpha
. The base case
\alpha=0
follows immediately from the assumption that
\left\|Tg_{|S}\right\|\geq\varepsilon
.
Assume the claim holds for
\alpha
. Let
g
be supported on
U
with
g=1
on
V
, where
V^{(\alpha+1)}\neq\emptyset
.
Choose a sequence of distinct points
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq V^{(\alpha)}
and a corresponding sequence of pairwise disjoint open neighborhoods
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq V
such that
x_{n}\in U_{n}
.
For each
n
, let
g_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
be a function supported on
U_{n}
such that
g_{n}=1
on a neighborhood of
x_{n}
.
By the inductive hypothesis, for each
n
there exists a point
y_{n}\in\{y\in S:\,Tg_{n}(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}
.

By Lemma
2.2
, the collection of sets
\{\{y\in S:\,Tg_{n}(y)\geq\varepsilon\}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is point-bounded. Consequently, the collection of their
\alpha
-derivatives is also point-bounded.
Since the points
y_{n}
belong to at most finitely many of these sets, the set
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{y\in S:\,Tg_{n}(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}
must be infinite.
Using the positivity of
T
and
g_{n}\leq g
, we obtain
Tg_{n}\leq Tg
. Therefore,

\{y\in S:\,Tg(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}\supseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{y\in S:\,Tg_{n}(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}.

Since the set on the left is compact and contains an infinite subset, it must have a nonempty derivative. Thus,
\{y\in S:\,Tg(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha+1)}\neq\emptyset
. Hence, the claim holds for
\alpha+1
.

If
\alpha
is a limit ordinal and the claim holds for each
\beta<\alpha
, then the claim holds for
\alpha
by compactness using the following intersection of nested compact sets:

\{y\in S:\,Tg(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\alpha)}=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}\{y\in S:\,Tg(y)\geq\varepsilon\}^{(\beta)}\neq\emptyset.

This completes the proof of
(a)
.

We now derive
(b)
from
(a)
. Suppose there exists a function
f
satisfying the hypothesis, but the set
K_{f}=\{y\in L:Tf(y)\geq\varepsilon\}
satisfies
K_{f}^{(\alpha)}=\emptyset
.
Let
S=K_{f}
. Then
\height(S)\leq\alpha
.
Since the support of
f
contains an open set
U
with
U^{(\alpha)}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\height(U)>\alpha\geq\height(S)
.
We can now apply
(a)
to find a norm-
1
function
h\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
supported on
U
such that
\left\|Th_{|S}\right\|<\varepsilon
.
Since
h\leq f
and
T
is positive, we have
Th\leq Tf
.
Consequently, if
y\notin S
, then
Tf(y)<\varepsilon
, and thus
Th(y)<\varepsilon
.
Combined with the fact that
\left\|Th_{|S}\right\|<\varepsilon
, we obtain
\left\|Th\right\|<\varepsilon
.
This contradicts the condition
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Apply Proposition
2.3
to the constant function
f\equiv\mathbf{1}
on
K
.
∎

2.2.
Positive classification of spaces on countable compacta

In order to formulate our second step towards the proof of Theorem
A
, let us recall the following notation. For an ordinal
\alpha
,
\Gamma(\alpha)
stands for the minimal ordinal of the form
\omega^{\beta}
which is not less than
\alpha
. That is, for any ordinal
\eta
we have
\Gamma(\omega^{\eta})=\omega^{\eta}
and
\Gamma(\alpha)=\omega^{\eta+1}
for ordinal
\alpha
satisfying
\omega^{\eta}<\alpha\leq\omega^{\eta+1}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
K
and
L
be infinite countable compact spaces. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a positive isomorphism from
C(K)
onto
C(L)
,

(ii)

\mathcal{C}(K)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}(L)
and
\height(K)\leq\height(L)
,

(iii)

\height(K)\leq\height(L)<\Gamma(\height(K))
.

We note that while proving
(iii)
implies
(i)
in Theorem
2.4
we also obtain quantitative estimates for the (one-sided) positive Banach-Mazur distance, some of which are new even in the classical setting. Some of those are mentioned in Theorem
B
above, for all the estimates we obtain in this section see Proposition
2.12
, Proposition
2.15
and Corollary
2.17
.

Before discussing proofs of our main results, let us mention some of the consequences. The first one is Corollary
A
stated in the introduction.

Proof of Corollary
A
.

The implication that if there exists a positive embedding of
\mathcal{C}(K)
into
\mathcal{C}(L)
, then
\height(K)\leq\height(L)
is shown in Theorem
2.1
.

For the other implication, the case when
K
is nonscattered follows from classical results. For the proof as well as related references, see
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
. If
K
is scattered, then it is homeomorphic to the ordinal interval
[1,\omega^{\alpha}n]
for some countable ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, again by
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
, there exists a positive isometric embedding of
\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}])
into
\mathcal{C}(L)
. Further, by Theorem
2.4
, there exists a positive isomorphism of
\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}n])
onto
\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}])
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let

be compact spaces such that

,

(ii)

(iii)

\mathcal{C}(K)

As an additional consequence of Theorem

we have the following.

.

For countable compact spaces

the following assertions are equivalent:

,

(ii)

onto

(iii)

(i)

Now, let us discuss the proof of Theorem
2.4
. We start with the following remark.

Remark 2.7
.

The equivalence between
(ii)
and
(iii)
is a direct consequence of the classical classification of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces for countable
K
by Bessaga and Pełczyński
[
4
]
. Recall that for infinite countable ordinals
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\alpha\leq\beta
, the spaces
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{C}(\beta)
are isomorphic if and only if
\beta<\alpha^{\omega}
. By basic ordinal arithmetic, this condition is equivalent to
\Gamma(\height(\alpha))=\Gamma(\height(\beta))
. Consequently, under the assumption
\height(K)\leq\height(L)
, isomorphism holds if and only if
\height(L)<\Gamma(\height(K))
.

The fact that
(i)
implies
(ii)
follows immediately from Theorem
2.1
. Concerning the implication that
(iii)
implies
(i)
we note the following.

Remark 2.8
.

Our proof of the implication
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
relies on a direct construction based on a decomposition of ordinal intervals, diverging from the classical method of Bessaga and Pełczyński (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Lemma 2.57]
). The classical argument utilizes the isomorphism between
\mathcal{C}(K)
and its
c_{0}
-sum. However, such a strategy is obstructed in the positive setting because
c
is not positively isomorphic to
c_{0}
(see Proposition
2.9
below). Consequently, a constructive approach appears necessary. Furthermore, this direct construction yields the explicit quantitative estimates for the positive Banach-Mazur distance formulated in Propositions
2.12
and
2.15
. We would like to note that we made a considerable effort to obtain optimal constants in Proposition
2.12
and Proposition
2.15
, if we were interested only in qualitative results, the proofs could be substantially simplified.

Proposition 2.9
.

There exists no order-preserving bijection
T:c\to c_{0}
such that
T^{-1}
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that such a map
T
exists. By replacing
T
with
f\mapsto T(f)-T(0)
, we may assume without loss of generality that
T(0)=0
. Let
L=\Lip(T^{-1})
.
Consider the constant sequence
\mathbf{1}\in c
. Since
T(\mathbf{1})\in c_{0}
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(T\mathbf{1})_{k}<\frac{1}{L}.

Define
x=\frac{1}{L}e_{k}\in c_{0}
, where
e_{k}
is the standard basis vector. Then
\left\|x\right\|=1/L
, and the Lipschitz condition implies

\left\|T^{-1}x\right\|=\left\|T^{-1}x-T^{-1}0\right\|\leq L\left\|x\right\|=1.

In the lattice structure of
c
,
\left\|y\right\|\leq 1
implies
y\leq\mathbf{1}
. Therefore,
T^{-1}x\leq\mathbf{1}
. Since
T
is order-preserving, this implies
x\leq T\mathbf{1}
. However, evaluating at the
k
-th coordinate yields

x_{k}=\frac{1}{L}>(T\mathbf{1})_{k},

which contradicts the inequality
x\leq T\mathbf{1}
.
∎

2.3.
Construction of the positive isomorphisms

In this subsection, we construct the positive isomorphisms required to establish the implication
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
of Theorem
2.4
. We note that, since homeomorphism classes of compact spaces correspond to Banach-lattice isometries of the respective spaces of continuous functions, we can instead construct positive isomorphisms of spaces of the form
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{K})
,
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{L})
, where
\tilde{K}\simeq K
and
\tilde{L}\simeq L
, which will enable us to simplify the notation several times. Our construction relies on a specific family of operators whose distortion is governed by a set of weights. We begin by determining the optimal constant associated with these weights, which will serve as a bound for the Banach-Mazur distance in our main results.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Delta_{n}=\{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in(0,1]^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1\}
. We define the constant
C(n)
as

C(n)=\inf_{\lambda\in\Delta_{n}}\left\{\max\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}}-1,\max_{i=2,\dots,n}\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{i}}+1\right)\right)\right\}.

Lemma 2.10
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
C(n)=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
, and the infimum is attained.

Proof.

Let

F(\lambda)\coloneqq\max\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}}-1,\ \max_{i=2,\dots,n}\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{i}}+1\right)\right),\qquad\lambda\in\Delta_{n}.

Fix
\lambda_{1}
and let
m\coloneqq\min_{2\leq i\leq n}\lambda_{i}
. Since
\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\geq(n-1)m
, we have

m\leq\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{n-1}.

The function
t\mapsto\frac{2}{t}+1
is strictly decreasing on
(0,1]
. We have

\max_{i=2,\dots,n}\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{i}}+1\right)=\frac{2}{m}+1\geq\frac{2(n-1)}{1-\lambda_{1}}+1.

Moreover, equality holds for
\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{n}=\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{n-1}
. Therefore,

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Delta_{n}\\
\lambda_{1}=t\end{subarray}}F(\lambda)=\max\left(\frac{2}{t}-1,\ \frac{2(n-1)}{1-t}+1\right).

Thus

C(n)=\inf_{t\in(0,1)}\max\left(\frac{2}{t}-1,\ \frac{2(n-1)}{1-t}+1\right).

Since
\frac{2}{t}-1
is decreasing and
\frac{2(n-1)}{1-t}+1
is increasing, the quantity above is minimized at the unique point
x\in(0,1)
where

\frac{2}{x}-1=\frac{2(n-1)}{1-x}+1.

This yields

x^{2}-(n+1)x+1=0.

The only root in
(0,1)
is
x=\frac{(n+1)-\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}}{2}
. Thus

C(n)=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

This infimum is achieved by setting
\lambda_{1}=x
and
\lambda_{2}=\dots=\lambda_{n}=\frac{1-x}{n-1}
where
x
is the root above.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\alpha
be a countable nonzero ordinal,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
. For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
there are
I_{i}\subset[1,\omega^{\alpha})
and surjective homeomorphisms
p_{i,j}:I_{i}\to[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(j-1)+1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j)
for
j=1,\ldots,k
such that

(p-a)

the sets
\{I_{i}:\,i=1,\ldots,k\}
are pairwise disjoint,

(p-b)

\overline{I_{i}}=I_{i}\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}\}
for every
i=1,\ldots,k
and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\overline{I_{i}}
is homeomorphic to
[0,\omega^{\alpha}]
,

(p-c)

For any sequence
(\gamma_{m})
in
I_{i}
we have
\gamma_{m}\to\omega^{\alpha}
if and only if
p_{i,j}(\gamma_{m})\to\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j
.

(p-d)

For every
i,j,v,w=1,\ldots,k
we have

p_{w,j}\circ p_{w,v}^{-1}\circ p_{i,v}=p_{i,j}
.

Proof.

In the proof we shall construct compact ordinal intervals
I_{i,n}\subset[0,\omega^{\alpha})
,
J_{j,n}\subset[0,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k)
such that if we let
I_{i}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i,n}
and
J_{j}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}J_{j,n}
, then

(i-a)

the intervals
\{I_{i,n}:\,n\in\mathbb{N},\;i=1,\ldots,k\}
are pairwise disjoint and
\{J_{j,n}:\,n\in\mathbb{N},\;j=1,\ldots,k\}
are pairwise disjoint as well,

(i-b)

\overline{I_{i}}=I_{i}\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}\}
for every
i=1,\ldots,k
and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\overline{I_{i}}
is homeomorphic to
[0,\omega^{\alpha}]
,

(i-c)

J_{j}=[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(j-1)+1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j)
for every
j=1,\ldots,k
.

This will imply conditions
(p-a)
and
(p-b)
hold. Further, we shall define surjective homeomorphisms
p_{i,j}^{n}:I_{i,n}\to J_{j,n}
and define
p_{i,j}:I_{i}\to J_{j}
as
p_{i,j}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i,j}^{n}
(that is,
p_{i,j}(x)=p_{i,j}^{n}(x)
for
x\in I_{i,n}
).

Consider first the case when
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, that is,
\alpha=\beta+1
for some countable ordinal
\beta
. Pick
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let

\begin{split}I_{i,n}&:=[\omega^{\beta}\cdot(kn+i-1)+1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot(kn+i)],\\
J_{j,n}&:=[\omega^{\beta+1}\cdot(j-1)+\omega^{\beta}\cdot(n-1)+1,\omega^{\beta+1}\cdot(j-1)+\omega^{\beta}\cdot n].\end{split}

It is obvious that
(i-a)
and
(i-c)
holds and that
\overline{I_{i}}=I_{i}\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}\}
. Further, since
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\overline{I_{i}}=[\omega^{\beta}\cdot k+1,\omega^{\alpha}]
, using the Mazurkiewicz-Sierpinksi classification it is easy to observe that
(i-b)
holds as well. Now, we define the homeomorphisms
p_{i,j}^{n}:I_{i,n}\to J_{j,n}
by

\begin{split}p_{i,j}^{n}(\omega^{\beta}\cdot(kn+i-1)+\gamma)&:=\omega^{\beta+1}\cdot(j-1)+\omega^{\beta}\cdot(n-1)+\gamma,\qquad\gamma\in[1,\omega^{\beta}].\end{split}

Now, it is easy to check that
p_{i,j}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i,j}^{n}
is homeomorphism and the properties
(p-c)
and
(p-d)
hold.

Consider now the case when
\alpha
is a limit ordinal and pick an increasing sequence of ordinals
(\beta_{n})
with
\beta_{0}:=0
and
\beta_{n}\to\alpha
. Pick
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let

\begin{split}J_{j,n}&:=[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(j-1)+\omega^{\beta_{n-1}}+1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(j-1)+\omega^{\beta_{n}}],\\
I_{1,n}&:=[\omega^{\beta_{n-1}}\cdot k+1,\omega^{\beta_{n}}]\end{split}

and if
i>1
then

I_{i,n}:=[\omega^{\beta_{n}}\cdot(i-1)+\omega^{\beta_{n-1}}+1,\omega^{\beta_{n}}\cdot i].

It is obvious that
(i-a)
and
(i-c)
holds. Further, using the Mazurkiewicz-Sierpinksi classification, it is easy to observe that each
I_{i,n}
is homeomorphic to
[1,\omega^{\beta_{n}}]
and
(i-b)
holds as well. Now, we define the homeomorphisms
p_{i,j}^{n}:I_{i,n}\to J_{j,n}
by

p_{i,j}^{n}(\omega^{\beta_{n}}\cdot(i-1)+\gamma):=\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(j-1)+\gamma,\qquad\gamma\in[\omega^{\beta_{n-1}}+1,\omega^{\beta_{n}}].

Now, it is easy to check that
p_{i,j}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i,j}^{n}
is homeomorphism and all the properties
(p-c)
and
(p-d)
hold.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\alpha
be a countable nonzero ordinal and
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
. Then

d_{BM}^{+}\Big(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}k),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha})\Big)\leq k+\sqrt{(k-1)(k+3)}.

Proof.

Let
p_{i,j}:I_{i}\to J_{j}
,
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
be homeomorphisms from Lemma 4.1. Put
I:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\overline{I_{i}}
, which is by Lemma 4.1 homeomorphic to
[0,\omega^{\alpha}]
. We shall construct a positive isomorphism
T:\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k])\to\mathcal{C}(I\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}+i:\,i=1,\ldots,k-1\})
with
\left\|T\right\|\leq 1
and
\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\leq k+\sqrt{(k-1)(k+3)}
(this is sufficient, because
[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
is homeomorphic to
I\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}+i:\,i=1,\ldots,k-1\}
).

In order to provide the reader with some insight we shall actually construct a family of isomorphisms out of which we shall pick the one with the best estimate we are able to get using our method.

Fix weights
\lambda\in\Delta_{k}
. We define the mapping
T\colon\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k])\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(I\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}+i:\,i=1,\ldots,k-1\})
as follows. Fix
f\in\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k])
. For
j=1,\ldots,k-1
we put

Tf(\omega^{\alpha}+j)\coloneqq f(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j),

further we let

Tf(\omega^{\alpha})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\cdot f(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j)

and finally, given
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
\gamma\in I_{i}
we let

Tf(\gamma)\coloneqq\sum_{j=i}^{k}\lambda_{j}\cdot f(p_{i,j}(\gamma))+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\lambda_{j}\cdot f(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j).

Pictorially, we may visualize the mapping
T
as follows.

\begin{pmatrix}Tf(\omega^{\alpha}+1)\\
\vdots\\
Tf(\omega^{\alpha}+k-1)\\
Tf(\omega^{\alpha})\\
\hline\cr Tf|_{I_{1}}\\
Tf|_{I_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
Tf|_{I_{k}}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc}1&0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&0&\vdots&\vdots&&&\vdots\\
0&0&1&0&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{k-1}&\lambda_{k}&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\cdots&\cdots&0&\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{k}\\
\lambda_{1}&0&\cdots&0&0&\lambda_{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&0&0&0&0&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{k-1}&0&0&0&0&\lambda_{k}\\
\end{array}\right)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}f(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot 1)\\
\vdots\\
f(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(k-1))\\
f(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k)\\
\hline\cr f(p_{i,1}(\gamma))\\
f(p_{i,2}(\gamma))\\
\vdots\\
f(p_{i,k}(\gamma))\\
\end{pmatrix}

First, let us notice that from
(p-b)
and
(p-c)
we easily obtain that
Tf
is a continuous function for each
f\in C([1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k])
. By the choice of coefficients we also easily obtain that
T\geq 0
and
\left\|T\right\|\leq 1
.

We now define the inverse operator

S:\mathcal{C}\Big(I\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}+i\}_{i=1}^{k-1}\Big)\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k])

by prescribing
Sh
on each block
J_{l}=[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(l-1)+1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot l)
and
at the endpoints
\omega^{\alpha}\cdot l
for
h\in\mathcal{C}(I\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}+i\}_{i=1}^{k-1})
. The idea to obtain the inverse is to formally describe the inverse of the matrix used to generate the mapping
T
.

Let

\displaystyle(Sh)(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)\quad(j=1,\ldots,k-1),

\displaystyle(Sh)(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\Big(h(\omega^{\alpha})-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{j}\,h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)\Big).

For
\gamma\in J_{k}
, the last block, define

(Sh)(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\Big(h\!\big(p_{k,k}^{-1}(\gamma)\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{j}\,h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)\Big).

We define the rest inductively. Fix
l\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
and assume that
Sh
has already been defined on

\bigcup_{j=l+1}^{k}J_{j}\quad\text{and on}\quad\{\omega^{\alpha}\cdot j\}_{j=l+1}^{k}.

For
\gamma\in J_{l}
define

(Sh)(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda_{l}}\Bigg(h\!\big(p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma)\big)-\sum_{j=l+1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\,(Sh)\!\big(p_{l,j}\circ p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma)\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\lambda_{j}\,h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)\Bigg).

Note that for
j>l
and
\gamma\in J_{l}
, the point

p_{l,j}\circ p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma)
belongs to
J_{j}

(since
p_{l,l}^{-1}
maps
J_{l}
to
I_{l}
, and
p_{l,j}
maps
I_{l}
to
J_{j}
),
so the value
(Sh)\big(p_{l,j}\circ p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma)\big)
is defined at the time it is used.

Now, a slightly tedious but routine verification leads us to the observation that
S
is defined in such a way that
S\circ T=\operatorname{Id}
. Now, we shall aim at obtaining a better formula for
Sh(\gamma)
. Using the convention that for
M<N
the sum
\sum_{N}^{M}
is equal to zero, for
l\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
and
\gamma\in[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(l-1)+1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot l)
, denoting
\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\coloneqq p_{l,j}(p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma))
for
j=l+1,\ldots,k
we may write

\begin{split}\lambda_{l}(Sh)(\gamma)&=h(p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma))+\lambda_{l}h(\omega^{\alpha}+l)-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\lambda_{j}h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)-\sum_{j=l+2}^{k}\lambda_{j}(Sh)(\gamma_{j}^{\prime})-\lambda_{l+1}(Sh)(\gamma^{\prime}_{l+1})\\
&=h(p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma))+\lambda_{l}h(\omega^{\alpha}+l)-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\lambda_{j}h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)-\sum_{j=l+2}^{k}\lambda_{j}(Sh)(\gamma_{j}^{\prime})\\
&\qquad-\Big(h(p_{l+1,l+1}^{-1}(\gamma_{l+1}^{\prime}))-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\lambda_{j}h(\omega^{\alpha}+j)-\sum_{j=l+2}^{k}\lambda_{j}(Sh)(p_{l+1,j}(p_{l+1,l+1}^{-1}(\gamma_{l+1}^{\prime})))\Big)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{it:ordHomeo2}}}{{=}}h(p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma))+\lambda_{l}h(\omega^{\alpha}+l)-h(p_{l+1,l+1}^{-1}(p_{l,l+1}(p_{l,l}^{-1}(\gamma)))).\end{split}

Using this formula for
Sh
and properties
(p-b)
,
(p-c)
we can easily check that
Sh
is indeed a continuous function. Finally, using
(p-d)
a slightly tedious but routine verification gives
T\circ S=\operatorname{Id}
.

This shows that
T
is a linear isomorphism with
T^{-1}=S
. Using
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1
, the above formula for
S
gives us the estimate

\left\|S\right\|\leq\max\Big\{\frac{2}{\lambda_{k}}-1,1+\frac{2}{\lambda_{l}}:\,l=1,\ldots,k-1\Big\}.

Now, by Lemma
2.10
we obtain that a suitable choice of
\lambda\in(0,1]^{k}
gives us the estimate
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\leq C(k)
as required.

∎

Lemma 2.13

\alpha

\{I_{n}:\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}

(i-a)

For every

and
\overline{I_{n}}=I_{n}\cup\{\omega^{\alpha}\}

(i-b)

We have
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\min I_{n})=\omega^{\alpha}
.

We shall define each
I_{n}

I_{n}^{k}
,

\alpha=\beta+1
for some countable ordinal
\beta

\begin{split}\text{block 1}:&\quad I_{1}^{1}:=[0,\omega^{\beta}];\\

\text{block 3}:&\quad I_{3}^{1}:=[\omega^{\beta}\cdot 3+1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot 4],\;I_{2}^{2}:=[\omega^{\beta}\cdot 4+1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot 5],\;I_{1}^{3}:=[\omega^{\beta}\cdot 5+1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot 6]\end{split}

and in general, for

and

I_{n+1-k}^{k+1}:=[\omega^{\beta}\cdot(\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}+k)+1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot(\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}+k+1)].

Then, as mentioned above, we put
I_{n}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}^{k}
. Condition
(i-a)
holds, because each

[\omega^{\beta}\cdot n_{k}+1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot n_{k+1}]

(n_{k})
and condition

\min I_{n+1}=\min I_{n+1}^{1}=\omega^{\beta}\cdot(\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2})+1\to\omega^{\beta+1}=\omega^{\alpha}

Now, consider the case when
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Pick an increasing sequence
(\beta_{n})
of ordinals which converge to
\alpha
. Then we let

\begin{split}\text{block 1}:&\quad I_{1}^{1}:=[0,\omega^{\beta_{1}}];\\
\text{block 2}:&\quad I_{2}^{1}:=[\omega^{\beta_{1}}+1,\omega^{\beta_{1}}\cdot 2],\;I_{1}^{2}:=[\omega^{\beta_{1}}\cdot 2+1,\omega^{\beta_{2}}];\\
\text{block 3}:&\quad I_{3}^{1}:=[\omega^{\beta_{2}}+1,\omega^{\beta_{2}}+\omega^{\beta_{1}}],\;I_{2}^{2}:=[\omega^{\beta_{2}}+\omega^{\beta_{1}}+1,\omega^{\beta_{2}}\cdot 2],\;I_{1}^{3}:=[\omega^{\beta_{2}}\cdot 2+1,\omega^{\beta_{3}}]\end{split}

\begin{split}I_{n+1}^{1}&:=[\omega^{\beta_{n}}+1,\omega^{\beta_{n}}+\omega^{\beta_{1}}],\\

Again, as mentioned above, we put

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be nonzero countable ordinals and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

Each ordinal
y\in[1,\omega^{\alpha n}\beta]
can be uniquely expressed in the form

y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega^{\alpha(n-i+1)}\gamma_{i}
,
for some
1\leq k\leq n+1
,
\gamma_{1}\in[0,\beta]
,
\gamma_{i}\in[0,\omega^{\alpha})
for
2\leq i\leq k
, and such that
\gamma_{k}\neq 0
.

(ii)

Each ordinal
y\in[1,\omega^{\alpha n})
can be uniquely expressed in the form

y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega^{\alpha(n-i)}\gamma_{i}
,
for some
1\leq k\leq n
,
\gamma_{i}\in[0,\omega^{\alpha})
for
1\leq i\leq k
, and such that
\gamma_{k}\neq 0
.

Sketch of the proof.

The proof is easy and standard (even though with full details quite tedious), so we give just a sketch of the proof and leave the details to the interested reader.

(i)
: We write
y
in its Cantor normal form and decompose further as

y=\underbrace{\omega^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot n_{1}+\ldots+\omega^{\alpha_{{k_{1}}}}\cdot n_{k_{1}}}_{=:y_{1}}+\ldots+\underbrace{\omega^{\alpha_{k_{n}+1}}\cdot n_{k_{n}+1}+\ldots+\omega^{\alpha_{{k_{n+1}}}}\cdot n_{k_{n+1}}}_{=:y_{n+1}},

where

\alpha_{1}>\ldots>\alpha_{k_{1}}\geq\alpha\cdot n>\alpha_{k_{1}+1}>\ldots>\alpha_{k_{i}}\geq\alpha(n-i+1)>\alpha_{k_{i}+1}>\ldots>\alpha_{k_{n+1}}\geq 0

and
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
for
i=1,\ldots,k_{n+1}
. Next, we find the unique
\gamma_{1}\in[0,\beta]
satisfying
y_{1}=\omega^{\alpha\cdot n}\cdot\gamma_{1}
and for every
2\leq i\leq n+1
we find the unique
\gamma_{i}\in[0,\omega^{\alpha})
with
y_{i}=\omega^{\alpha\cdot(n-i+1)}\cdot\gamma_{i}
. Finally, we pick
k
such that
y_{k}\neq 0
and
y_{i}=0
for
i>k
.

(ii)
: Is similar to the proof of
(i)
and therefore left to the reader.
∎

\lambda_{i}
, we obtain the bound
\left\|T\right\|\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\leq C(n)=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
.
∎

Next, before we proceed to the proof of Theorem
2.4
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be countable nonzero ordinals such that
\alpha\leq\beta<\alpha\omega
, and let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a countable ordinal
\gamma<\omega^{\alpha}
such that
[1,\omega^{\beta}m]
is homeomorphic to
[1,\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma]
.

Proof.

We find a maximal
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha n\leq\beta
. Further, we find an ordinal
\delta
such that
\alpha n+\delta=\beta
(yes, this is possible, see e.g.
[
16
, Lemma 2.25 (ii)]
), and we set
\gamma=\omega^{\delta}m
. Then,

\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma=\omega^{\alpha n}(\omega^{\delta}m)=\omega^{\alpha n+\delta}m=\omega^{\beta}m.

Now, assuming that
\gamma\geq\omega^{\alpha}
, we would have
\omega^{\beta}m=\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma\geq\omega^{\alpha(n+1)}
,
which implies
\beta\geq\alpha(n+1)
, and this contradicts the maximality of
n
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
2.4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

The implication
(i)

\Rightarrow

(ii)
follows directly from Proposition
2.3
(c). The equivalence between
(ii)
and
(iii)
is a consequence of the classical classification of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces by Bessaga and Pełczyński (see Remark
2.7
).

We prove the implication
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
. Let
K
and
L
be infinite countable compact spaces such that
\height(K)\leq\height(L)<\Gamma(\height(K))
. By the Mazurkiewicz-Sierpiński classification, we may assume without loss of generality that

K=[1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k]\quad\text{and}\quad L=[1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot m],

where
\alpha,\beta
are countable ordinals and
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
The condition on the heights implies that
\alpha\leq\beta<\alpha\omega
.

We construct a positive isomorphism between
\mathcal{C}(K)
and
\mathcal{C}(L)
via a chain of positive isomorphisms.
First, by Proposition
2.12
, there exists a positive isomorphism

\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot k])\simeq\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}]).

Next, since
\alpha\leq\beta<\alpha\omega
, we apply Lemma
2.16
to find
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma<\omega^{\alpha}
such that
[1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot m]
is homeomorphic to
[1,\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma]
. This homeomorphism induces a positive isomorphism (an isometry)

\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\beta}\cdot m])\simeq\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma]).

It remains to find a positive isomorphism between
\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}])
and
\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma])
.
Since
\gamma<\omega^{\alpha}
, the space
[1,\omega^{\alpha}]
is homeomorphic to
[1,\omega^{\alpha}+\gamma]
. Thus, it suffices to construct a positive isomorphism

T:\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha}+\gamma])\to\mathcal{C}([1,\omega^{\alpha n}\gamma]).

The existence of such an operator is guaranteed by Proposition
2.15
(i).
Composing these operators yields the required positive isomorphism from
\mathcal{C}(K)
to
\mathcal{C}(L)
.
∎

Furthermore, by combining our construction with the main result of
[
27
]
, we obtain the following tight bounds on the distances between spaces of continuous functions on ordinal intervals. Observe that the difference between the upper and lower bounds is at most
2
in all cases.

Corollary 2.17
.

For each countable nonzero ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

2n-1\leq d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha n}))\leq d_{BM^{+}}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha n}))\leq n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

Proof.

The upper estimate is a direct consequence of Proposition
2.15
(ii). To deduce the lower estimate, consider the Cantor normal form of
\alpha
. Let
\omega^{\beta}k
be the leading term of this expansion (where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta
is an ordinal). Then we have

\height([1,\omega^{\alpha}])=\alpha+1\leq\omega^{\beta}(k+1).

On the other hand,

\height([1,\omega^{\alpha n}])=\alpha n+1>\omega^{\beta}nk.

Consequently, by applying
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
, we obtain

d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha n}))\geq\frac{2nk-k}{k}=2n-1.

∎

Remark 2.18
.

The upper bound in Corollary
2.17
for the specific case
\alpha=1
can be derived from the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 1.4]
. Although that result is formulated for the general Banach-Mazur distance, an inspection of the proof reveals that the constructed isomorphism is indeed positive. However, even for
\alpha=1
, the model considered in the proof of Proposition
2.15
is more flexible: it relies on
n
variables (
\lambda_{i}
) allowing for finer optimization, whereas the model in
[
5
]
depends on a single variable
A
. Moreover, the use of our tuple notation allows for a more compact presentation of both the isomorphism and its inverse. Finally, we emphasize that extending this construction to the case
\alpha>1
required overcoming non-trivial topological obstacles not present in the
\alpha=1
setting.

3.
Better estimates of the distance

In this section, we focus on improving the estimates for the positive Banach-Mazur distance. Our main result is the following theorem, which establishes the exact value of the distance for a specific class of ordinals. This result is new even in the context of the classical Banach-Mazur distance. We note that the upper bound is a direct consequence of Corollary
2.17
, so the primary contribution here is the lower bound.

Theorem 3.1
.

For each countable ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\omega^{\alpha}}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\omega^{\alpha}n}))=d_{BM^{+}}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\omega^{\alpha}}),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\omega^{\alpha}n}))=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

Remark 3.2
.

For the case
\alpha=0
, the lower bound in Theorem
3.1
constitutes the main result of the recent paper by Malec and Piasecki
[
21
]
. Our proof of the lower bound adapts their argument, utilizing techniques from
[
27
]
to extend the result to this general ordinal setting.

We also obtain the following two results which are specific for the (one-sided) positive distance.

Theorem 3.3
.

For each ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

2n-1\leq d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}))\leq n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

Proposition 3.4
.

We have
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))\leq 2+\sqrt{3}
.

In the remainder of this section we head towards the proofs of the results announced above.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

We begin by proving Theorem

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be compact spaces and
T:\mathcal{C}(K_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(K_{2})
be an isomorphism with
\left\|T^{-1}\right\|=1
. Assume that for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
, norm-
1
functions
f_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1]),i=1,\ldots,n
, satisfy

•

\left\|Tf_{n}\right\|<C-(n-1)\varepsilon

•

for each
i=1,\ldots,n-1
,
\operatorname{supp}f_{i}\subseteq\{x\in K_{1}:f_{i+1}(x)=1\}
, and

•

the sets
\{y\in K_{2}:\left|Tf_{i}(y)\right|\geq\varepsilon\}_{i=1}^{n}
are pairwise disjoint.

Then, there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
such that

\left\|Tf_{i}\right\|\geq\frac{C(n-1)}{C-1}-\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{C-1}\varepsilon.

Proof.

We assume that for each
i=1,\ldots,n-1
,

\left\|Tf_{i}\right\|<\frac{C(n-1)}{C-1}-\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{C-1}\varepsilon,

and we seek a contradiction. By the assumptions, there exists a point
x\in K_{1}
such that for each
i=1,\ldots,n
,
f_{i}(x)=1
. Thus, it follows that

\left\|f_{n}+\frac{C-1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{i}\right\|=1+\frac{C-1}{n-1}(n-1)=C.

On the other hand, given any point
y\in K_{2}
, there are
n-1
indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that each of them satisfies
\left|Tf_{i}(y)\right|<\varepsilon
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\left\|T(f_{n}+\frac{C-1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{i})\right\|<

\displaystyle<\max_{y\in K_{2}}\Big\{\max\{\left|Tf_{n}(y)\right|,\max_{i=1,\ldots,n-1}\frac{C-1}{n-1}\left|Tf_{i}(y)\right|\}\Big\}+(n-1)\varepsilon<C,

which contradicts the assumption that
\left\|T^{-1}\right\|=1
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be compact spaces,
T:\mathcal{C}(K_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(K_{2})
be an isomorphism with
\left\|T^{-1}\right\|=1
, let
n\in\mathbb{N},n\geq 2
, and let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exist norm-
1
functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g,h\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
and a clopen set
U\subseteq K_{1}
such that:

(a-a)

for each
i=1,\ldots,n-1
,
\operatorname{supp}f_{i}\subseteq\{x\in K_{1}:f_{i+1}(x)=1\}
,

(a-b)

\operatorname{supp}f_{n}\subseteq U\subseteq\{x\in K_{1}:g(x)=1\}
,

(a-c)

U\cap\operatorname{supp}h=\emptyset
,

(a-d)

the sets
S_{i}=\{y\in K_{2}:\left|Tf_{i}(y)\right|\geq\varepsilon\}_{i=1}^{n}
are pairwise disjoint,

(a-e)

for each

S_{i}\subseteq\{y\in K_{2}:\left|T(T^{-1}(\mathbf{1}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{U})(y)\right|<\varepsilon\}
,

(a-f)

for each

S_{i}\subseteq\{y\in K_{2}:\left|Tg(y)-\xi\right|<\varepsilon\}\cap\{y\in K_{2}:\left|Th(y)-\xi\right|<\varepsilon\},

and

(a-g)

Then,
\left\|T\right\|\geq n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma in several steps. To shorten the notation, we again denote
C(n)=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
. We assume that
\left\|T\right\|<C(n)
, and we find a small enough
\varepsilon>0
so that all the inequalities

(i-a)

\left\|T\right\|<C(n)-(2n-1)\varepsilon
,

(i-b)

\left\|T\right\|<C(n)-2\varepsilon-\frac{4(n-1)^{2}\varepsilon}{C(n)-1}

(i-c)

\left\|T\right\|<C(n)-2\varepsilon-(n-1)\frac{C(n)-2n+1}{2}\varepsilon
,

are satisfied.

Next, before we proceed further, to make the proof simpler to read, we collect the relations that the constant
C(n)
satisfies and which are required for the following proof to work:

(r-a)

1+2\frac{(C(n)+1)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}=C(n),

(r-b)

C(n)-\frac{2C(n)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}=\frac{C(n)-2n+3}{2},

(r-c)

\frac{2(C(n)-1)}{C(n)-2n+1}=C(n)+1>C(n).

Now, we proceed to the first step of the proof. We first claim that there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
such that

(3.1)

\left\|Tf_{i}\right\|\geq\frac{C(n)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}-\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{C(n)-1}\varepsilon.

However, since
\left\|Tf_{n}\right\|\leq\left\|T\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:ineq_1}}}}{{<}}C(n)-(n-1)\varepsilon
, this follows immediately from Lemma
3.5
.

In the second step, we claim that

(3.2)

\left\|Tf_{n}\right\|\geq\frac{C(n)+1}{2}-(n-1)\varepsilon.

Assuming the contrary, using Lemma
3.5
again, there exists an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
such that

\left\|Tf_{i}\right\|\geq\frac{\frac{C(n)+1}{2}}{\frac{C(n)-1}{2}}(n-1)-\frac{(n-1)^{2}\varepsilon}{\frac{C(n)-1}{2}}=\frac{(C(n)+1)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}-\frac{2(n-1)^{2}\varepsilon}{C(n)-1}.

Hence, for an appropriate choice of sign
\lambda\in S_{\mathbb{R}}

\left\|\lambda T^{-1}(\mathbf{1}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{K_{1}\setminus U}+f_{n}-2f_{i}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:supports},\ref{it:supports2}}}}{{=}}1,

(notice that
\left\|\lambda T^{-1}(\mathbf{1}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{K_{1}\setminus U}\right\|\leq 1

\displaystyle\left\|T(\lambda T^{-1}(\mathbf{1}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{K_{1}\setminus U}+f_{n}-2f_{i})\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:almost 0},\ref{it:mapped supports}}}}{{\geq}}

a contradiction.

Next, we want to show that

1<\left|\xi\right|<\frac{C(n)-2n+3}{2}.

To prove the first inequality, we have

hence

\left|\xi\right|\geq C(n)+1-(2n-1)\varepsilon-\left\|T\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:ineq_1}}}}{{>}}1.

For the second inequality, we first find
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
such that
Tf_{i}
satisfies the estimate (
3.1
). Then, we pick an appropriate sign
\lambda\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
, and by
(a-a)
,
(a-b)
and
(a-c)
, we get

\left\|\lambda h+f_{n}-2f_{i}\right\|=1,

hence

\displaystyle\left\|T\right\|\geq\left\|T(\lambda h+f_{n}-2f_{i})\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:almost constant},\ref{it:mapped supports}}}}{{\geq}}\left|\xi\right|-\varepsilon-\varepsilon+2\frac{C(n)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}-2\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{C(n)-1}\varepsilon.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left|\xi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|T\right\|+2\varepsilon\Big(1+\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{C(n)-1}\Big)-2\frac{C(n)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:ineq_2}}}}{{<}}C(n)-2\frac{C(n)(n-1)}{C(n)-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:rel_2}}}}{{=}}\frac{C(n)-2n+3}{2}.

In the fourth step, we modify the estimate of the norm of

from the second step by proving

(3.4)

\left\|Tf_{n}\right\|\geq\frac{C(n)-\left|\xi\right|}{C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|}-(n-1)\varepsilon.

Notice that the estimate
\left|\xi\right|<\frac{C(n)-2n+3}{2}
obtained in the previous step implies that the above denominator is positive.

Assuming that (

such that

\displaystyle\left\|Tf_{i}\right\|\geq\frac{\frac{C(n)-\left|\xi\right|}{C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|}}{\frac{C(n)-\left|\xi\right|}{C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|}-1}(n-1)-\frac{(n-1)^{2}\varepsilon}{\frac{C(n)-\left|\xi\right|}{C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|}-1}=

\displaystyle=\frac{C(n)-\left|\xi\right|}{2}-\frac{(n-1)(C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|)\varepsilon}{2}.

Thus, for a appropriate sign
\lambda\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
, using

,
(a-b)
and

we get

\left\|\lambda h+f_{n}-2f_{i}\right\|=1,

and

\displaystyle\left\|T(\lambda h+f_{n}-2f_{i})\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:almost constant},\ref{it:mapped supports}}}}{{\geq}}

\displaystyle\geq\left|\xi\right|-\varepsilon-\varepsilon+C(n)-\left|\xi\right|-(n-1)(C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|)\varepsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\eqref{eq:value of xi}}}}{{\geq}}

\displaystyle\geq C(n)-2\varepsilon-(n-1)\Big(C(n)-2n+2-\frac{C(n)-2n+3}{2}\Big)\varepsilon=

\displaystyle=C(n)-2\varepsilon-(n-1)\frac{C(n)-2n+1}{2}\varepsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:ineq_4}}}}{{>}}\left\|T\right\|,

a contradiction once again.

Finally, in the last step, we get

\left\|g-2f_{n}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:supports2}}}}{{=}}1,

while

\displaystyle\left\|T(g-2f_{n})\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:almost constant 2},\eqref{eq:norm Tf_n again}}}}{{\geq}}\left|\xi\right|-\varepsilon+2\frac{C(n)-\left|\xi\right|}{C(n)-2n+2-\left|\xi\right|}-2(n-1)\varepsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\eqref{eq:value of xi}}}}{{>}}

\displaystyle>1+2\frac{C(n)-\frac{C(n)-2n+3}{2}}{C(n)-2n+2-1}-(2n-1)\varepsilon=\frac{2(C(n)-1)}{C(n)-2n+1}-(2n-1)\varepsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\ref{it:rel_3}}}}{{>}}

which is the final contradiction that finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be nonempty compact spaces. Suppose that
T:\mathcal{C}(K_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(K_{2})
is an isomorphic embedding, let
L_{1}\subseteq K_{1}
,
U
be an open set containing
L_{1}
, and let
L_{2}\subseteq K_{2}
be a compact set. If there exists an ordinal
\alpha
such that
\height(L_{1})>\omega^{\alpha}
and
\height(L_{2})\leq\omega^{\alpha}
, then for each
\varepsilon>0
there exist functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
, pairwise disjoint open sets
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq U
, and points
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq L_{1}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}=1
on an open neighbourhood of
x_{n}
,
f_{n}=0
on
K_{1}\setminus U_{n}
, and
\left\|Tf_{n}|_{L_{2}}\right\|<\varepsilon
.

Moreover, if
K_{1}
is zero-dimensional, then the sets
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be taken clopen.

Proof.

That there exists one such function is the content of
[
27
, Proposition 2.4(a)]
. To show that there are infinitely many such functions that are disjointly supported, we need to just slightly modify the argument from the proof of
[
27
, Proposition 2.4]
.

First, we assume that
\alpha=0
. Then, the assumptions of the proposition imply that
L_{2}
is finite, while
L_{1}
is infinite. Since
L_{1}
is infinite, there exist functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
, pairwise disjoint open sets
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq U
, and points
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq L_{1}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}=1
on an open neighbourhood of
x_{n}
,
f_{n}=0
on
K_{1}\setminus U_{n}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. It is sufficient to show that any finite subcollection
(f_{j})_{j\in M}
with
|M|>\|T\||L_{2}|/\varepsilon
contains
j_{0}\in M
such that
\|Tf_{j_{0}}|_{L_{2}}\|<\varepsilon
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
\|Tf_{j}|_{L_{2}}\|\geq\epsilon
for all
j\in M
. Then for each
j
, there exists
y_{j}\in L_{2}
such that
|Tf_{j}(y_{j})|\geq\epsilon
. By the pigeonhole principle, there exists
y_{0}\in L_{2}
and a subset
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
with
|M^{\prime}|\geq\frac{|M|}{|L_{2}|}
such that
y_{j}=y_{0}
for all
j\in M^{\prime}
. Choosing signs
\sigma_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
such that
\sigma_{j}Tf_{j}(y_{0})=|Tf_{j}(y_{0})|
, we obtain

|M^{\prime}|\varepsilon\leq\sum_{j\in M^{\prime}}|Tf_{j}(y_{0})|=\Big|\sum_{j\in M^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}Tf_{j}(y_{0})\Big|\leq\Big\|T\Big(\sum_{j\in M^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}f_{j}\Big)\Big\|\leq\|T\|\Big\|\sum_{j\in M^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}f_{j}\Big\|.

Since the functions
f_{j}
have disjoint supports,
\left\|\sum_{j\in M^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}f_{j}\right\|=1
. Thus,
\frac{|M|\varepsilon}{|L_{2}|}\leq|M^{\prime}|\varepsilon\leq\|T\|
, which contradicts our choice of
M
.

Next, we assume that
\alpha=\beta+1
is a successor ordinal. Hence, since
\height(L_{2})\leq\omega^{\alpha}
and
\height(L_{2})
is a successor ordinal, we even have
\height(L_{2})<\omega^{\alpha}
. Thus, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\height(L_{2})\leq\omega^{\beta}k
. For a given
\varepsilon>0
we find a natural number
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{k\left\|T\right\|+(n-k)\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}{n}<\varepsilon

for each
n\geq N
.

Next, since
\height(L_{1})>\omega^{\alpha}
, we can find pairwise disjoint open sets
(U_{n})_{n=N}^{\infty}\subseteq U
such that for each
n\geq N
,
\height(U_{n}\cap L_{1})>\omega^{\beta}n
. Finally, by
[
27
, Proposition 2.4(b)]
, for each
n\geq N
, we can find a point
x\in U_{n}\cap L_{1}
and a function
f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
such that
f_{n}=1
on a neighbourhood of
x_{n}
,
f_{n}=0
on
K_{1}\setminus U_{n}
and

\left\|Tf_{n}|_{L_{2}}\right\|\leq\frac{k\left\|T\right\|+(n-k)\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}{n}<\varepsilon.

This finishes the proof in the successor case.

Further, the case when
\alpha
is a limit ordinal follows trivially from the successor case. Indeed, if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal and
\height(L_{2})\leq\omega^{\alpha}
, then, since
\height(L_{2})
is a successor ordinal,
\height(L_{2})<\omega^{\alpha}
, hence, there exists a successor ordinal
\beta<\alpha
such that
\height(L_{2})\leq\omega^{\beta}
. At the same time,
\height(L_{1})>\omega^{\beta}
, hence the conclusion of the lemma follows from what was proved above.

Finally, it is easy to see that in the argument above, the sets
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be taken clopen if
K_{1}
is zero-dimensional.

∎

Before proceeding further, we now recall that for any compact space
K
and a point
x\in K
, the characteristic function
\chi_{x}
can be naturally viewed as an element of
\mathcal{C}(K)^{**}
via the formula

\chi_{x}(\mu)=\mu(\{x\})
,
\mu\in M(K)
. Furthermore, it is a standard fact that the function
\chi_{x}
is a weak
∗
limit of the net of functions
\{f_{U}:U\text{ is a neighbourhood of }x\}
, where the function
f_{U}\in\mathcal{C}(K,[0,1])
vanishes outside of the set
U
, and satisfies
f_{U}=1
on some open neighbourhood of
x
. Hence, if
T:\mathcal{C}(K_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(K_{2})
is an isomorphism, then
\{Tf_{U}\}
converges weak
∗
to
T^{**}\chi_{x}
, by the weak
∗
-weak
∗
continuity of
T^{**}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be scattered compact spaces, and assume that for some ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N},n\geq 2
,
\height(K_{1})>\omega^{\alpha}n
, and
K_{2}^{(\omega^{\alpha})}=\{y\}
for some
y\in K_{2}
. Then

d_{BM}(\mathcal{C}(K_{1}),\mathcal{C}(K_{2}))\geq n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}.

Proof.

We fix an isomorphism
T:\mathcal{C}(K_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(K_{2})
with
\left\|T^{-1}\right\|=1
, and let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. To prove the required lower bound, it is enough to find the objects from the statement of Lemma
3.6
.

To this end, we pick a point
x\in K_{1}^{(\omega^{\alpha}n)}
, and we denote
\xi=T^{**}(\chi_{\{x\}})(\delta_{y})
, where
\delta_{y}
stands for the Dirac measure at the point
y
. Further, we find an open neighbourhood
V
of
x
and a function
g\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
such that
g=1
on
V
, and such that
\left|Tg(y)-\xi\right|<\varepsilon
.

Further, we denote
L_{1}=V^{(\omega^{\alpha}(n-1))}
and
L_{2}=\{z\in K_{2}:\left|Tg(z)-\xi\right|\geq\varepsilon\}
. Then, since
x\in L_{1}
,
\height(L_{1})>\omega^{\alpha}
, and, since
y\notin L_{2}
,
\height(L_{2})\leq\omega^{\alpha}
. Notice also that the set
L_{2}
is compact. Consequently, by Proposition
3.7
we can find pairwise disjoint clopen sets
(U_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq V
(notice that
K_{1}
is zero-dimensional, since it is scattered), functions
g_{k}\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
, and points
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq L_{1}
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{k}=1
on an open neighbourhood
W_{k}
of
x_{k}
,
g_{k}=0
on
K_{1}\setminus U_{k}
, and
\left\|Tg_{k}|_{L_{2}}\right\|<\varepsilon
. Since the sets
U_{k}
are pairwise disjoint, we may assume that none of them contains the point
x
. Next, by a standard pigeonhole-like argument, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|T(T^{-1}({\mathbf{1}}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{U_{k}})(y)\right|<\varepsilon\text{ and }\left|Tg_{k}(y)\right|<\varepsilon.

We denote
U=U_{k}
,
V_{n}=W_{k}
and
f_{n}=g_{k}
. Thus, so far, we have found the functions
f_{n}
and
g
, and the clopen set
U
from the statement of Lemma
3.6
, and, we
have the conditions
(a-b)
and
(a-g)
satisfied.

In the next step, we find a function
h\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
such that
\left|Th(y)-\xi\right|<\varepsilon
and
\operatorname{supp}h\cap U=\emptyset
, so, also the condition
(a-c)
is satisfied.

Now, we aim the find the required functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1}\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
by reverse induction. Thus, we assume that for some
0\leq k<n-1
, we have found the functions
f_{n-k},\ldots,f_{n}
, and open sets
V_{n-k},\ldots,V_{n}
such that for each
i=n-k,\ldots,n
, we have
\height(V_{i})>\omega^{\alpha}(i-1)
,
f_{i}=1
on
V_{i}
, the sets

S_{i}=\{z\in K_{2}:\left|Tf_{i}(z)\right|\geq\varepsilon\}_{i=n-k}^{n}

are pairwise disjoint and do not contain the point
y
, and such that

S_{i}\subseteq\{z\in K_{2}:\left|Tg(z)-\xi\right|<\varepsilon\},\quad i=n-k,\ldots,n,

S_{i}\subseteq\{z\in K_{2}:\left|T(T^{-1}({\mathbf{1}}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{U})(z)\right|<\varepsilon\},\quad i=n-k,\ldots,n-1,

and

S_{i}\subseteq\{z\in K_{2}:\left|Th(z)-\xi\right|<\varepsilon\},\quad i=n-k,\ldots,n-1.

Now, to find the function
f_{n-k-1}
, we denote
L_{1}=V_{n-k}^{(\omega^{\alpha}(n-k-2))}
, and

\displaystyle L_{2}=

\displaystyle\{z\in K_{2}:\left|Tg(z)-\xi\right|\geq\varepsilon\text{ or }\left|Th(z)-\xi\right|\geq\varepsilon\text{ or }\left|T(T^{-1}({\mathbf{1}}_{K_{2}})\cdot\chi_{U})(z)\right|\geq\varepsilon\text{ or }

\displaystyle\left|Tf_{i}(z)\right|\geq\varepsilon\text{ for some }i=n-k,\ldots,n\}.

Then, since
\height(V_{n-k})>\omega^{\alpha}(n-k-1)
,
\height(L_{1})>\omega^{\alpha}
, while
\height(L_{2})<\omega^{\alpha}
, since
y\notin L_{2}
. And, the set
L_{2}
is again compact. Consequently, using Proposition
3.7
and using pigeonhole-like argument similarly as above, we
can find a function
f_{n-k-1}\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
such that
f_{n-k-1}
vanishes outside of the set
V_{n-k}
,
f_{n-k-1}=1
on an open neighbourhood
V_{n-k-1}
of some point from the set

(which, in particular, implies that
\height(V_{n-k-1})>\omega^{\alpha}(n-k-2)
), and such that
\left\|Tf_{n-k-1}|_{L_{2}}\right\|<\varepsilon
and

Now, from the construction it follows that also all the remaining required conditions
(a-a)
,
(a-d)
,
(a-e)
, and
(a-f)
from Lemma

∎

3.2.
Lower bound for
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}k),\mathcal{C}(\omega^{\alpha}))
:

In this subsection we prove Proposition
3.3
from which, using the results from Section
2
, Theorem
3.3
will almost immediately follow. We start with Lemma
3.10
, which implies that when dealing with the (one-sided) positive Banach-Mazur distance, we may without loss of generality assume the constant one function is a fixed point of our isomorphism.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be compact spaces and let
T:C(K_{1})\to C(K_{2})
be a positive surjective isomorphism. Then
\left\|T\right\|=\left\|T(\boldsymbol{1})\right\|
and
T(\boldsymbol{1})\geq\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}
.
Moreover, there exists a positive surjective isomorphism
S:C(K_{1})\to C(K_{2})
such that
\left\|S\right\|=1
,
\left\|S^{-1}\right\|\leq\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|
and
S(\boldsymbol{1})=\boldsymbol{1}
.

Proof.

We may without loss of generality assume that
\left\|T\right\|=1
. Now, since
T(\boldsymbol{1})\geq 0
and
\left\|T(\boldsymbol{1})\right\|\leq 1
, we have
0\leq T(\boldsymbol{1})\leq\boldsymbol{1}
. Furthermore, for any
f\in B_{C(K_{1})}
we have
0\leq T(f^{+})\leq T(\boldsymbol{1})
and
0\leq T(f^{-})\leq T(\boldsymbol{1})
, which implies

-T(\boldsymbol{1})(y)\leq T(f^{+})(y)-T(f^{-})(y)=Tf(y)\leq T(\boldsymbol{1})(y),\quad y\in K_{2},

so we obtain
|Tf|\leq T(\boldsymbol{1})
(in particular, we have
\left\|Tf\right\|\leq\left\|T(\boldsymbol{1})\right\|
and since
f\in B_{C(K_{1})}
was arbitrary, we obtain
\left\|T(\boldsymbol{1})\right\|=\left\|T\right\|=1
). Now, given
y\in K_{2}
we pick
g\in S_{C_{(K_{2})}}
with
g(y)=1
and then for
f:=\frac{T^{-1}g}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}\in B_{C_{K_{1}}}
we obtain
Tf(y)=\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
and therefore
T(\boldsymbol{1})(y)\geq Tf(y)=\frac{1}{\left\|T^{-1}\right\|}
.

For the “Moreover” part it suffices to put
Sf:=\frac{Tf}{T(\boldsymbol{1})}
. Then it is easy to check that
S
is positive surjective isomorphism with
S(\boldsymbol{1})=\boldsymbol{1}
and by the already proven part we have
\left\|S\right\|=\left\|S(\boldsymbol{1})\right\|=1
. For
g\in C(K_{2})
we have

\|S^{-1}g\|=\|T^{-1}(T(\boldsymbol{1})\cdot g)\|\leq\|T^{-1}\|\cdot\|T(\boldsymbol{1})\cdot g\|\leq\|T^{-1}\|\cdot\|T(\boldsymbol{1})\|\cdot\|g\|=\|T^{-1}\|\cdot\|T\|\cdot\|g\|.

This yields
\|S^{-1}\|\leq\|T\|\cdot\|T^{-1}\|
.
∎

Our final argument is then based on the following easy observation.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
T:C(K)\to C(L)
be a surjective positive isomorphism with
T\boldsymbol{1}=\left\|T\right\|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}
and
\alpha>0
. Assume there exits nonnegative function
f\in S_{C(K)}
with
\left\|Tf\right\|\leq\alpha\left\|T\right\|
. Then
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\geq\tfrac{2}{\alpha}-1
.

Proof.

Assume
0\leq f\leq 1
with
\left\|f\right\|=1
is such that
\left\|Tf\right\|\leq\alpha\left\|T\right\|
. Since
0\leq\tfrac{Tf}{\left\|Tf\right\|}\leq 1
, we have

\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\geq\left\|T^{-1}(1-2\tfrac{Tf}{\left\|Tf\right\|})\right\|=\left\|\tfrac{1}{\left\|T\right\|}-\tfrac{2}{\left\|Tf\right\|}f\right\|\geq\tfrac{1}{\left\|T\right\|}(\tfrac{2\left\|T\right\|}{\left\|Tf\right\|}-1)\geq\tfrac{1}{\left\|T\right\|}(\tfrac{2}{\alpha}-1).

∎

Proposition 3.12
.

Assume that
K_{1},K_{2}
are compact spaces such that for some ordinal
\alpha
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left|K_{1}^{(\alpha)}\right|=n
and
\left|K_{2}^{(\alpha)}\right|=1
. Then,

d_{BM}^{+}(C(K_{1}),C(K_{2}))\geq 2n-1.

Proof.

For
n=1
this is trivial, so we may assume
n\geq 2
. Pick a positive isomorphism
T:C(K_{1})\to C(K_{2})
. By Lemma
3.10
, without loss of generality, we may assume
T\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}
and
\left\|T\right\|=1
. Next, since
K_{1}
is scattered, it is zero-dimensional, hence we find pairwise disjoint clopen sets
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of height
\alpha+1
, covering
K_{1}
. We denote
f_{i}=\chi_{U_{i}}
for
i\leq n
, and let
y_{0}\in K_{2}
denote the point satisfying
K_{2}^{(\alpha)}=\{y_{0}\}
. Since

0\leq T(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i})(y_{0})=T(1)(y_{0})=1,

there is
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
T(f_{i})(y_{0})\leq\tfrac{1}{n}
. Further, we fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{n})
, and we consider the compact set

L=\{y\in K_{2}:\,|Tf_{i}(y)-Tf_{i}(y_{0})|\geq\varepsilon\}.

Then, since
y_{0}\notin L
, we have
\height(L)<\alpha+1=\height(U_{i})
. Consequently, by Proposition
2.3
(a), we can find a norm-
1
function
g\in\mathcal{C}(K_{1},[0,1])
supported by
U_{i}
, such that
Tg_{|L}<\varepsilon
. We also have

\left\|Tg|_{L^{c}}\right\|\leq\left\|Tf_{i}|_{L^{c}}\right\|\leq T(f_{i})(y_{0})+\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{n}+\varepsilon,

and so
\left\|Tg\right\|\leq\alpha(\varepsilon)\left\|T\right\|
for
\alpha(\varepsilon)=\tfrac{1}{n}+\varepsilon
. By Lemma
3.11
, this implies that
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\geq\tfrac{2}{\alpha(\varepsilon)}-1
and since
\varepsilon>0
could be chosen arbitrarily small, we obtain
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|\geq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\tfrac{2}{\alpha(\varepsilon)}-1=2n-1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

The lower bound follows from Proposition
3.12
, while the upper bound follows from Proposition
2.12
.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

Denote the upper/lower bound from Theorem
3.3
as
U(n):=n+\sqrt{(n-1)(n+3)}
and
L(n):=2n-1
, respectively. Note that
U(n)-L(n)<2
for every

and moreover
(U(n)-L(n))_{n=1}^{\infty}

3.3.
Estimate of
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))

We finish this section with the proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

In order to provide the reader with some insight we shall
actually construct a family of isomorphisms out of which we shall pick the one with the best estimate we are able to get using our method. We fix
\lambda\in(0,1)
and we define
T:C([1,\omega])\to C([1,\omega\cdot 2])
in such a way that for
f\in C([1,\omega])
we have

\begin{split}Tf(\omega)&:=\lambda f(\omega)+(1-\lambda)f(1),\\
Tf(\omega\cdot 2)&:=\lambda f(\omega)+(1-\lambda)f(2),\\
Tf(1)&:=\tfrac{1}{2}(f(1)+f(2))\\
Tf(n+1)&:=(1-\lambda)f(1)+\lambda f(2n+1),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\\
Tf(\omega+n)&:=(1-\lambda)f(2)+\lambda f(2n+2),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.\\
\end{split}

Now, it is easy to see that
T
is a well-defined linear operator,
T\geq 0
,
T(\boldsymbol{1})=\boldsymbol{1}
and
\left\|T\right\|=1
.

Further, we define
S:C([1,\omega\cdot 2])\to C([1,\omega])
in such a way that for
g\in C([1,\omega\cdot 2])
we have

\begin{split}Sg(\omega)&:=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\big(g(\omega)+g(\omega\cdot 2)\big)-\tfrac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}g(1),\\
Sg(1)&:=\tfrac{1}{2(1-\lambda)}\big(g(\omega)-g(\omega\cdot 2)\big)+g(1),\\
Sg(2)&:=\tfrac{1}{2(1-\lambda)}\big(-g(\omega)+g(\omega\cdot 2)\big)+g(1)\\
Sg(2n+1)&:=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\big(-g(\omega)+g(\omega\cdot 2)-2(1-\lambda)g(1)+2g(n+1)\big),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\\
Sg(2n+2)&:=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\big(g(\omega)-g(\omega\cdot 2)-2(1-\lambda)g(1)+2g(\omega+n)\big),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.\\
\end{split}

It is easy to see that
S
is a well-defined linear operator with
\left\|S\right\|=\max\{1+\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda},\tfrac{3}{\lambda}-1\}
. Finally, a slightly tedious but straightforward verification shows that
ST=\operatorname{Id}
and
TS=\operatorname{Id}
, so
T
is an isomorphism with
T^{-1}=S
.

Taking
\lambda:=\tfrac{3-\sqrt{3}}{2}
we arrive at
\left\|T\right\|\cdot\left\|T^{-1}\right\|=2+\sqrt{3}
.

(It is easy to see that this is the value where
1+\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda}=\tfrac{3}{\lambda}-1
, in other words the value where the function
(0,1)\ni\lambda\mapsto\max\{1+\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda},\tfrac{3}{\lambda}-1\}
attains its minimum.)
∎

Let us note that we considered many different models of an isomorphism between
\mathcal{C}(\omega)
and
\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2)
generated by certain tuples of regular matrices and, even after many numerical simulations reflecting various models of isomorphisms, we were not able to obtain a better upper bound than
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2))\leq 2+\sqrt{3}
and
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 2),\mathcal{C}(\omega))\leq 2+\sqrt{5}
. We also tried to deal with the case of
\omega\cdot 3
and after many numerical simulations, we were not able to get anything better than
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega),\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 3))\leq 2+\sqrt{5}
and
d_{BM}^{+}(\mathcal{C}(\omega\cdot 3),\mathcal{C}(\omega))\leq 2+\sqrt{5}
.

4.
Concluding remarks and open problems

In Question
0.1
we asked about the generalization of the Miljutin Theorem. Let us mention that all the proofs of the Miljutin Theorem that we are aware of, rely on the well-known Pełczyński decomposition method, which, as far as we understand, does not seem to be working for positive isomorphisms. Two particularly important instances of Question
0.1
this problem are the following.

Question 4.1
.

•

Is there a positive isomorphism of
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
onto
\mathcal{C}(2^{\omega})
?

•

Is there a positive isomorphism of
\mathcal{C}(2^{\omega})
onto
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
?

We note that for the non-surjective case, the answer is positive as it is known that an isomorphic embedding of
\mathcal{C}(2^{\omega})
in any Banach lattice
E
implies the existence of a positive isomorphic embedding (
[
10
, Theorem A]
), and, linear embeddability of
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
in
E
is equivalent to even lattice embeddability (
[
2
, Theorem A]
).

We also consider the following nonlinear version of our result. We recall that a long-standing open problem in the nonlinear theory of Banach spaces asks whether the Szlenk index is invariant under bi-Lipschitz bijections of Banach spaces. An important result hinting at a possible positive answer is
[
7
]
.
The problem remains open also when restricted to just the class of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces, and even when the considered compact spaces are countable. It is true when one of the spaces is isomorphic to
c_{0}
(see
[
12
, Theorem 2.2]
), in this case even for uniform homeomorphisms instead of bi-Lipschitz bijections (see
[
11
, Theorem 5.5(i)]
).
In spite of these important steps forward and a substantial effort of the mathematical community, the problem remains wide open. We suggest considering the assumption that the bi-Lipschitz bijection is order-preserving (which for linear mappings coincides with being positive), which might shed some new light on the question.

Question 4.2
.

Do order-preserving bi-Lipschitz bijections of
\mathcal{C}(K)
spaces over countable compact spaces
K
preserve the Szlenk index of
\mathcal{C}(K)
? Do they preserve even the height of
K
?

Related to this question, let us recall Proposition
2.9
by which there does not exist an order-preserving bijection from
c\simeq\mathcal{C}([1,\omega])
to
c_{0}\simeq\mathcal{C}_{0}([1,\omega])
with Lipschitz inverse. On the other hand,
c_{0}
is positively isomorphic to
c
. This is witnessed by the linear operator
T:\mathcal{C}_{0}([1,\omega])\rightarrow\mathcal{C}([1,\omega])
, defined by

Tf(\omega)=f(1),\quad Tf(n)=f(1)+f(n+1),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

which is a positive surjective isomorphism with inverse
S:\mathcal{C}([1,\omega])\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{0}([1,\omega])
,

Sh(1)=h(\omega),\quad Sh(n+1)=h(n)-h(\omega),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Another question related to our research concerns spaces of continuous functions with the pointwise topology. We recall that the classification of spaces of the form
\mathcal{C}_{p}(K)
, where
K
is a countable compact space, by linear homeomorphism, coincides with the classification of the Banach spaces
\mathcal{C}(K)
by linear isomorphisms, see
[
3
]
. Thus, the following problem seems natural.

Question 4.3
.

Assume that
K,L
are countable compact spaces. Is it true that there exists a positive linear homeomorphism of
\mathcal{C}_{p}(K)
onto
\mathcal{C}_{p}(L)
iff there exists a positive isomorphism of
\mathcal{C}(K)
onto
\mathcal{C}(L)
?
