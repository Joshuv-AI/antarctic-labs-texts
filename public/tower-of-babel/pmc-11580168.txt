Crystal Structure and Mutagenesis of an XYP Subfamily Cyclodipeptide Synthase Reveal Key Determinants of Enzyme Activity and Substrate Specificity
Authors: Jun-Bin He, Yichen Ren, Peifeng Li, Yi-Pei Liu, Hai-Xue Pan, Lin-Juan Huang, Jiayuan Wang, Pengfei Fang, Gong-Li Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyclodipeptide synthases (CDPSs) catalyze the synthesis of diverse cyclodipeptides (CDPs) by utilizing two aminoacyl-tRNA (aa-tRNA) substrates in a sequential ping-pong reaction mechanism. Numerous CDPSs have been characterized to provide precursors for diketopiperazines (DKPs) with diverse structural characteristics and biological activities. BcmA, belonging to the XYP subfamily, is a cyclo(l-Ile-l-Leu)-synthesizing CDPS involved in the biosynthesis of the antibiotic bicyclomycin. The structural basis and determinants influencing BcmA enzyme activity and substrate selectivity are not well understood. Here, we report the crystal structure of SsBcmA from Streptomyces sapporonensis. Through structural comparison and systematic site-directed mutagenesis, we highlight the significance of key residues located in the aminoacyl-binding pocket for enzyme activity and substrate specificity. In particular, the nonconserved residues D161 and K165 in pocket P2 are essential for the activity of SsBcmA without significant alteration of the substrate specificity, while the conserved residues F158 as well as F210 and S211 in P2 are responsible for determining substrate selectivity. These findings facilitate the understanding of how CDPSs selectively accept hydrophobic substrates and provide additional clues for the engineering of these enzymes for synthetic biology applications.

Cyclodipeptides (CDPs) and their derivatives, which contain a 2,5-diketopiperazine (DKP) heterocycle scaffold, are a large class of structurally diverse natural products produced by a wide variety of organisms.1−9 They frequently exhibit various and fascinating biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and herbicidal activities.1,3,5−7,10−14 For example, cyclo(l-His-l-Pro) (cHP) is an endogenous CDP in the human body and acts as an anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective agent.15−17 Bicyclomycin (Figure 1), a leucyl- and isoleucyl-containing DKP alkaloid,18 is a clinically promising antibiotic that selectively inhibits the bacterial transcription termination factor Rho.19−21 In nature, the DKP skeleton is biosynthesized either by nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) or by cyclodipeptide synthases (CDPSs).22−24 In contrast to multimodular NRPSs (>100 kDa) that use free amino acids as direct substrates,23,25 CDPSs are small (∼30 kDa) and tRNA-dependent enzymes that hijack aminoacyl-tRNAs (aa-tRNAs) from the ribosomal biosynthetic machinery to catalyze the formation of two amide bonds to produce various CDPs.22,24,26−29

Since the characterization of the first CDPS (Albc) in 2002,30 more than 1,000 CDPSs have been experimentally identified or bioinformatics-predicted.28,31−34 CDPSs can be classified into two subfamilies named NYH-CDPSs (corresponding to N40, Y202 and H203 residues in AlbC) and XYP-CDPSs (residues N and H are replaced by a nonconserved residue and residue P, respectively) based on the identity of two sets of characterized residues.28,32,33,35 To date, five NYH-CDPSs35−40 and five XYP-CDPSs35,41,42 (Table S1) have been structurally determined. All of the CDPSs, regardless of their subfamily, contain a common Rossmann-fold domain35−42 that shares high structural similarity to the catalytic domain of class Ic aa-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs),36,38,39 such as TyrRS and TrpRS. The structures of CDPSs have two separate solvent-accessible pockets (P1 and P2) which accommodate the specific aminoacyl moiety of aa-tRNA substrates.37 There are some differences in the structure of the two subfamilies in the first half of their Rossmann folds.35 However, structural analysis and mutation studies demonstrated that the conserved catalytic residues (S37, Y178, Y202, and E182 in AlbC) adopt similar positions and have a similar effect on the function in the two subfamilies, suggesting that NYH-CDPSs and XYP-CDPSs are likely to share the same catalytic mechanism.28,35

The catalytic mechanism of CDPSs has been well-studied by experimental and computational methods.36−39,42−46 In general, CDPSs utilize a sequential ping-pong mechanism in which two acyl-enzyme intermediates are formed (Figure 1A).36−39,46 The aminoacyl moiety (accommodated in P1) of the first aa-tRNA is transferred to the conserved catalytic residue S (S37 in AlbC) to form an aminoacyl-enzyme intermediate.36,38,39,46 Subsequently, the aminoacyl moiety (accommodated in P2) of the second aa-tRNA reacts with the intermediate to form a dipeptidyl-enzyme intermediate.37 Finally, the dipeptidyl moiety undergoes intramolecular cyclization facilitated by a conserved residue Y (Y202 in AlbC) to produce the final CDPs.37,44 In addition to residues S37 and Y202, the other two residues Y178 and E182 (in AlbC), which are highly conserved in the CDPS family, are also involved in catalysis, with residues N40 and H203 (in AlbC) playing a crucial role in the stabilization of Y178 and E182.37,44

Most CDPSs are promiscuous enzymes that synthesize one major CDP and one or more minor CDPs.32,33 It is demonstrated that the binding pocket P1 has a deep and hydrophobic character, whereas P2 is large and wide, which explains the less specificity for the second aa-tRNA substrate.36,37,41 Previous studies also reveal that the residues within the P1 and P2 binding pockets confer substrate specificity and influence enzyme activity.32,33,36,41,47−49 For example, substitutions of residues Y55, E174 and Y189 within the P1 pocket of ParcuCDPS resulted in a remarkable change in the production of cHP to cyclo(l-Leu-l-Pro) (cLP) and cyclo(l-Phe-l-Pro) (cFP).41 Nevertheless, the investigation of the effects of the residues in P2 on substrate selectivity and enzyme activity remains limited.

BcmA belongs to the XYP subfamily and utilizes Ile-tRNAIle and Leu-tRNALeu as substrates to synthesize cyclo(l-Ile-l-Leu) (cIL), which is the biosynthetic precursor of the antibiotic bicyclomycin (Figure 1B).50−55 It also recognizes Val-tRNAVal to catalyze the synthesis of cyclo(l-Val-l-Leu) (cVL).51 However, it is not fully understood how BcmA selectively recognizes these hydrophobic amino acids as substrates. Here, we determined the crystal structure of SsBcmA from Streptomyces sapporonensis. By combining crystallographic study, comparison analysis and site-directed mutagenesis, we identified the key determinants that affect the enzyme activity and substrate specificity of SsBcmA.

To characterize the catalytic function of SsBcmA from S. sapporonensis, we produced the protein in Escherichia coli and performed in vitro and in vivo enzymatic reactions (Supporting Information Figures S1 and S2). The results showed that SsBcmA could catalyze the synthesis of three CDP products. The two major products were cVL and cIL,50,51 as expected, and the minor product was identified as cyclo(l-Ala-l-Leu) (cAL) (Figures 1B,C and S2). It is worth mentioning that the formation ratios of the CDPs cAL, cVL and cIL produced by SsBcmA were consistent in vitro and in vivo reactions. These results highlight that SsBcmA is a relatively promiscuous enzyme that selectively recognizes hydrophobic amino acids to encode CDP products.

To explore how SsBcmA selectively recognizes these hydrophobic amino acids as substrates, we conducted crystallographic studies. Despite extensive attempts, we only obtained the crystal structure of SsBcmA in the apo form at a resolution of 2.10 Å (PDB accession code 9J61, Figure 2 and Table 1). The asymmetric unit contains eight molecules (Figure S3). However, no specific oligomer was observed in this structure. The model of each monomer contains residues 15–240 while the residues 161–175 in the loop are partially or completely invisible. Similar to other determined CDPS structures (Table S1), SsBcmA has a structure with helices α2−α8 and displays a Rossmann fold and XYP motif (N46, Y206, and P207) (Figure 2A). Notably, the elongated β2 and the bent α6 in SsBcmA are different from all other known CDPS structures. Additionally, the α4′ and α7 are also longer in SsBcmA compared with other CDPSs (Figures 2A,B and S4). Although belonging to the XYP subfamily, SsBcmA shows an unusually high similarity to NYH-CDPSs YvmC-Blic [PDB: 3OQH, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) value of 2.02 Å] and ShaeCDPS (PDB: 6EZ3, RMSD value of 2.13 Å) rather than other XYP-CDPSs in secondary structure matches (SSM) (Figures 2B and S4 and Tables S1 and S2). However, the sequence identity between SsBcmA and YvmC-Blic (13.44%) or ShaeCDPS (13.23%) is relatively low (Table S2). Further detailed comparison showed that the position of β2 (and β7 partially) in SsBcmA is similar to that in NYH-CDPSs. Additionally, the length and position of α6 are similar to NYH-CDPSs, whereas the absence of α7′ in SsBcmA is the same as other XYP-enzymes. The very short 310 helix α1 is also different from NYH-CDPSs (Figures 2B and S4). These characteristic features revealed that SsBcmA has a partial structural similarity to NYH-CDPSs, which is different from the previously reported divergence of the two subfamilies.35 And the structural similarity of SsBcmA to NYH-CDPSs does not appear to depend on sequence similarity directly. These results imply that there is possibly more complexity in the structural partition and evolution of CDPSs.

As identified in other known CDPS structures (Table S1), SsBcmA also has two substrate binding pockets P1 and P2 (Figure 2C). The pocket P1 (shown in yellow in Figure 2C) is narrow, branched and lined with mainly hydrophobic residues (F39, G41, L71, G73, C125, I192, F193, and M204), supposed to recognize the conserved leucine substrate (Figures 2C and 3A,B). However, the pocket P2 (shown in limegreen in Figure 2C, constituted with residues F158, D161, Y162, K165, G208, F210, and S211) is shallower and wider, supposed to accommodate isoleucine and valine (Figure 2D). The catalytic residues (S43, Y185, and E189) conserved in all CDPSs are located between two pockets (Figure 2C). We also individually substituted these catalytically significant residues with A and other relevant amino acid residues. Indeed, all the resulting variants except N46A completely terminated the cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activities (Figures S1 and S5), further confirming their crucial role in the catalytic mechanism of SsBcmA.36−39,44 Moreover, two patches of positive charges were observed in the surface electrostatic analysis. The basic residues may be involved in binding to the tRNA,36,42,43,45 which was also revealed by the superimposition of SsBcmA with the complex of Cglo-CDPS and Phe-tRNAPhe (PDB: 6Y4B) and mutation of these residues (Figures S1 and S6).

Based on the above structural analysis, we next focused on exploring the molecular basis for the enzymatic substrate specificity. Previous studies have shown that mutations in residues within the P1 and P2 pockets alter substrate specificity and affect enzyme activity in CDPSs.32,36,47−49 To verify the crucial residue(s) for enzyme activity and substrate selectivity of SsBcmA, we performed systematic site-directed mutagenesis of residues in both pockets P1 and P2. The resulting mutants were overexpressed in E. coli (Figure S1) and the in vivo cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activities were measured by LC-MS.

Among residues in pocket P1, substitution of F39, G41, L71, G73, F193, and M204 with A completely abolished the cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activities while substitution of C125 and I192 with A significantly decreased enzyme activities (Figure 3), suggesting that a single residue in the pocket P1 can obviously affect the accommodation of the aminoacyl moiety of the first aa-tRNA substrate, specifically Leu-tRNALeu. Sequence alignments demonstrated that the residues in pocket P1 are highly conserved in cLX- synthesizing (X is I or L) XYP-CDPSs (Figure S7).33 We also compared the consensus motif of pocket P1 with other CDPSs and engineered corresponding mutations to change substrate specificity.33 However, in addition to the variants I192V and M204L that retained the enzyme activities with the product ratio consistent with that of the wild type (WT), most of the other mutants totally lost the cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activities (Figure 3). These results further confirm that the conserved residues within the pocket P1 play a crucial role in the recognition of the first aa-tRNA substrate.

The pocket P2 is located outside the pocket P1. In addition to binding to the second aa-tRNA, pocket P2 may also affect the recognition of the first aa-tRNA substrate by SsBcmA. Specifically, residues G208, F210, and S211 are closer to the catalytic center, which may be related to substrate recognition. Indeed, the mutants G208A, and F210A exhibited a complete loss of cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activity (Figure 4). Meanwhile, mutation of F210 with W resulted in a reduction in the yields of cAL, cVL, and cIL, but also switched the substrate specificity to produce a tyrosyl-containing CDP cyclo(l-Tyr-l-Leu) (cYL) as the major product (Figure 4). This may be attributed to the formation of a hydrogen bond or an aromatic interaction between the indole group of W210 and the phenol group of the substrate tyrosyl. Furthermore, when residue S211 was substituted with P, the variant S211P only produced a very low amount (<2%) of cIL and mainly synthesized cAL and simultaneously generated cyclo(l-Pro-l-Leu) (cPL). Similarly, the mutant S211L also produced cPL and significantly reduced the production (<5%) of cIL (Figure 4).

On the other side of the pocket P2, residues F158, D161, Y162, and K165 are far away from the active center. These residues may affect enzyme activity or substrate selectivity by interacting with the CCA tail of tRNAs. The charged residues D161 and K165 are not conserved in cLX-synthesizing (X is I or L) XYP-CDPSs (Figure S7).33 The mutants D161A, D161R, D161N, and K165R increased the total product yield (by approximately 2.1–2.9-fold compared with that of the WT SsBcmA) without altering the substrate specificity (Figure 4), implying that the D161 mutations may avoid the potential electrostatic repulsion between the acidic side chain and the tRNA phosphate skeleton, while the K165R may enhance the basicity of the side chain. Previous study on CDPS-Np from Nocardiopsis prasine has revealed that mutations of residues T82 and Y196 in pocket P1 (corresponding to residues G73 and I192 of SsBcmA, respectively; Figure 3) significantly enhanced product formation.48 Our results highlighted the nonconserved residues in pocket P2 are also essential for the enzyme activity of CDPSs, providing additional evidence that the CDP yields can be affected by alteration of the substrate binding pocket without changing the substrate specificity. In addition, there may be stacking or hydrogen bonding interactions between Y162 and tRNA bases. The Y162A mutation exhibited a complete loss of cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activity (Figure 4).

Intriguingly, substitution of F158 in the pocket P2 with A resulted in dramatical change of the product formation, producing eight CDPs cAL, cVL, cIL, cyclo(l-Val-l-Val) (cVV), cyclo(l-Val-l-Met) (cVM), cyclo(l-Leu-l-Met) (cLM), cyclo(l-Ile-l-Met) (cIM), cPL with cAL and cVL as the major products (Figure 4). Further site-saturation mutagenesis of F158 substantiated its significance in determining substrate selectivity (Figure S8). Notably, the mutation of F158 also altered the substrate specificity of pocket P1 to produce cVV, cVM, and cIM. This residue is located on the outer edge of the active pocket where it may interact with the tRNA terminal base A76. The F158A mutation loses the large phenyl side chain, making it possible for tRNAs carrying different amino acids to enter the active pocket in a more flexible conformation, thereby reducing the control of the pocket P1 over selectivity. Taken together, these results demonstrate that residues F158, F210, and S211 in P2 are crucial for determining the substrate selectivity of SsBcmA, and highlight the value of the engineering of pocket P2 in the derivation of CDPs.

In conclusion, we reported the crystal structure of SsBcmA from S. sapporonensis, a relatively promiscuous XYP-CDPS involved in the biosynthesis of the antibiotic bicyclomycin. Structural analysis and systematic site-directed mutagenesis confirmed that the substitution of residues that constitute the pocket P2 of SsBcmA could influence the cyclodipeptide-synthesizing activity and the nature of the aa-tRNAs incorporated. Specially, residues D161 and K165 in pocket P2 are important for the activity of SsBcmA without significant change in substrate specificity, while residues F158, F210, and S211 in P2 exert a pivotal influence on substrate selectivity. Further structural studies on aa-tRNA binding to XYP-CDPS members will be performed to explore the specific interaction of these key residues in the substrate binding pocket.
