Trends in Sex Representation in the American Society for Surgery of the Hand Committee and Annual Meeting Leadership from 2013 to 2024
Authors: Abel Ruiz Diaz, Stephanie Malta, Micah K. Sinclair, Angelo R. Dacus, A. Bobby Chhabra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate longitudinal trends and distribution of sex representation within leadership positions of the American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH) over the past decade.MethodsDemographic data from 2013 to 2024 were obtained from ASSH for committee and annual meeting leadership positions. Self-reported sex was analyzed, and leadership participation was assessed longitudinally and by distribution among unique individuals.ResultsA total of 685 ASSH committee leadership positions were identified, of which the majority were held by men. Women representation in committee leadership increased over the study period. Key decision-making positions, such as committee Chair, represented a large proportion of all leadership opportunities, with women chairs accounting for a smaller share overall. Leadership positions were concentrated among a limited number of individuals, particularly among women leaders. Across leadership for the ASSH national meeting, women participation increased over time but remained lower than men. A disproportionate share of leadership positions was held by a small subset of individuals, with a greater concentration observed among women leaders compared with men.ConclusionsWomen representation within ASSH leadership has increased across committee and annual meeting leadership positions over the past decade. However, these leadership opportunities remain concentrated among a small number of recurring individuals, suggesting that gains in numeric representation have not fully translated into broader diversification of leadership. These findings highlight the importance of considering both representation and distribution when evaluating inclusivity within surgical societies.Type of study/level of evidenceRetrospective observational study IV.

Diversity within the physician workforce has been associated with improved innovation, organizational performance, and quality of care.1, 2, 3 Within surgery, professional societies play a critical role in shaping academic advancement, leadership development, and visibility through committee service and national meeting participation. Leadership positions within these societies influence educational priorities, research agendas, and professional culture, making access to such positions an important marker of inclusivity within the specialty.

Despite broader efforts to improve diversity in medicine, orthopedic surgery remains the least sex-diverse surgical specialty in the United States. Women comprise approximately 8% of practicing orthopedic surgeons and 21% of orthopedic residents, compared with substantially higher representation in other surgical fields.4,5 For example, plastic surgery has made greater strides at improving sex inclusivity within the training and academic pipeline.6,7 As of 2024, women comprised approximately 40% of plastic surgery residency applicants and 43% of integrated plastic surgery residents, and represented 25% of faculty and 22% of program directors.8 Prior studies examining demographics within orthopedic societies have demonstrated persistent underrepresentation of women in academic authorship, invited speaking roles, and leadership positions.9, 10, 11 Although incremental gains in women participation have been reported, less is known about how leadership opportunities are distributed among individuals within professional organizations.12,13

In response to these disparities, numerous initiatives have been developed to support recruitment, mentorship, and advancement of women in orthopedics. These include early exposure programs for medical students, structured mentorship and sponsorship initiatives for trainees and junior faculty, and the establishment of diversity-focused committees within national societies.14,15 In a recent study of orthopedic subspecialties, pediatrics and oncology were identified as having the highest proportion of women surgeons (30.4% and 23.6%, respectively), whereas hand surgery was comprised of only 16.7% women surgeons, despite having the greatest total number of women surgeons (n = 466).16 The American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH) is one example of a professional organization that has implemented several programs over the past decade aimed at improving inclusivity and leadership development. Prior studies examining ASSH have documented increases in women membership and participation in leadership-related activities, as well as commenting on early society advancement pathways.12,13 However, the existing literature has largely focused on overall representation, limiting the understanding of leadership positions and their distribution among members of the ASSH.

Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to evaluate sex representation within ASSH committee and annual meeting leadership from 2013 to 2024. We sought to assess longitudinal trends in women participation and to examine how leadership opportunities are distributed among unique individuals. We hypothesized that although women representation within ASSH leadership has increased over time, leadership positions remain disproportionately concentrated among a small subset of individuals. By characterizing both representation and distribution, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive assessment of progress toward inclusive leadership within hand surgery.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study was a retrospective observational analysis of sex representation in leadership within the ASSH from 2013 through 2024.

Data collection

Demographic data were obtained from available ASSH records and categorized into two distinct databases for analysis. The first database included demographic information for ASSH committee participants, including name, self-identified sex, and committee position. The second database included demographic information for participants in leadership positions during the annual ASSH meeting, including name, self-identified sex, position, and session type. Annual meeting sessions were categorized as instructional course lectures, instructional forums, pre and postcourses, symposia, podium presentations, and resident and fellows’ courses.

Data extraction and position classification

Analysis included positions with direct leadership or decision-making responsibility for the ASSH Committee and Annual Meeting. Nonleadership positions were excluded (see Appendix S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org, for the complete list of positions). Entries were excluded if sex or position information was unavailable. Individuals could contribute data across multiple years and/or positions, and each position-year entry was treated as a discrete observation.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of leadership positions held by men and women overall and stratified by type. Secondary analyses examined differences in sex representation across committee and annual meeting leadership positions.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Comparisons of proportions between men and women were performed using chi-square tests, as appropriate, based on counts. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Committee leadership demographics

From 2013 to 2024, demographic data were analyzed from 158 ASSH committees. Of 50 identified committee positions, 11 were classified as having direct leadership or decision-making responsibilities and were included in the analysis. These positions were comprised of the American Foundation for Surgery of the Hand (AFSH) board of trustees (AFSH Chair, AFSH President, AFSH Vice Chair, AFSH Vice President) in addition to leadership within various committees of the society (Chair, Co-Chair, Editor in Chief of the Journal of Hand Surgery, President, President-Elect, Vice President, Vice Chair). A total of 685 committee leadership positions were analyzed. Overall, 82.2% (n = 563) of committee leadership positions were held by men and 17.8% (n = 122) by women (Table 1). These positions were filled by 171 unique individuals, of whom 81.2% (n = 139) were men and 18.8% (n = 32) were women. In 2013, women held 5.1% (n = 8) of 156 committee leadership positions. Committee chair leadership distribution

The Chair position was the most frequently represented among committee appointments, accounting for 41.8% (n = 286) of all committee leadership. Of these, 16.1% (n = 46) were held by women and 83.9% (n = 240) by men (Table 2). A total of 20 unique individuals filled all women Chair positions. The three women with the highest number of Chair appointments accounted for 52.2% (n = 24) of all women Chair positions. In contrast, the three men with the highest number of Chair appointments accounted for 19.7% (n = 49) of men Chair positions. Annual meeting leadership demographics

From 2013 to 2023, annual meeting leadership data included individuals serving as Chairs, Moderator, or Faculty. Overall, men represented 78% (n = 925) of individuals holding leadership positions, while women represented 22% (n = 261) (Table 1). Men filled 80.2% (n = 6,267) of all leadership positions, compared with 19.8% (n = 1,545) filled by women. When stratified by leadership position, men comprised 77.4% of chairs (n = 294), 80.3% of faculty (n = 4,577), and 80.7% of moderators (n = 1,396). Trends and concentration of annual meeting leadership

In 2013, women held 10.9% (n = 40) of annual meeting leadership positions, compared with 89.1% (n = 328) held by men. By 2023, women participation increased to 34.4% (n = 242) (Fig. 2). Analysis of leadership concentration revealed that the top 10 men accounted for 10.8% (n = 675) of all men-held leadership positions. Discussion

This study evaluated longitudinal trends in sex representation within the ASSH committee and annual meeting leadership positions and examined whether increasing women participation corresponded to broader distribution of leadership opportunities. As of 2024, ASSH women surgeons comprised 18.8% (834 of 4,488) of North American members, reflecting persistent sex disparities in overall membership composition. Despite this disparity, women representation increased substantially across both ASSH leadership domains, exceeding baseline membership proportions. However, leadership appointments were disproportionately concentrated among a small subset of individuals, particularly among women leaders, suggesting that gains in numeric representation have not fully translated into diversification of leadership participation.

Our first objective was to characterize temporal trends in women leadership within ASSH. We observed steady increases in women representation in both committee and annual meeting leadership, with women participation rising from 5.1% to 32.1% in committee leadership and from 10.9% to 34.4% in annual meeting leadership over the study period. These findings are consistent with prior ASSH-focused analyses demonstrating incremental progress in membership engagement, committee participation, and early-career leadership involvement.12,13 When compared with broader orthopedic literature, ASSH appears to have achieved relatively higher levels of women leadership representation than many comparable surgical societies, where leadership and faculty remain predominantly men.10,17,18 These trends likely reflect the cumulative impact of sustained outreach, mentorship, and leadership development initiatives implemented within the society over the last decade.

Our second objective was to assess the distribution of leadership opportunities among individuals. Collectively, the composition of annual meeting leadership positions (Chair, Faculty, and Moderator) was similarly distributed between sex. However, despite improvements in overall representation, leadership positions were highly concentrated. A small number of individuals across both sex accounted for a substantial proportion of leadership positions, with a more pronounced concentration observed among women leaders. Similar patterns have been reported across orthopedic and surgical societies, where leadership pipelines often rely on repeated appointments of familiar individuals.19, 20, 21 These findings suggest that while recruitment efforts may successfully increase participation, mechanisms governing leadership allocation and advancement may inadvertently limit turnover and access to a broader pool of members.22

The concept of viewpoint diversity provides an important framework for interpreting these results. Viewpoint diversity emphasizes the inclusion of individuals with varied backgrounds, experiences, and perspectives, and has been linked to improved innovation, decision-making, and organizational performance in professional and academic settings.1, 2, 3 In the context of surgical societies, diversity is not solely defined by demographic representation but also by the number of distinct voices contributing to leadership and governance. Our findings indicate that increasing women participation alone may be insufficient to achieve this goal if leadership positions remain concentrated among recurring individuals. Although data within orthopedics is limited, prior work in plastic surgery has demonstrated that leadership structure can influence inclusivity within training environments, with programs led by women chairs exhibiting higher women faculty representation and recruitment rates.23, 24, 25 While institutional affiliation was not examined in the present study, concentration within specific training or professional networks may represent an additional structural factor influencing leadership allocation. Broadening access across multiple domains may therefore be essential to fully realize the benefits of diversity initiatives within hand surgery.

While empirical data on interventions to promote viewpoint diversity in surgical societies remain limited, insights from organizational and professional governance literature suggest several potential strategies. Transparent nomination processes, defined leadership term limits, structured mentorship and sponsorship pathways, and routine audits of leadership distribution may facilitate broader participation and succession.20 Tracking metrics such as the number of unique individuals in leadership positions over time may provide additional insight into whether leadership structures are becoming more inclusive beyond numeric representation. Although the effectiveness of these approaches within surgical societies warrants further study, they offer a framework for translating gains in representation into sustained diversification of leadership.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Sex data were self-reported and limited to binary categories, excluding nonbinary and sex-diverse identities. The retrospective design precludes causal inference, and the focus on a single professional society may limit generalizability. Leadership positions were defined based on decision-making capacity, and exclusion of other positions may overlook more subtle forms of influence. Additionally, other important dimensions of diversity, such as race, ethnicity, socioeconomic background, geographic location, training pathway, and practice setting, were not captured in the available data. Future studies incorporating intersectional analyses and examining how leadership diversity influences organizational outcomes will be essential to advancing inclusivity within surgical societies.
