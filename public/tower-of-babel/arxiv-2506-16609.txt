Abstract
Artificial intelligence for Science (AI4S) is poised to transform industrial manufacturing by enabling the accelerated discovery and optimization of advanced (bio)materials, dramatically reducing development cycles, and unlocking novel high-performance solutions. We introduce Aethorix v1.0, a platform that integrates large language models for objective mining, diffusion-based generative models for zero-shot inorganic crystal design, and machine-learned interatomic potentials for rapid property prediction at a ​ b ab i ​ n ​ i ​ t ​ i ​ o initio accuracy. The platform is developed to enhance the full materials development cycle, ranging from design to deployment in use cases, while incorporating critical operational constraints to meet rigorous manufacturing standards. We validated its industrial value through a real use case, showcasing how the framework can be seamlessly embedded into scalable materials R&D pipelines.

2
Aethorix v1.0 Framework

Our framework establishes a close-loop, data-driven inverse design paradigm to semi-automatically discover, design, and optimize unprecedented inorganic materials without experimental priors (see main pipeline in Figure
2
). The implementation protocol customizes materials development using industry-provided specifications as inputs. As a first step, LLMs are employed to retrieve relevant literature and extract key design parameters and innovation objectives, including (i) target chemical systems delineating compositional search spaces and elemental constraints, (ii) operational environment parameters defining temperature and pressure ranges, and (iii) quantifiable materials property determining the targets. Next, generative AI models are used to propose diverse, new, and unique crystal structures satisfying the specified chemical constraints. These candidate structures subsequently undergo large-scale computational pre-screening under the target operational conditions, using our in-house developed machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) to assess their thermodynamic stability. Structures exhibiting synthetic viability are then advanced to property prediction, where the same MLIP models are utilized to efficiently evaluate target performance metrics. Candidates that meet the design criteria proceed to experimental validation, where synthesis and characterization are conducted to confirm the predicted properties prior to industrial deployment.

2.2
Crystal Structure Generator

We use a diffusion-based generative model (adapted from MatterGen
[
2
]
) for zero-shot discovery of novel and thermodynamically stable inorganic materials. Periodic crystal materials are represented by
M=(A,X,L)
, where
A=(a^{1},a^{2},...,a^{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
represents the atom species inside the unit cell,
X=(x^{1},x^{2},...,x^{n})\in[0,1)^{3\times n}
denotes the fractional coordinates of atoms in the unit cell, and
L=(l^{1},l^{2},l^{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the lattice with three vector components. During the forward diffusion (noising) process,
A
,
X
, and
L
are independently corrupted toward a physically meaningful prior distribution as follows:

q(A_{t+1},X_{t+1},L_{t+1}|A_{t},X_{t},L_{t})=q(A_{t+1}|A_{t})\,q(X_{t+1}|X_{t})\,q(L_{t+1}|L_{t})

(1)

where
q
denotes distributions and
t
is the diffusion timestep. The model generates crystal materials by iteratively denoise a random initial structure through learned reverse diffusion. Atom types are corrected through discrete categorical transitions that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence and cross-entropy loss, fractional coordinates are updated using periodic score predictions to approximate the log-probability density, and the lattice matrix is adjusted via equivariant operations that preserve volume and symmetry. The total training loss is the weighted sum of the score matching loss across all parameters, expressed as:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\text{coord}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{coord}}+\lambda_{\text{cell}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cell}}+\lambda_{\text{types}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{types}}

(2)

Property-guided generation necessitates fine-tuning the unconditional score network by augmenting its node representations with property-specific features through trainable adapter modules.
[
49
]
The property-augmented node hidden representation is given by:

H_{j}^{\prime(L)}=H_{j}^{(L)}+f^{(L)}_{\text{mixin}}\Big(f^{(L)}_{\text{adapter}}(\mathbf{g})\Big)

(3)

where f
{}^{(L)}_{\text{mixin}}
and f
{}^{(L)}_{\text{adapter}}
are the
L
-th mix-in and adapter layer, respectively. Our current fine-tuned models incorporate formation energy as a conditioning property, which facilitates targeted generation of thermodynamically stable crystal materials. We have also developed fine-tuned models in the crystal structure prediction (CSP) mode, where the model denoises only the atomic coordinates and lattice not atom types, preserving the stoichiometry of the input composition. This is valuable for investigating systems involving polymorphic phases, as it allows a more efficient and exhaustive sampling of the configurational landscape for a fixed composition. For future model development, we intend to extend our workflow to multi-property conditioning, allowing generation of structures that concurrently satisfy multiple targeted requirements.

2.3
Structural Property Predictor

While DFT is typically the gold standard in computational chemistry, its computational cost scales as
\sim\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, where
N
is the number of electron in the system. This significantly limits its application to high-throughput studies, necessitating the development of surrogate models that reproduce
ab

initio
accuracy at orders-of-magnitude lower computational expense. Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) utilize parametric functionals to represent interatomic interactions. The training sets are constructed by incorporating the structural frames from DFT relaxation trajectories. Each data instance consists of atomic coordinates, calculated total energy, atomic forces, and virial stress. MLIPs were implemented through a graph neural network (GNN)-based architecture, which directly maps atomic configurations to their corresponding potential energy surface (PES). The end-to-end framework enables prediction of structural ground-state energetics without the need for handcrafted feature descriptors. As a representative implementation, the polarizable atom interaction neural network
[
3
]
(PaiNN) leverages SE(3)-equivariant message passing that preserves rototranslational and permutational symmetries to achieve high data efficiency compared to invariant methods and descriptor-based neural networks. The model training is implemented in a multi-target learning scheme, where the node features are optimized to simultaneously predict multiple properties. The loss function is given by:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\alpha_{E}\left\|E_{i}-\hat{E}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\alpha_{F}\left\|\mathbf{F}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\alpha_{S}\left\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]

(4)

where
\alpha_{E}
,
\alpha_{F}
, and
\alpha_{S}
are the weight coefficients for total energy, atomic force, and virial stress, respectively,
N
denotes the number of training samples,
X_{i}
and
\hat{X}_{i}
(
X=E,\mathbf{F},\sigma
) are the true and predicted targets for structure
i
, respectively. Optimized hyperparameters are typically determined using Bayesian optimization methods.
[
1
]
MLIP ensembles are constructed by training multiple models on identical datasets but with different parameter initializations. Therefore, rigorous statistical analysis of prediction variance can be performed across ensemble members, serving as an
a

priori
metric for evaluating model reliability when extrapolating beyond trained domains. In our framework, MLIPs are developed as high-fidelity surrogates for DFT to accelerate structural relaxation and
ab

initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations.
[
50
]
The efficient computation enables derivation of important chemical and mechanical properties across compositionally complex materials and extended timescales, which would otherwise be computationally intractable using direct DFT calculations.

2.4
Active Learning

MLIPs are inherently constrained by their transferability beyond well-learned domains, necessitating active learning (AL) strategies to enhance their predictive accuracy across a broader chemical space.
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
To maximize learning efficiency, new structures with high epistemic uncertainty are prioritized for inclusion in iterative retraining cycles. We use our generative models conditioned on atom types to generate brand new structures for AL. Ensemble variance analysis is used to quantify prediction uncertainty, where structures exhibiting the highest ensemble variances are flagged. DFT calculations are selectively performed on the flagged structures to obtain ground-truth references. In our framework, we define a statistically derived threshold for each target property (
e.g.,
energy, forces, stresses), such that the decision of inclusion for each new structure is made by comparing the calculated ensemble variances to all thresholds. We terminate AL cycles when majority (
\geq
90%) of newly generated structures exhibit uncertainties that meet the thresholds.

2.5.1
Structural Complexity and Disorder

Computational materials models are commonly categorized into bulk and surface models based on boundary conditions and setups.
[
28
,
29
]
Bulk models are simulated with unit cell repeating infinitely in all three dimensions to mimic infinite crystals. In contrast, surface models are constructed as periodically extended slabs with finite thickness, where vacuum spacings are added perpendicular to the exposed surfaces to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images. While pristine bulk structures are idealized representations for simulations, real-world materials typically exhibit various forms of disorder, including defects, dopants, strain, and non-stoichiometric compositions. Moreover, realistic surface models introduce additional degrees of freedom, including crystallographic orientation, surface facet termination, adsorbate interactions, substrate-support interactions, defect and dopant ion distributions, and surface reconstructions. Incorporating all these complexities has traditionally been prohibitive for DFT, but is now feasible within our framework, which constitutes a strategic foundation for engineering materials with tailored disorder and interfacial functionalities, opening immense opportunities in diverse industrial sectors, including heterogeneous catalysis, electrochemical energy storage, surface coatings, semiconductor manufacturing, and carbon capture and utilization.

2.5.2
Phonon Calculation

DFT provides insights into ground-state energy at 0 K, but real materials can behave very differently at finite temperatures. Vibrational contributions become especially important in systems at elevated temperatures, where they are indispensable for studies of temperature-dependent thermodynamic stability, phase transitions, and transport phenomena.
[
27
]
The vibrational motions of atoms are often described by phonons, which are quantized lattice vibrations that govern thermal and mechanical properties. Under harmonic approximations, atomic vibrations are treated as small displacements around equilibrium positions using the finite displacement method. There, Helmholtz free energy can be calculated (managed by Phonopy
[
4
]
) by:

F_{\mathrm{ha}}(T)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+k_{B}T\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\ln\left(1-e^{-\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}/k_{B}T}\right)

(5)

where
\mathbf{q}
and
\nu
are the phonon wavevector and branch index, respectively,
\omega
is the phonon frequency
\hbar
is reduced Planck constant, and
k_{B}
is Boltzmann constant. Gibbs free energy can be derived using the quasi-harmonic approximations, where volume dependence of ground-state energy and phonon frequencies are incorporated to account for thermal expansion, given as:

G(T,p)=\min_{V_{c}}\left[E_{\mathrm{DFT}}(V_{c})+F_{\mathrm{vib}}(V_{c},T)+pV_{c}\right]

(6)

where
V_{c}
is the volume of unit cell. Other key thermodynamic and vibrational properties, such as heat capacity (
C_{v}
) and density of states (DOS), can also be easily derived within harmonic approximations by:

C_{V}=\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}k_{B}\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{k_{B}T}\right)^{2}\frac{\exp\left(\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}/k_{B}T\right)}{\left[\exp\left(\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}/k_{B}T\right)-1\right]^{2}}

(7)

D(\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu})\sim\frac{V_{c}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{q}\sum_{\nu}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu})

(8)

Here, the Brillouin zone (BZ) integration is performed by discretizing a reciprocal primitive cell using a regular grid of
\mathbf{q}
. Additionally, anharmonic phonon calculations can enable the estimate of lattice thermal conductivity, given as:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\frac{1}{NV_{c}}\sum_{\lambda}C_{\lambda}\,\mathbf{v}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{\lambda}\,\tau_{\lambda}

(9)

where
\lambda
is the composite index for phonon modes,
C_{\lambda}
is the mode heat capacity,
\mathbf{v}_{\lambda}
is the phonon group velocity, and
\tau_{\lambda}
is the phonon lifetime.

2.5.3
Mechanical Property Calculation

Beyond thermochemical properties, mechanical behaviors are critical performance metrics for industrial material applications. Some mechanical properties can be computationally predicted using DFT through finite difference methods, where the material’s response to applied strains or stresses is evaluated by calculating the corresponding changes in energy and force profiles. For linear-elastic response, the second derivative of the energy with respect to infinitesimal strain components yields the elastic stiffness tensor
[
30
]
:

C_{ij}=\frac{1}{V_{0}}\frac{\partial^{2}E}{\partial\varepsilon_{i}\,\partial\varepsilon_{j}}

(10)

where
V_{0}
is the undeformed equilibrium volume,
E
is the system total energy, and
\varepsilon_{i}
,
\varepsilon_{j}
(Voigt indices 1-6) denote normal or shear components of the tensorial strain. The ideal shear strength probes large, nonlinear deformation
[
31
,
32
]
, which is obtained from the first derivative of the energy with respect to an engineering shear strain
\gamma
:

\tau_{xy}=\frac{1}{V_{0}}\frac{\partial E}{\partial\gamma}

(11)

where
\tau_{xy}
is the shear stress component in the xy-plane and is evaluated numerically by incrementing
\Delta\gamma
in steps of
\Delta\gamma
=0.01-0.10. The max shear stress
\tau_{max}
before instability marks the ideal shear strength.

Transport phenomena such as ionic diffusivity can be assessed through
ab

initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD), where the thermal motion of atoms is tracked in real time. The self-diffusion is computed via Einstein relation
[
33
,
34
]
:

D=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2d}\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\left|\mathbf{r}_{i}(t)-\mathbf{r}_{i}(0)\right|^{2}\right\rangle

(12)

where
N
is the number of atoms,
d
is the dimensionality of diffusion,
\mathbf{r}_{i}(t)
is the position of atom
i
at
t
, and the angular brackets denote an ensemble or time average.

Overall, we observed K ion doping (preferentially on Ca site) consistently stabilizes all desired crystal phases, while Mg doping (preferentially on Si site) tends to stabilize undesired polymorph, such as C

Taken together, these findings suggest that industrial clinker production should aim to retain reasonable amounts (5-10 mol.%) of K impurities while minimizing Mg content, in order to promote the formation of desirable crystal phases, enhance overall product consistency, and mitigate performance fluctuations.

Conclusion

We presented Aethorix v1.0, a platform purpose-built to tackle black-box challenges in industrial applications through AI-driven solutions. By pipelining LLMs, generative AI, and MLIPs, our platform enables rapid, cost-effective decision-making across complex materials processes, adhering to realistic operational constraints. Looking ahead, we envision Aethorix evolving into a transformative engine for scientific and industrial breakthroughs. As the platform advances, it is positioned to become a cornerstone for next-generation materials development across high-impact sectors, including but not limited to energy storage, sustainability, semiconductors, and biotechnology.
