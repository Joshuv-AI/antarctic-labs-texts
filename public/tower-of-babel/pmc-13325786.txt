Background

Ovarian cancer ranks as the second most lethal gynecological malignancy worldwide [1, 2]. Its asymptomatic onset and high rate of recurrence pose significant therapeutic challenges, resulting in a low 5-year survival rate of 30–50% [3, 4]. Consequently, ovarian cancer remains a substantial global health burden.

Over the past decade, there has been rapid progress in developing new therapeutic agents for ovarian cancer [5, 6]. Bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the VEGF signaling pathway, has demonstrated clinical efficacy by inhibiting tumor angiogenesis, thereby restricting the blood supply to tumors [7] and improving patient survival [8, 9]. Despite these advancements, the long-term effectiveness of anti-VEGF therapy is often compromised by the development of resistance. This is driven by mechanisms such as the activation of compensatory angiogenic pathways, the recruitment of pro-angiogenic immune cells, and the emergence of vascular mimicry [10, 11]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to identify alternative and more potent targets for anti-angiogenic therapy in ovarian cancer.

Focal adhesion kinase (FAK), encoded by the protein tyrosine kinase 2 (PTK2) gene, is a ubiquitous non-receptor tyrosine kinase [12, 13]. Abnormal upregulation or hyperactivation of FAK has been identified in numerous solid tumors and is closely linked to poor clinical outcomes [13]. Serving as a central signaling scaffold, FAK regulates multiple oncogenic processes, such as cell proliferation [14], metastasis [15], chemoresistance [16], and immune evasion [17], by triggering various downstream pathways, including PI3K-AKT, Ras-MAPK, and integrin-ECM signaling. Given its pleiotropic regulatory functions, FAK has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for ovarian cancer [18]. Currently, several FAK inhibitors, such as VS-6063, IN10018, and AMP-945, are undergoing preclinical and clinical investigation as potential anticancer therapies [19, 20].

Recent evidence highlights the indispensable role of FAK in both embryonic vascular development and pathological angiogenesis [21]. Endothelial-specific deletion of FAK results in severely impaired vascular formation, leading to embryonic lethality by day 8.5 [22]. In various malignancies, including melanoma [23], breast cancer [24], and ovarian cancer [25], blocking FAK has been shown to suppress angiogenesis and consequently impede tumor growth, possibly due to decreased VEGF expression [24].

Despite these observations, the precise mechanisms through which FAK inhibitors exert their antitumor effects in established tumor models remain incompletely elucidated. While FAK inhibition is recognized to impede tumor angiogenesis, the specific cellular processes and molecular pathways responsible for this anti-angiogenic outcome have yet to be comprehensively characterized, particularly within the context of the ovarian tumor microenvironment.

This study initially established the overexpression of FAK in ovarian cancer tissues and its correlation with unfavorable prognosis in ovarian cancer patients. Subsequently, a FAK inhibitor exhibited notably enhanced efficacy in ovarian cancer CDX models compared to in vitro cell cultures, indicating that its anti-tumor activity primarily operates through non-tumor cell pathways. Treatment with the FAK inhibitor in the CDX models resulted in a significant reduction in tumor blood perfusion, vascular density, and serum VEGF levels, underscoring the inhibitor’s mechanism of action through the inhibition of tumor angiogenesis. To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, hiPSC-ECs were utilized in a comprehensive in vitro system. Both pharmacological inhibition and siRNA-mediated knockdown confirmed the pivotal role of FAK in angiogenesis. Subsequent endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and sprouting assays revealed that FAK inhibition appears to play a particularly critical role during the sprouting phase of angiogenesis. In conclusion, our results offer compelling experimental support for the potential of FAK inhibitors as effective anti-angiogenic agents in ovarian cancer therapy.

Methods

Cell lines and reagents

Human ovarian cancer cell line A2780, SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-3 were purchased from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), while the TOV-21G cell line was purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). SK-OV-3 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Gibco, #16600082) and A2780, OVCAR-3, and TOV-21G cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, #11875093), both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Royacel, #RYS-KF22). HiPSCs and hiPSCs-GFP were purchased from Nuwacell (Hefei, Anhui, China) and cultured on hESC-qualified Matrigel Matrix (Corning, #354277) coated dishes at a density of 10 µg/cm2 in StemFlex™ medium (Gibco, #A3349401). All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored using the Myco-Lumi™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Beyotime, #C0298S) and all cells used in the experiments were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

IN10018 and AMP-945 were supplied by InxMed, while Paclitaxel was procured from MCE. Details of the antibodies used in the study can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Patient samples

We collected 15 HGSOC biopsy samples from patients clinically diagnosed at our institution between January 2021 and December 2024. Detailed patient characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Differentiation of hiPSCs into ECs

This methodology is adapted from the research conducted by Sarkawt Hamad et al. [26].

When reaching 70–80% confluency, hiPSCs were detached using ReLeSR™ (Stemcell, #100–0483) and seeded at a density of 0.02 × 10⁶ cells/cm² in StemFlex™ medium supplemented with 10 µM ROCK inhibitor (Adooq, #A11001-5) on day − 2. To induce mesodermal differentiation on day 0, the cells were cultured in E6 medium containing 6 µM CHIR99021. The E6 medium was composed of DMEM/F12 (1:1) + Glutamax (Gibco, #11320033) and supplemented with 64 µg/mL L-Ascorbic acid 2-phosphate sesquimagnesium (Selleck, #E2692), 20 µg/ml insulin (Selleck, S6955), 5 µg/ml transferrin (solarbio, #T8010), 14 ng/mL sodium selenite (Sigma-Aldrich, #S5261) and 100 ng/mL heparin sodium salt (MCE, #HY-17567 C). On day 1, the medium was changed to E6 medium without CHIR99021. Endothelial differentiation was initiated on day 2 using E6 medium supplemented with 300 ng/mL VEGF (Peprotech, #100 − 20), 200 ng/mL b-FGF (Peprotech, #100-18B), 1 mM 8-Bromo-cAMP (Selleck, #S7857) and 50 µM melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich, #M5250). Starting from day 4, the cells were cultured in E6 medium with 10 ng/mL VEGF, 10 ng/mL b-FGF and 10 µM hydrocortisone (Selleck, #S1696) for 48 h.

Flow cytometry

On day 6, dissociated hiPSC-ECs were fixed using 4% PFA for 15 min at RT and subsequently incubated with CD144-PE antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. Following centrifugation, the cells were suspended in 0.5% BSA and subjected to a flow cytometer (CytoFlex LX, BeckmanCoulter) to quantify the proportion of CD144-PE positive cells.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) assay

A total of 0.2 × 10⁶ hiPSC-ECs were seeded onto Poly-D-lysine-coated (Beyotime, #ST508) glass-bottom dishes (NEST, #801002) and cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM; ScienCell, #1001) for 24 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at RT, followed by blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at RT. Notably, to specifically detect the cell surface expression of CD31, no permeabilization step was performed. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with CD31 primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the cells were stained with an Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI for 1 h at RT. Following additional PBS washes, the samples were mounted (Solarbio, #S2100) and imaged using confocal microscopy (FV3000, Olympus).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (NCM Biotech, #C6005). Specifically, 0.8 × 10⁴ cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere. After a 72-hour exposure to the experimental compounds, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by an incubation period of 20–40 min at 37 ℃. Absorbance at 450 nm was quantified using a microplate reader (TECAN). Viability percentages and IC50 values were determined utilizing GraphPad Prism 8.4.2.

HiPSC-ECs/OVCAR-3 co-culture and migration assay

We utilized a transwell co-culture system (Corning, #3422) to evaluate the migratory capacity of hiPSC-ECs. In this system, triplicate inserts in a 24-well format were seed with 2 × 10⁴ hiPSC-ECs in 200 µL of serum-free ECM in the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 500 µL of ECM with 10% FBS with or without 1 × 10⁵ OVCAR-3 cells. After a 24-hour incubation period, the migrated cells were fixed with 4% PFA, stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, #C0121) and quantified at 5× magnification using light microscopy.

Tube formation assay

24-well plates were coated with Matrigel Matrix (Corning, #354234) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Next, 1 × 10⁵ hiPSC-ECs suspended in ECM were seeded into each well. Tube formation was assessed after 12 h of incubation. In inhibition experiments, cells were preincubated with FAK inhibitors (IN10018 and AMP-945; 75 and 300 nM) for 36 h before seeding. Angiogenic metrics, including branch length and node number, were quantified utilizing Image J.

Results

FAK is highly expressed in ovarian cancer tissues and is associated with poor prognosis

Transcriptomic data from the TCGA and GTEx databases were employed to compare PTK2 mRNA expression levels in ovarian cancer and normal ovarian tissues. The analysis demonstrated a notable increase in PTK2 mRNA expression in ovarian cancer tissues compared to normal ovarian tissues (P = 1.6 × 10− 7, Fig. 1A). Subsequent survival analyses using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database indicated that high FAK expression was significantly associated with shorter PFS in both the overall cohort of ovarian cancer patients (HR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.1–1.44, P < 0.05, Fig. 1B) and the serous ovarian cancer subtype (HR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.11–1.49, P < 0.05, Fig. 1C).

(A) Plots of PTK2 expression, the encoding gene of FAK, in HGSOC tissues (n = 418) versus normal ovarian tissues (n = 88), based on TCGA and GTEx databases. Kaplan-Meier analysis of PFS in the overall ovarian cancer cohort (B) and serous subtype (C), stratified by PTK2 expression. Data were obtained from the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database. (D) Representative IHC images of FAK and pY397 FAK expression in platinum-sensitive and platinum-resistant HGSOC tissues (scale bar = 50 μm). (E) Quantification of FAK and pY397 FAK staining intensity (n = 5 per group)

The clinical management of advanced ovarian cancer remains challenging due to the prevalence of platinum resistance, which limits the median overall survival to less than 12 months in this patient population [27]. This study examined the expression levels of FAK and its activated form, pY397 FAK, in platinum-sensitive (n = 5) and platinum-resistant (n = 5) HGSOC cohorts. Both FAK and pY397 FAK were detected in both groups, with significantly elevated levels observed in the platinum-resistant group compared to the platinum-sensitive group (P < 0.01, Fig. 1D and E).

These findings underscore a potential association between heightened FAK expression and unfavorable prognosis in ovarian cancer, suggesting a possible involvement of dysregulated FAK signaling in the emergence of platinum resistance.

FAK inhibitor IN10018 exhibits stronger efficacy in ovarian cancer in vivo models than in vitro

IN10018 is a potent and selective ATP-competitive FAK inhibitor. To evaluate its efficacy across diverse ovarian cancer contexts, we selected four human cell lines representing different histological subtypes and clinical features. The inhibitory effects of IN10018 and the standard ovarian cancer chemotherapy drug PTX were assessed over 72 h using CCK-8 assays. Cell viability decreased with escalating concentrations of both agents (Fig. 2A). The 72-hour IC50 values for IN10018 in SK-OV-3, A2780, OVCAR-3, and TOV-21G were 41.08 µM, 18.17 µM, 14.43 µM, and 14.81 µM, respectively. In comparison, the IC50 values for PTX were 0.003133 µM, 0.005795 µM, 0.0002830 µM, and 0.02014 µM (Fig. 2B). Notably, the IC50 values of IN10018 were significantly higher than those of PTX across all tested lines, suggesting that IN10018 exerts minimal direct cytotoxic effects on ovarian cancer cells in vitro.

(A) Dose-response curves of paclitaxel or IN10018 in four ovarian cancer cell lines after 72-hour treatment (n ≥ 3 per concentration). Concentrations were log10-transformed based on µM for both IN10018 and Paclitaxel. (B) Corresponding IC₅₀ values for IN10018 and PTX after 72 h of treatment. Antitumor efficacy (C) and body weight changes (D) in OVCAR-3 cell line-derived CDX model following IN10018 treatment (n = 5 per group). Antitumor efficacy (E) and body weight changes (F) in TOV-21G CDX model following IN10018 treatment (n = 4 per group). Antitumor efficacy (G) and body weight changes (H) in OVCAR-3 CDX model following paclitaxel treatment (n = 5 per group). Antitumor efficacy (I) and body weight changes (J) in TOV-21G CDX model following paclitaxel treatment (n = 5 per group)

Two ovarian cancer CDX models were established using OVCAR-3 and TOV-21G cells. Treatment with IN10018 (25 mg/kg) significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2C, Supplemental Fig. 1A and 1B; P < 0.001, Fig. 2E, Supplemental Fig. 1C and 1D). There were no significant differences in body weight between the treatment and control groups, indicating the safety of this dosage (P = ns, Fig. 2D and F). These findings highlight the potent anti-tumor effects of IN10018 in ovarian cancer CDX models, with no significant toxicity observed at the tested dose.

In the same models, PTX (10 mg/kg) significantly inhibited tumor growth (P < 0.001, Fig. 2G, Supplemental Fig. 1E and 1 F; P < 0.05, Fig. 2I, Supplemental Fig. 1G and 1 H) without affecting body weight significantly (P = ns, Fig. 2H and J). While a direct comparison between IN10018 and PTX is precluded by differences in dosing frequency and administration routes [28–30], our results demonstrate that IN10018 displays markedly superior in vivo antitumor efficacy compared to in vitro settings.

IN10018 suppresses tumor growth in the CDX model by inhibiting angiogenesis

The notable discrepancy in the anti-tumor impact of IN10018 between in vitro and in vivo settings suggests that the suppression of tumor growth by FAK inhibition primarily occurs by targeting pathways in non-tumor cells.

To identify potential non-tumor targets affected by FAK inhibition, we analyzed data from the cancerSEA database to evaluate the relationship between PTK2 expression and genes associated with fourteen distinct functional states of cancer at the single-cell level (Fig. 3A). In ovarian cancer, we observed significant positive associations between PTK2 expression and genes linked to stemness, quiescence, differentiation, and angiogenesis. Our pan-cancer investigation further demonstrated that PTK2 exhibits the strongest correlations with angiogenesis-related genes. IF staining of five HGSOC tissue samples confirmed the diffuse cytoplasmic distribution of pY397 FAK in tumor cells, alongside its pronounced enrichment in CD31⁺ vascular endothelial cells (Fig. 3B and Supplement Fig. 2A). Based on these observations, we investigated whether IN10018 impedes tumor growth in the CDX model by suppressing angiogenesis.

Data were obtained from the cancerSEA database. (B) IF staining showing the localization of CD31 (green), pY397 FAK (red) and nuclei (blue) in HGSOC tissues (scale bar = 20 μm). (C) Representative LSCI images showing blood perfusion in the tumor region of vehicle control and IN10018-treated groups. (D) Quantification of tumor blood perfusion intensity (n = 6 per group). (E) Serum VEGF levels measured by ELISA in vehicle control and IN10018-treated groups (n = 6 per group). (F) IF staining of CD31 (red) and nuclei (blue) in tumor tissues of vehicle control and IN10018-treated groups (scale bar = 100 μm and 20 μm). (G) Quantification of CD31-positive area as a percentage of total tissue area (n = 4 per group). (H) IF staining of Ki-67 (red) and nuclei (blue) in tumor tissues of vehicle control and IN10018-treated groups (scale bar = 100 μm and 50 μm). (I) Quantification of Ki-67-positive cell ratio (n = 3 per group)

LSCI was utilized to assess and quantify blood perfusion within the tumor areas. After a 20-day treatment period, perfusion maps (Fig. 3C) revealed that tumor blood flow intensity was notably decreased in the IN10018-treated group compared to the vehicle control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3D). Additionally, ELISA analysis of serum VEGF levels demonstrated a significant reduction in IN10018-treated mice (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E). Evaluation of angiogenesis through CD31 IF staining revealed a substantial decrease in micro-vessel density in the IN10018 group compared to the controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 3F and G), indicating inhibition of vascular growth. Furthermore, assessment of tumor cell proliferation using ki-67 IF staining showed a significantly lower number of ki-67-positive cells in the IN10018 group compared to the vehicle control group (P < 0.001, Fig. 3H and I). In summary, these findings consistently indicate that the suppression of tumor angiogenesis is a key factor in the anti-tumor effects of IN10018 within the CDX model.

Differentiation of hiPSCs into hiPSC-ECs

Figure 4A and B provide a schematic representation of the differentiation process along with the associated morphological transitions. The differentiation process of hiPSCs into hiPSC-ECs comprised two primary stages. During the initial phase (Day 0 to Day 1), hiPSCs were directed towards the mesodermal lineage using the GSK-3 inhibitor CHIR99021. Subsequently, in the second phase (Day 2 to Day 4), these mesodermal progenitors were differentiated into hiPSC-ECs using a cocktail comprising VEGF, bFGF, 8-Bromo-cAMP, and melatonin, followed by maintenance in media with reduced levels of VEGF and bFGF. On Day 6, the differentiated cells were dissociated and evaluated for the endothelial markers CD144 and CD31 via flow cytometry and IF staining.

(A) Schematic overview of the hiPSC-to-EC differentiation protocol. (B) Bright-field images of cells at various time points from day − 2 to day 6 during differentiation (scale bar = 100 μm). (C) Representative flow cytometric scatter plots showing CD144-PE staining in hiPSCs and hiPSC-ECs-GFP. (D) Quantification of GFP⁺/CD144⁺ hiPSC-ECs-GFP on day 6 by flow cytometry (n = 3 per group). (E) ICC staining of GFP (green), CD31 (red) and nuclei (blue) in hiPSCs-GFP and hiPSC-ECs-GFP (scale bar = 10 μm)

To facilitate downstream functional assays, we established a stable hiPSC-EC line expressing green fluorescent protein (hiPSC-ECs-GFP). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that over 95% of the hiPSC-ECs-GFP population were double-positive for GFP and CD144, a stark contrast to undifferentiated hiPSCs (Fig. 4C and D), thereby obviating the need for additional purification steps. IF staining further validated the uniform expression of both GFP and CD31 in the differentiated hiPSC-ECs-GFP (Fig. 4E). This successful generation of hiPSC-ECs-GFP offers a scalable and functionally robust cell source for endothelial cells, ensuring a consistent supply for subsequent large-scale experimental endeavors.

FAK is functionally required for angiogenesis in vitro

In this section, we assessed the involvement of FAK in vitro angiogenesis through a tube formation assay. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we utilized two distinct FAK inhibitors, IN10018 and AMP-945, for pharmacological validation. Initially, western blot analysis confirmed that both IN10018 and AMP-945 effectively inhibited FAK phosphorylation at the pY397 site in hiPSC-ECs without altering total FAK protein levels (Fig. 5A). Subsequent exposure of hiPSC-ECs to concentrations of 75 nM and 300 nM of either inhibitor for 36 h significantly suppressed tube formation. Notably, even at nanomolar concentrations, both inhibitors markedly reduced total branch length (P < 0.01, Fig. 5C) and the number of nodes (P < 0.01, Fig. 5D) compared to the control group (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, these suppressive effects exhibited a dose-dependent response (P < 0.05).

(A) Western blot analysis of total FAK and pY397 FAK expression in hiPSC-ECs after 36-hour treatment with 1 µΜ IN10018 or AMP-945. GAPDH served as a loading control. (B) Representative images of tube formation assay after 36-hour treatment with 75 nM and 300 nM IN10018 or AMP-945 (scale bar = 200 μm). (C) Quantification of total branch length (n = 3 per group). (D) Quantification of node number (n = 3 per group). (E) qRT-PCR analysis of PTK2 mRNA expression after 24-hour siRNA transfection. (F) Western blot analysis of total FAK and pY397 FAK expression in hiPSC-ECs after 48-hour siRNA transfection. GAPDH served as a loading control. Quantification of FAK (G) and pY397 FAK (H) protein expression (n = 3 per group). (I) Representative images of tube formation assay in hiPSC-ECs 48 h after PTK2 knockdown (scale bar = 200 μm). Quantification of total branch length (J) and node number (K) (n = 3 per group)

To mitigate potential off-target effects of the pharmacological inhibitors, we further validated FAK’s role in angiogenesis through siRNA-mediated gene silencing. Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed that siFAK1 and siFAK2 achieved mRNA knockdown efficiencies of 83.75% and 80.21% respectively, compared to the negative control group (Fig. 5E). Western blot analysis corroborated these results, with total FAK protein levels reduced by 47.46% and 50.14%, and pY397 FAK levels decreased by 37.80% and 49.93% in the siFAK1 and siFAK2 groups, respectively (Fig. 5F–H). Consistently, FAK knockdown significantly inhibited tube formation (Fig. 5I), with both siRNA groups showing substantial decreases in total branch length (P < 0.01, Fig. 5J) and the number of nodes (P < 0.05, Fig. 5K). These results closely align with the outcomes observed in the inhibitor-treated groups.

FAK plays a critical role in the sprouting phase of angiogenesis

Angiogenesis encompasses a multifaceted sequence of events, including endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and sprouting [31]. Given the recognized involvement of FAK in angiogenic regulation, our investigation aimed to identify the specific stages of the angiogenic cascade most sensitive to FAK activity.

The impact of FAK inhibitors on endothelial cell proliferation was evaluated through the CCK-8 assay. The viability of hiPSC-ECs exhibited a dose-dependent decrease with escalating concentrations of FAK inhibitors (Fig. 6A). The 36-hour IC₅₀ values for IN10018 and AMP-945 were 25.23 µM and 12.21 µM, respectively (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that FAK inhibitors exert relatively limited direct effects on endothelial cell proliferation.

(A) Dose-response curves of IN10018 or AMP-945 in hiPSC-ECs after 36-hour treatment (n = 3 per concentration). (B) Corresponding IC₅₀ values for IN10018 and AMP-945 after 36 h of treatment. (C) Representative crystal violet staining images of Transwell migration assay in hiPSC-ECs (H) co-cultured with OVCAR-3 (H/O) after 36-hour treatment with 2 µM and 4 µM IN10018 or AMP-945 (scale bar = 100 μm). (D) Quantification of migrated cell numbers. (E) Endothelial spheroid sprouting assay of hiPSC-ECs-GFP after 36-hour treatment with 300 nM IN10018 or AMP-945 (scale bar = 200 μm)

To evaluate the impact of FAK inhibitors on endothelial cell migration, we implemented a co-culture system (H/O) involving hiPSC-ECs (H) and OVCAR-3 (O) cells, a characterized HGSOC line. Our findings revealed a notable enhancement in the migration of hiPSC-ECs in response to chemotactic factors released by tumor cells (P < 0.0001, Fig. 6C and D). Subsequent treatment with 2 µM and 4 µM of IN10018 or AMP-945 significantly impeded this migration (P < 0.0001, Fig. 6C and D), with a discernible dose-dependent inhibitory trend (P < 0.001, Fig. 6C and D). These outcomes underscore the important role of FAK in governing endothelial cell migration.

Subsequently, a spheroid sprouting assay was conducted utilizing hiPSC-ECs-GFP to assess the impact of FAK inhibitors on endothelial cell sprouting. While the control spheroids demonstrated typical sprouting behavior, exposure to a mere 300 nM of either IN10018 or AMP-945 for 36 h resulted in a complete cessation of sprouting (Fig. 6E). Notably, this effective concentration was orders of magnitude lower than the dosage required to impede endothelial cell proliferation or migration. Through a comparative analysis of the effective dosages across these three assays, we confirmed that the sprouting phase of angiogenesis shows a high degree of sensitivity to FAK inhibition, suggesting it is a key regulatory node in vascularization.

Discussion

Despite previous research confirming the involvement of FAK in angiogenesis, there are still substantial gaps in understanding the precise mechanisms through which FAK inhibitors hinder tumor growth and exert anti-angiogenic effects. This study presents a systematic demonstration, for the first time, that FAK inhibitors hinder tumor progression in large part by targeting non-tumor cells, with a significant focus on inhibiting tumor angiogenesis. Additionally, we clarify the mechanistic foundation through which FAK inhibitors suppress angiogenesis in ovarian cancer by targeting endothelial sprouting. This contribution addresses a crucial theoretical gap in comprehending how FAK regulates vascularization in the tumor microenvironment.

Prior research has shown increased levels of FAK and pY397 FAK in HGSOC compared to normal tissue [32], with additional upregulation post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy [33]. Building on this, our study specifically investigated primary platinum-resistant HGSOC specimens. While the limited cohort size of these patient samples may constrain the broad applicability of our conclusions, this clinically uncommon subset al.lowed us to explore the population where research data is often scarce. Our analysis unveiled elevated FAK and pY397 FAK levels in platinum-resistant tissues compared to sensitive ones, suggesting FAK’s involvement in primary chemoresistance and underscoring its promise as a therapeutic target. Future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts will be essential to further validate these findings.

In vitro, FAK inhibitors show limited direct cytotoxicity against ovarian cancer cells. However, they display substantial antitumor effectiveness in CDX models, indicating that their impact is significantly mediated through the alteration of the tumor microenvironment rather than direct targeting of tumor cells. Although Baoyuan Zhang et al. [34] confirmed the efficacy of the FAK inhibitor IN10018 in various preclinical models, they did not investigate its comparative effects in vitro and in vivo or delve into the underlying mechanisms. Therefore, our results offer innovative mechanistic perspectives on FAK inhibition. However, we acknowledge that without tissue-specific knockout models, the relative contribution of endothelial-specific FAK inhibition versus tumor-cell–intrinsic effects in vivo remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies employing conditional knockout systems will be necessary to definitively decouple these distinct biological roles.

Tube formation assays were utilized in this study to mimic angiogenesis in vitro. Both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown were employed to further establish the crucial involvement of FAK in this process. Additional mechanistic exploration using CCK-8, transwell, and endothelial spheroid sprouting assays uncovered, for the first time, that the sprouting phase of angiogenesis shows a high degree of sensitivity to FAK inhibition. This finding represents a notable advancement beyond earlier studies that solely focused on the impact of FAK inhibitors on tube formation and endothelial migration [35]. Notably, the pronounced sensitivity of the sprouting phase to FAK inhibition may be influenced by the distinct biological contexts of the various assays used. While our findings highlight FAK’s critical role in sprout initiation, this hierarchy may reflect assay-dependent sensitivity rather than absolute stage specificity. Therefore, FAK should be considered a key regulator throughout angiogenesis, with its functional impact potentially being more manifest in the dynamic sprouting process.

Our development of a comprehensive in vitro system utilizing hiPSC-ECs, which includes tube formation, tumor-endothelial cell coculture, and sprouting assays, offers a valuable methodological framework with relevant scientific implications. This experimental framework offers a reliable instrument for forthcoming studies in vascular biology.

This study primarily investigated the involvement of FAK in endothelial sprouting. However, preliminary findings suggest broader mechanistic and therapeutic implications that necessitate further exploration. Initially, qPCR analysis indicated that FAK inhibitors potentially hinder endothelial sprouting by reducing the expression of Notch signaling components (Supplemental Fig. 3A). Nevertheless, validation of this observation through western blotting or genomic sequencing is required to confirm the modulation of this pathway. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that FAK inhibition induces changes in vascular architecture, affecting pericyte coverage (Supplemental Fig. 4A) and smooth muscle cell organization (Supplemental Fig. 4B), which could influence vessel stability and permeability (Supplemental Fig. 4C). Although these observations lack quantitative mechanistic analysis, they underscore the multifaceted role of FAK in vascular remodeling and suggest its potential involvement in tumor vascular normalization, a promising strategy for enhancing drug delivery and immunotherapy efficacy. Future investigations will delve into these hypotheses, utilizing advanced imaging techniques and functional assays to elucidate FAK’s impact on vascular maturation and barrier function in ovarian cancer.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the role of FAK in ovarian cancer and identified its pivotal involvement in the angiogenesis process.

First, bioinformatics analysis revealed that FAK is highly expressed in ovarian cancer tissues, and its expression level is closely associated with poor patient prognosis. Further validation using clinical specimens demonstrated that the expression levels of both total FAK and its activated form, pY397 FAK, were significantly elevated in primary platinum-resistant ovarian cancer tissues compared to platinum-sensitive samples.

We observed a notable discrepancy in the efficacy of FAK inhibitors between in vitro and in vivo experiments, suggesting that their antitumor effects are closely associated with targeting non-tumor cellular components. To test this hypothesis, we employed laser speckle contrast imaging and CD31 immunofluorescence staining in CDX models, which confirmed that FAK inhibitors suppress ovarian cancer growth in conjunction with the inhibition of tumor angiogenesis.

In in vitro experiments, we successfully differentiated hiPSCs into hiPSC-ECs. Using both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown approaches, we independently demonstrated the critical regulatory role of FAK in angiogenesis. More importantly, functional assays further revealed that the sprouting phase of angiogenesis shows a high degree of sensitivity to FAK inhibition.

In summary, this study provides compelling experimental evidence supporting FAK inhibitors as potential targeted therapeutics for ovarian cancer. Our findings suggest that FAK inhibitors block the FAK signaling pathway and disrupt tumor angiogenesis, particularly the sprouting process, thereby effectively reducing tumor blood supply and inhibiting ovarian cancer progression.
