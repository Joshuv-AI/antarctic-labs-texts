Chloroform as Source of Chlorido Ligands in Coordination Chemistry: A Case Study
Authors: Christina Stamou, Catherine P. Raptopoulou, Faidonas-Angelos P. Tsapalis, Evangelos G. Bakalbassis, Athanassios C. Tsipis, Dionissios Papaioannou, Vassilis Psycharis, Spyros P. Perlepes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The purity of the solvent is of paramount importance in synthetic coordination chemistry. This effect is demonstrated in the present work through investigation of the InX3/mebta reaction system in CHCl3 under refluxing conditions (X = Br, I; mebta = 1-methylbenzotriazole). Use of “old” batches of CHCl3 (i.e., containing COCl2, HCl, and H2O) has provided access to complexes [InCl2.1Br0.9(mebta)­(H2O)2] (1; X = Br) and [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2; X = I) in rather low yields, governed by the percentage of impurities in the solvent. It was evident that the chlorido ligand is produced from the neutralization of HCl by mebta, the former arising either directly from the slow photochemical autoxidation of CHCl3 or indirectly from the hydrolysis of phosgene, the other product of the oxidative decomposition of the solvent. The mechanistic proposal involves an X-bridged [In2X6] dimer (X = Br, I) as the key intermediate, followed by the preferential chloride attack at one of its electrophilic InIII centers. This mechanistic proposal is fully supported by theoretical studies in CHCl3 in the case of X = I (complex 2). Use of noncontaminated (“fresh”) solvent has led to the isolation of complexes [InBr3(mebta)2(H2O)]·H2O (3·H2O) when the starting material is InBr3 and [InI3(mebta)2] (4) when the reactant is InI3. Complexes 2 and 3·H2O are mononuclear octahedral. The trans donor atom pairs in 2 are Oaqua/Nmebta, Cl/Oaqua, and Cl/Cl, while they are Nmebta/Nmebta, Oaqua/Br, and Br/Br in 3·H2O. The molecular structure of 1 is similar to that of 2, the main difference being the occupancy of the three halido sites by both Cl– and Br– ligands; thus, the former is a solid solution. In 4, the InIII center is surrounded by three terminal iodido groups and two monodentate mebta ligands. The five donor atoms define an almost perfect trigonal bipyramid, in which the three iodido groups are in the equatorial position. In all the complexes, the mebta ligand is planar and monodentate, with the donor atom being the nitrogen of the position 3 of the azole ring. The crystal structures of the complexes are built with a variety of H-bonding and π–π stacking interactions. The solid-state IR and Raman spectra of the complexes are discussed in terms of their structural features. An attempt has been made to probe the solution behavior of the compounds using 1H and 13C NMR spectra, combined with molar conductivity data.

Introduction

The reaction solvent is an important “ingredient” in coordination chemistry. Sometimes, the same reaction in different solvents gives different metal complexes. One property that determines the utility of a solvent is its acceptor and donor (Lewis acid–base) characteristics. When solvents are good donors, they may participate as ligands in the resulting complexes. The coordination ability of some common solvents toward metal ions follows the order DMSO > DMF ≈ H2O > ROH > Me2CO > MeCN > MeNO2 > PhNO2≫CH2Cl2≈CHCl3. When solvents behave as acceptors, the positive ends of the solvent molecule dipoles will orient themselves toward the anions. Concerning their protonic acidity or basicity, and the nature and extent of autoionization, the solvents can be categorized as protic, e.g., H2O, liquid HF, and liquid NH3, which undergo autodissociation, and aprotic solvents. The latter can be divided into (i) nonpolar nondissociated liquids, e.g., CCl4 and hydrocarbons, which do not solvate strongly; (ii) nonionized but strongly solvating (most often polar) solvents, e.g., DMSO, THF, DMF, and MeCN; and (iii) polar autoionizing solvents, e.g., BrF3, IF5, and Cl3PO. CHCl3 is the solvent used in the present study; it is a nonpolar (dielectric constant ∼4.8), almost nondonor, and weakly protic solvent (some organic chemistry books classify it as aprotic) with negligible autodissociation. The rather inherent stability of CHCl3 makes it a common solvent in organic chemistry reactions; , its deuterated analogue CDCl3 is one of the most common solvents in NMR spectroscopy. It is also used in coordination chemistry when the metal ion reactant has a strongly covalent character.

Another important factor concerning organic solvents in coordination chemistry is the purity. It is obvious that a solvent should be pure if reproducible, and interpretable data are to be obtained; however, this is not always the case and subtle forms of contamination can occur. The main contaminants come from H2O and/or O2. H2O dissolves readily in solvents exposed to the atmosphere or to glass vessels that have not been baked dry. Even small quantities of H2O on a w/w % basis can influence a reaction. For example, MeCN, which contains only 0.1% w/w H2O, is ∼0.04 M in H2O, so that the behavior of 0.1 M solutions can be dramatically influenced by the “trace” of water. Concerning O2, it is slightly soluble in almost all organic solvents, and saturated solutions are derived from brief exposure to the normal laboratory atmosphere, e.g., when pouring. Oxygen can be partially removed by bubbling nitrogen through the liquid (a common process in coordination chemistry), but only repeated freezing and pumping on a vacuum line or use of a glovebox (uncommon in classical coordination chemistry, but a standard practice in organometallic chemistry) can completely remove it. Certain organic solvents react with oxygen upon long exposure forming undesirable species, e.g., peroxides in the case of ethers, where purification by distillation from reductants (often hydrides) or by passing through molecular sieves is necessary. Contamination from heating or photochemical decomposition (normally in the presence of oxygen) is another source of impurities in organic solvents, which might lead a reaction to different pathways. CHCl3, the solvent of the present work, suffers from the above-mentioned contamination sources, either directly or indirectly. Exposure to heat or photochemical decomposition, eq , in the presence of atmospheric O2, causes the formation of phosgene. , The latter can further react with H2O, eq , to produce HCl 1 2CHCl3+O2→hvorT2COCl2+2HCl 2 COCl2+H2O→CO2+2HCl

The present study demonstrates the influence of the purity of CHCl3 on the products obtained from the general InX3/mebta reaction system in this solvent, where X = Br, I, and mebta is 1-methylbenzotriazole (Scheme ). The stimulus of this work was a variety of reactions of copper­(II) starting materials and 2-acetylpyridine in CHCl3, where the products contained a chlorido ligand, not present in the starting materials; the only possible source of chlorides is the solvent used in the reactions. In those reactions, treatment of Cu­(ClO4)2·6H2O with (py)­(me)­CO and NBu n OMe (1:1:1) in CHCl3 at room temperature under aerobic conditions gave complexes [CuII 2Cl2(HLA)2]­(ClO4)2 and [Cu2Cl2(LB)2(ClO4)2]; (py)­(me)­CO is 2-acetylpyridine, and HLA and LB are the neutral ligand 3-hydroxy-1,3-di­(pyridin-2-yl)-butane-1-one and the zwitterionic-type molecule 3-hydroxy-1-methyl-3-(pyridin-2-yl)-3H-indolizin-4-ium, respectively, produced by copper­(II) ion transformations of (py)­(me)­CO. The two terminal chlorido ligands in the structures derive from CHCl3 under the strongly basic conditions used, through the intermediate formation of dichlorocarbene (:CCl2). Since all our previous reactions had been carried out in strongly basic conditions, we performed ab initio and DFT studies of the complete hydrolysis mechanisms of CHCl3; it was concluded that the most important mechanism for CHCl3 basic hydrolysis is the radical one producing :CCl2. Thus, we decided to perform all the reactions of InX3 (X = Br, I) and mebta in the absence of a strong external base.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating that the purity of a reaction solvent can dramatically influence the identity of coordination compounds formed from a given reaction system, an aspect that has surprisingly received little attention in synthetic coordination chemistry.

This work can be considered as continuation of our interest in indium chemistry , and in the coordination chemistry of benzotriazoles. In was an inconspicuous metal before the Second World War, but it is now a component of high-technology devices and instruments; this may deplete its worldwide resources. Nowadays, In­(III) is involved in various aspects of materials, − MOF, , medicinal, , supramolecular and synthetic inorganic chemistry, , catalysis (both homogeneous , and heterogeneous , ), and solid-state NMR spectroscopy. , 1-Methylbenzotriazole (mebta) is the archetype of N-substituted benzotriazoles with nondonor substituents. At first glance, the answer to the question “is mebta a boring ligand?” is “yes”. With one exception, this molecule behaves as a monodentate ligand, with the N atom of position 3 (Scheme ) being the donor atom. However, almost all complexes of mebta have different chemistry (composition, structures, reactivity, properties) compared with those of other typical aromatic ligands with one N donor atoms, even if the counterions (ClO4 –, BF4 –, PF6 –, CF3SO3 –,···) or ancillary inorganic coordinated groups (Cl–, Br–, I–, NO3 –, N3 –, SO4 2–,···) are the same. The mebta complexes are also different from most complexes of the closely related 1-methyl-1,2,3-triazole. Thus, the answer to the above question is “no”.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

The starting materials were reagent grade, purchased from standard suppliers and used as received. All manipulations were performed in the normal laboratory atmosphere. The reaction time was 2 h under reflux, unless otherwise stated. In the below mentioned procedures and discussions, we wish to make clear the following: the term “old chloroform” (or “old batch”) denotes CHCl3 taken from an already used (several times) bottle, which has been stored without any precaution, e.g., under the usual laboratory light. According to the IUPAC-NIST Solubility Database, this CHCl3 contains 0.076% w/w H2O at 20 °C when the bottle is opened for the first time, i.e., it is ∼0.06 M in H2O (the density of CHCl3 is ∼1.48 g/mL at 25 °C). We have not quantitatively analyzed the impurities (COCl2, HCl) present in the particular bottles of “old” CHCl3 used in the present work. Thus, it is clear that CHCl3, which has been opened multiple times, or which has been exposed to different climatic conditions (temperature, humidity, lighting), would develop a different impurity profile. This lack of control could make it potentially problematic to reproduce exactly the experiments that led to complexes 1 and 2 (vide infra) in other laboratories. We did not face reproducibility problems in all our experiments. The term “fresh chloroform” (or “fresh batch”) denotes CHCl3 taken from a newly opened bottle, washed twice with a 5% w/v aqueous NaHCO3 solution and copious amounts of H2O (to secure removal of COCl2 and HCl), and stored in a cool dry and dark area; batches of this chloroform were used within 3–4 d after their storage. Safety note: 1-methylbenzotriazole is potentially explosive, and caution is needed in handling this material. The use of a plastic spatula is recommended. We did not have any problems with the small quantities used in this work.

Instrumentation

Microanalyses for C, H, and N were performed by the instrumental analysis service of the University of Patras using a PerkinElmer 2400 analyzer. Conductivity measurements were carried out at 25 ± 1 °C with a Metrohm-Herisau E–527 bridge and a cell of standard value; the concentrations of the solutions were ∼10–3 M. FT-IR spectra (4000–400 cm–1) were recorded using a PerkinElmer 16PC spectrometer with samples prepared as KBr pellets. Raman spectra were obtained using a T-64000 Jobin Yvon-Horiba micro-Raman setup (Kyoto, Japan), employed in a single-spectrograph configuration. The excitation wavelengths were 632.8 or 514.5 nm. The former was emitted from a He–Ne laser (Optronics Technologies SA, model HLA-20P, 20 mW). The laser power on the samples was 1 mW. The backscattered radiation was collected from the monochromator after passing through a suitable edge filter (LP02–633RU-25, Laser2000 Ltd., Cambridgeshire, UK). The latter (514.5 nm) was emitted from a DPSS laser (Cobolt Fandango TMISO Laser). The collected backscattered radiation was directed to the monochromator (single configuration) and a Spectraview-2DTM liquid nitrogen-cooled detector. The laser was focused on the sample using a 50× microscope objective with a power of 2 mW on the sample. The calibration of the instrument was made through the Raman peak position of Si at 520.5 cm–1; the resolution of the spectra was 3 ± 1 cm–1. 1H and 13C room-temperature NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance DPX spectrometer at resonance frequencies of 600.13 and 150.62 MHz, respectively. The solvent used was DMSO-d 6, and the signals of its nondeuterated percentage were used as reference. The 13C­[1H] NMR spectrum of the free ligand was recorded in a solvent mixture comprising DMSO-d 6 and deuterated dioxane.

X-ray Crystallography

Colorless single crystals of 1 (0.34 × 0.38 × 0.47 mm), 2 (0.17 × 0.24 × 0.27 mm), 3·H2O (0.08 × 0.28 × 0.36 mm), and 4 (0.27 × 0.27 × 0.43 mm) were taken from the mother liquor and immediately cooled to −93 °C (1 and 3·H2O) and to −113 °C (2 and 4). Diffraction data were collected on a Rigaku R-AXIS SPIDER Image Plate diffractometer using graphite-monochromated Mo Kα radiation. Data collection (ω-scans) and processing (cell refinement, data correction, and empirical absorption correction) were performed using the CrystalClear program package. Important crystallographic data are listed in Table S1 of the Supporting Information; further information in textual form is also provided. The structures were solved by direct methods using SHELXS, ver. 2013/1 and refined by full-matrix least-squares techniques on F 2 with SHELXL, ver. 2014/6. The H atoms were either located by difference maps and were refined isotropically or were introduced at calculated positions as riding on their respective bonded atoms. All non-H atoms were refined anisotropically. For complex 1, it was not possible to obtain an acceptable refinement by assuming that the halogenido sites were occupied exclusively by either Cl or Br. Therefore, we examined the possibility that these sites are occupied by both atoms. This hypothesis produced very good refinement results, and the product is thus considered as a solid solution of Cl– and Br– donor atoms. During the refinement, the EADP and EXYZ instructions were applied. The refined occupancy values are [0.430(3)/0.570(3)], [0.331(2)/0.669(2)], and [0.157(2)/0.843(2)] for the sites labeled Br1/Cl1, Br2/Cl2, and Br3/Cl3, respectively (vide infra). Structural plots were drawn using the Diamond 3 program package.

Computational Details

Geometry optimizations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 software package (revision C.01). The B3LYP functional with D3BJ dispersion corrections was employed, in combination with the all-electron triple-ζ basis set with a polarization function (def2-TZVP) for all atoms. , Analytical frequency calculations were performed at the same level of theory to confirm the nature of each stationary point, identified by the number of imaginary frequencies: zero for local minima and one for transition states. Solvent effects were modeled using the PCM implicit solvation model, , with geometries optimized in the corresponding solvent environment. All reported energies are given in kcal mol–1, and bond lengths are in angstroms (Å).

Results and Discussion

Synthetic Comments in Brief

For reasons outlined in the Introduction, we were interested in investigating the general InX3 (X = Br, I)/mebta reaction system in CHCl3 in the absence of an external base. The 1:1 and 1:2 reactions of InBr3 and mebta in refluxing “old” CHCl3 (for the meaning of “old”, see “Chemicals” in the Experimental Section) gave a pale orange solution from which almost colorless crystals were isolated. Single-crystal X-ray crystallography revealed the formula [InCl2.1Br0.9(mebta)­(H2O)2] (1); the yield was low to moderate (ca. 35%). It was obvious that the halogenido sites are occupied by both anions, and that the chlorido source was the solvent. Analogous reactions performed with InI3 afforded complex [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2), again in rather low yield (ca. 30%), indicating complete substitution of I– by Cl–. Longer reaction times (6 h) under reflux gave again the same products (1 and 2) in comparable yields. The situation changed when “fresh” CHCl3 (for the meaning of “fresh”, see “Chemicals” in the Experimental Section) was used as the reaction solvent. The 1:1 and 1:2 reactions between InBr3 or InI3 and mebta under reflux in the dark for 2 h gave complexes [InBr3(mebta)2(H2O)]·H2O (3·H2O) and [InI3(mebta)2] (4), respectively, in good yield. As expected, no substitution of bromides or iodides by chlorido ligands was observed. The H2O molecules in 3 presumably arise from the treatment of CHCl3 with aqueous NaHCO3 and water (vide supra).

For a better interpretation of the results (vide infra), one further experimentnot mentioned in the Experimental Sectionwas performed. Addition of drops of hydrochloric acid (37% w/w) in the reaction mixtures that led to 3 and 4, before the reflux, gave crystals of [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2) in variable yields, as confirmed by unit cell determination. The same product was isolated using an “old batch” of CHCl3 washed with aqueous NaHCO3 and H2O, further supporting the interpretation of the reaction outcome.

Mechanistic Proposals

The 1:1 and 1:2 Reactions in “Old” Chloroform Batches

The 1:1 reaction of InI3 with mebta in “old” CHCl3 under reflux gives complex [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2); Scheme . This indicates that the three iodido ligands have been replaced by an equal number of chlorido ligands and, in addition, the metal ion has acquired two aqua molecules and one monodentate mebta ligand. The chlorido ligands have obviously originated from CHCl3, whereas the two H2O ligands come from the water present in the solvent. Simple calculations show that the ∼0.08% w/w of H2O in CHCl3 (vide supra) is more than required for the experimental ∼30% yield.

It is known that CHCl3 acts as a source of Cl– ions upon hydrolysis using either hydroxide solutions or alkoxides, e.g., NaOMe or KO t Bu. In both cases, the active reaction intermediate is the dichlorocarbene, :CCl2, which is formed by the abstraction of the relative acidic H of CHCl3 (pK a = 15.6) by the strong base, e.g., the hydroxide ion (pK a = 15.74 for its conjugate acid H2O), following by loss of the leaving group chloride ion.

However, in the present reaction system, the dichlorocarbene mechanism is not applicable as the possible bases present in the reaction are very weak; these are the iodide (in the case of 2, pK a = −10 for HI), H2O (pK a = −1.74 for H3O+) present in the solvent, and mebta (pK a = 1.4 for mebtaH+). , On the other hand, H2O alone cannot hydrolyze CHCl3 even at its refluxing point, the temperature employed in the present reactions. Therefore, the only possible reaction for the formation of Cl– ions from the solvent is the COCl2 and HCl present in CHCl3 arising from the UV light-mediated oxidation of the solvent, ,− eq , especially in cases where old batches of solvent are used for the reaction. Apart from this HCl, COCl2 will also provide more HCl (and CO2), eq , through hydrolysis. Finally, HCl will be neutralized by the weakly basic mebta present in the solution liberating Cl– ions.

It has been reported that the catalytic activity of InCl3 in various organic reactions is attributed , to the species [InCl2]+. Our theoretical calculations (not included in this work) for the dissociation of 2 InI3 to separate [I2In]+ and [InI4]− in CHCl3 (the reaction solvent), however, showed that this process is energetically unfavorable, obviously due to the low solvating ability of CHCl3 compared to MeCN or CH2ClCH2Cl employed in the reactions where [InCl2]+ was implicated as the catalytic species. , Accordingly, we considered the doubly iodido-bridged [In2I6] dimer to be the relevant reactive species. This dimer possesses two equivalent Lewis-acidic InIII centers, either of which may undergo nucleophilic attack by the species present in solution, namely, Cl–, mebta, and H2O, whose nucleophilicity follows the order Cl– > mebta > H2O. − This assumption is further corroborated by additional natural bond orbital population analysis (NBO, vide infra). Thus, in the X = I case, the conversion of two InI3 units to the neutral doubly iodido-bridged dimer [(InI2)2(μ-I)2] was shown to be energetically favorable (vide infra) and, therefore, we propose that this dimer is the actual species through which replacement of iodido ligands by the chlorido ones takes place. Accordingly, one of the two equivalent electropositive metals of the doubly bridged dimer is preferentially attacked by Cl–, giving the first intermediate, namely, the negatively charged mixed-halide, singly iodido-bridged dimer I (Scheme ). From this intermediate, a terminal iodido ligand is eliminated, being a better leaving group than the Cl– ion, with the creation of the neutral mixed-halide, doubly iodido-bridged dimer [(ClInI)­(InI2)­(μ-I)2]. The latter is then attacked by another Cl– ion on the more electropositive metal bearing the electron-withdrawing chlorido ligand, leading to the negatively charged mixed-halide, singly iodido-bridged dimer II, which is similarly converted to the neutral mixed-halide, doubly iodido-bridged [(Cl2In)­(InI2)­(μ-I)2] dimer by giving up an I– ion. The latter dimer is then attacked by another Cl– ion on the metal already bearing two electron-withdrawing chlorido ligands, giving the negatively charged mixed-halide, singly iodido-bridged dimer III, which finally dissociates to Cl3In and [InI4]−. Cl3In is subsequently attacked by mebta giving the 4-coordinate InIII in the precursor IV, which finally picks up two H2O molecules from the solvent to produce the final product [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2).

Simplified Proposed Mechanism for the Formation of Complexes [In­(Cl/Br)3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (1) and [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2)

The driving force of the reactions is believed to be the replacement of the weaker covalent In–I and In–Br bonds by the stronger ionic In–Cl bonds, and this may also be due to the insolubility of these complexes in CHCl3 compared, for example, to the alternative anticipated complexes [InBr3(mebta)­(H2O)2] and [InI3(mebta)­(H2O) x ] (x = 1 or 2).

The incomplete replacement of the bromido ligands in this case was suspected to be due to the slower elimination of the third Br– from the corresponding InCl2Br intermediate, because Br– is not as good as leaving group as I– and the presence of increased numbers of the strongly electron-withdrawing chlorido ligands on the metal ion holds back bromido elimination. Contrary to our expectations (for complete replacement or diminished percentage of the bromido ligands), extending the reaction time from 2 to 6 h resulted again in complex [InCl2.1Br0.9(mebta)­(H2O)2] (1). Another explanation might be that the In–Br bond is not as ionic (strong) as the corresponding In–Cl bond, but more ionic than the In–I bond.

It should be noted that the present reaction bears a resemblance to the classical Finkelstein reaction, , in which the reaction of sodium iodide with alkyl chlorides or bromides in Me2CO (NaI is soluble in acetone) provides alkyl iodides, through an SN2 reaction, and the NaCl or NaBr salt, insoluble in Me2CO, which precipitates out of the reaction mixture, and drives the reaction to completion. Similar to the Finkelstein is the Swarts reaction, , whereby heavy metal fluorides (e.g., AgF, Hg2F2, CoF2) react with alkyl chlorides or bromides in Me2CO, also through an SN2 reaction, to provide alkyl fluorides and the corresponding insoluble chloride or bromide salts of the metal ions.

The same products (i.e., 1 and 2) were also isolated by using the 1:2 InBr3 or InI3/mebta reaction ratios, with similar or slightly higher yields compared to the reactions with the 1:1 ratios; this indicates that the yield depends primarily on the availability of Cl– ions in solution.

Obviously, since there are no Cl– ions in the reaction solutions, the next more potent nucleophile present, apart from H2O, is mebta. The proposed mechanism for the formation of 3·H2O and 4 is depicted in Scheme . The first step is again the conversion of 2 InX3 to the neutral halide, doubly halido-bridged dimer [(InX2)2(μ-Χ)2]. One of the two equivalent electropositive metals is then attacked by mebta giving the intermediate V (the numbering is a continuation from Scheme ). The intermediate V is further attacked by a second mebta molecule to provide the new intermediate VI, which is further transformed to the 5-coordinate complex [InX3(mebta)2)] by acquiring a halide (I– or Br–) from the [InX4]− counterion. This difference with the previously described reactions might be attributed to the fact that the flat, 3-coordinate, intermediate V is easily approachable by a second mebta molecule from the top or the bottom. This approach is further facilitated by the longer In–I/In–Br bonds compared to the corresponding In–Cl bonds. In the case of intermediates such as IV (Scheme ), this is not possible obviously for steric reasons. In the latter cases (complexes 1 and 2) instead, the two small H2O molecules are added, converting the 4-coordinate InIII to 6-coordinate. We believe that for steric reasons (bulky iodido ligands) and/or the fact that the coordination sphere of [InI3(mebta)2] (4) is very lipophilic, a H2O molecule cannot be added to convert the 5-coordinate iodido complex to a 6-coordinate one. On the other hand, [InI3(mebta)2] is insoluble in CHCl3 and crystallizes out of the reaction mixture, thus driving the equilibrium to completion.

Complex 2 crystallizes in the centrosymmetric triclinic space group P. Its structure consists of two crystallographically independent, but structurally similar, mononuclear molecules [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2]. The two independent molecules interact through O3–H2­(O3)···Cl2 and O2–H2­(O2)···Cl4 H bonds. Their dimensions are O3···Cl2 = 3.076(2) Å, H2­(O3)···Cl2 = 2.34(4) Å, O3–H2­(O3)···Cl2 = 157(4)° and O2···Cl4 = 3.090(2) Å, H2­(O2)···Cl4 = 2.33(4) Å, O2–H2­(O2)···Cl4 = 156(4)°. In the molecules, the InIII atoms are 6-coordinate. The ligands are three terminal chlorido ions, two H2O molecules, and one planar monodentate mebta molecule; in the latter, the donor atom is (as almost always) the N atom of the position 3 of the azole ring (Scheme ). The metal coordination geometry is slightly distorted octahedral. The trans atom pairs are Oaqua/Nmebta, Cl/Oaqua, and Cl/Cl, the trans angles being in the range 167.3(1)–177.8(1)°; thus, the three chlorido donor atoms occupy cis and trans (or mer) positions in the octahedron. The In–Cl, ,,,− In–Oaqua, , and In–Naromatic ,,− bond lengths are typical for octahedral In­(III) complexes. The N1–N2, N2–N3, N4–N5, and N5–N6 bond lengths are 1.329(3), 1.315(3), 1.326(3), and 1.314(3) Å, respectively. In the structure of free mebta, the N1–N2 and N2–N3 bond distances (Scheme ) are 1.357(2) Å and 1.307(2) Å, respectively; thus, N2–N3 exhibits a higher double-bond character. As expected, the nine atoms of the fused rings in mebta and 2 are virtually planar, thus confirming pronounced aromaticity in the ligand system. In agreement with this, the Me group bonded to N1 is twisted out of the reference plane by only 6.9° in the free ligand, while the corresponding angles in 2 are 1.74° for the N1-containing mebta and 1.07° for the N4-containing mebta. Due to the resonance stabilization and coordination, the two N–N bond distances in each [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] molecule of 2 are similar (see the caption of Figure ) and in the range between the typical values expected for single and double nitrogen–nitrogen bonds.

Complex 1 crystallizes in the centrosymmetric triclinic space group P 1̅. Its molecular structure is strikingly similar to that of 2, the only differences being the presence of one crystallographically independent octahedral molecule and the occupancy of the three halido sites by both chlorido and bromido atoms in the former. As expected, the In–Cl/Br bond lengths in 1 [2.460(1)–2.526(1) Å] are longer compared with the In–Cl bond distances in 2 [2.424(1)–2.451(1) Å] and shorter than the pure In–Br ones in 3·H2O [2.567(1)–2.579(1) Å, vide infra].

Complex 3·H2O crystallizes in the noncentrosymmetric monoclinic space group P21. Its structure consists of mononuclear complex [InBr3(mebta)2(H2O)] molecules and lattice H2O molecules in a 1:1 ratio; the latter will be further discussed in the Supporting Information in the context of the supramolecular features of the structures. The InIII center is 6-coordinate, and it is surrounded by three terminal bromido ions, two monodentate planar mebta ligands, and one coordinated H2O molecule. The metal coordination polyhedron is a distorted octahedron, with the trans pairs being N3/N6,O1/Br2 and Br1/Br3; thus, the bromido donor atoms occupy mer positions in the octahedron. In the N(4)-containing mebta ligand, the N4–N5 [1.348(11) Å] and N5–N6 [1.297(11) Å] lengths suggest a slightly higher double-bond character for the latter bond than in the former; on the contrary, the N1–N2 and N2–N3 bond lengths in the other mebta ligand are equal, suggesting a slightly higher aromaticity character. In accordance with this, the Me group bonded to N1 is twisted out of the plane defined by the six C and the three N atoms by 2.6°, whereas the group bonded to N4 by 3.7°. The In–N bonds in 3·H2O are slightly weaker (by ca. 0.07 Å) than those in the two complexes that contain one mebta ligand, i.e., 1 and 2. The In–Br bond distances are typical for octahedral In­(III) complexes containing terminal bromido ligands. ,

The Br1–In1–Br3 bond angle deviates from linearity with a value of 167.9(1)°, suggesting a distortion of the octahedral geometry. Based on Figure S5 (bottom), the closer Br atom to Br3 are the Br2 atoms from neighboring complexes. The Br3···Br2′ = Br3′′···Br2 distance is 3.691(2) Å [symmetry codes: (′) −1 + x, y, z; (′′) 1 + x, y, z], slightly shorter than the sum of the VdW radii of Br atoms (3.72 Å). The geometric characteristics and parameters of the In1–Br3···Br2′-In1′ unit resemble those of the T2 configuration as defined by Scheiner, with the angles In1–Br3···Br2′ and Br3···Br2′-In1′ being 102.9(1) and 169.5(1)°, respectively. Thus, the combined effects of this intermolecular halogen–halogen interaction and the O1–H2­(O1)···Br2 (−1 + x, y, z) H bond [see Table S2 and Figure S5] most probably cause deviation of the Br1–In1–Br3 angle from linearity.

Complex 4 crystallizes in the centrosymmetric monoclinic space group C2/c. The crystal structure consists of molecules [InI3(mebta)2]. The molecule possesses a crystallographically imposed 2-fold axis of symmetry passing through In1 and I2; thus, the asymmetric unit contains half-molecule. The InIII center is surrounded by three terminal iodido ligands and two nitrogen atoms from two planar monodentate mebta molecules; again, the latter use the nitrogen of the position 3 of the azole ring as the donor atoms. The five donor atoms occupy the apexes of a trigonal bipyramid around the metal ion, with the three iodido ligands in the equatorial plane and the two mebta nitrogens at the axial positions. Analysis of the shape-determining angles using the approach of Reedijk, Addison, and co-workers gives a value of 0.90 for the trigonality index τ (0 and 1 for perfect square pyramidal and trigonal bipyramidal geometries, respectively). Thus, the geometry is slightly distorted, the distortions from the idealized D 3h symmetry appearing both in the three I–In–I angles (118.2–123.5°) and in the nonlinearity of the N–In–N moiety (177.6°). The In–N bond distance in 4 [2.400(2) Å] is longer than the distances in 1, 2, and 3·H2O [2.254(2)-2.331(8) Å], despite the lower coordination number of InIII in the former. The In–I bond lengths (ca. 2.71 Å) compare well with those reported for five-coordinate iodido In­(III) complexes. ,,, The closest to 4 mononuclear trigonal bipyramidal [InI3L2] complex with three equatorial iodido ligands is [InI3(flu)2], where flu is the O-donor fluorenone. The {In­(Iequatorial)3 (trans-N)2} trigonal bipyramidal coordination sphere is present in {[InI3(tpt)} n , but 2,4,6-tri­(4-pyridyl)-1,3,5-triazine (tpt) serves as a bidentate bridging ligand generating a 1D coordination polymer.

Two types of supramolecular forces (H bonds and π–π stacking interactions) create esthetically beautiful crystal structures. Full details are given in the Supporting Information (Figures S1–S8, Table S2).

With the exception of the adduct BH3(mebta), no other complexes of mebta with the group 13 metal ions had been reported prior to this work. However, a handful of organometallic and coordination complexes of B­(III), Ga­(III), and Tl­(I) with the neutral or deprotonated benzotriazole (benzotriazole, btaH, is the parent molecule, i.e., with a H atom instead of the methyl group on N1; see Scheme S1) have been structurally characterized. − In the complexes containing the neutral benzotriazole, ,, the donor atom is always the nitrogen of the position 3 of the azole ring (i.e., N3). The structure of an indium­(III)-neutral benzotriazole complex has been deposited in CCDC (but not published yet); it has the composition [InCl3(btaH)2(H2O)]·btaH·H2O, with the chlorido ligands in mer positions of the octahedral coordination sphere.

In order to support some of the mechanistic proposals mentioned earlier and delineate the reaction mechanism for the formation of the {InCl3(mebta)} species (the precursor of 2), we performed DFT electronic structure calculations. The actual species corresponding to the notations [InI2]+[InI4]−, [ClInI2]­[InI4]−, [Cl2InI]­[InI4]−, and [Cl3In]­[InI4]− are [(InI2)2(μ-I)2], [(ClInI2)­(InI3)­(μ-I)]−, [(Cl2InI)­(InI3)­(μ-I)]−, and [(Cl3In)­(InI3)­(μ-I)]−, respectively.

The calculated pathway (Figure ) begins from the neutral doubly iodido-bridged [(InI2)2(μ–I)2] dimer, whose optimized C 2v-symmetric structure contains two chemically equivalent indium centers joined by two bridging iodido groups. The bridging In–I distances are longer than the corresponding terminal ones, consistent with the weaker bridging interactions. Additional NBO population analysis yielded identical natural population analysis (NPA) charges for the two indium centers (+0.703 e), the two bridging iodido groups (−0.138 e), and the symmetry-equivalent terminal iodido ligands (−0.281 to −0.284 e). When one-half of the charge of each bridging iodide is assigned to each indium-containing moiety, the two symmetry-equivalent halves are electronically neutral. These results strongly support description of the parent species as a neutral doubly iodido-bridged dimer rather than as a localized [InI2]+[InI4]− pair. The latter notation is used in Figures and only as a formal mechanistic fragmentation. Chloride attack at either of the two equivalent indium centers breaks the C 2v symmetry and affords the asymmetric anionic intermediate [(ClInI2)­(InI3)­(μ-I)]−, formally representable as [ClInI2]­[InI4]−. This intermediate is stabilized by more than 43 kcal mol–1 relative to the reactants. Its calculated dissociation energy into [InI2Cl] and [InI4]− is approximately 21 kcal mol–1, indicating that the two indium-containing moieties remain strongly associated in CHCl3. The NPA charges also show that chloride coordination induces a clear electronic differentiation if the two initially equivalent halves of the dimer. Successive chloride-for-iodide substitution at the same indium center produces [(Cl2InI)­(InI3)­(μ-I)]− and subsequently [(Cl3In)­(InI3)­(μ-Ι)]−. These transformations are accompanied by progressive structural and electronic asymmetry, including elongation of the remaining In–I interactions associated with the chlorido-substituted center. Both intermediates are further stabilized relative to the starting system, and their calculated dissociation energies are 23–24 kcal mol–1, supporting their persistence as associated mixed-halide species in CHCl3. The NPA results reveal progressively increasing polarization between the chlorido-rich and iodido-rich parts of the structures along this sequence. Dissociation of [(Cl3In)­(InI3)­(μ-Ι)]− releases Cl3In and [InI4]−, with a calculated free energy barrier of only approximately 12 kcal mol–1. Subsequent coordination of mebta to Cl3In affords the {InCl3(mebta)} precursor of complex 2. The complete calculated process is strongly favorable, with ΔG = −76.3 kcal mol–1, and it is also exothermic with ΔH approximately −32 kcal mol–1. Thus, the calculations support a mechanism involving the chloride attack of an iodido-bridged dimer followed by sequential chloride-for-iodide substitution and formation of InCl3, without requiring a pre-existing localized [InI2]+[InI4]− pair. A combined quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) and reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis was carried out to characterize the bridging In–I interactions in the neutral iodido-bridged [(InI2)2(μ-Ι)2] dimer in CHCl3. Within the frameworks of QTAIM, a bond critical point (BCP) is a (3, −1) stationary point of the electron-density field located along a bond path connecting two atomic basins. The electron density and the associated topological and energy-density descriptors evaluated at the BCP provide information on the existence and nature of the corresponding interaction. Figure a shows the BCPs located along the four In–I bond paths involving the two bridging iodido ligands. No direct metal–metal bond path or significant red steric repulsion is observed between the two InIII atoms, demonstrating that the interaction is mediated exclusively through the iodido bridges. Taken together, these results show that the {In2I2} unit is best described as a cyclic, through-bridge connectivity motif in which the iodido ligands provide both electrostatic stabilization and partial covalent character, enabling electronic communication between the InIII centers without formation of a direct indium­(III)–indium­(III) bond. Replacing I– with Cl– groups is not expected to affect significantly the nature of the In–I bridging interactions and this is substantiated by the similar values of the energetic and topological parameters calculated for the chlorido species (Table ).

Concluding Comments and Perspectives

The general chemical message of this work is that the purity of a solvent is a crucial synthetic factor in coordination chemistry and may lead reactions to proceed with different pathways. In the present case, the quality of CHCl3 affects the identity of products from the InX3/mebta reaction system under refluxing conditions (X = Br, I). Using “fresh” CHCl3, complexes [InBr3(mebta)2(H2O)]·H2O (3·H2O) and [InI3(mebta)2] (4) were isolated in good yields. Employment of “old” CHCl3, which contains COCl2, HCl, and H2O, gave products [InCl2.1Br0.9(mebta)­(H2O)2] (1) and [InCl3(mebta)­(H2O)2] (2) in rather low yields, governed by the concentration of the impurities in the solvent; the chlorido ligand is produced from the neutralization of HCl by mebta, the former arising either directly from the well-known slow photochemical autoxidation of CHCl3 or indirectly from the hydrolysis of the other product (COCl2) of the oxidative decomposition of the solvent, eqs and (vide supra). However, we want to emphasize that a targeted substitution of bromido or iodido groups under the conditions described in this work is not possible. A full mechanistic scheme has been proposed for the formation of 1 and 2, i.e., for the complete replacement of iodido ligands by chlorido ones (in 2) and the partial replacement of bromido ligands by chlorido ones (in 1). The proposal involves formation of the neutral halide doubly bridged dimer [(InX2)2(μ-X)2] (X = Br, I) and preferential chloride attack at one of its electrophilic InIII centers. This mechanistic proposal is fully supported in the case of 2 by our theoretical studies in CHCl3. The four complexes described herein have interesting molecular and supramolecular structures. They are valuable additions in the areas of the indium­(III) halide chemistry and the coordination chemistry of mebta, being the first reported In­(III) complexes with benzotriazole ligation.

We believe that this particular research topic has more interesting chemistry to give. With the results obtained in this and our previous work, our current attempts are directed, among others, toward (1) the systematic use of “old” and “fresh” CHCl3 as solvent in reactions of transition metal (except CuII 7), p-metal (except InIII), and lanthanoid­(III) ions with various known terminal and bridging ligands; (2) employment of CHBr3 and melted CHI3 as solvents in analogous reactions; and (3) replacement of CHCl3 with other chlorinated organic solvents (both “old” and “fresh” batches) in coordination chemistry. The ultimate goal of our efforts is to isolate metal complexes with new chemical compositions (difficult to prepare under more direct conditions), and interesting structures and properties.
