Comparison of blood parameters of ring-tailed lemurs (Lemur catta) and ruffed lemurs (Varecia variegata) under human care
Authors: Fausto Quintavalla, Stefano Biffani, Emma Della Pietra, Mireia Mainez, Daniel Marí-Martorell, Rocio Canales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical monitoring is essential for disease prevention, both in an individual animal and even more in a captive animal population, where the susceptibility of different animal species in the same environment can lead to the onset of various pathologies. Periodic clinical monitoring must necessarily include an assessment of clinical status through a comprehensive physical examination, including body weight assessment and laboratory tests including fecal, urinary, and blood tests. The aim of this study was to monitor the health of a group of 45 lemurs (Lemur catta and Varecia variegata) in a zoo, focusing in particular on hematological and biochemical-clinical parameters obtained from blood samples collected annually over a three-year period. The hematological parameters measured were erythrocytes, packed cell volume, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, red blood cell distribution width, leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, eosinophils, monocytes, basophils, platelets, and mean platelet volume. The biochemical parameters measured were alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, glucose, amylase, lipase, cholesterol, albumin, globulin, A/G ratio, calcium, inorganic phosphate, total protein, creatinine, and urea.ResultsObserved parameters highlighted some differences between the two species over time and in relation to age. For example, red blood cell count consistently showed higher values ​​in V. variegata compared to L. catta. In Lemur catta, an increase in erythrocyte parameters, red blood cell distribution width and lipase was observed, compared to a decrease in mean corpuscular hemoglobin and bilirubin. Our results reveal a variation in hematological parameters in conjunction with the second blood sample with a greater increase in parameters in Varecia variegata than in Lemur catta.ConclusionsResults from this study partially disagree with those obtained in other lemur species kept in captivity and elsewhere. This would suggest that the management conditions, over three years, of the ring-tailed lemur and the variegated ruffed lemur at the zoo under study are particularly favorable, respecting the welfare of these animals.

Introduction

Madagascar lemurs are the most endangered primates in the world. A major decline is expected within the next seven decades, unless adequate conservation programs are developed [1–3]. Ring-tailed lemur (L. catta) and Black-and-white ruffed lemur (Varecia variegata) are among of the most endangered primate species [4], with population declines of approximately 80% over the past three decades, and their current distribution is largely limited to national parks and protected reserves [5–7]. Given the severe threats facing lemurs and their habitats, research on individuals under managed care represents an important opportunity to obtain biological and health data that are often unattainable in the wild. Such information can enhance our understanding of species’ physiology, inform veterinary and nutritional management, and provide valuable references for conservation planning. Studies on ex-situ populations, therefore, complement field research and contribute to a more comprehensive approach to species conservation.

Lemur catta, commonly known as the “ring-tailed lemur”, and Varecia variegata, the “black-and-white ruffed lemur”, both belong to the family Lemuridae. They are both diurnal and primarily frugivorous, inhabit dry bush, scrub, and closed forests, and exhibit female dominance within their social hierarchies [8–11]. Ring-tailed lemurs inhabit the southern and southwestern regions of Madagascar, are non-monogamous prosimian primates, living in groups and breeding seasonally [12]. Varecia variegata is endemic only to lowland and mid-altitude rainforests of eastern Madagascar [13]. Both species reach sexual maturity at approximately 2 years of age [14–16].

Hematological and biochemical parameters can vary widely among primate species, from prosimians to great apes, across different development stages, in addition to seasonal variations based on food availability [17, 18]. The literature contains numerous studies on various wild lemur species, often based on a limited number of animals, and few on captive ring-tailed lemurs and ruffed lemurs [10, 19–23].

The objectives of this retrospective study were to evaluate: (a) the behavior of hematological and biochemical variables in captive ring-tailed lemurs and various lemurs, housed in the same environment and with the same feeding plan over a three-year period, to obtain possible indications for the health management of these active species, (b) possible variations related to sex and age.

Materials and methods

Animals

The lemurs included in this study were housed at Mundomar, a zoological park located in Benidorm (Valencian Community, eastern Spain), a major tourist destination on the Mediterranean coast. Within this zoo, two breeding groups of lemurs were kept: ring-tailed lemurs (Lemur catta) and black-and-white ruffed lemurs (Varecia variegata), each consisting of 15–20 individuals. Family groups ranged from 2 to 21 years, with most individuals born within the park.

These lemurs were housed with the utmost respect for animal welfare in a spacious outdoor enclosure equipped with rocks, logs, branches, hammocks, climbing ropes, and cave shelters. This enclosure was connected to temperature-controlled indoor enclosures. Both lemur species shared a common diet. The diet consisted of a commercial food (Leaf-eating monkeys Old and New World, 79430 La Chapelle-Saint-Laurent, France), a complete extruded pellet diet suitable for the growth and maintenance of all herbivorous primates of the Old and New World, such as howler monkeys, african colobus monkeys, langurs, and lemurs. The food contains insects and fruit and is free of added sugars (contains stevia leaves), palm oil, lactose, GMOs (< 0.90%), gluten, colorings, or preservatives, ensuring compliance with the estimated minimum nutritional requirements. In addition, fresh fruits and vegetables were provided daily in adequate amounts, with items rotated regularly to maintain variety, a key factor in primate nutrition.

A complete health check was performed annually on all individuals from both groups. The assessments followed established veterinary procedures for animal management and welfare, using gentle manual restraint and light isoflurane anesthesia (administered via face mask) after 12 h fasting period with water withheld. Each animal was weighed, microchip-verified, and subject to a full physical examination, including measurement of body temperature, heart rate, and respiratory rate. General health status and potential injuries were assessed by inspection. Additionally, fecal sample and rectal swabs were obtained for parasitological and microbiological examinations. All lemurs were dewormed with ivermectin (0.2 mg/kg subcutaneously).

For this study, the lemurs were divided into three age groups: 2–5 years (young adults), 5–9 years (adults), and ≥ 10 years (elderly) as recommended by Singleton et al. [3].

Sample collection

Samples for this study were collected during routine annual health assessments in March 2022, 2023, and 2024. Venous blood samples were collected from the right jugular vein using a 5 ml syringe and a 23G needle. The blood was divided into tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), lithium heparin, with the remainder placed in plain serum separator tubes (Aquisel tubes, Grupo Selecta, 08630 Abrera, Barcelona, Spain) for biobanking.

Lithium heparin tubes were immediately processed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate plasma. EDTA tubes and separated plasma were sent on the same day under refrigeration to the laboratory (IDEXX Laboratories, 08038 Barcelona, Spain) for biochemical and hematological analysis and then processed within 24 h.

Blood analysis

The hematological parameters measured were erythrocytes, packed cell volume (PCV), hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, eosinophils, monocytes, basophils (percentage and number), platelets, and mean platelet volume (MPV). All they were performed using both Sysmex XT-2000iV or Sysmex XN-1000 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe 651 − 0073, Japan).

The biochemical analytes measured were alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, glucose, amylase, lipase, cholesterol, albumin, globulin, A/G ratio, calcium, inorganic phosphate, total protein, creatinine, and urea using a Beckman Coulter AU5800 chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Brea CA 92821, USA). Blood smears were prepared from each sample treated with anticoagulant, then fixed and stained with Giemsa or hematoxylin–eosin to check for possible hemoparasites.

Statistical analysis

To examine the distribution of the data a preliminary statistical analysis of all hematological and biochemical values was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., 2023, Armonk, NY, USA) using the one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. No significant deviations from normality were observed.

At the time of blood sampling, all animals showed no clinical signs of disease or significant lesions and were free from endo- and ectoparasitosis.

The ring-tailed lemur group had a mean age of 8.33 years for females (ranging from a minimum of 3 to a maximum of20 years) and 10 years ( min = 2, max = 21 years) for males, while the variegated lemur group had a mean age of 7.75 years for females (min = 2, max15 years) and 7.83 years (min = 2, max = 16 years) for males. The ring-tailed lemur group was predominantly male (16 males vs. 6 females), while the variegated lemur group is predominantly female (14 females vs. 9 males).

Comparing the mean weights of adult males and females (aged ≥ 2 years), no significant differences were found between the two sexes for either species: for L. catta females averaged 2.48 ± 0.50 kg and for males 2.78 ± 0.61 kg, for V. variegata females 3.58 ± 0.94 kg and for males 3.44 ± 0.73 kg.

Discussion

Not all lemur species are easily and sustainably kept in zoos, as husbandry constraints can affect their well-being, survival, and reproductive capacity in captive conditions. These animals tolerate a temperature range of -12 to 48 degrees Celsius [16, 26–28], a range rarely exceeded on the Mediterranean coast of Spain, where the lemurs enrolled in this study reside. The decision to divide the animals into age groups, as recommended by Singleton et al. [3], in our case could be misleading since, reaching sexual maturity at 18–20 months and although not all of them reproduce successfully [14], all the lemurs studied, being ≥ 2 years old, can be considered adults. The choice to enroll only adult animals also allows us to avoid interference due to growth-induced hematological and biochemical values.

Lemur diets can sometimes be difficult to define, as diets, in addition to differing between individual species in the wild, also exhibit highly seasonal variations and are often insufficiently studied or unknown [10, 29–32]. Both lemur species we enrolled are eclectic omnivores and have a very varied diet and, despite this, episodes of progressive nutritional disease characterized by obesity, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, soft tissue mineralization, impaired locomotion, increased alkaline phosphatase activity, and poor skeletal mineralization can occur in captive lemurs [33, 34]. The choice to provide a heterogeneous diet, as in our case, was therefore aimed at compensating for any nutritional deficiencies and also to stimulate the individual lemurs’ curiosity in terms of taste and smell, with a view to encouraging their appetite. Periodic assessment of body weight is one of important factors in making informed decisions about the appropriateness of specific feeding regimes. In our case, the dietary approach provided to the two groups of animals demonstrates that the diet offered was balanced and provided in adequate quantities. Their body weights fall within the normal range for each captive species [32, 35], with curves reflecting lower body weights in young individuals, increasing with maturity, although for older individuals, weight tends to decrease slightly. Similar observations, including weight loss in old age, have been previously reported for V. variegata by other authors [6, 13]. The difference in weight by sex in the two species examined also follows what was observed by Terranova and Coffman [36]. On the other hand, Schwitzer and Kaumanns [6] reported that there was no significant difference in body weights between males and females in captive ruffed lemurs from 14 European zoos.

Anesthetic protocols for captive prosimians rely largely on inhalant anesthetics [37] with mask induction and maintenance with isoflurane. Isoflurane can cause hypotension in ring-tailed lemurs [37] and has been shown to induce extensive apoptosis in neonatal macaques [38]. Sevofluorane and propofol were associated with decease platelet aggregation, while isoflurane has negligible effects on platelets at therapeutic concentrations [39]. During our study, no adverse effects were observed following each blood sampling.

These species, like almost all captive non-human primates, could be predisposed to congenital abnormalities (scoliosis, incomplete skull formation, internal organ anomalies, and neuromuscular disease, all of which may be related to the small number of founders), as well as captive-related diseases (diarrhea, hair balls, chapped feet in young animals, and canine-tooth abscesses), urolithiasis, hemoparasites (anaplasmosis, filarial infection, etc.), hemosiderosis, coccioidomycosis, and other infectious diseases, both viral and bacterial and neoplastic, including zoonotic ones: toxoplasmosis, SARS-CoV-2 infection [10, 18, 21, 35, 40–53]. All the animals we enrolled showed no pathological conditions or congenital diseases at clinical examination, and fecal examinations and blood smears did not reveal the presence of endoparasites infections.

The availability of average data for various hematological and biochemical-clinical parameters is extremely useful for clinicians when monitoring different diseases, including the previously mentioned nutritional ones, which can occur in ring-tailed lemurs and Lemur variegata variagata [40, 46, 49, 54], particularly for those living in captivity. In general, overnight fasting before blood sampling is recommended for all laboratory animal species, except mice. However, even a short fast can cause alterations in laboratory test results, particularly for glucose (which tends to decrease during prolonged fasting, but can also fluctuate), lipids (fasting is essential for correct assessment, otherwise values ​​can be distorted), and insulin (although cortisol and thyroid hormones can also vary). Total protein and albumin levels can vary with fasting, as can fluctuations in sodium and potassium.

The laboratory instruments we use are widely used and validated in several animal species, including monkeys and lemurs [55–60]. In our study, the animals subjected to periodic blood tests showed variations in parameter values over the years. The greatest variations were recorded in 2023, despite the fact that their diet was never modified and no environmental temperature fluctuations were recorded. Unfortunately, not all subjects were tested annually. In most cases, this was due to the transfer of individuals to other zoological institutions, which made follow-up sampling impossible. In a few instances, analyses could not be performed because the blood volume obtained was insufficient for all planned parameters. Red blood cell values showed a constant trend over the three-year period, with values consistently higher in V. variegata than in L. catta. In relation to the lemurs’ age, the greatest variations were observed in Varecia variegata, with a decline in some parameters (erythrocytes, RDW, bilirubin, and lipase), except for MCH, which showed an opposite trend. In Lemur catta, however, an increase in erythrocyte parameters, RDW, and lipase was observed, compared to a decline in MCH and bilirubin.

The Packed Cell Volume (PCV) values of L. catta we recorded were higher than those reported by Page et al. [23] but in agreement with what was observed by Dutton et al. [10] in free-ranging animals and still higher than those of Varecia variegata. The PCV values we observed in the latter species were slightly higher than those described by other authors [19, 22]. In nature, the higher values in L. catta compared to V. variegata could be attributed to the arid conditions of their natural habitat [19], therefore it is conceivable that fasting and the failure to administer water before blood sampling may also contribute to interfering with PCV values. It is known that erythrocytes swell in EDTA over time, so PCV and MCV will be falsely increased in older samples (sent by mail). In our case, shipments to the laboratory were always made at the same time for both species and this would invalidate this hypothesis. Furthermore, the MCV data we observed are almost superimposable to those observed by Page et al. [23] and glucose values ​​have always remained almost unchanged over the years in conjunction with the various blood tests.

Hemoglobin values ​​were almost comparable for both species. Compared to the values ​​of this hematological variant found by Page et al. [23], in adult L. catta, the values ​​we observed were consistently higher throughout the study period. It is known that prosimian primates have higher hemoglobin concentrations and total erythrocyte counts than humans [61, 62].

Red blood cell distribution (RDW) was significantly higher in V. variegata compared to L. catta (21.15 ± 0.29 vs. 18.40 ± 0.30). However, RDW decreased with age in V. variegata (-0.1974, Table 6) while the reverse was observed for L. catta (0.0065, Table 6). Evidence of an increased RDW may indicate an anemic state (even when anemia is not yet evident), especially due to malnutrition/malabsorption (iron, folate, cyanocobalamin) or an unfavorable prognosis [63]. In our case, none of these etiopathogenetic factors can be considered given the good clinical conditions observed at each sampling.

The trend in the MCH value is particularly interesting. The different behavior of the MCH value between the two sexes of each species is not found in the literature we consulted. MCH represents the average amount of hemoglobin contained in each red blood cell and provides valuable information on the oxygen-carrying capacity of red blood cells and can be useful in the evaluation of various medical conditions. In our case, it was always statistically significant both between the two species and in the sex/species and species/year ratios. The MCH values in L. catta are equal to those reported by other authors in captive-raised animals [23].

V. variegata consistently showed higher WBC counts than L. catta, suggesting a species-specific immune response or better adaptation to captivity, and in any case with values ​​higher than those described by other authors [10, 19, 21–23]. As described by Chmielewski and Strzelec [64], leukocyte count may reflect the rate of aging and therefore can predict long-term survival in the elderly. Bergeron and Buettner-Janusch [62] reported that leukocyte counts in prosimians were higher than those in humans. In unanesthetized and untrained macaques, physiological leukocytosis, characterized by lymphocytosis and sometimes mature neutrophilia, usually occurs. So much so that the importance of training monkeys to be handled and subjected to blood sampling is recognized to avoid stress or the so-called “alarm reactions” associated with physical restraint [65–67]. Therefore, in our case, it can be hypothesized that an elevated white blood cell count may be associated with the restriction due to mask induction. Leukocyte and monocyte counts are indicators of inflammation secondary to infection [64]. An unchanged increase in these values ​​would suggest the absence of infectious agents in the social environment. It is worth noting that the study period coincided with the circulation of SARS-CoV-2 in the human population, during which only a single lemur showed a positive serological response [53]. Furthermore, the neutrophil: lymphocyte ratio (NLR) of our animals remained almost constant over time. The mean values ​​of neutrophils and hemoglobin observed by us were higher than those of wild ring-tailed lemurs observed by Singleton et al. [3], while compared to L. catta living in captivity the values ​​of these parameters were almost superimposable [23]. These data suggested that in our lemurs, despite their age, they did not yet show immunosenescence.

Regarding platelets, anucleated cytoplasmic fragments derived from megakaryocytes that play a central role in both physiological processes such as hemostasis and pathological processes such as inflammation and atherosclerosis, thrombocytopenia was observed in both species we enrolled in the first two controls. Chronic infection and inflammation have both been identified as etiologies of immune-mediated thrombocytopenia in humans and companion animals, and thrombocytopenia was the most commonly observed hematologic abnormality in Ehrlichia chaffeensis infection in Lemur catta and Varecia variegata [21]. Progressive thrombocytopenia has been described in folate-deficient baboons and in rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta) [68]. Platelet size, measured as mean platelet volume (MPV), an indicator of platelet reactivity and a potential determinant of platelet function and aggregation, provides important information on the course and prognosis of a variety of inflammatory conditions. Under normal physiological conditions, MPV is inversely proportional to platelet count, which is associated with maintaining hemostasis and a constant platelet mass; therefore, an increase in platelet production is accompanied by a reduction in their mean volume [69]. L. catta has a consistently higher mean MPV than Varecia variegata, despite the fact that in 2023, the mean platelet count was significantly lower than in other years and still much lower than that of the other species. This behavior could be the result of a compensatory mechanism in exchange for a low platelet count. In human medicine, the wide variability of MPV is likely due to platelet count, sex, age, and ethnicity [70], and a similar pattern is assumed to occur in lemurs. Through the use of computer-facilitated morphometric analysis, significant differences among nonhuman primates were substantiated with respect to mean platelet size, the mean number of α-granules, and the number of dense bodies [71].

Interesting variations were also observed in clinical chemistry between the two lemur species. Specifically, L. catta showed higher values than V. variegata for ALT, total bilirubin, creatinine, glucose, urea, lipase, and A/G ratio in all years studied.

Urea and creatinine are both renal function parameters that show a slow and progressive increase over time, suggesting age-related degeneration, as also reported by Page et al. [23].

Regarding the evaluation of liver function parameters, the AST values we found in L. catta were lower than those reported by Dutton et al. [10] in captive animals, while ALT values were higher in our group. Bilirubin, on the other hand, is in line with what is reported in the bibliography and shows significantly lower values in V. variegata compared to L. catta, and it is also the only biochemical-clinical parameter that tends to decrease with age within the same species. This would confirm that some parameters, especially those of liver and kidney function, change with aging in many primate species, thus reflecting the deterioration of excretory organ function (e.g., age-related renal degeneration, a common cause of mortality in older captive lemurs), immune competence, or nutritional status [3, 10]. It is worth noting that all the animals we enrolled in the study are adults, and many of these animals are particularly elderly (in the L. catta group, five subjects are > 15 years old; in the V. variegata group, three animals are ≥ 15 years old).

Glycemic values in L. catta are also in line with what was previously reported by Dutton et al. [10] and lower than those described by other authors [3, 23] in wild and captive L. catta respectively, but still higher than those of V. variegata present in the same structure. Glycemic levels can be influenced by various factors (food consumption, drugs, prolonged sample storage time, etc.), not least the stressful event of their capture [23]. In fact, manual restraint can contribute to temporarily increasing the stress to which, as in our case, it is necessary to add the action of isoflurane, capable of reversing the glucose-induced inhibition of the activity of the pancreatic adenosine triphosphate-sensitive potassium (K(ATP)) channel, with consequent reduction in insulin secretion and alteration of glucose tolerance [72, 73].

The lipase values we found were significantly lower than those observed by Page et al. [23] and Dutton et al. [10] in L. catta but also V. variegata [22]. Lipases are enzymes that break down triglycerides into glycerol and fatty acids. Serum lipase is a parameter for assessing pancreatic function, but it tends to increase more slowly and remain elevated longer than amylase levels, which can be useful in delayed diagnosis. In rhesus monkeys lipase increased in prediabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus [74].

Lower mean values were found for GPT/AST, GOT/AST, and creatinine. In the V. variegata group, however, compared to what is described in the bibliography, lower values for creatinine, and P, GPT/ALT, GOT/AST, ALP were observed only in the third sampling. Interesting in our case is the interaction between species and sex which differs from what was observed in L. catta [54] and in Eulemur albifrons [75] in the reserves of Madagascar.

Conclusion

Research conducted in controlled environments such as zoological institutions can provide valuable insights into the physiology, behavior, and health of species that are difficult to study in the wild. The knowledge gained from these settings contributes to the development of evidence-based management and conservation strategies, ultimately supporting the long-term preservation of species in their natural habitats.

Captivity conditions can influence hematological and biochemical parameters in animals, highlighting the importance of regular veterinary monitoring and the establishment of species-specific reference intervals. The data presented here provide baseline values for captive lemurs, supporting clinical evaluation, diagnosis, and disease prevention. These findings contribute to a better understanding of physiological variation in lemurs under managed care and may assist future comparative and conservation-related research.

The study’s main limitations include the small sample size, the hematological comparison between anesthetized and unanesthetized individuals, and the absence of additional biochemical parameters, particularly electrolytes (e.g., Na, Cl, Mg, Fe, Cu, Zn) as well as folate and vitamin B12, which could provide further valuable information on potential nutritional requirements.
