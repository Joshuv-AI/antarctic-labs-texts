Perioperative esketamine-containing strategies and postoperative neurocognitive outcomes in older surgical patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with certainty assessment
Authors: Ruirui Qi, Yuchen Wu, Lin Zhang, Wenxiu Niu, Tian Hao, Haohao Xie, Xiaomeng Han, Hengyang Wang, Donghui Jia, Zhanhai Wan, Zhigang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative delirium (POD) is a common neurocognitive complication after surgery in older adults and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine and an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist with established analgesic effects and potential anti-inflammatory properties, may therefore influence postoperative neurocognitive outcomes. However, the efficacy of ketamine-containing perioperative strategies for preventing POD remains uncertain.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261370159). Randomized controlled trials evaluating perioperative esketamine in adults aged ≥ 60 years undergoing surgery were included. The primary outcome was POD incidence. Secondary outcomes included postoperative cognitive dysfunction or delayed neurocognitive recovery, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and psychiatric adverse events. Random-effects models, subgroup analyses, exploratory exposure-response analyses, and GRADE certainty assessments were performed.ResultsSeventeen randomized controlled trials involving 2,914 participants were included, with eleven trials contributing to the primary POD analysis. Esketamine-containing perioperative regimens were associated with reduced POD incidence compared with control interventions (OR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.82; P = 0.002; I² = 45.8%). Four studies evaluating postoperative cognitive dysfunction or delayed neurocognitive recovery suggested a possible reduction (OR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.22–0.71; P = 0.002; I² = 0.0%), but evidence certainty was very low. Esketamine was associated with reduced postoperative nausea and vomiting (OR = 0.49, 95% CI 0.32–0.75; P = 0.001), without increased psychiatric adverse events (OR = 1.41, 95% CI 0.67–2.96; P = 0.37). Exploratory exposure-response analysis showed no significant association between cumulative esketamine exposure and POD risk.ConclusionsPerioperative esketamine may be associated with reduced POD incidence in older surgical patients; however, given heterogeneity in perioperative management and limited certainty of evidence, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in future standardized trials.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD: 420261370159. Introduction

Perioperative cognitive complications are common after surgery in older adults and represent a major challenge to postoperative recovery. Postoperative delirium (POD) is the most frequent acute cognitive complication following surgery and is associated with prolonged hospitalization, functional decline, and increased mortality risk [1–4]. In addition to POD, longer-term postoperative cognitive impairment, including postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) and delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR), has also attracted increasing clinical attention because of its potential impact on functional independence and quality of life [5, 6]. Although POD and POCD/dNCR differ in their clinical manifestations and temporal trajectories, they share several biological pathways and both contribute to adverse postoperative outcomes in older surgical patients [4, 7–9]. Increasing evidence suggests that neuroinflammation, neurotransmitter imbalance, oxidative stress, and impaired synaptic plasticity may contribute to the development of both acute and longer-term postoperative cognitive impairment [10–14].

Older surgical patients are particularly vulnerable to POD and other postoperative cognitive complications because of reduced cognitive reserve, increased inflammatory susceptibility, and impaired physiological resilience [10–15]. The incidence of POD varies according to patient characteristics, surgical procedures, assessment tools, and follow-up duration; however, its substantial clinical burden highlights the importance of effective preventive strategies in this high-risk population [1–3]. Meanwhile, POCD/dNCR may represent additional manifestations of postoperative cognitive vulnerability and requires evaluation when investigating interventions targeting postoperative cognitive recovery.

The mechanisms underlying POD and POCD/dNCR are complex and multifactorial. Surgical trauma, anesthetic exposure, postoperative pain, and sleep disturbance may induce neuroinflammatory responses, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, neurotransmitter dysregulation, and impaired neuronal plasticity [10–15]. POD is characterized by an acute disturbance in attention and awareness, whereas POCD/dNCR reflects deterioration in cognitive performance detected through neuropsychological assessment after surgery [16–20]. Evaluating these outcomes separately allows assessment of whether perioperative interventions primarily influence acute delirium or broader postoperative cognitive recovery.

Various pharmacological strategies, including dexmedetomidine, anti-inflammatory agents, and other neuroprotective interventions, have been investigated for preventing POD and postoperative cognitive impairment; however, their efficacy remains inconsistent and limitations regarding safety and clinical applicability remain [15, 21–25]. Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, is a potent N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist with established analgesic effects and potential neuroprotective properties. Through modulation of glutamatergic signaling, reduction of neuroinflammatory responses, and regulation of synaptic plasticity, esketamine may influence biological pathways implicated in the development of postoperative cognitive complications [26–29]. These potential mechanisms provide a rationale for investigating whether perioperative esketamine-containing strategies may reduce POD and improve postoperative cognitive outcomes in older surgical patients.

However, whether these potential biological effects translate into clinically meaningful benefits remains uncertain. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated ketamine- or esketamine-related perioperative outcomes; however, differences in population selection, intervention characteristics, and outcome assessment may limit the applicability of their findings to older surgical patients receiving perioperative esketamine. Furthermore, variations in surgical procedures, anesthetic techniques, opioid administration, analgesic strategies, and other perioperative co-interventions may complicate interpretation of the effects associated with esketamine-containing strategies.

Several important questions remain unresolved. First, it remains unclear whether perioperative esketamine-containing strategies can reduce POD specifically in older surgical patients. Second, whether these strategies influence secondary cognitive outcomes, including POCD/dNCR and postoperative cognitive performance, remains uncertain. Third, the influence of perioperative treatment characteristics and clinical factors on treatment effects requires further exploratory evaluation. Although previous meta-analyses have provided important evidence regarding perioperative ketamine or esketamine, many included heterogeneous surgical populations, different ketamine formulations, or focused primarily on delirium outcomes. Therefore, whether esketamine-containing strategies provide clinically relevant benefits across postoperative neurocognitive outcomes specifically in older surgical patients remains unclear.

To address remaining uncertainties regarding the applicability and certainty of evidence, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to refine the evidence regarding perioperative esketamine-containing strategies in older surgical patients, with particular attention to postoperative neurocognitive outcomes, certainty of evidence, and clinically relevant sources of heterogeneity. The primary outcome was POD, while secondary outcomes included POCD/dNCR, postoperative cognitive scores, and adverse events. The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE framework, and exploratory analyses were performed to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, including surgical characteristics, comparator type, administration strategies, and perioperative co-interventions.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261370159). Registration was completed retrospectively after study initiation; however, no changes were made to the predefined eligibility criteria, outcomes, or analytical framework after registration.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, China Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), and Chinese Science and Technology Periodical Database (VIP) from database inception to March 2026. The search was limited to English- and Chinese-language publications. Detailed reproducible search strategies for all databases, including Boolean syntax, search date, and database-specific terms, are provided in Supplementary Methods S1 [30].

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were established according to the PICOS framework. Participants (P) were older surgical patients aged ≥ 60 years or defined as older adults by the original investigators. Interventions (I) consisted of perioperative esketamine administration.Comparators (C) included placebo or clinically appropriate control interventions receiving standard perioperative management. Outcomes (O) included POD, POCD/dNCR, postoperative nausea and vomiting, psychiatric adverse events, and postoperative cognitive scores. Study design (S) was restricted to parallel-group randomized controlled trials (RCTs).Exclusion criteria: (1) studies involving patients with pre-existing severe psychiatric disorders or established dementia at baseline; (2) insufficient data on our outcomes of interest; (3) articles that assessed the effect of esketamine combined with other neuroprotective interventions as a co-intervention without a comparable control group. Duplicate publications and reports derived from potentially overlapping cohorts were assessed during study selection. When multiple reports from the same cohort were identified, the publication providing the most complete data relevant to the prespecified outcomes was selected.

Study selection and data extraction

References were managed using EndNote, and duplicate records were removed. Two reviewers (RRQ and LZ) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles against the predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (YCW). Two investigators independently extracted data using a standardized extraction form, including first author, publication year, country, sample size, age, sex, surgical type, esketamine dose, timing and mode of administration, control regimen, background anesthetic techniques, opioid administration, analgesic strategies, and other relevant co-interventions. POD assessment tool and window, POCD/dNCR definition, event counts, means and standard deviations for continuous outcomes, adverse-event definitions and monitoring methods, and risk-of-bias information. Studies providing comparable dichotomous outcomes and eligible two-arm comparisons were included in quantitative meta-analysis. Studies with multi-arm designs or without an appropriate comparison for pooled analysis were summarized narratively. For multi-arm randomized trials, only clinically relevant comparisons between an esketamine-containing arm and an appropriate control arm were included in quantitative synthesis, and shared control groups were not double-counted. Background anesthetic and analgesic regimens were extracted to assess potential co-intervention differences. Active comparator trials were interpreted cautiously because variations in background protocols could introduce additional treatment effects beyond esketamine exposure.

Quality assessment

Two investigators independently assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool [31]. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer(YCW). The assessment covered five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selective reporting [31]. For delirium outcomes, particular attention was paid to allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, outcome-assessor training, missing outcome data, and selective reporting. Domain-level judgments and supporting rationales are provided in the supplementary risk-of-bias table.

Statistical analysis

Event counts and total sample sizes were used to calculate odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for binary outcomes, including POD, POCD/dNCR, PONV, and psychiatric adverse events. Continuous cognitive outcomes were summarized using mean differences or standardized mean differences when appropriate. When standard deviations were not directly reported, they were derived from standard errors, confidence intervals, medians, or interquartile ranges using standard methods [32, 33]. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, the I-squared statistic, and tau-squared [34]. Random-effects models were used because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated among included trials. To improve reproducibility, the extracted event counts, sample sizes, subgroup classifications, assessment windows, and dose-related variables are provided in the supplementary tables. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analysis, restriction to placebo-controlled trials, exclusion or separate interpretation of active-comparator trials, fixed-effect models, and alternative random-effects model specifications when feasible. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to surgery type, comparator type, administration timing, dosing regimen, and POD assessment window. Because several subgroups included few studies, subgroup findings were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of effect modification [35]. The analysis of publication bias for variables was conducted using the funnel plot test and Egger’s regression test [36, 37] for outcomes with at least 10 included studies (POD). A sensitivity analysis was additionally performed by restricting the analysis to placebo-controlled trials to explore whether comparator type influenced the pooled effect estimate.

Exploratory exposure-response analysis

Exploratory exposure-response analyses were performed to generate hypotheses regarding whether reported cumulative esketamine exposure was associated with POD risk. These analyses were not designed to establish optimal dosing strategies or clinical dose recommendations. Because included trials used heterogeneous administration schedules, including bolus injection, infusion, and postoperative patient-controlled analgesia, no pharmacological dose-equivalence conversion was performed. Analyses were therefore based on reported cumulative esketamine exposure and interpreted cautiously.

Grading the evidence

The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [38–40]. Evidence from randomized trials initially starts as high certainty and can be downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [38–40]. Two authors (RRQ and LZ) independently performed the GRADE assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (YCW). The certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. For POCD/dNCR, clinical heterogeneity in definitions, cognitive instruments, and assessment windows was explicitly considered when judging indirectness.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 3,069 records were identified from eight electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM) using predefined search strategies. After removing 835 duplicate records, 2,234 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 2,190 records were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria, including ineligible populations (n = 205), inappropriate interventions (n = 1,731), non-randomized or irrelevant study designs (n = 235), and nineteen records identified during screening were excluded because they were non-peer-reviewed dissertations or conference abstracts. No potentially eligible full-text reports were unavailable for retrieval. Forty-four full-text reports were assessed for eligibility, of which 27 were excluded for reasons detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Finally, 17 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis. Among these, 11 RCTs contributed to the primary meta-analysis of POD incidence. Four studies reported dichotomous POCD/dNCR outcomes and were included in the incidence analysis, whereas two studies reported only continuous cognitive outcomes and were summarized separately because they could not be pooled with incidence-based measures.

The study selection process is presented in Fig. 1. Baseline characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1, with outcome definitions and assessment methods provided in Supplementary Table S2. Across the included studies, a total of 2,914 participants were included in the reported analyses. Esketamine administration protocols varied across trials, including single intravenous bolus administration, continuous infusion, and postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA). Given the variability in perioperative regimens, exploratory exposure-response analyses were restricted to studies reporting sufficiently detailed esketamine administration information.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. The domain-level risk-of-bias judgments for all included randomized controlled trials are summarized in Supplementary Figure S1. Eight of 17 trials (47.1%) were rated as low risk and 9 (52.9%) raised some concerns, mainly because of incomplete reporting of allocation concealment or blinding procedures. When blinding or assessor training was unclear, this contributed to risk-of-bias concerns [31]. Attrition rates were generally low, and no clear evidence of selective outcome reporting was identified. Detailed domain-level assessments are provided in Supplementary Table S3, and a traffic-light plot is presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Primary outcome

Eleven RCTs involving 2,268 participants reported POD incidence. Perioperative esketamine-containing regimens were associated with a lower incidence of POD compared with control interventions (OR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.82; P = 0.002, I² = 45.8%). The pooled effect estimate for POD incidence is presented in Fig. 2. According to GRADE, the certainty of evidence was moderate for POD and very low for POCD/dNCR. The certainty of evidence for each outcome is summarized in Table 2. Funnel plot inspection did not suggest marked asymmetry (Fig. 3). Egger’s regression test was not statistically significant [36, 37] (intercept = -1.33, P = 0.199). When the analysis was restricted to the nine RCTs using placebo (normal saline) as the comparator, the pooled effect estimate remained consistent with the primary analysis (OR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.31–0.68; I² = 0.5%; P for heterogeneity = 0.052, Supplementary Table S4). The direction and magnitude of the effect estimate were comparable with those of the overall analysis (OR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.82; I² = 45.8%). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that sequential exclusion of individual studies did not materially change the pooled effect estimate. The pooled ORs after removal of individual studies ranged from 0.52 to 0.63, and all estimates remained statistically significant with a consistent direction of effect (Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Figure S2). These findings indicate that the overall association between perioperative esketamine administration and reduced POD incidence was not driven by any single included study.

To evaluate the influence of assessment timing, we performed a subgroup analysis comparing studies with a short assessment window (3 days or less) versus those with a longer assessment window (7 days or longer). The test for subgroup differences was not significant, indicating that the available evidence does not establish assessment window as a source of effect modification.

Secondary outcomes

Four RCTs involving 337 participants evaluated POCD or dNCR during the early postoperative period. The pooled estimate suggested a lower incidence of POCD/dNCR in the esketamine group compared with the control group (OR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.22–0.71, P = 0.002, I² = 0.0%, Supplementary Figure S3). However, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low due to concerns regarding variability in outcome definitions and assessment methods, risk of bias, and imprecision.

Seven studies reported postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Esketamine-containing strategies were associated with a lower incidence of PONV compared with control interventions (OR = 0.49, 95% CI 0.32–0.75, P = 0.001, I² = 21.0%, Supplementary Figure S4).

Four trials reported psychiatric adverse events, including hallucinations, nightmares, dizziness, or agitation. No statistically significant difference was observed between groups (OR = 1.41, 95% CI 0.67–2.96, P = 0.37, I² = 66.4%, Supplementary Figure S5), with moderate heterogeneity observed across studies.

Two studies reported postoperative cognitive scores using continuous cognitive measures. Because these outcomes differed in assessment scales, follow-up time points, and reporting formats, they were not pooled with POCD/dNCR incidence outcomes and were summarized separately.

Subgroup and exploratory exposure-response analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of clinical heterogeneity rather than to establish definitive treatment-effect modification. As shown in Table 3, In the surgery-type subgroup analysis, the effect estimate was numerically lower in orthopedic surgery (OR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.31–0.63) than in non-orthopedic surgery (OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.39–1.30). However, the test for subgroup differences did not show a statistically significant difference between these subgroups (P = 0.187).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials involving 2,914 older surgical patients. We found that perioperative esketamine-containing regimens were associated with a reduced incidence of postoperative delirium (POD). The magnitude and direction of this association remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of the primary finding. However, the observed benefit should be interpreted cautiously because esketamine was generally administered as part of multimodal perioperative management strategies, and therefore the pooled effect likely represents the clinical effect of esketamine-containing regimens rather than the isolated pharmacological effect of esketamine itself. Furthermore, exploratory exposure-response analyses did not demonstrate a significant relationship between reported cumulative esketamine exposure and POD risk, suggesting that current evidence is insufficient to determine an optimal dosing strategy. Overall, these findings indicate that perioperative esketamine may represent a potential adjunctive strategy requiring further validation for POD prevention in older surgical patients, while important uncertainties remain regarding external validity, long-term neurocognitive outcomes, and the specific mechanisms responsible for the observed association.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have investigated the effects of perioperative ketamine-based interventions on postoperative neurocognitive outcomes [58–62]. Consistent with previous findings, our meta-analysis demonstrated a potential protective association between perioperative esketamine-containing strategies and postoperative delirium (POD). Specifically, among 11 randomized controlled trials involving older surgical patients, esketamine-containing strategies were associated with a reduced risk of POD compared with control strategies (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.82; moderate certainty of evidence). Recently, Tang et al. reported a meta-analysis evaluating perioperative esketamine and POD in elderly patients [62]. Their findings also suggested a significant reduction in POD incidence with perioperative esketamine administration, which is broadly consistent with our results. The comparable direction of effect supports the potential role of esketamine-containing strategies in reducing acute postoperative neurocognitive complications. However, differences in pooled estimates may reflect variations in study eligibility criteria, included trials, comparator characteristics, and outcome definitions.

Beyond POD, our review additionally evaluated longer-term postoperative neurocognitive outcomes. In contrast to the relatively consistent findings for POD, evidence regarding POCD/dNCR remained highly uncertain because of limited available studies and substantial heterogeneity. This distinction suggests that the potential benefits of esketamine may be more evident for acute postoperative delirium prevention than for persistent cognitive decline. Unlike previous reviews, this meta-analysis focused specifically on older surgical patients receiving perioperative esketamine-containing strategies, rather than broader populations involving mixed ketamine and esketamine interventions. This focused approach, together with the incorporation of newly available randomized evidence and GRADE assessment, provides a more clinically targeted evaluation of the potential benefits and uncertainties of esketamine in postoperative neurocognitive outcomes.

Among 11 randomized controlled trials involving 2,268 participants, esketamine-containing strategies were associated with a lower risk of POD compared with control interventions (OR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.82; moderate certainty of evidence). Recently, Tang et al. reported a meta-analysis specifically examining perioperative esketamine administration and POD in elderly patients, with findings broadly consistent with ours [62]. The similar direction of effect across studies supports the potential role of esketamine-containing strategies in reducing acute postoperative neurocognitive complications. Nevertheless, differences in pooled estimates may be attributable to variations in eligibility criteria, included trials, comparator characteristics, and outcome definitions.

Beyond POD, our review further examined longer-term postoperative neurocognitive outcomes, including POCD/dNCR. In contrast to the relatively consistent findings for POD, the evidence for POCD/dNCR remains highly uncertain due to the limited number of available studies, variability in diagnostic criteria, and differences in cognitive assessment methods. These findings suggest that the potential benefits of perioperative esketamine may be more evident for acute delirium prevention than for persistent postoperative cognitive impairment. Compared with previous reviews that included broader surgical populations or mixed ketamine and esketamine interventions, the present study specifically focused on older surgical patients receiving perioperative esketamine-containing strategies. By incorporating recently available randomized evidence and systematically evaluating evidence certainty using the GRADE framework, this review provides a more targeted assessment of the potential benefits and remaining uncertainties regarding esketamine for postoperative neurocognitive outcomes.

Several biological mechanisms may potentially explain the association between perioperative esketamine-containing regimens and reduced POD incidence observed in this review. However, these mechanisms remain hypothetical because the available clinical studies were not designed to directly evaluate biological pathways. First, modulation of perioperative neuroinflammation may represent a potential mechanism. Older adults exhibit increased vulnerability to inflammatory responses after surgery due to age-related immune dysregulation and enhanced microglial reactivity [1, 5]. Surgical stress can induce cytokine release, blood–brain barrier disruption, and microglial activation, which are implicated in POD development [10, 13, 14]. Ketamine and esketamine have been reported to influence inflammatory signaling, glutamatergic neurotransmission, and neuroplasticity-related pathways [26]. Therefore, esketamine-containing strategies may potentially attenuate some stress-related neuroinflammatory processes, although direct evidence linking esketamine to reduced neuroinflammation and POD prevention remains limited. Second, the analgesic and opioid-sparing effects of esketamine may also contribute to reduced POD risk. Postoperative pain and excessive opioid exposure are recognized modifiable factors associated with delirium development in older adults [15, 63]. By improving analgesia and potentially reducing opioid requirements, esketamine may influence perioperative factors related to delirium susceptibility. However, because esketamine was frequently incorporated into multimodal analgesic or anesthetic protocols, the observed association should be interpreted as the effect of an esketamine-containing perioperative strategy rather than an isolated pharmacological effect of esketamine. In addition, inconsistent reporting of opioid consumption prevented evaluation of its potential mediating role. Overall, POD is a multifactorial syndrome resulting from interactions between patient vulnerability and perioperative insults, including inflammation, pain, medication exposure, and sleep disturbance [5, 8]. Thus, esketamine may act as an adjunctive component within multimodal perioperative care rather than through a single neuroprotective pathway.

The potential clinical relevance of these findings should be considered within the broader framework of multimodal delirium prevention. Although perioperative esketamine-containing regimens were associated with a reduced incidence of POD, the current evidence does not support replacing established non-pharmacological prevention strategies, including optimization of pain management, sleep promotion, early mobilization, and comprehensive geriatric-oriented care pathways [21–25]. Instead, esketamine may represent a potential pharmacological adjunct targeting modifiable perioperative stressors in selected high-risk populations. Subgroup analyses did not identify statistically significant effect modification according to surgical type, administration timing, or dosing regimen. Although the effect estimate appeared numerically more favorable in orthopedic surgery, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the interaction test was not statistically significant and subgroup analyses based on aggregate study-level data are susceptible to ecological bias. Nevertheless, the observation may provide a rationale for future risk-stratified investigations, particularly among older patients undergoing procedures associated with high baseline delirium risk, such as major orthopedic surgery. Similarly, the absence of significant differences according to administration timing or dosing regimen should not be interpreted as evidence that all strategies are equivalent. Rather, the available evidence remains insufficient to determine whether specific perioperative windows or dosing approaches provide superior neurocognitive protection. Future prospective trials directly comparing different dosing regimens and administration strategies are required before clinical recommendations regarding optimal use can be established.

The findings of this review suggested a potential protective effect of perioperative esketamine against POCD/dNCR in older surgical patients, with a reduced incidence observed among patients receiving esketamine. Although the pooled analysis suggested a potential reduction in POCD/dNCR with perioperative esketamine-containing strategies, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low, and this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The different effects on acute delirium and longer-term cognitive outcomes may be related to their distinct clinical trajectories and underlying mechanisms. POD is mainly influenced by acute perioperative stressors, including inflammation, pain, sleep disruption, and medication exposure, whereas POCD/dNCR involves more complex interactions among baseline cognitive vulnerability and postoperative recovery processes [5, 6, 8–10]. Although statistical heterogeneity among POCD/dNCR studies was low, differences in diagnostic criteria, cognitive assessment tools, and follow-up time points may limit the certainty and clinical interpretation of the findings. Therefore, current evidence remains insufficient to conclude that esketamine-containing strategies provide sustained cognitive protection beyond the early postoperative period. Regarding safety outcomes, no significant increase in psychiatric adverse events was identified; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because events were rare, definitions were inconsistent, and systematic monitoring was limited. Uncommon neuropsychiatric adverse effects associated with ketamine-class medications cannot be completely excluded [26–29, 63]. Importantly, several included trials compared esketamine-containing strategies with different anesthetic or analgesic approaches, particularly opioid-free anesthesia protocols. Therefore, the observed benefits should be interpreted as effects of broader perioperative strategies incorporating esketamine rather than isolated pharmacological effects of esketamine. Future randomized trials with standardized background anesthetic and analgesic protocols are needed to clarify the independent contribution of esketamine.

Several important priorities for future research emerge from the current findings. First, adequately powered multicenter randomized controlled trials involving diverse geographic regions and healthcare settings are needed to determine whether the observed association between perioperative esketamine use and reduced POD incidence is generalizable beyond the currently available evidence base. Such studies should include standardized delirium assessment methods [16–18, 21, 25] and consistent definitions of perioperative neurocognitive outcomes to improve comparability across trials. Second, future investigations should focus on clarifying the biological mechanisms underlying the potential neurocognitive effects of esketamine. Incorporation of mechanistic outcomes, including inflammatory biomarkers, neurophysiological indicators, perioperative opioid consumption, sleep-related parameters, and patient-centered recovery outcomes, may help determine whether esketamine modifies specific pathways involved in delirium development or provides broader stress-response modulation. Third, future randomized trials specifically designed to compare different esketamine dosing regimens, including standardized assessments of efficacy and safety outcomes, are needed to determine the optimal dose that maximizes neurocognitive benefits while minimizing potential adverse effects. Individual-patient-data meta-analyses incorporating detailed information on dose, timing, duration of administration, and patient characteristics may further clarify the dose–response relationship and support personalized perioperative esketamine strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several important strengths. First, it addressed a clinically focused question by specifically evaluating perioperative esketamine-containing regimens in older surgical patients, a population with particularly high vulnerability to postoperative neurocognitive disorders. Second, only randomized controlled trials were included, thereby reducing the risk of confounding associated with observational evidence. Third, POD and POCD/dNCR were analyzed separately according to contemporary perioperative neurocognitive terminology, allowing more precise interpretation of the potential effects of esketamine on different cognitive outcomes.

First, all included studies were conducted in China and most were single-center trials with relatively small sample sizes, which limits the generalizability of the findings and raises concerns regarding publication bias and small-study effects. Second, although this review focused specifically on older surgical patients receiving perioperative esketamine, substantial clinical heterogeneity existed among the included trials, including variations in surgical procedures, anesthetic protocols, concomitant medications, and outcome assessment methods. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as the effects of perioperative esketamine-containing strategies rather than the isolated pharmacological effect of esketamine. Second, the number of available randomized trials for some outcomes, particularly POCD/dNCR, was limited, and the certainty of evidence was therefore downgraded. In addition, exploratory exposure-response analyses were based on a relatively small number of studies and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence for optimal dosing strategies. Third, most included studies were conducted in single-center settings and within specific surgical populations, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader perioperative populations. Future large-scale, multicenter randomized trials with standardized anesthetic protocols, outcome definitions, and follow-up durations are warranted.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that perioperative esketamine administration is associated with a lower incidence of postoperative delirium and may have potential benefits for postoperative cognitive outcomes in older surgical patients. However, the interpretation of these findings requires caution because the included randomized trials showed variability in surgical populations, anesthetic and analgesic protocols, and perioperative co-interventions. Exploratory analyses regarding dose-response relationships and subgroup effects provide preliminary hypotheses but should not be considered definitive evidence for optimal dosing strategies or specific clinical populations. Future large-scale, well-designed randomized controlled trials with standardized anesthetic protocols, comparable perioperative management, and adequately powered cognitive outcome assessments are needed to determine the independent effect, optimal regimen, and clinical applicability of perioperative esketamine in older surgical patients.
