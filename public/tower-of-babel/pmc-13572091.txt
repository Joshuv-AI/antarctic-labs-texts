Trends in diagnosis of amyloid kidney disease in people who inject drugs (PWID)
Authors: Shivakumar Narayanan, Sumanth Khadke, Madeleine Purcell, Amgad Ghoprial
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo characterize temporal trends in amyloid kidney disease among people who inject drugs (PWID) and evaluate associated renal and mortality outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using TriNetX data from 2018 to 2025. Trends in amyloid kidney disease with proteinuria or chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage ≥3b were assessed among PWID. Outcomes were compared between PWID with and without renal involvement using propensity score–matched analyses.ResultsAge-adjusted incidence of amyloid kidney disease increased from 146 to 238 per 100,000 person-years between 2018 and 2025 (annual percent change 6.8%), while prevalence nearly doubled. Among affected individuals, dialysis incidence increased from 2.04 to 8.83 per 100 person-years. After propensity score matching, renal involvement was associated with higher hazards of death (HR 1.80, 95% CI 1.44,2.25) and dialysis or death (HR 2.54, 95% CI 2.02,3.19). Risks of skin and soft tissue infection and sepsis were similar between groups, while venous thromboembolism or catheter-associated bloodstream infection was modestly increased.ConclusionsAmyloid kidney disease among PWID is increasingly recognized and associated with substantially higher risks of dialysis and death. Earlier recognition of renal involvement may provide opportunities to prevent or slow progression.

Introduction

The prevalence of proteinuria among people who inject drugs(PWID) is estimated at 19% -25% and is twice as common as in the US population (Yanik et al., 2010; McGowan et al., 2020). Risk factors include older age, hypertension, diabetes, HIV infection, hepatitis C infection, heroin or acidifier use for dissolving heroin (McGowan et al., 2020; Harris et al., 2018). Proteinuria may predict both cardiovascular risk and progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD).Heroin use is independently associated with higher risk of progressive CKD and cocaine use is associated with elevated risk of all-cause mortality in PWID with CKD (Bundy et al., 2018; Jaffe and Kimmel, 2006).

AA or reactive systemic amyloidosis is an organ/life threatening complication of chronic inflammatory disorders caused by the deposition of serum amyloid A protein which frequently affects the kidneys (Lachmann et al., 2007). Amyloidosis is a relatively rare condition in the general population (1–1.8/million person years) (Aguirre et al., 2016; Hemminki et al., 2012) but may represent an important cause of progressive renal disease among PWID (Jung et al., 2012; Scott et al., 2018). Among PWID with proteinuria the prevalence of amyloid kidney disease has been estimated between 5% and 50% (Harris et al., 2018),and at some centers it is the leading cause of kidney failure requiring initiation of dialysis among PWID. Amyloidosis associated CKD also carries a worse prognosis, with a nearly two-fold worse end-stage renal disease-free prognosis at 1 year as compared to other diagnoses of CKD (Jung et al., 2012; Scott et al., 2018).

Risk factors for amyloid kidney disease in PWID include recurrent skin and soft tissue infections, subcutaneous injection practices, use of brown heroin, longer duration of injection, older age, and co-occurring blood-borne viral infections such as HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C (Jung et al., 2012; Lejmi et al., 2016). Following diagnosis, kidney function often deteriorates rapidly, with median time to diagnosis of end-stage renal disease needing dialysis of 1.4 years and high mortality (Sharma et al., 2018).

Existing literature about the risk of proteinuria and amyloid kidney disease in PWID is largely based on retrospective case series or cohorts which may be subject to sampling bias. Since the number of cases and hospitalizations from injection drug use related skin and soft tissue infections has consistently increased in the last decade (Capizzi et al., 2020; Malayala et al., 2022; Ciccarone et al., 2016), it is plausible that higher numbers of such patients with wounds may be at risk of proteinuric kidney disease. We have anecdotally noted a rising number of incident amyloid kidney disease cases at our clinical care sites in the past 4 years. We sought to assess the trends of incident amyloid kidney disease and associated proteinuric CKD stage3b or higher among PWID and mirror trends of putative risk factors including severe wounds, amputations or outcomes like initiation of dialysis in this population.

Methods

Study population and design

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort analysis using the TriNetX Global Research Network. TriNetX is a global federated health research network providing access to de-identified electronic health records (EHR) (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values) across large healthcare organizations (HCOs). Data within TriNetX are de-identified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule Safe Harbor standard (45 CFR §164.514[a]), and analyses are performed on aggregate counts within the secure platform without access to individual-level identifiers. Because the dataset contains only de-identified information, this study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Maryland, Baltimore and determined to be exempt from full review.

Adult patients (≥18 years) with a diagnosis of amyloidosis with CKD stage 3b or higher with/without proteinuria were identified within the TriNetX Global Research Network using ICD-10 diagnosis codes and curated EHR data. Amyloid kidney disease was diagnosed based on presence of amyloidosis ICD 10 diagnosis code (E85*) along with diagnosis codes of CKD stage 3b or higher (N18.32*) or nephrotic syndrome (N04.8*). Individuals with diagnosis codes indicating secondary glomerulonephritis or nephropathy unrelated to amyloidosis were excluded.Substance use disorder (SUD) diagnosis codes for substances used as injected drugs, including opioids, cocaine, methamphetamine, and other injectable substances were identified using ICD-10 diagnosis codes. Identification of persons who inject drugs (PWID) using substance use disorder diagnosis codes followed previously described methods for identifying injection drug use in administrative data (Janjua et al., 2018) (at least 2 ambulatory or one inpatient encounter). The SUD diagnosis had to occur before or concurrent with the amyloid kidney disease diagnosis. An internal manual chart review based validation of the eligibility criteria showed a positive predictive value of 83.5% for biopsy proven AA amyloidosis. 110 HCOs were queried, 48 providers responded and a total of 290 patients who matched the query criteria for amyloid CKD in PWID were identified. The study sample size was determined based on available data within the TriNetX network.

Incidence rates, and cumulative prevalence of amyloid CKD among PWID were calculated for annual time periods between 2018 and 2025 using ICD-10 diagnosis codes within the population with substance use disorder. Age-adjusted incidence rates were estimated using direct standardization to an internal PWID standard population based on pooled age distributions within the cohort. Age-specific annual incidence rates were calculated across 5-year age strata, multiplied by the corresponding weights from the internal PWID standard population, and summed to obtain annual age-adjusted incidence estimates.

Variance of the standardized rates was estimated assuming a Poisson distribution of events, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Wald method based on the variance of the directly standardized rate. Incidence rates were expressed per 100 or 100,000 person years, as appropriate.

PWID were further stratified into groups based on the presence or absence of advanced renal involvement. Advanced renal involvement was defined as chronic kidney disease stage ≥3b together with either amyloidosis or nephrotic syndrome. These groups were matched and secondary outcomes were assessed. Missing data were not explicitly imputed; analyses were conducted using available data within the TriNetX platform. Detailed extraction templates, including the ICD-10 and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes used to define exposures, outcomes, and exclusions, are provided in the Supplementary Material. Measures

Incidence rates and proportions as well as cumulative prevalence of CKD3+, proteinuria, and amyloidosis were calculated. Follow-up for outcome ascertainment began at the time of amyloid kidney disease diagnosis. The primary outcome was a composite of dialysis initiation or all-cause mortality in the 5-year period after the diagnosis of amyloid kidney disease. Secondary outcomes included dialysis initiation alone, all-cause mortality alone, skin/soft tissue or osteoarticular infection, catheter and line associated blood stream infection(CLABSI), and deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and acute care utilization any time after diagnosis. Patients with the corresponding outcome before cohort entry were excluded from that outcome specific risk set. Dialysis was identified using CPT codes for hemodialysis procedures. All-cause mortality was captured through EHR death records. Acute care encounters were identified through HL7 Version 3 visit type codes mapped through the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS), with emergency department encounters defined as EMR, inpatient hospitalizations as ACUTE or IMP, observation encounters as OBSENC, short-stay or same-day procedural encounters as SS, and routine outpatient visits as NONAC.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and categorical variables as number (%), as appropriate. Baseline characteristics were compared between PWID and non-PWID amyloid CKD groups using independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Temporal trends in incidence rates were evaluated using log-linear regression models to estimate annual percent change (APC). Final trends were summarized using log-linear models, with 95% confidence intervals derived from the standard errors of the regression coefficients assuming a Poisson distribution.

To control baseline differences between study cohorts, we performed 1:1 propensity-score matching (PSM) using greedy nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 times the pooled standard deviation of the linear propensity scores. Variables included in the propensity matching were age, sex, HIV diagnosis, liver cirrhosis, heart failure, hypertension, diabetes and prescription of medication for opioid use disorder(MOUD) at baseline. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with SMD <0.1 indicating adequate balance.

Following PSM, adjusted outcomes were compared between cohorts. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used for survival analysis. Cox proportional hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to compare outcomes over the follow-up period. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using integrated R for statistical computing on the TriNetX platform.

As a sensitivity analysis, E-values were calculated to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding on the observed associations. Larger E-values indicate that stronger unmeasured confounding would be required to explain away the observed effect estimates.

Results

Analysis of EHR data from TriNetX demonstrated a steady increase in the age-adjusted incidence of AA amyloid kidney disease among PWID over the study period. Incidence increased from 146 per 100,000 person-years 112, 180) in 2018 to 238 per 100,000 person-years (190, 286) in 2025. Rates rose gradually over time, with more pronounced increases after 2021 and the highest levels observed in 2024–2025, corresponding to an annual percent change (APC) of 6.8% (4.1, 9.6) (Fig. 2). In secondary analyses there was no significant difference in the hazard of skin and soft tissue or osteoarticular infections between groups (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.81, 1.51). Likewise, the hazard of sepsis did not differ significantly (HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.87, 1.68) In contrast, PWID with renal involvement had a modestly increased hazard of venous thromboembolism or central line–associated bloodstream infection (HR 1.69, 95% CI 1.04, 2.76), although absolute event rates remained low, with 5-year event-free survival of 92.5% vs 95.1%. The proportional hazards assumption was not violated for any of these outcomes (all P > 0.05) (Table 2B).

Discussion

The study characterized rising trends in amyloid kidney disease and associated dialysis/mortality outcomes in PWID. Our inquiry originated from a recently noted rising trend in cases at our own institution in the past 4 years. Published literature is dominated by case reports, single center case series, studies of small sample size, diagnosis late in clinical course or autopsy studies3,8,10,11,15. We found that the trend of amyloid kidney disease diagnosis is rising in PWID with a more recent inflection point around 2021 suggesting a higher rate of recognition, as well as higher rate of mortality and dialysis initiation beginning 2024 which may reflect the rate of progression of kidney disease in amyloid kidney disease among PWID. Amyloid kidney disease is often associated with relentless progression to end stage renal disease and dependence on dialysis (Sharma et al., 2018). We also show that PWID with amyloid kidney disease had a higher mortality risk than those without renal involvement. Clinical outcomes of PWID who transition to dialysis dependence are extremely poor and characterized by difficulties adhering to the demands of the dialysis regimen and high mortality (Scott et al., 2018).

Notably, we did not observe significant differences in the incidence of skin and soft tissue infections or sepsis between groups. However, PWID with renal involvement had a higher prevalence of ABSSSI at baseline, suggesting a greater antecedent infectious burden before cohort entry. (Table 1) This finding supports the hypothesis that chronic or recurrent infections may contribute to sustained inflammatory activation and serum amyloid A production, driving the development and progression of AA amyloidosis. These observations must also be interpreted in the context of evolving structural drivers, including changes in the illicit drug supply, particularly the emergence of xylazine, which has been associated with more severe and chronic wounds (Laurano et al., 2025), as well as disruptions in healthcare access and shifts in drug use patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic (Feder et al., 2023). The rising incidence may be reflective of downstream renal manifestations of inflammatory insults which occurred in the preceding years. This interpretation is supported by the temporal increase in detection of xylazine and xylazine associated wounds in the post 2019–2020 period (Laurano et al., 2025; Cano et al., 2024; Friedman et al., 2022).

While there was a modest increase in thrombotic or catheter-associated complications among those with renal involvement, the low absolute event rates suggest limited clinical impact, reinforcing that adverse outcomes in this population are largely mediated through kidney disease progression.

There are several limitations to our data. We used a pragmatic case definition based on diagnostic coding, which may not distinguish biopsy confirmed AA amyloidosis from other amyloid subtypes or causes of nephrotic syndrome. However, internal manual chart review of patients meeting the electronic eligibility criteria demonstrated a positive predictive value of 83.5% for biopsy-confirmed AA amyloidosis, supporting reasonable specificity of the phenotype while acknowledging the potential for residual misclassification. Additionally, the approach to identify PWID as studied by Janjua et al., identifies substance use disorders does have limitations in specificity in misclassifying route of administration, potentially including non-injection drug use. Electronic health record data may suffer from errors and variability in documentation at data entry level, incomplete records, lack important information on potential determinants or nuance of clinical diagnoses or confounders which may not be captured in structured fields or diagnoses codes. Without manual chart review clinical outcomes are inferred based on occurrence of diagnosis or procedure codes, and this limits ability to validate outcomes definitively. The TriNetX database selects cases based on health care system encounters, and PWID may face significant challenges in accessing care within traditional systems due to structural, systemic, and individual barriers. Important clinical barriers like severity of amyloid kidney disease, socioeconomic factors, duration or type of substance use, and access to care are not fully captured in TriNetX. The data in TriNetX could also leave some patient populations with amyloid kidney disease, particularly uninsured patients or those outside of participating systems. The TriNetX system also does not reliably provide laboratory markers, staging details or specific treatment regimens which limit the ability to stratify by disease severity or therapeutic context.

Additionally, since data is updated continuously and may be refreshed depending on uploading of HCO source data, data numbers can vary with successive retrievals. This data was also queried during the novel coronavirus 19 pandemic, which could have led some patients to seek healthcare later than they would if there was no pandemic. Additionally, we recognize that pathogenesis of reactive systemic amyloidosis may differ from other causes of proteinuria and chronic kidney disease in PWID, which could include comorbid diabetes, HIV or infectious hepatitis. However, the trends indicate proportional rise in the diagnosis of higher stages of CKD/proteinuria which is not explained necessarily by any known trends in rates of identified secondary risk factors.

The advantages of this approach include access to a large comprehensive set of real-world contemporaneous data from diverse sources which cannot be done realistically with claims based or other administrative datasets which may be fragmented due to regional variation, limited outcome measures or selection bias. TriNetX has been used to understand risk factors, trends and outcomes related to COVID-19 and was able to provide valuable real time data about these conditions (Janjua et al., 2018; Laurano et al., 2025; Feder et al., 2023; Palchuk et al., 2023; Ganatra et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). These data about an emergent trend are fairly current, and provide potential actionable cues to direct future research or targeted clinical interventions.

Finally, although amyloid kidney disease remains a relatively rare condition, our findings underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for renal involvement among PWID- particularly in those with chronic wounds or subtle clinical signs such as edema or hypertension (Narayanan et al., 2024). These encounters may represent critical opportunities for early recognition and intervention to modify risk factors and potentially alter disease trajectory. Future research should prioritize the identification of biomarkers that can predict the onset of amyloid kidney disease in the setting of chronic inflammation, enabling earlier detection and intervention. Additionally, studies examining genetic and environmental determinants of susceptibility may help explain why only a subset of PWID develop amyloid kidney disease. There is also a need to leverage longitudinal electronic health data, including infection events and substance use patterns, to develop predictive models capable of identifying individuals at highest risk and enabling preemptive, targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Amyloid kidney disease and related renal failure can have devastating consequences for PWID and trends indicate a similar rising incidence of this diagnosis. Patients with substance use disorder and amyloid kidney disease experience significantly higher odds of adverse clinical outcomes, and mortality as compared to those who do not have renal involvement. Clinicians who provide care for PWID should be aware of this condition and risk factors. Early recognition and intervention may play a role in prevention of progression to end stage renal failure.
