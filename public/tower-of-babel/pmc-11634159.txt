Using computed tomography to evaluate anatomic landmarks in Taiwanese trauma patients for insertion of resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chih-Ying Chien, Yueh-Lin Lee, Mei-Jy Jeng, Chia-Jen Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Hemorrhage, particularly from noncompressible torso hemorrhage (NCTH) in the abdominopelvic region, is a leading cause of preventable trauma deaths. Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), designed for aortic occlusion, has emerged as a tool for temporary hemorrhage control in recent years. However, attaining optimal REBOA placement in diverse demographic groups, such as Asian populations, may pose challenges owing to unique anatomical and physiological differences.Materials and methods:This retrospective study analyzed trauma patients who underwent torso computed tomography (CT) at tertiary hospitals in Taiwan from January 2014 to January 2017. The OsiriX software was used to measure the endovascular lengths in the CT images.Results:Among the 223 patients, the median vascular lengths and body measurements were higher in males. The optimal fixed insertion length was identified as 47.5 cm for zone 1 with 99.43% accuracy and 25.5 cm with 82.1% accuracy for zone 3. The landmark-guided method showed 100% accuracy for zone 1 when using the mid-sternum and 94.6% for zone 3 with the umbilicus as the guide with the distance between the umbilicus, xiphoid process, and pubic ramus. External validation confirmed the accuracy of the landmark-guided method.Conclusions:For zone 1 occlusions, a range of 44–48 cm insertion length cross-referenced with the mid-sternal landmark is recommended. For zone 3 occlusions, using the umbilicus as a guide with the distance between the umbilicus, xiphoid process, and pubic ramus provided the highest accuracy.

Hemorrhage remains the principal cause of preventable mortality in trauma patients, with noncompressible torso hemorrhage (NCTH) from the abdominopelvic region being a significant contributor to combat fatalities1–5. Although external compression methods, such as tourniquets, have improved survival in extremity hemorrhage, NCTH presents unique challenges that necessitate rapid hemostasis and resuscitation4. Emergency resuscitative thoracotomy (RT) with aortic cross-clamping is a traditional intervention for proximal blood flow control in cases of profound shock; however, its invasiveness and resource intensity limit its applicability6. Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) has emerged as a less invasive alternative, offering temporary aortic occlusion to mitigate hemorrhagic shock, particularly below the diaphragm7.

The inception of intra-aortic balloon occlusion (IABO) dates back to the Korean War, evolving from a rudimentary intervention to a sophisticated procedure with the advent of endovascular technology8. The procedural refinement of REBOA has been significant, with the demarcation of aortic zones for targeted occlusion. The aorta is divided into three zones for the purpose of stopping bleeding at the target source (Fig. 1): zone 1 encompasses the descending thoracic aorta, located between the origin of the left subclavian artery and the celiac artery; zone 2 is the paravisceral aorta, situated between the celiac artery and the lowest renal artery; zone 3 comprises infrarenal abdominal aorta, located between the lowest renal artery and the aortic bifurcation9. If the hemorrhage is from the intrabdominal organs, the REBOA could be inserted in zone 1. If the bleeding source is from pelvic fractures or junctional femoral injury, the REBOA could be inserted in zone 3 to avoid visceral organ ischemia (Supplemental Figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D418). The REBOA insertion involves accessing the common femoral artery with a vascular sheath. The REBOA catheter is then introduced through the sheath and guided to the target aortic zone (zone 1 or zone 3) typically under image guidance. The balloon is inflated to occlude the aorta, temporarily controlling hemorrhage. Once definitive hemorrhage control is achieved, the balloon is carefully deflated, and the catheter and sheath are removed by compression or with vascular repair performed as needed based on the sheath size. Additionally, the development of predictive models for catheter insertion without fluoroscopic guidance has enhanced its clinical feasibility9–14. Despite these advancements, the accuracy of REBOA placement, especially in zone 3, remains a concern, with the current literature suggesting a need for further validation across diverse populations and anatomical considerations13–18.

The majority of existing research on REBOA placement has been conducted on Western subjects; therefore, there is a paucity of studies focusing on Asian demographics, where BMI averages and body habitus differ significantly. These variations may influence the correlation between anatomical landmarks and endovascular lengths, potentially affecting the accuracy of REBOA placement18.

Our study aimed to address these gaps by validating fluoroscopy-free REBOA catheter insertion methods in an Asian population and identifying a superior approach to enhance insertion accuracy. Specifically, we used computed tomography (CT) scans to measure endovascular lengths and correlate these with external anatomic landmarks. We validated the fixed-length method for REBOA insertion; assessed variable methods that incorporate torso extent, body height, and weight as dependent variables for REBOA placement; and evaluated the reliability of external anatomic landmarks for guiding REBOA insertion in zones 1 and 3. In this study, we endeavored to contribute to the optimization of REBOA deployment in critical care settings, ultimately improving outcomes for patients suffering from traumatic hemorrhage.

Materials and methods

Study setting and population

This retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary teaching hospital with a capacity of 1089 beds, designated as an advanced emergency responsibility center. We included trauma patients aged ≥16 years who underwent either enhanced or nonenhanced torso CT scans in the emergency department (ED) between January 2014 and January 2017. All imaging was performed using a 16-slice multidetector CT scanner (BrightSpeed Elite) with contiguous 5 mm axial slices. We also included patients who underwent torso CT at another tertiary teaching hospital from December 2020 to April 2021 for external validation (Fig. 2). This work has been reported in line with the ‘Strengthening the Reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in Surgery’ (STROCSS) criteria19 (Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D419).

Flowchart of patients included. CT, computed tomography.

Study design and data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records (EMR), including sex, age, trauma mechanism, vital signs, injury severity score (ISS), body weight (BW), body height (BH), BMI, and body surface area (BSA). OsiriX software (Osirix MD, version 14.0, Pixmeo)20 was used to analyze the CT scans. Three-dimensional (3D) curved-multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) images facilitated the measurement of centerline arterial distances from the common femoral artery (CFA) at the level of the femoral head midpoint (Fig. 3). Key anatomical landmarks were identified, including the aortic bifurcation (AB), renal artery origins, celiac axis (CA), and left subclavian artery (LSA). Vascular lengths were calculated at these correlated points (Fig. 3C). We measured the distance from the skin to the CFA at the level of the mid-femoral head, assuming an entry angle of 45°, and included this in the total insertion length. The length of the aortic balloon was estimated to be 4 cm.

Statistical analysis

Sample size analysis for external validation was conducted using the umbilicus model with XuUpRatio and the fixed effect model by McNemar test via G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf). With an α-value of 0.05 (two-tailed) and a power of 90%, the odds ratio was 4.737, the proportion of discordant pairs was 0.24, and the calculated sample size was 95.

Continuous variables that were not normally distributed were described using the median and interquartile ranges (IQR). Simple linear regression was utilized to evaluate the correlation between dependent and independent variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 3.6.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Demographic data

A total of 292 patients were included initially. After excluding the patients who had only a chest CT scan, as well as those diagnosed at an age under 16, a total of 223 patients were included. The length from skin access to the aortic bifurcation on the right side was found to be, on average, 0.26 cm greater than that of the left side (Table 1). Among the patients, median vascular length, BH, BW, BMI, and BSA were observed to be higher in males compared to females (Table 1, Table 2). The median length of zone 1 was measured at 24.06 [22.64–25.42] cm, zone 2 at 2.94 [2.46–3.63] cm, and zone 3 at 9.95 [9.29–10.76] cm. For all zones of the aorta, the measurements in male patients were longer than those in female patients (Table 1).

Fixed catheter-length insertion method

We tried different insertion lengths for zone 1 and zone 3 to assess the accuracy. A previous study recommends inserting the catheter to lengths of 46 cm for zone 1 and 27 cm for zone 3, respectively21. As depicted in Figure 5, an insertion length of 46 cm for zone 1 resulted in very few plots falling within the balloon range, indicating good accuracy. In contrast, an insertion length of 27 cm for zone 3 produced numerous plots within the balloon area, suggesting that the accuracy was not as high as that for zone 1.

Plots from fixed catheter insertion length 46 cm for zone 1 (A) and 27 cm for zone 3 (B), which used a 4 cm-length balloon. The red plot is the cranial edge, and the black plot is the caudal edge of the zone. The wide gray line is the balloon range.

Our results reveal that the error rate of fixed insertion length methods for zone 1 is significantly lower than that for zone 3. The optimal insertion length for zone 1 was 47.5 cm, with an error rate of 0.57%. However, the error rate was only 1.15% when the insertion lengths were between 44 and 48 cm. In contrast, the error rate of zone 3 insertion was significantly higher than that for zone 1. The optimal length for zone 3 was identified as 25.5 cm, with an error rate of 17.9% in our study. The error rate ranged from 17.9 to 22.9% for the insertion lengths between 25 and 27 cm.

Variable catheter-length insertion method

Multiple independent variables, including BSA, BMI, BH, BW, TE, and age, were applied for the linear regression model to predict the central point of zone 3. We found BW is the best single variable predictor (Table 3). According to the linear regression model with BW as the predictor, we formulated the equation for zone 3 as follows: length (cm)=18.9+0.11×BW (kg). We validated the equation, which revealed an accuracy of 90.13% for zone 3 (Fig. 6).

Anatomic landmark guide-insertion method

A previous study confirmed that if we use the mid-sternum landmark for REBOA balloon placement in zone 1, the likelihood of balloon deployment in the landing zone will be 100%16. We measured the midpoint of the straight line between the xiphoid process and the sternal notch. The vascular length at the corresponding level on the aorta was recorded (Supplemental Figure 2, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D420). After analysis, the accuracy rate of using the mid-sternum method as a guide for balloon placement in zone 1 was 100%.

We measured the point 5.5 cm from the umbilicus to the head. The vascular length at the corresponding level on the aorta was recorded. After analysis, we found that the accuracy rate of using this point, 5.5 cm above the umbilicus towards the head, as a guide for balloon placement in zone 3, was 88.3%.

The distance between the xiphoid process and the umbilicus (Xu) and the distance between the umbilicus and the pubic symphysis (Up) were recorded. We found that the ratio of the xiphoid-umbilicus distance to the umbilicus-pubic distance (Xu/Up) was a good predictor for the distance from the aortic level at the umbilicus to the midpoint of zone 3 (Supplemental Figure 3, Supplemental Digital Content 4, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D421). Compared to BW, BH, TE, age, and BMI, Xu/Up is the best predictor for the distance from the aortic level at the umbilicus to the midpoint of zone 3 (Table 3). We used Xu/Up as the dependent variable and the distance from the aortic level at the umbilicus to the midpoint of zone 3 as the independent variable to perform a simple linear regression, leading to the equation: ‘The length from the umbilicus to the head’=−2 + 6×(Xu/Up). If we insert the catheter using the umbilicus as a reference landmark for zone 3, according to this equation, the accuracy will be 94.6%.

Validation of the equation from a different database

To validate the equation for zone 3 insertion with respect to the aorta at the level of this point, we retrospectively obtained image data from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) dataset at another tertiary teaching hospital from December 2020 to April 2021. Adult patients who underwent chest and abdominal CT were included. After excluding two patients with aortic dissection and one patient with poor image quality, 97 patients were ultimately enrolled in the study. The accuracy rate was found to be 95.88% according to this equation. The accuracy rate of using the point 5.5 cm from the umbilicus to the head as a reference for balloon placement in zone 3 was 89.69%. The accuracy was only 78.35% if the insertion length was fixed by 27 cm for zone 3 insertion. Similarly, the umbilicus guide method, which uses the equation ‘The length from the umbilicus to the head’=−2 + 6×(Xu/Up), provided the highest accuracy for determining the aortic insertion point at this level.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized 3D curved-multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) images from CT scans processed with OsiriX software to evaluate the optimal insertion lengths for the REBOA catheter. There are several methods to guide the insertion lengths for REBOA catheter. First, using the fixed method, it is suggested to insert the catheter to a depth of 46 cm for zone 1 occlusion. For zone 3 occlusion, insertion to a depth of 27 cm is recommended21. Our findings suggest that the fixed insertion length method—46 cm for zone 1 and 27 cm for zone 3—is less reliable for zone 3, with our optimal lengths being 47.5 cm for zone 1 (an error rate of 0.57%) and 25.5 cm for zone 3 (an error rate of 17.9%). Notably, zone 1 occlusions had a significantly lower error rate than zone 3 occlusions, potentially due to the shorter median length of zone 3 (9.95 cm) compared to zone 1 (24.06 cm).

Second, we employed multiple variables in a linear regression model to predict the midpoints of zones 1 and 3. Body weight was the variable with the highest R-squared value. Consequently, we formulate an equation based on these findings. For zone 1, the accuracy of this equation was nearly 100%, mirroring the results obtained using the fixed insertion method. More significantly, for zone 3, the equation enhanced the accuracy of insertion, reducing the error rate to 9.87% compared to the 17.9% error rate associated with the fixed method.

Third, the use of the mid-sternum as an external anatomical landmark for determining the optimal balloon location for zone 1 insertion proved to be 100% accurate, consistent with findings from previous studies16,17. For zone 3, employing the umbilicus as the external landmark appears to surpass the fixed method in terms of accuracy. When the balloon was placed in the aorta at a point of 5.5 cm cephalad from the umbilicus, the accuracy reached 88.3%. This accuracy can be further enhanced to 94.6% by employing the equation ‘The length from the umbilicus to the head’=−2 + 6×(Xu/Up) at this specific aortic level. Notably, the Xu/Up ratio emerged as the most reliable predictor for the distance from the aortic level at the umbilicus to the midpoint of zone 3.

For zone 1 occlusions, the accuracy of the fixed method was comparable to that of the external landmark or variable methods. We recommend a fixed insertion range of 44–48 cm, cross-referenced with the mid-sternal landmark. For zone 3 occlusions, the accuracy is less than in zone 1, possibly because the aortic segment in zone 3 is significantly shorter than in zone 1 (9.95 cm vs. 24.06 cm), making it easier for REBOA placement to exceed the intended range in zone 3. In addition, Attwell et al.22 revealed the umbilicus tends to lie significantly lower than the aortic bifurcation in men over the age of 65 with increased central abdominal adiposity. Using the umbilicus guide alone may still encounter some variation; therefore, we add the ratio of Xu/Up to provide a more accurate fluoroscopy-free method than other approaches, as confirmed by external validation.

In our study, we measured iliac tortuosity, which was found to be more pronounced in the elderly group. Iliac tortuosity was quantified by calculating the ratio of the vascular centerline distance to the straight-line distance from the aortic bifurcation to the common femoral artery entry point. A ratio greater than 1.5 was considered indicative of moderate tortuosity. In our cohort, 25.6% of patients were older than 60 years. The median right iliac tortuosity in the general population was 1.095, whereas that in the elderly subgroup was 1.21. Notably, only two patients exhibited iliac tortuosity exceeding 1.5. Consequently, iliac tortuosity did not significantly affect the outcomes of our study.

Eliason JL and colleagues conducted an analysis of computed tomography morphometry and found that using a standard REBOA catheter insertion length of 48 cm for zone 1 yielded an impressively low error rate of 0.6%, which is optimal18. Despite this, they noted a substantial residual error rate for the zone 3 placements. They suggested that positioning the balloon catheter an additional 5.5 cm beyond the distance from the umbilicus to the xiphoid process could reduce the zone 3 insertion error rate to below 10%. They also proposed that redesigning the lengths of existing catheter balloons or reconsidering the relative risks associated with placing the balloon catheter too low or too high might be necessary. In our study, we observed an error rate of 11.2% using their method. However, by applying the equation ‘The length from the umbilicus to the head=−2 + 6× (Xu/Up),’ we were able to reduce the error rate to 5.4%. This equation accurately measures the distance to the aorta at the level of this anatomical landmark.

Weng et al.23 conducted a retrospective analysis of CT angiograms from 57 subjects, determining that the midpoint between the xiphoid process and the umbilicus could serve as a reliable landmark for the rapid placement of REBOA in zone 3 in individuals of normal weight. The study population was Asian, but the sample size was low. The accuracy of this method ranged from 84.2 to 86%, which was lower than that observed in our study.

Compared to studies focusing on using CT morphology to simulate REBOA insertion methods (Table 4), our study demonstrates that a fixed insertion method for zone 1 is highly accurate, achieving 98–99% accuracy. For zone 3 insertion, our study found that the accuracy of the variable catheter-length insertion method based on torso extent was 78.7%, which did not perform as well as other studies mentioned13,15. We discovered that using the umbilicus as a guide, combined with the measurements between the umbilicus, xiphoid process, and pubic ramus, provided the highest accuracy.

In the most recent randomized controlled trial examining the use of REBOA in trauma patients with catastrophic hemorrhage, combining REBOA with standard emergency department care did not reduce but potentially increased mortality compared to standard care alone24. The procedure in this study took an average of 19 min. Shorter procedure times could potentially improve outcomes. Consequently, devising an efficient and straightforward method for balloon placement is crucial. Our study introduced a highly accurate technique for placing REBOA in zone 3 without fluoroscopy, which may reduce the overall procedure time.

This study had several limitations. First, trauma patients experiencing severe shock in extremis status without response to resuscitation may have been lacking in this series due to bypass CT. Second, the resolution of the 16-slice multidetector CT may not be optimal; high-resolution multidetector CT may be warranted in future studies. Third, the patient cohort consisted primarily of Taiwanese individuals; therefore, the findings may not be directly applicable to populations with different ethnic backgrounds. Fourth, the location of the umbilicus can vary, particularly in obese patients, making its use as a guide for insertion a point of caution. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the average BMI in our study population was 23.07, which is lower than the average BMI of 28.31 reported in Western countries25. Thus, the impact of obesity may be less pronounced in Asian populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, for zone 1 occlusion, the fixed method is nearly as accurate as the external landmark or the variable methods based on body weight. We recommend inserting the catheter to a depth of 44–48 cm and correlating this range with the mid-sternum as a simultaneous check. For zone 3 occlusion, we propose using the umbilicus as a guide, applying the formula ‘The length from the umbilicus to the head’=−2 + 6× (Xu/Up) to determine the insertion length to the aorta at the corresponding level.
