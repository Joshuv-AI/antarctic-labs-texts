Game-Theoretic Safe Multi-Agent Motion Planning with Reachability Analysis for Dynamic and Uncertain Environments (Extended Version)
Wenbin Mai, Minghui Liwang, Xinlei Yi, Xiaoyu Xia, Seyyedali Hosseinalipour, Xianbin Wang

Abstract
Ensuring safe, robust, and scalable motion planning for multi-agent systems in dynamic and uncertain environments is a persistent challenge, driven by complex inter-agent interactions, stochastic disturbances, and model uncertainties. To overcome these challenges, particularly the computational complexity of coupled decision-making and the need for proactive safety guarantees, we propose a Reachability-Enhanced Dynamic Potential Game (RE-DPG) framework, which integrates game-theoretic coordination into reachability analysis. This approach formulates multi-agent coordination as a dynamic potential game, where the Nash equilibrium (NE) defines optimal control strategies across agents. To enable scalability and decentralized execution, we develop a Neighborhood-Dominated iterative Best Response (ND-iBR) scheme, built upon an iterated ε \footnotesize\varepsilon -BR (i ε \footnotesize\varepsilon -BR) process that guarantees finite-step convergence to an ε \footnotesize\varepsilon -NE. This allows agents to compute strategies based on local interactions while ensuring theoretical convergence guarantees. Furthermore, to ensure safety under uncertainty, we integrate a Multi-Agent Forward Reachable Set (MA-FRS) mechanism into the cost function, explicitly modeling uncertainty propagation and enforcing collision avoidance constraints. Through both simulations and real-world experiments in 2D and 3D environments, we validate the effectiveness of RE-DPG across diverse operational scenarios.



I
Introduction

Recent advancements in robotics, artificial intelligence, and control methods have led to the widespread use of multi-agent autonomous systems in warehouse logistics
[
1
]
, autonomous driving
[
2
]
, and drone coordination
[
3
,
4
]
. These systems operate under a shared paradigm where agents pursue goals while adapting to their environment and interacting with each other. An illustration of this interplay can be found in automated warehouse systems, where heterogeneous fleets of autonomous mobile robots must simultaneously navigate without collisions and manage dynamic tasks (e.g., prioritizing urgent orders) within constrained operational spaces.

Nevertheless, real-world environments are inherently dynamic and stochastic, where external disturbances or model inaccuracies pose significant challenges to safe motion planning. Such uncertainties can lead to safety-critical situations, especially in densely populated settings where small trajectory deviations can cause cascading collisions. To ensure reliable operation, motion planning frameworks must address three key requirements:
(i)
explicit modeling of inter-agent interactions,
(ii)
scalability as the number of agents increases, and
(iii)
ensuring safety under environmental uncertainties. Meeting these requirements presents several interconnected challenges, three instances of which are of our interest in this work:

\footnotesize\bullet

Challenge 1: Interaction Modeling.

In multi-agent systems, behavior of each agent depends on its own goal and the evolving influence of neighboring agents. Capturing these dynamic couplings leads to significant computational complexity, and often renders the motion planning problem NP-hard
[
5
]
.

\footnotesize\bullet

Challenge 2: Scalability under Structural Coupling.

Decomposing multi-agent planning into smaller subproblems is essential for scalability. However, agents share a common environment and are constrained by inter-agent safety requirements, which introduce structural coupling that complicates decentralized coordination as the system scales.

\footnotesize\bullet

Challenge 3: Uncertainty-Aware Safety Assurance.

In uncertain environments, deviations from nominal trajectories can lead to unsafe proximity or collisions. Ensuring robust safety thus requires modeling the propagation of disturbances and defining safety margins that remain effective under model errors and environmental variations.

Addressing the above challenges forms the core motivation of our work. To this end, we propose a multi-agent motion planning methodology named
Reachability-Enhanced Dynamic Potential Game (RE-DPG)
, which integrates reachability analysis into dynamic potential game theory. This approach is designed to enable interaction-aware, safety assuring, and scalable motion planning in dynamic and uncertain environments.

Relevant Investigations

I-A

Game-based Multi-Agent Motion Planning

Game-theoretic frameworks provide a natural foundation for modeling strategic interactions among multiple agents
[
6
]
. A central goal in these formulations is identifying a
Nash equilibrium (NE)
, a stable solution where no agent can unilaterally improve its outcome by altering its strategy.
Among the diverse classes of games,
dynamic potential games (DPGs)
have emerged as particularly advantageous due to their mathematical properties, which guarantee the existence of computationally tractable NE solutions
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. These games offer efficient NE computation by aligning individual agent objectives with a
global potential function
, thereby transforming the multi-agent coordination problems into structured optimization formulations.

In this domain, several studies have focused on centralized solvers for computing NE in DPGs. For example,
Bhatt et al.
formulated a constrained optimal control problem, where solving it yields the NE for DPGs
[
10
,
11
]
. However, centralized approaches suffer from scalability issues, as their computational demands grow with the number of agents. Additionally, these methods rely on idealized assumptions, such as
perfect rationality
(i.e., agents are presumed to make fully optimal decisions with complete information and unlimited computational capacity), limiting their applicability in real-world settings, where agents face
bounded rationality
(i.e., agents make decisions based on limited information and computational constraints) and dynamic uncertainties.

To address scalability and real-world uncertainties, recent studies have explored
decentralized paradigms
that decompose coordination into localized decisions. These methods leverage agent-level computation and local communication to approximate Nash equilibria (NE) more efficiently. For example,
Williams et al.

[
12
]
used interaction graphs to cluster agents for NE search;
Wang et al.

[
13
]
applied iterative Best Response (iBR) for convergence refinement; and
Cui et al.

[
14
]
adopted iBR in a Nash-Stackelberg racing framework. However, these works focus mainly on coordination and convergence, with limited treatment of
safety
or
robustness
, motivating the need for decentralized approaches that are both scalable and safety-aware under uncertainty.

I-A
2
Safety-Aware Motion Planning under Uncertainty

In the domain of safety-aware motion planning, probabilistic approaches are often used to handle uncertainty through stochastic modeling. Recent methods
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
focus on uncertainty propagation in dynamic systems to generate risk-sensitive trajectories for single-agent scenarios, incorporating probabilistic representations of disturbances and model errors for formal safety guarantees. However, extending these methods to multi-agent systems remains challenging due to the computational burden of propagating uncertainties across coupled agents.

Another widely adopted strategy is Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis, which computes forward reachable sets (FRSs) — the set of all possible states an agent can reach under bounded disturbances — to enhance robustness in uncertain environments
[
18
]
. For example,
Seo et al.
used ellipsoidal bounds for error state FRSs, enabling real-time uncertainty-aware trajectory optimization for collision avoidance
[
19
,
20
]
. While effective in single-agent settings, these methods face challenges in multi-agent systems due to inter-agent coupling. Specifically, the complexity of jointly evaluating reachable sets across multiple agents limits the direct application of HJ reachability techniques in dense multi-agent settings.

Beyond traditional analytical methods, learning-based approaches have recently shown promise for safe multi-agent motion planning under uncertainty
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. These methods employ
data-driven models
to approximate intricate safety constraints or value functions, thereby enabling greater adaptability in unstructured or dynamic environments. Nonetheless, their effectiveness is strongly contingent upon the quality and representativeness of the training data. Moreover, establishing rigorous theoretical guarantees for safety remains challenging, which raises concerns regarding their dependability when deployed in safety-critical real-world systems.

In this work, we address
multi-agent motion planning in dynamic and uncertain environments, focusing primarily on ensuring safety
. To this end, we propose the

To the best of our knowledge, this work represents an initial attempt to integrate DPG with formal reachability-based safety guarantees for multi-agent motion planning under uncertainty, and to explore the distinctive properties of such integration. II-B

Overview and Key Modeling Components

In a nutshell, multi-agent motion planning centers on developing optimal control strategies for multiple agents operating collaboratively within a shared environment. The specific motion planning problem addressed in this work involves generating control inputs that guide agents from their initial to target states, while ensuring both time efficiency and robustness to achieve collision-free navigation in dynamic settings.

\footnotesize\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}

\footnotesize\mathcal{U}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
(e.g., acceleration commands, steering angles, or thrust forces applied to the agent), where
\footnotesize n_{x}
and
\footnotesize n_{u}
are the respective dimensions of these spaces. The joint state space and control input space across the agents are denoted by
\footnotesize\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{x}}
and
\footnotesize\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{U}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{u}}

\footnotesize t
, the state vector of agent
\footnotesize i

\footnotesize x_{i}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}

\footnotesize u_{i}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}

\footnotesize\boldsymbol{u}^{(t)}=(u_{1}^{(t)},\cdots,u_{N}^{(t)})\in\mathcal{U}

III
Safe Multi-Agent (MA) Motion Planning via Integrating Forward Reachable Set (FRS) into Dynamic Potential Game (DPG)

In this section, we present a solution for multi-agent motion planning, focusing on safety and robustness through a hybrid approach combining the DPG framework with an MA-FRS mechanism. We begin by formulating a DPG-based motion planning problem to determine safe control inputs over a finite time horizon, considering agent interactions (Sec.
III-A
). To ensure scalability, we then propose the ND-iBR scheme, which decomposes the problem into distributed subproblems, allowing each agent to iteratively compute its strategy and approximate the NE in a decentralized manner (Sec.
III-B
). Afterward, given the uncertainties in the scenario, such as external disturbances and dynamic environmental variations, we focus on modeling uncertainty propagation and inter-agent interactions. To this end, we incorporate MA-FRS-based penalties to enable uncertainty-aware trajectory planning and proactive collision avoidance (Sec.
III-C
), and then demonstrate an approach to reach the NE in our setting of interest (Sec.
III-D
). An overview of our proposed framework is illustrated by Fig.

.

\footnotesize\mathcal{X}

-best response (i
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-BR):
at iteration
\footnotesize k
, choose any agent
\footnotesize i
such that

\small J_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}))-\inf_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}J_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}))~\geq~\varepsilon,

and set
\footnotesize\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1}
as:

\footnotesize\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}J_{i}\big(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},\big(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}\big)\big)

; if no such agent exists, stop.
This process terminates in a finite number of iterations at a profile
\footnotesize\mathbf{u}^{\varepsilon}
that is an
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium, i.e.,

\small J_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{\varepsilon}))~\leq~\inf_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}J_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{\varepsilon}))+\varepsilon,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall i\in\mathcal{N}.

Proof.

Given fixed
\footnotesize k
and
\footnotesize i
, we consider updating only the strategy
\footnotesize\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}
of agent
\footnotesize i
while keeping
\footnotesize\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}
unchanged.
By definition of the best response, we have

\footnotesize J_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}))-J_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}))\;\geq\;0.

Now recall from (
6a
) and (
9
) that each cost admits the decomposition

\footnotesize J_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k})=\Phi(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k})+\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}),

where the potential function is defined by

\footnotesize\Phi(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{-i}):=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}P(\boldsymbol{x}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{u}^{(t)})+R(\boldsymbol{x}^{(T)}),

and the residual
\footnotesize\Psi_{i}
is independent of
\footnotesize\mathbf{u}_{i}
.
Therefore, when only
\footnotesize\mathbf{u}_{i}
is updated, we obtain

\footnotesize J_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k})-J_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k})=\Phi(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k})-\Phi(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{k}).

Thus the potential function
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
is non-increasing across iBR updates.
Under the i
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-BR rule,
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
decreases by at least
\footnotesize\varepsilon
at each update.
Since
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous and the strategy set is compact,
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
has a minimum value
\footnotesize\Phi_{\min}
.
Therefore, the total number of updates is bounded above by
\footnotesize(\Phi(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{0},\mathbf{u}_{-i}^{0})-\Phi_{\min})/\varepsilon
, and the process must terminate in finitely many steps.
At termination, no player can reduce its cost by
\footnotesize\varepsilon
or more, which is exactly the condition of an
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium.

\footnotesize\blacksquare

Remark 1 (Practical sufficiency of
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-NE and local minimum):

The convergence of
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-NE to an exact NE (i.e., when
\footnotesize\varepsilon=0
) is generally not guaranteed. Nevertheless, fixing a small tolerance and terminating i
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-BR at an
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-NE is typically sufficient in practice
[
26
]
.
Additionally, the potential function
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
may admit multiple local minima; the particular one reached by our method depends on the order in which agents update. Obtaining the global minimum would require enumerating all possible update sequences and comparing their values of
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
, which computationally expensive. We therefore accept local minimum of
\footnotesize\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
under a fixed update rule (e.g., ordered by agent index), and as long as the resulting NE avoids collisions, it suffices for safe multi-agent planning.

The preceding analysis assumes global state access, which is often unrealistic in practice. In real-world scenarios, agents rely on local observations from nearby neighbors. We formalize neighborhoods by defining agents
\footnotesize i,j\in\mathcal{N}
as neighbors at time
\footnotesize t
if their distance
\footnotesize d^{(t)}_{i,j}
is less than a proximity threshold
\footnotesize d^{\mathrm{Prox}}\geq 2v^{\mathrm{Max}}\Delta t
, representing the worst-case one-step closing distance. The set of neighbors for agent
\footnotesize i
is then
\footnotesize\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{(t)}_{i}=\{j~|~d^{(t)}_{i,j}<d^{\mathrm{Prox}},j\neq i,j\in\mathcal{N}\}
.
Given this definition of neighborhood, we introduce the Neighborhood-Dominated iterative Best Response (ND-iBR) scheme, where each agent updates its strategy based on the fixed strategies of its local neighbors. Note that while the procedure follows the i
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-BR framework, we retain ND-iBR as the name to highlight its reliance on local interactions. The localized cost function considering only neighbors’ influence is defined as
\footnotesize\tilde{J_{i}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and the ND-iBR update rule is modified to the following i
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-BR-based optimization:

\displaystyle\mspace{-20mu}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\mathcal{P}}}}^{k}_{i}:\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\;\tilde{J}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},\left(\mathbf{u}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{k}_{-i}\right)\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{{(C1)}},\tilde{J}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},\left(\mathbf{u}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{k}_{-i}\right)\right)-\inf_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}\tilde{J}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},\left(\mathbf{v}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{k}_{-i}\right)\right)\geq\varepsilon.

Remark 2 (Neighbor screening and computational benefits):

The proximity constraint
\footnotesize d^{\mathrm{Prox}}\geq 2v^{\mathrm{Max}}\Delta t
serves as a one-step conservative screening: agents beyond this bound cannot reach agent
\footnotesize i
within the next interval, even under worst-case closing speeds with different accelerations or turning rates. Also, neighbor sets
\footnotesize\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{i}^{(t)}
are recomputed at every planning step from the latest nominal predictions, reflecting agents entering or leaving the
\footnotesize d^{\mathrm{Prox}}
threshold. This neighborhood-based approach reduces the number of coupling terms per agent, leading to smaller optimization subproblems without unduly sacrificing coordination performance. Moreover, the coupling term
\footnotesize C_{i,j}^{\mathop{\mathrm{Coup}}}
will be redesigned in Sec.
III-C
to reflect collision-related costs, and the exclusion of non-neighboring agents introduces only negligible approximation error in practice. Consequently,
\footnotesize\tilde{J}_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)
retains the boundedness and monotonicity properties required by Theorem
1
, thereby justifying the use of ND-iBR for computing the
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-NE in a distributed manner.

III-C

Cost Enhancement via Reachability Analysis

Building on the decentralized framework in Sec.
III-B
, we enhance the cost function by incorporating safety through MA-FRS. A key innovation is the introduction of MA-FRSs, which capture uncertainty propagation and dynamic interactions among multiple agents, a largely unexplored aspect in the literature. We add tracking cost and velocity constraints to ensure goal-directed behavior and dynamic limitations. This formulation maintains compatibility with DPG properties while integrating both goal-oriented objectives and interaction-aware constraints for safe multi-agent motion planning. We revise the cost function components in (
6a

\displaystyle\tilde{J_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)},(\mathbf{u}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{-i}))=\sum^{T-1}_{t=0}\tilde{L_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(t)},{u}^{(t)}_{i})+\tilde{L}^{\text{F}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(T)}),

(13)

where we use the following neighborhood-based definitions:

\footnotesize\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(t)},{u}^{(t)}_{i})

\footnotesize\displaystyle=c_{i}^{(t)}+c_{i}^{\text{V},(t)}+\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{(t)}_{i}}c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(t)},

(14a)

\footnotesize\displaystyle\tilde{L}^{\text{F}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(T)})

\footnotesize\displaystyle=c^{\text{F}}_{i}+c_{i}^{\text{V},(T)}+\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{(t)}_{i}}c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(T)}.

(14b)

III-C

Tracking Cost

In (

\footnotesize c_{i}^{(t)}

\footnotesize c_{i}^{\text{F}}

\footnotesize\displaystyle c^{(t)}_{i}

\footnotesize\displaystyle=(x^{(t)}_{i}-r^{(t)}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i}(x^{(t)}_{i}-r^{(t)}_{i})+(u^{(t)}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{R}_{i}(u^{(t)}_{i}),

(15a)

\footnotesize\displaystyle c^{\text{F}}_{i}

\footnotesize\displaystyle=(x^{(T)}_{i}-r^{\text{F}}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\text{F}}_{i}(x^{(T)}_{i}-r^{\text{F}}_{i}),

(15b)

where
\footnotesize\boldsymbol{Q}_{i},\boldsymbol{Q}^{\text{F}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
with
\footnotesize\boldsymbol{Q}_{i},\boldsymbol{Q}^{\text{F}}_{i}\succeq 0
, and
\footnotesize\boldsymbol{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
with
\footnotesize\boldsymbol{R}_{i}\succeq 0

\footnotesize x_{i}^{(t)}
from the reference trajectory
\footnotesize r^{(t)}_{i}
and the control effort
\footnotesize u_{i}^{(t)}

\footnotesize r^{(t)}_{i}

\footnotesize\Delta t
.

III-C
2

The velocity constraint penalty
\footnotesize c_{i}^{\text{V},(t)}
in (

)-(
14b
) is introduced to discourage agents from exceeding a predefined speed limit
\footnotesize v^{\mathrm{Max}}
, defined as:

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\text{V},(t)}=\begin{cases}\infty,&v^{\mathrm{Max}}-\|v_{i}^{(t)}\|\leq 0,\\
0,&v^{\mathrm{Max}}-\|v_{i}^{(t)}\|>0.\end{cases}

(16)

where
\footnotesize v_{i}^{(t)}
denotes the velocity of agent
\footnotesize i
at time step

smooth approximation
by using an
exponential barrier function
with a tunable parameter
\footnotesize\lambda^{\text{V}}
as:

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\text{V},(t)}\approx\exp({-\lambda^{\text{V}}(v^{\mathrm{Max}}-\|v_{i}^{(t)}\|)}).

(17)

III-C
3
Collision Penalty with MA-FRS

To ensure safety under uncertainty, we penalize FRS overlaps via
\footnotesize c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(t)}
and
\footnotesize c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(T)}
in (
14a
)–(
14b

\footnotesize c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(T)}

\footnotesize c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(t)}

(a) MA-FRS Derivation:
For agent
\footnotesize i\in\mathcal{N}
, let its nominal state, nominal control input, disturbance and error state at time step
\footnotesize t
be denoted by
\footnotesize\overline{x}_{i}^{(t)}
,
\footnotesize\overline{u}_{i}^{(t)}
,
\footnotesize{w}_{i}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{w}}
, and
\footnotesize{e}_{i}^{(t)}:={x}_{i}^{(t)}-\overline{x}_{i}^{(t)}
, where
\footnotesize n_{w}
denotes the number of the channels (i.e., independent disturbance components acting along a specific dimension or input direction in the agent’s dynamics) of
\footnotesize{w}_{i}^{(t)}
. We consider the dynamics around
\footnotesize\overline{x}_{i}^{(t)}
and
\footnotesize\overline{u}_{i}^{(t)}
, approximated via first-order linearization as:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}_{i}\left({x}_{i}^{(t)},{u}_{i}^{(t)},{w}_{i}^{(t)}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\approx A_{i}^{(t)}{x}_{i}^{(t)}+B_{i}^{(t)}{u}_{i}^{(t)}+D_{i}^{(t)}{w}_{i}^{(t)},

where
\footnotesize A_{i}^{(t)}
,
\footnotesize B_{i}^{(t)}
, and
\footnotesize D_{i}^{(t)}
are the Jacobian matrices obtained by linearizing the nonlinear dynamics
\footnotesize\tilde{f}_{i}
around
\footnotesize(\overline{x}_{i}^{(t)},\overline{u}_{i}^{(t)},0)
. We assume a bounded disturbance envelope
\footnotesize\|w_{i}^{(t)}\|\leq\bar{w}
, within which the linearization around the nominal trajectory remains valid. The remainder term from the Taylor expansion is assumed to be negligible in this neighborhood.

Let
\footnotesize K_{i}^{(t)}
denote the feedback control gain, where the control policy is
\footnotesize u^{(t)}_{i}=K_{i}^{(t)}{e}_{i}^{(t)}+\overline{u}_{i}^{(t)}
.
To compute the feedback control gain
\footnotesize K_{i}^{(t)}
, we use the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR), a standard method in optimal control based on the Riccati equation
[
27
]
. This provides a reasonable and tractable implementation to obtain the feedback gain. Also, we apply an
ellipsoidal approximation
for the error state FRS, which simplifies the cost calculation by exploiting the tractable intersection condition of ellipsoid geometry. Specifically, the initial error state FRS is denoted as an ellipsoidal set
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(0)}
, centered at the initial state with a positive definite shape matrix
\footnotesize Q^{(0)}_{i}
, following Definition
2
.

To account for external disturbances, we define
\footnotesize\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
as the shape matrix for the disturbance-induced error state FRS. The matrix
\footnotesize\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
is computed by first solving the Lyapunov equation for
\footnotesize\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i,m_{w}}
, which characterizes the
\footnotesize m_{w}
-th disturbance channel:

\displaystyle-\Phi^{(t)}_{i}\left(\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i,m_{w}}-\eta t^{2}I\right)-\left(\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i,m_{w}}-\eta t^{2}I\right){\left(\Phi^{(t)}_{i}\right)}^{\mathsf{T}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\exp(-t\Phi^{(t)}_{i})N_{i,m_{w}}^{(t)}\exp(-{t\Phi^{(t)}_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}})-N_{i,m_{w}}^{(t)}.

The total disturbance-induced FRS shape matrix
\footnotesize\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
is then approximated by the Minkowski sum of individual disturbance channels:
\footnotesize\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i}=\mathop{\boxplus}\limits_{m_{w}=1}^{n_{w}}\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{i,m_{w}}
. The total FRS shape matrix
\footnotesize{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
is obtained by combining and propagating these components through the system dynamics:

\small{Q}^{(t)}_{i}=\exp{\left(t\Phi^{(t)}_{i}\right)}\left(Q_{i}^{(0)}\boxplus\tilde{Q}^{(t)}_{i}\right)\exp\left({t\Phi^{(t)}_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right).

(20)

By applying (
19
)–(
20
) over the planning horizon, we compute the FRS sequence
\footnotesize\{\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(t)}\}_{t=0}^{T}
for each agent
\footnotesize i
.
Each agent’s FRS shape
\footnotesize\tilde{Q}_{i}^{(t)}
is computed via small (
\footnotesize n_{p}{\times}n_{p}
) matrix operations, with
\footnotesize n_{p}
denoting the position dimension.
The total MA-FRS at each time step
\footnotesize t
is then defined as the union
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}^{(t)}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{(t)}
(i.e., the collective reachable space of all agents).

(b) Collision Penalty Calculation:

Before designing the collision penalty, we first introduce the following theorem:

Theorem 2 (Ellipsoidal intersection criterion):

Let
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
be two ellipsoids centered at
\footnotesize c_{1},c_{2}
with shape matrices
\footnotesize Q_{1},Q_{2}
. The two ellipsoids intersect if and only if
\footnotesize c_{2}-c_{1}\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\oplus\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
, where
\footnotesize O
represents the origin.

Proof:
We can translate
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{2}
and center them at
\footnotesize O
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1})=\{x\mid x^{\top}Q_{1}x\leq 1\},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})=\{y\mid y^{\top}Q_{2}y\leq 1\}.

(21)

If
\footnotesize c_{2}-c_{1}\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\oplus\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
, then we have
\footnotesize u\in\mathcal{T}_{O}({\mathcal{E}_{1}})
,
\footnotesize-v\in\mathcal{T}_{O}({\mathcal{E}_{2}})
(equivalent to
\footnotesize v\in\mathcal{T}_{O}({\mathcal{E}_{2}})
due to the symmetry property of
\footnotesize\mathcal{T}_{O}({\mathcal{E}_{2}})
with respect to the origin), and
\footnotesize c_{2}-c_{1}=u+(-v)
. Then, we let
\footnotesize z^{\prime}=u+c_{1}=v+c_{2}
, and obtain:

\displaystyle u=z^{\prime}-c_{1},v=z^{\prime}-c_{2}.

(22)

We use (
22
) in (
21
) and get
\footnotesize(z^{\prime}-c_{1})^{\top}Q_{1}(z^{\prime}-c_{1})\leq 1,~~(z^{\prime}-c_{2})^{\top}Q_{2}(z^{\prime}-c_{2})\leq 1
, meaning that there exists a point
\footnotesize z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\footnotesize z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\footnotesize z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Thus,
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{1}
intersect.

Conversely, if
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{2}
intersect, there exists
\footnotesize z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and:

\displaystyle(z-c_{1})^{\top}Q_{1}(z-c_{1})\leq 1\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt(z-c_{2})^{\top}Q_{2}(z-c_{2})\leq 1.

(23)

Let
\footnotesize p=z-c_{1}
and
\footnotesize q=z-c_{2}
, we have
\footnotesize c_{2}-c_{1}=p+(-q)
.
From (
21
), we know that
\footnotesize p\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
\footnotesize q\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
. Since
\footnotesize\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
is symmetric with respect to the origin
\footnotesize O
,
\footnotesize q\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
is equivalent to
\footnotesize-q\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
. We let
\footnotesize r=-q
, then the condition for intersection can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle c_{2}-c_{1}=p+r,\hskip 9.24994ptp\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1}),\hskip 9.24994ptr\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2}).

(24)

Combining Definition
1
, we get

\displaystyle c_{2}-c_{1}\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\oplus\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2}).

(25)

Thus, we have proven that the two ellipsoids
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{2}
intersect if and only if
\footnotesize c_{2}-c_{1}\in\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\oplus\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
.
\footnotesize\hfill\blacksquare

Solution for computing an

We now proceed to design the collision penalty for safe motion planning, focusing on maintaining separation between agents’ FRSs to prevent collisions. We consider only position-related uncertainties which causes primary collision risks. For each agent
\footnotesize i
, we extract the position vector
\footnotesize p_{i}^{(t)}
from
\footnotesize x_{i}^{(t)}
and define
\footnotesize\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{i,\text{pos}}
as the submatrix of
\footnotesize Q^{(t)}_{i}
corresponding to position dimensions. Therefore, for neighboring agents
\footnotesize i
and
\footnotesize j
, the resulting FRS ellipsoids
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{i,\text{pos}}^{(t)}
and
\footnotesize\mathcal{E}_{j,\text{pos}}^{(t)}
must not intersect at each time step. Based on Theorem
2
and Definition
2
, the following collision avoidance condition can then be derived:

\displaystyle\xi^{(t)}_{i,j}=(p_{i}^{(t)}-p_{j}^{(t)})^{\mathsf{T}}(\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{i,\text{pos}}\boxplus\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{j,\text{pos}})^{-1}(p_{i}^{(t)}-p_{j}^{(t)})-1>0,

(26)

where
\footnotesize\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{i,\text{pos}}\boxplus\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{j,\text{pos}}
is the shape matrix of
\footnotesize\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{i,\text{pos}}^{(t)})\oplus\mathcal{T}_{O}(\mathcal{E}_{j,\text{pos}}^{(t)})
.
Similar to (
16
), we encode this condition using a barrier function, defining the collision penalty
\footnotesize c_{i}^{\text{FRS},(t)}
as:

\small c_{i,j}^{\text{FRS},(t)}\approx\exp({-\lambda^{\text{FRS}}\xi^{(t)}_{i,j}}),

(27)

where
\footnotesize\lambda^{\text{FRS}}
is tunable. These formulations allow us to embed a differentiable, uncertainty-aware collision penalty into the DPG cost structure, preserving tractability while ensuring safety. Alternatively, neighbors can be defined anisotropically using the MA-FRS shape matrices, i.e.,
\footnotesize\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{(t)}_{i}=\{j~|~(p_{i}^{(t)}\!-\!p_{j}^{(t)})^{\top}(Q_{i}^{(t)}\boxplus Q_{j}^{(t)})^{-1}(p_{i}^{(t)}\!-\!p_{j}^{(t)})\leq 1,\;j\neq i\}
, consistent with the ellipsoidal collision metric in (

With the enhanced cost functions, tracking and velocity terms depend only on each agent’s local state and input, while the MA-FRS–based collision term introduces symmetric neighbor coupling. Thus, the resulting costs satisfy the structural conditions of Definition
6

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced a decentralized framework for safe multi-agent motion planning under uncertainty. The problem was formulated as a Dynamic Potential Game (DPG) and solved through a Neighborhood-Dominated iterative Best Response (ND-iBR) scheme, grounded in an iterated
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-best-response (
\footnotesize i\varepsilon
-BR) process to guarantee finite-step convergence to an
\footnotesize\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium. To ensure safety and robustness, we incorporated Multi-Agent Forward Reachable Sets (MA-FRSs) into the optimization, explicitly modeling uncertainty and enforcing collision-avoidance constraints. Both simulations and hardware experiments validated the effectiveness of our method across dynamic multi-agent scenarios.
Future directions include:
1) Robust planning under partial observability and communication constraints
, where unreliable or intermittent communication may limit neighborhood information;
2) Adaptive robustness against out-of-envelope disturbances
, noting that fixed disturbance bounds may be violated in practice, motivating online disturbance estimation and real-time FRS recomputation to maintain safety under unexpected conditions.

Appendix – Hyperparameter Selection

All hyperparameters in our framework are selected by a systematic grid search aimed at optimizing the task metrics: zero collision (hard requirement), minimum tracking cost, and maximum efficiency (shorter arrival time).
We predefine discrete grids for each hyperparameter, roll out each configuration over multiple random seeds, and evaluate:

(i)
collision ratio (must be
\footnotesize 0
),
(ii)
tracking cost (lower is better), and
(iii)
efficiency, indicated by distance to goal at
\footnotesize t=T-5
(lower is better).
Selection follows a
safety-first
lexicographic rule:
we first discard all configurations that exhibit any collision. Among the remaining collision-free configurations, we select the one with the smallest tracking cost
\footnotesize J
, and if multiple remain, we break ties by choosing the configuration with higher efficiency.
The final selected values for each scenario are listed in the manuscript.

\footnotesize\lambda^{\mathrm{FRS}}
