Congruent Weber-B ankle fractures do not alter tibiotalar contact mechanics
Authors: Matthias Peiffer, Siddhartha Sharma, Bedri Karaismailoglu, Arne Burssens, Soheil Ashkani-Esfahani, Christopher W. DiGiovanni, Emmanuel Audenaert, Daniel Guss
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Current treatment strategy for managing Weber B ankle fractures is mainly governed by mortise congruency, malleolar alignment, deltoid ligament competence and fracture stability. While nonoperative treatment has yielded good functional outcomes in satisfactorily aligned stable injuries, a biomechanical rationale is not firmly established. Furthermore, current radiographic analysis is obscured by observer inaccuracy and beam rotation. This study aimed to utilize weightbearing CT and computational biomechanics to analyse 3D mortise displacement and contact mechanics in Weber-B ankle fractures and compare them with the uninjured contralateral side. 32 patients were included who sustained a Weber-B ankle fracture and underwent bilateral weightbearing CT imaging at injury. Segmentation into 3D models of bone was performed semi-automatically, and individualized cartilage layers were modeled based on a previously validated methodology. The 3D mortise congruency was evaluated by use of following parameters: alpha angle, fibular length, talocrural angle, medial gutter- and tibiofibular clear space- distance mapping. Contact mechanics were evaluated by the mean and maximum contact stress of the tibiotalar articulation. Statistical analysis revealed that there were no significant differences for all anatomical parameters. There was also no significant difference between the mean contact stress of the injured (2.10 ± 0.42 MPa) and uninjured side (2.10 ± 0.41 MPa), nor the maximum contact stress of the injured (7.67 ± 1.55 MPa) and uninjured (7.47 ± 1.67), respectively. Contact mechanics were unaffected in congruent Weber-B fractures. These findings support consideration of nonoperative management in such cases, given their presumably low risk for posttraumatic arthritic development in the long term. Introduction

Treatment choice for ankle fractures has been largely dictated by the presence of lateral talar shift, with 1 mm purported to correlate with a reduced tibiotalar contact area of 40%1,2. Since abnormal contact mechanics have been implicated as a principal determinant of joint degeneration by numerous studies, the risk for post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis (PTAA) development is generally correlated with anatomic congruency3–6. Current clinical practice has shifted the focus towards evaluating ankle stability using weightbearing radiographs, which have shown promise in reducing surgical interventions while yielding favourable clinical outcomes up to two years7,8 However, a strong biomechanical rationale for these observations has yet to be firmly established9.

Stability of the ankle mortise is commonly evaluated using the medial clear space on plain radiographs, which is inconsistently evaluated because of x-ray beam rotation and manual measurement errors10,11. The recent advent of weightbearing cone-beam CT (WBCT) overcomes these drawbacks by depicting bilateral ankles during stance, allowing for 3D bilateral analysis of the ankle congruency under physiologic load. Furthermore, recent advances in computational biomechanics have permitted evaluation of patient-specific analysis of ankle joint contact mechanics12–15. Combining these novel techniques could further elucidate the complex interplay between the anatomical and mechanical environment of the ankle joint following Weber-B ankle fractures. We therefore performed this study to analyse the congruency and ankle joint contact mechanics in Weber-B ankle fractures, imaged by WBCT at the time of injury, by using patient-specific models. We hypothesized that congruent Weber B fractures on WBCT would not have significant alterations in ankle joint contact stresses in comparison to the normal uninjured side.

Methods

Study population and data acquisition

This retrospective case-control study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. The Institutional Review Board of MASS GENERAL BRIGHAM approved this study (IRB 2015P000464). Due to the retrospective nature of this study, The Institutional Review Board of MASS GENERAL BRIGHAM waived the informed consent. A database search was conducted for all patients with a Weber-B fracture who presented to one of three large tertiary hospitals between January 2016 and December 2022. Inclusion criteria included age ≥ 18 years, AO/OTA 43B1 fractures, and bilateral WBCT imaging at the time of injury. Exclusion criteria included injury to the contralateral side, additional malleolar fractures (i.e. medial or posterior malleolus), pilon fractures, pre-existing implants, or motion artifacts. WBCT imaging was requested at the time of injury for further investigation in addition to radiographs when deemed necessary by the surgeon. Following imaging parameters were used: 0.37 mm isotropic voxel size, 120 kV tube voltage and 5 mA tube current.

3D simulation and analysis

Three-dimensional bone models were reconstructed after selecting an image stack in DICOM format using DISIOR® Bonelogic Ortho Foot and Ankle Software version 2.0 (Paragon 28, Denver, US). Once the image stack was loaded, the software automatically rendered a 3D isosurface of the bone. The operator subsequently had to place at least one marker on each ankle bone (i.e. tibia, fibula, and talus) on the 3D isosurface. Thereafter, the software automatically rendered a separate 3D model of each bone, which was exported in the stereo-lithography (STL) format (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study Methodology Flowchart. The STL files were imported in a custom-made script in MATLAB® software (R2020b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), which was part of a custom-made "Patient-Specific 3D Automated Anatomical and Mechanical Analysis of the Ankle Joint" tool (Provisional Patent filed, IP No. MGH-2023-186). In this script, Alpha angle (Alpha°), fibular length (FL), talocrural angle (TC°) and distance maps of the syndesmosis (DMs) and medial gutter (DMm) were automatically determined. In Fig. 2, a visual representation of these parameters is depicted. The Alpha° was based on the description by Nault et al., representing the angle between a line connecting the most extreme anterior and posterior points on the tibial incisura and a line connecting the most posterior and anterior point on the fibula16. This angle is a measure of axial fibular rotation within the tibial incisura. The FL was based on the description by Panchbhavi et al. and calculated as the distance between center of the tibial plafond and most distal point on the fibula in the coronal plane17. The TC°, firstly described by Sarkisian in 1976, was represented as an angle between the tibial articular surface line and a line connecting the most distal points of the tibia and fibula in the coronal plane18. Distance maps were defined and calculated as the shortest surface-to-surface distance between each point of the 3D model to the opposing surface19,20. For DMs, this was represented by the distance between the tibial incisura and opposing fibular surface, whereas DMm represented the distance between the articular side of the medial malleolus to the opposing talar surface.Fig. 2Anatomical analysis consisted out of automated discrete measurements (left) and distance mapping (right). Contact mechanics of the 3D tibiotalar joint models were calculated using a previously validated ligament-constraining personalized discrete element algorithm (DEA)12. DEA models cartilage layers as an array of independent linear springs and has been validated against finite element analyses of ankle contact stresses, demonstrating to be both computationally efficient and accurate12,14,21. Contact area was defined as the sum of the surface areas of the talar cartilage that were in contact with the tibial cartilage.

Statistical analysis

All variables were tested for normality according to the Shapiro-Wilk test. Where the assumption of normality was met, a t-test was performed to analyse if the difference in anatomical and mechanical parameters was statistically different between the fractured and healthy side. In the case of non-normality, the Mann-Whitney U test was used instead. The STATA software package (Release 17. StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) statistical package was used to analyse the results. Data is presented as mean (Standard deviation; SD) and a P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Thirty-two patients with mean age 38.5 years (SD = 15) and weight 79.1 kg (SD = 16.2) were confirmed eligible for analysis. The mean FL for the contralateral healthy side and fractured side was 24.5 mm (SD = 3.6 mm) and 24.02 mm (SD = 3.3 mm), respectively. There was no significant difference regarding FL between the bilateral ankles (Difference = 0.5 mm, CI = [-1.2; 2.2], p= 0.55). The mean TC° for the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 14.4° (SD = 4.5°) and 14.2° (SD = 4.2), respectively, with no significant difference between the two sides (difference = 0.3°, CI = [1.9; 2.4], p = 0.81). The mean Alpha° for the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 21.3° (SD = 6.7°) and 18.5 ° (SD = 7.6°), respectively (Difference = 2.7, CI = [-0.9; 6.4], p = 0.1). The mean DMs for the healthy contralateral and fractured case was 6.5 mm (SD = 0.8 mm) and 6.6 mm (SD = 1.0 mm), respectively. There was no significant difference between the two (Difference = 0.1 mm, CI = [-0.4; 0.6], p = 0.65). The mean DMm for the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 3.4 mm (SD = 0.4 mm) and 3.5 mm (SD = 0.5 mm), respectively (Difference = 0.2 mm, CI = [-0.0; 0.5], p = 0.14).

The mean talar contact stress of the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 2.1 MPa (SD = 0.4 MPa) and 2.1 MPa (SD = 0.4 MPa), respectively. There was no significant difference between the bilateral ankles (difference = 0.0, CI = [-0.2; 0.2], P = 0.98). The maximum talar contact stress of the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 7.5 MPa (SD = 1.7 MPa) and 7.7 MPa (SD = 1.6 MPa), respectively (difference = 0.2, CI = [-1.0; 0.6], P = 0.63). The mean tibial contact stress of the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 1.9 MPa (SD = 0.4 MPa) and 1.9 (SD = 0.4), respectively (difference = 0.0, CI = [-0.2; 0.2], P = 0.81). The peak tibial contact stress of the healthy contralateral and fractured side was 7.0 MPa (SD = 1.8 MPa) and 7.0 MPa (SD = 1.4 MPa), respectively (difference = 0.0, CI = [-0.7; 0.9], P = 0.83) (Figs. 3 and 4).Fig. 3Mechanical Analysis consisted out of calculation of 3D contact-stress on the tibial (upper) and talar (lower) articular contact surface. Discussion

Weber-B ankle fractures with a symmetric medial clear space and maintained tibiotalar alignment on weightbearing X-ray radiographs have demonstrated to result in favourable outcomes when treated nonoperatively23–25. However, inaccuracies in weightbearing radiographic measurements and lack of physiologic load during supine CT scanning has rendered it historically difficult to investigate the effect of weight bearing on 3D congruency and contact mechanics of the ankle joint. Weightbearing radiographs are prone to measurement errors due to factors such as beam angulation, patient positioning, and manual variability in interpreting the medial clear space, which can lead to inconsistencies in evaluating fracture stability. Moreover, traditional CT scans, performed in a supine position, do not replicate the natural loading conditions of the joint, limiting their ability to provide a true representation of the functional congruency of the ankle. Merging WBCT and technological advancements in computational contact-stress algorithms, the present study was able to bridge the gap between weightbearing radiographs (by using an automated, 3D analysis of the mortise) and supine CT (by assessing the ankle during loading). The main finding of our study is that contact mechanics (i.e., contact-stress and area) are not significantly altered in Weber-B fractures with a congruent mortise, lending credence to currently published evidence.

At present, there is a growing body of evidence to demonstrate that nonoperative treatment of stable Weber-B fractures results in satisfactory long-term outcomes with respect to joint preservation, but a robust mechanical rationale for this has not been established. Rooney et al. found in their retrospective case series with 51 patients that stable Weber-B (as defined by medial clear space widening < 7 mm) fractures had good functional outcomes and low complication rates with non-surgical management23. Additionally, The Combined Randomized and Observational Study of Surgery for type B Ankle fracture Treatment (CROSSBAT) multi-centric prospective trial with 77 patients showed that nonoperatively treated stable Weber-B ankle fractures (as defined by medial clear space being at most 2 mm wider than the superior clear space on a mortise X-ray view) had equivalent outcomes to those who were operatively treated in a five-year follow-up26.

A major strength of our study is the use of automated measurements to determine mortise congruency on WBCT. The primary advantage is the reduction in measurement variability, since no human interference was involved. Furthermore, distance maps allows for a 3D investigation of medial gutter widening, rather than a single 2D measurement on plain radiographs. By using WBCT, the mortise was evaluated in its functional position, which was critical for understanding the true impact of a fracture on joint stability. However, there are also disadvantages to consider. Automated measurements may be limited by the quality of the imaging and the algorithms used, which could potentially overlook subtle abnormalities or artefacts. Moreover, the reliance on technology requires access to advanced imaging systems and expertise in interpreting the data, which may not be readily available in all clinical settings.

Distance mapping of the syndesmosis has been previously shown to be an accurate tool for determining syndesmotic instability27. Our present study built on this paradigm by applying distance maps of the syndesmosis and medial gutter to establish congruency of the mortise in ankle fractures. Linked to the mechanical parameters, our results demonstrated a mean fibular shortening of 0.52 mm, a mean syndesmotic widening of 0.11 mm and mean medial gutter widening of 0.17 mm, which were not statistically different from the healthy contralateral side. Previous cadaveric studies have shown that 2 mm of fibular shortening, 2 mm of lateral fibular displacement and 1 mm of lateral talar shift can significantly alter the contact area1,2,28. While these studies provided the foundation of our current indications for open reduction and internal fixation, manually deranging the physiological anatomy of the ankle (i.e. dissecting capsule, ligaments, tendons etc.) in cadaveric specimens does not reflect the mortise displacement of Weber-B fractures in clinical practice. By analysing fractures on WBCT, we were able to use the in-vivo, physiological mortise configurations as inputs for our contact-stress algorithm29,30.

Progressive ankle arthritis is predominantly posttraumatic in nature, as opposed to hip and knee arthritis which are primarily caused by intrinsic factors31,32. Although the pathogenesis of PTAA is not fully understood, abnormal mechanics has been implicated as a principal determinant of joint degeneration6. To our knowledge, only one study has previously investigated the relationship between increased contact-stress and onset of PTAA in the ankle joint. Anderson et al. have previously shown that a contact-stress threshold of 9.5 MPa was maximally discriminative between ankles that did and those that did not develop PTAA after tibial plafond fracture33. In the knee, Segal et al. have shown that the maximum articular contact-stress was 0.54 MPa higher in knees who presented with radiologic signs of OA compared to matched controls34. In this regard, peak contact-stress results of the healthy (7.47 MPa) and fractured (7.67 MPa) cases in our study are below the 9.5 MPa risk threshold for development of ankle PTAA. Additionally, the difference between the fractured and healthy side in our study (0.20 MPa) is below the difference of 0.54 MPa between knees that developed posttraumatic arthritis and controls. These findings suggest that nonoperative treatment in our Weber-B fractures series, consisting of an anatomically positioned mortise, is not expected to result in an increased long-term risk for PTAA, from a mechanical point of view, since the lateral malleolus can still act as bony bumper to maintain ankle mortise congruency.

There are several limitations to this study. First, our analysis relied solely on static analysis derived from WBCT imaging, which assumes that the joint reaction force is exclusively axially oriented. Future studies incorporating subject-specific kinematics and kinetics, along with patient-specific information on the status of the ligamentous structures (such as the deltoid ligament, derived from e.g. MRI), could provide a more comprehensive understanding of dynamic contact mechanics. Also, cartilage morphology and thickness could not be visualized on our WBCT images. To overcome this, we needed to utilize a statistical algorithm to estimate the outline and thickness of individual cartilage layers. Although previous research has demonstrated the accuracy of this method in predicting cartilage layers with sub-millimeter precision, this was an estimation and might differ from reality13. Our bone models were constructed as rigid bodies, and we did not take into account the different cortical and trabecular material properties of bone. Previous studies, however, have shown that the assumption of rigid bones is valid in the ankle joint35. Furthermore, we included patients that underwent a WBCT scan for additional investigation after radiographs when deemed necessary by the surgeon. This could induce selection bias, as some fractures, such as those that are severely displaced or unstable, may have been adequately appreciated on radiographs and therefore not referred for further WBCT evaluation. Lastly, we were not able to differentiate our Weber-B fractures into supination-external rotation (SER 2-4) patterns, since we did not have any definite information on the status of the posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament or deltoid ligament. Future studies including MRI and/or arthroscopy imaging could overcome this limitation and provide the specific contact mechanics for each SER pattern.
