Ultrasound criteria for shallow T2 gallbladder carcinoma ― comment on Sugimoto M et al. a simple method for diagnosing gallbladder malignant tumors with subserosal invasion by endoscopic ultrasonography. BMC cancer. 2021; 21: 288
Authors: Taketoshi Fujimoto
Published: 2026

I read the original article by Sugimoto M et al. [1] with great interest. However, several issues require clarification.

A deep hypoechoic area

The authors state that diagnosing gallbladder carcinoma (GBC) with subserosal invasion without a narrow or disrupted lateral hyperechoic layer is difficult. However, I have previously reported that a polypoid gallbladder tumor with a deep hypoechoic area on ultrasound suggests a T2 GBC regardless of the characteristics of the outermost hyperechoic layer [2–4], except in rare circumstances [5, 6]. How do the authors reconcile this discrepancy?

Patients with an early (T1) GBC, whether confined to the mucosa (T1a) or the muscle layer (T1b), have a good prognosis. Moreover, radical resection provides a favorable prognosis for patients with GBC limited to shallow subserosal invasion (subserosal-invasion depth ≤ 2 mm: shallow T2) [7]. Therefore, diagnosing T1b or shallow T2 GBC is crucial. How is T1b or shallow T2 GBC depicted on ultrasound?

Ultrasound and pathologic correlation through disclosure of the loupe view

Although the authors assessed the heterogeneity or homogeneity of the internal echo, they did not confirm the presence or absence of a deep hypoechoic area [1]. However, Fig. 1b of [1] shows a polypoid GBC with a deep hypoechoic area accompanied by a conically thickened lateral hyperechoic layer. Could the authors provide a loupe view corresponding to Fig. 1b of [1] in their response? If possible, histopathology would likely reveal a polypoid T2 GBC consisting of a superficial well-differentiated adenocarcinoma and a deep moderately to poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma invading the subserosa with abundant fibrosis and lymphocytic infiltration [2–4]. In addition, Fig. 1c of [1] depicts a polypoid GBC with a deep hypoechoic area accompanied by a thickened outermost hyperechoic layer with a worm-eaten hypoechoic area (center arrow). Could the authors offer a loupe view corresponding to Fig. 1c of [1] in their response? If possible, it would also be interesting to understand what the worm-eaten hypoechoic area represents.

In a protruded lesion, while a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma in the mucosa changes into moderately to poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma in the muscle coat, the lesion is drawn into the lumen. Thus, the outermost hyperechoic layer (yellow areas) is pulled up at first (B, C). Thereafter, the layer becomes thinner and eventually splits as carcinoma invasion progresses with enlargement of the deep hypoechoic area (D→E→F). In contrast, in a flat-elevated lesion, the vertical growth is expected to go directly from A to D without going through B and C (adapted from Fujimoto et al. [2] with modification).

Differences in the extension patterns of flat-elevated lesions and protruded lesions

The authors define a regular outermost hyperechoic layer as having no change, while an irregular outermost hyperechoic layer is characterized as being either narrow or thickened. However, these descriptions represent two opposite extremes, and the authors do not clarify how they are related. I believe that in T2 GBC, a narrow or thickened outermost hyperechoic layer corresponds to the extension pattern of a flat-elevated or protruded lesion, respectively. In other words, in the early stages of flat-elevated or protruded GBC, a regular outermost hyperechoic layer may become narrow or thickened, respectively, as carcinoma invasion progresses. Therefore, these two lesions behave differently and should be distinguished. In the case of a protruded lesion, a thickened outermost hyperechoic layer may later become narrower. Namely, in the case of a protruded lesion (30–40% [8, 9]), vertical growth is expected to progress from A through B and C to D, while in the case of a flat-elevated lesion (60–70% [8, 9]), it typically progresses directly from A to D (Fig. 1). Ultrasound and pathological correlations show that the superficial hyperechoic part coincides with papillary adenocarcinoma, while the deep hypoechoic area represents adenocarcinoma invading the subserosa with abundant fibrosis and lymphocytic infiltration. Fig. 1A, B, C and D denote T1a, T1b, shallow T2 and T2 GBC, respectively [2]. In contrast, a pedunculated GBC with a thin stalk is strictly expected to be T1a.

Katayama [10] documented, from a histological perspective, the presence of a pulled-up muscle coat in all five cases of protruded T1b GBC and in five out of six cases of protruded minute T2 GBC. Although the sample size is small, making it impossible to calculate sensitivity or specificity, the correlation between macroscopy and histopathology is crucial. This pulled-up muscle coat corresponds to a conically thickened outermost hyperechoic layer, which supports my hypothesis: T1b or shallow T2 GBC is depicted in Fig. 1B or C, respectively. Unfortunately, these findings are often overlooked by researchers. Ito et al. [11] demonstrated a case of shallow T2 GBC with a deep hypoechoic area and a conically thickened outermost hyperechoic layer (see their Figure 2 [11]), which they failed to notice.

The limit of delineation with ultrasound of a deep hypoechoic area

The limit of ultrasound resolution for delineating a deep hypoechoic area is generally 1–2 mm thick. A protruded gallbladder tumor with a conically thickened outermost hyperechoic layer but without a distinct deep hypoechoic area on ultrasound typically suggests a T1b GBC [2]. Here, the authors present most interesting cases of shallow T2 GBC, along with endoscopic ultrasound images, and compare them with the loupe view of the resected specimen in Fig. 3 [1]. Its histopathology demonstrates slight subserosal invasion forming a glandular cavity with the invasion depth of carcinoma less than 0.5 mm (Fig. 3b [1], 3c [1], 3e [1], and 3f [1]). Although Fig. 3a [1] and Fig. 3d [1] reveal a polypoid gallbladder tumor with a thickened outermost hyperechoic layer, they do not show an apparent deep hypoechoic area because the carcinoma invasion depth is less than the limit of ultrasound delineation, as described above.
