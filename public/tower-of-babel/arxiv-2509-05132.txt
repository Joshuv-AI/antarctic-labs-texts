Abstract
Property Testing is a formal framework to study the computational power and complexity of sampling from combinatorial objects. A central goal in standard graph property testing is to understand which graph properties are testable with sublinear query complexity. Here, a graph property P P is testable with a sublinear query complexity if there is an algorithm that makes a sublinear number of queries to the input graph and accepts with probability at least 2 / 3 2/3 , if the graph has property P P , and rejects with probability at least 2 / 3 2/3 if it is ε \varepsilon -far from every graph that has property P P . In this paper, we introduce a new variant of the bounded degree graph model. In this variant, in addition to the standard representation of a bounded degree graph, we assume that every vertex v v has a unique label 𝔫 ​ 𝔲 ​ 𝔪 ⁡ ( v ) \num(v) from { 1 , … , | V | } \{1,\dots,|V|\} , and in addition to the standard queries in the bounded degree graph model, we also allow a property testing algorithm to query for the label of a vertex (but not for a vertex with a given label). Our new model is motivated by certain graph processes such as a DFS traversal, which assign consecutive numbers (labels) to the vertices of the graph. We want to study which of these numberings can be tested in sublinear time. As a first step in understanding such a model, we develop a property testing algorithm for discovery times of a DFS traversal with query complexity O ⁡ ( n 1 / 3 / ε ) O(n^{1/3}/\varepsilon) and for constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 we give a matching lower bound.

Given a wide applicability of DFS and DFS numbering, a natural problem is to verify whether a given graph is correctly DFS numbered, i.e., the labels assigned to the vertices are a DFS numbering for some ordering of vertices and neighbors. The latter problem is easy to solve by simulating a DFS using the given numbering and locally verifying that the DFS is performed correctly. However, is it also possible to

The DFS algorithm, as given in Algorithm

Every vertex is initially

(

has exactly one orphan, which receives the smallest number in its connected component. At any point during the execution the

consists of all active vertices including the virtual vertex

, with every active vertex (other than

In an implicitly labeled graph

\displaystyle p(v):=\begin{cases}\max\{u\in N(v)\cap[v-1]\}&\text{ if }N(v)\cap[v-1]\neq\emptyset\kern 5.0pt,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Claim 4
.

If the numbers in an implicitly labeled graph correspond to a DFS numbering then

is the (possibly virtual) parent of

.

Proof.

If
v
is an orphan then it has the smallest number in its connected component so
\{u\in N(v):u<v\}=\emptyset
. Hence

, which is the virtual parent of orphans by definition.

Now assume

was discovered from a non-virtual parent

. Then
x\in N(v)
with

x\in\{u\in N(v):u<v\}
. To see that

Observe that any edge

with

Definition 5
.

A pair

is a

The following central lemma shows that the absence of conflicting pairs is necessary and sufficient for the validity of a DFS numbering.

Lemma 6
.

Let

be an implicitly labeled graph. The following are equivalent.

(i)

G
has a valid DFS numbering.

(ii)

There exists no conflicting pair.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

Consider a DFS run that agrees with the DFS numbering and let
(v,\{u,w\})\in V\subseteq E
be a pair with
p(v)<u<v
. By
4
,
v
was discovered from
p(v)
. Consider the time immediately before this happens. Vertex
u
was already discovered (due to
u<v
) and is not active (since
p(v)<u
is the end of the white path and
p(v)
is the largest active vertex), meaning
u
is finished. Thus all neighbors of
u
are already discovered, so

. In particular
(v,\{u,w\})
is not a conflicting pair.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

We use induction on
k
, showing that there is a DFS run assigning number
v
to each
v\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. For
k=0
the claim is trivial. Assume the claim holds for some

. Consider a corresponding DFS run that has just discovered vertex
k-1
; at that moment vertex

is undiscovered. We have

. If

does not appear on the white path then, because both

k-1
appear on the white path, there is a vertex
x
on the white path with

. This makes
(x,\{p(k),k\})
a conflicting pair, contradicting (ii). Therefore

does appear on the white path. There might be a vertex

further along on the white path. Let

. Thus

6
Conclusions

In this paper we introduced a variant of the standard bounded-degree graph model in the property testing setting that works for labeled graphs and allows also label queries. We demonstrated the strength of the model on our new study of DFS numbering. Our main technical contribution is a tight analysis for detecting whether the input labeled graph is properly DFS numbered or it is
\varepsilon
-far from having a valid DFS numbering. We demonstrated that this task can be solved with
\Omega(n^{1/3}/\varepsilon)
queries and also
\Omega(n^{1/3})
queries are necessary.

We observe that while our analysis is presented for undirected graphs, similar arguments hold also for
directed graphs
. The lower bound from
Theorem

7
trivially extends to directed graphs and a careful pass through the algorithm in
Theorem

11
shows that the analysis can be extended accordingly. However, to implement this algorithm efficiently in our setting, we need to allow access to incoming and outgoing edges. (See Appendix
A
for more details.)

Our analysis can be also extended (but only for undirected graphs) to the
DFS finishing numbers
(FIN-numberings
[
CLRS22
]
).
In Appendix
B
, we show that (for undirected graphs) a numbering
\num:V\rightarrow[n]
is a valid FIN numbering iff the reverse numbering
\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\longleftarrow$}}}{{\smash{\num}}}}
with
\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\longleftarrow$}}}{{\smash{\num}}}}(i)=n+1-i
is a valid DFS numbering. This immediately implies that our results for testing valid DFS numberings extend to testing valid FIN numberings in undirected graphs.

(ii)

Claim 25

If the numbers of an implicitly labeled graph correspond to a FIN numbering then

If

Otherwise

Appendix D
Reducing degrees in graphs with valid DFS numbering

In this section, we present a useful framework supporting our analysis in
Section

5.1
, showing that if a connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
of maximum degree
d
has a valid DFS numbering
\num
, then one can modify
O(k)
edges of

to obtain a connected graph on vertex set

whose maximum degree is smaller than

, where

is the number of vertices with degree
d
. (We observe that it is sufficient to prove the claim for connected graphs only, since if
G
is not connected, then the same construction can be applied to each connected component individually.)

Let us begin with two straightforward auxiliary claims that follow easily from the fact that the parent relation
p(v)
defines a DFS tree of a given connected graph equipped with a valid DFS numbering
\num
.

Claim 26
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an arbitrary connected graph equipped with a valid DFS numbering
\num
. Let
\{u,v\}
be an arbitrary edge in
E
with
\num(u)<\num(p(v))<\num(v)
. Then the graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E\setminus\{u,v\})
is connected and
\num
is its valid DFS numbering.
∎

Claim 27
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an arbitrary connected graph equipped with a valid DFS numbering
\num
. Let
v
be an arbitrary vertex in
V
and let
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
be all neighbors of
v
with DFS numbers greater than
\num(v)
, with
\num(u_{1})<\dots<\num(u_{k})
. Let
w
be vertex with
\num(w)=\num(u_{k})-1
. If
k>1
then the graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E\setminus\{v,u_{k}\}\cup\{w,u_{k}\})
is connected and
\num
is its valid DFS numbering.

Furthermore, if
k>1
then for every neighbor
x

\num(x)<\num(w)
.
∎

Using Claims
26
and
27
, we can easily prove the following.

Lemma 28
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an arbitrary connected graph of maximum degree
d\geq 3
equipped with a valid DFS numbering
\num
. Let
V_{d}
be the set of vertices of degree

. Then one can construct a connected graph

We process vertices in

one by one, in arbitrary order, and reduce their degrees without increasing the degree of any other vertex to more than

. For that, iteratively, take a vertex

.

•

If
v
has at least two neighbors
u
,

\num(u)<\num(w)<\num(v)

from

. By

.

•

If

has no two neighbors

,
w

\num(u)<\num(w)<\num(v)
, then it has

If we iterative proceed through all vertices from
