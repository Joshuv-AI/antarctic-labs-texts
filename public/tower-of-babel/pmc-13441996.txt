Multi-omics and electrophysiological examination of GABAA receptors in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of humans with alcohol use disorder
Authors: J. Granchi, B. Salameh, B. Miller, P. Scaduto, W. K. Russell, T. D. Meyer, A. L. Teixeira, G. R. Fries, L. Stertz, C. Walss-Bass, A. Limon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alterations of GABAA receptors (GABAARs) following chronic alcohol are thought to be related to deficits in GABAergic signaling in individuals with alcohol use disorder (AUD). Whether those modifications affect the function of synaptic GABAARs is not clear, as the electrophysiological characterization of native synaptic receptors from AUD individuals had not been done. To obtain this information, we microtransplanted synaptic membranes from postmortem dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) samples of AUD and non-AUD subjects to determine functional traits of GABAARs. To follow the path from transcription to function of potential changes of GABAARs in AUD, GABAARs currents and GABA pEC50 values were integrated with RNA-Seq and label-free proteomics datasets of bulk tissue and isolated synaptosomes from the same subjects. Our results outline significant reconfigurations in transcriptomic organization of GABAARs in AUD, higher levels of GABRG1 and significant decrease of mitochondrial transcripts in AUD individuals. Notably, transcriptional differences were gradually lost as the analysis moved from transcription to protein and function within our cohort. This suggests post-translational buffering in AUD resulting in unchanged GABA receptor synaptic activity. Our novel findings establish a proof of concept for reactivating AUD post-synaptic receptors and integrating this information with multiple levels of multi-omic analyses, as well as outline hypothesis-generating insights into this multifaceted disease.

Introduction

Most recent data from the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) estimated that there were 140,000 deaths per year attributable to excessive alcohol use [1, 2]. Similarly, the 2022 National Survey on Drug Use and Health estimated that 29.5 million people ages 12 and older meet the criteria for alcohol use disorder (AUD), which is defined by an impaired ability to stop or control drinking despite adverse social, occupational, or health consequences [3]. AUD is far-reaching and places an overwhelming burden on afflicted individuals, their families, and their communities. Currently available pharmacological therapies that aim to reduce dependence on alcohol have only modest success treating AUD [4, 5], highlighting, at least in part, our limited knowledge of brain changes in AUD patients, and the need to better understand alcohol-related changes to aid the development of more effective therapies. Notably, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is a key region involved in the reward-processing of alcohol use [6–8] and its modulation has been proposed as a potential treatment for AUD [9]. Indeed, prefrontal cortex (PFC) hyperexcitability in alcohol-dependent individuals [10] suggests deficits in cortical inhibitory transmission, which is carried out by γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptors (GABAARs), a well-known alcohol target [11]. This is also supported by previous research demonstrating reduction in cortical mRNA levels of specific GABAARs subunits in AUD postmortem brain samples [12].

GABAARs are pentametric ionotropic complexes that result from distinct subunit compositions through the combination of 19 subunits [13]. The combination of subunits, or the stoichiometry, of each receptor determines its trafficking, membrane location, biophysics, pharmacological properties, and function [14–21]. Importantly, previous evidence has shown that remodeling of GABAARs is implicated in alcohol dependence [12, 22, 23] suggesting that changes to GABAARs subunit combinations resulting from ethanol exposure can contribute to changes in GABAARs function. In support of this, animal models of chronic ethanol exposure consistently demonstrate decreases in synaptic α1 subunit expression [22, 24]. These deficits correlate with enhanced decay of cortical inhibitory post synaptic currents (IPSCs) of ethanol-treated rats, and higher frequency of spontaneous IPSCs without changes in amplitude [24]. Additionally, rat cortical neurons in culture exposed to ethanol exhibited significantly shortened decay time constant of miniature IPSCs without significant changes in frequency or current amplitude in comparison with controls [22], suggesting changes in the kinetics of GABAARs which may be associated to subunit rearrangements.

While the information gained from animal studies provides invaluable knowledge regarding ethanol’s effects on GABAARs in the mammalian brain, preclinical models of alcohol use and dependence are inherently limited due to rodents’ natural aversion to alcohol consumption [25]. Furthermore, experimental animal models in which ethanol-treated animals are compared to ethanol-naïve animals may not accurately reflect differences between alcohol dependence and typical human alcohol use because not all individuals that consume alcohol develop AUD [3]. Therefore, the potential structural and functional changes of native GABAARs in humans with AUD are not clearly understood. In this study, we directly recorded the electrophysiological activity of reactivated native synaptic GABAARs from the DLPFC of AUD individuals and correlated observed changes with different levels of multi-omics data to uncover potential GABAARs modifications associated with AUD. Our findings definitively prove that AUD post-mortem GABAAR from AUD post-mortem tissue can be reactivated and their functional activity can be measured. Additionally, we hypothesized that GABAARs from AUD individuals would exhibit significantly lower levels of GABAAR subunit transcripts and proteins, as well as significantly blunted function when compared with controls. However, our data suggests that chronic alcohol use can affect synaptic GABAARs at the level of the transcriptome through mechanisms that do not translate permanently to significant changes in GABAAR subunit composition or post-synaptic function in the DLPFC, similar to post-translational buffering processes which attenuate mRNA expression deviations and prevent them from propagating to the level of protein expression [26–30]. The methodology described in this project provides, for the first time, a solid schema for reactivating AUD post-synaptic receptors and integrating this information with multiple levels of multi-omics data. Additionally, our results provide hypothesis-generating insights into this complex disease and lay the foundation for future dynamic research of this distressing and prevalent disorder.

Materials and methods

Human tissue

Postmortem DLPFC samples were collected by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston (UTHealth Houston) Brain Collection, in collaboration with the Harris Country Institute of Forensic Science and with approval from the Institutional Review Board as previously described [31]. DLPFC Brodmann Area 9 (BA9) was defined between the superior frontal gyrus and cingulate sulcus [32]. Cerebellar pH, RNA integrity number (RIN), and interval from time of death until tissue preservation also known as post mortem interval (PMI) are described in Supplementary Table 1 [32]. DLPFC sections from the right hemisphere were obtained using a 4 mm punch through the cortex which yielded approximately 100 mg of tissue [32]. Autopsy reports, toxicology reports, demographic details, medical, and psychiatric notes were obtained for each subject [31, 32]. Comprehensive psychological autopsies for each subject were performed by using the UTHealth Psychological Autopsy Interview Schedule (UTH-PAIS) to interview next of kin, from which psychiatric disorders, age of onset of substance use, types of substances used, and any other co-morbidities were obtained [31–33]. A consensus diagnosis of AUD or designation as a non-psychiatric control was reached for each subject by an independent panel of three trained clinicians using the DSM-5 criteria after reviewing all available information [31, 32].

Nine non-psychiatric controls, referred also as non-AUD, samples (mean age of 57 ± 4 years; PMI 31 ± 9 h) and ten AUD samples (mean age 51 ± 8 years; PMI 28 ± 5) were used for this project (mean age of 54 ± 7 years; PMI 30 ± 7 h) (Supplementary Table 1). All non-AUD controls had no history of any mental health or substance use disorder. Two AUD subjects were diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD), one with MDD and cannabis use disorder, and another with cocaine use disorder (CUD). Optimally, the cohort would consist of subjects with only a diagnosis of AUD, but due to the availability of post-mortem brain samples, these subjects were included to maintain a reasonable sample size. Nevertheless, analyses were iteratively done with and without these samples and results remained consistent across analyses. Data collected from our AUD subjects remain highly translatable to AUD patient populations due to the fact that more than 40% of men with AUD have had another substance use disorder in their lifetime [34]. MDD is the most common co-occurring psychiatric disorder among people with AUD and those with AUD are twice as likely to have MDD [35, 36].

To determine the relationship between transcript levels and bulk protein expression we analyzed a transcriptomic dataset and a proteomic dataset previously published in Leung et al. [32] using n = 7 non-AUD and 8 AUD samples (all males; mean age of 53 ± 7 years; PMI 30 ± 8 h). From the 19 samples in the original cohort, 8 non-AUD and 8 AUD were used for microtransplantation of synaptic membranes (MSM) and two-electrode voltage clamp (TEVC) experiments based on their ability to elicit electrophysiological responses (all males; mean age of 54 ± 6 years; PMI 29 ± 7 h). All 19 DLPFC samples underwent synaptosome proteomic analysis. Detailed outline of which samples were used in each analysis as well as demographic and medical information is available in Supplementary Table 1. For all statistical analyses, Shapiro-Wilk test with α = 0.05 was used to test if transcript or protein expression levels exhibit normal distribution. Mann-Whitney was used as opposed to two-sided unpaired t-test in any instance of non-normal distribution.

Tissue preparation for next-generation RNA sequencing (RNASeq)

Tissue preparation for bulk transcriptomic analysis was completed according to methods described in Mendez et al., [31] Briefly, RNA was extracted from 50 mg of BA9 tissue using RNeasy Plis Mini kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and [RIN] was measured for RNA quality (Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). 1 µg RNA per sample was used for mRNA library construction using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina Inc, San Diego, CA). Sample sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC. Reads were mapped to the GRCh38 genome with TopHat v2.1.0 4 and Cufflinks v2.2.1 using default parameters. The annotation file was downloaded from NCBI (GCF_000001405.39_GRCh38.p13.gtf). Gene and transcript counts were obtained using htseq-count. From the raw counts, gene lengths and library size, counts per million (CPM), fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM), and transcripts per million (TPM) were calculated. To avoid inflation ratio, the expression of genes with FPKM and TPM values < 0.1 were set to 0.1. Differential expression (DE) analysis was performed using R Bioconductor packages edgeR and limma [11, 12]. Genes with CPM > 1 in more than 5 individuals were kept for analysis. Read counts were normalized by Trimmed Means of M (TMM) [13]. The linear model design formula consisted of control/OUD diagnosis, age, PMI, RIN, sex, and cerebellar pH ( ~ 0+Diagnosis+age+PMI + RIN+pH+sex). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using the following cutoffs: absolute fold change |FC | > 1.5, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.2 or FDR < 0.05 and at least one samples’ FPKM > 1 [31].

Tissue preparation for bulk proteomic quantification

Proteomics datasets have been already published in Leung et al., 2022 and were downloaded to be used in this work [32]. For the initial proteomic calculation the following procedure was used, fifty milligrams of tissue were lysed (RIPA), reduced (TCEP), alkylated (iodoacetamide), acetone-precipitated, and processed on S-Trap spin columns for tryptic digestion (1:25, 37 °C, 4 h). Peptides were analyzed by nanoLC–MS/MS (UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano coupled to Thermo Orbitrap Fusion; trap-and-elute on C18; 400 nL/min gradient). Data were acquired in positive-ion top-speed DDA (3-s cycle; survey scans m/z 350–1500 at 120,000 resolution at m/z 400; max IT 50 ms; AGC 4 × 10^5) using Xcalibur. Raw files were processed in MaxQuant and searched against the UniProt/Swiss-Prot human database; peptide IDs were accepted at > 95% probability, yielding 4612 proteins across samples. Data were normalized and imputed; differential expression used protein-wise linear models with empirical Bayes (DEP/limma) and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. The design included diagnosis, age, postmortem interval, cerebellar pH, and sex ( ~ 0 + Diagnosis + age + PMI + pH + sex). Differentially expressed proteins were defined by adjusted P < 0.05 and |fold change | >1.5. LGALS3 levels were validated in the same cohort by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Raw MS data: ProteomeXchange PRIDE, PXD025269 [31].

Synaptosome enriched preparations

To focus on GABAARs enriched at the synapse for additional proteomics analysis and electrophysiological recordings, we isolated synaptosomes from the DLPFC of each individual. This approach allows us to study the flow from transcription to function of GABAARs in AUD. Briefly, we used the Syn-PER method whereby brain tissue was homogenized using polytetrafluoroethylene and glass tissue grinders and then underwent two rounds of centrifugation to obtain three fractions [37]. These three fractions include: S1 which contains soluble cytosolic elements, the P1 which contains nuclei, myelin, and large non-homogenized tissue, and the P2 which is the synaptosome enriched region and the fraction used for MSM, TEVC, and synaptosome proteomic analysis [30–32]. These fractions were stored at -80 °C until used for downstream analyses. Protein quantification of synaptosomes was done using Qubit (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific Waltham, MA, USA) and A DeNovix QFX fluorometer instrument (Denovix Inc, Wilmington, USA). Supplementary Table 2 shows high presynaptic, and postsynaptic enrichment data from our isolated P2s, while enrichment in non-synaptic proteins is not statistically significant [38]. These enrichment analyses were completed with SynGO database [38].

Synaptic proteomic profile and differential synaptic protein expression

To assess how transcriptomics changes in AUD individuals affect the expression synaptic proteins, we analyzed the synapto-proteome in the same individuals. P2 fractions equivalent to 8 μg of total protein were prepared for analysis using standard preparation protocols including reduction and alkylation of cysteine and trypsin digestion [39, 40]. Each of the recovered peptide mixtures were analyzed using 120-minute gradient LC-MS/MS analysis source and HCD higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) as the selected fragmentation mode. Files were searched together using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 software (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with Sequest. MS Amanda nodes were used to perform peptide identification and the Minora node was used to perform Label-Free Quan (LFQ) using the MS peak areas for each of the peptide-spectral matches (PSMs). Data was searched against the human database and common contaminant proteome and output files were uploaded into Scaffold due to its stricter stringency for identification and quantification. Normalization was set to iBAQ [41].

GABAARs transcriptomic and proteomic organization analysis

To analyze the organizational diversity of GABAAR subunits at the transcriptional and proteomic levels across individuals, and between AUD and non-AUD controls, we first quantified inter-individual variability in the RNA sequencing and LFQ data by calculating each subject’s individual Euclidian distances (di) of each of the 19 GABAAR subunits using an R code developed from following previously described protocols (Supplementary Data 1–12) [42, 43]. We then determined the coefficients of correlation (R) (Pearson-product moment) between di and the consensus Euclidian distances (dc) composed by all control subjects which summarizes GABAAR organization into a single value, R(di,dc) with this same code [42]. Finally, we compared all individuals to the consensus controls using Ward’s hierarchical clustering with robust standardization and dendrograms to assist in visualizing associations between the different subunits.

Microtransplantation of synaptic membranes (MSM)

To study the electrophysiological properties of GABAARs in AUD we microtransplanted synaptic receptors into Xenopus oocytes [44]. First, oocyte extraction and preparation was completed in accordance with previous protocols [37, 39, 45]. Xenopus laevis frogs were placed in anesthetic solution (0.17% MS222) for 10–15 min before extracting the ovaries following procedures in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the IACUC at the University of Texas Medical Branch at Galveston (IACUC:1803024). Oocytes were isolated and defolliculated by delicately incorporating them in a 50 ml centrifuge tube with a 2 mg/mL collagenase containing solution for 2-4 h at 30°C. Oocytes were then transferred to a Petri dish containing standard Barth’s solution (SBS) [88 mM NaCl, 1 mM KCl, 0.41 mM CaCl2, 0.82 mM MgSO4, 2.4 mM NaHCO3, 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4)] and placed in a 16°C fridge for at least 24 h based on previous protocols [37]. Healthy stage V–VI oocytes were then manually separated and placed in fresh SBS for injection with synaptosome-enriched membranes. Synaptosome-enriched aliquots were sonicated 3 times for 5 s at 1 min intervals in ice, to create small proteoliposomes that result from the breakdown of synaptosomes before they were injected into oocytes. Each oocyte was injected with 50 nL of synaptic proteoliposomes (2 mg/mL protein concentration) and underwent electrophysiological recordings 18–36 h post-injection as described in previously [37, 39, 45–48].

Two-electrode voltage clamp electrophysiology

For TEVC recordings, oocytes were placed in a perfusion chamber (volume ≈0.1 ml) where they were continuously perfused (5–10 ml/min) with Ringer’s solution [115 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4)] at room temperature (19–21°C) [37, 39, 45].. Microtransplanted GABAARs were activated for 6 s with GABA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) at different concentrations (from 1 µM to 3 mM), followed by a 3-minute recovery period within each application, to allow recovery from desensitization. An n = 2-3 oocytes for each sample was collected and averaged. The Hill equation was used to determine EC50 values for each control and AUD sample. EC50 values were transformed to a negative logarithm or pEC50 to homogenize variance across groups.

To investigate the functional rundown of post-synaptic GABAAR in AUD individuals we completed additional experiments in using the Robocyte2 (Multichannel Systems, Reutlingen, Germany) to complete automated, high quality TEVC recordings. Each oocyte was dosed with six 10 s applications of GABA 1 mM separated by a 40 s waiting period between applications similar to work previously described in Li et al., 2011 [49]. An n = 3-6 oocytes was collected for each sample. The average functional rundown ratio was then calculated by dividing the average peak GABAAR amplitude of the sixth application by the average peak GABAAR amplitude of the first application for each sample [49, 50]. These values were used in significance testing using GraphPad Prism. For functional rundown experiments, data acquisition was performed using Robocyte2 (Multichannel Systems, Reutlingen, Germany) and data analysis was done using Robocyte2+ (Multichannel Systems, Reutlingen, Germany), JMP Pro 18, and GraphPad Prism. Additionally, deactivation kinetics were calculated using the first trace of each oocyte included in the functional rundown analysis by using the curve fitting tool from Clampfit 11.1 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) to fit each a portion of each trace to exponential standard to derive a mean τdeactivation for each sample. Mean τdeactivation values were then used in a significance testing between AUD and control cohorts. An n = 1–4 was collected for each sample.

To study the desensitization properties of post-synaptic GABAAR in AUD individuals each oocyte was perfused with 1 mM GABA to elicit initial change in current with the GABA application maintained until the current returned to baseline or stabilized. Using Clampfit 11.1 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA), we calculated the desensitization of each oocyte recording trace, as well as used the curve fitting tool to fit each trace to exponential standard to derive τ1 and τ2 time constant values. An n = 3-4 (for τ1 and τ2 values) oocytes for each sample were collected and averaged. Statistical comparisons were completed using JMP Pro 17 and GraphPad Prism. Data acquisition was performed using WinEDR V3.2.7 Strathclyde Electrophysiology software (John Dempster, Glasgow, UK) with Oocyte Clamp OC-725C amplifier (Warner Instruments, Holliston, MA) and NI USB-6341 X series multifunction DAQ digitizer (National Instruments, Austin, TX) for dose-response and kinetics experiments. Data analysis was done with WinEDR V3.2.7. and Clampfit 11.1 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) for dose-response and kinetic experiments.

Quality control measures for our electrophysiology experiments include implementing noninjected oocytes in every recording as a negative control and oocytes injected with the same rat cortex 2ug/ul preparation in every recording as a positive control. To minimize potential bias, electrophysiological recording of oocytes was completed by systematically alternating between injected oocytes from each experimental group. A total of 19 Xenopus laevis oocyte batches were used for this project. For dose-response and kinetics experiments, at least one AUD sample and at least one control sample were recorded per batch. Through the course of the experiment, an n = 2–3 oocytes for each sample were not always able to be collected, so multiple batches and recording days were implemented to make sure electrophysiological data was collected for each sample. For functional rundown experiments, we were able to record 2–4 AUD donors and 2–4 control donors per recording using the same oocyte batch using the automated Robocyte2 (Multichannel Systems, Reutlingen, Germany). Oocyte batch information has been outlined in Supplementary Table 3.

Electrophysiology data inclusion criteria

Upon arrival at our laboratory all AUD and control samples underwent baseline recording in response to a 6–10 s application of GABA 1 mM against a noninjected oocyte and a rat cortex injection oocyte. For dose-response recordings, individual samples’ GABAAR current amplitudes that were smaller than that donor’s baseline recordings were re-recorded and not included in the final analysis. Additionally, samples recordings that demonstrated varying GABAAR current peak amplitudes inconsistent with increasing GABA concentration application were re-recorded and not included in the final analysis. For τ1, τ2, and τdeactivation calculations, time constants included in analysis were derived from exponential standard curves fit to original traces with correlation coefficients ≥ 0.90. Based on this metric, one sample from the AUD (Subject 19/AUD994) and one sample from the control cohort (Subject 6/CON945) had to be excluded from the deactivation analysis. Additionally, for τdeactivation, percent coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated to ensure reliability of exponential fits for remaining individual tau values of each oocyte. CV was calculated by dividing the standard error by individual τdeactivation values and then multiplying by 100% [51]. Only traces with CV of < 10% were included in the final analysis. Recordings in which the GABA application was interrupted abruptly resulting in acute returns to baseline were excluded. All raw data from excluded recordings has been saved and is available on request.

Functional enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes and proteins

To integrate data across various levels of analysis, we performed an electrophysiologically-anchored dataset analysis by correlating multi-omics data with the electrophysiological responses of post-synaptic GABAARs [39, 45]. We then conducted functional enrichment analysis of significantly correlated transcripts and proteins using Metascape [52]. Transcriptomic analysis was done against the whole genome and using gene prioritization by evidence counting (GPEC) [52]. Pathway, gene, and protein enrichment implemented a minimum overlap of 3 and a p-value threshold of 0.01 [52]. Both sets of proteomics data were analyzed using GPEC and the total list of identified proteins by LC-MS/MS as background. The integrated analysis of transcriptomic, proteomic, synaptosome proteomic and the subset of genes and proteins with significant correlations (p < 0.01 for proteomics and p < 0.001 for transcriptomics) to the negative logarithm of GABA EC50 values and GABAAR ion current amplitudes was completed using linear analysis in JMP Pro 17 followed by Metascape multi-list feature [52].

Power analyses

We implemented G*Power 3.1 post hoc power analyses of all significance tests between our two experimental groups. We then compared those results with a priori power analyses of the same experiment using the effect size values calculated from our results. All data was tested for normal-distribution using Shapiro-Wilkes. If data exhibited non-normal distribution, we used the R “cliff.delta” function using the “effsize” package to calculate Cliff’s delta and then estimated effect size by converting Cliff’s delta into a probability of superiority and then transforming that value into a Z-score using concepts outlined by Kerby, 2024 [53].(Supplementary Data 13,14). This Z-score is scaled into Cohen’s d. To put our findings into the context of our experimental design we have included this information in Supplementary Table 4.

Results

Transcriptomic divergence of GABAAR and mitochondria in AUD individuals

Based on our scientific premise, we tested whether the expression of GABAAR subunits was altered in AUD vs. control individuals. We did not observe changes in most of the GABAARs subunits; however, we observed a borderline trend of reduction of GABRD (p = 0.05, Mann-Whitney test, two-sided) levels and a significant increase of GABRG1 (p = 0.03, unpaired t-test, two-sided) levels in AUD individuals (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 5). Genetic variation in the GABRG1 gene, which encodes for the γ1 subunit, has previously been associated with alcohol dependence [54, 55]. Our findings coincide with previous human research studies highlighting that variation to GABRG1 may be a genetic risk factor for alcohol-related problems [55, 56]. GABRD encodes for the extracellular δ subunit which is highly sensitive to alcohol [17, 18, 57]. Because GABAARs are pentameric, changes in one subunit may lead to stoichiometric rearrangements without change in abundance of subunits; therefore, we tested whether the organization of GABAARs transcripts in AUD diverges from controls. For this analysis, we used hierarchical clustering of Euclidian distances between GABAAR subunit transcript levels which is informative of the subunit organization [42]. Fig. 1B, C shows that the organization of GABAARs in AUD individuals is significantly distinct and more variable than in controls (p = 0.009, Mann-Whitney, two-sided) (Fig. 1B), and that this variability correlates with transcripts highly enriched in the metabolism of RNA and increased serum lactate (Fig. 1D, E). Remarkably, increased serum lactate is a predictor of morbidity in AUD as lactate clearance is attenuated by alcohol consumption increments [58], and has strong effects on inhibitory synaptic transmission [59, 60]. Both animal and human research confirms that chronic ethanol consumption can lead to PFC metabolic abnormalities [61, 62]. Furthermore, this project’s functional enrichment analysis results (Fig. 1D) confirms previous studies and guided our decision to investigate differences in mitochondrial gene expression within our cohort. We identified 1121 mitochondrial genes in our RNA sequencing dataset using an inventory of human genes outlined by MitoCarta 3.0 [63–65]. From those 1121 mitochondrial genes identified in our RNA sequencing data 59 transcripts were significantly decreased in AUD individuals and 9 transcripts were significantly increased (response screening analysis from JMP Pro 16, high-throughput unpaired t-tests, no normality testing, exact p-values outlined in Supplementary Table 6). Decreased genes are highly enriched in mitochondrial organization and mitochondrial gene expression which was further emphasized by protein-protein interaction network analysis (Fig. 1F, G). Proteome organization of GABAARs and mitochondria does not reflect transcriptomic alterations in AUD

Bulk proteomic analysis of the DLPFC identified 7 GABAARs subunits, all of which are recognized to be located at synapses. We did not detect extrasynaptic subunits suggesting lower abundance. Dendrograms of Euclidian distances for protein levels show that GABRA1, GABRB2, and GABRG2 are clustered together, reflecting the α1β2γ2 stoichiometry of the most abundant receptor in cortical regions (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Table 7). Interestingly, AUD shows reduced organizational divergence of GABAARs subunits, particularly for the subunits β3 and α5, but not statistically significant compared to controls (p = 0.152, Mann-Whitney, two-sided) (Fig. 2B). Proteins that positively correlate with the variability of GABAARs are highly enriched in vesicle-mediated transport, intracellular protein transport, and membrane organization and maintenance pathways (Fig. 2D). We also found that these proteins are functionally enriched in myoclonus, cerebral atrophy, and slowed electroencephalogram (EEG) activity, when annotated using human disease ontology databases (Fig. 2D). All of these symptoms can be associated with either abrupt alcohol abstinence [66] or alcohol use disorder [67–69]. To determine whether mitochondrial transcripts that were differentially expressed in AUD individuals translate into changes at the protein level, we ran a large scale oneway ANOVA of the bulk proteome dataset based on diagnosis. Of the 68 mitochondrial transcripts found altered in AUD, 38 were identified in the bulk proteome and only one (ACAA2, a key enzyme in energy production from fats) was abnormally high in AUD individuals (p = 0.008, Mann-Whitney, two-sided) (Fig. 2E). Proteomic analyses of isolated synaptosomes suggest reorganization of GABAARs and trafficking proteins in AUD

To confirm that the bulk proteome does not reflect transcriptomic changes, we did the same analysis on synaptosome-enriched preparations. Functional enrichment analysis of the 2810 proteins identified by LFQ proteomics confirmed strong presynaptic and postsynaptic enrichment, while the contribution of non-synaptic proteins was negligible (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 2). We detected 6 GABAARs subunits which were the same ones identified in the bulk proteome, except for α5 (GABRA5). We did not detect δ or γ1 which have historically exhibited abnormal transcriptomic expression in AUD individuals [12, 70, 71]. Hierarchical clustering of synaptic protein levels showed less divergence in the organization and variance of GABAAR subunits between AUD and control individuals (Fig. 3C; p = 0.36, Mann-Whitney, two-sided; Supplementary Table 8). The dynamic relationship between membrane insertion, lateral diffusion, and internalization greatly affects GABAARs [15]. To probe whether AUD may influence GABAAR function by modifications to trafficking proteins, which would not be observed by measuring subunit abundance or potential stoichiometric rearrangement alone, we did hierarchical clustering analyses including common synaptic GABAAR trafficking proteins [14, 15, 72] in tandem with GABAAR subunits. The synaptic GABAAR trafficking proteins selected for analysis were N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor (NSF), ADP ribosylation factor guanine nucleotide exchange factor 2 (ARFGEF2 which encodes for the brefeldin A-Inhibited guanine nucleotide-exchange protein 2 or BIG2 protein), phospholipase C like 1 (PLCL2), phospholipase C like 2 (PLCL2), cleft lip and palate transmembrane protein 1 (CLPTM1), and GABA receptor-associated protein-like 2 (GABARAPL2) (Supplementary Table 9) [14, 15, 72, 73]. Dendrograms illustrate some divergence of PLC1 and PCL2 in the synaptic proteome of AUD individuals (Fig. 3E) but did not significantly affect the overall organization of GABAAR trafficking proteins and subunits in AUD (p = 0.4, Mann-Whitney, two-sided) (Fig. 3F). We also investigated how mitochondrial transcriptomic changes translate to protein changes at the synapse level. Only 25 mitochondrial proteins were identified in the synaptic proteome with only IDH3A showing lower expression levels in AUD individuals (p = 0.002, unpaired t-test, two-sided) (Fig. 3G). IDH3A is an isocitrate dehydrogenase that catalyzes a step in the tricarboxylic acid cycle.

Both ex vivo and in vivo models of alcohol intoxication have demonstrated that GABAAR stochiometric arrangements were implicated in changes to the receptor’s kinetics acute activation with ethanol [74, 75]. With this in mind, another valuable functional metric of GABAARs that we wanted to investigate in the DLPFC of our AUD individuals was functional rundown. Functional rundown of ionotropic receptors offers insights into how functional populations of GABAAR decay over time with repeated agonist exposure and has been used previously to describe GABAAR function in diseased brain states related to GABAergic hypofunction [49, 50]. To measure functional rundown properties of GABAAR in AUD individuals, we dosed each oocyte with 1 mM GABA six times for 10 s with only a 40 s waiting period between GABA applications and then calculated the average functional rundown ratio for each sample [49]. 4 F, G exhibit representative ion currents traces of this experiment from AUD and control groups. 4H illustrates the average amplitude of each sample as well as group averages. Statistical testing for significant difference in average functional rundown ratio shows that there are no significant differences in mean functional rundown ratios between AUD and control cohorts (4I, p = 0.95, unpaired t-test, two-sided, Supplementary table 10). To calculate GABAAR deactivation (4 J, K), mean τdeactivation values for each sample were calculated from resulting traces and used in significance testing. 4 L demonstrates that there is no difference in GABAAR deactivation between AUD and controls (4 L, p = 1.00, unpaired t-test, two-sided, Supplementary table 10).

Because we observed transcriptomic changes to the γ1 subunit we wanted to ensure whether there was a functional impact, particularly because GABAAR subunit stoichiometry, (e.g the γ -position), play a crucial role in the desensitization of the receptor in response to GABA [76]. To test for significant differences in GABAAR desensitization between AUD and controls when agonist concentration is held constant, we dosed each oocyte with a continuous application of GABA 1 mM which was maintained until the current amplitude returned to baseline or stabilized (4 M, N). To gain a deeper understanding of the constituent fast and slow processes of GABAAR current amplitude desensitization in AUD individuals, we derived time constants (τ1 and τ2) from these same traces using curve fitting to the exponential standard. Significance testing of τ1 (4 O, p = 0.35, unpaired t-test, two-sided) and τ2 (4 P, p = 0.95, unpaired t-test, two-sided) shows that there are no significant differences in time constants describing desensitization between AUD and controls.

To identify genes and proteins that could explain the variance in amplitude and sensitivity measured in our electrophysiological experiments, we implemented large scale simple linear regression using the response screening tool in JMP Pro 16. Genes with p < 0.001 and proteins with p < 0.01 that correlated with the peak amplitude and pEC50 were chosen and categorized into negative or positive correlations based on slope. Neither transcript levels nor bulk protein measures of GABRA1 correlated with the amplitude of GABA currents. Analysis excluding AUD donors with comorbid MDD

MDD is associated with hypoactivity in cortical regulatory regions like the DLPFC [79]. Acute alcohol use can transiently counteract MDD symptoms by increasing neurotransmitter activity [80, 81], but chronic AUD can exacerbate MDD symptoms through its neurotoxic affects [82]. Because in some instances MDD and AUD may exert opposing effects in emotion related circuitry, we re-analyzed all electrophysiology results after excluding AUD donors with MDD comorbidity (Supplementary Figure 2). We re-analyzed dose-response curves, GABA pEC50 values, GABAAR current amplitudes, GABAAR functional rundown data, GABAAR deactivation data, and GABAAR desensitization data (Supplementary Figure 2). Removal of these donors did not change the lack of significant differences between AUD and control samples for any of our functional metrics. Next, we used an electrophysiology-anchored workflow to survey pathway-enrichment differences by major depressive disorder (MDD) status. In the “–MDD” subset (participants without comorbid MDD), we performed response-screening at each omic layer to identify transcripts and proteins whose abundances correlated significantly with GABA amplitudes and pEC50 values. We repeated the analysis in the “+MDD” subset (including participants with comorbid MDD) and compared the resulting pathway-enrichment profiles. For each gene/protein set, we then ran functional enrichment in Metascape to delineate pathways that differed between –MDD and +MDD. (Supplementary Figure 3, Supplementary Table 19,20) Based on this analysis, there is far more overlap between gene and protein lists when comorbid MDD cases are excluded, but there are multiple functional pathways relating to GABAAR function that overlap between +MDD and -MDD. For example, in both groups proteins that significantly and negatively correlate to GABA amplitudes are significantly enriched in functional pathways involved in cellular response to stress, pathways in neurodegeneration for multiple diseases, membrane trafficking, regulation of synaptic plasticity, and VEGFA VEGFRA signaling. However, to thoroughly answer this question, we believe a more in-depth electrophysiological-anchored analysis with a larger sample size is warranted.

Discussion

Our analysis shows significant alterations to the transcriptomic organization of GABAARs in AUD individuals along with a significant increase GABRG1 and a significant decrease in mitochondrial genes responsible for mitochondrial gene expression. Interestingly, Enoch et al., 2012 reported a trend towards downregulation of GABRG1 in postmortem hippocampal tissue of AUD individuals [70] which differs from our findings of upregulated transcripts in the DLPFC. This may point to brain region specific differences to GABRG1 expression in AUD individuals which may contribute to its role as a genetic risk factor for alcohol dependence and AUD. While behavioral affects from γ1-specific overexpression is limited, genetic studies found that individuals with genetic variation in GABRG1 reported needing to consume more drinks to feel typical intoxicating effects and more alcohol related problems such as fighting or drinking interfering with other responsibilities [55].

Our list of significantly decreased mitochondrial transcripts in AUD individuals was functionally enriched in mitochondrion organization and mitochondrial gene expression pathways. Mitochondrion organization and mitochondrial gene expression are crucial for synaptic plasticity and stability because of their role in the high energy demands and calcium buffering demands at synapses [83, 84]. Also, mitochondria support vesicle release and recycling [83] which are functions related to enriched pathways observed in our electrophysiologically-anchored analysis (Vesicle mediated transport and regulation of trans-synaptic signaling, Fig. 6B). Decreased levels of mitochondrial transcripts crucial to these functions could theoretically modulate GABAergic signaling, but it is unclear in our findings, as well as in the current literature the exact mechanism by which mitochondrial dysfunction directly cause changes to GABAARs. Although the significant decrease in these transcripts is most likely related to alcohol related pathology in our AUD cohort, analysis of mitochondrial genes and proteins should be performed with a larger sample size to prove this with certainty.

Our results also feature a trend of decreased GABRD in AUD individuals which aligns with previous findings regarding this subunit. Epigenetic modifications were found to affect the expression of GABRD in AUD postmortem brains [71] which has been linked to a significant decrease in GABRD expression in the PFC of AUD individuals [12]. GABRD knockout mice demonstrated reduced preference and voluntary consumption of ethanol [85] In other in vivo experiments, δ-knockdown animals exhibit lower alcohol consumption [86, 87] without a reduction in consumption of sucrose drinking control [87]. This highlights the δ subunit’s role in reinforcing effects of ethanol [86, 87]. Based on the function of GABRD in alcohol use and the development of alcohol dependence behaviors, our findings suggest a possible mechanism in which GABRD downregulation in the transcriptome could counter excessive GABAAR activation by ethanol. However, this phenomenon is not definitively proven or defined by this research and should be tested in a mechanism-focused experiment. Perhaps this trend toward decrease in GABRD and reorganization of its expression highlights a shift in the balance between phasic and tonic GABAAR signaling due to nature of synaptic γ1 subunit’s modulation of phasic inhibition [15, 17, 19, 20], and δ subunit’s contribution to tonic inhibition and high sensitivity to GABA and ethanol [19–21, 57]. This concept has been previously explored in the Herman, Contet & Roberto, 2016 in which chronic intermittent ethanol exposure to mice affected the kinetics of phasic inhibition specific a specific neuronal cell type in the central amygdala, while chronic exposure resulted in a loss of sensitivity to acute ethanol exposure [88].

Intriguingly, these changes to the transcriptome in AUD individuals were not observed at the protein level either bulk or in synapses. The lack of significant differences in the abundance of GABAARs subunits between AUD and controls was echoed in the functional analysis of post-synaptic GABAARs in that there were no significant differences in any of our electrophysiological measures between AUD and control individuals. Synaptic α1 (GABRA1) was the only subunit significantly correlated with GABAAR current amplitudes. However, its relative abundance did not differ significantly in the DLPFC between AUD and control individuals at transcriptomic, proteomic, or synaptoproteomic levels. This finding highlights a salient departure from previous animal studies which showed a significant decrease in α1 in alcohol treated animals [24, 89, 90]. Our results coincide with findings from previous AUD studies that describe lack of significant changes to GABAergic signaling proteins but instead demonstrate significant changes to mitochondria in AUD post-mortem brain tissue [91] and AUD primate models [92]. Additionally, prior research supports the presence of post-translational buffering mechanisms that can attenuate or “buffer” variations in mRNA levels so that transcriptomic deviations are prevented from translating fully to final protein abundance [26–29]. This disconnect between transcriptomic and proteomic expression in disease has been previously implied in a large-scale analysis of over 1000 human brain proteomes [30]. In this study Wingo et al., 2025 suggests that genetic influences that shape final protein expression related to neurological disease phenotypes may not be fully mediated by mRNA levels [30].

Results from our electrophysiologically-anchored analysis (Fig. 6) may demonstrate a mechanism by which the brain produces proteins that dampen GABA-ergic responses to GABA which correlates to a mitigation in neurodegenerative functional pathways. Additionally, this process may also be employed to counteract excessive inhibition caused by excessive alcohol use or reflect the damage caused by excessive alcohol use. Interestingly, proteins that negatively correlate with GABA amplitudes are functionally enriched in pathways related to the axon and genes that significantly and positively correlate with perineuronal oligodendrocytes in the BA9 region [77, 93, 94]. (Fig. 6B). This finding supports previous research which outlines the inflammatory responses resulting from chronic alcohol consumption effecting myelin fiber disruption [95, 96] which may interfere with synaptic integrity and neurotransmission which could lead to challenges maintaining proper GABAergic tone.

Possible limitations to experimental design include batch differences between Xenopus frogs that could have caused variability between sample recordings, however this was counteracted by using noninjected negative control oocytes and a standard rat cortex injected positive control oocytes which also aided in quantifying oocyte viability. Only male samples were available for AUD analysis from the UTHealth Houston Brain Collection which limits this experiment’s ability to account for sex differences. Within our cohort one of the AUD subject’s cause of death is carisoprodol toxicity. Carisoprodol is a skeletal muscle relaxant that acts on the central nervous system [97]. Although the mechanism of action of carisoprodol is unknown, this drug does have the ability to modulate GABAAR subunits [97]. This individual does not present as an outlier to any of our functional or multi-omic data, which is why is has been included in final analysis.

The main outcomes of this project describe a complex relationship between the transcriptome and DLPFC proteome organization of GABAAR in AUD individuals, downstream expression of GABAAR proteins, and post-synaptic GABAAR function in the DLPFC. Observations in our multi-omic and electrophysiology data highlight the subtlety of measurable neurobiological changes in humans who experience chronic alcohol use. This phenomenon was previously reported in longitudinal study of alcohol-related blackouts and their relationship to attenuated brain development by Lorkiewicz et. al [98]. Future research directions involving our unique AUD cohort include the inclusion of female postmortem DLPFC samples, investigating extrasynaptic GABAARs function, testing presynaptic mechanisms, characterizing glutamatergic receptors, and testing the post-synaptic response to ethanol. Information gained from our project enhances the current knowledge-based regarding the structural and functional characteristics of native GABAARs in the DLPFC of humans with AUD, which is crucial for developing effective pharmacotherapies for this pervasive and debilitating disorder.
