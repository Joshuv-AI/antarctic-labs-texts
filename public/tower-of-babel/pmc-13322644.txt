Noninferiority of Online Versus In-Person Training for Health Professionals Delivering the COPE Dementia Care Program in PACE Settings
Authors: Nancy A. Hodgson, Karen B. Hirschman, Liming Huang, Emily Summerhayes, Catherine Verrier Piersol, Patricia Pokrandt, Caroline McElroy, Laura N Gitlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study evaluates the equivalency (noninferiority) of two approaches to training clinicians in the delivery of an evidence-based dementia caregiving program in PACE (Programs of All-Inclusive Care of the Elderly) settings. Considered are clinician outcomes following each training method and PACE participant-caregiver outcomes following COPE receipt after 4 months.Design:11 PACE settings were randomized to have clinicians trained in-person (n = 7) or online (n = 4) to deliver COPE to caregivers of patients with dementia.Setting:PACE provides long-term care services and support to dually eligible older adults (PACE participants), most of whom have dementia diagnoses, to age in place at home.Participants:The study included occupational therapists (n = 14) and registered nurses (n = 8) employed at participating PACE sites. Caregivers (n = 17) and PACE participants (n = 17) with dementia were enrolled as dyads to receive COPE.Measurements:Primary outcomes included changes in clinician knowledge and training satisfaction via the Knowledge and Skills Survey and implementation competence via the Normalization Process Theory (NoMad) survey. For dyad outcomes, caregivers identified priority areas using validated assessments, selected up to four “target behaviors” and rated confidence in managing each.Results:No significant differences were found between training groups in clinician or dyad characteristics. Training modalities showed similar impacts on clinician knowledge and skills, with no statistically significant differences between groups in change scores across NoMad domains (all p >0.3). After COPE, all caregivers reported significant improvement in the Perceived Change Index (p = 0.0034) and increased confidence in managing target behaviors.Conclusion:Online training in COPE was noninferior to in-person training for clinician competence and PACE participant-caregiver outcomes. These findings support online training as a scalable approach for disseminating evidence-based dementia care programs without compromising effectiveness.

INTRODUCTION

Despite over 200 evidence-based dementia caregiver programs, few have been successfully translated to real-world settings, creating a critical public health implementation gap.1,2 This challenge is particularly urgent given that over 6 million Americans currently live with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias,a number expected to more than double by 2050.3 The economic and societal costs are substantial, with families providing an estimated 18 billion hours of unpaid care annually.4

A key barrier to implementing evidence-based dementia caregiver support programs is workforce preparation.5 The lack of streamlined, scalable training approaches for health professionals has received insufficient attention despite being a major contributor to the research-practice gap.6,7 Traditional face-to-face, expert-led training creates dissemination bottlenecks and limits intervention reach, particularly in underserved and resource-constrained settings. This approach also requires ongoing developer involvement, creating sustainability challenges even with train-the-trainer models.

Online platforms offer a promising alternative, providing flexible, accessible education that can improve knowledge retention, reduce costs, and increase guideline adherence.8,9 However, while self-paced online training demonstrates strong provider-level outcomes, evidence remains insufficient regarding its impact on actual program delivery and subsequent client outcomes. This represents a critical knowledge gap, as training success should be measured not only by provider competence but also by whether target populations derive benefits comparable to those found in efficacy trials.

This study addresses this gap by comparing online versus traditional in-person training for clinicians delivering COPE (Care of Persons in their Environment), an evidence-based dementia program, within Programs of All-Inclusive Care of the Elderly (PACE) settings.10 By simultaneously examining both clinician and PACE participant-caregiver (dyad) outcomes, this research provides novel insights into how training modality influences the complete implementation-to-outcome pathway in dementia care.

METHODS

Study Design and Procedures

We conducted a pragmatic, multisite randomized controlled noninferiority trial to evaluate two different approaches to training clinicians in the COPE program in PACE settings between 2019 and 2024 (ClinicalTrial.gov: NCT04165213). We used a noninferiority trial design as it is a particularly suitable approach for comparing two different training modalities. Rather than attempting to demonstrate superiority of a new approach, noninferiority trials aim to show that an alternative approach is not meaningfully worse than the established standard. We selected this approach because even if online training was slightly less effective, its advantages in terms of cost, accessibility, and scalability could justify its adoption if both clinician and dyadic outcomes remained within an acceptable margin of the traditional approach.

Two training approaches for clinicians delivering the COPE program were compared. The control condition consisted of traditional in-person, instructor-led training delivered as a synchronous two-day session at each participating PACE site. The intervention condition utilized asynchronous, self-paced online training comprising 10 web-based modules (up to 60 minutes each) delivered through an interactive learning management platform. Both training approaches covered identical content. Following training completion, clinicians in both conditions completed measures assessing knowledge and skill acquisition (described below), then implemented COPE with eligible dyads (PACE participants living with dementia and their caregivers) over a 4-month period. Data collection with dyads occurred at baseline (prior to receiving COPE) and at 4-month follow-up.

Two primary hypotheses were tested. First, we hypothesized that online training would be noninferior to in-person training (the gold standard) for enhancing clinician self-reported knowledge and competence in delivering the COPE program in PACE settings. Second, we hypothesized that the clinical outcomes for dyads would be similar for dyads receiving COPE from either online-trained or face-to-face instructor-trained clinicians.

We established a priori noninferiority margins based on previous COPE implementation studies and clinical significance thresholds for each outcome measure.11 For the first hypothesis, concerning clinician outcomes, we considered noninferiority established if the lower bound of the 90% confidence interval for the difference between groups did not exceed the predefined margin. For the second hypothesis concerning PACE participant and caregiver outcomes, we examined whether online training produced dyad outcomes that were not meaningfully worse than those achieved with traditional training. This comprehensive design allowed us to evaluate not only whether online training could effectively prepare clinicians to deliver COPE, but also whether any differences in training experience translated to meaningful differences in outcomes for the target population. Detailed study procedures have been previously documented.10

The COPE Program

COPE is an evidence-based program extensively described elsewhere11,12 that supports people living with dementia (PLWD) and their caregivers by targeting modifiable environmental stressors to reduce sensorial, physical, and cognitive demands while addressing underlying medical conditions contributing to challenging behaviors. The four-month home-based program is delivered by occupational therapists (OTs) providing up to 10 sessions and registered nurses (RNs) providing up to 3 sessions. A minimum of four occupational therapy sessions ensures adequate intervention dosage across three treatment elements: comprehensive assessments (PLWD capabilities/deficits, medical testing, home environment, caregiver communication); caregiver education (PLWD capabilities, medication effects, pain, constipation, dehydration); and caregiver training to address identified concerns and reduce stress.

Setting

COPE was implemented in PACE settings, which provide comprehensive community-based care to nursing home-eligible Medicare/Medicaid enrollees.13 While most PACE participants have dementia, evidence-based programs have not been integrated into their service mix.13,14 Among PACE participants with dementia, functional decline and caregiver strain are primary predictors of nursing home admission.14,15 Caregivers face significant physical and emotional burdens managing daily activities and behavioral symptoms, increasing their risk of depression and decisions to seek institutional care.15 The study was conducted across 11 Trinity Health PACE programs in eight states within the Northeast and Southeast regions of the United States. Programs were located in urban or suburban neighborhoods. Stakeholders—including providers, administrators, and caregivers—were engaged throughout the research process, from study design through implementation and dissemination.16

Randomization

PACE organizations were randomized using a balance match weighted (BMW) design to optimize covariate balance between groups.17 The process involved: 1) identifying key organizational covariates, 2) generating 20 random assignment lists, 3) calculating propensity scores for intervention assignment, and 4) selecting the assignment list that minimized propensity score differences between intervention and control organizations. This approach enhanced covariate balance and statistical power for detecting COPE program effects.

Sample

We used broad inclusion criteria reflecting PACE program heterogeneity and our pragmatic approach. Eligible staff were full-time OTs and RNs employed >1 year at participating sites. PLWD were eligible if enrolled at participating Trinity Health PACE sites, diagnosed with dementia or scoring ≥4 errors on the Mental Status Questionnaire,18 and English/Spanish-speaking. Caregivers were required to be the primary caregiver and to be available for telephone interviews at baseline, 4, and 9 months, and age ≥21 years.

Exclusion criteria for patients included schizophrenia/bipolar disorder, bedbound/unresponsive status, ≥3 hospitalizations in the past year, active terminal illness/hospice care, or participation in experimental behavioral drug studies. Dyads were excluded if planning to relocate or disenroll from their PACE service center within 9 months of joining the study. These criteria minimized attrition while excluding those unlikely to benefit from COPE due to high mortality risk.

Training interventionists

COPE in-person training (traditional):

The co-developer of the COPE program (CVP), an expert educator and primary COPE trainer, conducted in-person training using PowerPoint slides that framed the online training and presented case examples similar to those online. The in-person training took up to 10 hours and included literature, PowerPoint presentations, and case presentations. PACE sites were reimbursed for COPE staff members’ time to participate in either online or face-to-face training and follow-up coaching calls to troubleshoot cases. Standing bi-weekly coaching sessions were offered to all clinicians who received the in-person and online training.

COPE online training:

The COPE online training program delivered comprehensive content through ten self-paced online learning modules. Each module, requiring 45−60 minutes to complete, followed a structured four-dimensional hyper-learning model. This model progresses from initial learning objectives and core concepts through mini lectures, then advances to clinical reasoning exercises using case studies and vignettes, and concludes with postlearning assessments. The modules integrated interactive multimedia content and assessments, enabling clinicians to access training materials at their convenience. To ensure compatibility and shareability across institutions, the content adheres to the Shareable Content Object Reference Model (SCORM) specifications19. This standardization facilitated broader dissemination of the training program. For implementation, PACE organizations assigned to online training receive unique login credentials to complete the modules within a designated two-week window, aligning with the control group’s training timeline. The system comprehensively tracked training engagement through detailed session data, including dates, times, and duration of use.

Training equivalency and interactivity:

Both training conditions were designed to be equivalent in total instructional time and curriculum content. The in-person training was delivered over approximately 10 contact hours across two days; the online training comprised 10 modules of 45−60 minutes each, totaling a comparable 450−600 minutes of instructional engagement. All COPE content domains, including assessment protocols, caregiver education strategies, environmental modification approaches, and session delivery procedures, were addressed in both formats using the same program manual and clinical protocols.

The two modalities differed inherently in their mode of interactivity. The in-person training allowed for real-time interaction with the expert COPE trainer (CVP), peer-to-peer discussion among clinicians across the two-day sessions, and immediate clarification of clinical questions through live case presentations and Q&A. The online training achieved interactivity through embedded knowledge checks after each content section, multimedia case vignettes requiring clinical reasoning and decision-making, and module-level postassessments. The structured four-dimensional hyper-learning model was designed to replicate the scaffolded, active learning process characteristic of high-quality in-person instruction. Both groups also received standing bi-weekly coaching calls intended to standardize ongoing learning opportunities regardless of initial training modality.

Data collection

Outcome measures for clinicians were collected prior to training, post-training, and post-COPE delivery. Outcome measures for dyads were collected prior to the initiation of COPE (baseline) and after COPE delivery (4 months). Trained research assistants, blind to a site’s random assignment, collected outcome measures. An overview of the participant flow is provided in Fig. 1 (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram). The trial was prospectively registered with the ClinicalTrials.gov Registry (NCT04165213) with approval provided by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Pennsylvania.

Outcome measures

Aim 1: Clinician outcomes:

To evaluate clinician outcomes under Aim 1, we employed a comprehensive assessment approach using validated measures. The Knowledge and Skills Survey, previously validated in our trials,20 served as our primary tool for assessing changes in knowledge and training satisfaction. To understand competence, we administered the Normalization Process Theory (NoMad) survey before and after COPE implementation.21 This measure evaluated how implementation work was reconfigured both individually and collectively by the clinicians, examining four key domains: coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring. Clinicians rated 20 items on a five-point scale, with additional workload fit assessments rated from 0 to 10.

Aim 2: Dyad outcome measures:

To evaluate COPE’s efficacy across training modalities, we implemented a systematic assessment of dyads. The evaluation focused on clinically meaningful outcomes relevant to PACE participants with dementia, examining their priority needs related to behavioral symptoms and daily function, caregiver confidence managing care challenges and well-being, and quality of life. Data collection was conducted through telephone interviews by trained research assistants blinded to training group allocation, using assessments at baseline and 4-month follow-up. Selected outcome measures had strong psychometric properties, cultural sensitivity, brevity, and sensitivity to change. We employed a targeted measurement approach commonly used in medical, pharmacologic, psychotherapeutic, and behavior management trials to capture the most challenging problems (e.g., behaviors, dependence) for caregivers. To ensure the intervention addressed each family’s specific needs, caregivers actively participated in identifying their priority areas using two validated instruments of function and behaviors: the Caregiver Assessment of Function and Upset (CAFU)22 and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory−Questionnaire (NPI-Q).23 From these assessments, caregivers selected and ranked up to four “target behaviors,” they wanted to address and rated their confidence in managing each (on a 1−10 scale) and the duration of assistance required. These targeted behaviors were then reassessed at follow-up to measure whether confidence had improved in managing the area, an indicator of intervention effectiveness.

Caregiver confidence over the past month was measured by 5 investigator-developed items (identify daily activities the patient can do, involve the patient in activities, use activities to distract the PACE participant, manage boredom, set up activities) with ratings from 0 (not confident) to 10 (very confident).24 Mean scores were derived across items (actual range of means, 0.60−10.00), with higher scores indicating greater confidence (α = 0.87).

Caregiver well-being (improvement/worsening) was evaluated using the 13-item Perceived Change Index24 modeled after pharmacologic trial measures and shown to have strong psychometric properties. Caregivers rated change in their ability to manage dementia, their own emotional status (anger, distress), and somatic symptoms (energy, sleep quality) in the past month using 5-point scales (1, got much worse, to 5, improved a lot). The total mean score was derived by summing across items and dividing by the number of items. Higher scores indicated greater improvement (α = 0.86).

Quality of life, as responded by caregivers rating PACE participants, was measured using the 13-item Quality of Life—Alzheimer’s Disease (QOL-AD) scale (total score range 13−52; higher scores indicate better QOL).25 The QOL-AD scale uses a scale of 1−4 (poor, fair, good, or excellent) to rate a variety of life domains, including the PACE participant’s physical health, mood, relationships, activities, and ability to complete tasks (α = 0.82).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all measures at each time point. Given the small sample size (N = 14 dyads, N = 17 clinicians), nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine between-group differences. To demonstrate noninferiority of online training, Wilcoxon rank sum tests compared change scores between groups, with p < 0.1 considered significant for noninferiority inferences. All analyses used SAS 9.4.

RESULTS

Basic characteristics of study clinicians and dyads are presented in Table 1 and Table 2, respectively. The clinician sample included 17 female, non-Hispanic participants (mean age 47 years), with 8 in face-to-face and 9 in online training. Most were White (82.4%), college-educated (58.8%), and OTs (58.8%). The caregiver sample included 14 participants (7 per group), predominantly female (92.9%), family members (92.9%), who lived with the PACE participant (78.6%). PACE Participants were primarily male (57.1%) and White (57.1%). No significant between-group differences existed (all p >0.1), indicating successful site randomization.

Clinician Outcomes

Clinician outcomes (Tables 3 and 4)

Both groups demonstrated adequate COPE knowledge and self-reported competence with no significant differences between training modalities. Knowledge scores changed by 0.06 (in-person) versus − 0.25 (online) points (p = 0.3566); skills scores increased by 0.33 versus 0.06 points, respectively (p = 0.6944), supporting noninferiority. NoMad survey results showed significant improvements in COPE familiarity (2.82 points, p = 0.0087) and work integration (1.93 points, p = 0.0508) across all clinicians, with no between-group differences (all p >0.5). Training satisfaction was high in both groups, with no significant differences. All clinicians agreed or strongly agreed that COPE could benefit family caregivers and PLWD, and that they intended to use COPE in their clinical practice. On a scale of 1 to 10, participants reported high confidence in using COPE (mean score 7.82), with no significant differences between training groups (p >0.2), again supporting noninferiority.

Caregiver outcomes (Tables 5 and 6)

Caregivers showed significant improvement in the Perceived Change Index (change = 7.07, p = 0.0034). Confidence in managing target behaviors improved across both groups, with one notable exception: for the first target behavior, the online group showed significantly greater improvement than the in-person group (−0.33 versus 1.43, p = 0.0064).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that asynchronous online COPE training is noninferior to traditional in-person training across clinician and caregiver outcomes. The lack of significant differences between training modalities is particularly noteworthy given their substantially different resource requirements. These findings align with previous research on health professional education, suggesting that online learning can be as effective as face-to-face instruction for knowledge acquisition and self-reported competence, while offering greater flexibility and accessibility.7,8 It should be noted, however, that our outcomes were limited to self-reported knowledge, confidence, and NoMad implementation scores; direct observation of clinical skill performance was not assessed in this study.

The high ratings on the NoMad scale indicate successful normalization of COPE into routine practice across both training conditions. This successful integration reflects the importance of implementation strategies that extend beyond initial training to support practice change. Our results suggest that the crucial elements of training and implementation support—clear written protocols in the form of manuals, ongoing consultation, coaching and office hours, and performance feedback—can be effectively delivered in both synchronous and asynchronous formats.

Most importantly, noninferiority extended to the ultimate beneficiaries, namely, caregivers and participants living with dementia. The significant improvement in caregiver well-being and confidence across both groups demonstrates COPE’s clinical value regardless of training modality. The finding that online training produced superior outcomes for the first target behavior suggests potential advantages of standardized online content and repeated review capabilities.

Our findings have important implications for scaling evidence-based dementia care programs. The noninferiority of online training suggests that implementation efforts can utilize more efficient, accessible training approaches without compromising quality or outcomes. This is particularly relevant for programs like PACE that operate across multiple locations with geographically dispersed staff, where traditional in-person training presents logistical and financial challenges.

While a comprehensive cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this study, cost considerations are relevant to the practical adoption of either modality and merit a brief discussion. The development of the online training platform required a onetime investment of approximately $64,000 encompassing instructional design, module production, multimedia content, and SCORM-compliant learning management system integration. By comparison, delivery of the in-person training is estimated at approximately $5,000 per site plus staff time, reflecting costs associated with travel, site logistics, and trainer fees. Thus, there is a large upfront investment to develop online training but less long-term investment; online training is likely more cost-effective, and certainly more scalable over time. In-person training is not scalable, requiring the availability of personnel who are COPE experts. Future research incorporating formal cost-effectiveness analyses that account for all implementation costs, including staff time, technology infrastructure, and ongoing support, would provide more definitive economic guidance for healthcare systems considering the adoption of evidence-based dementia care programs.

The results also highlight the importance of examining both clinician and dyad outcomes. Had we focused exclusively on clinician training metrics without measuring PACE participant and caregiver outcomes, we would not have had conclusive evidence of the equivalency of online and traditional training approaches.

Limitations

High rates of COVID-19 as well as COVID-19 risk among PACE participants, caregivers, families, and clinicians resulted in fluctuating site closures, staff turnover, and caregiver burnout, which shortened follow-up periods and may have introduced patient selection bias. Small sample size limited subgroup analyses, though consistency across multiple measures strengthens confidence in noninferiority conclusions. Furthermore, outcome measures were limited to self-reported knowledge, confidence ratings, and NoMad implementation scores; direct observation of clinical skill performance in actual COPE delivery was not conducted. As a result, inferences regarding the equivalence of the two training modalities for developing clinical skill should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Both samples present demographic limitations worth noting. Caregivers were almost entirely female and predominantly White, non-Hispanic. While women represent roughly two-thirds of U.S. dementia caregivers, the near-absence of men limits conclusions about whether training modality effects generalize across gender, and the lack of racial and ethnic diversity constrains generalizability to populations who may differ in technology experience, health literacy, and modality preferences. The clinician sample was similarly homogeneous, potentially underrepresenting the broader PACE and dementia care workforce. Future studies should intentionally recruit more diverse samples and examine whether modality preferences or outcomes vary by race, ethnicity, gender, or technology experience. Geographic concentration within Trinity Health PACE programs in the Northeast and Southeast, combined with the small overall sample size, further limits generalizability to other PACE organizations and community-based settings.

Future Directions

Future research should examine the long-term sustainability of COPE implementation across PACE and community-based settings. Although our study found comparable outcomes at 4 months, it remains unclear whether modality differences emerge over longer periods—particularly as clinicians encounter complex cases or transition to independent delivery without coaching. Longitudinal studies assessing competence, fidelity, and caregiver outcomes at 12 and 24 months would meaningfully extend the evidence base.

Because outcomes here relied on self-report, future studies should incorporate direct observation of clinical skill during COPE delivery. Fidelity checklists administered by trained observers during home visits would provide more objective evidence of training equivalence and could reveal modality-specific advantages not captured by self-report. A mixed-methods design would advance understanding of the mechanisms underlying these findings. Qualitative interviews or focus groups with clinicians, caregivers, and administrators could clarify which training features were most valuable, what barriers clinicians encountered, and how individual factors—technology experience, learning preferences, practice context—shaped engagement and outcomes. This is especially important given that health professionals increasingly receive online-only continuing education despite documented preferences for in-person learning; optimizing online design for clinicians with varying learning styles warrants dedicated investigation.

Detailed process evaluation could also identify which specific online components—case vignettes, knowledge checks, virtual debrief—most strongly predict competence and caregiver outcomes, informing a more efficient design that preserves effectiveness while reducing burden. Finally, comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses accounting for development, delivery, staff time, coaching, and technology infrastructure are needed to guide healthcare systems and PACE organizations evaluating the adoption of evidence-based dementia care training.

CONCLUSION

There is still a great deal of research needed to ensure that evidence-based programs can be implemented in real settings to benefit PLWD and those caring for them. Our findings demonstrate that online training offers a scalable solution for implementing evidence-based dementia care programs without compromising effectiveness. This addresses a critical barrier to widespread program adoption in long-term care settings and strengthens the foundation for more effective, sustainable training approaches in dementia care.16 This work strengthens the foundation for more effective, scalable, and sustainable training of health providers in dementia care.
