Generation of new quadratic coefficients within Ginzburg-Landau theory: Applications for specific heat calculations in various high-temperature superconductors
O.C. Feulefack a , R.M. Keumo Tsiaze a , b † {}^{a,b^{{\dagger}}} , C. Tsague Fotio c , J. E. Danga c , A.J. Fotue c , M.N. Hounkonnou a

Abstract
We revisit Landau’s theory by renormalizing quadratic coefficients from nonlinear polynomial equations to adapt them to the system’s dimensionality. These coefficients are employed to perform detailed specific-heat calculations for several high-temperature superconductors near the superconducting transition. We phenomenologically explain the change in the specific heat jump using Ginzburg-Landau theory, which is applicable to any spatial arrangement and interactions between electrons that influence the system’s symmetries. We discuss the effects that may lead to the rapid and non-monotonic variation of the specific heat jump, Δ ​ C p / T c \Delta{C_{p}}/T_{c} , across the transition over relatively small ranges of fluctuations, focusing particularly on changes attributed to the Sommerfeld coefficient in the normal state. We quantitatively explain the considerable reduction, disappearance, or significant enhancement of the specific heat anomaly at the superconducting transition, considering strong fluctuation corrections to the Ginzburg-Landau theory of low-dimensional systems. Additionally, we study the evolution of specific heat jumps by varying the system’s dimensionality and discuss the results in relation to experimental observations of specific-heat jumps in iron-, yttrium-, and bismuth-based superconductors, as well as in β \beta -pyrochlore oxides superconductors like K ​ O ​ s 2 ​ O 6 KOs_{2}O_{6} .

II.1
Mean-field specific-heat calculations

Landau’s theory is the unified mean-field approach to phase transitions. By examining transitions where the symmetry group of the ordered phase is a subgroup of the symmetry group of the disordered phase (the most symmetric phase) and by neglecting long-wavelength fluctuations associated with the gradient term, one can formulate the Landau free energy in the following form:

The general approach to deriving a Landau functional for phase transitions involves treating interactions using the mean-field approximation, followed by solving the resulting jumps while considering the system’s fluctuations at the lowest level of approximation. This mean-field treatment leads to serious problems. Firstly, it predicts a constant temperature phase transition, thereby neglecting the intrinsic parameters of the system and, therefore, is in disagreement with Mermin-Wagner’s theorem. Secondly, the overlooked fluctuations play a crucial role across a wide range of temperatures

II.2

The renewal of Landau’s theory facilitates an ongoing development of a renormalization process for quadratic and quartic coefficients. This process does not challenge the previous findings in terms of structure; instead, it encourages us to reassess our understanding of mean-field approaches. III
Discussions

Second-order or continuous phase transitions are simpler to study than first-order ones because they do not involve latent heat, which is the key concept used to differentiate first-order transitions from higher-order ones. Continuous phase transitions exhibit a range of fascinating properties and can be characterized by parameters known as critical exponents. In Landau’s theory of critical phenomena, physics near
T_{c_{0}}
is described, in a leading approximation, by a few effective macroscopic parameters without explicit reference to the initial degrees of freedom. Among the simplest predictions of mean-field approaches (MFA), One can observe the universality of the singular behavior of thermodynamic quantities near
T_{c_{0}}
. For example, the specific heat always diverges as the temperature approaches
T_{c_{0}}
following the power-law

C\sim|{T-T_{c_{0}}}|^{-\alpha},\phantom{...}\textrm{where the mean-filed critical exponent}\phantom{...}\alpha=1/2.

(43)

There are additional critical exponents
\gamma,\delta,\eta
related to variations in several thermodynamic parameters near the critical point
41 41
. According to Equation (22), the conditions for a second-order phase transition require that
\alpha
be strictly less than 1, depending on the type of phase transition considered
42 42
. For the value of
\alpha
given in Eq. (22), the specific heat diverges at
T_{c_{0}}
; however, this divergence is not significant enough to produce latent heat. Such behavior of the specific heat is observed, for instance, in three-dimensional ferromagnetic materials. MFA effectively describes the transition from ferromagnetism to paramagnetism. It provides clear explanations for spontaneous magnetization, critical temperature, heat capacity anomalies, and other magnetic properties. Several properties of localized ferromagnets, non-conventional superconductors, and ferroelectrics exhibit power-law behavior in terms of
\big(T_{c_{0}}-T\big)
; however, their critical exponents do not align with the predictions of the mean-field approximation (MFA). The two-dimensional Ising model, for instance, does not follow this power law; instead, the specific heat exhibits a logarithmic divergence at
T_{c_{0}}
rather than a finite discontinuity
43 43
. Although the latter model is theoretical, it highlights several issues that need to be addressed, thereby underscoring the need to explore alternative approaches.

Zero-dimensional (0D) superconductors refer to systems characterized by a single point or variable, where all superconducting effects occur solely due to fluctuation-induced superconductivity. In such models, spatial variations of the OP are not considered, and the focus is on a single temperature for a given region. This simplification is valid because the accuracy of the 0D model depends on how effectively it represents the actual behavior of the system. In this context, specific heat refers to the intrinsic amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of confined geometries by a certain amount.

V
conclusion

Several experiments with high-
T_{c_{0}}
superconductors have shown the simultaneous presence of both electron-phonon and electron-electron interactions. This study examines the role of pairing mechanisms and one-electron excitations in influencing the thermodynamic properties of high-
T_{c_{0}}
superconductors. We present a renormalized phenomenological approach that enhances access to the research landscape, emphasizing the role of dimensionality in describing specific properties of superconductors in zero external fields to improve these properties.

The SHJ is a critical tool for identifying superconductors and determining the true critical temperature at which a material becomes superconducting. This jump occurs due to the sudden loss of normal electron excitations (quasiparticles), resulting in a decrease in the material’s thermal energy and a sharp increase in specific heat. Notably, this sharp increase does not occur in zero- and one-dimensional systems. The shapes of these systems are distinct and exhibit characteristic properties of materials with finite energy levels. The existence of a gap region prevents any coexistence between spin-density wave and superconducting orders, which explains the absence of the specific heat jump in zero- and one-dimensional systems.

The interplay between thermal fluctuations and mass renormalization near the critical point has a significant influence on the power-law dependence of the SHJ. Generally, quantum fluctuations of spin-density waves contributing to mass renormalization are lower than those due to thermal fluctuations. However, this trend can reverse as the system’s dimensionality decreases, indicating that a simple law cannot dictate the behavior of the SHJ.

Examining the Keumo-Landau equation in more detail, we find that fluctuations increase with the quadratic coefficient through the power of
d/2-1
, provided that fluctuation corrections cannot be ignored for
d\leq 2
. The dimension
d_{c}=2
represents a critical dimension below which fluctuations of the order parameter become highly significant, overshadowing thermal fluctuations. By introducing a renormalization constant
\eta
, which describes a free-energy barrier between a metastable state and a state of lower free energy, we outline the possible range of values for this quantity in typical experimental cases.

It is clear that adapting new quadratic coefficients to match the dimensionality of the system while adhering to the principle that increased fluctuation effects occur as dimensionality decreases supports and restores the Mermin-Wagner-Hohenberg theorem, particularly for
d\leq 2
. The evolution of SHJs with varying system dimensionality corresponds to a complete disappearance of the SHJ at
T_{c}
for families of high-temperature superconductors, such as
TlBaCaCuO
