Clinical profile of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and associated comorbidities in children aged 1–60 months: a prospective observational study
Authors: Sandesh Kini, Sainath Ghali, Juanitha George, Praveen C Samuel, Ramesh Bhat, Shrikiran A Hebbar, Suneel Mundkur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) in young children causes troublesome symptoms and complications affecting growth, feeding and quality of life, but symptom-based diagnosis correlates poorly with objective reflux severity. Contemporary clinical-profile data from South Indian children under 5 years and the accompanying nutritional and comorbidity burden are sparse.MethodsProspective observational study at a tertiary-care paediatric centre in South India (June 2023 to March 2025). Children aged 1–60 months with clinically suspected GERD were enrolled consecutively. Clinical profile, radionuclide gastro-oesophageal reflux (GER) scintigraphy grade, Indian Academy of Paediatrics weight-for-age nutritional status and confirmed comorbidities were documented; physiological reflux was distinguished from pathological GERD by predefined operational criteria (troublesome symptoms beyond 8 weeks plus at least one ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN red flag plus a scintigraphy grade of mild or above). Univariable logistic regression identified predictors of severe reflux and of no clinical improvement at follow-up. A prespecified sensitivity analysis was restricted to the 128 children with a positive scintigraphy result.ResultsOf 158 children, 40.5% (95% CI 33.2% to 48.3%) were aged 1–6 months and 62.0% (95% CI 54.3% to 69.2%) were male. Extraoesophageal respiratory symptoms predominated (cough 76.6%, 95% CI 69.4% to 82.5%; wheezing 67.1%), exceeding vomiting (48.7%). GER scintigraphy showed severe reflux in 35.4% (95% CI 28.4% to 43.2%), mild 29.7%, moderate 15.8% and normal 19.0%. Severe reflux clustered in younger infants (median age 4 vs 12 months in mild reflux; Kruskal-Wallis p<0.05). Protein-energy malnutrition was present in 39.9% (95% CI 32.6% to 47.7%) and any comorbidity in 81.0% (95% CI 74.2% to 86.4%), predominantly respiratory (recurrent wheezing 45.3%, bronchiolitis 18.8%, recurrent pneumonia 10.9%, asthma 6.3%). Symptom severity did not correspond to scintigraphic grade (χ2 p=0.40). Among 78 children with follow-up, severe reflux independently predicted no clinical improvement compared with mild reflux (OR 4.00, 95% CI 1.01 to 15.77, Fisher exact p=0.047). Every principal finding was reproduced in the confirmed-GERD sensitivity analysis (n=128).ConclusionsIn this tertiary-referral cohort of South Indian children under 5 years, GERD predominantly affected young infants with a respiratory-dominant symptom profile and a high burden of protein-energy malnutrition and respiratory comorbidity. Objective scintigraphy severity was independently prognostic of treatment non-response despite the absence of a symptom-severity correlation. The findings support structured nutritional and comorbidity screening, targeted objective diagnostic evaluation using the ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN red-flag criteria, and caution against reflexive empirical acid suppression; the tertiary-referral design limits generalisability to primary-care populations.Trial registrationCTRI/2023/07/055893.Keywords: Gastroenterology

Introduction

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is defined as a condition that develops when reflux of gastric contents causes troublesome symptoms and/or complications.1 In children, distinguishing physiological gastro-oesophageal reflux (GER)—a normal developmental phenomenon—from pathological GERD is clinically imperative, particularly in infants, in whom regurgitation is ubiquitous. Physiological GER occurs in approximately 50%–67% of healthy infants and typically resolves by 12–14 months as the lower oesophageal sphincter matures.2 Pathological GERD, however, manifests with oesophagitis, faltering growth, respiratory compromise and neurobehavioural disturbance, warranting clinical intervention.1

The epidemiology of paediatric GERD in India is heterogeneous; reported prevalence ranges from 5% to 28.5%, with a midpoint estimate of approximately 16.75%.3–5 A North Indian cohort identified GERD in 10% of infants under 2 years,6 and a Singaporean cohort of Asian infants reported a prevalence of 26.9% in children under 3 years, peaking in the first 6 months of life.7 Clinically, presentations are frequently dominated by extraoesophageal symptoms—recurrent respiratory infections, chronic cough, wheezing, stridor and hoarseness—through microaspiration, vagally mediated bronchoconstriction and airway inflammation.8–11 Comorbidities such as protein-energy malnutrition, iron-deficiency anaemia, recurrent pneumonia, asthma, otolaryngological disorders and neurological impairment amplify the clinical burden,12–14 and a recent genome-wide analysis demonstrated a genetic correlation of 0.48 between GERD and asthma.11

Critically, symptom severity frequently fails to correlate with objective disease severity on pH-impedance monitoring or radionuclide scintigraphy.15 16 Over-reliance on symptom scoring may lead to inappropriate empirical acid suppression in infants with physiological GER, while underdiagnosis may delay management of silent but severe reflux.17 As the clinical profile of GERD in Indian tertiary-care settings remains incompletely characterised, we aimed to describe the clinical profile of children aged 1–60 months with suspected GERD, identify and quantify comorbidities, assess nutritional status using Indian Academy of Paediatrics (IAP) weight-for-age classification, and determine whether clinical symptom severity correlates with objective GER scan grading and treatment outcome at follow-up.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, hospital-based observational study was conducted in the paediatric outpatient and inpatient departments of a tertiary academic medical centre in South India over 21 months (June 2023–March 2025). Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for observational studies (checklist provided as an online supplemental file 2); participant flow is shown in figure 1.

Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) participant flow diagram showing screening, enrolment, baseline evaluation and follow-up (baseline N=158; follow-up n=78). GER, gastro-oesophageal reflux; GERD, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease; IAP, Indian Academy of Paediatrics.

Participants

Children aged 1–60 months presenting with clinically suspected GERD—characteristic symptoms including regurgitation, vomiting, chronic cough, noisy breathing, feeding difficulty, irritability or faltering growth, in accordance with NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN criteria1—were eligible. Children previously diagnosed with GERD and already receiving anti-reflux pharmacotherapy were excluded to prevent confounding of symptom profiles. Consecutive sampling was used; all eligible children with written informed parental consent were enrolled sequentially.

Sample size

Based on a reported paediatric GER prevalence in Indian children of 16.75% (midpoint of the range 5%–28.5%),1 5 the required sample size was computed as n=Z2p(1−p)/d2 with Z=1.96, p=0.1675 and d=0.05 was 214, reduced to 203 after finite-population correction for a projected 24-month accrual. Enrolment was closed at 158 children because the fixed 21-month recruitment window specified by the postgraduate thesis ethics approval (08 June 2023 to 31 March 2025) elapsed before the 203 target was reached; extension of the recruitment period was not permitted under the original ethics approval and was outside the postgraduate thesis timeline. The achieved cohort provided precision of ±7.6 percentage points around the primary prevalence estimate at 95% confidence, and the primary analyses were adequately powered to detect a difference of approximately 20 percentage points between severity strata; subgroup analyses are therefore interpreted with acknowledgement of reduced power and are labelled as exploratory.

Data collection

A predesigned proforma captured demographic information; symptom history, including the type, duration and severity of gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms; clinical examination; radionuclide GER scintigraphy performed with orally administered technetium-99m sulphur colloid, with acquisition for 60 min and grading as normal, mild, moderate or severe according to the frequency of reflux episodes and the level of tracer ascent above the diaphragm; nutritional assessment by anthropometry with IAP weight-for-age classification; and confirmed comorbidities. Symptom severity was classified as mild, moderate or severe using a composite score incorporating the frequency and intensity of gastrointestinal, respiratory and systemic features, adapted from validated paediatric GERD scoring instruments.1 9 Multichannel intraluminal impedance-pH (MII-pH) monitoring is the current reference standard for the objective diagnosis of GERD and is recommended by the joint ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN paediatric GERD guideline1 and by recent Indian consensus statements.18 MII-pH was not available at the study centre during the recruitment period; radionuclide GER scintigraphy was used as the pragmatic objective diagnostic modality available in routine paediatric practice in this setting. Physiological GER, defined as passage of gastric content into the oesophagus without troublesome symptoms or complications, was distinguished from pathological GERD by the combination of (a) clinically troublesome symptoms lasting more than 8 weeks or of increasing intensity, (b) the presence of one or more predefined red flags (weight-for-age below the third centile, recurrent respiratory events, refusal to feed or haematemesis) and (c) a radionuclide GER scintigraphy grade of mild or above, applied to every enrolled child in accordance with the operational criteria proposed by the joint ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN guideline.1

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. The findings will be shared with participating families through the outpatient clinic during routine follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.0 and Python V.3.14 (scipy 1.13, statsmodels 0.14) for confirmatory recomputation. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR) based on normality assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test; categorical variables as frequencies with percentages and Wilson 95% CIs for headline proportions. Between-group comparisons used the Pearson χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for medians across GER severity groups. Univariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of severe reflux and of no clinical improvement at follow-up, with ORs and 95% CIs reported alongside exact p values. A prespecified sensitivity analysis restricted the cohort to the 128 children with a confirmed positive GER scintigraphy grade (mild, moderate or severe), excluding the 30 children with a normal scintigraphy result, to test whether the principal descriptive findings and associations were robust to case-definition. Two-sided p value less than 0.05 defined statistical significance throughout.

Results

A total of 158 children with clinically suspected GERD were enrolled; participant flow is summarised in figure 2. Most were aged 1–6 months (40.5%), followed by >1–5 years (34.8%) and >6–12 months (24.7%); 62.0% were male (male:female 1.63:1), with male predominance across all age groups (figure 3; online supplemental table 1).

Extraoesophageal respiratory symptoms predominated, with cough (76.5%) and noisy breathing/wheezing (67.1%) far exceeding the classical gastrointestinal symptoms such as vomiting (48.7%); cyanotic episodes occurred in 4.4% (table 1; Figure 4). GER scintigraphy demonstrated severe reflux in 35.4%, mild in 29.7%, moderate in 15.8% and normal findings were observed in 19.0%. A statistically significant inverse association between age and reflux severity was identified (Kruskal-Wallis, p<0.05): children with severe reflux had the lowest median age (4 months; IQR 2–9) compared with moderate (5 months; IQR 4–12) and mild reflux (12 months; IQR 9–22) (table 2).

Protein-energy malnutrition was present in 39.9% by IAP classification, most commonly grade I (20.3%) (table 3A). Clinical symptom severity did not correspond to GER scan grade (χ2, p=0.40): among 70 children with mild symptoms, 40.0% had mild and 21.4% had severe reflux, whereas among 30 with severe symptoms, 36.7% had severe reflux (table 3B). Comorbidities were identified in 81.0% of children, predominantly respiratory disorders (83.6% of those with comorbidities), followed by other nutritional disorders (49.2%), otolaryngological disorders (39.1%), iron-deficiency anaemia (22.7%), central nervous system disorders (18.8%), faltering growth as a distinct comorbidity (13.3%) and cardiovascular disorders (10.2%) (table 4A; Figure 2).

Follow-up data were available for 78 children. Treatment response was inversely related to reflux severity: 15 of 40 (37.5%) with severe reflux showed neither symptomatic improvement nor significant weight gain, versus 3 of 23 (13.0%) with mild and 3 of 15 (20.0%) with moderate reflux (table 4B).

Sensitivity analysis restricted to the 128 children with a confirmed positive GER scintigraphy grade (excluding 30 children with a normal scintigraphy result) reproduced every principal finding of the primary analysis to within one percentage point of the point estimates: infant predominance (median age 5 months; IQR 3–12), male predominance (61.7%), respiratory-dominant symptom profile (cough 77.3%), high prevalence of protein-energy malnutrition (40.6%), high comorbidity burden (86.7%) and the absence of correlation between clinical symptom severity and GER scintigraphy severity (χ2 p=0.51). The direction and magnitude of the age-severity inverse gradient, and of the treatment-response gradient at follow-up, were unchanged in the sensitivity analysis. Univariable logistic regression identified severe reflux as an independent predictor of no clinical improvement at follow-up compared with mild reflux (OR 4.00, 95% CI 1.01 to 15.77, Fisher exact p=0.047); moderate reflux was not significantly associated with no clinical improvement (OR 1.67, 95% CI 0.29 to 9.62, p=0.66). Symptom severity did not significantly predict severe reflux (severe symptoms vs mild symptoms: OR 2.12, 95% CI 0.83 to 5.42, Fisher exact p=0.14), consistent with the absence of correlation between symptom severity and scan grade reported above.

Discussion

GERD is defined as reflux of gastric contents that produces troublesome symptoms or complications, and its distinction from physiological GER is central to management in young children.1 Physiological reflux occurs in more than half of infants during the first 6 months of life and typically resolves by 12–18 months, whereas pathological reflux persists, produces extraoesophageal manifestations and impairs feeding, growth and quality of life.1 2 15 The clinical burden is disproportionately concentrated in infancy, and the presentation extends well beyond the classical picture of vomiting and regurgitation to include chronic cough, wheeze, apnoea, faltering growth and refusal to feed.9 10 Contemporary population-level data from South Indian children remain sparse, and most published paediatric cohorts originate from Western Europe, North America and East Asia. This asymmetry limits the direct transferability of published thresholds and management pathways to the Indian setting.

Against this background, the present study addressed three prespecified objectives in a consecutively enrolled cohort of 158 South Indian children aged 1–60 months with clinically suspected disease. We aimed first, to describe the demographic and symptom profile using objective radionuclide scintigraphy; second, to quantify the burden of protein-energy malnutrition and coexisting comorbidities; and third, to examine whether clinical symptom severity aligned with scintigraphy severity, and whether severe scintigraphy grade predicted absence of clinical improvement at follow-up. Each objective corresponds to a numbered subsection of the Results and is discussed in that order below.

The age distribution of the cohort confirmed the well-recognised concentration of paediatric reflux disease in early infancy: 40.5% of children were aged 1–6 months, 24.7% were between 6 and 12 months and the remaining 34.8% were between 1 and 5 years. This pattern reflects the developmental immaturity of the lower oesophageal sphincter, the shorter intra-abdominal segment of the oesophagus and the greater volume of liquid feeds relative to gastric capacity that together predispose infants to reflux.2 15 A comparable infant-dominant distribution has been reported by Nelson and colleagues in their North American primary-care survey and by the ESPGHAN-NASPGHAN 2018 clinical practice guideline synthesis, both of which describe the peak clinical presentation of physiological reflux between 2 and 6 months of age with tapering into the second year.1 Our finding therefore aligns with the international evidence while adding prospective South Indian data to a literature that has largely been drawn from other populations.

The symptom profile was strikingly respiratory-dominant. Cough was reported in 76.5% of children, noisy breathing or wheeze in 67.1% and any respiratory symptom in 83.6%, whereas vomiting was recorded in 48.7%. Extraoesophageal manifestations therefore exceeded classical gastrointestinal symptoms across the cohort. In the Asian paediatric literature, Eiamkulbutr and colleagues reported extraoesophageal reflux as the presenting phenotype in a substantial proportion of children evaluated with multichannel intraluminal impedance-pH monitoring,9 and Saniasiaya has more recently synthesised the paediatric airway-reflux literature emphasising the respiratory-dominant presentation as the norm rather than the exception outside Western infant cohorts.10 Gong and colleagues have demonstrated a shared genetic architecture between GERD and asthma in a large genome-wide association analysis, providing a mechanistic frame for the tight clinical association we observed between respiratory symptoms and reflux in this cohort.11

Protein-energy malnutrition was present in 39.9% of the cohort by IAP weight-for-age criteria, with grade I under-nutrition in 20.3%, grade II in 12.0% and grade III or IV in 7.6%. This nutritional burden is substantially higher than that reported in Western infant cohorts, in which malnutrition attributable to reflux disease is comparatively uncommon, but it is consistent with the background prevalence of undernutrition among South Asian under-fives and with the bidirectional relationship between paediatric reflux disease and impaired growth described in the wider literature.1 17 The convergence of respiratory illness, feeding difficulty and undernutrition in this cohort supports structured nutritional assessment as an integral part of paediatric reflux evaluation in the Indian setting, rather than as an optional add-on to symptom-directed treatment.

Coexisting comorbidities were confirmed in 128 of 158 children (81.0%). The most common were respiratory disorders (83.6% of the comorbid subgroup), followed by neurological conditions and prematurity-related morbidity. This proportion is higher than the comorbidity burden reported by Chevalier and colleagues in an otherwise healthy Canadian infant cohort17 and reflects the tertiary-referral character of our centre, to which children with complex phenotypes are preferentially referred. Kirianchuk has described a similar concentration of comorbid respiratory and gastro-duodenal disease in paediatric reflux cohorts drawn from tertiary care.12 These converging observations suggest that paediatric reflux disease encountered in the Indian tertiary setting is rarely an isolated diagnosis, and that comorbidity screening should be part of the routine work-up rather than an incidental finding on later review.

The absence of correlation between clinical symptom severity and scintigraphy severity (χ2 p=0.40) is the most clinically consequential finding of the study. Similar dissociations have been reported in paediatric impedance-pH cohorts by Rosen15 and by Pavić and colleagues in prospective impedance-pH monitoring of infants presenting with brief resolved unexplained events.16 The mechanistic explanation is well established: the majority of paediatric reflux episodes are weakly acidic or non-acidic, symptom generation reflects refluxate volume and oesophageal hypersensitivity rather than acid load alone, and paediatric reflux phenotypes therefore separate poorly on the symptom-severity axis without objective testing.15 This is the epidemiological basis for the ESPGHAN and NASPGHAN 2018 recommendation against reflexive empirical acid suppression on symptoms alone in most paediatric populations,1 a recommendation that is nonetheless calibrated rather than absolute: acid-suppressive therapy is in widespread routine use for clinically diagnosed reflux disease in the outpatient setting, and not every child undergoes scintigraphy or impedance testing at the point of initial diagnosis. In our reading of the guidance and our data, objective testing (scintigraphy or impedance-pH monitoring) is most valuable in characterising difficult-to-treat cases, in defining non-erosive reflux disease and reflux hyper-sensitivity, and in informing decisions about escalation or surgical management for severe disease. Symptom-directed acid-suppressive therapy therefore retains a legitimate place in first-line outpatient management, while objective testing should gate escalation, prolonged therapy and consideration of surgical intervention.

Among the 78 children with complete follow-up, severe scintigraphy grade independently predicted absence of clinical improvement, with an OR of 4.00 (95% CI 1.01 to 15.77, p=0.047). 15 of 40 children (37.5%) with severe reflux showed neither symptomatic improvement nor significant weight gain at follow-up, compared with a substantially smaller proportion of children with mild or moderate reflux. Prognostic imaging data specific to paediatric reflux disease are sparse, and the wide CI reflects the modest positive-outcome count in the follow-up subgroup; the direction of the signal nevertheless indicates that objective reflux severity carries prognostic information that symptom scoring alone does not. Verma and colleagues have described a similar concentration of refractory reflux disease among children with objective evidence of severe reflux at initial evaluation,8 and adult reflux-imaging cohorts have consistently demonstrated that objective reflux burden predicts refractoriness to standard pharmacological therapy. Applied cautiously to the bedside, our data support the use of scintigraphy findings in risk-stratifying children for closer follow-up and for earlier escalation of therapy, while emphasising that this prognostic signal must be re-tested in larger multicentre paediatric cohorts before it can be used to gate management decisions definitively.

This study has several limitations that warrant explicit acknowledgement. It is a single-centre tertiary-care investigation, and the cohort is enriched for symptomatic and complex phenotypes through referral patterns; the findings may not translate unchanged to primary-care populations in the same region. Radionuclide scintigraphy, although objective and widely available in Indian practice, is less sensitive than 24-hour multichannel intraluminal impedance-pH monitoring, which remains the reference standard for paediatric reflux characterisation. Follow-up was available for 78 of 158 children and the median duration was modest, which limits inference on longer-term outcomes. Symptom severity was captured through a structured proforma rather than a fully validated paediatric reflux questionnaire, and parental reporting may have introduced measurement error, particularly in infants. Finally, the prognostic signal from severe scintigraphy grade is precise in direction but wide in confidence and should be treated as hypothesis-generating pending multicentre replication.

Several extensions to this work would strengthen its clinical relevance. Multicentre replication across South Asian paediatric gastroenterology units would test the reproducibility of the respiratory-dominant and comorbid-child phenotype we observed. The prospective addition of multichannel intraluminal impedance-pH monitoring to a subgroup with severe scintigraphy grade would allow the prognostic signal to be re-tested against the paediatric reference standard and would enable phenotyping of non-erosive reflux disease and reflux hypersensitivity, entities in which objective testing is particularly informative. Introduction of a validated paediatric symptom instrument such as the Infant Gastro-oesophageal Reflux Questionnaire (Revised) would standardise symptom assessment across sites. Extending follow-up to 12 and 24 months would capture longer-term nutritional and respiratory trajectories. A nested biomarker sub-study of inflammatory and airway markers would clarify whether reflux-attributable airway inflammation is a quantifiable driver of the respiratory phenotype. Finally, a pragmatic randomised evaluation comparing empirical symptom-directed treatment with scintigraphy-guided structured evaluation would test whether the addition of objective testing translates into improved clinical outcomes in the Indian setting.

Taken together, these findings characterise paediatric GERD in a South Indian tertiary-care cohort as a predominantly infant, respiratory-dominant, nutritionally vulnerable and comorbid-child condition, in which symptom severity does not track with objective imaging severity and in which severe scintigraphy grade independently predicts absence of clinical improvement at follow-up. The clinical implication is twofold: first-line symptom-directed treatment retains its place in routine outpatient practice for clinically diagnosed reflux disease, while objective imaging is best positioned to inform risk stratification, therapy escalation and consideration of surgical referral for children with severe or refractory disease. Multicentre replication with multichannel intraluminal impedance-pH monitoring and a validated paediatric symptom instrument is the immediate next step, and the pragmatic randomised evaluation outlined above would move the field from prognostic description to guideline-level management evidence.

Conclusion

Paediatric GERD in this South Indian tertiary-care setting predominantly affected infants in the first 6 months of life and presented chiefly with extraoesophageal respiratory manifestations rather than classical gastrointestinal symptoms. Severe reflux clustered in the youngest infants, comorbidities were present in most children and protein-energy malnutrition affected nearly 40%. Because clinical symptom severity did not correlate with objective GER scan severity, symptom-based diagnosis alone is insufficient, reinforcing the need for objective diagnostic evaluation, systematic comorbidity and nutritional screening, and individualised multidisciplinary management.
