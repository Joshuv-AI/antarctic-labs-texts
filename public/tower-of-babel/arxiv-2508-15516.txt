The Digital Life of Parisian Parks: Multifunctionality and Urban Context Uncovered by Mobile Application Traffic
Andre Felipe Zanella, Linus W. Dietz, Sanja Šćepanović, Ke Zhou, Zbigniew Smoreda, Daniele Quercia

Abstract
Landscape architecture typically considers urban parks through the lens of form and function. While past research on equitable access has focused mainly on park form, studies addressing functional uses have been constrained by limited scale and coarse measurement techniques. Existing efforts have partially quantified park functions through small-scale surveys and movement data e.g., GPS, check-ins) or general usage data (e.g., CDR), but have not effectively captured the specific activities and motivations underlying park visits. As a result, our understanding of the functional roles urban parks play remains incomplete. To address this gap, we introduce a novel method that refines mobile base station coverage using antenna azimuths, enabling more precise distinction of mobile traffic within parks versus surrounding areas. Using Paris as a case study, we analyze a large-scale dataset of passively collected per-app mobile network traffic—492 million hourly records, representing a 35% market share—spanning 45 urban parks. We test two hypotheses: the central-city hypothesis, which posits that multifunctional parks emerge in dense, high-rent areas due to land scarcity; and the socio-spatial hypothesis, which views parks as reflections of neighborhood routines and preferences. Our analysis shows that parks have distinctive mobile traffic signatures, differing from both their urban surroundings and from each other. By clustering parks based on temporal and application usage patterns, we identify three distinct functional types—lunchbreak, cultural, and recreational parks—with different motivations for visitation. Centrally located parks (cultural and lunchbreak) display more diverse app usage and pronounced temporal variation, while suburban (recreational) parks reflect the digital behaviors of nearby communities, with app preferences aligned to neighborhood income. These findings demonstrate the value of mobile traffic as a proxy for studying the diversity of usage and activities within urban green spaces, with implications for park planning, public health, and well-being.

2
Results

We used large-scale traffic data from a major mobile network operator in Paris, collected between January 31 and May 31, 2023 (see
Section 4.1
for data and ethical details).
First, we identified parks where mobile traffic could be reliably linked to park visitors.
From a varied sample of 45 parks, we examined usage patterns based on mobile app activity and changes over time.
Our analysis showed clear differences in mobile app use between parks and other parts of the city, along with distinct time-based patterns.
These usage profiles allowed us to group parks into three types based on app use, which we linked to the socioeconomic indicators of nearby neighborhoods, validating the co-existence of both the Central-city and Socio-spatial hypothesis in Paris.

A central contribution of our research is moving beyond coarse-grained spatial analysis of mobile traffic, which traditionally relies on Voronoi geometries to approximate base station coverage. We refine earlier approaches that incorporated azimuth angles, i.e., dividing base station coverage into antenna sectors to optimize coverage
[
37
,
38
]
, to isolate mobile traffic at specific small-scale urban spaces, such as parks.
Our method is illustrated in
Figure 1
.
In Jardin du Luxembourg (
Figure 1
A), the traditional Voronoi geometry (dark orange outline) covers the park but also a wide surrounding area.
By considering the base station’s three antenna sectors (S1–S3), we can define a more precise coverage area (light orange outlines).
Sector S1 primarily covers the park (orange area) and is the most suitable for our analysis, while S2 and S3 point in other directions and should be excluded, as they do not cover the park.

Concretely, we followed three steps:
1) selecting antennas that sufficiently cover parks (
Figure 1
A/B),
2) identifying parks served by those antennas (
Figure 1
B), and
3) retaining parks where the assigned traffic exceeds the thresholds defined for reliable attribution (
Figure 1
C).
This process resulted in a final set of 45 parks for analysis (listed in
Table 4
in the Appendix).
We observed a direct correlation between the Quality of Coverage metric and park area (
Figure 1
C), indicating that our sample mainly includes medium- to large-sized parks.
This outcome is expected, as larger parks are more likely to be served by dedicated antennas, even though modern cellular networks are dense in urban areas, the individual coverage areas are too small to cover small neighborhood parks.

Temporal Patterns in Park Traffic Reflect Work–Leisure Utilization

To better understand temporal patterns in park usage, we divided traffic volumes into weekdays and weekends
[
39
]
, calculating the median daily volume for each. We found that approximately 75% of parks experience higher traffic on weekdays than on weekends (
Figure 1
E). However, the median ratio of weekday to weekend traffic in parks is
1.17
, compared to a slightly higher ratio of
1.19
in the rest of the city. This suggests that although mobile traffic generally declines on weekends, the drop is less pronounced in parks.
The weekday-to-weekend traffic ratio varies considerably across parks, ranging from
0.72
to
1.86
. To understand what types of parks are used more during different periods, we examined both ends of this spectrum.

Parks with high weekend traffic.
The three parks with the highest weekend traffic—Parc départemental de la Fosse Maussoin, Parc Georges Brassens, and Parc de la Vallée-aux-Loups—are primarily oriented toward nature-based activities. Parc départemental de la Fosse Maussoin and Parc de la Vallée-aux-Loups, both in the Parisian suburbs, feature dense green spaces. Parc Georges Brassens, located more centrally, includes a pond, meadows, tree groves, and several themed gardens, including a vineyard. These parks, which attract more visitors on weekends, are mainly situated in residential areas.

Parks with high weekday traffic.
Conversely, the three parks with the highest weekday traffic—Jardins Abbé Pierre, Parc Monceau, and Parc Suzanne Lenglen—are located in or near areas with high concentrations of offices, schools, or government buildings. Jardins Abbé Pierre and Parc Monceau are centrally located, near universities and business districts. Parc Suzanne Lenglen, on the southern edge of Paris near Issy-les-Moulineaux, includes a large multi-sport facility used for classes and is close to both the Ministry of Defense and a major digital business hub—factors that likely contribute to its high weekday traffic.

Three Park Types Emerge from Traffic Patterns

We used each park’s per-application RSCA values and its weekday-to-weekend traffic ratio as features in spectral clustering, aiming to classify parks according to their smartphone usage patterns. Based on the Silhouette score and additional qualitative analysis, we selected

The Functional Park Types in Paris: Cultural, Lunchbreak and Recreational

Combining these insights, we identified three primary functional profiles: cultural, lunchbreak, and recreational. To determine the names for the clusters and to understand how people use these parks, we analyzed Flickr image tags associated with each park.
Tags with higher relative importance highlighted thematic differences among clusters—such as natural and urban features for Lunchbreak parks, arts and festivals for cultural parks, and lower-density, french-oriented tags for recreational parks. This approach allowed us to assign meaningful names reflecting the functional identities of park clusters based on the distinct tag patterns of images shared within them (methodology details on
Section 5.3
).

Recreational parks
(in blue) are in the suburban areas of Paris, known as the
Banlieue
. These areas include separate cities in the departments of Hauts-de-Seine, Seine-Saint-Denis, and Val-de-Marne. According to the 2017 census, 68% of the people living in metropolitan Paris reside in these less densely populated, mostly residential zones. Examples of parks in this group include Parc départemental de la Fosse-Maussoin and Domaine départemental de la Vallée-aux-Loups. Parks in this group had the lowest traffic use compared with other parks and the rest of the city: 57% less median traffic than the city’s median. In contrast, the median traffic at parks in the other two clusters was close to the city’s overall level: lunchbreak parks had only 8% less traffic, while cultural parks had 3% more.

Lunchbreak Parks
are in densely built areas near business districts and universities. Their traffic patterns are distinct: traffic is much higher on workdays, with median traffic 58% greater than on weekends as can be seen in
Figure 2
B. In
Figure 1
E, these parks appear on the right side of the plot.
Lunchbreak Parks are smaller, with a median area just 36% of that of other parks. Their weekday traffic patterns, closeness to business areas, and the types of apps used on workdays (such as productivity, social, and music apps, see
Figure 4
in the Appendix) suggest that office workers use these parks during work breaks.
Examples include the Jardin du Luxembourg and the Jardin des Plantes. While these parks may have cultural attractions, their location near dense work and study areas, along with their usage patterns, suggests they are predominantly used by workers or students taking breaks during the week.

Cultural Parks
(in red) are known for their attractions, such as the Champ de Mars with the Eiffel Tower, Jardin des Tuileries, and Parc de la Villette, which includes a museum and concert venues.
These parks show the most even traffic distribution between workdays and weekends, with workdays having only 7% more median traffic. Their steady popularity likely stems from both their purpose and their location in a city with high tourist activity. This also explains why they had the highest median traffic among the three clusters, with 3% more than the city’s median.

To further validate the clustering outcome, we conducted a qualitative analysis of several key parks.
The park with the highest weekday-to-weekend traffic ratio was Parc Monceau, located in a mixed-use area with many offices and embassies. It saw a sharp drop in traffic on weekends, despite being surrounded by residential buildings. This suggests that local residents may not see it as a major weekend destination, or that the area’s population declines significantly outside the workweek.
The parks with the lowest traffic were those with a high density of natural features and classified mainly as recreational, such as the Arboretum de Paris and the Parc Nature du Plateau d’Avron. We suggest that the nature-focused design of these parks may influence visitor behavior, leading to reduced smartphone use.
Parks with above-average traffic were mostly classified as cultural or lunchbreak. The park with the highest overall traffic was the Jardin des Plantes, located in central Paris near Sorbonne University.
Parc Pierre-Lagravère and Parc Jean-Moulin–Les Guilands were also classified as cultural, despite being in suburban Paris. Both underwent recent renovations that introduced modern buildings and wide open spaces—features that differ from other suburban parks, which resulted being classified as cultural.

When visitors use their phones, they tend to favor apps for reading and planning.

2.4
Two Lenses on Park Multifunctionality: Economic Pressure versus Cultural Mediation

The three functional park types exhibit distinct patterns of traffic consumption, as explored in
Section 2.3
. To further investigate the effects of these patterns and to evaluate two competing hypotheses regarding park usage, we examined whether socioeconomic factors are associated with the observed differences. We computed p-values to determine the statistical significance of the Pearson correlation coefficients between traffic consumption, application preferences, and the associated socioeconomic indicators.

Socioeconomic indicators were collected at the IRIS level—the smallest geographical unit defined in the most recent French Census
[
40
]
—considering the IRIS in which each park has the majority of its area located. Because our data is aggregated at the antenna level, we lack information about the sociodemographics of individual users; thus, we cannot associate users’ socioeconomic conditions with their own neighborhoods. Instead, we analyze the socioeconomic context of the areas in which the parks are located. Previous studies have documented extreme segregation in large cities
[
41
]
, with limited socioeconomic mixing and a tendency for individuals to seek out others similar to themselves. This tendency strengthens the correlation between the socioeconomic status of an area and that of its residents and visitors, allowing neighborhood indicators to provide meaningful insight into the socioeconomic characteristics associated with each park.

The Central-City Hypothesis

The central-city hypothesis posits that centrally located parks exhibit greater diversity in human activity due to the combined effects of agglomeration and high land values. According to this view, space in dense, expensive areas must serve multiple functions to justify its use.

Park location and weekday usage.

Figure 3
(top) presents the relationship between the weekday-to-weekend traffic ratio and three key socioeconomic indicators: distance from the city center, median household income, and economic inequality (Gini Index).
We observed a modest negative Pearson correlation between distance to the city center and the weekend-to-weekday app traffic ratio (
\rho=-0.317,p=0.034
). Parks closer to the center exhibit higher weekday activity, while those farther away tend to attract more visitors on weekends. This pattern aligns with the expectation that centrally located spaces, such as lunchbreak parks, cater to workday populations. While the workday to weekend ratio of cultural parks is lower, their overall traffic demands during workdays is still on par with lunchbreak parks (evidenced by
Figure 2
B).

Affluence and multifunctionality.

Median income in the surrounding area showed a positive correlation with the weekday-to-weekend traffic ratio (
\rho=0.396,p=0.007
). Cultural and lunchbreak parks are predominantly situated in higher-income neighborhoods, whereas recreational parks are located in areas with low to average incomes. The Gini index pattern mirrors this result (
\rho=0.306,p=0.041
): lunchbreak and cultural parks are more common in areas with greater inequality (Gini
>0.5
), while recreational parks are found in areas with lower inequality (Gini
<0.4
).

These correlations support the central-city hypothesis: centrally located, premium-area parks exhibit concentrated weekday use and multifunctionality. Lunchbreak parks in particular show reduced weekend demand, likely due to their role in serving surrounding office and academic populations.
Lunchbreak and Cultural parks also exhibit similar application preference patterns (as previously discussed in
Figure 2
C)), showing that while they may have different temporal usage patterns, the way visitors interact with smartphones when inside these spaces remain similar.
These urban parks function as crucial support spaces—providing affordable opportunities to decompress in areas where leisure options are costly—illustrating the economic pressures that drive multifunctional use in high-rent districts, either as escape spaces for the work/study life (Lunchbreak parks) or from tourism, sightseeing and cultural activities in Paris (Cultural parks).

The Socio-Spatial Hypothesis

The socio-spatial hypothesis holds that park use patterns reflect the habits, resources, and cultural practices of surrounding communities. In this view, suburban park multifunctionality emerges from social mediation and lifestyle norms rather than from urban form pressures.

Application preferences and income levels.

We examined the relationship between application category preferences (measured via
RSCA
values) and socioeconomic indicators (
Figure 3
, bottom). While parks draw visitors from beyond their immediate neighborhoods, travel distance limits visitation
[
42
,
31
]
, making local socioeconomic context an important influence on park use.
No significant correlation was found between social app usage and any socioeconomic indicator for any park type. However, several other relationships emerged:

Gaming apps:
In recreational parks, gaming usage declines with increased median income, suggesting that visitors to lower-income areas engage more in passive entertainment activities. Cultural and lunchbreak parks show lower gaming usage overall, independent of income.

Music apps:
Music app usage increases with income in both lunchbreak and recreational parks; cultural parks display a similar but non-significant trend.

News apps:
Only cultural parks show a significant positive correlation between income and news app usage, indicating greater consumption of news content in higher-income contexts.

These findings support the socio-spatial hypothesis: suburban recreational parks mirror the digital habits of nearby communities. In lower-income neighborhoods, parks serve as low-cost leisure venues, with higher engagement in gaming. In wealthier suburban contexts, recreational parks see greater use for music and potentially productivity-related activities. For example, the News and Information category in recreational parks shows a positive (though non-significant) correlation with income (
\rho=0.317,p=0.215
). This aligns with the socio-spatial view that park activities are embedded in local digital cultures and lifestyles.

3
Discussion

By introducing a new approach to precisely pinpoint smartphone usage within small urban areas, we were able to accurately isolate mobile traffic within a defined set of parks.
This enabled the first large-scale analysis of how mobile traffic and applications are consumed differently in parks compared to the rest of the city. Through cluster analysis, we identified three main functional park types across a set of 45 parks in Metropolitan Paris, each showing distinct mobile app usage patterns that reflect different user behaviors and activities.
While our three park types partially align with existing categorizations
[
43
,
44
]
, our work establishes a new, data-driven classification grounded in behavioral usage data, exploring how competing hypothesis for spatial utilization of parks co-exist in the city of Paris.

3.1
Main Findings

Smartphone Usage is Diverse in Parks and Different From the Rest of the City

Our findings show that mobile application usage via smartphones differs significantly in parks compared to other parts of the city. This supports previous land-use studies that identified green spaces as a distinct category in mobile phone activity
[
18
,
39
]
, and further reveals that fitness, music, news, shopping, and travel apps are more frequently used in parks, while social media and gaming apps are less preferred.

Smartphone usage patterns also vary markedly across time and between parks, reflecting differences in weekday versus weekend preferences, as well as park location and function. Parks with higher weekday smartphone use are typically centrally located and situated near workplaces or educational institutions. In contrast, parks with greater usage on weekends tend to be found in residential suburbs and feature more natural landscapes. These findings are consistent with prior research on temporal patterns in urban green space usage
[
45
,
46
]
.

Mobile Traffic Reveals two Spatial Theories for Urban Parks

Mobile traffic consumption and application preferences enable the identification of three primary functional uses of parks: cultural, lunchbreak (work-related), and recreational. These categories are closely tied to the geographic and social contexts in which parks are located, underscoring a strong relationship between a park’s setting and the way mobile devices are used within it.
This variability of smartphone usage within parks allows us to test two hypotheses—sometimes conflicting, sometimes complementary—that reveal the complex roles urban parks play, showing the mediation between economic pressure and cultural mediation in how urban parks are shaped.

The
Central-City hypothesis
describes a top-down approach: parks in dense, centrally located neighborhoods become multifunctional out of necessity. In major cities like Paris, where land is scarce and expensive, urban green spaces serve overlapping groups—office workers, students, cafe-goers, and transit users—and must justify their place by accommodating a range of users.
Our findings support this hypothesis: social media apps are more commonly used in both
cultural and lunchbreak parks
, which are centrally located, historically significant, and attract the highest smartphone activity. These parks show marked temporal shifts in app usage—social media and productivity apps are favored on weekdays but decline on weekends. In contrast, recreational parks show steadier patterns. On weekends, app preferences in both central park clusters move closer to those seen in recreational parks (
Figure 4
), reflecting increased use of fitness and recreational apps. This flexibility highlights the adaptive, multipurpose role of central parks, in areas with presence of work, tourism and residence spots.

The
Social-spatial hypothesis
describes a bottom-up process: suburban parks develop diverse uses through ongoing social practices, cultural norms, and shared meanings
[
3
,
4
]
. In this framework, planners respond to how communities use these spaces rather than imposing functions in advance.
Suburban parks are typically larger and more nature-oriented, aligning with our observations of
recreational parks
, which see lower overall traffic but higher weekend use. These parks show increased fitness app usage and less social media activity—patterns consistent with research indicating that park visitors engage in more physical activity on weekends
[
47
]
.

Building on these hypotheses, we find that suburban parks exhibit
temporally distributed multifunctionality
that is strongly conditioned by neighborhood socioeconomic status and prevailing digital cultures. Parks located in lower-income neighborhoods are more likely to be used for passive digital recreation, such as gaming, while those in higher-income areas see “high-status” digital activities, such as fitness tracking and productivity app use. This observation is in line with lifestyle segmentation theory
[
48
]
, which posits that values such as health, flexibility, and visibility shape public space usage. In this perspective, park use reflects not just urban morphology but also the lived experiences of the surrounding community.

This pattern aligns with the
Social-spatial hypothesis
, as suburban parks—generally farther from the city center and in areas marked by lower median income and more homogeneous populations—exhibit more uniform, “single-use” mobile application behaviors. Their recreational emphasis and weekend peaks reflect a bottom-up, collective appropriation driven by the area’s social and demographic fabric.
Conversely, cultural and lunchbreak parks are primarily located near the urban core, in neighborhoods characterized by higher income levels and greater income inequality. The socioeconomic diversity of these central areas likely fuels the richer and more multifaceted patterns of smartphone usage observed. This supports the
Central-City hypothesis
: parks in dense, expensive central neighborhoods must serve diverse populations and function in multiple ways to respond to spatial and economic pressures.

Our findings echo previous empirical studies. Research in Berlin, for example, documented preferences for nearby parks on workdays regardless of park size, coupled with a willingness to travel further on weekends to large parks offering group amenities like picnicking
[
45
]
. This is echoed in our observation that centrally located lunchbreak parks experience heightened weekday usage, while larger suburban recreational parks see more weekend visitors. Similarly, in Coimbra, Portugal, accessibility motivated residents’ visits to urban green spaces: nearby parks attracted users for activities like biking and running, while centrally located parks drew younger users for group sports
[
49
]
. These sociodemographic and behavioral contrasts are reflected in our mobile data, as central parks demonstrate higher smartphone usage and greater app diversity compared to the primarily weekend-recreational activity observed in suburban parks.

Smartphone Utilization Guiding Better Urban Park Planning

The insights from this research can substantially inform urban planning practices and advance our understanding of citizen behavior in parks and other public spaces
[
50
]
. By leveraging mobile network data, planners and policymakers gain scalable, fine-grained tools to monitor real-time park usage and identify patterns that traditional surveys or static datasets may miss
[
51
,
19
]
. This capability enables more responsive and adaptive park management, supporting the equitable allocation of resources and guiding targeted interventions in both infrastructure and programming
[
52
,
8
]
.

Assessing under-use or single-functionality through smartphone data can reveal cases where parks may require investment in new amenities or programs tailored to diverse socioeconomic and cultural groups
[
53
,
54
]
. This evidence-based approach helps to promote greater equity and inclusion, key dimensions for sustainable urban development and environmental justice
[
55
]
.

Finally, the methodological framework established here can be extended beyond parks to other urban green spaces and domains, facilitating data-driven planning for biodiversity, climate resilience, and well-being across entire cities
[
53
,
56
]
.

3.2
Limitations and Future Directions

Our study is not without limitations. First, the quality metrics excluded small and pocket parks due to constraints of the data source: the spatial resolution of mobile traffic data does not allow us to fully isolate activity within small-scale parks from that in surrounding areas. As mobile networks continue to densify, in the future, it may become feasible to include more and more smaller parks using the same methods.

Second, while our approach relies on cellular network data for geolocation, incorporating a more precise source of spatial information (e.g., GPS data) could further validate and refine our localization methodology. Such data could serve as ground truth for benchmarking. However, accessing high-resolution mobility data typically requires linking information across institutions and potentially identifying individuals, which poses significant privacy concerns, limits scalability as it conflicts with current data protection regulations.

It is also important to note the limitations associated with the collection of app-level traffic data. Although measurements are passively conducted via the network operator, they still require each application to have a distinct
traffic footprint
. For example, our Gaming category includes titles such as Pokémon Go and Fortnite, which have a significant online component and may be biased toward certain demographics. In contrast, games that generate little to no network traffic may not be well represented by this method (e.g., Candy Crush).

Lastly, our analysis is limited to parks within the metropolitan area of Paris, potentially biasing the findings toward the spatial, cultural, and socioeconomic characteristics of this region. Future research could extend this framework to other French cities to assess national generalizability and, more broadly, to diverse global urban contexts to explore how relationships between smartphone usage and park activity may vary across sociocultural and infrastructural settings.

Methods

4.1
Mobile Traffic Data Collection

This study builds on real-world measurements from the network of a major mobile operator in France, which holds a 35% market share and serves over 20 million customers in the country.
The data collection period spans from January 31 to May 31, 2023, capturing traffic from 4G and 5G non-standalone radio access technologies. This traffic was collected through passive measurement probes at approximately 25,000 antennas in the Paris metropolitan area.
To confirm that the traffic is stable over the study period, we performed an Augmented Dickey Fuller test on each park, and found that it was stationary, as in, there was no significant change in volumes over the studied period.

User traffic is aggregated at the antenna level into one-hour intervals to protect user privacy and ensure compliance with applicable data protection laws
[
57
]
. Antennas serving fewer than 10 unique users on a given day are excluded from the analysis, and that resulted in analyzing more than 492 million records. The data format and volumes is detailed on
Table 1
.

4.2
Selecting Mobile Applications

The mobile applications are identified through a proprietary Deep Packet Inspection tool owned by the operator. It analyzes packet headers to determine the application they belong, but does not access packet payload content.
Applications exhibit an imbalanced traffic distribution that follows the Pareto principle with approximately 80% of traffic being generated by the top 20% of applications, an expected traffic pattern
[
58
]
.
To derive meaningful insights into traffic consumption in parks, we started with the 100 most popular apps and excluded non-informative categories such as App Store traffic, non identified encrypted traffic, and generic network protocols, which are not directly linked to specific user activities. The final selection of 41 applications accounts for 65.6% of the total traffic the mobile network sees (79.4% when excluding encrypted traffic and network protocols), capturing the majority of application usage in the studied area.
An expert panel was used to categorize individual apps into high-level categories. Their task was to group applications together by the activity the user is engaged in when using these apps. Any conflicts were resolved in a subsequent discussion phase, which also included naming the resulting categories: fitness, games, music, news & information, productivity, shopping, social, travel, and video. The applications included on each category can be seen on
Table 2
.

4.3
Quantifying the Quality of Coverage in Parks

Our methodology for selecting parks based on the quality of coverage of the antennas’ coverage is next detailed. This was done in three steps, each of them comparing the geometric overlap of Voronoi and park polygons. In the following, we use ‘illumination’ to refer to the area of the park that is serviced by a specific antenna. These steps were partially visualized in
Figure 1
A-C:

Step 1: Antenna selection.

Considering
A_{v}
as the expected Voronoi coverage area of antenna
v
, and
A_{p}
as the area of park
p
, we defined the intersecting illumination area as

A_{i,v}=A_{v}\cap A_{p}
.
Consequently, the illuminating ratio of an antenna was defined as

I_{pv}=\frac{A_{i,v}}{A_{v}}.

In the first step, we selected all antennas which have an
I_{pv}\geq\alpha
with any park
p
, where
\alpha=0.1
, excluding those below this threshold.
This step was mostly required to select antennas that overlap with parks, thus, a small
\alpha
was sufficient for our purposes.
For each park
p
, the selected set of Voronoi cells is denoted as
V_{sel,p}
. Aggregating this set from the perspective of a park
p
, we obtained the coverage precision as:

CP_{p}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V_{sel,p}\end{subarray}}\frac{A_{i,v}}{A_{v}}

(1)

As a result,
1141
parks were identified as not being serviced by any antenna with sufficient illumination (
V_{sel,p}=\emptyset
). These parks, shown in gray in
Figure 1
C, were predominantly small green spaces such as roadside strips or pocket parks, e.g., the park in
Figure 1
B2. These are significantly smaller than the Voronoi cells, making traffic assignment uncertain.

Step 2: Park area coverage.

For the parks where
V_{sel,p}\neq\emptyset
, we verified the percentage of
A_{p}
covered by the selected antennas.
We defined this as the park illumination, calculated as:

I_{p}=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V_{sel}\end{subarray}}A_{i,v}}{A_{p}}

(2)

We retained all parks where
I_{p}>\beta
, with
\beta=0.8
. This threshold ensures that the majority of each park’s area is covered, preventing the inclusion of parks with only a small portion adequately covered by antennas in the analysis.
A total of
76
additional parks were excluded at this stage, marked in red in
Figure 1
Cc.
In the example park in
Figure 1
B1, one can see almost all of the park area is illuminated by selected Voronoi cells (in green).

Step 3: Coverage quality.

Finally, we applied a coverage quality metric
Q_{p}
for each park
p
, which balances the ratio of selected Voronoi cells inside the park with how much area is covered by antennas deemed with good coverage.

Q_{p}
is inspired by the F-score and is defined as:

Q_{p}=\frac{2\cdot CP_{p}\cdot I_{p}}{CP_{p}+I_{p}}.

(3)

Applying a threshold of
Q_{p}\geq\gamma
, where
\gamma=0.4
, an additional
78
parks were excluded, marked yellow in
Figure 1
C.
To determine
\gamma
, we observed the trade-off between the statistics of the different coverage metrics and the number of parks that can be analyzed.
While the choice of thresholds for
[\alpha,\beta,\gamma]
can be adjusted to satisfy the needs of different use cases, we are confident that the 45 selected parks (
Table 4
in the Appendix) have reliable coverage from mobile traffic antennas while minimizing the inclusion of traffic from surrounding neighborhoods.

Obtaining ground truth for base station coverage is challenging and inherently complex to model
[
59
]
, largely because Voronoi approximations already deviate from the physical realities of wireless signaling.
Our method strikes a balance between the inevitable error introduced by Voronoi approximations and the number of parks that can be analyzed.
This approach enables an informed selection of spaces that can be reliably studied, while being able to quantify the error within the framework of Voronoi approximation.

0.529

33.26\%
increase compared to the cell-based method.
Conversely, if we applied the three steps with the same thresholds but using cell tower Voronois, it would only be possible to analyze 24 instead of 45 parks.

4.5
Converting Antenna-oriented Traffic to Park-oriented

With the selected set of parks and their respective antennas with good coverage, the final step of data processing involves converting the traffic
T_{v}(t)
, originally associated with Voronoi geometries, into the traffic
T_{p}(t)
observed within each park
p
.
This is achieved by aggregating the traffic from all selected antennas in
V_{sel,p}
, weighted by their respective illumination ratios:

T_{p}(t)=\sum_{v\in V_{sel,p}}I_{pv}\cdot T_{v}(t)

(4)

Thus, the traffic of park
p
at each time instant
t
is given by the weighted sum of the traffic from all its selected Voronoi cells in
V_{sel,p}
, where the weights correspond to their respective illumination ratios
I_{pv}
.

4.6
Measuring Mobile App Preferences

To quantify which app categories are used more in parks versus other parts of the city, we need a metric that captures relative traffic consumption across different areas. The simplest method would compare absolute traffic volumes, but this approach is problematic because different app types have very different traffic demands (e.g., video streaming uses much more data than instant messaging). Instead, we opted to use the Revealed Comparative Advantage (RCA)
[
60
]
metric, which has been successfully applied in mobile traffic analysis and clustering
[
61
,
62
,
38
]
. It defines the level of over- or under-utilization of a specific mobile application at a given antenna relative to the entire set of applications and antennas.

For a mobile application
a\in A
, and an antenna
v\in V
, the RCA is defined as:

RCA_{a,v}=\frac{T_{a,v}/\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}T_{a^{\prime}v}}{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}T_{av^{\prime}}/\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A,v^{\prime}\in V}T_{a^{\prime}v^{\prime}}},

(5)

where
T_{a,v}
is the traffic for application
a
at antenna
v
;
\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}T_{a^{\prime}v}
is the total traffic from all applications at antenna
v
;
\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}T_{av^{\prime}}
is the total traffic for application
a
across all antennas; and
\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A,v^{\prime}\in V}T_{a^{\prime}v^{\prime}}
is the overall traffic for all applications and antennas in the network.
Values of
RCA
above or below 1 indicate that an antenna has more or less consumption of a certain application compared to other antennas in the network. A downside of
RCA
is that while its lower boundary is 0, the upper boundary is
\infty
. This can unbalance the distribution of
RCA
values and cause issues in clustering algorithms
[
63
]
.

To address this, we used the
RSCA

[
63
]
defined as

RSCA_{a,v}=\frac{RCA_{a,v}-1}{RCA_{a,v}+1},

which yields values bounded in the interval
\left[-1,1\right]
. In this scale, values above 0 indicate over-utilization, and values below 0 indicate under-utilization. This results in a better-balanced distribution of values, eliminating the long tail observed with the original
RCA
metric.

4.7
Relative Importance of Flickr Tags

To compute the relative importance of a Flickr tag in a park (
Table 5
), we initially computed the probability that a image is tagged with this tag
p_{tag}
assuming an uniform distribution:

p_{tag}=\frac{|tag|}{\sum_{tag\in tags}|tag|}

In the second step, we compute the expected number of occurrences of a tag in a park
\hat{n}_{\{tag|park\}}
, by multiplying the relative frequency with the number of tags of this park.

\hat{n}_{\{tag|park\}}=p_{tag}\cdot|tag\in park|

Finally, we compute the ratio between the actual number of occurrences and the expected number of occurrences:

r_{\{tag|park\}}=\frac{n_{\{tag|park\}}}{\hat{n}_{\{tag|park\}}}

This provides us with a relative importance of each tag in each park. A value close to 1 means that this tag is occurring at an average rate, whereas values higher than 1 indicates that this certain tag is occurring more frequently than expected.
To clean the resulting, we have removed English and French Stop words, as well as irrelevant tags, such as “France”, “Paris”, “park”, as well as Flickr-specific tags referring to photography techniques (“black & white”, “square format”, “hdr” and synonyms thereof. Finally, as it is common that in addition to the geo-coordinates, the images get tagged with the name of a location, we removed all tags, that had a value above 1 in exactly one park leading (e.g., “jardinduluxembourg”, “champdemars”, “jardindesplantes”). Note that “toureiffel” (and synonyms) were not removed in this step, as they had a relative importance over 1 in both Champ de Mars and in the Jardins du Trocadéro.
