A cross-sectional survey benchmarking the coverage and importance of obesity-related competencies in U.S. dietetic supervised practice programs

Published: 2024

Background

Competence in treating people with obesity is critical for registered dietitian nutritionists (RDNs). Both the 2013 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/The Obesity Society Guideline for the Management of Overweight and Obesity in Adults [1] and the 2021 Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics Evidence Analysis Library Adult Weight Management guidelines [2] have strong recommendations for obesity interventions that are delivered by RDNs. RDNs have long been part of the interdisciplinary team recommended for obesity treatment, focusing primarily on behavior change, dietary interventions and lifestyle changes for weight loss, and nutrition counseling before and after metabolic bariatric surgery. Additionally, as obesity science evolves, the role of the RDN evolves with it. For example, the rapid development of anti-obesity medications requires that RDNs understand how to provide nutrition care as an adjunct to pharmacotherapy. Given the importance of the RDN on the interdisciplinary team, and the evolving role of the RDNs on said teams, obesity competence among RDNs is critical and highly valuable. Finally, obesity-related competence is important for all RDNs, not only those providing obesity care specifically. Though BMI alone should not be used to predict individual health risk [3], most recent U.S. national data indicates that 41.9% of adults have a BMI that places them in the ‘obesity’ category [4]. Therefore, whether or not an RDN treats patients for obesity, they almost certainly will be treating patients with obesity. Competent care should be expected in these cases as well.

Obesity education for RDNs is important not only for ensuring adequate knowledge but also may be important for reducing weight stigma. Weight stigma is known to be present among healthcare professionals [5], including RDNs [6–8]. Some have theorized that an understanding of the “controllability” of obesity, or lack thereof, may affect weight bias, i.e. those attributing obesity more strongly to non-modifiable factors may have less weight bias [9]. Therefore, education around genetic susceptibility to obesity, the physiology of energy homeostasis and appetite regulation, and social determinants of obesity risk has potential to decrease weight stigma by shifting attribution from personal control to non-modifiable factors. Several previous studies have demonstrated a decrease in weight bias among healthcare professionals following interventions based on this so-called ‘attribution theory’ [10–15].

Standards for dietetic education recognize that RDNs should receive education about obesity. The 2022 accreditation standards from ACEND (Accreditation Council for Education in Nutrition and Dietetics), the accrediting body for dietetic education programs, state that “Learning activities must prepare students for professional practice with patients/clients with various conditions, including…overweight and obesity.” [16] This guidance is included in the ACEND standards for didactic programs in dietetics, coordinated programs, and dietetic internship programs alike. However, ACEND does not provide any further guidance on precisely what obesity-related learning activities or objectives should be included. Because there is no guidance or standardization for obesity-related education in dietetic training programs, it is difficult to know how well-prepared entry-level dietitians are for obesity care.

Despite the importance of obesity education, to our knowledge there are no studies examining the extent to which obesity-related competencies are incorporated into dietetic supervised practice training or other dietetic education. The objective of this study was to benchmark the inclusion of obesity-related competencies in dietetic supervised practice programs, as self-reported by supervised practice directors, to begin to understand the training that U.S. RDNs receive in this broad and important practice area.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was an exploratory, cross-sectional, online survey of directors of ACEND-accredited U.S. dietetic supervised practice programs, including dietetic internships, coordinated programs in dietetics, graduate programs (called “future graduate programs” at the time of data collection), and individual supervised practice pathway programs. The survey was distributed in July of 2022. The list of supervised practice directors was obtained from the ACEND 2021–2022 Applicant Guide to Supervised Practice. An incentive of a $25 Amazon gift card was offered to the survey respondents for their participation. The survey was emailed to 347 program directors in the U.S., to the best of our knowledge this was complete distribution to all supervised practice program directors less two directors who completed pilot testing of our survey.

Survey instrument

The competencies published in the interprofessional 2017 Provider Competencies on the Prevention and Management of Obesity [17] served as the foundation of the survey. These competencies were developed via consensus among experts from 20 different organizations, including the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, who represented twelve different health professions (for example, medicine, nursing, kinesiology, psychology, pharmacy, and health education). The goal of the competencies is to address general concepts related to core obesity knowledge, interprofessional obesity care, and patient interactions related to obesity. We chose to use these 2017 Provider Competencies instead of the Dietetics Standards of Practice for adult weight management [18], which include standards for entry-level ‘competent’ RDNs, for two reasons. First, using the interprofessional competencies will allow us to compare results across disciplines in future research. Second, the interprofessional competencies allowed us to focus on obesity-specific skills, creating a more targeted survey tool. For example, the Standards of Practice state that a competent RDN should be able to “list actual or potential drug interactions” (standard 1.5C2) and “assess food preparation skills and knowledge” (standard 1.5D1). Although these are both important in obesity care, they are skills that are pertinent to nutrition assessment for almost any condition or population. Therefore, questions about these competencies are less likely to provide insight into obesity education specifically.

The online survey, administered via Qualtrics, asked about the extent to which each of the 31 competencies from the interprofessional 2017 Provider Competencies on the Prevention and Management of Obesity [17] is covered in the required (i.e. non-elective) components of their program. Participants responded using a four-point Likert scale ranging from ‘to a great extent’ to ‘not at all’. ‘I don’t know’ was included as a fifth response option. Supervised practice directors were also asked about their opinion of the importance of each competency. Again, a four-point Likert scale was used with response options ranging from ‘very important’ to ‘not at all important’. Additionally, we included questions about whether or not obesity is included in formal program objectives, barriers to inclusion of obesity-related competencies, preparedness of the students to provide nutrition care for obesity upon completion of their program, and professional and demographic information about the respondent. Our survey was largely modeled after a previously-published survey of family medicine residency program directors [19]. In the previous survey, Orjuela-Grimm & Butsch et al. studied the degree to which obesity-related competencies, published by the Obesity Medicine Education Collaborative [20] (a separate set of obesity-related competencies specific to the field of medicine), were incorporated into their family medicine resident programs. We pilot tested the survey with two current directors of supervised practice programs (who were later excluded from survey participation) and made minor edits to the survey based on their feedback.

Data analysis

We conducted descriptive frequency analysis for the responses to each survey question by dividing the number of responses in each response category by the number of total survey respondents. Our survey included an open-ended, qualitative question at the end of the survey, asking the respondents if they would like to share any comments about the survey or about the obesity-related competencies in their program. Given the lack of theoretical or methodological consideration in the development of this question, we did not plan to analyze this qualitative data a priori; it was included primarily to flag any problems with the survey. However, because this is a novel area of study and because several of the comments offered interesting insight, we ultimately elected to perform thematic content analysis. After familiarizing ourselves with the qualitative data, overarching themes and subthemes were identified and named.

This study was determined to be exempt by the Case Western Reserve University Institutional Review Board.

Results

Study Sample

We received 42 survey responses, however 6 responses were excluded from analysis due to participants completing < 20% of the survey (in all cases, responses were limited to those around their program description, i.e., what type of program they direct). After excluding incomplete surveys, our analysis included responses from 36 participants representing 10.4% of supervised practice directors in the U.S. The median reported time in their current director position was 4.0 years and median time as a registered dietitian was 19.0 years. Ten respondents (28%) indicated that “weight management” is one of their personal areas of practice expertise.

Coverage and preparedness

The competencies most frequently reported to be covered ‘to a great extent’ were ‘evaluate BMI’ and ‘perform effectively in an interprofessional team’; these were reported as being covered ‘to a great extent’ by 82% and 71% of respondents, respectively. Very few programs reported ‘not at all incorporated’ for any competencies; the most frequent were ‘potential role of genetics/epigenetics’ (13.9%, n = 5) and ‘physiology/pathophysiology of obesity’ (11.1%, n = 4). Details on the extent to which all competencies were reported to be incorporated are presented in Fig. 1 and Supplemental Table 1.

When asked how prepared their students are to provide nutrition care for patients with obesity upon completion of their supervised practice program, more than half (53%, n = 19) of the supervised practice directors reported their students are ‘fairly prepared’ and one-third of directors (33%, n = 12) reported that their students are ‘very prepared’. None of the directors reported their students are ‘not at all prepared’ to provide nutrition care for patients with obesity and 14% reported that they are ‘somewhat prepared’.

Importance

Of the 36 respondents, 58% (n = 21) indicated that teaching students about the prevention or treatment of obesity is an intentional (i.e. formal or organized) program objective. The competencies most frequently reported as ‘very important’ were ‘Perform effectively in an interprofessional team’ and ‘discuss obesity in a non-judgmental manner’. These were considered ‘very important’ by 86% and 81% of respondents, respectively. Out of the 31 competencies, only six were reported as ‘not at all important’ by any respondents and for all six of these competencies only one respondent reported them as ‘not at all important’. Therefore, we combined the ‘not at all important’ category with the ‘somewhat important’ category. The competencies most frequently reported as ‘somewhat or not at all important’ were ‘The demographics and evolution of the obesity epidemic’ and ‘The potential role of genetics/epigenetics’, each reported to be somewhat or not at all important by 17% (n = 6) of respondents. Barriers

The most frequently reported barriers, reported as a large or moderate barrier, to integrating obesity education into the curriculum were ‘lack of room (time) in the curriculum’ (64%) and ‘lack of obesity-related rotation sites’ (50% of respondents). The frequency of report of these and other barriers is presented in Fig. 3. Four participants reported ‘other’ as a large or moderate barrier. When prompted to specify, these ‘other’ barriers were reported as: “Lack of institutional will”, “Not adequately covered in the DPD [didactic program for dietetics, the undergraduate preparation for supervised practice] programs”, “Limited need, already a course that specifically addresses overweight and obesity”, and “Politics related to HAES [Health at Every Size ™]”.

Qualitative data

Due to the lack of methodological and theoretical framework used to develop the singular qualitative question, as well as the small sample size and inability to determine whether or not saturation was reached, the qualitative data should be interpreted with extreme caution. However, we identified two overarching themes in our qualitative data: approach to obesity education and treatment, and barriers to obesity education.

The respondents reported preference for a variety of approaches to educating about obesity care. Two respondents indicated a traditional approach based on the socioecological model, metabolic health, or medical nutrition therapy.

Several programs expressed the desire to teach weight-neutral/non-stigmatizing approaches, including trauma-informed approach and intuitive eating. For example, one respondent said, “The phrasing of your survey questions made them difficult for me to answer, as they seemed to presume that we would treat or teach about obesity as pathological.” The same respondent stated, “Teaching students to think critically about how to promote health and provide the best possible care for patients in larger bodies is important. Teaching students to specifically promote weight loss, however, is not among our priorities nor our intentional objectives.”

Another respondent explained, “I do not like competencies related to obesity, I believe most of the competencies run counter-intuitive to the principles of Intuitive Eating. We have been fighting the “obesity epidemic” for many years with minimal if any success, maybe our approach is just wrong.” Finally, one respondent stated that “students love this topic- esp [sic] HAES [Health at Every Size™]”.

Responses related to barriers reiterated some of the responses in the quantitative data. Four respondents indicated that it is difficult to include obesity-related competencies due to the large volume of competencies that are already required in the curriculum. For example, one respondent said, “The biggest barrier is addressing the topic of obesity without increasing the program length since we already have so many competencies to cover.”

One respondent indicated there are not enough rotation sites. Finally, one program director listed concerns about sensitivity as a barrier, stating, “I believe we need to incorporate the concepts/competencies related to obesity better than we do but many of our students and some of our faculty feel doing so is not sensitive to people with obesity.”

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the degree to which obesity-related competencies are integrated into dietetic supervised practice programs or other dietetic education and training programs. More than 40% of respondents indicated that obesity education is not a formal program objective. These results provide evidence that certain obesity-related competencies such as obesity assessment and intervention are fairly well-covered across supervised practice programs, while other competencies related to the etiology and pathophysiology of obesity do not receive as much coverage. Most supervised practice directors (81%) felt that it is very important that students learn how to discuss obesity in a non-judgmental way.

Previous research has indicated that highlighting the non-modifiable causes of obesity may be one strategy for reducing weight stigma among healthcare professionals [9]. The current study demonstrated that several of the competencies related to non-modifiable risk factors receive little to no coverage in supervised practice programs. For example, competencies covering the potential role of genetics/epigenetics in the development of obesity, the physiology and pathophysiology of weight regulation, and the role of inequities in determining obesity were reported as receiving very little to no coverage by 47%, 36%, and 36% of respondents respectively. Because there is no objective way to rank the importance of obesity-related competencies, it is difficult to say which competencies should take priority if time in the curriculum is limited. However, for programs hoping to both expand obesity education and decrease weight stigma, improving coverage of these competencies related to non-modifiable causes of obesity may be a place to start. The 2017 interprofessional competencies that served as the basis for this study [17] should also be used as a guide for further development of obesity education in dietetics. Specifically, Sect. 1.0 of these competencies lists six content areas that address the constellation of factors that lead to the development of obesity.

Although the authors did not study obesity-related competencies overall, Huebner et al. previously examined the inclusion of non-diet weight-neutral curricula among ACEND coordinated programs and didactic programs in dietetics [21]. A non-diet weight-neutral approach is a method for addressing weight concerns that emphasizes health and wellbeing and deemphasizes dietary restriction and weight as a primary outcome. The authors reported 71.6% (n = 83) of responding programs include information on non-diet weight-neutral approaches, most commonly as a single lecture or class period.

In the current study, only a third of supervised practice directors indicated students are “very prepared” to provide nutrition care for patients with obesity upon program completion, which is in line with what is reported by directors about U.S. medical students’ (10%) [22] and U.S. family medicine residents’ (21%) [19] preparedness to manage obesity. Together, this evidence indicates improvement in obesity education is likely needed across all health professional disciplines.

The reported lack of time in the curriculum for obesity-related training, reported as a moderate or large barrier by 64% of survey respondents, is concerning given this is perhaps one of the most prevalent conditions that students will encounter. Lack of room in the curriculum has also been reported as an important barrier for U.S. medical schools, with 85% of medical school leaders indicating that it is a moderate or large barrier to implementing or expanding obesity education [22]. Given the finite room in any curricula, the topics included reflect the program’s priorities. Because obesity is highly prevalent and because of the need for non-stigmatizing care, we encourage supervised practice programs to consider prioritizing obesity-related training among the many topics that deserve coverage. Existing asynchronous virtual options may help address the barrier of time as well. For example, the Obesity Medicine Association offers (for a fee) a virtual course, Fundamentals of Obesity Treatment (https://academy.obesitymedicine.org/), which would be appropriate for dietetic education.

Lack of obesity-related rotation sites was also a commonly-reported barrier in the current study; 50% of respondents indicated it is a moderate or large barrier to obesity training. Understanding of this barrier may encourage RDNs currently working in obesity-related positions to volunteer as preceptors. The rise of telehealth may make this more feasible as students and interns may be able to observe patient visits with RDNs who would otherwise be too far away for a rotation. Employers of RDNs can also help address this barrier by providing professional incentives for their dietitians to volunteer their time as preceptors.

The qualitative results demonstrate a lack of consensus across the profession and among students regarding the approach to obesity. Responses indicated that further research may be needed to explore how obesity is being taught across RDN training programs, and student and educator preferences for the approach. Though these results are extremely preliminary, future research may be warranted to explore how beliefs and attitudes about obesity and weight loss shape training for healthcare professionals.

This study had several limitations. The sample size was small, and we only surveyed directors of supervised practice programs. To understand obesity education in dietetics comprehensively, future research will need to examine didactic programs in dietetics (i.e. DPD programs) as well as master’s degree programs in nutrition. Additionally, it is possible that some respondents do not have full knowledge of the breadth or depth of obesity-related competencies that are addressed within the supervised practice program. Finally, we need to consider response bias carefully. Supervised practice program directors who themselves have a professional interest in obesity, and a positive view of its inclusion in dietetic education, may have been more likely to respond to this survey and may also be more likely to promote or include obesity-related competencies within their program. However, obesity can be a divisive topic in the dietetic community and it seems equally possible to us that respondents were more likely to hold a negative view of obesity education. This response bias may have affected our results, and future research may benefit from capturing the respondents’ personal views on how and whether weight and obesity should be addressed professionally.

Conclusions

RDNs are critical to the multidisciplinary approach to obesity treatment, and therefore training competent, unbiased practitioners is vital. Based on this sample of supervised practice directors, many obesity-related competencies are considered important and are being incorporated into supervised practice programs. In order to continue to work against stigma, directors are encouraged to prioritize obesity education, particularly competencies related to the pathophysiology of obesity and inclusive care. Practitioners working in obesity should consider serving as preceptors when possible to help address the lack of training sites. Further research is needed to understand how these competencies are incorporated into other stages of RDN training and how the reported coverage and importance of these competencies change over time, as well as how dietetic trainees themselves view obesity treatment. Additionally, more research leading to professional agreement on the approach to obesity education (ex- weight neutral, trauma informed, etc.) may help guide educators.
