Deconvolution of spatial transcriptomics data via graph contrastive learning and partial least square regression
Authors: Yuanyuan Mo, Juan Liu, Lihua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeciphering the cellular abundance in spatial transcriptomics (ST) is crucial for revealing the spatial architecture of cellular heterogeneity within tissues. However, some of the current spatial sequencing technologies are in low resolutions, leading to each spot having multiple heterogeneous cells. Additionally, current spatial deconvolution methods lack the ability to utilize multi-modality information such as gene expression and chromatin accessibility from single-cell multi-omics data. In this study, we introduce a graph Contrastive Learning and Partial Least Squares regression-based method, CLPLS, to deconvolute ST data. CLPLS is a flexible method that it can be extended to integrate ST data and single-cell multi-omics data, enabling the exploration of the spatially epigenomic heterogeneity. We applied CLPLS to both simulated and real datasets coming from different platforms. Benchmark analyses with other methods on these datasets show the superior performance of CLPLS in deconvoluting spots in single cell level.

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) measures gene expression at single-cell level and offers a highly detailed view of cellular heterogeneity [1, 2]. However, spatial information is lost in single-cell sequencing technologies. Recent developments in spatial transcriptomics (ST) advance the identification of structural organization and spatial distribution of heterogeneous cells within complex tissues [3–6]. Current ST sequencing technologies can be summarized into two categories, including image-based in situ hybridization [7–9] and next-generation sequencing-based technologies [10–12]. The image-based approaches achieve subcellular resolution but have limited genome-scale gene coverage while the sequencing-based approaches offer the whole-transcriptome scale gene coverage but suffer from low resolution issue, leading to a spot being composed of a mixture of heterogeneous cells. Revealing the composition of the spots at the cellular level is crucial [13, 14] for interpreting the underlying biological processes and identifying relevant cell types involved in disease mechanisms or therapeutic responses [15–17].

Some computational methods have been developed to estimate the relative proportions of different cell types in each spatial location by integrating ST data and scRNA-seq data [18–28]. Among these, several classical methods, such as Seurat [24], RCTD [25], SPOTlight [26], DestVI [27], DSTG [28], and GraphST [22], have played foundational roles in advancing the field. Specially, Seurat leverages canonical correlation analysis to align scRNA-seq and ST data and estimate the cell type composition with transfer learning. RCTD applies a probabilistic model to estimate cell type proportions, using a likelihood-based approach to map scRNA-seq data onto spatial locations. SPOTlight incorporates non-negative matrix factorization and non-negative least squares to deconvolute ST spots with single-cell reference data. DestVI employs a deep generative model based on variational inference to infer cellular compositions in spatial contexts. DSTG first generates pseudo-ST data from scRNA-seq and then apply a semi-supervised graph convolutional network (GCN) model for deconvolution. GraphST leverages an augmentation-free contrastive learning to obtain a mapping matrix that projects the scRNA-seq data into the ST space. A comprehensive benchmarking study [19] for cellular deconvolution of ST illustrated that Tangram [20] and Cell2location [21] were the best methods for addressing the cellular deconvolution task compared to other existing methods. Tangram uses a deep learning framework to align single-cell data in space. Cell2location combines ST data with reference cell type signatures derived from scRNA-seq, employing a Bayesian model to infer cell distributions and localizations effectively. In addition to these established approaches, new methods [29–32] continue to emerge. For example, SDePER [30] performs cell type deconvolution by combining conditional variational autoencoder with graph Laplacian regularized model. However, despite excelling in single-modal data scenarios, all these deconvolution methods except for SpaTrio [23] tend to overlook the information coming from diverse modalities.

Single-cell multi-omics data provide a comprehensive understanding of cell state [33, 34]. However, spatial information is lost in single-cell multi-omics sequencing, which is critical for understanding cellular microenvironments and cell–cell interactions. Therefore, integrating single-cell multi-omics data and ST data is necessary, which enables to investigate the multimodal topography of tissues on a spatial scale. Although SpaTrio achieves comprehensive spatial mapping of single cells by integrating the assay for transposase accessible chromatin (ATAC) [35] and RNA assay from single-cell multi-omics and ST data through probabilistic alignment with the optimal transport algorithm, it maps cells to the spatial space without directly obtaining cell compositions. Moreover, the existence of batch effects between single-cell sequencing data and ST data provides a big challenge for current deconvolution methods.

To fill the gap, we propose CLPLS, a computational method for deconvoluting cell type composition at each spatial location by combining graph-based self-supervised contrastive learning and partial least squares regression (PLSR) for spot deconvolution. Graph contrastive learning in an efficient method to extract features of ST data by integrating gene expression and spatial information. Partial least squares regression is a statistical method that combines the advantages of principal component analysis regression [36] and canonical correlation analysis [37], which has been illustrated to be an efficient tool in removing batch effects [38]. We applied CLPLS to both simulated dataset and real datasets coming from different sequencing platforms. CLPLS has demonstrated its superior performance in cell type deconvolution. Moreover, CLPLS can be applied to deconvolute ST data by utilizing single-cell multi-omics dataset, effectively capturing the differences among various modalities within spatial contexts.

Methods

CLPLS consists of two modules: the representation learning module and the PLSR module (Fig. 1). CLPLS treats ST data and single-cell reference data as inputs. When the paired chromatin accessibility data is available, we convert the chromatin accessibility data into gene activity for further representation learning. In the representation learning module, we employ the alpha-complex-based [39] method for graph construction leveraging spatial location information. We compute the shared nearest neighbor as the graph for scRNA-seq data. A two-layer GCN is then built as the encoder for ST data to embed the gene expression and spatial similarity into a latent embedding. We adopt self-supervised contrastive learning to force the latent embedding to capture the spot-specific local context and the variability between spots. To make the low-dimensional embeddings of scRNA-seq data and the ST data be comparable, parameter sharing approach is adopted to share the parameters of the ST encoder with single-cell encoder. In the PLSR module, the low-dimensional embeddings from the representation learning module are inputted into the PLSR algorithm to obtain a regression matrix that transfers cell type annotations from scRNA-seq data to spatial locations for cell type deconvolution.

The overview of CLPLS. The inputs of CLPLS are spatial transcriptomics data, scRNA-seq and paired scATAC-seq data (if available). The low-dimensional embeddings are obtained by the representation learning module and are then inputted into the PLSR module. The output of CLPLS is a spot-by-cell matrix, which can be used for estimating cell type compositions.

Data preprocessing

We preprocessed the single-cell and spatial datasets (Table 1) by the Scanpy package [40]. We first filtered out genes expressed in fewer than three cells and cells with fewer than 100 expressed genes. Then raw count matrices were normalized and log-transformed. We selected top 50 marker genes for each cell type using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test on scRNA-seq data. Finally, the intersected genes of the marker genes and genes of ST data were used. When single-cell chromatin accessibility data was available, we preprocessed scATAC-seq assay using the standard analysis procedure of Signac package [41]. Specially, we used GeneActivity function to get gene activity scores, which summarized the counts in the 2-kb upstream region and in the gene body, providing a rough estimate of the transcriptional activity for each gene. Then gene activity matrix was preprocessed in the same manner as the scRNA-seq data mentioned above.

Experiments and results

CLPLS outperforms other methods on simulated dataset

To test the ability of CLPLS in the cell type deconvolution task, we generated a simulated ST dataset. Specifically, we used the scRNA-seq data of the mouse brain cerebral cortex region [46] coming from Smart-seq to generate the synthetic spatial data. The simulated spatial data had three layers and the position of each layer is randomly generated at a certain radius. The spots in the inner layer were composed of L2/3 IT, L4, and L5 IT cells with probabilities equaling 25%, 25% and 50%. And spots in the middle layer were composed of L5 PT and L6b with probabilities equaling 50% and 50%. While the spots in the outer layer were composed of L6 CT and L6 IT with probabilities equaling 50% and 50%. Finally, we randomly set 70% values to 0 to add dropouts to the simulated spatial data.

CLPLS, Seurat, RCTD, SPOTlight, DestVI, DSTG, SDePER, Cell2location, and Tangram could reveal three layers, while GraphST failed to reveal the structures (Fig. 2). The pie chart at each spot represented the heterogeneous cell composition of each localized spot. The pie chart of CLPLS was similar to that of ground truth than Seurat, SPOTlight, DestVI, DSTG, SDePER, Cell2location, and Tangram. On the contrary, other methods except for RCTD suffered from inaccurate cell type compositions for each layer. Additionally, we checked the predicted distributions of each cell type on the spatial coordinates. The results obtained from CLPLS were consistent with the regional enrichment of each cell type in the ground truth (Supplementary Fig. 5). CLPLS accurately predicted the existence of each cell type across spatial coordinates. Other methods except for RCTD produced results with more noise, leading to the high presence of cell types in regions where they should not be. In addition, we conducted an ablation study, referred to as PLSR, which directly fed the processed data into the PLSR module to obtain the results, demonstrating the positive effect of each module.

The performance of CLPLS and other methods on the simulated dataset. Spatial plots with pie chart show inferred cell-type compositions on each spatial location from different deconvolution methods. Compared deconvolution methods include Cell2location, GraphST, and Tangram. Right: an accurate spatial pie chart depicts the ground truth composition of cell types. PLSR is an ablation case of CSPLS that directly feeds the data into the PLSR module to obtain the results.

We applied four commonly used metrics including Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC), Structure Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) that were defined in literature [18] to quantitatively assess the deconvolution performance. Unlike the majority of methods that calculated these metrics in single view, we computed them in both the spot and cell type views, allowing for a more comprehensive elucidation of the experimental comparative results. CLPLS achieved competitive performance compared to other methods in the analysis in spot view (Fig. 3A). Notably, CLPLS outperformed other methods in terms of PCC and SSIM metrics. From the cell type view, RCTD and CLPLS had higher PCC and SSIM values and lower RMSE and JSD values than other methods (Fig. 3B). Moreover, CLPLS had the smallest running time among the methods during the deconvolution process (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Comparison of CLPLS against other methods using metrics on the simulated dataset. (A) Boxplot showing the four metrics (PCC, SSIM, RMSE, and JSD) calculated for each spot. (B) Boxplot showing the four metrics (PCC, SSIM, RMSE, and JSD) calculated for each cell type.

CLPLS accurately deconvolutes known layers on the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex dataset

We further applied CLPLS to the ST data of the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) obtained by 10X Visium and scRNA-seq data obtained by 10X Genomics Chromium [47, 48]. We checked the deconvolution performance in identifying hierarchical structures from the outer to inner layers on the slice of 151673. CLPLS showed spatially restricted patterns in the corresponding layers, while Cell2location, Tangram, Seurat, RCTD, SPOTlight, DestVI, DSTG, and SDePER exhibited vague results (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 7). Then we checked the marker genes’ expression levels across spatial location to further examine the deconvolution results. As expected, MBP which is the marker of Oligo has significant signals in the Oligo_1 predominated spots, which is consistent with the predicted proportions of this cell type by all methods except Tangram. The top markers of Ex10_L2_4, Ex7_L4_6, and Ex8_L5_6 enrich at regions with correspondingly high abundance of Ex10_L2_4, Ex7_L4_6, and Ex8_L5_6 cells. The consistency between the spatially expressed patterns of these markers and the predicted proportions of the corresponding cell types illustrated the accuracy of CLPLS in cell type deconvolution.

To quantitatively compare the deconvolution performance of these methods, we computed the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) based on the true labels of the spots and cell compositions, with higher score indicating better method. For example, in the case of the WM class, spots labeled as WM are assigned a value of 1, while all other spots are assigned a value of 0. Specifically, CLPLS had highest AUC scores on the cell types including Ex10_L2_4, Ex8_L5_6, and Oligos_1. PLSR exhibited significantly lower AUC values compared to CLPLS on the cell type Ex10_L2_4 and Ex8_L5_6, indicating the importance of representation learning module of CLPLS. Overall, CLPLS outperformed other methods in predicting specific neuronal layer localization patterns (Fig. 4B). For comprehensive comparison, we evaluated all the methods on the entire 12 slices. CLPLS achieved the best performance across all these four hierarchical cell types (Fig. 4C).

CLPLS uncovers distinct cell populations of mouse hippocampus

Then we applied CLPLS to the ST data of mouse hippocampal tissue generated from Slide-seq V2 platform [12] to test its deconvolution capability. We focused on three delicate regions in mouse hippocampal tissue, namely CA1, CA3, and Denate, respectively (Fig. 5A), which are annotated by Allen reference Atlas [49]. Pou3f1, Nptxr, and C1ql2 are markers of CA1, CA3, and Denate, which is illustrated in Fig. 5(B). CLPLS, DSTG, RCTD, SPOTlight, and DestVI accurately delineated these cell type compositions across regions, highlighting the three specific anatomical structures (Fig. 5C). Cell2location’s visualization of the proportions of each cell type across all spots could not achieve the discrimination as CLPLS. The limitation of GraphST lied in its inability to differentiate the distribution of CA1 and CA3 categories. Tangram could not recover CA1 and CA3 regions, while Seurat revealed CA1 and CA3 clearly. PLSR failed to deconvolute CA3 regions. Then we quantified to compare the effectiveness of these methods in predicting the predominate cell types by calculating PCC values between the deconvolute proportions and the fold-changes of marker genes’ expression across regions (Supplementary Fig. 8). CLPLS had the highest PCC values with smallest deviation, outperforming other methods. Moreover, CLPLS had better performance than traditional PLSR method, which indicating the importance of spatial information in deconvolution.

CLPLS reveals unique cellular populations within the zebrafish embryo

Next, we applied CLPLS to the zebrafish embryo dataset [50] obtained by stereo-seq. To evaluate the deconvolution performance, we summed the expression levels of multiple markers and treated them as the distribution of cell types. Cmn, col2a1a, and ntd5 are markers of the Notochord, while apoa1a and apoa1b serve as markers of the Epidermis_egfl6, YSL (YSL). Etv2 and flt4 are markers of Blood Vessel Endothelial Cells. CLPLS could depict the cell type proportion patterns of Notochord, YSL, and Blood Vessel Endothelial Cell, while Seurat, DSTG, and SDePER had certain limitations in visualizing the cell type proportions across spots (Fig. 6A). We further quantified the effectiveness of these methods by calculating the PCC values between predicted cell type proportion and markers’ gene expression. CLPLS achieved the highest PCC value for the YSL categories while also yielding satisfactory results for the remaining two categories (Fig. 6B), demonstrating its effectiveness in handling this binned subcellular resolution ST data.

CLPLS reveals spatially epigenomic heterogeneity of mouse brain

Finally, we applied CLPLS to the single-cell multi-omics dataset and ST dataset of mouse brain [42], including single-cell gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and ST data. Integrating such data yields more comprehensive understand of the spatial heterogeneity. We filtered some cell clusters annotated in the previous study [42] with the number of cells less than 50. Compared to SpaTrio, CLPLS accurately predicted spatial patterns of each RNA cluster and ATAC cluster (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Fig. 9). Specially, the SpaTrio predicted results were dispersed and did not correspond to their expected positions such as Ex-L6 IT. Moreover, the results derived from CLPLS were consistent with the expression patterns of cell type specific markers.

The spatial distributions of the predominant cell types inferred at each spatial location further confirmed that CLPLS could accurately depict distinct layered structure (Fig. 7B). Then we compared the predominant cell types’ distributions between gene expression and chromatin accessibility (Fig. 7C). As revealed in the previous study [23] spots predominated by L4/5 IT cells were divided into A2, A3, and A9 ATAC clusters. To investigate the spatial heterogeneity, we extracted submatrix of the deconvoluted chromatin accessibility matrix obtained by CLPLS with spots predominated by L4/5 IT cells. Three layers from outer to inner were obtained by Leiden algorithm on the subset matrix (Fig. 7D). We named these layers with L4/5 IT-1, L4/5 IT-2, and L4/5 IT-3, respectively.

We identified marker peaks of the cells predominated by L4/5 IT-1, L4/5 IT-2, and L4/5 IT-3, respectively. Then we conducted motif enrichment analysis and Gene Ontology Biological Process with these marker peaks by HOMER [51] and GREAT [52] to investigate the spatial chromatin heterogeneity of the mouse brain. NeuroG2 [53] and Atoh1 [54] are known for their roles in neurogenesis and neural differentiation are enriched in the L4/5 IT-1 layer (Fig. 7E). The transcription factor activator protein-1 (AP-1) [55], including Fra1, Fos, and JunB, that are involved in cellular proliferation, transformation, and death are enriched in L4/5 IT-3 layer. Different biological processes such as adult walking behavior and long-term synaptic potentiation are enriched in these three layers (Fig. 7F). These biological processes are essential for motor functions and synaptic plasticity, which is consistent with the function of the L4/5 IT layer.

Discussion

Here, we introduce CLPLS to deconvolute ST data in single cell level. The advantage of CLPLS is that it combines the metrics of both graph contrastive learning and partial least square regression. Partial least square regression is good at aligning the variables in same space and decoupling the relationship between variables at the same time. PLSR is directly appropriate for integrating single-cell and spatial data, which has batch effect. The experimental results demonstrate that CLPLS exhibits superior deconvolution capabilities on both simulated dataset and real spatial datasets coming from different platforms. Furthermore, CLPLS enables the integration of single-cell multi-omics data and ST data, allowing for comprehensive understanding spatially epigenomic heterogeneity.

CLPLS is a flexible two-step method, which can be extended to integrate multiple single-cell RNA-seq reference data to deconvolute ST data. Integrating multi-omics data especially scATAC-seq to deconvolute ST data is important yet challenging for joint analysis of different features. In the current CLPLS model, the scATAC-seq data need to be transformed to gene activity scores. More complex and accurate models are needed to include various features, for example, the corresponding histological images. To incorporate the histological images, a constraint related to histological features can be added into the objective function of the PLSR module with a Laplacian graph constraint on the principal component vector v. The Laplacian graph is built based on the histological image features’ similarity of spots. Alternatively, the histological images can be utilized to refine the spatial graph in the representation learning module of CLPLS. While it remains to be done, CLPLS provides a computational tool for integrating single-cell multi-omics and ST data.
