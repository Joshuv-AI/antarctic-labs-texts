Why did menopause evolve?
Authors: Amy McDermott
Published: 2026

Researchers are identifying more species that experience menopause, prompting fresh theories about its evolution.

Martha Robbins arrived in the mountains of Uganda in 1998. A behavioral ecologist, she came to study a troop of gorillas in the misty forests of Bwindi Impenetrable National Park. Low-lying clouds drift over the canopy there, feeding a lush ecosystem of dense moss-covered trees and an understory of thick, hip-high shrubs and vines. Mountain gorillas wade through the jungle, looming from the sea of green.

Gorillas in Bwindi Impenetrable National Park in Uganda are among a select few species that appear to undergo menopause, spurring questions about evolutionary advantages of living past fertility. Image credit: Martha M. Robbins (Max Planck Institute of Evolutionary Anthropology, Leipzig, Germany).

A gorilla named Matu captured Robbins’ curiosity. She was the only female with the wrinkled face and crooked fingers of an older ape. Her chest also sagged, suggesting that Matu had babies and breastfed in the past. Robbins thought Matu was over 30 and wondered how many more years she could live. As Robbins made her career in Uganda, now spanning 29 years, she stayed close to Matu. The elderly gorilla lived another decade but never reproduced again. That was a surprise. In most animals, females keep reproducing for as long as they live.

Robbins wondered if she had witnessed a gorilla go through menopause. Only a handful of other species, including humans and certain whales, were known to experience this change of life—the point where ovulation naturally ceases—and to live well beyond it, far past the end of reproduction. (Infertility is the general term, Robbins says, but menopause is specific to later-age onset.)

The question stayed with her through the next two decades, as she observed more female gorillas that reached late-middle age and stopped having babies. In 2025, Robbins’ research group, which works between field stations in Bwindi and labs in Germany’s Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, published a PNAS article finding evidence for likely menopause in the gorillas (1). Using demographic data, her team showed that females at Bwindi reproduce every five years, on average. Going 7.7 years or more without a baby (the average interval between births plus two standard deviations) is the cutoff to be considered infertile. The researchers found that two moms pushing 50—named Siatu and Binyindo—had lived for 15 years without reproducing.

Gorillas are not the only ape to enjoy an extended life after the childbearing years. In 2023, hormone data from wild chimpanzees, 7 hours away in Uganda’s Kibale National Park, showed that females over age 50 undergo menopause (2). And evidence from captive chimps and gorillas in zoos has shown that females stop breeding in middle age but can live another 10 or more years in the absence of predators (3).

These apes have deepened the mystery of menopause. In light of evolution, menopause has never made much sense. To maximize their evolutionary fitness, individuals should strive to pass on the most genes possible to the next generation, and lifelong reproduction would seem to be the most efficient way to do so. But multiple species undergo menopause and live decades beyond, suggesting that postreproductive life may also offer some evolutionary benefit. For decades, the prevailing theory was that grandmothers could secure their genetic legacy by helping younger relatives have more babies. But that may not hold true in some of the more recent examples, including the mountain gorillas. That’s prompting a fresh look at the evolution of menopause.

“The biological puzzle,” Robbins says, “is why should female animals have such a long period where they’re not reproducing?”

Living Longer

Every fertile human woman, if she lives long enough, will experience menopause. Despite it being an extremely rare reproductive strategy compared to the rest of the animal kingdom, though, neither Charles Darwin nor Alfred Russel Wallace wrote about the change of life. George C. Williams was the first evolutionary biologist to mention it in writing, when he published a theoretical paper in February 1957 on the evolutionary basis for aging (4). Just a few paragraphs are dedicated to reproductive decline, which Williams notes is “rather abrupt” in women. Maybe after age 45 or 50, he mused, new pregnancies become too dangerous, and the most successful evolutionary strategy is to help existing kids, rather than having more babies.

Then, in the early 1960s, William Donald “W. D.” Hamilton shook up biology with his theory of inclusive fitness, an idea taught in every introductory evolutionary biology class today. According to the theory, related individuals cooperate to help each other survive and pass more genes into the next generation, ensuring their genetic success (5–8). Inclusive fitness helps explain why evolution sometimes favors cooperation, even self-sacrifice, over one’s own reproduction (9).

Williams and Hamilton developed their ideas during a decade of social upheaval and changing norms in the United States. Before that, “people didn’t talk about menstruation and menopause in polite society,” and “it’s now in the public conversation” more than ever, says anthropologist Kristen Hawkes at the University of Utah.

Hawkes led research in the 1990s formulating the Grandmother Hypothesis, the first widely recognized theory for the evolution of women’s long lifespans after menopause (10). She focused on the Hadza, hunter-gatherers on the southern edge of the Serengeti region in Tanzania. They are highly mobile, living in pop-up homes of grass and branches to follow seasonal access to water. Hawkes lived in the bush alongside the Hadza during the 1980s and early 1990s, recording how the group members spent their time, paying special attention to the women. Each morning, the women and children walked outside of the residential center, tapping the ground with long sticks. They’d listen for the echo of enormous sweet pea tubers (Vigna frutescens) deep underground. When they found a good spot with tubers, they’d begin the day’s work of digging and build a cooking fire. The women cooked and ate the tubers right there, then carried any leftovers back to camp.

Offering cornmeal for their cooperation, Hawkes measured the Hadza people’s heights and weights. She then looked for any relationship between height, weight, and tuber digging—and found an intriguing correlation. While young children did help to dig their own tubers, they were not collecting enough food to cover their own nutritional needs. Initially, their mother’s work made up for the deficit: the number of tubers she dug was correlated with the weight of her weaned children. But when that mother gave birth to a second infant, her weaned children’s weight became dependent on how many tubers their grandmother could collect (11).

Why should grandmothers help feed their grandkids, rather than having their own babies? Inclusive fitness suggests that animals can choose cooperation over breeding if doing so gets more of their genes into the next generation. So maybe middle-aged women were doing the same thing. Modeling studies have since borne this out; helping fertile daughters and nieces have more kids, more quickly, gets more of grandma’s genes into the next generation (12).

The Grandmother Hypothesis therefore posits that, at a certain point in a woman’s life, the inclusive fitness benefits of helping her relatives have more babies, sooner, outweigh the benefits of having another child herself. That tipping point seems to occur around age 45 or 50, Hawkes says.

The Mother-in-Law Problem

The Grandmother Hypothesis has been the go-to explanation for menopause since the 1990s. But recent discoveries in other species haven’t fit that mold, and some evolutionary biologists think there’s more going on.

At Kibale National Park, primatologist Kevin Langergraber co-directs the Ngogo Chimpanzee Project, a long-term field study of about 200 wild chimps, the largest group ever studied. In 2023, he coauthored a study finding that middle-aged females stop reproducing. Hormonal data in their urine showed that they had stopped ovulating. And demographic data tracking births and deaths revealed that the average female at Ngogo spends 20% of her life in a postreproductive state (2).

Marlene was the oldest chimp Langergraber ever knew at Ngogo. She lived into her early 70s and spent most of her time alone in the north of the park, eating mainly fruit. When other chimps came near, Marlene would show up and take meat those chimps had hunted, and “chew on it with her gummy mouth,” Langergraber says. “It’s the exact opposite of the Grandmother Hypothesis. She’s just taking the meat.”

Although Marlene had grandchildren and great-grandchildren, she didn’t help them. Why, then, would female chimps live so long after menopause? Competition could be one answer, Langergraber says. Chimps are promiscuous, freely mating with other members of the troop, so fathers often don’t know who their children or grandchildren are. Consequently, most females leave home at puberty and thereby avoid inbreeding. When they join a new troop, they’re in competition with older, genetically unrelated females. Over the years, those young females have their own babies and become more closely related to the group as a whole. As they age, and their sons breed, these females eventually become grandmas like Marlene—with a strong genetic connection to the group, but with no clear individual relatives to help.

This scenario illustrates a second explanation for the evolution of postmenopausal survival, called the Reproductive Conflict Hypothesis. The idea is that in a group where females become more related to the troop as they age, elder females can benefit by ceding reproductive opportunities to youngsters. The younger females will selfishly compete for more resources (for example, by not sharing food) in order to have more babies. Meanwhile, older females will forego breeding to avoid the costs of conflict (with less food, any babies they have might starve). In principle, they reap inclusive fitness benefits if youngsters breed with the older females’ sons (13).

This theory may also help explain menopause and life after fertility in toothed whales like orcas, says Darren Croft, a professor of animal behavior at the University of Exeter in the United Kingdom, who also directs the Center for Whale Research, in Friday Harbor, Washington. In killer whale society, grandmothers reign. Elder females lead the pod, swimming out front to guide their group toward the best hunting grounds. Calves stay with their family pod for life, so the oldest matriarchs are genetically the most related to everyone in the pod. However, there comes a point when grown mothers and daughters may be in competition to breed (14). Each female needs 40% more salmon to sustain lactation, and new moms often won’t share the fish they catch, Croft says. A matriarch needs to live long to care for her family. But eventually, the competition from younger females may get too strong, and the best evolutionary strategy—to protect her genetic legacy—may switch from breeding to helping her children reproduce, which has significant inclusive fitness benefits (15).

Brave New World

A third possibility for the existence of menopause in gorillas and chimps posits that it could be a new trait that hasn’t been around long enough to be shaped by evolution—that is, a nonadaptive trait. Maybe there is an inevitable point when female apes run out of eggs, but most die off before they reach that point because of predators or disease. The elder apes in Uganda could be rare exceptions that are fortunate enough to live long after their ovaries run out of eggs. But if more females continue to survive past the biological end of reproduction over the next few thousand years, it’s possible that natural selection could favor that trait and extend lifespans even longer past menopause.

Nikolaos Smit, a behavioral ecologist at Finland’s University of Turku, coauthored the 2025 study of mountain gorillas in Uganda (1). He and Robbins found that female gorillas can live 15 years without reproducing at the end of their lives. That’s about a quarter of their lifespan, Smit says. Yet, curiously, gorillas, “don’t fit the two main hypotheses—no grandmother, and no conflict.”

Neither hypothesis fits gorillas because both males and females can disperse to new groups several times through life. About 85% of babies are sired by a group’s alpha male, so females leave to avoid inbreeding, and lower-ranking males disperse for a chance to become alpha elsewhere. Genetically speaking, females don’t necessarily become more closely related to their group through time; they don’t live with their daughters or sons past puberty. Hence, gorillas neither fit the competition hypothesis nor the Grandmother Hypothesis.

One remaining possibility is the nonadaptive scenario: that evolution would not have selected for menopause, back when gorillas all died before they stopped ovulating. It’s a real possibility in these protected gorilla populations, Smit says. “Some gorillas or chimps experience these favorable conditions, and this allows them to live longer,” he notes. Historically, gorillas would have died by the age of 40 or so, before reaching menopause, so evolution would never have had the opportunity to select for that trait. So, if females continue to survive past the biological end of reproduction over the next few thousand years, natural selection could, in principle, favor that trait and extend lifespans even longer past menopause.

Anecdotally, it’s not unusual for females in lots of species to live beyond their reproductive years in any population with artificially low death rates. “We see it in zoos and captive settings,” Croft notes, “or lab populations of rats or mice that live into their postreproductive years, because they’re not at risk of starvation or predation.”

A Tangled Web

All that is to say, what’s happened in humans and toothed whales—species where most females live past reproductive age—may have a different explanation than what’s going on in other apes. Multiple hypotheses could even work together to explain menopause, possibly even within the same species.

Short-finned pilot whales, false killer whales (a species of dolphin), killer whales, narwhals, and belugas all experience menopause. Called Odontocetes, they’re a particularly interesting bunch that show evidence for both the grandmother and competitive hypotheses, even in the same species. In his work studying southern resident killer whales around Seattle, Croft has often seen grandmother orcas out on the water. One that he remembers particularly well, named J2, stopped breeding in 1972. She lived another 46 years. “We often saw her out in front, sharing fish and leading her family around,” Croft says. Other females in the pod could at times be in competition with their daughters for salmon, their main prey (15).

In 2024, Croft coauthored a study comparing theories of why menopause evolved in toothed whales (16). First, he used life history data to estimate total female lifespans in 18 whale species (5 of which undergo menopause). He then modeled the reproductive lifespan of each species using postmortem necropsy data from strandings, which include assessments of ovarian health at the time of death. Croft found that species with menopause live 40-plus years longer than closely related species of similar size that don’t have menopause.

But whale species who experience menopause don’t seem to stop breeding any earlier in life than other whales; they just live way longer. That’s a key point. Croft found that females of nearly all 18 whale species stopped breeding when their daughters reached maturity, regardless of whether the species experienced menopause.

This suggests that moms compete with their daughters to breed, but then abandon that competition once the younger whales start to win out. After that, there is no point in staying fertile long-term.

Why, then, would some whale species live for many years beyond menopause? In the same study, Croft found that whales with menopause usually survived long enough to see one grandchild reach maturity, whereas species without menopause did not. That points to intergenerational assistance as a driver for longer life, supporting the Grandmother Hypothesis.

So, it could be that both the Grandmother Hypothesis and the Reproductive Conflict Hypothesis play a role in menopause in Odontocetes. Reproductive conflict explains why fertility ends, while inclusive fitness explains the extended lifespan. Multiple drivers could play a role in other animals, too. Rather than menopause having a single evolutionary cause, it’s “multifactorial, as everything in evolution,” Smit says. “There’s not one force.”

In principle, researchers could glean further clues about female mountain gorillas by testing hormones in the apes’ blood, urine, or feces. While the demographic data strongly suggest that the Ugandan population undergoes menopause, a drop in hormone levels at late-middle age would offer proof. However, getting samples is a challenge, with only about 1,000 critically endangered individuals left in the wild. Robbins collects urine ad hoc when she can, which ends up being about twice a month. “You see them pee,” she says with a laugh. The plants are bushy, “so we use a fine-tip pipette, and you can pipette it off the [dry] ground or vegetation.” But to analyze a female’s full ovulatory cycle would require sampling every other day for at least two months, “and that’s extremely difficult to do,” she says.

Robbins did lead an initial study with mountain gorillas in Rwanda in 2016, assessing hormone data from urine and feces for just one 38-year-old female who had bred when she was younger (3). Progesterone and estradiol never peaked over several months of tracking. “It was clear she wasn’t ovulating,” Robbins says. That’s indicative of menopause, but data from just one individual weren’t enough to draw any definitive conclusions.

Robbins adds that menopause could even have multiple independent evolutionary origins at different time points over millions of years. “We come up with nice evolutionary explanations for things, but also nature can be a bit messy,” she says. “There’s no reason this couldn’t have evolved several times.”
