Transmission resonances in scattering by δ ′ \delta^{\prime} -like combs
Yuriy Golovaty, Rostyslav Hryniv ∗, Stanislav Lavrynenko

Abstract
. We introduce a new exactly solvable model in quantum mechanics that describes the propagation of particles through a potential field created by regularly spaced δ ′ \delta^{\prime} -type point interactions, which model the localized dipoles often observed in crystal structures. We refer to the corresponding potentials as δ θ ′ \delta^{\prime}_{\theta} -combs, where the parameter θ \theta represents the contrast of the resonant wave at zero energy and determines the interface conditions in the Hamiltonians. We explicitly calculate the scattering matrix for these systems and prove that the transmission probability exhibits sharp resonance peaks while rapidly decaying at other frequencies. Consequently, Hamiltonians with δ θ ′ \delta^{\prime}_{\theta} -comb potentials act as quantum filters, permitting tunnelling only for specific wave frequencies. Furthermore, for each θ > 0 \theta>0 , we construct a family of regularized Hamiltonians approximating the ideal model and prove that their transmission probabilities have a similar structure, thereby confirming the physical realizability of the band-pass filtering effect.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study quantum tunnelling effects in Hamiltonians with potentials that we call
\delta_{\theta}^{\prime}
-combs, and the principal results are illustrated in Fig.
1
. These potentials serve as exactly solvable models for electron transport in crystal structures and consist of multiple equally spaced, scale-invariant point interactions naturally associated with
\delta^{\prime}
-distributions, i.e., the distributional derivatives of Dirac’s delta function. Fig.
1
illustrates the dependence of the transmission probability
T
of a quantum particle on its wave frequency
k
; notably,
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-combs produce sharp quantum filters permitting particle transmission only within specific narrow energy bands while suppressing tunnelling at all other energies.

Figure 1.
Peaks in the probability of tunneling through the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb.

The scale-invariant point interactions in the constructed
\delta_{\theta}^{\prime}
-combs effectively model non-vanishing dipole moments caused by the asymmetric distribution of positive and negative charges in certain crystal structures. Such dipole moments lead to important physical properties, including spontaneous polarization, piezoelectricity, pyroelectricity, and ferroelectricity. For instance, Fig.
2
illustrates the crystallization of zinc and oxygen into a wurtzite lattice
[
1
]
, typical of binary compounds such as ZnO. The structure consists of two interpenetrating hexagonal close-packed sublattices—one for each type of atom (Zn and O)—and the relative displacements of these sublattices induce local electric dipole moments.

Particle transport along the
x
-axis in such media involves scattering from a sequence of these dipole-induced potentials, which can be effectively modeled by
\delta^{\prime}
-like
point interactions. Moreover, under certain conditions, hexagonal graphite-like layers can be extracted from such crystals, and these may then roll into cylindrical nanotubes
[
1
]
. The transport of particles through such nanotubes is effectively one-dimensional, and the influence of local asymmetry-caused dipoles can be approximated by a finite array of equally spaced
\delta^{\prime}
-like fields. This motivates the study of the scattering problem for periodic
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-combs, which serve as an analytically tractable model for electron transport in such nanostructures.

Figure 2.
Crystal structure of zinc oxide ZnO with non-vanishing local dipole moments.

To further explain the motivation and set up the notation, we start with the case of a single
\delta_{\theta}^{\prime}
-potential. We denote by
S_{\theta}
the free Schrödinger operator with point interaction at
x=a
acting on the functions in
W_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a\})
subject to the interface conditions

\psi(a+0)=\theta\psi(a-0),\quad\psi^{\prime}(a+0)=\theta^{-1}\psi^{\prime}(a-0)

(1.1)

for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. As we explain below,
S_{\theta}
can be obtained as the norm resolvent limit of the regular Schrödinger operators with scaled
\delta^{\prime}
-type resonant potentials, and the parameter
\theta
is determined by the corresponding half-bound state. For that reason, we call the operator
S_{\theta}
the
Schrödinger operator with
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
potential
and denote it

S_{\theta}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}(x-a).

(1.2)

From the point of view of quantum mechanics, the
\delta^{\prime}
-type potentials can be thought of as an idealized model for localized dipoles, i.e., narrow sharp potentials combining a high potential barrier followed by a deep potential well as in Fig.
2
(c).
A Hamiltonian with such an idealized dipole can be modelled as the limit of the Schrödinger operators

H_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-2}V(\varepsilon^{-1}(x-a))

(1.3)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
V
is an arbitrary short-range potential. Indeed, if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\,dx=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}xV(x)\,dx=-1
, then the potentials
\varepsilon^{-2}V(\varepsilon^{-1}(x-a))
converge to
\delta^{\prime}(x-a)
in the sense of distributions as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
.

The arguments of
[
2
]
demonstrated that the operators
H_{\varepsilon}
should converge to the direct sum
H_{\mathrm{D}}
of two free half-line Schrödinger operators subject to the Dirichlet condition at
x=a
. From the quantum particle scattering perspective, that result would mean that the resulting
\delta^{\prime}
-type potentials should split the quantum system into two non-interacting parts, i.e., that a localized dipole should be impenetrable to quantum particles. However, a later study
[
3
]
suggested an example contradicting that property and described resonances for the transmission probability through a piecewise constant
\delta^{\prime}
-like potential. More generally, it was proved in
[
4
,
5
]
that for an arbitrary real-valued potential
V
of the Marchenko class
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},1+|x|)
the operators
H_{\varepsilon}
converge in the norm resolvent topology as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and that the limit is
H_{\mathrm{D}}
if
V
is non-resonant and
S_{\theta}
if
V
is resonant.

Here, we call a potential
V

resonant
, or having a
zero-energy resonance
if the equation
-u^{\prime\prime}+Vu=0
has a solution
(half-bound state)

u
that is bounded on the whole line. This half-bound state has then non-zero limits
u(\pm\infty)
at both infinities, and the parameter
\theta
represents the contrast of
u
and is equal to the ratio of these limits,

\theta=\frac{u(+\infty)}{u(-\infty)}.

(1.4)

This fact justifies the identification of
S_{\theta}
as the Schrödinger operator with potential
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}(x-a)
, where the subscript
\theta
refers to the interface conditions (
1.1
).
We should stress, however, that there is no explicit dependence of
\theta
on the shape
V
in the family of potentials
V_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-2}V(\varepsilon^{-1}(x-a))
: the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}
may converge to some
S_{\theta}
even if
V_{\varepsilon}
have no limit in the distributional sense, and two resonant families of

converging to the same distribution
\beta\delta^{\prime}
may correspond to different limiting Hamiltonians
S_{\theta}
.

In
[
6
]
, the authors studied the scattering properties of the family
H_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. It was proved therein that the transmission probability
T_{\varepsilon}(k)
of
H_{\varepsilon}
vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
if
V
is non-resonant (in agreement with
[
2
]
); however, if
V
has a zero-energy resonance with a half-bound state

, then this probability converges to the non-zero value

T(k)=\frac{4\theta^{2}}{(\theta^{2}+1)^{2}}

(1.5)

for all energies
E=k^{2}
, with
\theta
given by (
1.4
).
It is worth noting that resonant potentials are not uncommon. For example, for any integrable function
V
that vanishes sufficiently quickly at infinity, there is a countable set of constants

such that the potentials
c_{n}V
have a zero-energy resonance (see
[
6
]
for details).

The main results of this paper reveal striking properties of the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
for the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}(x-jh),

whose potential is a
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb consisting of

point interactions
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
placed at
a_{j}=jh
,
j=0,1,\dots,n-1
.
In Section
4
, we demonstrate that this Hamiltonian defines a new exactly solvable quantum mechanical model, in the sense that its scattering coefficients and transmission probability can be expressed explicitly as analytic functions of the interface parameter
\theta
and the wave frequency
k
. It turns out that this model exhibits strong band-pass filtering and tunnelling effects through potentials with structured point interactions.
Namely, for each
\theta
not equal to
0
or

, we prove that
T_{n}
is
\pi
-periodic with respect to

and possesses exactly

, at which
T_{n}=1
. These resonances are explicitly determined in terms of

and
\theta
and are shown to converge to the midpoint

as
\theta\to 0
. Moreover, for values of

not equal to
\pi/2
, the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
decays as
O(\theta^{2n})
, demonstrating that the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb functions as a highly efficient band-pass frequency filter, cf. Fig.
1

To the best of our knowledge, no exactly solvable quantum mechanical models have been reported that exhibit the same combination of transmission tunnelling and filtering properties as the one proposed here. The classic Kronig–Penney model
[
7
]
, introduced in 1931 to describe electron transport in periodic crystals, employed a periodic array of local potentials, which converge to a Dirac
\delta
function in the limit. This was seemingly among the first non-trivial exactly solvable models of quantum mechanics. A rigorous mathematical treatment of such models, including an extensive review of related results up to the early 2000s, can be found in
[
8
,
9
]
. These works systematically analyze Schrödinger operators with point interactions, particularly of
\delta
- and
\delta^{\prime}
-type. It is important to note that the
\delta^{\prime}
-interactions studied in these references differ from the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-potentials considered in this paper. Notably, the work
[
10
]
offers a thorough analysis of spectral singularities occurring in the general four-parameter family of complex point interactions, with particular attention to
\mathcal{P}
-,
\mathcal{T}
-, and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric cases, as well as the coalescence of singularities and bound states.

For models involving multiple regularly spaced
\delta
-potentials—often referred to as Dirac
\delta
-combs—the transmission probability was explicitly derived as early as 1974 in
[
11
]
, and many similar results have been reported since (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
13
,
15
,
12
,
16
]
). A systematic treatment of wave propagation in structured periodic media, where a localized potential is repeated
n
times, is presented in
[
17
]
. That work derives fundamental relations for scattering coefficients, and, in particular, discusses the case of electromagnetic scattering by
\delta
-comb potentials and demonstrates the characteristic behaviour of the transmission probability in the Kronig–Penney limiting case
[
7
]
as
n\to\infty
.

Recently, in
[
18
]
, the authors investigated the periodic behaviour of transmission probability for a square quantum well. However, this periodicity was not in terms of energy
E=k^{2}
or wave frequency
k
, but rather with respect to the thickness of the quantum well. For a fixed energy
E=k_{0}^{2}
, the potential shape
V
in (
1.3
) was designed so that, for all small
\varepsilon
, the transmission probability
T_{\varepsilon}(k)
has a peak at
k=k_{0}
. The authors further proposed placing this quantum well outside the background potential to function as a wave filter at a specific frequency
k_{0}
.

In contrast, the model introduced in this paper provides a broader and more adaptable filtering mechanism, acting as a band-pass filter over a range of frequencies rather than a single resonance peak. This distinction makes our model significantly more versatile for applications where selective transmission over multiple energy bands is required. Moreover, as demonstrated in Section
5
, Hamiltonians (
2.1
) with appropriately regularized
\delta^{\prime}
-type locally periodic potentials, which approximate
\mathcal{H}
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, also exhibit similar transmission resonances and filtering properties.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce Hamiltonians with
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb potentials and discuss the associated scattering solutions and scattering coefficients. Section

. In Section

for small values of

(

and

for arbitrary

\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb potentials. In Section

\theta>0
, a resonant potential

with this parameter

for the half-bound state, and then prove that the transmission probabilities for the scaled Hamiltonian

in (

2.
Setting of the problem

Let us consider the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}V\left(\frac{x-jh}{\varepsilon}\right),

(2.1)

where
V
is an integrable function of compact support,
h>0
is the spacing between interaction centers, and
\varepsilon>0
is a small scaling parameter. Such a Hamiltonian describes the interaction of particles with localized potentials concentrated near the points of the set
X_{h}=\{jh\}_{j=0}^{n-1}
; for small
\varepsilon
, these potentials mimick
\delta^{\prime}
-type dipole behaviour.

Assume that
V
exhibits a zero-energy resonance (in the sense explained in the Introduction), with associated parameter
\theta
defined via (
1.4
). The results of
[
4
]
imply that, as

\varepsilon\to 0
, the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
converge in the norm resolvent sense to the limiting operator formally expressed as

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}(x-jh).

(2.2)

Here, each point interaction
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}(x-jh)
imposes an interface condition of the form (
1.1
) at
a=jh
.
More precisely, the limiting operator
\mathcal{H}
acts as

\mathcal{H}\psi=-\psi^{\prime\prime}

(2.3)

on the domain

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{H}=\big\{\psi\in W_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus X_{h})\colon

\displaystyle\psi(x+0)=\theta\psi(x-0),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(x+0)=\theta^{-1}\psi^{\prime}(x-0)\text{ for all }x\in X_{h}\big\}.

We refer to (
2.2
) as the Hamiltonian with a
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-
comb potential
. The main aim of this paper is to analyze the scattering properties of such Hamiltonians and to demonstrate the unusual behaviour of their transmission probability functions.

For
k\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider a left scattering solution
\psi_{-}
of
\mathcal{H}\psi=k^{2}\psi
such that

\psi_{-}(x)=\begin{cases}e^{ikx}+r_{-}(k)e^{-ikx}\quad&\text{for }x<0,\\
t_{-}(k)e^{ikx}\quad&\text{for }x>(n-1)h.\end{cases}

The coefficients
r_{-}(k)
and
t_{-}(k)
are called the
left reflection
and
transmission amplitudes
respectively and are unknown here. The solution
\psi_{-}
describes the scattering of an incoming flux of particles moving left to right. If the particles are incident from the right, the scattering solution is then

\psi_{+}(x)=\begin{cases}t_{+}(k)e^{ikx}\quad&\text{for }x<0,\\
e^{ikx}+r_{+}(k)e^{-ikx}\quad&\text{for }x>(n-1)h\end{cases}

with the
right reflection
coefficient
r_{+}(k)
and
right transmission
coefficient
t_{+}(k)
. It is known that
t_{-}(k)=t_{+}(k)
,
|r_{-}(k)|=|r_{+}(k)|
, and
|t_{\pm}(k)|^{2}+|r_{\pm}(k)|^{2}=1
for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Our main goal is to study the
transmission probability

T(k)=|t_{-}(k)|^{2}
.

Note that it is sufficient to study this model only for the case when the point interactions in the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb are arranged with the step size
h=1
. In fact, changing the variable
x
to
h^{-1}x
preserves the scale-invariant
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
interface conditions (
1.1
) but positions them at points of the set
X_{1}
; simultaneously, the energy
k^{2}
scales to
h^{-2}k^{2}
, and the Schrödinger equation becomes
\mathcal{H}\psi=h^{-2}k^{2}\psi
. As a result, scaling the coordinates leads to scaling the energy but does not change the model itself.

Therefore, for given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, we consider the problem

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime\prime}+k^{2}\psi=0,\qquad k\in\mathbb{R},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0,\dots,n-1\},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\psi(j+0)=\theta\psi(j-0),\quad\psi^{\prime}(j+0)=\theta^{-1}\psi^{\prime}(j-0),\quad j=0,\dots,n-1,

(2.6)

corresponding to (
2.2
) with
h=1
, and we look for the left scattering solution

\psi_{-}(x)=\begin{cases}e^{ikx}+r_{n}(k)e^{-ikx}\quad&\text{for }x<0,\\
t_{n}(k)e^{ikx}\quad&\text{for }x>(n-1)h.\end{cases}

(2.7)

We simplify the notations for the left reflection and transmission coefficients by omitting the minus sign in the subscript but instead explicitly indicate the number
n
of the interactions in the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb. Our aim is to study the dependence of the transmission probability

T_{n}(\theta,k)=|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{2}

on
\theta
,
k
, and
n
.
Note that the probability
T_{1}(\theta,k)
is given by (
1.5
) and is independent of
k
; also, the point interactions (
2.6
) are trivial for
\theta=1
, so
T_{n}(1,k)=1
for all
k
.

3.
Properties of the transmission probability

Quantum mechanical tunnelling through the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb is typically analyzed using the transfer matrix method. A comprehensive and rigorous treatment of transfer matrices, including the derivation of their main properties such as the composition rule, can be found in the review
[
19
]
. In our setting, all computations are relatively straightforward and explicit, so we present the construction in full detail for completeness.

Each solution of the equation
\psi^{\prime\prime}+k^{2}\psi=0
on the set
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a\}
can be written as

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha_{1}e^{ikx}+\alpha_{2}e^{-ikx}&\text{for }x<a,\\
\beta_{1}e^{ikx}+\beta_{2}e^{-ikx}&\text{for }x>a,\end{cases}

where
\vec{\alpha}=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{\top}
and
\vec{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]^{\top}
are complex vectors.
If
\psi
satisfies the interface conditions (
1.1
), then
\vec{\alpha}
and
\vec{\beta}
are related by the linear transformation
\vec{\beta}=M(\theta,ak)\vec{\alpha}
,
where

M(\theta,z)=\dfrac{1}{2\theta}\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{2}+1&(\theta^{2}-1)e^{-2iz}\\
(\theta^{2}-1)e^{2iz}&\theta^{2}+1\end{bmatrix}.

(3.1)

The wave components of the left scattering solution (
2.7
) to the left and right of the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb are similarly related by a matrix
M_{n}(\theta,k)
called the
transfer matrix
for problem (
2.5
)–(
2.7
); namely,

\begin{bmatrix}t_{n}(\theta,k)\\
0\end{bmatrix}=M_{n}(\theta,k)\begin{bmatrix}1\\
r_{n}(\theta,k)\end{bmatrix},

(3.2)

where

M_{n}(\theta,k)=M(\theta,(n-1)k)\cdots M(\theta,2k)\,M(\theta,k)\,M(\theta,0).

(3.3)

The transfer matrix belongs to the special group
SU(1,1)
consisting of all complex matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}&\bar{z}_{2}\\
z_{2}&\bar{z}_{1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad|z_{1}|^{2}-|z_{2}|^{2}=1.

This property together with (
3.2
) allows us to express the transfer matrix in terms of the scattering amplitudes:

M_{n}(\theta,k)=\begin{bmatrix}1/\overline{t_{n}}(\theta,k)&-\overline{r_{n}}(\theta,k)/\overline{t_{n}}(\theta,k)\\
-r_{n}(\theta,k)/t_{n}(\theta,k)&1/t_{n}(\theta,k)\end{bmatrix}.

(3.4)

Thus, to calculate the transmission probability, it suffices to compute the transfer matrix (
3.4
). Observe that the fact that
M_{n}(\theta,k)\in SU(1,1)
yields the known relation

|r_{n}(\theta,k)|^{2}+|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{2}=1;

(3.5)

also,
t_{n}(\theta,k)
vanishes for no
k\in\mathbb{R}
as (
3.2
) would mean that
M_{n}(\theta,k)
is singular.

The following theorem summarizes the basic properties of the transmission probability that can be obtained from the properties of the transfer matrix without explicitly calculating it.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
\theta
is different from
0
and
1
.
The transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
has the following properties:

(i)

T_{n}(\theta,k)
is a
\pi
-periodic function of
k
;

(ii)

T_{n}(\theta,k)=T_{n}(\theta,\pi-k)
for all
k\in(0,\pi)
, i.e., the graph of
T_{n}(\theta,\,\cdot\,)
on the interval
(0,\pi)
exhibits symmetry with respect to the line
k=\frac{\pi}{2}
.

(iii)

T_{n}(\theta,k)
is invariant under the transformations
\theta\mapsto-\theta
and
\theta\mapsto\theta^{-1}
, i.e.,

T_{n}(\theta,k)=T_{n}(-\theta,k)=T_{n}(\theta^{-1},k)

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

(i)

By construction, the entries of
M_{n}(\theta,k)
are trigonometric polynomials of
k
. In particular, for the transmission amplitude we find by induction that

\frac{1}{t_{n}(\theta,k)}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}c_{m,n}(\theta)e^{2imk},\quad k\in\mathbb{R},

demonstrating
\pi
-periodicity of
T_{n}(\theta,k)
in
k
.

(ii)
We have
M(\theta,j(\pi-\varkappa))=\overline{M(\theta,j\varkappa)}
for integer
j
, and thus
M_{n}(\theta,\pi-k)=\overline{M_{n}(\theta,k)}
due to (
3.3
). The explicit formula (
3.4
) implies that
t_{n}(\theta,\pi-k)=\overline{t_{n}(\theta,k)}
, so that
T_{n}(\theta,\pi-k)=T_{n}(\theta,k)
.

(iii)
We also have
M(-\theta,\varkappa)=-M(\theta,\varkappa)
and thus
M_{n}(-\theta,k)=(-1)^{n}M_{n}(\theta,k)
.
Again, using representation (
3.4
), we have
t_{n}(-\theta,k)=(-1)^{n}t_{n}(\theta,k)
, and therefore
T_{n}(-\theta,k)=T_{n}(\theta,k)
.

Replacing
\theta
by
\theta^{-1}
in point interactions (
2.6
) gives

\psi(j-0)=\theta\psi(j+0),\quad\psi^{\prime}(j-0)=\theta^{-1}\psi^{\prime}(j+0),

so the transformation
\theta\mapsto\theta^{-1}
is equivalent to a change of direction on the real axis.
This assertion is further supported by the obvious equalities

M(\theta^{-1},\varkappa)=M^{-1}(\theta,\varkappa),\qquad M_{n}(\theta^{-1},k)=M_{n}^{-1}(\theta,k).

Consequently,
T_{n}(\theta,k)=T_{n}(\theta^{-1},k)
is a statement that the transmission probability is independent of the incidence direction.

The following theorem states that the probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
tends to zero, as
\theta\to 0
, almost everywhere.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
and
\theta\in(0,1)
. Then, for any
k\in(0,\pi]
other than
\frac{\pi}{2}
, the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
tends to zero as
\theta\to 0
.

Proof.

Since

2\theta M(\theta,\varkappa)=\begin{bmatrix}1&-e^{-2i\varkappa}\\
-e^{2i\varkappa}&1\end{bmatrix}+\theta^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{-2i\varkappa}\\
e^{2i\varkappa}&1\end{bmatrix},

we have the formula

2^{n}\theta^{n}M_{n}(\theta,k)=A_{n}(k)+\theta^{2}B_{n}(\theta,k),

(3.6)

where

A_{n}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}1&\kern-4.0pt-e^{-2i(n-1)k}\\
-e^{2i(n-1)k}&\kern-4.0pt1\end{bmatrix}\cdots\begin{bmatrix}1&\kern-4.0pt-e^{-4ik}\\
-e^{4ik}&\kern-4.0pt1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&\kern-4.0pt-e^{-2ik}\\
-e^{2ik}&\kern-4.0pt1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&\kern-4.0pt-1\\
-1&\kern-4.0pt1\end{bmatrix}.

In addition, the norm of the matrix
B_{n}(\theta,k)
is uniformly bounded on
(0,1)\times(0,\pi]
.
Arguing by induction, one proves that

A_{n}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}(e^{-2ik}+1)^{n-1}&-(e^{-2ik}+1)^{n-1}\\
-(e^{2ik}+1)^{n-1}&\;(e^{2ik}+1)^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Using (
3.6
) along with matrix representation (
3.4
), we estimate

\Big|2^{n}\theta^{n}|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{-1}-|e^{2ik}+1|^{n-1}\Big|\leq c_{1}\theta^{2},

where
c_{1}
depends only on
n
. Since
|e^{2ik}+1|=2|\cos k|
, this inequality can be rewritten as

\Big|2\theta^{n}|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{-1}-|\cos k|^{n-1}\Big|\leq c_{2}\theta^{2}.

If
k\neq\frac{\pi}{2}
, then the ratio
\theta^{n}/|t_{n}(\theta,k)|

has the strictly positive limit as
\theta\to 0
, and therefore
|t_{n}(\theta,k)|
tends to zero.
∎

The convergence of
T_{n}(\theta,k)
described in the theorem is not uniform with respect to
k
. In fact, for every
\theta>0
, there are
n-1
frequencies
k
in the range
(0,\pi)
at which the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
is equal to
1
, as we demonstrate in the next section.

4.
Transmission resonances in scattering through
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb

The values
k
at which the function
T_{n}(\theta,\,\cdot\,)
reaches its local maxima are called
transmission resonances
. The set of all transmission resonances on
(0,\pi)
is denoted
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\theta}
. Based on the results of computer simulations, we hypothesized that the scattering process through the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb with
n
point interactions demonstrates
n-1
resonant values, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\theta}=\big\{k_{1}(\theta),k_{2}(\theta),\dots,k_{n-1}(\theta)\big\},

and that the probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
at these points is
1
. Below, we demonstrate this for
n=2,3,4
, and then derive the explicit formula for
T_{n}
allowing a thorough description of the transmission through the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb.

In view of Theorem
1
, the set
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\theta}
is symmetric with respect to the point
k=\frac{\pi}{2}
and shrinks to this point as
\theta
tends to zero:
k_{n-j}(\theta)=\pi-k_{j}(\theta)
and
k_{j}(\theta)\to\frac{\pi}{2}
, as
\theta\to 0
for all
j=1,\dots,n-1
. For even
n
, the set
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\theta}
contains an odd number of points, and therefore, one of them must coincide with point
\frac{\pi}{2}
, namely
k_{n/2}(\theta)=\frac{\pi}{2}
. Furthermore, we observe that

M\left(\theta,\tfrac{\pi m}{2}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2\theta}\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{2}+1&(-1)^{m}(\theta^{2}-1)\\
(-1)^{m}(\theta^{2}-1)&\theta^{2}+1\end{bmatrix}

for every integer
m
, and
M\big(\theta,\tfrac{\pi(m+1)}{2}\big)M\big(\theta,\tfrac{\pi m}{2}\big)=E
,
where
E
is the identity matrix. Hence,
M_{n}(\theta,\tfrac{\pi}{2})=E
because product (
3.3
) has an even number of factors, and therefore
T_{n}(\theta,\frac{\pi}{2})=1
. However, this is the only case where the probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
has a “stationary” resonance that does not change with
\theta
; other resonances are moving as
\theta\to 0
.

4.1.
Transmission probabilities and resonance sets for a small number of point interactions

For ease of calculations, we introduce the notation
\theta=e^{\varkappa}
; then

\cosh\varkappa=\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta},\qquad\sinh\varkappa=\tfrac{\theta^{2}-1}{2\theta},

and the matrix (
3.1
) can be written as

M(\varkappa,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\varkappa&\sinh\varkappa\>e^{-2iz}\\
\sinh\varkappa\>e^{2iz}&\cosh\varkappa\end{bmatrix}.

The potential with two
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-centers is the simplest case: then
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\theta}=\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}
and
T_{2}(\theta,\frac{\pi}{2})=1
, as we explained above. The elements of
M_{2}(\theta,k)
are of the form

\frac{1}{t_{2}(\theta,k)}=e^{2ik}\sinh^{2}\varkappa+\cosh^{2}\varkappa,\qquad\frac{r_{2}(\theta,k)}{t_{2}(\theta,k)}=-\sinh\varkappa\cosh\varkappa\;(e^{2ik}+1).

(4.1)

The fact that the transmission probability

T_{2}(\theta,k)=\frac{1}{4\sinh^{2}\varkappa\cosh^{2}\varkappa\cos^{2}k+1}=\frac{4\theta^{4}}{(1-\theta^{4})^{2}\cos^{2}k+4\theta^{4}}

has no other resonances follows from a simple analysis of this function (see Fig.

).

For a Hamiltonian with three point interactions, the resonance set

,
k_{2}(\theta)
that are symmetric with respect to
\frac{\pi}{2}
. Direct calculation of the transfer matrix
M_{3}(\theta,k)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{3}(\theta,k)}

\displaystyle=\sinh^{2}\varkappa\cosh\varkappa\;(e^{2ik}+1)^{2}+\cosh\varkappa,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{r_{3}(\theta,k)}{t_{3}(\theta,k)}

\displaystyle=-\cosh^{2}\varkappa\sinh\varkappa\;(e^{2ik}+1)^{2}+e^{2ik}\sinh\varkappa.

Then the transmission probability

T_{3}(\theta,k)=\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\varkappa\big(16\sinh^{2}\varkappa\cosh^{2}\varkappa\cos^{4}k-8\sinh^{2}\varkappa\cos^{2}k+1\big)}\\

, this equation can be written as

4\cosh^{2}\varkappa\cos^{2}k=1.

Thus,
\cos k_{1}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^{2}}
,
\cos k_{2}(\theta)=-\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^{2}}
, and the resonances are expressed as

k_{1}(\theta)=\arccos\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^{2}},\qquad k_{2}(\theta)=\pi-\arccos\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^{2}}.

They can also be calculated approximately

k_{1}(\theta)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta+\tfrac{5}{6}\,\theta^{3}+O(\theta^{5}),\qquad k_{2}(\theta)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\theta-\tfrac{5}{6}\,\theta^{3}+O(\theta^{5}).

4.2.
Transmission probability and resonance set in general case

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are explicit formulas for the scattering amplitudes
t_{n}(\theta,k)
and
r_{n}(\theta,k)
and thus for the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
; in this sense, the point interaction Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
of (
2.2
) gives a new exactly solvable quantum mechanical model.

Theorem 3
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
k\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{t_{n}(\theta,k)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1+\theta^{2}}{2\theta}\,U_{n-1}\left(\tfrac{1+\theta^{2}}{2\theta}\cos k\right)\,e^{i(n-1)k}-U_{n-2}\left(\tfrac{1+\theta^{2}}{2\theta}\cos k\right)e^{ink},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\tfrac{r_{n}(\theta,k)}{t_{n}(\theta,k)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1-\theta^{2}}{2\theta}\,U_{n-1}\left(\tfrac{1+\theta^{2}}{2\theta}\cos k\right)\,e^{i(n-1)k},

where
U_{n}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of second kind. The transmission probability equals

T_{n}(\theta,k)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}{4\theta^{2}}\>U_{n-1}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1+\theta^{2}}{2\theta}\cos k\right)}.

(4.4)

Chebyshev polynomials are natural in the context of scattering on finite periodic potential arrays, cf.
[
11
,
17
]
.
Recall
[
20
]
that the Chebyshev polynomials
U_{n}
of second kind are defined by the relation

\sin\phi\>U_{n}(\cos\phi)=\sin(n+1)\phi.

(4.5)

For
n=0,1,2,3
, these polynomials are explicitly given by

U_{0}(\alpha)=1,\quad U_{1}(\alpha)=2\alpha,\quad U_{2}(\alpha)=4\alpha^{2}-1,\quad U_{3}(\alpha)=8\alpha^{3}-4\alpha,

and for all
n\geq 2
, they satisfy the recurrence relation

U_{n}(\alpha)=2\alpha U_{n-1}(\alpha)-U_{n-2}(\alpha).

(4.6)

For ease of notation, we will write
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
instead of the entries
1/t_{n}
and
r_{n}/t_{n}
of the transfer matrix
M_{n}(\theta,k)
, respectively. For
n=2
and
n=3
, these entries are calculated in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). Setting
\theta=e^{\varkappa}
and
\alpha=\cosh\varkappa\cos k
, and using the relations

\sinh^{2}\varkappa=\cosh^{2}\varkappa-1,\quad 2e^{ik}\cos k=1+e^{2ik},\quad 2e^{2ik}\cos k=e^{ik}+e^{3ik},

we represent the corresponding
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle a_{2}=2\alpha\>e^{ik}\cosh\varkappa-e^{2ik},

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\>e^{ik}\sinh\varkappa,

\displaystyle a_{3}=(4\alpha^{2}-1)e^{2ik}\cosh\varkappa-2\alpha e^{3ik},\qquad

\displaystyle b_{3}

\displaystyle=-(4\alpha^{2}-1)e^{2ik}\sinh\varkappa.

Proof.

The proof of (
4.3
) is by induction. The above relations show that the statements hold for
n=2
and
n=3
. Assuming that the formulas hold for the entries
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
of the transfer matrix
M_{n}(\theta,k)
, we then find the entries of
M_{n+1}(\theta,k)
from the relation
M_{n+1}(\theta,k)=M(\theta,nk)M_{n}(\theta,k)
, i.e.

\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\bar{a}_{n+1}&-\bar{b}_{n+1}\\
-b_{n+1}&\phantom{-}a_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\varkappa&\sinh\varkappa\>e^{-2ink}\\
\sinh\varkappa\>e^{2ink}&\cosh\varkappa\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\bar{a}_{n}&-\bar{b}_{n}\\
-b_{n}&\phantom{-}a_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Replacing
\sinh^{2}\alpha
with
\cosh^{2}\alpha-1
and
e^{i(n-1)k}+e^{i(n+1)k}
with
2e^{ink}\cos k
, we get

{a}_{n+1}=\cosh\varkappa\,{a}_{n}-\sinh\varkappa\>e^{2nik}\,\bar{b}_{n}\\
=\cosh^{2}\varkappa\,U_{n-1}(\alpha)\,e^{i(n-1)k}-\cosh\varkappa\,U_{n-2}(\alpha)e^{ink}+\sinh^{2}\varkappa\,U_{n-1}(\alpha)\,e^{i(n+1)k}\\
=\big(2\alpha U_{n-1}(\alpha)-U_{n-2}(\alpha)\big)\>e^{ink}\cosh\varkappa-U_{n-1}(\alpha)\,e^{i(n+1)k}\\
=U_{n}(\alpha)\>e^{ink}\cosh\varkappa-U_{n-1}(\alpha)\,e^{i(n+1)k},

and, similarly,

-b_{n+1}=\sinh\varkappa\>e^{2nik}\,\bar{a}_{n}-\cosh\varkappa\,{b}_{n}=\sinh\varkappa\cosh\varkappa\,U_{n-1}(\alpha)\,e^{i(n+1)k}\\
-\sinh\varkappa\,U_{n-2}(\alpha)\,e^{ink}+\sinh\varkappa\cosh\varkappa\,U_{n-1}(\alpha)\,e^{i(n-1)k}\\
=\sinh\varkappa\>(2\alpha U_{n-1}(\alpha)-U_{n-2}(\alpha))\>e^{ink}=\sinh\varkappa\>U_{n}(\alpha)\>e^{ink}

due to the recurrent relation (
4.6
) satisfied by
U_{n}
, thus proving (
4.3
).

Since
|a-be^{ik}|^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos k
for any two real numbers
a
and
b
, we have

\frac{1}{|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{2}}=\left|\cosh\varkappa\>U_{n-1}(\alpha)-U_{n-2}(\alpha)e^{ik}\right|^{2}\\
=\cosh^{2}\varkappa\>U_{n-1}^{2}(\alpha)+U_{n-2}^{2}(\alpha)-2\alpha U_{n-1}(\alpha)U_{n-2}(\alpha).

The recurrent relation (
4.6
) allows us to rewrite the above as

\frac{1}{|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{2}}=\cosh^{2}\varkappa\>U_{n-1}^{2}(\alpha)-U_{n-2}(\alpha)U_{n}(\alpha).

(4.7)

Next, we note that
U_{n-2}U_{n}=U_{n-1}^{2}-1
; this follows from the defining relation
\sin\phi\>U_{n}(\cos\phi)=\sin(n+1)\phi
for the Chebyshev polynomials and straightforward transformations:

U_{n-2}(\cos\phi)U_{n}(\cos\phi)=\frac{\sin(n-1)\phi\>\sin(n+1)\phi}{\sin^{2}\phi}\\
=\frac{\cos 2\phi-\cos 2n\phi}{2\sin^{2}\phi}=\frac{\sin^{2}n\phi-\sin^{2}\phi}{\sin^{2}\phi}=U_{n-1}^{2}(\cos\phi)-1.

Finally, we conclude from (
4.7
) that

\frac{1}{|t_{n}(\theta,k)|^{2}}=(\cosh^{2}\varkappa-1)\>U_{n-1}^{2}(\alpha)+1\\
=\sinh^{2}\varkappa\>U_{n-1}^{2}(\alpha)+1=\tfrac{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}{4\theta^{2}}\>U_{n-1}^{2}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)+1.

The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 4
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, the resonance set
\mathcal{R}_{\theta,n}
for the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
contains
n-1
points
k_{1}(\theta),\dots,k_{n-1}(\theta)
, where
k_{j}(\theta)
is the root of the equation

\cos k_{j}=\frac{2\theta}{\theta^{2}+1}\cos\frac{\pi j}{n}

(4.8)

on the interval
(0,\pi)
,
j=1,2,\dots,n-1
. At these points,
T_{n}(\theta,k)
reaches its global maximum, which is equal to
1
.

Proof.

It follows from (
3.5
) that, for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
,
T_{n}(\theta,k)\leq 1
and that
T_{n}(\theta,k)=1
if and only if
r_{n}(\theta,k)=0
.
By Theorem
3
, the equation
r_{n}(\theta,k)=0
is equivalent to

U_{n-1}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)=0.

It is known
[
20
]
that the polynomial
U_{n-1}(\alpha)
has
n-1
simple roots

\alpha_{j}=\cos\frac{\pi j}{n},\quad j=1,2,\dots,n-1.

Hence, the roots
k_{j}(\theta)
of (
4.8
) belong to
\mathcal{R}_{\theta,n}
and
T_{n}(\theta,k_{j}(\theta))=1
.
We next prove that there are no other transmission resonances. Observe that if
\beta_{j}
and
\beta_{j+1}
are two consecutive critical points of
U_{n-1}
, then
U_{n-1}(\beta_{j})U_{n-1}(\beta_{j+1})<0
and
U_{n-1}
is strictly monotone on
[\beta_{j},\beta_{j+1}]
. Since the derivative
\frac{\partial T_{n}}{\partial k}
has zeros at the points
k\in[0,\pi)
that are roots of the equation

U_{n-1}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)U_{n-1}^{\prime}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)\sin k=0,

we conclude that all critical points of
T_{n}(\theta,\,\cdot\,)
other than
k_{j}(\theta)
,
j=1,2,\dots,n-1
, are points of its local minimum.
∎

T_{80}(\theta,k)
for

.

For a fixed
\theta
, the peaks of the transmission probabilities
T_{n}(\theta,k)
, for all
n
, belong to the interval of
k
with

\frac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}|\cos k|<1
, i.e., to the interval

I_{\theta}=\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\arcsin{\tfrac{2\theta}{\theta^{2}+1}},\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\arcsin{\tfrac{2\theta}{\theta^{2}+1}}\right).

The plot of
T_{n}(\theta,k)
for a fixed
\theta
and
n=80
is given in Fig.

; note the dense array of transmission peaks over the range
I_{\theta}
.

The explicit representation (
4.4
) of the transmission probability allows us to analyze its behaviour as
\theta\to 0
more precisely.

Corollary 1
.

For any
k\in(0,\pi)
other than
\frac{\pi}{2}
, the following estimate holds:

T_{n}(\theta,k)\leq\frac{c\>\theta^{2n}}{|\cos k|^{2(n-1)}},\qquad\text{as }\theta\to 0,

where
c
is independent of
\theta
and
k
.

Proof.

For large
\alpha
, we have
U_{n-1}(\alpha)=2^{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}+O(\alpha^{n-3})
. Hence,

|U_{n-1}(\alpha)|\geq 2^{n-2}|\alpha|^{n-1}

as
|\alpha|\to\infty
. If
d=|\cos k|>0
, then

\left|\frac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right|=\frac{d}{2\theta}+O(\theta),\quad\text{as }\theta\to 0,

and therefore

\left|U_{n-1}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{n-1}}{\theta^{n-1}}.

Finally, we conclude from (
4.4
) that

T_{n}(\theta,k)\leq\frac{1}{1+c_{1}d^{2(n-1)}/\theta^{2n}}\leq\frac{c\>\theta^{2n}}{d^{2(n-1)}},

as claimed.
∎

Another interesting observation is that the infimum of
T_{n}(\theta,k)
over
n
coincides with limit inferior, is positive for
k\in I_{\theta}
, and takes the following explicit form.

\zeta_{\theta}(k)

.

Corollary 2
.

For a fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
and all
k\in[0,\pi)
, it holds

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{n}(\theta,k)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{(\theta^{2}-1)^{2}}{(\theta^{2}+1)^{2}\sin^{2}k},\qquad&k\in I_{\theta};\\
0,&k\in[0,\pi)\setminus I_{\theta}.\end{cases}

Proof.

In view of the explicit formula (
4.4
) for
T_{n}(\theta,k)
, it suffices, for each fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
and
k\in[0,\pi)
, to find the supremum of
U^{2}_{n-1}(\cosh\varkappa\cos k)
, where
\varkappa
satisfies
\theta=e^{\varkappa}
. Assume that
\cosh\varkappa\>|\cos k|<1
and take
\phi\in(0,\pi)
such that
\cosh\varkappa\>\cos k=\cos\phi
. Then, by the defining property (
4.5
) of the Chebyshev polynomials of second kind,

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}U^{2}_{n-1}(\cos\phi)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\sin^{2}(n\phi)}{\sin^{2}\phi}=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\phi}=\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\phi},

impying that

\displaystyle\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{n}(\theta,k)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1+\sinh^{2}\varkappa/(1-\cosh^{2}\varkappa\>\cos^{2}k)\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\cosh^{2}\varkappa\>\cos^{2}k}{\cosh^{2}\varkappa-\cosh^{2}\varkappa\>\cos^{2}k}=1-\frac{\sinh^{2}\varkappa}{\cosh^{2}\varkappa\>\sin^{2}k}.

If
\cosh\varkappa|\cos k|\geq 1
, then
U^{2}_{n-1}(\cosh\varkappa\cos k)\geq U^{2}_{n-1}(1)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, showing that
T_{n}(\theta,k)
decays to zero. The proof is complete.
∎

One can also prove that the curve
\zeta_{\theta}(k)=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{n}(\theta,k)
is the envelope of the family
T_{n}(\theta,k)
over the interval
I_{\theta}
, see Fig.
8
.

Finally, we demonstrate that transmission prevails for
\theta
close to
1
; thus the
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb becomes completely penetrable as
\theta\to 1
, see Fig.
3
.

Corollary 3
.

As
\theta
tends to
1
, the functions
T_{n}(\theta,k)
converge to
1
uniformly in
k\in(0,\pi]
; moreover, the inequality

1-T_{n}(\theta,k)\leq 4n^{2}(1-\theta)^{2}

holds for all
\theta
close to
1
.

Proof.

First, we note that

\max_{|\phi|\leq\pi}|U_{n}(\cos\phi)|=\max_{|\phi|\leq\pi}\frac{|\sin(n+1)\phi|}{|\sin\phi|}=n+1.

Since
U_{n}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)\to U_{n}(\cos k)
as
\theta\to 1
, we can estimate

\left|U_{n}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)\right|\leq 2(n+1)

for all
\theta
close to
1
.
Set
\beta_{n}(\theta,k)=\frac{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}{4\theta^{2}}\>U_{n-1}^{2}\left(\tfrac{\theta^{2}+1}{2\theta}\cos k\right)
. Then

\beta_{n}(\theta,k)\leq 4n^{2}(1-\theta)^{2},\qquad\text{as }\theta\to 1.

We conclude from this that

1-T_{n}(\theta,k)=1-\frac{1}{1+\beta_{n}(\theta,k)}=\frac{\beta_{n}(\theta,k)}{1+\beta_{n}(\theta,k)}\leq\beta_{n}(\theta,k)\leq 4n^{2}(1-\theta)^{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

5.
Scattering from locally periodic smooth resonant potentials

As shown above, the exactly solvable model Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}
with
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb potentials exhibit distinctive and useful transmission properties, making them valuable for understanding quantum filtering and tunnelling phenomena. Assume that
V
is a resonant potential with associated parameter
\theta
as defined in (
1.4
). Then the family of scaled Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
in (
2.1
) converges, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to the model Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
of (
2.2
) defined by (
2.3
)–(
2.4
), in the norm resolvent sense
[
4
]
. In this section, we pursue two goals: firstly, we construct, for every
\theta>0
, a compactly supported potential
V
having a zero-energy resonance with this parameter; secondly, we prove that the transmission probability for
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
converges as
\varepsilon\to 0
to that of
\mathcal{H}
(cf.
[

]
). This demonstrates that the non-trivial band-limited behaviour of the Hamitonians with
\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}
-comb potentials persists under realistic approximations.

Lemma 1
.

For any
\theta>0
, there exists a potential
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with compact support such that the Schrödinger operator
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V(x)
has zero-energy resonance with a half-bound state
u
satisfying

\frac{u(+\infty)}{u(-\infty)}=\theta.

(5.1)

Proof.

Let
u
be a smooth positive function on the line such that
u(x)=1
for
x<-1
and
u(x)=\theta
for
x>1
and set

V(x)=\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(x)}{u(x)}.

(5.2)

By construction,
u
solves the equation

-y^{\prime\prime}+V(x)y=0

(5.3)

and is bounded on the line, so that
V
is a resonant potential with half-bound state
u
. In addition, the support of
V
lies in
[-1,1]
and (
5.1
) holds.
∎

Let us consider the scattering problem

-y^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,V\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)y=k^{2}y

(5.4)

on a single dipole localized at the point
x=x_{0}
.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
V
is a resonant potential,
\mathop{\rm supp}V\subset[-1,1]
, and that
u
is the corresponding half-bound state. Then the transfer matrix of problem (
5.4
) has the form

M_{\varepsilon}(V;x_{0},k)=\dfrac{1}{2\theta}\begin{bmatrix}(\theta^{2}+1+i\varepsilon k\eta)\,e^{-2i\varepsilon k}&(\theta^{2}-1-i\varepsilon k\eta)\,e^{-2ikx_{0}}\\
(\theta^{2}-1+i\varepsilon k\eta)\,e^{2ikx_{0}}&(\theta^{2}+1-i\varepsilon k\eta)\,e^{2i\varepsilon k}\end{bmatrix},

(5.5)

where
\theta=u(1)/u(-1)
and
\eta=\theta^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}u^{-2}(x)\,dx
.

Proof.

Since
\mathop{\rm supp}V\subset[-1,1]
, the half-bound state
u
is constant outside the interval
(-1,1)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that

u(-1)=1,\qquad u(1)=\theta;

(5.6)

then
u(\pm\infty)=u(\pm 1)
, and
\theta
is the parameter associated with the resonant potential
V
via (
1.4
).

A solution of (
5.3
) that is linearly independent of
u
can be taken

v(x)=u(x)\int_{-1}^{x}\frac{dt}{u^{2}(t)}.

(5.7)

Then
uv^{\prime}-u^{\prime}v=1
, so that on account of (
5.6
) and the equalities
u^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0
, we conclude that

v(-1)=0,\quad v^{\prime}(-1)=1,\quad v(1)=\theta\int_{-1}^{1}u^{-2}(x)\,dx,\quad v^{\prime}(1)=\theta^{-1}.

(5.8)

We are looking for a solution of (
5.4
) of the form

y(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha_{1}e^{ikx}+\alpha_{2}e^{-ikx}&\text{for }x<x_{0}-\varepsilon,\\
c_{1}u\left(\tfrac{x-x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)+c_{2}v\left(\tfrac{x-x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)&\text{for }x_{0}-\varepsilon<x<x_{0}+\varepsilon,\\
\beta_{1}e^{ikx}+\beta_{2}e^{-ikx}&\text{for }x>x_{0}+\varepsilon.\end{cases}

The transfer matrix
M_{\varepsilon}(V;x_{0},k)
maps the vector
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{\top}
into
[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]^{\top}
, thus we need to express the constants
\beta_{j}
in terms of
\alpha_{j}
. Since the solution
y
must be continuously differentiable at
x=x_{0}-\varepsilon
, the equalities
y(x_{0}-\varepsilon-0)=y(x_{0}-\varepsilon+0)
and
y^{\prime}(x_{0}-\varepsilon-0)=y^{\prime}(x_{0}-\varepsilon+0)
yield

\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}&e^{-ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}\\
ike^{ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}&-ike^{-ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u(-1)&v(-1)\\
\varepsilon^{-1}u^{\prime}(-1)&\varepsilon^{-1}v^{\prime}(-1)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{bmatrix};

similar continuity conditions at
x=x_{0}+\varepsilon
produce

\begin{bmatrix}u(1)&v(1)\\
\varepsilon^{-1}u^{\prime}(1)&\varepsilon^{-1}v^{\prime}(1)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}&e^{-ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}\\
ike^{ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}&-ike^{-ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Using the values of
u
and
v
at the points
\pm 1
listed in (
5.6
) and (
5.8
) and introducing
\eta
as suggested, we conclude that

\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}&e^{-ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}\\
e^{ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}&-e^{-ik(x_{0}+\varepsilon)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta&ik\varepsilon\eta/\theta\\
0&1/\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}&e^{-ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}\\
e^{ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}&-e^{-ik(x_{0}-\varepsilon)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Solving for the vector
[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]^{\top}
, we derive the transfer matrix
M_{\varepsilon}(V;x_{0},k)
as in (
5.5
).
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let
T_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)=|t_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)|^{2}
be the probability of a particle tunneling through the array of localized dipoles

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}V\left(\frac{x-j}{\varepsilon}\right),

(5.9)

with potential
V
as in Theorem
5
. Then
T_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)
converges as
\varepsilon\to 0
to the transmission probability
T_{n}(\theta,k)
for the
\delta_{\theta}^{\prime}
-comb

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta^{\prime}_{\theta}(x-j).

Proof.

Comparing the transfer matrices in (
5.5
) and (
3.1
), we see that

\|M_{\varepsilon}(V;x_{0},k)-M(\theta,kx_{0})\|\leq c_{1}|k|\varepsilon

(5.10)

with a constant
c_{1}
independent of
k
.
The transfer matrix for the potential
V_{\varepsilon}
is equal to

M_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)=M_{\varepsilon}(V;n-1,k)\cdots M_{\varepsilon}(V;1,k)M_{\varepsilon}(V;0,k),

(5.11)

and using (
5.10
) in (
3.3
) and (
5.11
), one concludes that

\|M_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)-M_{n}(\theta,k)\|\leq c_{2}n|k|\varepsilon.

Therefore,
|t^{-1}_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)-t_{n}^{-1}(\theta,k)|\leq c_{2}n|k|\varepsilon
, so that, for every fixed
k\in\mathbb{R}
,

T_{n,\varepsilon}(V;k)\to T_{n}(\theta,k),

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The proof is complete.
∎
