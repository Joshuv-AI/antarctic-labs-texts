Encapsulated droplet array for intensified micromole-scale high-throughput reaction screening
Authors: Jie Chen, Huijie Chen, Wei Qu, Junjie Huang, Yajun Tan, Haowen Zhong, Rentao Zhang, Yilan Liu, Qiang Zhang, Hanyu Gao, Xiaoxue Wang, Peng Wang, Yiming Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe automated high-throughput experimentation (HTE) technology facilitates exploring enormous parametric space of chemical reactions with minimal time and material consumptions. However, the widely used microwell plate-based and flow-based screening approaches are limited by their ability to handle harsh reaction conditions and highly parallel combinatorial screening, respectively. Herein, we report an encapsulated droplet array (EDA) technology to overcome these challenges. By confining microliter-scale reaction droplets within a chemically inert polypropylene pocket reinforced by an aluminum chassis, it enables reactions to be executed under elevated temperatures (up to 105 °C) and pressures (up to 7 atm) in a massively parallel manner (48 reactions per EDA plate). Its versatility and applicability for combinatorial screening under harsh conditions are demonstrated across various bond-forming chemistries, including aminolysis reaction, amide condensation, nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) reaction, Buchwald-Hartwig C–N amination, solid-supported palladium catalyzed Suzuki-Miyaura coupling, and metallophotoredox catalyzed C(sp2)–C(sp3) coupling. In addition, a fully automated platform is developed to achieve end-to-end droplet preparation, encapsulation, reaction, and analysis, illustrating the seamless integration with standard automated liquid-handling systems.Subject terms: Organic chemistry, Chemical engineering

Introduction

The identification of synthesis conditions with high efficacy is one of the rate-limiting factors for accessing various potent drug candidates for the biological activity studies. The multidimensionality of chemical structures sometimes leads to reactivity cliffs making it challenging to predict the reaction outcome for unexplored transformations1. The recent emerging implementations of artificial intelligence (AI) in predictive chemistry tasks have provided a viable approach, complementary to the conventional expert rules, to extract the reactivity relationships from experimental data2,3. However, the quality and quantity of experimental data used for model training are the key for constructing a meaningfully precise reactivity AI prediction model4,5. Instead of relying on scattered literature data, it has gradually become a routine for chemists to use high-throughput experimentation (HTE) systems to collect self-consistent reaction datasets for building predictive models for widespread transformations in pharmaceutical synthesis, such as Buchwald-Hartwig cross-coupling6–8, Suzuki-Miyaura coupling9,10, nickel-catalyzed cross-coupling11,12, amide condensation reaction13, photoredox catalysis8,14–16, deoxyfluorination17, cross-dimerization18, and C–H functionalization19. The adaptation of microscale HTE reaction systems significantly expands structural and conditional diversity of the investigated reactions with reduced time and cost. Nonetheless, the existing HTE systems are often infrastructure-intensive and tailored to specific types of chemistry, thus hindering the broader chemistry applicability using readily available laboratory facilities (e.g., liquid handlers and high-performance liquid chromatography).

Microwell plates adopted from biological-assay screening serve as a simple and cost-effective option for large-scale screening tasks20. Standardized liquid handling technologies, compatibility with existing analytical equipment (e.g., HPLC and HPLC-MS), and disposable microwell plates allows effortless automated combinatorial screening using 1–50 μL volume per reaction7,20. For instance, HTE of palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions and electrosynthesis can be conducted in 384-well or 1536-well plate with a scale of 1–20 μL7,21,22. It is worth noting that the reported examples were often constrained to using nonvolatile solvents (e.g., dimethyl sulfoxide and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone) under ambient temperature and pressure conditions, which was because of the lack of effective plate sealing technology to avoid solvent evaporation for organic chemistry applications10,20. The limited choice of solvents and deviation from real synthesis conditions would require further optimization efforts, undermining the value of the screening results obtained from microwell plates. This challenge of using microwell plates for chemistry reaction screening has been listed as an urgent problem by the Pharmaceutical Enabling Technology Industry Council (ETC)23. Various strategies have been developed to overcome this limitation, such as using capped microvials (e.g., crimped aluminum vial caps)12,24, using film/tape sealed microwell plate25, and adding an aluminum clam shell and chemical-resistant gasket (e.g., polytetrafluoroethylene) on a microwell plate8,26,27 or glass shell vials in a 96-vial format12,28–30 for mechanical sealing. Despite the success of expanding the solvent choice and operation conditions, these strategies require specificized apparatus, expensive consumables, or complex operations to achieve automated reaction screening, inevitably comprising the cost and throughput.

Concurrent with the development efforts in parallel microscale batch screening, the continuous flow screening technology derived from microfluidic flow chemistry concept allows to operate reaction under elevated temperature and pressure. The seminal work from Pfizer demonstrated a nanomole-scale reaction screening platform with integrated inline analytic ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) achieving >1500 reactions/day with a single-channel flow reactor. The key to this remarkable throughput was operating the flow reactor above the solvent boiling point of Suzuki-Miyaura coupling, thus completing the reaction within 1 min of residence time10. Other reaction intensification technologies are also possible within the miniaturized reaction zone, such as micro-interdigitated electrodes for electrochemistry screening31 and high energy density laser for photochemistry screening15. In addition, with the feedbacks from inline analytics, continuous flow system can achieve closed-loop iterative reaction optimization with the black-box optimization algorithms (e.g., Bayesian optimization14,32 and SNOBFIT algorithm33) to find the top-performing conditions. For chemistries requiring long reaction time, the oscillatory droplet systems can be used instead of single-pass flow systems to decouple reaction time and mixing performance34–36. Despite these attractive attributes of flow screening technology, it lacks the solid handling capability37,38, thus limiting to liquid or gas-liquid applications. The required intermittent rinse to avoid cross-contamination and steady-state waiting compromises the screening throughput and necessitates result reproducibility validation39. Furthermore, the single-10,14,15,31,34 or few-40 channel configurations are not amenable to conducting combinatorial screening of discrete variables. Thus, flow screening technology still remains as a specialized research tool instead of a routine equipment to use in common laboratories.

In this work, we present an encapsulated droplet array (EDA) HTE technology (Fig. 1) that simultaneously possesses the advantageous attributes of microwell plate screening and flow screening, including (1) microliter-scale volume for reduced reagent consumption, (2) wide reaction condition applicability, (3) amenability for both discrete and continuous variables screening, (4) highly multiplexed for increasing screening throughput, (5) disposable and low-cost consumables, and (6) easy integration with common laboratory automation facilities. To achieve these, we encapsulate a 50 μL reaction droplet inside a confined pocket built by chemical-resistant polypropylene (PP) film reinforced by aluminum chassis. The confinement of microliter-scale reaction droplets allows to execute the reaction under elevated temperature and pressure (up to 105 °C and 7 atm), creating the opportunity to intensify the reaction similar to microfluidic flow screening. Notably, when a comparable kinetic regime is established between EDA and the flow reactor, the reaction parameters obtained from the encapsulated droplet screening can be transferred, without further optimization, to a continuous flow reactor for scale-up synthesis. To increase screening throughput, these encapsulated droplets are multiplexed in an array with a form factor of a standard 96-well plate, such that common liquid handlers and HPLC autosampler can be directly used for droplet preparation and reaction analysis, respectively. A fully automated droplet array screening system was constructed to achieve end-to-end droplet preparation, encapsulation, reaction, and analysis, illustrating the seamless and effortless integration with existing systems. The broad applicability of this encapsulated droplet array technology was demonstrated for various chemistries under a wide range of pressure and temperature, including aminolysis reaction, amide condensation, SNAr reaction, Buchwald-Hartwig C–N amination, silica-supported palladium catalyzed Suzuki-Miyaura coupling, and visible-light photocatalytic C(sp2)–C(sp3) coupling.

Results and discussion

Design of encapsulated droplet array

The core design concept of EDA screening strategy is to confine individual reaction droplet inside a chemically inert, mechanically reinforced, and miniaturized space, such that harsh reaction conditions can be tolerated. Inspired by blister packing technology for extending the shelf life of the pharmaceutical tablets or capsules, we sought to create the above-mentioned confined space for droplet reaction using thermally bonded plastic pockets. Considering the necessity for low cost and broad applicability, a workflow for realizing such an encapsulation mechanism was engineered (Fig. 1b). The affordable and chemical-resistant thermoplastic, polypropylene (PP), was selected as the base material for this proof-of-concept study. Other fluorinated thermoplastics, such as perfluoroalkoxy alkane (PFA) and fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP), could further be considered for wider operating reaction temperature and better chemical compatibility.

In this workflow, starting from a PP sheet (85 mm × 128 mm × 0.5 mm), a vacuum thermoforming workstation was built to transform the 2-dimensional (2D) sheet into a 3-dimensional (3D) droplet array plate (DropArrPlate). Preheat PP sheet to 155 °C above its glass transition temperature (Tg, 140–150 °C) using an array of infrared heaters. An infrared temperature sensor determines the timing for activating the pneumatic actuators to move the sheet heated to target temperature onto a vacuum-pulled mold, thus forming the desired 3D vessel shape (see Supplementary Movie 1). The DropArrPlate was designed to conform the ANSI/SLAS Microplate Standards41 (85 mm × 128 mm), such that it could seamlessly fit into the standard liquid pipetting apparatus or HPLC analytical equipment (see details in Supplementary Information Section 2). To allow sufficient space for the following droplet encapsulation, we placed 48 droplet wells (4 × 12 with each of 100 μL volume) on a single DropArryPlate.

Once reaction droplets are prepared and filled inside each droplet well through automatic/manual pipetting, the next step is the droplet encapsulation, for which a thermosealing workstation was constructed (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Movie 2). A biaxially oriented polypropylene (BOPP) film was used for its good thermal sealability as the top cover to fuse with the DropArrPlate. To achieve uniform and defect-free sealing of all 48 droplet wells, we engineered a spring-loaded ring-shaped heating post that can realize individual control of the compression force over each droplet well (Supplementary Information Section 4). These posts were heated above the BOPP melting point before sealing the DropArrPlate and BOPP film. The uncontrolled temperature rise of the reaction droplet during the sealing process might lead to undesired or unpredictable reactions. Therefore, we conducted a finite-element analysis (FEA) to optimize the post heating temperature and compression time to minimize the heat transferred to the droplet. It was found that 210 °C and 2-s compression gave the optimal droplet temperature control, with only 0.7 °C rise from the room temperature (25 °C) in the droplet, while the temperature in the fused area of BOPP film and DropArrPlate reaches 155 °C (see detailed FEA in Supplementary Information Section 5).

Vital to the success of executing microscale droplet reaction screening under various harsh conditions is tolerating the large solvent vapor pressure under the elevated temperature. BOPP film fused DropArrPlate provides a good chemically inert droplet confinement, but lacks the required mechanical strength to withstand the pressure. To address this, an aluminum chassis was designed as a jacket around the sealed DropArrPlate to support the flexible PP/BOPP film under pressurized environment Thus, the sealed DropArrPlate functions as a protective insert inside the chassis to hold reaction droplets, and the disposable and low-cost nature of DropArrPlate allows practical reaction HTE without the need for vessel cleaning. In addition, after the reaction, the BOPP film can be easily punctured via needle piercing in existing HPLC autosampler (Supplementary Movie 3), such that no further liquid handling is necessary.

Compatibility and performance benchmark of encapsulated droplet array

Solvent loss and cross-contamination among wells are the critical metrics for evaluating the performance of the droplet confinement effectiveness. Common solvents used for organic synthesis, including methanol (MeOH), ethanol (EtOH), acetone, acetonitrile (MeCN), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), were selected to compare the commercial 96-well microplate and EDA developed in this work. When heated at 50 °C for one hour, conventional sealing methods for 96-well microplates, including microplate lid and adhesive sealing tape commonly used for aqueous biological applications, failed to prevent evaporation for low-boiling-point solvents. Along with solvent loss from the failed sealing, solvent cross-contamination among adjacent microwells also occurred (see details in SI Section 6). Only high-boiling-point DMSO survived under this mild heating condition with 2% loss for both tape-sealed 96-well microplate and capped 96-well microplate, explaining why DMSO (DMF and NMP behave similarly) is the solvent of choice for previous HTE studies using microwell plates. In comparison, EDA exhibited <2% solvent loss for all tested solvents when heated at 50 °C. When increasing the heating temperature to 105 °C, above the boiling points of all tested solvents, there was still negligible solvent loss without solvent cross-contamination observed, highlighting its ability to withstand the harsh reaction conditions.

Furthermore, we devised a pressure gauge (Figs. S13 and S14) to measure the force experienced by the top sealing BOPP film, which indirectly indicated the solvent vapor pressure inside the encapsulated droplet. As expected, the measured temperature dependence of inner vapor pressures for different solvents aligned closely with Antoine equation42 (Fig. S15). Notably, there was no encapsulation failure even for 6.7 atm vapor pressure of dichloride methane (DCM) at 97 °C and 7.0 atm vapor pressure of 25–28 wt.% aqueous ammonia at 100 °C. Operating reactions under elevated temperatures can accelerate the reaction, such that the throughput of HTE can be significantly improved. This kinetic acceleration could be further exploited on EDA because of the capability to superheat above solvent boiling point. For example, 552% increase in kinetic constant from 60 to 100 °C for SNAr reaction in EtOH (Supplementary Information section 11.1), 218% increase in kinetic constant from 60 to 90 °C for SNAr reaction in MeCN40, and 167% increase in kinetic constant from 70 to 100 °C for the oxidative ammonolysis of lignins43.

In addition to reaction acceleration, EDA opens up the opportunity to screen high-pressure reactions that are challenging to realize under small scale. For example, the aminolysis of esters using low-molecule-weight amines is typically conducted in pressurized autoclave reactors, microwave reactors44, or flow reactors45 due to the high volatility of amines. We used the aminolysis of methyl nicotinate as a benchmark reaction to compare different screening methods. The autoclave reactor gave excellent reaction yield, but its low throughput and large volume limit its applicability in rapid condition screening tasks. The single-channel CEM microwave reactor (10 mL reaction volume) could also result in good yield (99%), but the specialized microwave equipment might not be accessible by general chemistry labs. As expected, commonly used non-pressure-resistant vials and 96-well plates both failed to finish the reaction. The specially engineered glass shell vial with aluminum clam shell and chemical-resistant gasket sealing gave satisfying results, but the expensive glass shell vial consumables were used and the sealing gasket required cleaning to avoid cross-contamination. Remarkably, the low-cost and automation-friendly EDA gave equivalent reaction outcomes at 50 microliter scale. Then, we used EDA to screen the aminolysis reactivity between a range of esters and amines (i.e., ammonia, methylamine, ethylamine, and dimethylamine), the vapor pressures of which are generally much higher (~7 atm) than ambient pressure. Of four examined ester substrates, only furan exhibited low aminolysis reactivity towards all four amines, possibly due to the electron-rich furan ring makes carbonyl group less electrophilic and therefore less susceptible to nucleophilic attack by amines (Fig. 2d, and see more reactions in Supplementary Information Section 10.2).

Combinatorial screening of discrete variables for C–N bond forming reactions

As demonstrated above, EDA is able to execute reactions under high temperature and pressure conditions, with similar capability as microfluidic flow screening technologies. In the meantime, it preserves the ability to perform combinatorial screening of discrete variables like the microwell plate screening approach. Two example C–N bond forming reactions, including amide condensation and SNAr reaction, were demonstrated to illustrate its efficiency in rapid evaluation of discrete reaction variables, such as substrates and solvents.

The amide condensation reaction is a top-1 frequently utilized reaction for both medicinal and process chemistry in pharmaceutical industry46. The amide formation reactivity between carboxylic acids and amines are highly dependent on their individual structures. With hexafluorophosphate azabenzotriazole tetramethyl uronium (HATU) as the acid activation agent and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as the base, a range of carboxylic acids and amines were paired to synthesize their amide coupling product. Carboxylic acids and amine substrates were selected to ensure good structural diversity among the buyable compound library (Mcule dataset47, Fig. 3b, c, see details in Supplementary Information Section 15)48. To match up the analysis throughput with reaction throughput of a single DropArrPlate, we implemented UPLC-MS with an analysis rate of 2.5 min/sample for identifying and quantifying product peaks. 400 reactions encompassing 20 different carboxylic acids and 20 different amines were performed and analyzed within 27 h (Fig. 3d). The screening results were further validated with our previously published Bayesian neural network model for predicting the amide-formation reactivity, which was trained using 11,669 experiments for 8095 distinct target compounds49. With receiver operating characteristic (ROC) plot, area under curve (AUC) was 0.93 and prediction accuracy was 0.87, showing the good consistence between experimental data and model prediction (Fig. S37).

In addition to amide formation, the SNAr reaction stands as third popular bond formation reactions in drug synthesis to construct aromatic C(sp2)–N/O/S bonds46. Here, two key discrete variables, including solvent and base, were examined for the SNAr reaction between 3,4-difluoronitrobenzene (5) and morpholine (6) (Fig. 3e). Taking the advantage of the EDA’s ability to intensify the reaction under solvent superheating conditions, we decided to perform the reaction under 100 °C for all tested solvents. Aprotic solvents, including DMSO, NMP, and DMF, showed significantly higher reaction rates compared to protic solvents (MeOH and EtOH) due to the absence of hydrogen bonding effect with the amine nucleophiles50. The reduced polarity of aprotic dioxane solvent compared to DMSO, NMP, and DMF is less favored for stabilizing of ionic intermediates, thus leading to slower observed reaction kinetics51. Bases and their equivalence generally gave similar performance except for DBU because of its inherent nucleophilicity leading to the formation of undesired byproducts. The optimal reaction condition was achieved with DMSO as the solvent and 2 equivalents of DIPEA as the base, resulting in 98% yield of the desired product.

Furthermore, EDA offers an encapsulated reaction space that is similar to that of flow reactors, such that EDA and flow reactors should share comparable kinetic behaviors, facilitating the reaction condition transfer from EDA to flow reactor. We first evaluated the kinetic behavior of SNAr reaction within EDA and flow reactor to ensure a good consistency between the two (Supplementary Information Section 11.2). Then, we directly transferred the optimal reaction condition obtained in EDA to the continuous flow scale-up synthesis, thus eliminating the need of further condition optimization. We constructed a microfluidic tubular reactor system with two high-pressure syringe pumps delivering 5 and 6 separately, and a back-pressure regulator was used to maintain pressure. The optimal condition identified above was directly used in the flow reactor, achieving the same yield of 98% as that in EDA, with a space-time yield (STY) of 340 mg h−1 mL−1 (Fig. 3f). Therefore, EDA could serve as a tool for rapid evaluation of discrete variables to find the optimal flow synthesis condition for the subsequent flow scale up, thus avoiding the low-efficiency discrete variable screening in flow.

Screening of heterogeneous palladium-catalyzed C–X coupling reactions

Buchwald-Hartwig C–N amination and Suzuki-Miyaura coupling are representative palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions that serve as pivotal methods for C(sp²)–N and C(sp²)–C(sp²) bond construction method in the synthesis of pharmaceuticals. As the high reaction temperature is often beneficial for accelerating these reaction, conventional microwell plates, however, have trouble in withstanding the heating of organic solvent. On the other hand, palladium-catalyzed couplings often employ insoluble salt or metal additives, such heterogeneous systems are difficult to adapt to flow-based screening because of the potential channel clogging52. EDA avoids above-mentioned limitations, serving as a rapid screening tool for solid-containing microscale reaction screening.

We firstly applied EDA in Buchwald-Hartwig C–N amination between cyclic amide (8) and aryl bromides (7a–c) with 1,4-dioxane as solvent and Pd2(dba)3 as palladium catalyst, investigating the combination of 12 ligands and 3 poorly soluble bases (Cs2CO3, K3PO4 and NaOtBu) (Fig. 4a)53,54. Higher temperature and weaker base Cs2CO3 and K3PO4 with better functional group tolerance are favorable for the amination54. When monodentate symmetrical PR3-type ligands (L6, L7, L12) and IPr·HCl (L10) were used, the reaction showed low yield for the amination of 4-bromobenzonitrile (7a). As for 4-methoxybromobenzene (7b) and 4-tert-butylbromobenzene (7c), which have electron-donating substituents at the para position, only the combination of Cs2CO3 and Xantphos (L2) gave a considerable yield at 100 °C55.

Besides the commonly used metal catalysts like above-mentioned Pd2(dba)3, the recyclable solid-supported metal catalysts have emerged to align with the demands for cost reduction, minimizing metal content in final drug substance, and SiliaCat-DPP-Pd is a representative solid-supported palladium catalyst that is air- and moisture-stable56. We then employed EDA for evaluating the efficacy of SiliaCat-DPP-Pd for cross-coupling of a range of (hetero)aryl halides and boronic acids/esters (Fig. 4b). Based on the 440 data points obtained, a large language model (GPT-4o as the backend) with chain-of-thought mechanism (LangChain architecture) was used to automate the construction of a predictive model (SI Section 16). The three molecular descriptors (i.e., Morgan fingerprints, Morgan fingerprints of the simplified molecule (simplified Morgan fingerprints), and a set of chemical descriptors calculated by RDKit that characterizes the differences among the substrates (RDKit descriptors)) performed similarly, while Random forest (RF) performed better among the classification algorithms (i.e., Random forest (RF), AdaBoost, k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)).

Fully automated screening of photoredox coupling reaction

The recent advancement in the automation in chemistry has streamlined the reaction preparation, execution, and analysis, thus significantly increasing the throughput of HTE. However, to handle high temperature and pressure conditions encountered during reaction screening, automation facilities tailored to chemistry application are often complex and high-cost, increasing the barrier for common chemistry laboratories to adopt. Notably, the encapsulated droplet array, designed following the ANSI/SLAS microplate standards, can seamlessly be integrated into widely used microwell plate automation protocols. To demonstrate its facile integration with liquid handler and HPLC autosampler designed for microwell plates, we constructed a fully automated system with two low-cost SCARA robots and a linear track for DropArrPlate transportation. The end-to-end automated reaction screening procedure involves: (1) droplet preparation on DropArrPlate using automated pipettes on the liquid handler, (2) droplet encapsulation on the thermosealing workstation, (3) reaction in the shaker module on the liquid handler, and (4) HPLC analysis (Fig. 5a, see details in Supplementary Information Section 17–18 and Supplementary Movie 3).

Reaction condition: 11 (5 μmol), 12 (10 μmol), morpholine (7.5 μmol), nickel salt (5 mol%), ligand (5 mol%), and photocatalyst (1 mol%) in DMF (50 μL) reacting for 120 min under 450 nm illumination; 14 (5 μmol), 15 (10 μmol), morpholine (7.5 μmol), nickel salt (5 mol%), ligand (5 mol%), and photocatalyst (1 mol%) in DMF (50 μL) reacting for 120 min under 450 nm illumination.

To demonstrate this automated workflow, we considered a recently developed photoredox amino radical transfer (ART) strategy for cross-coupling of alkyl boronic esters and aryl halides. Mechanistically, the excited photocatalyst oxidizes morphine to generate N-centered radical in the photocatalytic reductive quenching cycle. Then, the N-centered radical cleavages of C–B bond on the boronic pinacol ester to foster an alkyl radical, which is trapped by Ni(Ⅱ)-aryl intermediate. The subsequent reductive elimination generates the cross-coupled product forms57. We initiated the automated reaction condition investigation of nickel sources, photocatalysts, and ligands. The screening results of the two model reactions (Fig. 5b) revealed that the performance of this photocatalytic reaction is relatively insensitive to choices of photocatalysts and nickel salts, but highly dependent on the ligands. Reactions involving electron-rich bipyridine ligands (L1 and L2) exhibited yields over 60%. The organic photocatalyst 4CzIPN, with a similar oxidative strength to Ir-based photocatalyst, led to similar product formation efficiency58. Among the three nickel salts, NiCl2•glyme and NiBr2•glyme performed similarly, which were slightly better than Ni(NO3)2•6H2O.

Discussion

In summary, EDA methodology effectively integrates the strengths of microwell plate screening and microfluidic screening, enabling highly parallelized microliter-scale reaction evaluation under harsh conditions, while delivering comparable outcomes to established platforms (e.g., glass shell systems). Its capability to tolerate elevated temperatures (up to 105 °C) and pressures (up to 7 atm) without minimal solvent loss substantially broadening scope for synthetic chemistries commonly employed by practicing chemists. Moreover, the capability to operate above solvent boiling points significantly intensifies the reaction kinetics, thus enhancing screening throughput. We successfully demonstrated rapid combinational screening across a variety of synthetic reactions, including aminolysis, amide condensation, SNAr, Buchwald-Hartwig C–N amination, solid-supported palladium-catalyzed Suzuki-Miyaura coupling, and metallophotoredox-catalyzed C(sp²)–C(sp³) coupling. Importantly, the EDA system conforms to the ANSI/SLAS microplate footprint, ensuring seamless integration with existing HTE infrastructure, enabling us to demonstrate a fully automated droplet array screening system with standard liquid handler and liquid chromatograph apparatus. We envision that adoption of the EDA technology would expand the reaction scope accessible to standard microwell-based HTE workflows, thus significantly advancing capabilities in synthetic chemistry research and discovery.

Method

Design and operation of vacuum thermoforming workstation

The vacuum thermoforming workstation, arranged from top to bottom, consists of an array of infrared heaters (550 W), plastic sheet holder controlled by two pneumatic actuators, a vacuum-pulled mold, and an infrared temperature sensor. During the thermoforming process, PP sheet is firstly secured on the sheet holder. Pneumatic actuators then lift the sheet upward toward the array of infrared heaters, where it is heated until it reaches the target temperature of 155 °C and becomes sufficiently softened. An infrared temperature sensor continuously monitors the sheet’s temperature and signals the pneumatic actuators to transfer the softened sheet onto the vacuum mold at the appropriate timing. Subsequently, the vacuum pump (−0.08 MPa) draws the softened sheet onto the mold surface for 10 s to solidify into the desired pocket shape for DropArrPlate. After sufficient cooling, the thermoformed DropArrPlate is removed from the mold, and ready to use for reaction screening. The thermoformed droplet pocket is hemispherical in shape with a cross-sectional diameter of approximately 6 mm.

Design and operation of thermosealing workstation

The thermosealing workstation consists of a DropArrPlate holder, a pneumatic actuator, an electrically heating plate with 48 spring-loaded ring-shaped heating posts. The key to defect-free sealing of each pocket on DropArrPlate is to apply uniform pressure on top of each pocket. Each ring-shaped sealing post is controlled by a spring. During the thermosealing process, DropArrPlate loaded with reaction droplets and BOPP film are firstly placed on the carrier board. Then the carrier board is secured on the holder. Then the holder is pushed to the position just below the heating plate with 48 spring-loaded ring-shaped heating posts. Then the pneumatic actuator moves these heating posts (195–205 °C) towards the top of the BOPP film. After contact heating and pressurization for 1–2 s, the pneumatic actuator moves these heating posts back. Then, the holder is pushed back. In this process, the BOPP film and DropArrPlate are sealed together.

General procedure for aminolysis reaction

Each droplet pocket on the DropArrPlate was charged with ester (6 μmol), amine (25–28 wt.% aqueous ammonia, or 27–32 wt.% aqueous solution of methylamine, or 25–30 wt.% aqueous solution of ethylamine, or 40 wt.% aqueous solution of dimethylamine, 30 μL), and methanol (30 μL). The DropArrPlate was thermosealed, and jacked with the aluminum chassis. Then, the reaction droplets were allowed to react at 105 °C for 8 h. The yield was analyzed by gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 8890) with trimethylbenzene as the internal standard.

General procedure for amide condensation

Each DropArrPlate pocket was charged with amine (4 μmol), carboxylic acid (4 μmol), HATU (8 μmol), DIPEA (12 μmol), and DMF (50 μL). The DropArrPlate was thermosealed, and the reaction droplets were allowed to react at 30 °C for 30 min. The result was analyzed by UPLC-MS (Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class).

General procedure for SNAr reaction

Each DropArrPlate pocket was charged with 3,4-difluoronitrobenzene (12.5 μmol), morpholine (15 μmol), base (25 μmol), and solvent (50 μL). The DropArrPlate was thermosealed, and the reaction droplets were allowed to react at 100 °C for 10 min. The result was analyzed by HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity Ⅱ) with biphenyl as the internal standard.

General procedure for Buchwald-Hartwig C–N coupling reaction

Each DropArrPlate pocket was charged with aryl bromide (7.5 μmol), 2-pyrrolidinone (9 μmol), Pd2(dba)3 (2.5 mol%), ligand (7.5 mol%), inorganic base (~3.4 mg Cs2CO3, ~2.2 mg K3PO4, ~1.0 mg NaOtBu), and 1,4-dioxane (50 μL). Considering the oxygen sensitivity issue for Buchwald-Hartwig C–N coupling reaction, we built a glove box (Fig. S32) for handling reagents and EDA thermosealing process. The DropArrPlate was thermosealed, and the reaction droplets were allowed to react at 60/100 °C for 16 h. The result solution was filtered with silica gel and analyzed by HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity Ⅱ) with biphenyl as the internal standard.

General procedure for Suzuki-Miyaura coupling reaction

Each DropArrPlate pocket was charged with boric acid (ester) (4.8 μmol), (hetero)aryl halides (4 μmol), potassium carbonate (6 μmol), siliaCat DPP-Pd (~2.0 mg), and DMF:H2O (4:1, 50 μL). The DropArrPlate was thermosealed, and the reaction droplets were allowed to react at 105 °C for 2 h. The result was analyzed by UPLC-MS.

Automated platform for photoredox C–C coupling reaction screening

The automated platform consists of four core functional modules, including liquid handler (Opentrons, OT-2 Robot) for droplet preparation and photochemical reaction, thermosealing workstation, robotic plate transportation system (two HITBOT Z-Arm SCARA robots with a linear track), and HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity with G7167A Multisampler). The whole system was automated using python program, the robotic arms were controlled through TCP protocol, the liquid handler was controlled through HTTP protocol, and the other modules were controlled through TCP-RS485 protocol (SI Section 17). The detailed automation workflow is as follows: (1) preparing droplets on DropArrPlate using automated pipette; (2) transferring the prepared DropArrPlate to the thermosealing workstation; (3) transferring a BOPP film onto the top of DropArrPlate; (4) executing thermosealing of the DropArrPlate; (5) transferring the sealed DropArrPlate to the shaker module on the liquid handler for photochemical reaction; (6) transferring reacted DropArrPlate to HPLC for reaction analysis.

Each DropArrPlate pocket was charged with brominated aromatic compound (5 μmol), pinathol borate (10 μmol), morpholine (7.5 μmol), nickel salt (5 mol%), ligand (5 mol%), photocatalyst (1 mol%), and DMF (50 μL). The DropArrPlate was thermosealed, and the reaction droplets were allowed to react under 450 nm LEDs for 2 h. The result was analyzed by HPLC with biphenyl as the internal standard.
