“Schrödinger’s Rat”: Testing Consciousness Under Anesthesia Alters Brain State and Neuronal Responses in Auditory Cortex
Authors: Aaron J Krom, Lottem Bergman, Yaniv Sela, Hanna Cattan-Hayat, Noa Regev, Yuval Nir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIn anesthesia research and clinical practice, the level of consciousness is often assessed by stimulating subjects and observing their responses. It is possible that such stimulation alters the very state of consciousness being assessed. Whether this phenomenon is accompanied by specific cortical activities remains unknown. Here, we tested the effects of behavioral assessments on neuronal activity in the rat primary auditory cortex (A1) and perirhinal cortex (PrC).MethodsNeuronal spiking activity was recorded from microwire arrays targeting A1 and/or PrC in rats (n = 9), whilst presenting 40 Hz click-trains and gradually increasing infusion rates of propofol, remifentanil, ketamine or dexmedetomidine. We assessed righting reflex, pain withdrawal, whisking reflex, and corneal reflex every 12 minutes (every 360 auditory trials). We searched for significant changes in baseline firing rate and stimulus-evoked activities (where present) due to the behavioral assessments, by comparing 4 min intervals before and after the behavioral tests.ResultsOut of 1004 units in 20 sessions, we found significantly elevated activity (p < 0.01) following behavioral tests in 32% and 20% of A1 auditory-responsive units and 8.6% and 5.3% of auditory-unresponsive units under propofol and dexmedetomidine, respectively, but not under remifentanil or ketamine. A higher proportion of units modulated their firing following behavioral tests in A1 compared to PrC, with median gains ranging from +0.23 to +1.06, depending on the specific response feature.ConclusionBehavioral testing increases ongoing firing rate and responses to sounds in some cortical neurons. This increase is more prevalent in A1 than in PrC, mirroring clinical observations that patients exhibit basic responses under sedation, while complex processing, such as memory encoding, is impaired. The degree of neuronal modulation due to behavioral testing varies between anesthetic agents, mirroring their clinical phenotypes.

Introduction

It is standard practice, in both sleep and anesthesia research1–3 as well as in the clinical anesthetic setting,4,5 to assess the depth of sleep or anesthesia by presenting a sensory stimulus and observing the behavioral response. In humans, this may go from verbally asking4 the patient to open their eyes or calling their name,6 through gentle shaking,6 all the way to applying a painful surgery-like stimulus (eg, skin pinch pre-incision7). In rodents, it often involves turning them on their back to assess for righting reflex,1,2,8 through to more painful tail/paw pinches.9–11 Furthermore, even technologies used to assess consciousness without stimulation and observing responses, eg exhaled gas analysis,12 EEG,13 entropy,14 SEF-95%15 and BIS4,15 typically calibrate these against gold-standard behavioral tests.

However, an implicit assumption is often made when performing these tests, that the behavioral response observed after testing reflects the state prior to the test, when it is possible, and even likely, that the test itself changes the state of the subject. In a loose analogy to quantum physics, we coined the term “Schrödinger’s Rat” to describe this effect.

This effect extends beyond rats; non-invasive evidence in humans16–18 has shown that scalp EEGs resemble a more awake state following painful stimuli. Furthermore, it is acknowledged that more active tasks during behavioral testing may mask reduced levels of consciousness/sleepiness owing to their stimulating effect.19 In this manuscript, we test the extent to which cortical neurons modulate their spiking activity due to behavioral testing employing external sensory stimuli, which may or may not occur simultaneously with behavioral awakening of the rat itself.

In this study, we asked the following questions: To what extent do individual neurons modulate their ongoing or sound-evoked firing rates around behavioral testing? Are neurons in primary cortical regions more affected than those in higher-level association regions? This last question is particularly intriguing as there is evidence that certain anesthetic agents, at sub-hypnotic doses, cause conscious amnesic states.20,21 Conceivably, a subject who has woken after a painful stimulus may still be unable to lay down memories or perform certain higher brain functions, and this will be reflected in an absence of neuronal modulation in corresponding brain regions.

To address these questions, rats with electrodes implanted in the primary auditory cortex (A1) and perirhinal cortex (PrC) were gradually administered deepening anesthesia whilst we played sound stimuli. The stimulus chosen was 40 Hz click-trains - clicks because of their broad frequency spectral content able to stimulate a large range of auditory neurons,22 and 40 Hz repetition rate because others have used this to drive auditory steady state responses23 that were sensitive to anesthesia.24,25 Indeed our own previous work compared different repetition rates and found 40 Hz to elicit strong neuronal responses that were attenuated during states of reduced consciousness.26 The PrC was chosen as a pre-hippocampal region known to be high in the auditory pathway,27–29 responding differently to complex30–32 versus simple stimuli,29–33 which may lack responses under general anesthesia.34

We chose to study several anesthetics, each from a different class of agents commonly used clinically, and each giving different features of anesthesia.35,36 These included propofol (a typical GABA-A agonist, amnesic at low doses, then rousable sedation, and eventual causing deep anesthesia), dexmedetomidine (an alpha-2 agonist, giving NREM sleep-like rousable sedation), remifentanil (a potent synthetic opioid, used mainly for analgesia, causing sedation at higher doses), and ketamine (NMDA-antagonist, analgesic at low doses, giving a “disconnected consciousness”37,38 dream-line state at intermediate doses, and full unconsciousness at higher doses).

Every 12 minutes, the rats were subjected to a number of standard behavioral tests, and the neuronal responses to sounds were recorded and compared before and after testing.

Neuronal responses evoked by testing may be accompanied by overt behavior (eg, movement after a pinch) or may occur without any observable behavioral correlate, reflecting an internal change in brain state without a motor response. To distinguish between these possibilities, we quantified pre- to post-test changes in electromyography (EMG) as an index of muscle tone and examined their correlation with changes in neuronal activity.

Materials and Methods

This research used a modified version of an experimental paradigm we39 and others1 have previously published.

Animals and Housing

This study was conducted on nine adult male Long-Evans wild type rats (250–350 g) (sourced from ENVIGO Research Models and Services, Indianapolis, IN). The rats were housed individually in clear Perspex cages with free access to water and food. Environmental conditions were maintained at a temperature of 22–23° Celsius with a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 10 a.m). All experimental procedures, including surgical procedures, animal handling, and data collection, were approved and monitored by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Tel Aviv University (approval M-15-066) and were conducted and reported here according to the ARRIVE guidelines.40

Surgical Procedures and Electrode Implantation

Prior to surgery, anesthesia was induced with Isoflurane 4–5% and then maintained at 1–2%. Rats were positioned in a stereotactic frame (David Kopf Instruments, CA, USA) while euthermia was maintained using a closed-circuit heating pad (Harvard Apparatus, MA, USA) and rectal temperature probe system. We implanted a jugular central venous catheter (Braintree R-JVC-M37-R, 120 mm) under microscopic guidance,41 tunneled it subcutaneously to the dorsal aspect of the neck, and filled it with a heparin lock solution.42 We then shaved the animal’s head following which we applied liquid Viscotear gel to the eyes for protection. A midline incision was made to expose the skull. Steel, 1 mm diameter screws were placed in frontal and parietal locations on the left side of the skull for electroencephalogram (EEG) recording. Electrical reference and ground screws were placed above the cerebellum, and an anchor screw above the right frontal lobe was placed for stabilization (Figure 1a). Bilateral wires were inserted under the neck muscles for electromyography (EMG) measurement, and soldered, along with the EEG wires, to a headstage connector (Omnetics Connector Corporation, MN, USA). We performed a small craniotomy after which we reflected the dura under careful microscopic guidance. The animals were then implanted with either a 32-channel tungsten microwire array (8 rats, Tucker-Davis Technologies Inc, “TDT”, Alachua, FL, USA) or 16-channel array (1 rat, same supplier). The microwires (50 μm diameter, 45° tip angle) were arranged in rows (4 × 8 or 2 × 8 channels) with 375 μm medial-lateral spacing and 250 μm anterior-posterior spacing. Sixteen long microwires (9.6 mm and 9.4 mm) targeted the perirhinal cortex, whereas sixteen short microwires (7.7 mm and 7.2 mm) targeted the primary auditory cortex. The single rat with a 16-channel array was implanted to target the PRC. The arrays were inserted at 3.6 mm posterior and 4.2 mm lateral to Bregma with 22° angle and advanced to 8 mm depth (Figure 1a). Prior to insertion, we applied DiI fluorescent dye (DiIC18 (3), Invitrogen) to the microwires to aid later histological verification of location. After the electrode arrays were advanced, we sealed the craniotomy with silicone (Kwik-Sil; World Precision Instruments, FL, USA) followed by Fusio (Pentron, Czech Republic). Finally, we applied dental cement to secure all wires and screws.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis and Analgesia

Cefazolin (20 mg/kg i.m.) was injected as antibiotic prophylaxis prior to incision, and at the end of surgery, chloramphenicol ointment (3%) was applied to the incision site. Intraperitoneal Carprofen (5 mg/kg) and dexamethasone (0.5 mg/kg) were injected following anesthetic induction, and local anesthetic (lidocaine 3 mg/kg) infiltrated into the scalp surgical site prior to incision. Following surgery, and as the rat started to awake from anesthesia, buprenorphine (25 µg/kg s.c.) was administered for post-operative analgesia. In order to reduce edema, pain, and scar tissue around the intracranial electrodes in the days following surgery, dexamethasone (1.3 mg/kg) was provided orally with food.

Electrophysiology

Neuronal activity was continuously recorded using an RZ2 digital signal processor (TDT). Extracellular spiking activity (sampled at 24.4 kHz and bandpass filtered at 300–5000 Hz) was collected. EEG (filtered at 0.5–300 Hz) and EMG (filtered at 10–100 Hz) were fed through an RA16LI preamplifier (TDT), then along with the microwire data, amplified and digitized by a PZ2 amplifier (TDT), before recording simultaneously with a video stream collected on an RV2 video tracking system (TDT). EEG and EMG were initially sampled at 256.99 Hz and before resampling offline at 1 kHz using custom code in MATLAB (version 9.6.0 R2019a, The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The EEG signal was notch filtered (0.1–0.2 Hz full-width half-maximum) to remove 50 Hz line noise and its harmonics. A notch filter was also applied at 7 Hz, to remove noise observed at that frequency in some sessions. Spike sorting was performed using the “wave_clus”44 semi-automated algorithm, with a detection threshold of 5 standard deviations above the median. This algorithm applies automatic superparamagnetic clustering of wavelet coefficients, which is then manual refined based on inter-spike-interval distribution and consistency of spike waveforms.

Auditory Stimulation

Experiments were conducted in a sound-attenuating chamber (H.N.A, Kiryat Malachi, Israel), which reduced ambient noise by 55 dB. Auditory stimuli were synthesized in MATLAB, converted into voltage signals (195 kHz sampling rate, RZ6, TDT), fed into a speaker amplifier (SA1, TDT) and played via magnetic ultrasonic speaker (MF1, TDT) mounted 55 cm above the cage center (Figure 1b). A high-quality ultrasonic calibration microphone (model 378C01, PCB Piezotronics, Depew, NY, USA), positioned below the speaker at the center of the cage floor, was used to estimate sound intensity (however the precise sound volume experienced by the animal would depend on their exact location in the cage). During experiments, rats were exposed to 500ms-long 40 Hz click-trains (ie, 20 clicks) at three intensities (76, 82, and 93 dB SPL) and a sham stimulus. Preliminary analysis found no significant intensity effect on neuronal responses, and therefore trials of all three intensities were pooled for analysis. Stimuli were presented with 1 ± 0.4 seconds interstimulus intervals in 120-trial blocks (ie, 360 stimuli), lasting 12 minutes per block.

Experimental Design and Anesthesia Protocol

Experiments commenced following a minimum 7-day post-operative recovery period, and two extra days to allow habituation to tethering and auditory stimulation (1–5 hours). On the day of recording, animals were placed in the experimental chamber with the wire bundle running to a counterbalanced tether at the top, next to the IV catheter, connected to a computer-controlled syringe pump (Chemyx Fusion Touch Series, Chemyx Inc., Stafford, TX, USA).

Four anesthetic agents were investigated: propofol, remifentanil, ketamine and dexmedetomidine. Following one block of anesthesia-free recording, anesthesia was commenced using a computer-controlled infusion pump and the IV catheter. In the cases of the first three drugs, the rate was started low (100 µg/kg/min for propofol, 1–2 µg/kg/min for remifentanil, 30 mg/kg/h for ketamine), and then increased in a stepwise fashion, every 12-minutes, at the same time as behavioral tests were performed (Figure 1c). In the case of dexmedetomidine, due to its much longer half-life, a higher-rate “loading dose” was given at the start (0.12 mg/kg/hr over 10 minutes), followed by a steady state infusion of 0.024 mg/kg/hr.

Behavioral Testing

Behavioral tests were conducted every 12 minutes, between the blocks of auditory stimulation, in line with common practice to assess responsiveness under anesthesia.1,2,8,10,11 These included a variety of standardized behavioral tests:45 testing for loss of righting reflex (LORR) by turning the animal on its back, assessing long spinal reflex by pinching tail and hind-paw, assessing short spinal reflex by pinching fore-paw, testing corneal reflex by dropping sterile saline on the eye, and assessing whisking reflex by touching whiskers. Attempts were made to consistently perform tests in this order, testing the righting reflex before the more arousing and painful pinch testing to maximize confidence in the moment of LORR.2,46,47 To be clear, all the six tests (from LORR, through pinches, to corneal and whisking reflexes) were performed together between each block of auditory stimuli, such that the arousing effect of any of the tests would be appreciated.

Loss of righting reflex (LORR) was used as an operational surrogate for loss of consciousness (LOC).2,46,47 Some animals exhibited responses to testing following LORR (eg, withdrawal to paw pinch), and in some cases, these persisted until the session’s end.

Neurophysiological Measures and Definition of Auditory Responsive Units

The neurophysiological measures assessed were the baseline firing rate (firing rate in the 500 ms before stimulus onset), 2nd click firing rate (firing rate in the 25–50 ms following stimulus onset; a measure of the post-onset silence observed under anesthesia and a sensitive measure of sleepiness26), steady-state firing rate (firing rate in the 250–500 ms following stimulus onset), and steady-state ITPC (inter-trial phase coherence in the 250–500 ms following stimulus onset). We chose these measures of neuronal firing and neuronal auditory responsiveness based on previous work39 showing that these are sensitive to depth of anesthesia.

The baseline firing rate can be calculated for any neuronal unit, whether responding to the auditory click-train stimulus or not. However, the other measures all depend on a unit being auditory-responsive, and so for the results presented here we applied the following test: an auditory responsive unit was one with p-value48 of steady state ITPC (250–500 ms post-stimulus onset) <0.05 and with ITPC value >0.15. Definition of a Significant Neuronal TIM Neuron

Auditory responsive neuronal units were defined as exhibiting significant neuronal TIM if they showed statistically significant (p < 0.01, right-tailed Wilcoxon sign-rank with FDR correction for multiple comparisons across neuronal units and behavioral tests) neuronal TIM in any neurophysiological measure (baseline firing rate, 2nd click firing rate, steady-state firing rate, or ITPC) in response to at least two behavioral tests.

Because we could not measure the auditory response neurophysiological measures for non-auditory responsive neurons, these were defined as exhibiting significant neuronal TIM based only on the baseline firing rate (p < 0.01, Wilcoxon sign-rank with FDR correction).

Quantification of Electromyogram (EMG)

To better characterize the effects of each behavioral test and whether it was accompanied by an increase in muscle tone, we quantified the EMG signal around each behavioral test (Figure 2b). To this end, EMG was used as a proxy for overall muscle tone and was quantified as a single number over a block of 30 trials by taking the same 1.5 s window around each stimulus repetition and extracting the EMG (previously filtered around 10–100 Hz, resampled at 1,000 samples/s). The median average deviation (MAD) was calculated for each trial, and then the median of these MADs for the 30 trials in question. MAD, rather than a mean-based measure (such as root-mean-squared/RMS), was chosen to minimize the impact of transients (such as ECG/cardiac depolarizations), which would often affect the EMG during quiet periods of low muscle tone.

Statistical Analysis

We used a nested (hierarchical) mixed linear model analysis throughout the manuscript with two levels (neurons and rats), unless stated otherwise. Only neurons with significant neuronal TIM were included in this analysis. For the neurophysiological measures which required auditory responsiveness (2nd click firing rate and steady state response firing rate) only auditory responsive neurons were included. The effect of drug type on TIM was quantified using a linear model with nested random effects of neuron and rat identity, and fixed effects of drug and brain region (A1 vs PRC). These drug-specific effects are reported with nominal p-values, unadjusted for multiple comparisons, and so should be interpreted as exploratory. To estimate the correlation of EMG TIM with neuronal measure TIM, a nested mixed model was developed, with EMG TIM as fixed effect, and neuronal TIM as the response variable.

Results

To study the effect of behavioral “depth of anesthesia” testing on neuronal firing and auditory responses, we implanted rats (n = 9) with 16–32 electrodes targeting A1 and/or perirhinal cortex. Additionally, a jugular IV catheter was used to gradually infuse a variety of anesthetic agents (each on a separate day; see Methods), while playing auditory click-trains every 1.5 seconds on average. Standard behavioral tests, from loss of righting reflex (LORR) to painful pinching, were performed every 12 minutes (every 360 auditory trials). “Neuronal TIM” (testing-induced modulation, the degree to which neuronal firing or neuronal responsiveness is altered on behavioral testing compared to a control period), was calculated for each behavioral test, allowing each unit to be classified as either showing neuronal TIM or not, and the degree of this effect was quantified.

Number of Neuronal Units Showing Neuronal TIM

Table 1 shows the number of neuronal units identified in each brain region, those showing auditory responsiveness, and whether they showed significant neuronal TIM during behavioral testing. We used a relatively stringent definition of “auditory responsiveness”, requiring a phase-locked response to the 40 Hz click train. Here, we see that units showing neuronal TIM are found mainly under propofol or dexmedetomidine anesthesia and mainly in A1, especially in auditory responsive units.

The key results in A1 are that a subset of neurons, both auditory and non-auditory, exhibit neuronal TIM (Figure 3a, left), demonstrating an increase in baseline firing rate (TIM mean = +0.13 gain, p = 0.024, Figure 3b), 2nd click firing rate (TIM mean = +0.17 gain, p = 0.22, Figure 3c), and steady-state firing rate (TIM mean = +0.12 gain, p = 0.109, Figure 3d), although the latter two did not reach statistical significance. Simultaneous recordings in the PrC showed Neuronal TIM on behavioral testing in a smaller subset of neurons (Figure 3a, right and Figure 3b, p = 0.0019, two-tailed two-proportion z-test), and, overall baseline firing rate TIM in this brain region was not statistically significantly different from zero (mean = +0.022, df = 6.1, t = +0.28, p = 0.79). Correlation analysis revealed that the more the behavioral testing was associated with an increase in muscle tone (EMG), the weaker was the increase in neuronal firing responses, as measured by baseline firing rate across brain regions (correlation = −0.138, df = 3901, t = −5.07, p = 4 × 10−7) and also separately in A1 (correlation = −0.093, t = −2.74, df = 2132, p = 0.006) and PRC (correlation = −0.18, df = 1716, t = −4.2, p = 3 × 10−5). This significant negative correlation was not observed in the other measures of 2nd click firing rate (correlation = +0.025, t = +0.24, df = 565, p = 0.81), or steady state firing rate (correlation = +0.07, t = +1.25, df = 569, p = 0.21).

Propofol recorded over nine sessions showed positive neuronal TIM in all firing rate parameters, including the baseline activity (Figure 4a) of both A1 (mean = +0.23, p = 0.003) and PrC (mean = +0.17, p = 0.013), as well as A1 responses of 2nd click firing rate (mean = +0.31, p = 0.043) and steady-state FR (mean = +0.21, p = 0.003), as shown in Figure 4b and c. Individual neuronal units showing significant neuronal TIM were identified in both A1 and PrC, with a greater number in A1 (32% of auditory units and 8.6% of non-auditory units) compared to the higher brain region (8.4% of units in PrC, p = 0.029, two-tailed two-proportion z-test). Under propofol, EMG TIM was negatively correlated with the neuronal TIM of the baseline firing rate across brain regions (Figure 4e, correlation = −0.11, df = 2023, t = −2.32, p = 0.02), a result which almost but not quite reached statistical significance in A1 (Figure 4e, correlation = −1.07, df = 1085, t = −1.84, p = 0.066).

Ketamine, recorded over three sessions, showed negative neuronal TIM in all firing rate parameters, indicating a reduction in firing rates following behavioral testing compared to a similar control period without testing – either reaching statistical significance, as in PRC baseline firing rate (mean TIM = −0.17, p = 0.018) and A1 steady state response firing rate (mean TIM = −0.19, p = 0.009), or almost significance, as in A1 baseline activity of A1 (mean TIM = −0.12, p = 0.06) and 2nd click firing rate (mean TIM = −0.29, p = 0.09). This is a mirror image of the propofol results (Figure 4a–c). However, similarly to propofol, ketamine showed negative correlation between EMG awakening and baseline firing rate TIM, particularly in the PRC (correlation = −0.29, p = 0.0003, ie, the stronger the EMG awakening, the more negative the baseline firing rate TIM) (Figure 4e–g). Consistent with these results, no neuronal units in either brain region showed significant positive neuronal TIM at the individual level (Table 1).

Dexmedetomidine, recorded over four sessions, showed a result reminiscent of propofol, only weaker, with A1 showing positive TIM (Figure 4a–c), although only steady state response firing rate reached statistical significance (mean TIM = +0.26, p = 0.005, Figure 4c). PrC baseline activity showed negative TIM (mean = −0.17, p = 0.02, Figure 4a). In addition, and similar to other drugs, negative correlation was found between EMG awakening and A1 baseline firing rate (Figure 4e, correlation = −0.23, p = 0.012).

Remifentanil, recorded over four sessions, showed negative TIM in PrC baseline activity (mean TIM = −0.16, p = 0.019) and almost significant negative TIM in steady state firing rate (mean = −0.19, p = 0.067), in a result reminiscent of, but less dramatic than ketamine. Similar to the other drugs, a negative correlation between EMG TIM and baseline firing rate TIM was observed (Figure 4e, A1: correlation = −0.19, p = 0.007).

EMG Testing Induced Modulation (TIM)

In parallel to quantifying how behavioral testing affects neuronal activity, we also assessed how this affected muscle activity, as a proxy for behavioral response or full awakening. To this end, we calculated the level of EMG testing-induced modulation (TIM, ie, the amount of modulation in the EMG (see Methods) on behavioral testing, compared to the control period, calculated in the same way as the neuronal TIM). The results are shown in Figures 3e and 4d and was not significantly different from zero.

Discussion

Behavioral Testing Awakens A1 Neuronal Responses, More Than Higher Level PrC Responses – Result from Pooled Data Across Drugs

We found a higher proportion of both auditory and non-auditory units showing neuronal TIM on behavioral testing (21% and 5.4%, respectively) in A1 than in the perirhinal cortex (0% and 4.7%, respectively). This result mirrors clinical experience and practice, whereby behavioral testing stimuli under sedation initially waken simple brain stem reflexes (such as respiration rate16), then allow simple tasks (eg, recognizing immediately repeated words18), but long-term memory generation or the ability to perform complex tasks continues to be suppressed.18,52–54 Indeed, clinical sedation scales4,6 use this fact that deeper sedation selectively blocks higher purposeful brain functions and requires stronger stimuli to antagonize it, as a way to assess depth of sedation. Furthermore, this continued suppression of higher regions, such as the PrC that precedes the hippocampus (required for memory formation), may be associated with the conscious amnesic states noted in several studies20,21 and used practically by clinicians to suppress recall in surgeries with a high risk of intraoperative awareness.53,55

While the EMG mean TIM was not statistically significantly above zero, which would imply that the rat was not behaviorally waking up on testing, this is perhaps better explained by a relatively lower “N”, given that each session recorded a single EMG channel but potentially dozens of neuronal units.

A possible if intriguing interpretation of the lack of positive correlation between neuronal and EMG TIM, would be that behavioral testing may be causing neuronal TIM without a behavioral correlate. Perhaps there is a change in the rat’s internal brain state that is not necessarily showing in behavior. We note however that the result went further, often showing a negative correlation, and the explanation for this remains unclear.

Neuronal TIM Results According to Anesthetic Drug

A secondary analysis looked at the anesthesia testing effect for each anesthetic agent separately (Figure 4). While these experiments recorded from many neuronal units and behavioral tests (296–1618 tests and units), the limited number of independent sessions (see Table 1) resulted in weaker p-values following mixed linear model testing. Indeed, when inspecting neuronal TIM across individual sessions and animals we noticed considerable inter-session variability, the source of which is unclear at present. Future experiments may be needed to draw conclusions on how much of this is due to differences between animals, between how sessions were conducted, and between drug types. However having stated this note of caution, the following findings are of interest.

Propofol recorded over the greatest number of sessions of any of the drugs (nine), showed the strongest neuronal TIM in A1 (effect size = +0.21 – +0.31 gain across all measures) and also demonstrated it in the higher PrC units (TIM magnitude 0.17, p = 0.013, Figure 4a). This result corresponds to the known awakening from sedation discussed above in the pooled data across agents. Given the known ability of propofol to cause conscious amnesia at lower doses than those required for unconsciousness,53 the apparent ability of behavioral testing following propofol administration to awaken the PrC as well as A1 raises an interesting question as to where this amnesic action of this drug occurs. Our results suggest that it may be happening higher in the memory pathway, for example, in the hippocampus itself; however, we note that this finding would contradict theories explaining retrograde facilitation effects of propofol56 and other GABA-agonists,21 which propose that they act before the hippocampus.57 Alternatively, propofol may be disrupting the content of neuronal transmission in post-A1 brain regions,51 without greatly affecting the baseline firing rate.

Remifentanil did not show neuronal TIM in either brain region, with perhaps even an anti-neuronal TIM effect (Figures 4a and c). This lack of neuronal TIM on behavioral testing may paradoxically be caused by a failure of the remifentanil to cause loss of consciousness. The rats under remifentanil showed a phenomenon of tail-rigidity58,59 and reduced respiratory rate, but largely did not lose righting reflex until very late, if at all. Opiate-only anesthesia in clinical practice has long been known to be a risk for intra-operative awareness60–62 and it is possible that the rats often did not lose consciousness and so did not show changes on attempts to “wake” them. Indeed, despite its adjuvant action in sedations,63 remifentanil is truly better classified as an analgesic rather than sedative or hypnotic drug.64,65

Dexmedatomidine showed some neuronal TIM effect in A1, but not in PrC, and no positive correlation with EMG TIM (Figure 4). The wakening of some neuronal responses in A1 on behavioral testing fits very well with known properties of dexmedetomidine as it is used in clinical practice, namely, that it gives a sleep-like unconscious state from which patients can be readily woken by external stimuli. The lack of positive neuronal TIM in the perirhinal cortex could explain reports of anterograde amnesia under dexmedetomidine,66 despite this being more commonly associated with GABA-A agonist drugs, such as propofol mentioned earlier. The finding of negative or anti-neuronal TIM, with reduced neuronal responses on behavioral testing, remains difficult to explain.

Ketamine showed no- or even anti-TIM in both A1 and PrC, with negative correlation with EMG TIM in PrC (Figure 4). The absence of neuronal TIM may be explained by the characteristic dissociative anesthesia observed with this drug,36 whereby the EEG remains active and wake-like67 sometimes with hallucinations, but not influenced by external events or sensory input. In such cases, behavioral testing would be expected to have less of a wakening effect on an already active cortex. However, this explanation does not explain any anti-TIM results, with reduced neuronal responses following behavioral testing. The explanation for this result remains an open question.

Study Limitations

This study has a few limitations that should be explicitly acknowledged. Firstly, we did not perform tonotopic mapping and auditory field mapping for the neurons recorded in our electrodes targeting A1; however, histology and latency (reported previously39) were consistent with primary auditory cortex neurons.

Secondly, our definition of auditory responsiveness for neurons was relatively strict, requiring clear phase locking to our click-train stimulus; as a result, only a minority of neurons were defined as auditory responsive, even in A1. We made this conservative decision to ensure that neurons being analyzed for changes in auditory features such as 2nd click firing rate and steady-state response firing rates are truly auditory. However, it is certainly possible that neurons responding to the auditory stimulus with, for example, only an onset response or a continuous increase in firing without locking to each click would have been missed and categorized by us as “non-auditory”.

It is also worth noting that while the total number of units and behavioral tests for each region and drug was relatively large (296–1618), these were recorded over relatively few sessions (3–9, Table 1). Therefore, some caution should be exercised when interpreting these results. We addressed this by using nested random effects linear models in our statistical analysis, avoiding exaggeration of statistical significance that would occur if we assumed units were completely independent.

A further practical limitation is that there may be some variation in how the behavioral testing was conducted. We tried to limit this as much as possible, by performing the tests in the same order, by the same researcher. However, even subtle variations in how rapidly a rat is turned on its back or the force applied when pinching could impact on how stimulating the test is. This limitation applies equally to how these behavioral tests are performed in standard common practice. We also note that this experimental design does not separate the effects of painful behavioral tests (eg, pinching) from non-painful (LORR, corneal reflex). A future, larger experiment could separate these effects by performing each test separately and measure TIM, however we note that in standard research practice10,68 multiple tests are often combined.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have shown a modest yet statistically significant modulation of neuronal responses following behavioral testing of depth of anesthesia. In addition, this neuronal TIM is more prominent in lower-order A1 neurons than in higher-order PrC neurons, which may help explain the clinical phenomenon of continuing loss of higher brain functions when patients are woken under sedation. When focusing on individual drugs, these are shown to affect the two brain regions to different extents, which mirror the different clinical phenotypes observed when using the different classes of sedating agents.
