Beyond tumor control: skeletal morbidity in giant prolactinoma
Authors: Taner Dandinoğlu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the recent Endocrine Journal article by Unver Ozkahraman et al., ‘Multimodal management of giant prolactinoma with extensive amyloid spherule deposition: a case report with review of the literature’ [1].

The authors present a remarkable and highly informative case of giant prolactinoma with extensive amyloid spherule deposition, emphasizing dopamine agonist resistance, aggressive local behaviour, and the potential value of multimodal treatment. The detailed discussion of radiotherapy, temozolomide, and possible restoration of dopamine sensitivity provides an important clinical perspective on a rare and challenging entity [1].

I would like to highlight another clinically relevant aspect of giant prolactinoma that may deserve greater attention, particularly in male patients: skeletal morbidity. In the present case, the patient had longstanding hypogonadal symptoms, documented hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and later required testosterone replacement; however, bone health evaluation was not described [1]. Given the established adverse effects of hyperprolactinemia and hypogonadism on bone metabolism, assessment of fracture history and bone mineral density may provide useful complementary clinical information in such patients [2].

This issue may be especially relevant in men with giant prolactinomas, in whom delayed diagnosis and prolonged endocrine dysfunction are common. Vertebral fractures have been reported in male patients with prolactinoma, and severe osteoporosis with multiple fragility fractures, including vertebral and hip fractures, has also been described in association with prolactinoma-related hypogonadism [2, 3]. Recognition of skeletal involvement may therefore help reduce potentially avoidable long-term morbidity.

I believe that, alongside evaluation of tumour extent, treatment resistance, and biochemical response, consideration of bone health may further strengthen the clinical assessment of giant prolactinoma. In selected patients, especially men with prolonged hypogonadal symptoms, structured skeletal evaluation may identify an important but easily overlooked component of disease burden.
