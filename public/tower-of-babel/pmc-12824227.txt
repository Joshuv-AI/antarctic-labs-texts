Diversity and persistence of the RNA virome of Philaenus spumarius, the European vector of Xylella fastidiosa
Authors: Sara Ottati, Luciana Galetto, Francesco Volpe, Nicola Bodino, Cristina Marzachí, Domenico Bosco, Simona Abbá
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Philaenus spumarius is the primary vector of the quarantine plant pathogen Xylella fastidiosa in Europe and is responsible for the devastating Olive Quick Decline Syndrome outbreak in Southern Italy. Despite its importance, little is known about its natural viral community, which could offer novel and sustainable strategies for vector control. In this three-year study, we conducted the first comprehensive characterization of the viral community of P. spumarius from multiple ecologically diverse European sites, including X. fastidiosa-affected areas in Southern Italy. Deep transcriptomic sequencing of 209 field-collected individuals pooled into 11 RNA-seq libraries revealed the presence of 26 RNA viruses. Our findings revealed a rich and structured viral community in populations from Northern Italy and France, contrasting sharply with the reduced viral diversity observed in populations from Southern Italy, where most individuals were virus-free. Temporal comparisons revealed recurrent virus–host associations over the years, and laboratory rearing provided initial insights into viral persistence and transmission dynamics. Although none of the detected viruses caused overt signs of mortality or sterility, their potential sublethal effects and ecological interactions remain unexplored. This study lays the groundwork for future research on the functional roles of insect-associated viruses and emphasizes their potential for developing sustainable, environmentally friendly approaches to managing vectors and reducing the impact of X. fastidiosa on European agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32597-4.

Introduction

Philaenus spumarius L. (Hemiptera: Aphrophoridae), commonly known as the meadow spittlebug, is the main vector of the Gram-negative bacterium Xylella fastidiosa Wells (Proteobacteria: Lysobacteraceae) in Europe and, namely, of the sequence type 53 (subsp. pauca) that causes the devastating outbreak of the Olive Quick Decline Syndrome in the Southern Italy (OQDS). To date, the control of P. spumarius has primarily focused on soil tilling against nymphs1 and the use of synthetic insecticides, such as pyrethroids and neonicotinoids against adults2,3. However, in light of the European Commission’s goal of reducing pesticide use (Directive 2009/128/EC; 6[4]), there is a need for innovative, more environmentally sound control strategies to reduce the insect populations and/or disrupt the bacterium transmission dynamics. Several efforts have been made to identify biological control agents (BCA) and agronomic measures to control spittlebug populations within an Integrated Pest Management framework. Among BCAs, entomopathogenic fungi (EPF)5–7 and an egg parasitoid8 have been discovered, but there is a need to explore other tools.

Insect-specific viruses (ISVs), most of which have RNA genome, show great potential for reducing or even eliminating the need for insecticides in agriculture9. They spread naturally within insect populations and might provide long-term pest control, eliminating the need for continuous insecticide applications. They can interfere with the transmission of pathogens10 to host plants and have minimal off-target effects. Unlike arboviruses, ISVs cannot replicate in vertebrate hosts, infecting only insects11,12. Although some highly pathogenic ISVs, such as baculoviruses, have been successfully used as sustainable biocontrol agents, their narrow host range (predominantly Lepidoptera) and tendency to develop resistance limit their practical application on a large scale13,14. However, it has been shown that ISVs causing covert infections can also influence key insect traits such as fecundity, fitness and longevity15–17. They can also induce behavioral and ecological changes in the host18–20. In the Mediterranean fruit fly Ceratitis capitata, the ISV Ceratitis capitata nora virus (CcaNV) has been shown to enhance the attractiveness of insect larvae to its parasitoid, thereby reducing the probability of survival for the insect host20. Similarly, in Spodoptera exigua, infections with Spodoptera exigua iflavirus 1 (SeIV1) increase both the susceptibility to Bacillus thuringiensis formulation and the mortality caused by the parasitism of Hyposoter didymator18. The role of ISVs in modulating the spread of human pathogens has been widely explored in mosquitoes, where various ISVs interfere with arbovirus replication, possibly affecting insect immune responses or through direct virus–virus interactions22–25.

Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies, metagenomics and phylogenetics have notably improved the identification of RNA viruses associated with insects26,27. This revolution has not only enriched our understanding of the complex co-evolutionary dynamics between insects and their virosphere, but has also prompted the exploration of ISVs as potential tools for plant protection9,28.

In this context, the present study provides a comprehensive characterization of the RNA viruses naturally associated with P. spumarius across different ecotypes and plant hosts in Europe, sampled over two consecutive years. By analyzing only live insects, we captured intact viral RNA and preserved the natural virome composition. This approach enabled the detection of viruses that may not be necessarily lethal, but which could affect insect physiology, reproduction, or vector competence. Furthermore, we evaluated the persistence of these viruses under controlled laboratory conditions and across one generation, despite the significant challenges posed by the insect biological cycle, which is characterized by adult parapause in summer and an obligate egg diapause in winter21. This study establishes the foundation for identifying potential vector-borne viruses as key components of a more sustainable vector control strategy.

Materials and methods

Insect specimen collection

Adult P. spumarius individuals were collected during the summers of 2022 and 2023. Six locations were selected throughout Italy (Cisano, Albenga, Bellino, Chieri, Gallipoli, Valenzano), including two sites within Xylella fastidiosa-infested areas (Gallipoli, Valenzano) (Fig. 1). Additionally, samples were obtained from two locations in France, one in a continental region (Sault) and one on the island of Corsica (Canale di Verde) (Fig. 1). Sault is located in a X. fastidiosa-free area. Conversely, Corsica has been designated a containment area by the EFSA Panel on Plant Health, as X. fastidiosa is established and eradication is no longer feasible.

To maximize ecotype diversity, locations included habitats that varied in terms of vegetation type, moisture regime, elevation, and disturbance levels, ranging from intensively managed agricultural sites to natural environments (Table 1). In 2022, a total of 11 to 30 adult insects were collected per site (Fig. 1, Table S1) from bushes and tree canopies using a sweeping net. The insects were preserved in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before being stored at − 80 °C. To assess the temporal persistence of viral communities, three of the most virus-rich sites sampled in 2022, i.e. Sault, Chieri, and Bellino, were re-sampled in 2023, with approximately 200 specimens collected at each site.

Laboratory rearing and progeny analysis

To assess the persistence of viral communities and investigate their transmission routes (Table S1), two of the three populations sampled in 2023, Sault and Bellino, were reared in laboratory conditions (with a 14:6 h light: dark photoperiod). Insects were kept in clean cages provided with healthy, greenhouse-grown host plants and straw. The Sault population was reared at 26 °C, while the Bellino population was maintained at 20 °C to reflect differences in the original habitat temperatures.

For the Bellino population, 26 mating pairs (one male and one female) were reared in individual cages on chicory (Cichorium intybus), and all females were sampled after 27 days for virome analysis. As regards the Sault group, approximately 100 adults were mass-reared in a single cage on grapevine (Vitis vinifera), chicory and broad bean (Vicia faba). Adult males and females were collected after 82 days for virome analysis. Each rearing (26 mating pairs and one mass-reared population), which was presumed to contain eggs, was transferred to semi-field conditions, consisting of netted boxes placed in a green outdoor area in Turin (Italy) (45.017624 N, 7.641197E). This setting exposed the eggs to natural fluctuations in temperature and humidity, thereby mimicking the overwintering phase of P. spumarius while still preventing external contamination.

In spring, the boxes were brought back to controlled laboratory conditions on grapevine, chicory and broad bean to induce synchronized hatching. Adult insects were collected after approximately two weeks from the emergence of the first individual (Table S1), then stored at − 80 °C for virome analysis. A schematic representation of the experimental workflow is depicted in Fig. 2.

RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

We focused on RNA viruses because they represent the most diverse and abundant component of insect virospheres and are the primary targets of studies on hemipteran-associated viruses27,29,30. Their typically smaller genomes, compared with those of DNA viruses, also make them easier to manipulate for biotechnological applications.

Nucleic acid extraction was performed from single insect according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the Spectrum™ Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich). The DNAse step was omitted to extract both DNA and RNA at the same time. Nucleic acid extracts from 11 to 30 individuals collected at the same location were pooled in an equimolar ratio and sent to Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) for both total RNA and small RNA sequencing. The Ribo-Zero Gold Kit (Human/Mouse/Rat) (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to deplete ribosomal RNA. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (v2) (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced using the NovaSeq system (2 × 150). For smallRNA (sRNA) sequencing, libraries were prepared with SMARTer smRNA-Seq Kit for Illumina (Takara Bio, USA) and sequenced by NovaSeq X (2 × 150).

Bioinformatics analysis and viral name assignment

For transcriptome analysis, raw paired-end sequence files were quality-checked, cleaned, and filtered using the BBTools suite v37.62. Briefly, adapters, short reads, artifacts and ribosomal sequences were removed using BBDuk. P. spumarius-mapping reads were also removed using the insect transcriptome assembly GenBank accession GCZA00000000.1 BBMerge and BBNorm were then used to merge overlapping paired-end reads and normalize read coverage. The remaining reads were de novo assembled using Trinity v2.9.031 with default parameters. Viral sequences were selected by querying the assembled sequences against the NCBI non-redundant protein database (last accessed on 07-02-2024) using DIAMOND blastx. For those sequences with a significant match (Evalue < 0.0001), the best viral hits were considered for taxonomic classification. Viral names were assigned as follows: the name of the host Philaenus spumarius, followed by the root of the viral family name (e.g. Ifla = Iflaviridae), plus the word “virus”, and a progressive identification number to distinguish different viruses within the same family (e.g. Philaenus spumarius iflavirus 1). Abbreviations of viral names report the initials of the host name (Ps).

For sRNA sequencing, raw reads were trimmed from adapters with Cutadapt v. 4.632 and filtered out according to (a) quality and length (minimum 18 nt; maximum 32 nt) and (b) mapping onto P. spumarius identified viruses. Bowtie v. 1.1.2 software33 with no mismatch allowed in the alignment was used to determine sRNA abundance profiles. sRNA counts were normalized by the total number of high-quality reads to account for the technical differences across libraries. As a negative control, one sRNA library was prepared from virus-negative insects only.

The R packages UpSetR v1.4.0 and ComplexHeatmap v1.3.3 were used to generate the heatmaps and the UpSet plots, respectively.

Viral prevalence analysis and coinfection index calculation

To confirm the RNA-seq results, primers were designed to amplify a small region of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) gene of each putative viral species (Table S2). First, we tested virus-specific primer pairs by quantitative PCR (qPCR) on non-DNAse-treated and non-reverse-transcribed aliquots of each RNA pool sent for sequencing to exclude the presence of potentially Endogenized Viral Elements (EVEs). Then, cDNA was synthesized from 400 ng of DNAse-treated RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) with random primers. The resulting cDNA was used as a template for amplification in a 10 µL reaction with 1x iTaq Universal SYBR green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Reactions were run in duplicate. Cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, and 40 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s and 58 °C for 30 s of annealing/extension step. Melting curve was analyzed by slow heating from 65 °C to 95°Cat a rate of 0.3 °C/cycle.

When pooled samples resulted positive for a given viral species, species-specific primers were used on RNA extracted from individual P. spumarius specimens to assess virus prevalence. Prevalence was calculated as the percentage of individuals testing positive for each newly identified virus within each population.

For the Sault rearing, each offspring was screened for viruses that had been detected in at least one parent, either male or female. For the Bellino rearing, all offspring, regardless of which mating pair they originated from, were tested for any virus detected in at least one of the 26 parental females.

For each population, the coinfection index was calculated as the average number of viral species infecting that population, divided by the sum of P. spumarius individuals that tested positive for at least one viral species in that population.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the deduced protein sequences of the conserved domains of RNA-dependent RNA polymerases (RdRps).

Phylogenetic trees of potential non-ISVs were built using clades, nomenclature and sequences provided by Shi et al. 201626 (Table S3). The phylogenetic tree of bunyaviruses was based on the recent reclassification proposed by Kuhn et al., 202434 (Table S3). RdRp amino acid sequences of the newly discovered viruses were aligned using MAFFT V7.52635 and then trimmed to include only conserved domains. Phylogenetic trees were then generated using the maximum likelihood approach (ML) implemented in IQ-TREE2 V2.3.536 with default parameters, and visualized and edited using iTOL37.

Results

Diversity of RNA viruses associated to field-caught Philaenus spumarius

A total of 26 distinct RNA viruses (Table 2) were identified in 11 RNA-seq libraries obtained from pools of P. spumarius individuals collected at eight locations in Italy and France in 2022, three of which (Sault, Bellino, and Chieri) were re-sampled in 2023. Only complete or near-complete viral genomes were reported, defined as sequences encompassing the full coding potential expected based on their closest known viral relatives (Fig. 3, Table S4). Detailed information regarding the normalized abundance of viral reads detected in each RNA-seq library are reported in Table S4.

Fifteen were single-stranded positive-sense RNA (ssRNA+) viruses. Based on their genome organization, blastP results and phylogeny, six are presumably ISVs of P. spumarius. Four were assigned to the family Iflaviridae (Ps_Ifla1, Ps_Ifla2, Ps_Ifla3, Ps_Ifla4), one to the family Flaviviridae (Ps_Jigmen1), one to the order Nidovirales (Ps_Nido1), and one (Ps_Quenya1) to the recently described lineage of quenyaviruses38. Of the remaing ssRNA + viruses, seven are potential mycoviruses belonging to the families Narnaviridae (Psa_Narna1, Psa_Narna2, Psa_Narna3), Mitoviridae (Psa_Mito1, Psa_Mito2), and Botourmiaviridae (Psa_Ourmia1, Psa_Ourmia2), while one (Psa_Tombus1) showed a distant relationship to the plant virus family Tombusviridae. These putative non-ISVs were provisionally named Philaenus spumarius-associated viruses (Psa).

Going into more detail, jingmenviruses represent a divergent lineage within the family Flaviviridae, which is normally characterized by a monopartite + ssRNA genome. Although most jingenviruses are composed of four segments39, the genome of Ps_Jingmen1 was found to comprise six putative viral segments, similarly to some arthropod-associated jingmenviruses identified by Valle and colleagues40.

Quenyaviruses typically possess five genome segments. In our study, however, we identified six segments associated with Ps_Quenya1. Notably, segments 5 shared over 50% amino acid identity with a putative segment 6 (named “Hypothetical protein” in Table S4), suggesting possible duplication or functional redundancy.

As regards the putative non-ISVs, phylogenetic analyses were conducted to clarify their host assignment, whether fungal, plant, or insect. The results indicated that Psa_Narna2, Psa_Narna3, Psa_Mito1, sa_Mito2, Psa_Ourmia1, and Psa_Ourmia2 clustered with viral sequences previously detected in non-arthropod hosts, confirming their mycoviral origin (Figure S1). By contrast, PSa_Narna1 grouped with other putative insect narna-like viruses. Similarly, Psa_Tombus1 clustered within a sister clade of tombus-like viruses previously identified in arthropods (Figure S2). Therefore, the phylogenetic analyses tended to support the identification of Psa_Narna1 and Psa_Tombus1 as true ISVs. In addition, plant tombusviruses are characterized by a monosegmented genome with five ORFs, whereas Psa_Tombus1 appeared to have a bipartite genome, with at least three predicted ORFs: two on the longer segment that codes for the RdRp and at least one on the second segment. No movement protein typically associated with plant-infecting tombusviruses was identified.

Nine single-stranded negative-sense RNA (ssRNA−) ISVs were also identified. Among these viruses, two belonged to the family Rhabdoviridae (Ps_Rhabdo1, Ps_Rhabdo2), two to the family Orthomyxoviridae (Ps_Ortho1, Ps_Ortho2), and five to lineages formerly grouped under the family Bunyaviridae, which has been recently reclassified by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) into the class Bunyaviricetes and the orders Elliovirales and Hareavirales34. According to the phylogenetic analysis (Figure S3), Ps_Phenui1 and Ps_Phenui2 clustered with members of the family Phenuiviridae (order Hareavirales), whereas the other three identified bunyaviruses clustered with members of the order Elliovirales, more specifically Ps_Phasma1 and Ps_Phasma2 were assigned to the family Phasmaviridae. The third was generically named Philaenus spumarius Elliovirales virus 1 (Ps_Elliovirales1), because, despite being phylogenetically close to Hantaviridae and Phasmaviridae, it formed a distinct well-supported clade with other four unclassified bunyaviruses identified in insects. Notably, Ps_Elliovirales1 was the only bunyavirus identified in this study that appeared to have only two genomic segments.

As regards the two identified rhabdoviruses, Ps_Rhabdo2 showed a typical monosegmented genome organization, whereas Ps_Rhabdo1 appeared to have a bi-segmented genome. Despite our best efforts, we were unable to identify any reads linking the two segments of Ps_Rhabdo1. However, it is noteworthy that Ps_Rhabdo1 had approximately tenfold lower read coverage than Ps_Rhabdo2, so we cannot definitively exclude the possibility that the two segments are connected.

Finally, we identified two double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) viruses belonging to the order Reovirales, Ps_Reo1 and Ps_Reo2, characterized by ten and twelve segments, respectively.

Small-interfering RNA

The small RNA libraries labeled 1BXY to 8BXY did not correspond to the sampling locations as the RNA-seq libraries. These libraries were constructed ad hoc to investigate the insect antiviral response to viruses identified in the 2022 sampling, with a focus on separating members of the Iflaviridae family and the Bunyaviricetes class (the most prevalent groups) to minimize cross-mapping of short reads (Table S5). Library 3BXY, which included only virus-negative insects, served as the negative control. Only libraries Sau_BXY, Bel_BXY, and Chi_BXY matched the RNA samples used in the 2023 RNA-seq libraries. Detailed information regarding normalized abundance of virus-derived small RNAs (vsRNAs) reads detected in each small RNA-seq library are reported in Table S6.

Virus-derived small RNA reads were quantified across all libraries for each virus and mapped to the corresponding genome, considering strand specificity and restricting the analysis to read lengths between 18 and 32 nucleotides (Figure S4). The results revealed varying levels of vsRNAs mapping across viral species, likely reflecting differences in viral abundance, replication dynamics, and recognition by the host RNA silencing machinery. The proportion of vsRNAs mapping to viral sequences ranged from 0.0% (3_BXY, the negative control) to 0.55% in Sau_BXY of the total sRNA reads. Most vsRNAs were 21–23 nt in length, indicative of Dicer-mediated processing. Ps_Nido1, Psa_Mito1, Psa_Mito2, Psa_Ourmia1, Psa_Ourmia2, Ps_Ifla1, Ps_Ifla3, Ps_Ifla4, Ps_Reo2, Ps_Rhabdo1, Psa_Narna2, Ps_Narna3, Ps_Ortho2, Ps_Phasma2, and Ps_Phenui2 were characterized by less than 300 mapping reads (Fig. 4, Table S6). This result was expected for the putative mycoviruses, whereas for ISVs the low counts may indicate incidental or transient infections.

The mapping profile of Psa_Tombus1 was rather atypical, with a distinct 25-nt peak that was not observed in any other virus analyzed in this study.

Spatial and temporal dynamics of virome composition in P. spumarius field populations

The viral prevalence and diversity varied markedly across locations, with frequent occurrences of mixed infections (Fig. 5, Table S7). Detailed information regarding prevalence of viral infections and coinfection indexes across the 11 P. spumarius RNA-seq libraries from 2022 to 2023 are presented in Table S7. Coinfection index ranged from 1.0 (SD = 0.0) in Val_22 to 2.8 (SD = 1.7) in Bel_23, where most individuals carried multiple viruses.

Sau_23 and Bel_23 harbored the highest number of viral species (10 and 11, respectively), whereas only one species was found in Cis_22 and Val_22. The Gal_22 library showed no detectable viral infections.

Prevalence varied widely among virus species, ranging from 3.2% for Ps_Reo1, Ps_Rhabdo2 and Ps_Quenya1 in Sau_22 to 100% for Ps_Ifla2 in Bel_22 and Bel_23.

Regarding the three populations sampled across two consecutive years (Sault, Chieri, and Bellino), although viral prevalence changed considerably, most of the viral species identified in 2022 were also detected in 2023. However, ten viral species that were absent in 2022 were identified in 2023, including five mycoviruses and five ISVs. Interestingly, all mycoviruses were each found in only a single individual. Despite the in silico identification of Psa_Ourmia2 in Bel_23, qPCR failed to detect any positive individuals. Nevertheless, it is likely that only one insect was positive, as the normalized read counts for both botourmiaviruses were similar. In contrast, the putative ISV Psa_Narna1 was consistently detected in field populations from Bellino over two consecutive years. The other putative ISV, Psa_Tombus1, was also persistently found in 2022 and 2023 in field populations from Sault and Chieri, with prevalence ranging from 45.2% in Sau_23 to 19% in Chi_23.

Overall, the coinfection index of the three populations remained relatively stable over two years. Notably, one individual from Bel_23 was simultaneously infected with nine different viral species.

Persistence of viral infections in P. spumarius under controlled laboratory conditions

We evaluated the persistence of the virus identified in Sau_23 and Bel_23 by rearing field-collected individuals under controlled laboratory conditions. The Sault population was mass-reared, with adults allowed to mate freely within a single cage for 82 days. In contrast, the Bellino population was divided into 26 mating pairs housed in individual cages for 27 days. At the end of the rearing period, adults from both populations were collected for virome characterization (Table 3). This setup was designed to minimize the possibility that viral detections in field-collected individuals were due to ingested plant sap or contaminations from microorganisms (e.g., fungi, nematodes).

Following confinement, most ISVs that were initially identified in the field persisted under laboratory conditions. In contrast, mitoviruses, and botourmiaviruses appeared to be more transient, as they were only detected in field samples. This pattern facilitates the differentiation between insect-specific viruses and probable environmentally acquired viral contaminants. Notably, Psa_Tombus1 and Psa_Narna1 remained detectable following laboratory rearing.

Overall, viral diversity declined slightly under laboratory conditions compared to field populations. In Sault the number of detected viral species decreased from 10 to 8, while in Bellino from 11 to 8 (Table 3). Despite this reduction in richness, the overall viral prevalence increased in Sault, with the proportion of virus-positive individuals rising from 52.4% in the field to 100% after rearing. Conversely, Bellino lab rearing exhibited a slight decrease in the overall prevalence (− 3%).

The prevalence of each viral species also shifted notably under laboratory conditions. Psa_Tombus1, Ps_Ifla1, Ps_Ifla4, and Ps_Phasma2 showed increased prevalence under laboratory conditions. These patterns suggest the presence of efficient horizontal transmission routes for these viruses.

Finally, the coinfection index remained stable in Bellino, but increased significantly in Sault, rising from 1.8 to 2.7. This result suggests that horizontal transmission is more efficient in mass-reared populations.

Persistence of viral infections in the F1 generation after egg diapause for overwintering

After overwintering, three of the eight viral species present in the parental generation were detected in the F1 generation of the Sault population: Psa_Tombus1 (with 100% prevalence), Ps_Elliovirales1 (with 36.8% prevalence) and Ps_Jingmen1 (with 78.9% prevalence). Therefore, it appears that these viruses may be transmitted vertically. Ps_Ifla1, Ps_Ifla3, Ps_Phasma2, Ps_Reo1 and Ps_Quenya1 were not present in the F1 generation.

In the Bellino pairings, only 9 out of 26 females successfully produced adult offspring (Table S8). Of these, two females infected with Ps_Ifla4 produced offspring that tested positive for the same virus, which suggests that vertical transmission may have occurred. Ps_Ifla1, Ps_Ifla2, and Ps_Ortho1, although detected in parental individuals, were not found in any F1 offspring.

Notably, two out of 20 offspring from a female that was negative for Ps_Phenui1 were positive for this virus, while the progeny of three Ps_Phenui1-positive females resulted negative for this virus (Table S8). Similarly, Ps_Ifla4 was detected in the offspring of uninfected female. This unexpected pattern raises the possibility of patrilineal vertical transmission. No offspring were obtained from females infected with Ps_Ifla3, Psa_Narna1 or Ps_Ortho2, preventing the assessment of their potential vertical transmission.

Discussion

The role of natural regulation of P. spumarius populations remains still poorly explored and, in this perspective, the contribution of biocontrol agents could be essential for developing effective and sustainable pest management strategies. A comprehensive assessment should therefore include parasitoids, predators, and insect-pathogenic microorganisms. Notably, however, viruses associated with spittlebugs have received no attention to date. In this study, we aimed to characterize the virosphere associated with natural populations of P. spumarius. Our objective was not only to identify potentially lethal pathogens, but also to detect viruses that may affect insect fitness or vector competence. This approach increases the range of candidate agents that may prove relevant for future biocontrol or biotechnological applications.

Geographic patterns in P. spumarius virome composition

Here, we present the first comprehensive virome analysis of P. spumarius from multiple field sites, including areas affected by Xylella in Apulia (Italy). Transcriptomic sequencing of 11 RNA-seq libraries from diverse ecological contexts revealed 26 novel viral species, highlighting virome differences between X. fastidiosa-infected and uninfected areas. Despite that, no X. fastidiosa- mapping reads were detected in any library, confirming the absence of active bacterial infection in the sampled populations. This finding ensures that the virome profiles reported here reflect natural insect–virus interactions, unconfounded by host responses to bacterial infection. Viral richness and prevalence varied notably across geographic regions. Populations from Northern Italy and France exhibited greater viral diversity and higher coinfection indexes compared to those from Southern Italy, where X. fastidiosa-affected areas harbored only one viral species (Valenzano) or none (Gallipoli). This spatial heterogeneity likely reflects regional differences in climate, vegetation, and host plant diversity and association41,42, all of which are known to influence hemipteran ecology and behavior43.

Host assignment of putative non-insect specific viruses

Seven of the identified viruses are phylogenetically related to known mycoviruses and one to a plant-infecting virus. The mycoviruses Psa_Ourmia1, Psa_Mito1, Psa_Mito2, Psa_Narna2, and Psa_Narna3 were each detected in only one of the 209 field-collected individuals and frequently co-occurred within the same specimen. Their extremely low prevalence in field-caught individuals and phylogenetical analysis suggest that they represent an environmental contamination rather than a stable association with P. spumarius. In contrast, there are several lines of evidence that indicate that Psa_Tombus1 and Psa_Narna1 are likely insect-specific viruses.

Psa_Tombus1 was detected in field-collected populations from both Sault and Chieri in 2022 and 2023. In the Sault mass rearing, its prevalence increased from 45% to 100% under controlled laboratory conditions, suggesting efficient horizontal transmission, as no offspring were present during that period. Environmental contamination through the ingestion of tombusvirus-infected plants is unlikely becauseall host plants were greenhouse-grown under virus-free conditions and sourced from the same provenance for all the rearing experiments. Psa_Tombus1 was only observed in the rearing initiated with infected field individuals and was never detected in any other rearing. In addition, it was detected with 100% prevalence in the F1 generation following egg diapause for overwintering. These findings point to a mixed transmission strategy for this virus, likely involving both vertical and horizontal routes. The phylogenetic analysis, along with the absence of a typical plant movement protein, further reinforces the hypothesis of Psa_Tombus1 as an ISV. Recent studies have reported similar tombus-like viruses with both mono- and bipartite genome structures in diverse non-plant hosts, including marine invertebrates and terrestrial arthropods26,44. The placement of Psa_Tombus1 within a clade largely composed of arthropod-associated viruses aligns with emerging hypotheses that some plant-associated viruses may have originated in arthropods through horizontal gene transfer45,46. Interestingly, the vsRNA profile of Psa_Tombus1 showed an unusual 25-nt peak, distinct from typical insect or plant RNAi responses, which requires further investigations.

PSa_Narna1 exhibited more than fivefold higher read coverage compared to confirmed mycoviruses and was consistently detected in Bellino populations over two consecutive years. Its prevalence over time was comparable to that of iflaviruses Ps_Ifla3 and Ps_Ifla4, which are well-characterized insect-specific viruses. Phylogenetic analysis further placed Psa_Narna1 within a clade of narnaviruses previously identified in association with other insect hosts. Collectively, these lines of evidence support the classification of Psa_Narna1 as a bona fide insect-specific virus, consistent with prior findings in other insect species20,47,48.

Temporal variation in P. spumarius virome composition and viral transmission routes

Most viruses identified in Bellino, Chieri and Sault in 2022 were also detected in 2023. This pattern aligns with observations in Aedes and Culex mosquitoes, where a stable, species-specific “core eukaryotic virome” has been observed despite environmental fluctuations47,49–51.

Interestingly, some viruses maintained or even increased in prevalence under laboratory conditions. For instance, Ps_Ifla1 prevalence notably increased in the Sault mass rearing, suggesting efficient horizontal transmission, likely via direct contact, shared feeding, or contaminated excretions, as seen in other insect-specific viruses52–54. Its absence in the F1 generation further supports horizontal transmission as the primary route. Similar transmission patterns are hypothesized for Ps_Ifla2, Ps_Ifla3, Ps_Reo1, Ps_Phasma2, Ps_Ortho1, and Ps_Ortho2.

In contrast, the prevalence of Ps_Jingmen1 and Ps_Elliovirales1 remained relatively stable during lab rearing, but both viruses were detected in the F1 generation, suggesting the occurrence of vertical transmission. However, horizontal transmission among offspring cannot be fully excluded because (a) overwintering and hatching occurred on the original straw substrate, possibly contaminated by parental excretions, and (b) F1 adults were collected two weeks after emergence, allowing time for potential horizontal transmission. Nonetheless, vertical transmission appears to be the preferential route in both cases.

Ps_Phenui1 and Ps_Ifla4 showed unusual transmission patterns: Ps_Phenui1 appeared only in progeny of virus-negative females, while Ps_Ifla4 was found both in offspring of infected and uninfected females. These suggest possible, though low-efficiency, paternal transmission. While maternal vertical transmission is extensively documented, the paternal inheritance remains less well characterized, with reports primarily limited to mosquitoes, fruit flies, and leafhoppers55–57. However, the lack of infection data for males prior to oviposition limits our ability to determine the exact transmission routes of these two viruses.

Despite the lack of data on the fitness and longevity of infected insects, preliminary observations indicate that viruses persisting under laboratory conditions did not induce overt symptoms or premature mortality.

Conclusions

As viral genome databases continue to expand, a growing gap has emerged between the accumulation of sequence data and our understanding of virus biology, including host range, pathogenicity, and transmission mechanisms58. Most virome studies focus on cataloging viral diversity but rarely explore how insect-specific viruses are transmitted. This study addresses that gap by not only identifying diverse viruses associated with P. spumarius, but also proposing hypotheses regarding their transmission routes. This contribution is of particular significance given the biological complexity of working with P. spumarius, a univoltine species with adult summer parapause and obligate egg diapause in winter21. Rearing this insect under semi-field conditions requires precise control of temperature and photoperiod to simulate seasonal changes. By successfully maintaining populations through diapause, we assessed potential vertical transmission and monitored virome persistence across one generation. These findings are both novel and foundational, offering critical insights that may guide the selection of candidate viruses with favourable traits, such as stability and transmissibility, for future development as biological control agents. Although none of the viruses identified appear lethal, prior studies show that covert infections can produce sublethal effects, including altered development, skewed sex ratios, or increased susceptibility to parasitoids18,59. Such effects could still contribute to population suppression and warrant further investigation within integrated pest management strategies against P. spumarius, particularly considering the low viral richness and diversity observed in populations sampled from X. fastidiosa-infected areas.
