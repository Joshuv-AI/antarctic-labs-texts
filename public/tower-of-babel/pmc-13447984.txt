Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction: A Technique Utilizing Tibial Double Knotless Anchors Fixation and Femoral Suspensory Fixation
Authors: Zhenlong Liu, Defu Liu, Tianying Tang, Tong Pan, Xintong Jiang, Xi Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is a critical stabilizing structure within the knee joint. Although its injury is relatively uncommon, severe cases can lead to significant dysfunction and long‐term sequelae. PCL reconstruction is an essential treatment for PCL injuries, and among traditional techniques, trans‐tibial interference screw fixation is a common method. However, due to the higher graft length requirement in PCL reconstruction, interference screw fixation is prone to fixation laxity resulting from insufficient graft length. To address the issue, we describe an improved PCL reconstruction technique utilizing an autologous hamstring tendon graft with tibial double knotless anchor fixation and femoral suspensory fixation. This method enhances graft‐bone tunnel contact, effectively resolves fixation failure issues caused by graft length limitations, and provides greater fixation security and biomechanical advantages.

This approach enables the functional reconstruction of the PCL while providing greater fixation security and biomechanical advantages. The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is a crucial stabilizing structure within the knee joint, primarily functioning to limit posterior tibial translation. PCL injuries are relatively uncommon, ranging from 2% to 3% of all knee joint injuries and 3% to 37% of knee ligament injuries. 1 , 2 However, they are frequently associated with multiligament damage, often resulting from knee dislocations or high‐energy sports trauma, which leads to significant functional deficits and long‐term sequelae. 3 Although nonoperative treatment is appropriate for low‐grade, isolated injuries (e.g., Grade I or II laxity), surgical reconstruction has become the standard of care for symptomatic Grade III injuries or cases involving multiple ligament tears. 4

In recent years, PCL reconstruction techniques have continuously evolved, driven by advancements in arthroscopic methods and fixation devices. Current PCL reconstruction involves various approaches, with the predominant techniques including single‐bundle and double‐bundle reconstruction, 5 a range of graft choices (such as autologous hamstring 6 , 7 or quadriceps tendons 8 ), and different fixation modalities. For trans‐tibial fixation, the femoral side commonly utilizes suspensory devices 7 , 8 (e.g., TightRope), while the tibial side often employs an interference screw. 9 , 10 However, tibial fixation using interference screws presents several significant disadvantages. 5 , 9 , 10 , 11 Due to the presence of the PCL facet, the required intra‐articular graft length is greater, typically necessitating an additional approximately 1 cm. Concurrently, the tibial tunnel is generally longer than that required for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, which can result in the graft barely exiting the tibial tunnel. If interference screw fixation is used, the potential insufficient graft length may lead to the screw pushing the graft further into the tunnel during fixation, consequently causing graft laxity. Furthermore, due to the inadequate graft length, the interference screw may need to be positioned deeper within the tunnel. This prevents fixation against the cortical bone, resulting in fixation primarily within the cancellous bone, which compromises overall fixation strength. Recent technical developments aim to address these issues, such as the “all‐inside” technique with suspensory fixation on both sides to minimize bone tunnel length, 6 the use of a bone‐patellar tendon autograft with cortical suspensory fixation, 8 and the augmentation of the graft with mesh combined with transtibial tuberosity fixation to reduce graft abrasion and enhance fixation strength. 7

We describe an improved PCL reconstruction technique utilizing an autologous hamstring tendon graft. This approach combines tibial double knotless anchor fixation with femoral suspensory fixation, which facilitates better graft‐to‐tunnel contact to promote healing. Compared with tibial interference screw fixation, the double knotless anchor fixation method effectively addresses issues such as fixation laxity caused by insufficient graft length.

Preoperative Evaluation

Physical examinations, radiographs, and magnetic resonance imaging are conducted before the operation to evaluate PCL injuries.

Preparation

The patient is positioned in the supine position. Aseptic preparation and anesthesia is performed, and the corresponding anatomical positions of the knee joint are marked.

Surgical Procedure

An approximately 3 cm longitudinal incision is made on the medial side of the tibial tuberosity, and the subcutaneous fat is cleared to expose the pes anserinus. The semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are harvested using a standard technique, and all attached muscle tissue is removed. Both tendons, measuring 28 cm or more in length, are doubled to create a quadrupled graft. The graft diameter is then measured, and it is typically ranges from 8 to 10 mm. Finally, the end of the graft are sutured with Ethibond to prevent unraveling (Figure 1).

Superior view of the graft (blue arrows), fully prepared and tensioned. Each tendon, with a minimum length of 28 cm, is folded twice to create a quadrupled graft. Both ends are firmly woven with knitting thread.

The arthroscope is inserted through the conventional approach (anterolateral (AL) and anteromedial portals) to explore the internal structure of the knee joint, and evaluate the stump of PCL, as well as the combined injury of the meniscus and cartilage. The PCL remnant and the medial wall of the intercondylar notch are cleared (Figure 2). Subsequently, the superior border of the medial wall is exposed, and an intercondylar notchplasty is performed. Finally, the fat pad and anterior synovial folds between the condyles are cleared to expose the field of vision.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral portal with the patient in the supine position (right knee). Observe the remnant of the posterior cruciate ligament (blue arrow) under arthroscopy.

Using a PCL femoral tunnel guide introduced via the AL portal (Figure 3), the anatomical landmark for the PCL femoral insertion is located on the medial femoral condyle. For single‐bundle reconstruction, a position slightly anterior to the anatomical AL bundle insertion is typically chosen, ensuring it is superior to the posterior horn of the meniscus and avoids damaging the articular cartilage. The femoral tunnel is then drilled to the size matching the graft (typically 8‐10 mm) using a 4.5 mm drill (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) initially (Figure 4). The length of the total femoral tunnel is 35‐40 mm (Figure 5). A long guide pin is used to pass a No. 5 suture through the femoral tunnel, preparing for subsequent graft passage.

The posteromedial portal is established 2 cm above the posterior edge of the medial joint line with the knee flexed to 90° (Figure 6). This portal is used for visualization to ensure the popliteal artery is not injured during the drilling for the tibial tunnel. During tibial tunnel preparation, protection of the posterior neurovascular structures is paramount. The established posteromedial portal allows for direct arthroscopic visualization of the posterior capsule and the PCL tibial footprint. The tip of the PCL tibial guide (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is carefully positioned at the desired insertion point. While drilling, the guide not only ensures accurate tunnel trajectory but also serves as a physical protector, shielding the posterior capsule and the underlying popliteal neurovascular bundle (which includes the popliteal artery, vein, and tibial nerve) by preventing inadvertent advancement of instruments posteriorly.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral portal with the patient in the supine position (right knee). The blue arrow represents the established posteromedial portal.

The anatomical insertion footprint for the PCL on the posterior of the tibial plateau is located using a PCL tibial guide through the medial aspect of the initial tendon harvest incision (Figure 7). The positioning guide is set to 55°, and an 8 mm drill is used for drilling, until the size of the graft is achieved. Simultaneously, a double‐strand polydioxanone suture is passed through the tunnel using a tube (Figure 8). A suture retriever (Arthrex, Shanghai) is then used to pull the polydioxanone suture loop from posterior to anterior, serving as the traction suture for the tibial tunnel. The tunnel's entry point is carefully cleared and shaped using a shaver (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA). The tibial insertion site of the PCL is anatomically located at the center of the PCL footprint, positioned slightly posterolateral.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the posteromedial portal with the patient in the supine position (right knee). The blue arrow represents the posterior cruciate ligament tibial guide for the tibial tunnel.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the posteromedial portal with the patient in the supine position (right knee). The blue arrow represents the double‐strand polydioxanone suture passing through the tunnel.

Both traction guides (sutures) from the femoral and tibial tunnels are within the joint space. These two traction guides are then pulled out through the AL portal to the exterior, and the graft is introduced. Use one of the traction loops to pull the graft into the femoral tunnel through the braided suture at the end of the graft (Figure 9). After passing through the femoral tunnel, the suspensory device is deployed and flipped for femoral fixation. The superior‐medial portal is established to directly visualize the deployment of the titanium button (Arthrex, Shanghai) as it exits the tunnel, ensuring it flips correctly and is fixed to the femoral tunnel without catching on surrounding soft tissues (Figure 10). Using the second traction loop, the graft is pulled into the tibial side by the braided suture at the other end of the graft (the two traction loops are respectively used to pull the braided lines at both ends of the graft, and both are pulled out from the AL entry point to the bone tunnels). The graft is then fixed under manual anterior drawer stress with the knee flexed between 70 and 90°. Two 4.75 mm bone tunnels are drilled for tibial fixation. After the tendon graft passes through the tibial tunnel, two 4.5 mm PUSHLOCK knotless anchors (Arthrex, Shanghai) are fixed to two newly drilled tunnels. One knotless anchor tightens the tension reduction wire, and the other fixes the braided suture (used when the graft length is insufficient to exit the tibial side; Figure 11). Rinse, suture each layer, apply pressure bandaging, and fix the splint in a straight position.

DISCUSSION

This article describes an improved PCL reconstruction technique utilizing tibial double knotless anchors fixation, which aims to address the challenges associated with traditional interference screw fixation. The advantages and disadvantages are summarized in Table 2. Conventional interference screw fixation is susceptible to rupture of the posterior tibial cortex due to radial forces, is constrained by insufficient graft length, and its inability to adjust tension post‐fixation often results in postoperative laxity. 12 Due to the typically longer bone tunnels and consequent greater graft length requirement in PCL reconstruction, conventional interference screw fixation is susceptible to issues arising from insufficient graft length. 9 , 11 , 12 Specifically, the screw may potentially push the graft further into the tunnel during fixation, leading to graft laxity. Furthermore, the interference screw may need to be placed deeper within the tunnel, preventing secure fixation against the cortical bone and resulting in fixation failure.

In contrast, the knotless anchor fixation provides a better solution to the PCL construction. Knotless anchors fixation can successfully address issues such as inadequate fixation strength resulting from insufficient graft length. Compared with interference screws, knotless anchors are smaller in size and induce fewer adverse tissue reactions. Furthermore, this fixation method increases the contact area at the graft‐bone interface, thereby promoting superior healing. The knotless anchor technique has also been successfully employed in other complex knee ligament reconstructions, showing its utility for secure fixation in confined spaces or where graft length may be variable. 13

Femoral suspensory fixation is widely accepted and utilized in various contemporary techniques. 6 , 8 However, its success relies on the reliable flipping of the titanium plate on the lateral femoral cortex. Intraoperative direct visualization of this deployment via the superomedial portal is a crucial step to ensure fixation security.

Although this improved technique offers biomechanical advantages, long‐term clinical follow‐up data are essential to further validate its effectiveness and stability in restoring posterior knee stability and improving patient functional outcomes.
