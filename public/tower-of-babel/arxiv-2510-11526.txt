Buildings for Synthesis with Clifford+R
Mark Deaconu, Nihar Gargava, Amolak Ratan Kalra, Michele Mosca, Jon Yard

Abstract
We study the problem of exact synthesis for the Clifford + 𝖱 +\mathsf{R} gate set and give the explicit structure of the underlying Bruhat-Tits building for this group. In this process, we also give an alternative proof of the arithmetic nature of the Clifford + 𝖱 +\mathsf{R} gate set.

1.1
General synthesis problem

Let
\PU(d)
be the group of unitary matrices up to phases and let
d(\cdot,\cdot)
be some natural distance function on
\PU(d)

The general circuit synthesis problem can be stated as follows: Given a target unitary matrix
U\in\PU(d)
and a universal gate set
G=\{g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\}\subseteq\PU(d)
, output a circuit consisting of words over
G
that realizes
U
. A good synthesis algorithm is one in which, given a target error
\varepsilon>0
and
U\in\PU(d)
, the algorithms outputs a circuits
\gamma=g_{i_{1}}g_{i_{2}}\cdots g_{i_{r}}
for some

.

In the special case where an exact decomposition of the unitary

over

is possible, the problem is called exact synthesis. One method to address this problem is to give an explicit algebraic characterization of those unitaries that are exactly implementable using
G
. This boils down to proving that the group generated by

consists of matrices having entries in a particular ring
R\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. For example,
[
22
]
shows an exact synthesis characterization for the single qubit Clifford
+T
gate set. They prove that the group generated by the single qubit Clifford
+T
gate is
PU_{2}(\smash{\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}},i])
.

This approach of giving an algebraic characterization for the group generated by a gate set
G
, has led to some of the best-known algorithms, both for exact and approximate synthesis. In particular, very efficient algorithms (both in terms of
T

[
22
,
23
,
24

25

Bruhat-Tits Theory and Gate Synthesis

In number theory, the statement “Clifford + T satisfies a ring equality” would be usually stated as “the Clifford gates and the T-gate generate an S-arithmetic group”.
The theory of
S
-arithmetic group is a classical object studied in the theory of general arithmetic groups
[
41
,
42
]
.
One of the main tools to study these groups and their finitely generated subgroups is by studying their action on certain combinatorial structures known as Bruhat-Tits buildings. This interplay of number theory and gate synthesis has been explored for various arithmetic gate sets
[
43
,
31
,
44
,
45
,
28
]
.

One of the aims of this paper is to also further this connection and give a relatively self-contained exposition explaining these mathematical methods (in particular Bruhat-Tits theory) used to establish synthesis results of this nature. In particular, we present an exposition on the construction of the Bruhat-Tits building for
PU(3)
while avoiding traditional definitions involving root spaces of algebraic tori inside p-adic Lie groups.

For the case of
S
-arithmetic unitary groups that the synthesis problem is concerned with one is greatly aided by the use of the explicit lattice chain models of
[
46
]
that distill the mathematical content of the general theory and provide an explicit simplicial complex to work with.
This makes the sophisticated number-theoretic machinery more amenable to explicit calculations over local rings and their finite residue fields.
This way, we hope that our paper helps to bridge a gap between mathematicians
and quantum computing specialists interested in this topic.

2.1
Thin groups vs arithmeticity

Bruhat-Tits theory has been useful in showing that when finitely generated subgroups are not arithmetic, they are thin
[
31
]
.
Thin groups, because of the lack of arithmetic structure, are more difficult to deal with because
one does not have an efficiently computable membership criteria
[
47
]
. For the circuit synthesis problem the implication is that if the gateset G in question is thin it does not satisfy a ring equality. This makes the problem of finding good synthesis algorithms for the gateset
G
much harder. So in brief, thinness is undesirable and arithmeticity is desirable for finitely generated groups of quantum gates.

Suppose

Let’s consider

4.2
Self-dual lattice chains

Observe that when we have a nested chain of lattices

\cdots\subset\Lambda_{-2}\subset\Lambda_{-1}\subset\Lambda_{0}\subset\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}\subset\cdots,

(34)

we can dualize each lattice in the chain and obtain

\cdots\supset{\Lambda}^{\sharp}_{-2}\supset{\Lambda}^{\sharp}_{-1}\supset{\Lambda}^{\sharp}_{0}\supset{\Lambda}^{\sharp}_{1}\supset{\Lambda}^{\sharp}_{2}\supset\cdots.

(35)

This is called the dual lattice chain.

Our interest is in self-dual lattice chains.
This is defined in the following way.

Definition 4.6
.

A lattice chain over
\mathcal{O}_{\pi}
is called a self-dual lattice chain if it is the same chain when we dualize each lattice in the chain.

Lemma 4.7
.

For a self-dual lattice chain
\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, at least one of the two statements are true.

1.

One has
\Lambda_{j}={\Lambda_{j}}^{\sharp}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, or

2.

One has
\Lambda_{j}={\Lambda_{j+1}}^{\sharp}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

A lattice chain is equal to its dual if and only if there exists a bijection
d:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that
\Lambda_{i}={\Lambda_{d(i)}}^{\sharp}
that respects the condition
d(i-1)=d(i)+1
. This map enforces that the lattices in the dual chain are the same as in the original chain, but in a reversed order.

1.

Let
2j\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
d(2j)=0
.
d(j)=d(2j-j)=j
implies that
\Lambda_{j}={\Lambda_{j}}^{\sharp}

2.

Let
2j+1\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
d(2j+1)=0
.
d(j)=d(2j+1-(j+1))=j+1
implies that
\Lambda_{j}={\Lambda_{j+1}}^{\sharp}

∎

Definition 4.8
.

Given a self-dual lattice chain
\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we define the anchor of the lattice chain as the unique half-integer
i\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
defined as the following.

1.

If
\Lambda_{j}={\Lambda_{j}}^{\sharp}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote the anchor to be
i=j
.

2.

If
\Lambda_{j}={\Lambda_{j+1}}^{\sharp}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote the anchor to be
i=j+1/2
.

From now on we take it that the labeling of a lattice chain
\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is such that the anchor point is at
i=0
or
i=\frac{1}{2}
for each case respectively.

So finally we are ready to define the building on which our finitely generated gate sets might act.

4.3.2
Some subspaces in finite field vector spaces

We are interested in establishing some general facts about quadratic forms on vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
.

First we consider the case of
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
.
Consider
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
with the standard inner product
\langle\ ,\ \rangle:\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\times\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\to\mathbb{F}_{3}
given by

\langle x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\rangle=x^{(1)}_{1}x^{(2)}_{1}+x^{(1)}_{2}x^{(2)}_{2}+x^{(1)}_{3}x^{(2)}_{3}

(51)

\text{ where }x^{(i)}=(x^{(i)}_{1},x^{(i)}_{2},x^{(i)}_{3})\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\text{ for }i=1,2.

For any bilinear form
\langle\ ,\ \rangle:\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\times\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{3}
, one defines the dual space of a vector space

V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
with respect to
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
to be

V^{\perp}=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\mid\langle v,w\rangle=0\text{ for all }w\in V\}.

(52)

Lemma 4.14
.

Consider
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
and let
A\in\M_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
be a symmetric invertible matrix.
Consider the bilinear form
\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{A}
given by

\langle x,y\rangle_{A}=\langle Ax,y\rangle.

(53)

Then, there are exactly
4
one-dimensional subspaces
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
such that
V\subseteq V^{\perp}
, where
V^{\perp}
is the dual of
V
with respect to
\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{A}
.

Proof.

We know by
[
49
]
that in a finite field of odd characteristic, every non-degenerate quadratic form can be diagonalized.
Hence, for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
, one gets that there exists some
B\in\GL_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
such that

q(Bx)=\langle Bx,ABx\rangle=ax_{1}^{2}+bx_{2}^{2}+cx_{3}^{2}

(54)

for some
a,b,c\in\{\pm 1\}
.
We want to count the number of
x\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
such that
q(Bx)=0
.
For this purpose one sees that without loss of generality, we can assume that

q(Bx)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}\text{ or }x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}.

(55)

The exact diagonalized form of
q(Bx)
depends on whether
\det A
is a quadratic residue in
\mathbb{F}_{3}
.
In both the cases, we have exactly
8
solutions and they must give rise
4
subspaces
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
that are one-dimensional. For each
V
, it is clear that
V\subseteq V^{\perp}
and one sees that these are all.
∎

We also need to consider the situation of anti-symmetric linear forms.

Lemma 4.15
.

Consider
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
and an anti-symmetric matrix
A\in\M_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
given by the following for some
(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
:

A=\begin{pmatrix}\ &a&b\\
-a&&\\
-b&&\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

Consider the bilinear form
\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{A}
given by

\langle x,y\rangle_{A}=\langle Ax,y\rangle.

(57)

Then, the vector
v=(0,b,-a)^{T}\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
is the unique vector up to scaling satisfying
\langle v,w\rangle_{A}=0
for all
w\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
. Furthermore, there are exactly two subspaces
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{3}
of
\mathbb{F}_{3}
-dimension 2 such that
\mathbb{F}_{3}\cdot v\subseteq V
and
V^{\perp}=\{w\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}\mid\langle v,w\rangle_{A}=0\text{ for all }v\in V\}=V
.

Proof.

We know that for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and
y=y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
, one has

\langle x,y\rangle_{A}=a(x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})+b(x_{1}y_{3}-x_{3}y_{1}).

(58)

Then, it is clear that the only way one can have
\langle x,y\rangle_{A}=0
for all
y\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
is when
x_{1}=0
and
ax_{2}+bx_{3}=0
. This shows that
v=(0,b,-a)
is the vector as required.

Then assuming
a\neq 0
, one can identify
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})+\mathbb{F}_{3}\cdot v\leftrightarrow(x_{1},x_{2})
and get an isomorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{3}
-spaces
{\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}}/{\mathbb{F}_{3}\cdot v}\simeq\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
. Because of the property of
v
, the map
\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{A}
quotients on
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}/\mathbb{F}_{3}\cdot v
to the map

((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))\mapsto a(x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}).

(59)

One can check that exactly
w=(\pm 1,\pm 1)
are the self-dual lines. Hence
V=\mathbb{F}_{3}v+\mathbb{F}_{3}w
are the required subspaces.

We leave the case of
a=0
for the reader which is similar.
∎
