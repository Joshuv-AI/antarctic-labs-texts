Introduction

In mathematical biology, an active research problem is to find, given a set
\mathcal{S}
of phylogenetic trees, a so-called
consensus tree

that, intuitively, represents the trees in
\mathcal{S}
by a single one.
There exist various candidates for consensus trees and different
consensus methods
to compute them; see
[
4
]
for a survey.

However, computing a consensus tree only makes sense if the trees in
\mathcal{S}

are
“similar enough”
to be unified.
Otherwise, if the set of trees is too heterogeneous, it becomes necessary to partition
\mathcal{S}
into subsets of
“sufficiently similar”
trees,
and represent each of these by an individual consensus tree.
To accomplish this, we apply a method called
k
-means clustering for this task.
We briefly summarize the method.

Given a finite subset
S
of
sites
in a metric space
(V,d)
, an integer
k\leq\lvert S\rvert
and a positive real number
q
,
the
k
-means clustering
method seeks to partition the set
S
into a set
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\dotsc,C_{k}\}
of subsets of
S
, called
clusters
,
such that the
loss

(1)

\ell(\mathcal{C})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{v\in C_{i}}d(v,\mu_{i})^{q}

becomes minimal
[
23
]
.
Here,
\mu_{i}
denotes the
q
-mean
of
C_{i}
in
V
; i.e., the (or a) point
\mu_{i}\in V
for which

(2)

\sum_{v\in C_{i}}d(v,\mu_{i})^{q}

is minimized.
In the context of clustering, the representative
\mu_{i}
of the cluster
C_{i}
is also called the
centroid
of
C_{i}
.

For example, if
V
is an euclidean space with euclidean metric
d(s,t)=\lVert s-t\rVert
for the
L^{2}
-norm
\lVert-\rVert
,

\mu_{i}
is the
center of mass
of
C_{i}
, given as the the coordinate-wise
2
-mean of the vectors in
C_{i}
.

In general, finding the local optimum
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of
??
in arbitrary metric spaces is NP-complete
[
12
, Theorem 8]
.
While in principle,
??
with
q=2
can be solved in
d
-dimensional Euclidean spaces in time
\mathcal{O}(N^{(kd+1)})

[
10
]
,
the large exponent usually renders this algorithm unfeasible in practice.

However, a local optimum
\mathcal{C}
of
??
(for any
q
) can be obtained
through a simple iterative process called
Lloyd’s algorithm

[
17
, §3.6]
.
By seeding the initial state of this iterative process appropriately,
it is possible to bound the expectation value of the
competitive factor

\ell(\mathcal{C})/\ell(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.
With this seeding, the algorithm is usually called
k
-means++ clustering

[
3
]
; see
??
for details.

For
q=1
in
??
, the
1
-mean
\mu_{i}
is also called a
Fermat–Weber point
of
C_{i}
.
In euclidean spaces, if
C_{i}
is in general position, the Fermat–Weber point of
C_{i}
is unique and called the
median
of
C_{i}
.
While no explicit formula for the euclidean median exists, it can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy through an iterative process;
e.g., through
Weiszfeld’s algorithm

[
26
]
.

Observe however that neither the definition of the
q
-mean,
nor the definition of the
k
-means clustering, depend on
V
being a metric space.
In fact, it is even perfectly natural to generalize the problem to quasi-metric spaces
V
.

This aspect of generalizability becomes vital when it comes to applying the method to phylogenetic trees.
The set of trees with a fixed set of
N
taxa has an inherent structure as a
tropical subspace
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n=\binom{N}{2}
.

The latter is equipped with the
asymmetric tropical distance

(3)

\displaystyle d_{\Delta}(x,y)\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-x_{i})+n\max\{x_{i}-y_{i}\}\ ,

which defines a
quasi-metric
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
i.e., it is positive definite (meaning that
d_{\Delta}(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
)
and satisfies the triangle inequality

(4)

d_{\Delta}(x,z)\leq d_{\Delta}(x,y)+d_{\Delta}(y,z),

but is not symmetric.

A method for computing a mean with respect to
d_{\Delta}
(for
q=1
) is given in
[
6
]
,
which gives rise to the
tropical median consensus method
.
With this, we develop and study a method for
tropical
k
-means clustering
with respect to the asymmetric tropical distance.
In the following, we briefly summarize our main results.

Using

Finally, we show that if

We provide an implementation of our algorithm in the

1.3.
Related work

Clustering phylogenetic trees via
k
-means has been first considered by
[
24
]
with respect to the Robinson–Foulds distance.
This metric measures the distance of two phylogenetic trees as the number of different splits between them.
The distance of two trees is therefore an integer value.
The algorithm of Stockham et al. was further developed by
[
25
]
to allow for a dataset of trees with different sets of taxa.
A qualitative anaylsis of different clustering methods of phylogenetic trees with respect to multiple tree distances intended to quantify the similarities of subtrees has been conducted by
[
22
]
.
In his paper, Smith points out that “the Robinson–Foulds tree spaces […] often fail to group trees according to phylogenetic similarity” as it lacks resolution
which demonstrates the need for a different approach.
The tropical asymmetric distance on the other hand shows high resolution which is why we propose this alternative clustering.

The tropical asymmetric distance
d_{\Delta}
was first introduced by
[
1
]
.

[
6
]
studied Fermat–Weber sets with respect to
d_{\Delta}
and introduced the consensus method which our paper relies on.
Finally, we want to mention two key contributions connecting tropical geometry and phylogenetic trees, namely
[
7
]
and
[
2
]
.

2.
Phylogenetic trees from a tropical perspective

In this section we introduce the main tools required for tropical
k
-means clustering.
We begin by introducing our main object of study:
phylogenetic trees
.
For us, a phylogenetic tree is a rooted tree
\mathsfbfit{t}=(V,E)
with nodes
V
and edges
E
whose leaves are labeled by a set of
taxa

X
,
whose edges are assigned a length.
In particular, internal edges are assigned a positive length, while external edges may be assigned negative length.
The
depth
of a node
v
is the length of the path from the root to
v
.
A tree is called
equidistant
if all leaves have the same depth.

Via path length, a phylogenetic tree
\mathsfbfit{t}
turns
X
into a metric space, with metric
d{t}\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
.
If
\mathsfbfit{t}
is equidistant, then
d{t}
is an
ultrametric
, i.e., if for all pairwise distinct
a,b,c\in X
, the maximum

(5)

\displaystyle\max\{d{t}(a,b),d{t}(a,c),d{t}(b,c)\}

is attained at least twice.
Conversely, every ultrametric on
X
corresponds uniquely to an equidistant phylogenetic tree with taxa
X
; see
[
21
, Theorem 7.2.5]
.

We identify
d{t}
with
\mathsfbfit{t}
and, since the metric
d{t}
is symmetric, we view both as a point
\mathsfbfit{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n=\binom{N}{2}
and
N\coloneq\lvert X\rvert
.

We define the following equivalence relation
\sim
on the set
\mathcal{T}_{X}
of equidistant phylogenetic trees with taxa
X
:
for
\mathsfbfit{t},\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{X}
, we let
\mathsfbfit{t}\sim\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}
if
\mathsfbfit{t}
is obtained from
\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}
by lengthening (or shortening) the paths from every leaf of
\mathsfbfit{t}
to its respective parent node by the same amount.
Viewing
\mathsfbfit{t}
and
\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}
as elements of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\mathsfbfit{t}\sim\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}
if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathsfbfit{t}=\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}+\lambda\mathbb{1}
, where
\mathbb{1}
is the all ones vector.
We may view thus the set of equidistant phylogenetic trees up to
\mathcal{T}_{X}/{\sim}
as a subset of

\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}\ \coloneqq\ \{\mathsfbfit{x}+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathsfbfit{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\ .

The latter is called
n
-dimensional tropical torus
.
We will see in the following section that both the tropical torus and
\mathcal{T}_{X}/{\sim}
appear naturally in tropical geometry.

2.1.
Tropical perspective

Before we elaborate on the tropical geometric nature of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{T}_{X}/{\sim}
we introduce some basic notions.
For a more detailed introduction to tropical geometry see
[
16
]
or
[
11
]
.

Often, tropical geometry is termed the combinatorial shadow of algebraic geometry.
Like in algebraic geometry we can define
tropical hypersurfaces
,
tropical curves
and
tropical linear spaces
.
These objects live in a tropical (sub-)space.
One dimensional tropical space, namely the
tropical semiring

(\mathbb{T},\oplus,\odot)
with ground set
\mathbb{T}\coloneq\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, replaces classical addition
+
with
\oplus\coloneq\max
and classical multiplication
\cdot
with
\odot\coloneq+
.
Hence,
-\infty
becomes the neutral element with respect to tropical addition
\oplus
and
0
the neutral element with respect to tropical multiplication
\odot
.
Note, that there exists no unique additive inverse element.
The monoid
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with componentwise tropical addition becomes a semimodule over
\mathbb{T}
via tropical scalar multiplication

\odot:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{n},\ (\lambda,v)\mapsto\lambda\odot v\coloneq(\lambda+v_{1},\ldots,\lambda+v_{n})=\lambda\mathbb{1}+v\ .

Ardila and Klivans
[
2
, Theorem 3]
showed that
\mathcal{T}_{X}
coincides exactly with a tropical linear space, namely the
the Bergman fan
\mathcal{B}(K_{N})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the complete graphical matroid on
N
elements.
We omit its precise definition as it will not be relevant and would require a more extensive introduction to matroids in particular which is beyond the scope of what will follow.

The Bergman fan
\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
is a polyhedral complex, in particular, as the name suggests, a polyhedral fan.
One often differentiates between two different fan structures of the Bergman fan, known as the
coarse
and
fine subdivision
.
For the coarse subdivision, there exists a bijection between its cones and combinatorial tree types; see
[
2
, Proposition 3]
.
While known before, in
[
9
, Proposition 2]
Hampe gave a proof that
\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
is
tropically convex
,
meaning that for
x,y\in\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, the tropical linear combination

(6)

\lambda\odot x\oplus\mu\odot y\ =\ \max\{x+\lambda\mathbb{1},y+\mu\mathbb{1}\}

is again an element of
\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
.
This implies that
\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
has a
lineality space
of dimension
\geq 1
and contains the line spanned by
\mathbb{1}
, i.e.,
for any point
x\in\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
the whole line
x+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
is contained in
\mathcal{B}(K_{N})
.
Therefore its quotient
B(K_{N})\coloneq\mathcal{B}(K_{N})/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
is well defined and remains tropically convex.

.

The motivation behind the tropical torus
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
comes from tropical convexity.
In fact any set

Hence we always consider tropically convex sets in the tropical torus

In the following we will only consider equidistant trees.
Mostly, we will be considering equivalence classes

For brevity, we also refer to

as the Bergman fan.

2.2.
Tropical median consensus tree

A consensus tree of a set of phylogenetic trees
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
aims to best represent all trees in
\mathcal{S}
.
One candidate, the
tropical median consensus tree
, was introduced by
[
6
, Definition 20]
; see
??
below.
It is defined in terms of the asymmetric tropical distance as follows.
The quasi-metric
d_{\Delta}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
from (
3
) is invariant under tropical scalar multiplication;
i.e.,
d_{\Delta}(x+\lambda\mathbb{1},y+\mu\mathbb{1})=d_{\Delta}(x,y)
for every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore, it induces a quasi-metric
d_{\Delta}
on the tropical torus
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}

which we call the
asymmetric tropical distance on
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
.

The
d_{\Delta}
-unit ball

\{x\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}d_{\Delta}(x,0)\leq 1\}

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
is the standard simplex
\Delta+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}={\operatorname*{conv}}\{e_{1}+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1},\ldots,e_{n}+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}\}
,
where the
e_{i}
denote the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
, we have that

(7)

d_{\Delta}(x,y)=\min\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}y\in x+\lambda(\Delta+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1})\}\ .

The
“amount of asymmetry”
of
d_{\Delta}
is measured by its
skewness

(8)

\sigma_{n}\ =\ \min\big\{\lambda\geq 1\nonscript\>\big|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}d_{\Delta}(x,y)\leq\lambda\cdot d_{\Delta}(y,x),\ \forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1},\,x\neq y\big\}\ ;

see
[
20
]
.
Clearly,
\Delta+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
is compact.
Hence we can apply
[
20
, Lemma 10]
and obtain

(9)

\sigma_{n}=\max\left\{d_{\Delta}(x,0)\mid x\in\Delta+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}\right\}=n-1\ .

We conclude with the following inequality.

Lemma 2.4

(Pseudo-triangle inequality)
.

Let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
, then

\frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(x,y)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(x,z)+d_{\Delta}(y,z)\ .

Proof.

We have the triangle inequality

\frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(x,y)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(x,z)+\frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(z,y)\ .

Using that
d_{\Delta}
has skewness
n-1
, we obtain

\frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(x,y)+\frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(z,y)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{n-1}d_{\Delta}(x,z)+d_{\Delta}(y,z).\qed

Next we give and explicit description of how the tropical median consensus tree
t
of a set of equidistant trees
\mathcal{S}
is computed.
First, we note that
t
minimizes the total distance

(10)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{\Delta}(s,t)

from the trees
s\in\mathcal{S}
to
t
.
This characterization alone does not render
t
unique.
In fact, the dimension of the set of points which minimize
??
, called the
Fermat–Weber set

\FW(\mathcal{S})
, is bounded by

(11)

\min\big\{N-1,\gcd(\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert,n)\big\}-1\ .

It can be computed with a linear program in polynomial time
[
6
, Corollary 16]
.
Geometrically, the Fermat–Weber set
\FW(\mathcal{S})
is a
polytrope

[
6
, Theorem 4]
,
i.e., a classical polytope which in addition is also tropically convex.

Like a classical convex hull, the Fermat–Weber polytrope
\FW(\mathcal{S})
has a minimal generating set
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{l}
, called
tropical vertices
,
such that any point in
\FW(\mathcal{S})
is a tropical linear combination of
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{l}
.
This allows us to give a unique definition of the tropical median consensus tree.

Definition 2.5
.

The
tropical median consensus tree
of
\mathcal{S}
is the ordinary, componentwise mean of the tropical vertices of
\FW(\mathcal{S})
.
More explicitly, it is the tree

\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}v_{i}\ .

Remark 2.6
.

Comăneci and Joswig showed that any point in the Fermat–Weber set
\FW(\mathcal{S})
can be written as a tropical linear combination of the points in
\mathcal{S}
; see
[
6
, Theorem 4]
.
This is particularly true for the tropical median consensus tree of
\mathcal{S}
.

Remark 2.7
.

There does exist a metric on the tropical torus
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
, called the
symmetric tropical distance

(12)

d_{\sym}(x,y)\ =\ \max_{i\in[n]}\{x_{i}-y_{i}\}-\min_{j\in[n]}\{x_{j}-y_{j}\}\ .

Although one might be inclined towards the symmetric distance, the asymmetric distance has certain theoretical and practical advantages.
Most notably, contrary to the symmetric distance, there exists a consensus method for equidistant phylogenetic trees.

Note that another way to determine the skewness
??
of the tropical distance is to compare it with the symmetric tropical distance (
12
); see
[
5
, Section 2]
.

3.
tropical
k
-means++ clustering

In this section we will introduce the
tropical
k
-means++
algorithm.
We start by recalling the
k
-means clustering.

For a subset
S\subseteq V
of
sites
in a quasi-metric space
(V,d)
,

k
-means clustering aims at partitioning
S
into
k
disjoint subsets
C_{1},\dotsc,C_{k}
, called
clusters
,
by finding
k
points
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{k}\in V
, called centroids, that minimize the objective function

(13)

\sum_{s\in S}\min_{i\in[k]}d(s,c_{i})^{q}.

The clusters are the
Voronoi regions

C_{i}=\Vor_{c_{i}}(S)=\{s\in S\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}d(s,c_{i})^{q}\leq d(s,c_{j})^{q}\ \forall\ j\}
.

The problem cannot be solved analytically in practice.
By using the following iterative algorithm due to Lloyd
[
14
]
, it is possible to find a local optimum:
choose initial values for
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{k}\in S
, and run the following steps in turns:

1.

Set
C_{i}\coloneqq\Vor_{c_{i}}(S)
. We call this the
A-step
.

2.

Set
c_{i}\coloneqq\argmin_{c\in S}\sum_{s\in C_{i}}d(s,c)^{q}
. We call this phase the
M-step
.

The algorithm terminates if either step does not change any
C_{i}
or
c_{i}
, respectively.
If
S
is finite, the above iterative algorithm is easily seen to terminate,
though, possibly, in a local optimum.

3.1.
Tropical clustering

For us, the sites we consider correspond to equidistant phylogenetic trees.
Hence we consider points in the tropical torus
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
and apply the tropical asymmetric distance

.

Algorithm 1

k
-means clustering

0:

2:

The tropical

Proof.

Let

be the respective tropical medians.

As shown above, the local optimum

4.1.
Tropical
k
-means clustering in floating point arithmetic

oscar
and
polymake
are capable of computing tropical medians
(and hence, through our implementation, tropical
k
-means clustering)
in exact arithmetic; e.g., using rational numbers.
However, the computational effort for computing the tropical
k
-means clustering quickly becomes prohibitive
with growing dimension and number of points.
Therefore, computing the tropical
k
-means clustering of a set of phylogenetic trees over rational numbers
usually is not feasible.

Instead, our computational experiments reported on in this section use floating point arithmetic (floats).
The tropical medians algorithm in
polymake
calls
MCF

[
15
]
for solving the underlying optimal transport problem
over floats.

While computationally efficient, the discrete nature of floats
inevitably causes computational artifacts that do not occur in exact arithmetic.
For example, the vectors representing trees will not lie exactly on the Bergman fan.

To make this precise, we call a point
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
an
\epsilon
-ultrametric
for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small if

(1-\epsilon)\max\{t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc}\}\leq\Mid(t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc})
for all
a,b,c\in X
where

\Mid(t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc})\coloneqq t_{ab}+t_{ac}+t_{bc}-\max\{t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc}\}-\min\{t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc}\}\ .

The set of
\epsilon
-ultrametrics is called the
\epsilon
-thickening of the Bergman fan

B(K_{N})_{\epsilon}
.
Clearly, this set is full-dimensional which prohibits it from being a tropical linear space.
In particular, the dimension of the Fermat–Weber set of a set of
\epsilon
-ultrametrics is not bounded by
??
but instead by
\gcd(m,n)-1
where
m
is the number of
\epsilon
-ultrametrics; see
[
6
, Theorem 7]
.
Moreover, we do not know if the
\epsilon
-thickening of the Bergman fan is tropically convex.

Question 4.1
.

Does there exist
\epsilon>0
such that the
\epsilon
-thickening of the Bergman fan is tropically convex?
If not, does it contain the tropical convex hull of
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in B(K_{N})_{\epsilon}
if its dimension is smaller or equal than
N{-}2
?

An answer to this question would imply when the tropical median of a set of
\epsilon
-ultrametrics is again an
\epsilon
-ultrametric.
Unfortunately, a full investigation is beyond the scope of this article.

Due to the numerics of floating point numbers, it might happen that given a set of
\epsilon
-ultrametrics,

polymake
computes a
\delta
-ultrametric,
\delta\gg\epsilon
, as the tropical median of
\mathcal{S}
.
We stress the fact that this does not disprove
??
as computations with floating point numbers are by definition not exact.

In order to keep these numerical instabilities from piling up, we employ the following correction scheme.
Let
\epsilon_{0}\approx 10^{-16}
be difference between the floating point number one and the next largest floating point number.
After the computation of a tropical median consensus tree
t=\mathsfbfit{t}+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
,
if
\mathsfbfit{t}
is a
\delta
-ultrametric only for
\delta>\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}
,
we replace
t
by
t^{\prime}=\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}+\mathbb{R}\mathbb{1}
where
\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the largest ultrametric smaller than
\mathsfbfit{t}
.
Again, due to the discrete nature of floating point numbers, it might happen that
\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}
is not exactly representable;
however, it seems always possible to find
\mathsfbfit{t}^{\prime}
as an
\epsilon
-ultrametric for
\epsilon<\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}
.

Last, we encountered empty Fermat–Weber sets in our experiment as they became overdetermined.
While the
tropical_median
function in
oscar
returns a point which is not ultrametric
we consider this point a mere artifact of floating point arithmetic and not of any further use.
We only observed this when the number of trees
m
was divisible by
n
where the dimension of the Fermat-Weber set can be full-dimensional due to the use of floats.
Note that by reducing the number of trees by one to
m-1
, the tropical median becomes unique for both rational and floating point numbers.
As the tropical median has shown to be very robust to single outliers
[
6
, Example 23]
,
our strategy is to compute the tropical median of a subset of
m-1
points.

5.
Outlook

For a homogeneous dataset of phylogenetic trees with only a few different combinatorial types, the question whether we can recover a partition into combinatorial types becomes interesting.
By a similar argument as in
??
one can show that the maximal cones of the coarse subdivision of the Bergman fan
B(K_{N})
are tropically convex.
Assume that the trees
t
are “sufficiently binary”, i.e., there exists
\zeta>0
such that for every three taxa
a,b,c\in X
we have
\max\{t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc}\}-\min\{t_{ab},t_{ac},t_{bc}\}>\zeta
.
Is there a lower bound for
\zeta
and an upper bound on
\eta(t)
for the trees
t
such that we can recover the partition (into combinatorial types) with tropical
k
-means++ clustering?

In order to better analyse the resulting clustering produced by tropical
k
-means clustering, it is essential to understand all possible trees which can be contained in a given cluster.
Since all such trees are contained in a ball around the corresponding centroid with respect to the tropical asymmetric metric,
this motivates the study of the intersection of such a ball with the Bergman fan
B(K_{N})
.
