Local–global correlations of dynamics on disordered energy landscapes
Jacob Calvert ​​ Dana Randall

Abstract
The stationary distribution of a continuous-time Markov chain generally arises from a complicated global balance of probability fluxes. Nevertheless, empirical evidence shows that the effective potential, defined as the negative logarithm of the stationary distribution, is often highly correlated with a simple local property of a state—the logarithm of its exit rate. To better understand why and how typically this correlation is high, we study reversible reaction kinetics on energy landscapes consisting of Gaussian wells and barriers, respectively associated with the vertices and edges of regular graphs. We find that for the correlation to be high it suffices for the heights of the barriers to vary significantly less than the depths of the wells, regardless of the degree of the underlying graph. As an application, we bound below the expected correlation exhibited by dynamics of the random energy model, known as the Bouchaud trap model. We anticipate that the proof, which combines a general lower bound of the expected correlation with the Gaussian concentration inequality, can be extended to several other classes of models.

Introduction

Continuous-time Markov chains play a fundamental role in stochastic thermodynamics, which seeks to extend the ideas of classical thermodynamics to systems that are “small” or “far” from equilibrium
[
26
]
. In this context, Markov chains primarily serve to model the mesoscopic dynamics of physical system, but can also describe the effective macroscopic dynamics that emerges from it
[
15

[
29
]

The stationary distributions of nonreversible Markov chains, which are instead maintained by a global balance of probability fluxes along cycles of states, correspondingly model nonequilibrium steady states. The key observation is that important thermodynamic properties of a system, like entropy production, can be expressed in terms of these cycle fluxes
[
24
]
. For this reason, many results of stochastic thermodynamics—including fluctuation theorems
[
10
,
20
,
3
]
, thermodynamic uncertainty relations
[
6
,
17
]
, bounds on nonequilibrium response
[
22
,
2
]
, and force-flow relations
[
28
]
—concern the way and extent to which Markov chains violate detailed balance.

1.2
Local–global correlations in Markov chains

While the results of stochastic thermodynamics generally concern the way and extent to which detailed balance fails, an emerging perspective instead emphasizes the relationship between local dynamical information and the global steady state distribution
[
7
,
9
,
8
]
. In Markov chain terms, the key observation is that, in many cases, the effective potential of a state is highly collinear with the logarithm of its exit rate

q_{i}=\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}Q_{ij},

where
j\sim i
indicates that
(i,j)
is an edge of
G
(
Fig.

1
b). We measure the collinearity of these quantities using their correlation for a uniformly random state
I\in[n]
:

\rho=\Corr(-\log\pi_{I},\log q_{I})=-\frac{\Cov(\log\pi_{I},\log q_{I})}{\sqrt{\Var(\log\pi_{I})\Var(\log q_{I})}}.

(3)

Note that
\rho
is well defined whenever
\pi_{I}
and
q_{I}
have positive, finite variances.

The effective potential
-\log\pi_{i}
is a global property of a Markov chain, in the sense that estimating it from a trajectory generally requires the observation of multiple returns to state
i
, which can entail visits to faraway states (see
[
19
]
, for example). In contrast, to estimate
q_{i}
, it suffices to repeatedly initialize the Markov chain in state
i
and average the times it takes to leave, because the exit rate
q_{i}
is equal to the reciprocal of the expected duration of a visit to state
i
. In this sense,
\log q_{i}
is a local property of a state, and
\rho
measures a local–global correlation.

An intriguing aspect of
\rho
, especially in the context of stochastic thermodynamics, is that the extent of correlation is generally unrelated to whether a Markov chain satisfies detailed balance (
Fig.

1
c). It is therefore especially important to understand why and how typically systems exhibit high correlation. In
[
9
]
, we derived a formula for
\rho
in terms of two further Markov chain parameters, and empirically studied
\rho
in Markov chains that arise in various scientific domains. However, no previous work has rigorously estimated
\rho
in a broad class of Markov chains.

2
Main results

To better understand why high correlation arises, and how typically it does so, we fix a graph
G
and analyze the correlation exhibited by Arrhenius-like dynamics on a disordered energy landscape consisting of wells and barriers alone (
Fig.

2
). Given well depths
\mathbf{W}=(W_{i})_{i\in V(G)}
and barrier heights
\mathbf{B}=(B_{ij})_{(i,j)\in E(G)}
on
G
, we define
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{Arr}_{G}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
to be the transition rate matrix with entries

Q_{ij}=\exp(W_{i}-B_{ij}),\quad(i,j)\in E(G).

(4)

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\sigma_{W}
and
\sigma_{B}
be positive real numbers and let
G
be a graph. We define a
(\sigma_{W},\sigma_{B})
-disordered energy landscape on
G
to be a pair
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2})
well depths
\mathbf{W}=(W_{i})_{i\in V(G)}
, and barrier heights
\mathbf{B}=(B_{ij})_{(i,j)\in E(G)}
that satisfy
B_{ij}=B_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{B}^{2})
for every
(i,j)\in E(G)
.

While the barrier heights in
Fig.

2
are i.i.d. (aside from the symmetry condition
B_{ij}=B_{ji}
), our main results concern a broader class of disorder. Specifically, we allow the barrier heights adjacent to a state
i
to depend on one another through the corresponding well depth. This flexibility is important for an application that we shortly present.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that a disordered energy landscape
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
on
G
is separable if there exist
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
such that, for every
i\in V(G)
,
(B_{ij}-f(W_{i}))_{j:\,j\sim i}
is a vector of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
random variables, which are also independent of
W_{i}
.

The first of our main results explains that, if the typical barrier height in a separable energy landscape is small relative to the typical well depth, then the expected correlation
\rho
exhibited by Arrhenius-like dynamics is close to
1
. To ensure that
\rho
is well defined, we stipulate that the exit rates
\mathbf{q}
are a.s. non-identical, meaning that there are states
i
and
j
such that
q_{i}\neq q_{j}
.

.

Let

be a connected, regular graph with
n\geq 6
vertices, and let
\sigma_{W}
and
\sigma_{B}
be positive real numbers. If
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a separable,

-disordered energy landscape on

The condition that

We expect that the proof of

2.1
Application to spin-glass dynamics

Our second main result is an application of
Theorem

1
to a family of dynamics associated with the random energy model (REM). Unlike the examples considered in
Figs.

1
,
2
, and
3
, these dynamics entail barrier heights that depend on the depths of adjacent wells.

The REM is a mean-field model of a spin-glass consisting of
N
spins, the configurations of which have random energies
[
11
,
12
]
. More precisely, it is a random Boltzmann distribution on the hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{N}
that arises from setting the well depths
W_{i}
in
Eq.

2
to be i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2})
random variables, where
\sigma_{W}^{2}=\beta^{2}N
and
\beta>0
denotes inverse temperature. We consider dynamics of the REM that Bouchaud introduced to study the phenomenon of aging
[
18
]
, a generalization of which is known as the Bouchaud trap model
[
5
]
.

As before, we let
G
be a connected, regular graph with
n
vertices. For a parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
and well depths
\mathbf{W}=(W_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, we denote by
\mathrm{REM}_{G}(\lambda,\mathbf{W})
the transition rate matrix
\mathbf{R}
with entries

R_{ij}=\exp\left(\lambda W_{i}-(1-\lambda)W_{j}\right),\quad(i,j)\in E(G).

(6)

Note that
\mathbf{R}
satisfies detailed balance with respect to the Boltzmann stationary distribution (
2
), for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

By varying
\lambda
from
0
to
1
, the transition rates interpolate between two extremes. The first entails transitions from
i
to
j\sim i
that occur at a rate of
\exp(-W_{j})
. In other words, transitions occur at a rate that depends on the energy of the destination. The second extreme entails rates of
\exp(W_{i})
that instead depend on the energy of the originating state, again for
j\sim i
. For this reason,
\lambda
is said to measure the “locality” of the dynamics
[
5
]
.

With this interpretation of
\lambda
,
Theorem

1
states the intuitive fact that, for the REM dynamics to exhibit high correlation between the effective potential and its local part, it suffices for the dynamics to be highly local. It further shows that it is enough for
\lambda
to be close to
1
in a way that does not depend on the underlying graph
G
or any other model parameters. In the following statement, we use
\mathbf{I}_{n}
to denote the
n\times n
identity matrix.

Theorem 2
.

Let
G
be a connected, regular graph with
n\geq 6
vertices, let
\sigma_{W}>0
, and let
\lambda\in[0,1]
. If
\mathbf{W}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{W}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, then the correlation
\rho=\rho(\mathbf{R})
exhibited by the REM dynamics
\mathbf{R}=\mathrm{REM}_{G}(\lambda,\mathbf{W})
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\rho)\geq 1-16\left(1+O(n^{-1/2})\right)(1-\lambda)^{2}.

We emphasize the fact that the lower bound holds uniformly over all possible values of
\sigma_{W}^{2}
, hence all values of the inverse temperature
\beta
as well. Considering that both the distribution and dynamics of the REM exhibit phase transitions as
\beta
crosses a critical value of
\beta_{c}=\sqrt{2\log 2}

[
1
]
, it is interesting that
\beta
plays no role in the correlation lower bound. This observation is reminiscent of the fact that the dynamical phase transition in the REM dynamics is not accompanied by a qualitative change in the spectral gap
[
16
]
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

We consider
\lambda\in[0,1)
, because the correlation trivially satisfies
\rho=1
a.s. when
\lambda=1
. We can express the transition rates
R_{ij}
in the Arrhenius-like form (
4
) by taking the barrier heights to be
B_{ij}=(1-\lambda)(W_{i}+W_{j})
for every
(i,j)\in E(G)
:

R_{ij}=\exp(\lambda W_{i}-(1-\lambda)W_{j})=\exp(W_{i}-(1-\lambda)(W_{i}+W_{j}))=\exp(W_{i}-B_{ij}).

It is easy to verify that the corresponding exit rates
q_{i}=\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}R_{ij}
are a.s. non-identical because the well depths
(W_{i})_{i\in[n]}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2})
random variables. For the same reason, the barrier heights satisfy
B_{ij}=B_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{B}^{2})
with
\sigma_{B}=\sqrt{2}(1-\lambda)\sigma_{W}
. Furthermore, taking
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
to be
f(x)=(1-\lambda)x
and
\sigma=\sigma_{B}/\sqrt{2}
, the barrier heights adjacent to
i
satisfy

(B_{ij}-f(W_{i}))_{j:\,j\sim i}=((1-\lambda)W_{j})_{j:\,j\sim i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}),

in terms of the degree
d
of
G
. Since
B_{ij}-f(W_{i})
is independent of
W_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
,
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a separable,
(\sigma_{W},\sigma_{B})
-disordered energy landscape.
Theorem

1
therefore implies that

\mathbb{E}(\rho)\geq 1-8\left(1+O(n^{-1/2})\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{W}}\right)^{2}=1-16\left(1+O(n^{-1/2})\right)(1-\lambda)^{2}.

Organization

The proof of
Theorem

1
has two main steps. The first step bounds below
\mathbb{E}(\rho)
in terms of a ratio of two variances, one concerning the well depths and the other concerning a function of the barrier heights (
Section

3
). The second step calculates one variance and estimates the other using the Gaussian concentration inequality (
Section

4
). In
Section

5
, we combine the results from the two steps to prove
Theorem

1
.

3
Relating the correlation to a ratio of variances

The goal of this section is to prove the following proposition, which implements the first step of the proof of
Theorem

1
. We state it terms of a function
A_{i}
of the barrier heights
(B_{ij})_{j:\,j\sim i}
, which plays an important role in the proof:

A_{i}=\log\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}\exp(-B_{ij}).

(7)

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with
n\geq 2
vertices. If
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E(G)|}
are random vectors satisfying
B_{ij}=B_{ji}
for all
(i,j)\in E(G)
and such that the correlation
\rho=\rho(\mathbf{Q})
of
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{Arr}_{G}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a.s. well defined, then

\mathbb{E}(\rho)\geq 1-2\,\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\Var A_{I}}{\Var W_{I}}\right),

(8)

where
I\in[n]
is uniformly random and independent of
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{B}
.

Proposition

3.1
requires less of
G
,
\mathbf{W}
, and
\mathbf{B}
than
Theorem

1
. In particular,
G
does not need to be a regular graph, and the distributions of
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{B}
are limited only by the requirement that
\rho
is well defined. Note that
\rho
is well defined if and only if both the stationary distribution
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and exit rates
\mathbf{q}
have non-identical entries. If
B_{ij}=B_{ji}
for all
(i,j)\in E(G)
, then
\mathbf{Q}
has the Boltzmann stationary distribution (
2
). In this case,
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
\mathbf{q}
have non-identical entries if and only if
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{A}
do.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

For the moment, suppose that
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{B}
are fixed vectors such that
B_{ij}=B_{ji}
for every
(i,j)\in E(G)
and the correlation
\rho
exhibited by
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{Arr}_{G}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is well defined. We claim that

\rho=\frac{1+\widehat{\rho}r}{\sqrt{1+2\widehat{\rho}r+r^{2}}},

(9)

in terms of the quantities
\widehat{\rho}
and
r
, defined as

\widehat{\rho}=\Corr(W_{I},A_{I})\quad\text{and}\quad r=\sqrt{\frac{\Var A_{I}}{\Var W_{I}}}.

Indeed, by the definition of the Arrhenius-like dynamics (
4
), the exit rates of
\mathbf{Q}
satisfy

q_{i}=\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}Q_{ij}=e^{W_{i}}\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}e^{-B_{ij}}=e^{W_{i}+A_{i}}.

Moreover, since
\mathbf{B}
is symmetric, the stationary distribution of
\mathbf{Q}
is the Boltzmann distribution
\pi_{i}\propto\exp(-W_{i})
. The correlation therefore equals

\rho=\Corr(-\log\pi_{I},\log q_{I})=\Corr(W_{I},W_{I}+A_{I}).

To verify
Eq.

9
, we use the definition of correlation and linearity of covariance, and then identify factors of
\widehat{\rho}
and
r
in the resulting expression:

\displaystyle\Corr(W_{I},W_{I}+A_{I})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Var W_{I}+\Cov(W_{I},A_{I})}{\sqrt{(\Var W_{I})(\Var W_{I}+2\Cov(W_{I},A_{I})+\Var A_{I})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\widehat{\rho}r}{\sqrt{1+2\widehat{\rho}r+r^{2}}}.

Next, let
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{B}
be random vectors that satisfy the hypotheses. To complete the proof, we must show that

\mathbb{E}(\rho)\geq 1-2\,\mathbb{E}(r^{2}).

(10)

Let
\rho_{+}=\rho\1_{\{\rho\,\geq\,0\}}
denote the nonnegative part of the correlation. We claim that

\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+})\geq 1-\mathbb{E}(r^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\rho<0)\leq\mathbb{E}(r^{2}).

These two bounds together imply
Eq.

10
because
\rho\geq-1
:

\mathbb{E}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+})+\mathbb{E}(\rho\1_{\{\rho<0\}})\geq\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+})-\mathbb{P}(\rho<0)\geq 1-2\,\mathbb{E}(r^{2}).

We address the lower bound of
\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+})
first.

By assumption,
\rho
is a.s. well defined, hence
\rho
satisfies
Eq.

9
. By
Eq.

9
, when
r\leq 1
,
\rho
is nonnegative and minimized by
\widehat{\rho}=-r
, which implies the bound

\rho_{+}^{2}\geq 1-r^{2}.

However,
r
is random and can take values greater than
1
, so we must first condition on the occurrence of
\{r\leq 1\}
to apply it. In terms of the probability
p=\mathbb{P}(r\leq 1)
, we find that

\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+}^{2})\geq\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+}^{2}\mid r\leq 1)\,p\geq\left(1-\mathbb{E}(r^{2}\mid r\leq 1)\right)p.

(11)

To bound above the conditional expectation of
r^{2}
, we note that

\mathbb{E}(r^{2})=\mathbb{E}(r^{2}\mid r\leq 1)\,p+\mathbb{E}(r^{2}\mid r>1)(1-p)\geq\mathbb{E}(r^{2}\mid r\leq 1)\,p+(1-p).

Rearranging this expression gives

\mathbb{E}(r^{2}\mid r\leq 1)\leq\frac{1}{p}\big(\mathbb{E}(r^{2})-(1-p)\big).

We substitute this bound into
Eq.

11
to conclude that

\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+}^{2})\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\big(\mathbb{E}(r^{2})-(1-p)\big)\right)p=1-\mathbb{E}(r^{2}).

Since
\rho_{+}\in[0,1]
,
\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+})
is at least
\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+}^{2})
, which proves that
\mathbb{E}(\rho_{+})\geq 1-\mathbb{E}(r^{2})
.

To conclude, we note that the probability
\mathbb{P}(\rho<0)
is at most
\mathbb{E}(r^{2})
because

\mathbb{P}(\rho<0)\leq\mathbb{P}(r>1)=\mathbb{P}(r^{2}>1)\leq\mathbb{E}(r^{2}).

The first inequality holds because
Eq.

9
implies that
\rho\geq 0
whenever
r\leq 1
. The second inequality is due to Markov’s inequality.

Bounding the expected ratio of variances

Recall that
A_{i}
is the function of the barrier heights introduced in

7
, and
I
is a uniformly random state that is independent of all other randomness. The goal of this section is to prove an upper bound on the expectation of
r^{2}=(\Var A_{I})/(\Var W_{I})
. Substituting this bound into
Proposition

3.1
will imply
Theorem

1
.

Bounding above
\mathbb{E}(r^{2})
is difficult for two reasons. First, the quantities
\Var W_{I}
and
\Var A_{I}
are not necessarily independent. Second, while
\Var W_{I}
is the sample variance of i.i.d. Gaussian random variables, which has a known distribution,
\Var A_{I}
is an apparently complicated function of (possibly dependent) barrier heights. To address the first difficulty, because
\Var W_{I}
is the simpler of the two quantities, we replace
\mathbf{W}
with an i.i.d. copy
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
and bound the associated error:

\mathbb{E}(r^{2})=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\Var A_{I}}{\Var W_{I}}\right)=\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\Var W_{I}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left(\Var A_{I}\left(\frac{1}{\Var W_{I}}-\frac{1}{\Var W_{I}^{\prime}}\right)\right).

(12)

The difference of reciprocals

D_{I}=\frac{1}{\Var W_{I}}-\frac{1}{\Var W_{I}^{\prime}}

satisfies
\mathbb{E}(D_{I}^{2})=2\mathbb{V}(1/\Var W_{I})
, where
\mathbb{V}
denotes variance with respect to all randomness, in contrast to
\Var(\cdot)=\mathbb{V}(\cdot\mid(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B}))
. Hence, by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the term
\mathbb{E}((\Var A_{I})D_{I})
in
Eq.

12
, we find that

\mathbb{E}(r^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left((\Var W_{I})^{-1}\right)+\sqrt{\,\mathbb{E}\big((\Var A_{I})^{2}\big)\cdot 2\,\mathbb{V}\big((\Var W_{I})^{-1}\big)}.

(13)

The next two subsections address the factors involving
\Var W_{I}
and
\Var A_{I}
, respectively. We combine them in the last subsection.

4.1
The inverse distribution of
\Var W_{I}

In the statement and proof of the following lemma, we use
\mathrm{InvGam}(a,b)
to denote the distribution of an inverse gamma random variable with shape and scale parameters
a
and
b
, both positive real numbers. For reference, if
U\sim\mathrm{InvGam}(a,b)
, then the probability density function of
U
is

\frac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}x^{-a-1}e^{-b/x},\quad x\in(0,\infty),

which implies that
\mathbb{E}(U)=b/(a-1)
for
a>1
and
\mathbb{V}(U)=b^{2}/(a-1)^{2}(a-2)
for
a>2
. The fact that
a>2
is required for
\mathbb{V}(U)
to be finite underlies the condition in
Theorem

1
that the adjacency graph
G
must have
n\geq 6
vertices.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 6
be an integer and
\sigma_{W}>0
. If
\mathbf{W}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{W}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
and
I\in[n]
is independent and uniformly random, then the distribution of
U=(\Var W_{I})^{-1}
is
\mathrm{InvGam}\big((n-1)/2,n/(2\sigma_{W}^{2})\big)
. In particular, the mean and variance of
U
are

\mathbb{E}(U)=\frac{n}{(n-3)\sigma_{W}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}(U)=\frac{2n^{2}}{(n-3)^{2}(n-5)\sigma_{W}^{4}}.

Proof.

Due to the independence of the well depths
W_{i}
and because
I
is uniformly random, the quantity
(n/(n-1))\Var W_{I}
is the (unbiased) sample variance of
n
i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2})
random variables. It is well known that scaling by
(n-1)/\sigma_{W}^{2}
produces a chi-squared random variable with
n-1
degrees of freedom:

\frac{n-1}{\sigma_{W}^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{n-1}\Var W_{I}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\chi_{n-1}^{2}.

Using the scaling properties of gamma random variables, we find that

U=\frac{1}{\Var W_{I}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{n}{\sigma_{W}^{2}\chi_{n-1}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\,\,\mathrm{InvGam}\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n}{2\sigma_{W}^{2}}\right).

The expressions for the expectation and variance of
U
follow from the formulas preceding the statement of the lemma. In particular, we require
(n-1)/2>2
, hence
n\geq 6
, to ensure that the variance of
U
is finite.
∎

4.2
Bounds on the moments of
\Var A_{I}

Next, we consider the factors in
Eq.

13
involving
\Var A_{I}
. According to the definition of a separable disordered energy landscape (
Definition

2.2
), there are
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
such that, for every state
i\in[n]
, the shifted barrier heights
(B_{ij}-f(W_{i}))_{j:\,j\sim i}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
random variables. To use this property, we will denote
Z_{i}=f(W_{i})
and write
A_{i}
as

A_{i}=\log\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}e^{-B_{ij}}=\log\sum_{j:\,j\sim i}e^{Z_{i}-B_{ij}}-Z_{i}.

(14)

Expressing
A_{i}
in this way is useful because the “log-sum-exp” function in
Eq.

14
is Lipschitz, as we elaborate below.

For every integer
d\geq 1
, we define the log-sum-exp function
\mathrm{lse}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{x})=\log\sum_{i\in[d]}e^{x_{i}}.

(15)

Additionally, for a real number
L\geq 0
and an integer
d\geq 1
, we say that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz (with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm) if

\forall\ \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})|\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every integer
d\geq 1
,
\mathrm{lse}
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the
L^{2}
norm.

Proof.

By the intermediate value theorem, for every
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists some
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
|\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{x})-\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{y})|=|\nabla\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{z})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|
. The partial derivatives of
\mathrm{lse}
lie in
[0,1]
and sum to
1
, so
\|\nabla\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{z})\|_{2}\leq 1
. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality then implies that

|\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{x})-\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\|\nabla\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{z})\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

∎

Lipschitz functions of i.i.d. Gaussians exhibit dimension-free concentration according to the Gaussian concentration inequality (e.g.,
[
27
, Theorem 5.2.2]
). Being a standard result, we state it without proof.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer and
\sigma>0
. If
\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
1
-Lipschitz, then

\mathbb{P}(|f(\mathbf{g})-\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{g})|\geq t)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})},\quad\quad t\geq 0.

We will use
Lemma

4.3
to obtain bounds on the central moments of
A_{1}
that do not depend on the dimension
d
. In our setting, the dimension is
d-1
, where
d
is the degree of the adjacency graph
G
. This partly explains why the conclusion of
Theorem

1
holds regardless of
d
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer, let
\sigma>0
, and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
1
-Lipschitz. If
\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
, then, for every integer
m\geq 1
, the
m
th central moment of
f(\mathbf{g})
satisfies

\mu_{m}(f(\mathbf{g}))=\mathbb{E}\left(|f(\mathbf{g})-\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{g})|^{m}\right)\leq m\,\Gamma(m/2)(2\sigma^{2})^{m/2}.

Proof.

By the tail integral formula and
Lemma

4.3
, the central moments of
f
satisfy

\mu_{m}(f(\mathbf{g}))=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|f(\mathbf{g})-\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{g})|^{m}\geq t\right)dt\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}2e^{-t^{2/m}/(2\sigma^{2})}dt=m\,\Gamma(m/2)(2\sigma^{2})^{m/2}.

∎

While we could use
Lemma

4.4
to bound the central moments of
A_{1}
, these moments are not the same as the quantities
\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I})
and
\mathbb{E}((\Var A_{I})^{2})
that appear in
Eq.

13
. Intuitively, the variance of
A_{I}
over a fixed landscape should be closely related to the variance of
A_{1}
over different realizations of the landscape, because the distribution of barrier heights adjacent to a state
i
is the same for every
i
, and
I
is independent of the landscape. This is the idea behind the proof of the next result, which will allow us to bound the first two moments of
\Var A_{I}
using the second and fourth central moments of
A_{1}
. We accept a crude bound on the second moment of
\Var A_{I}
, because its coefficient will matter little to our main result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. If
\mathbf{X}=(X_{i})_{i\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector of identically distributed, square-integrable random variables, and if
I\in[n]
is uniformly random, independently of
\mathbf{X}
, then

\mathbb{E}(\Var X_{I})\leq\mathbb{V}(X_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\big((\Var X_{I})^{2}\big)\leq 40\mu_{4}(X_{1}).

(16)

Proof.

We prove the first bound in
Eq.

16
by applying the law of total variance to the variance of
X_{I}
. Since
X_{I}
is square-integrable, the law of total variance implies both

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(X_{I})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbb{V}(X_{I}\,|\,\mathbf{X})\big)+\mathbb{V}\big(\mathbb{E}(X_{I}\,|\,\mathbf{X})\big)\quad\text{and}

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbb{V}(X_{I}\,|\,I)\big)+\mathbb{V}\big(\mathbb{E}(X_{I}\,|\,I)\big).

(18)

Note that the quantity
\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{V}(X_{I}\,|\,\mathbf{X}))
in
Eq.

17
is the one the first bound concerns, which we previously denoted as
\mathbb{E}(\Var X_{I})
. Since
I
is independent of the
X_{i}
, which have identical distributions, the terms on the right-hand side of
Eq.

18
satisfy

\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbb{V}(X_{I}\mid I)\big)=\mathbb{V}(X_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}\big(\mathbb{E}(X_{I}\mid I)\big)=0.

The first bound in
Eq.

16
follows from substituting the preceding equalities into
Eq.

18
and then by using the nonnegativity of variance:

\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbb{V}(X_{I}\mid\mathbf{X})\big)=\mathbb{V}(X_{1})-\mathbb{V}\big(\mathbb{E}(X_{I}\mid\mathbf{X})\big)\leq\mathbb{V}(X_{1}).

We turn to proving the second bound in
Eq.

16
. We first note that, because
I
is uniformly random, we can write
(\Var X_{I})^{2}
as

(\Var X_{I})^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\in[n]}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{4}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}(X_{j}-\bar{X})^{2},

(19)

where
\bar{X}
denotes the arithmetic mean
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}X_{i}
. Since the
X_{i}
are identically distributed, by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the summands of the second term in
Eq.

19
, we find that

\mathbb{E}\left((\Var X_{I})^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left((X_{i}-\bar{X})^{4}\right)+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left((X_{i}-\bar{X})^{4}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left((X_{1}-\bar{X})^{4}\right).

This is not quite the second bound in
Eq.

16
, because
\bar{X}
is not the same as its mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}X_{1}
. We substitute
X_{i}-\bar{X}=(X_{i}-\mu)+(\mu-\bar{X})
into the preceding bound and use the inequality

|a+b|^{k}\leq 2^{k-1}(|a|^{k}+|b|^{k}),

(20)

which holds for all real numbers
a
,
b
, and
k\geq 1
, to find that

\mathbb{E}\left((\Var X_{I})^{2}\right)\leq 8\left(\mathbb{E}\left((X_{1}-\mu)^{4}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left((\bar{X}-\mu)^{4}\right)\right)=8(\mu_{4}(X_{1})+\mu_{4}(\bar{X})).

To address the second term on the right-hand side, we note that
\bar{X}-\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}Y_{i}
, where
Y_{i}=X_{i}-\mu
. Using the multinomial theorem, we can expand
\mu_{4}(\bar{X})
as

\mu_{4}(\bar{X})=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+\,\cdots\,+k_{n}=4\\
k_{1},k_{2},\,\dots,\,k_{n}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\binom{4}{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{n}}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\prod_{l\in[n]}Y_{l}^{k_{l}}\Bigg).

Since the
Y_{i}
are identically distributed, we can crudely bound expectations of their products by

\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\prod_{l\in[n]}Y_{l}^{k_{l}}\Bigg)\leq 4\,\mathbb{E}(Y_{1}^{4})=4\,\mathbb{E}\left((X_{1}-\mu)^{4}\right)=4\,\mu_{4}(X_{1}).

Lastly, because the multinomial coefficients sum to
n^{4}
, the preceding bounds together imply that

\mathbb{E}\left((\Var X_{I})^{2}\right)\leq 8(\mu_{4}(X)+\mu_{4}(\bar{X}))\leq 40\,\mu_{4}(X_{1}).

∎

Remark 4.1
.

Although the estimates of
Theorems

1
and
2
do not depend on the degree of
G
, the simulations in

show that higher degree favors higher correlation. It should be possible to improve the lower bounds in our main results with degree-dependent terms by specializing the first bound in

to the case when

in this case appears to require a good lower bound on the covariance of
A_{i}(\mathbf{B})
and
A_{j}(\mathbf{B})
for

.

We now combine the preceding lemmas to establish upper bounds on the first two moments of
\Var A_{I}
. As in
Lemma

4.5
, the coefficient in the bound on the second moment matters little to
Theorems

1
and
2
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\sigma_{W}
and
\sigma_{B}
be positive real numbers, and let
G
be a regular graph. If
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a separable,
(\sigma_{W},\sigma_{B})
-disordered energy landscape on
G
, then the variance of
A_{I}=A_{I}(\mathbf{B})
with respect to an independent, uniformly random
I\in V(G)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I})\leq 4\sigma_{B}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left((\Var A_{I})^{2}\right)\leq 1720\sigma_{B}^{4}.

Proof.

We aim to apply
Lemma

4.5
with
X_{i}=A_{i}(\mathbf{B})
, which requires the
A_{i}(\mathbf{B})
to be identically distributed and
A_{I}(\mathbf{B})
to be square-integrable. To verify the first condition, we recall
Eq.

14
. It states that, because
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a separable energy landscape, there are
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
such that

A_{i}(\mathbf{B})=\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{i})-Z_{i},

in terms of a vector
\mathbf{b}_{i}=(Z_{i}-B_{ij})_{j:\,j\sim i}
of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
random variables and
Z_{i}=f(W_{i})
, which is independent of
\mathbf{b}_{i}
. In particular, the conditional distribution of
A_{i}(\mathbf{B})
given
W_{i}
is the same for every
i\in V(G)
. Since the
W_{i}
are identically distributed, so too are the
A_{i}(\mathbf{B})
.

Next, we note that
A_{I}(\mathbf{B})
is square-integrable because
\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{i})
and
Z_{i}
are square-integrable for every
i\in V(G)
. Indeed, it is well known that, if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{x})
lies between
\max_{k\in[d]}x_{k}
and
\max_{k\in[d]}x_{k}+\log d
. Hence, if
d
is the degree of
G
, then the Bhatia–Davis inequality implies that the variance of
\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{i})
cannot be larger than
(\log d)^{2}
. Regarding
Z_{i}
, since
B_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{B}^{2})
for every
j\sim i
and
Z_{i}
is independent of
\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{i})
, the variances
s_{i}\equiv s_{ij}=\mathbb{V}(Z_{i}-B_{ij})
and
t_{i}=\mathbb{V}(Z_{i})
satisfy
\sigma_{B}^{2}=s_{i}+t_{i}
. In particular, the variance of
Z_{i}
is at most
\sigma_{B}^{2}
.

Using
Lemma

4.5
and the independence of
\mathbf{b}_{1}
and
Z_{1}
, we find that
\Var A_{I}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I}(\mathbf{B}))\leq\mathbb{V}(A_{1}(\mathbf{B}))=\mathbb{V}(\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{1}))+\mathbb{V}(Z_{1}).

Since
\mathrm{lse}
is
1
-Lipschitz (
Lemma

4.2
), an application of
Lemma

4.4
with
m=2
shows that
\mathbb{V}(\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{1}))\leq 4s_{1}
, hence

\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I}(\mathbf{B}))\leq 4s_{1}+t_{1}\leq 4\sigma_{B}^{2}.

The second inequality follows from the fact that
\sigma_{B}^{2}=s_{1}+t_{1}
.

Concerning the second moment of
\Var A_{I}(\mathbf{B})
,
Lemma

4.5
states that

\mathbb{E}\big((\Var A_{I}(\mathbf{B}))^{2}\big)\leq 40\,\mu_{4}(A_{1}(\mathbf{B}))=40\big(\mu_{4}(\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{1}))+6\mathbb{V}(\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{1}))\mathbb{V}(Z_{1})+\mu_{4}(Z_{1})\big).

Here, we have used the fact that, if
S
and
T
are independent random variables with finite fourth moments, then

\mu_{4}(S+T)=\mu_{4}(S)+6\mathbb{V}(S)\mathbb{V}(T)+\mu_{4}(T).

Applying the same fact with
S=Z_{1}
and
T=B_{1j}-Z_{1}
shows that

\mu_{4}(Z_{1})=\mu_{4}(B_{1j})-6\mathbb{V}(Z_{1})\mathbb{V}(B_{1j}-Z_{1})-\mu_{4}(B_{1j}-Z_{1})\leq\mu_{4}(B_{1j})=3\sigma_{B}^{4}.

By
Lemma

4.4
(with
m=4
), the fourth central moment of
\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{1})
is at most
16\sigma_{B}^{4}
. We use the simple bounds
\mathbb{V}(Z_{1})\leq\sigma_{B}^{2}
and
\mathbb{V}(\mathrm{lse}(\mathbf{b}_{1}))\leq 4\sigma_{B}^{2}
from above to conclude that

\mathbb{E}\big((\Var A_{I}(\mathbf{B}))^{2}\big)\leq 40(3\sigma_{B}^{4}+6\sigma_{B}^{2}\cdot 4\sigma_{B}^{2}+16\sigma_{B}^{4})=1720\sigma_{B}^{4}.

∎

4.3
Bound on the expected ratio of variances

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section, which is an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}(r^{2})
. The statement uses the same hypotheses as
Theorem

1
, except the requirement that the exit rates are a.s. non-identical.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
G
be a regular graph with
n\geq 6
vertices, and let
\sigma_{W}
and
\sigma_{B}
be positive real numbers. If
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a separable,
(\sigma_{W},\sigma_{B})
-disordered energy landscape on
G
, then the ratio
r=r(\mathbf{Q})
associated with the Arrhenius-like dynamics
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{Arr}_{G}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(r^{2})\leq 4\left(1+O(n^{-1/2})\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{W}}\right)^{2}.

(21)

Proof.

Our starting point is
Eq.

13
:

\mathbb{E}(r^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}(\Var A_{I})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left((\Var W_{I})^{-1}\right)+\sqrt{\,\mathbb{E}\big((\Var A_{I})^{2}\big)\cdot 2\,\mathbb{V}\big((\Var W_{I})^{-1}\big)}.

The hypotheses on
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{B}
are precisely those required for both
Lemmas

4.1
and
4.6
to apply.
2
2

2

Technically, the proof of
Lemma

4.6
does not require the barriers to be symmetric.
We substitute the expressions for the moments of
(\Var W_{I})^{-1}
from
Lemma

4.1
, as well as the upper bounds on the moments of
\Var A_{I}
from
Lemma

4.6
, to find that

\mathbb{E}(r^{2})\leq 4\sigma_{B}^{2}\cdot\frac{n}{(n-3)\sigma_{W}^{2}}+\sqrt{1720\,\sigma_{B}^{4}\cdot\frac{4n^{2}}{(n-3)^{2}(n-5)\sigma_{W}^{4}}}=4\big(1+O(n^{-1/2})\big)\left(\frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{W}}\right)^{2}.

∎

5
Combining the propositions

We now combine
Propositions

3.1
and
4.7
to prove
Theorem

1
. Recall that we are given a connected, regular graph
G
with
n\geq 6
vertices, and positive real numbers
\sigma_{W}
and
\sigma_{B}
. We must show that, if
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
is a separable,
(\sigma_{W},\sigma_{B})
-disordered energy landscape for which the transition rate matrix
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{Arr}_{G}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
has a.s. non-identical exit rates, then the correlation
\rho=\rho(\mathbf{Q})
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\rho)\geq 1-8\left(1+O(n^{-1/2})\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{W}}\right)^{2}.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Assume for the moment that
\rho
is a.s. well defined. Then, according to
Proposition

3.1
,
\rho
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\rho)\geq 1-2\,\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\Var A_{I}}{\Var W_{I}}\right),

where
I\in[n]
is uniformly random and independent of
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{B})
. By
Proposition

4.7
, which has the same hypotheses
Theorem

1
, the expected ratio of variances satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\Var A_{I}}{\Var W_{I}}\right)\leq 4\left(1+O(n^{-1/2})\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{W}}\right)^{2}.

The preceding inequalities together imply the claimed lower bound of
\mathbb{E}(\rho)
.

To finish the proof, we explain why
\rho
is a.s. well defined. For
\rho
to be well defined,
\boldsymbol{\pi}
must be well defined, and
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
\mathbf{q}
must have non-identical entries. The former is a.s. true because
\mathbf{Q}
is a.s. irreducible, due to the fact that
G
is connected and
Q_{ij}=\exp(W_{i}-B_{ij})
is a.s. positive for every
(i,j)\in E(G)
. The latter is true because
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is the Boltzmann distribution (
2
) with i.i.d. Gaussian well depths, which are a.s. non-identical, while
\mathbf{q}
has a.s. non-identical entries by assumption.
∎
