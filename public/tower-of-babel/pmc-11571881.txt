Trends of drug-resistant tuberculosis and risk factors to poor treatment-outcome: a database analysis in Littoral region-Cameroon, 2013–2022
Authors: Teyim Pride Mbuh, Patricia Mendjime, Irene-Adeline Goupeyou-Wandji, Valerie Flore Donkeng-Donfack, Judith Kahou, Laurent-Mireille Endale Mangamba, Severin Mefoug, Patricia Annie Bisso Ngono, Linda Esso, Georges Alain Etoundi Mballa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTuberculosis(TB), currently has limited treatment options, and faces worldwide threat of drug-resistance(DR). In 2022, the DR-TB prevalence in Cameroon was 1.4% among new-cases and 8.3% among retreatment-cases. We analyzed the DR-TB database to descript the trends and DR-TB profile, treatment-outcome and associated risk-factors so-as-to propose measures to enhance program performance in Cameroon.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study, analysed the DR-TB database of the Littoral region from 2013 to 2022. We appreciated the data-quality using zero-reporting, completeness, consistency, and validity indicators. We categorized DR-TB into Rifampicin-resistant-TB(RR-TB), multi-drug-resistant-TB(MDR-TB), pre-extensive-drug-resistant-TB(pre-XDR-TB), and XDR-TB and performed descriptive statistics. We assessed DR-TB treatment outcome targeting > 80% cure and/or completed treatment. Multiple logistic regression was used to determine risk factors related to poor treatment outcomes, and adjusted relative risk(RR) was considered significant at p < 0.05.ResultsOverall database quality was 93.7% with uniqueness 100%, data-completeness 82.5%, consistency 97% and validity 95.1%. A total of 567 DR-TB cases were reported, with median age of 34 (1–80) years, male-to-female sex ratio (3:2). Cases were classified as 19(3.4%) RR-TB, 536(94.6%) MDR-TB, 7(1.3%) pre-XDR-TB, and 4(0.7%) XDR-TB. Case-reporting increased from 2013, reaching their peak in 2018. The overall treatment refusal rate was 123(11.9%) and treatment outcomes of 270(60.8%) cured, 116(26.4%) completed, 32(7.2%) deaths, 19(4.3%) lost-to-follow-up, and 6(1.4%) failure were recorded. We identified 84 dead (CFR:14.8%) amongst whom 52(62%) refused treatment, 17(20%) occurred during the first month of therapy and 13(15.5%) HIV-TB co-infected. Male gender [p = 0.006, RR = 2.5 (95% CI: 1.3–4.7)], HIV positive status [p = 0.012, RR = 2.1 (95% CI: 1.2–3.7)], and previous DR-TB status [p = 0.02, RR = 3.9 (95% CI: 1.3–12.0)] were statistically associated to poor treatment outcomes.ConclusionIn the Littoral Region-Cameroon, cases of DR-TB increased from 2013, reaching their peak in 2018 befor dropping right up to 2022. RR-TB, MDR-TB, Pre-XDR-TB and XDR-TB represented 3.4%, 94.6%, 1.3% and 0.7% of all reported DR-TB cases. Overall, DR-TB treatment success rate was 87.2%. Male-gender, HIV-positive status, and previous DR-TB are associated with poor TB treatment outcomes. We recommend universal drug susceptibility testing to ensure early/maximum DR-TB case-detection and proper pre-treatment counselling to limit the high death rates and anti-TB treatment refusal rates which are setbacks from achieving end-TB strategies.Keywords: Drug-resistant TB, Treatment outcomes, Associated risk factors, Littoral-Cassmeroon

Introduction

Robert Koch’s landmark discovery in 1882 that tuberculosis (TB) is caused by an infectious agent, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tb) demystified the age-old scourge around tuberculosis (TB) and introduced the possibility of treating it with antimicrobials [1]. TB is a communicable disease transmitted principally by the respiratory route. It has an incubation period of 1–3 months [2]. Globally, people with an immune compromised HIV/AIDS status are reported to be 17–23 times more likely to develop TB than immunocompetent persons, and have a 50% lifetime chance of developing active disease [3–5].

Eighty-one years after the discovery of streptomycin and para-aminosalicylic acid as TB medication in 1943, TB claims an enormous global health burden. It is the second leading infectious killer after COVID-19 [6] and kills over 1.6 million people annually [6]. Resistance to streptomycin was reported only four years after its discovery. Drug resistance TB (DR-TB) is a worldwide public health security threat that has greatly jeopardized the effective TB control in Africa and globally leading to prolonged hospital stays, increased catastrophic costs, and mortality [7]. WHO defines Multi-Drug Resistance (MDR-TB) as strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis that are resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin, with or without resistance to other first-line drugs [8]. DR surveillance data show that 3.9% of new and 21% of previously treated TB cases were estimated to have had multidrug-resistant/rifampicin tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) in 2015 [9]. WHO, estimated 450 000 incident cases of DR-TB with 191 000 (42.4%) deaths to have occurred due to DR-TB in 2021 [10].

TB surveillance in Cameroon is disease-specific with activities aimed at obtaining targeted health data. Since 2013, the Littoral region alone has systematically accounted for about a quarter of all TB reported cases in Cameroon. The difference between WHO estimates and the actual number of cases reported by any country is generally referred to as the country’s reporting gap. According to the WHO country TB report, Cameroon has an overall TB reporting gap of 45% and a DR-TB reporting gaps of 80% [11]. Several reasons could be attributed to this significant and systematic reporting gaps amongst many is a poor data cleaning and analysis system before reporting. This applies for all disease-specific reporting surveillance systems. If effective data quality assurance policies are not implemented, national level data will be less reliable and mislead policy decisions made using weak data. Regular data cleaning, analysis, and dissemination are essential in implementing an effective and efficient disease program control [3]. This move is vital for the peripheral levels to be able to report, completely, accurately, and timely data to all levels of reporting. The aim of this study was to assess the national DR-TB database, evaluate treatment -outcome and associated risk-factors for drug resistance to enhance program policies.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective cohort study carried out at the Regional Technical Groups for TB (RTG/TB) in the Littoral Region and lasted 6 months from April to September 2023. This study covered the TB surveillance period of January 1st, 2013 to December 31, 2022. Authorization to analyze the DR-TB data base in the Littoral Region was obtained from the National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTCP). All DR-TB cases reported for drug resistant TB from 1st January 2013 to 31 December 2022 in the Littoral region were included for this study. The Littoral region has four administrative departments, which are divided into two TB epidemiologic regions by the NTCP. The Wourri administrative division is considered as the Douala TB epidemiologic region while the Nkam, Moungo and Sanapa-Maritime are considered the Littoral TB epidemiologic region as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The Douala and Littoral TB epidemiological regions

The data base was evaluated for data quality in terms of Zero reporting, unicity, completeness, consistency and validity. Data unicity was calculated as = ( )*100, where n = duplicated data entries, N = total data entries. The data completeness was the proportion of variable entries (n) effectively entered in the data base to the expected number (N) of variables. This was calculated as: Completeness = ( )*100. Data consistency consisted of checking to ensure that each unit of a data variable was written in a consistent manner, it was calculated as a proportion of consistently entered variable units (c) to the total number of units of that variables (C) and calculated as = ( )*100. To qualify the data base, we graded each of the attributes of unicity, completeness, consistency and validity as 1 point for < 50%, 2 point for 50%≤x < 90%, and 3 point for ≥ 90%. The overall quality was classified as poor, moderate or good if the average corresponded to 1, 2, or 3 respectively.

DR-TB cases were described in term of demographic, clinical characteristics, year of diagnosis and proportion initiated on treatment. Treatment efficacy for cases on second line anti-TB medications was monitored by microscopy and culture. Monthly microscopy results were available and reported semi-quantitatively as (rare, 1+, 2+, 3 + and no acid-fast bacillus (AFB) seen). These results were transformed into qualitative variables of positive and negative. The percentage negativity for each month (M) was calculated and plotted on a line curve to represent the smear-negativity rate during the treatment procedure. treatment outcomes of cured and completed treatment were considered to be treatment successes. Treatment failure, death, and loss-to-follow-up were considered treatment failures. Good treatment success was set at ≥ 80%.

Since this was a database analysis where we didn’t have direct contact with the patients or their specimens, we couldn’t obtain participants’ consent. We also judge it as not being obligatory to obtain an ethical clearance. So we didn’t apply for any ethical clearance from an Institutional Review Board (IRB). We however obtained administrative clearance from the Ministry of Public Health with decision number No04671/L/MINSANTE/SG/CCOUSP to evaluate the drug-resistant tuberculosis database analysis in the Littoral Region-Cameroon, 2013–2022. Participant information was anonymized by assigning a unique protocol number and the data set was stored in a computer protected by a password to which only authorized persons had access. The database was exported into EPI info 7.2.5 and qualitative variables were expressed as frequencies, proportions and ratios. Measures of central tendency were used for the analysis of quantitative variables. Simple logistic regression was used to determine risk factors related to poor outcomes, variable with p = 0.2 were introduced into a multiple logistic regression model and the adjusted relative risk (RR) was considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Data quality

The database was made of 561 rows and 171 columns giving an overall 96102 cell counts. There was zero reporting practiced meaning all cell values were marked as ‘NA’ when a variable of interest wasn’t applicable, the cell was not left empty but clearly reported as not being applicable. No duplicate variable was observed. The data completeness was originally 43,064 (56.6%). After excluding 115 rows because they were currently on treatment and 117 colums of noninterest (specimen type, specimen reception dates, dates for treatment controls etc.) for this analysis, we were left with a total of 444 rows and 56 columns giving a total of 24,920 cell counts. The completeness of the cleaned database was 20,442 (82.2%). A total of 24,173 (97%) variable consistency was recorded. Out of the 888 cell entries assessed for validity, 844 (95.1%) were valid. The overall data quality was good (95.1%).

Describing DR-TB cases

From 2013 to 2022 in the Littoral region, a total of 567 DR-TB case were reported. Case reporting increased from 2013 to 2016. A mild decrease was reported in 2017, but the number of cases quickly rose back reaching its peak in 2018. Since then, the number of cases reporting has dropped. On the other hand, the number of males reported for DR-TB were systematically higher than those of females. Peak disparities between males and females of 25 (47.2%) cases were recorded in 2019.

Of the 24 health districts present in the Littoral region, only 14 (58.3%) reported cases of DR-TB from 2013 to 2022. Most 430 (96.8%) of the participants were diagnosed in the Douala epidemiologic region. Those diagnosed in the Deido health district were the most represented 197 (44.6%), followed by those in the New Bell district 72 (16.2%).

The 567 DR-TB cases were classified as 20 (3.5%) rifampicin resistant (RR)-TB, 536 (94.6%) multi-drug resistant (MDR)-TB, 7 (1.3%) pre-extensively drug resistant (XDR)-TB and 4 (0.7%) XDR-TB. Their median age was 34 (Range:1–80) years. More males 272 (61.3%) were infected with a male to female sex ratio of 3:2. The age range of 21–40 years was the most affected both in the male 206 (59.4%) and female 134 (60.9%) population as illustrated in Fig. 2.

A total of 444 (78.3%) cases were given second-line TB treatment in the Littoral region. Amongst the 123 (21.7%) cases not placed on DR-TB medication, 33 (26.8%) refused to admit their diagnostic status as being DR, 38 (30.9%) transferred out of the region for treatment, and 52 (42.3%) died.

Of the 444 cases on DR-TB medication, 439 (98.9%) cases had pulmonary DR-TB while 5(1.1%) cases had extra-pulmonary DR-TB. One hundred and seven (24.1%) cases tested positive for HIV. Among those with HIV-TB comorbidity, 75 (70.1%) were on ART treatment. Those with no anti-TB medication history represented 143 (32.2%). Treatment outcome

Overall, 444 DR-TB cases were on anti-TB medication for at least a nine-month period. After a four-month intensive phase of treatment, 407 (91.7%) cases had a non-TB detectable bacterial load by microscopy. The non-TB detection rate was geometric with 240 (54.1%) and 329 (74.1%) cases having a non-TB detection rate at M1 and M2 respectively as illustrated on Fig. 3.

TB Detectability during 9 months of DR-TB treatment in the Littoral region from 2013–2022

The overall 386 (86.9%) cases recorded good treatment outcome ( treatment success) amongst whom 270 (60.8%) were cured and 116 (26.1%) completed their treatment. Amongst those who had poor treatment outcomes, 32 (55.2%) died [most 17 (53%) within the first month of therapy], 6 (1.4%) failed treatment, and 19 (4.3%) were lost to follow-up.

Associated risk factors to poor treatment outcome

Males were 2.5 (95% CI: 1.3, 4.7) times more at risk of having a poor treatment outcome as compared to females, which was statistically significant (p = 0.006). Being HIV co-infected was 2.1 (95% CI: 1.2, 3.7) times more at risk of having a poor treatment outcome as compared to not being, which was statistically significant (p = 0.012). Extra-pulmonary TB status resulted in a 4.5 (95% CI: 0.7, 27.3) times higher risk of poor treatment outcome compared to having pulmonary TB, which was not statistically significant (p = 0.105).

Male gender [p = 0.006, RR = 2.5 (95% CI: 1.3, 4.7)], HIV-positive status [p = 0.012, RR = 2.1 (95% CI: 1.2, 3.7)], and previous DR-TB status [p = 0.02, RR = 3.9 (95% CI: 1.3. 12.0)] were statistically associated with poor treatment outcomes Table 2.

Discussion

A program database of high quality is essential for healthcare programs to make informed decisions and provide patients with the best possible care. An accurately analyzed and timely reported database can help healthcare professionals identify errors and trends in negative practices, improve patient management and predict outcomes [12]. On the other hand, sub obtimal data quality in healthcare can lead to poor decision making with negative financial and health consequences [12]. This study covered a period of 10 years and the overall data quality was good (93.7%) with 100% uniqueness, 82.5% data-completeness, and 97% consistency. This provides strong evidence that the outcome of this study can be comfortably implemented to DR-TB patients.

Overall, we noticed that case reporting increased from 2013 to 2016, with a mild decrease in 2017, but the number quickly rose back reaching its peak in 2018. Since then, the number of cases reported dropped within the years up to 2022. These results are similar to those reported in China where the annual incidence of notified DR-TB increased over time in Hunan province from 2012–2018 [13] but different from the Global Tuberculosis Report of 2022 which shows a continuous decrease in the estimated number of incident cases of MDR/RR-TB in WHO African Region from 2015–2021 [14]. We think that the increase in case reporting from 2013 to 2018 could be attributed to several factors such as the progressive introduction of GeneXpert point of care diagnostic instruments in the region, revision of the TB diagnostic algorithms to include priority new TB suspects, the introduction of intensified case-finding activities and the creation of additional diagnostic and treatment centers. The slight decrease in 2017 could be attributed to a prolonged downtime of the GeneXpert instrument coupled with its cartridges’ stock out. This situation was worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020–2023 with patients showing similar signs and symptoms as TB. The pandemic not only scared people from consulting health facilities but also caused a total halt of international trade, diversion of resources (including human and financial), stress and anxiety, illness, quarantine, or dying of health personnel, and therefore reduced routine health service staffing. With the increasing death reporting rate and international travel restrictions, TB diagnosis was no longer a priority.

Despite several efforts put in place by the NCTB since 2019, NCTP data shows that Cameroon has reported only 19–22% of WHO estimated cases of MDR-TB in Cameroon. This gap is difficult to criticize given that Cameroon like most of the countries who have made significant progress in TB Management have not organized a national drug susceptibility testing survey. We thing if this nationwide drug susceptibility testing survey, was conducted, the gap between WHO’ estimates and the NCTP’ cases reported will be better appreciated.

This study reveals that most (96.8%) of the DR-TB cases were diagnosed in Douala. Those diagnosed in the Deido health district were the most represented (44.6%), followed by those in the New Bell district (16.2%). These findings are in accordance with those of other studies [13, 15, 16] and those of the National Tuberculosis control Program (NCTP) in 2022 which reported that TB is mostly diagnosed in major cities of Cameroon with Douala and Yaoundé accounting for 17% and 15% respectively [17]. This could be partially justified by the fact that most of the sick or people who suspect TB sickness tend to seek health services in the main cities, especially Yaoundé and Douala, despites the presence of Diagnostic and Treatment Centres (DTCs) in their localities [18]. Factors such as poverty and over-crowding have been attributed to increased TB incidence in major cities [19]. In Douala, economic growth has not kept up with urbanization, and the government has not put in place the required resources to manage the rapid urban expansion and infrastructure needed to accommodate an increasing influx of migrants. Deido and New Bell districts are amongst the most populated [20] districts in Cameroon and equally harbor major attracting structures such as several super-markets, many inter-urban traveling agencies, and institutional administrative headquarters. These districts are also known to have many slums and informal settlements as a result of rapid and unplanned urbanization. Slums and informal settlements have been reported as ideal conditions for transmission of infectious diseases, including TB and drug-resistant TB [21–23]. According to WHO reports, half of the 10.4 million incident cases of TB in 2016 came from five countries where 20–50% of the urban population living in slums [24]. Most (67.8%) of the patients who had developed TB resistance had previous TB treatment exposure. These findings were similar to our previous studies [25], [18] in the Littoral region of Cameroon.

A total of 567 DR-TB cases were reported from 2013 to 2022 in the Littoral region with a treatment enrolment rate of 444 (78.3%). This was better than the 55% global cumulative total percentage of people reported as enrolled on treatment for MDR/RR-TB from 2018 to 2022 [26]. In our study, 26.8% of non-treatment enrollment was due to refusal to acknowledge their test results. Factors such as the fear of stigmatization [27], the fear that TB is linked to HIV in the minds of community members [28], and the fear of losing their job or not being able to provide for their homes [29] have been associated with MDR-TB treatment non-enrollment. Dying between MDR-TB diagnoses and treatment accounted for 42.3% of all the DR-TB non-treatment enrollment. Factors such as old age [30] previous TB episodes [31], diabetes, and HIV infection [32] were associated with mortality among MDR-TB cases.

Overall, this study found a DR-TB treatment success rate of 86.9%. This finding shows Cameroon achieved the national target of 85% MDR-TB treatment success in 2022 [17]. it was also better than the treatment success of first-line treatment recorded in other studies in Yaoundé and Douala, Cameroon [33], [34]. We think the good treatment rates could be explained by the fact that all those on second-line TB regimen were: (1) tested for HIV before initiation of treatment; (2) hospitalized and observed as they took their daily medication (DOTs) during the intensive phase of therapy; (3) screened for co-morbidities before initiation and during TB regimen free of charge; (4) prohibited from cigarette smoking and advised to limit alcohol consumption; and (5) given alimentary assistance. These strategies were not implemented for the first-line TB regimen. To better understand phenomenon, we propose a treatment efficacy study be carried out both amongst patients on first and second line TB treatment to elucidate factors associated with good treatment outcome.

Conclusion

In the Littoral Region-Cameroon, cases of DR-TB increased from 2013, reaching their peak in 2018 befor dropping right up to 2022. RR-TB, MDR-TB, Pre-XDR-TB and XDR-TB represented 3.4%, 94.6%, 1.3% and 0.7% of all reported DR-TB cases. Overall, DR-TB treatment success rate was 87.2%. Male-gender, HIV-positive status, and previous DR-TB are associated with poor TB treatment outcomes. We recommend universal drug susceptibility testing to ensure early/maximum DR-TB case-detection and proper pre-treatment counselling to limit the high death rates and anti-TB treatment refusal rates which are setbacks from achieving end-TB strategies.

Recommendations

We recommend the implementation of universal drug susceptibility testing in Cameroon to ensure early and/or maximum DR-TB case detection. Proper pre- treatment counseling is also recommended to limit the non-treatment enrollment, loss to follow-up, and morality rates, which are setbacks to achieving end-TB strategies in the Cameroon.
