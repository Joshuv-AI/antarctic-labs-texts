Amino acid metabolic reprogramming drives pathogenesis and therapy in hematologic malignancies
Authors: Dexiang Ren, Rong Liu, Wei Li, Linzhou Li, Xueyin Lv, Xiaojun Xia, Xudong Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHematological malignancies exhibit distinct patterns of amino acid metabolic reprogramming, which support uncontrolled proliferation, immune escape, and therapy resistance. Rather than merely fueling biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism intricately modulates tumor progression and therapeutic responses through interactions with signaling pathways such as mTOR, β-catenin/c-Myc, and NF-κB. This review emphasizes the rewired utilization of key amino acids—including glutamine, arginine, leucine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine—across leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, and discusses how these changes orchestrate immune suppression and redox imbalance. Emerging studies reveal that metabolic vulnerabilities can be therapeutically exploited via enzyme depletion, transport inhibition, or combination regimens with immunotherapies and mTOR inhibitors. Moreover, amino acid-driven drug resistance mechanisms, particularly involving stromal support and transcriptional reprogramming, pose both challenges and opportunities for next-generation treatment design. By decoding the complex metabolic-immune-tumor network, we highlight strategic interventions that leverage amino acid metabolism as a therapeutic axis in hematological cancers.Keywords: Metabolic reprogramming, Hematological malignancies, Amino acid dependencies, Tumor–Immune metabolic crosstalk, Therapeutic resistance mechanisms, Targeted metabolic therapies

Introduction

Hematologic malignancies, including leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, are severe diseases characterized by clinical manifestations such as anemia, infections, bleeding, and organ infiltration [1, 2]. According to the latest statistics, in 2022, lymphoma ranked first among hematologic malignancies in China, with an incidence of 85,200 cases, followed closely by leukemia with 81,900 new cases annually. Among all cancer-related deaths, leukemia ranked tenth with 50,100 deaths, while lymphoma ranked twelfth with 41,600 deaths [3]. The high incidence and mortality rates of hematologic malignancies impose a significant burden on society. Although current treatment strategies—including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and immunotherapy—have improved therapeutic outcomes to some extent, challenges such as relapse, refractory disease, and drug resistance persist [4].

In recent years, growing insights into the Warburg effect have drawn widespread attention to metabolic reprogramming in the study of hematologic malignancies. Hematologic tumor cells obtain nutrients through systemic circulation and often exhibit heightened metabolic demands. These cells are capable of adjusting their metabolic pathways to adapt to adverse intra- and extracellular environments, thereby sustaining their unlimited proliferative capacity and survival [5]. Among these metabolic alterations, amino acid metabolism has been shown to play a pivotal role [6]. For instance, lymphoma cells rely on glutamine (Gln) metabolism to support oxidative phosphorylation and energy production [7]. Many hematologic tumor cells exhibit arginine (Arg) auxotrophy due to the lack of enzymes required for endogenous Arg synthesis, such as argininosuccinate synthetase, thereby depending on exogenous Arg for survival [8, 9]. Moreover, hematologic malignancies can suppress immune responses by modulating amino acid metabolism. Tumor cells can impair dendritic cell (DC) and natural killer (NK) cell maturation and inhibit T cell proliferation through the regulation of tryptophan (Trp) metabolism [10], contributing to immune evasion. Additionally, recent studies have indicated that resistance to therapy in hematologic malignancies is associated with reprogrammed amino acid metabolism [11]. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of amino acid metabolism in the initiation, progression, and prognosis of hematologic malignancies.

Amino acid metabolism

Amino acids are not only the fundamental building blocks of proteins—essential for cell structure, enzymatic activity, and nucleic acid synthesis—but also serve as central regulators in metabolic reprogramming. Based on their catabolic pathways, amino acids can be classified into glucogenic amino acids (e.g., glutamine), which contribute to gluconeogenesis; ketogenic amino acids (e.g., leucine), which generate acetyl-CoA for lipid synthesis; and those that are both glucogenic and ketogenic (e.g., phenylalanine) [12].

Amino acid metabolism refers to the synthesis, degradation, interconversion, and involvement of amino acids in other metabolic processes within living organisms. This complex network involves a variety of enzymes and transporters, including glutaminase (GLS), arginase, and glutamine transporters such as SLC1A5 and SLC38A1. Rather than functioning as isolated events, amino acid metabolic pathways are highly integrated and dynamic. Studying individual metabolic routes or metabolites can gradually reveal their roles in cellular physiology and pathology, thereby contributing to a broader understanding of the overall network.

For example, glutamine (Gln) has been identified as an indispensable bioenergetic and biosynthetic substrate required for proper differentiation and function of T cells; its depletion impairs the quantity and activity of effector T cells [13]. Protein arginine methyltransferase 2 (PRMT2) has been shown to promote inflammatory signaling in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), correlating with poor prognosis [14]. Furthermore, GLS-mediated glutaminolysis meets the energy demands of tumor cells and promotes their proliferation [15]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of amino acid metabolism in both physiological and pathological cellular processes.

Amino acid metabolism and hematologic malignancies

Glucogenic amino acids

Glutamine

The metabolic enzymes involved in glutamine (Gln) metabolism significantly influence the progression of hematologic malignancies. For example, glutaminase (GLS) promotes tumor cell proliferation [15]; glutamine synthetase is associated with poor prognosis [16]; and glutamate dehydrogenase enhances leukemia cell proliferation in murine models [17]. Transporters involved in Gln uptake, such as SLC38A1 [18] and SLC1A5 [19], support energy metabolism in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells.

Gln metabolism also regulates immune function. It is essential for the activation of immune cells such as T cells and macrophages (Mac) [13, 20]. The Gln transporter SLC38A2 modulates mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling to promote T cell production [21]. Moreover, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), a metabolite derived from Gln, contributes to the differentiation and stability of regulatory T cells (Tregs) through epigenetic mechanisms such as demethylation of the Foxp3 gene [22, 23].

Targeting Gln metabolism offers potential therapeutic strategies for hematologic malignancies. Studies have shown that depletion of Gln using asparaginase (ASNase) may serve as a potential treatment for AML [24]. However, components of the tumor microenvironment, such as adipocytes and bone marrow stromal cells, can confer resistance to ASNase by supplying Gln to leukemia cells [25]. Therefore, Gln metabolic reprogramming plays a crucial role in both immune modulation and tumor cell survival.

Arginine

Arginine (Arg) metabolism also plays a pivotal role in hematologic malignancies. Argininosuccinate synthetase 1 (ASS1) is frequently deficient or expressed at low levels in lymphoma and leukemia [8, 9]. On one hand, Arg supports lymphocyte and T cell proliferation and activation, enhances the generation of central memory T cells, and improves the phagocytic and cytotoxic activity of macrophages, thereby exerting anti-tumor effects [26, 27]. On the other hand, tumor cells can deplete Arg from the microenvironment, leading to immunosuppression via activation of NOD and arginase enzymes, which inhibit T cell proliferation and neutrophil infiltration, promote M2 macrophage polarization, and impair host immunity, thereby facilitating tumor progression [28–30]. Thus, targeting NOD and arginase may represent a novel immunotherapeutic approach in hematologic malignancies.

In addition, protein arginine methyltransferase 2 (PRMT2) promotes inflammatory signaling in AML, contributing to poor prognosis [14]. This suggests that Arg metabolism may also promote tumor progression through modulation of inflammation. STAVROU et al. [31] found that AML induces immunosuppressive conditions for conventional T cells via arginase-2-mediated Arg depletion, although invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells remain unaffected. Therefore, enhancing iNKT cell activity may offer a promising strategy for treating Arg-depleted AML. Furthermore, Arg-based nanovaccines have been shown to elicit long-lasting immune memory and demonstrate significant anti-lymphoma effects [32]. These findings underscore the clinical potential of Arg-based combination therapies and vaccines in the treatment of hematologic malignancies.

In summary, glucogenic amino acids play essential roles in cellular metabolism, energy supply, protein synthesis, antioxidant defense, and immune regulation. They can directly affect tumor cells via metabolic enzymes and transporters or indirectly influence tumor progression and prognosis through immunomodulation. Notably, Arg also contributes to inflammation-mediated tumor development in AML.

Ketogenic amino acid

Leucine

Leucine (Leu), a classic ketogenic amino acid, plays a pivotal role in the metabolic reprogramming of certain hematologic malignancies. Studies have shown that branched-chain amino acid transaminase 1 (BCAT1), which is highly expressed in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), catalyzes the transamination of Leu and other branched-chain amino acids to produce α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), thereby supplying energy and metabolic intermediates to tumor cells [33]. Chen et al. [34] demonstrated through in vitro and in vivo experiments that doxorubicin-loaded Leu polymers could release Leu and activate the mTORC1 signaling pathway, potentially inhibiting AML progression. Similarly, Wang et al. [35] found that Leu has been shown to activate the mTOR pathway to enhance β-catenin stability, which may subsequently upregulate c-Myc expression and promote leukemia cell proliferation in specific contexts. These findings suggest that Leu metabolism may exert dual effects on hematologic malignancies through different signaling pathways.

Lysine

Lysine (Lys) metabolism has a significant impact on the progression of hematologic malignancies. Lysine oxidase, a key enzyme in the Lys catabolic pathway, exhibits antitumor properties in various cancers by consuming Lys and generating large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and hydrogen peroxide, leading to tumor cell apoptosis [36]. Lys also plays a vital role in regulating immune cell function, promoting the proliferation and differentiation of T and B lymphocytes, thus supporting immune surveillance.

Moreover, Lys-derived metabolites such as acetyl-CoA and α-KG serve not only as intermediates in cellular metabolism but also as essential substrates or cofactors for epigenetic modifications. Fluctuations in their levels can directly influence the activity of epigenetic enzymes, leading to altered gene expression patterns that may drive the development of hematologic malignancies [37]. These findings indicate that Lys metabolism may contribute to disease progression via epigenetic regulation and immune enhancement.

Tryptophan

Tryptophan (Trp) plays a dual role in the initiation and progression of hematologic malignancies. Under the catalysis of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), Trp is converted into kynurenine. IDO promotes the expression of non-coding RNA molecules such as microRNA-18a, which downregulates the NKG2D receptor on natural killer (NK) cells, thereby reducing their cytotoxicity [38]. Kynurenine also activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), suppressing the expression of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86 on dendritic cells (DCs), impairing antigen presentation and inhibiting DC maturation [10]. Activation of AhR further induces immunosuppressive genes such as PD-1 and IL-10, ultimately inhibiting T cell proliferation [10] and facilitating immune evasion in hematologic malignancies.

Wang et al. [39] reported that benzene disrupts normal hematopoiesis via Trp-derived metabolites such as kynurenine and indole-3-propionic acid, contributing to the onset of AML and pediatric leukemia. This process involves the upregulation of rate-limiting enzymes in kynurenine synthesis, thereby promoting AML progression [40]. Conversely, Karmakar et al. [41] found that Trp metabolites such as serotonin enhance the expression of activating receptors (e.g., NKG2D and CD16) on NK cells, thereby strengthening their recognition and cytotoxicity against tumor cells. These observations suggest that Trp metabolism may either promote or inhibit hematologic malignancies depending on the context.

Currently, IDO1 inhibitors targeting Trp metabolism—such as Indoximod and Epacadostat—have shown promising results in both preclinical and clinical studies of hematologic malignancies. Their therapeutic effects are attributed to increased T cell numbers and impaired activity of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [42, 43]. Moreover, combination therapy of IDO1 inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been shown to reduce relapse in NK/T-cell lymphoma [44]. These findings suggest that modulating Trp metabolism, particularly in combination with immunotherapies, could enhance immune responses and remodel the tumor microenvironment, offering novel therapeutic strategies for hematologic malignancies.

Amino acids with both glucogenic and ketogenic properties

Phenylalanine

Phenylalanine (Phe) metabolism plays a critical role in the development of hematologic malignancies. Fu et al. [45] reported elevated fasting plasma levels of Phe in 27 patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) undergoing high-dose methotrexate treatment. Conversely, Cheng et al. [46] found that Phe deprivation induced endoplasmic reticulum stress, leading to apoptosis in multiple myeloma (MM) cells via the ATF3-CHOP-DR5 signaling pathway. Moreover, Li et al. [47] demonstrated that L-Phe polymers impaired the viability of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and enhanced T cell-mediated immunosurveillance in a murine model of ALL, thereby suppressing the progression of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL). Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted effects of Phe metabolism on apoptosis, immune regulation, and therapeutic responsiveness in hematologic malignancies. Further exploration of its underlying mechanisms may help clarify the precise roles of this pathway in disease progression.

Tyrosine

Tyrosine (Tyr), a non-essential amino acid, can be metabolized via several pathways to generate fumarate and the ketogenic precursor acetoacetate (its primary catabolic route), as well as neurotransmitters such as dopamine and catecholamines (norepinephrine and epinephrine). Tyr metabolism exerts both immunosuppressive and immunostimulatory effects in the context of hematologic malignancies.

For instance, Huang et al. [48] reported significant alterations in Tyr-related metabolites in patients with MM compared to healthy individuals. Cheng et al. [49] found that hematologic cancer cells reprogram their metabolism to increase Tyr-derived succinyl derivatives (e.g., succinylacetone), which in turn suppress immune cell activity, potentially enabling immune evasion. In contrast, Wu et al. [50] demonstrated that dopamine enhances T cell-mediated antitumor immunity by activating dopamine receptors and suppressing MDSC function. Thus, Tyr metabolism can either contribute to immune evasion or support immune-mediated tumor suppression, depending on the metabolic context and immune landscape.

They contribute to glucose homeostasis, fat metabolism, and weight control. Such dual-function amino acids can suppress the proliferation of hematologic tumor cells through various mechanisms. Specifically, Phe metabolism modulates both tumor cell viability and T cell immune function, thereby influencing leukemia and lymphoma progression. Molecular mechanisms underlying amino acid metabolism regulation

mTOR pathway

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is an atypical serine/threonine kinase that exists in two distinct complexes: mTORC1 and mTORC2. Among them, mTORC1 serves as a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism, integrating signals from growth factors, energy status, cellular stress, and amino acid availability to promote anabolic processes [57]. Amino acid metabolism plays a crucial role in modulating mTOR signaling and thereby influencing the progression and prognosis of hematologic malignancies. Nguyen et al. [58] reported that enhanced glutamine (Gln) catabolism activates the mTORC1 pathway and promotes the development of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). However, other studies found that doxorubicin-loaded leucine (Leu) polymers activate the mTOR pathway and inhibit autophagy in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, thereby prolonging survival in AML mouse models [59]. These opposing effects of mTOR pathway activation by different amino acids may be attributed to the specific leukemia subtype (ALL vs. AML), as well as to the context of amino acid supplementation or deprivation (e.g., low Gln vs. high Leu). Therefore, a deeper understanding of the amino acid-specific regulatory mechanisms governing mTOR signaling is warranted.

β-Catenin/c-Myc pathway

In cancerous cells, the Wingless/Int (Wnt) signaling pathway is often aberrantly activated, leading to the nuclear translocation of β-catenin. This event triggers the overexpression of the proto-oncogene c-Myc, which in turn promotes epithelial cell hyperproliferation and tumorigenesis [60]. The activation of c-Myc is thus closely associated with tumor initiation, progression, and clinical outcome [61]. Leu metabolism has been shown to promote leukemia cell proliferation and survival via activation of the mTOR/β-catenin/c-Myc signaling axis [34]. These findings suggest that reprogramming of amino acid metabolism contributes to the survival advantage of tumor cells by modulating the β-catenin/c-Myc pathway, thereby accelerating the progression of hematologic malignancies.

NF-κB pathway

Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) is a widely expressed transcription factor that regulates various biological processes, including inflammation, immune responses, cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis. Emerging evidence suggests that inhibition of Gln metabolism can activate NF-κB signaling while suppressing signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, thus promoting the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. This polarization enhances antitumor immunity [62]. These findings indicate that targeting amino acid metabolic pathways can modulate NF-κB activity to boost immune responses and suppress tumor cell proliferation and survival.

Therapeutic potential and challenges of targeting amino acid metabolism

The dependency of hematologic malignancies on specific amino acids presents a compelling opportunity for therapeutic intervention. Strategies targeting amino acid metabolism—including amino acid-depleting enzymes, metabolic inhibitors, and analogs—aim to disrupt tumor progression by restricting the availability of critical nutrients, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Preclinical in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that NEI-01 induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in AML cells by depleting extracellular arginine (Arg), and effectively suppresses AML progression in murine models [63]. Tumors frequently exhibit low expression of argininosuccinate synthetase 1 (ASS1), rendering them auxotrophic for Arg. Enzymes such as arginine deiminase (ADI) and arginase 1 (ARG1) degrade extracellular Arg into citrulline and ornithine, respectively, leading to systemic Arg depletion. This mechanism has been exploited to suppress tumor growth. For instance, a phase I dose-escalation study revealed that ADI-PEG20 improved azacitidine resistance in relapsed/refractory ALL by depleting Arg, achieving an overall response rate (ORR) of 44.4% and a median overall survival (OS) of 8.0 months [64]. However, clinical trials investigating ARG1-based Arg depletion in hematologic malignancies remain limited and warrant further exploration.

Additionally, inhibitors of oxidative phosphorylation such as IACS-010759 and quizartinib have been shown to synergistically disrupt glucose and glutamine (Gln) utilization in glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, impairing energy production and nucleotide biosynthesis to inhibit AML cell growth [65]. Amino acid metabolism inhibitors also demonstrate enhanced efficacy when combined with mTORC1 inhibitors, leading to significant suppression of tumor cell proliferation and survival [66]. Notably, the GLS1 inhibitor CB-839, when used in combination with the BCL-2 inhibitor ABT-199, induces a surge in reactive oxygen species (ROS), exerting potent cytotoxic effects against lymphoma cells [67]. These findings underscore the therapeutic value of combinatorial approaches involving amino acid metabolism regulation. In particular, the synergistic use of amino acid inhibitors with redox modulators or mTOR pathway inhibitors may provide multi-targeted blockade of tumor metabolism and growth.

Despite these promising results, systemic targeting of amino acid metabolism in hematologic malignancies poses challenges due to the widespread distribution of malignant and normal hematopoietic cells. For example, asparaginase (ASNase), which depletes asparagine, is clinically effective in treating ALL but is associated with adverse effects such as hypersensitivity reactions and pancreatitis [68, 69]. Similarly, although CB-839 combined with ABT-199 is effective in lymphoma, amino acid deprivation may impair energy metabolism in immune cells, resulting in immunosuppression [67].

Encouragingly, studies have shown that combining amino acid metabolism inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) can enhance antitumor immunity while mitigating off-target toxicity to normal cells [70]. Such combinatorial therapies hold great promise for the treatment of hematologic malignancies. Amino acid metabolism and drug resistance

Amino acid metabolism has been increasingly recognized as a contributing factor in the development of drug resistance in hematologic malignancies. Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) promotes therapeutic resistance in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) by stimulating the expression of several metabolic genes, including argininosuccinate synthetase 1 (ASS1) and the amino acid transporter SLC1A5. This leads to the remodeling of amino acid metabolism and activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway, thereby facilitating resistance to therapy [11].

Additionally, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells express high levels of asparagine synthetase, enabling ALL cells to evade the effects of asparaginase (ASNase), a key therapeutic agent. These findings indicate that metabolic adaptations involving amino acid pathways are intricately linked to resistance mechanisms in hematologic malignancies.

Targeting amino acid metabolism may help overcome drug resistance when used in combination with existing therapies. For instance, glutaminase (GLS) inhibitors have been shown to reverse resistance to ibrutinib in lymphoma cells [15]. Moreover, the GLS inhibitor telaglenastat, when used in combination with chemotherapeutic agents such as pomalidomide and dexamethasone, has demonstrated the potential to overcome drug resistance in multiple myeloma (MM) [73].

In summary, therapeutic strategies that target amino acid metabolism may offer a novel and effective approach to circumvent resistance in the treatment of hematologic malignancies.

Future perspectives: Spatial metabolomics in cancer research

Spatial metabolomics has emerged as a transformative tool in cancer research, offering insights into the metabolic heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Unlike traditional metabolomics, which provides bulk tissue data, spatial metabolomics enables visualization of metabolite distributions within tissues, revealing critical interactions between cancer cells, immune cells, and stromal components[74].

This technology holds great potential for elucidating immune evasion mechanisms and guiding the development of combination therapies that integrate metabolic modulation with immunotherapy. By identifying localized metabolic changes, spatial metabolomics helps us understand how tumors reshape their microenvironment to suppress immune responses, offering opportunities for more effective immunotherapies.

In the future, multi-omics integration, combining spatial metabolomics with transcriptomics and proteomics, will provide a comprehensive view of tumor biology. This approach can help identify predictive biomarkers, enhance personalized treatments, and improve drug resistance strategies.

Moreover, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning will further refine spatial metabolomics, enabling the analysis of complex datasets and enhancing the precision of tumor profiling and immune modulation. As technology evolves, spatial metabolomics will play a crucial role in precision oncology, guiding the development of next-generation therapeutic strategies.

Limitations

While the targeting of amino acid metabolism holds considerable promise in the treatment of hematologic malignancies, several important limitations in the current body of research must be acknowledged.

First, the majority of the studies discussed in this review are preclinical and have been conducted in cell and animal models. While these studies provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying amino acid metabolism in hematologic malignancies, they lack human trial data, which is essential for confirming the clinical relevance and effectiveness of these approaches. The lack of comprehensive clinical trial evidence limits the ability to translate these findings directly into therapeutic strategies for patients.

Second, the clinical effectiveness of amino acid-targeting agents, such as CB-839 and IDO inhibitors, remains inconsistent. Although preclinical studies have shown promising results, the clinical data is still lacking or inconclusive. Furthermore, while these agents can effectively target tumor cells, their use in normal cells can lead to immune system suppression, complicating their therapeutic application. This underscores the need for a more nuanced approach that balances tumor targeting with the preservation of immune system function in healthy tissues.

Additionally, the effects of key signaling pathways, such as mTOR and NF-κB, on amino acid metabolism show inconsistent results that are highly dependent on the specific context. These pathways may have different roles in various types of hematologic malignancies, and their influence can be altered by factors such as cancer type, stage of the disease, and experimental model used. The variability in results highlights the complexity of targeting these pathways and indicates that further investigation is needed to better understand their precise roles in tumor progression.

Moreover, different blood cancer types may show distinct responses to amino acid metabolism-targeting strategies. Hematologic malignancies are heterogeneous, and findings from one cancer type may not be directly applicable to others. The differences in response across models and malignancies underscore the need for tailored therapeutic strategies based on the specific cancer being treated.

In conclusion, while the potential of targeting amino acid metabolism is clear, clinical validation is required to confirm the findings of preclinical studies. The translation of these results into effective therapies will require further research, particularly in human clinical trials, to ensure that these strategies can be safely and effectively integrated into clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, amino acid metabolism plays a crucial role in the initiation, progression, and immune regulation of hematologic malignancies. Specific amino acids such as glutamine (Gln), arginine (Arg), and tryptophan (Trp) not only support tumor cell proliferation and survival but also modulate the function of immune cells, which may influence tumor progression. These findings highlight the significant association between metabolic reprogramming and therapeutic resistance, providing novel directions for future research.

Targeting amino acid metabolism—particularly in combination with other therapeutic approaches such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or mTOR pathway modulators—may hold promise for achieving multi-target synergistic interventions. However, it is essential to emphasize that these strategies require further validation in clinical settings. While preclinical studies have shown encouraging results, clinical translation remains an ongoing process and necessitates additional research to determine the effectiveness and safety of such interventions.
