The kernel of the Goldberg homomorphism is not finitely generated
Martin Scharlemann

Abstract
. Let M M be a closed surface not the sphere or projective plane. Goldberg [ 5 ] defined a natural homomorphism from the n n -stranded pure braid group of M M to the n-fold product of π 1 ​ ( M ) \pi_{1}(M) and showed that the kernel of the homomorphism is finitely normally generated. Here we show that the kernel is not finitely generated. The proof is an elementary application of covering space theory and the geometry of the euclidean or hyperbolic plane.



1.
Introduction

Let
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}\},n\geq 2
be a set of
n
points in a closed surface
M
that is not the sphere or projective plane. Let
F_{n}M
be the space of all embeddings of
X
in
M
, with
\{x^{0}:X\to M\}\in F_{n}M
the inclusion. Then
\pi_{1}(F_{n}M,x^{0})
is commonly called the pure braid group
P_{n}(M)
of
M
on
n
strands. Goldberg
[
5
]
defines a natural surjection

g_{*}:P_{n}(M)\to\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{1}(M,x_{i})

and shows that the kernel
K
of
g_{*}
is the normal closure of
P_{n}(D)
,
D\subset M
any disk containing
X
in its interior. (See also
[
1
, Theorem 1.7]
.) The map
g_{*}
can be thought of as induced by the inclusion
(F_{n}M,x^{0})\to(M^{n},x^{0})
.

Corollary 1.1

(Goldberg)
.

K
is finitely
n
ormally generated.

In contrast, we will here show

Theorem 1.2
.

K
is not finitely generated.

Proving that a group is not finitely generated can be difficult. See
[
3
]
for an example reminiscent of Theorem
1.2
, and
[
2
]
for an example of a sophisticated proof gone wrong. The proof of Theorem
1.2
given here is elementary, requiring only covering space theory and simple euclidean or hyperbolic geometry in the plane. Indeed, the proof can be viewed as a re-imagining of Goldberg’s 1973 proof in this journal, one in which it is the geometry rather than just the topology of the universal cover
\tilde{M}
of
M
that is invoked. In view of this connection, we adopt much of the notation of Goldberg’s proof, at least where it does not conflict with current sensibilities, and restrict the theorem, as Goldberg does, to closed surfaces. (Requiring only that
M
have infinite fundamental group appears to suffice.) But we also note that Theorem
1.2
follows immediately from the more sophisticated structure theorem for
K
derived in
[
6
, Proposition 2.4]
(see also
[
8
]
) or, as the referee points out, can be derived from the general theory of covering spaces and subspace arrangements.

2.
Winding number in
P_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

The pure 2-braid group
P_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of the plane is isomorphic to the integers: the isomorphism
\omega:P_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{Z}
assigns to a braid class the winding number of any braid in that braid class. When
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is endowed with either the euclidean or hyperbolic metric there is a useful geometric definition of the winding number. Let
b:I\to F_{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a pure 2-braid in either the euclidean or hyperbolic plane. For each
t\in I
consider the geodesic ray
r(t)
from
b(t)(x_{1})
to
b(t)(x_{2})
. In the Euclidean case,
r(t)
defines an angle
w(t)\in S^{1}
; in the hyperbolic case the ray
r(t)
determines a point
w(t)\in S^{1}
, viewed as the circle at infinity in the Poincaré disk model of the hyperbolic plane. Thus in either case the pure braid
b(t)
determines a map
w:I\to S^{1}
, with
w(0)=w(1)
. Since
w(0)=w(1)
,
w
determines a map
\overline{w}:S^{1}\to S^{1}
.

With this as background we have:

Definition 2.1
.

The winding number

\omega([b])\in\mathbb{Z}

is the degree of the map
\overline{w}
.

Using this definition the following Proposition is elementary.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
b:I\to F_{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a pure 2-braid and let
d:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
be either the euclidean or hyperbolic metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Suppose, for each
t\in I
and each
i=1,2
,
d(x_{i},b(t)(x_{i}))<d(x_{1},x_{2})/2
.

Then
\omega([b])=0
.

Proof.

Let
L
be the geodesic in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that orthogonally bisects the geodesic segment connecting the points
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. Then each point
\ell\in L
is equidistant from
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, and
d(\ell,x_{i})\geq d(x_{1},x_{2})/2
for
i=1,2
. So the hypothesis implies that, for all
t
, the two points
b(t)(x_{1})
and
b(t)(x_{2})
lie on opposite sides of
L
. See Figure
1
. In particular, the geodesic ray from
b(t)(x_{1})
to
b(t)(x_{2})
never achieves the angle (when
d
is euclidean) or the limit point at
\infty
in
S^{1}
(when
d
is hyperbolic) of any infinite ray in the line
L
. That is, the corresponding map
\overline{w}:S^{1}\to S^{1}
is not surjective, so
\overline{w}
has trivial degree, completing the proof.

3.
The breadth of braids and braid classes

For
M
a closed surface not the sphere or projective plane, fix a constant curvature metric on
M
, euclidean if
\chi(M)=0
, hyperbolic if
\chi(M)<0
. Lift that metric to the universal cover
\tilde{M}
of
M
, giving
\tilde{M}
the structure of either the euclidean or hyperbolic plane. Denote by
d:\tilde{M}\times\tilde{M}\to[0,\infty)
this standard metric. With no loss, assume the points
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}\}
lie in the interior of an evenly covered closed disk
D\subset M
of radius
\rho>0
. For each
x_{i}\neq x_{j}\in X
there is a natural isomorphism
\pi_{1}(M,x_{i})\cong\pi_{1}(M,x_{j})
obtained by conjugating a loop at
x_{i}
by a path in
D
between
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
. When the choice of base point
x_{i}\in D
is unimportant we will use the generic notation
\pi_{1}(M,x_{\bullet})
.

For
p:\tilde{M}\to M
the covering projection fix a component
\tilde{D}
of
p^{-1}(D)
and, for
1\leq i\leq n
let
\tilde{x}_{i}
denote the lift of
x_{i}
that lies in
\tilde{D}
and let
\tilde{X}=\{\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2},...,\tilde{x}_{n}\}
. For each
\mbox{$\alpha$}\in\pi_{1}(M,x_{\bullet})
let
\tilde{D}(\mbox{$\alpha$})
denote the image of
\tilde{D}
under the covering translation of
\tilde{M}
determined by
\alpha
. Similarly, let
\tilde{x}_{i}(\mbox{$\alpha$})
denote the lift of
x_{i}
that lies in
\tilde{D}(\mbox{$\alpha$})
. In particular, for
e\in\pi_{1}(M,x_{\bullet})
the identity,
\tilde{D}=\tilde{D}(e)
and
\tilde{x}_{i}(e)=\tilde{x}_{i},1\leq i\leq n
.

A
pure braid of
n
strands
in
M
is a map
b:I\to F_{n}M
so that
b(0)=b(1)=x^{0}
. Denote by
[b]
the class of
b
in
\pi_{1}(F_{n}M,x^{0})
.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
the path
b(t)(x_{i}),t\in I
is a loop in
M
based at
x_{i}
. Denote by
\tilde{b}_{i}:I\to\tilde{M}
the path that is the lift of the loop
b(t)(x_{i})
for which
\tilde{b}_{i}(0)\in\tilde{D}
. Then
\tilde{b}_{i}(1)
lies in a lift
\tilde{D}(\mbox{$\alpha$}_{i})
of
D
in
\tilde{M}
, for some
\mbox{$\alpha$}_{i}\in\pi_{1}(M,x_{i})
; Goldberg defines

g_{*}([b])=(\mbox{$\alpha$}_{1},...,\mbox{$\alpha$}_{n})\in\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{1}(M,x_{i}).

In particular, if the braid class
[b]\in K=ker(g_{*})
then each
\mbox{$\alpha$}_{i}=e
so each path
\tilde{b}_{i}
is a loop in
\tilde{M}
. That is,
\tilde{b}=(\tilde{b}_{1},...,\tilde{b}_{n})\in F_{n}\tilde{M}
is itself a braid on the points
\tilde{X}\subset\tilde{D}\subset\tilde{M}
. By the covering homotopy property, the class
[\tilde{b}]\in P_{n}(\tilde{M})=\pi_{1}(F_{n}\tilde{M},\tilde{x}^{0})
is well defined. (Here
\tilde{x}^{0}
denotes the inclusion of
\tilde{X}
into
\tilde{D}\subset\tilde{M}
.)

Definition 3.1
.

For any braid
b
such that
[b]\in K\subset P_{n}(M)
let
\tilde{b}:I\to F_{n}\tilde{M}
be the braid in
\tilde{M}
just defined. Then the
breadth

\nu(b)\in[0,\infty)
of
b
is given by

\nu(b)=\max_{i\in\{1,...,n\}}\max_{t\in I}d(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{b}_{i}(t))

Here are two obvious properties:

Lemma 3.2
.

For any braid
b
such that
[b]\in K\subset P_{n}(M)
, if
\nu(b)=0
then
b
is the constant braid in
M
.

Proof.

If
\nu(b)=0
then for each
i
and every
t\in I
,
\tilde{b}(t)(\tilde{x}_{i})=\tilde{x}_{i}
. This implies that for every
t
,
\tilde{b}(t)
is just the inclusion, so
\tilde{b}
is the constant braid in
\tilde{M}
. Then its projection
b
is the constant braid in
M
, as claimed.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
b
and
b^{\prime}
are two braids so that
[b],[b^{\prime}]\in K
and
b\cdot b^{\prime}
is their braid product. Then

\nu(b\cdot b^{\prime})=\max\{\nu(b),\nu(b^{\prime})\}

Proof.

Let
\tilde{b}
and
\tilde{b}^{\prime}
be the lifts of
b
and
b^{\prime}
respectively. Then the braid product
\tilde{b}\cdot\tilde{b}^{\prime}
is the lift of
b\cdot b^{\prime}
. Moreover for each
i
, the function
d(\tilde{x}_{i},(\tilde{b}\cdot\tilde{b}^{\prime})(t)(\tilde{x}_{i}))
takes on the union of the values taken by
d(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{b}(t)(\tilde{x}_{i}))
and
d(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{b}^{\prime}(t)(\tilde{x}_{i}))
. So the maximum over all values is the same, as claimed.
∎

Similarly

Definition 3.4
.

For any braid class
[b]\in K\subset P_{n}(M)
define the
breadth

\nu([b])\in[0,\infty)
by

\nu([b])=\inf_{b\in[b]}\nu(b).

Again this leads to two obvious lemmas:

Lemma 3.5
.

For any braid class
[b]\in K\subset P_{n}(M)
, if
\nu([b])=0
then
[b]
is the identity in
P_{n}(M)
.

Proof.

Let
\mbox{$\epsilon$}>0
be so small that an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
X\subset M
(that is, a collection of disjoint disks centered on the
x_{i}
, each of radius
\epsilon
) deformation retracts to
X
. Then a lift
\tilde{\eta}
of this neighborhood to
\tilde{D}\subset\tilde{M}
consists of a disjoint collection of
\epsilon
-disks centered on the
\tilde{x}_{i}
. If
\nu([b])=0
then there must be a braid
b\in[b]
such that
\nu(b)<\mbox{$\epsilon$}
. That is, for each
i\in\{1,...,n\}
and
t\in I
,
d(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{b}_{i}(t))<\mbox{$\epsilon$}
. Then the deformation retraction of
\tilde{\eta}
to
\tilde{X}
homotopes
\tilde{b}
to the constant braid in
\tilde{M}
and its projection homotopes
b
to the constant braid in
M
, as claimed.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose
b
and
b^{\prime}
are two braids so that
[b],[b^{\prime}]\in K
. Then

\nu([b\cdot b^{\prime}])\leq\max\{\nu([b]),\nu([b^{\prime}])\}

Proof.

For any
\mbox{$\epsilon$}>0
there are, by definition, representative braids
b\in[b]
and
b^{\prime}\in[b^{\prime}]
so that
\nu(b)<\nu([b]))+\mbox{$\epsilon$}
and
\nu(b^{\prime})<\nu([b^{\prime}]))+\mbox{$\epsilon$}
. Then by Lemma
3.3
,

\nu([b\cdot b^{\prime}])\leq\nu(b\cdot b^{\prime})=\max\{\nu(b),\nu(b^{\prime})\}\leq\max\{\nu([b]),\nu([b^{\prime}])\}+\epsilon

Since this is true for all
\mbox{$\epsilon$}>0
we conclude
\nu([b\cdot b^{\prime}])\leq\max\{\nu([b]),\nu([b^{\prime}])\}
, as required.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose the kernel
K
is finitely generated. Then there is a
C>0
so that for any
[b]\in K
,

\nu([b])<C
.

Proof.

Suppose
[b_{1}],...,[b_{m}]
are a finite set of generators for
K
. Then any braid class
[b]\in K
can be written as the braid product of copies of the
[b_{j}],1\leq j\leq m
. It follows then from
3.6
that
\nu([b])\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\nu([b_{j}])
, as claimed.
∎
