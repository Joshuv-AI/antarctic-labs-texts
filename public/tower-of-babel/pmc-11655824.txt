A temporal-spectral graph convolutional neural network model for EEG emotion recognition within and across subjects
Authors: Rui Li, Xuanwen Yang, Jun Lou, Junsong Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEEG-based emotion recognition uses high-level information from neural activities to predict emotional responses in subjects. However, this information is sparsely distributed in frequency, time, and spatial domains and varied across subjects. To address these challenges in emotion recognition, we propose a novel neural network model named Temporal-Spectral Graph Convolutional Network (TSGCN). To capture high-level information distributed in time, spatial, and frequency domains, TSGCN considers both neural oscillation changes in different time windows and topological structures between different brain regions. Specifically, a Minimum Category Confusion (MCC) loss is used in TSGCN to reduce the inconsistencies between subjective ratings and predefined labels. In addition, to improve the generalization of TSGCN on cross-subject variation, we propose Deep and Shallow feature Dynamic Adversarial Learning (DSDAL) to calculate the distance between the source domain and the target domain. Extensive experiments were conducted on public datasets to demonstrate that TSGCN outperforms state-of-the-art methods in EEG-based emotion recognition. Ablation studies show that the mixed neural networks and our proposed methods in TSGCN significantly contribute to its high performance and robustness. Detailed investigations further provide the effectiveness of TSGCN in addressing the challenges in emotion recognition.Keywords: EEG signal, Affective computing, Graph neural network, Cross-subjects

Introduction

Affective computing and emotion recognition

Affective computing is an interdisciplinary research area involving computer science, psychology, and neuroscience [1], whose ultimate goal is to build computers with human-like emotions that can recognize, interpret, and simulate those emotions. Affective computing is applied widely in various fields, such as health monitoring [2], online social interaction [3], and brain-computer interaction [4]. One of the most important research areas in affective computing is emotion recognition, which is fundamental for computers to recognize human emotions. Emotion recognition leverages computers to build emotional systems and detect human emotional states.

The first step in building an emotional system is to define the emotion category. Psychologists have built two models (discrete emotion and dimensional emotion) to describe the emotion. The discrete emotion model consists of fixed emotional words, such as those in Plutchik’s wheel of emotion (anger, fear, sadness, disgust, surprise, expectation, trust, and joy) [5]. The dimensional emotion model is a continuous multidimensional representation of emotions, such as valence, arousal, and dominance [6]. Valence indicates the degree of pleasure of emotion, arousal indicates the intensity of emotion, and dominance indicates the autonomy of emotion. These two models, which define emotion from different aspects, are applied pervasively to distinguish different kinds of emotions in psychology and neuroscience.

To detect human emotional states, various data, including non-physiological and physiological signals, are recorded. Non-physiological signals, which mainly arise from human behavior, such as facial expression, human gestures, texts, and voice, are feasible to collect but easily influenced by variation in illumination, target partial occlusion, and deformation. Besides, humans may intentionally hide their emotions, sometimes making non-physiological signals less conceivable [7, 8]. At this point, the method of emotion recognition based on physiological signals demonstrates more significant potential because internal neural changes cannot be intentionally hidden or controlled [9]. Physiological signals, such as the Electroencephalogram (EEG), Magnetoencephalogram, functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy, and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), detect neural activity from the human brain without invasive surgery. Data recorded from these techniques reflect the brain activity of emotional recognition and genuinely capture the inner response to emotional stimuli. Among these techniques, EEG is widely applied in emotion detection due to its advantages of high temporal resolution, relatively low price, and portability [10].

However, there are several challenges to building a robust emotion recognition system based on EEG signals. One challenge is extracting high-level feature patterns, which could accurately reflect different emotions from EEG signals. Scalp EEG signals are often subject to a low signal-to-noise ratio and artifacts, like ocular artifacts, muscle movements, head movements, etc. [11]. Besides, according to the neural mechanism of emotion cognition, high-level features related to emotion are distributed sparsely in three domains: space, time, and frequency. In the spatial domain, multiple channels across the brain scalp record neural activity from brain regions processing emotion [12]. In the time domain, cognitive processing of emotional stimuli occurs in different time windows [13]. In the frequency domain, emotional responses are achieved by communicating between brain regions through neural oscillation in specific frequency bands [14]. Thus, distinct patterns of neural activity in the three domains are essential to forming robust high-level features for emotion recognition.

Another challenge in emotion recognition is the difference between emotional responses across subjects. Variations toward emotional stimuli across subjects are caused mainly by subjective ratings and mismatched domains. Subjective rating, which indicates a person’s cognitive reappraisal of emotional stimulus [15], is related to personal cultural background, experience, and traits [16]. Consider a movie scene in which one audience moves while leaving another unaffected. The differences in subjective ratings lead to high individual variability in emotion evaluation, which causes inconsistencies between predefined labels and emotions elicited by individuals. A mismatched domain means the feature distributions of emotional data across subjects are not identical [17, 18]. Most previous studies focused on emotion recognition, where training and testing data came from the same domain. Under this assumption, the feature distribution of training data is identical to that of testing data. The learning model from the source domain data is used directly to predict the label of the target domain data [19]. However, in real situations, the feature distribution of data across different subjects violates this assumption, which leads to a decreased generalization for the learning model when it learns features from one subject to predict the labels of another. Thus, to solve the challenge of cross-subject emotion recognition, both subjective ratings and mismatched domains must be considered.

EEG-based emotion recognition

In recent years, computational algorithms for emotion recognition have extended from traditional machine learning to deep learning to extract robust features from EEG signals. Compared with models in traditional machine learning, such as Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbor, etc., deep neural networks in deep learning automatically extract representative features from original EEG signals without much expert knowledge [20]. Thus, more and more researchers have adopted deep learning for emotion recognition. Several deep learning models have been developed to extract robust features from EEG signals from spatial, time, and frequency domains.

Previous studies mainly focused on extracting features in the spatial or time domain of EEG signals. Researchers have developed various Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to extract temporal information from EEG signals. For example, to recognize emotion, Alhagry et al. used LSTM to extract temporal features from EEG signals [21]. Ma et al. proposed a residual Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model that shares time weights among multiple modalities for emotion recognition [22]. Xiao et al. integrated the temporal attention mechanism into a bi-directional LSTM to explore temporal dependencies of the four-dimensional EEG signal representations, which can extract the temporal dependencies and explore the importance of different temporal slices [23]. The commonly used method to extract spatial information is to build a feature map from EEG signals and use Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to extract high-level representation from the feature map. Wen et al. used the Pearson Correlation Coefficients to reconstruct multi-channel EEG signals, which were then used as CNN input to obtain spatial information for emotion recognition [24]. Li et al. used Diffusion Entropy (DE) features and electrode positions to generate 2-D maps [14]. Siddharth et al. used Power Spectral Density (PSD) in three frequency bands to compose an RGB heat-map image [25]. Then, they used CNNs to capture spatial information from these feature maps to recognize a subject’s emotions. Moon et al. proposed a CNN model combined with brain connectivity features to explain the synchronous activation of emotional responses between different brain regions [26]. However, features in a single domain cannot fully represent emotional responses in EEG signals.

The mentioned methods overlook the complementary information of spatiotemporal connections in the brain, which, to some extent, limits the classification capability of the model. Therefore, some research has begun to combine multiple feature extraction methods to achieve enhanced results. Li et al. extracted temporal, spatial, and connectivity features from EEG signals for emotion recognition [27]. Yang et al. proposed a Parallel Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (PCRNN) that combines CNNs with LSTM [28]. In PCRNN, sequential EEG signals are converted into a sequence of 2D-like frames, and a CNNs module is used to mine the inter-channel correlation between adjacent channels. Then, the LSTM module is used to mine the temporal information from EEG signals. Zhang et al. proposed a Spatial–Temporal Recurrent Neural Network, which contains a multi-directional RNN and a bidirectional RNN [29]. The multi-directional RNN captures remote spatial information by traversing the spatial region of each time slice in different directions. The bidirectional RNN learns discriminative features that describe the time dependence of human emotional responses. Although these studies utilized CNNs to learn spatial information from adjacent channels, extracting information between remote channels isn't easy. In other words, CNNs are unsuitable for extracting sparsely distributed information in the spatial domain.

Graph Neural Network (GNN), built under graph theory to capture relations between adjacent and remote neighbors, solves the problem well. In GNN, weights of the gradual connections between channels are set according to prior knowledge from brain anatomical or functional networks. The graph representation method has recently been applied in many fields [30, 31]. Song et al. proposed a Dynamic Graph Convolutional Neural Network (DGCNN) model to recognize emotion [32]. In DGCNN, a learnable adjacency matrix, which learns the connectivity between each vertex node of the graph, is used to learn the internal relationship between EEG channels. Pan et al. proposed a Spatio-Temporal Self-Constructing Graph Neural Network (ST-SCGNN) for cross-subject emotion recognition and consciousness detection [33]. ST-SCGNN combined the connection pattern features and activation pattern features, enhancing cross-subject emotion recognition effectiveness in a complementary manner. Zhong et al. developed a Regularized Graph Neural Network to capture local and global relationships between EEG channels [34]. In addition, Song and his colleagues proposed a Variational Instance Adaptive Graph approach to reduce both individual differences and dynamic uncertainty relationships between different EEG channels [35]. Despite the powerful performance of GNN in extracting sparsely distributed information from the EEG spatial domain, it is difficult for GNN to extract contextual information in the time domain. Thus, it is still challenging to integrate multi-dimensional EEG features more effectively and capture sparsely distributed information.

Learning with noisy labels

Subjective rating causes inconsistency between predefined labels and actual emotional responses, which can be regarded as noisy labels in a predefined label set. To solve the learning problem caused by noisy labels, previous studies commonly developed new approaches based on three key ingredients: data, objective function, and optimization strategy [36].

Data For data, the key is to discover a potential noise transfer matrix, which is essential for analyzing patterns from true labels to noisy labels. The noise transfer matrix specifies the transition probability from each true label to each noisy label for the objective function. In deep learning, the most straightforward learning with noisy labels is to add an adaptation layer to estimate the probability transition matrix or learn through noise transfer moments [37]. That is, the training of noisy labels is approximately equal to clean labels through loss correction. Patrini et al. used a noise transfer matrix to correct the loss function in forward and backward propagation for noisy labels [38].

Objective function For objective function, the key is to design a robust and noise-resistant loss function. For example, Azadi et al. proposed an auxiliary regularization to select reliable labels from noisy labels by exploiting the mutual context information in training data [39]. Berthelot and his colleagues introduced an explicit regularization, Minimum Entropy Regularization, in the MixMatch model for semi-supervised learning [40]. Zhang et al. proposed a generalized cross-entropy loss function that considered both Categorical Cross Entropy (CCE) loss and Mean Absolute Error loss [41].

Optimization strategy For optimization strategy, the key is to dynamically optimize the exploring progress, such as MentorNet proposed by Jiang et al. and co-teaching proposed by Han et al. [42, 43]. In Jiang’s work, the MentorNet is proposed to dynamically learn a data-driven sample weight scheme that makes StudentNet focus on correct labels. In Han’s work, co-teaching is two deep neural networks that simultaneously select data with possibly clean labels and communicate with each other to update each network dynamically.

Although these studies have proposed new methods to address the challenge of learning with noisy labels, they did not focus on the subjective and objective ratings in affective computing. How to address the consistency between subjective rating and predefined labels in emotion recognition is still a challenge.

Transfer learning on emotion recognition

Transfer learning mainly aims to transfer knowledge between training and test data while relaxing the assumption that these two data are independent and identically distributed. Transfer learning uses knowledge in the source domain to help learn in the target domain; thus, transfer learning is very suitable for dealing with mismatched domains across subjects in Affective computing. Quan et al. proposed a domain transfer learning model incorporating substructure selection, multi-source domain selection, and subdomain adaptation from various sources for emotion classification [44].

There are two main kinds of transfer learning methods. The first kind is based on statistical moment matching methods, including Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and CORrelation ALignment [45–47]. Li et al. used a Deep Adaptation Network to improve the accuracy of cross-subject emotion recognition based on EEG signals [48]. Zhang et al. used MMD to measure the similarity between individuals [49]. Then, they extracted pre-existing EEG data of similar individuals and used the TrAdaBoost algorithm to build a user-dependent model for the target individual.

The second kind is based on the adversarial loss method. Borrowing the idea from Generative Adversarial Network, this method, Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN), uses a discriminator to distinguish whether extracted features are from the source domain or target domain [50]. Inspired by the lateralization of brain neural activity during emotional cognition, Li et al. proposed a Bi-hemispheres Domain Adversarial Neural Network model (BiDANN), which combines asymmetry between left and right hemispheres with the DANN confrontation mechanism to recognize emotional responses from different subjects [51].

Considering these challenges in emotion recognition, we propose a novel neural network: Temporal-Spectral Graph Convolutional Neural Network (TSGCN). TSGCN not only extracts robust feature patterns from EEG signals in multiple domains but also minimizes the differences in emotional responses across subjects.

First, to extract robust feature patterns from EEG signals, TSGCN uses a Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory network (BiLSTM) to extract temporal-spectral features from the time–frequency domains. Then, TSGCN regards discrete EEG channels across the brain scalp as nodes in the graph and uses a Dynamic Graph Convolutional Neural Network (DGCNN) to capture topological relationships between EEG channels in the spatial domain. Specifically, to fuse multi-dimensional features in time-spatial-frequency domains and form a high-level representation of emotional response, TSGCN retains temporal information while passing the features from BiLSTM to DGCNN.

Second, to minimize the individual difference in response to emotional stimuli, the subjective rating problem is formalized as noisy tags, which are fuzzy classes with emotional confusion. Then, to reduce the influence of noisy tags on prediction results, a Minimum Category Confusion (MCC) loss function is added in TSGCN. In addition, to solve the problem of mismatched domains across subjects and further improve the generalization of TSGCN, we propose a Deep and Shallow feature Dynamic Adversarial Learning (DSDAL) that aligns shallow time–frequency features with deep graph node features in TSGCN. DSDAL uses dynamic factors to evaluate the importance of both shallow and deep features. Then, it aligns the feature distribution of different individuals from the shallow to the deep neural network to improve the accuracy of cross-subject classification.

Proposed method

We propose a TSGCN model to deal with the problem of cross-subject emotion recognition. Our methodology contains three parts: First, we introduce the framework of TSGCN for emotion recognition. Second, we describe a minimum category confusion loss function, which is used to reduce the influence of subject rating. Third, we introduce details of the deep and shallow feature dynamic adversarial learning for cross-subject problems and the training process of TSGCN.

Temporal-spectral graph convolution network

TSGCN aims to extract high-level emotional representation from time, frequency, and spatial domains of EEG signals. The framework of TSGCN used for emotion recognition is shown in Fig. 1.

The Framework of TSGCN. The main frame part of TSGCN consists of two modules, the BiLSTM module, and the multi-graph dynamic graph convolution module, which are indicated by gray dash lines. DSDAL represents the module of Deep and Shallow feature Dynamic Adversarial Learning. The inputs of TSGCN are DE features in frequency bands. DE features are sent to two BiLSTM neural networks to extract information in time and frequency domains, respectively. Then, time–frequency features from BiLSTM are fused and sent to a multi-graph dynamic graph convolution neural network, which is constituted by three parallel dynamic graph convolution neural networks using different connectivity matrices. There is a fully connected layer behind the multi-graph dynamic graph convolution, and the predicted labels are derived using softmax. The prediction loss function and the MCC loss function are derived from estimating the difference between the predicted labels and the true labels. The loss function of DSDAL is derived from both the loss of the shallow discriminator and the loss of the deep discriminator. GRL (Gradient Reversal Layer) helps with domain adaption by reversing the gradient. G_N processes node features in the graph, and G_TF processes time–frequency features

Experiments and results

We conducted experiments with two datasets: SJTU Emotion EEG Dataset (SEED) and DEAP [55, 56].

Datasets

The SEED dataset contains emotional EEG data recorded from fifteen Chinese subjects (eight females and seven males) while watching movie clips. The EEG signals were recorded from 62 channels with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, and then the EEG signals were downsampled to 200 Hz and band-pass filtered to 0–75 Hz. In the SEED dataset, movie clips elicited three kinds of emotion: positive, negative, and neutral. An experiment consisted of three sets of sessions, with each session containing fifteen trials. Each trial had a 5-s hint before the movie clip was presented. Then, participants were required to watch a 4-min long movie clip. After watching the movie clip, each participant participated in a 45-s self-assessment. Between two trials, there was a 15-s resting period.

The DEAP dataset contains EEG data from 32 subjects (sixteen females and sixteen males) while watching music videos. The EEG signals were recorded from 32 channels with a sampling rate of 512 Hz. In addition, eight peripheral signals were recorded. With DEAP, an experiment consisted of forty trials, with a short break beginning with the twentieth trial. In each trial, there was a 3-s presentation of a fixation cross for baseline recording. Then, participants watched a 1-min display of a pre-selected music video, followed by a nine-point self-assessment of the video regarding arousal, valence, and dominance. In the preprocessing step, EEG signals were down-sampled to 128 Hz. Line noise, blinks, and other artifacts were removed.

We evaluated the performance of our TSGCN model based on these two datasets. The TSGCN model was used to predict three categories of emotion labels (positive, negative, and neutral) for the SEED dataset. According to Yin’s study, the TSGCN model was used to predict two categories of emotions on the arousal and valence dimensions (high valence vs. low valence, high arousal vs. low arousal) for the DEAP dataset [57].

Preprocessing and model implementation

SEED dataset For the preprocessed EEG signals in the SEED dataset, we used the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to filter the continuous EEG signals into the following five frequency bands: Delta [1-3 Hz], Theta [3-8 Hz], Alpha [8-13 Hz], Beta [13-30 Hz], and Gamma [30-50 Hz]. We divided the continuous EEG signals into 4-s non-overlapping epochs. Finally, for each sample, we computed DE features for every second in each epoch from each EEG channel in the five frequency bands.

DEAP dataset For the DEAP dataset, we used the FFT method to filter the EEG data into the following four frequency bands: Theta [4-8 Hz], Alpha [8-13 Hz], Beta [13-30 Hz], and Gamma [30-45 Hz]. We segmented the filtered data into 60-s trials and removed a 3-s pre-trial baseline. Finally, we used a 1-s non-overlapping Hamming window to extract DE features from each EEG channel in the four frequency bands. Then, we combined three seconds of DE features into one sample.

From the data preprocessing, we obtained a total number of 842 samples for each subject in the SEED dataset and a total number of 800 samples for each subject in the DEAP dataset. To train our TSGCN model, we used gradient descent and Adam to optimize model parameters. The hyperparameters of TSGCN were selected as follows: the dropout rate of the fully connected layer was 0.5, the learning rate, η, was set at 1e-4, and the batch size was set to 64. The number of layers for both GCN and LSTM is 2, and they are used to extract time and frequency domain information. d, d', and d'' denote 4, 256, and 128, respectively. Other hyperparameters were adjusted according to the experiment.

To validate the performance of our TSGCN model, we used the following as benchmarks for comparison on the SEED dataset: Support Vector Machine (SVM) [58], Deep Belief Network (DBN) [59], Dynamical Graph Convolutional Neural Network (DGCNN) [32], Bi-hemisphere Domain Adversarial Neural Network (BiDANN) [51], and Regularized Graph Neural Network (RGNN) [34]. Additionally, as benchmarks for comparison on the DEAP dataset, we used the following: SVM, DBN, BiDANN, DGCNN, and Attention-based LSTM with Domain Discriminator (ATDD-LSTM) [60].

Validation of subject-dependent classification on SEED and DEAP datasets.

In the subject-dependent classification experiments for the SEED dataset, we evaluated our TSGCN model according to the previous experimental settings [34, 51]. As seen in Table 5, -DGL_TF performs better than -DGL_N because the shallow features can be adjusted while the deep features of the graph network are aligned. In addition, compared with other methods, DSDAL significantly improves the accuracy of subject-independent classification, which proves that aligning the features of both deep and shallow layers improves the accuracy of cross-subject emotion recognition. In DSDAL, the shallow features are first aligned; then, the deep features are adjusted and aligned. By applying this strategy, the influence of shallow feature distribution on deep feature distribution is reduced.

Visualization of channel relations

We visualized weights of the learnable connectivity matrix to study the topological relationship between channels across the brain scalp. The visualization is as follows: we first normalized the learnable connectivity matrix, Aall, and plotted it as a heatmap. As shown in Fig. 5a, increased connection weight is indicated by a darker color in the matrix. We found that, although there are obvious connections between local adjacent channels, there is no obvious connection between channels that are far apart. In addition, connections between different brain regions were visualized according to the connectivity matrix. The results are shown in Fig. 5b. We found tight connections between local channels in different brain regions. In contrast, there are relatively sparse connections between different brain regions, which is in line with the small-world characteristics of the brain network [61]. Among these brain regions, the connectivity between parietal and occipital regions is relatively tight, whereas there are relatively sparse long-range connections between prefrontal-central and central-parietal regions, indicating the prefrontal regions are involved in the formation of high-level emotional reactions [62].

Influence of subjective rating

We performed additional analysis on our experiments to further investigate the relationship between subjective ratings and emotion recognition accuracy. MCC loss is designed to capture the discrepancy between a subjective rating and predefined labels. We performed a correlation analysis between MCC loss and recognition accuracy on the SEED and DEAP datasets. As shown in Fig. 6, there is a significant correlation between MCC loss and recognition accuracy in both datasets (r = 0.63, p < 0.01 for the SEED dataset, r = 0.414, p < 0.01 for the Valence label of the DEAP dataset, r = 0.432, p < 0.01 for the Arousal label of the DEAP dataset). When MCC loss increases, recognition accuracy decreases. These results verify that subjective ratings play an essential role in emotion recognition. Inconsistencies between subjective ratings and predefined labels can impact classification accuracy.

Relationship between subjective ratings and emotion recognition accuracy. (a) The accuracy is negatively correlated with MCC loss on the SEED dataset. The classification accuracy increases, and the MCC loss decreases after implementing the MCC loss function. (b) The accuracy is negatively correlated with MCC loss on Arousal labels of the DEAP dataset. The classification accuracy on Arousal labels increases, and the MCC loss decreases after implementing the MCC loss function. (c) The accuracy is negatively correlated with MCC loss on Valence labels of the DEAP dataset. To verify the effectiveness of the proposed MCC in eliminating the influence of subjective ratings, we statistically compared recognition accuracy and MCC loss before and after using the MCC loss function. A T-test was carried out for statistical analysis. As shown in Fig. 6, after implementing the MCC loss function in our model (p-value for SEED and DEAP datasets are shown in Tables 7 and 8, respectively), accuracy significantly increases, and MCC loss significantly decreases. Thus, our MCC loss function effectively reduces the influence caused by inconsistencies of subjective ratings.

Effectiveness of DSDAL algorithm

To further verify that DSDAL reduces the influences of mismatched domains across subjects and improves recognition performance, we visualized feature distribution with four subjects in the SEED dataset using the t-SNE method. Figure 7 shows the visualization results of discriminative features the Temporal-Spectral Graph Convolution Network learned on the subject-independent classification of the SEED dataset using DGL_TF, DGL_N, and DSDAL. After training with the three methods, the three emotion labels are well distinguished, and positive emotion labels are identified more easily than neutral and negative emotion labels. Conclusion

We proposed a novel TSGCN model to deal with the challenges in EEG emotion recognition. Extensive experiments were conducted on the SEED and DEAP datasets to validate the performance of our method. Results demonstrate that, on these datasets, our proposed TSGCN method achieves better performance than the state-of-the-art methods. Detailed analysis of each module provides insights into the superiority of our proposed method. The better performance of TSGCN is attributed mainly to the fact that TSGCN not only uses discrete information from time, spatial, and frequency domains to improve recognition performance but also minimizes the effect of noisy labels caused by subjective ratings. We also proposed the Deep and Shallow feature Dynamic Adversarial Learning and integrated it with TSGCN to deal with the challenge of cross-subject emotion recognition. Experimental results confirm that TSGCN with DSDAL performs better than the original TSGCN in terms of subject-independent classification.
