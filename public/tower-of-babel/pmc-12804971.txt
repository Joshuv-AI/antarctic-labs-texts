Penetration explosion damage and discharge of high arch dam with high reservoir water level
Authors: Liang Wu, Anqi Wu, Chuang Yu, Rui Huang, Junru Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates penetration explosion damage and subsequent discharge flow in high-arch dams under high reservoir water levels. Indoor model tests were conducted to analyze the damage process at vulnerable dam sections. A three-dimensional peridynamics model was developed to simulate the dynamic damage evolution in concrete structures under penetration explosion. The results demonstrate that peridynamics effectively captures both damage evolution and structural failure processes in rock-like media under explosive loading. Furthermore, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were employed to analyze discharge flow patterns through the damaged sections, elucidating scouring mechanisms on the dam foundation and body. The study reveals rapid flow field changes in the reservoir and quantifies the impact of water discharge following penetration explosion damage.

Introduction

In light of the current volatile global environment, several explosive attacks targeting dams and their associated power generation facilities have already occurred. Once a high dam structure is struck, it can lead to structural failure of the project, and the large-capacity, high-head reservoir water inevitably poses a threat to the safety of downstream areas. With the rapid advancement of precision guidance and explosive charge technologies, particularly the significant improvement in the destructive power and accuracy of guided bombs, along with the widespread production of aerial drones and the development of underwater remote-controlled devices, the probability of explosive attacks has greatly increased. Additionally, given the distribution characteristics of cascade reservoir systems in river basins, their blast resistance safety issues are more pronounced compared to single reservoirs. The release of reservoir water following the breach of an upstream dam can cause substantial impact on downstream hydropower stations, and if a chain reaction occurs, the consequences would be even more severe1. Therefore, important water-related structures such as long-distance water conveyance projects and high dam reservoirs are facing increasingly severe attack environments. Research on blast resistance, explosion damage, and discharge flow issues for high-arch dams under high reservoir water levels is an urgent requirement and challenge at present, with significant safety implications2,3.

The arch structure of a concrete arch dam enables the transfer of immense water pressure to the rock masses on both banks, allowing the dam body to be designed relatively thin, often only half the volume of a gravity dam. It is a safe and economical dam type and is currently the preferred choice for high dam design. Significant progress has been made in research on the damage mechanisms and blast resistance of high arch dams. Zhang Sherong et al.4,5 employed a fully coupled underwater explosion computational model for arch dams to analyze the damage range and failure mechanisms of concrete arch dam structures under explosive impact loads. They also discussed the influence of explosive charge quantity and source distance on the damage patterns of arch dam structures. The results indicate that the arch shape of the dam provides high overall structural resistance to impacts. However, the vulnerable areas of the arch dam to impacts are located in the middle and upper sections of the dam body. Therefore, small-scale explosions can only cause localized damage and cracking to the arch dam without compromising the safety of the overall dam structure. Zhang Qiling et al.6,7 analyzed the damage and failure patterns of keyways between dam sections under explosive loading and discussed the damage characteristics of the middle section of a concrete arch dam subjected to underwater explosive loading in front of the dam. The results showed that the degree of dynamic response of the dam section is proportional to the distance from the explosion source. The dynamic response is transmitted to adjacent dam sections through the keyways between the dams, thereby influencing the spatial distribution characteristics of the damage. Xue Xinhua et al.8,9 investigated the dynamic response characteristics of a roller-compacted concrete arch dam under impact loading. They found that stress concentration occurs at the dam abutments when the middle section of the dam is subjected to impact loading, and the peak dynamic displacement of the dam body in the streamwise direction is the largest. However, due to the strong blast resistance of the overall arch dam structure, small-scale explosions only cause damage to the dam body near the explosion source, leaving the overall safety of the arch dam unaffected. Moradloo et al.10 proposed a method for determining the time history of underwater explosive loads acting on the body of a concrete arch dam. Jin Liang et al.11 studied the effects of underwater explosive loading on the powerhouse located at the dam toe and the flood discharge outlets. They established a fully coupled model of the reservoir water-arch dam-powerhouse-flood discharge tunnel-dam foundation and discussed the damage characteristics and failure mechanisms of the powerhouse at the dam toe and the overall arch dam structure. They concluded that the flood discharge tunnels and intake openings in the dam structure weaken the local strength and arch effect of the dam body. Pan et al.12–14 used numerical methods to study the influence of contraction joints on the nonlinear dynamic and damage patterns of high arch dams under far-field underwater explosions. They discussed the nonlinear dynamic response characteristics of high arch dams with different initial stress states under far-field underwater explosions from the perspectives of damage patterns, residual displacement, and damage dissipation energy. Based on different damage patterns and indicator values from a large number of computational cases, they proposed damage limit states.

Most existing studies predominantly employ numerical simulation methods, lacking experimental research to further substantiate these numerical findings. Jiang et al.15 conducted small-scale model tests on arch dams to investigate their dynamic response to underwater explosions. Zhu et al.16 utilized both numerical simulations and model experiments to discuss the dynamic response and damage of arch dam structures under underwater explosion shock loads. They analyzed time-history curves of displacement and acceleration at typical locations, concluding that the intensity of the dam’s response is significantly influenced by the distance from the explosion source. Additionally, the horizontal displacement and acceleration responses were found to be much greater than the vertical ones. Moradi et al.17, considering the influence of transverse joints between dam sections, studied the overall dynamic response of a practical double-curvature arch dam under underwater explosion shock loads. Their results indicated that the displacement response at the dam crest is inversely proportional to the distance from the explosion source and directly proportional to the explosive equivalent. Altunisik et al.18,19 used model tests to discuss the dynamic response patterns of arch dams under normal storage levels and empty reservoir conditions, with the reservoir water level as a variable. Chen et al.20 explored the energy transfer and dissipation during the damage process of arch dam structures. They explained the dynamic damage mechanism of concrete arch dams through energy absorption and dissipation during the elastic deformation of the medium and established a damage analysis and evaluation criterion for dam structures based on the elastic strain energy of the concrete medium.

However, the majority of existing research predominantly focuses on non-contact underwater explosions occurring in the reservoir in front of the dam6–9,12–14. This type of explosion generates a uniform shock wave load on the dam surface, primarily inducing global dynamic response and potential structural vibration. In contrast, a penetration explosion refers to the detonation of a warhead after it has penetrated into the dam body. Its failure mechanism is markedly different: the explosive energy is transferred more directly to a confined volume of concrete, generating extremely high local strain rates that cause intense pulverization, fracturing, and through-thickness failure, creating the initial discharge channel. This localized high-intensity damage mode and its subsequent effects—including high-velocity discharge flow, water scouring, and secondary damage to the dam foundation and body—represent an area that has not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, a critical research void exists regarding the damage and discharge flow problems of high-arch dams under high reservoir water levels subjected to penetration explosions. This study aims to address this gap through an integrated approach combining physical model testing, Peridynamics (PD), and Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations. The novelty of this work is threefold: (1) It establishes a first-of-its-kind comprehensive experimental–numerical framework for analyzing the complete process from penetration explosion to discharge flow in arch dams; (2) Utilizing PD theory, it reveals the dynamic damage evolution and meso-mechanism of through-thickness failure in concrete dam structures under penetration explosion; (3) It provides, for the first time, a quantitative analysis of the post-breach discharge flow patterns and the ensuing scouring mechanisms acting on the dam foundation and body. This work offers new insights and a basis for assessing dam safety under such extreme loading conditions.

High arch dam penetration explosion damage and discharge model test

Model design

A hydropower station located on the upper reaches of the Jinsha River is the fourth in China and the seventh in the world to join the ranks of giant hydropower stations with a capacity exceeding ten million kilowatts. It features a parabolic concrete double-curvature arch dam with a maximum height of 270 m. The experimental model is based on the dimensions of this dam, including the foundation, dam body, reservoir, and boundaries. The length scale of the model is constrained by the size of the experimental site and is set at 200. Since ordinary water is used to simulate the reservoir liquid in the experiment, the material density scale is set at 1.0. Additionally, the experiment can only be conducted under normal gravity conditions, so the acceleration scale is also set at 1.0. The indoor experimental model includes only a limited area of the reservoir. To minimize the impact of the finite foundation boundaries observed in previous experiments, the model is positioned below ground level. Concrete support boundaries are installed around the model foundation, and dense clay is used to simulate infinite boundaries. This setup approximates the dynamic effects of the infinite domain beyond the finite foundation of the model through energy dissipation, making the experimental conditions more closely resemble actual engineering conditions. Experimental plan and process

According to existing study in 4 and 5 , the arch structure endows arch dams with a high resistance to explosions. The relatively weaker parts of the dam in terms of blast resistance are the middle and upper sections. The presence of water intakes and flood discharge tunnels in the dam body reduces its local strength and arch effect. Therefore, the experimental model is constructed entirely of concrete, with the middle and upper overflow sections of the dam simulated using gypsum material, as its mechanical properties fall within the same order of magnitude as concrete and it exhibits analogous elastic-brittle failure mechanics. Based on data from Ref. 35, the gypsum used has an elastic modulus of 20–22 GPa and a compressive strength of 18–19 MPa, compared to typical C50 high-strength concrete values of 35 GPa and 48 MPa, respectively. Although relatively weaker, this makes gypsum suitable for simulating the “weakened” sections of the dam and effectively replicating the cracking and fragmentation patterns under impact loading, as shown in Fig. 1a.

Analysis of Test Results

In the experiment, given the difficulty in achieving complete synchronization of black powder ignition and considering the highest probability of sequential penetration explosions under realistic conditions, the test was conducted with two separate penetration explosions, spaced approximately 1 to 2 s apart. This time interval, significantly longer than the duration of the blast load itself, was designed to simulate the most probable scenario of two independent strike events. It ensures that the damage from the first explosion fully develops, providing additional free surfaces for the second explosion, thereby allowing the study of cumulative damage effects—a key objective of this experiment. Peridynamics theory and program

Peridynamics (PD) theory was proposed by Silling21 of Sandia National Laboratory in the United States in 2000. It is a non-local numerical method that describes the mechanical behavior of materials by solving spatial integral equations. This method theoretically overcomes the dependence of localized methods’ numerical results on the computational grid21,22. PD combines the advantages of molecular dynamics and meshless methods while avoiding the singularity problems encountered by traditional macroscopic methods, which are based on continuity assumptions, when modeling and solving spatial differential equations or addressing discontinuous problems21,22. Additionally, it overcomes the limitations of classical molecular dynamics methods in terms of computational cost and the challenges faced by traditional multi-scale models in transmitting mechanical parameters across different scales23,24.

PD theory includes bond-based21,22 and state-based25 formulations. Bond-based PD theory is concise, but it restricts the material’s Poisson’s ratio to 1/4 (or 1/3 in 2D models)21, which limits its applicability. To address this limitation, state-based peridynamics was introduced. Consequently, state-based PD has been extended into ordinary and non-ordinary forms25,26. Non-ordinary state-based PD has demonstrated excellent results in simulating the failure of anisotropic materials27.

In the field of rock mechanics, the PD method has been successfully used to simulate the initiation, propagation, intersection, and penetration of cracks28. A coupled thermo-mechanical bond-based peridynamics method has been developed to simulate thermal cracking processes in rocks29. Rabczuk et al.30 proposed a dual-horizon peridynamics formulation to model fractures in granular and rock-like materials. Their method incorporates contact algorithms as well as artificial damping to simulate rocks subjected to quasi-static compressive failure. However, PD has not yet been applied to study the problem of rock damage and failure induced by dynamic unloading.

The applicability of the peridynamics method for simulating explosion-induced damage and fracture in brittle and composite materials is well-established and has been successfully demonstrated in numerous studies. For instance, the study in 36 effectively employed a three-dimensional peridynamic model to simulate damage and penetration in composite plates subjected to localized explosive blasts, capturing complex fracture patterns. Similarly, Ref. 37 presented a peridynamic approach combined with a sophisticated constitutive model to simulate blasting-induced rock fractures, showing excellent agreement with experimental records. These works, among others, confirm the capability of PD to handle the high-strain-rate, large-deformation, and complex fracture processes inherent in explosion problems. Therefore, the PD framework adopted in this study is built upon a validated foundation for analyzing explosive damage mechanisms.

Numerical model and computational analysis of high arch dam penetration explosion damage

Model establishment

In this study, the blasting load is applied in the form of a triangular load34, with a rise time of 0.01 ms, a fall time of 0.09 ms, and a peak load of 250 MPa. The simulation adopts a simultaneous penetration explosion approach. This approach differs from the sequential explosions (1–2 s apart) in the experiment. The discrepancy is due to the significant computational cost that would be incurred by simulating the full time interval between the two events. Given the extremely small time step required for numerical stability (dt = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ s), simulating a 1–2 s interval would require 10–20 million steps, which is computationally prohibitive. However, since the blast loading duration (0.1 ms) is infinitesimal compared to the interval, the primary cumulative effect of the sequential explosions is the creation of a weakened zone and additional free surfaces by the first explosion. The simultaneous loading applied in this study represents a more severe scenario that captures the upper-bound structural response and the combined effect of both explosions, which is sufficient for investigating the fundamental damage mechanisms and final failure mode, as validated by the experimental results in Sect. "Simulation result analysis". Additionally, since the load generated by the explosion far exceeds the static load of the arch dam structure in the penetration explosion zone, the static load is temporarily neglected in the numerical calculations to simplify the analysis.

The gypsum material used in the arch dam model has an elastic modulus of 2.1 × 1010 Pa, a density of 2330 kg/m3, and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.25. The critical stretch s₀, a key parameter governing bond breakage in the PD model, was determined through a calibration process against the experimental results of the present study. As noted in Ref. 38, for complex material behaviors, a direct analytical determination of s₀ is challenging, and inverse analysis based on experimental data is a practical and widely used alternative. The value of s₀ = 2.0 × 10⁻3 was selected as it yielded the best agreement between the simulated and experimentally observed final damage morphology (Sect. "Simulation result analysis"). This approach ensures that the model accurately captures the macroscopic fracture behavior of the specific gypsum material under the explosive loading conditions of interest. The model is discretized as follows: 130 layers along the length (Y-direction), 56 layers along the thickness (X-direction), and 156 layers along the height (Z-direction). The material point spacing is dx = 0.0085 m, the horizon size is δ = 3 dx, the time step is set to 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ s, and the calculation runs for a total of 2000 steps (2 ms).

When the material damage level reaches 75%, it is considered fully fractured. This threshold is a commonly adopted criterion in PD and other damage mechanics simulations for defining the macroscopic fracture zone from a continuum damage field35,36. It signifies a high concentration of broken bonds leading to a complete loss of load-bearing capacity at the material point, effectively representing macro-crack formation. While the absolute value is somewhat arbitrary, it provides a consistent and objective metric for comparing the extent of damage between simulation and experiment, as performed in this study. It can be seen that by the 1750th calculation step (1.75 ms), the fracture zones of the two penetration explosions start to connect, and by the 2000th step (2.00 ms), the upper and lower penetration explosion zones are completely interconnected. Numerical model and calculation analysis of discharge in damaged areas

After the arch dam is damaged by the penetration explosion, the reservoir water will discharge downstream through the damaged breach. The discharged flow differs from normal flood discharge or overflow trajectory and may cause scouring at the base and body of the dam. Additionally, the rapid changes in the flow field of the water in front of the dam will generate dynamic hydraulic loads, further affecting the stability of the dam structure. Therefore, this section analyzes the discharge patterns through the damaged area using a numerical model, aiming to reveal the impact of reservoir water release on the dam following the penetration explosion damage.

Calculation model

For the numerical model of discharge through the damaged section of the arch dam, the Fluent computational model is employed. Based on the location and size of the breach observed in the experiment, a part is added at this location and designated as a high-pressure zone. Since the shock load in the water was not measured during the experiment, and to simplify the calculations, it is assumed that the primary effect of the penetration explosion load is the structural failure to the dam, while the impact in the water is mainly due to the high-pressure gas generated after the explosion. In this calculation, the peak shock pressure acting on the water is assumed to be 0.1 MPa, with a load duration of 0.1 s. This simplified pressure load, while not capturing the full transient shockwave, is intended to represent the dominant, longer-duration pressure pulse from the expanding explosion gases. The chosen peak pressure of 0.1 MPa is a conservative estimate for the gas pressure phase following a penetration event of this scale, ensuring that the initial water disturbance and discharge initiation are simulated. A more detailed transient shockwave analysis, while valuable, is beyond the scope of this study which focuses on the subsequent quasi-steady discharge phase and its scouring effects. The overall model in Fluent is shown in Fig. 11, where the entire model uses tetrahedral unstructured meshes. The arch dam and its boundary conditions are set as rigid non-reflective boundaries, and the Eulerian multiphase flow model is adopted, as illustrated in Fig. 12.Fig. 11Numerical model of discharge through the damaged section of the arch dam.Fig. 12Mesh diagram of the discharge model for the damaged section of the arch dam (generated with ANSYS 2021R1, https://www.ansys.com).

Mesh diagram of the discharge model for the damaged section of the arch dam (generated with ANSYS 2021R1, https://www.ansys.com).

The dam is modeled as a rigid boundary, implying that fluid–structure interaction (FSI) effects are neglected. This simplification is justified for the following reasons: (1) The primary focus of this CFD analysis is on the hydrodynamic characteristics of the discharge flow and the resulting scouring potential at the dam base, rather than the dynamic response of the dam structure itself, which was the focus of the PD analysis. (2) The timescale of the scouring process is much longer than the natural vibration period of the massive dam structure. Therefore, the quasi-steady pressures exerted by the flow are unlikely to induce significant structural vibrations that would, in turn, alter the flow field. While FSI could modify local pressures at the fluid–structure interface, its omission is not expected to qualitatively change the overall flow patterns and scour mechanisms identified in this study.

Simulation result analysis

At the instant of the explosion impact, the water medium, unable to move in time, can be considered as an incompressible medium. At this moment, the shock wave is primarily transmitted, propagating outward from the penetration explosion site. When the shock wave reaches the free surface, it generates a strong rarefaction wave reflected back into the water and a transmitted shock wave propagating into the air. Due to the significantly lower acoustic impedance of air compared to water, the intensity of the transmitted shock wave in the air is much weaker than that of the reflected rarefaction wave. The strong reflected rarefaction wave interacts with the incident shock wave, causing the water pressure near the free surface to drop rapidly. This results in cavitation below the free surface, with local peaks and troughs forming, leading to a highly disturbed water surface. When the shock wave is strong enough, some of the water medium can be ejected from the surface. Over time, under the influence of gravity, the water surface fluctuates, and the waves propagate outward. As water flows out through the damaged breach, the surface at the breach forms a concave curve, as shown in Fig. 13.Fig. 13Changes in the reservoir water surface after the arch dam is damaged by penetration explosion.

From macroscopic analysis and observational data, it is evident that in studying the coupled phenomena of the arch dam body-reservoir water-foundation-pulsating load, the energy generated by the pulsating load from the flood discharge jet impacting the downstream riverbed is transmitted to the dam body through the riverbed foundation, causing vibrations in the dam. Therefore, when the jet trajectory is farther from the dam toe, the pulsating load is significantly reduced. However, the discharge breach caused by the penetration explosion of the arch dam does not have the designed trajectory distance for overflow discharge, causing the jet trajectory to approach the dam toe and greatly increasing the overall safety risk of the dam. Additionally, in this experiment, the deflected flow caused by the incoming water hitting the plunge pool floor has already impacted the base of the dam, affecting the structural integrity of the dam body.

Conclusion

Main findings and validation

This study integrated physical model testing, peridynamics (PD), and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to investigate the damage and discharge flow of a high-arch dam under high reservoir water level subjected to penetration explosions. The main findings are summarized as follows: The experimental model successfully simulated the penetration explosion damage at the vulnerable middle-upper section of the arch dam, resulting in a vertically connected breach. A quantitative comparison between the experimental and PD-simulated damage dimensions is presented in Table 1. The simulation accurately captured the vertical extent of the damage (20.4 cm vs. 20 cm experimentally, an error of 2%). The horizontal width was overestimated (10.2 cm vs. 8 cm, an error of 27.5%), which can be attributed to the idealized homogeneity of the PD material model and the simplified simultaneous loading, which did not fully capture the more localized crushing and compaction observed in the physical test. Engineering implications

The findings offer concrete engineering implications for dam safety. The confirmation of the middle-upper dam sections as critical weak points against penetration threats underscores the necessity for targeted reinforcement designs in these zones, such as using enhanced concrete grades or composite armoring. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrates that discharge from an unscheduled breach directs high-energy flow directly towards the dam toe, highlighting the importance of ensuring the robustness of the plunge pool and apron and suggesting the consideration of defensive measures like deflection walls or pre-excavated scour basins located away from the dam toe in both new designs and safety evaluations of existing structures.

Limitations and future work

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study, which also chart a course for future work. The conclusions are derived from a scaled model using gypsum as a concrete simulant, indicating a need for future large-scale or full-scale tests with prototype materials to better understand size effects. Additionally, the highly transient and destructive nature of the penetration explosion tests prevented the acquisition of quantitative time-history measurements (e.g., pressure or displacement), limiting the validation of the PD model to the final damage morphology rather than direct comparison of dynamic response data. The simplified pressure pulse applied in the CFD analysis, while serving as a practical engineering approximation to initiate discharge, lacks direct physical derivation from the explosion event. The assumption of a rigid dam boundary in the CFD simulations, while reasonable for characterizing the primary scouring hydrodynamics, neglects potential fluid–structure interaction effects that could influence local damage at the already compromised dam toe. The modeling simplifications, including synchronous explosion loading in PD and rigid boundaries in CFD, while practical for this study’s scope, introduce uncertainties that warrant future research into sequential explosion modeling and fully coupled fluid–structure interaction analysis. Specifically, implementing a sophisticated coupled PD-CFD (e.g., ALE) framework would not only ensure physically consistent transfer of structural damage to fluid response but also enable investigation of the fluid–structure feedback during the discharge process. Finally, this work focused on a single breach scenario, and investigating the effects of multiple, asynchronous explosions and cascade failure in reservoir systems represents a critical and complex frontier for future investigation.
