Assessment of quality control and effect of radiolysis on the in vivo biodistribution of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T
Authors: Eline L. Hooijman, Carolline M. Ntihabose, Stijn L. W. Koolen, Maryana Handula, Savanne Beekman, Debra Stuurman, Corrina de Ridder, Jan R. de Jong, Jordy R. J. P. van de Merbel, Nyah Rook, Else A. Aalbersberg, Tessa F. F. T. Ververs, Peter Laverman, Jeroen J. M. A. Hendrikx, Erik de Blois
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne of the challenges in optimizing PSMA-based therapies for the treatment of prostate cancer is the stability of the radiopharmaceutical, defined by the degradation of radiolabeled compounds due to exposure to ionizing radiation (radiolysis). Radiolysis of a radiopharmaceutical is difficult to measure and can influence the biodistrubion of radiopharmaceuticals in vivo, possibly leading to toxicity or suboptimal outcomes. Therefore, an inter-laboratory study was performed to confirm accurate detection of radiolysed [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T, and the biodistribution of radiolysed radiopharmaceutical was investigated in vivo, and HPLC methods are compared.ResultsMultiple HPLC-methods were compared to evaluate the analysis of degraded radiopharmaceutical and an inter-laboratory comparison was conducted to evaluate the consistency of current analytical methods across various institutions and with current methods from literature. Additionally, for the biodistribution experiments, a radiolabeling of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T was adjusted to achieve a radiochemical purity of 50%, 70%, and 97% of the final product. A PC3-PIP patient-derived xenograft mouse model was used to investigate in vivo behavior of the formed impurity, followed by SPECT/CT imaging and biodistribution. When evaluating radio-HPLC methods for the impurity detection, the European Pharmacopoeia method (with a phosphate buffered eluent) shows no significant difference in radiochemical purity, an increased peak separation but decreased recovery (< 75%). The inter-laboratory comparison demonstrated the ability to measure the radiolysed radiopharmaceutical across multiple hospitals, with a maximum standard deviation of 6.6%. The in vivo SPECT/CT and biodistribution results show that as ingrowth of impurities caused by radiolysis increasing the tumor-to-kidney ratio is decreasing (97%/50% RCP, p < 0.05).ConclusionThe inter-laboratory study confirms that impurities caused by radiolysis can be measured accurately across different institutions, ensuring consistency and reliability in clinical diagnostics and therapy, contributing to improved patient safety. Phosphate buffered HPLC-methods showed no significant benefits in quality control. Increased impurities caused by radiolysis lead to lower tumor-to-kidney ratios, with a decrease in specific tumor binding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-025-00414-y.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is globally one of the most prevalent cancers among men, with significant implications for mortality and morbidity (Merrill 2024). Over the past few decades, advancements in diagnosis and treatment have been critical in managing the disease (Posdzich et al. 2023; Le et al. 2023). For example, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is becoming increasingly recognized and utilized in the field of nuclear medicine, particularly in the treatment of metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) (Golan et al. 2023). The ability to target PSMA has resulted in both imaging (diagnostics) and therapy of PCa, offering a high degree of specificity (Chatalic et al. 2016). PSMA ligands can be radiolabeled with various radioisotopes, such as gallium-68 (Ga-68) or lutetium-177 (Lu-177), to serve either as diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. When labeled with Lu-177, the radiopharmaceutical can deliver beta-radiation to PCa cells, thereby sparing surrounding healthy tissues. Although uptake in the salivary glands and intestinal organs can be observed, this approach has shown promising results, and the use of PSMA-targeting radiopharmaceuticals is increasing (Sadaghiani et al. 2022a).

During the production and administration of radiopharmaceuticals, particularly those labeled with therapeutic isotopes such as Lu-177, ionizing radiation can alter the chemical composition of the compound by a process defined as radiolysis (Hooijman et al. 2021). Among the PSMA-targeting radiopharmaceuticals, [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T ([177Lu]Lu-zadavotide gurexetan) and [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 ([177Lu]Lu-vipivotide tetraxetan) are frequently compared. Although [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T shows promising results, it appears to be more sensitive to degradation, compared to the [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 (Ruigrok et al. 2021). In regards to the degradation products, the monograph of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T published in the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur.) (becoming in place in version 11.7 in 2025) specify limits for each impurity (Europe E.E.P.S.C 2025). However, the degradation products are not identified or further specified.

Within this research, the radiopharmaceutical [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T is considered the intact product and the impurities are defined as the free Lu-177/[177Lu]Lu-DTPA and radiolysed species of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T. Presently, among the release criteria of radiopharmaceuticals, two radiochemical criteria are monitored namely the radiochemical yield (RCY) and radiochemical purity (RCP), by thin layer chromatography (radio-TLC) and high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis, respectively (Blois et al. 2012).

Understanding the influence and the effects of radiolysis is important, as the radiolysed radiopharmaceutical may show an altered biodistribution and might influence the treatment efficacy (Hooijman et al. 2022). Previously, studies showed that induced radiolysed radiopharmaceutical in cell experiments, result in altered cellular uptake and kinetics (Hooijman et al. 2021). These changes can potentially impact the therapeutic outcomes and may increase the risk of adverse effects, making it essential to ensure that the radiopharmaceuticals administered to patients, are of the highest quality and stability (Kratochwil et al. 2019; Sadaghiani et al. 2022b; Kalmthout et al. 2020).

To gain insight into the impact of radiolysis on [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T, an inter-institutional comparison was first carried out to explore potential variability in analytical outcomes between laboratories. This is followed by a comparison of multiple HPLC methods to evaluate which approach most reliably detects the degradation by radiolysis and their impurities. Beyond assessing analytical performance, it is also relevant to explore whether undetected impurities might influence the biological behavior of the product. Therefore, the biodistribution of radiolysed [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T was investigated as a follow-up to previously published in vitro studies (Hooijman et al. 2022).

To further investigate the impact of radiolysis on [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T, it is important to consider the biological behavior of the radiolysed [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T, when introduced into in vivo models. If the biodistribution of those impurities are altered, it is of high importance that the analytical methods can detect these impurities. This research is focused on whether the impurities caused by radiolysis can be effectively separated and quantified by the analytical methods.

Inter-laboratory comparison

The interlaboratory comparison involved 4 Dutch institutes that routinely prepare [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T as an in-house preparation. All HPLC-systems and methods of the collaborating institutes were validated according to the EANM guidelines (Kratochwil et al. 2019; Gillings et al. 2020). A summary of the validation results and overview of the used HPLC equipment is presented in Table S1 and S2 of supplementary material. Each institute reported their HPLC-results of the samples, and corresponding chromatograms were sent to the comparative center.

Samples for inter-laboratory comparison

Each institute received a sample of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T from the different supplying centers, and each institute has served as a supplying institute (except center 4). The sample originated from a [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T batch administered within the validated shelf-life. The RCY (> 97%) and RCP (> 95%) were confirmed using validated methods. A sample of ± 300 MBq was split into four batches (± 70 MBq each) and stored in a 1.5 mL maximum recovery HPLC-vial (Waters, Etten-Leur, The Netherlands). Each institute performed an HPLC-injection at 24(± 1) and 48(± 2) hours after radiolabeling. These time points that are dependent on the institutes and after expiration for clinical use, were intentionally selected to ensure that degradation by radiolysis has occurred, allowing for a critical evaluation.

Statistical analysis

The evaluation of the obtained results from the imaging and biodistribution studies was first performed using a Grubbs' outlier test to identify and exclude the presence of outliers (no outliers present). Subsequently, a two-tailed t-test was conducted to compare the data. To evaluate the HPLC-data, the standard deviation was calculated to measure the dispersion of data points from the mean. Additionally, the relative standard deviation (coefficient of variation) was computed to assess the variability of the data relative to the mean, expressed as a percentage. All data is processed using GraphPad version 9.0.0.

HPLC processing methods

After integration the chromatograms were reintegrated by 1 trained operator from the comparative center to assess operator variability in comparison to the original analysis performed by the institutes themselves. The parameters were manually selected on background, selection in region of interest of the expected peaks and selection of the main peak. Each of the initial measurements were compared with the reintegration of the chromatograms by the trained operator from the comparative center, thereby determining the mean and the standard deviation. Center 2, which utilized standardized processing methods (Empower, Waters), was excluded from the variation analysis due to the uniform analysis conditions.

One approach involves the use of phosphate buffers as an eluent, in contrast to the more commonly employed TFA-based systems and is incorporated into the Ph. These methods were also compared to the comparative method (Table 2) including recovery measurements (Gillings et al. 2020). A standard activity of 3 MBq/100 µL was injected and for all methods a flow of 1 mL/min was used. For comparison of the radiolysed impurity detection, samples of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T were injected at 24 (± 1 h) and 48 (± 2 h) hours after synthesis to ensure the presence of radiolysed radiopharmaceutical. Imaging and biodistribution studies

All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Erasmus Medical Center’s animal welfare committee, and adhered to the established ethical guidelines (application number SP2300235/1). The experiments are based upon studies performed by Ruigrok et al. (2021). The animal experiments were performed with 6–8 week old male mice, with a PC3-PIP PDX xenograft (prostate cancer PSMA-positive cell line, Uppsala University, Sweden). The cells for the xenograft (PC3-PIP) were cultured in RPMI 1640, Glutamax medium, supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, penicillin (100 units/mL) and streptomycin (100 units/mL, Gibco), and in presence of puromycin (10 µg/mL) (Invivogen). The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Thereafter, NMRI nu/nu -nude mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 106 PSMA-expressing PC3-PIP cells suspended in Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA). The tumors were grown for 10 days, according to pilot-data to an average tumor volume of 280 mm3 (Ruigrok et al. 2021). After reaching the desired tumor volume, the animals were injected in the tail vein with either 20 MBq/0.7 nmol/100 µL (imaging) or 5 MBq/0.7 nmol/100 µL (biodistribution experiments) of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T (RCP 50%, 70% or 97%) (diluted in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with Kolliphor® HS 15 (2.0 mg/mL)) which is added to prevent sticking of the radiopharmaceuticsl.

SPECT/CT imaging

The imaging study includes single-photon emission computed tomography and computed tomography (SPECT-CT). Of each group (50%, 70% and 97% RCP), one animal was imaged at 4 or 24 h. Whole body images of the euthanized mice were acquired using the VECTor + /CT (MILabs, Netherlands) within 40 min, using the extra ultrahigh sensitivity (XXUHS) collimator (trans axial FOV 8 cm) in list mode using 94 bed positions. The SPECT data was reconstructed using SROSEM with 9 iterations and maximally 128 subsets and a voxel size of 0.8 mm3 (MILabs REC 8.0 software). The photopeaks 113 and 208 keV were used to visualize Lu-177, scatter and background were corrected for with the triple-energy-window method. Furthermore, the CT was reconstructed using filtered back-projection. The SPECT and CT were registered; SPECT data was corrected for attenuation using the CT data. The resulting imaging data was analyzed using PMOD (Bruker Software). After the scan, the organs of these imaging mice were collected, and the data was added to the biodistribution groups of 4 and 24 h, respectively.

Biodistribution studies

The biodistribution study was performed (including the imaged mice) at 4 h (RCP 50%, 70% and 97%, n = 4 per RCP/group) and 24 h (RCP 50%, 70% and 97%, n = 4) post injection (p.i.). The organs were collected including blood (via orbital punction), stomach, heart, skin, tumor, pancreas, liver, spleen, small intestine, colon, adrenals, kidneys, prostate (anterior), lungs, salivary glands, muscle, bone (femur) and tail. To verify the specificity of the [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T uptake, an additional group was co-injected with a 100-fold molar excess of unlabeled PSMA-I&T (block) and sacrificed at 4 p.i.. The tissues were weighed, and radioactivity was measured using a gamma counter (2480 Wizard-2 (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA)) and the Lu-177 activity was measured (calibrated energy at 113 keV and 208 keV). The results are expressed in %IA/g (Fig S1 and S2 of in supplementary material).

Discussion

In this study, the impact on the biodistribution of radiolysed radiopharmaceutical ([177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T) is explored within a murine mouse model. Detection of radiolysed radiopharmaceutical is one of the main challenges in quality control (Hooijman et al. 2022), and the impact in vivo needed to be further investigated.

Within this study, an inter-laboratory analysis was performed, showing that when a method is both optimized at each institute individually and validated according to the EANM criteria (Gillings et al. 2020; EDQM xxxx), the deviation does not exceed 6.6% of SD and 9.1% of RSD. The RSD measurements are focused on the main product of interest [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T. The overall deviation may be caused by multiple factors; Firstly, the performance of radio-detectors coupled to an HPLC for RCP measurement is crucial and might be affected by factors such as geometry, size, solvent, flow and volume. The type, size, and energy range of the detector also influences the detection accuracy. Detectors with lower background noise and higher signal-to-noise ratios improve the detection sensitivity, enhancing the precision of RCP measurements (Saldarriaga Vargas et al. 2020). Additionally, a maximum deviation of 2.8% occurs when different trained operators analyze 1 chromatogram. Current software often lacks the flexibility in selection of different algorithms needed for analysis standardization, specifically for radio-HPLC. Consequently, when combining the deviation of 6.6% and 2.7%, a maximum of 7.2% deviation in the overall process was observed. Thereby, it should be noted that radiolysis increases the variability in analytical results, making HPLC evaluation of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T at later timepoints more challenging. Degradation can alter peak profiles and reduce the accuracy. Therefore, the radiopharmaceutical stability should be optimized before analysis.

Additionally, the short shelf-life of the product introduces further complexity and variability, as radiolysis occurs randomly and continuously, which makes it challenging to maintain consistent sample conditions and thereby introducing unsystematic impurities (see Sect. "Inter-laboratory comparison") (Ramos-Méndez et al. 2018). This is further supported by the chromatogram analysis from center 2 (see table S4 supplementary material) (Kraihammer et al. 2023; Iudicello et al. 2021), thereby underlining the need for further investigation of the structural differences between the derivatives of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T.

Furthermore, HPLC QC methods from the literature (Kraihammer et al. 2023) were compared, one of them will be implemented in the Ph. Eur. monograph (2025) (Europe E.E.P.S.C. 2025). In this case of phosphate-buffered methods, although peak separation of the non-labeled Lu-177/[177Lu]Lu-DTPA and radiolysed product can be obtained, no better results than the TFA-based methods could be obtained. While phosphate buffers may offer a sufficient peak resolution for HPLC analysis, caution regarding salt formation in the used system is important, because of system blockages. Additionally, not commonly used eluents, lead to frequent system changes and an increased workload in clinical care (EDQM xxxx; Ioppolo et al. 2023). Additionally, recovery optimization poses a notable challenge, where in this example addition of unlabeled PSMA-I&T did not enhance recovery (< 90%).

The biodistribution studies showed that higher RCP correlates with a higher—and thus better—tumor-to-kidney ratio. Since reactive radicals are form randomly and damage the radiopharmaceutical unpredictably, the variation in biodistribution increases as the RCP decreases (see Fig S2 supplementary material section 7.1) (Ramos-Méndez et al. 2018). To make a comparison of the biodistribution of solely the radiolysed radiopharmaceutical, a purification step is necessary to obtain the pure radiolysed product. However, this might introduce a potential matrix effect, and result in a difference in mass, as the unlabeled PSMA-I&T-fraction is also removed during purification, therefore no purification was performed.

Additionally, a low RCP corresponds to a decreased specificity to the tumor site, and a higher retention in the kidneys, which may increase the toxicity and decrease the treatment efficacy posing a concern for patient safety. Reduced tumor uptake at lower RCP likely reflects reduced binding of radiolysed radiopharmaceutical, reducing competition with the unlabeled compound. In the kidneys, strong blocking may result from non‑specific binding combined with mass‑dose effects that interfere with reabsorption. Radiolysis radiopharmaceutical therefore could alter the biodistribution, and have to be further investigated. Therefore, a low RCP is associated with reduced tumor specificity and increased renal retention, which may elevate toxicity and compromise therapeutic efficacy, raising concerns regarding patient safety.

Conclusion

In conclusion, due to the challenges of radiolysed radiopharmaceutical detection, caution is required when optimizing HPLC-methods. However, when working according to the most recent guidelines in regards to equipment and method validation, the inter-laboratory study confirms that radiolysed radiopharmaceutical measurements can be consistently performed across various institutions. Additionally, when assessing the impact, a lower RCP of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T is associated with lower tumor uptake resulting in reduced tumor-to-kidney ratios, which can impact treatment efficacy. By recognizing and addressing the challenges of measuring radiolysed radiopharmaceuticals, patient safety can be enforced, and the optimal therapeutic outcomes can be realized.
