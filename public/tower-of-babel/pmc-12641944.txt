EU protection requirements for ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education
Authors: Mieke Embo, Clara Wasiak, Simon Verschaeve, Lisse Van Acker, Margot Timmers, Eva Lievens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhen using ePortfolios in healthcare education, the collection and processing of personal data from various stakeholders, also known as data subjects (e.g., students, mentors, supervisors) is inevitable. This is why it is crucial to identify the stakeholders who need to comply with legal obligations imposed by data protection law, and to assess the legal grounds for processing personal (health) data. Research on the legal aspects of such ePortfolios is lacking. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify and document the data protection requirements for ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education that apply in the EU.MethodsDesk research based on a traditional legal analysis of legislation, policy documents, guidelines, case law, and legal doctrine was performed during a multidisciplinary ePortfolio research project.ResultsThe analysis resulted in a description of the relevant EU data protection requirements covering the Charter of Fundamental Rights and the General Data Protection Regulation, a translation of these legal requirements into the context of ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education and the formulation of recommendations for data protection compliance based on these insights: (1) the duties and responsibilities of educational institutions and the healthcare student must be clarified in an agreement before the start of an internship, (2) ‘(substantial) public interest’ is the most appropriate legal basis for the processing of health data in ePortfolios, and (3) adequate and appropriate measures to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the data subjects must be provided.ConclusionThis study contributes to the limited literature on the legal aspects of the use of digital technologies, such as ePortfolios, in healthcare education. There is a need for rigorous evidence on how to design legally compliant ePortfolios for healthcare education.Keywords: Data protection, Health data, EPortfolios, Digital portfolios, GDPR, Consent, Workplace learning

Introduction

Healthcare education increasingly relies on digital or ePortfolios to support workplace learning [1, 2]. Workplace learning is essential to healthcare education as it allows learners to develop their competencies in authentic clinical environments [3–5]. Digital or electronic/ePortfolios have been introduced to scaffold learning and assessment, facilitate monitoring of competency development, and support learners in their self-directed learning. Additionally, ePortfolios assist healthcare professionals and teachers in providing feedback and learning support. There is already extensive literature on ePortfolios in healthcare [1], but less research has been conducted on their legal aspects [2]. Various legal questions arise, ranging from intellectual property issues to concerns about privacy, data protection and professional secrecy of the healthcare professionals involved. This study focuses on the EU data protection requirements for ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education.

Traditionally, paper-based portfolios have been used as a means to keep track of learners’ experiences and progress at the workplace [1, 2]. ePortfolios became more prevalent in the early 2000 s as a repository to store evidence and reflections [2]. However, in recent years ePortfolios have evolved from a repository to an articulation of authentic learning and development. Janssens and co-authors formulated in their review two types of ePortfolio platforms: the ‘store and communicate’ and the ‘work and learn’ ePortfolios [1]. EPortfolios offer several benefits compared to paper-based portfolios, such as the ability to ‘tag’ experiences so that the corresponding entry appears automatically in the appropriate section of the portfolio [6]. Additionally advantages include the prevention of document loss, no size restrictions, the ability to receive digital notifications when feedback is provided, and the capacity to upload, manage and store specific sources of evidence such as multimedia content (e.g., audio/video fragments, external links, YouTube videos, podcasts, etc.). The digital format enhances interactivity and efficiency for both learners and supervisors at the workplace [1]. However, the growing variety of ePortfolio technologies, hosting systems and evolving professional and ethical standards has introduced various challenges, including how to meet the increasing data protection requirements for ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education.

In an ePortfolio used in healthcare settings, personal data of various stakeholders, including healthcare students, peers, educators, and patients, is processed. Under the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [7], ‘personal data’ is a very broad notion, meaning ‘any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person’ (Article 4.1 GDPR) [7]. That person is called a ‘data subject’. The types of data may range from names, email addresses, student numbers, online identifiers to information about (physical or mental) health from patients with whom the students interact. In the EU, the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of their personal data is acknowledged as a fundamental right (Article 8 Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union; Recital 1 GDPR) [7, 8]. As a result, when using ePortfolios that process different types of data from different data subjects (including students, supervisors and patients), questions arise as to which legal requirements must be met. Moreover, for healthcare education, these questions become more pressing given the fact that ‘health data’ are considered to be an especially sensitive category of personal data, which is subject to stricter rules than other types of personal data (Article 9 GDPR) [7]. Therefore, compliance with the GDPR is essential.

This study aimed to explore the most pressing questions about the collection and processing of personal data in ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education. The following three research questions are answered:

(1) Which stakeholders are involved and who is accountable for the data processing?

(2) Which lawful ground under Article 6 of the GDPR [7] can be relied upon for this data processing, and under what conditions?

(3) Which derogation under Article 9 of the GDPR [7] can justify the processing of health data?

Based on the results, practical recommendations are provided to ensure compliance with EU legal requirements when processing data and sharing information within ePortfolios during workplace learning internships.

Method

Context

This study was conducted as part of a larger Flemish-funded interdisciplinary research program, the FWO/SBO SCAFFOLD project (2020–2023). The aim of this project was to design an evidence-based, state-of-the-art ePortfolio prototype for workplace learning in healthcare education that provides maximum support to students regarding their competency development. We defined the scaffold ePortfolio as a digital learner-centered tool that documents, stimulates, visualizes, and proves the continuous competency development of (future) healthcare professionals during workplace learning. The Scaffold ePortfolio facilitates and promotes the learning cycle through goal setting, reflection, feedback, and (self)-assessment, as well as interaction among all stakeholders. Students are instructed to reflect on performances during daily practice and on competency development over a period of time.

Design

The researchers conducted desk research based on a traditional legal analysis of legislation, policy documents, guidelines, case law, and legal doctrine. This study focused on the requirements laid down by the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [7], a crucial instrument in the current legislative framework for data protection in the EU. The guidance documents issued by the Article 29 Working Party (WP29) and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), relevant case-law of the European Court of Justice and academic literature were considered, in light of their relevance to the subject of the research, focusing on the processing of personal data, including health data, of and by a variety of actors in the context of the use of ePortfolios. Where the EU data protection framework relies on national legislation, the study focuses on the applicable legislation of Belgium or Flanders (depending on the relevant competencies) – the national context of the SCAFFOLD project – by means of illustration.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the different sub-projects of the research project funded by the Flemish government (S003219N, www.sbo-scaffold.com/EN) was obtained from the Ethical committees from the different universities (UGent and KULeuven). Given the aim of the legal sub-project it was not necessary to seek advice from the Ethics Committee, as it was limited to desk research.

Results

The results are presented in line with the research questions, followed by recommendations.

Research question 1: actors and accountability

This first research question “Which stakeholders are involved and who is accountable for the data processing?” is answered in two sections: (1) who qualifies as data controllers and data processors under the GDPR [7] and (2) the application of this qualification in the context of ePortfolios.

Legal qualification of stakeholders under the GDPR

In the context of an ePortfolio, it is essential to determine who is accountable for data processing. In other words, to identify the ‘data controller(s)’ and ‘data processor(s)’. The data controllers are natural or legal persons, public authorities, agencies or other bodies which, alone or jointly with others, determine the purposes and means of the processing of personal data. The data processors are natural or legal persons, public authorities, agencies or other bodies which process personal data on behalf of the controller (Article 4.7 and 4.8 GDPR) [7]. The question ‘who is accountable for the data processing?’ is important to answer in order to know who must comply with the obligations imposed by the GDPR.

However, the correct qualification of the different stakeholders for a specific processing activity always depends on the context. Both terms ‘controller’ and ‘processor’ are functional concepts. They aim to assign responsibilities according to the actual roles of the parties.1 This implies that the capacity of a stakeholder should be determined by how that stakeholder actually behaves in practice and thus not (only) by any formal designation (e.g. in a contract or another agreement).2

The data controller bears the primary responsibility for compliance with the obligations of data protection law and the data subjects rights. It is the data controller who determines the purpose (‘why’) of and means (‘how’) for processing personal data. The WP29 developed a 5-step-plan to identify the data controller, which was confirmed by the EDPB [9, 10]. Control can either stem from legal provisions or factual influence [9 para 21].

To qualify as a data processor, two conditions apply: (i) the processor must be a legal entity distinct from the controller, and (ii) the processor must process the personal data in question ‘for the benefit of the controller’ [9 para 79]. The latter means that the processor must act for (the purposes of) someone else and do so according to the instructions of the controller, at least with respect to the purposes and essential means of processing [9 para 80]. It is possible that more than one processor may be involved in a particular processing activity.

The educational institution initiates processing for feedback and learning purposes. Each educational institution is free to choose an ePortfolio and provides its students and supervisors with the necessary instructions and user manuals to preserve training uniformity. The educational institution thus determines the purpose of and means for processing and can be qualified as a data controller in light of the GDPR [7].

Linked to this educational institution are the training supervisors. Since the EU regulator prefers to consider the institution itself as data controller, and not a specific person within that institution, the training supervisors are not held personally responsible for the processing operations they carry out in the name and under the responsibility of the educational institution [9 para 17]. The training supervisors are, in this sense, part of the educational institution that acts as the controller. They can still be qualified as a data subject themselves if their personal data are processed when using the ePortfolio (e.g., to give them access to the content uploaded by students, or to log provided feedback).

The workplace is the environment where students perform their internship and carry out activities under the supervision of experienced healthcare suppliers. In general, the workplace is a separate legal entity from the educational institution, that processes personal data on behalf of and in accordance with the instructions of the educational institution. For the purpose of processing personal data within the ePortfolio, the workplace will in most cases act as a processor. However, it is possible that the workplace is closely linked to or even part of the same legal entity as the educational institution (e.g. a university hospital). In the latter scenario, one of the requirements to qualify as a processor is not met (as the processor must be a legal entity distinct from the controller). In that case, the university hospital together with the affiliated university acts as the controller. Although the educational institution will usually decide on the purposes and means of processing personal data within the ePortfolio, it might in certain circumstances be possible that the educational institution and the workplace could be considered joint controllers, if they jointly determine the purposes and means of processing (Article 26 GDPR). In this situation, they will need to determine the distribution of their respective responsibilities by means of an arrangement (e.g. a contract) between them.

For the practice supervisors from whom the student learns at the workplace, an analogous argument can be made as for the training supervisors. They process personal data in the name and under the responsibility of the workplace, meaning that no data protection responsibilities are assigned to them in their own name. Once again, practice supervisors can also be considered data subjects for certain processing activities (supra).

The healthcare student is a student who is enrolled at an educational institution and who is doing an internship at the workplace as part of their education. The students can be considered to be affiliated with the educational institution respectively the workplace, insofar as they process personal data in their capacity as a student (undergraduate) or as an intern (post-graduate). In both capacities, the healthcare student also qualifies as a data subject. A significant amount of personal data about the student is processed within the ePortfolio. After all, in addition to the data required for organisational purposes (e.g., email address), the ePortfolio is designed to supervise and assess the student’s performance during their internship and to monitor their competency growth. When linked to the student’s personal profile on the ePortfolio, this information inherently constitutes personal data.

Peers of the student also play an important role. They can learn from each other, exchange experiences and provide feedback. Two different groups are distinguished: first, the other students who are enrolled in the same programme at the same educational institution; second, students from other educational institutions who are learning at the same workplace. Furthermore, depending on whether they qualify as student or intern they are considered part of the educational institution or the workplace. Peers can also be data subjects, according to the same reasoning as for the healthcare student.

The role of the IT provider strongly depends on the agreement with the client of the ePortfolio. If the IT provider assumes the role of operating the ePortfolio, it will be a processor under the GDPR for the processing of personal data for training purposes. After all, the processing is done on behalf of the educational institution, which determines the purpose and means. Both parties must enter into a processor agreement to shape the mutual relationship and context of the processing (Article 28 GDPR) [7]. The IT provider remains the data controller for the processing of personal data for its own purposes and processing beyond the training purposes (e.g., processing operations for optimising the platform).

If personal (health) data of patients is included in the ePortfolio, even in a pseudonymised form, they will also be considered data subjects (Recital 26 GDPR) [7].

Research question 2: lawful grounds to process personal data

This second research question “Which lawful ground under Article 6 of the GDPR [7] can be relied upon for this data processing, and under what conditions?” will be answered in two sections: (1) lawful grounds for processing personal data in the GDPR [7], and (2) identification of the lawful grounds in the context of ePortfolios.

Lawful grounds for processing personal data

Article 5 GDPR lists a number of fundamental data protection principles (lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability). The principle of lawfulness entails that in order to process personal data, a data controller (see Table 1) must be able to rely on one of six lawful grounds: (1) consent, (2) contract, (3) legal obligation, (4) vital interests, (5) public interest or (6) legitimate interests (Article 5.1. GDPR) [7]. For the processing of personal data of the data subjects in an ePortfolio, the controller must be able to rely on such a processing ground (Article 6.1 GDPR) [7]. The choice of a legal ground must be made before the processing activity is started. This means that the controller cannot simply switch from one ground to another after the processing has started (e.g. during inspection by the Data Protection Authority) if it appears that not all conditions for the previously chosen legal ground are met [11 para 123].

Lawful grounds in the context of ePortfolios

Determining what the appropriate legal ground is for the processing of personal data in the context of an ePortfolio is crucial. It is essential that the identified lawful ground corresponds to the objective and essence of the processing [12 para 1]. The choice of a legal basis is challenging, since the available grounds may depend on who is acting as the data controller for the processing. As discussed above, for ePortfolios, this will most likely be the educational institution. In the context of ePortfolios, the following grounds for processing are the most relevant: (1) consent, (2) contract, (3) public interest and (4) legitimate interest (Article 6,1, a), b), e) and f) GDPR) [7]. Different lawful grounds might be appropriate for various processing activities within the context of the ePortfolio (e.g. for the processing of students’ data, a different lawful ground might be suitable than for the processing of patients’ data).

Consent

Principle

Although the GDPR does not establish a hierarchy between the different lawful grounds [21 para 1], obtaining consent from the data subject is sometimes considered preferable compared to the other grounds in certain circumstances as it gives the data subject a certain degree of control over the use of their personal data [20 p. 16, 11 para 3, 35]. According to the EDPB, given the fact that “data protection law is aiming at the protection of fundamental rights, an individual’s control over their personal data is essential” [11 para 27]. However, in order for consent to serve as a legal basis for processing personal data, it must meet strict conditions. In particular, consent must be (a) freely given, (b) specific, (c) informed and (d) unambiguous (Article 4.11 GDPR) [7]. It is up to the data controller to prove that a legitimate consent was obtained from the data subject (Article 7.1 GDPR) [7].

In this section, the possibility to rely on consent as a lawful ground required by Article 6 GDPR is investigated. In a next section, the processing of health data of patients as a special category of data, on the basis of explicit consent as a derogation to the general prohibition (Article 9 GDPR) will be examined.

A. The consent must be freely given

The requirement that consent must be ‘freely given’ necessitates genuine choice and meaningful control by the individual [11 para. 3]. This means that any element that involves undue pressure or influence that prevents an individual from exercising their free will renders the consent invalid [11 para 1].

Depending on how educational institutions are shaped in a given EU jurisdiction, it may be the case that the educational institution (and perhaps even the workplace) qualifies as a public or government body. Where the data controller is a government entity, there is an assumption that consent will not be freely given, because it assumed that there might be a power imbalance in this relationship (Recital 43 GDPR) [7]; in this case, for instance, between the educational institution and students, who might feel pressured to consent to the processing of their personal data in the context of an ePortfolio. This does not mean that the use of consent as a legal basis by public authorities is completely excluded. A government may exceptionally still use consent as a legal basis, in specific circumstances where particular attention was paid to ensuring that the data subject has a real choice, where the optional nature of the offer is emphasised, and where there are no disadvantages or negative consequences associated with a refusal [11 para 16–17].

Specifically regarding the processing of personal data of patients, the relationship between the patient who might be in a vulnerable position wanting to obtain healthcare and the healthcare provider may also be unequal [16 para 20]. When a patient is asked to give consent to the processing of their (health) data in the context of an ePortfolio, they may feel coerced into giving that consent (see also Research question 3). This ‘power imbalance’ can prevent free consent, unless the factual circumstances show that there are sufficient safeguards in place (Recital 43 GDPR) [7, 13]. Important in that regard is that the provision of healthcare or treatment would not be conditional on giving consent to the processing of personal data for the purposes of the ePortfolio [11 para 26]. It could be argued that the processing of personal data for the latter purpose is not necessary for the provision of healthcare, and as such, patients should be able to refuse it, without negative repercussions.

B. The consent must be specific

The consent of the data subject must relate to one or more specific purposes. The data subject must have a choice in relation to each of them, to ensure a degree of user control [11 para 55]. Consent may cover multiple operations as long as these serve the same purpose (Article 5.1, b) GDPR; Recital 32 GDPR) [7] [11 para 57]. For example, video-recording a consultation and uploading it in the ePortfolio are two different operations serving the same purpose, which is to enable more qualitative feedback and self-reflection. To comply with this condition, the purpose of the processing activity or activities must be justified, well-defined and explicitly described (Article 5.1, b) GDPR) [7].

C. The consent must be informed (Article 4.11 GDPR)

This requirement is closely linked with the previous one. The EDPB has listed the minimum information that must be communicated [11 para 64]. This information must be easily accessible, the controller must ‘take active steps to furnish the information to the data subject or to actively direct the data subject to the location of it’ (e.g., via a direct link or a QR code) (Article 12.1 GDPR; Recital 39 GDPR) [7] [14 para 33]. Moreover, the language used must be clear and easy to understand for the average person [14 para 9].

The data controller is free to determine how the information is provided. Written communication is not mandatory; oral statements may suffice (see Explicit consent). However, relying only on verbal communication may increase the risk of obtaining invalid consent. Since the GDPR imposes minimum transparency requirements, all required information elements must be included to ensure valid consent. That set of minimum information is relatively extensive. A verbal statement will require the healthcare student to take the necessary time and effort to inform the patient. Inaccuracies or omissions or summaries of the minimum information may lead to a subsequent consent not being ‘informed’, and therefore invalid as a legal basis for the processing of the patient’s personal data. In addition, lengthy verbal information could overwhelm the patient and may not be sufficiently clear and understandable (Article 12 GDPR) [7]. Furthermore, the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the consent given was informed, which is more difficult when the information is given orally.

D. The consent must be unambiguous

Consent must be given through a clear affirmative act, such as a verbal statement indicating that the data subject consents to the processing of their personal data or ticking a box on a written form (Recital 32 GDPR) [7]. Silence or inactivity do not lead to unambiguous consent (Recital 32 GDPR) [7]. The verbal consent of the patient in the ‘consultation’ setting may meet the requirement of unambiguity as long as the patient actively does or says something to indicate their agreement. For this purpose, the patient must explicitly consent to the processing of their personal data, including health data (see Explicit consent), within the healthcare student’s ePortfolio for training purposes. Phrases such as ‘You will let me know if you do not agree’, which interpret silence as consent, cannot lead to a consent that can serve as a ground for processing.

E. The consent must be obtained before processing

The controller must obtain consent before starting the processing for which that consent must serve as a legal basis [11 para 90]. For specific scenarios, such as the one when a consultation between a student and a patient is video-recorded and uploaded to the ePortfolio, allowing the mentor to give feedback on the way in which the consult was conducted, this might pose problems. When recording the information disclosure and the consent given by the patient, this entails that processing of personal data is already started before consent is given. Hence, consent is not provided prior to the start of the processing activity, specifically the recording. A practical solution to this problem could be to legally and technically distinguish the purpose for processing when the recording is started from the purpose for the continuation of the recording. The processing for the first purpose (which aims at verifying whether the data subject wishes to give consent), would then not be based on the recorded consent, but on another legal basis from Article 6.1 GDPR [7] (infra). Should consent be given, processing for the second purpose (recording the consultation for integration into the ePortfolio) could then rely on the recorded consent as its legal basis.

Sometimes, however, the fact that a person consults a particular healthcare provider is in itself personal data about the person’s health. According to the UK Information Commisioner’s Office (ICO), an individual appointment with a general practitioner, such as for a check-up or routine screening, does not necessarily provide information about a person’s health. However, health data can ‘reasonably be inferred from a person’s list of appointments with an osteopath or from an invoice detailing multiple physiotherapy sessions’ [15]. In these cases, the principle of prohibition of processing sensitive data of Article 9.1 GDPR [7] (infra) applies. Only in the limited circumstances described in Article 9.2 of the GDPR [7] can these sensitive data still be processed (see research question 3).

The advantages of starting the recording when the patient enters the consultation room, for legal purposes of demonstrability or proof, must be weighed against the data subject’s interests. This balancing exercise should consider other options. For example, the provision of information and consent might take place electronically (rather than verbally) before the consultation begins, such as when the patient books an appointment online via the website or through a tablet provided in the waiting room.

F. Further difficulties with consent as legal basis

It is possible that the patient may be legally incompetent to give consent, which can apply to both minors and adults. In such cases, the consent of the parents, caregivers or representatives will need to be obtained. Moreover, the vulnerabilities of specific groups of data subjects should also be taken into account from a data protection perspective. The GDPR provides specific protection for children, ‘as they may be less aware of the risks, consequences and safeguards concerned and their rights in relation to the processing of personal data’ (Recital 38 GDPR) [7]. For adults who are not competent to give consent, the GDPR contains less specific provisions, although Recital 75 GDPR [7] states that the processing of ‘personal data of vulnerable natural persons, in particular of children’ may constitute a circumstance that could involve an increased risk. Yet, from an educational perspective, it is equally important to provide sufficient learning and feedback opportunities for these patient groups during training. Therefore, it is essential to consider whether, and under what conditions, they (or their representative) can give consent.

A final complexity is that consent can be withdrawn at any time, and individuals always have the right to do so. Furthermore, withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it (Article 7.3 GDPR) [7]. This must be carefully considered when determining which lawful ground can be relied upon.

G. Practical considerations related to consent and ePortfolios

Given the strict interpretation of the requirements for consent to be considered valid, consent may not be the appropriate legal basis for certain processing activities within the context of an ePortfolio. If data controllers still wish to rely on consent, they must ensure it is legally valid. For patients, several steps can be taken to inform them prior to the consultation. Assuming each workplace has an online presence, the required information can be made available on the website (separately from other information). However, simply making the information accessible online on the website is unlikely to be sufficient. If there is online communication with the patient prior to the consultation, such as to confirm an appointment, a link to the information can also be included. Moreover, a clear (and preferably visually attractive) information statement could be displayed in the waiting room or at the reception, possibly accompanied by a printed version that summarises the most important information and is available for the patient to take with them. However, even if the consent could be considered to be ‘informed’, there might still be a risk that the consent is not ‘freely given’. To remedy this, the healthcare provider should always clearly indicate that this is a neutral and fully optional request, and that the refusal will not have any adverse effect on the provision of care to the person concerned. It remains debatable, however, whether reliance on consent can be accepted as a legal basis when it comes to patients who: (i) are not in good health, (ii) belong to an economically or socially disadvantaged group or (iii) are in a situation of institutional or hierarchical dependency [16 para 20].

Contract

Principle

It is lawful to process personal data when necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party (Article 6.1, b) GDPR) [7]. This legal ground reflects that, in some cases, contractual obligations towards a data subject cannot be fulfilled without the data subject providing certain personal data. The concept of ‘necessity’ means that the processing must be essential for the performance of the contract and, hence, that the purpose of the contract cannot be achieved without the specific processing. Therefore, the assessment is not about what the contract permits or what the contractual provisions include, but rather a reflection of its purpose [12 para 23].

Contract in the context of ePortfolios

This legal ground is only applicable insofar as there is a contract with the data subject. Both the learning agreement between the educational institution and the student, as well the internship agreement between the educational institution, the student and the workplace could in this context be relevant. Such agreements (usually) also cover the supervision and assessment of the student, which in turn requires the use of an ePortfolio for reporting. Consequently, it can be argued that the processing of the student’s personal data in the ePortfolio for educational purposes is necessary for the performance of the contract between the educational institution (and workplace) and the student, in which the former undertakes to provide the student with qualitative education. The same could be argued about the processing of the supervisors’ personal data and their contract with their employer (the educational institution or the workplace). However, since a contract can only be used as a legal ground for processing the personal data of contracting parties, the data of the patient cannot be processed on this basis. Even if there is a contract between the workplace and the patient, this is aimed at the provision of care, and not at training, which means that, with regard to personal data about the patient, the agreement cannot be invoked as a legal basis for training purposes.

In conclusion, the data controller may rely on contract as a legal ground to the extent that it has concluded an agreement with the data subject regarding the training purposes as referred to in the context of the ePortfolio. However, given that the ePortfolio may process personal data relating to various data subjects, including those with whom the educational institution has not entered into an agreement, this legal ground provides only a partial answer for specific groups of data subjects.

Public interest

Principle

Processing may also be lawful if it is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller (Article 6.1, e) GDPR) [7]. To use this legal ground, the public interest task must have been entrusted to the controller (i) by a Union or Member State provision (Article 6.3 GDPR) [7]. In addition, it is required that (ii) the purpose of the processing is laid down in that legal basis or is necessary for the fulfilment of a task carried out in the public interest or for the exercise of official authority vested in the controller (Article 6.3 GDPR) [7]. Union or national law must (iii) ‘meet an objective of public interest’ and (iv) ‘be proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued’ (Article 6.3 GDPR) [7]. Whereas there is much literature on the meaning of the notion ‘public interest’ in the area of research [17], there is much less clarity on how it plays a role in the provision and organisation of education.

Public interest in the context of ePortfolios

In the context of an ePortfolio, the question arises whether educational institutions are assigned a public interest task by law as a legal basis for processing personal data?

First, there must be a legal provision. The EU legal framework on the recognition of professional qualifications provides at least a set of standards that healthcare providers must meet in order to be recognised and gain access to the profession [18]. This forms the framework for a specific healthcare training, of which the successful completion of an internship is an integral part. To pass a training course, it is inevitable that an assessment of the competence and acquired skills of the healthcare student takes place. However, this European legal framework is only a Directive, this means that it needs to be verified whether it has been transposed into national law. This European legislative framework together with the national transposition could be argued to serve as a legal provision from which the educational institutions can derive a public interest task assigned to them. Furthermore, there might be national legislation which establishes the regulatory framework for educational institutions, such as universities and higher education institutions. Such legislation as well might provide a legal provision, as long as it assigns a task of public interest to the data controller. This task of public interest might be the provision of higher education, in the ‘interest of society’ which is the exact phrasing of Article II.18. of the Codex Higher Education of the Flemish Community (Belgium). Finally, personal data may only be processed lawfully on the basis of the public interest if the purpose and the processing are necessary for the fulfilment of the public interest task (Article 6.1, e) GDPR) [7]. This necessity test, according to ICO [19] means that the processing ‘must be a targeted and proportionate way of achieving your purpose’. Furthermore, this legal basis cannot be used if reasonable and less far-reaching measures exist to achieve the same purpose [19]. The objective of processing personal data within an ePortfolio (covering both data of students, supervisors and/or patients) could be argued to be necessary for the fulfilment of the public interest task, as it aims to improve the quality of healthcare education, and in particular to achieve an increased learning effect and a more qualitative assessment of competencies of the healthcare student [1].

Legitimate interest

Principle

A final lawful ground that might be relevant is the legitimate interest ground. Data processing is lawful if it is ‘necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party’ (Article 6.1, f) GDPR) [7]. A legitimate interest may be legal (e.g. use in legal claims), economic (e.g. direct marketing) or intangible (e.g. exercise of the right to freedom of expression) [20, 21]. It may be a legitimate interest of the controller or of a third party [21 para 19–20].

To be able to rely on this ground, the data processing must be necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interest. This ‘necessity test’ has to check whether the measure is (i) appropriate to achieve the desired goal, (ii) sufficient and (iii) necessary for the intended purposes. If less far-reaching measures are available (in terms of the rights and freedoms of the data subjects) that also allow the purpose to be achieved, the measure cannot be considered ‘necessary’ [21 para 19].

Moreover, legitimate interest cannot be used ‘where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject’ (Article 6.1, f) GDPR) [7]. This means that a balancing of interests must take place to determine whether the legitimate interest can be used as a legal basis [21 para 41 et seq.]. In doing so, the controller is obliged to assess in advance (i) the controller’s legitimate interests and (ii) the impact on the rights of the data subject, taking into account their reasonable expectations [21 para 41 et seq.]. This assessment should be made on a case-by-case basis and be carried out with care [21 para 41 et seq.].

Legitimate interest in the context of ePortfolios

It is not possible for public authorities to base their processing on Article 6.1, f) GDPR [7] if the processing is carried out ‘in the performance of their tasks’ (Article 6.1 sentence 2 GDPR) [7]. Thus, in the context of ePortfolios, the relevant question is whether the data controller (most likely the educational institution) qualifies as a public authority under national law. Yet, public authorities are not always completely excluded from the use of this legal basis. Since the entry into force of the GDPR, several supervisory authorities have found that public authorities in certain cases may still use legitimate interest as a legal basis (Article 4.21 and 51 GDPR) [7]. For example, according to the Belgian Data Protection Authority, public authorities are not excluded from the legal basis in Article 6.1, f) GDPR [7] when they perform ‘purely’ commercial services and thus do not carry out their public service duties [22]. If the controller (in casu an educational institution, supra) is considered a public authority under national law, and if the processing of personal data in an ePortfolio is undertaken in the course of its duties (such as the provision of qualitative education), the controller will not be able to rely on legitimate interest as legal ground (but will instead be able to rely on public interest as a lawful ground, if the conditions explained above are fulfilled).

Research question 3: processing of health data

The third research question ‘Which derogation under Article 9 of the GDPR [7] can justify the processing of health data?’ will be answered in two sections: (1) what are the general principles regarding sensitive types of data in Article 9, and (2) how can it be applied in the context of ePortfolios.

General principles on the processing of sensitive data in Article 9 GDPR

In the context of using ePortfolios for healthcare education, it is likely that health data – which is a very broad notion [23 para. 50], defined as any data related to the health status of a data subject, revealing information about their past, current or future physical or mental health status – will be processed (Recital 35 GDPR; Article 4.15 GDPR) [7]. This may occur, for example, when complex patient cases handled by the student are described in order for mentors to provide feedback or when consultations are recorded. Even if directly identifying data (such as names or birth dates) are not included, information about the case and context could still lead to the identification of the patient. For example, cases that are interesting due to the rarity of a particular disease might make the patient identifiable based on that alone.

The processing of health data – as a special category of data, identified in Article 9 GDPR [7] – is prohibited in principle. The rationale is that personal data which are, by their nature, particularly sensitive and closely related to fundamental rights and freedoms merit specific protection (Recital 51 GDPR) [7]. The processing of health data may give rise to risks in relation to an individual’s right to privacy or the right to non-discrimination and may affect them in different spheres of their lives (personal, professional and social). Article 9, however, provides a number of derogations to this prohibition – i.e. situations in which the processing will be allowed. Articles 6 and 9 GDPR [7] must be read together thus should not be considered as alternative conditions. Hence, both a lawful ground (Article 6) and a derogation (Article 9) must be applicable in order to process health data in an ePortfolio [24].

Derogation for the processing of health data in the context of ePortfolios

Two derogations included in Article 9 GDPR [7] are relevant in the ePortfolio context: the derogations based on explicit consent (Article 9.2, a) GDPR) [7] and a substantial public interest (Article 9.2, g) GDPR) [7].3

Explicit consent

‘Explicit consent is required where a high level of individual control over personal data is deemed appropriate’ [11 para 91]. Explicit consent should comply with all criteria that must be fulfilled to obtain valid ‘regular’ consent. This means it should be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous (supra).

The main difference between consent and explicit consent is in the form or method in which consent is expressed by the data subject. Explicit consent requires an ‘explicit statement of consent,’ i.e. a written and/or signed statement that explicitly confirms the consent (Article 9.2, a) GDPR) [7]. Theoretically, other ways remain possible, such as an oral statement. This is possible, ‘provided that the information about the choice is fair, intelligible and clear, and it asks for a specific confirmation from the data subject’ [11 para 95]. In the digital or online context, a data subject may be able to issue the required statement by for example filling in an electronic form or by using an electronic signature. Again, mention must be made of the fact that (i) the conditions for a valid consent must be strictly interpreted, (ii) there might be a risk of an unequal relationship between the controller and the patient, and (iii) consent can be withdrawn by the data subject, which may lead to unpredictability and the sudden cessation of the processing of the data. All this means that, as was argued above, explicit consent may not be the appropriate derogation for processing sensitive personal data within an ePortfolio.

Substantial public interest

Processing on this ground is possible only (i) if provided by a Union law or Member State provision which clearly defines the purposes and grounds for processing special categories of personal data – and which must reflect a substantial public interest (Article 9.2, g) GDPR) [7]. Additionally, the processing of special categories of personal data for reasons of substantial public interest must (ii) be proportionate to the purpose pursued and (iii) respect the essence of the right to data protection. Finally, (iv) appropriate and specific measures must also be taken to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject (Article 9.2, g) GDPR) [7].

The public interest must be ‘substantial’ in nature. This means that there must be a balancing act between the public interest and the risks for the data subjects. The threshold for meeting this criterion is therefore high [25]. In the context of an ePortfolio focusing on healthcare education, it could be argued that the task of substantial public interest is providing quality education to the healthcare students in particular, and increasing the quality of care in general. After all, the need for a high standard of quality training in healthcare is particularly important. Ensuring adequate supervision and assessment of the student is not only in the interest of the student and the protection of the physical integrity of individual patients with whom the student comes into contact during the internship, but also in the interest of public health in general. Qualitative training with attention to practical experience is the foundation for ensuring safe, patient-oriented and effective care. Hence, it could be argued that the direct impact of the quality of healthcare education on the well-being of society and the physical integrity of individual patients justifies the need for processing health data in the ePortfolio. In any case, as required by Article 9.2, g) GDPR [7], the processing of personal data should be limited to what is proportionate and appropriate and specific measures must also be taken to ensure protection of the patient’s interests. This will include requirements regarding the confidentiality of health data and the duty of professional secrecy incumbent on the healthcare provider/student, access control measures and other measures such as a strict delimitation of which types of data may be included in which way.

Towards data protection compliance in ePortfolios

Based on the results of the study four recommendations for data protection compliance can be formulated. These recommendations aim to reconcile healthcare education purposes with data protection requirements when using an ePortfolio to scaffold workplace learning in healthcare education.

Internship agreements to clarify the data protection responsibilities of the different actors

It is recommended to assign the responsibilities and duties of the actors under data protection law by means of a contract or agreement before an internship begins. The internship agreement, which is usually drawn up by the educational institution and is signed by the healthcare student, the educational institution and the workplace, can be the appropriate document for this purpose.

‘(Substantial) public interest’ as the most appropriate legal bases for processing of health data in the context of education

To choose a particular legal basis is challenging, as it must align with the purpose and essence of the data processing, and the options may vary depending on the nature of the party acting as the data controller (public authority or not) and the specific type of processing activity. Whereas the processing of personal data of students and supervisors could be based either on the lawful grounds of contract or public interest, the lawful ground for processing health data of patients is less obvious. Based on the analysis, it may be argued that the most appropriate lawful ground for educational institutions to process personal data of patients is ‘public interest’, and the most suitable derogation ground is ’substantial public interest’.

Adequate and appropriate measures to protect the fundamental freedoms and rights of the data subjects

In any case, it is particularly important to devote sufficient attention, time and resources to the provision of adequate and appropriate organisational and technical measures to protect the fundamental freedoms and rights of the data subjects [Article 9.2, g) GDPR; Article 24 GDPR [7]. Such measures may include: (1) training and awareness-raising for students and supervisors on what constitutes personal data and how to minimize the integration of patients’ personal data in the ePortfolio, (2) access control measures to ensure that only authorized individuals can access certain data (e.g., recordings of consultations), and (3) the involvement of the Data Protection Officer at the relevant institutions to provide guidance on the decisions that need to be made and the measures to be implemented.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify and document the EU data protection requirements for ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education. Three research questions about the collection and processing of personal data in ePortfolios were addressed by qualifying the relevant actors, describing the legal grounds and contextualizing them in relation to ePortfolios. This study was conducted as part of an interdisciplinary research project ‘supporting workplace learning in healthcare through ePortfolios’ (www.sbo-scaffold.com). The project consisted of four generic and three context-specific work packages. One of the generic work packages was ‘Policy, Legislation and Ethics’, in which this study was embedded. The challenge for the lawyers, as identified in the literature [2], was to translate the legal grounds into actionable solutions so that a legal compliant prototype was developed.

Four recommendations for data protection compliance were formulated in the results section. In this discussion, we will further elaborate on them, drawing on the literature and an unpublished report [26].

Internship agreements to clarify the data protection responsibilities of the different actors.

Internship agreements to clarify the data protection responsibilities of the different actors.

Data protection law imposes obligations on certain actors (specifically ‘data controllers’ and ‘data processors’) and grants rights to others (specifically ‘data subjects’) in the context of processing personal data (see Table 1). It is crucial to properly identify and classify all actors before implementing the ePortfolio. The Data Protection Officer (DPO) of the educational institution (usually the ‘data controller’) has an important role to play. An agreement between the educational institution, workplace, and healthcare provider in training (student/resident) can explicitly define the responsibilities and rights of the actors.

2.(Substantial) public interest as the most appropriate legal bases for processing health data in the context of education.

(Substantial) public interest as the most appropriate legal bases for processing health data in the context of education.

Whereas (explicit) consent could – in specific circumstances – a possible legal ground for data processing of personal data of patients, it presents challenges in the healthcare education context. Such challenges include the difficulty of obtaining and renewing consent regularly, power imbalances – hindering freely given consent –, and potential consent fatigue. In contrast, (substantial) public interest may offer a stronger justification, aligning better with the goals of healthcare education and the development of competent professionals. Educational institutions, universities and research organisations which qualify as public bodies, increasingly rely on this legal basis for processing personal data. The challenge related to this ‘public interest’ basis is to provide an evidence-based justification that the use of an ePortfolio is ‘necessary’ for delivering quality healthcare education, as alternatives (e.g., paper-based portfolios) do not sufficiently meet the standards of high-quality education. An ePortfolio contributes to the support of self-directed learning, supervision and assessment, which is central to qualitative training [1, 27–33]. This requires practical experience in which the supervisors can provide adequate feedback to the student by observing and monitoring them during the course of the internship. In reality, however, it is neither feasible nor desirable for practice supervisors to be present at all times during a student’s interactions with patients. In such cases, recording consultations and uploading them instead could provide valuable opportunities for feedback [34]. Moreover, scientific literature indicates that ePortfolios are more effective than their paper counterparts for several reasons [1, 27]. Among other things, ePortfolios aim to reduce the high workload associated with paper-based versions, which can be a major obstacle to the success of the portfolio [1].

3.Adequate and appropriate measures to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the data subjects.

Adequate and appropriate measures to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the data subjects.

When relying on substantial public interest, measures must be put in place to safeguard the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject (Article 9.2.g) GDPR) [7]. More in general, data controllers must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure compliance with the GDPR (Article 24 GDPR) [7]. Such measures include training, access control measures, the appointment of a DPO, and the design with a multidisciplinary team.

First, training and awareness-raising for all ePortfolio users on the importance of protecting (sensitive) personal data (such as health data) and confidential information are crucial [2]. It is also necessary to train users how to minimize the integration of patient/client personal data into the ePortfolio. Legal aspects of the ePortfolio should be covered in training before starting the use of the ePortfolio. Additionally, clear information on these topics should be integrated interactively into the ePortfolio (e.g., video, icons, pop-up windows, and an accessible, easy-to-find help page).

Second, access control measures to ensure that only authorized individuals can access certain data (e.g., recordings of consultations). The inflow and outflow of sensitive and confidential information in the ePortfolio should be maximally limited without compromising the effective support and quality of education on the healthare provider [2]. Furthermore, careful consideration of access rights to sensitive and confidential information is essential so that, depending on the specific role of a particular actor, they only get access to the information on a need-to-know basis. Reflecting on access rights and other organisations and technical measures (e.g., authentication and logging policy, encryption, tagging of personal data and information covered by professional secrecy, automatic blurring of faces) should be part of the design process.

Third, the DPO at the relevant institutions should be involved to provide guidance on the decisions that need to be made and the measures to be implemented.

A final key message is that a close collaboration between ePortfolio developers, legal experts, DPOs of the involved organisations, and all users in the design and implementation of the ePortfolio is required to create a useful tool that complies with the legal framework [2, 26].

A key strength of this study is that it is based on the expertise of legal professionals who continuously sought solutions during the development of an ePortfolio prototype, translating European GDPR regulations [7] into a legally acceptable framework for data protection compliance. Given the limited literature on this topic in healthcare education, this study offers valuable insights. However, a limitation is that the topic of ‘professional secrecy’ is not addressed in this study, as it is regulated at the national level and falls outside the scope of this research. In the context of the SCAFFOLD project, an analysis of the Belgian legal framework regarding professional secrecy in the context of ePortfolios was conducted. More information (in Dutch) is available at https://www.sbo-scaffold.com/sites/default/files/2022-05/4._beroepsgeheim_executive_summary_1.pdf.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the limited literature on the legal aspects of digital technologies, such as ePortfolios, in healthcare education. ePortfolios are an essential component of healthcare education, in order to provide students with individual feedback to improve their competencies and hence to increase the overall quality of healthcare. In the course of using ePortfolios, it is inevitable that personal data of a range of data subjects is collected and processed. This is why it is essential that the relevant actors are qualified and that those actors can rely on an appropriate lawful ground and derogation to process (health) data. Based on a desk research, conducted during a multidisciplinary ePortfolio research project, researchers identified and documented the EU data protection requirements for ePortfolios in clinical healthcare education. Several recommendations were formulated to support interested bodies in developing legal compliant ePortfolios for (continuing) healthcare education in Europe. First, it is recommended to assign the responsibilities and duties of the actors under data protection law by means of an agreement before an internship begins. Second, we have argued that the most appropriate legal bases for educational institutions to process health data is ‘public interest’; ‘substantial public interest’ could be a suitable derogation in the context of Article 9.2 GDPR [7]. Finally, it is essential to provide adequate and appropriate measures to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the data subjects, such as training, access control measures, a DPO, and a multidisciplinary ePortfolio design team.
