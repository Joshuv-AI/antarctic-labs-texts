Semi‐Mechanistic Modeling of S‐531011, a Humanized Anti‐CCR8 Monoclonal Antibody, for Prediction of CCR8 Receptor Occupancy in Human Tumor Tissues
Authors: Daichi Yamaguchi, Wataru Nogami, Yudai Sonoda, Hitomi Morihara, Takayuki Katsube
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSemi‐mechanistic modeling approaches could be useful for optimal dose selection, and one of the approaches is pharmacokinetic (PK)/receptor occupancy (RO) model for monoclonal antibodies. This study aimed to develop a semi‐mechanistic PK/RO model of S‐531011, a humanized monoclonal antibody against human CCR8 and under development for the treatment of solid tumors, in order to predict human RO values in tumor tissues which are not available in the ongoing clinical study. The model consists of three compartments (central, peripheral, and tumor) and includes four compartments (S‐531011, CCR8, and complexes 1/2 within each compartment). The model parameters were set using available clinical data, non‐clinical data, and physiological information. The human PK/RO model was developed by refinement of a mouse PK/RO model. The time courses of RO values in the tumor compartment were simulated using the model, together with a conservative condition of slow transfer of S‐531011 from blood to the tumor. It was predicted that the RO value in the tumor compartment at 21 days (trough after the third dose after administration of S‐531011 80 mg) would be maintained at > 90% under typical model assumptions. Under the conservative condition, the RO value in the tumor after administration of S‐531011 800 mg would remain at > 90%. The developed semi‐mechanistic PK/RO model could predict human RO values of S‐531011 in tumor tissues. Under typical model assumptions, simulations suggested the dose range of 80–800 mg of S‐531011 every 3 weeks would achieve > 90% RO in tumor tissues.Keywords: C‐C motif chemokine receptor 8, modeling and simulation, population pharmacokinetic pharmacodynamic analysis, quantitative systems pharmacology, S‐531011

Introduction

C‐C motif chemokine receptor 8 (CCR8) is distinctively overexpressed in tumor‐infiltrating regulatory T cells (Tregs) across various types of cancer, inhibiting the anti‐tumor immune response [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Studies have indicated that poor prognosis in certain cancer types in humans is correlated with the presence of tumor‐infiltrating Tregs that exhibit high CCR8 expression [3, 6]. Thus, enhancing the recovery of anti‐tumor immunity through the selective depletion of CCR8‐positive Tregs could potentially exert anti‐tumor effects in cancer patients. S‐531011 is a humanized monoclonal antibody that works against human CCR8 with antibody‐dependent cell‐mediated cytotoxicity against tumor‐infiltrating CCR8‐positive Tregs [1]. An ongoing first‐in‐human Phase 1b/2 study (aCCeleR8‐001, NCT05101070) has been assessing the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and preliminary anti‐tumor activity of S‐531011 as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with various types of advanced solid tumors [7].

Historically, identification of the maximum‐tolerated dose (MTD) was a primary aim of first‐in‐human oncology studies. However, for monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), the recommended Phase 2 dose (RP2D) is increasingly selected based on an integrated assessment of safety, PK, PD, and target engagement, and biological activity rather than MTD alone [8, 9, 10]. Therefore, quantitative evaluation of receptor occupancy (RO) may provide useful information for dose optimization [11, 12]. It has been reported that RP2D based solely on MTD may be unnecessarily high for achieving adequate receptor occupancy with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapies [13]. However, observed RO values of S‐531011 in human tumor tissues are not available in the current Phase 1b/2 study.

Modeling and simulation have been used for evaluating and estimating human PK and PD data, as well as enhancing efficiency and effectiveness in drug discovery and development, including RP2D considerations in oncology [14]. Translational PK/PD models, which assemble a quantitative framework describing the relationship between the pharmacology of drug action and downstream biomarker or efficacy responses, including PK and physiological data relevant to humans, could predict the effects of new drugs in humans [14, 15]. As one of the modeling and simulation approaches in oncology, PK/RO models have been used for drug development and could be useful for establishing effective doses and regimens to enhance the chances of success when developing new drugs [16].

In this study, we tried to predict human RO values of S‐531011 in tumor tissues. Initially, a semi‐mechanistic PK/RO model of S‐531011 for humans was developed using available clinical PK data for S‐531011, non‐clinical data, and physiological information. A mouse PK/RO model was then developed to evaluate and refine the human PK/RO model. Subsequently, the RO values in human tumor tissues were simulated using the human PK/RO model.

Methods

Non‐clinical Data

The followings were measured in non‐clinical studies: the binding affinity (Kd) of S‐531011 against human CCR8‐expressing cells, the association rate constant (kon) of S‐531011 to human CCR8, and the degradation rate constant (kdeg) of cell surface S‐531011 to calculate the half‐life of complex (Thalf,CPX) value. Data from non‐clinical PK studies were used to calculate the concentration ratio of S‐531011 in tumor to serum and the PK parameters of the two‐compartment model with inter‐compartmental transfer rate constants between the central and peripheral compartments (k12, k21), first‐order elimination rate constant (kel), volume of distribution in the central compartment (Vcen), and volume of distribution in the peripheral compartment (Vper). The mouse RO values in the blood and the tumor after a single intravenous administration of S‐531011 were evaluated. Details of these non‐clinical studies are presented in the supplemental material.

Clinical Data

Clinical Study

The first‐in‐human Phase 1b/2 study of S‐531011 consists of four parts: dose escalation as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab (Parts A‐1 and A‐2), dose expansion as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab (Parts B and C) [7]. Eligible participants included those who were 18 years or older with a histologically or cytologically confirmed diagnosis of locally advanced (locoregional recurrence not suitable for curative treatment) or metastatic solid tumors. These patients either had no access to standard therapies with established clinical benefit, were intolerant of these treatments, or had declined them. They exhibited an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1. Informed consent, either in written or electronic form, was acquired from all participants. In Part A‐1 (40 participants in total), S‐531011 was given intravenously every 3 weeks (Q3W) at escalating doses of 8, 24, 80, 240, 800, and 1600 mg. The first dose (Cycle 1) was infused over 2 h, while subsequent doses (Cycle 2 or later) were infused over 1 h. Blood samples for the measurement of serum S‐531011 concentrations were collected on days 1 (pre‐infusion, end‐of‐infusion, 1 and 3 h after end‐of‐infusion), 2, 3, 4, 8, 15, and 21 of Cycle 1 and pre‐infusion of Cycle 2.

PK of S‐531011 in Humans

The PK parameters of S‐531011 in humans were estimated using serum S‐531011 concentration data from three participants in the 24 mg group in Part A‐1 that were available when this analysis was conducted. The mean serum concentrations were fitted to the two‐compartment model, and unweighted least squares regression analysis was performed to estimate the following PK parameters: k12, k21, kel, Vcen, Vper.

PK/RO Model Building

The model structure of the PK/RO model for S‐531011 was developed based on a previous study [16]; the scheme is illustrated in Figure 1. The model consists of three compartments: the central compartment, which comprises the serum and rapidly perfused tissues; the peripheral compartment, which comprises slowly perfused tissues except tumor tissues; and the tumor compartment, which comprises the tumor tissues. Furthermore, four compartments (S‐531011, CCR8, complex 1, and complex 2) are included in each compartment. S‐531011 is administered intravenously to the central compartment and is distributed to the peripheral and tumor compartments at the inter compartmental transfer rate constants (k12, k21, k13, and k31). S‐531011 is eliminated from only the central compartment at kel. We assume a two‐site sequential model where S‐531011 has two binding sites to CCR8. S‐531011 combines with CCR8 reversibly to become complex 1 (S‐531011: CCR8 = 1: 1), and complex 1 then combines with CCR8 reversibly to become complex 2 (S‐531011: CCR8 = 1: 2) in the central, peripheral, and tumor compartments. It is assumed that kon and the dissociation rate constant (koff) are the same for complex 1 and complex 2. CCR8 is an endogenous substance that is assumed to be synthesized at a synthesis rate constant (ksyn) and degraded by anabolism at kdeg in the central, peripheral, and tumor compartments. Complex 1 and complex 2 are also assumed to be degraded by anabolism at kdeg. These model equations are shown in Table S1.

PK/RO Model Parameters for Humans

The equations for calculating constants of PK/RO model parameters for humans are shown in Table S2. The volume of distribution in the tumor compartment (Vtum) was assumed to be a typical value [16]. The values of k13 and k31 were calculated using a reported method with Vcen, Vtum, the half‐time of drug perfusion from the central to the tumor compartment (Tdist), a perfusion coefficient of the drug from the central to the tumor compartment (Pdist), and a fraction of the interstitial space volume in the tumor (fint) [16]. It was assumed that Tdist was the same as the half time of drug perfusion from the central to the peripheral compartment, which is ln(2)/k12. The concentration ratio of S‐531011 in tumor to serum from non‐clinical study was assumed to be Pdist. It was assumed that S‐531011 could combine with CCR8 in the interstitial space of the tumor, and the value of fint (0.55) for mice was reported [17]; it was assumed that fint was the same for mice and humans. The value of koff was calculated using kon and Kd which were obtained from non‐clinical studies. It was assumed that CCR8 was expressed in Tregs, conventional T cells (Tconv), and CD8 T cells in the central, peripheral, and tumor compartments. Therefore the amount of CCR8 at a steady state was calculated using the expression level of CCR8 in these cells [18, 19] and the number of these cells in the central, peripheral, and tumor compartments [3, 20, 21, 22]. In order to adjust the units, the Avogadro constant (NA, 6.022 × 1023), a fraction of the unit (funit), and a fraction of the volume (fvol) were considered. The value of funit was calculated using the approximate formula [22] for calculating the volume (mm3) of the tumor tissue assuming a square of tumor tissue with one side of 1 mm (area = 1 mm2). The difference in units with respect to the volume (μL and mm3) in the peripheral and tumor compartments was adjusted using fvol which was assumed to be 1 μL/mm3. The expression levels of CCR8 per cell in the central, peripheral, and tumor compartments were obtained using human samples [18, 19]. The number of CD4 and CD8 T cells in the central compartment (nCD4,cen and nCD8,cen) and the cumulative frequency of Treg among CD4 T cells in the central compartment (fTreg,CD4,cen) were obtained from the literature [3, 20]. The numbers of Treg and Tconv in the central compartment (nTreg,cen and nTconv,cen) were calculated using these values. The number of Treg in the tumor compartment (nTreg,tum) and the cumulative frequency of Treg among CD4 T cells in tumor compartment (fTreg,CD4,tum) were obtained from the literature [3, 21], and the number of Tconv in the tumor compartment (nTconv,tum) was calculated. The ratio of the number of CD4 T cells to that of CD8 T cells in the tumor compartment (rCD4,CD8,tum) was obtained from the literature [3] and the number of CD8 T cells in the tumor compartment (nCD8,tum) was calculated. The numbers of Treg, Tconv, and CD8 T cells in peripheral compartment (nTreg,per, nTconv,per, and nCD8,per) were assumed to be the same as those in the tumor compartment because these parameters for the peripheral compartment were not available. It was assumed that the values of kdeg for CCR8, complex 1, and complex 2 in the central, peripheral, and tumor compartments were the same. Since the amount of CCR8 was assumed to be in a steady state, ksyn in each compartment (ksyn,cen, ksyn,per, and ksyn,tum) was calculated.

PK/RO Model Parameters for Mice

The equations for calculating constants of PK/RO model parameters for mice are shown in Table S3. The baseline tumor volume in mice was obtained from the literature [23] and the value was used for Vtum in mice with fvol which was assumed to be the same as the value for humans. The values of k13 and k31 in mice were calculated using the same formula as for humans, and the values used in the parameters (Tdist, Pdist, and fint) were assumed to be the same as those for humans. The values of kon and koff in mice were assumed the same as those in humans. There was no information about the expression level of CCR8 in CD8 T cells in mice; therefore, it was assumed that CCR8 was expressed in only CD4 T cells. The amount of CCR8 at steady state was calculated using the expression level of CCR8 per CCR8‐positive CD4 T cells (expCD4), the rate of the number of CCR8‐positive CD4 T cells in CD4 T cell (rCCR8,CD4), and nCD4 in each compartment. It was assumed that nCD4,cen, fTreg,CD4,tum, and nTreg,tum in mice were the same as those in humans. The value of nCD4,tum was calculated using the same formula as for humans. The number of CD4 T cells in the peripheral compartment (nCD4,per) was assumed to be the same as that in the tumor compartment. The value of kdeg used for mice was the same as that for humans, and ksyn in each compartment was calculated using the formula used for humans.

Model evaluation and refinement of the PK/RO model of S‐531011 for humans were performed using the PK/RO model for mice and non‐clinical data. The simulated RO values at 24 h after administration of S‐531011 were compared with the observed values from non‐clinical study.

Second, the sensitivity analysis was performed to estimate the RO value in the tumor compartment at 168 h after S‐531011 administration (corresponding to the trough time point in non‐clinical studies [1]). In the sensitivity analysis, the values of the following tested model parameters were set from one‐hundredth to hundredfold values of the mouse PK/RO model parameters: Vcen, Vper, Vtum, kel, k12, k21, kon, Kd, Pdist, fint, Thalf,CPX, expCD4,cen, expCD4,per, and expCD4,tum. The sensitive parameters that would have an influence on the estimation of RO in the tumor compartment were investigated based on the first‐order index (for evaluation of only the main influence of each parameter without considering the interaction with others) and the total order index (for assessment of the impact of each parameter including all possible interactions with others) calculated from the sensitivity analysis.

Next, the value of the high uncertainty parameter was optimized using the observed RO values in the tumor from non‐clinical study. The optimal value of the high uncertainty parameter was selected based on visual inspection for the fit between simulated RO values in the tumor compartment using the mouse PK/RO model and observed RO data.

Finally, the optimized value of the high uncertainty parameter for the mouse PK/RO model was reflected in the human PK/RO model and this model became the final model.

Simulation

The time courses of RO values in the tumor compartment were simulated using the final human PK/RO model. The simulations were performed with 30% (coefficient of variation; CV) variabilities for kel and Vcen, and 50% variabilities for kon, Pdist, fint, Kd, Thalf,CPX, and Vtum as between‐subject variabilities for each dose regimen. The tested dose regimens of S‐531011 based on the ongoing clinical study were intravenous administration of S‐531011 at 8, 24, 80, 240, 800, and 1600 mg Q3W with 1 h infusion. Additionally, some conservative conditions were also tested focusing on Pdist. The time courses of RO values in the tumor compartment were simulated using the final PK/RO model with assumed Pdist values multiplied by 1/3 and 1/10 of the value from the final model.

Software

Human and mouse PK parameters were estimated using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.3 (Certara, NJ). For model building, simulation, sensitivity analysis, data summarization, and illustrating plots, MATLAB version 9.12 (R2022a) (The MathWorks Inc., US) with SimBiology version 6.3 were used. For construction of plots, R version 3.6 was used.

Results

Non‐clinical Data

The values of Kd, kon, Thalf,CPX, and kdeg from non‐clinical studies are shown in Table S4. The observed values of Kd, kon, and Thalf,CPX were 18.62 pmol/L, 11.85 L/nmol/h, and 1.71 h, respectively, and the calculated value of kdeg was 0.4053 1/h.

The mean concentration ratio of S‐531011 in the tumor to serum at 24 h after S‐531011 0.5 mg/kg administration to CT26. WT tumor bearing human CCR8‐KI mice was 0.61 and the value was assumed to be Pdist. The estimated PK parameters of S‐531011 in mouse using mean serum concentrations of S‐531011 at 5 mg/kg are shown in Table S5, and the predicted and observed mean serum concentration profiles of S‐531011 are illustrated in Figure S1. The predicted serum concentration profile captured the observed mean serum concentrations well.

The observed RO values in blood and tumor 24 h after the administration of S‐531011 to mouse were available, and the individual raw RO values were adjusted assuming that the mean RO value for 5 mg/kg was 100%, and adjusted values were used for the analyses.

Clinical Data

The human PK parameters of S‐531011 were estimated using mean serum concentrations of S‐531011 after a single intravenous administration of 24 mg (shown in Table S5). The predicted and observed mean serum concentration profiles of S‐531011 are illustrated in Figure S2. The predicted serum concentration profile well captured the observed mean serum concentrations.

Model Evaluation and Refinement

The simulated time courses of RO values in the central and tumor compartments after administration of S‐531011 at 0.05, 0.5, 5 mg/kg to mice are illustrated in Figure S3. The simulated values in the central and tumor compartments at 24 h after administration were more than 80% for 0.05 mg/kg and almost 100% for 0.5 and 5 mg/kg, and these values were higher than the adjusted RO values in the non‐clinical study.

Results from the sensitivity analysis for estimation of the RO value in the tumor compartment at 168 h after S‐531011 administration with the mouse PK/RO model are presented in Figure S4. The most sensitive parameter was Thalf,CPX (first and total order indices were 0.8832 and 0.9058, respectively). This parameter was calculated based on an in vitro study evaluating the internalization of S‐531011 in human CCR8‐expressing Ramos cells; therefore, the current setting for this parameter is reasonable. The second and third sensitive parameters were kel (first and total order indices were 0.02319 and 0.08374, respectively) and Vcen (first and total order indices were 0.02046 and 0.07063, respectively). These parameters were calculated using the observed mouse serum concentration data based on compartmental analysis. Therefore, the current settings for these parameters (kel and Vcen) are reasonable. The first and total order indices of Kd were −0.01998 and 0.03714, and this parameter was calculated from the in vitro study. However, it is generally known that Kd can vary easily depending on the study conditions. In addition, Kd of S‐531011 (18.62 pmol/L) was extremely low compared to other drugs [24]. Based on these results, parameter optimization was performed for Kd (the high uncertainty parameter) using the observed data.

The optimal value for Kd was suggested to be 558.6 pmol/L, which was 30 times the initial value from the in vitro study, based on the fit between the simulated RO values in the tumor compartment using the mouse PK/RO model and observed RO (adjusted) data, as shown in Figure S5. It was confirmed that the simulated RO profiles based on the refined mouse PK/RO model (using the optimized Kd value) would be similar to the adjusted observations. In addition, the simulated RO profiles in the central compartment were generally consistent with the observed RO values in the blood (Figure S6). Based on these results, the optimized Kd value (558.6 pmol/L) was applied to the human PK/RO model and the optimized value of koff was changed to 6.621 1/h. This was chosen as the final model. The model code of this final model is provided in Data S1 as model report generated by SimBiology.

Simulation

After intravenous administration of S‐531011 at 8, 24, 80, 240, 800, and 1600 mg Q3W with 1 h infusion using the final human PK/RO model, the simulated S‐531011 concentrations in the central compartment and the simulated RO time courses in the tumor compartment are shown in Figure S7 and Figure 2, respectively. It was predicted that the RO value in the tumor compartment at 1512 h (trough after the third dose) after administration of S‐531011 80 mg could be maintained at more than 90%. In addition, the simulated RO time courses in the tumor compartment with Pdist multiplied by 1/3 and 1/10 are presented in Figures S8 and S9, respectively. When Pdist was 1/3 of the final model value, the RO value in the tumor after administration of S‐531011 240 mg could be maintained at more than 90%. Additionally, when Pdist was 1/10 of the final model value, the RO value in the tumor after administration of S‐531011 800 mg could be maintained at more than 90%. The simulated RO values in the tumor compartment at predose in Cycle 2 (Q3W) using the final PK/RO model with normal and conservative (1/10) Pdist values are shown in Figure 3. More than 90% of RO in tumor tissues is likely to be maintained in the dose range of 80 to 800 mg of S‐531011 Q3W.

Discussion

We developed a semi‐mechanistic human PK/RO model of S‐531011 using available clinical PK data for S‐531011, non‐clinical data, and physiological information. This model was refined based on the comparison between the mouse PK/RO model prediction and mouse in vivo data. The RO values in tumor tissues, which were not available in the clinical study, could be simulated using the developed PK/RO model.

Many modeling approaches similar to our PK/RO model have been reported. Semi‐mechanistic PK/RO models for next‐generation anti‐NKG2A monoclonal antibody (KSQ mAb) and monalizumab (anti‐NKG‐2A monoclonal antibody) have been developed and the effects between these antibodies have been examined [16]. The target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) model of anti‐EGFR monoclonal antibody was used for the prediction of human PK and human RO from monkey data [25]. The translational PK/PD model of pembrolizumab, an anti‐PD‐1 monoclonal antibody, has been developed and tumor size dynamics could be estimated based on the RO of PD‐1 [22]. The TMDD model for anti‐CD47 monoclonal antibody has been used to establish the optimal dosage of the antibody [26]. These modeling approaches can provide quantitative understanding of relationships among dose, PK, tumor concentration, RO, and effect. These modeling studies have been performed without using observed tumor RO values in humans. Simulations from these models can be used for many purposes: determination of effective doses, optimization of first‐in‐human study design, avoiding overdosing, and understanding internal drug action. Our modeling approach can suggest therapeutic doses of S‐531011 and could provide information to avoid unnecessary dose increases beyond the maximum effect.

The values of the intercompartmental parameters between blood and tumor tissues (k13 and k31) calculated by Vcen, Vtum, Tdist, Pdist, and fint were the same as those in a previous report [16]. Antibodies can relatively easily leak into the interstitial space of tumor tissues from tumor blood vessels through gaps in vessels which are characterized by weak endothelial junctions and large fenestrations; this may depend on tumor type, vascular density, and blood flow [27, 28, 29]. The PK/RO model does not define the tumor type and it is assumed that all tumor types have the same profiles (the rate constant between blood and tumor tissues, the fraction of the interstitial space, and perfusion coefficient). The influence of these parameters used to calculate k13 and k31 in order to predict RO value in the tumor was evaluated by sensitivity analysis, but these parameters did not seem to have a significant effect. However, the inter‐compartmental parameters might have high uncertainty due to assumptions about the tumor type and the species differences between humans and mice. Therefore, the simulation of RO values was also performed under conservative conditions (1/3 and 1/10 of Pdist) and the optimal dose regimen was considered including the results from the conservative conditions.

As a result of the sensitivity analysis, Kd was identified as a sensitive parameter for the estimation for RO values in tumor tissues. The Kd value of S‐531011 from the in vitro study was extremely low compared to the other drugs [24]. This value would be dependent on the study conditions (temperature, pH, ionic strength, buffer composition, etc.) and the condition differences between in vitro and in vivo conditions would lead to uncertainties of Kd. In vitro Kd values may differ from in vivo values because the native structure of the cell membrane receptor could not be reproduced [30]. In addition, this difference could be caused by the internalization and elimination of the antibody‐target complex, target turnover, and permeability limitation [31]. In vitro Kd values would not directly reflect human PK and it is important to interpret the values in conjunction with other physiological parameters [32]. Therefore, the Kd value from an in vitro study could have high uncertainty and it would be better to optimize the Kd value based on data from an in vivo study. Additional sensitivity analyses on the model parameters which had assumptions were conducted using the mouse PK/RO model (Figure S10). The analyses demonstrated that the difference between the values of kon for complex 1 and complex 2 had minimal impact on RO values. In addition, the assumptions that the values of nCD4,cen, fTreg,CD4,tum, and nTreg,tum in mice were identical to those in humans did not have a significant impact on RO values as well. These results suggest that the model predictions are generally robust to these assumptions.

One major limitation of our modeling is that the observed RO values of S‐531011 in blood and tumor tissues in humans were not available. We could not perform the comparison between the simulated RO values and the true values. Therefore, a mouse PK/RO model was developed and refinement of the human PK/RO model was performed based on the observed and simulated RO values in mice. The initial parameter set for humans (without optimization of the Kd value) might result in over‐prediction. As described above, the RO simulations in humans were also conducted under conservative conditions (Pdist values multiplied by 1/3 and 1/10 of the value in the final model), which included the uncertainty of the uninformative true RO values in humans. Another limitation of our modeling is that the value of Vtum was fixed as a constant value during the S‐531011 treatment. The tumor size of actual patients would fluctuate due to progression of the disease and the drug effects of S‐531011. In our model, change in Vtum could lead to changes in k13, k31, CCR8tum, and ksyn,tum, and the predicted RO values in tumor tissues might fluctuate. Although rapid changes in tumor size over a short period are rare in humans, modification of the model may be required to appropriately predict long‐term trends in RO values. There are no studies that directly evaluate the quantitative relationship between RO value and tumor size, and the relationship needs to be established. The dose‐dependent effect of CCR8 occupancy has been confirmed in the non‐clinical study (Figure S5), and the dose‐dependent effect of Treg depletion and the reduction of tumor size have been also confirmed within the same dosage range [1]. The RO values measured at Day 7 in non‐clinical study could have been influenced by reductions in target‐cell abundance and therefore may not directly reflect receptor‐binding driven occupancy. A similar phenomenon might also occur in humans. Consequently, the model predictions should be interpreted as receptor‐binding driven RO values rather than experimentally observed RO values that might additionally reflect pharmacological depletion of CCR8‐positive Tregs. By incorporating Treg dynamics as a mediator, the quantitative relationship between RO and tumor size could be established. In addition, the assumption that the values of kdeg for unbound CCR8 and complexes was the same should also be considered a limitation of this study. The kdeg value used in the model was derived from Thalf,CPX, and the value could be interpreted as the internalization rate constant of the complexes. Because no quantitative data describing the turnover rate constant of CCR8 have been reported, the kdeg value of unbound CCR8 was assumed to be identical to that of the complexes, consistent with a previously published modeling approach [16]. An additional sensitivity analysis using the mouse PK/RO model (Figure S10) indicated that differences between the kdeg value of unbound CCR8 and the complexes could significantly affect predicted tumor RO. Thus, the prediction performance of the PK/RO model may be further improved if quantitative information on the value becomes available. In addition, another sensitivity analysis using the human PK/RO model (Figure S11) suggested that the uncertainty associated with this parameter might have a larger impact on RO predictions at 80 mg Q3W than at higher dose levels; however, the predicted RO values in tumor remained generally high (> 80%) even under worst scenario regarding CCR8 turnover.

In conclusion, the human semi‐mechanistic PK/RO model of S‐531011 that we developed can predict human RO values of S‐531011 in tumor tissues. Under typical model assumptions, simulations suggested the dose range of 80 to 800 mg of S‐531011 Q3W would achieve more than 90% RO in tumor tissues.
