The impact of glenoid/humeral lateralization and distalization angles based in the pattern of reverse shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Giovanni Di Giacomo, Leonardo Moreno Rodriguez, Petar Golijanin, Andrea Vespasiani, Valerio Antonini, Matthew T Provencher, Nicola de Gasperis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe biomechanics of reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) rely on deltoid function. The center of rotation of the glenohumeral joint in RSA is more medial and distal when compared to the native joint, and proper soft-tissue tensioning is essential to achieve a stable joint with a good range of motion (ROM). The current RSA systems on the market allow for various placements of humeral and glenoid components (lateralized, medialized, distalized). No post-operative radiographic parameters currently consider the glenoid and humeral components' position separately. The aims of this study are to (1) measure glenoid and humeral component angles specific to different implants and (2) to investigate whether these angles correlate with clinical outcomes.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 74 patients who underwent reverse shoulder prosthesis between 2018 and 2022 was performed. Different subtypes of RSA prosthesis were identified (medialized glenoid/lateralized humerus, lateralized glenoid/medialized humerus, and lateralized glenoid/lateralized humerus). Angle measurements were defined to determine the lateralization and distalization of each component in true anteroposterior radiographs (Grashey view) of the shoulder. The clinical results were collected, including a Simple Shoulder est, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, and Constant score.ResultsAll angles showed excellent intra-rater and inter-rater agreement. No correlation was found between ROM and post-operative radiographic measurements, and there were no significant differences between the different RSA subtypes in clinical outcomes.ConclusionThe main objectives to be met are the post-operative scores, ROM, and patient satisfaction after RSA; however, there are still no reliable measures in post-operative radiographs that correlate with clinical outcomes. The current angles obtained on post-operative radiographs allow us to assess the prosthesis's placement degree due to each of the components but not to predict ROM or clinical outcomes.Keywords: Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Rotator cuff arthropathy, Irreparable cuff tear, Glenohumeral osteoarthritis, Functional outcomes, Shoulder angle, Lateralization, Distalization

The biomechanics behind the reverse prosthesis and how they play a fundamental role in the outcome of patients undergoing reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) are of great interest to the field.2,7 Various prostheses have been developed to achieve the best possible patient outcomes based on a patient's specific anatomy. In addition, reliable pre-operative imaging assessments have been established to better understand the placement of the prosthesis.4

The goal of RSA is to optimize soft-tissue tensioning, account for glenoid deformity, increase glenohumeral stability and deltoid lever arm, and, as a result, various implant concepts are available.5,6,18 Even though the term lateralization is commonly used during RSA placement, it is relative lateralization since the center of rotation in RSA is still medial compared to the native joint. Several prosthetic components that can lateralize the glenosphere and account for glenoid deformity are available, such as BIO-RSA (bony increase offset RSA) and the MIO-RSA (Metallic increase offset RSA). Similarly, various humeral stems and inserts are available (curved, straight, inlay, onlay, and flushlay prostheses).1,12, 13, 14, 15

With the advancement of technology, our understanding of radiographic measurement has evolved too. The lateralization shoulder angle (LSA) and the distalization shoulder angle (DSA) were described in order to find a correlation with clinical outcomes; however, these did not allow for differentiation of the percentage of distalization or lateralization attributable to the humeral and/or glenoid component separately.

Given the impossibility of knowing which was responsible for lateralization and distalization, new measures were introduced to determine what was happening in both the glenoid and humeral components. The new angles described to individualize each portion were: in the glenoid, the glenoid lateralization angle (GLA), the glenoid distalization angle (GDA), and in the humerus, the humeral lateralization angle (HLA) and the humeral distalization angle (HDA), which had a different reference point to the previously described DSA, for which a new measurement was added which was called the modified distalization shoulder angle (mDSA).21

The description of these measurements enables us to quantify the lateralization and distalization of each component. To our knowledge, only one study has analyzed these angles in the context of clinical outcome;20 however, with a single lateralizing stem. No subgroups were analyzed to determine the associations between clinical outcomes and the different possible reverse prosthesis patterns, medialized Gglenosphere (MedG), lateralized lenosphere (LatG), and/or medialized humerus (MedH), lateralized humerus (LatH).

This study aims to measure component-specific angles within these subgroups (MedG/LatH, LatG/MedH, and LatG/LatH) and assess what correlations there are with respect to their clinical outcomes. We hypothesize that thanks to these measurements, which allowed us to analyze each component individually, we were able to determine whether one configuration is superior to another based on its correlation with clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

Retrospective analysis of 74 patients who underwent RSA between 2018 and 2022 at Concordia Hospital, Rome. Different subtypes of reverse prosthesis (MedG/LatH, LatG/MedH, and LatG/LatH) were identified, and angle measurements were performed to determine the lateralization and distalization of each of the components in true anteroposterior radiographs of the shoulder according to the study done by Schippers et al.21 The clinical results were assessed based on range of motion (ROM), the Simple Shoulder Test, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE), and Constant score. This study was conducted in full compliance with ethical standards and received approval from the institutional review board. Approval was granted under the reference number CH202402. The inclusion criteria were primary RSA, availability of radiographs at more than 2 years post-operative, and minimum clinical follow-up of 2 years. Patients who underwent revision from total shoulder arthroplasty to RSA, revision of hemiarthroplasty to RSA, RSA due to proximal humerus fractures, or revision of RSA due to prior infection were excluded.

The medical records and surgical descriptions of the patients who met the inclusion criteria were reviewed. Subgroups were created according to the characteristics of the implants and grouped according to whether they medialized or lateralized the glenoid or humeral component.

All prostheses with an increased offset, either with a bone or a metal increased offset, were cataloged as LatG. At the same time, in the humerus, the anatomy of the stem was considered to determine if these were lateralized or medialized according to the axis of the stem (Ascend Flex and Perform Stems by Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA).22,24

Surgical technique

Pre-operative software planning was performed (Blueprint; Stryker, Tornier SAS). Glenoid positioning was guided by pre-operative 3D planning using the scapular axis, glenoid retroversion and the reverse shoulder angle as primary references. Components were implanted using standard instrumentation without the aid of patient-specific instrumentation. All surgeries were performed through a deltopectoral approach with a subscapularis peeling technique. Depending on the pre-operative decision, the humeral head graft was taken or not in case of performing a BIO-RSA. Depending on the intraoperative bone quality and the stem stability, the decision was made on whether to cement or not to cement the humeral component. The glenoid component was always positioned to correct the reverse shoulder angle, placing the central peg parallel to the supraspinous fossa and maintaining the anatomical retroversion. At the end of the surgical procedure, according to the characteristics of the subscapularis tendon, repair was performed with 3 transosseous holes and sutures around the stem in order to transfer the tension exerted by the repair to the stem and not to the humeral cortex.

Clinical evaluation

Age, sex, SANE, Simple Shoulder Test, and Constant score were collected. Blinded to the type of prosthesis, using a goniometer, the post-operative range of movement was evaluated in all patients in active elevation, external rotation, and internal rotation.

Radiographic evaluation

All patients underwent a true anteroposterior radiograph of the shoulder two years or more after the surgical procedure. For the measurement of the LSA and DSA, 3 anatomic landmarks were taken as reference: (1) the superior glenoid tubercle, (2) the most lateral part of the acromion, and (3 the most lateral border of the greater tuberosity.

For measuring the new angles proposed by Schippers and Boileau et al,21 the 3 previously described landmarks were taken into account together with the glenoid pivot point (the most lateral part of the glenosphere) and the most medial part of the humeral cortex at the level of the calcar.

Measurements of the angles proposed to evaluate the general lateralization and distalization of the reverse prosthesis (LSA and DSA), those proposed to individualize the lateralization and distalization of the glenoid segment (GLA, GDA) and the humeral segment (HLA, HDA), and the modified shoulder distalization angle (mDSA) were performed (Fig. 1).

A fellow-trained shoulder surgeon (L.M.R.) and 2 senior orthopedic residents (A.V. and V.A.) performed the measurements. To test the angles' reproducibility, intraobserver and interobserver measurements were repeated after four weeks in a blinded and randomized way.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the STATISTICA 10 by StatSoft, Inc. 1984-2011 (Tulsa, OK, USA). The intraobserver rate reliability for each surgeon as a correlation coefficient r and then the Cronbach alpha for each of the 7 angles (LSA, GLA, LHA, DSA, mDSA, GDA, HDA) as an index of the inter-rate reliability.

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for the continuous variables: age, active elevation, active external rotation, and constant postsurgery, as well as all angles. We calculated the chi-square on every categorical variable: sex, lateralization, prosthesis type (LatG/MedH, MedG/LatH, and LatG/LatH) (Fig. 2.), and internal rotation. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed for matched pairs test for the variable simple shoulder test before and after surgery and an ANOVA for repeated measures (multivariate ANOVA) for SANE before and after surgery.

This image shows the different patterns evaluated during the study. (A) Medialized glenosphere/lateralized humerus. (B) Lateralized glenosphere/medialized humerus. (C) Lateralized glenosphere/lateralized humerus.

We calculated Kendall's Tau correlation between angles and SANE post-intervention, Constant, and Simple Shoulder Test, and the same parameter was calculated in relation to active elevation, active external rotation, and internal rotation.

Lastly, we set up an ANOVA for the active elevation, external rotation, and 7 angles. We also calculated the chi-square for the internal rotation. Subsequently, we created a multiple regression model to explain active elevation, external rotation, and internal rotation as a function of the angles. To summarize the results, we used the backward stepwise method, with a P < .01 for an angle to enter the model and a P > .05 as a limit to be excluded. For each model thus developed, we added the nonlinear quadratic component of each angle to verify if it was possible to increase the model's goodness (R and R2).

Results

A total of 504 patients were operated on during the study period, but only 74 patients met the inclusion criteria. 55 were female, and 19 were male. The mean age of study patients was 75.7 and a mean follow-up was 43.14 months. The mean age was 77.6 ± 7.5 for patients with rotator cuff arthropathy, 76.8 ± 7.6 for patients with osteoarthritis, and 66.9 ± 9.1 for patients with massive irreparable rotator cuff lesions. Thirty-two patients had a MedG/LatH pattern (standard base plate with Ascend Flex stem), 21 patients LatG/MedH (BIO-RSA/MIO-RSA with Grammont-style stem), and 21 patients LatG/LatH (BI-RSA/MioIORSA with Ascend Flex stem). Discussion

This study aimed to determine what prognostic value can be attributed to the specific measurement of lateralization and distalization of each component of the reverse shoulder prosthesis according to the new proposed angles.21 The positioning of the prosthesis components at both the humeral and glenoid levels exerts tension on the soft tissues and it affects the lever arm that allows the reverse prosthesis to function.9,10,16,17 Moreover, studies have shown that lateralization of components allows for greater recruitment of muscle groups after RSA.8,19 In addition, in patients with RSA the LatG/LatH pattern obtains better elevation and abduction. Given this information, determining how much each component lateralizes or distalizes helps determine the ideal position to obtain the best possible results in the future.

In search of the ideal position, these new angles allow us to determine the final position of each component. It is known that measurements are reliable for determining how much a component is lateralized or distalized. However, this current study did not find any direct relation to ROM or post-operative functional outcomes. Moreover, multiple linear regression showed only a weak effect between post-operative mobility and some of the angles, unlike previously conducted studies.6,20

The current findings were similar to those described by Imiolczyk et al11 in which there was no significant correlation between LSA, DSA, and clinical outcomes in post-operative scores or ROM. Neither GLA, GDA, HLA, nor HDA also have no strong relationship with the results obtained in patients undergoing reverse prosthesis.

Previously, some studies had explored correlations between LSA and DSA with functional outcomes and arcs of motion and found contradictory results. It was published that ROM and the outcome score after RSA are correlated with specific ranges of LSA and DSA6,23; however, later, a larger cohort of patients concluded that different patients may have different clinical outcomes even if they have the same LSA and DSA.11 At the same time, other studies describe insignificant relationships between the measurement of these angles and clinical outcomes.3

The current study is the first study to assess various components patterns (medialization/lateralization) by using the recently proposed post-operative radiographic measurements, assess the reproducibility, and analyze any potential clinical outcomes correlation with the measurements. The individual measurement of each of the angles shows that there is no correlation with the functional results determined by the scores studied (SANE, Constant, and Simple Shoulder Test); however, the prosthesis's three-dimensionality would allow inferring that when specific angles' results are combined together, they can predict, at a low-level, ROM and post-operative scores. As explained in the multiple linear regression results, the union of several angles may eventually affect the post-operative results. This was also analyzed with a multiple linear regression analysis, where we found that these measurements have little effect on the movement arcs and postoperative scores. The current findings raise a need to explore other factors that may be more influential in the functionality after RSA.

There are some limitations to the study. It is a retrospective design that has not in consideration baseline ROM or patient-reported outcome measures, and it has a relatively small patient cohort in each subgroup, which may create selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Further similar studies involving a larger number of patients are needed to confirm the validity of the findings described in this study. Also, the mix of diagnoses/indications may influence the results.

Conclusion

The post-operative scores, ROM, and patient satisfaction after RSA are the main objectives to be met; however, at present, there are still no reliable measures in post-operative radiographs that correlate with clinical outcomes. The current angles obtained on post-operative radiographs allow us to assess the degree of placement of the prosthesis due to each of the components but not to predict ROM or clinical outcomes.
