Efficient Localization of Directional Emitters via Joint Beampattern Estimation
FRASER WILLIAMS, AKILA PEMASIRI, DHAMMIKA JAYALATH, TERRENCE MARTIN, CLINTON FOOKES

Abstract
Localization methods designed for omnidirectional emitters degrade on directional sources, where directive beampatterns induce pronounced received signal strength (RSS) modulations. We present a direct position determination (DPD) approach that augments standard angle of arrival (AOA) and time difference of arrival (TDOA) measurements with RSS modulation to jointly estimate emitter position and beampattern parameters. Two algorithms reduce the four-dimensional search to two dimensions: a block coordinate ascent (BCA) method that alternates between position and beampattern subproblems, and a profile likelihood method that concentrates the beampattern parameters out via a generalized eigenvalue solve, a weighted least squares fit, and Newton refinement on the constrained Rayleigh quotient. We derive the Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB) and benchmark against an AOA–TDOA baseline and a prior-art approach that assumes known beamwidth and estimates only the orientation. The profile likelihood method approaches the CRLB, substantially outperforms all baselines at low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and remains robust under model mismatch with the assumed von Mises template. A sensitivity analysis shows performance peaks when receivers lie on the main-lobe edges where RSS gradients are maximized.

Introduction

Passive localization of radio frequency (RF) emitters is a fundamental capability across navigation
[
1
]
, telecommunications, and surveillance
[
2
]
. Estimators typically fuse measurements from multiple spatially distributed receivers using an a priori model of signal transformations
[
3
]
, including time difference of arrival (TDOA)
[
1
]
, angle of arrival (AOA)
[
3
,
4
]
, frequency difference of arrival (FDOA)
[
5
,
6
]
, and received signal strength (RSS)
[
2
,
7
]
.

Most localization estimators implicitly assume an omnidirectional emitter. Modern radio systems, however, routinely employ directive antennas or phased arrays for long-range communications, spatial multiplexing, and radar
[
8
,
9
]
, producing pronounced RSS variations across spatially distributed receivers. Ignoring these variations introduces substantial localization error
[
10
]
. Few prior works address directional emitters explicitly: the most directly comparable,
[
10
]
, assumes the emitter beamwidth is known a priori and estimates only the orientation. As we show, localization performance is highly sensitive to beamwidth misspecification, motivating an estimator that recovers orientation and beamwidth jointly with position. To our knowledge, the present work is the first DPD method to estimate all three blindly, with no prior on the beamwidth.

Direct position determination (DPD) incorporates signal measurements directly into a physics-based model via maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE), and has improved localization at low signal to noise ratio (SNR) by folding path loss into the transformation model
[
11
]
. This and related RSS and path-loss-aware DPD methods
[
2
,
7
,
12
,
13
]
model the isotropic link budget but assume an omnidirectional emitter. We extend the DPD framework to directional emission by jointly estimating the emitter’s position and beampattern parameters. Since emitters can employ arbitrary beampatterns, we introduce a generalized beampattern model that captures the main lobe through two parameters, orientation
\phi
and half-power beamwidth
\beta
, at a fidelity sufficient for localization while remaining tractable.

Adding the beampattern parameters lifts the search from two dimensions (position) to four (position plus orientation and beamwidth), so that the cost of an exhaustive search grows in proportion to the beampattern grid resolution. To address this, we develop two algorithms that both reduce the four-dimensional joint search to two dimensions. The first is a block coordinate ascent (BCA) method that alternates between position and beampattern subproblems. The second is a profile likelihood method that analytically concentrates the beampattern parameters out: the inner solve recovers the unconstrained amplitude pattern via a generalized eigenvalue decomposition, fits the beampattern parameters by weighted least squares, and refines them with Newton iterations on the constrained Rayleigh quotient using the analytic Hessian.

We further derive the CRLB for position estimation under the generalized beampattern model. Beyond establishing a numerical performance floor, the bound depends explicitly on the RSS gradient at each receiver, with a concrete geometric implication: receivers contribute most when positioned on the steep flanks of the main lobe rather than at the beam peak or in the sidelobes.

Problem Formulation

Consider a stationary directional emitter, and
L

M

l

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{l}(t)

\displaystyle=d_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p})s(t-\tau_{l}(\bm{p})-t_{0})+\bm{n}_{l}(t),

(1)

where
\bm{r}_{l}(t)
is the
M\times 1
received signal vector at time

\bm{p}=[x,y]^{\mathsf{T}}

is the directional path attenuation incorporating beampattern effects,
\bm{\psi}=[\phi,\beta]^{\mathsf{T}}
is the beampattern parameter vector where
\phi
is the emitter orientation and
\beta
is the emitter main-lobe beamwidth in radians,
\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p})
is the receiver steering vector,

\tau_{l}(\bm{p})

represents noise at the
l

d_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

The directional path attenuation decomposes into an isotropic path loss term and a directional beampattern gain:

\displaystyle d_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle=\rho_{l}(\bm{p})\,g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi}),

(2)

where

captures geometric spreading independent of beam direction and

captures the angular dependence of the emitter’s radiation pattern. This decomposition is the key distinction from omnidirectional models, where

for all receivers.

The isotropic path attenuation is

\displaystyle\rho_{l}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\kappa_{l}}{\norm{\bm{p}-\bm{u}_l}},

(3)

\displaystyle\kappa_{l}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{P_{T}G_{0}G_{R}\lambda^{2}}{\left(4\pi\right)^{2}L_{l}}},

(4)

where
\kappa_{l}
combines all position-independent parameters:
P_{T}
the transmit power,
G_{0}
the peak gain of the emitter,
G_{R}
the gain of an individual antenna element,
\lambda
the radio wavelength, and
L_{l}
system losses such as filter insertion loss. The
l
th receiver is at position
\bm{u}_{l}=[x_{l},y_{l}]^{\mathsf{T}}
. It is possible to calibrate receivers such that
\kappa_{l}
is constant across receivers through compensation of
L_{l}
.

The beampattern gain is approximated by a von Mises structure

\displaystyle g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(\beta)\left[\cos\left(\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p})-\phi\right)-1\right]}

(5)

\displaystyle\alpha(\beta)

\displaystyle\triangleq-\frac{\log(2)/2}{\cos(\beta/2)-1},

(6)

where
\beta
is the half-power beamwidth of
g_{l}
in radians, and
\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p})
is the angle from the transmitter to the
l
th receiver. The transmit angle is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\arctantwo\left(y_{l}-y,x_{l}-x\right)

(7)

where
\arctantwo
is the quadrant-aware arc-tangent function and the
l
th receiver position is
\bm{u}_{l}=[x_{l},y_{l}]^{\mathsf{T}}
.

The von Mises form is smooth, even, and
2\pi
-periodic, and is chosen for tractability rather than physical fidelity. Its two parameters keep the joint search low-dimensional and enable the inexpensive inner solve of

. Fig.

4

quantifies this mismatch.

The receiver steering vector is an

\displaystyle[\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p})]_{m}

\displaystyle=e^{-j2\pi\frac{(m-1)\Delta}{\lambda}\cos(\theta^{(r)}_l(\bm{p}))},

\displaystyle=\arctantwo(y-y_{l},x-x_{l}),

(9)

where

is the physical separation between antenna elements,

is the wavelength of the radio signal, and
\theta^{(r)}_{l}(\bm{p})
is the angle from the

th receiver to the position
\bm{p}
.

The propagation delay is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{l}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{\norm{\bm{p}-\bm{u}_l}}{c},

(10)

where

is the speed of light in a vacuum.

Expressing (
1
) in the frequency domain, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{r}}_{l,k}

\displaystyle=d_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p})e^{-j\omega_{k}[\tau_{l}(\bm{p})+t_{0}]}\tilde{s}_{k}+\tilde{\bm{n}}_{l,k},

(11)

\displaystyle\omega_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{2\pi k}{N_{s}T},

(12)

where
N_{s}
is the number of signal samples,

is the sampling period,

is the discrete frequency bin, and
\tilde{s}_{k}
and

are the Fourier coefficients of

and

forming the vectors
\tilde{\bm{s}}=[\tilde{s}_{0}\dots{}\tilde{s}_{N_{s}-1}]^{\mathsf{T}}

\tilde{s}_{k}\leftarrow e^{-j\omega_{k}t_{0}}\tilde{s}_{k}

Stacking all receivers, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq[\tilde{\bm{n}}_{1,k}^{\mathsf{T}}\dots{}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{L,k}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}

(13)

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{k,l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle\triangleq d_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})e^{-j\omega_{k}\tau_{l}(\bm{p})}\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p})

(14)

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle\triangleq[[\bm{a}_{k,1}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{T}}\dots{}[\bm{a}_{k,L}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq[\tilde{\bm{r}}_{1,k}^{\mathsf{T}}\dots{}\tilde{\bm{r}}_{L,k}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(16)

The received signal is then given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\tilde{s}_{k}+\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}.

(17)

We wish to estimate the emitter position
\bm{p}
using the observations of (
17
).

III
Cramér-Rao Lower Bound

We derive the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) for position estimation, which provides the minimum variance achievable by any unbiased estimator under our signal model.

III-A
Fisher Information Matrix

Noise is assumed to be drawn from the complex Gaussian distribution
\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
. The log-likelihood function of the observed signal is

\displaystyle\ln P(

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{r}})=-MLN_{s}\log(\pi\sigma_n^2)

\displaystyle\qquad\ -\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{s}-1}\norm{\tilde{\bm{r}}_k-\bm{a}_k(\bm{p}, \bm{\psi})\tilde{s}_k}^{2}.

(18)

The position bound is obtained from the Fisher information matrix (FIM) by concentrating out the nuisance parameters
\bm{\psi}
and
\tilde{\bm{s}}
via the partitioned-inverse identity.

The Fisher information matrix is given by

\displaystyle\bm{J}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\ln P(\tilde{\bm{r}})}{\partial\bm{\zeta}^{2}}\right]

(19)

where the vector
\bm{\zeta}
contains all unknown parameters

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle\triangleq[\bm{p},\bm{\epsilon}],

(20)

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\triangleq[\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}},\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathsf{T}}].

(21)

The FIM has the partitioned structure

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{\bm{\zeta}\bm{\zeta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{J}_{\bm{p}\bm{p}}&\bm{J}_{\bm{p}\bm{\epsilon}}\\
\bm{J}_{\bm{\epsilon}\bm{p}}&\bm{J}_{\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}}\end{bmatrix},

(22)

where sub-blocks
\bm{J}_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{j}}
are computed from partial derivatives of the mean signal model

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k}=\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\tilde{s}_{k}

(23)

with respect to position
\bm{p}
, beampattern parameters
\bm{\psi}
, and signal
\tilde{\bm{s}}
. Full derivations are given in Section S1 of the supplementary material.

III-B
Position Bound

Using the partitioned matrix inversion formula
[
14
]
, the CRLB for position can be extracted from the full FIM by

\displaystyle\text{CRLB}_{\bm{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{2}\left[\bm{J}_{\bm{p}\bm{p}}-\bm{J}_{\bm{p}\bm{\epsilon}}\bm{J}^{\shortminus 1}_{\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}}\bm{J}_{\bm{\epsilon}\bm{p}}\right]^{\shortminus 1}.

(24)

The scalar position error bound is then

\displaystyle\sigma_{\bm{p}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tr(\text{CRLB}_{\bm{p}})}.

(25)

III-C
Geometric Interpretation

The bound depends on receiver geometry through the beampattern gradient. As the emitter moves, the transmit angle to receiver
l
changes, and with it the gain the receiver sees. Differentiating (
5
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})}{\partial\theta_{l}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=-\alpha(\beta)\,g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\sin\!\left(\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p})-\phi\right),

(26)

which vanishes at boresight where the lobe is flat and peaks on its steep flank. Near the peak a receiver sees almost no change in received power as the emitter moves, and constrains position through delay and array phase alone. On the flank it registers a large swing in power, supplying RSS information that tightens the bound. The gradient also scales as
\norm{\bm{p}-\bm{u}_l}^{\shortminus 1}
, favouring receivers close to the emitter.

The orientation and beamwidth are unknown apriori, so this sensitivity cannot be used to sit a receiver on a known lobe edge. However, it may be used to inform the search formation of receivers. Spreading them across a wide span of aspect angles ensures some fall on a flank however the beam is pointed, and requires no prior. Clustering them near an assumed boresight is fragile: a small error in the assumed direction strands the whole formation on the flat peak. The prescription concerns the shape of the formation, not any individual receiver, and the estimator needs no beam prior.
Section

V-E
quantifies both claims.

IV
Directional Emitter Localization Algorithm

In this section we develop a directional emitter-aware DPD cost function for localization.

IV-A
Cost Function

Following the standard maximum likelihood (ML) approach, we minimize the negative log-likelihood function (
18
), which is equivalent to minimizing

\displaystyle Q(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{s}-1}\norm{\tilde{\bm{r}}_k-\bm{a}_k(\bm{p}, \bm{\psi})\tilde{s}_k}^{2}.

(27)

The signal coefficients
\tilde{s}_{k}
are nuisance parameters. The value of
\tilde{s}_{k}
that minimizes (
27
) for a given
(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
is the pseudoinverse solution

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{s}}_{k}

\displaystyle=[\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\dagger}\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k}

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{[\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{H}}}{M\norm{\bm{d}(\bm{p}, \bm{\psi})}^{2}}\,\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k},

(29)

where
\bm{d}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\triangleq[d_{1}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\dots{}d_{L}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{T}}
collects the directional path attenuations. The simplification follows because the elements of the receiver steering vectors
\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p})
in (
8
) have unit magnitude, so
[\bm{a}_{k}]^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{a}_{k}=M\norm{\bm{d}}^{2}
with all phase terms canceling.

Substituting (
29
) into (
27
) and expanding,

\displaystyle Q(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{s}-1}\norm{\tilde{\bm{r}}_k}^{2}-\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{N_{s}-1}\left|[\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{H}}\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k}\right|^{2}}{M\norm{\bm{d}(\bm{p}, \bm{\psi})}^{2}}.

(30)

Since
\norm{\tilde{\bm{r}}_k}^{2}
is independent of the candidate parameters, minimizing
Q(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
is equivalent to maximizing

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{s}-1}\frac{[\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R}_{k}\,\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})}{\norm{\bm{d}(\bm{p}, \bm{\psi})}^{2}},

(31)

where
\bm{R}_{k}\triangleq\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k}\tilde{\bm{r}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}
is the outer product at frequency bin
k
. This is the matched Bartlett cost function: the numerator measures phase coherence across receivers and frequencies, and the denominator normalizes by the total RSS so that the cost depends on the relative amplitude distribution rather than absolute power.

In the general DPD formulation, unknown per-receiver channel gains are concentrated out via eigenvalue maximization
[
3
]
, which is appropriate when the cost function depends only on phase information. However, when RSS information is exploited, unknown channel gains are indistinguishable from the directional attenuation
d_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
, and the eigenvalue approach systematically flattens the perceived beampattern
[
15
]
. Recent work incorporates path loss models that constrain channel gain ratios as a function of position to regularize this ambiguity
[
10
,
12
,
13
]
.

The emitter position and beampattern parameters are then estimated by

\displaystyle(\hat{\bm{p}},\hat{\bm{\psi}})=\argmax_{\bm{p},\bm{\psi}}\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi}).

(32)

Direct evaluation of (
32
) requires a four-dimensional grid search over
(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
, whose cost scales with the product of the position and beampattern grid sizes (
Section

IV-D
). We present two methods to address this: a block coordinate ascent algorithm that iteratively alternates between position and beampattern subproblems, and a profile likelihood method that analytically concentrates the beampattern parameters out of the cost function at each candidate position.

IV-B
Block Coordinate Ascent (BCA) Algorithm

A natural simplification is block coordinate ascent: alternately maximize
\tilde{Q}
over position
\bm{p}
with
\bm{\psi}
fixed, then over beampattern
\bm{\psi}
with
\bm{p}
fixed, each step being a two-dimensional search. Since each block maximization is non-decreasing, the sequence converges to a stationary point of
\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

[
16
]
, which is in general only a local maximum. Algorithm
1
details this procedure. The initial position estimate is obtained by setting
d_{l}=1
for all
l
, removing all RSS information and reducing the cost function to phase-only localization. The convergence behaviour and the resulting low-SNR performance are examined in
Sections

V-B
and
V-F
.

IV-C
Profile Likelihood Algorithm

The block coordinate ascent approach has two limitations: the beampattern subproblem still requires a grid search over
\bm{\psi}
at each iteration, and the solution depends on initialization quality, which degrades at low SNR. Both are addressed by analytically concentrating the beampattern parameters out of the cost function at each candidate position, producing a position-only search surface called the profile likelihood

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{\text{PL}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{\psi}}\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi}).

(33)

This reduces the four-dimensional joint optimization to a two-dimensional grid search over position, where the inner maximization over
\bm{\psi}
is carried out independently at each grid point by the low-dimensional procedure developed below.

At a fixed candidate position
\bm{p}
, the transmit angles
\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p})
and isotropic path attenuations
\rho_{l}(\bm{p})
from (
3
) are known for all
l
. Define

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k,l}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\triangleq\rho_{l}(\bm{p})\,e^{-j\omega_{k}\tau_{l}(\bm{p})}\,\bm{a}_{l}(\bm{p}),

(34)

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\triangleq\operatorname{blkdiag}\!\big(\bm{v}_{k,1}(\bm{p}),\dots{},\bm{v}_{k,L}(\bm{p})\big),

(35)

\displaystyle\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle\triangleq[g_{1}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\dots{}g_{L}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})]^{\mathsf{T}},

(36)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\triangleq[\rho_{1}(\bm{p})\dots{}\rho_{L}(\bm{p})]^{\mathsf{T}},

(37)

where
\bm{v}_{k,l}(\bm{p})
is the
M\times 1
phase-steered vector for the
l
th receiver at frequency
k
, carrying every position-dependent factor except the beampattern gain, and
\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{p})
is the resulting
ML\times L
block-diagonal matrix. Since
d_{l}=\rho_{l}\,g_{l}
by (
2
), the stacked steering vector (
15
) factors as
\bm{a}_{k}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})=\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{p})\,\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
, and substituting into (
31
) gives a generalized Rayleigh quotient

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{C}}(\bm{p})\,\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})}{\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{W}(\bm{p})\,\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})},

(38)

where

\displaystyle\bm{C}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{s}-1}\bm{V}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}(\bm{p})\,\bm{R}_{k}\,\bm{V}_{k}(\bm{p}),

(39)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{C}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\{\bm{C}(\bm{p})\},

(40)

\displaystyle\bm{W}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\rho}(\bm{p})\odot\bm{\rho}(\bm{p})).

(41)

Since
\bm{g}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
is real-valued, only the real part of
\bm{C}(\bm{p})
contributes to the quadratic form.

The unconstrained maximum of (
38
) over all
\bm{g}
(not restricted to the parametric beampattern family) is the largest generalized eigenvalue of the pencil
(\tilde{\bm{C}},\bm{W})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{C}}(\bm{p})\,\bar{\bm{g}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{p})\,\bm{W}(\bm{p})\,\bar{\bm{g}}.

(42)

Since
\bm{W}
is diagonal and positive definite, this reduces to a standard
L\times L
real symmetric eigenvalue problem, inexpensive for typical array sizes. The eigenvector
\bar{\bm{g}}
is the amplitude pattern the data favors at
\bm{p}
with no beampattern constraint, and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{p})
upper-bounds
\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
for any beam parameters.

The eigenvector
\bar{\bm{g}}
will not in general correspond to any
\bm{\psi}
in the parametric model. Since the unconstrained amplitude pattern at receiver
l
is
\rho_{l}(\bm{p})\,\bar{g}_{l}
and the model predicts
\rho_{l}(\bm{p})\,g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
, factoring out
\rho_{l}
yields the weighted least-squares fit

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\psi}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\psi}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\rho_{l}^{2}(\bm{p})\left(\bar{g}_{l}-g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\right)^{2},

(43)

where the weights
\rho_{l}^{2}(\bm{p})
arise naturally from the amplitude decomposition. This two-dimensional problem has no closed-form solution in
\bm{\psi}
, but a closed-form
approximate
solution, used as a seed for the Newton refinement below, does. Taking logs of the von Mises gain,

\displaystyle\ln g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})=\alpha(\beta)\!\left[\cos(\theta_l^{(t)}(\bm{p}) - \phi)-1\right]=\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{l},

(44)

which is linear in the coefficient and regressor vectors

\displaystyle\bm{a}

\displaystyle=[\alpha\cos\phi,\ \alpha\sin\phi,\ -\alpha]^{\mathsf{T}},

(45)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{l}

\displaystyle=[\cos\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p}),\ \sin\theta_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{p}),\ 1]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(46)

Fitting
\ln\bar{g}_{l}
to
\bm{x}_{l}
by weighted linear least squares, with log-domain weights
w_{l}=\rho_{l}^{2}(\bm{p})\,\bar{g}_{l}^{2}
, gives the coefficients in closed form as the solution of a
3\times 3
system,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{a}}=\Bigl(\sum_{l}w_{l}\,\bm{x}_{l}\bm{x}_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr)^{\shortminus 1}\sum_{l}w_{l}\,\bm{x}_{l}\ln\bar{g}_{l},

(47)

computed in
\mathcal{O}(L)
with no grid. The seed is recovered as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{0}=\arctantwo(\hat{a}_{2},\hat{a}_{1}),\qquad\alpha(\hat{\beta}_{0})=\sqrt{\hat{a}_{1}^{2}+\hat{a}_{2}^{2}}.

(48)

Dropping the exact constraint
\hat{a}_{3}=-\sqrt{\hat{a}_{1}^{2}+\hat{a}_{2}^{2}}
is what makes the fit linear; the Newton step restores it. When the receivers subtend too narrow a range of transmit angles for this fit to be well conditioned, we fall back to enumerating the
(\phi,\beta)
candidate set used by BCA, which maximizes the scale-concentrated objective

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\psi}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\bm{\psi}}\frac{\left(\sum_{l}\rho_{l}^{2}\,\bar{g}_{l}\,g_{l}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})\right)^{2}}{\sum_{l}\rho_{l}^{2}\,g_{l}^{2}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})},

(49)

at
L
operations per candidate. The two seeds reach stationary points of equivalent localization accuracy (Section S4), so we use the closed-form seed by default for its lower cost (
Section

V-G
).

The profile likelihood at position
\bm{p}
is then
\tilde{Q}_{\text{PL}}(\bm{p})=\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\,\hat{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{p}))
, evaluated via (
38
) with
\bm{g}=\bm{g}(\bm{p},\hat{\bm{\psi}})
.

The WLS fit (
43
) is an approximate inner solve: it minimises a weighted squared error on the amplitude pattern rather than directly maximising the constrained Rayleigh quotient. This warm start is refined towards the constrained maximiser by Newton iteration on
\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\bm{\psi})
, using the analytic gradient and Hessian derived in Section S3 of the supplementary material. The refinement is safeguarded: when the Hessian is not negative definite the Newton step falls back to the normalized gradient, with step length set by Armijo backtracking. Every accepted step then increases
\tilde{Q}(\bm{p},\cdot)
, bounded above by
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{p})
via (
42
), so the iteration is monotone and cannot diverge, reaching a local maximum near the warm start (
Section

V-F
). We adopt the Newton-refined inner solve as the default throughout the remainder of the paper.

IV-D
Computational Complexity

All three methods share the same per-position precomputation. At a candidate
\bm{p}
, beamforming the
L
receivers over
N_{s}
frequency bins costs
\mathcal{O}(LMN_{s})
and forming
\tilde{\bm{C}}(\bm{p})
in (
39
) costs
\mathcal{O}(L^{2}N_{s})
, giving

\displaystyle C_{\text{pre}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(LN_{s}(M+L)\right).

(50)

Once
\tilde{\bm{C}}
is available, evaluating the cost for a given
\bm{\psi}
is only the quadratic form
\mathcal{O}(L^{2})
, so the methods differ in how often, and at which positions, they perform the beampattern search.

An exhaustive search evaluates every
\bm{\psi}
candidate at every position,

\displaystyle C_{\text{ex}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}|\left[C_{\text{pre}}+|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|L^{2}\right]\right).

(51)

The profile likelihood also visits every position but does not enumerate
\bm{\psi}
: it forms the closed-form log-domain seed in
\mathcal{O}(L)
, takes one
\mathcal{O}(L^{3})
eigendecomposition, and runs
N_{\text{n}}
Newton steps,

\displaystyle C_{\text{PL}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}|\left[C_{\text{pre}}+L^{3}+N_{\text{n}}(1+N_{\text{ls}})L^{2}\right]\right),

(52)

where
N_{\text{ls}}
is the average number of backtracking evaluations per Newton step; the enumeration fallback, when triggered, adds an
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|L)
seed term. BCA differs structurally: it searches
\bm{\psi}
at a
single
position per alternation rather than at every grid point, so the beampattern grid enters only once per iteration,

\displaystyle C_{\text{BCA}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(N_{\text{it}}\left[|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}|C_{\text{pre}}+|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|L^{2}\right]\right).

(53)

The exhaustive search carries the beampattern grid at every position, costing
|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}||\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|
. The profile likelihood and BCA both avoid this product: the profile likelihood replaces the grid with the closed-form seed and continuous Newton refinement, scaling with
|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}|
alone, and BCA searches
\bm{\psi}
at one position per iteration, scaling with
N_{\text{it}}(|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}|+|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|)
. BCA is the cheapest of the three at the small
N_{\text{it}}
seen in practice.

The
|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|
factor is what separates the exhaustive search from the profile likelihood as accuracy demands grow. For the exhaustive search the grid
is
the estimate: its
\bm{\psi}
-precision is the grid spacing, so improving it forces
|\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\psi}}|
to grow and, by (
51
), the cost with it. The profile likelihood resolves
\bm{\psi}
continuously through the Newton step, so its accuracy is decoupled from any grid. At a coarse shared grid the profile likelihood is already the cheaper of the two, and the gap widens without bound as the exhaustive grid is refined. This is the sense in which the four-dimensional joint search is avoided; measured runtimes are reported in
Section

V-G
.

V-F
Convergence Behaviour

The BCA cost rises monotonically, up to the grid quantization of the position block, and comes within
1\%
of its converged value within two alternations at every SNR tested, taking a median of three at
0
dB and four at
-10
dB. Rapid cost convergence does not imply a correct position: at low SNR the iteration can settle in a poor local optimum (
Section

IV-B
), which is the source of the large BCA error in Fig.
4
.

The safeguarded iteration is stable: the inner solve never diverges over all coarse-grid points and
100
trials per SNR. Newton refinement averages
6.5
iterations at
0
dB, rising to
8.0
at
-10
dB, and the gradient fallback is exercised at a third to a half of grid points, so the inner problem is non-concave at points reached in normal operation and the safeguard is not a formality. Within a given basin the refinement barely moves the cost, by a median of at most
4.5\times 10^{-3}
in relative terms, so the warm start already sits at its local optimum and the method behaves as a profile likelihood despite the inexact inner maximization. Which basin is reached is set by the seed: the closed-form and enumerated seeds diverge on a fraction of trials, but the stationary points they reach are of equivalent localization accuracy (Section S4), so we use the cheaper closed-form seed by default. The cost-versus-iteration trace, the full inner-solve statistics, and the seed comparison are given in Section S4 of the supplementary material.

VI
Conclusion

This article extended the DPD framework to directional emitters by jointly estimating position and beampattern parameters. Two algorithms were developed to handle the four-dimensional joint optimization: a BCA method that alternates between position and beampattern subproblems, and a profile likelihood (PL) method that concentrates the beampattern parameters out via an eigenvalue decomposition and a Newton-refined inner solve on the constrained Rayleigh quotient. The two differ structurally: BCA enforces the von Mises constraint throughout but is sensitive to its phase-only initialization and falls into poor local optima at low SNR, whereas the profile likelihood recovers the unconstrained amplitude pattern from a single eigendecomposition before fitting the model, trading an exact inner maximization for a well-conditioned, grid-independent inner solve that parallelizes across grid points.

Simulations on a communications and a radar scenario show the profile likelihood substantially outperforming both the AOA–TDOA baseline and BCA: it matches the CRLB above
-5
dB in the communications scenario, with up to
50\%
RMS error reduction over AOA–TDOA in the threshold regime, and tracks the CRLB across the operational range in the radar scenario while AOA–TDOA sits about a factor of two above the bound. The advantage is largest at low SNR, where the BCA initialization breaks down and AOA–TDOA lacks the RSS information to constrain position. An oracle-
\beta
baseline mirroring
[
10
]
confirms that knowing the true beamwidth offers only marginal benefit, and the profile likelihood stays robust when the true pattern departs from the von Mises template.

Two structural properties of the cost function shape these results. Normalization by
\norm{\bm{d}(\bm{p}, \bm{\psi})}
in (
31
) cancels absolute power, leaving localization invariant to transmit power and uniform system losses and elevating the beampattern parameters to the dominant sensitivities. The CRLB analysis further shows that the position bound is governed by which receivers occupy the gradient region of the main lobe, supporting deliberate spread of distributed formations across the beampattern profile rather than concentration near boresight; additional receivers help primarily by raising the probability that the formation samples this lobe-edge region.

The model assumes a common time reference across receivers, allowing the transmission time to be absorbed in (
11
). Residual clock offsets then act like ranging errors: a timing jitter of standard deviation
\sigma_{\delta}
contributes on the order of
c\,\sigma_{\delta}
to the position error. The beampattern estimation depends on the relative RSS rather than absolute timing, so these errors degrade the position while leaving the orientation and beamwidth essentially unaffected. Mitigation relies on disciplined clocks or two-way time transfer, or on TDOA formulations that estimate the offsets jointly with position
[
17
]
.

The chief simplification is the von Mises beampattern itself: two parameters describe a single symmetric main lobe with a real-valued gain, leaving sidelobes, asymmetry, and any angle-dependent phase outside the model. The mismatch study of
Section

V-D
shows the method tolerates a departure from this template, the simulator radiating from a uniform linear array, but the evaluation is still entirely synthetic. Validation against measured emitter patterns and field-collected data, where these effects are present, would be more convincing and is an important next step.

The treatment here is restricted to a single stationary emitter observed in one snapshot. Future work will extend the framework to multi-emitter scenarios, multi-snapshot operation with moving platforms (where varying transmit angles provide additional angular diversity), and alternative parametric beampattern models.
