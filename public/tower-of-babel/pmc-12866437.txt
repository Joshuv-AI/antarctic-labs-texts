New sights of tumor-specific growth factor (TSGF) in T2DM patients with coronary artery disease
Authors: Ya Fang, Zaifei Yin, Yuzhu Zhu, Minghui Shao, Hong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsTo investigate the correlation between tumor-specific growth factor (TSGF) and the presence of coronary artery disease (CAD) in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).Methods and resultsData from a total of 223 subjects with T2DM were cross-sectionally analyzed. Serum TSGF concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in both CAD-positive and CAD-negative subjects. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between TSGF and CAD, while controlling for major risk factors. The predictive power of TSGF for CAD was determined through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Among the 223 subjects, 136 had no CAD and 87 exhibited clinically significant CAD. The median serum TSGF level was significantly higher in the CAD group (66.8 U/ml) compared to the non-CAD group (59.4 U/ml; p < 0.001). Binary logistic regression, adjusted for major risk factors, confirmed a strong association between TSGF and CAD (p < 0.001). The ROC analysis identified an optimal TSGF cutoff of 62.5 U/ml, with a sensitivity of 70.1% and a specificity of 64% in predicting CAD in T2DM patients.ConclusionThe results showed a significant increase in TSGF levels in CAD patients with T2DM. These findings suggest that TSGF could be a promising biomarker for CAD diagnosis in T2DM patients, and further research is warranted to evaluate its clinical utility.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a global health crisis affecting 1 in 10 individuals aged 20–79, which is a substantial contributor to cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. DM patients experience coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction (MI), and fatal CAD at rates two to three times higher than those without diabetes [3, 4]. A previous classic study revealed that the 7-year occurrence rate of MI in diabetic patients without a prior MI history was equal to that in non - diabetic patients with a prior MI history, indicating that diabetes mellitus is a cardiovascular risk equivalent [4]. Over a 7 - year follow - up, individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) yet without CAD present a cardiovascular death risk comparable to those having a history of MI, as reported in [5, 6]. Substantial evidence indicates that inflammation has a significant part to play in endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis progression [7]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia in patients with T2DM accelerate atherosclerosis and CAD development [8]. T2DM, when concurrent with CAD, independently increases atherosclerosis incidence, inflammatory cell infiltration, and plaque necrosis [9].

Tumor-specific growth factor (TSGF), discovered in 1989 at the University of Toronto, refers to a group of certain carbohydrates and metabolites, including lipoproteins, amino acids, and enzymes, that facilitate tumor growth and angiogenesis [10]. TSGFs, which are released during tumor initiation, promote cell proliferation and angiogenesis, enhancing the blood supply to cancer tissue [11]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the high sensitivity of TSGF for detecting malignancies [12, 13]. Besides, TSGF has a proinflammatory effect. Current research indicates that TSGF levels are elevated in various diseases, such as mechanical trauma, pulmonary inflammation, tuberculosis, and chronic kidney disease [14–17]. Notably, diabetes patients exhibit higher TSGF levels than healthy individuals [18]. CAD is one of the most serious complications of T2DM, and inflammation is an important factor in promoting its progress. The TSGF is commonly used for the screening and efficacy testing of various cancers in China, while European and American countries are more likely to test substances with similar concepts - angiogenesis 2 (Ang-2). Previous study reported that Ang-2 remains elevated in patients with T2DM with a history of CAD [19]. However, the association between circulating TSGF and CAD in diabetic patients remains unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the predictive value of TSGF for CAD in T2DM patients by analyzing clinical data from our center.

Methods

Study population

This cross - sectional study was carried out at the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, Jiangsu Province, China. We enrolled 223 patients who underwent diabetes mellitus aged ≥18 years from 1 October 2022 to 31 October 2023. The diagnosis of diabetes was based on the 1999 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. The inclusion and exclusion criterion were based on the principles as described in previously reports [20]. The inclusion criterion was aged 18 years or older and the presence of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Patients who first received coronary angiograms, images of computed tomography angiography, or had a history of CAD were also included. Exclusion criteria consisted of having an acute infectious disease during the evaluation period; renal failure with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2; those with liver function problems, a history of mental illness, cancer, or autoimmune diseases were also excluded; and a history of solid tumors or hematological tumors or active tumors. The study employed blinded evaluation by an experienced cardiologist for images of computed tomography angiography or coronary angiograms. Patients with ≥50% major coronary artery stenosis or a history of CAD were assigned to the CAD group. A total of 223 participants were included and divided into non-CAD (n = 136) and CAD (n = 87) groups.

This study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (number: 241036) and it followed the principles of the Helsinki Declaration (2013) and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. All participants provided their informed consent. All patients underwent physical examination, data on their demographic characteristics, and concomitant medication were obtained. Anthropometric measurements included height (in meters), weight (in kilograms), and BMI (body mass index), which was calculated as weight divided by height squared.

Laboratory tests

We measured the biochemical parameters upon admission as previously reported [20]. Each subject had a 10-ml fasting peripheral blood sample collected. This sample was then centrifuged for 5 min at 3,000 ×g after completion of clotting. The separated serum was stored at −80 °C immediately. Measurements included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were carried out by means of standard enzymatic techniques with the biochemical analyzer (HITACHI 7450, Tokyo, Japan). The glucose oxidase method was used to measure fasting glucose levels. High-performance liquid chromatography was utilized to assay HbA1c with the VARIANT II hemoglobin testing system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Serum TSGF levels were quantified by a commercially available ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each serum sample was measured twice, and then the average was calculated for the results.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 19.0 statistical software (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the variables was estimated using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. Normally distributed data presented as the mean ± standard error, abnormally distributed data expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables were presented as the number (percentage). χ2 test and t test were utilized to compare the demographic and clinical data of the groups. Logarithmic transformation was carried out on non-normally distributed variables prior to further analysis. For normally distributed variables, linear polynomial contrasts in ANOVA were employed to assess the trends of continuous variables among different groups, while for non-normally distributed data, the Mann‒Whitney U test was used. Rate trends were analyzed by the Cochran-Armitage trend test. The effect of serum TSGF on the risk of CAD was analyzed through binary logistic regression. Covariates previously linked to TSGF or diabetes/CAD in existing studies [11, 21, 22] and variables known to influence CAD or diabetes were prioritized，including age, sex, BMI, fasting glucose, hypertension, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, HbA1c, AST, ALT, uric acid, creatinine, therapeutic factors. To assess the sensitivity and specificity of TSGF in predicting CAD in patients with T2DM, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The maximal Youden index was utilized to determine the optimal cutoff for each ROC curve. MedCalc statistical software (Ostend, Belgium) was used to analyze the AUCs (areas under the curves) for TSGF and/or other clinical parameters. p < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference. Diagrams were made using GraphPad Prism 6.

Discussion

Epidemiological evidence supports a well-established link between T2DM and an increased risk of CAD, with diabetes patients having a higher incidence of CAD [23]. Biomarkers play a pivotal role in disease diagnosis owing to their widespread availability, noninvasive sampling procedures, and cost-effectiveness. To our knowledge, this study is the first to report the relationship between serum TSGF levels and the risk of CAD in T2DM patients.

The main finding of this study is that TSGF serum level is elevated in patients with CAD with T2DM (66.8 U/ml(61.100;71.200)) in comparison with patients with T2DM without CAD (59.4 U/ml;(50.500;65.350)). Besides, HbA1c and the incidence of CAD increased with increasing TSGF quartiles. A previous study by Liu et al. stratified 60 diabetic patients (diabetes group) into three subgroups (n = 20 each) based on hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels: Group A (HbA1c > 11.1%), Group B (HbA1c 7.0%-11.0%), and Group C (HbA1c < 7.0%). Consistent with our results, they found a graded decrease in TSGF levels across the diabetic subgroups (A > B > C, p < 0.05) [18]. However, Liu et al. did not investigate the potential association between diabetic complications and TSGF levels. As we all know, hypertension, hyperlipidemia and obesity are high risk factors for CAD in diabetic patients. However, medication can affect levels of blood pressure and blood lipid. For example, in this study, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C and blood pressure remained nonsignificant between the CAD group and the non-CAD group, maybe its due to the former had a higher propensity to receive antihypertension agents and statins. We have conducted comparative analyses between the medication and non-medication groups, which revealed no significant differences in TSGF levels. However, given the limited sample size of our study and potential residual confounding factors, these findings cannot conclusively demonstrate that these medications have no effect on TSGF levels. We found no prior studies have investigated the association of antihypertension agents/statins with TSGF, thus further research is warranted to determine whether these medications influence TSGF expression.

In this study, the ROC analysis identifies a TSGF cutoff of 62.5 U/ml for predicting CAD, with moderate sensitivity (70.1%) and specificity (64%). While TSGF shows promise, its sensitivity and specificity remain lower than those of traditional biomarkers. For example, a previous study reported that the ROC analysis identifies a NT-proBNP cutoff of 165.5 pg/ml for predicting CAD in patients with diabetes, with sensitivity (95%) and specificity (100%) [24]. Increased NT-proBNP, hsCRP, and IL-6 levels in patients with T2DM demonstrate the concurrent endothelial injury, inflammatory activation, and angiogenic dysregulation characteristic of acute coronary syndromes [19, 25]. Further large-scale studies are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the prognostic value of TSGF and elucidate its pathophysiological role in myocardial infarction and arteriosclerosis. Such investigations, conducted in broader patient cohorts, could establish the clinical utility of TSGF for identifying high-risk cardiovascular patients and facilitating earlier therapeutic intervention.

It has been shown in this study that there is a strong correlation between high serum levels of TSGF and the occurrence of CAD in patients with T2DM. However, the mechanisms underlying the association between TSGF and CAD remain unclear. In clinical practice, TSGF serves as a common cancer biomarker in China, whereas Western nations typically favor angiogenesis markers like angiopoietin-2 for comparable diagnostic purposes. Although these represent distinct molecular entities, both biomarkers share common associations with neovascularization and demonstrate similar clinical significance in oncological applications. Ang-2 is expressed by endothelial cells and functions as an antagonist of the Tie-2 receptor [26]. Patients with T2DM [27] and diabetic retinopathy [28] have been reported to have increased plasma levels of Ang-2. Elevated Ang-2 levels has a connection to the progression of atherosclerosis as well, and its expression in advanced-stage lesions is higher compared to that in early-stage lesions [29]. Furthermore, it has been reported recently that increased levels of Ang-2 after percutaneous coronary interventions may serve as a predictive indicator for adverse cardiovascular events in the short to medium term [30]. To our knowledge, no published study has reported TSGF’s mechanistic role in CAD progression among T2DM patients. Studies of similar biomarkers such as Ang-2 may provide valuable insights for further mechanistic investigations. Earlier investigations showed that in diabetic db/db mice, the overexpression of Ang-2 can impede angiogenesis and worsen cardiac fibrosis [31] via the suppressing of the Tie2 signaling, and also raise the levels of intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) markers. A significant loss of capillary density presented through immunohistochemical analysis [31, 32]. Conversely, over-expression Ang1 to counterbalance Ang2 can reverse the loss of capillary density and the formation of fibrous tissue in the db/db mouse hearts [31, 32]. It was shown that Ang-2 can lead to an increase in endothelial cell death, sensitizes myocardial microvascular inflammation, and promotes cardiac fibrosis, which in turn results in a decrease in capillary density in diabetic conditions [31]. However, whether TSGF contributes to CAD progression through the aforementioned mechanisms requires further validation via in vitro and animal experiments.

There are several major limitations in this study which need to be pointed out. Firstly, it was not possible to establish a causal link between serum TSGF and CAD development, in virtue of the cross-sectional design of the study. Secondly, this study lack the data on other well-established CVD biomarkers, including NT-proBNP, hsCRP, and IL-6 levels, which made it impossible to compare the predictive performance of TSGF with these biomarkers. Furthermore, the cross-sectional measurements may not precisely reflect participants’ consistent levels of serum TSGF, warranting cautious interpretation of the findings. The sample consisted of hospitalized diabetic patients in Suzhou, Jiangsu, China, necessitating validation of the generalizability of the results to other diabetic populations, ethnic groups and, as well as multi-center validation. Finally, selection bias is inherent due to the inclusion of T2DM patients at greater risk for CAD. The absence of a reference for normal TSGF levels in healthy controls is another limitation, as there is a dearth of research comparing TSGF levels across different health demographics, such as age, ethnicity, genetics, and nutrition. Addressing these questions could enhance the potential utility of TSGF as a biomarker in T2DM patients with CAD. Prospective longitudinal studies should evaluate whether TSGF can predict CAD progression in T2DM patients over time. Validation in broader and more diverse populations (e.g., different ethnicities or comorbidities) would strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Mechanistic investigations are warranted to elucidate how TSGF contributes to atherosclerosis or other CAD-related pathways in diabetes. Incorporating validated dietary and physical activity questionnaires, exploring polygenic risk scores for CAD in diabetic populations, utilizing Mendelian randomization to assess causal relationships should be included in the future studies. Despite these limitations, our findings offer valuable groundwork for future larger-scale investigations.

Conclusion

The main limitations of this study are the relatively small sample size and low event rate, both of which could potentially compromise the reliability of the ROC analysis results regarding the specificity and sensitivity for CAD.

In summary, our study revealed a significant association between circulating TSGF levels and CAD in the diabetic population. However, further research, as mentioned above (including longitudinal studies, mechanistic research, and validation in diverse populations), is needed to clarify the clinical and mechanistic implications of this relationship.
