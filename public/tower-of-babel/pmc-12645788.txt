PERSIAN cohort biobank: creating a large-scale national resource for biomedical research in Iran
Authors: Amaneh Shayanrad, SeyedehFatemeh Mousavi, Sara Eftekhari, Gholamreza Roshandel, Rana Irilouzadian, Hossein Poustchi, Reza Malekzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiobanks are essential for advancing biomedical research by providing access to high-quality biological specimens and associated data. In the Middle East, large-scale biorepositories are limited, hindering regional epidemiological and genetic studies. The PERSIAN Cohort Study Biobank was established to address this gap and support diverse research initiatives in Iran.MethodsThe Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN), established in 2014, is a nationwide cohort study comprising multiple sub-cohorts. The adult cohort includes Iranian residents aged 35–70 from 18 diverse regions, capturing the country’s ethnic, environmental, and socioeconomic diversity. Upon obtaining informed consent, participants undergo standardized interviews conducted by trained personnel, completing a comprehensive 482-item questionnaire to collect detailed health and lifestyle data. Biological specimens—blood, urine, hair, and nails—are systematically collected and processed according to internationally recognised biobanking standards, ensuring sample integrity. Rigorous quality-assurance and -control checks accompany every step. The study utilizes a decentralized infrastructure with dual storage facilities to prevent sample loss, ensuring long-term preservation and accessibility. Data management is facilitated by a secure, web-based system with robust access controls, safeguarding participant confidentiality and enhancing data reliability.ResultsBy 2020, enrollment was completed at all participating centers, resulting in the collection of samples from 170,564 participants (55% female, 45% male). The cohort exhibited a diverse age distribution: 22% were aged 35–40 years, 35% were aged 41–50 years, 29% were aged 51–60 years, and 14% were over 60 years old. Geographically, over 75% of participants resided in urban areas, while 25% were from rural regions.ConclusionsThe PERSIAN Cohort Biobank now provides Iran—and the wider research community—with a rigorously curated, ethnically and geographically diverse repository of biospecimens. By integrating genetic, environmental, and socioeconomic data from a large national cohort, it establishes a new benchmark for large‑scale biobanking in resource‑limited settings and opens unprecedented opportunities for inclusive, globally relevant health research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25006-y.Keywords: Biobank, PERSIAN cohort, Iran, Epidemiology, Genetic research, Biological specimens

Background

Biobanks have become a cornerstone of contemporary biomedical research, providing a systematic framework for collecting, processing, and preserving human biospecimens alongside rich health and demographic metadata [1, 2]. Biorepositories are essential to both basic and translational research, supporting the discovery of disease mechanisms, novel biomarkers, and innovative diagnostic and preventive approaches. By integrating comprehensive health, genetic, and phenotypic data, biobanks enable the stratification of individuals based on disease risk, pharmacogenomic profiles, and clinical characteristics. This capacity enhances the design and efficiency of clinical trials and provides a critical foundation for developing targeted therapies, thereby advancing personalized medicine and improving health outcomes [2–4]. However, a major limitation in current precision medicine research is the lack of scientific data on diverse ancestral groups. Most genomic studies have focused on populations of European descent, while African, Latin American, Middle Eastern, and many Asian populations remain significantly understudied. This gap limits our understanding of how molecular biomarkers function across different ancestries, reducing the accuracy and relevance of genetic risk assessments and therapies. Without a stronger scientific foundation that includes these populations, precision medicine cannot achieve its full potential and may unintentionally widen global health disparities [5–7].

Historically, cohort studies have been fundamental to epidemiological research, involving the long-term tracking of participants through comprehensive medical assessments at specialized centers. This systematic collection of extensive health data over many years or decades has significantly advanced our understanding of disease progression and prevention. Nearly fifty years ago, a pivotal advancement occurred with the integration of biosample collection into these longitudinal studies. In the past two decades, this approach has spurred substantial growth in population-level biobanking, with many countries launching comprehensive national initiatives. These efforts underscore the recognized value of large-scale biorepositories in supporting diverse research agendas aimed at improving health outcomes [8].

Iran sits at the geographic hinge between Western and Central Asia, and over the past millennia has absorbed successive Indo-European, Arab, and Turkic migrations that layered onto long-standing local populations. This deep record of movement and admixture has produced striking genetic, cultural, and environmental heterogeneity—traits rarely represented in current global biobank datasets [9]. The Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) represent the nation’s most comprehensive cohort study, encompassing diverse regions across the country. A central objective of PERSIAN is to establish Iran’s largest biobank to support both fundamental and epidemiological research [10]. However, creating such a biobank in a resource-limited setting like Iran presents unique challenges, including biospecimen management, securing adequate funding, navigating ethical considerations, and developing specialized infrastructure and training [1, 11]. This article explores the methodologies employed and challenges encountered in the establishment of the PERSIAN Cohort Study’s biobank, highlighting its potential to facilitate innovative research endeavors.

Methodology

Study design and setting

The PERSIAN project is an ongoing, nationwide, population-based prospective cohort that began enrolment in 2014 to characterise environmental, lifestyle, and genetic determinants of non-communicable diseases across Iran’s diverse populations. While the term “PERSIAN Cohort Study” primarily refers to the main adult cohort, the programme also encompasses several purpose-built subcohorts—Birth, Youth, Elderly, Employee and Accident cohorts—each tailored to address specific research questions, with an Eye Cohort nested within the adult component. Each subcohort operates its own biobank; however, the repository described in this article was established specifically to support the adult component. Fig. 1 summarises the hierarchical relationship between the PERSIAN Cohort and its subcohorts, alongside the number of participating centres and enrolled participants in each. Throughout this article, the term “PERSIAN Cohort” refers specifically to the adult cohort. Detailed methodologies and baseline characteristics for most subcohorts have been reported previously [12].

Organisational structure of the PERSIAN Cohort Study

Participant recruitment

Iran’s population forms a mosaic of ethnolinguistic communities—Fars (Persian), Turkic/Azari, Kurd, Lur, Balouch, Arab, Turkmen and smaller groups—distributed across ecological zones that range from humid Caspian forests and alpine plateaus to hyper-arid central deserts and semitropical southern coastlines. The PERSIAN Cohort was established to investigate how this interplay of ancestry, culture and environment shapes non-communicable-disease risk. All adults aged 35–70 years residing in one of 18 pre-selected catchments are invited (Fig. 2). Iranian descent is verified by a national identity document showing at least one Iranian-born parent. Individuals born in the catchment must have lived there for at least nine of the preceding twelve months. Those who have relocated from other regions of Iran—or, in rare cases, from outside the country—become eligible after one uninterrupted year of residence. Exclusions apply to individuals unable to provide consent or those with physical or psychological disabilities that interfere with enrollment.

Geographic distribution of the 18 PERSIAN cohort adult-recruitment sites across Iran

Sampling is adapted to settlement size and demographic composition. In the smaller towns every age-eligible resident is approached, effectively producing a local census. In large cities dominated by a single ethnic group, districts are stratified by socioeconomic level so that high-, middle- and low-income neighbourhoods are proportionally represented. Where multiple ethnicities coexist in the same city, recruitment focuses on neighbourhoods known to contain balanced representation of the groups of interest. Enrolment follows a two-stage outreach protocol. A public announcement—delivered through town-hall meetings, local media, leaflet drops and posters—raises awareness. Trained field-workers then visit each household listed in municipal registers, confirm eligibility face to face, explain study procedures and schedule a clinic appointment for every qualified resident. Temporary absence does not preclude participation: individuals receive up to three invitation attempts, and anyone who later presents voluntarily is enrolled.

The target sample size is approximately 180,000 participants (≈ 10,000 per site), providing ample statistical power for a broad spectrum of exposures and outcomes. Data are collected with a 482-item questionnaire covering sociodemographics, medical history and detailed nutrition, administered by trained interviewers under standardised protocols. Comprehensive information on the PERSIAN Cohort Study design—including sample size calculation, field implementation strategies, and baseline assessments—has been detailed in previous methodological publications [10, 12].

Infrastructure and risk management in biobanking

The PERSIAN Cohort operates a decentralized network of 18 university-affiliated mini-biobanks—one in each recruitment region—supplemented by four high-capacity central repositories, designed to align with the key principles and quality standards outlined in ISO 20387:2018 for biobanking. These four central repositories—located at the Universities of Medical Sciences in Ahvaz, Kermanshah, Shahid Beheshti, and Tehran—also feature temperature-controlled cold rooms for bulk consolidation, pre-freezing staging, and secure backup storage. The Tehran facility, the largest of the four, functions as the national archive and primary active backup within the dual-storage system. The other three central repositories currently act as local reserves and standby facilities, with the capacity to assume full backup roles if future operational demands or risk assessments warrant it. Their locations were selected to match institutional capabilities, minimize transport distances, and facilitate rapid specimen transfer during emergencies.

The PERSIAN Cohort employs a dual-storage model, that mitigates risks arising from natural disasters, equipment failures, or other unforeseen events. Since 2021, approximately 30% of the liquid biospecimens collected at enrolment—including whole blood, its processed fractions, and urine—have been relocated to the Tehran repository, thereby fully operationalising the redundancy strategy. The primary biobank facility serves as the main operational repository, actively supporting ongoing research projects. In parallel, a secondary backup facility—accessed only when specimens at the primary site are exhausted or compromised—ensures continuity of sample availability and long-term preservation.

The biobank infrastructure is purpose-built to safeguard specimen integrity over decades while streamlining routine operations. Wide, unobstructed entryways enable safe movement of cryogenic containers, and a windowless design eliminates direct solar heat gain, enhancing both environmental stability and facility security. Dedicated heating, ventilation, and air‑conditioning (HVAC) units keep processing laboratories and freezer rooms at ≤ 22 °C—an essential threshold for minimising thermal stress on equipment and biospecimens during handling [13]. Each site is also equipped with uninterruptible power-supply (UPS) systems to bridge outages and buffer voltage fluctuations. A central monitoring platform continuously records freezer temperatures, door-seal status, ambient temperature and humidity, and smoke-detector signals, issuing real-time alarms whenever any parameter deviates from validated limits. To maintain uniform procedures across all sites, the coordinating center centrally procures and distributes all reusable and disposable laboratory and storage equipment (see Supplementary File 1 for the complete inventory).

Biological sample collection and processing

Protocol design for the PERSIAN Cohort Biobank was driven by scientific priorities yet continuously balanced against real-world constraints of cost, logistics, and site capacity. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) for biospecimen collection, handling, and archival were formulated after a rigorous appraisal of internationally endorsed guidance. Foundational guidance was drawn from the 3rd and 4th editions of the International Society for Biological and Environmental Repositories (ISBER) Best Practices for Repositories [13, 14], and from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) Common Minimum Technical Standards and Protocols for Biobanks Dedicated to Cancer Research [15, 16], and validated workflows disseminated by the UK Biobank and Lifelines Biobank [16, 17]. These sources informed our operational design across all stages, including sample labeling, aliquoting, processing timelines, temperature control, and long-term storage. Adaptations were made where necessary to accommodate local infrastructure, while striving to maintain alignment with international standards to ensure biospecimen integrity and reproducibility for future research. To stay aligned with evolving standards, we regularly review updates from ISBER, IARC, UK Biobank, and similar authorities. While core protocols still remain stable, we have invested in advanced storage systems—including high-efficiency freezers, real-time monitoring, and redundant power supplies from multiple vendors—to enhance resilience and ensure long-term biospecimen integrity.

Blood sample collection and processing protocol

Blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 9:00 AM to minimize diurnal variations affecting paraclinical parameters. Participants were required to fast for 12 h prior to collection to standardize metabolic conditions. Using a Vacutainer system, we drew 25 mL of blood—first a 7 mL clot-activator tube, then three 6 mL EDTA tubes—in the WHO-recommended order for plastic vacuum tubes (clot activator before EDTA) [18]. The clot-activator tube was left at ambient temperature for 30–40 min to allow clot formation. It was then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 to 15 min in a refrigerated centrifuge to separate the serum. An aliquot of 0.5 milliliters of serum was immediately used for biochemical analyses, while the remaining serum was divided into 1-milliliter cryotubes for long-term storage. From the EDTA tubes, 3 milliliter of whole blood was stored directly in specialized tubes for potential future analyses. The remaining EDTA blood was centrifuged under the same conditions to separate plasma and buffy coat. Each of these components was carefully aliquoted into labeled cryotubes and stored at − 80 °C. In areas without on-site laboratory facilities, particularly rural sites, blood samples were collected and transported at 4–8 °C on the same day to the central laboratory for centrifugation, aliquoting, and long-term storage.

Urine samples

Urine samples were collected using the clean-catch midstream method to ensure high quality and minimize contamination. Participants provided 15 to 25 milliliters of urine in sterile containers. From each sample, 1 milliliters was stored in the biobank under controlled conditions for future research purposes.

Comprehensive testing

In addition to storing blood and urine samples, a comprehensive range of assays was conducted to evaluate various biochemical, hematological, and urinary parameters. These assays included complete blood count, lipid profile, liver function tests, kidney function tests and fasting blood glucose. For each individual in the biobank, a total of 16 samples, each with a volume of 1 milliliter, were stored.

Hair samples

Hair samples were collected from the posterior region of each participant’s scalp, close to the root, to ensure consistency across the cohort. When feasible, about 300 strands (≈ 100–150 mg) were collected; if hair length or participant preference limited collection, a minimum of 50–100 strands (≈ 50 mg), each 1–3 cm long, was accepted. Samples were required to be clean and free from chemical treatments—such as dyeing, henna, or keratin treatments—for at least three months prior to collection.

After collection, hair samples were wrapped in aluminum foil with the root ends clearly marked by technicians. The samples were then placed in specialized zip-lock bags containing a desiccant to facilitate preservation and future analysis. To prevent contamination, all sampling procedures were conducted using powder-free gloves, and scissors were sterilized with bleach-soaked cotton after each use to ensure sample integrity.

In cases where scalp hair was insufficient, particularly among male participants, hair was collected from alternative body areas such as the arms or legs. These instances were meticulously documented in the hair and nail archive list, specifying the exact body area from which the sample was obtained to maintain accuracy in data collection and analysis.

Nail samples

Nail samples were collected from all fingers and toes of each participant. Before sampling, laboratory personnel inspected the nails to ensure they had not been trimmed for at least two weeks, guaranteeing adequate length for analysis. Participants were instructed to thoroughly clean their nails to remove any dirt or contaminants. Stainless steel nail clippers were used for sampling, and to minimize the risk of cross-contamination, participants were encouraged to use their personal clippers. If clippers were shared, they were sterilized between uses with a bleach solution applied with cotton. Each nail sample—including those from fingernails and toenails—was stored separately to prevent mixing, with special attention given to samples from the big toes, which were isolated for more detailed analysis. If a participant had trimmed their nails before the scheduled visit, this was documented, and they were asked to return for sampling after a waiting period of one to ten days. Reception staff ensured that nail sampling was prioritized during the follow-up visit. Once collected, all samples were stored at room temperature in a controlled, low-humidity environment to ensure consistent handling and preservation throughout the study.

Anthropometric measurements

Essential anthropometric measurements—including weight, height, waist circumference, hip circumference, and wrist circumference—were carefully recorded in accordance with protocols set by the National Institutes of Health [19, 20]. To reduce the risk of measurement errors or biases, these measurements were taken in the morning, immediately following sample collection, while participants remained in a fasting state.

Sampling in case of desired outcomes in the Persian adult cohort

The PERSIAN Cohort Study follows each participant for at least 15 years from the time of enrollment, with plans to extend this period if funding permits. During the follow-up phase, trained personnel at each cohort site conduct annual telephone interviews to collect updated information on major health outcomes, including myocardial infarction, stroke, cancer, hospitalizations, and death. When a significant health event is reported, the study team arranges a home or hospital visit to obtain supporting medical documentation and, when necessary, perform focused clinical assessments. In the event of a participant’s death, a verbal autopsy—validated for use in the Iranian population—is conducted when medical information is not otherwise available. All reported outcomes are independently reviewed by two trained internists, with a third internist resolving any discrepancies to determine the final diagnosis or cause of death [21, 22]. When such an outcome is confirmed among participants, they are invited to provide a blood sample at associated clinics or disease registry centers, where a laboratory technician collects the samples. To facilitate this process, we compile a weekly list of these participants, including their scheduled blood collection dates, in coordination with the center’s supervisor. This list is provided to the laboratory technician. If additional analyses are required, the technician conducts the necessary tests and reports the results. For each participant during the follow-up phase, a 9-milliliter EDTA anticoagulant tube is filled. Subsequently, six separate 1-milliliter aliquots are prepared for each participant and stored in the PERSIAN Biobank for long-term preservation.

Sampling in the reassessment phase of the Persian adult cohort

To assess changes in environmental and epidemiological risk or protective factors, and considering the operational limitations of the study, re-evaluation and sampling will be conducted for 30% of the cohort participants, randomly selected at five-year intervals—specifically during the fifth, tenth, and fifteenth follow-up anniversaries. For these individuals, questionnaires on lifestyle and dietary habits, similar to those used at baseline, will be administered again. Additionally, anthropometric measurements will be retaken, and new blood and urine samples will be collected and stored. During each reassessment, 15 milliliters of blood will be collected, consisting of one 9-milliliter EDTA tube and one 6-milliliter clot activator tube. Centrifugation and sample separation will be performed according to the protocols used at study entry.

Optimizing sample storage and identification in biobanking

To ensure effective long-term storage, the PERSIAN Biobank utilizes cold and moisture-resistant boxes along with metal racks specifically tailored to the freezer systems. Recently, the biobank upgraded its storage containers to cryotubes equipped with embedded two-dimensional (2D) barcodes. These cryotubes are designed to withstand extreme temperature fluctuations, ranging from + 100 °C to −196 °C, and feature easy-to-open soft lids that provide a secure, impact-resistant seal, safeguarding their contents.

The barcoded cryotubes are strategically placed in designated boxes and racks that correspond with the freezer shelves, organized by participant. Comprehensive details of each sample—including its box, row, and shelf location within the freezer—are meticulously recorded in the biobank’s software system, ensuring precise tracking and management of biospecimens.

Prior to the adoption of 2D barcoded cryotubes, barcodes for all biobank samples were generated using LabelNice software. These barcodes, based on a unique 11-digit code specific to each participant and sample type, were verified for accuracy by cross-referencing with participant details. Once confirmed, the barcodes were printed and affixed to the cryotubes. The transition to cryotubes with embedded 2D barcodes has significantly reduced the potential for errors and enhanced operational efficiency.

Data management and security

During the design phase of the PERSIAN Adult Cohort Study, we opted to collect data entirely electronically to efficiently manage the extensive volume of information. To achieve this, the Biobank Information Management System (BIMS) was developed by the technical team at the Digestive Diseases Research Institute, Tehran University of Medical Sciences. using Visual Studio 2012 and implemented with the.NET Framework 4.5. Microsoft SQL Server 2012 was selected as the database management system to ensure secure and efficient data handling. To enhance data security, a hardware firewall was installed alongside a software security layer to prevent potential internet-based attacks. Additionally, an automatic backup system was established, with backup copies stored in three separate locations. For active systems still receiving data, backups are performed daily; for inactive systems where data entry is complete, backups occur weekly.

The database design prioritizes the confidentiality of participant information. All data are stored using a unique 11-digit code specific to each individual, ensuring that personal identifiers remain confidential. Access to the system and data entry are regulated through user roles with defined access levels, allowing individuals to access only the sections pertinent to their responsibilities. These roles are assigned by the cohort center’s administrator, who configures these settings for the operational team. To prevent the entry of incorrect information or characters that could compromise data integrity, the system includes intelligent validation features based on recommendations from the training team. This ensures that only valid data are accepted, thereby reducing errors and maintaining the quality of the stored information.

All security measures outlined for the data registration phase of the PERSIAN Adult Cohort Study are also implemented during the reassessment phase.

Quality assurance and quality control

An integrated quality-management system embeds quality assurance (QA) and quality control (QC) throughout the PERSIAN Biobank workflow. Identical equipment was centrally procured for all 18 centres, and a dedicated training team implemented harmonised SOPs; staff competence is re-certified annually via proficiency testing and refresher courses. Scheduled site audits—including periodic unannounced visits—verify adherence to protocols for specimen collection, labelling, temperature-controlled transport and storage, and electronic data capture, providing an additional safeguard against procedural drift.

During the pre-analytical phase, measures were taken to prevent errors related to sample collection, labeling, handling, transportation, and storage. In the analytical phase, rigorous monitoring and control measures were employed to ensure the accuracy of laboratory analyses performed on the biobank samples. This included the use of calibrated equipment, participation in external quality assessment programs, and the implementation of internal quality controls with each batch of analyses. Control results are plotted on Levey–Jennings charts, interpreted with Westgard rules and summarised by means, standard deviations and coefficients of variation; any value outside predefined limits triggers immediate corrective action such as instrument recalibration or targeted staff retraining. The post-analytical phase focused on minimizing errors that could occur after analysis. Integrity at this stage was safeguarded through an independent dual review of all reports prior to release, supported by the BIMS, which incorporated automated validation checks and restricted, structured data entry.

This layered QA/QC architecture couples preventive oversight with real-time verification, ensuring uniform sample quality, reliable assay performance and high-integrity data across the entire PERSIAN Biobank.

Ethical considerations

The biobank operates under rigorous ethical standards that safeguard participant rights, privacy, and welfare. Each participant signs a clear, comprehensive consent form that explains the study’s purpose, the types of biospecimens and data collected, and potential research applications. Because participants grant broad consent for future research on their stored samples, protocol changes that involve only secondary use do not require re-consent. However, in cases where new biospecimen collection is planned—such as during reassessment or following a clinical diagnosis—participants will be informed of the updated protocol in person or by phone at the time of re-contact.

All identifiers are removed, and materials are stored in secure, access-controlled facilities to prevent unauthorized use. Regular audits are conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of data protection measures and update them according to evolving legal and ethical standards.

In 2013, the design of the PERSIAN Cohort Study, which supports the biobank, was approved by the ethics committees of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, the Digestive Diseases Research Institute at Tehran University of Medical Sciences, and the ethics committees of each participating university. An independent ethics committee continues to oversee the biobank’s activities, reviewing all research proposals involving biobank resources to ensure compliance with ethical standards. This committee also monitors ongoing projects for adherence to ethical guidelines and addresses any ethical issues that arise.

Results

Between 2014 and 2020, the PERSIAN Cohort enrolled 170,564 adults across 18 study districts. Women comprised 55% of the cohort (n = 93,770) and men 45% (n = 76,794). The age distribution was broad: 22% were 35–40 years old, 35% were 41–50 years, 29% were 51–60 years, and 14% over 60 years old. Geographically, the majority of participants (over 75%) resided in urban areas, while the remaining 25% were from rural regions. Follow-up and retention

The study maintains a robust longitudinal design, achieving an overall follow-up rate of ≈ 90%, indicative of strong participant engagement across all sites.

First reassessment phase

All participants receive annual telephone follow-ups, and a randomly selected 30% subsample is invited for an in-person reassessment five years after baseline. By May 2025, 37,334 individuals (16,971 men and 20,363 women) had completed this first reassessment visit.

Discussion

The PERSIAN Cohort Study Biobank, Iran’s largest and most comprehensive biorepository, serves as a pivotal resource for advancing epidemiological and genetic research across diverse populations. Its establishment addresses the historical underrepresentation of Middle Eastern populations in global biomedical research, providing an invaluable platform for investigating the intricate interactions among genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors influencing health outcomes.

Biospecimen collection and utility

The biobank encompasses a diverse range of biological specimens, including blood, urine, hair, and nails. Blood is the most informative and widely used biospecimen in biobanking, offering high-resolution access to dynamic physiological processes. Its unique ability to yield genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data makes it central to precision medicine and disease mechanism research. While less suitable for capturing long-term accumulation of environmental toxins, blood excels at reflecting real-time systemic changes. Challenges such as invasiveness and volume limitations—especially in large-scale population studies—are acknowledged, but often addressed through protocol optimization [23, 24]. Urine is preferred for its noninvasive collection and easy accessibility. It has recently become increasingly important in the discovery of protein-based biomarkers and the monitoring of various physiological processes and diseases [25, 26]. Hair and nails, also collected noninvasively offer stability and ease of storage. They serve as effective indicators of long-term exposure to environmental toxins such as heavy metals [27–30]. Additionally, they present significant potential as sources of DNA, expanding their utility in genetic and genomic studies [31–33]. The comprehensive collection of these diverse biospecimens enables multifaceted research approaches, enhancing the potential for novel discoveries.

Considerations in sample volume and preservation

The current protocol of the PERSIAN Cohort Study Biobank has been shaped by financial and infrastructural limitations, including those imposed by international sanctions. As a result, the volume and diversity of biospecimens collected and stored are more limited compared to large-scale biobanks in high-income countries. We collect approximately 25 mL of blood, which is processed into serum, plasma, and buffy coat, and 15–25 mL of urine, of which only 1 mL is stored for long-term use. In contrast, UK Biobank collects around 45 mL of blood and 9 mL of urine, the All of Us Research Program collects approximately 50–55 mL of blood and stores 10 mL of urine, and the Lifelines Biobank collects 40–45 mL of blood and includes 24-hour urine samples, which enable more comprehensive metabolic and renal function assessments. These biobanks also use a standardized panel of vacutainer tubes for blood collection—including EDTA, serum separator (SST), lithium-heparin, sodium citrate, and acid-citrate-dextrose (ACD)—to support a wide range of downstream analyses, either through immediate laboratory assessment or long-term storage. In contrast, the PERSIAN Cohort Study currently uses only EDTA tubes for plasma and buffy coat, and clot activator tubes for serum. The choice of anticoagulant agents and preservatives critically impacts sample utility. Lithium-heparin is preferred for metabolomic profiling due to its low interference with small-molecule detection. ACD, especially when combined with DMSO, is required for the cryopreservation of viable lymphocytes, enabling future applications such as immortalized cell line production. Sodium citrate is associated with higher RNA and DNA quality, increased nucleic acid yield, and improved lymphocyte viability. It is also the anticoagulant of choice when functional clotting factor assays are required. Nonetheless, fibrinogen antigen can still be measured from EDTA-collected samples using enzyme immunoassays that detect immunologically intact fibrinogen. While EDTA may alter magnesium concentrations and interfere with cytogenetic studies—by increasing sister chromatid exchanges or decreasing the mitotic index—it remains the most widely used anticoagulant in population biobanks and constitutes the primary blood collection tube in the UK Biobank, Lifelines, and All of Us, due to its broad compatibility with DNA extraction, PCR, protein assays, and biomarker profiling [3, 17].

Another current infrastructural limitation of the PERSIAN Cohort Study Biobank is the absence of cryogenic storage systems based on liquid nitrogen (LN₂). According to ISBER guidelines, storage at temperatures ≤ − 150 °C—typically achieved in the vapor phase of LN₂ (–196 °C)—is recommended for the long-term preservation of viable cells and other highly sensitive biospecimens. With a boiling point of − 196 °C, LN₂ enables ultra-low temperature storage far below the glass transition point of water (approximately − 135 °C), beyond which molecular motion effectively ceases. These ultra-low temperatures are considered optimal for halting enzymatic activity, preventing ice crystal formation, and preserving molecular integrity over extended periods. While major biobanks in high-income countries—such as UK Biobank, Lifelines, and the All of Us Research Program—also store the majority of their biospecimens at − 80 °C, they utilize LN₂-based systems for a portion of their collections. Although LN₂-based cryogenic storage is not currently in place, the existing − 80 °C infrastructure ensures reliable preservation for most molecular applications, with future expansions aimed at enhancing cryopreservation capabilities.

Operational, Logistical, and strategic challenges

The design of the PERSIAN Cohort Biobank was directly informed by prior experience gained during the establishment of the Golestan Cohort Biobank, which was developed in collaboration with the IARC and the U.S. National Cancer Institute (NCI) [34]. During its early years, the Golestan biobank faced persistent heat and humidity, particularly during the summer months, with ambient temperatures occasionally exceeding 50 °C, rapid freezer attrition, unreliable temperature-alarm systems, slow specimen retrieval, incomplete tracking of sample movements, manual vial labelling and a fully paper-based archive. A subsequent three-year modernisation effort renovated infrastructure, expanded storage capacity, implemented BIMS and updated all standard operating procedures. These corrective measures were incorporated from the outset of the PERSIAN project, reducing start-up challenges and aligning operations with contemporary international biobanking standards.

The development of the PERSIAN Cohort Biobank unfolded under stringent financial constraints characteristic of a lower-middle-income, developing country and further compounded by international sanctions, which restricted the import of devices like ultra-low-temperature freezers, barcode scanners and monitoring devices, substantially increasing costs and extending delivery lead-times. Nevertheless, persistent negotiation and oversight allowed the team to procure most essential equipment from abroad. For components that remained unobtainable, the biobank collaborated with domestic engineering firms to design and fabricate freezers that achieved the required − 80 °C operating temperature, met the thermal-uniformity requirements for biobanking, and incorporated dual, independent refrigeration circuits to guarantee uninterrupted performance if a single compressor failed. To secure the capital resources required for both construction and long-term maintenance, the project assembled a composite funding portfolio that combined Ministry of Health allocations, university co-financing and competitive research grants.

Constructing and operating facilities across 18 provinces whose climates range from humid subtropics to arid deserts imposed additional technical demands. In Khuzestan, for example, ambient temperatures exceed 50 °C. Window-less, thermally insulated rooms, redundant HVAC systems and uninterrupted power conditioning are standard prerequisites for high-quality biobanking worldwide; however, under such extreme climatic conditions maintaining stable storage temperatures proved substantially more demanding. Continuous real-time monitoring, shortened preventive-maintenance cycles and recurrent emergency interventions were required.

Transferring and maintaining the integrity of biospecimens in a nationwide cohort requires meticulous coordination—especially when operating under diverse infrastructural and regulatory conditions. In 2021, approximately 30% of the liquid biospecimens collected during the enrolment phase—whole blood, its derivatives and urine—were relocated from the 18 regional sites to the Central Biobank at the University of Tehran. The primary challenge was preserving sample quality during transport. Each shipment needed to remain within a narrow temperature range and arrive within a defined transit time. To ensure compliance, the team conducted an online refresher workshop for all cohort centers, reinforcing key procedural requirements. Transport coordination posed additional difficulties. Because refrigerated trucks were costly and frequently unavailable, the protocol authorised two alternative modes: samples packed on dry ice inside dedicated insulated containers could be dispatched by commercial flight or inter‑city bus. Shipments by any of the three modes were allowed only after prior approval from the central coordinating team and receipt of continuous temperature logs demonstrating that the − 10 °C to + 4 °C range had been maintained throughout transit. Another key challenge at the study’s outset was recruiting and retaining participants across Iran’s diverse sociocultural landscape. Meeting this challenge required targeted outreach efforts, culturally tailored informed consent processes, and ongoing community engagement to navigate sensitivities around genetic research and data privacy, while complying with ethical and legal standards. In regions where Persian was not the primary language, bilingual staff fluent in both Persian and the local language were employed to facilitate effective communication with participants and ensure proper understanding of study procedures. The initial goal was to recruit 180,000 participants, with an equal target of 10,000 individuals per each of the 18 cohort centers. However, this was not fully achievable in several smaller or less populated cities due to logistical and demographic constraints. As a result, additional participants were enrolled from centers with greater recruitment capacity—such as Ardabil—to compensate for the shortfall. Ultimately, approximately 170,000 participants were successfully recruited. Maintaining high-quality samples across multiple sites presented another challenge, given variations in local resources and expertise; standardized protocols, regular training sessions, audits, and inter-laboratory comparisons ensured uniformity and adherence to international biobanking standards [35, 36].

Future directions and impact

The PERSIAN Cohort Biobank is poised to propel the next wave of discovery. Baseline biospecimens have already been secured from every adult participant, and further collections are planned during reassessment visits and whenever clinically significant events arise. With adequate funding, these efforts could scale to more frequent sampling, tighter protocol harmonization, and greater analytical throughput. Key upgrades would deepen the biobank’s utility: incorporating additional blood tubes such as lithium-heparin, sodium citrate, and acid-citrate-dextrose would broaden the scope of downstream assays; raising the per-participant sample volume would enable comprehensive multi-omic profiling and repeated-measures analyses; and installing liquid-nitrogen (≤ − 150 °C) cryogenic storage would allow long-term preservation of viable cells and RNA, bringing the repository into alignment with leading international biobanking standards. Collectively, these enhancements would reinforce the cohort’s longitudinal framework and unlock richer insights into population health and disease mechanisms. Future expansions may also include the collection of additional biospecimen types, such as tissue samples in the context of clinically significant events like cancer diagnoses, as well as the integration of imaging and radiological data. Such linkages would provide a more comprehensive biological and phenotypic dataset, fostering translational research in disease progression, early detection, and precision medicine.

At present, the cohort conducts annual active follow-ups: each year all participants are contacted, and those reporting a clinical event attend a study visit where the diagnosis is confirmed and—if verified—biospecimens are collected. This approach is highly valuable for slower-evolving diseases such as cancer; however, for acute events like myocardial infarction or stroke, the delay of weeks or months means the most time-sensitive molecular signals may have faded. Integrating real-time links to hospital information systems and national registries would close this gap, enabling immediate post-event sampling, sharper temporal resolution for biomarker discovery, and greater translational impact for the biobank. Establishing such integration with clinical data systems is a critical next step, as it would enable more responsive sampling, improve the temporal accuracy of biomarker discovery, and substantially increase the translational value of the biobank.

Beyond these operational enhancements, achieving global interoperability is the next critical frontier. The resource is presently confined to national infrastructure and is not linked to international open-data portals (e.g., UK Biobank) or large consortia. We are therefore assessing the technical and legal requirements for a secure, standards-based data enclave. Any implementation will proceed only if it fully complies with Iranian data-protection laws and receives all necessary regulatory and ethical approvals.

Looking forward, the PERSIAN Cohort Biobank has the potential to significantly impact public health policies and clinical practices in Iran and beyond. The wealth of data and biospecimens available can facilitate studies on noncommunicable diseases, infectious diseases, environmental health, and more. By making its protocols publicly accessible, the biobank encourages collaboration, data harmonization, and methodological transparency, which are critical for advancing scientific knowledge.

Conclusion

The establishment of the PERSIAN Cohort Biobank marks a substantial advancement in Iran’s biomedical research landscape. By systematically collecting and storing a diverse array of biological samples from a large, geographically and ethnically diverse population, the biobank serves as an invaluable resource for both national and international researchers. It not only captures the genetic, environmental, and socioeconomic diversity of the Iranian population but also sets a new standard for large-scale biobanking initiatives in resource-limited settings, paving the way for more inclusive and representative global health research.
