Automated calculation of healthcare quality and safety indicators for head and neck cancers: a multicentric study using electronic health records
Authors: Pierre Riebler, Marie Verdoux, Corentin Sinanovic, Rémi Flicoteaux, Bertrand Baujat, Françoise Colombani, Sarah Atallah, Christel Daniel, Vianney Jouhet, Emmanuelle Kempf, a CRAB initiative (CRAB: Cancer Research Application on Big Data)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo develop and validate algorithms to automate the calculation of Healthcare Quality and Safety Indicators (HQSIs) from electronic health records (EHR) multi-sources data.MethodsThree HQSIs of interest were identified by experts. The required variables were identified and algorithms for extracting these variables from various sources of EHR data, prioritized based on their quality, were performed and evaluated on the Assistance Publique - Hôpitaux de Paris (AP-HP) and Bordeaux University Hospital clinical data warehouses (CDWs). Algorithm performances (positive predictive value (PPV), accuracy, f1-score) were assessed to a manual review of 100 EHRs randomly selected among newly referred patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) in 2023.ResultsThree HQSI were computed: number of newly referred HNC, number of HNC cases treated by front line anticancer surgery and the number of resected HNC cases requiring a post-operative surgery based on ICD-10 and their French Association pour le Développement de l’Informatique en Cytologie et en Anatomie Pathologique (ADICAP) pathology codes, 6,943 and 7,112 HNC patients were identified as referred to AP-HP and Bordeaux University Hospitals between 1998 and 2023, respectively. The algorithm related to the number of newly referred HNC diagnoses in 2023 had the following performances: PPV of 37% and 87% when ICD-10 codes solely were used, up to 89% and 100% when both data sources (ICD-10 and ADICAP codes) were used, in AP-HP and Bordeaux University Hospitals, respectively. The average discrepancy rate between both data sources was 44%. Based on surgery French coding of medical procedures (CCAM) and pathology ADICAP codes, 4, 231 newly referred HNC patients were identified with a frontline cancer surgery in AP-HP, between 1998 and 2024. The accuracy of such algorithm ranged from 65% for patients identified by CCAM solely to 84% for patients identified by CCAM and ADICAP codes. Among them, 436 patients had a surgical re-intervention in the 28 following days. Floor of the mouth (OR = 1.72; CI95, 1.02–2.90), mouth (OR = 1.59; CI95, 1.07–2.38), pyriform sinus (OR = 4.83; CI95, 1.65–14.13), and facial sinus cases (OR = 0.11; CI95, 0.02–0.82) were primary tumor sites with a modified risk of surgical re-intervention, respectively.ConclusionsLeveraging data from heterogeneous sources of EHR remains promising to improve the HQSI automated measurement for HNC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03163-9.Keywords: Quality indicators, Head and neck cancer, Data warehouse, Data quality

Introduction

In 2022, head and neck cancers (HNC) represented the sixth most frequent cancer with 946,000 new cases and the sixth cause of death by cancer with 482,000 deaths worldwide [1]. The prevention of risk factors is likely to decrease the incidence of these risks, but these cancers are still a public health issue and rely on multidisciplinary and complex treatment. The quality of care provided to patients newly diagnosed with an HNC is a prognostic factor and is associated with functional long-term outcomes [2].

Healthcare Quality and Safety Indicators (HQSI) play a role in the evaluation of services and healthcare facilities taking care of cancer patients and in the improvement of clinical practices [3–5]. For instance, there have been calls for a consensus on defining quality standards for cancer care, treatment, and research in Europe, particularly focusing on cancer hospitals, centers, and networks. This has led the Organisation of European Cancer Institutes (OECI) to establish a quality assurance Accreditation and Designation Program for cancer centers, encompassing numerous HQSIs [6]. Monitoring HQSIs in HNC patients assists physicians and stakeholders in identifying areas for care improvement, enhancing practice homogeneity across different sites, and reducing costs [7]. Consequently, mastering the calculation of these indicators has become a significant challenge for healthcare establishments aiming to improve their practices. However, the reproducibility of HQSI calculations among HNC patients is lacking in the literature. Furthermore, their manual assessment is time-consuming [8, 9]. In this context, automating the computation of HQSIs offers an opportunity to standardize their production methods, facilitate the comparability of results, and save medical time otherwise dedicated to administrative tasks.

Such automation of cancer HQSI production can be performed with real-life data, namely the French national health data system (SNDS), which includes key diagnostic and treatment steps in the management of HNC patients [10]. Yet, some HQSI rely on items missing in national medico-administrative databases, such as the coding of pathology activity [11]. Therefore, data from electronic health records (EHRs) can be used to compute HQSI automatically and more exhaustively, potentially through clinical data warehouses (CDWs) [12–14]. Our hypothesis was that leveraging real-life data from EHRs could improve the quality, the exhaustivity and the reliability of the HNC patient HQSI computation, beyond the use of claims data [15].

The primary objective was to develop and validate algorithms to automate the calculation of HQSIs from a hospital cohort of HNC patients and the secondary objectives were to assess algorithms’ performance metrics and if algorithm could be transferred between two hospital CDWs.

Methods

Ethics

The work was approved by the Scientific and Ethical Committee of Assistance Publique - Hôpitaux de Paris (AP-HP) CDW (IRB00011591). We conducted and reported this study following the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) statement (Supp Data). The French Data Protection Agency (CNIL, Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés) authorized the constitution of the APHP CDW on January 19, 2017 (approval n°19800120). AP-HP declared to the CNIL a single commitment to comply to the national reference methodology MR-004 (https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000037187498) governing the processing of personal data for the purposes of studies, evaluations or research of a public interest nature that do not involve the human person, in particular studies on the reuse of data. This study, conforming to the MR-004 and being conducted using AP-HP’s CDW, was approved by APHP’s Scientific and Ethics Committee (IRB00011591) (approval ° CSE-22-24_OncOMOP-Terabase) on September 5th, 2022. Patients may, at any time and without justification, object to the use of their data for research by sharing an electronic or postal notification to the APHP IT and digital services department. Only the data that was strictly necessary and relevant to the objectives of the research have been collected and processed during this project.

Population

To compute HQSI related to HNC surgeries, we first had to identify the cohort of patients referred with yearly newly diagnosed HNC among the patients’ EHR present in the CDW. Secondly, this cohort was used to identify the subcohort of patients undergoing a HNC surgery. Finally, we used this subcohort to identify, among the second indicator, HNC cases requiring a post-operative surgery.

Cohort identification

We performed a multicentric cross-sectional study using EHR data available in the CDW of two tertiary care center: the AP-HP and the Bordeaux University Hospital, which contain data of more than 11 million and 2 million patients, respectively. We included all the patients who were referred with a HNC between the first of January 1998 and the 31st of December 2023, thanks to their International Classification of Diseases 10th edition (ICD-10) code (Appendix 1) or their French Association pour le Développement de l’Informatique en Cytologie et en Anatomie Pathologique (ADICAP) code (Appendix 2 and Supp Methods). ICD coding practices did not change at APHP and at Bordeaux University Hospital during this period. APHP is a group of 38 hospitals in Paris and we only focused on the 8 hospitals who manage HNC patients. The databases were extracted on 24th April 2024.

ADICAP codes consist of four groups of two alphanumeric characters that describe the sampling site, the tumor nature (begnin or malignant), the histological type of the tumor and the sampling mode (biopsy, partial or total organ resection). They are available in a semi-structured format into the pathology reports, and are extracted using natural language processing (NLP) methods [16].

Non-inclusion criteria were patient consulting for a second medical opinion without any hospital stay, patients without any history of pathology diagnosis identified with the ICD-10 code D48 (neoplasm with uncertain or unknown behaviour.

Exclusion criteria was the recurrence of a previous diagnosed HNC within the three years after initial diagnosis. These recurrences were identified by searching for any history of ICD-10 or ADICAP code during the three years before any occurrence of ICD-10 or ADICAP code. The three-years window was previously tested for breast cancer and assure a limited rate of false negative [17].

HQSI choice, algorithm Building and calculation

Three HQSIs of interest were chosen by a group of experts made of HNC and medical information experts involved into the multidisciplinary care of both APHP and Bordeaux University Hospital, following guidelines of academic societies such as the Organization of European Cancer Institutes (OECI) [18], the French Network of Cancer Registries (FRANCIM), and the French Institute of Cancer (INCa) [3]. We focused on the OECI criteria of identification of newly diagnosed and treated patients by tumor type. We also focused on the quality of initial HNC surgery, with the surrogate marker of the rate of short-term post-operative surgery (third indicator). For each HQSI, we identified the underlying elementary variables and the sources available to build them. The computing rules of each variable were established based on completeness and quality of the multiple available data sources (Supp Methods). To build algorithms, we used data of the eight APHP’s hospitals from 1998 to 2023. The first HQSI portability was assessed at the Bordeaux University Hospital.

To evaluate algorithm’s performances, we performed a manual chart review and use its results as the gold standard. One hundred randomly selected charts among newly referred patients referred with HNC in 2023 at APHP were manually reviewed by an expert (PR). The same procedure was carried out at Bordeaux Hospital and the project manager (CS) reviewed the files. Doubtful cases were reviewed by a surgeon expert (BB).

The positive predictive value (PPV), the accuracy (true positives and true negatives) and the f1-score of the algorithms were computed (Supp Methods). The metrics’ Confidence Interval at 95% (CI95%) were computed as follows: CI95% = [– ]. The same data set was used to evaluate each algorithm.

Identification of newly referred HNC

A newly referred HNC was defined according to 2 characteristics: 1/ the HNC topography; 2/ the absence of a HNC diagnosis during the 3 previous years to exclude patients managed not for a new HNC but for a recurrence: (Supp Fig. 1).

Identification of HNC topography

We mapped every ICD-10 code used to identify HNC patients with ADICAP classification (Appendix 3 and Supp Methods). We computed, among the population who had at least one ICD-10 code and one ADICAP code, the percentage of discordance across those codes. We assigned a theoretical ICD-10 code to patients with missing data (Appendix 4).

Identification of HNC diagnosis date

The date of hospital cancer diagnosis was defined as the date of sampling of the first available hospital pathology specimen that confirmed the HNC diagnosis. When a pathology report was available in the CDW, 3 related dates were identified: sampling and sample reception dates were extracted from these pathology reports using NLP methods and the date of creation of the pathology report was extracted from the EHR. We prioritized such data sources with the following priority: (i) sampling date, (ii) reception date, and (iii) the date of creation of the pathology report and we computed the differences between them. When no pathology report was available, we identified 3 types of ICD-10 codes related to an HNC in one patient EHR: the first occurrence of a HNC-related ICD-10 code in the patient health pathway timeline, the first occurrence of the most frequent HNC related ICD-10 code in the patient health pathway timeline, the first occurrence of the lastly used HNC-related ICD-10 code in the patient health pathway timeline. Based on the data of patients with an ICD-10 code, an ADICAP code and a sampling date available, we computed the difference between the sampling date and the start date of the hospital stay related to the 3 types of ICD-10 codes and we selected the one related to the smallest difference.

Identification of HNC cases treated by anticancer surgery

These cases were identified among the cases identified by the first indicator. The expert committee identified the codes of the French coding of medical procedures “Common Classification for Medical Acts” (CCAM) related to a HNC surgical excision. The ADICAP code was extracted to identify the samples obtained from a surgical excision. We identified the occurrence of one of these codes from ninety days before to ninety days after the hospital diagnosis date.

Identification of the resected HNC cases requiring a post-operative surgery

These cases were identified among the cases identified by the second HQSI. We identified the codes of the CCAM related to a surgical re-intervention after a HNC surgery. We considered as post-operative surgical re-interventions the cases with one of these codes occurring between the day of the surgery and 28 days later. We focused on the cancers that had been identified by two methods for the diagnosis and for the surgery as a first line treatment. For each ICD-10 location, we computed the proportion of surgical re-intervention and the Odd-Ratio in reference to all other head and neck cancers aggregated and the CI95% of undergoing such a surgery.

Results

Cohort identification

Overall, 6,943 HNC patients referred to AP-HP were identified by at least one method between 1998 and 2023: 6,050 patients (87%) had at least one ICD-10 code with an average of 8 distinct codes by patient and a maximum of 159 codes; 4,485 patients (65%) had at least one ADICAP code with an average of 1,7 codes by patient and a maximum of 13 codes (Fig. 1). At the Bordeaux Hospital, 7,112 HNC patients were identified by at least one method between 2005 and 2023: 5,742 (81%) had at least one ICD-10 code and 5,877 (83%) had at least one ADICAP code (Fig. 1).

Number of yearly newly referred HNC patients

The 6,943 and 7,112 patients presented a total of 7,139 and 7,410 newly referred HNC diagnoses at the AP-HP and the Bordeaux Hospital, respectively (Fig. 2 and Supp Fig. 2). In 2023, we identified 718 and 398 cancers of the lips, oral cavity and pharynx newly; 85 and 55 cancers of the sinuses, nasal cavities and ear newly; 112 and 66 cancers of the larynx newly managed at the AP-HP and at the Bordeaux Hospital, respectively. Table 1 summarizes the metrics for each identification algorithm. The PPV related to newly referred diagnoses in 2023 ranged from 37% at AP-HP and 87% at the Bordeaux Hospital for cancers identified solely by ICD-10 to 89% at AP-HP and 100% at the Bordeaux Hospital for cancers identified by ICD-10 and ADICAP. NPV couldn’t be computed for this HQSI because we couldn’t use hospital registers to identify false negatives. Among the population who had at least one ICD-10 code and one ADICAP code, the percentage of discordance for the anatomical localization between every ICD-10 code and their related ADICAP code ranged from 16 to 92%, for an average of 44%. When using anatomical groups (lips, oral cavity, pharynx vs. larynx vs. ear, nasal cavity, sinuses) we were able to lower the rate of discordance. Number of resected HNC cases requiring a post-operative surgery

We identified 70 CCAM codes compatible with a surgical re-intervention due to a complication after an HNC excision (Appendix 6). Overall, 436 patients (12%) and 439 cancers (12%) were identified between 1998 and 2023 (Fig. 3). Due to the rarity of the event, we could not compute the performance of this algorithm. Supp Figs. 5 and 6 display the predictive value of the tumor location on the post-operative re-intervention risk. The pyriform sinuses had a risk of surgical re-intervention of 43%. Three tumor anatomical associated with an increased risk of post-operative surgical re-intervention: oral cavity (C06) (OR = 1.59; CI95 = [1.07; 2.38]), mouth floor (C04) (OR = 1.72; CI95 = [1.02; 2.90]) and pyriform sinuses (C12) (OR = 4.83; CI95 = [1.65; 14.13]). One anatomical location was statistically associated with a lower risk of surgical re-intervention: sinuses (C31) (OR = 0.11; CI95 = [0.02; 0.82]). The absence of events for C00, C08 and C11 ICD-10 codes did not allow us to compute the related CI95.

Discussion

We developed algorithms to automate the calculation of 3 HQSIs from an hospital cohort of HNC patients built from multi-source EHR data available. We optimized the performance of the algorithms in prioritizing the sources of EHR data based on their availability and quality. We highlighted sources’ quality issues through sources’ discrepancies. The rules we proposed to define our algorithms were based on experts’ opinion and on the analysis of discrepancies. The performances of the algorithms could be improved thanks to the crossing of multiple sources of information. Unfortunately, algorithm’s performances remained insufficient to allow their implementation, and future projects will aim to improve their performances. Our algorithms did not use machine learning methods, but their use could broaden the sources of data using unstructured data that are present in medical reports. Some patients were only identified with their ICD-10 codes: in some cases, the biopsy was not performed at APHP or at Bordeaux University Hospital and the patients were referred at these tertiary care centers for the HNC treatment. This situation explains why pathology reports may not be available in the patient’s EHR. Also, we had the surprise to find that some reports were available in the CDW but that the pipelines used to anonymise the report made it the dates not readable [19]. This highlight the fact that despite a clinical or pathological confirmation, the information might be lacking in the EHR or CDW. We could not evaluate the overlaping of pathology report with ICD-10 and ADICAP since we could not identify specific HNC pathology reports from other pathology reports. This could be a way to improve our algorithm since our pipeline used to extract ADICAP codes might fail to identify few ADICAP codes inside the pathology reports. Both team agreed not to extract demographic data since age and sex are not a challenge for a surgery and that the decision for a surgical treatment mainly relies on patients’ general condition and comorbidities.

For the second HQSI, we used a window of ninety days before to ninety days after HNC diagnosis date. This can be surprising at first, but we noticed that the HNC surgery date was sometimes earlier than the HNC diagnosis date we used. After manual review of the charts, we found two factors that may explain this result: (1) the need to sometimes approximate the diagnosis date when no pathology report was available and (2) coding errors or delay.

For the last HQSI, we were not able to find an international recommendation for the the follow up duration and we choose 28 days which was the duration used in routine in our institutions. Some studies use a duration of 30 days, and we believed that for HNC surgery they would be no major differences with 28 days. Nonetheless, having a national or international standard would allow better comparison between different healthcare facilities.

Due to a lack of human resources only the portability of the algorithm used to identify the cohort was tested and the portability of the indicators linked to surgical procedures are in progress and we should have the results next year.

Also, we were not able to compare the results of this HQSI to hospital’s registers since this HQSI was not routinely computed at APHP and Bordeaux University Hospital. Moreover, most of the available literature reports complications of very specific situations (e.g., comparing different surgical approaches, rate of complication after reconstructive surgery) and would make irrelevant the comparison with our HQSI which address to any HNC surgery.

Our validation sample was limited to 100 files which is small. This was due to the high-consuming time of file reviewing and the availability of only one reviewer in each centre. Having bigger sample would increase the reliability of our results.

An increasing number of initiatives for HQSI automatization are emerging and most of them are based on two data sources: medico-administrative data, including vital status [11–15], heterogeneous data source of EHRs [20] which quality may be disputable. Their quick spread among hospitals allowed the automatization of the HQSI calculation, accompanied, notably in the US through the project Meaningful Use [21], by a policy of incentivizing healthcare facilities based on performance. In this study, we developed algorithms to automate the calculation of HQSIs from a hospital cohort of HNC patients built from multi-source EHR data available at the APHP’s CDW. Recent reviews report similar initiatives, approaches, methodologies, and automation outcomes of HQSI from EHR in various fields: cancer [5, 22], infectious diseases [23], pain management [24], diabetes [25], gastroenterology [26], nursing care [27], cardiology [28, 29], nephrology [12, 30, 31], clinical laboratories [32, 33] and rheumatology [34, 35].

We identified 3 HQSIs related to HNC patients’ care management. Our methodology for selecting and specifying HQSI in a specific pathology relied on an approach combining literature analysis and consensus among experts for the identification and specifications of the HQSI [10, 13]. The selection and specification of indicators, often driven by institutional agencies evaluating healthcare quality, is a crucial step that significantly influences the perceived effectiveness of improvement initiatives, sometimes in a biased manner [36]. This could lead to misalignment for entities focused on population health management [10].

We built an algorithm to identify the target population for which HQSIs will be calculated. In similar studies, such phenotyping algorithms are derived from demographic, clinical and sometimes management data [22]. Some articles address the issues related to the use of specific data sources [31]. The quality of data sources which enable the consolidation of algorithm-based variables is essential for calculating HQSI. A recent review identified the facilitating factors for using EHR in automating the HQSI calculation across 3 dimensions: quality and safety of care, management and efficiency. The potential automation rate of HQSIs from EHR data was assessed at 22.3% and the most useful EHR data for this purpose were identified [13]. In our study, we assessed the overlapping of different data sources to identify HNC diagnosis and surgery, and we assessed the quality of different dates to prioritize the source used for the diagnosis date, with the aim to develop HQSI computation algorithms.

The three indicators were successfully calculated, and the performances of the first one were evaluated, as a proof of concept. The best performances were obtained for cancers identified by multiple sources. Sharing the algorithm and implementing it in the CDW of another University Hospital enabled to assess the portability of the algorithm and its external validation. The results observed for the first indicator between the two centres looked similar. There was a difference, with a higher proportion of cases identified by an ADICAP code solely at Bordeaux Hospital, than at AP-HP. This difference can be explained by the fact that prior to 2010, diagnosis data from the ICD-10 was not fully integrated into the Bordeaux Hospital CDW.

We observed relatively poor performances of our algorithm used to identify the newly referred HNC patients, with approximately one patient out of three that was falsely identified. Most of the false positive identifications occurred in the sub-cohort that was identified by only one method. Nevertheless, we had high performances for patients identified by two methods. We could also compare the concordance of ICD-10 and ADICAP classifications. Several factors could explain the poor performances observed: (i) complex and imperfect ICD-10 rules of coding due to numerous proposed anatomical sub-localizations and the confusion with other cancers (e.g., skin cancers of lips and nose); (ii) coding errors of the users such as patients who were attributed an ICD-10 code of HNC for highly suspicious lesions that revealed to be a benign tumor; (iii) imperfect algorithms that identify hospital stays not related to HNC care on a patient with a medical history of a HNC.

Several ways may improve our first algorithm: (i) to exclude anatomical locations related to high rate of false positives; (ii) to exclude cases with a hospital stay related to an hemopathy; (iii) to restrict our analysis to the level of the hospital services (Head and Neck Surgery, Maxillo-facial surgery, oncology and radiotherapy) instead of the hospital level. The first option may decrease the rate of false positive cases, as contiguous primary tumor sites are likely to be associated with such misclassification in the APHP CDW. The second option may decrease the false positive due to cancers with a same topography but with distinct histologies, such as the difference between carcinoma and lymphoma of the same organ. The third option may decrease the patients with a history of HNC, whose current hospital visit relates to another condition.

Although we did not compute the performance for the second surgical HQSI, our results for this HQSI seemed consistent with the previous results. A way to make this HQSI more accurate would be to differentiate surgeries specifically performed for cancer from those that can also be performed for indications other than cancer. Yet, such algorithms were based on the CCAM French coding of medical procedures and the ADICAP French pathology thesaurus because international standards such as SNOMED-CT was not available in our CDW, which limits the reproducibility of such a strategy abroad. In this case, the mapping with local coding methods of surgery and pathology procedures should be performed in each country. Among other limitations, we could not assess the rate of false negative cases for the first indicator, as we had no access to cancer registries with incidence data. Finally, the validation sample size was limited to 100 EHRs and performed by a unique medical expert manually. Such validation might suffer coding errors and could be improved by a double blind EHR classification.

Some studies addressed the topic of data source quality and the challenge of “reimagining” the EHR to establish an assertive policy for collecting high-quality data at the source [27]. Other studies advocated for the establishment and consolidation of hospital data registries to enhance the quality of data used for HQSI calculations [28, 35]. In the field of HNC, Ebbers and al developed automated computation methods to derive several HNC HQSI and had high rates of successful automated variables extraction compared to manual variable extraction with the lowest rate concerning dates of referral (53.9%) and of physiotherapist consult (20.0%). The authors only used structured variables of a single tertiary oncologic center and the manual validation showed a high quality of the database. Achieving such high rates of data quality becomes harder when addressing to CDW gathering multiple healthcare facilities such as APHP CDW, especially concerning unstructured data. We believe that this point explains the higher performance of our algorithm at Bordeaux Hospital (one hospital) compared to APHP (eight hospitals).

The IDEA4RC (Intelligent ecosystem to improve the governance, the sharing and the re-use of health data for Rare Cancers) is a European project which aims to federate rare cancers’ data of twelve European countries into making harmonized distributed databases findable, accessible and reusable for data users. Database to make easier clinical research about rare cancers [37]. Our work could find applications within the framework of this project to compute variables using multi-sources data and through the methods of external validation and of portability evaluation that we proposed.

Conclusion

The automatic calculation of HQSI can be performed using multiple EHR data sources. Yet, a complex task of inter-source consistency assessment is needed to optimize HQSIs calculation algorithm by prioritizing the data sources based on the quality of the data. Similarly, only the portability of the cohort identification algorithm successfully tested on other CDWs appears promising and more work has to be done concerning the portability of HQSI related to surgical procedures.
