Orthopedic Mechanophenotype: A Structured Narrative Review of Multiscale Biomechanics, Mechanotransduction, and Clinical Decision‐Making
Authors: Zihao Lan, Zhipeng Mu, Hongjian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOrthopedic disorders are still classified mainly by anatomy or end‐stage morphology, yet osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, fracture nonunion, spinal degeneration, and bone‐implant interface failure share a common mechanobiological logic: external load is filtered by hierarchical tissue architecture, converted into cell‐scale signals, and integrated with inflammation, metabolism, and repair capacity to determine whether adaptation or failure occurs. This structured narrative review synthesizes recent evidence on multiscale biomechanics, finite element analysis (FEA), mechanotransduction biology, tissue engineering, and emerging digital‐twin approaches. Rather than introducing a separate disease entity, we define the orthopedic mechanophenotype as a clinically oriented integrative framework that describes the disease‐relevant state generated by three interacting axes: load exposure, tissue susceptibility, and biological adaptability. We summarize how this three‐axis state differs across bone, articular cartilage and the osteochondral unit, the intervertebral disc, fracture repair, and bone‐implant interfaces. Particular emphasis is placed on evidence hierarchy, model assumptions, and persistent uncertainties, including timescale mismatch, idealized boundary conditions, incomplete cross‐scale validation, and overinterpretation of cell‐level causality from organ‐level surrogates. We further illustrate how mechanophenotyping can be integrated with existing clinical pathways for osteoarthritis load redistribution, fracture fixation and rehabilitation, and spinal or implant‐related planning. The central argument is that future progress will depend less on accumulating isolated mechanical variables and more on validated cross‐scale markers that connect real‐world loading, patient‐specific structure, and stage‐dependent biology to actionable orthopedic decisions.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders remain a leading cause of pain, disability, and healthcare utilization worldwide, yet their mechanistic interpretation in both research and clinical practice remains fragmented. Osteoporosis is often reduced to low bone mineral density, osteoarthritis to cartilage wear and inflammation, fracture nonunion to inadequate biological healing, and spinal degeneration to disc dehydration or tissue aging. These labels are clinically useful, but they are mechanistically incomplete because they do not explain why similar anatomic findings may produce different symptoms, progression rates, or treatment responses [1, 2, 3]. Mechanobiology reframes this problem by showing that force is not simply an exposure variable; it is a biologically encoded regulator whose effect depends on how load is transmitted through hierarchical structure and how cells adapt to that local mechanical environment [4, 5, 6]. This point is especially relevant in orthopedics because load‐bearing tissues are anisotropic, adaptive, and path‐dependent: geometry, material composition, porosity, fluid transport, cellular sensing, and repair reserve all change across lifespan, disease stage, and treatment history [3, 4, 7, 8].

Over the past decade, the methodological landscape has also changed. FEA and hybrid computational models now estimate tissue stress, strain, fluid pressure, interface micromotion, and cellular‐scale surrogates from organ‐level kinematics or imaging [2, 9, 10, 11]. In parallel, atomic force microscopy, mechanically tunable hydrogels, microdevices, organotypic bioreactors, and multi‐omics pipelines permit more precise interrogation of compression, shear, stretch, stiffness, osmotic challenge, and interface loading than was previously possible [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Explainable artificial intelligence and digital‐twin thinking have further expanded the field from mechanism discovery toward patient‐specific prediction and intervention planning [17, 18, 19, 20]. However, these advances also expose a central weakness: the literature contains many local associations but far fewer validated chains that connect real‐world loading, internal mechanical states, biological response, and clinically meaningful outcomes. A critical narrative synthesis is therefore needed because translational claims are sometimes stronger than the evidence supporting them, particularly when simplified boundary conditions, short‐term cell systems, animal models, or idealized simulations are extrapolated directly to patient selection, surgical planning, or rehabilitation dosing [21, 22, 23, 24].

In this structured narrative review, we synthesize English‐language literature selected according to the predefined criteria described below and use the orthopedic mechanophenotype as an integrative clinical framework rather than as a newly named disease category. We define it as the clinically relevant state produced when three axes interact: the mechanical loads to which a patient is exposed, the tissue features that make those loads harmful or tolerable, and the biological capacity for adaptation or repair. This definition is intended to connect prior mechanobiology, biomechanics, and orthopedic mechanomedicine into a clinically usable framework while preserving the uncertainty that still surrounds cross‐scale validation. Using this framework, we first summarize hierarchical load transfer and signal integration in bone, cartilage, and the intervertebral disc. We then compare osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, fracture healing/nonunion, spinal degeneration, and bone‐implant interfaces as distinct but partially convergent mechanobiological modules, with explicit attention to evidence strength, controversy, and clinical usability. Finally, we outline how mechanophenotyping can realistically contribute to patient stratification, surgical planning, rehabilitation prescription, and device selection at the present time [19, 20, 25].

Literature Inclusion Criteria

This review was designed as a structured narrative review rather than a quantitative systematic review or meta‐analysis. Literature was identified through searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar, supplemented by reference tracking of key articles. The search focused on English‐language publications addressing orthopedic mechanobiology, multiscale biomechanics, mechanotransduction, finite element or computational modeling, digital‐twin approaches, tissue engineering, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, fracture healing/nonunion, intervertebral disc degeneration, and bone‐implant interfaces. Priority was given to peer‐reviewed articles published from 2019 onward, while earlier landmark studies were retained when they provided foundational concepts or methods that remained directly relevant. Studies were included when they (i) examined load transmission, mechanical microenvironments, mechanosensing pathways, or biomechanical modeling in musculoskeletal tissues; (ii) linked mechanical variables with biological response, disease progression, repair, or clinical intervention; or (iii) offered translational implications for patient stratification, rehabilitation, surgery, implant/device planning, or regenerative strategies. Studies were excluded if they were unrelated to orthopedic tissues, focused only on general molecular biology without a mechanical component, presented insufficient methodological detail, were non‐peer‐reviewed commentaries without substantive data or synthesis, or were not available in English. Because of heterogeneity in study designs, mechanical endpoints, and disease contexts, no pooled quantitative analysis was undertaken; instead, evidence was narratively synthesized according to tissue type, disease module, methodological toolchain, and translational relevance.

Conceptual Basis of Multiscale Orthopedic Mechanobiology

Hierarchical Load Transfer and Amplification

A defining property of orthopedic tissues is that the mechanical input experienced by a joint, bone, or spinal segment is not identical to the stimulus perceived by resident cells. Hierarchical organization filters and redistributes forces across scales. In cortical and trabecular bone, organ‐level gait loading becomes spatially heterogeneous strain, fluid flow, and matrix deformation within the lacuno‐canalicular network, thereby changing the effective stimulus seen by osteocytes [9, 11, 26, 27]. In articular cartilage, load is shared between the solid matrix and interstitial fluid, so changes in contact area, loading rate, and permeability alter not only tissue deformation but also nutrient transport and local osmotic conditions [7, 28, 29, 30]. In the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus, fiber architecture, hydration, and osmotic swelling determine how axial compression and torsion are converted into local tensile, shear, and pressure‐related signals [31, 32, 33, 34]. The same external action—walking, jumping, malalignment, or implant loading—can therefore generate very different internal environments depending on geometry, tissue quality, and disease stage.

This cross‐scale filtering has two consequences. Biologically, cells respond to proximate surrogates such as integrin tension, ion‐channel activation, cytoskeletal prestress, nuclear deformation, fluid shear, and matrix tethering rather than to nominal joint reaction force alone [4, 6, 35, 36]. Clinically, patients with apparently similar imaging findings or gait abnormalities may occupy different internal states because their stress distribution, fluid transport, or adaptation thresholds differ. This helps explain structure–symptom discordance in osteoarthritis and degenerative spinal disease and also explains why the same rehabilitation or implant strategy can work well in one subgroup but disappoint in another [21, 37, 38].

Recent reviews emphasize that hierarchical mechanics should not be understood as a simple “zooming in” exercise but as a coupled systems problem in which architecture both transmits and regulates biology [2, 3, 19, 22]. In bone, age‐related changes in geometry and material properties modify how tissue‐level strain is amplified toward the cellular scale, shifting remodeling thresholds before gross fracture occurs [9, 11, 39]. In cartilage, fluid‐pressure–dominated load support is protective under physiological conditions, yet the same mechanism can become pathogenic when altered contact mechanics or matrix degeneration disrupt permeability and metabolic homeostasis [7, 28, 40]. In the disc, omission of elastic‐fiber hierarchy or realistic osmotic behavior can materially distort predicted loading pathways and therefore the inferred biology of degeneration and repair [32, 33, 34, 41, 42]. A recurring limitation is that many models represent the filtering process convincingly at one scale but validate it incompletely across adjacent scales; this is precisely where translational overclaim often begins.

Mechanosensing, Signal Integration, and Immune–Metabolic Coupling

At the cellular level, orthopedic mechanobiology is organized around several interconnected sensing and signal‐conversion systems. These include integrin‐based adhesions, mechanosensitive ion channels, the actin–microtubule cytoskeleton, the LINC complex, the nuclear lamina, and downstream transcriptional programs such as YAP/TAZ, MRTF‐A/SRF, and Rho/ROCK‐associated signaling [4, 35, 36, 40, 43]. These systems are not functionally redundant. Rather, they decode different features of the local environment—matrix stiffness, stretch, shear, confinement, membrane tension, or interface geometry—and integrate them into context‐dependent transcriptional, metabolic, and inflammatory responses.

This integration is especially important because mechanical signaling rarely acts in isolation. Mechanical imbalance can amplify inflammation, senescence, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered cell fate, while inflammatory or metabolic disease can lower mechanical competence and change adaptation thresholds [44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. In diabetic bone disease, altered mineral chemistry, cortical structure, and osteocyte density coexist with impaired tissue toughness, illustrating that metabolic disturbance and mechanical fragility are interacting layers of one problem rather than parallel phenomena [46, 47, 49]. In osteoarthritis, abnormal joint loading interacts with inflammatory mediators and chondrocyte mechanosensitive pathways to shape matrix catabolism, cell death, and pain‐relevant tissue changes [37, 38, 40, 50]. In disc degeneration, altered load transmission, inflammatory activation, hypoxic stress, and matrix remodeling form a self‐reinforcing cascade rather than separate pathogenic compartments [31, 42, 48].

A theme that deserves more emphasis is time dependence. Cells can show stage‐specific and sometimes biphasic responses to the same cue, and tissues adapt to their loading history. This is evident in immune cells, where mechanical context affects activation, migration, and functional polarization over time [45, 51]. The same principle likely explains why similar micromotion may be beneficial in early fracture repair but harmful in later healing or at unstable implant interfaces [52, 53, 54]. Homeostasis is therefore better understood as a dynamic target range shaped by prior loading, age, co‐morbidity, and repair stage rather than as a fixed set point [3, 39, 55]. Any clinically useful model must represent not only how much force is present, but when it acts, how long it persists, and whether the local biological state is still adaptive. Many current studies acknowledge this in principle but still rely on static snapshots, which limits their direct use for treatment timing.

A Clinically Oriented Construct: The Orthopedic Mechanophenotype

For clinical translation, the orthopedic mechanophenotype can be understood as a patient‐ and tissue‐level state rather than a single biomarker or a synonym for biomechanics. It describes how a given external load becomes biologically meaningful after being filtered by tissue structure and interpreted by adaptive cellular systems. The first axis is load exposure, including magnitude, direction, rate, frequency, duration, and spatial distribution of forces generated by gait, posture, muscle function, trauma, rehabilitation, fixation constructs, or implanted devices. The second axis is tissue susceptibility, including geometry, matrix composition, hydration, mineralization, architecture, defect morphology, and interface conditions that determine how load is internally distributed. The third axis is biological adaptability, including the capacity of resident cells, immune cells, vasculature, and systemic metabolism to sense, buffer, remodel, or recover from that internal environment [4, 6, 27, 40, 45]. The three axes are interactive rather than additive. Excess load can damage a structurally vulnerable tissue, but the same load may be tolerated if matrix architecture, fluid transport, and repair reserve are preserved. Conversely, moderate load can become pathogenic when diabetes, inflammation, sarcopenia, senescence, impaired vascularity, or previous treatment reduces adaptive reserve [44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. This crosstalk distinguishes the mechanophenotype from general orthopedic biomechanics: biomechanics describes the forces and structural responses, whereas mechanophenotyping asks which mechanical‐biological state is present, which axis dominates current risk, and which therapeutic lever is most modifiable [54, 55]. Operationally, clinicians can approximate a mechanophenotype by asking four linked questions: (i) what is the dominant loading problem—underloading, overload, instability, malalignment, abnormal motion, or interface mismatch; (ii) which structural feature makes the tissue vulnerable—low bone mass, pore geometry, cartilage permeability, annulus fibrosus disruption, endplate compromise, fracture gap, or implant surface mismatch; (iii) how much adaptive reserve remains, considering age, inflammation, diabetes, sarcopenia, infection, vascularity, and healing stage; and (iv) which lever can be modified now—exercise, bracing, gait retraining, offloading, osteotomy, fixation stiffness, implant design, staged weight bearing, or a biologically targeted adjunct [20, 62, 63, 64, 65]. This logic avoids the simplistic dichotomy of “good load” versus “bad load” because the same external demand may be protective in one mechanophenotype and harmful in another [57, 59, 60, 61].

Tissue‐Specific Multiscale Rules in Orthopedics

Bone: Adaptive Mineralized Composite and Osteocyte‐Centered Mechanoregulation

Bone remains the canonical example of an adaptive load‐bearing tissue. Its competence derives from the coupling of mineralized collagen fibrils, porosity, trabecular and cortical architecture, vascularity, and osteocyte network organization [66, 67, 68]. Importantly, this competence is anisotropic and site‐specific. The femoral neck, vertebrae, and long‐bone diaphysis experience different combinations of compression, bending, torsion, and shear, which means that “bone strength” cannot be inferred from a single scalar descriptor [9, 69, 70]. This is one reason why reliance on bone mineral density alone fails to fully capture fracture risk. Current cross‐scale studies suggest that aging and disease alter not only the absolute tissue response but also the amplification rules by which tissue strain is transmitted to osteocytes, thereby changing the biological meaning of a given organ‐level load [9, 11, 39].

Osteocytes are central to this process. Embedded within lacunae and connected through the canalicular network, they experience a mechanically filtered environment dominated by matrix deformation, fluid shear, and pericellular interactions [11, 27]. Integrin signaling, cytoskeletal continuity, mechanosensitive ion channels, LINC‐complex coupling, and YAP/TAZ‐dependent transcriptional programs determine whether local loading is interpreted as anabolic, homeostatic, or injurious [4, 27, 36, 56]. For example, osteocyte integrin alpha5 signaling has been shown to participate in the anabolic response to mechanical loading in mice, supporting the view that tissue‐level strain must be translated through specific cellular receptors before remodeling can occur [27]. Bone adaptation is therefore an information‐processing problem as much as a structural one: architecture conditions the signal, osteocytes decode it, and remodeling implements the response. Direct human validation of exact thresholds remains limited, but the pathway‐level evidence is now strong enough to justify integrating bone quality, geometry, loading exposure, and biological reserve in risk interpretation.

Recent work has broadened bone mechanobiology beyond remodeling alone. Mechanical stimuli can regulate osteogenesis in regenerative settings, influence macrophage behavior and inflammatory resolution, and interact with scaffold properties during defect repair [12, 45, 54, 71, 72]. Low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound, cyclic compression, and bioreactor‐based loading all exemplify the idea that mechanically defined regimens can serve as therapeutic inputs rather than merely experimental perturbations [12, 73, 74]. However, translation remains constrained by a recurring weakness: nominal device parameters are often reported without a credible estimate of the actual cellular field, such as local strain, fluid shear, or interface stress. This weakens comparability across studies and partly explains why promising preclinical signals have not yet produced standardized clinical dosing rules [6, 12].

Another emerging theme is the interface between bone quality and systemic biology. In diabetic osteoporosis and related fragility states, changes in mineral chemistry, collagen–mineral interaction, osteocyte density, and microdamage behavior can all contribute to embrittlement [46, 47, 49]. Bone organoid and single‐cell studies further suggest that skeletal pathology should not be conceptualized solely as deficient mass or impaired turnover, but as a failure of coordinated material, cellular, metabolic, and immune adaptation [13, 75, 76]. For clinicians, the practical implication is not that mechanophenotyping has replaced BMD, but that BMD should be interpreted alongside geometry, loading exposure, fall context, and biological reserve when estimating risk or tailoring exercise‐based intervention.

Articular Cartilage and the Osteochondral Unit: Load Sharing, Transport, and Metabolic Vulnerability

Cartilage mechanobiology differs from bone in a fundamental way: physiological function depends on a fluid‐rich matrix that supports load while permitting low‐friction movement and molecular transport [7, 28, 29]. The balance between interstitial fluid pressurization, matrix deformation, and osmotic swelling is central to chondrocyte homeostasis. Under healthy conditions, this system limits excessive solid‐matrix strain while enabling nutrient and metabolite exchange. Under diseased conditions, changes in permeability, collagen organization, proteoglycan content, or contact mechanics can destabilize the same protective mechanism and shift the tissue toward catabolism and metabolic stress [7, 28, 40]. Cartilage degeneration is therefore better understood as a failure of integrated load sharing and mechano‐metabolic regulation than as simple mechanical wear.

This has direct implications for the osteochondral unit and the whole‐joint concept. Mechanical disturbances originating in alignment, gait, muscle coordination, meniscal function, or subchondral bone architecture can alter local contact patterns and fluid‐pressure distributions long before gross cartilage loss becomes evident [21, 37, 77, 78]. Meanwhile, the cellular response is mediated through Piezo channels, integrin‐associated adhesions, cytoskeletal signaling, YAP/TAZ‐related pathways, and inflammatory crosstalk, meaning that overload and inflammatory sensitization should be viewed as mutually reinforcing rather than competing explanations [37, 40, 50, 56, 58]. Recent original studies support this interpretation: Piezo1‐mediated mechanotransduction can drive chondrocyte catabolic activity and mechanical‐function loss in early OA models, while YAP/TAZ‐related signaling helps link compressive loading to inflammatory responses in chondrocytes [56, 58]. The strongest evidence supports a syndrome‐level view of OA as a mechanically stratified whole‐joint disorder; the weakest evidence remains the patient‐specific mapping from organ‐level descriptors, such as gait moments or static alignment, to the local chondrocyte environment.

The translational consequence is that successful cartilage repair or OA intervention must restore more than a nominal compressive modulus. Mechanical gradients, transport, and the local cell microenvironment matter. Reviews in cartilage biomechanics and tissue engineering have highlighted persistent discrepancies between microscale and bulk‐scale stiffness measurements, meaning that a construct optimized for one scale may fail at another [7]. Likewise, chemo‐mechano‐biological models indicate that tissue evolution depends on coupling between composition, transport, and loading history rather than on static structural descriptors alone [29, 30]. This helps explain why some interventions improve symptoms transiently yet show poor durability. They modify gross mechanics without adequately restoring the conditions required for long‐term cellular homeostasis. For clinicians, the implication is straightforward: a mechanically informed OA plan should combine structural phenotype, alignment, gait, and symptom irritability rather than rely on radiographic grade alone.

Intervertebral Disc and Associated Interfaces: Hydrated Fiber Architecture Under Multiphysics Loading

The intervertebral disc is a particularly demanding test case because its function depends on hydration, proteoglycan content, annulus‐fibrosus fiber architecture, nucleus‐pulposus swelling, endplate transport, and whole‐spine kinematics [31, 32, 34, 42]. Changes in any one of these factors reshape the others. Reduced hydration alters pressurization; altered pressurization changes annular loading; annular damage changes fiber recruitment; and cell stress, senescence, and matrix remodeling feed back into structure. This is why recent literature increasingly frames disc degeneration as a multiphysics, multiscale, and multicellular process rather than a simple age‐related loss of water [31, 42, 48].

Work on annulus‐fibrosus mechanics has shown that specimen geometry, fiber–matrix interactions, loading rate, and osmotic state strongly influence apparent modulus and Poisson‐like behavior [32, 41, 79]. These findings are important not merely as experimental cautions but as mechanobiological clues: they indicate that degeneration‐related changes in microstructure and hydration can fundamentally alter how physiological loads are partitioned across the lamellae. For the nucleus pulposus, the recognition of elastic‐fiber hierarchy has been particularly influential. Multiple groups now argue that historical underrepresentation of elastic fibers in disc models and tissue‐engineering scaffolds is one reason why many regenerative designs have failed to reproduce native mechanics [33, 34, 80]. This is a strong example of how missing microscale realism can misdirect translational strategy at the macroscale.

Clinical spinal degeneration further demonstrates the need to connect regional biomechanics with device performance. Artificial disc replacement and other motion‐preserving technologies alter range of motion, interface strain, and adjacent‐segment mechanics in ways that may be beneficial for some phenotypes but problematic for others [81, 82]. Yet much of the evidence still relies on idealized simulations, short‐term kinematic endpoints, or isolated structural readouts. A more mature framework would integrate endplate transport, disc composition, segmental motion, sagittal alignment, and patient‐specific loading history when evaluating degeneration risk or device suitability. At present, spinal mechanophenotyping is clinically promising but less mature than OA load redistribution or fracture construct optimization (Table 2).

Disease Modules and Their Actionable Mechanophenotypes

Osteoporosis and Diabetic Bone Fragility

From a mechanobiological perspective, osteoporosis is not simply a low‐mass state but a disorder of insufficient or misinterpreted adaptation. Reduced physical activity, altered muscle function, endocrine aging, and disease‐related changes in cell state can all diminish the anabolic value of habitual loading while increasing structural susceptibility to microdamage [39, 54, 70]. Multiscale analyses of the femoral neck suggest that elderly bone may exhibit higher local strain and strain‐energy‐density surrogates at tissue and osteocyte scales, meaning that similar external activities can generate more pathologic internal environments in older adults [9, 86]. This supports a clinically important point: fracture risk depends on how loads are internally amplified, not only on how much bone mass is present.

This framework becomes even more relevant in diabetic bone fragility. Combined chemical, structural, and mechanical analyses show that diabetes can alter carbonate content, cortical thickness, osteocyte density, and fracture toughness simultaneously [46, 47, 49]. These are not isolated abnormalities; they reshape the material and cellular context in which load is sensed and repaired. As a result, anti‐fracture strategies that focus solely on density or turnover may miss the true source of vulnerability. A mechanophenotype‐based interpretation instead combines geometric assessment, multiaxial loading exposure, sarcopenia or fall profile, and metabolic burden when estimating risk or selecting intervention. The evidence is strongest for mechanistic plausibility and moderate for improved risk stratification; it remains weak for precise patient‐specific loading thresholds.

For current practice, the most defensible use of this knowledge is subgroup identification rather than overconfident prediction. Patients with low density plus high local stress concentration, or with metabolic fragility plus low adaptive reserve, are plausible candidates for more intensive combined pharmacological and mechanical management [20, 55, 87, 88, 89]. Exercise prescription, fall‐risk reduction, and selected adjunctive mechanical stimuli may be tailored more rationally within this framework, but prospective trials linking patient‐specific models directly to fracture prevention are still limited. Mechanophenotyping therefore adds clinical nuance, but it does not yet replace established osteoporosis algorithms.

Osteoarthritis as a Whole‐Joint Mechanophenotype

Among common orthopedic conditions, osteoarthritis may best exemplify the value of a mechanophenotype framework. Contemporary literature increasingly supports a whole‐joint view in which cartilage, subchondral bone, menisci, ligaments, synovium, and muscle operate as a mechanically coupled organ system [21, 37, 38]. In this model, progression is driven not by “wear” alone but by persistent discordance between functional load pathways and the tissue's capacity to distribute, dissipate, and biologically tolerate those loads. Malalignment, obesity, instability, altered muscle activation, and abnormal gait all reshape local contact mechanics, but the downstream effect depends on cartilage condition, subchondral bone response, and mechanosensitive cellular programs [37, 50, 77, 78, 90].

Recent biomechanics reviews reinforce three translational trends. First, there is stronger emphasis on real‐world loading rather than laboratory snapshots, including wearable and mobile assessments of gait and activity [21, 38]. Second, computational models and AI‐assisted pattern recognition are increasingly used to identify mechanically meaningful subgroups from imaging or movement data [17, 91, 92]. Third, the field is moving from generic load‐reduction concepts toward more targeted correction of load pathways through bracing, gait retraining, exercise dosing, osteotomy, or other realignment strategies [63, 93, 94, 95]. The practical message is that mechanophenotype is most useful when it changes management: for example, distinguishing patients whose dominant problem is medial compartment overload from those whose limitation is neuromuscular control or inflammatory irritability.

Important limitations remain. Much of the OA literature still infers cell‐level biology from joint‐level descriptors without intermediate validation. Joint moments, ground‐reaction‐force asymmetry, or static alignment are clinically useful, but they do not directly reveal local fluid pressurization, nutrient transport, or chondrocyte stress in an individual knee [7, 28, 29]. Symptom severity is also not determined by cartilage alone; bone, synovium, periarticular soft tissues, and neural sensitization contribute materially [37, 90]. For clinicians, the safest interpretation is therefore to use mechanophenotyping to refine subgroup selection and intervention choice, not to claim deterministic predictions of progression for a single patient.

Fracture Healing, Nonunion, and Bone–Implant Interfaces

Fracture healing is a paradigmatic mechanobiological process because tissue differentiation, angiogenesis, matrix deposition, and interface maturation are highly sensitive to the local mechanical environment [12, 52]. Axial compression, interfragmentary strain, shear, fluid flow, and fixation rigidity influence whether the repair tissue progresses toward bone, cartilage, or fibrous tissue. The key clinical difficulty is that these variables are dynamic and stage dependent rather than universally beneficial or harmful. A degree of interfragmentary micromotion that promotes callus formation during early secondary healing may prolong instability at a later stage or at a poorly vascularized interface [52, 96]. Therefore, fracture mechanobiology is best interpreted as a time‐dependent mechanical‐dosing problem rather than as a single‐threshold phenomenon.

Recent original modeling studies have made this point more concrete. Computational fracture‐healing models increasingly link tissue mechanics with cell migration, adhesion, proliferation, matrix remodeling, and long‐term remodeling, allowing the mechanical effect of fixation to be studied as the callus evolves rather than as a static boundary condition [5, 59, 60]. Biomaterial and scaffold studies further show that pore geometry, fiber orientation, stiffness, and interface topography influence not only structural support but also cell infiltration, vascular ingrowth, and local signal transmission [97, 98, 99]. At bone‐implant interfaces, macro‐texture, surface roughness, and micromotion affect whether the interface supports osseointegration or progresses toward fibrous encapsulation and loosening [53, 62, 100, 101, 102]. Taken together, these findings support a mechanophenotype in which constructs do not simply hold fragments; they define the local mechanical ecology in which repair occurs. This interpretation is clinically relevant because it identifies several modifiable levers: fixation method, construct stiffness, surface design, scaffold architecture, staged weight‐bearing progression, and externally applied stimulation such as low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound or controlled loading [13, 53, 62, 73, 101]. However, the evidence hierarchy remains mixed. Much mechanobiological knowledge still comes from animal studies, simulations, or simplified in vitro systems, whereas human nonunion is additionally shaped by infection, vascularity, endocrine status, systemic inflammation, diabetes, smoking, and treatment history. At present, the most defensible clinical role of fracture mechanophenotyping is risk staging and lever selection—identifying whether instability, biological insufficiency, or interface failure is likely to dominate—rather than imposing a universal micromotion threshold on every fracture pattern [52, 59, 60].

Spinal Degeneration: Disc Failure, Segmental Mechanics, and Interface‐Aware Intervention

Spinal degeneration is often managed through symptom groupings or imaging grades, but mechanobiology provides a more integrated explanation. Disc hydration, annulus fibrosus integrity, nucleus pulposus swelling, endplate transport, segmental motion, sagittal balance, and adjacent‐tissue mechanics jointly determine whether physiological loading remains tolerable or becomes degenerative [31, 32, 42, 103]. Once degeneration begins, reduced disc pressurization and structural damage redistribute load toward the annulus fibrosus, facet joints, endplates, and adjacent segments, amplifying a feedback loop between mechanics and biology. Because inflammatory activation, cellular senescence, hypoxia, nutrient limitation, and matrix breakdown are closely coupled with altered microenvironmental mechanics, degeneration is better conceptualized as progressive failure of a hydrated fiber‐reinforced system rather than as simple disc dehydration [31, 42, 48, 104].

The translational obstacle in spinal disease is that decision‐making usually occurs at the segment or patient level, whereas key biology occurs at smaller scales. Motion‐preserving devices may restore selected kinematic features while still altering interface stress, adjacent‐segment compensation, or endplate mechanical conditions that are not captured by routine imaging [81, 82]. Similarly, injectable hydrogels or regenerative scaffolds may appear promising in simplified experiments but fail clinically if they do not reproduce physiological swelling behavior, fiber hierarchy, and load transmission through the disc‐endplate system [33, 34, 80]. Recent lumbar‐spine FEA and physics‐informed neural‐network work illustrate the promise of patient‐specific modeling, but it also reinforces the need for validation against real loading histories and clinical outcomes before such models can direct treatment independently [85].

Future progress in spinal mechanotherapy will depend on three forms of integration: more realistic constitutive modeling of osmotic, poroviscoelastic, and fiber‐reinforced behavior; better characterization of patient‐specific loading, muscle function, and sagittal balance; and clearer biological staging so that rehabilitation, stabilization, regeneration, or motion preservation can be matched to the dominant lesion [31, 32, 42, 48]. Compared with OA load redistribution or fracture construct optimization, the current evidence base in spinal mechanophenotyping is more model‐driven and less prospectively validated. Thus, spinal mechanophenotyping is currently most reliable as a framework for hypothesis generation, device planning, and identification of validation targets, not as a completed clinical classification tool. Experimental and Computational Toolchain

Experimental Platforms From Cell Mechanics to Organotypic Loading

The experimental arm of orthopedic mechanobiology has advanced substantially in the past. Atomic force microscopy and related nanoscale methods enable characterization of local stiffness, viscoelasticity, and cell–matrix interactions at resolutions previously unattainable in living systems [8, 14]. Tunable hydrogel platforms allow systematic modulation of matrix stiffness and ligand presentation, which is critical for dissecting how stem cells, osteoblasts, chondrocytes, or immune cells interpret their local environment [16]. Microdevices with deformable structures and organotypic loading systems add another dimension by permitting three‐dimensional and time‐dependent stimulation of multicellular aggregates or tissue models [15]. These tools provide a stronger experimental foundation for claims about cellular mechanosensitivity, yet most remain discovery tools rather than direct clinical assays.

However, cell‐level assays alone are insufficient for load‐bearing tissues. Organotypic or tissue‐level systems remain indispensable, as many orthopedic phenomena depend on structure and transport. Multiphoton imaging combined with mechanical testing can reveal collagen or fiber network reorganization under loading, while tissue‐level tensile, compressive, or permeability assays are required to study annulus fibrosus and cartilage under clinically relevant loading regimes [32, 105, 106]. In disc research, appreciation of elastic fiber hierarchy and osmotic dependence has shown that oversimplified culture conditions distort mechanical and biological phenotypes [33, 34, 41]. Biological conclusions drawn from models that fail to recapitulate native mechanical environments can be directionally misleading.

Recent technical reviews have also highlighted advances in intracellular force sensing, optogenetic or magnetic perturbation, and high‐resolution optical methods [107]. These developments are important because they bridge the long‐standing gap between tissue‐level loading and intracellular responses. Yet they also highlight a translational asymmetry: the more precise the experimental method, the farther it often lies from routine clinical care. The key question is therefore not whether a technique is sophisticated, but whether its outputs can ultimately be mapped to clinically actionable variables such as imaging features, movement patterns, or device parameters.

Multiscale Modeling, Hybrid Approaches, and Image‐Based Inference

Computational models remain the primary bridge connecting observable biomechanical phenomena to hidden internal states. Finite element models of bone, cartilage, joints, and spinal segments can estimate stress, strain, fluid pressure, contact mechanics, and interface micromotion—parameters not directly measurable in vivo [2, 9, 10, 28, 82]. Hybrid and agent‐based frameworks add the ability to simulate time‐dependent remodeling, healing, or multicellular interactions [5, 10, 91]. These methods are especially valuable in orthopedics, where invasive measurement of relevant internal environments is usually not feasible. When properly constrained, models allow investigators to ask not only what happens under a given load, but why a given tissue or interface fails in one phenotype while adapting in another.

The credibility of these models rests on three conditions. First, geometry and boundary conditions must be realistic; even the most sophisticated constitutive laws cannot compensate for inaccurate loading assumptions, and many simulations still rely on generic rather than patient‐specific inputs [18, 23, 108, 109]. Second, cross‐scale links must be biologically interpretable. A model may appear mechanistically complex yet be weakly evidenced if it infers cell‐level outputs from tissue‐level surrogates without intermediate validation [22, 23]. Third, uncertainty must be explicit. Material properties, contact conditions, and biological thresholds often lack tight constraints yet are frequently assigned deterministic predictions. This is one reason model‐based surgical or rehabilitation recommendations are often suggestive rather than prescriptive.

Image‐based approaches have made this landscape more tractable. Explainable deep learning and related methods can identify morphological risk patterns, while biomechanical models translate these patterns into physical hypotheses about force redistribution, transport limitations, or local tissue stress [17, 92]. In bone and cartilage, advances in imaging and computational characterization are also improving extraction of structure‐related features from clinical data [83, 84]. The most promising near‐term workflow is therefore hierarchical rather than monolithic: imaging and AI identify candidate phenotypes, mechanistic models estimate hidden states and test interventions, and longitudinal data refine models over time. This workflow is more realistic than expecting a single model class to simultaneously solve prediction, causal inference, and clinical application.

Digital Twins, Explainable AI, and Why Translation Remains Difficult

Digital twins have become a common term in orthopedic bioengineering, but their practical meaning varies widely. In its most useful form, a musculoskeletal digital twin is not merely a detailed geometry or an AI risk score; it is an updatable patient‐specific model that links anatomy, mechanics, and disease evolution in a decision‐supporting manner [18, 19]. In orthopedics, it should ideally include anatomical information from imaging, loading information from motion or activity data, tissue properties inferred from multi‐modal characterization, and stage‐dependent biological response models. Such a system could, in principle, compare osteotomy, rehabilitation, implant selection, or scaffold design within the same decision framework.

Current reality is more limited. Most so‐called orthopedic digital twins remain static or partially coupled representations rather than continuously updated patient‐specific models. Data harmonization, external validation, and clinically acceptable uncertainty bounds remain major barriers [18, 20, 23, 84]. Artificial intelligence introduces further challenges: dataset shift, lack of mechanistic transparency, potential confounders, and the temptation to treat retrospective predictive performance as equivalent to biological understanding [17, 92]. This is particularly risky in orthopedics, where interventions can permanently alter load pathways. Clinicians need to know not only that a system predicts risk, but also which structural and mechanical assumptions drive that prediction.

Mechanomedicine offers a useful translational balance. Recent perspectives argue that the field should focus on measurable and modifiable mechanical determinants of disease rather than computational complexity alone [20, 24, 25]. For orthopedics, the key question is whether a model or tool helps identify clinically actionable states. In the near term, this means prioritizing repeatable mechanomarkers, trial enrichment strategies, and task‐specific decision support (e.g., for alignment correction, implant comparison, staged loading, or rehabilitation tuning) rather than fully autonomous care.

Clinical Translation, Critical Evidence Gaps, and Research Priorities

What Is Already Actionable and How Clinicians Can Use It

Although important evidence gaps remain, several mechanobiological principles already influence orthopedic care. In osteoarthritis, alignment correction, load redistribution, bracing, gait retraining, and movement‐based rehabilitation are practical attempts to modify harmful load pathways rather than simply treat end‐stage cartilage loss [21, 38, 63, 65]. In fracture care, fixation stiffness, reduction quality, interfragmentary strain, and staged weight bearing are already used to shape the local mechanical environment of repair [52, 59, 60]. In spine and implant surgery, patient anatomy, segmental motion, bone quality, and interface mechanics increasingly inform device selection and surgical planning [61, 82, 85]. These examples show that mechanophenotyping does not replace established clinical algorithms; it makes the mechanical‐biological assumptions behind them more explicit and testable. A practical clinical workflow can be summarized as four steps: first, identify the dominant mechanical problem using history, imaging, physical examination, gait or movement assessment, and, where appropriate, FEA or other modeling; second, identify the susceptible structure, such as weak trabecular bone, medial‐compartment cartilage loss, a large fracture gap, annulus fibrosus disruption, or an unstable implant interface; third, estimate adaptive reserve from age, metabolic disease, inflammation, sarcopenia, vascularity, infection risk, and healing stage; and fourth, choose the most modifiable lever, such as exercise dose, offloading, bracing, osteotomy, fixation stiffness, implant surface or geometry, staged weight bearing, or biologically targeted adjuncts [20, 62, 63, 64, 65]. This workflow is deliberately not a deterministic algorithm [59, 60, 61]. Its value is to structure clinical reasoning, expose uncertainty, and identify where additional validation is needed before personalization can be claimed.

Clinical Practice Examples for Mechanophenotype‐Guided Decision‐Making

Mechanophenotype‐guided decision‐making is most useful when the three‐axis framework—load exposure, tissue susceptibility, and biological adaptability—is translated into explicit clinical choices among modifiable interventions. Rather than replacing established disease‐specific algorithms, it helps explain why patients with similar imaging grades, fracture patterns, or surgical indications may require different strategies because their dominant mechanical‐biological risks differ. In clinical practice, this approach can support load redistribution in osteoarthritis, mechanical or biological optimization in fracture healing, and interface‐aware planning in spinal or implant‐related surgery.

In mechanically stratified knee OA, a patient with symptomatic medial‐compartment OA, varus alignment, and high medial loading represents a different mechanophenotype from a patient with similar radiographic grade but dominant inflammatory irritability or neuromuscular instability. Load exposure can be approximated from gait, alignment, knee adduction moment surrogates, and activity pattern; tissue susceptibility from cartilage loss, meniscal status, subchondral bone changes, and body mass; and biological adaptability from pain irritability, inflammation, sarcopenia, and metabolic disease [21, 37, 77, 78]. This profile can guide selection among gait retraining, unloading brace, strengthening, weight management, high tibial osteotomy, or arthroplasty timing, and randomized evidence supports personalized foot‐progression‐angle gait retraining for reducing medial knee loading and improving outcomes in mild‐to‐moderate medial‐compartment OA [65]. In fracture healing and nonunion risk, a tibial shaft fracture with acceptable biology but excessive interfragmentary strain should be distinguished from an atrophic nonunion dominated by vascular compromise, infection, or metabolic insufficiency. Load exposure includes construct stiffness, fracture gap, interfragmentary motion, and weight‐bearing progression; tissue susceptibility includes bone quality, defect size, comminution, and the implant‐bone interface; and biological adaptability includes vascularity, inflammatory state, endocrine status, smoking, diabetes, and treatment history [52, 54, 59, 60]. This distinction helps determine whether mechanical revision, biological augmentation, infection control, staged loading, or a combined strategy is most rational, and it also explains why a single micromotion threshold is unlikely to fit all fracture patterns. In spine and implant‐interface planning, patients with similar imaging grades may differ in sagittal balance, endplate quality, disc hydration, annulus fibrosus integrity, adjacent‐segment demand, and bone‐implant interface mechanics [31, 81, 82]. The framework therefore identifies whether the dominant risk is abnormal segmental motion, impaired load sharing, weak endplate or bone quality, or limited biological adaptability, while patient‐specific modeling and image‐based inference should remain supervised planning adjuncts until externally validated against pain, function, revision, or adjacent‐segment outcomes [61, 82, 85].

Why Translation Remains Limited

Translation of orthopedic mechanophenotyping remains constrained by a general evidence gap: current studies can often describe mechanics, mechanotransduction, or modeling outputs at one level, but they less often connect real‐world loading, internal tissue fields, cellular responses, temporal adaptation, and patient‐relevant outcomes into one externally validated decision chain [21, 22, 23, 24]. This limitation is amplified by the underrepresentation of time, because static models and short‐term assays cannot fully capture adaptation, fatigue, inflammation, repair‐stage transitions, or treatment history, although these factors often determine whether a mechanical stimulus becomes protective or harmful [3, 39, 55]. It is also amplified by uneven ecological validity, since laboratory boundary conditions, animal models, and idealized simulations rarely reproduce the combined loading, co‐morbidity, behavioral variability, and treatment complexity seen in clinical orthopedics [22, 23, 85]. These general limitations can be seen in several translational examples: gait variables may associate with OA progression, imaging features may associate with implant loosening, and simulated strain may associate with fracture risk, but such associations do not automatically define treatment thresholds or prove that changing one variable will improve outcome [21, 23, 65, 83]. Therefore, clinically useful mechanophenotyping should be externally validated, calibrated against patient‐relevant endpoints, and reported with transparent uncertainty. At present, the framework is most defensible as a tool to refine hypotheses, stratify risk, and select modifiable levers—such as load redistribution in OA, staged mechanical dosing in fracture healing, or interface‐aware implant planning—rather than as a deterministic precision‐medicine algorithm before the evidence supports it.

Research Priorities for the Next Phase

The next phase should shift orthopedic mechanobiology from measuring isolated mechanical variables to building validated, time‐aware, and outcome‐tested decision frameworks. Three priorities are therefore most urgent. First, the field needs cross‐scale biomarkers that connect clinical imaging and movement data with tissue mechanics and biological state; specific examples include CT‐ or MRI‐derived indicators of bone quality, cartilage transport vulnerability, intervertebral disc hydration and endplate function, fracture‐callus maturation, and implant‐interface stress [29, 30, 83, 85]. Second, models must represent time as a core variable by incorporating loading history, repair stage, fatigue, inflammation, remodeling, rehabilitation dose, and treatment history. Third, clinical studies should determine whether mechanophenotype‐guided choices improve pain, function, healing, implant survival, or degeneration prevention compared with standard care, rather than only proving that more variables can be measured [20, 24, 25]. In practical terms, the future value of the mechanophenotype framework lies in stratifying patient groups and generating testable knowledge rather than naming a new clinical category. The key task is to identify which mechanical‐biological state is present, which axis—load exposure, tissue susceptibility, or biological adaptability—is dominant at a given stage, which modifiable lever can realistically modify that axis, and which endpoint proves that the modification matters. Thus, the framework should be understood as a structured synthesis of existing evidence, a safeguard against current overextension, and a roadmap for future studies that connect real‐world loading, patient‐specific structure, stage‐dependent biology, and clinically meaningful outcomes.

Discussion

Contemporary orthopedic science makes it increasingly difficult to sustain simple end‐stage explanations of musculoskeletal disease. Bone fragility, cartilage degeneration, disc failure, fracture nonunion, and implant loosening are not merely anatomical outcomes; they are dynamic states produced when external loading, hierarchical structure, cellular mechanosensing, inflammation, metabolism, and repair capacity fail to remain aligned [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The value of the mechanophenotype framework is that it integrates these layers without collapsing them into a single variable. It asks how real‐world load is filtered by tissue architecture, how the resulting internal field is interpreted by cells, and how that interpretation changes with age, co‐morbidity, and treatment stage.

The literature reviewed here supports several synthesized insights. First, the same external load can generate divergent internal mechanical states because bone, cartilage, disc, fracture callus, and implant interfaces filter forces differently. Second, mechanosensing pathways such as integrin signaling, Piezo channels, LINC‐complex coupling, and YAP/TAZ‐related transcription are shared across tissues but produce tissue‐specific outcomes because matrix architecture, fluid transport, and biological reserve differ [27, 40, 56, 58]. Third, current clinical translation is strongest when mechanics is already modifiable—as in OA load redistribution, fracture fixation, staged rehabilitation, and implant/interface planning—and weakest when organ‐level associations are used to infer cell‐level causality without intermediate validation [21, 52, 59, 63, 65]. These insights make the review more than a list of studies: they define where current evidence can inform care and where it should instead generate cautious hypotheses [60, 61]. At the same time, mechanophenotyping must not become a new source of overclaim. A clinically useful framework must make uncertainty visible, incorporate comorbidities and time, and distinguish mechanistic plausibility from validated decision support. Its immediate value is to improve subgroup reasoning, refine experimental design, and expose missing validation links. Its future value will depend on prospective studies that connect patient‐specific loading, tissue susceptibility, biological adaptability, and outcomes in the same evidentiary chain. This balanced position is consistent with the central purpose of the review: to synthesize past knowledge, critique present limitations, and direct future knowledge creation.

Conclusion

Recent orthopedic research supports a unified but cautious view of disease development in which external load, hierarchical structure, signal conversion, inflammation, metabolism, and repair are tightly coupled. Bone, articular cartilage, the osteochondral unit, the intervertebral disc, fracture callus, and bone‐implant interfaces all display tissue‐specific versions of the same broader problem: load becomes harmful or beneficial only after it is filtered by structure and interpreted by biology [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The orthopedic mechanophenotype provides a clinically oriented framework for integrating this evidence across three interacting axes—load exposure, tissue susceptibility, and biological adaptability—and links them to current clinical levers such as load redistribution, fixation strategy, rehabilitation dosing, and interface‐aware device planning. At present, this framework should be used to strengthen clinical reasoning and research design rather than to claim fully personalized prediction. Its future clinical value will depend on validated cross‐scale markers, time‐aware models, and prospective studies demonstrating that mechanophenotype‐guided decisions improve pain, function, healing, implant survival, and prevention of degeneration.
