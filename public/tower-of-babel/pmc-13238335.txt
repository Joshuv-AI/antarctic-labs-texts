Labour of Love: A Sequential Exploratory Mixed‐Methods Study on the Impact of Caring for a Child With Dravet Syndrome
Authors: Amber Postma, Megan Milota, Marian J. Jongmans, Eva H. Brilstra, Janneke R. Zinkstok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDravet syndrome (DS) is a rare genetic epilepsy syndrome, characterized by refractory seizures, delayed development, intellectual disability and behavioural difficulties. Caring for a child with DS can negatively affect parents' mental well‐being. Therefore, this study aimed to (1) explore experiences of parents caring for a child with DS; and (2) assess distress and empowerment in these parents and the interplay between these aspects.MethodsWe used a sequential exploratory mixed‐method design. We conducted focus groups with parents of children/adults with DS, analysed these data through iterative coding to identify themes and used the Distress Thermometer for parents (DT‐P) and the Dutch Family Empowerment Scale (FES) for quantitative measures. Results were validated in a final focus group.ResultsTwenty parents participated in focus groups and 65 completed questionnaires. Four themes emerged: handling the unpredictable behaviour of a child with DS, attending to one's mental health needs and well‐being, adjusting life and accepting changes to meet the child's needs and struggles navigating the (health)care system. Quantitative analyses showed a significant correlation between higher parental empowerment and lower distress, and more behavioural difficulties in their child with DS and higher parental distress.ConclusionsParental distress is high, especially for those parenting a child with DS and behavioural difficulties. Effective management of these issues and enhancing empowerment can improve overall family well‐being.

Background

Dravet syndrome (DS) is a rare genetic epilepsy syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in the SCN1A gene. DS is characterized by refractory seizures, delayed development, intellectual disability, motor impairment, behavioural difficulties, eating difficulties and sleep disturbances (de Lange et al. 2019; C. Minderhoud et al. 2023; Sullivan et al. 2022). About half of all individuals with DS experience daily or weekly seizures and have a severe or profound intellectual disability (ID) (de Lange et al. 2019; Postma, Minderhoud, et al. 2024). Additionally, between 37% and 100% of individuals with DS have behavioural difficulties (de Lange et al. 2019; Postma, Minderhoud, et al. 2024; Sinoo et al. 2019; Jansson et al. 2020; Brunklaus et al. 2011). Developmental disorders are also frequently diagnosed in the DS population, with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) affecting between 31.0% and 61.0% of this population, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) affecting between 17.9% and 40.0% individuals (Sullivan et al. 2022; Postma, Minderhoud, et al. 2024; Bjurulf et al. 2024). DS is a complex disease, which requires extensive care and significantly impacts parents and families.

The substantial demands and stress associated with caring for a child with a rare and complex disease pose many challenges for parents. For example, parents report difficulties such as extreme fatigue, sleep deprivation, stress, being on high alert and constant worrying (Jensen et al. 2017; Postma et al. 2023; Salom et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023a; Strzelczyk et al. 2023). This may negatively impact their mental well‐being, and often results in feelings of anxiety, guilt, helplessness and anger; it can also negatively impact the partner relationship (Jensen et al. 2017; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023b; Tsai et al. 2013; Witt et al. 2023; Soto Jansson et al. 2024a; C. A. Minderhoud et al. 2024). Psychological trauma has been documented among parents of children with DS who perceived their child to be in mortal danger, as well as in relation to exposure to medical procedures, the receipt of information from medical professionals and the experience of receiving the diagnosis (Mercier et al. 2025). In addition, parents report that it is difficult to prioritize leisure time or maintain their social network (Jensen et al. 2017; Salom et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023b; Soto Jansson et al. 2024a; Nabbout et al. 2020; Nolan et al. 2006). Caring for a child with a rare and complex disease can also significantly impact the financial and professional situation of both parents, albeit more often mothers (Jensen et al. 2017; Postma et al. 2023; Salom et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023a; Strzelczyk et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023b; Witt et al. 2023; Soto Jansson et al. 2024a; Nabbout et al. 2020; Sánchez Marco et al. 2024). Frequently, parents reduce work hours or quit working all together to attend to their child's needs, potentially leading to financial stress. Although many studies have highlighted the high burden of care and high stress levels (Jensen et al. 2017; Postma et al. 2023; Nabbout et al. 2020; Nolan et al. 2008), there is also evidence that parents caring for a child with severe epilepsy may experience increased confidence and ability in advocating and caring for their child (Jensen et al. 2017; Mercier et al. 2025). This represents a broad concept of family empowerment, which is considered to be important in strengthening parents' position in healthcare (Barlow and Ellard 2004; Hook 2006). Family empowerment is the broad process of gaining control over one's life. Empowered parents feel capable and willing to engage with and influence the people, organizations and environments that can positively impact their child's care and can furthermore reflect on the impact of their own actions (Brodsky and Cattaneo 2013; Segers et al. 2019; Fumagalli et al. 2015). However, studies quantifying empowerment among parents of individuals with DS remain lacking.

Furthermore, feelings of empowerment and resilience may play an important protective role for parents of children with DS, who often struggle to find suitable care resources and placements, and frequently report feeling isolated in their search for appropriate support (Postma et al. 2023; Witt et al. 2023; Mercier et al. 2025; Boyce et al. 2020; Soto Jansson et al. 2024b). The symptom presentation of individuals with a rare and complex disease like DS may not conform to traditional assumptions of medical care (Perestelo‐Pérez et al. 2017; Babac et al. 2019). This, coupled with variations in clinicians' knowledge about rare diseases like DS and lack of knowledge of particular care needs, can further hamper the organization of appropriate care for individuals with DS (Witt et al. 2023; Soto Jansson et al. 2024b; Perestelo‐Pérez et al. 2017). Moreover, in our previous qualitative study among parents caring for DS children, we suggested that the current healthcare system in the Netherlands may not fully address all their needs (Postma et al. 2023). In a study in the UK parents mentioned feeling ‘failed by the healthcare’ throughout the Dravet journey, which led to becoming a medical professional parent (Mercier et al. 2025). The financial, professional, social, relational and mental health challenges increase the already significant burden faced by parents caring for these children and result in elevated stress levels as highlighted in several studies (Jensen et al. 2017; Postma et al. 2023; Salom et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023a; Soto Jansson et al. 2024a; Nabbout et al. 2020; Nolan et al. 2008; Dorris et al. 2026; Freeman‐Jones et al. 2024). Based on these qualitative findings, we hypothesize stress levels among Dutch parents of a child with DS to be similarly elevated when assessed using a quantitative method. To date no quantitative assessment of these stress levels and feelings of empowerment combined with identification of contributing factors has been carried out among Dutch parents of a child with DS. In previous studies (C. Minderhoud et al. 2023; Jensen et al. 2017; Postma et al. 2023; Nolan et al. 2008) parents reported the substantial impact of their child's epilepsy, behavioural difficulties and eating difficulties on their personal well‐being, suggesting these may serve as risk factors for elevated stress levels. However, quantitative research specifically examining such risk factors and possibly protective factors among parents of children with DS is lacking. Although the negative aspects of caring for a child with DS—such as perceived stress or burden—has been extensively documented in literature, equal attention should be given to family empowerment and resilience, which may play a valuable role in supporting parental well‐being. Therefore, this study aimed to (1) explore experiences of parents caring for a child with DS; and (2) asses the interplay between distress and empowerment in these parents.

Methods

Study Design

This study has a sequential exploratory mixed‐method design and included parents caring for a child with DS. The current study is part of a larger study investigating medical, health‐related quality of life and behavioural aspects of DS and experiences of parents (C. Minderhoud et al. 2023; C. A. Minderhoud et al. 2024; Postma et al. 2023; Postma et al. 2024). In Figure 1, the study design is displayed. The study began with the qualitative part, followed by the quantitative part.

Study design.

Participants and Recruitment

Parents of individuals with heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the SCN1A gene (Classes IV and V, according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria) (Richards et al. 2015) were included. Potential participants were invited to participate by the researchers if they had given prior consent to be recontacted; or by the physician of their child with DS; or via an invitation to participate in the newsletter of the parent/patient organization ‘Dravet syndrome Foundation Netherlands/Flanders’ (Stichting Dravetsyndroom Nederland/Vlaanderen). Inclusion criteria were having a child with SCN1A‐related DS and speaking Dutch. Three participants who expressed interest did not take part in the study due to time constraints.

Qualitative Data Collection and Analysis

Three focus groups were organized between June 2021 and November 2021 and were held online through Microsoft Teams, following the Standards for Qualitative Research and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (Tong et al. 2007; O'Brien et al. 2014). The focus groups were semistructured in nature with a predefined topic list and a duration between 60 and 90 min. The topic list (see Supporting Information A) comprised two overall themes: parents' experiences caring for their child with DS and, more specifically, their experiences with behavioural problems in their child with DS. A paper describing parental experiences with behavioural difficulties in their child with DS was published previously (Postma et al. 2023). In the current study, we focused exclusively on the burden of care and well‐being of parents. Each focus group session was facilitated by a moderator (AP), a psychiatrist in training, familiar with the subject of the study and with the participants. Other observing researchers were a psychiatrist (JZ), and an expert on qualitative research (MM). The focus groups were video recorded after verbal consent was obtained at the onset of the focus group. The recordings were then transcribed and anonymized, with the inclusion of nonlinguistic observations (e.g., facial expressions, laughing, sighs and gestures). The qualitative data were analysed using an inductive thematic analysis following the guidelines of Braun and Clarke (2006). The thematic approach was used to identify emerging patterns in the data, which were then grouped into themes. The primary researcher (AP) read the transcripts and generated codes with the help of Atlas.ti. The code structure was discussed among the research team. Members (AP, JZ, MM) coded parts of the transcript. Differences in coding among the researchers were discussed until consensus was reached. Data on group characteristics were reported as descriptive statistics.

Quantitative Data Collection and Analysis

Primary measures assessing parental distress and empowerment included the norm‐referenced and validated Distress Thermometer for parents (DT‐P) (Haverman et al. 2013; van Oers et al. 2017) and the validated Dutch Family Empowerment Scale (FES) (Segers et al. 2019; Koren et al. 1992). These questionnaires were administered between May 2022 and April 2023. Data previously collected between October 2021 to December 2022 on the parents and the child with DS were used as background information. Additional information concerning quantitative measures are presented in Supporting Information B. Data were analysed as follows: demographics, parental and child characteristics and quantitative FES and DT‐P data were reported as descriptive statistics. Differences between the two FES domains were explored by a paired t‐test. Correlations between the level of distress and FES domains were analysed with a Pearson correlation. To determine potential risk and protective factors for experienced parental distress, we used univariable and multivariable regression analysis with the DT‐P score as dependent factor. Similarly, these analyses were employed to ascertain potential risk and protective factors influencing parental empowerment, with the FES Family domain and Child services domain as dependent factors. Factors included in these analyses were parental characteristics, including gender, number of children and relationship status; and characteristics of their child with DS, including age, living situation, level of intellectual disability, minor and major seizure frequency, behavioural difficulties, sleep disturbances, functional mobility and eating difficulties. To handle item level missing values, we used multiple imputations (‘mice’ R package) with Rubin's rule (Rubin and Schenker 1986). We created and analysed 20 datasets with imputed values and pooled the estimates. The determinants in our final multivariable regression analyses were created using a stepwise model selection. All tests were performed two‐tailed with an alpha level of significance of p‐value of < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed with R version 3.2.2.

Data integration and Validation

The description of the qualitative and quantitative results was discussed among the research team and the participants to validate the results through an online focus group, recorded via Microsoft Teams with a duration of 90 min. The findings were presented in a brief Power Point presentation, and parents were invited to comment and articulate their views. The qualitative data derived from this focus group was exploratively analysed and discussed among core members of the research team (AP, JZ, MM). Differences in interpretation of the data were discussed until consensus was reached.

Results

Qualitative Findings

In total, 20 parents participated in the focus groups (mothers only). The age of their children with DS ranged from 3 to 22 years with a median age of 10.5 years (range 3–22). Sixteen (80%) of these parents reported that they reduced working hours or quit their job to take care of their child(ren). Fourteen parents (70%) reported to be in a form of employment. Most parents (17 [85%]) were currently in a relationship with the other parent of the child with DS. Of those who took part in the qualitative phase of the study, 17 also participated in the quantitative phase, and nine were involved in the data integration and validation phase (Figure 1).

The focus groups showed that parenting a child with DS (aptly described by some parents as ‘extreme parenting’) is very challenging. From the thematic analyses (Figure 2) we identified multiple challenges for parents, each described below.

Thematic analyses of qualitative findings.

Handling the Unpredictable Behaviour of a Child With DS

Parents reported a behavioural pattern characterized by hyperactivity, impulsivity and routinized tendencies, leading to unpredictable and dangerous situations. Consequently, parents reported being on continuous high alert, keeping their child nearby and actively trying to prevent them from encountering harmful situations. In addition, these children appear to have difficulties comprehending the extent of their own limitations. As a result, parents reported that they must constantly monitor and correct their child's behaviour to prevent overstimulation, which otherwise frequently results in seizures. This can cause difficult situations as illustrated in the following citation:

I felt anxious going with her to the supermarket. I constantly worried that something might happen in the middle of the store, which requires me to handle multiple situations at once. During these situations, I'm alone and she is hyperactive and is eager to go shopping. Indeed, the shopping cart gets filled and she disappears behind the counter where I cannot see her, and I think: ‘Oh no, I hope nothing goes wrong’.

During these situations, I'm alone and she is hyperactive and is eager to go shopping. Indeed, the shopping cart gets filled and she disappears behind the counter where I cannot see her, and I think: ‘Oh no, I hope nothing goes wrong’.

In addition, multiple participants reported that they adjusted their approach when interacting with the child with DS, as opposed to interactions with their other child(ren). For example, parents try to give concise and concrete instructions to the child in a calm manner. However, parents reported difficulties maintaining this approach when they themselves felt frustrated. For example:

When he becomes angry, it is challenging for us as parents not to get upset and say: ‘Stop now, for once!’. No, instead, we have to speak in a calm manner and hope to distract him. This requires a lot of effort for us as parents and carers. However, we noticed that his mood stabilizes more quickly this way. But on occasions when we are having a bad day and shout ‘Stop now for once!’, he ends up in a state of total panic.

This is even more challenging for other children in the household who are themselves still in the process of learning and developing. Furthermore, as care demands for a child with DS are all time and energy consuming, attending to other children or their partner gets compromised. This leads to parents feeling guilty toward their other family members. Additionally, some parents mentioned disappointment in their own parenting skills. For example, a mother frequently showed videos on her phone to prevent an anger tantrum. These alternative methods are effective but were perceived by some parents as inadequate strategies when parenting ‘healthy children’ as mentioned by one of the parents:

Yes, I am struggling with my parenting strategies. I know that I am currently not raising my child in the way it ideally should be done. However, I also recognize that, with traditional parenting strategies, I am not able to reach her.

Attending to One's Mental Health Needs and Well‐Being

These parents reported that parenting a child with DS is very stressful and takes up all their energy. Due to the sleep difficulties and night‐time seizures of their child, these parents experience sleep deprivation themselves, resulting in extreme fatigue. As mentioned by one of the parents:

When she is sleeping, the infrared camera is next to our bed. So, our bedroom is never really dark. As a result, our nights are also quite short. But recently, she has been able to sleep on her own. I used to lie next to her for an hour, and if I was then still awake, I would go downstairs feeling completely exhausted. I would tell my partner: ‘I'm going back to bed because I'm very tired’.

I used to lie next to her for an hour, and if I was then still awake, I would go downstairs feeling completely exhausted. I would tell my partner: ‘I'm going back to bed because I'm very tired’.

Other difficulties these parents mentioned were despair, loneliness, irritability and persistent worrying. Handling these intense emotions is a challenging task. For some of these parents, caring for a child with DS led to mental health issues like depression. Some parents sought out professional help to cope with these challenges. Moreover, parents prioritize the care of the child with DS, neglecting self‐care. One of the mothers mentioned searching for her own identity besides the mother who is taking care of her child with DS. Another parent reported professionals advising her to prioritize self‐care. She responded that she no longer knew what that would entail.

Adjusting Life and Accepting Changes to Meet the Child's Needs

Caring for a child with DS results in adjustments in every aspect of the parents' life. Many parents had to reduce their working hours or even quit working altogether. Additionally, some parents changed their living situation to address their child's needs, as one of the parents illustrated:

We moved so that the helicopter emergency medical services could land in our backyard and the ambulance could reach us at the front of our house in case of an emergency; this occurred multiple times per month. This way he could continue living with us at home.

We moved so that the helicopter emergency medical services could land in our backyard and the ambulance could reach us at the front of our house in case of an emergency; this occurred multiple times per month. This way he could continue living with us at home.

These families implement numerous adjustments that would not even occur to others. For instance, they may not always celebrate holidays due to risks of escalations and are more reluctant to have pets because of the dangerous and unpredictable behaviour of the child with DS. Additionally, these adjustments also concern the siblings of the child with DS. Spontaneous activities are often impossible, and having friends over is discouraged to protect these children from the aggressive outbursts of the child with DS. Moreover, parents harbour fears of harm befalling their child with DS and reported feeling reluctant to undertake activities with their child or hesitation to be far away from their child due to concerns about potential incidents. This leads to additional constraints on the parents' lives. Parents expressed that adjusting one's life to be able to care for their child is something they do without hesitation. Nonetheless, accepting these changes or realizing that certain things are no longer feasible can be confrontational. Job loss, strained friendships and an overall sense of uncertainty about the future are common experiences for these parents. Additionally, professionals often advise these parents to accept the situation as mentioned by one of them:

I found it becoming even more difficult. Okay, I understand this is part of it, so we must accept it. But we have a family, so I cannot just readily accept this.

I found it becoming even more difficult. Okay, I understand this is part of it, so we must accept it. But we have a family, so I cannot just readily accept this.

However, parents added that professionals often failed to provide guidance on how to gain acceptance.

Struggles Navigating the (Health) Care System

Due to the complex and rare phenotype and the multitude of specialist and healthcare providers involved, parents experienced numerous challenges in the Dutch healthcare system. First, parents reported that care was often fragmented, with each specialist having their own area of expertise and limited interdisciplinary communication taking place. Second, parents mentioned they had to take on the additional responsibilities of being their child's ‘case manager’ to advocate for their child's access to high‐quality care. Lastly, parents reported that finding competent individuals to assist in the care of their child can be a challenge. Many parents rely on in‐home support from professional carers to impede feeling overwhelmed, allowing them some personal time to do household chores and attend to other children. However, this arrangement can be stressful and often comes at the cost of sacrificing privacy, as one of the parents reported:

Six different people come into our home, and I find it quite burdensome. At times, I consider handling it on my own. Having healthcare providers in our home adds stress, and I am the one responsible for guiding them in the care of our child.

Six different people come into our home, and I find it quite burdensome. At times, I consider handling it on my own. Having healthcare providers in our home adds stress, and I am the one responsible for guiding them in the care of our child.

These challenges lead parents to feel like they must fend for themselves. As one of the parents mentioned:

I often remark that surely the wheel has been invented. Nonetheless, each time it feels like I have to reinvent the wheel.

Parental Distress

The outcomes of the DT‐P are displayed in Table 2. The median score on the thermometer was 6 (range 0–10) and 45 parents (69%) scored in the clinical distress range (≥ 4). In each domain, parents reported numerous problems. In the practical problems domain, 41 parents (63.1%) reported problems with leisure activities/relaxing. In the social problems domain, many parents (21 [32.3%]) reported problems interacting with their child(ren). Regarding emotional problems, 42 (64.6%) parents reported problems with feeling tense or nervous. In the physical domain, most parents reported (52 [80%]) problems with fatigue, and in the cognitive domain, 37 parents (56.9%) reported problems with concentration. To the question of whether they would like to talk to a professional about their situation, 31 parents (47.7%) answered yes or maybe.

Parental Empowerment

The median score in the FES Child Services domain was 51 (range = 37–60), whereas the median score in the FES Family domain was 47 (range = 31–60). Moreover, the mean score in the FES Child Services domain (mean = 4.26; SD = 0.43) was significantly higher than the mean score in the FES Family domain (mean = 3.93; SD = 0.49) (CI = −0.41 to −0.24, p < 0.001).

Correlations Between Parental Empowerment and Parental Distress

Regarding the Family domain, parents who scored low on the DT‐P thermometer (< 4) scored an average of 4.14 (SD = 0.52), whereas parents who scored in clinically elevated distress levels on the DT‐P thermometer (≥ 4) scored an average of 3.84 (SD = 0.46). This resulted in a significant correlation (r = −0.266, CI = −0.48 to −0.02, p = 0.032) between the score on the DT‐P thermometer and the score on the FES Family domain. In other words, parents with high distress levels reported lower empowerment in the Family domain. Concerning the Child services domain, parents who scored low on the DT‐P thermometer (< 4) obtained an average of 4.40 (SD = 0.44) on the Child services domain, whereas parents who scored in clinically elevated distress levels on the DT‐P thermometer (≥ 4) scored an average of 4.20 (SD = 0.42). However, this distinction did not yield a significant correlation between scores on the DT‐P thermometer and in the FES Child services domain.

Correlations Between Parental Distress and Characteristics of Parents and Their Child With DS

The results of the univariable regression analyses show that there is a significant relationship between the parental level of distress (measured by the DT‐P thermometer) and the child living in institutional care (β = −1.861, CI = −3.338 to −0.384, p = 0.014), the severity of behavioural difficulties (measured by total problems A/CBCL) (β = 0.146, CI = 0.074–0.218, p < 0.001) and the severity of sleep difficulties (measured by SQ‐SP CSI) (β = 0.515, CI = 0.216–0.814, p = 0.001). In the multivariable regression end model, only the severity of behavioural difficulties (measured by total problems A/CBCL) was significantly associated with a higher score on the parental level of distress (DT‐P thermometer) (β = 0.146, CI = 0.074–0.218, p < 0.001). This indicates that more behavioural difficulties in the child with DS were associated with a higher level of experienced parental distress. This model explains 20.9% of the variation (adjusted R 2 = 0.2089). See Supporting Information C for information on the factors included in these analyses and Supporting Information D for all the results of the univariable and multivariable regression analyses.

Correlations Between Parental Empowerment and Parent and Child With DS Characteristics

The univariable and multivariable regression analyses showed that there is no significant relationship between the parental empowerment domains and parental and affected child characteristics. For the results of the univariable and multivariable regression analyses, see Supporting Information E.

Integration and Validation of Results

Nine parents took part in the validation focus group, all of whom affirmed our research findings. They expressed that the four themes, identified in the qualitative findings, strongly resonated with their experiences. One of the parents mentioned that she fully recognized the challenges associated with the healthcare system, including the problem of fragmented care with limited multidisciplinary interactions. As one of the parents mentioned:

When the child reaches the age of 18, the care is typically assigned to a doctor specialized in intellectual disabilities and possibly a neurologist, but no additional specialists. As a result, the paediatric treatment team is disbanded without a care transition plan for adulthood. This really should be improved.

Other parents agreed that this was a problem. Moreover, parents described that integrating the care for a child with DS into their lives requires major adjustments. However, a guide on how these parents can implement these adjustments and which adjustments are necessary is currently non‐existent. Parents reported implementing various adjustments like reducing work hours, changing living situations and implementing in‐home support from healthcare providers. Adjusting is crucial for the overall well‐being of these parents but presents a challenging task. Thus, these parents developed powerful strategies to sustain the care for their child and their families. As one of the parents shared:

The message should be: ‘be very kind to yourself’, which could mean something different for everybody.

According to the parents, this includes ensuring leisure time, ask for help and not losing sight of the personal pursuits that parents themselves value. The distress thermometer data demonstrated strikingly high distress levels, with 69% of the parents experiencing clinically elevated distress. The results also showed that challenging behaviour was significantly correlated to parental distress, accounting for 20.9% of the variation. Parents corroborated these findings in the validation focus group and acknowledged the difficulty of living a balanced life amidst such high stress levels. Examples mentioned by parents to achieve some form of balance included attending concerts, receiving massages, engaging in sports, seeking professional help and planning weekends away. Data from the initial focus groups showed that parents highlighted the importance of family empowerment, meaning to what extent a parent regains control over one's life (Brodsky and Cattaneo 2013; Segers et al. 2019; Fumagalli et al. 2015). Parents emphasized the significance of feeling empowered in being able to care for oneself and one's family. In addition, the score on the FES empowerment item within the family domain was inversely correlated with the level of experienced distress. This suggests that parents who feel more empowered with respect to managing their child and their family, tend to experience less distress and vice versa. In the validation focus group, parents agreed with these findings but noted the difficulty of creating guidelines or interventions to assist parents in taking care of their families and themselves, because each parent and each family is different. The type and level of support required, and the timing for accepting support may differ between families and may change according to the child's age and developmental needs. In addition, parents noted that they typically sought help only after their personal well‐being had become cause of concern. However, they recommended that parents caring for a child with DS should have regular check‐ins with a healthcare professional who focusses specifically on the health of the parent and who can identify concerning signs before parents themselves may notice them. Lastly, parents stressed the importance of being persistent and resourceful in finding ways to self‐care.

Discussion

This sequential exploratory mixed‐method study provides an overview of the positive and negative aspects of caring for a child with DS integrating qualitative and quantitative data, with a validation of results by parents themselves. Overall, our findings show that the impact of caring for a child with DS is enormous and requires adjustments in every aspect of parents' lives. Moreover, there is a need for care management strategies that include addressing behavioural challenges among children with DS, enhancing family well‐being and empowerment and addressing the organization of the healthcare system.

Our findings indicate a significant psychological, practical, social, emotional, physical and cognitive burden on parents, aligning with conclusions drawn in previous research (Jensen et al. 2017; Postma et al. 2023; Salom et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023a; Strzelczyk et al. 2023; Domaradzki and Walkowiak 2023b; Witt et al. 2023; Soto Jansson et al. 2024a; Mercier et al. 2025; Nabbout et al. 2020; Sánchez Marco et al. 2024; Nolan et al. 2008). Some findings were particularly noteworthy. First, 69% of parents reported high stress levels, which was correlated with behavioural difficulties in their child with DS. Other studies focusing on the relationship between parental stress and behavioural difficulties among children/adults with rare genetic syndromes, similarly observed a positive correlation between parental stress and the child's behavioural difficulties (Briegel et al. 2008; Briegel et al. 2007; Fitzgerald and Gallagher 2022). These findings highlight the importance of care management strategies focusing on behavioural difficulties in individuals with DS to improve the well‐being of the individual and their families. Second, parental feelings of empowerment within the family domain were inversely correlated with the level of experienced distress. After integrating and validating the findings, it became evident that prioritizing parental empowerment and reducing stress levels is essential to improve these parents' quality of life. Due to constraints of our data, it was impossible to establish causal relationships. Parents who feel more empowered may be better equipped to handle stress, but being able to cope well with stress is likely to lead to feelings of empowerment. The correlation between stress and parental empowerment may be understood through the coping framework proposed by Mercier et al., whose grounded theory model of coping and adjustment distinguishes between two coping strategies (Mercier et al. 2025). Initial coping is characterized by a prolonged heightened state of vigilance and a felt pressure to monitor all aspects of DS, whereas secondary coping reflects a transition toward receiving and accepting support. The coping strategy employed at a given point in time may dependent on feelings of parental empowerment and distress. Either way, strategies aimed at enhancing parental empowerment and reducing stress levels may both result in better outcomes for parents. An intervention study that aimed to enhance empowerment among mothers of premature infants admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) found that promoting empowerment—operationalized through increases in parental knowledge, reductions in stress and enhanced confidence in parent–infant interactions—resulted in reduced levels of overall parental stress (Nieves et al. 2021). Additionally, two studies conducted among parents of children with autism highlighted that increased parental resilience, empowerment and well‐being were associated with better outcomes in their children (Crowell et al. 2019; Tarver et al. 2019). Similar results may be observed in children with DS. However, factors contributing to elevated stress levels or increased parental empowerment remained undefined within this study. Additionally, the identified correlations are most likely bidirectional; for example, parents experiencing more stress may have a more negative view of their child's behaviour, whereas a child exhibiting behavioural difficulties can, in turn, heighten parental stress. In this context, the secondary coping strategy described by Mercier et al.—characterized by an openness to receiving and accepting support—may prove particularly beneficial in helping parents manage their child's behavioural difficulties (Mercier et al. 2025). Conversely, parents who have not yet reached this stage may feel overwhelmed and are at risk for a psychological breakdown in case of great behavioural challenges. Future research should encompass additional parental characteristics (e.g., educational level, social network, sleep disturbances and financial stability) that could potentially contribute to parental feelings of distress and empowerment. Our results show that parents feel significantly more empowered in the realm of healthcare provision compared to the realm of managing their child and family. This could be the result of mutual support parents offer one another through the parent/patient organization ‘Dravet syndrome Foundation Netherlands/Flanders’ (Stichting Dravetsyndroom Nederland/Vlaanderen) (Stichting Dravetsyndroom Nederland/Vlaanderen 2014). Through this organization parents share practical information, foster connections and address challenges associated with caring for a child with DS. As suggested in previous literature, parental peer support has a positive impact on the emotional and psychological well‐being of parents caring for a child with a neurodevelopmental disorder like DS (Postma, Ketelaar, et al. 2024; Wong and Shorey 2022; Chakraborti et al. 2021). Sharing experiences with parents who have similar lived experiences could possibly improve parental empowerment. However, the way the current healthcare system is structured, still results in parents having to manage their child's health, thereby increasing the burden on parents. Addressing this gap in service provision, and providing valid and accessible information on the child's illness and the available resources (Mercier et al. 2025; Pelentsov et al. 2015), may lead to improved parental well‐being and reduce parental burnout.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. The first is the sequential exploratory mixed‐method approach, which included qualitative data derived from focus groups and quantitative data derived from questionnaires. In addition, the results were validated in a focus group where parents also shared their thoughts on clinical implications. However, several limitations of this study need mentioning. First, a potential response bias exists as parents who feel more comfortable in sharing their experiences were more likely to participate. Furthermore, although the online setting of the focus groups may have facilitated participation and ensured low‐threshold participation for parents, it may simultaneously have hindered natural group dynamics and the flow of conversation. However, recent research comparing online and in‐person data collection suggests no significant difference in thematic findings (Guest et al. 2020). Second, the focus groups addressed behavioural difficulties as a topic, potentially leading to an information bias. However, the quantitative findings show that the behavioural difficulties of the child with DS are significantly correlated to parental distress without being affected by an information bias. Third, only a small number of parental factors were included in the multivariable analyses researching contributing factors to parental distress and empowerment. Future research should include more potentially contributing factors. Fourth, the nonconcurrent administration of measures constitutes a potential source of bias that may have affected the findings. And last, most participants were mothers. Therefore, the presented results may not be generalizable to fathers.

Implications

These findings have significant implications. First, we recommend that care management strategies should prioritize optimizing treatment and management of behavioural difficulties in individuals with DS, given their correlation with parental distress, to improve the child's and family's well‐being. Second, interventions aimed at promoting parental empowerment and reducing parental distress should be provided. These could include empowerment courses (e.g., improvement in disease knowledge, self‐esteem and enhanced confidence in parent–infant interactions), psychoeducation, peer‐to‐peer support, psychological interventions (e.g., acceptance and commitment therapy), trauma therapy and self‐care programmes, as previous research with similar populations has shown these to be effective (Mercier et al. 2025; Nieves et al. 2021; Crowell et al. 2019; Tarver et al. 2019; Postma, Ketelaar, et al. 2024; Wong and Shorey 2022; Chakraborti et al. 2021; Byrne et al. 2021). We recommend that the care management of children with DS should not focus solely on the child but on the whole family caring for the child. Parental well‐being should be integrated into routine care, with these interventions tailored to meet the individual needs of the family, as receptiveness to support may vary considerably across families and over time. This variability may be understood through Mercier et al.'s grounded theory model of coping and adjustment, which distinguishes between initial coping—characterized by heightened vigilance and a felt pressure to monitor all aspects of DS—and secondary coping, reflecting a transition toward accepting support. The coping strategy employed at a given moment may therefore determine which intervention is likely to be most effective. And last, the current Dutch healthcare system is complex and difficult to navigate, adding to parental burden and distress. Assisting parents with securing adequate support and resources should become part of regular healthcare, before and after the child reaches adulthood.
