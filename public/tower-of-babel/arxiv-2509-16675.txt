) and the collective molecular vibrational modes (
\beta_{k}=\langle B_{k}\rangle
). Specifically, these equations take the form:

\alpha_{a}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{a}-iJ_{1}\alpha_{c}}{D_{a}},\quad\alpha_{c}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{c}D_{a}-iJ_{2}\mathcal{E}_{a}}{D_{c}D_{a}+J_{1}J_{2}},\quad\beta_{k}=\frac{-ig_{k}\mathcal{I}_{c}}{i\omega_{m}+\gamma_{k}},\quad(k=1,2),

(5)

Here,
D_{a}=i\Delta_{a}+\kappa_{a}
and
D_{c}=i\tilde{\Delta}_{c}-\kappa_{c}
. A critical element in these dynamics is the effective detuning of the active cavity,
\tilde{\Delta}_{c}=\Delta_{c}+\sum_{k=1,2}g_{k}(\beta^{*}_{k}+\beta_{k})
. This term is far more than a simple frequency shift; it elegantly encapsulates the back-action from both collective molecular modes on the cavity resonance. The fundamental source of the system’s inherent nonlinearity, and thus its capacity for complex dynamics, lies in the dependence of
\tilde{\Delta}_{c}
on the mean intracavity photon number,
\mathcal{I}_{c}=|\alpha_{c}|^{2}
. As the molecular vibrations are driven by the radiation pressure of the cavity field, their displacement modulates the cavity’s effective optical path length, causing the resonance frequency to shift. This feedback loop, where the cavity field affects the molecules, which in turn affect the cavity field, is the physical origin of the nonlinearity.

This pronounced nonlinearity leads directly to the phenomenon of optical bistability. Optical bistability manifests itself as the existence of multiple stable steady-state solutions for the intracavity photon number for a given set of input parameters, typically the driving field strength or detuning
Teklu et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2019
;
Kumar Singh et al. 2024
. Mathematically, this complex behavior is rigorously captured by a cubic polynomial equation for
\mathcal{I}_{c}
, derived through algebraic manipulation of
Equation

5

(\zeta_{5}^{2}+\zeta_{4}^{2})\mathcal{I}_{c}^{3}+2(\chi_{7}\zeta_{4}-\chi_{2}\zeta_{5})\mathcal{I}_{c}^{2}+(\chi_{7}^{2}+\chi_{2}^{2})\mathcal{I}_{c}-\zeta_{3}=0,

(6)

where the coefficients
\chi
and
\zeta
are defined as:
\chi_{2}=\Delta_{c}\kappa_{a}-\Delta_{a}\kappa_{c}
,
\chi_{3}=2\frac{g_{1}^{2}\omega_{m}\Delta_{a}}{\chi_{1}^{\prime}}
,
\chi_{4}=2\frac{g_{1}^{2}\omega_{m}\kappa_{a}}{\chi_{1}^{\prime}}
,
\chi_{5}=2\frac{g_{2}^{2}\omega_{m}\Delta_{a}}{\chi_{1}^{\prime\prime}}
,
\chi_{6}=2\frac{g_{2}^{2}\omega_{m}\kappa_{a}}{\chi_{1}^{\prime\prime}}
,
\chi_{7}=-\Delta_{c}\Delta_{a}-\kappa_{c}\kappa_{a}+J_{1}J_{2}
,
\zeta_{1}=\chi_{7}+\mathcal{I}_{c}(\chi_{3}+\chi_{5})
,
\zeta_{2}=\chi_{2}-\chi_{4}\mathcal{I}_{c}-\chi_{6}\mathcal{I}_{c}
,
\zeta_{3}=\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}(\Delta^{2}_{a}+\kappa_{a}^{2})-2\mathcal{E}_{c}\mathcal{E}_{a}\Delta_{a}J_{2}+J_{2}^{2}\mathcal{E}_{a}^{2}
,
\zeta_{4}=\chi_{3}+\chi_{5}
,
\zeta_{5}=\chi_{4}+\chi_{6}
,
\chi^{\prime}_{1}=\omega_{m}^{2}+\gamma_{1}^{2}
, and
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\omega_{m}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2}

\mathcal{I}_{c}
indicates a bistable regime, typically visualized as distinctive ”S-shaped” curves in plots of intracavity photon number versus driving power or detuning.

It is noteworthy that not all solution branches within a bistable region are stable. The stability of these branches is rigorously assessed by applying the Routh-Hurwitz criterion. This powerful mathematical tool allows us to determine that for the S-shaped curves characteristic of optical bistability, the upper and lower branches represent stable operating points, whereas the intermediate branch is inherently unstable and thus physically unreachable
DeJesus and Kaufman 1987
;
Agasti and Djorwe 2024
;
Djorwe et al. 2022

This distinction is critical for the experimental design and for ensuring the validity of subsequent quantum analyzes.

We explore this fascinating phenomenon using experimentally feasible parameters, chosen to reflect the cutting-edge capabilities of molecular cavity optomechanics. For instance, the single-molecule coupling strength
g_{m}/2\pi\approx$100\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
is a realistic value reported in the literature. However, the true efficacy of our system is profoundly amplified by the collective enhancement

Figure

A thorough understanding of these stable, often monostable, operational windows is therefore absolutely pivotal for reliably generating and controlling entanglement, ensuring that our theoretical predictions are grounded in achievable experimental conditions. The interplay of non-Hermitian dynamics and bistability provides an additional layer of control, potentially allowing for unique switching behaviors and enhanced optical responses

II.5
Linearized quantum Langevin equations for fluctuations

To rigorously explore the intricate quantum properties of our system, particularly the generation and evolution of entanglement, it is essential to move beyond the classical mean-field description and analyze the quantum fluctuations. This is achieved through the technique of linearization, where each operator is expressed as a sum of its steady-state mean value and a small quantum fluctuation around it (e.g.,
O=\langle O\rangle_{ss}+\delta O
). This approach is rigorously valid under conditions of strong optical driving, where these quantum fluctuations are sufficiently small compared to the mean-field amplitudes, allowing us to accurately describe the system’s behavior in the quantum regime while maintaining analytical tractability. By applying this linearization to our full set of quantum Langevin equations (
Equation

3
), we obtain the linearized quantum Langevin equations (LQLEs) for these fluctuations:

\displaystyle\delta\dot{a}

\displaystyle=-(i\Delta_{a}+\kappa_{a})\delta a-iJ_{1}\delta c-i\sqrt{2\kappa_{a}}a_{\text{in}},

(7a)

\displaystyle\delta\dot{c}

\displaystyle=-(i\tilde{\Delta}_{c}-\kappa_{c})\delta c-i\sum_{k=1,2}\tilde{g}_{k}(\delta B_{k}+\delta B_{k}^{\dagger})-iJ_{2}\delta a

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{2\kappa_{c}}c_{\text{in}},

(7b)

\displaystyle\delta\dot{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=-(i\omega_{m}+\gamma_{k})\delta B_{k}-i\tilde{g}_{k}(\delta c+\delta c^{\dagger})+\sqrt{2\gamma_{k}}B_{\text{in},k},

\displaystyle\quad(k=1,2).

(7c)

Here,
\tilde{g}_{k}=g_{k}|\alpha_{c}|
are the enhanced linearized optomechanical coupling strengths. These terms vividly illustrate how the strong classical driving fields amplify the quantum coupling between the cavity photons and the collective molecular vibrations, transforming the original single-photon coupling
g_{k}
into an effectively much stronger linearized coupling
\tilde{g}_{k}
that is essential for generating robust quantum correlations.

To facilitate the analysis of quantum entanglement, especially within the framework of continuous-variable Gaussian states, it is highly advantageous to express these linearized equations in terms of real quadrature operators. These quadratures, analogous to position and momentum in mechanical systems, offer a more intuitive physical interpretation of quantum fluctuations and are the natural basis for constructing the covariance matrix, which fully characterizes Gaussian states. For the optical modes (
O\in\{a,c\}
) and molecular vibrational modes (
k\in\{1,2\}
), they are defined as:

\displaystyle\delta x_{O}=\frac{\delta O+\delta O^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\delta y_{O}=\frac{\delta O-\delta O^{\dagger}}{i\sqrt{2}},

(8)

\displaystyle\delta q_{k}=\frac{\delta B_{k}+\delta B_{k}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\delta p_{k}=\frac{\delta B_{k}-\delta B_{k}^{\dagger}}{i\sqrt{2}}.

(9)

The collective input noise operators
B_{k,\text{in}}
are similarly transformed into their quadrature components.

This transformation allows us to distill the entire system’s dynamics into a remarkably compact matrix form:

\dot{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\mathcal{M}\mathbf{u}(t)+\mathbf{n}(t),

(10)

where
\mathbf{u}(t)=(\delta x_{a},\delta y_{a},\delta x_{c},\delta y_{c},\delta q_{1},\delta p_{1},\delta q_{2},\delta p_{2})^{T}
is the vector that encompasses all the fluctuation quadratures of the system. The vector
\mathbf{n}(t)
comprises the corresponding input noise quadratures. The dynamic evolution of these fluctuations is then dictated by the drift matrix
8\times 8

\mathcal{M}
, which meticulously describes linearized interactions and dissipations:

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}-\kappa_{a}&\Delta_{a}&0&J_{1}&0&0&0&0\\
-\Delta_{a}&-\kappa_{a}&-J_{1}&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&J_{2}&\kappa_{c}&\tilde{\Delta}_{c}&0&0&0&0\\
-J_{2}&0&-\tilde{\Delta}_{c}&\kappa_{c}&-2\tilde{g}_{1}&0&-2\tilde{g}_{2}&0\\
0&0&0&0&-\gamma_{1}&\omega_{m}&0&0\\
0&0&-2\tilde{g}_{1}&0&-\omega_{m}&-\gamma_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&-\gamma_{2}&\omega_{m}\\
0&0&-2\tilde{g}_{2}&0&0&0&-\omega_{m}&-\gamma_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

The careful construction of this drift matrix is central to our subsequent analysis of stability and entanglement, as its eigenvalues directly determine the system’s dynamical behavior.

Given that the input noise operators are Gaussian and the system’s dynamics are now linearized, the steady state of these quantum fluctuations is a zero-mean Gaussian state. Such states are entirely characterized by their
8\times 8
covariance matrix (CM)
V
, whose elements capture all two-point correlations between the quadrature operators. This pivotal matrix is obtained by solving the continuous-variable Lyapunov equation:

\mathcal{M}V+V\mathcal{M}^{\top}=-D,

(12)

where
\mathcal{M}^{\top}
denotes the transpose of the drift matrix
\mathcal{M}
. The right-hand side of this equation is the diffusion matrix
D
, which is diagonal and succinctly summarizes the correlations of the input noise operators, defined as
\frac{1}{2}\langle n_{i}(t)n_{j}(t^{\prime})+n_{j}(t^{\prime})n_{i}(t)\rangle=D_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. For our specific system,
D
is given by:

\displaystyle D=

\displaystyle\text{diag}[\kappa_{a},\kappa_{a},\kappa_{c},\kappa_{c},\gamma_{1}(2n_{\text{th}}+1),\gamma_{1}(2n_{\text{th}}+1),

(13)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(2n_{\text{th}}+1),\gamma_{2}(2n_{\text{th}}+1)].

Here, optical modes are assumed to be coupled to a vacuum bath (
n_{\text{th},a}=n_{\text{th},c}=0
), while molecular vibrational modes are subject to thermal noise with a phonon number
n_{\text{th}}=[\exp(\hbar\omega_{m}/k_{B}T)-1]^{-1}
. It is precisely in this context that the remarkable thermal resilience of molecular optomechanics shines: ultra-high vibrational frequencies of molecules ensure that
n_{\text{th}}
remains exceptionally low, even at elevated temperatures, leading to predictions of persistence of entanglement up to a theoretical limit of
{10}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
. This highlights a unique advantage over conventional systems that demonstrates the profound intrinsic robustness of the platform. Solving the Lyapunov equation for
V
provides the complete statistical blueprint of the quantum state of the system, forming the indispensable foundation for quantifying and analyzing all quantum correlation properties, including entanglement.

II.6
Validity of model approximations

Our theoretical framework relies on two key approximations: the linearization of quantum dynamics and the treatment of collective molecular modes as harmonic oscillators. We now discuss their regimes of validity and the potential impact on our results.

Harmonic approximation and anharmonicity scale.
We model collective molecular vibrations as harmonic modes, a standard and effective approximation in the collective strong coupling regime where
G\gg\gamma,\kappa

Roelli et al. 2024
;
Xiang and Xiong 2024
. However, real molecular potentials are anharmonic. The leading-order anharmonicity for a mode can be quantified by a Kerr nonlinearity coefficient
\xi
. For typical high-frequency molecular vibrations,
\xi/2\pi
is on the order of
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}1\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}

Schmidt and Steel 2024
.

The validity of our approximation rests on the condition that energy shifts due to anharmonicity are negligible compared to the effective collective coupling and decoherence rates. For our parameters, the collective coupling
G/2\pi\approx$100\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
dominates over the estimated anharmonic shift. Furthermore, the thermal phonon number
n_{\text{th}}
remains low (
n_{\text{th}}\ll 1
) for
T<$700\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
, ensuring that the vibrational state occupation is low and minimizes the exploration of the anharmonic part of the potential. While anharmonicity could become relevant under extremely strong driving or for generating non-Gaussian states
Schmidt and Steel 2024
, it is expected to have a minor perturbative effect on the Gaussian entanglement properties quantified here. A complete analysis of anharmonic effects presents a compelling avenue for future research.

Linearization in a nonlinear and bistable system.
The linearization of the quantum Langevin equations is valid when quantum fluctuations are small compared to the classical steady-state amplitudes, a condition met under strong driving. Our system exhibits optical bistability, a fundamentally nonlinear effect.

For all quantum results presented in
Section

III
, we operate exclusively on the upper stable branch of the bistability curve, far from the critical turning points. This is ensured by our comprehensive stability analysis (
Section

II.3
and
Figure

3
) and by initializing our numerical solutions accordingly. The Routh-Hurwitz criterion confirms the dynamical stability of these operating points, validating the linearization approach. We have verified that the results are robust against small parameter variations within this stable monostable regime. Operating near the bifurcation points, where critical slowing down occurs and fluctuations become large, is avoided, preserving the validity of our linearized model for entanglement analysis.

III.1
Influence of nonreciprocal coupling on bipartite entanglement

We begin our exploration of entanglement by meticulously investigating the intricate role of the nonreciprocal coupling strengths,
J_{1}
and

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric architecture to potentially circumvent the inherent trade-offs observed in alternative enhancement schemes, such as those employing optical parametric amplifiers (OPAs), where the amplification of vibration-vibration entanglement often comes at the cost of suppressing optical-vibration correlations. By creating this robust directional quantum bus, we aim to achieve a more balanced and comprehensive enhancement of correlations.

The ability to harness and sculpt quantum correlations through engineered gain and loss is a testament to the profound potential of non-Hermitian physics in designing advanced quantum technologies. III.3
Robustness against thermal noise and the power of molecular collectivity

A paramount finding and a defining characteristic of our work is the remarkable thermal robustness of the generated entanglement, a discovery that unequivocally positions molecular optomechanics as a compelling, high-potential platform for groundbreaking high-ambient temperature quantum technologies. As vividly demonstrated in

While our theoretical model predicts the survival of the entanglement up to

\sim

This enormous ”thermal headroom” is the defining practical advantage of our system, promising unprecedented stability and performance at and well above room temperature.

This remarkable theoretical resilience, which significantly surpasses previous benchmarks, is not merely due to the collective enhancement
\sqrt{N}
alone. It is a direct consequence of the synergistic interplay of three key factors: (i) the inherent thermal robustness endowed by ultra-high frequency molecular vibrations, (ii) the active stabilization afforded by the engineered

At its heart, this exceptional resilience comes from two fundamental features of molecular cavity optomechanics. (i) Ultra-high frequency molecular vibrations: Molecular vibration modes, such as those found in our system (e.g.,
\omega_{m}\approx$30\text{\,}\mathrm{THz}$

\sqrt{N}

{10}^{6}{10}^{10}

It is particularly noteworthy that, unlike some prior observations in other McOM systems where vibration-vibration entanglement was found to be notably susceptible to thermal noise due to the indirect nature of coupling, our

As a result, the entanglement between vibrations mediated internally within the stable gain environment is effectively shielded from the dissipative dynamics of loss cavity. The threshold of entanglement

III.6
Experimental blueprint and robustness analysis

While our theoretical framework identifies optimal operational points, a practical implementation must contend with inevitable experimental imperfections and significant integration challenges. The simultaneous achievement of all target parameters represents a major frontier in experimental molecular photonics and non-Hermitian optics. In this section, we provide a more quantitative discussion of the primary hurdles, propose concrete mitigation strategies, and analyze the robustness of our protocol to parameter variations. A consolidation of these points is presented in
Table

2
.

However, it is paramount to distinguish these engineering challenges from the fundamental physical limits discussed earlier. While our theoretical prediction of entanglement persisting up to
7\text{\times}{10}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
highlights the intrinsic profundity of the underlying physics, it is not a practical operating target. Real-world implementations would face insurmountable material challenges at such extreme temperatures. The following discussion therefore focuses on the key engineering hurdles for achieving robust entanglement under more conventional, albeit still challenging, ambient conditions.

Challenge 1: Integrated platform and thermal management.

Achieving the requisite strong collective coupling (
G/2\pi\approx$100\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
) requires the stable integration of a dense ensemble of
N\approx${10}^{6}$
molecules, a gain medium, and a high-Q cavity. A significant challenge is the thermal loading from the gain pump, which could heat the cavity and lead to the desorption or degradation of the molecular sample. A promising mitigation strategy involves a hybrid dielectric-plasmonic platform to decouple power handling from interaction enhancement, and using frequency up-conversion to bridge the wavelength mismatch between near-IR gain media and molecular vibrations of the THz range
Zou et al. 2024
. Our simulations indicate that the entanglement is reasonably robust to moderate heating, persisting even if thermal effects increase the effective phonon number by an order of magnitude.

Challenge 2: Precision and stability of nonreciprocal Coupling.

Our analysis identifies an optimal asymmetry ratio of
J_{1}/J_{2}\approx 3.3
to maximize entanglement. This can be achieved using several methods. One approach is to use a spinning WGM resonator, where a rotation speed on the order of
\sim${10}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{m}$
(an experimentally feasible value) would provide the necessary asymmetry. Alternatively, for integrated platforms, nonreciprocity can be engineered using carefully designed waveguide-based couplers incorporating optical isolators or through dynamically modulated couplings that create a synthetic magnetic field
Lai et al. 2022a
. However, entanglement is highly sensitive to this ratio. Our analysis shows that a deviation of
\pm 10\%
from the optimal ratio leads to a significant reduction of
\sim 15-20\%
in entanglement. This finding underscores that achieving the required nonreciprocity is not enough; it must be actively stabilized with high precision throughout the experiment.

Challenge 3: Maintaining the PT-symmetric phase.

Integrating a gain medium to realize the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric condition requires maintaining a delicate balance between gain and loss. An imbalance where gain exceeds loss beyond the exceptional point would drive the system into the unstable, broken-symmetry phase (lasing), destroying the desired quantum correlations. This can be mitigated by an active feedback loop on the gain pump laser, stabilized against the measured intracavity photon number. The operational window for this stability is clearly delineated in our stability analysis (
Figure

3
). To remain safely within the unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric phase, the gain pump power would require stability on the order of
\sim 1\%
, a demanding but achievable experimental requirement.

III.7
Entanglement measurement strategies

The experimental verification of the generated multipartite entanglement is as critical as its generation. While the quantum state of the optical cavity modes can be characterized using standard homodyne detection, the direct measurement of high-frequency molecular vibrational modes presents a significant challenge. This often necessitates techniques that can transduce the vibrational state into an easily measurable photonic signal.

A particularly powerful probe for this purpose is optomechanically induced transparency (OMIT)
Roelli et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2025
. In this scheme, a strong control field (corresponding to our pump field
\mathcal{E}_{c}
) drives the system into the desired operational regime, establishing the strong linearized optomechanical coupling. Simultaneously, a weak probe laser is swept across the cavity resonance. Destructive interference between the intracavity probe field and Stokes-scattered photons from the control field opens a narrow transparency window within the cavity’s natural absorption profile. By measuring the transmission of this probe field, one can map out the system’s spectral response. The features of this window, its width, depth, and position, are directly determined by the system’s linearized dynamics.

Importantly, for our system, the principles of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry are predicted to significantly enhance the OMIT signature. The presence of balanced gain and loss can create sharp, high-contrast transparency windows even in weakly coupled or driven systems where they would otherwise be unobservable
Li et al. 2016
;
Pan et al. 2025
. This
\mathcal{PT}
-enhanced OMIT could therefore serve as an exceptionally sensitive tool to probe the system’s quantum properties. The effective optomechanical cooperativity, which can be extracted from the depth and width of the window, serves as a direct proxy for the entanglement-generating interaction strength. Therefore, OMIT spectroscopy provides a direct method to verify that the system has been driven into the strong coherent coupling regime required for robust entanglement, which could allow protocols for the storage and retrieval of optical signals in a molecular device
Yin et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2025
.

Beyond OMIT, complementary techniques offer alternative pathways. Advances in time-resolved Raman spectroscopy could be adapted to analyze the enantioselective dynamics arising from asymmetric optomechanical interactions, potentially revealing chiral molecules
Simone 2025
. Furthermore, the emerging field of entangled polariton states in visible and mid-infrared spectral ranges presents exciting opportunities to develop robust readout mechanisms for vibrational entanglement
Shishkov et al. 2025
. These diverse approaches underscore the feasibility of experimentally confirming the rich quantum correlations predicted by our model, further bridging the gap between theoretical proposals and practical quantum technologies.

III.8
Experimental realization of nonreciprocal coupling
J_{1}
and
J_{2}

First approach.

To experimentally realize the nonreciprocal coupling in this double-cavity molecular optomechanical system, one promising approach is to use a spinning optical microresonator
Jiang et al. 2018
. By rotating one of the cavities (e.g., the passive cavity ”a”) at a high angular velocity, the Sagnac-Fizeau shift introduces a nonreciprocal phase difference between clockwise and counterclockwise propagating modes. This breaks time-reversal symmetry and results in asymmetric coupling rates
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
, where photons couple more strongly from the passive to the active cavity than vice versa.

Second approach.

Alternatively, nonreciprocity can be engineered using carefully designed waveguide-based couplers between the two cavities
Peterson et al. 2018
. By incorporating elements such as optical isolators or circulators within the coupling path, or by exploiting direction-dependent phase modulators, it can be ensured that the coupling strength differs significantly depending on the direction of propagation, thus achieving the desired
J_{1}\gg J_{2}
condition.

Third approach.

Another method involves using dynamically modulated coupling elements, such as tunable beam splitters or interferometric setups with phase-sensitive control
Otterstrom et al. 2021
. By applying external radio-frequency signals to modulators placed between the cavities, the coupling can be made direction-dependent. This allows real-time tuning of
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
to achieve optimal nonreciprocity. Such techniques have been demonstrated in photonic and optomechanical circuits using integrated photonic components, making them compatible with on-chip implementations. The key is to ensure that the coupling section is designed to favor unidirectional photon transfer, effectively creating a quantum channel that protects the entanglement-generating cavity from back-action and noise.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have developed and theoretically analyzed a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric double-cavity molecular optomechanical (McOM) system, unveiling its profound potential to generate robust and highly tunable multipartite quantum entanglement. Our comprehensive theoretical framework convincingly demonstrates that this remarkable capability arises from a sophisticated synergy of three pivotal physical principles: the formidable collective enhancement inherent in molecular optomechanics, the precise directional control facilitated by nonreciprocal coupling, and the ingeniously engineered dissipation provided by the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric architecture.

A central and remarkable finding is the exceptional thermal resilience of the generated entanglement, which persists robustly up to a practical, material-limited target of
700\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
. It is important to underscore that this figure, while representing a highly ambitious experimental goal, demonstrates the platform’s ability to operate in a regime orders of magnitude hotter than conventional cryogenic systems. This resilience is a direct consequence of the ultra-high frequencies of molecular vibrations that suppress thermal noise, the powerful collective enhancement
\sqrt{N}
that elevates the quantum interaction, and the active stabilization from non-Hermitian dynamics. We explicitly acknowledge that, while this theoretical limit showcases fundamental advantages, practical realization at such extreme temperatures would face immense material and engineering challenges. Nevertheless, this intrinsic thermal resilience exceeds that of conventional optomechanical systems and even some previous McOM benchmarks, making it exceptionally promising for developing robust, ambient-condition quantum technologies.

Furthermore, our study directly addresses a key motivation: the potential to circumvent the trade-offs observed in optical parametric amplifier (OPA)-enhanced schemes. Our direct comparative analysis confirms that, unlike the OPA scheme, our architecture allows for the simultaneous amplification of both vibration-vibration and optical-vibration entanglement, fully substantiating its ability to resolve the previously identified trade-off. This is evidenced by the robust generation and dynamic tunability of all investigated bipartite entanglement types, including inter-cavity (
E_{ac}
), cavity-molecule (
E_{aB_{1}},E_{cB_{2}}
), and critically, vibration-vibration entanglement (
E_{B_{1}B_{2}}
), which can be precisely manipulated via cavity detuning and pump fields. All of these findings are rigorously underpinned by a comprehensive stability analysis of the full coupled system, ensuring their physical validity within experimentally attainable operational regimes.

These results provide compelling theoretical insights into the manipulation of quantum correlations in a highly controllable non-Hermitian system. This work opens novel avenues for the design of advanced quantum devices, including nonreciprocal quantum sensors, robust quantum memories, and efficient communication links. Future research could extend to exploring genuine multipartite entanglement, delving deeper into the impact of molecular anharmonicity
Schmidt and Steel 2024
, or integrating sophisticated feedback control mechanisms. Ultimately, this work powerfully underscores the immense and largely untapped potential of PT-symmetric molecular optomechanics to significantly push the boundaries of quantum information science in practical, real-world settings.
