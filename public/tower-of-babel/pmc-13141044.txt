Mental health after the sirens: moral injury in responders and survivors of disaster, mass casualty, and crisis care
Authors: Natasha Keric, Chidinma Tiko-Okoye, Gerald Wang, Ian J Thomas, Purvi Pravinchandra Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Healthcare professionals responding to disasters, mass casualty incidents, pandemics, and sustained high-acuity clinical environments face extraordinary ethical and emotional demands. While burnout and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have historically framed clinician distress, these constructs incompletely capture the moral and existential harm experienced when clinicians are forced to act in ways that conflict with deeply held professional and ethical values. Moral injury has emerged as a distinct framework to explain this phenomenon. This review synthesizes current understanding of moral injury in disaster, mass casualty, and crisis care settings, distinguishing it from burnout and PTSD, examines prevalence and impact on clinicians and healthcare systems, identifies unique individual, organizational, and situational risk factors, and reviews mitigation and recovery strategies across the disaster continuum. Critical gaps in measurement and research are highlighted, particularly in command-driven healthcare environments. Addressing moral injury requires system-level interventions, ethical transparency, and leadership accountability to sustain the healthcare workforce beyond the immediate crisis.Keywords: Post-traumatic stress disorder, Ethics, Mass Casualty Incidents, Delivery of Health Care

Introduction

Disaster response, mass casualty care, and crisis standards of care represent some of the most ethically complex environments in modern medicine. Clinicians in these settings are routinely exposed to high mortality, resource scarcity, command-driven decision-making, and repeated ethical dilemmas.1,3 Although burnout and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are frequently used to describe distress in healthcare workers, these constructs fail to fully account for the moral dimension of suffering experienced when clinicians are constrained from providing care aligned with their professional values.4 5

Moral injury, originally described in military populations, is of increasing relevance to healthcare professionals responding to disasters and sustained crises.4 6 This review provides an overview of moral injury as it applies to disaster, mass casualty, and high-acuity healthcare environments, integrating ethical theory, empirical evidence, and emerging mitigation strategies.

Defining moral injury in healthcare

Moral injury refers to the profound psychological, emotional, and existential distress resulting from perceived violations of one’s moral or ethical code in high-stakes situations.4 6 Unlike PTSD, which is driven primarily by fear-based trauma responses, moral injury is rooted in guilt, shame, betrayal, and loss of moral meaning.6 Importantly, moral injury is not a psychiatric diagnosis and is not codified within the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders.4 6

In trauma and disaster settings, moral injury often manifests in scenarios such as withholding operative intervention due to resource scarcity, triaging patients away from life-saving care based on survivability, or providing care perceived as non-beneficial in the setting of overwhelming system constraints. For example, during mass casualty incidents, trauma surgeons may be required to prioritize patients with higher likelihood of survival over those with potentially salvageable injuries under normal conditions. Similarly, prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) boarding, inability to provide timely operative care, or deviation from standard trauma protocols due to resource limitations may create sustained moral conflict.

In healthcare, moral injury most often arises not from malicious intent or individual failure, but from systemic constraints—such as inadequate resources, staffing shortages, or institutional policies—that force clinicians into ethically untenable positions.5 7 This distinction reframes clinician distress as a predictable consequence of broken systems rather than insufficient individual resilience.5

Ethical shifts in disaster and mass casualty care

Crisis standards of care represent a substantial shift from conventional and contingency care, prioritizing population-level outcomes over individual patient-centered decision-making. Unlike legal standards of care, which are grounded in established norms and liability frameworks, crisis standards are invoked during declared emergencies and rely on ethically justified deviations from usual practice to maximize overall survival.

Under routine conditions, medical decision-making prioritizes patient autonomy. During disasters, pandemics, and mass casualty incidents (MASCAL) events, ethical priorities necessarily shift toward beneficence and justice, emphasizing population-level outcomes over individual preferences.1,3 Crisis standards of care and triage protocols may create situations where clinicians are forced to make allocation decisions that may conflict with professional identity and moral expectations.2 3

In this context, “decision-making” occurs at multiple levels: the institutional decision to activate crisis standards of care, and the downstream bedside decisions—such as triage prioritization, operative delay, or withdrawal of resource-intensive therapies—that directly impact patient outcomes. These layered decisions place clinicians at risk for moral injury, particularly when they are perceived as externally imposed or insufficiently transparent.

Command-driven protocols, while necessary for coordinated disaster response, may conflict with frontline clinical judgment. Trauma surgeons and ICU teams may experience moral tension when institutional directives or triage algorithms override individualized clinical assessment, particularly in cases where perceived survivability is uncertain. These ethically justified shifts place clinicians at high risk for moral injury, particularly when decision-making processes lack transparency or ethical rationale is poorly communicated.1 3 Command-driven rules of engagement, especially during prolonged crises, amplify moral strain and contribute to long-term psychological harm.8 9

Prevalence and impact of moral injury

Available evidence suggests that moral injury affects 30%–50% of healthcare workers in high-stress environments, with increasing prevalence during disaster response and pandemic conditions.8 10 Moral injury is strongly associated with depression, emotional exhaustion, PTSD symptoms, and suicidal ideation.6 8 Severe moral injury has been linked to increased thoughts of self-harm, underscoring its significance as a clinician safety and public health issue.4 6

Beyond individual mental health consequences, moral injury profoundly affects professional identity and engagement. Clinicians experiencing moral injury report decreased job satisfaction, impaired clinical judgment, erosion of teamwork and belonging, and early departure from practice.11,13 At the institutional level, these outcomes contribute to workforce attrition, loss of institutional knowledge, and destabilization of care delivery systems during times of maximal demand, often compounding the problem.13

Unique risk factors across domains

Individual factors

Individual risk factors associated with moral injury include younger age, female gender, and pre-existing mental health conditions.8 These associations reflect differences in exposure, role expectations, and cumulative moral burden rather than inherent vulnerability.

Organizational factors

Organizational contributors are among the strongest drivers of moral injury. Inadequate staffing, insufficient resources, excessive workload, lack of leadership visibility, and cultures that discourage ethical dialog significantly increase moral injury risk.7 10 13 Perceived misalignment between institutional priorities and frontline ethical realities intensifies moral distress and accelerates progression to moral injury.5 7

Situational factors

Situational risk factors include direct involvement in disaster response, repeated exposure to patient death, ethical dilemmas surrounding triage and resource allocation, and command-driven medical rules of engagement.1 8 9 Importantly, moral injury may arise from cumulative “everyday strain,” even outside formal disaster activation, leading to re-traumatization over time.12

Measurement challenges and research gaps

Most existing instruments assess moral distress rather than moral injury. The Measure of Moral Distress for Healthcare Professionals demonstrates good reliability and validity for adult healthcare professionals but does not fully capture the depth, persistence, and identity-level disruption characteristic of moral injury.14 15

Significant research gaps remain, including the absence of validated tools designed for disaster responders and command-driven healthcare environments.14 Emerging data suggest higher risk among women, nursing professionals, and community-based providers, highlighting the need for inclusive and interdisciplinary measurement approaches.8 16

Mitigation and recovery: a multilevel framework

Predisaster mitigation is critical and should include development of trauma-specific triage protocols, simulation-based training for mass casualty scenarios, and explicit ethical frameworks that are reviewed and understood by frontline clinicians. Institutions should establish predefined escalation pathways, define thresholds for crisis standards activation, and incorporate moral injury awareness into disaster preparedness curricula.

Importantly, creating psychologically safe environments where clinicians can openly discuss ethical concerns prior to crises allows for shared mental models and reduces moral isolation during real events. Addressing moral injury requires recognition that it is a system-level problem rather than an individual failure.5

Predisaster preparedness

Pre-event strategies include establishing ethical frameworks, triage protocols, and transparent decision-making structures before crises occur.2 3 Building trust and psychological safety within teams allows clinicians to contextualize morally difficult decisions when they arise.7

During disaster response

During active response, mitigation strategies should include real-time ethical support through embedded ethics consultation, structured interdisciplinary huddles, and explicit communication of decision rationale from leadership.

Mitigation should include structured team debriefs, “moral check-ins,” rotation strategies to limit cumulative exposure, and transparent leadership communication regarding constraints and decision rationale.1 10 These interventions support shared ethical processing and reduce isolation. Trauma teams benefit from clear delineation of roles, transparent triage criteria, and acknowledgment of moral burden as part of operational briefings.

Postdisaster recovery

Post-event recovery requires more than symbolic wellness initiatives. Institutions must provide structured debriefing focused on ethical processing, access to mental health and peer support resources, and longitudinal follow-through to support moral repair.4 6 11 Post-event interventions should include structured after-action reviews that incorporate both operational and ethical analysis. Trauma surgeons play a critical role in facilitating these discussions, helping teams process decisions made under constraint and identifying opportunities for system improvement. Longitudinal follow-up, peer support programs, and access to mental health services are essential to support moral repair.

Toward sustainable institutional solutions

For trauma surgeons, moral injury is uniquely tied to the identity of providing definitive, life-saving care under time-sensitive conditions. Deviation from this role—whether due to resource limitation, triage constraints, or delayed intervention—can create profound ethical conflict. Trauma systems must therefore integrate moral injury awareness into both clinical operations and performance improvement processes.

Long-term solutions to moral injury require changes in the operational and business frameworks of healthcare. Clinician well-being must be recognized as integral to quality, safety, and financial sustainability.13 17 Open communication between frontline clinicians and leaders empowered to enact system-level change is essential.

While this review focuses on clinicians, moral injury may also extend to survivors and families, particularly when care is perceived as delayed, altered, or insufficient due to system constraints. This dual burden further complicates the ethical landscape of disaster care.

Rebuilding professional community—particularly after disasters—is central to moral repair. Ethical transparency, leadership accountability, and shared ownership of complex decisions restore trust, meaning, and professional identity.4 6 16

Conclusion

Moral injury is a predictable and consequential outcome of disaster response and sustained crisis care. Distinct from burnout, it is rooted in ethical conflict and systemic constraints rather than individual weakness.4,6 Recognizing moral injury as a system-level phenomenon shifts responsibility toward healthcare leadership to redesign workflows, incentives, and policies that restore clinicians’ moral agency and professional integrity. Disaster and MASCAL environments amplify moral strain, but early preparation, transparent leadership, and system-level mitigation strategies can meaningfully reduce harm.1,3

Trauma surgeons serve a central leadership role in both acute response and post-event recovery. Beyond clinical decision-making, they are uniquely positioned to lead debriefings, facilitate after-action reviews, and advocate for system-level changes that reduce future moral burden. Their involvement in performance improvement processes allows integration of ethical reflection into traditional morbidity and mortality frameworks.

Addressing moral injury is both an ethical imperative and a strategic necessity for healthcare institutions committed to sustaining their workforce long after the sirens fade.
