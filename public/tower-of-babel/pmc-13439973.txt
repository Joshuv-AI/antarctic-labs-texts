Dysphanosides A–E, five new 13α-malabaricane saponins from Dysphania ambrosioides and their nitric oxide inhibitory activity
Authors: Nguyen Thi Viet Ai, Nguyen Huy Hoang, Bui Huu Tai, Nguyen The Cuong, Bui Van Thanh, Do Thi Thao, Phan Thi Thanh Huong, Pham Hai yen, Nguyen Viet Dung, Nguyen Phuong Thao, Phan Van Kiem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Five new 13α-malabaricane saponins, named dysphanosides A–E (1–5), were isolated from the methanol extract of the aerial parts of Dysphania ambrosioides. Their chemical structures were elucidated through comprehensive analyses of HR-ESI-MS, one-dimensional and two-dimensional NMR spectra, as well as by experimental and theoretical calculations of ECD spectra. Compounds 1–5 exhibited moderate inhibitory activity against nitric oxide (NO) production in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells, with the IC50 values ranging from 58.86 ± 3.01 to 77.09 ± 2.46 µM.

Introduction

The genus Dysphania (family Amaranthaceae) comprises aromatic herbaceous plants that were previously classified within the genus Chenopodium. Several species have long been used in traditional medicine, particularly Dysphania ambrosioides (commonly known as epazote or Mexican tea), which is the most extensively investigated species in this genus.1,2 Phytochemical studies have revealed that Dysphania species contain diverse secondary metabolites, including volatile oils, terpenoids, flavonoids, phenolics, and saponins, although their chemical composition varies considerably depending on the species, geographical origin, and extraction methods.2–5 Traditionally, Dysphania species, especially D. ambrosioides, have been used as anthelmintic agents for the treatment of intestinal parasitic infections. In addition, D. ambrosioides has demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects in several experimental models through the modulation of inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress pathways, and immune signalling mechanisms.2–5 In Vietnamese folk medicine, this plant is commonly employed as an anthelmintic, digestive aid, and remedy for respiratory and gastrointestinal disorders.2

As part of our continuing efforts to identify anti-inflammatory constituents from Vietnamese medicinal plants, the methanolic (MeOH) extract of D. ambrosioides was evaluated and exhibited significant inhibitory activity against nitric oxide (NO) production, showing 67% inhibition at 100 µg mL−1. Consequently, this extract was selected for further phytochemical investigation. In the present study, we report the isolation and structural elucidation of five new 13α-malabaricane saponins (1–5), together with the evaluation of their inhibitory effects on NO production in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophage cells.

Experimental

General experimental procedures

Optical rotations were measured using a Jasco P-2000 polarimeter. Electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra were recorded on a Chirascan spectrometer (Applied Photophysics, Leatherhead, Surrey, UK) using a 1 mm path-length quartz cuvette. One- and two-dimensional NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance NEO 600 MHz spectrometer. High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) data were obtained using an Agilent 6530 Accurate-Mass QTOF LC/MS system. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analyses were performed on pre-coated silica gel and reversed-phase C18 (RP18) plates. Column chromatography was conducted using silica gel (40–63 µm) and RP18 (150 µm) as stationary phases. Semi-preparative HPLC separations were conducted on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a binary pump, autosampler, and diode-array detector (DAD), employing a YMC J'sphere ODS-H80 column (20 × 250 mm, 4 µm). Isocratic elution was performed with methanol–water or acetonitrile–water mixtures as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 3.0 mL min−1.

Plant material

The aerial parts of Dysphania ambrosioides (L.) Mosyakin & Clemants (Amaranthaceae), were collected at Quang Tri, Vietnam, in March 2025 and taxonomically identified by one of the authors, botanist Nguyen The Cuong. Voucher specimens were kept at the Institute of Chemistry, VAST.

Extraction and isolation

The dried powdered aerial parts of Dysphania ambrosioides (4.2 kg) were ultrasonically extracted with MeOH (three times, 15 L, each) to get a methanolic residue (260 g). The residue was suspended in H2O and successively partitioned with n-hexane, dichloromethane, and ethyl acetate to give the corresponding extracts, ICA1 (13.6 g), ICA2 (17.2 g), ICA3 (43.6 g), and the remaining aqueous layer. The aqueous layer was subjected to a Diaion HP-20 column, washed with water, and eluted with 25% and 100% methanol in water (each, 2.5 L) to get fractions W1 (2.0 g) and W2 (22.1 g), respectively. Fraction W2 was subjected to a silica gel column, eluting with CH2Cl2/MeOH (40/1, 10/1, 2.5/1, 1/1; each 1.5 L) to give four fractions, W2A–W2D. Fraction W2C (3.6 g) was further separated by RP18 column chromatography using MeOH/H2O 2.5/1 (3.0 L) as the eluent to yield six fractions, W2C1–W2C6. Fraction W2C1 was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using 60% MeOH in water to give compounds 1 (9.9 mg, tR 27.3 min) and 3 (32.0 mg, tR 31.5 min). Fraction W2C4 was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using 30% ACN in water to get compounds 5 (10.0 mg, tR 41.7 min) and 4 (12.0 mg, tR 44.3 min). Fraction W2C6 was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using 65% MeOH in water to give compound 2 (7.2 mg, tR 29.5 min).

Nitric oxide assay was performed as previously described and referred to SI.6

Theoretical calculation of ECD spectra

Conformational searches and geometry optimizations were carried out using Spartan18 program (Wavefunction Inc., Irvine, CA, USA). A side chain stereoisomer (23S) was submitted to conformational search using the Molecular Mechanics Force Field (MMFF), followed by geometry optimization at the semi-empirical PM3 level. The ten lowest-energy conformers with relative energies lower than 40 kJ mol−1 were further optimized by DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) basic set and IEFPCM models for the solvent of methanol using Gaussian 16 program (Gaussian Inc., Wallingford, CT, USA). The optimized conformers were then subjected to TDDFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) levels in the presence of methanol with IEFPCM models. The ECD spectra at 30 excited states for each conformer were collected and summed to obtain theoretical ECD spectra of calculated stereoisomer using SpecDis v1.71 software (University of Wuerzburg, Wuerzburg, Germany). The half-band was set to ζ = 0.3 eV. UV correction was red-shifted by 10 nm. The ECD spectrum of the enantiomer (23R) was generated as the mirror image of that of the (23S) stereoisomer.6,7

Results and discussion

Compound 1 (Fig. 1) was obtained as a colourless amorphous powder. The negative-ion HR-ESI-MS spectrum of 1 displayed a deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 1045.4961 [M + Cl]− (calcd for [C48H82O22Cl]−: 1045.4991), establishing the molecular formula as C48H82O22 and indicating eight degrees of unsaturation. The 1H-NMR and HSQC spectra of 1 revealed the presence of seven methyl groups [δH 1.70, 1.27, 1.27, 1.21, 1.00, 1.00, and 0.80 (each 3H, s)], one acetyl methyl group [δH 2.11 (3H, s)], one olefinic proton [δH 5.29 (t, 4.8 Hz)], and three anomeric protons [δH 4.54 (d, 8.4 Hz), 4.56 (d, 6.0 Hz), and 4.47 (d, 7.8 Hz)]. The 13C-NMR and HSQC spectra of 1 showed a total of 48 carbon signals, comprising 30 carbons attributable to a triterpene aglycone, 2 carbons of an acetyl group, and 16 carbons corresponding to one hexose and two pentose monosaccharide units (Table 1). In addition, the aglycone exhibited five oxygenated methine carbons [δC/δH: 67.8 (C-2)/3.75 (m), 96.5 (C-3)/2.99 (d, 9.0 Hz), 74.1 (C-12)/4.65 (m), 69.1 (C-23)/4.14 (t, 7.2 Hz), 75.6 (C-24)/3.33 (br s)], two oxygenated quaternary carbons [δC 78.0 (C-14) and 75.8 (C-25)], and one oxygenated methylene group [δC/δH: 67.5 (C-26)/3.54 (2H, s)]. The NMR data of the aglycone of 1 were closely comparable to those reported for astramalabaricoside J, except for the presence of additional signals attributable to a hydroxyl group at C-2,8 suggesting that 1 possesses a hydroxylated malabaricane aglycone. The NMR data of the sugar moiety were consistent with those of 3-O-β-d-xylopyranosyl-(1 → 2)-α-l-arabinopyranosyl-(1 → 3)-β-d-glucopyranoside, except for the presence of additional signals corresponding to an acetyl group.9 A COSY correlation between H-2 (δH 3.75) and H-3 (δH 2.99), together with HSQC correlation from H-2 to C-2 (δC 67.8), from H-3 to C-3 (δC 96.5), as well as HMBC correlations from H3-28 (δH 1.00) and H3-29 (δH 0.80) to C-3 (δC 96.5) established the presence of oxygenated carbons at C-2 and C-3. The side-chain structure was assigned by comparison of its NMR data with those of astramalabaricoside J and confirmed by HSQC, COSY, and HMBC analyses (Fig. 2). A COSY cross peak of H-12/H-13, H-22/H-23/H-24, together with HMBC correlations from H3-18 (δH 1.27) to C-13 (δC 61.2)/C-14 (δC 78.0)/C-15 (δC 44.8), from H3-21 (δH 1.70) to C-17 (δC 129.0)/C-20 (δC 132.7)/C-22 (δC 45.7), from H3-27 (δH 1.21) to C-24 (δC 75.6)/C-25 (δC 75.8)/C-26 (δC 67.5), and from H-26 (δH 3.54) to C-24/C-25/C-27 (δC 23.3) established six hydroxy groups at C-12, C-14, C-23, C-24, C-25, and C-26. In addition, the NMR data of the sugar moieties were assigned based on HSQC, COSY, and HMBC analyses (Fig. 2). Key HMBC correlations from acetyl methyl protons (δH 2.11) and H-2′ (δH 4.96) to the carbonyl carbon (δC 173.1); from H-1′ (δH 4.54) to C-3 (δC 96.5); from H-1″ (δH 4.56) to C-3′ (δC 83.3); and from H-1‴ (δH 4.47) to C-2″ (δC 80.4) established a 3-O-xylopyranosyl-(1 → 2)-arabinopyranosyl-(1 → 3)-2′-O-acetyl-glucopyranoside moiety. This trisaccharide unit was attached to C-3 of the aglycone through an ether linkage. The anomeric proton signals all appeared as doublets with large 3J1,2 coupling constants (Table 2), consistent with β-glycosidic linkages. The large vicinal coupling constant (3J2,3 = 9.0 Hz) suggested a trans diaxial relationship between H-2 and H-3, which was further confirmed by the observed NOESY correlations between H-2 (δH 3.75) and H3-19 (δH 1.00) and between H-3 (δH 2.99) and H-5ax (δH 0.93) (Fig. 3). The NOESY correlations from H-5 to H-9 (δH 0.98), from H-9 to H-12 (δH 4.65), and from H-12 to H-13 suggested that H-12 and H-13 are α-oriented. The stereo chemistry of the tricyclic aglycone (6/6/5-tricyclic skeleton system), the key NOESY correlations shown in Fig. 3 and the nature of the β-orientation of C-19 supported the absolute configuration as (3S, 5R, 8R, 9R, 10R, 12R, 13S, 14R).8 The geometry of the Δ17,20 double bond was assigned as E configuration, as determined by NOESY correlations from H2-16 to H3-21 and from H-17 to H2-22 (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the complete match of the NMR spectral data of the side-chain of 1 and astramalabaricoside J,8 together with the small coupling value of H-23/H-24 (JH-23-H-24 ≈ 0.0 Hz) indicated the relative configurations at C-23, C-24, and C-25 to be (23S*,24R*,25S*).8,10 These relative configurations were also supported by conformational analysis in combination with NOESY correlations. Specifically, the small coupling value of H-23/H-24 indicated a gauche-conformation and the NOESY correlations between H-23 (δH 4.14) and H-24 (δH 3.33)/H-27 (δH 1.21), and the NOESY correlation between H-22 (δH 2.28) and H-24 (δH 3.33) further confirmed their relative configurations as shown in Fig. 3. Acid hydrolysis of 1 yielded d-glucose, d-xylose, and l-arabinose, which were identified by comparison with standard monosaccharides using TLC and confirmed by the positive signs of optical rotations (SI).11,12 Accordingly, compound 1 was elucidated as (23S*,24R*,25S*,2α,3β,12β,14β,17E,25α)-13α-malabaricane-17(20)-en-2,12,14,23,24,25,26-heptol 3-O-β-d-xylopyranosyl-(1 → 2)-α-l-arabinopyranosyl-(1 → 3)-2′-O-acetyl-β-d-glucopyranoside, a new compound named dysphanoside A.

The molecular formula of 2 was C53H88O24 with ten degrees of unsaturation, as determined by a chlorine adduct ion in the HR-ESI-MS spectrum (found m/z 1143.5337 [M + Cl]−; calcd for [C53H88O24Cl]−, 1143.5360). The 1D and 2D NMR spectral data (Table 1) of 2 were similar to those of 1, except additional signals corresponding to a arabinose moiety [δC/δH: 103.3/4.42 (d, 6.0 Hz), 72.6/3.62, 74.1/3.58, 69.2/3.83, 66.2/(3.53 and 3.88)], and the signals corresponding to two hydroxy groups at C-25 (δC 75.8) and C-26 (δC 67.5) in 1 were replaced by the signals of Δ25,26 double bond [δC 145.4 (C-25) and 112.9 (C-26)]. This structural change also resulted lower field signal of protons H3-27 (from 1.21 in 1 to 1.78 in 2). The complete NMR assignments and structure of 2 were established by comparison with those of 1 and further confirmed by HSQC, COSY, HMBC, and NOESY analyses (Fig. 2 and 3). The small vicinal coupling constant between H-23 and H-24 (JH-23-H-24 ≈ 0.0 Hz) indicated a gauche relationship, supporting the relative configuration of C-23 and C-24 as shown in compound 1.8,10 The additional arabinose sugar attached to C-23 as confirmed by COSY cross peaks of H-22/H-23/H-24, and by HMBC correlation from H-1⁗ (δH 4.42) to C-23 (δC 79.8). The large 3J1,2 coupling constant of the anomeric proton at δH 4.42 (Table 1) suggested β-glycosidic linkage. Acid hydrolysis of 2 yielded d-glucose, d-xylose, and l-arabinose, which were identified by comparison with standard monosaccharides using TLC and confirmed by the positive signs of optical rotations (SI).11,12 Consequently, compound 2 was identified as (23S*,24S*,2α,3β,12β,14β,17E)-13α-malabaricane-17(20),25-dien-2,12,14,24-tetrol 3-O-β-d-xylopyranosyl-(1 → 2)-α-l-arabinopyranosyl-(1 → 3)-2′-O-acetyl-β-d-glucopyranoside 23-O-α-l-arabinopyranoside, a new compound named dysphanoside B.

Compound 3 had a molecular formula of C53H88O25 with ten degrees of unsaturation, as determined by its chlorine adduct ion at m/z 1159.5299 [M + Cl]− in the HR-ESI-MS spectrum (calcd. for [C53H88O25Cl]−, 1159.5309). The NMR data of 3 were closely resembling those of 2, except the Δ25,26 double bond signals in 2 were replaced by signals corresponding to an oxygenated tertiary carbon at δC 62.9 (C-25) and a quaternary methyl group [δC 20.0 (C-26)/δH 1.40 (s)], and 25-CH3 group signals in 2 [δC 19.9 (C-27)/δH 1.78 (s)], was replaced by an oxygenated methylene group signals in 3 [δC 64.2 (C-26)/δH 3.65 (2H, s)]. The upfield signals of oxygenated carbons C-24 (δC 66.0) and C-25 (δC 62.9), along with the HRESIMS data, indicated the presence of 24,25-epoxy group. These replacements were further confirmed by COSY cross peaks of H-22/H-23/H-24, and the HSQC and HMBC correlations (Fig. 2). The large vicinal coupling constant between H-23 and H-24 (JH-23-H-24 = 8.4 Hz) indicated an anti-conformation, supporting relative configurations of C-23, C-24 as (23S*, 24S*).8,10 Because of the structure of the epoxy group, it can be inferred that the relative stereochemistry at C-25 must be S*. Acid hydrolysis of 3 yielded d-glucose, d-xylose, and l-arabinose, which were identified by comparison with standard monosaccharides using TLC and confirmed by the positive signs of optical rotations (SI).11,12 Consequently, compound 3 was characterized as (23S*,24S*,25S*,2α,3β,12β,14β,17E)-13α-malabaricane-17(20)-en-24,25-epoxy-2,12,14,26-tetrol 3-O-β-d-xylopyranosyl-(1 → 2)-α-l-arabinopyranosyl-(1 → 3)-2′-O-acetyl-β-d-glucopyranoside 23-O-α-l-arabinopyranoside, a new compound named dysphanoside C.

Compound 5 was isolated as a white amorphous powder. The molecular formula of 5 was determined as C53H86O24 based on its chlorine adduct ion in the HR-ESI-MS spectrum (m/z 1141.5203 [M + Cl]−; calcd for [C53H86O24Cl]−: 1141.5203). Interestingly, the majority of reported malabaricane triterpenoids occur as aglycones, whereas glycosylated derivatives are relatively rare.8,13,14 To date, only 59 naturally occurring malabaricane triterpenoids have been identified, among which merely 23 are glycosides, highlighting the limited occurrence of malabaricane triterpenoid glycosides in nature.8,15–28

Compounds 1–5 were evaluated for their effects on nitric oxide (NO) production in LPS-activated RAW 264.7 cells (SI, Table S1). All the isolated malabaricane compounds exhibited moderate effects with the IC50 values ranging from 58.86 ± 3.01 to 77.09 ± 2.46 µM, compared to that of the positive control, dexamethasone, which showed an IC50 value of 12.44 ± 1.29 µM (Table 3). Malabaricane saponins represent a relatively rare class of triterpenoid glycosides compared with the more extensively studied oleanane, ursane, and dammarane saponins. Consequently, their inhibitory activity against NO production has remained largely unexplored. Among compounds 1–5, the major structural differences were found in the side chain, particularly the presence or absence of glycosylation at C-23. Compounds bearing a glycosyl residue at C-23 generally exhibited stronger NO inhibitory activity than those lacking this substitution, suggesting that glycosylation at C-23 may contribute positively to the inhibitory activity against NO production.

Conclusions

In conclusion, phytochemical investigation of the aerial parts of D. ambrosioides led to the isolation of five new 13α-malabaricane saponins, named dysphanosides A–E (1–5). Their structures were determined based on HR-ESI-MS and extensive NMR, and ECD analyses. Dysphanosides A–E exhibited moderate inhibitory effects on NO production in LPS-activated RAW 264.7 cells. Notably, all these compounds possess a 13α-malabaricane saponin skeleton, a class of natural products that is rarely encountered in nature.
