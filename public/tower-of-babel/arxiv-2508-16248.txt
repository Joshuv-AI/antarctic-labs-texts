A rigorous study on the longitudinal beam coupling impedance of a cylindrical lossy pipe in normal and anomalous regimes Preprint: APS/123-QED
A. Curcio, M. Migliorati, A. Mostacci, L. Palumbo

Abstract
One of the primary issues in designing particle accelerators is the effect of energy losses and collective instabilities caused by the conductivity of the vacuum chamber. Due to its relevance, many authors have long focused on studying the coupling impedance of lossy pipes with various geometries and metals. Most studies were developed for relativistic beams and room-temperature conductivity. In recent years, there has been increasing interest in the high-frequency impedance behavior of ultra-short bunches, as in the case of FELs, and in the anomalous conductivity at cryogenic temperatures of the vacuum chambers. This work introduces a new unifying theoretical framework for analyzing electromagnetic interactions in cylindrical pipes excited by charged particles traveling on the axis. In this context, a key achievement is the derivation of a rigorous expression for the electric conductivity, based on the Boltzmann theory of a Fermi gas, which forms the foundation for subsequent developments. New formulas for the surface impedance of cylindrical pipes are presented, from which a more general impedance expression is derived. Our approach captures the system’s behavior across various energy and frequency regimes. Eventually, the anomalous regime is studied rigorously, providing analytical solutions for the electromagnetic field at cryogenic temperatures. Indeed, new and exact expressions for the anomalous surface impedance for carriers’ reflection coefficients p = 0 and p = 1 are obtained, offering deeper insight into the electromagnetic properties of such systems under extreme conditions.

Introduction

The beam impedance

\sigma

v=\beta c
(with
c
the speed of light in vacuum). Analytical models were developed for the DC (Direct Current) conductivity and AC (Alternate Current) conductivity cases
Piwinski 1984

Semi-analytical models have also been developed for multilayer walls

;
Mounet 2012
;
Migliorati et al. 2019
.

Unlike in the ideal perfectly conductive case, the electromagnetic fields generated by a point charge traveling in a vacuum chamber penetrate the wall, and the tangent electric field at the wall is not zero. The induced current dissipates energy in the material because of Joule’s effect, which corresponds to an energy loss of the source charge. Compared to the perfectly conducting case, the pattern of the fields substantially differs. They remain behind the charge over a distance depending on the value of the electrical conductivity and the energy of the charge.

The present study is motivated by several fundamental questions concerning the validity and applicability of some classical electomagnetic models used in the literature. Specifically, a central issue is how finite particle energy affects both surface and coupling impedance, as well as energy losses, when the true cylindrical geometry of an accelerator pipe is taken into account. Moreover, we investigate whether and under what conditions the concept of anomalous surface impedance, originally developed by Reuter and Sondheimer
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
for electromagnetic waves impinging on flat surfaces, can be meaningfully extended to the case of a cylindrical pipe excited by a Coulombian field of a moving point charge.

In our study, we assume the wall conductivity is so high that the depth of the skin is much smaller than the thickness of the pipe. In deriving fields, we apply the ”Green function” method introduced for a cylindrical lossy pipe in Refs.
Palumbo and Vaccaro 1987
;
Palumbo et al. 1995
, and applied in Refs.
Zimmermann and Oide 2004
;
Stupakov 2020
;
Al-Khateeb et al. 2001
;
Gluckstern 2000
. As a first step, we applied the Boltzmann transport equation
Grosso and Parravicini 2013
to derive a more general expression of the electric conductivity in cylindrical pipe walls, which depends on the relaxation time, the geometry and the velocity of the charge generating the primary fields. We then obtained the surface impedance at the cylindrical pipe wall under normal and anomalous regimes. Further, we derived general expressions of the longitudinal beam impedance, showing the dependence on the energy for copper, aluminum and steel.
Finally, we discussed the results obtained for the wake potential in the case of copper, varying the energy in both normal and anomalous conductivity regimes.
Whenever possible, the results are compared with the existing literature.

To facilitate comparison with articles on the same topic, we recall that in the Refs.
Chao 1993
and
Bane and Sands 1996
developed a theory of impedance and wakefields for infinite particle energy and normal surface impedance.

A theory of coupling impedance for normal surface impedance at finite particle energy was developed by Refs.

In Refs.

In Ref.

In Ref.

In the present paper, we encompass all the above studies in a unique, rigorous frame valid for any particle energy and any regime of conductivity/surface impedance in a cylindrical pipe.

II
Coordinate system and relevant definitions

II.1
Energy loss and wake function

Let’s consider a charge
q_{0}
traveling with constant velocity
\vec{v}=\beta c\hat{z}
on the axis
\hat{z}
of a vacuum chamber with a circular cross-section. The charge
q_{0}
at the position
s_{0}=\beta ct
, called
leading\ charge
, generates electromagnetic fields during the flight in the vacuum chamber. Let us now consider a ”test charge”
q
moving on the axis with the same velocity but at the position
s=s_{0}+z
, where
z
is the distance of the two charges. The parameter
z
gives the distance of the test charge from the leading one, such that
z<0
for a
trailing\ charge
.
The energy variation of the test charge, obtained as the work done by the longitudinal electric force
F_{z}
generated by the leading charge due to the interaction with the pipe walls,

\mathcal{E}(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{z}(s_{0},s=s_{0}+z)ds

(1)

is used to define the longitudinal wake potential
Palumbo et al. 1995
;
Zotter and Keifets 1998
:

w_{z}(z)=-\frac{1}{q_{0}q}\mathcal{E}(z)=-\frac{1}{q_{0}q}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{z}(s_{0},s=s_{0}+z)ds

(2)

which is positive for energy loss. For longitudinally uniform pipes, where
F_{z}
depends only upon
z
, we introduce the energy variation per unit length for longitudinally uniform pipes:

\frac{d\mathcal{E}(z)}{ds}=F_{z}(z)

(3)

Accordingly, the wake function per unit length is defined as:

w^{\prime}_{z}(z)=\frac{dw_{z}(z)}{ds}=-\frac{1}{q_{0}q}\frac{d\mathcal{E}(z)}{ds}=-\frac{E_{z}(z)}{q_{0}}

(4)

II.2
Loss factor and beam loading theorem

The leading charge
q_{0}
can also be subject to the effects of its own fields that change its energy. The
loss\ factor
per unit length is the energy variation of the leading charge per unit length and unit charge and can be written as:

k^{\prime}=w^{\prime}_{z}(0)=-\frac{E_{z}(z=0)}{q_{0}}

(5)

The above definitions imply that the wake function gives the loss factor in the
z\rightarrow 0
limit. This is true as long as
\beta<1
, however, in the relevant limit
\beta\rightarrow 1
, it results in:

w^{\prime}_{z}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}\frac{dw_{z}(z)}{ds}=\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime}_{z}(0^{+})

(6)

which expresses the beam loading theorem.
Indeed, when
\beta\rightarrow 1
, due to the causality, the fields vanish ahead of the leading charge, and the wake potential shows a discontinuity at
z=0
.

II.3
Wake potential of a charge distribution

A bunch is represented here by a distribution of charges moving as an ensemble with velocity
\vec{v}=\beta c\hat{z}
. We describe the bunch as a linear charge distribution
\lambda(z)
, such that:

Q_{b}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda(z)dz

(7)

The wake potential of a point charge is a Green function and allows us to compute the total wake potential generated by any distribution
\lambda(z)
. We split the bunch into infinitesimal slices of charge
dQ_{b}(z^{\prime})=\lambda(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}
, the energy change of this slice with a charge
q
at the position
z
is:

d\mathcal{E}(z-z^{\prime})=-q\lambda(z^{\prime})w^{\prime}_{z}(z-z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}ds

(8)

By summing up the effects of all slices, normalizing to
Q_{b}
we obtain the wake function
W^{\prime}_{z}
of the bunch distribution:

W^{\prime}_{z}(z)=-\frac{1}{qQ_{b}}\frac{d\mathcal{E}(z)}{ds}=\frac{1}{Q_{b}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda(z^{\prime})w^{\prime}_{z}(z-z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}

(9)

In the ultra-relativistic limit
\beta\to 1
, the folding integral has
z
as a lowermost limit due to the causality principle.

II.4
Longitudinal beam impedance and loss factor

The longitudinal impedance per unit length
Z^{\prime}(\omega)
is obtained from the frequency spectrum of the wake function:

Z^{\prime}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\beta c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w^{\prime}_{z}(z)e^{i\frac{\omega z}{\beta c}}dz

(10)

Namely,

Z^{\prime}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{q_{0}\beta c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{E_{z}(z)}e^{i\frac{\omega z}{\beta c}}dz=-\frac{1}{q_{0}\beta c}\tilde{E}_{z}(\omega)

(11)

and

w^{\prime}_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Z^{\prime}_{r}(\omega)e^{-i\frac{\omega z}{\beta c}}d\omega

(12)

Eq.
11
represents our definition of the Fourier transform, thus also establishing the relationship between the domain
z
and the domain
\omega
, which in our work are reciprocals of each other because the phase velocity of the wakefield is equal to the speed of the leading particle.
The impedance is a complex quantity:

Z^{\prime}(\omega)=Z^{\prime}_{r}(\omega)+iZ^{\prime}_{i}(\omega)

(13)

Since
w_{z}(z)
is a real function,
Z_{r}(\omega)
and
Z_{i}(\omega)
are even and odd functions of
\omega
respectively. From the above properties, it results that:

k^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}Z^{\prime}_{r}(\omega)d\omega

(14)

II.5
Loss factor and wake potential of a charge distribution

The total energy lost by a bunch with a frequency spectrum
\tilde{\lambda}(\omega)
is given by:

k^{\prime}_{bunch}=\frac{1}{\pi Q_{b}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}Z^{\prime}_{r}(\omega)|\tilde{\lambda}(\omega)|^{2}d\omega

(15)

where:

\tilde{\lambda}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\beta c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda(z)e^{i\frac{\omega z}{\beta c}}dz

(16)

Finally, the wake potential is given by:

W^{\prime}_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi Q_{b}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Z^{\prime}_{r}(\omega)\tilde{\lambda}(\omega)e^{-i\frac{\omega z}{\beta c}}d\omega

(17)

II.6
Surface impedance

In this paper, we make use of the Leontovich boundary condition
A. 1948
to define the surface impedance
Z_{s}
at the wall of the cylindrical beam pipe:

Z_{s}(\omega)=\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(\omega)}{\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(\omega)}

(18)

where
\tilde{E}_{z}(\omega)
and
\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(\omega)
are the time Fourier transform of the electric and magnetic field components, respectively, evaluated at the pipe surface.

The beam impedance and the loss factor will be expressed in terms of the surface impedance.

III
electrical conductivity inside the walls of the cylindrical pipe

The electromagnetic fields in the pipe walls can be determined from the electrical conductivity properties of the medium.
In Appendix
A
, we derive the general expression of the electrical conductivity for the case of a cylindrically symmetric conducting pipe excited by a point charge traveling at constant speed on its axis:

\sigma(k_{r},\omega)=\frac{3n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{4m}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{3}{\theta}d\theta}{1+i\omega\tau-ik_{r}v_{F}\tau\cos{\theta}}

(19)

where
n_{e}
is the number of carriers per unit volume,
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity of the carriers,
\tau
is the relaxation time (i.e. the mean time between two consecutive collisions of the carriers with the lattice ions),
m
is the effective mass of the carriers in the conduction band,
e
is the elementary charge.
Remarkably, the conductivity depends on the cylindrical symmetry and on the speed of the charge. In particular, the electric conductivity depends on the radial wave-vector
k_{r}
of the fields propagating into the walls, which considers the carriers’ non-local response in the conductive medium, associated with so-called anomalous effects
Grosso and Parravicini 2013
.
We distinguish two regions of interest for the conductivity, which are relevant for calculating the fields inside the walls. These two regions are customarily called ”normal” and ”anomalous”, depending on the value of the
|\mathcal{s}|
parameter:

\mathcal{s}=\frac{-ik_{r}v_{F}\tau}{1+i\omega\tau},

(20)

The normal region is obtained for
|\mathcal{s}|<<1
, where the electric conductivity can be expressed as:

\sigma(\omega)\simeq\frac{3n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{4m}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{3}{\theta}d\theta}{1+i\omega\tau}

(21)

The integral in Eq.
21
can be analytically solved
and it tends to the classical conductivity of the Drude model:

\sigma(\omega)\simeq\frac{n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{m(1+i\omega\tau)}=\varepsilon_{0}\omega_{p}^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}\right)

(22)

also expressed through the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}
.
For
|\mathcal{s}|\gtrsim 1
, no approximations can be used; we are in the anomalous conductivity region where the skin depth is smaller than the electron mean path
v_{F}\tau
, and the general expression in Eq.
19
must be used.

For
|\mathcal{s}|>>1
, the extremely anomalous region is accessed and analytical treatments are possible
Podobedov 2009
.

Generally speaking, the response of the cylindrical pipe to the Coulomb field of the point charge travelling on-axis occurs, in general, through a conductivity which differs from the Drude model.

In the normal regime, another relevant distinction can be made between DC (Direct Current) and AC (Alternating Current) conductivity. The DC approximation of the conductivity may be enough to perform a study when
\omega\tau<<1
in Eq.
22
, thus obtaining:

\sigma_{0}\simeq\frac{n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{m}

(23)

In the above case, the conductivity is purely real, accounting for the loss due to the collisions of the electrons with atoms.

If, on the contrary,
\omega\tau>>1
the AC conductivity is approximated by

\sigma(\omega)\simeq-i\frac{n_{e}e^{2}}{m\omega}

(24)

which does not depend on the relaxation time. Indeed, when the frequency
\omega
of the fields is very high, their oscillation period becomes very short compared to the relaxation time
\tau
. As a result, electrons cannot follow the fast variations of the electric field, they do not frequently collide with the lattice, and there are no losses.
The conductivity shows only an imaginary part that behaves like an inductance, which can store and exchange energy.

IV
Electromagnetic field in the vacuum pipe

The charge density associated with a point charge moving along the main axis
z
of a cylindrical beam pipe of radius
b
at constant velocity
\beta c
can be expressed, in cylindrical coordinates, as:

\rho=q_{0}\frac{\delta(r)}{2\pi r}\delta(z-\beta ct)

(25)

The equation for the scalar potential
\phi
generated by the above monopolar term is:

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial z^{2}}=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_{0}}

(26)

where
\varepsilon_{0}
is the dielectric constant of vacuum and
\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}
is the Lorentz factor of the particle, and we have assumed that
\phi=\phi(r,z-\beta ct)
, i.e. the phase velocity of the electromagnetic field carried by the point charge must be equal to its velocity.
The solution of Eq.
26
can be found through the following expansion of the scalar potential:

\phi=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega=k_{z}\beta c)e^{-ik_{z}(z-\beta ct)}dk_{z}

(27)

where the angular frequency of the field is recognized to be
\omega=k_{z}\beta c
. In the
(r,\omega)
domain, the solution can be written as
Palumbo and Vaccaro 1987
:

\begin{split}\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)&=\frac{q_{0}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigg[K_{0}\left(\frac{\omega r}{\gamma\beta c}\right)-\frac{K_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega r}{\gamma\beta c}\right)+\\
&+B_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega r}{\gamma\beta c}\right)\Bigg]\end{split}

(28)

where
I_{n}(x)
and
K_{n}(x)
are the nth-order modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind, respectively.
The first two terms represent the Coulomb field propagating in a perfectly conductive cylindrical pipe while, in the term proportional to
B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)
, we have isolated the resistivity effect, which must tend to zero for a perfectly conductive pipe.
The axial component
A_{z}
of the vector potential (the only non-zero component for the monopolar term) can be found through the Lorentz gauge:

\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial z}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=0

(29)

Therefore, in the
(r,\omega)
domain:

\tilde{A}_{z}(r,\omega)=\frac{\beta}{c}\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)

(30)

The electric field for
r<b
is then calculated as:

\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)=-ik_{z}\beta c\tilde{A}_{z}(r,\omega)+ik_{z}\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)=\frac{i\omega}{\gamma^{2}\beta c}\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)

(31)

For the cylindrical symmetry, we also notice that:

\tilde{H}_{\varphi}=-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\frac{\partial\tilde{A}_{z}(r,\omega)}{\partial r}=-\frac{\beta}{\mu_{0}c}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)}{\partial r}

(32)

where
\mu_{0}=1/\varepsilon_{0}c^{2}
is the magnetic permeability of vacuum.
Therefore:

\begin{split}\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(r,\omega)&=\frac{q_{0}\omega}{2\pi\gamma}\Bigg[K_{1}\left(\frac{\omega r}{\gamma\beta c}\right)+\frac{K_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}I_{1}\left(\frac{\omega r}{\gamma\beta c}\right)+\\
&-B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)I_{1}\left(\frac{\omega r}{\gamma\beta c}\right)\Bigg]\end{split}

(33)

We know that the Coulomb field inside a perfectly conductive pipe vanishes as
1/\gamma^{2}
and, because of the absence of losses, the beam impedance is purely imaginary. Thus, our main interest regards the term with
B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)
, which gives an electric field on the axis

\tilde{E}^{\sigma}_{z}(0,\omega)=\frac{iq_{0}\omega}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}\gamma^{2}\beta c}B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)

(34)

and a longitudinal beam impedance from (Eq.
11
)

Z^{\prime\sigma}(\omega)=\frac{dZ^{\sigma}(\omega)}{ds}=-\frac{i\omega Z_{0}}{2\pi c(\gamma\beta)^{2}}B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)

(35)

where
Z_{0}=(\varepsilon_{0}c)^{-1}
is the vacuum impedance.

Here we adopted the same approach as in Ref.
Stupakov 2020
and previously in Ref.
Palumbo and Vaccaro 1987
, where the space charge part of the impedance is defined as the
impedance in the same pipe in the limit of perfect conductivity. We have introduced a
B_{0}^{\sigma}
parameter, which is zero for infinite conductivity. It is clear from our equations (see for example Eqs.
28
and
31
for the longitudinal electric field) that in the limit of infinite conductivity the only impedance that remains in the pipe is the space charge part. However, in the definition of longitudinal beam impedance (Eq.
35
) it is possible to notice that the latter is proportional to
B_{0}^{\sigma}
, therefore it does not include the space charge part.

In the following, we derive the quantity
B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)
for all the cases of interest in the normal and anomalous regimes. To this end, we use the general expression of electric conductivity, Eq.
19
and its approximations, deriving fields inside the pipe’s walls.

V
Electromagnetic fields inside the pipe’s walls: normal regime

To study the fields in the normal conducting regime, we start with the wave equation for the electric
potential
Palumbo and Vaccaro 1987
:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r}-\left(\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}+i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c\sigma(\omega)\right)\tilde{\phi}=0

(36)

A general solution of Eq.
36
is found to be:

\tilde{\phi}=\frac{q_{0}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}r\right)

(37)

in which we have introduced the unknown quantity
C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)
that guarantees the continuity of the electric field at the vacuum-pipe interface, and we defined:

\bar{\eta}=\frac{\omega}{\beta c}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+i\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}}

(38)

The Lorentz gauge in the resistive medium of the pipe walls is expressed as:

\tilde{A}_{z}(r,\omega)=\frac{\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\beta}{c}\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)

(39)

Furthermore, the electric field inside the pipe walls can be calculated using Eq.
39
, and it is:

\begin{split}\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)=\frac{\omega}{c}\left(\frac{i}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{q_{0}\beta}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}r\right)\end{split}

(40)

Therefore, the magnetic field is found to be:

\begin{split}\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(r,\omega)&=-\frac{\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\beta}{\mu_{0}c}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}(r,\omega)}{\partial r}=\\
&=\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{q_{0}\beta c}{2\pi}\bar{\eta}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)K_{1}\left(\bar{\eta}r\right)\end{split}

(41)

V.1
Surface Impedance of a cylindrical pipe

By using the definition of
Z_{s}
(Eq.
18
), we get the expression of the surface impedance on the wall of the cylindrical pipe:

Z_{s}(\omega)=\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(b,\omega)}{\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(b,\omega)}=Z_{0}\frac{\sqrt{-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}-i\frac{\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}}}{\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)}\frac{K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)}{K_{1}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)}

(42)

It’s worth noting that when
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
and with high conductivity,
Z_{s}
tends to the surface impedance of a conductor lighted by a plane wave.

The continuity of the axial electric and azimuthal magnetic fields on the pipe wall
r=b
determine the parameters
B^{\sigma}_{0}
and
C^{\sigma}_{0}
uniquely :

\begin{split}&C^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)=\frac{I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}+i\frac{\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)}B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\\
&B^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)=\frac{i\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}c}{\omega bI^{2}_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)\left[\frac{Z_{0}}{Z_{s}}+i\frac{\beta\gamma I_{1}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}\right]}\end{split}

(43)

where we have considered Eq.
42
for the surface impedance
Z_{s}
.

V.2
Longitudinal Coupling Impedance

By using Eq.
43
in Eq.
35
, we obtain the longitudinal beam impedance per unit length:

Z^{\prime\sigma}(\omega)=\frac{dZ^{\sigma}(\omega)}{ds}=\frac{Z_{0}}{2\pi bI^{2}_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)\left[\frac{Z_{0}}{Z_{s}}+\frac{i\beta\gamma I_{1}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{\omega b}{\gamma\beta c}\right)}\right]}

(44)

It’s worth of note that for
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
, Eq.
44
tends to:

Z^{\prime\sigma}(\omega)\simeq\frac{Z_{0}}{2\pi b\left[\frac{Z_{0}}{Z_{s}}+\frac{i\omega b}{2c}\right]}

(45)

that was found in Ref.
Chao 1993
for a beam with the shape of a thin ring propagating with velocity
c
in a cylindrical pipe.

Eq.
44
has a general validity; the longitudinal impedance depends on the energy
\gamma
, the conductivity
\sigma(\omega)
and the radius of the pipe
b
.
The Bessel function
I_{0}
at the denominator shows that even for a point charge, the impedance frequency spectrum does not extend much beyond the frequency
\omega^{*}=\gamma\beta c/b
where there is exponential decay. In fact, due to the relativistic contraction of the primary fields, the point charge has an effective excitation length
b/\beta\gamma
on the conducting walls. Noticeably, the exponential drop does not exist if
\gamma\to\infty
.

If
\omega^{*}\tau<<1
, the impedance spectrum of the point charge essentially falls below the frequency
1/\tau
, thus it is sufficient to consider a DC conductivity (Eq.
23
) in the expression of the surface impedance, since
\omega\tau<<1
is always verified.

Conversely, if
\omega^{*}\tau\gtrsim 1
, one has to consider the general AC conductivity (Eq.
22
).
For instance, in the case of copper, where
\sigma_{0}\simeq 5.7\times 10^{7}\ \Omega^{-1}m^{-1}
and
\tau\simeq 2.2\times 10^{-14}s
, the DC condition is verified when
b/\beta\gamma>>7\times 10^{-6}m
.

and

, we show a case of DC conductivity, corresponding to
\gamma=10
and
b=2.5\ mm
, for three different materials whose parameters are reported in Tab.

VI
Electromagnetic fields inside the pipe’s walls: Anomalous regime

For the calculation of the electromagnetic field in anomalous conductivity conditions inside the pipe walls, we consider the following wave equation:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r}-\left(\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{\phi}+i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p}\right)=0

(52)

where the conductivity
\sigma=\sigma(k_{r},\omega)
is function of both the radial and longitudinal wavevectors,
k_{r}
and
k_{z}=\omega/\beta c
respectively. The convolution
(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p}
is intended to be applied in the radial plane, with respect to the coordinate
r
, and is dependent on the parameter
p
, as shown in Appendix
C
. Specifically, the
p
-index is the probability of specular reflection of the charge carriers on the pipe surface.
For the case of perfect specular reflection,
p=1
, while for completely diffuse reflection,
p=0
. For specular reflection,
(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p}
is defined as (see Eq.
96
):

\begin{split}&(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p=1}=\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\times\\
&\times\tilde{\phi}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}\end{split}

(53)

while, for
p=0
:

\begin{split}&(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p=0}=\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\int_{b}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\times\\
&\times\tilde{\phi}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}\end{split}

(54)

The solution of Eq.
52
is rather complex.
For the case
p=1
, one can proceed in two ways. The first makes use of the similarity approach, seeking a solution analogue to that found in Sec.
IV
, while exploiting the properties of the Hankel-transform (see Appendix
C.2
for more details).

Otherwise, the wave equation for
p=1
can be found by following an analogue procedure by Reuter and Sondheimer, as described in Appendix
C.2.2
. Such a procedure is based on the equivalence principle: the specular reflection of the carriers is justified by fictitious mirror charges placed in the space
r<b
, which, as field sources for the region
r>b
, behave equivalently to surface electric currents. In this picture, the field is decaying on the scale of the skin depth
\delta
on both sides of the surface. This approach presents no issues for planar geometry, as demonstrated by Reuter and Sondheimer, where the half-space on one side of the surface is semi-infinite, as is the space on the opposite side. For cylindrical symmetry, additional complications may arise if the conductivity is not such that
\delta<<b
in the region
r>b
. Therefore, a Reuter/Sondheimer-like approach must be intended for cylindrical symmetry only to be valid for good conductors.

VI.1
Surface Impedance in the anomalous regime

For
p=0
the solution can be found by generalizing the approach found in Ref.
Dingle 1953
, originally developed for a planar surface, to cylindrical symmetry.

In this section, we report the results found for
p=0
(see Appendix
C.1
) and for
p=1
, the latter obtained through the Hankel-transform approach (see Appendix
C.2
).

Thus, for
r>b
, the electric field is found to be:

\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)=\frac{i\omega}{\beta c}\frac{q_{0}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}C^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\xi_{p}(r,\omega)

(55)

where, again, we have defined a parameter
C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)
that guarantees the continuity of the electric field at the vacuum-pipe interface.
The dependence of the field on
r
is fully contained in
\tilde{\xi}_{p}(r,\omega)
.
In the Appendix
C
it is demonstrated that:

\xi_{0}(r,\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}J_{0}(k_{r}r)k_{r}\tilde{\xi}_{0}(k_{r},\omega)

(56)

where

\tilde{\xi}_{0}(k_{r},\omega)=b\frac{J_{1}\left(k_{r}b\right)}{k_{r}}e^{-\frac{k_{r}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(\kappa,\omega)}{\kappa^{2}}\right]}}{\kappa^{2}+k_{r}^{2}}d\kappa}

(57)

While:

\xi_{1}(r,\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}

(58)

The magnetic field in the resistive walls of the pipe is then found via Eq.
32
, considering the electric field in Eq.
55
.
In the anomalous region, the surface impedance will differ according to the
p
-parameter that best fits the microscopic properties of the vacuum-pipe interface. Thus, we define a surface impedance dependent on
p
.
For
p=0
, as shown in Appendix
C.1
, it is possible to obtain:

Z_{s,0}\simeq-\frac{i\pi Z_{0}\omega}{c\int_{0}^{\infty}\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{k_{r}^{2}}\right]}dk_{r}}

(59)

Instead, for
p=1
, it is found that (see Appendix
C.2
):

Z_{s,1}=\frac{i\omega Z_{0}}{c}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(\beta^{2}-\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{1}\left(k_{r}b\right)k^{2}_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}}

(60)

For good conductors, the surface impedance for
p=1
can also be simplified into (see Appendix
C.2.2
):

Z_{s,1}\simeq-\frac{2iZ_{0}\omega b}{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{r}J^{2}_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)dk_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}

(61)

The above expressions of surface impedance are not only an extension of the Reuter theory to a cylindrical geometry, but also take into account the features of the primary fields, which are generated by a point charge traveling on the axis with speed
\beta c
. It is worth noting that Eq.
60
reproduces the classical expression Eq.
42
in the limit of local response of the charge carriers (normal region of conductivity) and, for good normal conductors, both Eqs.
59
and
60
reproduce the classical results.

VIII
Conclusions

This paper outlines a rigorous study of surface and coupling impedance in cylindrical symmetry.
To this end, we adopted the convention to separate the space-charge from the resistive wall impedance as in Refs.
Palumbo and Vaccaro 1987
and
Stupakov 2020
but differently from Ref.
Zimmermann and Oide 2004
.

The theory we developed is valid for any value of particle energy, thus it can provide an important tool for the correct evaluation of impedance, loss rate and wakefields in both normal and anomalous conductivity regimes.

Our goal is unifying approximate theories published in the past (although valid in many practical cases) into a single coherent framework, also for clarifying their range of validity.

We have developed a new theoretical approach and derived a rigorous formula for the electric conductivity of a cylindrical pipe excited by a charged particle (Eq.
19
, or
96
), starting from the Boltzmann theory of a Fermi gas for the carriers. Additionally, we obtained new general formulas for the surface impedance of cylindrical pipes (Eq.
42
), which also led to a new impedance expression (Eq.
44
via Eq.
42
). A thorough discussion has been provided on the transition from low to high energy and from low to high frequency, governed by the dimensionless parameter

\omega b/\gamma\beta c
. The analysis includes a rigorous treatment of the anomalous regime (Eqs.
52
,
53
, and
54
), and presents analytical solutions for the electromagnetic field in the cylindrical pipe at cryogenic temperatures (Eqs.
55
,
56
,
58
). Finally, we derive new and rigorous expressions for the anomalous surface impedance for both

p=0
and
p=1
cases (Eqs.
59
and
60
/
61
).

To be more specific in the comparison with previous literature, our Eq.
44
is formally similar to the result found in Ref.
Stupakov 2020
, however, the surface impedance that we consider is not the same. In fact, in Eq.
44
, we consider the rigorously derived surface impedance for a cylindrical beam pipe in any regime of conductivity and particle energy. Differently, Ref.
Stupakov 2020
considers the surface impedance for an electromagnetic plane wave impinging on a normal flat conductor. It’s important to highlight that the surface impedance for an electromagnetic wave impinging on a flat surface (Reuter and Sondheimer formula
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
) is a good approximation for very large
\gamma
, but it may be an inconsistent choice within a theory that aims to be general for any particle energy.

It’s worth recalling that neither Ref.
Zimmermann and Oide 2004
nor
Stupakov 2020
treat the case of anomalous conductivity, and other works that have treated the anomalous regime (see Refs.
Stupakov et al. 2015
;
Podobedov 2009
) have been developed for infinite energy and only for specific cases of the anomalous conductivity (
p=0

Stupakov et al. 2015
, extremely anomalous region
Podobedov 2009
).

Indeed, the real behavior of any anomalous material is determined by a combination of the two cases
p=0
and
p=1
(both derived in this paper). We posed the foundations for a general treatment of the cryogenic pipes, necessary for a comparison with measurements of the wakefields effects in the anomalous regime.

Beside its theoretical value, the work is relevant to particle accelerator design, with particular attention to machines based on cryogenic structures and/or moderate-energy ultra-short particle bunches.

For instance, we mention the case of moderate
\gamma
values (
\lesssim 500
) and millimetric pipes in the case of FELs piloted by sub-picosecond bunches, as well as the case of low-energy accelerators as hadron accelerators at non-fully relativistic energy.

Appendix A
Theory of the electric conductivity via the Boltzmann equation

The Boltzmann equation describing the transport of a non-relativistic electron under the action of an external electromagnetic field and in the presence of collisions with background particles (atoms, electrons, ions) is given by
Grosso and Parravicini 2013
:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}-\frac{e}{m}\left(\vec{E}+\vec{\mathcal{v}}\times\vec{B}\right)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\mathcal{v}}f+\mathcal{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{r}}f=-\frac{f-f_{0}}{\tau}

(63)

The function
f
represents the phase space density of the charge carriers (here assumed to be electrons),
-e
is the electron charge, and
m
is the effective mass of the carriers in the conduction band. The velocity vector
\vec{\mathcal{v}}
represents here the velocity of the carriers. The phase space density at the equilibrium is
f_{0}
. The relaxation time of the system is
\tau
.
For non-relativistic carriers, the interaction with the magnetic field can be neglected. Moreover, by using the perturbative approach
f=f_{0}+f_{1}
and transforming the Boltzmann equation from the
(\vec{r},t)
domain into the Fourier domain
(\vec{k},\omega)
, it is possible to obtain an equation for the perturbative term

i\omega f_{1}-\frac{en_{e}}{m}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{\mathcal{v}}}f_{FD}-i\vec{\mathcal{v}}\cdot\vec{k}f_{1}=-\frac{f_{1}}{\tau}

(64)

which is solved by:

f_{1}=\frac{en_{e}\tau\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{v}}}{m\mathcal{v}\left(1+i\omega\tau-i\vec{\mathcal{v}}\cdot\vec{k}\tau\right)}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}}

(65)

To obtain Eq.
65
, the hypothesis of isotropic unperturbed distribution
f_{FD}
has been recalled, for which:

\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{\mathcal{v}}}f_{FD}=\frac{\vec{\mathcal{v}}}{\mathcal{v}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}}

(66)

The general definition of current density associated with the electric carriers is given by:

\vec{j}=-e\int f_{1}\vec{\mathcal{v}}d^{3}\mathcal{v}

(67)

Thus, the current density component along the
z
-axis is:

j_{z}=-e\int f_{1}\mathcal{v}_{z}d^{3}\mathcal{v}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathcal{v}\frac{e^{2}n_{e}\tau E_{z}\mathcal{v}\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}}{m\mathcal{v}\left(1+i\omega\tau-ik_{z}\mathcal{v}\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}\tau-ik_{r}\mathcal{v}\cos{\theta}\tau\right)}\left(-\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}}\right)\mathcal{v}\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}\mathcal{v}^{2}\sin{\theta}

(68)

Therefore, it is eventually possible to demonstrate that:

\sigma(k_{r},\omega)=\frac{j_{z}}{E_{z}}=\frac{\pi n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathcal{v}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin{\theta}\frac{\mathcal{v}^{3}\sin^{2}{\theta}\left(-\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}}\right)}{1+i\omega\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{v}}{\beta c}\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}\right)\tau-ik_{r}\mathcal{v}\cos{\theta}\tau}

(69)

By neglecting thermal effects, the derivative of the Fermi-Dirac distribution can be approximated as:

\left(-\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}}\right)\simeq\frac{3\delta(\mathcal{v}-v_{F})}{4\pi v_{F}^{3}}

(70)

For normal metals,
v_{F}<<c
, and for
\beta\simeq 1
, the conductivity can be simplified as:

\sigma(k_{r},\omega)\simeq\frac{3n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{4m}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{\sin^{3}{\theta}}{1+i\omega\tau-ik_{r}v_{F}\cos{\theta}\tau}

(71)

The above integral can be solved by introducing the variable

\mathcal{s}=\frac{-ik_{r}v_{F}\tau}{1+i\omega\tau},

(72)

from which the conductivity is finally obtained as:

\sigma(k_{r},\omega)=\frac{3n_{e}e^{2}\tau}{4m(1+i\omega\tau)}\left[\frac{2}{\mathcal{s}^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{s}^{2}-1}{\mathcal{s}^{3}}\log{\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{s}}{1-\mathcal{s}}\right)}\right]

(73)

Appendix B
Resonance frequency

The frequency of the wakefield generated by a particle with
\beta\lesssim 1
can be calculated by studying the poles of the function:

\frac{Z_{0}}{2\pi b\left[\sqrt{-\frac{i\sigma_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega(1+i\omega\tau)}}+\frac{i\omega b}{2c}\right]}

(74)

where the Bessel
I
functions appearing in Eq.
44
have been approximated for large argument, consistently with the high-energy limit. However, it must be noticed that, differently from Ref.
Bane and Sands 1996
, no assumption is made on the value of the
\omega\tau
parameter.
Eventually, our study of the poles leads to a resonance frequency equal to:

\begin{split}\omega_{res}&=Re\Bigg[\frac{i}{4\tau}-\frac{1}{4}\Bigg(-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}-\frac{32\sqrt[3]{4}\sigma_{0}c^{2}}{\left(27b^{4}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}+3\sqrt{81b^{8}c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}^{4}\sigma_{0}^{2}+3072b^{6}c^{6}\varepsilon_{0}^{3}\sigma_{0}^{3}\tau^{3}}\right)^{1/3}}+\\
&+\frac{4\left(18b^{4}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}+2\sqrt{81b^{8}c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}^{4}\sigma_{0}^{2}+3072b^{6}c^{6}\varepsilon_{0}^{3}\sigma_{0}^{3}\tau^{3}}\right)^{1/3}}{3^{2/3}b^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\tau}\Bigg)^{1/2}+\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}+\frac{8\sqrt[3]{4}c^{2}\sigma_{0}}{\left(27b^{4}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}+3\sqrt{81b^{8}c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}^{4}\sigma_{0}^{2}+3072b^{6}c^{6}\varepsilon_{0}^{3}\sigma_{0}^{3}\tau^{3}}\right)^{1/3}}+\\
&-\frac{\left(18b^{4}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}+2\sqrt{81b^{8}c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}^{4}\sigma_{0}^{2}+3072b^{6}c^{6}\varepsilon_{0}^{3}\sigma_{0}^{3}\tau^{3}}\right)^{1/3}}{3^{2/3}b^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\tau}+\\
&\frac{i}{2\tau^{3}\sqrt{-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}-\frac{32\sqrt[3]{4}\sigma_{0}c^{2}}{\left(27b^{4}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}+3\sqrt{81b^{8}c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}^{4}\sigma_{0}^{2}+3072b^{6}c^{6}\varepsilon_{0}^{3}\sigma_{0}^{3}\tau^{3}}\right)^{1/3}}+\frac{4\left(18b^{4}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}+2\sqrt{81b^{8}c^{4}\varepsilon_{0}^{4}\sigma_{0}^{2}+3072b^{6}c^{6}\varepsilon_{0}^{3}\sigma_{0}^{3}\tau^{3}}\right)^{1/3}}{3^{2/3}b^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\tau}}}\Bigg)^{1/2}\Bigg]\end{split}

(75)

Appendix C
Wave equation for an arbitrary reflection of charge carriers at the pipe’s surface

We retrace the Reuter and Sondheimer approach
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
for an arbitrary value of the specular reflection probability
p
, generalizing it to the cylindrical case. First of all,
we write the Boltzmann transport equation:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}-\frac{e}{m}\left(\vec{E}+\vec{\mathcal{v}}\times\vec{B}\right)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{\mathcal{v}}}f+\vec{\mathcal{v}}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{r}}f=-\frac{f-f_{0}}{\tau}

(76)

In the following, we’ll consider non-relativistic carriers, therefore the interaction with the magnetic field can be neglected. Using the perturbative approach
f=f_{0}+f_{1}
, where
f_{0}=n_{0}f_{FD}
, with
n_{0}
the number of carriers per unit volume in equilibrium and
f_{FD}
the Fermi-Dirac distribution of the conductive fluid, since at the equilibrium the momenta of the quantum carriers obey such a distribution. Being
f_{1}
of higher order compared to
f_{0}
, Eq.
76
becomes:

\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial t}-\frac{e}{m}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{\mathcal{v}}}f_{0}+\vec{\mathcal{v}}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{r}}f_{1}=-\frac{f_{1}}{\tau}

(77)

We now specialize Eq.
77
to the case of a cylindrical conductive pipe, with the electric field propagating along
z
inside the pipe, longitudinally polarized:

\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial t}-\frac{e}{m}\tilde{E}_{z}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}+\mathcal{v}_{r}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial r}+\mathcal{v}_{z}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial z}=-\frac{f_{1}}{\tau}

(78)

In the frequency domain, Eq.
78
can be written as:

i\omega f_{1}-\frac{e}{m}\tilde{E}_{z}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}+\mathcal{v}_{r}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial r}-\mathcal{v}_{z}\frac{i\omega}{\beta c}f_{1}=-\frac{f_{1}}{\tau}

(79)

Finally, one obtains:

\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial r}+\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}f_{1}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}f_{1}=\frac{n_{0}e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)

(80)

The solution of Eq.
80
can be easily found as:

f_{1}=n_{0}e^{-\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r-b)}\left(\mathcal{F}(\vec{\mathcal{v}})+\frac{e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-b)}\right)dr^{\prime}

(81)

where
\mathcal{F}(\vec{\mathcal{v}})
is an arbitrary function of the velocity and
r_{0}
is an arbitrary point, both determined by boundary conditions. To avoid non-physical unlimited fields, the solution for
\mathcal{v}_{r}<0
, i.e. for the electrons that move towards the surface, will be set as:

f_{-}=f_{0}-n_{0}e^{-\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r-b)}\frac{e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\int_{r}^{r_{0}\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-b)}dr^{\prime}

(82)

For the electrons that move inner inside the surface (
\mathcal{v}_{r}>0
), one can assume that part of them comes from the fraction
p
of electrons that move towards the surface (
\mathcal{v}_{r}<0
) and, once they reach it, they are specularly reflected
\mathcal{v}_{r}\rightarrow-\mathcal{v}_{r}
. The remaining electrons have no directionality after reflection because they are backscattered at random angles.
The boundary condition in this case, for the above reasons, is written as:

f_{+}(r=b,r_{0}\rightarrow b)=pf_{-}(r=b,\mathcal{v}_{r}\rightarrow-\mathcal{v}_{r})+(1-p)f_{0}

(83)

\small f_{0}+n_{0}\mathcal{F}(\vec{\mathcal{v}})=pf_{0}+pn_{0}\frac{e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\int_{b}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{-\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-b)}dr^{\prime}+(1-p)f_{0}

(84)

which determines
\mathcal{F}(\vec{\mathcal{v}})
for
\mathcal{v}_{r}>0
:

\mathcal{F}(\vec{\mathcal{v}})=p\frac{e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\int_{b}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{-\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-b)}dr^{\prime}

(85)

Therefore, the dynamic solution of the Boltzmann equation for the electrons with velocity
\mathcal{v}_{r}>0
is found as:

f_{+}=f_{0}+\frac{n_{0}e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\left[p\int_{b}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-r)}dr^{\prime}+\int_{b}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-r)}dr^{\prime}\right]

(86)

After a straightforward manipulation, defining
\tilde{E}_{z}(r)=\tilde{E}_{z}(|r|)
different from zero only for
r>0
, one can also find the expression:

f_{+}=f_{0}+\frac{n_{0}e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\left[p\int_{0}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-r)}dr^{\prime}+(1-p)\int_{b}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-r)}dr^{\prime}\right]

(87)

By its definition, we now write the electron current density as:

j_{z}=-e\int\left[\delta(\mathcal{v}_{r})f_{+}+\delta(-\mathcal{v}_{r})f_{-}\right]\mathcal{v}_{z}d^{3}\mathcal{v}

(88)

For convenience, we can define two currents in such a way that
j_{z}=j_{+}+j_{-}
:

j_{\pm}=-e\int\delta(\pm\mathcal{v}_{r})f_{\pm}\mathcal{v}_{z}d^{3}\mathcal{v}

(89)

where:

\footnotesize j_{+}=-e\int\delta(\mathcal{v}_{r})\frac{n_{0}e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\left[p\int_{0}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-r)}dr^{\prime}+(1-p)\int_{b}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r^{\prime}-r)}dr^{\prime}\right]\mathcal{v}_{z}d^{3}\mathcal{v}

(90)

j_{-}=e\int\delta(-\mathcal{v}_{r})\frac{n_{0}e}{m\mathcal{v}_{r}}\frac{\partial f_{FD}}{\partial\mathcal{v}_{z}}\left[\int_{r}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{\mathcal{v}_{r}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\mathcal{v}_{z}}{\beta c\mathcal{v}_{r}}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr\right]\mathcal{v}_{z}d^{3}\mathcal{v}

(91)

In the following, we work out the integrals that define the forward and backward currents. We start from
j_{-}
:

j_{-}=-\pi e\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathcal{v}\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{n_{0}e}{m\mathcal{v}}\frac{3\delta(\mathcal{v}-v_{F})}{4\pi v_{F}^{3}}\mathcal{v}^{3}\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\left[\int_{r}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr\right]

(92)

By integrating over
v
, it is easy to obtain:

j_{-}=-\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\left[\int_{r}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr\right]

(93)

Thus, for the opposite current, one obtains:

\begin{split}j_{+}&=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\Bigg[p\int_{0}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}+\\
&+(1-p)\int_{b}^{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}\Bigg]\end{split}

(94)

The integrals over
\theta
appearing in the current densities can be solved analytically, giving cumbersome expressions and involving transcendent functions.

The wave equation for the electric field inside the metal with anomalous conductivity is, eventually:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{E}_{z}-i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c(\sigma\star\tilde{E}_{z})_{p}=0

(95)

where we have defined the convolution between the electric conductivity and field as:

\begin{split}(\sigma\star\tilde{E}_{z})_{p}=j_{+}+j_{-}&=\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\Bigg[p\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}+\\
&+(1-p)\int_{b}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}\Bigg]\end{split}

(96)

Therefore, generally speaking, Eq.
95
is an integro-differential equation. In analogy to what is shown in the paper for calculating the electromagnetic field in conditions of normal conductivity, a wave equation for the electric potential may be needed. This would pave the way for finding the surface impedance and, ultimately, the coupling impedance, at least for the specular reflection of charge carriers (
p=1
). The wave equation for the electric potential is straightforwardly found as:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r}-\left(\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{\phi}+i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p}\right)=0

(97)

The above equation is quite complicated due to the presence of a convolution integral, so it may not seem useful. However, we will show methods to solve it or find an expression for the surface impedance in different cases of interest.

C.1
Resolution of the wave equation for p=0 and surface impedance

Following the analogous procedures of Ref.
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
, we would now like to approach the solution of the equation:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{E}_{z}-i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\int_{b}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}=0

(98)

Similarly to Ref.
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
, it is possible to define a function
g
such that:

g(r,\omega)=-i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\int_{0}^{b}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}\ for\ r<b,\ 0\ otherwise

(99)

In this way, Eq.
98
can be recast into:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{E}_{z}-i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c\frac{3n_{0}e^{2}}{4mv_{F}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\sin^{3}{\theta}\sec{\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{E}_{z}(r^{\prime},\omega)e^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)(r-r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}=g(r,\omega)

(100)

Moreover, one can define:

F(k_{r})=\int_{0}^{\infty}drJ_{0}(k_{r}r)r\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)

(101)

G(k_{r})=\int_{0}^{\infty}drJ_{0}(k_{r})rg(r,\omega)

(102)

H(k_{r})=\int_{0}^{\infty}drJ_{0}(k_{r}r)r\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}drJ_{0}(k_{r})\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\right)

(103)

K(k_{r})=\int_{0}^{\infty}drJ_{0}(k_{r}r)re^{\left(\frac{(1+i\omega\tau)\sec{\theta}}{v_{F}\tau}-\frac{i\omega\tan{\theta}}{\beta c}\right)r}

(104)

A successive integration by parts of Eq.
103
shows that:

H(k_{r})=F(k_{r})k_{r}^{2}-\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}bJ_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)-\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})bJ_{1}\left(k_{r}b\right)k_{r}

(105)

The electric field that solves Eq.
98
can be found following the considerations in Ref.
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
, based on a mathematical procedure introduced in Ref.
Hopf 1934
.
However, using the simplified result shown in Ref.
Dingle 1953
and in full analogy with it, one can obtain the following expression:

F(k_{r})=\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})\xi_{0}(k_{r},\omega)=bJ_{1}\left(k_{r}b\right)\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})}{k_{r}}e^{-\frac{k_{r}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(\kappa,\omega)}{\kappa^{2}}\right]}}{\kappa^{2}+k_{r}^{2}}d\kappa}

(106)

It is important to note that the electric field can be determined if its value at the boundary of the pipe is known. The continuity of the field at the boundary of the pipe has been discussed in the main text of this manuscript.
Inserting Eq.
106
into
105
, and considering the limit
k_{r}b>>1
, i.e.
H(k_{r})\rightarrow 0
, allows obtaining:

-k_{r}\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})e^{-\frac{k_{r}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(\kappa,\omega)}{\kappa^{2}}\right]}}{\kappa^{2}+k_{r}^{2}}d\kappa}-\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}-\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})k_{r}\simeq 0

(107)

Furthermore, the same limit allows finding an expression for the surface impedance starting from Eq.
105
.
In fact:

\frac{\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}}{\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})}\simeq\left[k_{r}e^{-\frac{k_{r}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(\kappa,\omega)}{\kappa^{2}}\right]}}{\kappa^{2}+k_{r}^{2}}d\kappa}-s\right]\simeq-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{k_{r}^{2}}\right]}dk_{r}

(108)

Indeed, based on Eq.
18
and on the relation between fields and potentials, it is generally possible to write for the surface impedance:

Z_{s}=\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+},\omega)}{\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(b^{+},\omega)}=\frac{ik_{z}\tilde{\phi}(b^{+},\omega)-ik_{z}\beta c\tilde{A}_{z}(b^{+},\omega)}{-\varepsilon_{0}c^{2}\frac{\partial\tilde{A}_{z}}{\partial r}\big|_{r=b^{+}}}=\frac{-i\omega Z_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}J_{0}(k_{r}b^{+})k_{r}\left(1-\beta^{2}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\widetilde{\phi}(k_{r},\omega)}{c\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}J_{0}(k_{r}b^{+})k_{r}\left(\beta^{2}-\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\widetilde{\phi}(k_{r},\omega)\right)}

(109)

At this point, we recall that the relation between the electric field and the potential in the reciprocal domain is:

\widetilde{E}_{z}(k_{r},\omega)=\frac{i\omega}{\beta c}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\widetilde{\phi}(k_{r},\omega)

(110)

For conductors good enough that
\sigma>>\varepsilon_{0}\omega/\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}
, the latter becomes approximately:

\widetilde{E}_{z}(k_{r},\omega)\simeq-\frac{\beta\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}c}\widetilde{\phi}(k_{r},\omega)

(111)

Therefore, the surface impedance can be now recast into:

Z_{s}\simeq\frac{i\omega Z_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}J_{0}(k_{r}b^{+})k_{r}\left(-\frac{\beta\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}c}\right)\widetilde{\phi}(k_{r},\omega)}{c\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}J_{0}(k_{r}b^{+})k_{r}\left(-\frac{\beta\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}c}\right)\widetilde{\phi}(k_{r},\omega)\right)}=\frac{i\omega Z_{0}}{c}\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(b^{+})}{\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}}

(112)

Finally, using Eqs.
108
and
112
, the surface impedance
Z_{s}
for
p=0
can be expressed as:

Z_{s}\simeq-\frac{i\pi Z_{0}\omega}{c\int_{0}^{\infty}\log{\left[1+\frac{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{k_{r}^{2}}\right]}dk_{r}}

(113)

C.2
Resolution of the wave equation for p=1 and surface impedance

In the region occupied by the metallic pipe, i.e. for
r>b
, the wave equation for the electric potential is written as:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}}{\partial r}-\left(\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{\phi}+i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c(\sigma\star\tilde{\phi})_{p=1}\right)=0

(114)

The solution of Eq.
114
can be expressed through the following inverse Hankel transform:

\tilde{\phi}=\frac{q_{0}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}dk_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}

(115)

where:

\bar{\eta}=\frac{\omega}{\beta c}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+i\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma(\omega,k_{r})}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}}

(116)

Such a result derives from the similarity with the solution in the vacuum region enclosed by the pipe.
Indeed, it is possible to notice that if the conductivity does not depend on
k_{r}
, the above solution becomes:

\tilde{\phi}\simeq\frac{q_{0}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}dk_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}=\frac{q_{0}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}r\right)

(117)

which is identical to Eq.
37
, as it should be. The dependence upon a
K_{0}
function is the one that must be expected for the field generated by a charged particle travelling on-axis in cylindrical symmetry when the anomalous response is negligible.
Otherwise, in the anomalous case, where the response of the medium in the radial plane is non-local, the electric potential must be calculated through the non-trivial integral in Eq.
115
. For the electric field, by applying the Lorentz gauge in the
(k_{r},\omega)
domain, it is straightforwardly found that:

\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)=\frac{i\omega}{\beta c}\frac{q_{0}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}C^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}

(118)

The magnetic field is also calculated through the electromagnetic potential:

\tilde{H}_{\varphi}=-\frac{q_{0}\beta c}{2\pi}C^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}

(119)

Both the electric and magnetic fields have been expressed through inverse Hankel transforms. The surface impedance can be calculated, for
p=1
, as:

Z_{s,1}=\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(b,\omega)}{\tilde{H}_{\varphi}(b,\omega)}=\frac{i\omega Z_{0}}{c}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\left(\beta^{2}-\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(k_{r},\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{J_{1}\left(k_{r}b\right)k^{2}_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}}

(120)

C.2.1
Limit of normal conductivity for fields and surface impedance

In the normal region, the metallic pipe response is described by the electric conductivity
\sigma(k_{r},\omega)\simeq\sigma(\omega)
, therefore Eqs.
118
and
119
become:

\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)\simeq\frac{i\omega}{\beta c}\frac{q_{0}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}C^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}=\frac{i\omega}{\beta c}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{q_{0}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}r\right)

(121)

\tilde{H}_{\varphi}\simeq-\frac{q\beta c}{2\pi}C^{\sigma}_{0}(\omega)\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}=\frac{q_{0}\beta c\bar{\eta}}{2\pi}C_{0}^{\sigma}(\omega)\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)K_{1}\left(\bar{\eta}r\right)

(122)

Moreover, for the surface impedance of Eq.
120
in the limit
\sigma(k_{r},\omega)\rightarrow\sigma(\omega)
, we obtain:

Z_{s}\simeq\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)}{\left(\beta^{2}-\frac{i\beta^{2}\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)}\frac{i\omega Z_{0}}{c}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\frac{J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)k_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}\frac{J_{1}\left(k_{r}r\right)k^{2}_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\bar{\eta}^{2}}}=Z_{0}\frac{\sqrt{-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}-i\frac{\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}}}{\left(1-\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega}\right)}\frac{K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)}{K_{1}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)}

(123)

which is identical to Eq.
42
, as to be expected.

C.2.2
Resolution of the wave equation for p=1 (surface currents’ approach) and surface impedance

Here, we follow the approach introduced in Ref.
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
for the case of specular reflection of charge carriers on the metallic surface of a cylindrical beam pipe of radius
b
. Indeed, for the probability of reflection
p=1
, the wave equation for the electric field can be written as:

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\tilde{E}_{z}-i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c\sigma\star\tilde{E}_{z}-2\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}\delta(r=b)=0

(124)

to consider the space
r<b
filled with another piece of the same
metal. The two pieces of metal are mirror images of one of the other. The electric field
is damped in both
r>b
and
r<b
directions and the boundary condition on the electric
field is:

\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}=-\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{-}}=0

(125)

Indeed, integrating Eq.
124
upon
r
, it is possible to obtain:

\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}-\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{-}}-2\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}=0

(126)

which is equivalent to Eq.
125
, i.e. the boundary condition is verified.
A discontinuity of the magnetic field (the derivative of the electric field) tangent to the surface corresponds to the presence of surface electric currents.
By applying the Hankel transform of zeroth order to Eq.
124
, we obtain:

-k_{r}^{2}\widetilde{E}_{z}-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\widetilde{E}_{z}-i\mu_{0}k_{z}\beta c\sigma(k_{r},k)\widetilde{E}_{z}-2b\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}J_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)=0

(127)

where the Hankel transform of zeroth order of the electric field is defined as:

\widetilde{E}_{z}(k_{r},\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}drrJ_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)

(128)

We also recall that
\omega=k_{z}\beta c
. By recasting Eq.
127
, it is possible to write:

\widetilde{E}_{z}(k_{r},\omega)=\frac{-2b\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}J_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)}{k_{r}^{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},k)}

(129)

Finally, the inverse Hankel transform of the last equation provides the electric field inside the beam pipe (region II):

\tilde{E}_{z}(r,\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{r}k_{r}J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)\widetilde{E}_{z}(k_{r},\omega)=-2b\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\Big|_{r=b^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{r}J_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)J_{0}\left(k_{r}r\right)dk_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},k)}

(130)

In this way, while using the same approximation as in Eq.
112
, valid for good conductors, the surface impedance can be easily calculated as:

Z_{s}\simeq\frac{i\omega}{c}Z_{0}\frac{\tilde{E}_{z}(b,\omega)}{\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{z}}{\partial r}\big|_{r=b^{+}}}=-\frac{2ib\omega}{c}Z_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{r}J^{2}_{0}\left(k_{r}b\right)dk_{r}}{k_{r}^{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}c^{2}}+i\mu_{0}\omega\sigma(k_{r},k)}

(131)

In the limit
\sigma(k_{r},\omega)\rightarrow\sigma(\omega)
, Eq.
131
becomes:

Z_{s}\simeq-\frac{2i\omega bZ_{0}}{c}K_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)I_{0}\left(\bar{\eta}b\right)

(132)

Eq.
132
does not immediately coincide with Eq.
123
, calculated for the case
p=1
. This discrepancy shows that the approaches of Appendices
C.2
and
C.2.2
are not exactly equivalent. However, for large conductivities, both Eqs.
123
and
132
tend to the classical surface impedance for (quasi-)planar surfaces:

Z_{s}\simeq\frac{\omega Z_{0}}{c\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}c^{2}}-i\frac{\mu_{0}\omega\sigma_{0}}{1+i\omega\tau}}}

(133)

The above result shows that, for cylindrical symmetry, the Reuter and Sondheimer approach of Ref.
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
can be considered valid only for good conductors. Indeed, Eq.
123
is more rigorous than Eq.
132
. The physical reason which lies behind the above is the fact that the boundary condition in Eq.
125
, introduced in Ref.
Reuter and Sondheimer 1948
for the planar geometry (where it is exactly valid), is only approximately valid in cylindrical geometry. Such a boundary condition can be a valid approximation in cylindrical geometry only for a strongly attenuated field inside the material, i.e. high-conductivity materials. Due to the particular topology of the hollow beam pipe, a field that does not decay rapidly near the wall of the pipe can interfere with the field located in a diametrically opposite position, making it impossible for Eq.
125
to be exactly satisfied.
