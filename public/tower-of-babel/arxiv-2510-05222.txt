Scalable accuracy gains from postselection in quantum error correcting codes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Hongkun Chen, Daohong Xu, Grace M. Sommers, David A. Huse, Jeff D. Thompson, Sarang Gopalakrishnan

Abstract
Decoding stabilizer codes such as the surface and toric codes involves evaluating free-energy differences in a disordered statistical mechanics model, in which the randomness comes from the observed pattern of error syndromes. We study the statistical distribution of logical failure rates across observed syndromes in the toric code, and show that, within the coding phase, logical failures are predominantly caused by exponentially unlikely syndromes. Therefore, postselecting on not seeing these exponentially unlikely syndrome patterns offers a scalable accuracy gain. In general, the logical error rate can be suppressed from p f p_{f} to p f b p_{f}^{b} , where b ≥ 2 b\geq 2 in general; in the specific case of the toric code with perfect syndrome measurements, we find numerically that b = 3.1 ​ ( 1 ) b=3.1(1) . Our arguments apply to general topological stabilizer codes, and can be extended to more general settings as long as the decoding failure probability obeys a large deviation principle.



Large-scale quantum computation needs to be fault-tolerant. Fault tolerance can be achieved by encoding quantum information in a quantum error correcting code with a nonzero threshold and performing fault-tolerant operations on the encoded data, under which the spreading of errors is controlled
Gottesman 2009
.
A key question for near-term quantum computation—where the number of good qubits is a limiting resource—is how to efficiently protect and manipulate quantum information on a fixed qubit budget. A strategy that is often used in practice is
postselection
: because it is easier to detect errors than to correct them, one can improve the performance of an algorithm by repeating it and discarding (or aborting) trials in which errors are detected
Google Quantum AI 2021
;
Ryan-Anderson et al. 2021
;
Sivak et al. 2023
;
Bluvstein et al. 2024
. In near-term applications, postselection is a valuable error-mitigation technique
Cai et al. 2023
;
Aharonov et al. 2025
. However, if the abort rate per measurement is finite, postselection is not scalable: only an exponentially small fraction of trials are kept. Several strategies have been developed to leverage postselection in scalable circuits, including syndrome extraction with verified ancillas
Shor 1996
;
Knill 2004
, constructing cluster states from fixed-size resource states using fusion operations
Bartolucci et al. 2023
and magic state distillation
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
;
Bravyi and Haah 2012
. In these approaches, the exponential overhead of postselection is mitigated by applying postselection to fixed-size chunks of the circuit.

In the present work, we instead consider directly postselecting on a circuit whose size scales with the code distance
d
.
This approach was introduced in Ref.
Smith et al. 2024
, which pointed out that postselection yields scalable accuracy gains for the toric code (see also Refs.
Bombín et al. 2024
;
English et al. 2024
;
Gidney et al. 2025
).
The arguments of Ref.
Smith et al. 2024
pertain to the surface code in the limit of physical error rate
p\to 0
. Here, we consider the more general setting of nonzero
p
, and present a statistical mechanics perspective that generalizes naturally beyond the surface code, and provides a physically intuitive argument for why postselection is useful.

Recall that the decoding problem for a general stabilizer code maps to a disordered statistical mechanics problem, in which the randomness comes from the observed error syndrome, i.e., the pattern of violated error checks
Dennis et al. 2002
;
Chubb and Flammia 2021
;
Bombin et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2003
;
Katzgraber et al. 2009
;
Wu et al. 2025
. In these mappings, the likelihood ratio between logical sectors is associated with a free-energy difference
\Delta F
, which in the toric code is between periodic and antiperiodic boundary conditions. We argue on general grounds that, for error rates below the error correction threshold, at small
|\Delta F|
the probability distribution
P(\Delta F)
obeys a large deviation form
Varadhan 2010
,
P(\Delta F=sd)\sim\exp(-I(s) d)

(e.g.,
d
is the linear size of the system in the examples of the toric or surface codes). Generic convexity properties of this distribution imply that the probability of incorrectly decoding a typical syndrome is exponentially lower (in
d
) than that of encountering a rare dangerous syndrome with
|\Delta F|
near zero that is likely to be decoded incorrectly.
This exponential separation implies that even weak postselection (where one discards only those exponentially few trials with small
|\Delta F|
) can decrease the logical failure rate by a factor that scales exponentially in
d
:
thus one can enhance the effective code distance by a finite
O(1)
factor. For nonzero but subthreshold error rates, we argue on convexity grounds that this factor is always at least
2
, but is generically larger: our numerical results on the toric code suggest a distance enhancement of a factor of
3.1(1)
. A striking consequence of our results is
that for certain tasks, strategies with postselection can scalably outperform those without. We check these arguments by numerically evaluating
P(\Delta F)
for the toric code under independent
X
and
Z
errors and perfect measurements, using an optimal decoder, as well as for a more realistic circuit-level noise model under a minimum-weight perfect matching decoder
Gidney et al. 2025
.

Setup.
—We begin by briefly reviewing the optimal decoding problem for the toric (or surface) code, and its mapping to a statistical mechanics model. For simplicity, we consider an error model of independent bit-flip (
X
) and phase-flip (
Z
) errors: since these are CSS codes, if the two types of errors occur independently, they can also be decoded independently, so without loss of generality we focus on bit-flip errors. In the standard representation of the toric code, qubits are placed on the links of a square lattice. Bit-flip errors are detected by checks (syndromes) that are products of
Z
operators around plaquettes of the lattice. Errors form strings of contiguous flipped qubits, and the checks detect the endpoints of these strings.
Error correction consists of matching these endpoints (by guessing a pattern of error strings consistent with them, and applying bit-flips to join them along the guessed strings); it fails only if the true error string and the correction string combine to form a noncontractible loop around the torus. Thus, given a reference error string
E
that is consistent with the observed syndrome, all other errors
E^{\prime}
consistent with the same syndrome can be classified into homology classes, depending on whether
E
and
E^{\prime}
form a noncontractible loop around the torus. The objective of error correction is to use the syndrome data to guess the homology class of
E
correctly.

Under the assumption that syndrome measurements are perfectly reliable, the decoding problem for the toric (or surface) code has a well-known mapping to a statistical mechanics model, the random-bond Ising model (RBIM)
Dennis et al. 2002
.
Under the statistical mechanics mapping, checks become Ising spins
s_{i}
, while physical qubits, labeled as
l_{ij}
, become Ising couplings
J_{ij}
between spins. For error chain
E
, the Hamiltonian of the corresponding statistical mechanics model is

H_{E}=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}J_{ij}s_{i}s_{j},~~~~\text{where}~J_{ij}=\begin{cases}+1~~~~\text{if $l_{ij}\notin E$}\\
-1~~~~\text{if $l_{ij}\in E$}\end{cases}.

(1)

The Nishimori condition,

P(J_{ij}=+1)=e^{2\beta}P(J_{ij}=-1)~,

(2)

defines an inverse temperature
\beta
that is set by the error probabilities,
P(J_{ij})
.
The total probability of errors within the same homology class as
E
is proportional to the partition function
{\cal{Z}}(\beta,E)
.

When decoding a single logical
Z
or
X
for the toric code, the two homology classes correspond to periodic vs. antiperiodic boundary conditions: they differ by a relative domain wall. When the error density is below threshold, the RBIM is in its ferromagnetic phase, and the domain wall typically has a free energy cost that scales with the length of the system. The free energy of a domain wall in a random two-dimensional magnet can be mapped onto that of a directed polymer in a random environment
Huse and Henley 1985
.

Given Ising coupling strengths
J_{ij}
arising from a given error chain
E
, the free-energy difference
\Delta F
between the two homology classes relative to
E
is defined as

\beta\Delta F=\log(\cal{Z}_{\text{PBC}}/\cal{Z}_{\text{APBC}})~,

(3)

where
\cal{Z}_{\text{PBC}}
and
\cal{Z}_{\text{APBC}}
are the partition functions under periodic and antiperiodic boundary conditions, respectively. Since
\Delta F
depends on the error configuration, it is a random variable which takes the same value on all error configurations that produce the same syndrome and are in the same homology class. This immediately implies the Nishimori symmetry constraint on the distribution
P(\Delta F)
:

P(\Delta F)=P(-\Delta F)e^{\beta\Delta F}~.

(4)

Since every RBIM on the Nishimori line is associated with optimal decoding of a toric code with some error model
sup
, the relation in Eq. (
4
) always holds, regardless of whether
P(J_{ij})
is a continuous distribution or a discrete one.
We choose to rescale the
J_{ij}
such that
\beta=1
, and therefore use
\beta\Delta F
and
\Delta F
interchangeably in the remainder of this Letter. The partition function is computed using the TEBD method implemented with the ITensors.jl package
Fishman et al. 2022a
;
Fishman et al. 2022b
; details of the tensor network construction are provided in the Supplemental Material
sup
.

To determine the logical failure probability, we proceed as follows: We first sample an error chain
E
from the distribution over errors. This is the “ground-truth” error and generates a particular syndrome (which is experimentally accessible). The optimal decoder then computes the free energies for the statistical mechanics model with errors in both homological classes consistent with the observed syndrome, and infers that the error chain belonged to the class with the lower free energy. (Decoders lack access to the true error chain, and therefore do not know the sign of
\Delta F
; however, they can estimate their confidence in the error correction operation based on
|\Delta F|
.) Decoding fails when the inferred homology class does not match that of
E
,
i.e., when
\Delta F<0
. Therefore, the logical failure probability is

p_{f}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}P(\Delta F)\differential\Delta F=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{P(\Delta F)}{1+e^{|\Delta F|}}\differential\Delta F~.

(5)

Large deviation analysis
.—We now discuss the asymptotics of
P(\Delta F)
. Since this is the free-energy cost of a domain wall in a random ferromagnet, it can be parameterized as
\Delta F=sd
, where
s
is an intensive random variable, the line tension of the domain wall. This free-energy cost obeys a large deviation principle (LDP): For
|s|
less than its typical value,
P(\Delta F=sd)\sim\exp(-I(s) d)
, where
I(s)\equiv\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log P(sd)}{d}
is called a “rate function”
Varadhan 2010
;
Carmona and Hu 2004
;
Carmona 2010
;
Janjigian 2015
. Physically, this quantity obeys a LDP because, for a domain wall to have an atypically low value of the absolute value of its free energy per unit length, it must pass through a string of regions with atypical disorder configurations. Thus, one can regard
\Delta F
as a sum of weakly correlated random variables, which generically obey a LDP.

The quantity
P(\Delta F)
is the probability of the error configurations which produce that domain wall free energy. Numerical results from the RBIM (toric code) are shown in Fig.
1
. The scaling collapse in Fig.
1
(b) illustrates that the LDP holds for small
|\Delta F|
. At the peak of the distribution,
I(\overline{s})=0
. The constraint Eq. (
4
) implies a symmetry relation for
I(s)
:

I(s)+s=I(-s)~.

(6)

We assume two standard properties of the rate function
I(s)
, which are also apparent in the numerics: that it is monotonically decreasing for
s\leq\overline{s}
and convex.

By the Laplace principle,

p_{f}(d)\sim\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-I(s)d}\,ds\sim e^{-I(0)d}~,

(7)

showing that logical failures are dominated by atypical syndromes in the vicinity of
\Delta F=0
. Typical syndromes, corresponding to
|\Delta F|=\overline{s}d
, give an exponentially smaller contribution
\sim e^{-I(-\overline{s})d}=e^{-\overline{s}d}
to
p_{f}
.

The basic idea behind scalable postselection (which we will elaborate on below) is that by suppressing syndromes with
|\Delta F|\leq sd
for
0<s<\overline{s}
, we can suppress the logical failure probability from
\sim e^{-I(0)d}
to
\sim e^{-I(-s)d}
—which is exponentially smaller—while only aborting an exponentially small fraction of trials.
Thus, postselecting on not seeing atypically dangerous syndromes gives a maximal gain in the effective code distance that is upper-bounded by a factor of
b\equiv I(-\overline{s})/I(0)
. It is straightforward to show that the convexity of
I(s)
guarantees that
b\geq 2
for any code where the failure probabilities follow an LDP, regardless of whether Eq. (
6
) holds.

Numerically, it is usually computationally hard to sample rare events near
\Delta F=-\langle\Delta F\rangle
sufficiently; however, the symmetry of
I(s)
enables us to use
\overline{s}
to evaluate
I(\overline{s})
. For the RBIM, we numerically estimate
\overline{s}
from the slope of
\langle\Delta F\rangle
versus
d
, and
I(0)
from the slope of
\log p_{f}(d)
versus
d
, as shown in Fig.
1
(c), and find
b=3.1(1)
.
This ratio
b\approx 3
appears rather robust for the toric code, even if one changes the error model (e.g., by introducing some density of heralded errors
sup
). Interestingly,
b=3
is the ratio that one would get by extrapolating the Tracy-Widom distribution, which governs
typical
fluctuations of the domain wall free energy
Tracy and Widom 2009
(
s-\overline{s}\sim d^{-2/3}
), and extrapolating it to the large deviation regime.

Gains from code-splitting.
—
Next, we discuss a surprising implication of the convexity relation
b\geq 2
: for certain tasks, the optimal strategy might involve postselection even for a fixed spacetime budget. To illustrate this we consider protecting a known, and thus clonable, logical state
\ket{\psi}
against noise. Using
N
qubits, we can do this by encoding
\ket{\psi}
in a
\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{N}
toric code, or by preparing
r
copies of
\ket{\psi}
and encoding each in a toric code with dimensions
\sqrt{N/r}\times\sqrt{N/r}
. When
r>1
, we estimate
|\Delta F|
for each copy, keep the copy with the largest
|\Delta F|
, and discard the rest. Since
r=1
has the largest code distance, one might expect that splitting always increases the logical failure probability. However, this is not the case. Let us first consider
r=2
, for which the logical failure probability can be computed in closed form:

p_{f}^{(2)}\left(d\right)\sim\exp\left\{-\min_{s\geq 0}[I(s)+I(-s)]\,d\right\}=e^{-2I(0)d}.

(8)

Indeed, Eq. (
8
) also implies that asymptotically, two distance-
d
codes with the postselection strategy described above have the same asymptotic distance as a single distance-
2d
code. For a code, such as the toric code, that has the code distance
d
growing slower than linearly in the number of qubits, this provides the same logical failure rate while using fewer qubits. We emphasize that this result is not specific to the toric code but is a generic consequence of the LDP and convexity.

More generally, the probability of a logical failure when code-splitting to
r
codes, each with distance
d
is given by
sup

p_{f}^{(r)}(d)\simeq\exp\{-\min_{s\geq 0}[rI(s)+s]\,d\}~.

(9)

The window of
r

r=3
appears to be optimal

Postselection
.—The splitting protocol discussed above illustrates that postselection can yield gains even for a fixed spacetime budget.
In practice, however, splitting one’s qubits into subcodes is manifestly a suboptimal use of resources: a better strategy is to abort if one finds a syndrome with an unacceptably small value of
|\Delta F|
. We are interested in the setting where postselection is exponentially rare in
d
, so we consider aborting if we see syndromes with
|\Delta F|\leq s^{*}d
, where
s^{*}<\overline{s}
. For a single round of postselection, the abort rate is
\exp(-I(s^*) d)
, and the logical error rate conditional on not aborting is
\exp(-I(-s^*) d)
. For a given
d
and an operation consisting of
\mathcal{N}
logical operations, under what conditions does postselection help?

We can estimate this as follows. For concreteness, the estimate below will focus on the toric code with perfect syndrome measurements, but similar estimates can be made in other settings. Suppose we require that postselection be scalable in the sense that the probability of not aborting remains
O(1)
as
d\to\infty
. For circuits with
\mathcal{N}
logical operations, we thus require that
\mathcal{N}\exp(-I(s^*) d)\lesssim 1
, assuming that one round of error correction happens between any two operations.
This fixes
I(s^{*})\simeq d^{-1}\log\mathcal{N}
, subject to the constraint that
s^{*}\geq 0
. This equation has nontrivial solutions when
d^{-1}\log\mathcal{N}
is sufficiently small; the postselected logical failure rate in these cases scales as
\exp(-I(-s^*) d)
. In the limit of few logical operations (
\mathcal{N}=O(1)
),
s^{*}=\overline{s}
, since typical syndromes occur with high probability. Indeed, this holds for any circuit of depth polynomial in
d
, since
\log\mathcal{N}/d\sim\log d/d
. However, for circuits of depth scaling exponentially in
d
, the amount of postselection that can be tolerated while maintaining an
O(1)
abort rate decreases, until for deep enough circuits
s^{*}=0
and the gain from postselection is entirely lost. In addition, when the system is not in the decodable phase,
\overline{s}=0
. Thus, postselection with finite acceptance probability does not alter the error threshold, but effectively increases the code distance from
d
to
I(-s^{*})d/I(0)
, as conjectured in Ref.
English et al. 2024
.

The postselection strategy outlined above yields no advantage in the normal setting of fault-tolerant computation. In this setting, the relevant figure of merit is the minimum code distance required to complete an algorithm with
\mathcal{N}
logical operations. Without postselection, the condition for reliable computation with distance-
d
codes is

\mathcal{N}e^{-I(0)d}\lesssim 1.

(10)

With postselection on
|\Delta F|\geq s^{*}d
, the logical error rate is further suppressed, seemingly reducing the distance overhead. Yet scalability requires a finite acceptance probability, which imposes

\mathcal{N}p_{\text{abort}}\simeq\mathcal{N}e^{-I(s^{*})d}\lesssim 1.

(11)

Since
I(s^{*})<I(0)
for all
s^{*}>0
, the abort probability becomes the limiting factor: requiring an
O(1)
abort rate imposes a more stringent constraint on
d
than requiring an
O(1)
logical failure probability. Thus, postselecting on an entire circuit cannot be used to reduce overhead, but rather provides a scalable gain in
accuracy
, in settings where the key objective is to minimize the probability of a logical failure. Whether these techniques can be applied to smaller parts of circuits to achieve an end-to-end overhead reduction remains a task for future work.

Discussion.
—Above, we showed that postselection with parametrically low abort rates can almost triple the effective distance of the surface code. This gain in the effective distance is asymptotic, and follows from a large deviation principle (LDP) that we expect to hold much more generally. We focused above on the case with perfect syndrome measurements, but the extension to imperfect measurements is direct: this case maps to a different statistical mechanics model in spacetime
Dennis et al. 2002
, and the logical operator is a directed polymer in a three-dimensional disordered medium. The free energy distribution of this polymer again takes a large deviation form. For this case, the optimal decoder is numerically intractable; however, the LDP also holds for suboptimal decoders, and we verify it numerically for the minimum-weight perfect matching decoder in Fig.
3
(see also
sup
).

. The rescaled distribution of the complementary gap
G

sup
), exhibits clear data collapse across code distances, consistent with large deviation scaling within the range
|s|<\overline{s}
.
The behavior is analogous to that of the two-dimensional RBIM, demonstrating that the large deviation form persists under realistic circuit-level noise.

More generally, in topological codes where the logical operator lives on a lower-dimensional surface of the system, the LDP is expected to apply for the same reason as in the surface code.
We also present numerical evidence
sup
for this scaling in concatenated codes
Sommers et al. 2025
. An interesting question for future work is whether an analogous LDP continues to hold in families of codes such as generic hypergraph product codes
Tillich and Zémor 2013
and good quantum LDPC codes
Panteleev and Kalachev 2022
.
Whenever the LDP holds, an immediate consequence is that logical failures are dominated by rare events with
\Delta F\approx 0
. As these events get exponentially rare in the code distance, and lead to syndromes that can be identified as dangerous, there is scope for scalable postselection strategies that abort if they see dangerous syndromes.

For large system sizes, exact enumeration of all spin configurations becomes computationally intractable due to the exponential growth of the Hilbert space. To overcome this barrier, we employ the time-evolving block decimation (TEBD) algorithm, which efficiently approximates the thermal partition function as a tensor-network contraction. II
The efficiency of postselection

Postselecting on the maximal
|\Delta F_{i}|
among
r
independent copies.

As introduced in the main text, one strategy is to prepare
r
independent copies of the toric code, each with code distance
d
, and postselect on the block with the largest free-energy difference magnitude
|\Delta F_{i}|
. We claim that the logical failure probability under such postselection is

p_{f}^{{(r)}}(d)\sim\exp\{-\min_{s\geq 0}[rI(s)+s]d\}~.

(S8)

We derive this form via maximum order statistics, which provides a baseline for analyzing postselection efficiency at fixed qubit overhead. For a distance-
d
code, the left-tail PDF of the free-energy difference
\Delta F
obeys

P_{d}(\Delta F)\sim\exp(-I(s)d),\qquad I(\overline{s})=0,\quad I(s)+s=I(-s),

(S9)

for
|s|\leq\overline{s}
. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
|\Delta F|
is

\mathcal{F}_{d}(M)\equiv\int_{-M}^{M}P(\Delta F)\differential\Delta F~,\qquad M\geq 0~.

(S10)

From order statistics, the PDF of the maximum
|\Delta F_{i}|
among
r
copies is

P_{d}(|\Delta F_{i}|_{\max}=M)=r\,\mathcal{F}_{d}(M)^{r-1}\,[P_{d}(\Delta F=M)+P_{d}(\Delta F=-M)]~.

(S11)

Because of the symmetry
P(\Delta F)=P(-\Delta F)e^{\beta\Delta F}
, the logical failure probability can be written as

p_{f}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}P(\Delta F)\differential\Delta F=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{P(\Delta F)}{1+e^{|\Delta F|}}\differential\Delta F.

(S12)

Therefore, after postselection, the logical failure probability is

p_{f}^{(r)}(d)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{P_{d}(|\Delta F_{i}|_{\max}=M)}{1+e^{M}}\differential M~.

(S13)

Since
P(\Delta F)
obeys large deviation scaling, for
M\leq\overline{s}d
, the CDF behaves as

\mathcal{F}_{d}(M)=\int_{|\Delta F|\leq M}P(\Delta F)\differential\Delta F\sim\exp\!\left[-I\!\left(\tfrac{M}{d}\right)d\right],

(S14)

and Eq. (
S11
) gives

P_{d}(|\Delta F_{i}|_{\max}=M)\sim\mathcal{F}_{d}(M)^{r-1}P_{d}(\Delta F=M)\sim\exp\!\left[-rI\!\left(\tfrac{M}{d}\right)d\right].

(S15)

Substituting into Eq. (
S13
) yields

p_{f}^{(r)}(d)\sim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\exp\!\left(-rI\!\left(\tfrac{M}{d}\right)d-M\right)\differential M\sim\exp\{-\min_{s\geq 0}[rI(s)+s]d\}~,

(S16)

as stated in the main text.

Postselection efficiency for splitting into four subcodes.

By convexity of
I(s)
, we showed in the main text that two distance-
d
codes scale equivalently to a single distance-
2d
code. Consequently, splitting a distance-
d
toric code into two distance-
(d/\sqrt{2})
codes suppresses logical errors as

p_{f}^{(2)}(d/\sqrt{2})\simeq e^{-\sqrt{2}I(0)d}\ll e^{-I(0)d}\simeq p_{f}(d).

(S17)

Because
\sqrt{2}
is irrational, it is not feasible to directly simulate both distance-
d
and distance-
(d/\sqrt{2})
codes. Instead, we compared
p_{f}(2n)
,
p^{(2)}_{f}(n)
, and
p^{(4)}_{f}(n)
, and observed numerically that
p^{(4)}_{f}(n)
decays exponentially faster in
n
. We now provide the mathematical explanation.

Selecting the maximal
|\Delta F_{i}|
among four distance-
n
patches gives

p_{f}^{(4)}(n)\simeq\exp\{-\min_{s\geq 0}[4I(s)+s]d\}.

(S18)

Since
I(s)+s=I(-s)
implies
I^{\prime}(0)=-1/2
, convexity guarantees that
4I(s)+s
has a unique global minimum at some
s_{m}>0
. As
s=0
is the minimum for
2I(s)+s
, one finds

\min_{s\geq 0}[4I(s)+s]=4I(s_{m})+s_{m}\geq 2I(s_{m})+s_{m}>\min_{s\geq 0}[2I(s)+s]=2I(0).

(S19)

Thus, splitting into four always provides a positive gain, demonstrating the advantage of postselection under a fixed qubit budget. This improvement, however, does not extend to arbitrary
r
. Since
\min_{s\geq 0}[rI(s)+s]\leq\overline{s}
, when
r>b^{2}
one obtains

p^{(r)}_{f}(\frac{d}{\sqrt{r}})\simeq\exp\!\left\{-\tfrac{\min_{s\geq 0}[rI(s)+s]}{\sqrt{r}}\,d\right\}\geq e^{-\overline{s}d/\sqrt{r}}>e^{-I(0)d}\simeq p_{f}(d).

(S20)

Hence, the efficiency gain of code-splitting eventually diminishes as
r
increases.

III
Postselection for general stabilizer codes

Marginal decoding.
In the main text we have argued that for a toric code with independent
X
and
Z
errors and an optimal decoder one can define the free-energy differences
\Delta F
, and most logical errors occur due to
\Delta F
near zero.
However, such arguments might be flawed when (i)
X
and
Z
errors are correlated, for instance, depolarizing noise; (ii) for more general qubit stabilizer codes. Here we try to generalize our previous discussion into general error models and more general codes.

Consider a general
[[n,k,d]]
qubit stabilizer code with logical operators
\{L_{i}^{X},L_{i}^{Z}\}
on the
i^{\text{th}}
logical qubit (
1\leq i\leq k
). Under maximum-likelihood decoding (MLD), one constructs a canonical error chain
\tilde{E}
consistent with the measured syndrome and evaluates
P([\tilde{E}\mathcal{P}_{l}])
(where
[\cdot]
denotes an error class modulo stabilizers) for all
4^{k}
logical Pauli operators
\mathcal{P}_{l}
generated by
\{L_{i}^{X},L_{i}^{Z}\}
. Decoding then selects
\arg\max P([\tilde{E}\mathcal{P}_{l}])
.

Let
\Omega
denote the probability space of all physical errors. For any error
E\in\Omega
, we may define

\Delta F_{l}(E)\equiv\log\frac{P([E])}{P([E\mathcal{P}_{l}])}~.

(S21)

For large
k
, evaluating all
(4^{k}-1)
quantities is infeasible. An efficient alternative is marginal decoding
Nelson et al. 2025
, which assigns each logical qubit to the most likely single-qubit Pauli class by marginalizing over errors on the other
(k-1)
qubits. This procedure effectively projects multi-qubit errors onto single-qubit marginals. More precisely, the marginal decoder defines the probability of a single-qubit logical error on the
i^{\text{th}}
qubit by summing over all logical errors on the other
(k-1)
qubits:

P_{m}([EL_{i}^{\alpha}])=\sum_{l=1}^{4^{k-1}}P([EL_{i}^{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{l}^{(-i)}]),

(S22)

where
L_{i}^{\alpha}\in\{L_{i}^{I},L_{i}^{X},L_{i}^{Y},L_{i}^{Z}\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{l}^{(-i)}
are generated by
\{L_{j}^{X},L_{j}^{Z}\mid j\neq i\}
.

In the decoding process, the decoder determines some canonical error chain
\tilde{E}
and then seeks the operator
L_{i}^{\alpha_{\max}}
maximizing
P_{m}([\tilde{E}L_{i}^{\alpha}])
, yielding the final correction as
E=\tilde{E}\prod_{i}L_{i}^{\alpha_{\max}}
.

For each nontrivial
L_{i}^{\alpha}
(
\alpha=X,Y,Z
), given the actual error
E\in\Omega
, we define a free-energy difference

\Delta F_{i,\alpha}\equiv\log\frac{P_{m}([E])}{P_{m}([EL_{i}^{\alpha}])}.

(S23)

There are
3k
such quantities (corresponding to
L_{i}^{X},L_{i}^{Y},L_{i}^{Z}
). According to the marginal probability definition in Eq. (
S22
), all error chains
E^{\prime}\in[EP_{l}^{(-i)}]
share the same marginal probability, i.e.,
P_{m}([E^{\prime}L_{i}^{\alpha}])=P_{m}([EL_{i}^{\alpha}])
. Moreover, the error chains
E\in[EP_{l}^{(-i)}]
yield
\Delta F_{i,\alpha}
values opposite to those of the chains
E\in[EL_{i}^{\alpha}P_{l}^{(-i)}]
. By construction,
\Delta F_{i,\alpha}
therefore satisfies the same constraint as in the
k=1
case.

P(\Delta F_{i,\alpha})=P(-\Delta F_{i,\alpha})e^{\Delta F_{i,\alpha}},

(S24)

and are expected to satisfy a large deviation principle (LDP) of the form

P(\Delta F_{i,\alpha}=sd)\simeq e^{-dI_{i,\alpha}(s)},

(S25)

with
I_{i,\alpha}(s)
convex, monotonically decreasing for
s\leq\overline{s}_{i,\alpha}
, obeying
I_{i,\alpha}(s)+s=I_{i,\alpha}(-s)
, and vanishing as
I_{i,\alpha}(\overline{s}_{i,\alpha})=0
. These properties imply

\overline{s}_{i,\alpha}=I_{i,\alpha}(\overline{s}_{i,\alpha})+I_{i,\alpha}(-\overline{s}_{i,\alpha})\geq 2I_{i,\alpha}(0).

(S26)

Notably, it has been revealed that the marginal decoding is also optimal when the physical error rate is below the threshold and the code distance
d
is sufficiently large
Farrelly et al. 2022
, providing a generally feasible approach to decode a stabilizer code with multiple logical qubits.

Efficiency of postselection.
In either MLD or marginal decoding, the problem reduces to evaluating
\mathcal{M}
free-energy differences
\Delta F_{i}
, with
\mathcal{M}=3k
for marginal decoding and
\mathcal{M}=4^{k}-1
for MLD. Decoding succeeds iff all
\Delta F_{i}\geq 0
. For scalability,
\mathcal{M}
must grow subexponentially with
d
, i.e.

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathcal{M}e^{-cd}=0,\qquad\forall c>0.

(S27)

Let
\Omega
be the space of physical errors, and
\mathcal{W}_{i}=\{E\in\Omega|\Delta F_{i}(E)<0\}
. The set of logical failures is
\mathcal{W}=\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{W}_{i}
, with probability bounded as

\max_{i}P(\mathcal{W}_{i})\ \leq\ P(\mathcal{W})\ \leq\ \mathcal{M}\,\max_{i}P(\mathcal{W}_{i}).

(S28)

thus the dominant contribution is controlled by the largest
P(\mathcal{W}_{i})
.

A natural postselection strategy is to discard all errors with
|\Delta F_{i}|<M_{i}
, choosing thresholds
M_{i}
such that

P(\Delta F_{i}=-M_{i})=P(\Delta F_{j}=-M_{j}),

(S29)

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{E\in\Omega|M_{i}>|\Delta F_{i}(E)|\}
and
\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
be the abort set. Then

\max_{i}P(\mathcal{A}_{i})\ \leq\ P(\mathcal{A})\ \leq\ \mathcal{M}\,\max_{i}P(\mathcal{A}_{i}).

(S30)

Since each
P(\Delta F_{i})
has the same large deviation properties as in the surface-code setting, we propose discarding syndromes with
|\Delta F_{i}|<s^{*}_{i}d
, where the
s^{*}_{i}
are chosen such that
I_{i}(-s^{*}_{i})
is equal across
i
and
s^{*}_{i}\leq\overline{s}_{i}
to ensure finite acceptance probability. The logical failure rate after postselection scales as

p_{f}\ \simeq\ e^{-I_{i}(-s^{*}_{i})d}\ \geq\ e^{-\min\overline{s}_{i}\,d}.

(S31)

By contrast, without postselection one has

p_{f}\ \simeq\ e^{-\min I_{i}(0)\,d}.

(S32)

Hence the maximum possible gain from postselection is quantified by

b\equiv\frac{\min I_{i}(-\overline{s}_{i})}{\min I_{i}(0)}\ \geq\ \frac{2\min I_{i}(0)}{\min I_{i}(0)}=2.

(S33)

Thus, for any stabilizer code, postselection can improve the effective distance by at least a factor of two, with the precise value of
b
determined by the large deviation functions
I_{i}(s)
.

IV
Verifications of large deviation scaling

MWPM decoding.
As discussed in the main text, both MLD decoders and marginal decoders that obey large deviation scaling within certain regimes provide a scalable accuracy gain with
b\geq 2
. However, evaluating the exact probability of an error class becomes impractical under circuit-level noise, where measurement and CNOT gate errors must be taken into account. In practice, minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) decoding is typically employed to efficiently handle circuit-level noise. In this setting, the complementary gap
G
serves as a key indicator of code performance
Gidney et al. 2025
. For every error configuration
E
, let

\mathcal{C}_{\text{corr}}(E)
and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{inc}}(E)

denote the sets of error chains in the correct and incorrect homology
classes, respectively. Define

\displaystyle w_{\text{success}}(E)

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{corr}}(E)}-\log P(\mathcal{E}),

(S34a)

\displaystyle w_{\text{fail}}(E)

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{inc}}(E)}-\log P(\mathcal{E}).

(S34b)

A MWPM decoder always selects the class containing the matching with the minimum weight; in particular, the correct class is chosen if
w_{\rm success}<w_{\rm fail}
. Analogous to the definition of
\Delta F
, the complementary gap
G

Gidney et al. 2025
;
Hutter et al. 2014
;
Bombín et al. 2024
in MWPM is defined as

G=w_{\text{fail}}-w_{\text{success}},

(S35)

which plays the same role for MWPM as the free-energy difference
\Delta F
does in MLD, but is computationally more tractable. From a statistical-physics perspective, MWPM decodes at zero temperature rather than along the Nishimori line. Using Stim
Gidney 2021
and PyMatching
Higgott 2022
, we numerically investigated the distribution
P(G)
for the rotated surface code under both the code-capacity and circuit-level noise models.
In the rotated surface code
Bombin and Martin-Delgado 2007
;
Horsman et al. 2012
, boundaries are classified as
X
-type or
Z
-type, depending on whether
X
- or
Z
-syndrome checks are measured along that boundary. Logical operators are Pauli strings that connect opposite boundaries of the same type, with
X_{L}
connecting the two
X
-type boundaries and
Z_{L}
connecting the two
Z
-type boundaries.

As shown in Fig.
S4
and Fig.
S5
, the gap distributions
P(G)
satisfy large deviation scaling within the range
|G|\leq\langle G\rangle
as
P(G=sd)\sim e^{-I(s)d}
, and we continue to presume the monotonicity and convexity of
I(s)
.

However, the distribution of the complementary gap breaks the symmetry
P(\Delta F)=P(-\Delta F)e^{\Delta F}
, and instead appears to obey
Gidney et al. 2025

P(G)=P(-G)e^{cG},

(S36)

with
c
fitted from the distribution
P(G)
. This enables us to estimate
I(-\overline{s})
using the scaling of
\langle G\rangle
. Furthermore, the maximum possible gain from postselection is still bounded by

b\equiv\frac{I(-\overline{s})}{I(0)}=\frac{I(-\overline{s})+I(\overline{s})}{I(0)}\geq 2.

(S37)

Thus, replacing the MLD decoder with the MWPM decoder does not change the lower bound of
b
, implying a finite improvement in MWPM decoding via postselection. Numerically, decoding under circuit-level noise with
p=0.005
gives
b\approx 2.87
, while the gap distribution of the rotated surface code with perfect stabilizer measurement at bit-flip error rate
p=0.06
yields
b\approx 3.44
, in contrast to the
b\approx 3
result in MLD.

V
Observation of
b\approx 3
in two-dimensional RBIM

Partially heralded error and bond-diluted RBIM.

In the main text we quantify the gain in effective code distance by
b\equiv I(-\overline{s})/I(0)
. In the extreme case where all Pauli errors are heralded, logical failures occur only when bonds percolate
Stace et al. 2009
. Since such percolation events form an exponentially small fraction of error chains, aborting them yields perfect logical fidelity (
b=\infty
). A natural next step is to ask: what is the value of
b
when only a fraction of Pauli errors are heralded
Wu et al. 2022
;
Chang et al. 2024
?

Suppose each qubit undergoes a
partially heralded
bit-flip channel: with probability
pr_{e}
the error is flagged as an erasure, with probability
p(1-r_{e})
the error is unflagged, and with probability
1-p
no error occurs. Thus each qubit experiences a mixture of two channels:

(i)
Heralded branch (
\varepsilon_{e}=1
)
— with probability
pr_{e}
:

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{e}=1}(\rho)=\tfrac{1}{4}\!\left(I\rho I+X\rho X+Y\rho Y+Z\rho Z\right),

(S38)

(ii)
Unheralded branch (
\varepsilon_{e}=0
)
— with probability
p(1-r_{e})
:

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{e}=0}(\rho)=(1-p(1-r_{e}))\,\rho+p(1-r_{e})\,X\rho X,

(S39)

where the decoder is unaware of the flip.

Including the no-error component
(1-p)\rho
, the full channel is

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+pr_{e}\,\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{e}=1}(\rho)+p(1-r_{e})\,\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{e}=0}(\rho),

(S40)

with
\varepsilon_{e}\in\{0,1\}
revealed to the decoder. Conditional on
\varepsilon_{e}=0
, the effective flip probability is

p_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{p(1-r_{e})}{1-pr_{e}},\qquad K_{e}=\tfrac{1}{2}\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1-p_{\mathrm{eff}}}{p_{\mathrm{eff}}}\Bigr).

Hence the bond distribution in the RBIM is

\boxed{P(J_{e})=pr_{e}\,\delta(J_{e})+(1-p)\,\delta(J_{e}-K_{e})+p(1-r_{e})\,\delta(J_{e}+K_{e})},

with Nishimori relation
p_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/(1+e^{2K_{e}})
. Non-erased edges carry
J_{e}=K_{e}
, while flagged edges correspond to
J_{e}=0
and are deleted from the dual Ising graph. Thus the noise maps to an RBIM with bond dilution at density
pr_{e}
.

Two limits illustrate the model:
(i)
r_{e}=0
gives
p_{\mathrm{eff}}=p
, i.e. the standard RBIM on the Nishimori line.
(ii)
r_{e}\to 1
(with
p
finite) yields vanishing unflagged flips and diverging couplings (
J_{e}\to\infty
); decoding is then determined by percolation of erased bonds (
J_{e}=0
) at density
p
.

Importantly, changing the bond distribution does not affect the universality of the transition. Near the critical point
p_{c}(r_{e})
, the domain-wall free energy scales as

\Delta F(p,d;r_{e})=f\!\left(\frac{p-p_{c}(r_{e})}{d^{1/\nu}}\right),\qquad\nu\simeq 1.5,

(S41)

with
d
the lattice size. Fig.
S7
shows that data for
r_{e}=0.98
collapse with
\nu=3/2
, matching the undiluted case (
r_{e}=0
). Thus all partially heralded RBIMs with
0\leq r_{e}<1
belong to the same universality class
Picco et al. 2006
. In contrast, the percolation fixed point at
r_{e}=1
and
p=0.5
exhibits
\nu_{\mathrm{perc}}=4/3
, marking a distinct universality class
Smirnov and Werner 2001
.

Observation of
b\approx 3
in bond-diluted two-dimensional RBIM.

We now quantify the distance gain
b
in the diluted RBIM using the free-energy difference
\Delta F
. Recall that
b=\overline{s}/I(0)
, where
I(0)
is extracted from the scaling of
\log p_{f}
and
\overline{s}
from the linear growth of
\langle\Delta F\rangle
. In the limiting case
r_{e}=1
the model reduces to percolation of erased bonds; aborting the exponentially rare percolating instances forces the logical failure rate to vanish faster than any fixed-rate large deviation cost, so that
b\to\infty
at fixed code distance. Consequently, at small
d
one expects
b
to increase as
r_{e}
is tuned upward toward
1
.

Our finite-size simulations, however, reveal a different picture. Fig.
S8
shows that even when
r_{e}
is very close to one (
90\%
and
99\%
), the fitted values remain around
b\approx 3
. Specifically, we find
b=2.91
at
r_{e}=90\%
and
b=2.92
at
r_{e}=99\%
using data from
d=6,8,10
. This suggests that, within the accessible sizes, increasing the heralded rate does not significantly change the value of
b
, and the system continues to behave similarly to the RBIM without dilution.

A heuristic rationale for
b\approx 3
can be obtained by extrapolating the Tracy–Widom distribution near
\Delta F\simeq\langle\Delta F\rangle
. In this regime one expects

I(s)=c_{0}\,(\overline{s}-s)^{3/2},\qquad s\leq\overline{s}.

Using the symmetry relation
I(s)+s=I(-s)
to evaluate
I^{\prime}(0)
gives
c_{0}=1/\sqrt{9\overline{s}}
and hence
I(0)=\overline{s}/3
, implying
b=3
. However, the Tracy–Widom form is rigorously justified only for fluctuations
|\Delta F-\langle\Delta F\rangle|\sim d^{1/3}
, corresponding to
|s-\overline{s}|\sim d^{-2/3}
. Outside this narrow window the functional form of
I(s)
is unknown, so at present we cannot claim a derivation of
b=3
, only a suggestive consistency.
