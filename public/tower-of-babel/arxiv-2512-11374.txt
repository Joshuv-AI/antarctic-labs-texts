Mining Legal Arguments to Study Judicial Formalism
Tomáš Koref, Lena Held, Mahammad Namazov, Harun Kumru, Yassine Thlija, Christoph Burchard, Ivan Habernal

Abstract
Courts must justify their decisions, but systematically analyzing judicial reasoning at scale remains difficult. This study refutes claims about formalistic judging in Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) by developing automated methods to detect and classify judicial reasoning in Czech Supreme Courts’ decisions using state-of-the-art natural language processing methods. We create the MADON dataset of 272 decisions from two Czech Supreme Courts with expert annotations of 9,183 paragraphs with eight argument types and holistic formalism labels for supervised training and evaluation. Using a corpus of 300k Czech court decisions, we adapt transformer LLMs for Czech legal domain by continued pretraining and experiment with methods to address dataset imbalance including asymmetric loss and class weighting. The best models successfully detect argumentative paragraphs (82.6% macro-F1), classify traditional types of legal argument (77.5% macro-F1), and classify decisions as formalistic/non-formalistic (83.2% macro-F1). Our three-stage pipeline combining ModernBERT, Llama 3.1, and traditional feature-based machine learning achieves promising results for decision classification while reducing computational costs and increasing explainability. Empirically, we challenge prevailing narratives about CEE formalism. This work shows that legal argument mining enables reliable judicial philosophy classification and shows the potential of legal argument mining for other important tasks in computational legal studies. Our methodology is easily replicable across jurisdictions, and our entire pipeline, datasets, guidelines, models, and source codes are available at https://github.com/trusthlt/madon/ .

Related work

2.1
Jurisprudential debate on formalism

A simple example illustrates formalistic judging. Suppose a statute fixes a 15-day deadline to file an appeal. If a party files on day 16 because the counsel was hospitalized, a formalistic judge would dismiss the appeal as inadmissible, since the statute contains no exception. A non-formalistic judge would accept the appeal, invoking access-to-justice principles, the purpose of the rule, and the unfairness of harsh consequences under the individual circumstances.

Formalism concerns how lawyers, typically judges, approach legal questions. It is an approach to law and judicial adjudication that prioritizes the binding authority of legal norms and their wording. A formalist sees law as distinct from politics and limits the influence of non-legal norms and extra-legal considerations in answering legal questions, such as social, economical, political, or moral factors
(
72
;
59
;
73
)
.

The jurisprudential debate on legal formalism is longstanding and widespread. Some authors argue that the term has lost all meaning, while others have sought to rediscover and clarify it
(
59
;
56
)
. The debate spans sociology of law
(
72
)
, jurisprudence and legal argumentation
(
59
)
, Law and Economics
(
60
)
and critical legal studies
(
27
)
. Despite the variety of perspectives on formalism, prominent scholars from these different traditions usually discuss the question already noted at the outset: to what extent are the judges bound by existing legal rules and their wording?

Formalism can be approached analytically, descriptively, or normatively. The analytical approach asks, e.g., what formalism is and whether it is possible (e.g. can a judge simply apply a rule given the very same legal system already contains open-textured principles). The descriptive approach asks whether judges in fact decide cases in a formalistic manner (e.g. would an average judge indeed reject the appeal on strictly procedural grounds?). The normative approach asks whether formalism is desirable and what judges should do when deciding cases - is it right that judges stick to statutory wording rather than purpose?

2.3
Empirical analysis of formalism

We now discuss existing studies that investigate formalism empirically—both within the CEE region and beyond.

Three main approaches can be distinguished. The first approach studies the types of arguments courts employ. It quantifies the frequency of various argument types (e.g., linguistic, systematic, teleological) that are then classified as either formalistic (e.g., linguistic arguments) or non-formalistic (e.g., teleological arguments). This approach is not new; legal theory has investigated argument types for centuries, and typically US scholars like
10
or
33
;
34
have been empirically investigating argument types to assess prevailing interpretative theories. Fewer studies focus on argument types to measure formalism specifically. Most of these concern CEE judiciaries
(
48
;
49
;
7
)
. The underlying assumption of this approach is that the more formalistic arguments a court uses, the more formalistic the court is.

The second approach
(
64
;
67
)
directly labels paragraphs as formalistic or non-formalistic without analyzing specific argument types. This simplification enabled Stiglitz and Thalken not only to manually code decisions but also to provide large-scale analysis using NLP. This approach assumes that the higher the number of formalistic paragraphs appearing in courts’ decisions, the more formalistic the court is.

The third approach captures formalism’s different dimensions by examining various parameters simultaneously, including the court’s rhetorical style and procedural choices alongside argumentation patterns. This approach offers a holistic perspective on how formalism manifests across different dimensions of judicial decision-making
(
2
;
1
)
.

Despite valuable contributions, current approaches to measuring legal formalism face several problems. Most rely on small, manually coded samples without NLP, and none use argument mining to measure formalism directly. They typically cover only narrow slices of case law—e.g., a handful of criminal cases
(
7
)
or selected administrative cases
(
48
;
49
)
. More importantly, many suffer from methodological shortcomings: missing or insufficient coding manuals and a lack of reported inter-annotator agreement, which undermines reproducibility
(
49
;
7
)
.

Each of the three approaches has additional limitations. First, the argument type approach can be insufficient: simple counts often miss crucial nuances. While this method yields useful quantitative data about which argument types courts use and how often, it can mislead as a measure of formalism when, for example, non-formalistic arguments are typically decisive but scarce while formalistic ones appear very often but are merely supplementary or peripheral. Simply put, a single well-put policy argument may outweigh multiple brief references to the wording, when assessing formalistic nature of a decision.

Second, the paragraph labeling approach likewise downplays the importance of particular passages. It also downplays argument types, the standard lens in legal theory and legal argumentation
(
70
;
3
)
and provides less informative data on reasoning practices.

Finally, the multiparameter approach, though comprehensive, brings significant complexity to annotation.

We differentiate substantially from previous works and combine the best of all worlds. First, we analyze argument types and go further by publishing detailed annotation guidelines, reporting inter-annotator agreement, and employing state of the art LLMs. Second, we combine fine-grained argument mining classifying paragraphs into 8 argument types with a binary decision level label formalistic/non-formalistic; to this end, we design unique pipeline that reduces computational requirements, increases explainability and boosts performance. Third, we consider multiple aspects of formalism as the last approach, but condense them into a single binary holistic label, formalistic or non formalistic, and also employ NLP to predict this variable. As such, our approach builds on existing methods, but enables more comprehensive measurement of judicial reasoning and formalism.

The discussed shortcomings of existing approaches show the need for methods that remain true to legal theory but can work on a larger scale. Legal argument mining offers such a path.

3.2
Measuring formalism through argument types

The key literature on formalism discusses legal argumentation; the three core tenets of CEE formalism concern arguments. We therefore begun with arguments.

On their own, the tenets are too abstract for empirical coding of concrete cases. For example, the first core tenet of CEE formalism (heavy reliance on text based arguments) requires a precise specification: what counts as a text-based argument, and how can we identify the linguistic argument is really present in an actual decision? Without such specification, empirically analyzing real world case law is challenging, if not impossible.

We then applied the guideliness to annotate the below described MADON dataset of 272 decisions from Supreme Court and Supreme Administrative Court of Czechia. We used set of cases distinct from the pilot decisions to support reliability of the annotation.

The court decisions are structured into paragraphs. We reflected this structure and chose paragraph-level annotation over sentence-level coding for annotating arguments. Sentence-level coding produced very low Krippendorff unitized Alpha scores—annotators disagreed where exactly arguments start and end. From the perspective of our research question, we were not so much interested in whether a particular sentence belonged to an argument, but more about whether a particular argument is present in a certain paragraph. Thus, this decision should not negatively influence the results.

Each argument type would be counted only once per paragraph, even if multiple instances within one paragraph appeared. However, one paragraph could include two different argument types. For example, if a paragraph cited five previous judgments and referenced two explanatory notes, we would code it once for case law (CL – Case Law) and once for historical interpretation (HI – Historical Interpretation). We hypothesize that this approach reflects how legal theorists typically analyze argumentation: focusing on argument types rather than counting individual citations within one single sentence or paragraph.

We excluded rejected arguments from annotation. When courts discussed competing interpretations, we coded only the reasoning ultimately adopted. For example, if a court noted that historical interpretation supported outcome X while linguistic interpretation pointed to non-X, but the court then rejected linguistic interpretation and decided for X, we annotated only the historical interpretation.

3.3
Holistically labelling judgments as formalistic

Although arguments are crucial to determine prevailing reasoning style, formalism is not just about quantity of arguments. In our pilot studies, simply counting argument frequencies proved important, but insufficient. Some decisions dominated by formalistic arguments turned out non‑formalistic once assessed holistically—against the core tenets, the whole decision, its context, facts, and procedural history. Although quantification of arguments seems to be crucial prerequisite to study formalism and would likely suffice in most cases as our quantitative results show, it can also be misleading in some cases. There are three main reasons:

First, arguments differ in weight and form. A judgment may invoke a fundamental principle once or twice in a decisive paragraph, yet be packed with dozens of formalistic arguments in peripheral sections on procedure. Such a decision likely would be, in substance, non‑formalistic, but simple counting would suggest the opposite. Argument clarity also varies: some arguments are explicit; others are barely there and must be inferred from context. The problem deepens when courts cite statutory provisions that themselves embed non‑formalistic considerations (e.g., principles), blurring whether the reasoning is formalistic or not. And some “principles” are formalistic by design—think of the ”concentration principle,” which strictly bars new evidence in later stages of the proceedings. While arguments significantly matter, their quantification alone might therefore mislead.

Second, context matters. A Supreme Court decision critising lower courts for excessive formalism may still be filled with citations to statutory text and case law (formalist arguments) while ultimately pushing for a purposive, non-formalistic decision-making.

Third, the last two tenets, i.e., dismissal on formal grounds and insufficient reasoning, are not capturable by simply counting arguments.

To address this limitation, we annotated each decision with a binary label ‘formalistic/non-formalistic’. The expert decision is based on an holistic assessment of the entire case. The evaluations is complex and considers multiple contextual factors beyond argument counting: adherence to the five core tenets of formalism, the overall approach to legal interpretation, the weight and centrality of different arguments, whether the court critiques previous courts for formalistic reasoning, and the broader procedural and substantive context of the case.

3.4
Annotation process

The systematic annotation of Czech Supreme Courts’ decisions was conducted between July and September 2024, building on a pilot study from early 2024 that analyzed 160 decisions and helped refine the methodology. We employed four law students from the Faculty of Law, Charles University, as research assistants, that were lead by a PhD student in legal argumentation. All of them had a background in legal theory and argumentation.

The process had four phases: Introduction (Week 1), Training (Weeks 2–6, 60+ decisions), Coding (Weeks 7–12, 272 decisions with regular reviews), and Finalization (Week 13). The team met on weekly bases to discuss the annotations in the Introduction and Training phase. We used the INCEpTION
(
28
)
interface for accessible annotation. We followed methodological guidance for the legal domain and drawed mainly from content analysis literature
(
23
;
57
)
and recommended practices by
6
for annotating legal NLP datasets. This approach involved several hundred hours of training and guideline development, with at least two independent annotators coding each decision in the MADON dataset. We measured inter-annotator agreement and report on the annotation process. In total, the annotation took 1,000+ hours, covered more than 450 decisions including pilot studies (272 after pilot), and produced the MADON dataset with 272 decisions consisting of 9,183 annotated paragraphs, including 1,913 legal arguments in total and 1,237 paragraphs with at least one argument.

3.4.1
Inter-annotator agreement and dataset curation

We measured inter-annotator agreement to asses reliability of our annotations. We calculated two different inter-annotator agreement metrics corresponding to our two types of labels: Cohen’s kappa for the holistic label and Krippendorff’s unitized Alpha for argument types.

Our holistic assessment achieved a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.65 on the overall category formalistic/non-formalistic, indicating “substantial agreement” among annotators despite the complexity of the assessment
(
40
)
. This success yields two significant findings for future empirical research. First, research assistants can be effectively trained to achieve sufficient inter-coder agreement on complex holistic assessments of formalistic/non-formalistic reasoning. Second, we found a strong correlation between decisions with higher frequencies of non-formalistic arguments and holistic nonformalistic assessments, suggesting that while the dual approach provides valuable validation, the relationship between argument types and overall formalism remains
strong.

Stratified sampling of documents for expert annotation

The MADON dataset spans the period from 1997 to 2024, with a focus on decisions made between 2003 and 2023. To ensure a representative sample, stratified sampling was employed, balancing cases across time periods, court agendas (civil, criminal, and administrative), and types of cases (procedural and on the merits).
This method ensured proportional representation across the Supreme Court’s civil branch (122 cases), criminal branch (58 cases), and the Supreme Administrative Court (90 cases). Within these subpopulations, we drew random samples.

The distribution of holistic labels differs across courts. The SC had 116 formalistic (64%) and 66 non-formalistic (36%) decisions for a total of 182 cases. The SAC had 45 formalistic (50%) and 45 non-formalistic (50%) decisions for a total of 90 cases. Combined, there were 161 formalistic (59%) and 111 non-formalistic (41%) decisions out of 272 total cases.

4.3
Key legal empirical findings from the MADON dataset

The MADON dataset allows us to get the first systematic picture of both Czech Supreme Courts’ reasoning practices. Contrary to the Tale of Two Courts discussed in Section
2.2
, i.e., a Tale of formalistic SC and non-formalistic SAC, we found that during the SAC’s initial decade (2003-2013), both courts exhibited remarkably similar levels of formalism on most indicators. For instance, both courts had approximately two-thirds of their decisions classified as formalistic, with the SAC showing even slightly more formalism on some indicators.

However, the temporal trends in Figure
3
show a notable divergence which started around 2011 and continued throughout the second decade (2014-2023), where the SAC shifted towards non-formalistic reasoning. During this second period, the proportion of non-formalistic SAC decisions increased by 70 percent (Figure
3
c), and the average number of non-formalistic arguments per decision rose by more than 100 percent (Figure
3
b), while the SC’s practices remained relatively stable. This divergence is also evident in the ratio of non-formalistic to formalistic arguments (Figure
3
a) and the decreasing share of SAC decisions with zero non-formalistic arguments (Figure
3
d). This indicates that the Tale of Two Courts—the narrative about formalistic SC and non-formalistic SAC—became close to reality only in the later period, if ever.

By contrast, non-formalistic arguments from legal principles and values (PL) and teleological interpretation (TI) are much more common, together making up 32.3% of all arguments. Case law (CL) is the most common type at 37.4%. Notably, case law was by far most dominant argument type already in 2006 at 29%; this indicates that while its use has increased over time (both in percentage share and absolute numbers), it was already the most frequent category of legal arguments at that earlier point in the study period.

These results demonstrate that Czech judicial reasoning, particularly its heavy reliance on case law and non-formalistic argumentation, diverges from traditional definitions of formalism prevalent in both CEE and U.S. contexts, where formalism is connected to textualism. Experiments

5.1
Experiment setup and tasks

We design three different experiments to investigate the connection between court document texts, arguments, and formalism in the MADON dataset. In general, mining arguments is often split into several subtasks
(
19
)
and we follow this pattern by defining our subtasks into the following:
As an initial probe whether models are capable of detecting arguments at all, we train it to predict the presence and absence of arguments in a paragraph. We do not distinguish between a single argument and multiple arguments and focus only on detecting the presence. This experiment design also serves as a sanity check to see if our models are able to distinguish argumentative and non-argumentative paragraphs.
The second and most difficult task requires the extraction of the exact argument types from a given paragraph. Since there can be more than one argument in a paragraph, we formulate the problem as a multilabel classification problem with a binary representation for the presence of each argument type separately. If there is no argument present, the output should be ‘False’ for each argument type.
Finally, we tackle the question of formalism for the entire document. This third task should also provide us with insights whether models are capable of understanding the document holistically without having additional labels. Here we experiment with different approaches and pipelines; the simplest approach passes the entire text of the decision to the model and performs a binary classification (formalistic/non-formalistic). In the following, we refer to our experiments as:

Task 1: Argument presence detection

We classify paragraphs into argumentative and non-argumentative based on the presence or absence of any argument in it.

Task 2: Argument type classification

Entire paragraphs are classified in a multilabel setting into the eight types of traditional legal argument types described in Section
3.2
.

Task 3: Holistic formalism classification

We classify entire judicial decisions as formalistic or non-formalistic.

5.2
Models

For all three tasks, we use large language models (LLMs) that can handle large documents efficiently. However we also keep computational efficiency at inference time in mind to enable empirical analysis of the entire judiciary of 230,000 Supreme Courts decision in future work. We therefore experiment with two complementary state-of-the-art architectures: the decoder-only Llama 3.1 8B
(
52
)
and the encoder-only ModernBERT
(
71
)
. Llama 3.1 is trained on multilingual data, making it suitable for our Czech data, while ModernBERT provides a strong encoder baseline with long-context support. Moreover, for the holistic formalism classification task, we also experiment with feature-based multi-layer perceptron (MLP).

5.2.1
Llama

We use two variants of Llama 3.1 8B: the base model without instruction fine-tuning, which is suited for multilabel task finetuning, and a base model version which received continued pretraining (CPT) on Czech legal texts (Section
5.2.3
below).
We also test the performance of instruction tuning (IT) on Llama 3.1 70B with different variants of prompting using annotation guidelines in zero-shot and few-shot settings. To optimize computational costs, we also test the performance of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT, via LoRA adapters
(
24
)
). However, we find that instruction tuning performed significantly worse than fully fine-tuned models.

5.2.2
ModernBERT

ModernBERT 395M
(
71
)
is a recent encoder-only transformer model supporting up to 8,192 tokens. Compared to earlier models of the BERT family, it integrates modifications such as Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)
(
65
)
, GeGLU activations, alternating local–global attention, and Flash Attention
(
16
)
. These features make it a suitable encoder baseline for our classification tasks, overcoming the token limitation and English bias of Legal-BERT
(
8
)
.

5.2.3
Continued pretraining of transformers

Neither Llama 3.1 8B nor ModernBERT was originally trained for the legal domain or the Czech language specifically. To address this limitation, we perform continued pretraining of both models using Czech legal data. This approach allows us to investigate whether domain- and language-specific adaptation improves performance, building on previous findings in legal NLP
(
76
;
21
)
.
For pretraining, we curate a corpus of approximately 300,000 legal decisions from the Supreme Court (SC), Supreme Administrative Court (SAC), and regional administrative courts, excluding all decisions already included in the MADON dataset to prevent data contamination.
4
4

4

Data available at
https://github.com/trusthlt/madon/
.

Due to computational constraints, we continue pretraining the Llama 3.1 8B base model for one epoch, using an effective batch size of 16 and a maximum input length of 32,000 tokens, which is large enough to fully cover most of the documents. We use unsloth
5
5

5

https://unsloth.ai
to optimize the training process.

We follow a two-phase pretraining process for ModernBERT. First, we train a custom tokenizer using byte-pair encoding on our pretraining dataset. We then continue pretraining from the last checkpoint of ModernBERT-large using a masked language modeling objective.

In phase 1, we include documents with fewer than 8,192 tokens (the model’s input limit), train with a 30% masking probability and an effective batch size of 32. In phase 2, we reduce the effective batch size to 8 and the masking probability to 15% and train using the remaining documents.

5.2.4
Multi-layer perceptron

For holistic formalism classification, we also experiment with an alternative input representation. We simply use the annotated argument types and extract various statistics into a document-level feature vector which we feed into a multi-layer perceptron (MLP; 2 hidden layers with 20 and 50 neurons, respectively).
6
6

6

Simpler linear models such as logistic regression did not produce any usable results.
The feature set extracted from a document includes the document length in tokens, number of annotated arguments, average argument length, and for each argument type from our inventory its relative frequency in percent. Note that this feature-based approach is only applicable to documents annotated with arguments (which is the case for the MADON dataset) but cannot be used for inference on purely textual data, unlike the transformer models. We will touch on mitigating this limitations later in Section
5.3.3
.
For further training details see Appendix

5.3
Results

For all three tasks, we rely on the macro-averaged F1 score.
To provide a balanced evaluation, we distinguish between two complementary perspectives:
the positive F1 score (
\text{F1}^{+}
), which measures how well the model identifies labels that are actually present,
and the negative F1 score (
\text{F1}^{-}
), which measures how well the model avoids predicting labels that are absent.

For
Task 1
(argument presence detection) and
Task 3
(holistic formalism classification),
the evaluation is binary: either an argument (resp. formalism) is present or not.
We compute the macro-averaged F1 score over the two classes (present vs. absent), following the standard definition.

For
Task 2
(argument type classification), the evaluation is multi-label with an inventory of eight argument types

L=\{\text{LIN},\text{SI},\text{CL},\text{D},\text{HI},\text{PL},\text{TI},\text{PC}\}
. Initial experiments with standard metrics for multi-label evaluation, such as Hamming distance, did not capture the nuances in misclassification and did not help us interpret the results across various argument types, which is essential for empirical legal analysis. We therefore propose an evaluation metrics tailored to our task.

Each label
\ell\in L
is treated as an independent binary classification problem.
Concretely, for a given label
\ell
:

•

if
\ell
is annotated in the gold data and predicted, this counts as a
true positive
(tp), contributing to
\text{F1}^{+}
,

•

if
\ell
is not annotated but predicted, this is a
false positive
(fp), which lowers both
\text{F1}^{+}
and
\text{F1}^{-}
,

•

if
\ell
is annotated but not predicted, this is a
false negative
(fn), which also lowers both
\text{F1}^{+}
and
\text{F1}^{-}
,

•

if
\ell
is neither annotated nor predicted, this is a
true negative
(tn), contributing to
\text{F1}^{-}
.

For each label
\ell
, we compute both the positive and negative F1 scores, namely

\text{F1}^{+}_{\ell}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{tp}_{\ell}}{2\cdot\mathrm{tp}_{\ell}+\mathrm{fp}_{\ell}+\mathrm{fn}_{\ell}},\qquad\text{F1}^{-}_{\ell}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{tn}_{\ell}}{2\cdot\mathrm{tn}_{\ell}+\mathrm{fp}_{\ell}+\mathrm{fn}_{\ell}}.

(1)

We then define the macro-F1 for a given label
\ell
as

\text{Macro-F1}_{\ell}=\frac{\text{F1}^{+}_{\ell}+\text{F1}^{-}_{\ell}}{2}.

(2)

Finally, the overall macro-F1 is obtained by averaging across all labels:

\text{Macro-F1}_{\text{all}}=\frac{1}{|L|}\sum_{\ell\in L}\text{Macro-F1}_{\ell}.

(3)

Gold labeled arguments as features

Arguments are crucial for holistic evaluation of formalism. Using human expert annotations of arguments from MADON dataset as features, a MLP reaches macro-F1 of 91.7% in classifying decisions, substantially higher than any contemporary end-to-end LLMs (see Table

other

Contemporary end-to-end LLMs for holistic classification

Direct classification of decisions as non/formalistic—without argument features—gives very promising results. Continued pretraining improves both model families, and ModernBERT-large-Czech-Legal achieves 82.2% macro-F1, the best among end-to-end models. ModernBERT outperforms Llama even though Llama can process the entire document context without a cut-off due to a token limit. This indicates that lexical and semantic cues, which also play an important role in expert annotation, may be more valuable than full-context processing. ModernBERT outperfroms the baselines by 40+ points, yet still falls behind the argument-based approach by about ten points.

Multi-step pipeline for holistic classification

We experimented with different approach to holistic classification. Since even the best LLMs fall behind the feature-based performance, we designed a multi-step pipeline that uses features and mirrors how legal scholars would evaluate decision and assess formalism: first filtering irrelevant passages, then identifying argument types, and finally assigning a holistic label formalistic/non-formalistic. This architecture provides better explainability and even slightly improves performance. The pipeline consists of three stages:

1.

In the first stage, ModernBERT Large identifies which paragraphs contain legal arguments, filtering out ca 85% of the decisions’ text (i.e. non-argumentative parts of the decisions; these are often repetitive or procedural texts usually not crucial for assessing formalism).

2.

In the second stage, Llama 3.1 8B Base (+ Asy), classifies the filtered text into argument types such as Teleological Interpretation, Principles of Law, and Systematic Interpretation.

3.

Finally, MLP extracts features from the identified arguments to make the final formalistic/non-formalistic classification, using our previous finding that argument frequency strongly predicts the overall non/formalistic nature of the decision. See Figure
5
for a visual depiction.

Figure 5
:
A multi-step pipeline combining several distinct customized LLMs and MLP delivers strong and explainable performance for holistic formalism classification.

The three stage pipeline yields 82.8% macro F1 and slightly outperforms the best end-to-end model. Interestingly, if we omit the filtering stage and apply Llama to entire decisions, the score rises marginally to 83.2%. These results provide two key findings. First, decomposing NLP classification task according to legal theory does not sacrifice performance; on the contrary, the pipeline scores slightly better than end-to-end models and provides greater explainability (due to the reliance on features in MLP that can be explained by SHAP methods). Second, filtering as much as 85% of the text of the classified documents has negligible impact on performance for this task but significantly safes compute costs. Overall, it proved beneficial to let legal theory to guide the NLP architecture for this classification task and opt for feature based approach.

To sum up, our results show that computational methods can classify complex judicial philosophies even in non-English judicial decisions.

6
Discussion

Our study provides three main findings. First, we developed state-of-the-art NLP methods that are able to detect argumentative paragraphs, can classify traditional types of legal arguments but struggle with some of them, and can identify formalistic versus non-formalistic decisions in Czech Supreme Courts judgments with high accuracy. Second, we show that decomposing legal classification tasks and using more explainable, segmented pipelines across model families can improve performance and explainability while reducing compute costs. Third, the results refute dominant narratives about judicial formalism in Central and Eastern Europe, particularly the Tale of Two Courts in Czechia. We believe that achieving very promising judicial philosophy classification in a non-English setting represents a significant advance for legal NLP and the rising field of computational legal studies.

6.1
Contributions to legal NLP

This is the first LLM-based argument mining study on CEE courts. Our experiments and lessons learned on non-English court judgments could benefit the legal NLP community. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning was inadequate, whereas continued pretraining proved important for ModernBERT (namely for argument-type classification and holistic formalism classification). By contrast, Llama benefited primarily from full fine-tuning rather than continued pretraining. Class imbalance—prevalent in legal datasets—needed explicit treatment: for multi-label argument-type classification, Asymmetric Loss consistently outperformed Binary Cross-Entropy across model architectures, improving macro-F1 by up to nine points. Finally, macro-averaged metrics were essential for multi-label evaluation; the scores would be otherwise dominated by the majority negative class for each argument type.

We also show that interdisciplinary combination of legal theory and NLP can be fruitful. Rather than classifying reasoning style at the decision level directly, this NLP task benefits from decomposing the pipeline according to legal theory: first, filter legally relevant parts of the text, then classify traditional legal arguments, and only then classify legal formalism. We expect that decomposing classification tasks in accordance with how lawyers or legal theorists approach problems could boost explainability and performance in other legal NLP tasks.

Crucially, our pipeline to investigate reasoning patterns in court decisions is replicable in other jurisdictions (e.g., Poland, Germany, US) and paves the way for comparative legal NLP studies.

6.2
Implications for computational legal studies

A key strand of computational legal studies is “law as data”—an empirical approach to legal research that uses computational tools to study courts and judicial culture
(
9
)
. We show how LAM advances this agenda: imperfect argument mining enables very promising classification of judicial philosophies. Even when argument detection is noisy for some categories, the resulting distributions can capture the key aspects of judicial reasoning we were interested in.

More broadly, our work illustrates how computational legal studies using LAM can move legal research beyond anecdotes. Empirical analysis at scale can challenge entrenched narratives, provide evidence on judicial practice, and support the legitimacy of courts by addressing criticisms with systematic data. The release of our MADON dataset (with an accompanying raw corpus of 300k decisions) and all models provides the necessary resources for this research agenda.

6.3
Implications for legal scholarship

Our findings challenge decades of assumptions about CEE courts. The divergence between the Supreme Court and Supreme Administrative Court appears only after 2011, and seem not be a direct consequence of communist-era legacies as commonly claimed
(
37
;
48
)
. Both courts use textual arguments scarcely (5.7% of all arguments) while case law dominates (37.4%). This contradicts the claims that CEE courts are text-bound and had been precedent-averse
(
47
;
7
;
62
)
. This preliminary empirical findings await further verification with a large scale analysis.

6.4
Implications for legal practice

For legal practice, LAM enables efficient document analysis. Paragraph-level filtering achieves 82.6% macro-F1 in identifying argumentative content, letting lawyers focus on argumentative passages which are often the crucial ones. Argument classification could help practitioners quickly find concrete argument types or specific reasoning patterns across single documents as well as large document collections (e.g., to assess what type of argumentation prevails in most filings and how they correlate with success rates at courts).

Beyond assessing argumentation patterns, LAM supports often very complicated evaluation of legal argumentation. Since each argument type has distinct evaluation criteria, automated detection simplifies the analysis. Practitioners can verify whether cited cases actually apply or whether teleological arguments connect to statutory purposes. Since evaluation is central to both practice and argument theory (lawyers want to know which arguments hold and which not), we believe future research could automate some parts of it. For instance, we could train models to check whether used teleological arguments indeed align with statutory purpose, a simple binary classification on top of detected arguments. This would create a two-stage method that would first identify arguments and then evaluate their strength.

Our approach also enables studying relationships between argumentation and case outcomes. Decisions with certain argument patterns may face higher reversal rates, which could inform both courts and participant (e.g. their litigation strategies). Courts and practitioners could also benefit from data about prevailing argument patterns of specific courts (and senates) for specific legal problems. For instance, if a lawyer knew she is preparing a filing for a court senate that predominantly relies on linguistic interpretation and rarely employs teleological arguments, she could strategically emphasize text based arguments to strengthen her position and improve her chances of success.

6.5
Limitations and future directions

Our research has several limitations.

First, empirical findings are restricted to 272 decisions, which, while representative through various sampling methods, limits generalizability. Nonetheless, this issue shall be solved by the large scale analysis with our models we plan to pursue as a next step.

Second, our formalism classification employs a CEE-specific definition that may be only partially applicable outside CEE. For instance, we classify Historical Interpretation (HI) as non-formalistic following CEE literature, whereas German or US scholarship might consider it formalistic. However, this limitation has minimal practical impact since HI arguments were extremely scarce in our dataset. Besides, our approach to formalism reflects our research objective: empirically testing existing claims about CEE formalism rather than proposing new theoretical framework. We conducted thorough literature review of prominent or recent CEE scholarship on the topic and extracted the core tenets of formalism to enable empirical verification of prevailing claims. This methodology aligns with our goal of replacing anecdotal claims with systematic evidence. And last but not least, the main core tenets seem to be shared outside CEE, especially the reliance on text-based arguments, focus on the most applicable rule while ignoring broader principles, and adherence to formal procedure to avoid substantive reasoning.

Third, the sparse occurrence of text-based arguments (5.7%) partially reflects our research design choices in our annotation guidelines. We required courts to engage at least partially with statutory language—analyzing wording, referencing dictionaries, or discussing grammar—rather than merely citing provisions. However, our threshold remained low: any reference to wording or explicit textual analysis qualified as linguistic interpretation. If we counted every single reference to a statute as linguistic interpretation, the frequency would be much higher; however, we believe our approach aligns better with legal argumentation theory, which treats analyzing wording, referencing dictionaries, and discussing grammar as the core aspects of linguistic interpretation.

Lastly, both empirical and NLP components focus exclusively on the two Czech apex courts. We cannot generalize empirical findings to lower courts or Constitutional Court nor simply use the models on judgments of these courts. Although applying the models especially to lower court decisions would be valuable to provide broader picture of the whole legal system, their applicability requires further examination and testing.

Several research directions follow. First, we plan to employ the trained models to the full corpus of 230,000 apex-court decisions to validate and refine our empirical results. Second, testing performance on lower-court decisions will show whether the approach needs adaptation across different courts in one jurisdiction. Third, extending our methodology and pipelines to other CEE jurisdictions would better test the existing claims about CEE region and would indicate whether the Czech patterns are country specific. Comparative LAM across CEE and non-CEE jurisdictions could directly test claims about CEE’s distinctiveness, or more broadly, verify existing claims about civil and common law reasoning that had been recently heavily critiqued
(
62
)
. Finally, we show that LAM could support related tasks in computational legal studies, including predicting outcomes from reasoning patterns. Whether it is indeed so, remains open.

7
Conclusion

We asked to which extent state-of-the-art NLP methods can detect and classify traditional types of legal arguments in non-english judicial decisions and whether they can distinguish between formalistic and non-formalistic reasoning styles at the decision level. After designing annotation guidelines for formalism and arguments and annotating 272 Czech Supreme Court decisions (9,183 paragraphs) resulting in the new MADON dataset, our extensive set of experiments shows that ModernBERT successfully distinguishes argumentative from non-argumentative paragraphs with a macro-F1 of 82.6%. Argument-type classification achieves macro-F1 of 77.5% using fully fine-tuned Llama 3.1 with asymmetric loss, though linguistic interpretation, systemic interpretation, and practical consequences remain hard to detect, unlike other argument types. Holistic formalism classification reaches impressive macro-F1 of 82.8% thanks to a novel three-stage pipeline, which at the same time also reduces computational costs and increases explainability. Empirically, both apex courts show similar formalism levels from 2003 to 2011, contrary to existing claims. After 2011, the SAC shifts toward non-formalistic reasoning while the SC remains stable. Text-based arguments are rare in both courts, while case law and purposive reasoning dominate.

8
Arguments

For each argument type, our guidelines included a structured description that connects the theoretical concept to real‑world decisions. For most types, we (1) give a brief description, (2) list its subtypes, (3) note “trigger” phrases that usually signal its presence in a decision, (4) provide typical and borderline examples, (5) add comments and annotator tips and (6) include annotation chart.

All details are in the Guidelines. What follows is a brief description intended to illustrate how we understand the particular argument types:

Linguistic Interpretation (LIN)

This argument type focuses on the literal text of a legal provision. This includes analyzing the ordinary meaning of words, explicitly reflecting syntax or grammar rules, referencing explicit legal definitions within a statute, and employing reasoning a contrario. We considered trigger words like “unambiguously worded,” “diction,” or “formulation” as strong indicators.

Example:

“In addition to the above, the Supreme Administrative Court adds that the wording of Section 87e(1)(i)(1) of the Act on the Residence of Aliens is very unambiguous and leaves no room for a different interpretation.”

Systemic Interpretation (SI).

This argument involves interpreting a provision by placing it within the broader context of the legal system to ensure a ‘logical, consistent and non-contradictory interpretation’. This includes applying the collision rules (
lex specialis, lex posterior, lex superior
), narrowly construing exceptions, and explicitly performing conforming interpretation (either constitutional conforming or EU conforming interpretation) to align domestic law with higher-order legal norms.

Example:

“Since the provisions of Section 281 of the Criminal Procedure Code do not contain special provisions for decisions pursuant to Section 288(3) of the Criminal Procedure Code, the general provisions on the subject matter and local jurisdiction of the court (Sections 16 to 22 of the Criminal Procedure Code) apply.”

Case Law (CL).

This category captures express reliance on specific prior judicial decisions from domestic courts, the CJEU, or the ECHR. For an annotation to be made, the court must cite a particular case; a vague reference to “according to the case law” without a specific citation was not considered sufficient.

Example:

“In support of the conclusion, the Court also referres to the judgment of the Supreme Court of 11 May 2005, Case No. 30 Cdo 64/2004 (available at www.nsoud.cz).”

Doctrine (D).

This argument type refers to citations of academic or expert commentary, such as legal textbooks, scholarly articles, or commentaries. References to legal dictionaries are also marked as Doctrine, whereas non-legal dictionaries fall under Linguistic Interpretation.

Example:

“Current commentary literature on both the Code of Civil Procedure and the Civil Code derives from the nature of a power of attorney that the attorney‑in‑fact’s signature is not among its formal requirements (Smolík, P., § 28 [Granting a Power of Attorney and its Termination], in: Svoboda, K. et al., Občanský soudní řád, 3rd ed., Prague: C. H. Beck, 2021, pp. 121–122).”

Historical Interpretation (HI).

This argument type seeks to determine the lawmaker’s intent behind a provision. When court seeks to determine the intent, it typically refers to historical documents for legislative process, such as explanatory memoranda, stenographic records of parliamentary debates, or the circumstances of a law’s adoption. Due to its simmilarity, we also included arguments based on the assumption of a rational lawmaker.

Example:

““cf. the wording of (…) the special part of the explanatory report [“On points 4 and 5 (section 274)”] to the governmental bill No. 31/2019 Coll., which was debated by the Chamber of Deputies of the Parliament of the Czech Republic in its 8th term (2017-2021) as Document No. 71.”

Principles of Law and Values (PL).

This non-formalistic category includes appeals to higher-order norms that transcend the literal text of a single rule. It covers general legal principles (e.g., good faith, legitimate expectations, legal certainty) and explicit references to fundamental rights and values found in the Czech Constitution (incl. the European Convention on Human Rights) or EU Law. A principle does not need to be explicitly labeled as such by the court to be annotated.

Example:

“[…] which constitutes an interference with the right to a fair trial enshrined in Article 36(1) of the Charter”

Teleological Interpretation (TI).

This argument focuses on the purpose, goal, or “telos” of a legal provision, often signaled by terms like “purpose,” “aim,” “sense,” or “function”. This category also includes special forms of purpose-driven reasoning, such as arguments ad absurdum (to avoid a nonsensical result), a fortiori, and reasoning by analogy.

Example:

“An appeal is conceived as an extraordinary remedy and is therefore intended to remedy only serious legal defects in final decisions.”

Practical Consequences (PC).

This argument type involves the court considering the real-world effects and practical outcomes of its decision. Instead of focusing on the wording of legal norms, the court weighs what will happen if it chooses one interpretation over another, answering the question “What will it lead to?”. This can include impacts on the parties as well as the society.

Example:

““In the event of a contrary conclusion, it would be difficult to clearly identify and distinguish what personal manifestations of the taxi driver in the course of his interaction with the customer can still be characterized as his work activity and what already constitutes his private life.”
