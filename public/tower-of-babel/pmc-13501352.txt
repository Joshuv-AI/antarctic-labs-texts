Association of Easier Biologic Access with Remission and Other Clinical Outcomes in Severe Asthma: A Global Real-World Study
Authors: Susanne Hansen, Ming-Ju Tsai, Trung N Tran, John D Blakey, Benjamin Emmanuel, Shawn D Aaron, Yahya Habis, Valeria Perugini, Alan Altraja, Rohit K Katial, Luis Perez-de-Llano, Paul E Pfeffer, Nikolaos G Papadopoulos, John Townend, Ghislaine Scelo, Aaron Beastall, Lakmini Bulathsinhala, John Busby, Andrew W Lindsley, Wendy C Moore, Reynold A Panettieri Jr, Giorgio Walter Canonica, Enrico Heffler, Cristina Cardini, Giuseppe Guida, Celeste M Porsbjerg, Anne-Sofie Bjerrum, Anna Von Bülow, Ole Hilberg, Kjell Erik Julius Håkansson, Marianne Baastrup Soendergaard, Charlotte Suppli Ulrik, Eileen Wang, Michael E Wechsler, Nicholas Chapman, Flavia C L Hoyte, Kanao Otsu, Liam G Heaney, David J Jackson, Pujan H Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTime to biologic initiation for the treatment of severe asthma (SA) is affected by many factors, including ease of access to biologics, which is often subject to approval by regulatory authorities and reimbursement criteria by relevant agencies. We investigated the association between ease of biologic access (using the biologic accessibility score [BACS] as a proxy) and post-biologic asthma outcomes, including remission.MethodsThis ecological study, using data from CHRONICLE (a US severe asthma registry), the International Severe Asthma Registry (ISAR), and the Optimum Patient Care Research Database (OPCRD), included patients with SA from 21 countries. Associations at the country level, between BACS, a composite score of prescription criteria for biologics in SA, and the proportion of patients with a favorable asthma outcome 1-year post-biologic in each setting (ie ISAR country or in the CHRONICLE or OPCRD datasets) were tested. Several definitions of favorable outcome were used, including proportion of patients who achieved clinical remission (defined using 2, 3 and 4 domains), experienced no exacerbations, had well- or partly controlled asthma, a percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (ppFEV1) or percent predicted peak expiratory flow rate (ppPEFR) ≥80%, and no long-term oral corticosteroid (LTOCS) use.ResultsA total of 9,183 patients were included. A higher BACS (as a proxy of easier access to biologics) was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving clinical remission (p≤0.001), no exacerbations (p<0.001), well- or partly controlled asthma (p=0.047), a ppFEV1 or ppPEFR ≥80% (p=0.004), and no need for LTOCS (p=0.045) 1 year post-biologic initiation.ConclusionEasier access to biologics for patients with SA, a prerequisite for shorter time-to-initiation, was associated with a greater probability of achieving clinical remission and other favorable asthma outcomes. Initiating biologics earlier in the asthma disease course may help unlock greater therapeutic potential in SA. These findings warrant confirmation in additional studies to further establish the causal relationship between biologic accessibility, timing of initiation, and clinical outcomes in SA.Keywords: control, exacerbations, long-term oral corticosteroids, lung function, time to biologic initiation

Introduction

Six biologics are currently approved for treating uncontrolled and/or severe asthma (SA) in different countries around the world. Benefits associated with biologic use include a reduction in exacerbations by approximately 50%, alleviation of symptoms, improved lung function and disease control, reduction or elimination of the need for oral corticosteroids (OCS), achievement of on-treatment clinical remission in 20–50% of patients with SA (depending on the definition used), and prevention of the development of new onset OCS-related comorbidities such as diabetes, major cardiovascular events and anxiety/depression.1–8 Achieving remission is an important treatment goal in SA, as it is associated with improved symptom control, reduced exacerbations, lower inhaled and systemic corticosteroid exposure, better quality of life, and potentially improved long-term prognosis.2,9–11 Given these benefits, biologics have transformed SA management.1,9 However, variability in response to biologics has prompted researchers to investigate factors influencing their efficacy and effectiveness, and to identify predictors of response and remission, both in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and real-world evidence studies. Generally, a better response is observed in those with more severe disease (ie, more room for improvement), but a higher chance of remission is observed in those with less severe disease at biologic initiation.2,4,12 With the recognition that achieving remission is now an attainable goal for many patients with SA, it has been suggested that starting treatment earlier in the disease process may increase the possibility of achieving remission.8,13 Early treatment with biologics is recommended in other chronic inflammatory conditions (eg rheumatoid arthritis).14 In asthma, early treatment with inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs) has already proven effective in achieving better disease control;15–17 but it is not clear whether earlier therapy with biologics improves long-term outcomes.

Timing of treatment initiation is a complex issue.18,19 It is also difficult to define in chronic, variable conditions like asthma, particularly as some of the pathophysiological changes may be present long before disease manifestations. This difficulty is exemplified by the considerable variation found in the literature to define “early” intervention with ICSs, including (but not limited to): “at diagnosis of persistent disease”, “before diagnosis of persistent disease”, “at an early age”, “in those presenting with mild disease”, “in preference to other treatments”, and “in patients with a diagnosis of asthma, but within a short period of time (<2 years) after the first symptoms”.17 The concept of time to biologic initiation is relatively new. At present, there is no universally accepted definition, consensus, or validated framework for its measurement or interpretation, but some proposed time-based proxies include time from: asthma diagnosis, development of potential SA, lung function impairment, and frequent exacerbations, before starting biologics.20 However, multiple factors can delay biologic initiation, including misperceptions of asthma severity, poor recognition of the need for treatment intensification (ie, medical inertia), medical costs, suboptimal patient education, and prolonged referral times to specialist centers.18,19 Moreover, access to biologics often involves a complex process of healthcare insurance or other funding/reimbursement systems and is subject to strict approval criteria set by many regulatory authorities.18

Variability in approval criteria by regulatory authorities has led to considerable global variability in biologic access for patients with SA. The European Respiratory Biologics Forum of 2018 noted variability by country in biologic prescriptions owing to differences in national health care systems regarding referral networks, access criteria and reimbursement policies,21 a finding corroborated by data from both the Severe Heterogeneous Asthma Research collaboration – Patient centred (SHARP)22 database and the International Severe Asthma Registry (ISAR).23 In the latter publication, ease of access to biologics was measured using a composite score, called the Biologic Accessibility Score (BACS) (see methods for more details). The BACS was informed by country-specific biologic prescription criteria, with higher scores denoting easier access.23 BACS, therefore, allows categorization and cross-country comparison of biologic access (a function of variability in biologic prescription criteria), but may also serve as a proxy of time-to-biologic initiation.23 A lower BACS would suggest longer time to biologic initiation, by which time more complications (including irreversible damage) may have developed due to recurrent exacerbations, exposure to systemic corticosteroids, and airway remodelling. Indeed, a connection between BACS and time-to-biologic initiation is suggested from data obtained in the UK, with a BACS ranging from 40–55 for available biologics (ie, representative of moderately difficult access), and an average patient journey from referral to specialist center to biologic initiation taking 63.5 weeks.19 However, this association is yet to be validated.

To date, no RCT has investigated the effect of the lag time between SA diagnosis and initiation of biologics in terms of clinical outcomes, as it would not be feasible due to ethical considerations. This lack of RCT evidence is reflected in the position of biologics in current stepwise therapy guidelines, which recommend considering biologic therapy for those with uncontrolled disease despite high-dose ICS/long-acting β2-agonist (LABA), evidence of T2 inflammation in most cases, and after consideration of local payer eligibility criteria, co-morbidities, predictors of response, and cost.24 As a result, only a small proportion of patients who might benefit from biologics, receive them.25 We hypothesized that easier biologic access would be associated with improved post-biologic asthma outcomes, possibly reflecting earlier biologic initiation due to easier access. This study aimed to investigate the association between BACS and post-biologic asthma outcomes, including remission.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This was an ecological study (ie analyses performed at the country rather than at the patient level) using pooled data from the CHRONICLE US severe asthma registry, ISAR (https://www.isar.opcglobal.org/), and the Optimum Patient Care Research Database (OPCRD; https://opcrd.optimumpatientcare.org/), and included data from 21 countries (Argentina, Bulgaria, Brazil, Canada, Colombia, Denmark, Estonia, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Kuwait, Mexico, Portugal, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, South Korea, Spain, Taiwan, UK, and USA). See e-Appendix 1 for registry details. The association between ease of biologic access (using the previously described BACS)23 and clinical outcomes was assessed (Figure 1). The study was registered with the European Network Centers for Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacovigilance (HMA-EMA/ENCePP; EUPAS1000000530), and designed, implemented, and reported in compliance with the HMA-EMA/ENCePP and with all applicable local and international laws and regulations (e-Appendix 2).

Study design BACS: the BACS quantifies the ease of access to biologic therapies for severe asthma, with higher scores indicating easier access.23 Outcomes: nearest available to 1-year post-biologic initiation included assessments within the period 24–80 weeks post for lung-function and asthma control, 48–80 weeks for exacerbation rate, and 24–52 weeks for LTOCS use.

Abbreviations: BACS, Biologic Accessibility Score; Bx, biologic; ISAR, International Severe Asthma Registry; LTOCS, long-term oral corticosteroid; OPCRD, Optimum Patient Care Research Database.

Patients

Patients with SA were required to be aged ≥18 years at biologic initiation, have documented proof of biologic initiation (including initiation date), a documented BACS for the biologic they initiated, and data available for at least one asthma outcome. Within CHRONICLE and ISAR, SA was defined as receiving treatment at Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2018 Step 5 or with uncontrolled asthma at GINA Step 4.26 Uncontrolled asthma was defined as having SA symptoms or frequent exacerbations (≥2/year) requiring OCS. SA was similarly defined within OPCRD concerning GINA treatment steps for patients prescribed a biologic, with at least one year of data prior to the biologic initiation date. Uncontrolled asthma in OPCRD was defined as in the other data sources and also included poor asthma control scores and/or impaired lung function (ie, percent predicted forced expiratory volume in the first second [ppFEV1] or percent predicted peak expiratory flow rate [ppPEFR] <80%). BACS were derived in 2021, following guidelines at that time. Patients who initiated their first biologic after 2022 were excluded from this study to avoid the risk of BACS criteria becoming out of date and the influence of other biologics being introduced.

Biologic Accessibility Score and Asthma Outcomes

The BACS is a composite score including 10 biologic prescription criteria, each with a maximum score of 10 points, referenced to European Medicines Agency marketing authorization specifications; a higher score reflects easier access (Table 1).23 The access or accessibility to SA biologics investigated in our study, therefore, refers to prescription criteria, and not to conditions or barriers to access health services in each country. A higher BACS may be considered a proxy for earlier biologic initiation because it reflects how easily patients can qualify for and obtain biologic therapy within a healthcare system. Countries with higher BACS generally have fewer reimbursement restrictions, broader eligibility criteria, and lower administrative barriers, which may allow patients to commence biologics earlier in the course of SA rather than after prolonged disease burden or repeated exacerbations. Consequently, easier biologic accessibility may facilitate more timely treatment escalation and reduce delays associated with specialist referral, approval processes, or corticosteroid dependence. Full details may be accessed at https://www.jaci-inpractice.org/article/S2213-2198(21)01454-9/fulltext. and are summarized in Table 1.23 BACS are only available at the country level. The score varied between different biologics within each country, so the most easily accessible biologic (ie highest BACS) within each country was used across all available biologics in 2021 to represent the ease of biologic access in that country. Our assumption was that patients would be offered a biologic if they met the criteria for the most easily available biologic.

Clinical outcomes were assessed 1-year pre-biologic and as close as possible to 1-year post-biologic initiation (Figure 1). A 1-year follow-up period was considered sufficient to evaluate outcomes (ie remission, exacerbations, symptom control, lung function, and long-term oral corticosteroid (LTOCS) use) as clinically meaningful responses to biologic therapy are typically observed within the first 12 months of treatment. This timeframe is also commonly used in SA studies to assess treatment efficacy, effectiveness and disease stability.2,33,34

Remission (defined across 2, 3, and 4 domains) was the primary outcome variable (Table 1). Secondary outcome variables included exacerbations, asthma control, lung function, and LTOCS use (Table 1).

Statistical Analyses

The statistical analysis plan was pre-defined. R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Stata v18 (StataCorp., College Station, TX) were used.35 Patient characteristics were summarized, by setting (individual countries within ISAR plus the OPCRD or CHRONICLE data sources), using means (standard deviation, SD), medians (interquartile range, IQR), or numbers (percent of non-missing values). Variables are described in e-Table 1. Associations between BACS and the proportion of patients with a favorable asthma outcome in each setting were tested using Pearson’s correlation. Countries included in different data sources were considered as different settings (eg UK ISAR and UK OPCRD). Patients with insufficient data to assess the outcomes of interest were excluded from the analyses. A favorable outcome was defined as the proportion of patients 1-year post-biologic with: 2-, 3-, and/or 4-domain remission; no exacerbations; well- or partly controlled asthma; ppFEV1 or ppPEFR ≥80%; or no need for LTOCS use. The analyses examined only unadjusted correlations, adjusting for country size, but not for other potential confounders, including country-level socioeconomic status, gross domestic product (GDP), individual healthcare systems, and access. Investigation of mechanisms by which countries come to have different BACS, or the relative contribution of BACS and other socio-economic factors, was beyond the scope of the current study. Pearson’s correlations were weighted using the inverse of the variance of each estimate. This applied greater weight to settings for which the proportion of patients with a favorable outcome could be estimated more accurately (ie, settings in which the estimated proportions had lower variance due to larger sample sizes and/or values closer to 0 or 1. Each of the outcomes used in the correlation tests was tested for bivariate normality with BACS. Leave-one-out analysis was used to confirm whether any individual setting had undue influence on the associations observed. Correlation coefficients were categorized as moderate (r = 0.40–0.59), strong (r = 0.60–0.79), or very strong (r ≥ 0.80).36 As a sensitivity analysis, the mean BACS of the biologic actually initiated by each patient was calculated for each country and used in the correlation analyses in place of the highest BACS per country.

P values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients

Of a total of 16,463 eligible patients (N=3,020, N=12,220, and N=1,223 from CHRONICLE, ISAR, and OPCRD, respectively), 9,183 were included in the analysis (e-Table 2). Exclusions were due to missing biologic initiation date, missing BACS (ie, not included in original BACS publication),23 biologic initiation date after 2022 and missing outcomes data (e-Table 2).

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics (Association Analysis)

The majority of patients were female (63.1%), of White race (79.7%), overweight/obese (72.7%), and never-smokers (65.6%). The median [IQR] age at study entry was 55 [44, 64] years, and the median [IQR] age of asthma onset was 30 [12, 46] years (Table 2 and Table 3). In the year before biologic initiation, 57.7% of patients had experienced ≥2 exacerbations, 64.5% had uncontrolled asthma, and 59.2% had impaired lung function (ie, ppFEV1 or ppPEFR <80%) (Table 3). Most patients had elevated biomarker concentrations indicative of T2-high disease (ie, IgE IU/mL ≥75: 70.8%; fractional exhaled nitric oxide ≥25 ppb: 61.7%; and blood eosinophil count ≥300 cells/µL: 73.5%) (Table 3). Approximately 1/3 of patients had used LTOCS in the past year at a mean (SD) daily dose of 8.5 (8.6) mg. Anti–interleukin (IL)-5/5R was the most frequently prescribed biologic class (54.0%), followed by anti-immunoglobulin E (IgE) (33.8%) and anti–IL-4Rα (12.1%). There was some variability in baseline data observed between datasets; notably, a greater tendency for patients to: be overweight/obese, have a lower exacerbation rate, be less likely to have persistent airflow obstruction, more likely to have received leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA) or long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) add-on to ICS/LABA therapy, and to be treated with anti-IgE in the US-specific CHRONICLE study compared with ISAR and OPCRD (Table 2 and Table 3).

Association Between BACS and Proportion of Positive Outcomes

Higher BACS was associated with a higher probability of achieving remission. The correlation was very strong for 2-domain (r=0.81; p<0.001; Figure 2A) and 3domain with lung function remission (r=0.81; p<0.001; Figure 2C), and strong for 3-domain with asthma control (r=0.67; p=0.001; Figure 2B) and 4-domain remission (r=0.71; p<0.001; Figure 2D) a year after starting biologics. Leave-one-out analyses confirmed that these correlations were not strongly dependent on the result from any one setting (e-Table 3). Correlations were also tested between BACS and the individual components of remission. Higher BACS was strongly correlated with no exacerbations (r=0.68; p<0.001; Figure 3A), and moderately correlated with absence of lung function impairment (r=0.58; p=0.004; Figure 3C), having well- or partly controlled asthma (r=0.42; p=0.047; Figure 3B) and non-use of LTOCS (0.42; p=0.045; Figure 3D) 1 year post-biologic initiation. In a sensitivity analysis using the mean BACS of the biologic actually initiated by each patient within each country, similar correlations with outcomes were observed (e-Table 4).

Discussion

Global disparities in access to asthma biologics are well-documented.23,38 Our study examined the association of variable biologic access on asthma outcomes, and the likely implications for asthma management with easier access and presumably earlier biologic initiation in a large, global, pooled patient population. This is the first study to report an association between ease of biologic access (ie higher BACS) and improved post-biologic asthma outcomes for patients with SA in real-life. Easier biologic access was associated with a significantly greater likelihood of achieving on-treatment clinical remission (all definitions assessed), experiencing no exacerbations, having a ppFEV1 or ppPEFR ≥80%, having well- or partly controlled asthma and having no need for LTOCS. This latter finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the LTOCS outcome did not meet bivariate normality assumptions, because relatively few patients remained on LTOCS one year after biologic initiation. Considering the current benchmark of biologic efficacy/effectiveness when introduced later in the disease process (eg, achievement of 3- or 4-domain remission in approximately 20–35% of patients and reduction of exacerbation rate and LTOCS both by 50%),2,39–41 more equitable access and timely initiation could “move the needle” for these outcomes, unlocking the true potential of biologics and providing patients with the best possible outcomes in keeping with a precision-based management strategy – the right treatment to the right patient AND at the right time. For low-access countries, our findings highlight the need for policies that improve affordability and equitable access, such as expanding reimbursement schemes, simplifying eligibility criteria, strengthening specialist SA services, and improving access to biomarker testing and referral pathways.

Evidence from other chronic inflammatory diseases suggests that earlier biologic treatment is associated with improved outcomes. In rheumatoid arthritis, studies have consistently demonstrated a “window of opportunity,” whereby earlier initiation of biologic or disease-modifying therapy is associated with improved remission rates, reduced radiographic progression, better physical function, and less irreversible joint damage.42 Similarly, in atopic dermatitis, earlier use of targeted biologic therapies has been associated with more rapid disease control, improved symptom burden, and reduced long-term inflammatory complications, supporting the concept that early intervention may alter disease trajectory and improve long-term outcomes.43 However, defining optimal timing for biologic initiation in asthma is more challenging because of disease heterogeneity and variability in disease trajectories. We decided to approach the question from a non–time-based perspective using BACS as a surrogate measure of biologic accessibility. BACS has already been calculated for five biologics (ie, omalizumab, benralizumab, reslizumab, mepolizumab, and dupilumab) in 28 countries across five continents, captures prescription eligibility criteria across 10 domains, and is anchored to European Medicines Agency regulatory information.

Although evidence supporting earlier biologic intervention in asthma remains limited, several studies have identified predictors of biologic response and remission that indirectly support earlier treatment initiation. Predictors associated with better outcomes, suggestive of shorter time to biologic initiation, include shorter asthma duration, more preserved lung function, and shorter exposure to OCS.2,39,44–48 For example, prolonged OCS exposure (>21 months) before biologic initiation was associated with a 71% reduced odds of achieving OCS elimination post-biologic compared to those exposed to OCS for ≤12 months.44 Others demonstrated higher remission rates in those with recent onset SA (32%) compared to those with chronic SA (17%), characterized by longer disease duration.48 Similarly, those with an asthma duration <10 years pre-biologic were >3 times more likely to achieve biologic super-response (vs those with duration >10 years), and had an equally elevated odds of achieving this super-response if their ppFEV1 was ≥80% pre-biologic.45 The association of shorter asthma duration and better pre-biologic lung function with better post-biologic outcomes was even more apparent in a small study of Japanese patients with SA, which found 10 times greater odds of achieving clinical remission post-biologic for those with an asthma duration ≤5 years.47 A more recent study from ISAR found that the odds of achieving 4-domain remission decreased by 15% for every additional 10 years of asthma duration (odds ratio: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.73, 1.00), and increased by 29% for every 5% greater ppFEV1 before biologic initiation.2

Earlier biologic initiation may improve asthma outcomes by interrupting inflammatory pathways before irreversible airway changes occur. Current evidence suggests that biologics may reduce airway hyper-responsiveness,49 slow accelerated lung function decline,50,51 and potentially prevent or attenuate airway remodelling.52 Our observed association between easier biologic access and higher probability of preserved lung function 1 year post-biologic initiation supports this hypothesis. Biologics can produce rapid effects on glucocorticoid-unresponsive inflammation within days or weeks of starting treatment,53,54 such as improvements in symptoms and lung function, and reduction in inflammation and structural changes, including small airway obstruction and mucus plugging.52 Emerging evidence also suggests that biologics may influence longer term structural changes, including basement membrane thickening,55 airway smooth muscle hypertrophy/hyperplasia,56,57 and neuronal remodelling,58 suggesting a potential for reversing or ameliorating established airway remodelling.52 In addition, biologics may help prevent progression to chronic asthma,59 development of secondary structural changes,60 progressive loss of pulmonary function,61 long-term dependence on ICS and OCS therapy62,63 and the development of new-onset OCS-related adverse outcomes in patients with SA.7 Collectively, these findings suggest that although biologics are beneficial when introduced at any time (eg, even at the latter part of the asthma disease process as currently recommended),24 their utility may even be higher when introduced in the early stages of remodelling, and even earlier, before remodelling has occurred at all.

Our study has some limitations. As an ecological study, the analysis was adjusted for country size, but not for other potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, GDP, individual healthcare systems, and access. This may have limited individual-level inference. However, previous research found no correlation between GDP and BACS,23 suggesting that GDP was unlikely to confound our findings. The ecological design of this study also meant that observed associations should not be interpreted as causal, as analyses were performed at the population rather than the individual patient level. Observed associations may be influenced by unmeasured confounding factors or country-level differences, and may not necessarily apply to individual patients (ie, the ecological fallacy). The BACS is also country-specific, not validated and may change over time as new biologic availability and reimbursement criteria evolve. However, the association of higher BACS with better asthma outcomes shown in the current study lends some validity to the score as a biologic accessibility metric. Moreover, the scores used in this study were from 2021, which is close to the median date for patients initiating a biologic in OPCRD, but slightly later than the median dates for the patients in ISAR and CHRONICLE (2019 and 2018, respectively). Regular BACS updates are planned for each country that contributes data to ISAR to ensure its continued utility and relevancy to future research. Ease of access may also have been greater or less at the time individual patients initiated biologic therapy. The highest BACS was used for each country, which may simplify within-country variability; however, similar correlations with outcomes were observed when mean BACS values for initiated biologics were used. Finally, the BACS does not capture all biologic accessibility issues, such as cost, permitted prescribers, health system factors (eg co-payment schemes or government subsidies) and time to referral to specialist care (if necessary). In the UK, for example, as many as 8% of patients managed in primary care have potential SA, but the vast majority are not referred.64 Other limitations include those common to all observational studies, including inability to infer causality, regression to the mean, referral bias, and missing data (predominantly outcomes and mainly for ISAR and OPCRD).

The study is strengthened by inclusion of a large international cohort of patients with SA from 21 countries and multiple real-world data sources. Merging the CHRONICLE, ISAR, and OPCRD datasets increased statistical power and reduced the influence of individual countries. We also used routinely collected clinical data to define multiple outcomes, including a 4-domain definition of remission. Notwithstanding the limitations of the BACS as outlined above, the BACS is informed by structured reviews of health authority databases and guidelines and input from local prescribers of biologics to verify real-world practice, is based on a comprehensive range of clinical access criteria across 10 domains, and is agreed by consensus at the country level for each available biologic.23 The wide range of BACS values across ISAR countries provided a valuable opportunity to examine associations between biologic accessibility and clinical outcomes across diverse healthcare settings, with the broad range strengthening correlation analyses, enabling assessment across a spectrum of access levels. Future avenues of research could include investigation of whether the association is consistent across phenotypes, endotypes, biologic classes, in the shorter-term and longer-term follow-up periods (eg, beyond 2 years), and a re-evaluation of associations using the most recent BACS (if available). Investigation of whether the correlation of BACS and asthma outcomes is independent of confounders (eg healthcare system and socio-economic status), and confirmation of our findings using other metrics of ease of biologic access is also warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, easier access to biologics for patients with SA has the potential to transform asthma care by increasing the probability of achieving better outcomes, including clinical remission. Earlier integration of biologics within stepwise treatment pathways may be associated with improved outcomes in SA.
