Compartmentalized Liquid Microenvironments of Pickering Emulsion Droplets for Continuous-Flow Catalysis
Authors: Peng Yang, Yahong Yao, Rammile Ettelaie, Yumeng Mi, Nan Xue, Anpeng Hu, Ming Zhang, Hengquan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transitioning catalysis from batch to continuous flow remains a key objective of sustainable chemistry, yet the effective retention of homogeneous catalysts and enzymes under flow conditions is a persistent challenge. This Perspective highlights Pickering emulsion droplet (PED)-based fixed-bed reactors as a modular strategy that enables continuous-flow catalysis by confining homogeneous catalysts and enzymes within particle-stabilized liquid microdomains that can be directly packed into column reactors. Owing to the high structural stability of droplets under flow, their strong confinement capability, and favorable liquid microenvironments that preserve catalytic activity, as well as the near-plug-flow behavior of the packed-bed, PED-based systems fundamentally differ from conventional solid-supported fixed-bed reactors and exhibit enhanced catalytic efficiency and long-term operational stability. We summarize the evolution of PED architectures from simple Pickering emulsion droplets to shell-engineered microcapsules, multicompartmentalized biomimetic microreactors, and mechanically reinforced supraparticles. Applications in enzymatic catalysis, enzyme–cofactor systems, chemoenzymatic cascades, homogeneous catalysis, and photo- and electrocatalysis are discussed. Finally, we outline future directions toward establishing a theoretical framework for PED-based continuous-flow systems, advancing operando characterization, expanding applications in pharmaceutical continuous manufacturing, and constructing PED-based biomimetic cell factories for carbon upgrading. PED-based fixed-bed systems thus emerge as a versatile platform for sustainable chemical manufacturing.

Introduction

Continuous-flow catalysis has emerged as an important and widely applied platform for the sustainable and green synthesis of fine chemicals, pharmaceutical intermediates, and specialty chemicals. − Compared with traditional batch processes, continuous-flow systems allow reactants to be continuously fed into the reactors, enabling uninterrupted production. This operational mode offers significant advantages, such as enhanced mass and heat transfer, improved catalytic efficiency, precise control over reaction parameters, and facile separation of products from catalysts. More importantly, continuous-flow catalysis provides superior operational stability and scalability, making it particularly attractive for large-scale industrial manufacturing.

Currently, a variety of continuous-flow reactor configurations have been developed, including continuous stirred tank reactors (CSTRs), fixed-bed reactors, fluidized-bed reactors, and microfluidic systems (Figure A). However, to enable efficient catalyst recovery and reuse, most continuous-flow catalytic processes rely on solid-supported catalysts. In such systems, metal catalysts, homogeneous catalysts, and enzymes are typically immobilized onto solid supports through methods such as impregnation, physical adsorption, covalent bonding, or spatial confinement. Despite significant advances, these strategies still present intrinsic limitations, particularly for homogeneous catalysts and enzymes. Weak interactions between catalytically active centers and the solid supports may lead to catalyst leaching under flow conditions, whereas strong binding or rigid immobilization can restrict their conformational flexibility, thereby diminishing catalytic performance. More critically, biocatalysts require a physiological liquid microenvironment to maintain their native structure and activity, and efficient strategies for the coimmobilization of enzymes and their cofactors remain lacking. , To date, realizing both high activity and long-term stability for homogeneous catalysts and enzymes remains a major challenge in continuous-flow catalysis.

Schematic illustration of conventional continuous-flow reactors and PED-based fixed-bed reactors. (A) Conventional reactor configurations. (B) Pickering emulsion droplet (PED)-based fixed-bed reactors.

To address these limitations, Pickering emulsion droplet (PED)-based fixed-bed reactors, constructed by packing PEDs into a column, have emerged as a promising alternative. Pickering emulsions are solid particle-stabilized microcompartments featuring robust and tunable oil–water interfaces. Compared with conventional surfactant-stabilized systems, they offer superior structural stability and effective confinement of homogeneous catalysts and enzymes while simultaneously providing a suitable liquid microenvironment. Benefiting from their well-defined interfacial architecture and confined spaces, PEDs further enable spatial confinement and coimmobilization of single enzyme, multienzymes, enzyme–cofactor pairs, as well as chemoenzymatic systems within a single- or multicompartmental structures. More importantly, long-term continuous-flow catalysis with high catalytic efficiency (CE) has been demonstrated to be feasible by directly packing PEDs into a column reactor. Here, CE refers to the catalytic efficiency metric introduced for PED-based systems, defined as the moles of reactant converted per mole of catalyst per hour (mol·mol–1·h–1) for homogeneous catalysts and as the specific activity (U·mg–1) for enzymatic systems. As reactants pass through the packed column, they interact with catalysts at the oil–water interface or within the confined droplet interior, where catalytic transformations occur (Figure B). By regulating the spatial distribution and assembly of catalysts at the interface or within the droplet microdomains, and by precisely controlling the droplet structure and microreactor parameters, both reaction kinetics and molecular diffusion can be effectively tuned. This coordinated regulation of reaction and mass transfer enhances the efficiency and controllability of continuous-flow catalysis, highlighting the potential of PED-based fixed-bed reactors as a versatile catalytic platform.

This Perspective provides a comprehensive overview of PED-based fixed-bed systems, elucidating the fundamental principles underpinning their stability, confinement effects, and flow behavior, as well as the theoretical framework established for droplet-based fixed-bed catalysis. Beyond summarizing established archetypes, we trace the structural evolution of these microreactors, from simple liquid compartments to hierarchically engineered shells and multicompartmental architectures. We further highlight their successful implementation in continuous-flow synthesis encompassing enzymatic catalysis, enzyme–cofactor systems, chemoenzymatic transformations, homogeneous catalysis, and PED-based photo- and electrocatalysis. Finally, we outline the future trajectory of this field, envisioning a shift from droplet-based microreactors toward synthetic cell factories that function as robust, self-sustaining, and programmable catalytic units capable of orchestrating complex cascade reactions under continuous-flow conditions.

Stability, Confinement Effect, and Flow Behavior

As a novel catalytic platform, the PED-based fixed-bed reactor is characterized by three pivotal features: structural stability, effective catalyst confinement, and favorable flow behavior. The reactor’s stability originates from the robust, particle-stabilized emulsion droplets, which function as stationary pseudosolid phases within the column. This architecture creates numerous confined microspaces, effectively immobilizing catalysts (whether enzymes or homogeneous catalysts) within their liquid compartments to prevent leaching under continuous-flow conditions. Furthermore, these confined spaces also provide enzymes and homogeneous catalysts with the micrometer-scale motional freedom, thereby preserving the steric flexibility and conformational dynamics required for optimal catalytic performance. Moreover, the fluid passing through these packed droplets exhibits near-plug-flow behavior. This characteristic ensures well-defined residence times and maintains steady-state operation by minimizing disturbances caused by fluid back-mixing.

Displacement of particles from an interface involves significant additional energy dissipation. Even if the process takes place very slowly (quasi stationary), it has been shown that the dissipated energy can be several times that estimated by eq for realistic particle to droplet size rations. This consideration applies to both cases involving a moving contact line and that with a pinned contact line. − When a particle is displaced from its equilibrium position, the interface between the two liquid phases is distorted. The liquid neck formed between the particle and the surface has a substantial amount of stored interfacial energy. This energy is thus dissipated as the particle is finally detached from the surface, allowing the liquid neck to relax back. Such consideration only makes the energy needed to dislodge the particles even greater than that given by eq , thus effectively aiding the stability of the Pickering emulsions. For this reason, we shall not discuss the issue further, though for problems involving the demulsification of Pickering emulsions (e.g., during retrieval of the catalyst), it should not be overlooked. It is noteworthy that the detachment energy (ΔG det) is equal to the sum of three contributions, namely, the oil–solid, water–solid, and oil–water interfacial Gibbs free energies. Note that the physical process of removing a nanoparticle from the interface is not associated with any chemical reactions or breaking of covalent bonds. As a typical example, the required thermodynamic detachment energy (ΔG det) to remove a silica particle (r = 10 nm) from the toluene–water interface phase (γow = 0.036 N·m–1) into the water (or oil) bulk phase is about 2700 k B T (Figure A). This value is much larger than the chemical bond energy (several hundreds of k B T) and intermolecular interaction (several k B T), where the k B and T are the Boltzmann constant and temperature, respectively. Note that the energy of the detachment of the particles from the surface of droplets varies as the square of the radius of the particles. Hence, for very small particles, this energy can become low enough to allow for the spontaneous detachment of the particles. If so, then this can lead to the instability of the Pickering emulsion. This is particularly the case where the continuous phase is a dielectric, nonaqueous medium. For such systems, the electrostatic repulsive forces are far smaller and less effective in keeping the droplets apart. Nonetheless, the particle radius has to be substantially smaller than those considered here to cause such issues, and therefore the Pickering emulsion is expected to be very stable. Hence, this adsorption can be regarded as irreversible adsorption, guaranteeing the structural integrity of static individual Pickering droplet.

It should be noted that the force analysis presented here is intended only as a simplified analysis to illustrate the force balance between particle retention and the drag force exerted by the flowing fluid. The drag force is approximated using the classical O’Neill model, which was originally developed for an isolated rigid sphere under creeping-flow conditions. In practical PED-based continuous-flow systems, however, finite particle coverage, interparticle interactions, interface curvature, and droplet deformation may all influence the local hydrodynamic forces and interfacial stability. Therefore, this simplified analysis should not be regarded as a rigorous quantitative model for predicting droplet stability under all operating conditions. A more comprehensive understanding of particle retention and droplet stability will require future studies that account for the coupling between flow behavior, droplet deformation, and interfacial properties.

Confinement of Catalysts within Droplets

In Pickering emulsion systems, catalysts can be confined within the droplets or at the interface of the oil–water droplets. This confinement not only creates a highly controlled microenvironment for catalytic reactions but also ensures the freedom of motion for catalysts. Typically, the droplets act as microreactors where homogeneous catalysts and enzymes freely interact with reactants within a confined liquid domain, with minimal catalyst loss or deactivation. This feature is analogous to confinement-enabled pseudohomogeneous catalysts. Theoretical calculations prove that this confinement effect depends on the partition coefficients (K w/o) of homogeneous catalysts within oil and water. The K w/o is defined as the ratio of the catalyst concentration in the water phase to its concentration in the oil phase. A higher K w/o leads to a greater distribution of the homogeneous catalyst into the water phase, suggesting that the water-in-oil droplets exhibit the stronger confinement effect to homogeneous catalysts in the continuous fluid. Furthermore, enzymes can be effectively immobilized within the PED system. The stable microenvironment created by the droplets ensures that the enzyme remains active and stable for prolonged periods, allowing for repeated use and increased reusability compared with traditional batch systems.

Unlike traditional immobilization strategy using (in-)­organic solid materials, − the PED confines homogeneous catalysts and enzymes into individual droplets, fully preserving their micrometer-scale mobility within each of these droplets. This feature preserves the inherent spatial effects of homogeneous catalysts and enzymes. Packing these droplets into a fixed-bed reactor constructs a hierarchical system that couples stationary confinement with dynamic transport across three distinct scales: macroscopic flow through the catalytic fixed bed, mesoscale stationary droplets composing this fixed bed, and molecular-scale catalyst motion within any single droplet.

Flow Behavior and Flow Rate Regulation

The steady state of the entire PED fixed-bed reactor system is heavily dependent on the flow behavior. The flow behavior of PED-based fixed-bed reactors has been investigated by RTD measurements using Sudan I as a tracer (Figure C,D). The resulting RTD curve exhibits a narrow and nearly symmetrical distribution with minimal tailing, and the concentration of Sudan I rapidly reached a maximum value before leveling off, indicating limited axial dispersion and flow characteristics approaching those of an ideal plug-flow reactor (Figure E). This behavior can be attributed to the relatively uniform packing of PEDs and the homogeneous interstitial flow paths generated within the packed bed. The RTD results therefore provide experimental support for describing PED-based fixed-bed reactors as exhibiting near-plug-flow behavior. However, it should be noted that this behavior has been demonstrated under specific operating conditions and should not be regarded as a universal characteristic of all PED systems. Future studies incorporating quantitative Peclet number analysis and broader operating conditions will further improve our understanding of transport phenomena in these systems. This favorable flow behavior guarantees the two key points: (1) uniform residence time of reactants over fixed droplets and (2) the entire flow phase without the disturbances induced by fluid back-mixing. In terms of the entire PED-based fixed-bed reactor systems, these key points ensure the dynamic equilibrium of reactions and the force balance of fixed droplets in flow fluid.

The flow rate in a PED fixed-bed reactor plays a crucial role in influencing the reaction rate and mass-transfer efficiency. A critical relationship between the compartmentalized droplet size and the oil phase flow rate was established, providing a fundamental principle for accurately tuning the flow rate in PED fixed-bed reactor systems. Based on the behavior in conventional packed beds, , the volumetric flow rate (V) under the influence of the gravitational field is given by eq 4 V=ε3D2Sρgh1180(1−ε)2μh2 in which ε is the voidage of the packed column, D is the droplet diameter, S is the cross-sectional area of the column, ρ is the oil density, g is the acceleration due to gravity, μ is the oil viscosity, h 1 is the height of the oil phase (above the Pickering emulsion), and h 2 is the height of the Pickering emulsion.

The flow rate, as described by this equation, exhibits a positive dependence on the droplet diametera parameter that is readily tuned via the emulsifier concentration and shear force during the emulsion process, thereby establishing it as a key variable for precise flow control in this novel catalytic reactor platform. Nevertheless, PEDs are fundamentally soft-matter entities rather than rigid particles. Therefore, the application of conventional packed-bed correlations should be considered within the assumptions under which they were developed. At elevated flow velocities, hydrodynamic stresses may induce droplet deformation, potentially altering bed porosity, pressure drop, flow patterns, and the effective liquid–liquid interfacial area available for mass transfer and reaction. Conversely, at very low flow rates, axial dispersion, stagnant zones, bypassing, and wall-induced channeling may become increasingly important. Understanding the interplay between droplet mechanics, transport phenomena, and reactor hydrodynamics therefore remains an important consideration for the future development and scale-up of PED-based continuous-flow systems.

Despite these hydrodynamic complexities, the exceptional stability of Pickering emulsions remains one of the key features that distinguishes PED systems from conventional surfactant-stabilized liquid–liquid reactors. For typical nanoparticles employed in PED systems, the desorption energy required to detach particles from the liquid–liquid interface is often several orders of magnitude greater than thermal energy (k B T), providing a robust thermodynamic basis for maintaining compartmentalization during continuous-flow operation. At the same time, reaction-induced changes in the composition of the continuous and dispersed phases may influence interfacial tension, particle wettability, and local transport properties. Although such effects are generally expected to have a limited influence on particle desorption because of the large thermodynamic barrier involved, their impact on droplet stability, flow behavior, and long-term reactor operation remains an important topic for future investigation.

Theoretical Models for PED-Based Fixed-Bed Reactors

Rational design of PED fixed-bed reactors requires a thorough understanding of the interplay between molecular diffusion rate and catalytic reaction rate within individual droplets. Unlike macroscopic batch reactors, where bulk mixing dominates, PED systems operate in a regime in which diffusion and reaction are intrinsically coupled inside confined droplet microreactors. In particular, the diffusion within droplets continues to be a limiting factor. To this end, our detailed mathematical analysis of diffusion–reaction equations inside the droplet, coupled with the diffusion outside the droplet, found in our previous work, clearly demonstrates this limiting role of the diffusion occurring within the droplets. It is also through these same calculations where a precisely defined length scale (ζ), and hence a limited shell of active reaction zone within the droplets, emerges. Previous studies have established theoretical models describing both single-step and cascade reactions within a single droplet, providing a quantitative framework to predict the coupling and matching between mass transfer and reaction kinetics and to guide the rational design of these systems.

Single-Step Reaction

The concentration distribution equations of reactants and products within the emulsion droplets are established (Figure A). On the basis of the equations, the profile of concentration to distance from the droplet center is depicted. The concentration of reactants is maximum at the droplet interface and gradually decreases toward the droplet center. Conversely, the concentration of the product gradually decreases from the center to the interface of the droplet. This result demonstrates that the reactant diffusion into droplets of and the product diffusion out of droplets arise from the concentration gradient, constructing the overall flow process from flow fluid into droplets and then back to flow fluid. This continuous-flow process ensures that the reaction keeps away from the equilibrium state and the PED fixed-bed reactor maintains steady state.

Single-step reaction is first in need of the diffusion of reactants from the flow fluid to catalysts within droplets. Therefore, the matched diffusion rate and reaction rate are very important. On the basis of this cognition, a vital parameter of the critical reaction depth (ζ) is established. Consider a molecule of the product in a simple reaction of type A + B ↔ C, formed at a distance y inside the droplet away from its surface. This molecule will typically take a time ∼y 2/D to get to the surface and enter the continuous oil phase. Here D is the diffusion coefficient of the molecule in the droplet. Now, the time scale for this product molecule to survive and not disintegrate back to A and B is ∼1/K bk, where K bk is the reaction rate coefficient for the backward reaction. Thus, if y 2/D ≫ 1/K bk (i.e., y ≫ (D/K bk)1/2), then molecule C would not be able to get out. Similarly, a molecule of A (for now assuming a far higher excess of reactant B inside the droplet, so that this remains roughly uniform at [B]0 in the droplet) will first react and is converted to C, at a time ∼1/([B]K f), thus only diffusing a distance of y ∼(D/[B]K f)1/2 before the first conversion. For the sake of simplicity here the diffusion coefficients of A and C are taken to be very similar. It is apparent that any molecules, residing far deeper than y away from the surface inside, within the core of the droplet, will encounter many backward and forward conversions before they are able to escape. This implies that for distances far larger than y into the droplet, the reaction has reached its equilibrium. Nothing is gained here in this core region, as the rate of backward and forward reactions is in balance and the product is consumed as fast as it is produced. A more accurate expression, obtained through the detailed mathematical analysis of the reaction–diffusion equations inside the droplet, coupled with diffusion outside, is shown to give this length scale as ζ = (D/(K b+[B]0 K f)1/2. This parameter correlates the diffusion kinetic with intrinsic reaction kinetics inside the droplet, representing the depth of reaction occurring inside the droplets away from the interface. Three regimes can be distinguished inside a droplet with a radius of R (1)when R < ζ, some substrates do not have sufficient time to react with catalysts before diffusing out of the droplets, resulting in the lower reaction efficiency.(2)when R > ζ, some inner catalysts approximating the center of the droplet are insufficiently utilized, thereby decreasing the reaction efficiency.(3)when R ≈ ζ, the diffusion rate matches the reaction rate, guaranteeing the maximum reaction efficiency.

Applications in Biocatalysis

Pickering emulsion droplets with well-defined interfacial architectures and confined liquid microenvironments possess key characteristics of natural cellular systems, including spatial compartmentalization, dynamic mass-transfer regulation, and multicomponent organization, making them highly advantageous platforms for biocatalysis, enzyme–cofactor coconfinement, and chemoenzymatic synthesis.

Enzyme Catalysis

Maintaining high enzymatic activity and stability under flow conditions remains a central challenge for conventional biocatalytic systems. PED-based fixed-bed reactors provide a confined liquid microenvironment that preserves enzyme structural integrity and catalytic flexibility, and the tunable interfacial permeability further promotes efficient substrate diffusion and reaction regulation. As a representative example, our group pioneered this strategy by confining Candida antarctica lipase B (CALB) within aqueous droplets of water-in-oil (W/O) Pickering emulsions packed into a column reactor. Under continuous-flow operation, this system enabled highly efficient enzymatic hydrolysis of racemic esters to produce the corresponding chiral alcohol. The system demonstrated exceptional durability, maintaining an enantiomeric excess (ee) of the chiral alcohol consistently above 99% over 2000 h of continuous flow (Figure A). Notably, the catalytic efficiency was found to be directly correlated to the interfacial solid emulsifier coverage and droplet size. Remarkably, the system processed 34 kg of substrate per gram of enzyme, representing a performance benchmark that is difficult to achieve with conventional immobilization strategies.

Building on these findings, the architecture was further evolved to incorporate ionic liquids (ILs) as nonaqueous, enzyme-compatible reaction media (Figure B). Owing to their excellent solvation ability toward organic substrates, IL droplets facilitate the preferential partitioning and sequestration of reactants into the droplet interior. Under continuous-flow conditions, such differential partitioning establishes concentration gradients from the droplet interface toward the interior, with reactants enriched at the oil–liquid interface while products are continuously extracted from the droplets. This dynamic exchange ensures a constant supply of reactants and simultaneous removal of products, thereby effectively alleviating product inhibition. This thermodynamic driving force enables the PED system to operate far from equilibrium during the transesterification of 1-phenylethyl alcohol, achieving a steady-state specific activity of 5.18 U mg–1, corresponding to a 25-fold enhancement compared with the batch counterparts. Moreover, theoretical modeling enabled the establishment of a quantitative relationship between droplet size and catalytic efficiency. Notably, this fixed-bed reactor operated stably for over 4000 h without detectable enzyme leaching, processing 272 kg of substrate per gram of enzyme.

To further improve the practical applicability of PED-based fixed-bed systems, interfacial cross-linking strategies were employed to construct robust microcapsules suitable for operation under high-pressure and chemically demanding conditions. This evolution began with the exploration of sol–gel process to grow porous silica shells directly at the Pickering emulsion interfaces. , This approach proved efficacious in the enzymatic kinetic resolution of alcohols and hydrolysis of chiral esters. The resulting system exhibited remarkable robustness, withstanding pressures of up to 1 MPa and tolerating highly polar media and elevated substrate concentrations (0.4 mol L–1). Stable continuous operation for 1500 h was achieved, significantly surpassing the durability of conventional systems (Figure C). Based on a similar interfacial cross-linking strategy, Qiao and co-workers constructed three-tiered colloidosomes comprising a cross-linked silica–polymer shell, an enzyme-rich sublayer, and an adsorptive silica lumen. The hierarchical, sponge-like interior enriched hydrophobic substrates and enhanced the specific activity under continuous-flow conditions by nearly 6-fold compared with non-cross-linked systems. Moreover, metal–organic framework (MOF) cross-linked microcapsule reactors were developed by inducing nanocrystal nucleation and growth at the Pickering emulsion interface (Figure C). The resulting MOF shell protects the enzymes from external interference while imparting size-selective permeability to the microreactor, enabling continuous-flow operation for over 300 h. Importantly, molecular transport can be finely regulated by tuning shell parameters, thereby enabling shape-selective catalysis under continuous-flow conditions.

Enzyme–Cofactor Coconfinement Catalysis

For many oxidoreductases and transaminases, maintaining efficient cofactor recycling while preventing cofactor leaching remains a central bottleneck in continuous-flow biocatalysis. Conventional coimmobilization strategies often restrict the mutual accessibility between enzymes and cofactors or rely on complex chemical grafting procedures. Interestingly, PED-based fixed-bed systems provide a unique coconfinement approach in which enzymes with intrinsic interfacial activity preferentially adsorb at the oil–water interface of Pickering droplets, while water-soluble cofactors (e.g., NADP+/NADPH or PLP) are retained within the aqueous droplet interior (Figure A). This configuration allows cofactors to freely diffuse within the droplets and dynamically access enzymes localized at the interface. As a result, enzymes and cofactors are effectively coimmobilized without the need for covalent linkage, ionic interactions, or artificial tethering.

Using NADPH-dependent ketone reduction as a representative case, the PED-based coconfined system enabled in situ cofactor regeneration through a coupled oxidation reaction, establishing a self-sufficient catalytic cycle within each microcompartment. Under continuous-flow operation, the system achieved sustained high conversion and enantioselectivity (>99% ee) for over 350 h, with an outstanding total turnover number (TTN) of 59204 mol mol–1 for NADPH (Figure B). Notably, the catalytic efficiency was enhanced by 2.7-fold compared to traditional biphasic reactions (Figure C). Furthermore, a sol–gel strategy to construct Pickering droplet-derived microreactors (PDMRs) is developed for the coconfinement of enzymes and cofactors recently. These PDMRs achieve the high conversion and enantioselectivity (>99% ee) with up to 45552 mol mol–1 TTN for pyridoxal 5-phosphate (PLP)-dependent transaminase (TA)-catalyzed continuous-flow transamination reactions, and any deactivation is observed over 2000 h. Importantly, no additional cofactor supplementation was required during operation, demonstrating effective retention and regeneration within the droplets. Furthermore, a theoretical model was developed to provide a quantitative framework for optimizing reaction efficiency via substrate loading and interfacial transport control. Beyond reductase systems, this coconfinement strategy was also extended to PLP-dependent transaminases. In this case, similar interfacial enzyme localization and cofactor distribution within the interior of the droplets were observed.

Chemoenzymatic Cascade Catalysis

Chemoenzymatic cascade reactions represent one of the most powerful strategies for asymmetric synthesis, yet their practical implementation remains limited by the intrinsic incompatibility between the chemical and biological catalysts. Chemical catalysts can deactivate enzymes, whereas aqueous or proteinaceous environments often poison organometallic active sites. Overcoming this mutual deactivation without sacrificing catalytic efficiency is therefore a central challenge. PED-based fixed-bed reactors provide a modular solution based on spatial orthogonality. By segregating incompatible catalysts into distinct microdomains within droplet microcompartments, these systems spatially isolate mutually incompatible catalytic centers while simultaneously maintaining a close proximity between them, thereby enabling efficient intermediate transfer and cascade catalytic coupling. The earliest demonstration of this concept involved colocalization of different catalytically active subcompartments within droplets of a Pickering emulsion, yielding cell-like microreactors, which can be packed in a column reactor for continuous-flow cascade catalysis (Figure A). This system exhibited catalytic efficiencies 420 times higher than those of the corresponding batch systems and operated stably for 240 h while maintaining >99% enantiomeric excess. Moreover, by regulating the concentration and spatial distribution of catalysts within the confined droplet domains, the kinetics of the cascade reactions could be effectively tuned, thereby facilitating the efficient coupling of multistep transformations under continuous-flow conditions.

(C) Ti­(Salen)-CALB cascade synthesis of chiral O-acylated cyanohydrin in the microsphere and PED copacked system.

To further enhance the robustness of this biomimetic microreactor, multicompartmentalized liquid-containing microreactors (MLMs) were fabricated, in which the internal architectures could be precisely tuned in a bottom-up manner. With this strategy, enzymes and metal catalysts were separately confined within distinct subcompartments of the MLMs for chemoenzymatic cascade reactions. As exemplified by the enzyme-catalyzed kinetic resolution of racemic amine, these systems demonstrated remarkable durability, operating for up to 500 h while maintaining 99% of enantioselectivity (Figure B). Similarly, these concepts were solidified into millimeter-sized supraparticles via liquid marble templates, proving that soft interfaces can be hardened for industrial fixed-bed use. Most recently, this concept was advanced by introducing molecular crowding effects, a hallmark of cellular interiors that is often missing in synthetic reactors. Membraneless coacervate droplets were transformed into robust, multicompartmental hybrid microreactors via an interface-confined sol–gel process. This architecture not only physically segregates incompatible catalysts (e.g., Pd and CALB) but also creates a crowded microenvironment that stabilizes enzyme conformation. In a chemoenzymatic cascade for ketone hydrogenation and kinetic resolution, this system exhibited exceptional durability, operating continuously for 1600 h with >99% ee and achieving a 9.2-fold activity enhancement due to the synergistic confinement and crowding effects. Additionally, interfacial engineering enables oxidative transformations that are typically forbidden in biological settings due to enzyme deactivation. Recent work introduced an engineered “interphase” at the Pickering emulsion interface, consisting of a porous, nanometer-thick silica shell that serves as a scaffold for enzyme incorporation. Within this “interphase”, enzymes can simultaneously be in contact with the enzyme-preferred aqueous microenvironment and the oil phase containing organic reactants. The porous “interphase” with its tunable structure and properties allows modulation of transport of reactants, crudely akin to a cell membrane, and of local concentration of reactants (e.g., H2O2). Impressively, this system achieved long-term stability over 800 h in chemoenzymatic alkene epoxidation, delivered a 16-fold enhancement in catalytic efficiency compared with batch reactions, and realized 99% H2O2 utilization efficiency.

More challenge is that homogeneous catalysts and enzymes typically require specific liquid reaction media, whereas heterogeneous catalysts are usually solid particles. Integrating these three types of catalysts, together with their respective reaction media, into a single reactor for continuous-flow operation represents an ideal green and efficient synthetic paradigm. However, such integration faces substantial technical and mechanistic challenges. To address this challenge, a copacked system of PEDs and solid microspheres (SMs) was developed (Figure C). In this system, enzymes are confined in PEDs, whereas chemical catalysts are immobilized on porous SMs. By tuning the surface hydrophobicity of the SMs, stable coexistence of PEDs and SMs can be achieved. More importantly, the system enables the directional transport of reaction intermediates from SM to neighboring PED microreactors. This is because the ionic liquid within the PEDs can enrich organic substances from the surrounding environment. In the chemoenzymatic synthesis of chiral O-acylated cyanohydrins, this copacked reactor exhibits catalytic efficiencies 7–77 times higher than those of the corresponding batch systems and operates stably for 240 h with >99% ee. In the dynamic kinetic resolution of 1-phenylethanol involving an acid catalyst and lipase, 99% conversion and 99% ee are maintained. These results demonstrate that, by organizing liquid PED microreactors and solid SM microreactors within a single fixed-bed reactor, complex cascade reactions integrating homogeneous catalysts, heterogeneous catalysts, and enzymes can be efficiently realized under continuous-flow conditions, thereby establishing a versatile platform for multicomponent catalytic processes.

Applications in Chemical Catalysis

The unique liquid microenvironment within Pickering emulsion droplets enables homogeneous catalysts to be stably confined inside the droplets while fully retaining their intrinsic molecular activity. This confinement strategy allows highly efficient continuous-flow catalysis while simultaneously facilitating catalyst retention and recyclability. Importantly, it also enables the transformation of conventional water–oil biphasic photo- and electrocatalytic systems into efficient PED-based continuous-flow platforms, overcoming intrinsic mass transfer and low reaction efficiency.

Confinement of Homogeneous Catalysts

In chemical catalysis, commonly used homogeneous catalysts such as inorganic and organic acids or bases, ionic liquids, and metal salts are often viscous, corrosive, or difficult to recycle. The PED-based fixed-bed system enables effective confinement of these liquid or molecular catalysts without altering their intrinsic reactivity. A foundational advance in this domain was achieved by confining sulfuric acid within water-in-oil Pickering emulsion droplets to catalyze the addition of alcohols to 3,4-dihydro-2H-pyran (Figure A). This system maintained a conversion consistently above 90% over 1000 h of continuous operation, exhibiting a catalytic efficiency >10-fold higher than the batch counterpart. Crucially, using this reaction as a model, catalytic efficiency was quantitatively correlated with the droplet interfacial area and particle coverage, fundamentally confirming that the creation of a massive reaction interface and shortened molecular diffusion distances are the primary drivers for intensified reactivity. This strategy was further extended to the confinement of heteropolyacids for the cyclization of citronellal, where the compartmentalized flow not only ensured exceptional durability (>1440 h) but also induced a unique interfacial effect under continuous flow that markedly improved product selectivity. Similarly, IL-based PED fixed-bed reactors were developed for the Mannich condensation of propionaldehyde to methacrolein, a key monomer in acrylic plastic production. , By confining the amine-functionalized ionic liquid ([HDEA]­Ac) within droplets, the system achieved complete conversion and >95% yield, sustaining stable operation for over 150 h and significantly outperforming conventional processes (Figure B).

(B) Mannich condensation of acetone with propionaldehyde. (C) Reduction of 4-hydroxynitrobenzene. (D) Friedel–Crafts acylation. (E) Cascade synthesis of α-alkylated nitriles.

Beyond acid catalysts and ionic liquids, interfacial engineering has also enabled metallic catalytic systems. Silver-decorated cellulose nanofibers (ACNF@Ag) stabilized oil-in-water Pickering emulsions through pH-modulated assembly. The entangled nanofiber network formed a mechanically robust interfacial scaffold, enabling stable continuous-flow hydrogenation (Figure C). Exploiting structural anisotropy, poly­(ionic liquid)-functionalized Janus particles were employed to immobilize heteropolyacids at the toluene–IL interface. In continuous Friedel–Crafts acylation, this amphiphilic interfacial catalyst delivered 96% conversion with 99% selectivity toward p-methylacetophenone, markedly surpassing conventional biphasic systems (Figure D).

To bridge droplet architectures and fixed-bed engineering, millimeter-sized ionic supraparticles (ISPs) were fabricated via electrostatic coassembly of nanoparticles within liquid marbles (Figure E). , Unlike fragile droplets, ISPs withstood >50 N per bead, exceeding industrial requirements. The versatile method enabled ternary ISPs and a spatially orthogonal cascade catalyst (acid, base, and metal) for continuous-flow synthesis. The catalyst showed enhanced efficiency from proximate active sites and outstanding stability (200 h). This design combines internal droplet architecture with solid robustness, enabling industrial fixed beds without an excessive pressure drop.

Electrocatalysis and Photocatalysis

In aqueous organic electrosynthesis, the biphasic systems are limited by severe mass-transfer resistance at oil–water interfaces, inefficient intermediate coupling, and poor product separation, particularly under high current densities. The conventional catalytic layer on the electrode surface typically provides insufficient interfacial area, while unstable intermediates are prone to over-reduction or side reactions that form byproducts. Instead, the Pickering emulsion droplet electrode offers a solution by creating a densely interconnected interfacial microreactor network with continuous charge-transfer pathways (Figure A). A representative advance was reported in the electrosynthesis of cyclohexanone oxime using a Pickering emulsion droplet-integrated electrode. , In this system, amphiphilic silver particles simultaneously served as electrocatalysts and emulsifiers, forming an oil-in-water droplet network immobilized on the electrode surface. The droplet interfaces created a localized microenvironment characterized by orientationally ordered interfacial water and weakened hydrogen bonding with organic substrates, which facilitated the coupling between electrochemically generated hydroxylamine and cyclohexanone. As a result, continuous-flow oxime production was achieved at industrially relevant current densities (100 mA cm–2), with a Faradaic efficiency of 83.8%, long-term operational stability over 100 h, and scalable synthesis at the 10 g level. This interfacial engineering strategy was further extended to cascade electrosynthesis in the production of adipic acid from crude cyclohexene. By constructing a Pickering-droplet-integrated electrode with Ni­(OH)2 serving as both catalyst and emulsifier, a localized oil–water interface was created to stabilize bromine radicals and suppress the competing oxygen evolution reaction. This unique interfacial microenvironment enabled the smooth cascade of bromide-assisted epoxidation and subsequent epoxide oxidation within a single continuous-flow system. The process delivered ∼80% Faradaic efficiency, ∼95% selectivity, exceptional durability over 1000 h, and scalability to 100 g production at 100 mA cm–2. These results demonstrate that integrating droplet reactors onto electrodes overcame mass-transfer bottlenecks while enabling efficient continuous-flow electrocatalysis.

Schematic illustration of Pickering emulsion-based continuous-flow electrocatalysis and photocatalysis with representative reaction results. (A) Electrocatalytic synthesis of cyclohexanone oxime and oxidation of cyclohexene. (B) Continuous-flow photocatalytic oxidation of ethylbenzene.

Beyond electrocatalysis, Pickering emulsion interfaces also provide a powerful platform for regulating the generation of reactive oxygen species in photocatalysis processes. In conventional photocatalytic oxidation of C–H bonds, the uncontrolled generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide anion radicals (O2 •–) often compromises the reaction selectivity. To achieve highly selective oxidation of ethylbenzene to acetophenone, our group constructed an interfacial photocatalytic system by positioning BiOBr photocatalysts at the outer side of Pickering emulsion droplet interfaces (Figure B). This system simultaneously suppressed •OH formation and promoted O2 •– generation. The underlying mechanism is attributed to the loose hydrogen bonding at the droplet interface, which increases the dissociation barrier of water molecules and thereby inhibits hydroxyl radical formation, while the oxygen-rich lipophilic solvation environment enhanced •O2 – production. As a result, ethylbenzene oxidation proceeded with >99% conversion and >99% selectivity toward acetophenone under visible light irradiation without the need for sacrificial reagents. Importantly, the system operated continuously for 200 h with stable performance, highlighting the feasibility of translating interfacial photocatalysis into continuous-flow configurations.

Comparison with Alternative Continuous-Flow Technologies

It is important to recognize that PED-based continuous-flow systems are not intended to replace existing continuous-flow technologies universally. Rather, their value lies in addressing a specific gap between the homogeneous and heterogeneous catalytic processes. Conventional solid-supported packed-bed reactors remain the dominant technology for industrial continuous manufacturing because of their mechanical robustness, operational simplicity, and established scale-up strategies. Immobilization frequently alters the local catalytic environment and may affect catalyst activity, particularly for enzymes, cofactors, and homogeneous catalysts. In contrast, PED systems retain catalysts within confined liquid microenvironments, preserving their native catalytic behavior while preventing catalyst loss during the continuous-flow operation.

Beyond conventional packed-bed reactors, a variety of continuous-flow technologies have been developed to address different catalytic and engineering challenges. Membrane reactors provide highly selective reaction–separation integration, whereas segmented-flow microreactors deliver exceptional heat and mass transfer together with precise residence-time control. Similarly, micellar catalytic systems create dynamic aqueous microenvironments through surfactant self-assembly and have emerged as attractive alternatives to conventional organic solvents. Recent sustainability studies have shown, however, that the environmental performance of such systems depends not only on reaction efficiency but also on factors including material synthesis, energy consumption, process integration, and recycling strategies. Therefore, as highlighted in recent technoeconomic and life cycle assessment studies, the sustainability and industrial viability of any reactor technology should be evaluated within the context of the entire process rather than through isolated reaction metrics alone.

Within this broader continuous-flow landscape, PED systems offer distinctive advantages for multiphase catalytic processes involving homogeneous catalysts, enzymes, cofactors, and multicatalytic cascade reactions that benefit from spatial compartmentalization and controlled molecular transport. At the same time, challenges associated with droplet fabrication, emulsification energy input, pressure drop management, and large-scale reactor implementation remain important areas for future development. Consequently, PED-based systems should be viewed not as universal alternatives to existing reactor technologies but as complementary platforms whose greatest potential lies in applications requiring simultaneous catalyst confinement, compartmentalization, and continuous-flow operation.

Outlook and Future Directions

PED-based continuous-flow catalysis demonstrates significant potential by enabling structured liquid microenvironments to operate stably under continuous-flow conditions, overcoming a longstanding limitation of conventional solid-supported systems. Through the stabilization of oil–water interfaces with solid nanoparticles, these architectures transform liquid–liquid systems into spatially organized catalytic compartments capable of hosting homogeneous catalysts, enzymes, cofactors, and even multiple catalytic active centers within a single reactor. Such compartmentalization preserves the intrinsic molecular properties of homogeneous and enzymatic catalysts while maintaining the liquid environments essential for their activity and stability. At the same time, it endows the system with enhanced mechanical robustness and operational continuity that are required for industrial processing. The structural evolution from simple Pickering emulsion droplets to shell-engineered microcapsules, multicompartmentalized biomimetic microreactors, and mechanically reinforced supraparticles underscores the versatility of PED-based architectures. Collectively, these developments have expanded the scope of continuous-flow catalysis to include homogeneous catalysis, enzymatic transformations, enzyme–cofactor systems, chemoenzymatic cascade reactions, and emerging photo- and electrocatalytic processes.

Future challenges and opportunities for PED-based continuous-flow catalysis lie at the convergence of mechanistic elucidation, advanced in situ and operando characterization, and industrial implementation. From a scientific perspective, a central task is to establish quantitative correlations between droplet architecture and catalytic performance. Although an enlarged interfacial area and shortened diffusion distances are frequently invoked to rationalize enhanced catalytic efficiency, the specific contributions of interfacial polarity gradients, selective permeability, hydrogen-bond networks, and the internal droplet microenvironment remain insufficiently resolved. Clarifying these contributions is essential for understanding how reactive intermediates are generated, stabilized, transported, and selectively transformed within confined droplet domains. Such mechanistic resolution is particularly important in the asymmetric synthesis of pharmaceutical intermediates and in the selective production of high-value chemicals, where even subtle variations in interfacial and spatial microenvironments can profoundly influence the catalytic activity, enantioselectivity, catalyst longevity, and space–time yield.

To achieve this level of mechanistic resolution, advanced in situ and operando characterization techniques must be incorporated into PED-based catalytic systems to capture droplet structural evolution under flow together with the diffusion and variations of reactive intermediates. On the structural level, it is essential to capture droplet deformation, interfacial reconstruction, and shell permeability changes during the operation. Techniques such as operando optical microscopy, in situ confocal laser scanning microscopy, and dynamic light scattering can provide direct insight into the droplet size distribution, interfacial stability, and structural integrity under realistic pressure and flow conditions. At the molecular scale, resolving diffusion dynamics, spatial localization, and redox-state evolution of reactive intermediates is essential for linking the structure to reaction kinetics. Operando Raman and infrared spectroscopy enable identification of transient intermediates and monitoring of hydrogen-bond interactions, while fluorescence spectroscopy and high-resolution fluorescence imaging allow visualization of molecular diffusion pathways and the dynamic distribution of enzymes, cofactors, and substrates in biocatalytic systems. For redox-driven transformations, in situ electrochemical techniques and time-resolved spectroscopies enable the direct observation of electron transfer pathways and intermediate oxidation states. Such combined structural and chemical diagnostics are indispensable for correlating droplet architecture with reaction kinetics, mass-transfer efficiency, and catalytic selectivity under continuous-flow conditions.

From an application perspective, many potential applications of PED-based continuous-flow systems remain at an early stage of development, yet they highlight promising directions for the future evolution of this emerging reactor platform. Realizing these opportunities will require overcoming a series of engineering challenges associated with industrial implementation and scaling up. These challenges include the large-scale and reproducible fabrication of PED microreactors with uniform droplet size and well-controlled interfacial and internal structural properties as well as the scalable preparation and stabilization of confined catalysts. Reactor scale-up requires careful design of column geometry, packing density, and pressure drop management to ensure mechanical integrity and plug-flow behavior under industrial flow rates. In parallel, optimization of engineering parameters such as RTD, heat and mass-transfer efficiency, substrate concentration, and flow stability is essential to maintaining consistent catalytic performance during prolonged operation. Furthermore, future evaluation of PED-based continuous-flow systems should incorporate standardized engineering metrics, including STY, catalyst productivity, and energy consumption, to enable more quantitative benchmarking against those of established continuous-flow technologies. In addition, successful industrial implementation also requires integration with upstream and downstream processing units, the incorporation of online monitoring and process control systems, and compliance with safety requirements.

From a sustainability perspective, future development of PED-based continuous-flow systems should be evaluated using comprehensive frameworks that extend beyond conventional reaction-level metrics. Although PED reactors offer several advantages, including catalyst retention, enhanced catalytic efficiency, process intensification, and continuous operation, these features alone do not necessarily guarantee overall sustainability. Recent studies have emphasized the importance of integrating life cycle assessment (LCA), technoeconomic analysis (TEA), and planetary-boundary considerations into the evaluation of emerging chemical technologies. − For PED systems, particular attention should be paid to the environmental impacts associated with stabilizer synthesis, droplet fabrication, emulsification energy requirements, and downstream separation processes. In addition, systematic assessment of catalyst lifetime, recyclability, resource utilization, and energy consumption will be essential for determining whether the performance benefits observed at the reactor level translate into genuine environmental advantages at the industrial scale. Therefore, future development of PED-based continuous-flow reactors should combine advances in catalytic performance with integrated sustainability assessments, providing a more rigorous basis for their industrial implementation and long-term environmental impact.

Among potential future applications of PED-based continuous-flow systems, pharmaceutical manufacturing stands out as a particularly promising domain. Many high-value pharmaceutical intermediates arise from asymmetric reductions, transaminations, epoxidations, and dynamic kinetic resolutions that rely on enzyme–cofactor catalytic systems. PED systems, with their confined liquid microenvironments, offer distinct advantages in maintaining enzyme activity and stability while enabling effective enzyme–cofactor coconfinement. In addition, the combination of liquid confinement and continuous-flow operation permits reactions to proceed at high substrate concentrations with reduced product inhibition, thereby enhancing the overall catalytic efficiency. However, to be industrially viable, such systems must demonstrate kilogram-scale productivity, long-term stability beyond thousands of hours, and compatibility with good manufacturing practice standards. Beyond pharmaceutical intermediates, PED architectures may also provide a foundation for the future development of continuous-flow biomimetic cell factories for carbon upgrading (Figure ). A particularly compelling direction is the integration of electrochemical or photochemical CO2 reduction with downstream multienzyme cascades organized across spatially segregated droplet compartments. In such systems, CO2 could first be converted into C1 intermediates such as formate or methanol, which are subsequently upgraded through enzyme-mediated carbon–carbon bond formation into l-lactic acid, rare sugars, or specialty starches. Achieving this level of carbon fixation under continuous flow will require precise regulation of intermediate flux, efficient NAD­(P)H regeneration, ATP/ADP cycling, and protection of sensitive enzymes from oxidative stress. The ability of PED systems to maintain localized liquid environments, accommodate incompatible catalytic species, and control the transport of reactive intermediates may provide a structural framework for the future realization of such biomimetic reactor assemblies.

PED-based continuous-flow systems provide a new framework for integrating liquid microenvironments with fixed-bed reactors. By precise regulation of droplet interfaces and confined microenvironments, these systems bridge the gap between molecular-level high catalytic activity and industrial process robustness. As mechanistic insight, advanced characterization methodologies, and scalable reactor engineering continue to converge, PED-based continuous-flow systems have the potential to evolve from laboratory concepts into practical platforms for sustainable and high-value chemical manufacturing.
