Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on cancer incidence, stage, mortality, and short-term survival in Ireland: a population-based analysis
Authors: A. Brennan, J. McDevitt, H. Heidarian Miri, S. O’Reilly, L. Burke, K. Bennett, M.T. Redaniel, D. Murray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConcern exists that disruptions to health services in combination with changed health-seeking behaviour as a consequence of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic would negatively impact cancer outcomes. The purpose of this study was to assess how the COVID-19 pandemic affected cancer incidence, stage at diagnosis, mortality, and 1-year net survival in Ireland.Materials and methodsAge-standardised incidence and mortality rates were generated for three periods (2018-2019, 2020-2021, and 2022). Quarterly age-standardised incidence rates, incidence rate ratios (IRRs), and percentage change (PC) by cancer site were generated for Q1 2020 to Q4 2021. Annual incidence rates, IRRs and PC by stage at diagnosis were estimated. Age- and stage-adjusted 1-year net survival was estimated using the Pohar–Perme method.ResultsThe age-standardised quarterly incidence rate of all invasive cancers (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer) decreased significantly in Q2 2020, by 27% (95% CI −42% to −7%, P = 0.01), compared with 2018-2019. Decreases in the incidence of early-stage cancers (stages I or II) were seen in 2020, particularly for cancers in which organised or opportunistic screening occurs. There was little evidence of consistent increases in the incidence rate of stage III or IV cancers in 2021 or 2022. Age- and stage-adjusted 1-year net survival for all cancer sites in 2020-2021 remained in line with previous years.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted cancer detection and diagnostic activity in Ireland during 2020. The early overall picture suggests that the resilience and adaptability of the Irish health care system helped to safeguard cancer outcomes during an unprecedented public health crisis.

Introduction

The emergence of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) in early 2020 precipitated unprecedented challenges for health care systems worldwide. In Ireland, a series of escalating public health restrictions—including a nationwide lockdown—were implemented to curtail viral transmission.1 Services were rapidly reorganised to accommodate surges in COVID-19 cases and infection control protocols were strengthened to protect both patients and health care staff.2 This reconfiguration, coupled with widespread staff absenteeism due to infection and quarantine requirements,3 strained the system’s capacity to support ongoing care for non-COVID conditions, including cancer, with most disruption occurring in 2020 and 2021. COVID-19 vaccination commenced in December 2020, and as vaccination coverage increased, public health restrictions were eased. By 2022, virtually all restrictions had been removed.1,4

People living with cancer were at heightened risk of severe COVID-19 illness and mortality,5, 6, 7 adding complexity to treatment decisions and necessitating substantial modifications to care pathways.2 At the same time, individuals without a prior cancer diagnosis faced new barriers to help-seeking. Concerns about virus exposure, reluctance to burden health care providers, and the widespread shift to remote consultations all contributed to a marked reduction in primary care attendance during the early pandemic period.2,8,9 All three national population-based cancer screening programmes in Ireland—BreastCheck, CervicalCheck, and BowelScreen—were also paused for several months.2

By mid-2020, there were early reports of noticeable declines in cancer diagnoses, particularly during the first wave of the pandemic.10,11 In Ireland, cancer registration data showed ∼10% fewer diagnoses than expected in 2020, with deficits persisting, although narrowing, into 2021.12 By 2022, case counts had returned to within the projected range.13 Similar declines were reported globally, although with considerable heterogeneity across cancer sites, countries, and demographic groups.10,11,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

Despite this growing body of evidence, significant research gaps remain. It remains unclear whether temporary reductions in diagnostic activity translated into stage migration, particularly increases in late-stage presentations. Disruptions to diagnostic pathways, altered treatment modalities, delays in initiation of therapy, or reduced intensity of treatment might affect survival, but evidence to date has been mixed, and analysis has been limited to shorter-term (1 and 2 years) survival.14,15,21,22

The aim was to provide a comprehensive, population-based assessment of how the COVID-19 pandemic affected cancer incidence, stage at diagnosis, mortality, and 1-year net survival in Ireland.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

Incidence data on new cases of primary cancer [International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes C00-C96 excluding C44] diagnosed in the years 2018-2022 were downloaded from the National Cancer Registry Ireland (NCRI) database. The NCRI is a population-based registry operating under a statutory instrument and funded by the Department of Health in Ireland. Data on newly diagnosed cases are collected from a wide variety of sources, including pathology laboratories, radiotherapy centres, hospital in-patient enquiry records, and hospital records (public and private hospitals). Data on registered deaths are obtained from the Death Events Publication Service and the Central Statistics Office (CSO). Variables downloaded from the NCRI included age group, sex, ICD-10 cancer site, stage (if relevant), and survival time (censoring date 31 December 2022). Mortality data for 2018-2021 and 2022 were downloaded from the CSO website.23,24

The main outcomes of interest are incidence, mortality, and 1-year net survival.

Age-standardised incidence and mortality rates were calculated using published mid-year population data from the CSO and the 2013 European standard population.25,26

Net survival is the expected survival in the hypothetical situation in which cancer is the only possible cause of death and it is based on observed survival of patients with cancer scaled against the expected survival of persons of the same age and sex in the general population.27,28

Data analysis

Incident cancer cases selected on various cancer sites were analysed using counts stratified by calendar period, age group (<50, 50-69, and 70+ years), and sex. The outcome variable was the number of observed cases, with corresponding population denominators used as exposure terms.

Calendar period was specified as a categorical variable representing a prepandemic baseline period (2018-2019) and quarterly intervals from 2020 Q1 to 2022 Q4.

Incidence rates for all cancer types combined, as well as for each cancer site, were modelled using generalised linear models with a log link and population offset using Stata19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Poisson regression models were initially fitted to incidence counts with population denominators as offsets. Evidence of overdispersion was assessed using negative binomial regression. For each cancer site, comparison of the Akaike information criterion and likelihood ratio tests of the overdispersion parameter (α) rejected the Poisson assumption (P ≤ 0.05) and negative binomial models were therefore preferred for inference.

Adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for calendar period were summarised graphically using coefficient plots, with the 2018-2019 baseline period as the reference category.

Predicted mean case counts were obtained from the fitted negative binomial model. Pearson residuals were calculated using the estimated dispersion parameter and standardised to unit variance. Standardised Pearson residuals were plotted against fitted values to assess symmetry and potential model misspecification. Model fit was further evaluated using plots of observed versus predicted case counts.

Percentage change (PC) in IRRs was calculated as PC = [IRR-1]∗100.

Analyses were stratified by stage, if relevant, with stage defined using TNM 7th edition,29 and summarised as stages I-IV. For stage-specific analyses, separate negative binomial models were fitted for each stage.

Because restricting analyses to staged cancers would otherwise lead to a mismatch between the numerator (staged cases only) and the denominator (total population), population denominators were adjusted to reflect the proportion of cancers with recorded stage within each cancer site, age–sex–period stratum. Within each stratum, the proportion of unstaged cases was calculated and the corresponding population denominator was reduced by this proportion. The adjusted population was then used as the exposure term in negative binomial regression models, ensuring consistency between the population at risk and the set of cases included in the analysis. Unadjusted estimates were also calculated as a sensitivity analysis and are available in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100767).

One-year net survival was estimated using the Pohar–Perme estimator (strs command in STATA).30 To account for changes in case-mix during the pandemic, age-standardised, stage-standardised, and combined age-stage-standardised survival were estimated. Age standardisation used the site-specific International Cancer Survival Standard weights,31 whereas stage weights were based on overall stage distribution across the dataset. Life tables, up to 2021, were constructed following the methodology developed for the CONCORD programme, using a flexible Poisson regression model to estimate smooth age-, sex-, and year-specific mortality rates from death and population counts.28,32 Mortality rates for 2019, 2020, and 2021 were modelled individually, to take into account the fluctuation in the number of deaths and population due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Survival estimates were considered significantly different if their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) did not overlap.

Significant reductions in incidence of stage I, II, and III breast and colorectal cancer were evident during 2020 and stage I and III cervical cancer also showed significant declines in 2020 compared with the 2018-2019 baseline period. These decreases are consistent with disruptions to screening programmes and diagnostic services.

Outside of cancers with population-based screening programmes in place, prostate cancer also experienced decreases in stage-specific incidence across all stages in 2020. These trends extended partly into 2021, especially for stage I and II disease, indicating possible delays in detection due to reduced primary care consultations and changes in prostate-specific antigen testing patterns.

For lung cancer, significant reductions were observed in stage II (−27%) and stage III (−17%) in 2020. Stage III lung cancer remained 19% lower than baseline in 2021, suggesting a slower recovery for thoracic diagnostic pathways relative to other cancer types.

Mortality rates

The average annual number of cancer deaths increased modestly from 9468 (2018-2019) to 9680 (2020-2021) and to 10 100 in 2022, corresponding to increases of 2% and 7%, respectively. These rises were consistent with historical trends, which predict annual increases of ∼1.3% in cancer mortality for both sexes.33

However, age-standardised mortality rates did not increase in the pandemic or recovery period. In fact, the age-standardised mortality rate of all invasive cancer decreased significantly from 2018-2019 to 2020-2021. Data on age-standardised mortality rates by site are available in Supplementary Table S4 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100767).

Survival

One-year net survival analyses revealed little evidence of pandemic-related deterioration once estimates were adjusted for age and stage. For colorectal cancer, a decline in unadjusted 1-year survival was observed in 2020 (79.5%; 95% CI 77.5% to 81.36%) compared with 2018 (84.5%; 95% CI 82.78% to 86%) and 2019 (83.3%; 95% CI 81.59% to 84.9%). However, this difference disappeared after adjusting for case-mix (age and stage).

Lung cancer showed lower unadjusted survival in 2020 (43.3%; 95% CI 41.16% to 45.49%) compared with 2018 (48.1%; 95% CI 45.92% to 50.21%) but not compared with 2019 (44.7%; 95% CI 42.62% to 46.83%) or 2021 (44.2%; 95% CI 42.03% to 46.32%); again, adjusted survival estimates for 2020 remained stable relative to prepandemic years.

Across all cancer sites where survival estimates could be generated, no significant pandemic-associated reduction in adjusted 1-year net survival was detected. One-year net survival estimates are available in Supplementary Table S5 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100767).

Discussion

This report provides a comprehensive assessment of the COVID-19 pandemic’s early impact on cancer incidence, stage at diagnosis, mortality, and short-term survival in Ireland. The reduction in incidence seen in the pandemic period 2020-2021, with incidence returning to expected levels by 2022, is consistent with global reports of decreases in diagnostic activity, driven by both health care system disruptions and changes in public behaviour, including hesitancy to seek medical attention and reduced opportunities for in-person clinical assessment.2,10,11,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20,34

Stage at diagnosis

Concerns that diagnostic delays would result in cancers presenting at more advanced stages are not strongly supported by this study. Stage-specific incidence analyses show reductions across multiple stages during the COVID period, with no clear compensatory increase in advanced-stage incidence possibly reflecting disruptions to diagnostic pathways rather than stage migration, although increasing proportions of unstaged tumours limit interpretation.

Some stage-specific declines occurred in 2020 and were most notable in cancers, where a national screening programme was in place. For breast, colorectal, and cervical cancers, reductions were observed across early-stage diagnoses, reflecting both the temporary suspension of screening programmes and reduced access to diagnostic investigations.9,34 However, crucially, there was no consistent increase in late-stage cancers in 2021 or 2022.

Some exceptions were noted, such as increases in stage IV thyroid cancer (2020) and stage IV Hodgkin lymphoma (2021), but these findings involved small numbers of cases and are likely to reflect random variation rather than systemic shifts. More broadly, the data show considerable heterogeneity in stage-specific incidence and interpretation is limited by the increasing proportion of unstaged cancers in 2021 and 2022.

Mortality trends

The analysis found no significant increases in cancer mortality during the pandemic period and age-standardised mortality rates for all invasive cancers actually decreased slightly in 2020-2021 compared with 2018-2019. Similar reductions were observed across several individual cancer sites in 2022. These patterns are consistent with longstanding downwards trends in cancer mortality in Ireland and offer reassurance that early pandemic disruptions did not translate into measurable increases in cancer deaths during the study period. However, mortality rates for the years presented in this report mainly reflect trends in diagnosis and treatment before the pandemic. If the pandemic did have an impact on cancer mortality, it may not become apparent for several more years.

Survival outcomes

For cancers with generally good prognosis, any pandemic-related effects may not be evident for many years. In contrast, for cancers with poorer outcomes, 1-year survival may provide an initial signal of care disruption. In this report, survival outcomes remained largely stable during the pandemic, with no consistent evidence of declines when estimates were adjusted for differences in age and stage at diagnosis. Although unadjusted survival fell slightly for colorectal and lung cancer in 2020, these declines disappeared after accounting for case-mix, indicating that shifts in the distribution of age or stage, rather than changes in treatment effectiveness or access, explained the observed differences.

These findings suggest that despite the significant pressures placed on the health care system during COVID-19, cancer treatment pathways remained sufficiently resilient to prevent deterioration in short-term survival. This reflects the efforts of health care providers to prioritise cancer care, adapt treatment protocols, and rapidly resume diagnostic and therapeutic services following initial disruptions.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study are that it used data from a national population-based cancer registry to examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the incidence and 1-year survival for a wide range of cancer sites. Stage-specific incidence rates were used to identify possible effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on stage at diagnosis. Using stage-specific rates avoids the issue of induced correlation, which is inherent if proportional stage distributions are reported.

There are, however, a number of limitations to the analyses presented. A large number of comparisons across multiple time periods and cancer sites are presented using the standard 5% significance threshold; we did not adjust for multiple testing. As a result, some statistically significant findings may be due to chance. It is important to interpret results in the broader context rather than focusing on isolated statistically significant outcomes.

We have defined the pandemic period as 2020-2021, as these years were when most of the public health controls/restrictions were in place. Although preparations for a possible pandemic had started in January 2020, there were no restrictions implemented until March of that year.1,4,35 The inclusion of January and February 2020 in the pandemic period definition used in this study could underestimate the effect of the pandemic on cancer incidence.

The increasing proportion of tumours with unknown stage in 2021 and 2022 (due to ongoing data collection of stage data) limits interpretation of stage-specific trends. The denominator-adjusted analysis was specified as the primary analysis because stage completeness declined substantially during the pandemic period, with the decline varying by cancer site (Figure 2). Analyses that ignored this change in stage ascertainment risked attributing reductions in staged cancers to changes in incidence when they may instead have reflected migration of cases into the unstaged category. The adjustment was intended to account for temporal variation in stage completeness without making assumptions about the distribution of stages among unstaged cases. Unadjusted estimates are presented as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100767). This denominator-adjusted approach is not a formal missing-data solution and does not recover the true stage distribution. Rather, it is a pragmatic correction for changing stage completeness over time. It was not possible to impute stage because the mechanism underlying missing stage information was unlikely to satisfy the missing at random assumption. Patients with unknown stage represent a distinct clinical subgroup with incomplete predictive diagnostic variables required for effective imputation of stage. Therefore, unknown stage was retained as a separate category to avoid introducing potentially biased stage assignments.

The number of deaths and mortality rates appear lower in 2022 for many cancer sites; however, mortality data for 2022 rely on unrevised CSO counts (as revised estimates for 2022 had not been published at the time of analysis), potentially limiting comparability with previous years. In addition, it is likely that any potential impact on cancer mortality would not be evident at the time of writing, as for most cancers there is a time lag of several years between diagnosis and death.

The impact of COVID-19 on cancer survival is difficult to interpret because observed changes reflect a mix of real effects, such as delays in diagnosis and treatment, and artefactual effects, such as loss of lead time due to reduced screening, changes in the types of cancers diagnosed, possible reductions in overdiagnosis associated with screening programmes and other diagnostic activity, and competing mortality from COVID-19.21,36 Survival analysis was limited to 1-year due to the short time that has elapsed since the COVID-19 pandemic and availability of complete validated registry data. Longer follow-up will be necessary to fully understand the pandemic’s impact on survival and mortality, especially for cancers with high survival rates.

The possible impact of the COVID pandemic on recurrence rates and treatment utilisation and timing, which could affect survival and mortality, were not assessed in this analysis.

Implications in practice

The results of this study show the value of having high-quality data available to assess the impact of large-scale disruptions, in this case, the COVID-19 pandemic and related health care disruption/reorganisation.

The positive finding that short-term survival does not appear to have been negatively impacted is a testament to the work done by the National Cancer Control Programme (NCCP) to standardise and centralise cancer care in Ireland, particularly since 2007.37 In the face of the COVID-19 pandemic the Health Service Executive and NCCP worked with clinical stakeholders to ensure cancer care was maintained, e.g. through the relocation of surgery to private hospitals, the publication of updated guidelines, and regular meetings with clinical stakeholders.2

It is becoming increasingly clear that the relationship between COVID-19 infection, vaccination, and cancer is highly complex, with potentially beneficial and adverse effects being reported.38, 39, 40 Although these complex interactions are beyond the scope of this article, they may warrant consideration in future work.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted cancer detection and diagnostic activity in Ireland during 2020. However, most impacts were temporary, with incidence levels returning to expected patterns by 2022 and no evidence of sustained increases in late-stage disease or deterioration in one-year survival. Although the evidence is still emerging, the early overall picture suggests that the resilience and adaptability of the Irish health care system helped to safeguard cancer outcomes during an unprecedented public health crisis.
