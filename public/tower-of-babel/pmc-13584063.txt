Improving protein extractability from oats using malted cereal extracts under acidic fermentation-like conditions
Authors: Ewoud Blontrock, Frederik Janssen, Lieselot Lauwers, Christophe M. Courtin, Arno G.B. Wouters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid oat-based food products typically contain low protein levels due to poor oat protein extractability. Lactic acid food fermentation and associated endogenous oat enzymatic activity have previously been shown not to significantly improve oat protein extractability. Therefore, this study investigates the potential of oat malt extract (OME) and wheat malt extract (WME) addition, as a source of cereal-derived enzymes, to increase protein extractability during oat incubation at fermentation-relevant pH as well as during fermentation. Kilned and non-kilned oat whole meal suspensions were incubated for 24 h at pH 4.0 with added WME or OME and the effects on protein extractability, degree of hydrolysis and molecular weight (MW) distribution were evaluated. OME and WME additions increased protein hydrolysis and extractability, most notably in non-kilned oat, reaching protein extractabilities up to 74.5% after 24 h incubation with 10.0% WME, being triple those of samples incubated without OME/WME. Notably, under specific conditions, significant amounts of 4-85 kDa proteins, including species with MWs consistent with those of intact 12S globulins subunits, were rendered extractable. Comparable results were obtained when oat suspensions were fermented for 24 h with a commercial L. plantarum strain in the presence of WME/OME. Interestingly, in addition to expected proteolytic action, other malt-derived enzymatic as well as non-enzymatic effects were found to contribute to increasing the oat globulin extractability. Further examination suggested that, for the most part, the effect of WME/OME could not be attributed to disulfide bond reduction, altered protein–phytate interactions or removal of structural barriers. Overall, the addition of WME/OME to oat-based fermentation processes enhances protein extractability, which may improve the nutritional and functional characteristics of fermented liquid oat-based foods.

Introduction

Oat-based drinks are increasingly popular among consumers worldwide (Future Market Insights Inc, 2025). Besides the trend of (partially) shifting from animal-based to plant-based products driven by sustainability and animal welfare concerns, the popularity of oat drinks can also be ascribed to their neutral taste and suitability for people with lactose intolerance (Haas et al., 2019). However, nutritionally, oat drinks are inferior in terms of average protein content (0.8 ± 0.5 g/100 mL) in comparison with regular cow's milk (3.3 ± 0.3 g/100 mL) (Silva et al., 2020; Singh-Povel et al., 2022). An important reason for this is low oat protein extractability, which in turn can be attributed to several factors.

First, the main protein fraction in oats are globulins (50-80%), which primarily consists of hexameric 12S globulins, with each monomer composed of an α- and β-subunit linked by a disulfide bond (Klose and Arendt, 2012; Mäkinen et al., 2017). The high number of hydrophobic regions in these 12S globulins results in a low solubility at neutral pH (Jiang et al., 2015; Mel and Malalgoda, 2022). The isoelectric point of these proteins near pH 5.0 leads to intrinsically low solubility across the pH range most common in food applications (Klose and Arendt, 2012; Mäkinen et al., 2017). Besides globulins, oats also contain a significant amount of albumins (1-12% of all proteins) (Klose and Arendt, 2012). Second, oat kernels are traditionally heat-treated (or ‘kilned’) to prevent rancidity development in oat-based products by inactivating lipases and lipoxygenases (Pynket et al., 2024). This heat treatment induces structural changes in oat proteins and in the oat matrix (Pynket et al., 2026a, Pynket et al., 2026b), reducing the oat protein extractability by a factor of 2 to 4, depending on the heat treatment and extraction conditions (Norlander et al., 2024; Pynket et al., 2024; Runyon et al., 2015). Third, oat proteins can be encapsulated within cellular structures, limiting their extractability. Indeed, a ball mill treatment that causes cellular disruption has been shown to increase the extractability of oat protein by 2-fold in water or at pH 9.0 (Janssen et al., 2023). Fourth, charged amino acids in oat proteins may associate with phytate, possibly forming insoluble complexes, limiting the overall protein extractability (H. Li et al., 2014).

To mitigate the low protein content in oat drinks, producers often add proteins from other plant-based sources during formulation. In parallel, alternative processing technologies are being developed to enhance oat protein functionality (Rafique et al., 2025). However, it would be economically, nutritionally and technofunctionally advantageous to extract a greater amount of proteins directly from oats. This can be achieved by using exogenous enzymes, such as protein deamidases or proteases during the oat juice production step (Jiang et al., 2015; Singh-Povel et al., 2022; Triantafyllou, 2014). Alternatively, food fermentation is a promising bioprocessing strategy in this regard. The use of food fermentation shows promise, not only for its associated health benefits but also for its effects on constituents within the matrix, including proteins. Indeed, studies in wheat-based systems have demonstrated that during fermentation endogenous enzymes are activated upon acidification, which can modify protein molecular structures, thereby influencing their extractability (Arte et al., 2015). However, previous research from our group on both kilned and non-kilned oat whole meal demonstrated that a 24 h incubation, in the presence of antibiotics to prevent spontaneous fermentation, did not substantially enhance protein extractability (Blontrock et al., 2025). Similarly, an inoculated lactic acid fermentation with a commercial Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strain did not result in a substantial improvement in protein extractability (Lambrechts et al., 2026). This shows that the endogenous enzymatic potential of oats, even in the absence of prior kilning, is not sufficient to induce substantial changes in the oat protein structure.

Here, we propose an innovative approach where malted cereals can serve as a source of cereal-derived enzymes (Yang et al., 2023), in support of an acidic food fermentation process. Cereal malt has been used for millennia in beer production, yet its potential for other applications remains largely unexploited, despite its considerable potential as a clean-label ingredient (Mäkinen and Arendt, 2015). The addition of wheat and oat malt to wheat-based doughs has been shown to significantly enhance wheat protein hydrolysis, whereas oat malt addition enhances wheat protein solubility under aqueous ethanol, denaturing, and reducing conditions (Mäkinen and Arendt, 2012). However, the effect of malt extract addition – under fermentation-relevant conditions – on the molecular structure of oat proteins and their aqueous extractability remains unexplored. By using this approach, the present study investigated the use of malt extracts as functional ingredients in the context of fermented oat beverages. In contrast to approaches relying on the addition of isolated enzymes, malt extracts provide a complex mixture of soluble endogenous enzymes and low-molecular-weight compounds and represent an economically feasible approach. Moreover, in comparison to a germination-based approach, the use of malt extracts avoids the compositional changes in oat constituents during germination itself, allowing more basic understanding of the effect of the malt extract constituents on the oat wholemeal matrix. As wheat and oats differ in their composition and biochemical activities, both wheat malt extract (WME) and oat malt extract (OME) were evaluated with regard to their respective effects on oat protein extractability.

In this research paper, the effect of the addition of WME and OME during an incubation at fermentation-relevant pH (in the presence of antimicrobial compounds to prevent microbial growth) or during an inoculated fermentation of both kilned and non-kilned oat suspensions on the protein extractability is studied. Moreover, the observed effect of malt extract addition is also explored mechanistically through targeted enzymatic or physical treatments. The overall aim is to determine how malt extracts can be leveraged to overcome the challenges associated with oat protein extractability. The outcomes of this research hold promise for improving protein extractability, e.g. during the production of fermented oat-based drinks and provides insights that may lead to industrial clean-label formulations.

Materials and methods

Materials

A batch of dehulled oat kernels, comprising a mixture of cultivars harvested in 2022 in Finland, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Sweden, Estonia and Latvia, was provided by Maselis (Roeselare, Belgium). Part of the batch was provided prior to kilning and part after an industrial kilning process. The specific processing conditions for kilning were not disclosed to the authors, but it is known that a peroxidase activity of zero (full enzyme inactivation) was targeted. The groats were stored at −18 °C until further use. Château oat malt was purchased from Castle malting (Beloeil, Belgium). Wheat kernels were kindly provided by Boortmalt NV (Antwerp, Belgium).

Hexane was obtained from Chem-lab Analytical (Zedelgem, Belgium), while sodium dodecyl sulfate, ethanol (99% v/v, denatured) and sodium dihydrogen phosphate dihydrate were purchased from VWR International (Leuven, Belgium). All other chemicals are purchased from Merck (Bornem, Belgium). Commercial proteases, Flavourzyme (500-1000 U/g) and papain (30.000 U/mg) were obtained from Novozymes (part of Novonesis, Bagsværd, Denmark) and Merck (Bornem, Belgium), respectively. The Quantum Blue phytase was kindly provided by AB Vista (Wiltshire, United Kingdom). A commercial starter culture (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum) was provided by Chr. Hansen (part of Novonesis, Hørsholm, Denmark).

Sample preparation

The oat groats were milled to a particle size below 0.5 mm using a Retsch ZM 300 Ultra Centrifugal Mill (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany). Immediately after milling, the oat whole meal was defatted using hexane in a 1:5 (w/v) ratio, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min. Given the use of non-kilned oats, defatting was necessary to reduce potential interference with analytical methods and to prevent lipid oxidation and rancidity development during storage. Preliminary results indicated that defatting had minimal effects on both the enzymatic activity and fermentability of the oat whole meal, with the latter shown in Fig. S1 of the supplementary data. The resulting pellet was air-dried overnight and the defatted oat whole meal (DOW) was stored at 4 °C until further use.

The wheat kernels were malted in-house using a micro-malting device (Joe White Malting Systems, Perth, Australia). Both oat and wheat malts were milled with a Retsch ZM 300 Ultra Centrifugal Mill (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) equipped with a 0.5 mm sieve, and immediately stored at −18 °C until further use. To prepare wheat and oat malt extracts (WME and OME, respectively), 1:5 (w/w) malt:water suspensions were made by shaking for 15 min at 150 strokes per minute. After centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min) and freeze-drying, dried malt extract powders were obtained.

Malt extract enzymatic characterisation

The enzymatic activities of the malt extracts were further characterised. Protease activity was determined using an azocasein assay. The reaction mixture contained 350 μL azocasein (1.4% (w/v), in 0.05 M phosphate buffer, pH 6.5) and 250 μL OME or WME enzyme extract (5.0 mg/mL), and was incubated at 40 °C for 2 h. The reaction was terminated by adding 0.5 mL 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min) to remove precipitated proteins. An equal volume of 0.5 M NaOH was added to the supernatant, and after 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 440 nm. Protease activity was expressed as the increase in absorbance at 440 nm per hour per mg of malt extract under assay conditions. β-glucanase and phytase activities were determined according to the methods described by Blontrock et al. (2025) and enzyme activities were defined as reported therein.

Incubation and fermentation of oat whole meal suspensions

Incubation of kilned and non-kilned oat whole meal suspensions with oat and wheat malt extracts

DOW from kilned and non-kilned oat was suspended in duplicate at a 1:6 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio in deionised water supplemented with antibiotics [0.05% (w/v) chloramphenicol and 0.02% (w/v) cycloheximide] to prevent spontaneous fermentation. Each suspension had a total volume of 4.5 mL. To these suspensions, WME or OME was added at concentrations of 2.0%, 6.0%, and 10.0% (w/w on a dry weight basis).

The suspensions were adjusted to pH 4.0 using pure lactic acid. This pH of 4.0 was selected for incubations as a fermentation-relevant pH, at which endogenous cereal enzymes are expected to be active (Arte et al., 2015; Blontrock et al., 2025; Loponen et al., 2007). Then the suspensions were incubated for 5 h or 24 h in closed 5 mL Eppendorf tubes, while shaking at 30 °C in a shaking incubator at 150 rpm. After incubation, an aliquot of each suspension was stored as such at −18 °C, while the remaining portion was diluted to a 1:10 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio with deionised water and centrifuged (10 min at 10,000 × g). The supernatant (referred to as the "extract") was immediately heat-treated (10 min at 100 °C) to inactivate residual enzymatic activity. The heat-treated extracts were then stored at −18 °C until further analysis. Incubated DOW suspensions (24 h, pH 4.0) without malt extract addition and aqueous extracts derived therefrom were included as reference materials.

Alternative bioprocessing strategies to mechanistically understand the effect of malt extract addition on protein extractability

To mechanistically investigate the effect of malt extract addition during incubation on oat protein extractability, various specifically designed incubation experiments were performed.

As a first approach, the contribution of enzymatic and non-enzymatic effects in OME and WME to alterations in protein extractability was investigated. To this end, OME and WME were heat-treated to inactivate all enzymes. Specifically, malt extract suspensions (1:20 w/v) were prepared in 99.5% (v/v) ethanol and subjected to two boiling cycles in a 100 °C water bath until all ethanol was evaporated. Subsequently, suspensions were prepared in duplicate from kilned and non-kilned DOW at a 1:6 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio in the presence of antibiotics, and supplemented with 7.8% (w/w on dry weight) heat-treated WME or 5.0% (w/w) heat-treated OME. These suspensions were incubated for 24 h and extracts were prepared and heat-treated as described in Section 2.3.1.

As a second approach, reduced glutathione was added during incubation to investigate the potential effect of reducing compounds in the malt extracts on oat protein extractability. DOW suspensions at a 1:6 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio were made in duplicate from both kilned and non-kilned oat and 0.17% (w/w) reduced glutathione was added. The suspension was shaken under a nitrogen atmosphere for 15 min at 150 strokes per min, after which an extract was prepared as in Section 2.3.1.

As a third approach, the role of physical barriers and phytate–protein interactions in reducing oat protein extractability was further investigated. Therefore, DOW derived from both kilned and non-kilned oats was first cryo-milled to disrupt cell walls and reduce the average particle size. Cryo-milling was performed using a Retsch Cryomill (Aartselaar, Belgium) with three cycles of 15 min at 30 Hz. The resulting cryo-milled DOW was then used to prepare suspensions at a 1:6 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio in duplicate and in the presence of added antibiotics. These suspensions were incubated for 24 h with Quantum Blue Phytase (at approximately 2.5 U/g DOW) in 5 mL Eppendorf tubes, shaken at 30 °C and 150 rpm in a shaking incubator. Quantum Blue phytase is a highly efficient enzyme derived from the feed industry that, in addition to its primary phytase activity, also demonstrates side activities of β-glucanase and xylanase. After incubation, extracts were prepared as in Section 2.3.1.

As a fourth approach, exogenous proteolytic enzymes were employed during incubation to assess the potential impact of protein hydrolysis on the extractability of globulins. An endo-protease (papain) and an enzyme mixture containing both exo- and endo-proteases (Flavourzyme) were evaluated at different concentrations. DOW from kilned and non-kilned oat was suspended in duplicate at a 1:6 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio in the presence of antibiotics and enzymes were added at dosages of 0.01, 0.1, and 1.0 U/mg protein. Additionally, papain was applied at a dosage of 10 U/mg protein, which was not feasible with Flavourzyme due to its lower unit activity. The suspensions were incubated for 24 h at 30 °C in 5 mL Eppendorf tubes, while shaking at 150 rpm. After incubation, extracts were prepared as in Section 2.3.1.

Lactic acid fermentation of kilned and non-kilned oat whole meal suspensions with oat and WME

The effect of malt extract addition was also tested during lactic acid fermentation. DOW from kilned and non-kilned oats was suspended in duplicate at a 1:6 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio in sterile deionised water at a total volume of 4.5 mL. The inoculum, a commercial L. plantarum strain, was added to achieve an initial cell density of 107 CFU/mL suspension. Fermentation was performed for 24 h at 30 °C in a shaking incubator at 150 rpm. WME and OME were included at a concentration of 6.0% (w/w) in the fermentations. Independent fermentations were conducted in duplicate, with every replicate performed on a different day. The pH of the suspension was determined immediately after inoculation and after 24 h of fermentation and a dilution series therefrom was plated on De Man–Rogosa–Sharpe agar to quantify microbial growth. In addition, samples were taken after 24 h of fermentation, diluted to a 1:10 (w/v) whole meal:water ratio and extracts were prepared and heat treated as in Section 2.3.1.

Protein analysis of bioprocessed oat whole meal suspensions

Protein extractability

Protein extractability was assessed by measuring the protein content (N × 5.83) of heat-treated extracts using a total combustion method (Dumas Elemental Analyzer model EA1108, Carlo Erba, Hindley Green, United Kingdom) (FAO, 1986). The protein extractability (%) was defined as the proportion of protein recovered in the heat-treated extracts relative to the total protein amount present in the DOW used for incubation or fermentation. This value was corrected for the contribution of added proteins/nitrogen from OME or WME (under the same pH, centrifugation and heat treatment conditions as applied to the samples), as well as from proteolytic enzymes or glutathione, if applicable.

Degree of protein hydrolysis

The degree of protein hydrolysis (DH), reflecting the percentage of cleaved peptide bonds, was quantified using the o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) assay, as described by Spellman et al. (2003) and further detailed by Blontrock et al. (2025). An aliquot of incubated or fermented DOW suspensions (resulting in 2–3 mg protein) was solubilised in duplicate in a 0.050 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 2.0% w/v sodium dodecyl sulfate and 1.0% w/v 1,4-dithiothreitol to disrupt non-covalent interactions and disulfide bonds and solubilise all proteins. After vortex mixing, samples were shaken (30 min, 150 rpm) and centrifuged (10 min, 10,000 × g). To aliquots of the supernatants (400 μL, in duplicate), 3.0 mL freshly prepared OPA reagent (0.10 M borate buffer with 1.0% SDS, 0.088% DTT, and 0.080% OPA in 8% ethanol) at room temperature was added. Absorbance was measured at 340 nm after exactly 20 min incubation. Free amino groups were quantified using a calibration curve prepared from L-leucine standards (0–2 mM) and corrected for the amount of free amino groups that were present in the WME or OME. The DH was calculated by comparing the free amino group content of incubated/fermented and non-incubated/unfermented samples as explained in detail by Blontrock et al. (2025): DH=100*nN, with n the calculated hydrolysis-equivalents (mmol/g protein) and N the total theoretical number of peptide bonds present in the sample (i.e. 7.8 mmol/g oat protein) (Spellman et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2015).

Protein molecular weight distribution

The apparent molecular weight (MW) distribution of proteins was determined using size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SE-HPLC), as described by Blontrock et al. (2025). Incubated/fermented DOW suspensions and heat-treated extracts prepared therefrom were diluted tenfold in 0.050 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 2.0% w/v sodium dodecyl sulfate and 1.0% w/v 1,4-dithiothreitol to solubilise all proteins, followed by shaking (60 min, 150 strokess/min) and centrifugation (10 min at 10,000 × g). Samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone membrane filter (Whatman Millex-HP, Millipore, Carrigtwohill, Ireland), and a 10 μL (suspensions) or 50 μL (extracts) aliquot was injected into a Biosep SEC-S2000 column (0.2–75 kDa separation range, in 0.1% w/v SDS buffer; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) mounted in a Shimadzu Prominence modular SE-HPLC system (Kyoto, Japan), equipped with an LC-20 AT pump, SIL-20 AC HT autosampler, CTO-20 AC oven, and SPD-20 A UV–Vis detector set at 214 nm. Elution was performed with 0.050 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 0.1% w/v SDS at 0.5 mL/min and 30 °C. As a reference material, kilned and non-kilned DOW were analysed by suspending 1.0 mg protein per mL in the same buffer as above and, after preparing the samples as above, injecting 10 μL onto the column. MW marker proteins [bovine serum albumin (66.5 kDa), carbonic anhydrase (30 kDa), α-lactalbumin (14 kDa), aprotinin (6.5 kDa), and (Ala)5 (373 Da)] were analysed under identical conditions. To enable comparison between chromatograms of heat-treated extracts and DOW suspensions, extract profile areas were corrected for injection volume and the amount of starting material used in extract preparation. The corresponding peak annotation is explained by Blontrock et al. (2025) and is supported by SDS-PAGE analysis, as shown in Fig. S2 in the supplementary data: oat 12S globulin monomers and protein aggregates (10.0–12.0 min), 12S globulin α- and β-subunits (12.0–12.5 min and 12.5–13.5 min, respectively), albumins (13.5-15.0 min) and low MW peptides (15.0–23.0 min). To further explain this peak annotation, By comparing the total area between 10.0 and 17.0 min for an extract and its corresponding suspension, an estimate of the percentage of intact globulin subunits, albumins or hydrolysis products with a similar MW was obtained, as these proteins were extractable after incubation or fermentation.

Statistics

The incubation and fermentation experiments were performed in duplicate, while analytical measurements were performed once per biological replicate. This was done under the assumption that the biological replicates would cause more variance than the analysis method. Averages and standard deviations were calculated using MS Excel. With two biological replicates, the standard deviations reflect the difference between these two measurements rather than a full estimate of variability. Comparisons between sample means were done qualitatively in this study. The general effects of kilning, incubation time, malt source, and malt concentration were evaluated based on the complete dataset in a full-factorial linear model using JMP software (student edition 19). Kilning was included as a nominal factor (kilned vs. non-kilned), whereas malt source was included as a categorical factor with three levels (oat malt extract, wheat malt extract, and no malt extract). Incubation time and malt concentration were treated as continuous variables, in order to estimate their overall linear effects. The analysis assumed normality of the data within each group and homogeneity of variances across groups.

Results and discussion

To investigate the effect of WME and OME addition on oat protein extractability, 24 h incubations of DOW at pH 4.0 were performed in the presence of antibiotics to prevent microbial growth and possible associated microbial enzymatic activities (Section 3.1). Both kilned and non-kilned oats were included in the experimental setup to account for kilning-induced differences in the oat matrix and the activity of endogenous oat-associated enzymes and to evaluate their potential synergistic interactions with malt-derived enzymes in influencing protein extractability. To investigate the mechanisms underlying the effect of malt extract on oat proteins, different bioprocessing approaches were tested (Section 3.2). Then, the observed effects of malt extract addition were also verified during 24 h inoculated fermentations of DOW with L. plantarum (Section 3.3).

Two types of malt extract were used, WME and OME. For the in-house malted wheat, an extraction yield of 18.7 ± 1.4% was achieved, while for Château oat malt, an extraction yield of 12.2 ± 0.3% was obtained. The dry matter content of the freeze-dried WME and OME was approximately 95% (w/w), while the protein content was respectively 17.7 ± 0.8% (N × 5.70) and 18.5 ± 0.3% (w/w) (N × 5.83) on dry weight (FAO, 1986). With respect to enzymatic activities, the endoprotease activity of OME was low (0.08 ± 0.04 U/g dry matter), while WME exhibited a somewhat higher activity (0.56 ± 0.07 U/g dry matter). This is in contrast with literature findings, where oat malt has been reported to exhibit a slightly higher proteolytic activity than wheat malt (Mäkinen and Arendt, 2012). Nevertheless, endoprotease activity in both extracts was low compared with values reported in the literature in rye malt (Brijs et al., 2002). In contrast, β-glucanase activity was more than tenfold higher in OME (261.9 ± 6.0 U/g dry matter) than in WME (22.6 ± 3.9 U/g dry matter), with both values being within the range reported in the literature (Hübner et al., 2009; Rieder et al., 2015). Phytase activity was higher in WME (239.9 ± 12.8 μmol PO43−released/h.g dry matter), than in OME (127.8 ± 4.5 μmol PO43−released/h.g dry matter).

Incubation of kilned and non-kilned oat whole meal suspensions with oat and wheat malt extract

Protein extractability

Fig. 1 presents the protein extractability of kilned and non-kilned DOW, incubated for 5 h and 24 h at pH 4.0 without and with OME (Fig. 1A) or WME (Fig. 1B) addition at concentrations of 2.0%, 6.0% and 10.0% (w/w). The effects of kilning, incubation time, malt source, and malt concentration on protein extractability were evaluated using a statistical full-factorial linear model, the results of which are presented in Table S1 of the supplementary data. As starting material, the kilned and non-kilned DOW at pH 4.0 showed a protein extractability of 3.9 ± 0.4% and 17.3 ± 0.3%, respectively (data not shown). The same samples after 24 h of incubation at pH 4.0 without OME or WME had protein extractabilities of 6.1 ± 0.2% and 26.4 ± 4.6%, respectively (Fig. 1). Thus, the protein extractability of kilned oat is 4-5 times lower compared to non-kilned oat, for reasons already discussed in the introduction. Because kilning inactivates endogenous oat enzymes, the protein extractability of kilned oat remained unchanged even after 24 h of incubation. In contrast, non-kilned oat showed a gradual increase in protein extractability during incubation, due to some protein hydrolysis by oat-associated enzymes (Blontrock et al., 2025). The extractability is defined as the percentage of total protein recovered in the extract relative to the original protein in the defatted oat whole meal.

After 5 h of incubating kilned oat with OME, a slight decreasing trend in protein extractability was observed across the three OME concentrations. This may be due to an overcorrection of protein extractability. Although the effect of acidic pH was accounted for, incubation at pH 4.0 may have further reduced the solubility of certain proteins, particularly 12S globulins near their isoelectric point (Klose and Arendt, 2012; R. Li and Xiong, 2021). Regardless, no notable positive effect of OME addition on protein extractability was noted after 5 h incubation. Even after 24 h incubation, no such effects were observed (Fig. 1A). That no changes occurred in protein extractability implies that there were no or only minor structural changes in the protein fraction or the oat matrix. For non-kilned oat samples, an OME concentration-dependent decrease in protein extractability (13.2 - 15.9%) during the initial 5 h of incubation was noted, attributable to the effects mentioned above for kilned oats. Interestingly, after 24 h incubation, adding 2.0% OME resulted in a protein extractability of 28.4 ± 0.5%, whereas at 6.0 and 10.0% OME addition, the protein extractability increased substantially, reaching 55.8 ± 3.2% and 61.3 ± 0.3%, respectively.

In the case of WME addition, a slight decrease in protein extractability was noted compared to the starting material when both kilned and non-kilned DOW were incubated for 5 h. Since this decrease was WME concentration-dependent, it is believed to originate from a similar overcorrection of the malt protein as explained before for samples with OME addition. This is illustrated by the kilned oat sample incubated for 5 h with 10.0% WME, for which an extractability of 0.0% was obtained. For kilned oat incubated 24 h with 2.0% WME, a minimal increase in protein extractability to 5.8 ± 0.1% was observed. However, at higher WME concentrations (6.0% and 10.0%), the protein extractability increased significantly, reaching 24.7 ± 0.1% and 31.3 ± 0.6%, respectively. In non-kilned oat samples, 24 h incubation in the presence of 2.0%, 6.0% and 10.0% WME led to substantial increases in protein extractability at all WME concentrations, reaching oat protein extractability values of 53.1 ± 0.1%, 69.8 ± 3.4%, and 74.5 ± 0.1%, respectively.

Thus, OME and WME addition can significantly enhance the protein extractability in non-kilned oats, particularly at sufficiently high dosages and long incubation times. Both WME and OME contain a variety of proteases, including endo- and exopeptidases, as well as cell wall-degrading enzymes such as β-glucanases and xylanases, that can act on the oat constituents (Faltermaier et al., 2014; Hübner et al., 2009). Thus, the observed increase in protein extractability may result from both matrix alterations, including cell wall degradation, and structural modifications of the oat proteins. Notably, the increase in extractability was much more pronounced in non-kilned than in kilned oats, consistent with the significant effect of kilning identified by a full-factorial statistical analysis (Table S1). In non-kilned oats, endogenous enzyme activity is still present, potentially leading to synergistic effects with OME constituents. At the same time, the matrix in non-kilned oats has not undergone structural changes induced by kilning. The proteins in kilned oats occur in an aggregated and less soluble state (Norlander et al., 2024; Pynket et al., 2024; Pynket et al., 2026a, Pynket et al., 2026b), which may reduce their accessibility to the malt enzymes. It is also important to note that the effect of OME was less pronounced than that of WME, as supported by the significant effect of malt source (Table S1). For OME, the maximum achievable protein extractability was lower, and no increase in protein extractability was observed in kilned oat samples. Differences in OME and WME composition, such as their differences in the quantified protease, β-glucanase and phytase activity, can explain this, although it is currently not clear which constituents are responsible for the observed effects.

Protein hydrolysis and molecular structure

Protein hydrolysis is known to improve protein solubility and extractability. Nella et al. (2023) were able to solubilise all proteins from an oat protein concentrate when treated with 4% alcalase (genzyme/gprotein) for 5 h (Nella et al., 2023). Moreover, endoprotease activity was higher in WME than in OME, which is consistent with the greater increase in protein extractability observed for WME. Therefore, the observed increase in protein extractability after incubation with OME or WME is hypothesised to originate mainly from proteolytic activity in the malt extracts (Faltermaier et al., 2014; Klose et al., 2009). However, to maintain the functional properties of oat proteins in foods such as oat-based beverages, it is preferred that protein hydrolysis be limited. Indeed, limited proteolysis can enhance protein solubility while simultaneously improving their functionality in food systems (Wouters et al., 2016). Thus, to further investigate the extent of protein hydrolysis induced by malt extract addition, free amino group contents were determined for the incubated kilned and non-kilned oat DOW suspensions with OME or WME. Additionally, the protein MW distributions were analysed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the changes in the protein profile after 24 h of incubation.

The DH was assessed in all samples incubated with OME (Fig. 2A) and WME (Fig. 2B). In kilned oat, the DH increased with the malt extract concentration, reaching a maximum of 7.3 ± 0.4% and 8.0 ± 0.9% when 10.0% OME and 10.0% WME were added, respectively. Already after 5 h, this dose-response relationship was visible, indicating that protein hydrolysis occurred throughout the 24 h incubation period. This trend was even more pronounced in non-kilned oat, where DH values reached 24.8 ± 4.5% and 27.4 ± 3.0% upon addition of 10.0% OME or WME, respectively. The dose-response relationship was confirmed by the significant positive effect of malt concentration on protein degree of hydrolysis observed in a full-factorial statistical analysis (Table S1). These DH values are higher than, for example, those reported by Brückner-Guhmann et al. (2018), who found that a DH of 3%, calculated using the pH-stat method, already induced a 3-fold increase in oat protein concentrate solubility (Brückner-Gühmann et al., 2018). When considering the suspensions after incubation, the MW distribution of the kilned and non-kilned oat samples with OME/WME (Fig. 3, Fig. S3 and S4) confirmed that some exopeptidase activity occurred, as evidenced by the elution of some smaller peptides (eluting after 17 min). The formation of smaller peptides was more pronounced in incubated non-kilned oat and increased proportionally with the malt extract concentration. Only at 10.0% OME/WME, a clear decrease in the peak area of the globulin β-subunits (12.5 – 13.5 min) occurred, accompanied by an increase in the peak area corresponding to the albumin fraction or proteins with similar MW (13.5–15 min). This effect was also proportional to the OME/WME concentration, supporting the interpretation that globulins were hydrolysed by malt-derived enzymes into proteins with MWs similar to those in the albumin fraction. Similarly, other researchers have reported hydrolysis products with MWs between 10 and 20 kDa when oat protein isolates were hydrolysed with alcalase (Nieto-Nieto et al., 2014; Zheng et al., 2020).

In the aqueous extracts after incubation, the smaller peptides (eluting after 15 min) formed in both kilned and non-kilned oat suspensions incubated with either OME or WME were present, indicating these are expectedly largely soluble (Fig. 3, Fig. S3 and S4). Interestingly, however, extracts from incubated kilned oat with 6.0% or 10.0% WME also showed the presence of larger MW proteins eluting in the range of globulin monomers, globulin subunits and albumins (10–15 min). Based on this observation, the percentage of extractable globulin subunits and albumins that remain intact or hydrolysis products with a similar molecular weight (eluting between 10 and 17 min, corresponding with a molecular weight approximately between 4 and 85 kDa) was estimated from the area under the chromatograms, as presented in Table 1. For example, 28.3 ± 0.4% of these 4 – 85 kDa proteins became extractable when kilned oat was incubated for 24 h with 10.0% WME, whereas in samples incubated without WME, these proteins were not extractable at all. A similar phenomenon was observed in extracts derived from non-kilned oat suspensions incubated with WME, where more than 60% of the 4 – 85 kDa proteins became extractable when incubated with 10.0% WME. A comparable trend was noted when non-kilned oats were incubated with OME at concentrations of 6.0 and 10.0%, with around 40-50% of 4 – 85 kDa proteins being extractable (Table 1). Furthermore, full-factorial statistical analysis revealed that the abundance of these high-MW proteins was significantly affected by both kilning and malt extract concentration (Table S1). It is a highly notable observation that the addition of OME and WME increases the extractability of these proteins, including significant amounts of presumably oat 12S globulin subunits, even at low pH. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time such an effect of malt extract addition is described. Mechanistic understanding of the effect of malt extract addition on protein extractability

Several hypotheses can be formulated to explain the observed increase in globulin subunit extractability. These include: the breakdown of phytate-protein complexes due to malt-derived phytase activity, cleavage of disulfide bonds by malt-derived reducing agents, structural modifications within the oat matrix, and limited proteolysis within complex oligomeric 12S globulins that alters protein conformation and enhances the solubility of globulin subunits or other similarly sized proteins.

First, given the high enzymatic potential of malt samples, it was postulated that the observed increase in protein extractability is primarily driven by one or more enzymatic activities. The observation that the effect of malt extract addition was observed only under acidic and not under neutral incubation conditions (data not shown) supports this, given the pH-dependent activities of oat and wheat endogenous enzymes (Arte et al., 2015; Blontrock et al., 2025). To further investigate this, enzymes in the malt extract were inactivated through ethanol boiling, and the heat-treated malt extract was added during incubation. For the DOW incubation with heat-treated OME (Table 2), protein extractabilities were comparable to those of suspensions without malt extract addition, indicating that the observed increase in protein extractability in non-kilned oats was driven by enzymatic activity in the oat malt. When heat-treated WME was added during 24 h incubation of kilned oats, protein extractability remained low (Table 2). However, in non-kilned oats, protein extractability was lower than that of the sample incubated with active WME, yet still increased to 39.3 ± 2.4%. This indicates that both enzymatic and non-enzymatic effects contribute to altering the oat proteins or oat matrix in ways that improve protein extractability. While not experimentally tested, a synergistic effect with the endogenous oat enzymatic system is plausible, alongside structural differences between kilned and non-kilned oat matrices (Pynket et al., 2024). Wheat malt extracts mainly contain extractable carbohydrates (dextrins, sugars and soluble dietary fibre) and, to a lesser extent, proteins other than enzymes, phenolics and minerals. One possible, although here unverified, hypothesis is that phenolic or other redox-active compounds may help maintain oat proteins in a more soluble state by limiting oxidative protein cross-linking and aggregation during incubation (Shahidi and Dissanayaka, 2023). Alternatively, soluble minerals from the WME may increase the ionic strength of the medium, thereby promoting protein solubilisation through electrostatic shielding and reduced protein-protein or protein-matrix interactions (R. Li and Xiong, 2021). Second, we hypothesised that malt-derived enzymes or reducing constituents may have caused the reduction of the disulfide bond between the two subunits of 12S globulins, potentially enhancing the solubility of the separate subunits. To test this hypothesis, reduced glutathione was added during incubation under a nitrogen atmosphere at a concentration intentionally higher than commonly reported, allowing assessment of the effects of an overdosed treatment. However, as shown in Table 2, this treatment did not increase protein extractability.

Third, it was considered that malt-derived phytases may have altered phytate–protein complexation, thereby affecting the protein extractability. The WME used in this study exhibited noticeably higher phytase activity than the OME, which would be consistent with the greater increase in protein extractability observed for WME. To this end, we tested the addition of Quantum Blue phytase during incubation to hydrolyse phytate naturally present in the DOW. This experiment was performed on cryo-milled DOW to prevent the possible effect of cell wall barriers and ensure enzyme-substrate contact. Preliminary experiments confirmed that, at the applied concentration of Quantum Blue phytase, all phosphate groups in DOW were cleaved from inositol (data not shown). However, the combined effect of phytate hydrolysis and physical cell wall breakdown did not lead to an increased protein extractability, compared with the incubated samples without phytase (Table 2). Indeed, although the highest β-glucanase activity was observed in OME, which would be expected to promote degradation of oat β-glucans and associated cell wall structures, this did not result in a more pronounced increase in protein extractability, compared to incubation with WME. This finding contrasts with the observations of Janssen et al. (2023), who reported enhanced protein extractability at pH 9.0 after ball milling-induced cell wall disruption. This discrepancy can probably be attributed to a different extraction pH, i.e. pH 9.0 in the study of Janssen et al. (2023) and pH 4.0 in the present study, as well as to the fact that ball milling can modify protein conformation and aggregation state, thereby influencing its extractability.

As a final strategy, two types of proteases were applied at varying dosages during incubation to evaluate whether similar levels of protein extractability could be achieved while maintaining extractable intact globulin subunits or other similarly sized proteins. The latter was hypothesised to occur at low dosages, as both OME and WME exhibited relatively low proteolytic activity. Under these conditions, limited protein hydrolysis within oligomeric globulins may enhance the extractability of intact oat globulin subunits. Previous studies have demonstrated that proteolytic enzymes such as alcalase and trypsin can significantly enhance oat protein extractability, even under acidic conditions, such as pH 4.0 (Brückner-Gühmann et al., 2018; Nella et al., 2023; Shahbal et al., 2023). As expected, both proteolytic enzymes used in our study led to an increased protein extractability, with maximum values of 40.1 ± 2.5% and 67.8 ± 1.2% for kilned and non-kilned oats with Flavourzyme, and 74.3 ± 2.1% and 85.8 ± 0.8% with papain, respectively (Fig. 4). For both oat types, a clear trend was observed: higher enzyme dosages resulted in greater protein extractability. Also here, the effect of protein hydrolysis was more pronounced when non-kilned oat was incubated, than when kilned oat was incubated, for the same reasons as explained in Section 3.1. Future research could focus on identifying and characterising other proteolytic enzymes that may induce modifications capable of improving the water solubility of oat proteins. In addition, the presence of other enzyme classes, such as glutaminases and cell wall-degrading enzymes could be examined in malt extracts, as these have been shown to increase oat protein extractability (Immonen et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2015; Spaen and Silva, 2021; Triantafyllou, 2014). Once the underlying mechanisms are clarified, further optimisation can be carried out to achieve sufficiently high extractability yields.

Lactic acid fermentation of kilned and non-kilned oat whole meal suspensions with oat and wheat malt extract

In section 3.1, the positive effect of malt extract addition was observed under acidic incubation conditions, a controlled model system for an acidic fermentation process. Here, we assessed whether the observed effects also occur during a lactic acid fermentation process, which represents a system closer to a real food production process. Kilned and non-kilned oat DOW suspensions containing 6.0% of OME or WME were fermented with L. plantarum. All fermentations began at an initial pH of approximately 6.1 ± 0.1 and underwent acidification, reaching a final pH of 3.2 ± 0.1. Representative acidification profiles for both kilned and non-kilned oats are provided in the supplementary data (Fig. S6). L. plantarum was inoculated in kilned oat suspensions at actual concentrations of 2.0 × 107 and 2.3 × 107 CFU/g suspension with OME and WME, respectively, and after 24 h, it was found to have proliferated to 5.0 × 109 and 2.3 × 109 CFU/g suspension. For non-kilned oat, the actual inoculation concentrations of L. plantarum were 2.9 × 107 and 3.3 × 107 CFU/g suspension with OME and WME, respectively, while after 24h, the microbial count reached 3.3 × 109 and 3.4 × 109 CFU/g suspension.

Both Loponen et al. (2007) and Lambrechts et al. (2026) stated that during lactic acid fermentation of kilned oat, starch and protein hydrolysis are limited, mainly due to the inactivated endogenous enzymatic system in kilned oat. The lack of fermentable sugars and free amino acids resulted in slower growth kinetics and an associated slower acidification (Loponen et al., 2006). However, when malt extract was added as an additional source of proteases and amylases to the fermentation process with L. plantarum, the strain grew significantly better. The final pH was much lower for kilned oats than for fermentations without malt extracts (pH around 4.3) (Lambrechts et al., 2026). In non-kilned oats, endogenous enzymes remain active and help produce nutrients available for the fermentation micro-organisms. As a result, the final pH dropped below 3.5, regardless of the malt extracts added.

In terms of their effect on the protein fraction, lactic acid bacteria are expected to have a minor influence on overall extractability, since they mainly have exoproteolytic activity to generate small nitrogen-containing compounds, used for their own metabolism (Kieliszek et al., 2021). Indeed, Lambrechts et al. (2026) demonstrated that a 24 h lactic acid fermentation of both kilned and non-kilned oats with L. plantarum did not lead to a greater effect than the activity of the endogenous oat-associated proteases and therefore did not lead to significant increases in protein extractability. Table 3 presents the observed protein extractabilities, DH and the amount of globulin subunits and albumins (or hydrolysis products with a similar molecular weight as these proteins, i.e. 4 – 85 kDa proteins) after 24 h of fermentation with L. plantarum with 6.0% (w/w) added OME or WME. As a reference, values obtained from fermentation experiments without any malt extract addition are included. Overall, a significant increase in protein extractability was observed when kilned oat was fermented with WME and non-kilned oat fermented with OME or WME, which was also reflected in the DH. This aligns with the incubation experiments described in Section 3.1. Also, similarly to the incubation experiments in Section 3.1, kilned oats after lactic acid fermentation exhibit lower protein extractability than non-kilned oats. Thus, the aggregation of proteins in kilned oat, together with kilning-induced changes in the matrix, reduce the availability of proteins for interaction with the malt enzymes or non-enzymatic components. When comparing the data from Table 3 to incubations with OME and WME (Fig. 1), the protein extractabilities were slightly lower after fermentation. Differences may arise from the slower acidification during fermentation compared with the incubation treatment (with instant acidification), resulting in different time windows during which enzymes are optimally active or become inhibited. Fig. 6 shows that fermentation of kilned and non-kilned oat suspensions with both OME and WME led to a significant reduction in the areas corresponding to globulin monomers, globulin subunits and albumins (or degradation products derived therefrom with a similar MW), closely matching the effects observed during incubation with malt extracts (Fig. 3, Fig. S3 and S4). This reduction was more pronounced with WME than with OME. In fermented non-kilned oat, regardless of whether OME or WME was applied, a higher abundance of low-MW peptides (eluting after 17 min) was observed compared to fermented kilned oat, and these peptides were fully recovered in the corresponding extracts. Although fermentation with malt extracts clearly enhances protein extractability, the extent to which proteins between 4 and 85 kDa (elution times between 10 and 17 min) were made extractable was slightly lower than in the incubated samples (Table 1). The main reason for this is probably that the final pH after fermentation was lower at pH 3.4 than the pH 4.0 of the incubation. Additional experiments have shown that a lower incubation pH led to a lower amount of extractable globulin subunits. Fig. 6 also shows that high-MW proteins (4-85 kDa) are more pronouncedly solubilised when WME is introduced during fermentation. Conclusions

The addition of malt extracts proved an effective strategy to enhance protein extractability from DOW suspensions under acidic fermentation-like conditions. The addition of WME increased protein extractability in both kilned and non-kilned oats, while OME addition only did so in non-kilned oats. Overall, malt extract addition during incubation/fermentation (with L. plantarum) had a more pronounced positive effect on protein extractability from non-kilned than from kilned oats, attributed to the lower availability of aggregated proteins in kilned oats. The most notable observation following such malt-supported incubation/fermentation was that proteins with a MW between 4 and 85 kDa (including globulin monomers, globulin subunits and albumins) were rendered extractable from the oat matrix, indicating that proteolytic solubilisation of peptides was not the only mechanism responsible for increased extractability. Further investigation revealed that the impact of malt extracts was primarily enzymatic, although non-enzymatic effects, especially in non-kilned oats and when using WME, also contributed to improved extractability. Neither disulfide bond reduction, assessed by glutathione addition, nor phytate degradation, assessed by phytase addition, significantly improved extractability, suggesting that neither protein linkages via disulfide bridges nor matrix-bound phytate play a critical role in determining the oat protein extractability. Protease treatments with Flavourzyme and papain substantially increased extractability, primarily through extensive hydrolysis into low-molecular-weight peptides. Lower enzyme dosages did not release extractable globulins or other similarly sized proteins, as was the case in the malt extract-supplemented samples. Overall, proteolytic enzymes are clear drivers of the increased protein extractability observed in this study, but there is an important additional contribution from other enzymes, such as glutaminase, or non-enzymatic compounds, which warrants further study. Although these findings were consistently observed across the biological replicates included in this study, further validation using a larger number of replicates, a broader range of oat and wheat malts, and additional fermentation microorganisms are needed to demonstrate their broader relevance.

Overall, these findings underscore the complexity of oat protein solubilisation and highlight the potential of targeted enzymatic strategies for improving extractability in future applications. The enhanced extractability of relatively intact, high-MW proteins observed in the present study may be particularly relevant, as such proteins are generally expected to contribute more effectively to interfacial functionality than extensively hydrolysed peptides. In the context of food applications such as oat-based beverages, it will be therefore essential to evaluate the functional properties of the extracted oat proteins, particularly their foaming and emulsifying capacities, and their contribution to product stability and sensory quality.
