Spatiotemporal evolution of carbon sequestration and the impact of key drivers under multiple SSP-RCP scenarios in the Yangtze River economic belt
Authors: Jin Sun, Xiangyang Liu, Youzhi An, Peng Zhao, Jiayun Li, Mengyi He, Huili Bao, Fengtai Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon sequestration (CS), a key component of climate change mitigation and carbon neutrality efforts, is strongly influenced by land use/land cover change (LUCC). However, the spatiotemporal evolution of CS in response to future LUCC trajectories under urbanization remains underexplored. Therefore, this study focuses on the Yangtze River Economic Belt (YREB), a region characterized by rapid urbanization and high carbon emissions, and develops an integrated SD-PLUS-InVEST modelling framework to evaluate the impacts of LUCC under three SSP-RCP scenarios on regional CS dynamics. Results show that, cropland is projected to decline by 5%, 10%, and 3%, while forestland increases by 8%, 3%, and 5% under SSP1-2.6, SSP5-8.5, and SSP2-4.5, respectively. Correspondingly, CS shows a 12% increase under SSP1-2.6, an 8% decrease under SSP5-8.5, and a moderate enhancement under SSP2-4.5. The XGBoost-SHAP analysis quantified the impacts of key drivers, revealing that elevated temperature and population growth are strongly correlated with declines in CS, while economic growth is positively correlated with enhanced CS capacity. This research provides valuable insights into how LUCC affects CS under varying development scenarios, offering actionable guidance for formulating regional land-use policies that promote CS and contribute to climate mitigation.Keywords: Carbon sequestration (CS), Land use/land cover change (LUCC), Multi-scenarios, Yangtze river economic belt (YREB), Carbon neutrality target

Introduction

Carbon sequestration (CS) in terrestrial ecosystems is a cornerstone of global climate regulation, playing a vital role in offsetting anthropogenic carbon emissions and advancing the carbon neutrality agenda [1, 3]. Terrestrial landscapes such as forestlands and grasslands act as critical carbon sinks by fixing atmospheric CO₂ into biomass and soil organic carbon [4, 8]. However, accelerating Land Use and Land Cover Change (LUCC)—including deforestation, urban sprawl, and agricultural expansion—has substantially undermined these carbon sinks, disrupted regional carbon balances, and exacerbated atmospheric carbon loads [9, 10]. Recent work underscores the need to evaluate CS under credible, scenario-consistent LUCC pathways so that assessments speak directly to planning and policy.

Numerous models have been developed to simulate LUCC, such as Logistic Cellular Automata (CA), CA-Markov, and the Future Land Use Simulation (FLUS) model [11, 12]. These models have been widely applied for spatial prediction of future LUCC patterns, yet they often fall short in capturing the dynamic interactions between socio-economic development and environmental processes, which are essential for scenario-based land system forecasting [13]. To overcome this limitation, system dynamics (SD) models have been increasingly used for LUCC research due to their strength in simulating feedback mechanisms, delayed effects, and nonlinear interactions among key drivers such as population, economy, and environment [14, 15]. By incorporating these interdependencies, SD models enhance the realism of future land demand projections; however, they are inherently non-spatial and unable to produce gridded LUCC maps, which restricts their capacity to analyze spatial heterogeneity. In contrast, the Patch-generating Land Use Simulation (PLUS) model integrates CA mechanisms with rule-mining algorithms and stochastic seed initialization, enabling it to identify spatial drivers and accurately simulate the expansion patterns of different land types. It not only provides detailed spatial projections but also reveals the underlying mechanisms of land conversion [16]. Therefore, coupling SD and PLUS models strategically integrates top-down socio-economic feedbacks with bottom-up spatial simulation, addressing the limitations of existing LUCC models and offering a robust framework for simulating future LUCC scenarios under complex socio-environmental conditions [17, 21].

Despite methodological advances, many LUCC models often focus on a limited range of scenarios, such as economic development, ecological protection, or urban expansion, and typically consider only a narrow set of driving factors [22, 24]. While these models provide valuable insights, they cannot fully capture the complex and uncertain interactions between socio-economic and environmental processes. Moreover, traditional models often fail to integrate feedback mechanisms that govern both the spatial and temporal dynamics of LUCC [19, 21, 25]. In contrast, the SSP-RCP framework, which combines Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) with Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), offers a more integrated approach by considering both socio-economic development and climate change projections [26, 29]. Rather than juxtaposing single “development versus protection” narratives, the SSP–RCP matrix provides multiple policy-relevant futures that can be translated into model inputs—such as land demand and climatic exposure—and examined for their implications for CS. Specifically, the SSP1-2.6 scenario represents an ideal path for low-carbon transformation, targeting substantial greenhouse gas emission reductions through stringent mitigation efforts. The SSP2-4.5 scenario offers a balanced approach with moderate emission reductions, while the SSP5-8.5 scenario illustrates a high-emission future with continued reliance on fossil fuels, exacerbating climate risks. Within this framework, the YREB serves as a policy-relevant setting to investigate how distinct socio-economic and climatic trajectories shape land-use pathways and consequently alter CS patterns that matter for regional governance.

In parallel, various methods have been developed for estimating CS in past studies, such as the cumulative approach, assimilation-based models, and chamber-based micro-gas techniques [30, 31]. While effective in localized or single-type ecosystems, these methods struggle to capture the spatial heterogeneity and long-term dynamics of CS, particularly in large, mixed-use landscapes. To address this, the Integrated Valuation of Ecosystem Services and Trade-offs (InVEST) model, widely adopted for spatially explicit CS assessments, enables both quantification and visualization of CS across heterogeneous landscapes [32]. Unlike methods limited to forests or croplands [33, 36], InVEST allows integration across multiple land types, offering a more precise and realistic characterization of carbon dynamics at the spatial grid scale.

Although quantifying CS is essential, it cannot alone guide effective land-use decisions. Most existing studies emphasize the impact of LUCC on CS, yet they overlook the identification and quantification of its key driving factors-an equally critical step for developing targeted policies and sustainable management strategies [37, 40]. Recent advances in machine learning, particularly the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm, provide a powerful tool for attributing CS responses to socio-economic and environmental factors. While XGBoost has been applied in ecological prediction and land suitability assessments, its use in explaining CS variation across multiple LUCC scenarios remains limited. By leveraging environmental samples and automatically optimizing parameters, XGBoost not only enhances prediction accuracy but also provides interpretable insights into the key drivers of CS change [41, 42].

As China strives to peak carbon emissions by 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality by 2060, the YREB-one of the most urbanized and economically significant regions in China-is central to achieving these goals. Accounting for nearly half of the nation’s GDP, the YREB faces pressing challenges due to rapid urbanization and industrialization. Extensive LUCC, including the conversion of forestlands and grasslands to built-up areas, have markedly reduced the region’s CS capacity, thereby intensifying carbon management difficulties. Understanding the complex interactions between socio-economic development, LUCC, and CS within the YREB is thus crucial for informed policy-making. In light of these challenges, this study innovatively applies XGBoost-SHAP to quantify and analyze the key drivers of CS, to provide deeper insights into how socio-economic, climatic, and land-use factors influence CS. Meanwhile, adopting a more in-depth perspective, a provincial-level analysis across the YREB is conducted to offer a more detailed and region-specific assessment of CS dynamics under multiple SSP-RCP scenarios. These improvements help to put forward more targeted policy suggestions for various provinces and cities to achieve their carbon neutrality goals. The study’s objectives are: (1) to simulate future LUCC dynamics under different socio-economic and climate scenarios; (2) to assess the spatiotemporal evolution of CS under these scenarios; and (3) to identify key drivers influencing CS across these scenarios, with a particular focus on regional disparities to inform more effective CS strategies.

Study area

As shown in Fig. 1, the Yangtze River Economic Belt (YREB) spans 11 provinces, traversing three major regions in eastern, central, and western China. It covers an area of approximately 2.5 × 106 km2, accounting for 21.3% of the country's total area. The region's topography is characterized by a west-to-east gradient, with three primary landforms: hills, plains, and mountains [43]. The YREB plays a crucial role in soil conservation, water retention, and biodiversity preservation in China [44]. From 2011 to 2021, the region's GDP experienced substantial growth, with its share in the national economy gradually rising from 43.81% to 46.30%. Concurrently, the expansion of build-up land in the YREB grew by 39% between 2001 and 2021, primarily through the conversion of ecological land types such as forests and grasslands. This urban expansion has significantly contributed to carbon emissions, which totaled approximately 3.34 × 1013 tons during the same period—equivalent to one-fourth of the country's total emissions [45]. Given its ecological importance and intensive development pressure, the YREB offers a representative and policy-relevant case for exploring the interplay between LUCC and CS under future climate and socio-economic scenarios.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The datasets used in this study include land use, socio-economic, population, and climate variables, supporting the implementation of SD, PLUS, and InVEST models under multiple SSP-RCP scenarios.

LUCC Land use/cover data were obtained from the China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD) developed by Yang and Huang [46], covering six land categories: Cropland, Forestland, Grassland, Water, Built-up land and Unused land. The data were resampled to a spatial resolution of 1000 m. Raster layers from 2000, 2010, and 2020 were used for CS estimation via InVEST model, while 2020 data served as the base map for study area overview.

For SD modeling, annual statistical data from 2000 to 2019 were sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook and the YREB Big Data Platform. These include GDP, secondary industry output, total population, population growth rate, and climate variables (annual mean temperature and precipitation).

The PLUS model employed raster-based driving variables, including population density, GDP, annual mean temperature, precipitation, NPP, and soil type. These datasets were provided by the Resource and Environmental Sciences Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, at 1000-m spatial resolution and annual temporal resolution.

Climate scenario data were derived from Wang et al. (2022) based on CMIP6 projections under different SSP-RCP combinations[21]. These include annual projections of mean temperature and precipitation used for future LUCC and CS simulation.

In this study, some datasets cover the time period from 2000 to 2019, while others span 2000 to 2020. To ensure that these datasets with different time spans can be used consistently in the analysis, we performed precise data matching. First, for the annual statistical data from 2000 to 2019, we used the SD model to accurately capture the trends and changes during this period. For the 2020 data, we used it as a reference point to ensure the model's accuracy. Furthermore, in estimating CS, we used raster data from 2000 to 2020 for the calculations and applied the same driver selection criteria, ensuring effective matching between the datasets.

LUCC forecasting framework based on SD-PLUS modeling

In this study, we established a complete research framework (Fig. 2). Firstly, we developed an SD model based on the highly economically developed YREB region to capture these interactions and integrated four key subsystems: economic, population, climate and land cover changes. Each subsystem is customized according to the characteristics of YREB. In the economic subsystem, based on the actual situation of YREB, we selected variables such as GDP and secondary industry output that can reflect economic development. The impact of economic growth driven by urbanization, economic development and infrastructure development on LUCC was simulated. The population subsystem tracks urban and rural population shifts, influencing the demand for agricultural products and urban infrastructure, which, in turn, drives LUCC transitions. The climate subsystem, based on YREB's abundant water resources and extensive distribution of ecological land, simulates the long-term impacts of temperature and precipitation changes on agricultural productivity, water resources, and ecosystem services. It is predicted that climate change will further intensify agricultural production and natural ecosystems in this region. Finally, the LUCC subsystem models the conversion of forestland, grassland, and water into urban and cropland, which reduces the region's CS capacity.

Multiple -scenarios settings

In this study, three representative SSP-RCP scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5) were integrated into the SD model to simulate LUCC under varying socio-economic and climatic conditions. To simulate LUCC under divergent future pathways, this study incorporated three representative SSP-RCP scenarios, i.e., SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5, into the SD model framework [49]. These scenarios reflect contrasting socio-economic and climate trajectories: SSP1-2.6 emphasizes sustainability and low emissions, SSP2-4.5 represents a middle-of-the-road development pathway, while SSP5-8.5 simulates a fossil fuel-intensive, high-emission future.

The SD model was parameterized using four key variables-average annual population growth rate, average annual GDP growth rate, annual average temperature change, and annual precipitation change-to capture the interplay between socio-economic development and climate change (Table 2). Future trajectories of these parameters were extracted from published scenario datasets and adjusted to align with the core assumptions embedded within each SSP-RCP storyline [50]. This multi-scenario approach allows for a robust exploration of LUCC responses to varying development and climate conditions.

Carbon sequestration calculation based on InVEST model

To estimate CS, we employed the carbon storage and sequestration module of the InVEST model. This module quantifies the total carbon stock across land cover types by integrating land use maps with predefined carbon density parameters for different carbon pools. Specifically, the model considers four carbon pools: aboveground biomass, belowground biomass, soil organic carbon, and dead organic matter. The formula used in InVEST for calculating CS is:

Ctotal represents the aggregate carbon stock with in YREB. This total comprises, Cabove is aboveground biomass, Cbelow is belowground biomass, Csoil is soil organic biomass, and Cdead is dead organic biomass. Accurate estimation of each carbon pool relies on their respective carbon density parameters, which characterize the amount of carbon stored per unit biomass or soil mass. In this study, these carbon density parameters were derived from the comprehensive synthesis by Pan et al. [51], who compiled extensive empirical data and analyses on carbon densities across various ecosystems.

Driver analysis using the XGBoost model and SHAP model

In this study, we employed the XGBoost model [52] to assess the impact of various driving factors on CS under different SSP-RCP scenarios. XGBoost is an advanced gradient boosting algorithm renowned for its exceptional performance and flexibility. It combines multiple decision trees into a robust predictive model, widely used for big data analysis and complex predictions [37, 40, 53]. Key advantages of XGBoost include support for parallel computation, the ability to handle features without explicit classification, assignment, or weighting computations, and its effective handling of sparse data.

To enhance the interpretability of the XGBoost model outputs, we introduced Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) [54], a fast and efficient method for explaining model predictions. SHAP quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model's predictions and helps distinguish whether the contributions are positive or negative, offering deeper insights into the relationships between model inputs and outputs [55]. By seamlessly integrating with XGBoost, the Tree SHAP algorithm allows for efficient estimation of SHAP values, which enables us to explain the contributions of each driver to CS predictions.

In this study, six key driving factors were selected to assess their impact on CS under different SSP-RCP scenarios. These factors include climate variables (such as temperature and precipitation), land-use dynamics (such as forest cover and urbanization), and socio-economic indicators (such as GDP and population density). The selection of these factors was based on their direct and indirect influence on CS processes, and their relevance and availability in the study region (YREB). Although previous studies have mainly focused on a set of smaller natural factors, our approach first considers the correlation between driving factors and the study area, integrating socio-economic driving factors and climatic factors to ensure the validity of the results as well as their professionalism for the study area.

Results

Historical and future trends in LUCC

Historical trends in LUCC from 2000 to 2020

As shown in Fig. 4, forestland remained the dominant land cover type in the YREB throughout 2000–2020, followed by cropland. Over this period, a net reduction of 24,390 km2 was observed in cropland area, accompanied by a modest increase of 5968 km2 in forestland, indicating ongoing land conversion from cropland to forestland, likely influenced by afforestation and ecological restoration policies. Built-up land exhibited the most significant expansion, increasing by 32,909 km2, which strongly reflects accelerated urbanization and industrial development in the region. In contrast, grassland experienced a substantial loss of 15,648 km2, primarily due to its conversion to built-up or cropland. The area of water decreased slightly by 47 km2, suggesting relatively stable hydrological land cover during this period. Unused land, after an initial period of decline, showed a gradual upward trend, potentially resulting from the abandonment of marginal cropland or changes in land classification standards. These changes collectively illustrate the dynamic shifts in land resource allocation driven by rapid economic development and policy interventions in the YREB.

Historical and future trends in CS

Historical trends in CS from 2000 to 2020

The InVEST model was employed to quantify land-use CS in the study area from 2000 to 2020 (Fig. 8). The total CS was estimated at 2.744 × 1010 tons in 2000, declining to 2.735 × 1010 tons in 2010 and 2.721 × 1010 tons in 2020. These figures represent marginal declines of 0.3% from 2000 to 2010 and a further 0.32% from 2010 to 2020. Over the entire 20-year period, CS declined by approximately 1.71 × 10⁸ tons, reflecting a continuous and gradual downward trend. This persistent loss is likely linked to land use transitions such as urban expansion and the conversion of high-CS ecosystems (e.g., forestland and grassland) into built-up or agricultural areas.

Spatial distribution of CS of the YREB from 2000 to 2020

Spatially, the distribution of CS remained relatively consistent across the three time points. High CS values were concentrated in forested regions, reflecting the strong CS capacity of mature vegetation. In contrast, lower CS levels were observed in areas dominated by built-up land and intensive cropland use, particularly around the Yangtze River Estuary and parts of the Sichuan Basin. These regions are typically subject to high anthropogenic pressure and land development. Additionally, grassland and water showed noticeable reductions in CS, with grassland experiencing the most significant decline due to widespread land conversion. Meanwhile, unused land showed a gradual increase following a brief decline, potentially due to the abandonment of marginal agricultural land or changing land classification practices.

Multi-scenario CS projections for 2030 and 2060

Figure 9 illustrates the spatial and temporal patterns of projected CS in the YREB under three SSP-RCP scenarios for 2030 and 2060. The total CS values under SSP1-2.6 are estimated at 2.703 × 1010 tons in 2030 and 2.701 × 1010 tons in 2060, indicating a relatively stable carbon sink. In comparison, CS under SSP2-4.5 declines moderately to 2.671 × 1010 tons in 2030 and 2.624 × 1010 tons in 2060. The most substantial reduction is observed under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, where CS drops to 2.668 × 1010 tons in 2030 and 2.623 × 1010 tons in 2060. These results suggest that CS potential is highest under low-emission, sustainability-oriented development and lowest under fossil fuel-intensive, high-emission pathways.

Spatial distribution of future CS of the YREB in 2030 and 2060 under three scenarios

Spatially, the highest CS values are concentrated in the southern and southwestern parts of the region, where vegetation cover and forest density are relatively high. In contrast, areas with intensive built-up land-particularly the Yangtze River Estuary, central urban agglomerations, and the eastern coastal zone-consistently exhibit low CS potential due to vegetation loss and landscape fragmentation. The SSP5-8.5 scenario demonstrates the most pronounced spatial degradation, characterized by widespread declines in CS in peri-urban and marginal ecological areas. This reflects the compounded impact of land conversion and climate-induced vegetation stress under high-emission trajectories. Conversely, the SSP1-2.6 scenario maintains ecological stability, preserving forest-dominated regions and limiting urban encroachment, thereby supporting sustained CS.

Figure 10 shows the analysis of the CS trend in the YREB region from 2000 to 2060, revealing significant regional differences, with provinces and cities exhibiting varying patterns of CS changes. These differences reflect the complex interplay of LUCC, economic development, and climate change. Overall, CS capacity has been declining in most provinces due to urbanization, industrialization, and agricultural intensification. However, the rate of decline differs across regions, with some areas experiencing more significant losses than others. Provinces like Shanghai and Jiangsu have seen sharp declines in CS, while areas like Sichuan and Yunnan have shown more favorable trends, primarily due to their natural ecosystems and lower levels of urbanization. These variations highlight the diverse capacities of regions to maintain or enhance CS amid growing socio-economic pressures.

Comparison of CS by provinces from 2000 to 2060 under three Scenarios

Shanghai, Jiangsu and Anhui are the regions where CS has declined from 2000 to 2060, especially under the high-emission SSP5-8.5 scenario. This decline is primarily driven by the rapid urbanization, industrial growth, and agricultural expansion that have led to widespread land conversion, significantly reducing the region's CS potential. For example, Shanghai experienced the largest reduction in CS, with a 25% decrease from 2000 to 2060, largely due to the rapid expansion of built-up areas and the conversion of forestland and grassland into urban infrastructure. Similarly, Jiangsu and Anhui also exhibit a pattern of forestland being gradually converted into urban areas, further exacerbating the loss of CS capacity. Despite their high levels of economic development, these provinces face challenges in balancing industrial growth with ecological preservation, leading to the depletion of natural carbon sinks. The rapid transformation of agricultural and ecological lands into urban infrastructure has resulted in considerable losses in CS potential, while forestland preservation remains limited. This demonstrates the need for policies that support both economic growth and ecological sustainability.

Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou have exhibited positive trends in CS from 2000 to 2060. These regions, located in southwestern and central China, have benefited from higher NPP which has contributed to an increase in their CS capacity. Yunnan and Guizhou, with their rich natural landscapes, have implemented strong ecological policies prioritizing afforestation and reforestation, which have led to an expansion of forestlands and a significant enhancement of CS. Similarly, Sichuan's forestland expansion and grassland protection have substantially increased CS. These regions have relatively low levels of urbanization and industrial development, which has mitigated the pressure to convert natural ecosystems into urban and agricultural land. The positive trends in these areas emphasize the importance of protecting natural vegetation and supporting sustainable land-use practices to boost CS. The success in these provinces shows that focusing on ecosystem health, especially through the protection and expansion of forestland, can help mitigate the negative impacts of LUCC on CS.

Provinces like Hubei and Jiangxi demonstrate relatively stable CS trends, characterized by fluctuations but without significant long-term increases or decreases. These provinces have experienced moderate urbanization and industrial growth, but the extent of LUCC has been slower compared to more economically developed regions. The conversion of forestland and grassland to urban land has been limited in these areas, allowing their CS capacity to remain stable over time. The balance between urban development and effective ecological protection has played a crucial role in maintaining stable CS levels. The stability of forestland and grassland in these regions contributes to the overall stability of CS, underscoring the role of prudent land management practices in preserving natural carbon sinks. The ability of Hubei and Jiangxi to maintain their CS levels highlights the importance of balanced land use policies that prioritize ecological conservation alongside economic development.

In summary, the YREB exhibits varied CS trends across different provinces, which reflect the regional differences in urbanization, industrialization, and ecological protection. In high-GDP and rapidly urbanizing provinces like Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Anhui, CS has declined significantly due to extensive land conversion, urban sprawl, and intensified industrial activities. These processes have led to substantial losses in forestland and grassland, reducing the capacity of natural ecosystems to sequester carbon. Conversely, provinces with lower economic development levels and less urban pressure, such as Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou, have experienced increases in CS, primarily driven by forest expansion and ecological restoration initiatives. Regions like Hubei and Jiangxi have maintained relatively stable CS levels by balancing moderate urban growth with effective ecological conservation measures. These contrasting trends underscore the importance of region-specific strategies that integrate economic development with environmental sustainability to enhance CS capacity and support China's carbon neutrality goals.

Analysis of drivers for CS in multi-scenarios

The SHAP summary plot presented in Fig. 11 illustrates the dynamic influence of key variables on CS across historical and scenario-based futures. In 2000, GDP and NPP exerted the strongest positive effects on CS, indicating that economic development and ecosystem productivity were central drivers of carbon sink capacity. In contrast, POP, showed a more dispersed pattern with relatively limited impact. Between 2010 and 2020, the positive contribution of GDP to CS further intensified, while NPP also demonstrated a gradually increasing effect, although accompanied by fluctuations. Over the same period, the influence of TEM and Soil became more pronounced, with TEM showing a clear and growing negative effect on CS by 2020, likely reflecting ecosystem stress induced by climate warming.

Looking ahead to the year 2030 under the three SSP scenarios, GDP and NPP consistently remain the primary positive drivers of CS. In the low-emission SSP1-2.6 scenario, NPP's contribution strengthens markedly, underscoring the rising importance of vegetation productivity in sustainable carbon management. Under the moderate-emission SSP2-4.5 scenario, both GDP and NPP maintain significant influence, while the role of TEM becomes more prominent, suggesting that warming and ecosystem responses will play a larger role in shaping CS. In the high-emission SSP5-8.5 scenario, although GDP and NPP still contribute positively, the negative effect of TEM increases sharply, indicating that intensifying climate pressure could erode carbon sink potential despite continued economic and ecological inputs.

By 2060, these trends diverge further across scenarios. In SSP1-2.6, GDP continues to provide a stable positive contribution, while NPP remains the most influential factor, consistent with a trajectory of sustained ecological investment. Under SSP2-4.5, the importance of NPP increases substantially and surpasses GDP, while TEM exerts a more significant negative effect. In SSP5-8.5, both GDP and NPP show a slight decline in their influence, whereas TEM becomes the dominant negative factor, highlighting the compounding challenges posed by long-term warming on terrestrial CS. Collectively, these findings reveal the evolving hierarchy of CS drivers, emphasizing that while economic growth and ecosystem productivity remain vital for enhancing carbon sinks, the escalating influence of temperature will be a critical constraint in high-emission futures.

Discussion

Analysis of land use changes and CS under different SSP-RCP scenarios

This study develops an integrated modeling framework that couples the SD, PLUS, and InVEST models to simulate LUCC and CS trajectories under multiple SSP-RCP scenarios for the YREB in 2030 and 2060. Unlike conventional single-model approaches [56, 58], this multi-model framework effectively captures the dynamic interactions among socio-economic factors, spatial land-use strategies, and ecosystem service responses, providing a more system-oriented perspective for ecological planning. The integration of these models enables the representation of both macro-scale socio-economic processes and fine-scale spatial land use patterns, capturing feedback loops between urban expansion, ecological conservation, and CS. It also addresses the spatial and temporal limitations of traditional models by incorporating scenario-specific development constraints, sectoral growth rates, and ecosystem service valuation. As a result, the framework produces more realistic LUCC projections and allows for a nuanced assessment of how alternative land use strategies and policy decisions may affect regional CS capacity [28, 29, 37, 40, 59].

The simulation results reveal pronounced divergence in LUCC and CS outcomes across the three scenarios. Under the low-emission SSP1-2.6 pathway, characterized by ecological prioritization and strong mitigation policies, forestland expands significantly and grassland is preserved, resulting in a 12% increase in CS by 2060. These findings align with previous studies [56] highlighting forestland recovery under sustainability-focused trajectories in China. In contrast, the high-emission SSP5-8.5 scenario leads to intensive urban sprawl and agricultural expansion, accompanied by substantial forestland and water loss. This results in an 8% decline in CS due to widespread ecosystem degradation and landscape fragmentation. Built-up land becomes dominant, displacing high-carbon land covers and reducing the region’s carbon sink capacity. The intermediate SSP2-4.5 scenario reflects a development-conservation trade-off. Moderate urban growth is observed, but forest gains are limited and less cohesive than in SSP1-2.6. Although CS losses are smaller than in SSP5-8.5, the scenario still reflects missed opportunities for maximizing ecological co-benefits. This trajectory mirrors those reported by [37, 40], where partial mitigation pathways yield modest but insufficient CS outcomes.

In the study of land use CS in Henan Province and the Yellow River Basin, the CS in the SSP1-2.6 scenario, which focuses on ecological protection, was the highest, while the CS in the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios decreased to varying degrees. It is expected that the CS will gradually decline in the coming years. These findings are consistent with the trends identified in this study [28, 29, 59]. Similarly, [6, 7] also indicates that from 2000 to 2020, carbon sinks in Sichuan and Chongqing showed a declining trend due to urbanization, consistent with the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios. However, the SSP1-2.6 scenario is projected to maintain or increase carbon sinks in certain regions. These results highlight the sensitivity of CS to different development strategies, with the SSP1-2.6 scenario enabling synergistic gains in land-use efficiency and ecological function, while SSP5-8.5 illustrates the ecological cost of unconstrained economic growth. These findings underscore the need for land-use strategies that integrate spatial planning, forest protection, and emission controls to enhance regional CS. When viewed in the context of global carbon management, the importance of integrating sustainable land-use strategies into China’s carbon neutrality efforts becomes even more pronounced. These strategies, particularly in high-emission scenarios, align with global efforts to optimize terrestrial carbon sinks and limit carbon emissions in line with international climate agreements, such as the Paris Agreement. The integration of ecological restoration and low-carbon development policies is critical to ensuring that regional land-use changes positively contribute to global carbon balance, ultimately supporting the global carbon budget and enhancing efforts to mitigate climate change.

Furthermore, as the global community works toward achieving net-zero emissions, regional CS must play a crucial role in offsetting emissions and contributing to global carbon neutrality. The balance of CS across regions, like the YREB, directly impacts global carbon management goals. The need for policies that promote sustainable land-use, forest preservation, and the restoration of degraded ecosystems is increasingly urgent, particularly in regions facing intensive urban expansion and land conversion. By aligning regional strategies with global carbon balance targets, policymakers can maximize the contributions of carbon sinks at both local and global levels.

The CS estimates obtained in this study align closely with other regional studies, further confirming that the InVEST model provides reliable projections for CS dynamics under different socio-economic and land-use scenarios. This comparative validation serves as an additional layer of confidence in the robustness of our model’s outputs, reinforcing its applicability not only to regional contexts but also to global carbon management efforts, offering valuable insights into how land-use decisions can influence both regional and global carbon budgets.

Implications for policy and regional management

The spatial divergence in CS trends across the YREB underscores the critical need for region-specific policy design to support China's carbon neutrality goals. In economically developed provinces such as Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang, CS has shown a consistent decline, primarily driven by rapid urbanization and extensive land conversion. To reverse this trajectory, it is essential to transition from rapid economic growth to high-quality, ecologically sustainable development, thereby maintaining the positive influence of GDP on CS and enhancing regional environmental performance [60, 61].

In contrast, less developed provinces such as Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou have exhibited increasing CS trends, benefiting from favorable natural conditions and relatively low levels of urbanization. In these regions, policy interventions should prioritize ecological restoration, forestland expansion, and sustainable agricultural practices to strengthen carbon sink capacity. For example, Yunnan and Guizhou, with their abundant forest cover and relatively stable ecosystems, could benefit from policies that focus on forest regeneration, agroforestry practices, and biodiversity conservation. Strengthening eco-tourism and sustainable agricultural practices can also help increase CS in these regions, providing a model for low-carbon rural development. Similarly, Sichuan, with its diverse geography and relatively lower urbanization, should focus on conservation of high-carbon ecosystems such as forests and wetlands while promoting green infrastructure in urbanizing areas. In more developed provinces, such as Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang, CS has shown a consistent decline, primarily driven by rapid urbanization and extensive land conversion. In these provinces, policy interventions must shift from conventional urban development to high-quality, ecologically sustainable development. These regions could prioritize green urban planning, which involves integrating green spaces, urban forests, and sustainable infrastructure in rapidly expanding urban areas. Moreover, the expansion of carbon capture technologies and renewable energy sources such as solar and wind should be prioritized to offset the loss of carbon sinks due to land-use changes. Eco-compensation mechanisms could be implemented to incentivize the restoration of degraded areas and the protection of critical natural resources, such as forested areas and wetlands, to help preserve carbon sinks.

Provinces such as Hubei and Jiangxi, where CS has remained relatively stable, represent a middle ground where both development and conservation can be balanced. For these regions, policies should focus on maintaining the delicate balance between urban expansion and ecological conservation. Urbanization should be managed through smart city planning, where urban development is designed with green infrastructure, such as parks and green rooftops, while maintaining forest and agricultural land in peri-urban areas. The implementation of urban–rural integration policies could help mitigate land-use conflicts and promote sustainable agricultural practices that enhance CS.

The spatial heterogeneity in CS is closely linked to the influence of key driving factors such as GDP, TEM, and NPP. This study identifies GDP, TEM, and NPP as the primary determinants of CS variation. While GDP can have a positive effect on CS by enabling investment in green infrastructure and land management, this benefit may be offset by concurrent increases in temperature and population pressure. Higher temperatures and denser populations tend to reduce CS through increased ecosystem stress and land conversion. For regions with high NPP, such as Guizhou, Yunnan, and Sichuan, greater vegetation productivity and healthier ecosystems lead to stronger carbon uptake. Forestlands, owing to their high nutrient cycling and biomass accumulation capacity, make substantial contributions to regional CS.

To mitigate the negative impacts of rising temperatures and urban expansion on CS, governments should implement comprehensive climate mitigation strategies [42]. These include reducing greenhouse gas emissions, accelerating the transition to low-carbon energy, and promoting the widespread use of renewable energy sources such as solar and wind. In regions characterized by high NPP, policy efforts should center on expanding forest cover, restoring degraded ecosystems, and promoting sustainable forestry practices. These policies are essential for enhancing long-term CS and for ensuring that CS continues to contribute significantly to national carbon neutrality targets [5, 8].

This study reinforces the importance of regionally tailored strategies for maximizing CS and achieving national climate targets. To support China’s carbon neutrality goals, policymakers must not only focus on national-level strategies but also adapt policies to regional contexts. By promoting synergy between economic development and ecological protection, policymakers can effectively optimize the interaction between LUCC and carbon sink potential [62]. Specifically, policy interventions must focus on region-specific land-use strategies that balance urbanization with ecological conservation, ensuring that the CS potential is fully realized. This approach would contribute to long-term regional sustainability and national carbon neutrality, ensuring that high-carbon ecosystems are preserved while enabling low-carbon economic growth [63, 64]. Furthermore, regional policies should be designed to integrate green infrastructure development, sustainable agriculture, and forest protection into urban planning to promote carbon neutrality across diverse geographic contexts.

Limitations and perspectives

This study provides valuable insights into the dynamics of LUCC and CS, particularly in the YREB under various SSP-RCP scenarios. However, certain factors should be considered when interpreting the findings. One key aspect to note is the sensitivity of the results to the assumptions used in the SD model and the carbon density parameters in the InVEST model [65, 66]. The assumptions regarding sectoral growth rates and development constraints in the SD model could potentially influence the projected LUCC patterns and their resulting CS impacts. Similarly, the carbon density parameters used in the InVEST model, which are based on existing literature and general assumptions, could introduce uncertainties in the CS estimates. Future studies should explore the sensitivity of the model’s results to variations in these assumptions and examine alternative growth trajectories to assess the robustness of the findings.

Additionally, while the XGBoost-SHAP analysis provides valuable insights into the drivers of CS, potential multicollinearity among the drivers could affect the interpretation of the results. Although XGBoost is robust in handling complex, non-linear relationships, the presence of multicollinearity might influence the accuracy and stability of the SHAP values, leading to less reliable interpretations of the individual contributions of each driver [67]. XGBoost’s ability to handle such challenges comes from its regularization techniques and tree-based structure, but for even greater interpretability, future research could incorporate advanced methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) or regularization techniques specifically designed to address multicollinearity issues [68]. These approaches would enhance the model's ability to provide more reliable and meaningful interpretations.

In light of the inherent uncertainties in socio-economic development and climate policies, future research could integrate more dynamic modeling approaches that allow for real-time data incorporation and feedback mechanisms, improving the responsiveness of the model and enhancing predictive accuracy [69]. This study primarily focuses on LUCC and CS but does not address broader ecosystem services such as water quality, biodiversity, and air purification. Expanding future models to incorporate a wider range of ecosystem services could offer a more comprehensive understanding of the ecological trade-offs involved in different land-use scenarios.

Conclusion

Enhancing our understanding of spatiotemporal variations in CS under alternative development scenarios is essential for informing effective land use and climate policies in support of carbon neutrality. Taking the Yangtze River Economic Belt (YREB) as a case study, this research employed an integrated modeling framework that couples System Dynamics (SD), PLUS, and InVEST models to simulate LUCC and its impacts on CS from 2000 to 2060 across three SSP-RCP scenarios. The main conclusions are drawn as follows:

Land use trajectories differ markedly across scenarios. Under the sustainability-oriented SSP1-2.6, forestland expands by 8% and grassland remains largely intact, while built-up land increases moderately by 3.5%. SSP2-4.5 reflects a development-conservation trade-off, with forestland increasing by 5% and built-up land by 5.6%. In contrast, SSP5-8.5 shows intensified ecological degradation, including a 3% reduction in forestland, a 1.8% loss in water, and a substantial 9.1% rise in built-up land.
