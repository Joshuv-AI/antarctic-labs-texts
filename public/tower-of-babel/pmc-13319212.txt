Ubiquitin-specific protease 11 suppresses cuproptosis in colorectal cancer by regulating the ubiquitination and stability of ISCU
Authors: Hailin Ke, Guowei Zhang, Weili Lin, Yuecui Jiang, Meina Wu, Yueyi Zhang, Weiyang Song, Fuhai Wang, Songling Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the most common and deadly malignancies, with limited effective treatment options. Cuproptosis is a novel regulated cell death triggered by mitochondrial copper overload (Cu2+) and has been implicated in several cancers. However, the mechanisms governing cuproptosis in CRC are largely unknown. In this study, we investigated the role of ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), key regulators of protein stability, in modulating cuproptosis in CRC. Through integrative analysis of publicly available CRC transcriptomic datasets, we identified oncogenic USPs potentially involved in cuproptosis regulation. Among them, USP11 emerged as significantly overexpressed in CRC tissues, and its elevated expression was correlated with poorer patient survival. We next examined the functional role of USP11 in two CRC cell lines and patient-derived CRC organoids. Cells were treated with elesclomol and CuCl2, a combination known to induce cuproptosis. Functional assays revealed that USP11 promotes cell viability, colony formation, survival, and migration/invasion and suppresses cuproptosis in response to treatment. Mechanistic studies showed that USP11 directly binds to and deubiquitinates ISCU, a scaffold protein essential for iron–sulfur cluster assembly and known to bind Cu2+. This deubiquitination stabilizes ISCU, thereby preventing its proteasomal degradation. Rescue experiments further confirmed that ISCU is a key downstream effector mediating USP11’s cuproptosis resistance effect. Finally, in patient-derived CRC organoid models, knockdown of USP11 significantly enhanced cuproptosis upon elesclomol/CuCl2 treatment, validating our in vitro findings. Collectively, our work identifies USP11 as a novel inhibitor of cuproptosis in CRC, acting through the stabilization of ISCU. This newly uncovered USP11–ISCU axis contributes to tumor resistance against cuproptotic stress and highlights a potential therapeutic target for sensitizing CRC to copper-induced cell death.Subject terms: Colorectal cancer, Ubiquitylation, Ubiquitylation

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a major global health burden and remains one of the most prevalent causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Although therapeutic advances have improved outcomes for some patients, those with advanced disease still face poor prognoses due to treatment resistance and aggressive tumor behavior [4, 5]. These challenges are largely driven by the intricate molecular networks that govern CRC development and progression [6, 7]. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms is therefore essential for advancing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to improve patient prognosis.

Cuproptosis is a recently characterized form of regulated cell death, initiated by mitochondrial accumulation of copper ions (Cu2+). This process leads to aggregation of the lipoylated enzyme dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), a component of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, triggering proteotoxic stress and cell death [8]. Notably, cuproptosis primarily affects cells that rely heavily on oxidative phosphorylation for energy production [9]. It is marked by both DLAT aggregation and degradation of iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster proteins [10]. Although recent studies have implicated cuproptosis in cancer pathogenesis and treatment, including CRC [11–13], its regulatory landscape in this context remains poorly defined.

Protein ubiquitination is a critical post-translational modification that governs protein degradation, localization, and function [14]. This tightly controlled reversible process involves a cascade of enzymatic reactions mediated by E1-activating enzymes, E2-conjugating enzymes, and E3-ubiquitin ligases. Conversely, deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) remove ubiquitin chains, thereby modulating protein stability and signaling pathways. Among these, ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs) comprise the largest subclass of DUBs, with over 50 members implicated in diverse cellular processes [15]. Dysregulation of USPs has been linked to CRC progression. For instance, USP1 promotes CRC cell survival, USP4 stabilizes oncogenic PRL-3, and USP44 exerts tumor-suppressive effects by deubiquitinating Axin1 and inhibiting Wnt signaling [16–18]. However, the functions of many USPs in CRC remain uncharacterized.

Recent evidence suggests that ubiquitination machinery may also regulate cuproptosis. For example, the DUB UCHL3 promotes cuproptosis by stabilizing PKM2 in hepatocellular carcinoma [19], while the E3 ligase TRIM21 induces cuproptosis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma through ID1 ubiquitination [20]. Nevertheless, the involvement of USPs in modulating cuproptosis, particularly in CRC, remains largely unexplored.

To answer these questions, we conducted a bioinformatic analysis to identify oncogenic USPs that are potentially involved in regulating cuproptosis in CRC. Among several candidates, USP11 emerged as a compelling target due to its elevated expression in CRC tissues and its association with poor patient prognosis. Functional assays revealed that USP11 promotes malignant phenotypes in CRC cells while concurrently suppressing apoptosis and cuproptosis in the presence of CuCl2 and elesclomol, a known cuproptosis inducer.

Mechanistically, mass spectrometry analysis identified ISCU, an Fe–S cluster assembly protein, as a substrate of USP11. We found that USP11 physically interacts with and deubiquitinates ISCU, thereby stabilizing it in CRC cells. Rescue experiments confirmed that ISCU mediates USP11’s anti-cuproptotic function. These findings were further validated in patient-derived CRC organoid models, supporting the translational relevance of the USP11–ISCU axis.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

Transcriptomic data for CRC were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-COAD) using the R package ‘TCGAbiolinks’ (v2.29.6), and corresponding clinical information was obtained from UCSC Xena (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). An additional CRC dataset, GSE44076, was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Differential expression analysis was performed using the ‘limma’ R package (v3.56.2) in R (v4.3.0), applying a cutoff of log2 fold change (log2FC) > 0 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Comparisons of USP expression levels between tumor and adjacent normal tissues were conducted using Student’s t-tests and visualized via ‘ggplot2’ (v3.5.1). Venn diagrams were generated using the Venny tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/).

Associations between USP expression and overall survival were initially explored using KM plotter (https://www.kmplot.com/) and validated in the TCGA-COAD cohort using Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis with the ‘survival’ R package (v3.5–7). Optimal expression cutpoints were determined using the ‘survminer’ package (v0.4.9) based on log2 (TPM + 1) transformed values. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted on differentially expressed genes between USP11-high and USP11-low groups, stratified by the median USP11 expression. Enrichment analysis was performed using the ‘GSEA’ R package (v4.3.3), and the top 20 enriched terms or pathways were visualized using ggplot2. Pearson correlation coefficients between USP11 expression and cuproptosis-related genes were calculated using the ‘cor.test’ function from the ‘stats’ package (v4.3.0) and visualized with the ‘graphics’ package (v4.3.0).

Cell culture

HEK293T, HCT-116, and SW480 cell lines were obtained from ATCC. 293T cells were cultured in DMEM (C11995500BT, Gibco, USA), HCT-116 cells in McCoy’s 5a medium (16600082, Gibco), and SW480 cells in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium. All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A5256701, Gibco) and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (15140148, Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% air. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored using the MycoFluor™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (M7006, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Plasmids

For gene knockdown experiments, three short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting USP11 or ISCU, along with a non-targeting control (shNC), were cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector. For overexpression studies, the coding sequence of USPs was inserted into the pcDNA3.3-HA vector to generate the HA-USP11, HA-USP27X, HA-USP35, and HA-USP39 constructs. Additional coding sequences for MT-CO1, SIRT2, MBOAT7, and ISCU were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-FLAG vector to produce the corresponding FLAG-tagged constructs: FLAG-MT-CO1, FLAG-SIRT2, FLAG-MBOAT7, and FLAG-ISCU. The Ubiquitin coding sequence was cloned into the pcDNA3.3-His vector, generating the His-Ub construct. All plasmids were sequence-verified before transfection. Plasmid delivery into cells was carried out using Lipomaster 2000 Transfection Reagent (TL201-01, Vazyme, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences used for plasmid construction are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the Total RNA Kit (19211ES60, Yeasen, China), and RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-8000-GL, Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (A28573, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the One Step RT-qPCR SYBR Green Kit (11143ES50, Yeasen). All reactions were run in technical and biological triplicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with normalization to 18S. The following primers were used: qUSP11-F: 5’- TCAGTCACCGCTTCTATA-3’, qUSP11-R: 5’- ACACCTCATAGACGAAGA -3’; qISCU-F: 5’- GGGTCCCTTGACAAGACATCT-3’, qISCU-R: 5’- CCTTTCACCCATTCAGTGGCTA-3’; q18S-F: 5’-CGACGACCCATTCGAACGTCT-3’, q18S-R: 5’-CTCTCCGGAATCGAA CCCTGA-3’.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013J, Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with SDS loading buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, and resolved by SDS–PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were then blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST (TBS with 0.1% Tween-20), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (34577, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and detected via X-ray film exposure. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) and normalized to β-actin. Western blot antibody details are shown in Table 1. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate to ensure reproducibility and statistical reliability.

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (ST316, Beyotime). CRC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and cultured for the indicated durations. Subsequently, 20 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After removing the medium, 150 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, followed by gentle shaking for 10 min at room temperature. The OD490 values were measured using a microplate reader (A51119500C, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each condition was analyzed in technical triplicate, and all experiments were independently repeated three times.

Colony formation assay

CRC cells were seeded in 6-well plates at approximately 800 cells per well and cultured for 10–12 days to allow colony development. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P1110, Solarbio, China) for 30 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 15 min. Plates were washed and imaged, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software. Experiments were performed in biological triplicate.

Cell cycle and apoptosis analysis

For cell cycle analysis, 6 × 106 CRC cells were fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at –20 °C, then washed and incubated with 20 μL RNase A at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were stained with 5 μL 7-AAD (Aoruicell, China) in PBS at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by flow cytometric analysis using a NovoCyte 1300 system (Agilent, USA). Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (CA1020, Solarbio) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 6 × 105 CRC cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in 100 μL binding buffer, then stained with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. After adding 400 μL binding buffer, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry with 488 nm excitation. Single-color controls were used for compensation. All flow cytometric experiments were conducted in biological triplicate.

Transwell assay

Migration and invasion assays were performed using 8 μm-pore Transwell chambers (3422, Corning, USA) in 24-well plates. For invasion assays, chambers were pre-coated with 100 μL of 1:5 diluted Plurigel Matrix (GL101, Vazyme) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. CRC cells were resuspended in serum-free medium at 3 × 105 cells/mL, and 200 μL of the suspension was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 700 μL complete medium. After 48 h, migrated/invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.05% crystal violet for 5 min, and imaged using an OLYMPUS inverted microscope. Quantification was performed with ImageJ.

Evaluations of glutathione, LDH, ATP, and Cu2+ levels

Reduced and oxidized glutathione levels (GSH/GSSG) were detected using the GSH/GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime). LDH levels were determined using a Solarbio kit (BC0680). Intracellular ATP was quantified using the ATP Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime). Cu2+ concentration was measured using the Copper (Cu²⁺) Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K300-M, Elabscience, China). All assays were conducted following the manufacturers’ protocols.

DCF-DA staining

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCF-DA staining. Briefly, CRC cells (~1 × 107 cells/mL) were incubated with 10 μM DCF-DA (50101ES01, Yeasen) in PBS for 15 min in a light-protective environment, followed by PBS washing, digestion, and flow cytometry analysis. DCF fluorescence intensity reflects ROS levels.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

Cells were co-transfected with the indicated plasmids. After 48 h, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer and incubated with antibody-conjugated magnetic beads at 4 °C for 2 h. Beads were washed three times with RIPA buffer, and eluted immunoprecipitates were denatured and analyzed by Western blotting. The following magnetic beads were used (Beyotime): Anti-HA (P2121), Anti-FLAG (P2115), and Anti-GST (P2138).

GST pull-down

His-ISCU, GST-USP11, and GST proteins were purified from E.coli. Equal amounts of His-ISCU were incubated with GST or GST-USP11 proteins, with part of each mixture used as input controls. The remainder was incubated with 20 μL Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (17075601, Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C for 6 h. Beads were washed several times with cold PBS, and bound proteins were eluted in 80 μL elution buffer, denatured, and analyzed by Western blotting.

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

HEK293T cells were transfected with either empty vector or HA-USP11 plasmid with 3 biological replicants, then lysed using the lysis buffer from HA-tag IP Kit (P2185M, Beyotime) after 24 h. Lysates of each group were combined equally and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were subjected to IP. Eluted proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and silver-stained. IP products were submitted for MS using a timsTOF Pro instrument (Bruker, Germany). Then, calculate USP11-binding proteins’ ratio in vector or USP11 overexpression group to select USP11 interact candidates.

Organoid establishment and culture

Human CRC tissue specimens (~0.25–1 cm3) were collected during surgery and transported in cold preservation solution (Immocell, IMV-A004). Tissues were minced into ~1 mm3 fragments and digested using pre-warmed digestion solution (Immocell, IMV-A006), then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. After centrifugation, pellets were resuspended in liquid Plurigel Matrix, and 30–40 μL was seeded per well in 24-well plates. After polymerization at 37 °C for 15–20 min, 500 μL of CRC organoid culture medium supplemented with 10 μM Y27632 was added. Organoids were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2, with medium changes every two days and passaging every 10 days. All procedures were approved by the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen Medical College (approved No. 2022043), and obtained written informed consents from all patients.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

CRC organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, cryosectioned at 6 μm, and processed for staining. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, and then with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Slides were mounted using antifade reagent and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope. Antibodies used included: Ki-67 (84192-4-RR, Proteintech, 1:200), CK20 (82428-1-RR, Proteintech, 1:200), β-catenin (51067-2-AP, Proteintech, 1:200), and CoraLite594-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (SA00013-4, Proteintech, 1:800).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.0) and GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For bar-dot plots, each dot represents an individual biological replicate and/or the average of technical replicates. Specific statistical tests are described in the respective figure legends. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening of oncogenic USPs potentially regulating cuproptosis in CRC

To uncover USP family members that may drive tumorigenesis and modulate cuproptosis in CRC, we analyzed transcriptomic data from the TCGA-COAD and GSE44076 datasets. Eighteen USPs were significantly upregulated in CRC tumor tissues (Fig. 1A). Of these, USP11, USP27X, USP35, and USP39 showed the most significant overexpression relative to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A, B). Stratification by clinical features indicated that only USP11 expression was significantly elevated in TNM stage phase III-IV patients, with limited variation observed by gender or survival status (Supplementary Fig. S1A–D).

C Kaplan–Meier survival plots from KM Plotter showing correlations between individual USP expression and overall survival in CRC patients. D Kaplan–Meier analysis based on TCGA-COAD data demonstrating the prognostic significance of USP expression in CRC patients.

We next examined the prognostic relevance of these USPs using Kaplan–Meier plotter. High expression of USP11 and USP35 correlated with shorter overall survival, while USP39 was associated with improved prognosis. USP27X showed no significant association (Fig. 1C). Analysis of the TCGA-COAD cohort further validated that USP27X and USP39 were predictive of poor outcomes, whereas USP11 and USP35 had weak or no prognostic value (Fig. 1D). Gene enrichment analysis of USP11-associated transcripts indicated involvement in cancer-related pathways, epigenetic regulation, and axonogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Pearson correlation analysis revealed that USP11 expression positively correlated with cuproptosis regulators such as ATP7A, CDKN2A, and ATP7B, and inversely correlated with LIAS, DLAT, and DLD (Supplementary Fig. S2B). These findings suggest USP11 may act as an oncogenic modulator of cuproptosis in CRC.

USP11 suppresses cuproptosis in CRC cells treated with elesclomol/CuCl2

To further dissect the role of USP11 in the cuproptosis of CRC, we utilized two CRC cell lines, HCT-116 and SW480. Cuproptosis was induced using elesclomol, a well-recognized cuproptosis inducer [21], along with 10 μM CuCl2 for 24 h. MTT assays determined IC₅₀ values of 5 nM for HCT-116 and 10 nM for SW480 (Supplementary Fig. S3A), with corresponding increases in intracellular Cu²⁺ confirming effective induction of cuproptosis (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Among multiple USPs tested, only USP11 overexpression consistently enhanced cell viability following treatment (Supplementary Fig. S3C). To manipulate the expression of USP11, we constructed three shRNAs targeting USP11 and validated their knockdown efficacy in HEK293T cells by qPCR (Supplementary Fig. S3D).

Subsequent MTT assays showed that USP11 overexpression increased viability in HCT-116 cells exposed to elesclomol/CuCl₂, C318A mutation mitigated USP11’s pro-survival capacity, whereas knockdown reduced viability in SW480 cells (Fig. 2A). These trends were corroborated by colony formation assays, showing increased colony numbers upon USP11 overexpression than C318A mutant overexpression, and reduced colony formation following USP11 knockdown (Fig. 2B). Cell cycle analysis by 7-AAD staining revealed cell cycle progression from G0/G1 to S in wild type USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells, while USP11 deficiency arrested SW480 cell cycle progression (Fig. 2C). Apoptosis was suppressed by wild type USP11 overexpression and enhanced by knockdown, as indicated by Annexin V/PI staining (Fig. 2D). Transwell assays demonstrated that USP11 enhanced migration and invasion, while its depletion had the opposite effect (Fig. 2E, F).

DCF-DA staining revealed reduced ROS production in USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells and increased ROS levels in USP11-deficient SW480 cells in the presence of elesclomol/CuCl2 (Fig. 3A). Biochemical analyses demonstrated elevated GSH and ATP levels, along with decreased GSSG, Cu2+, and LDH levels, in elesclomol/CuCl2-treated HCT-116 cells following USP11 overexpression. Conversely, USP11 depletion in SW480 cells caused reduced GSH and ATP levels, and increased GSSG, LDH, and Cu2+ content (Fig. 3B–E). Western blot analysis demonstrated that excessive wild type USP11 increased ATP7B and FDX1, and reduced LIAS, SLC31A1, DLAT in elesclomol/CuCl2-treated HCT-116 cells. Conversely, USP11 depletion exerted an opposite effect in elesclomol/CuCl2-treated SW480 cells (Fig. 3F, G). These findings demonstrate that USP11 suppresses cuproptosis in CRC cells, which was impaired by the C318A inactive mutant.

USP11 increases ISCU stability by removing its ubiquitination in CRC cells

To clarify how USP11 inhibits cuproptosis, we conducted IP-MS in HEK293T cells overexpressing HA-tagged USP11. Among the interacting proteins, ISCU was identified alongside other functionally relevant proteins, MT-CO1, SIRT2, and MBOAT7, which are known to participate in cuproptosis or copper metabolism [22–25]. Co-IP assays confirmed that only ISCU interacted with USP11 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 4A). To assess whether ISCU degradation is regulated via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, we treated HCT-116 and SW480 CRC cells with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) in combination with increasing concentrations of the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Western blot analyses revealed that ISCU, but not USP11, underwent degradation that was progressively attenuated by MG132 in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that ISCU is subject to proteasomal degradation (Fig. 4B). Further Co-IP assays in HCT-116 and SW480 cells demonstrated a strong interaction between USP11 and ISCU endogenously and exogenously (Fig. 4C, D). To determine whether USP11 directly interacts with and deubiquitinates ISCU, we conducted GST pull-down and IP experiments. These assays confirmed a direct binding between USP11 and ISCU, and ISCU precipitated by GST-tagged USP11 displayed markedly lower ubiquitination levels than ISCU pulled down by GST control (Fig. 4E, F). Given that USP11 is a DUB, we next investigated its effect on ISCU ubiquitination. IP assays showed that overexpression of USP11 in HCT-116 cells significantly reduced ISCU ubiquitination, whereas USP11 knockdown in SW480 cells led to an increase in ISCU ubiquitination levels (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, CHX chase assays indicated that USP11 prolongs the stability of ISCU protein over 18 h in CRC cells (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these findings illustrate that USP11 directly binds to ISCU and removes its ubiquitin chains via deubiquitinase activity, thereby stabilizing ISCU in CRC cells.

ISCU mediates USP11’s cuproptosis-suppressive role in CRC cells

To explore the functional role of ISCU in USP11-mediated suppression of cuproptosis, we silenced ISCU in USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells and overexpressed it in USP11-deficient SW480 cells under treatment with elesclomol and CuCl2. The efficiency of ISCU knockdown and overexpression was confirmed via qPCR and Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. S3E and Fig. 6F, G). MTT and colony formation assays revealed that ISCU knockdown impaired both cell viability and clonogenic potential in USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells. In contrast, ISCU overexpression enhanced these properties in USP11-deficient SW480 cells (Fig. 5A, B). In addition, ISCU depletion led to G0/G1-phase cell cycle arrest, diminished migratory and invasive capabilities, and increased apoptosis in HCT-116 cells with high USP11 expression. Conversely, ISCU overexpression reversed these phenotypes in USP11-deficient SW480 cells, as evidenced by 7-AAD staining, Annexin V/PI assays, and Transwell migration and invasion assays (Fig. 5C–F).

Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test.

To directly assess the contribution of ISCU to USP11-mediated regulation of cuproptosis, we examined intracellular ROS levels following elesclomol/CuCl2 treatment. DCF-DA staining revealed that ISCU knockdown elevated ROS levels in USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells, whereas ISCU overexpression reduced ROS accumulation in USP11-deficient SW480 cells (Fig. 6A). Biochemical analyses further demonstrated that ISCU depletion resulted in reduced GSH and ATP levels, accompanied by increased GSSG, Cu2+, and LDH levels in USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells. By contrast, ISCU overexpression increased GSH and ATP while reducing GSSG, Cu2+, and LDH in USP11-deficient SW480 cells (Fig. 6B–E). At the molecular level, Western blot analyses showed that ISCU knockdown suppressed the expression of key cuproptosis-related proteins, including ATP7B and FDX1, while upregulating LIAS, SLC31A1, and DLAT in USP11-overexpressing HCT-116 cells. Conversely, ISCU overexpression in USP11-deficient SW480 cells had the opposite effect on the expression of these marker genes (Fig. 6F, G). These data collectively support that ISCU is a key downstream effector of USP11 in mitigating cuproptosis in CRC cells.

USP11 deficiency enhances cuproptosis in patient-derived CRC organoids

To further validate the role of USP11 in a more physiologically relevant context, we established CRC organoid models derived from primary tumor tissues of three CRC patients. These organoids displayed typical three-dimensional morphology and structure (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B) and expressed proliferation marker Ki-67 and the known CRC marker CK20 [26] and CRC regulator β-catenin [27] (Supplementary Fig. S4C–E), confirming their identity as CRC organoids. To induce cuproptosis, the organoids were treated with increasing concentrations of elesclomol. ATP-based viability assays identified 200 nM elesclomol as the minimum effective dose for inducing cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. S4F), and this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments.

We then knocked down USP11 in the CRC organoids and exposed them to 200 nM elesclomol combined with CuCl2. Morphologically, USP11-deficient organoids exhibited significantly attenuated expansion following treatment, compared to control organoids (Fig. 7A, B). H&E staining further revealed that USP11 depletion exacerbated architectural damage caused by elesclomol/CuCl2 exposure (Fig. 7C). Biochemical assays showed further reductions in ATP content and increased Cu2+ accumulation in USP11-deficient organoids under treatment (Fig. 7D, E). At the molecular level, Western blotting demonstrated that elesclomol/CuCl₂ treatment had little effect on USP11 protein levels but upregulated the expression of ISCU, LIAS, SLC31A1, and DLAT, while downregulating ATP7B and FDX1 in control organoids. Notably, USP11 knockdown exacerbated all these molecular alterations (Fig. 7F). Taken together, these findings establish that USP11 plays a crucial role in shielding CRC organoids from cuproptotic stress.

Discussion

In this study, we uncover a previously unrecognized role of USP11 in promoting CRC progression by inhibiting cuproptosis. Our findings demonstrate that USP11 exerts this effect by directly interacting with and stabilizing ISCU, a key iron–sulfur cluster scaffold protein, through deubiquitination. This stabilization of ISCU is essential for suppressing cuproptosis and sustaining the survival and aggressiveness of CRC cells under cuproptotic stress, as validated in both established cell lines and patient-derived organoid models.

USP11 has been previously implicated in CRC progression, with studies showing its ability to stabilize oncogenic proteins such as IGF2BP3, NRF2, and PPP1CA, thereby promoting proliferation, metastasis, and resistance to apoptosis [28–30]. In other cancer types, USP11 may act either as an oncogene or a tumor suppressor [31]. Our findings expand on this oncogenic role by identifying an anti-cuproptotic function for USP11 in CRC. Specifically, we show that USP11 reduces the ubiquitination of ISCU, thereby enhancing its stability. This newly defined USP11–ISCU axis adds a mechanistic layer to how CRC cells evade cuproptosis and may reveal new vulnerabilities for therapeutic intervention. Although ISCU is a direct substrate of USP11 in this context, it remains possible that additional USP11-regulated targets also contribute to cuproptosis resistance in CRC, a possibility that warrants further investigation.

The mechanisms underlying USP11 overexpression in CRC remain unclear. While genomic amplification or epigenetic deregulation (e.g., hypomethylation) cannot be ruled out, post-transcriptional regulation may play a more prominent role. For instance, circ_DOCK1 has been shown to regulate USP11 by acting as a sponge for miR-132-3p [32]. Whether such non-coding RNAs also modulate USP11 expression in the context of cuproptosis remains to be determined. Interestingly, our results show that USP11 protein levels are unaffected by MG132 treatment, indicating that USP11 is likely not degraded via the proteasome. This suggests that its turnover may be governed by lysosome-mediated pathways, at least in CRC cells, a hypothesis requiring further validation.

ISCU, the key substrate of USP11 identified in this study, is central to Fe-S cluster biosynthesis, which supports critical cellular functions such as mitochondrial respiration, DNA replication, and metabolic homeostasis [33]. Human ISCU exists in two isoforms—ISCU1 (cytoplasmic) and ISCU2 (mitochondrial) [34]. Pathogenic mutations in ISCU have been associated with hereditary myopathies [35, 36]. In cancer, ISCU plays diverse roles depending on tissue context. For example, ISCU expression is downregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma and acts downstream of p53 to regulate iron metabolism [37], whereas in pancreatic cancer, ISCU is upregulated and promotes tumor progression through modulation of α-ketoglutarate metabolism and DNA methylation [38]. Our findings add CRC to the list of malignancies where ISCU plays an oncogenic role, although further research is needed to determine whether ISCU is also upregulated in CRC patient tissues and whether its expression correlates with clinical outcomes.

While ISCU’s role in iron metabolism is well established, recent findings also suggest it has a high copper-binding capacity [23], pointing to a potential role in modulating cuproptosis. Our data provide compelling evidence that ISCU acts downstream of USP11 to suppress cuproptosis in CRC cells. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which ISCU inhibits cuproptosis remains undefined and warrants further exploration.

Regarding ISCU expression regulation, we observed that elesclomol/CuCl2 treatment upregulates ISCU protein expression in CRC organoids without altering USP11 levels, suggesting the involvement of USP11-independent regulatory mechanisms. Prior studies have shown that ISCU can be post-transcriptionally repressed by miR-210 and post-translationally stabilized through mTORC1-mediated phosphorylation at Ser14 [39, 40]. Whether elesclomol modulates ISCU through these mechanisms remains to be determined.

Despite the insights gained from our cell and organoid models, several limitations exist. First, our study relies primarily on elesclomol/CuCl2-induced models of cuproptosis. Validation using additional cuproptosis inducers or models mimicking endogenous copper dysregulation is essential. Second, we have not yet validated our findings in in vivo models, such as xenografts or genetically engineered CRC mice, which are crucial for understanding the physiological relevance of USP11’s anti-cuproptotic role. Third, although we established that USP11 interacts with and deubiquitinates ISCU, the specific domains responsible for this interaction and the specific type of ubiquitin linkage removed by USP11 have not been characterized. Finally, larger cohorts with detailed clinical data are needed to validate USP11 as a biomarker for patient prognosis prediction.

In conclusion, our study reveals a novel regulatory pathway wherein USP11 promotes CRC progression by stabilizing ISCU and suppressing cuproptosis. Through deubiquitination of ISCU, USP11 prevents its proteasomal degradation, thereby supporting CRC cell survival and tumor aggressiveness in both traditional and organoid models under cuproptotic stress. These findings highlight the USP11–ISCU axis as a critical driver of cuproptosis resistance in CRC and suggest that targeting this pathway may represent a promising strategy to sensitize CRC to cuproptosis-inducing therapies.
