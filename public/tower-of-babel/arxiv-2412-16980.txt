Visualizing Linear Prediction
Peter J. Rousseeuw

Abstract
Many statistics courses cover multiple linear regression, and present students with the formula of a prediction using the regressors, slopes, and an intercept. But is it really easy to see which terms have the largest effect, or to explain why the prediction of a specific case is unusually high or low? To assist with this the so-called grill plot is proposed. Its simplicity makes it easy to interpret, and it combines much information. Its main benefit is that it helps explainability of the linear formula as it is, without depending on how the formula was derived. The regressors can be numerical, categorical, or interaction terms, and the model can be linear or generalized linear. Another display is proposed to visualize correlations between predictors, in a way that is tailored for this setting.



Keywords:

Effect size;
Explainable prediction;
Generalized linear model;
Grill plot;
Regression.

1
Introduction

Suppose someone has carried out a linear
regression and tells you they predict
the horsepower of a car by the formula

-206.9+246.6*\mbox{topspeed}-13.1*\mbox{length}+0.063*\mbox{displacement}

where
topspeed
is in miles per hour,

length
is in meters and engine

displacement
is in cubic centimeters.
Which of these regressors has the biggest
effect on the prediction? It is not enough to
look for the largest coefficient.
To resolve this, the typical approach
is to first standardize each regressor by
dividing it by its standard deviation.
That works fine, but cannot be done for
categorical regressors.
We would like to assess the effect
magnitude of regressors visually, in a way
that includes categorical regressors. That
is the topic of the next section.

A closely related issue is the question: Why
is the prediction for a particular case
so high or so low? An answer may be required
when the prediction determines an important
decision such as granting or denying a loan
request, or treating a patient’s medical
condition by surgery or medication.
The ability to answer such a
question is called explainability.
It is often stressed that a regression
tree is explainable because you can follow
its branches, whereas most neural nets
are not.
Is a linear prediction explainable?
On the one hand, a linear combination has
a simple expression. On the other hand, how
can you tell which of its terms were mainly
responsible? This is a topic that deserves
to be touched upon in a basic statistics
class. We will show a visualization in
Section
3
,
and look at correlations between
predictors in Section
4
.

In this note we are not concerned with the
methodology used to obtain the fit.
We will consider the prediction formula as
a given, and study what it does.
Our displays will not use the observed
response variable either, or describe how
well the prediction approximates it. That
is addressed by other tools such as residual
plots.

2
Spread and orientation of predictors

A linear prediction on numerical regressors

x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}
is of the form

\mbox{total\_pred}=a+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{j}x_{j}

(1)

where
a
is the intercept and the
b_{j}
are
slopes. We assume that the regressors have
already been transformed if needed, and that
outlying cases have been deleted or corrected.
Let us now center the prediction terms,
and denote them as
f_{j}:=b_{j}(x_{j}-\overline{x_{j}})
. The
terms
f_{j}
will be called
predictors

to distinguish them from the original
regressors
x_{j}
. We can also compute the
average prediction

\overline{\mbox{total\_pred}}

which is sometimes called the
centercept

(
Wainer 2000
)
, a name coined by John Tukey,
because for the
center
ed predictors it is
the inter
cept
. Next we also center the
total prediction and denote it as

f:=\mbox{total\_pred}-\overline{\mbox{total\_pred}}

so that (
1
) becomes

f=\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}

(2)

without any intercept.
In fact (
2
) is more general
than (
1
) because a
categorical variable also yields a predictor

f_{j}
, that is obtained by coding its
levels by binary dummies and adding up their
predictions. We do not
standardize the predictors
f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}

so all terms are in the units
of the total prediction.
Finally, we measure the spread of each
predictor
f_{j}
by its standard deviation.

Our first example uses the Top Gear
data from the
R
package

robustHD

(
Alfons 2016
)
.
It contains numerical and categorical variables
about 297 cars, scraped from the website of the
British television show Top Gear.
We want to predict a car’s fuel efficiency
from the type of
fuel
it
uses (petrol or diesel), its
weight

in kilograms, the time in seconds it takes to
accelerate from standstill to 60 miles per
hour (
accel
), and the variable

drive
which has three levels:
rear-wheel drive, front-wheel drive, and
four-wheel drive (4WD).
Following
Henderson and Velleman 1981
who analyzed
a similar dataset we predict gallons per
mile (GPM), the inverse of miles per
gallon (MPG).

predictors
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}
and the
total prediction
f
of (
2
).

After a standard regression analysis we
have the predictors
f_{1}
obtained from

accel
,
f_{2}
from
drive
,

f_{3}
from
weight
,
f_{4}
from

fuel
, and the total prediction

f
which by (
2
) is the
sum of the four terms.
Figure
1
plots
all of them. We see that the
f_{j}
and

f
are centered, and they all
have the same units of gallons per mile.
The predictors
f_{1}
and
f_{3}
come from
numerical regressors and take many
values (here
n=297
), so their
distributions are shown as histograms.
The predictors
f_{2}
and
f_{4}
take
very few distinct values, so they are
represented by bar charts. We clearly see
that the effect of predictor
f_{2}
is quite
small compared to that of
f_{1}
. One might
have expected that the average of predictor

f_{4}
which only takes two values would lie
exactly in the middle of the two bars, but
it doesn’t because the bars have different
lengths, indicating that the positive
prediction occurred more often than the
negative one in this dataset.

Figure
1
already
gives some intuition, but we can do more.
It turns out that the
weight

predictor has the largest spread, followed
by
accel
,
fuel
, and

drive
. In order to visualize this
we can order the
f_{j}
by decreasing spread.
More importantly, we can label the
‘anonymous’ predictions in the scale
of the original regressors, as in
Figure
2
.
For the numerical regressors
weight

and
accel
we read off the values of

x_{j}
without their slope coefficients.
Predictions from the categorical variables

fuel
and
drive
are labeled
by their levels. The vertical axis on the
left measures the contribution of each term

f_{j}
to the total prediction. For
instance, increasing the weight of a car
from the average to 2000 kg, while keeping
everything else fixed, increases the
prediction by about 0.005 gallons/mile.
Replacing a diesel engine by a petrol engine
raises the prediction by 0.008
gallons/mile. The effects of four-wheel drive
and rear-wheel drive are about the same so
their labels overlap, whereas front-wheel
drive decreases the prediction.

Figure 2:
Grill plot of the predictors in
the Top Gear data.

Note that the
weight
line has an
upward arrow and its histogram is shown in
green, which indicates that increasing

weight
yields a higher prediction
(again, if the other characteristics
remain the same).
On the other hand, longer acceleration
times yield lower predictions, as reflected
by the downward arrow and brown color.
The lines of the categorical variables

fuel
and
drive
have no
arrow because their levels are not
ordered, and their distributions are
shown in neutral grey. The same
would happen for
predictions from logical (Yes/No)
variables and interaction terms.
The rightmost line with the total
prediction
f
is labeled in its
original scale that includes the
centercept (which is
about 0.026 here). Its range is a bit
truncated in the plot, to put the focus
on the predictors
f_{j}
. So we do
not see its entire grey histogram, but
that option is available.

We call Figure
2
a

grill plot
. With a little bit of
imagination one can see the horizontal axis
as a skewer which pierces some grilled pieces
of food, both juicy numerical regressors and
crispy categorical ones. The colors of the
predictors evoke vegetables and meat,
but can of course be changed by the user.
An advantage of the grill plot over a list
of predictor spreads is that we visualize
the relative effects of the predictors.
We would also notice it immediately if a
predictor were highly skewed, and any
outlier would stick out like a sore thumb.

The grill plot also works for generalized
linear models, where the
prediction (
2
) becomes

f=g^{-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}\Big).

(3)

Here
g
is a monotone
link function
.
In logistic regression the link function is the
logit function
g(p)=\log(p/(1-p))
,
so (
3
) predicts a value between
0 and 1 using
g^{-1}(y)=1/(1+\exp(-y))
.

To illustrate this we analyze the well-known
Titanic dataset which contains information
on the 1309 passengers of the RMS Titanic.
For its history and visualizations see

Friendly et al. 2019
.
The data are freely available from

https://www.kaggle.com/c/titanic/data

and the
R
package
classmap

(
Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2023
)
.
The binary response variable indicates whether
the passenger survived or was a casualty.
We want to predict survival by a logistic
regression on the variables
pclass
,

sex
,
age
,
sibsp
,
and
parch
.
Here
pclass
is the cabin class,

sex
is M or F, and the
passenger’s
age
is in years.
The variables
sibsp
and
parch

count the number of siblings+spouses and
parents+children aboard.

Figure 3:
Grill plot of the logistic regression
on the Titanic data.

Figure
3
shows the
grill plot. It plots the
f_{j}
and the total
linear prediction
\sum_{j}f_{j}
as before, but
the labels on the rightmost axis are on the
scale of the predicted survival probability

g^{-1}(\sum_{j}f_{j})
.
This time we opted to plot estimated
densities of
age
and the total
prediction instead of their histograms.
We see at first glance that the gender of the
passenger is the most important variable, with
predicted survival higher for females.
This catches the eye to a similar extent as
regression trees on this dataset making their
first split on this variable, see e.g.

Raymaekers and Rousseeuw 2022
. Next up is the cabin
class, followed by
age
which has a
down arrow, meaning that younger people had
a better chance at survival. Predictors

sex
and
age
reflect the
motto ‘women and children first in the
lifeboats,’ but being a first class passenger
increases the prediction too. Clearly variable

parch
has only a tiny effect.

The grill plot is
a member of the extensive family of parallel
coordinate plots
(
Inselberg 2009
)
.
It bears a slight
resemblance to the so-called forest plot in
meta-analysis, see e.g.
Chang et al. 2022
,
which displays the outcomes of different
studies with their confidence intervals.
The grill plot contains no scientific
novelty, but it is hoped that the way it
visually combines much information provides
helpful insight in the output of a regression.
Figures
2

and
3
were obtained
by the function
grillplot
in the
Supplementary Material using the single

R
command
grillplot(fit)

where
fit
is an output object of
the function
lm()
or
glm()
.
The function
grillplot
figures out
the rest.
There are of course many options: the
maximal number of predictors to be shown,
whether to display density estimates instead
of histograms, graphical
parameters for colors and line widths, and
so on. The formula of the regression may
include logarithms of variables and
interaction terms, such as

y\sim x_{1}+\log(x_{2})+x_{3}:x_{4}

as illustrated in Section A of the
Supplementary Material.

3
Why is the prediction for my case
so high (low)?

The grill plot can also be used to explain the
prediction of a single case, which may be one
of the cases the model was fitted on
(‘in-sample’), or a new case (‘out-of-sample’)
for which we need a prediction.
We illustrate this with another benchmark, the
German credit dataset which is available from

https://archive.ics.uci.edu/ml/datasets/statlog+(german+credit+data)
and
from the
R
package

fairml

(
Scutari 2023
)
. It contains
1000 loan applications. We predict the success
probability of a loan by logistic regression on
the size of the loan (
amount
), its
duration (
months
), the interest

rate
, the intended
purpose

of the loan (categorical with 10 levels), the
number of clients responsible for paying back
the loan (
nclients
), and the main
client’s
sex
and
age
.
Figure
4
shows
the grill plot for the first case in the
dataset.

Figure 4:
Grill plot for case 1 of the German
credit dataset.

In the grill plot we see that the

purpose
of the loan typically has the
largest effect, followed by the three financial
variables. The latter have downward
arrows, so a longer

duration
, a higher
rate
, and
a higher
amount
each decrease the
predicted success probability.
The variable
age
points upward, the
model deems females more reliable than males,
and being a one-client loan is considered
positive. Now all the distributions are shown
in grey, because the focus is on the predictor
values. Those are red when the predictor value
is above average, and blue if below average.
Figure
4
is
about a woman in her sixties requesting a small
loan with short duration to buy a TV. Her
predictor values are all above average except
the one due to the high interest rate. Therefore
the total prediction on the rightmost axis is
very high, with estimated success probability
over 90%.

Figure
4
suggests that
the individual predictors are added, but does not
actually show that. A variation of the plot that
does visualize the addition is available as
the option
staircase = TRUE
,illustrated
in Figure
5
for case 2
of the dataset.
In this plot the predictor values are added up by
shifting each predictor up or down so its
average aligns with the cumulative
prediction to its left. Case 2 is a young man
requesting a larger loan with longer duration,
and his total prediction comes out rather low.
If a loan is refused based on such a prediction,
a graph like Figure
4

or Figure
5

facilitates explaining why to the client.

Figure 5:
Grill plot for case 2 of the German
credit dataset, in staircase style.

The choice between the basic style in
Figure
4
and the
staircase style in
Figure
5
is a
matter of taste. Whereas the latter shows
the addition explicitly the plot also looks
busier, and its appearance depends more
strongly on the order of the individual
predictors. The choice is left to the user.
More examples of both styles are shown in
the Supplementary Material.

While it can be very useful or even mandatory
to be able to explain a prediction as in
Figure
5
,
making the entire prediction
equation (
3
) public
may have the undesired side effect that people
can game the system in favor of their desired
outcome. After seeing
Figure
5
, the
client might be tempted to increase his
chances of getting a loan by indicating the
intended purpose is to buy a used car because
the
u.car
level is at the top,
selecting a smaller number of months, and asking
his mother to fill in the new application form
to get more favorable values of
age
and

sex
, while keeping the other regressors
unchanged. Running
grillplot
on this
new set of regressors yields the figure in
Section C of the Supplementary Material, in
which the predicted probability becomes 89%
instead of 48% .

4
Correlations between predictors

It is well-known that the coefficient of a
regressor in multiple regression can have
the opposite sign of its slope in a simple
regression with that regressor alone.
This often happens when that regressor
is correlated with other regressors in the
multiple model. Therefore it is useful to
look at the correlations between regressors.
One often visualizes these correlations
by a heatmap, in which correlations of zero
are shown in white, correlations close to 1
in dark red, and correlations close to -1
in dark blue, with a gradual change in
between the extremes.

It is proposed to enrich such a display in
two ways. First, by switching from
regressors to predictors, so that also
categorical regressors can be included.
The left panel of
Figure
6

shows such a display for the German
credit data. For a
numerical regressor, the sign of its slope
coefficient matters. For a categorical
regressor, its predictor is numerical so
its correlation with other predictors
exists.

Figure 6:
Correlations between predictors
of the German credit data. Left: in
typical heatmap form. Right: here the
side of a diagonal square is proportional
to the spread of its predictor.

Note that this does not yet take the effect
magnitude of the predictors into account.
Our second modification is to plot the
diagonal cells with sides that are
proportional to the spread of their
predictor. In this way the area of each
diagonal cell is proportional to the
variance of its predictor.
The right panel of
Figure
6

shows the result. We immediately
see that the variable
purpose

has a big effect, whereas that of

nclients
is the smallest.
Interestingly, such a correlation display
with varying cell sizes did not appear
to exist yet in the literature or on the
internet, nor heatmaps of that type.

The off-diagonal part of the display
consists of rectangles, which also have
a meaningful interpretation. The area of
such a rectangle is the product of the
spreads of its predictors, and when we
multiply that with the value of the
correlation (represented by its color)
we obtain the covariance between the
predictors. So a dark color is more
important in a larger rectangle (e.g.
formed by
months
and

amount
) than in a smaller
rectangle (e.g. formed by
rate

and
amount
).
The proposed display thus depicts
the covariance matrix between
the predictors, which is relevant in a
regression. Such a display is not
recommended for a covariance matrix
between variables in different units,
but here all predictors are in the same
units (those of the total prediction).

The
R
function
corpred

which produces this display has various
options, such as sorting the predictors by
decreasing spread, plotting the absolute
values of the correlations, and making the
area of the diagonal cells proportional
to the spread of the predictors rather
than their variance.

To illustrate what can happen when two
regressors are highly correlated, we carry
out a small experiment. We replace the
variables
months
and

nclients
by their sum

x1 = months + nclients

and their difference

x2 = months - nclients
.
Of course this is an artificial example,
but it does yield two regressors with a
correlation over
0.99
, and the same
total prediction.

Figure 7:
Grill plot of the German credit
data when the regressors

months
and
nclients

are replaced by their sum
x1

and their difference
x2
.

Figure
7
shows
the resulting grill plot.
It looks rather suspicious, because
the artificial variables
x1
and

x2
have a wider spread than the
total prediction on the right, which is
the sum of these and other terms.
Moreover
x1
points downward and

x2
points upward! They partly
cancel each other.
The correlation display in
Figure
8
also
looks unusual, with the big dark blue
rectangles between
x1
and

x2
. The correlation between

x1
and
x2
that was close
to 1 has flipped sign between their
predictors, again reflecting the
canceling effect.
In general, predictors are easiest to
interpret when they only have small
correlations with each other.

Figure 8:
Correlations between predictors
when the regressors

months
and
nclients

are replaced by their sum
x1

and their difference
x2
.

5
Discussion

There are many other ways to measure the
contribution of regressors.
Verdinelli and Wasserman 2024

reviewed several, and found that there can be no
‘neutral’ way to do so, and that there is
currently no ‘overall best’ way.
The displays in this note may seem oversimplified
but that makes them easy to interpret.
Their main benefit is that they
help explainability of the prediction rule
as it is given, without depending on how it was
derived.
It would be possible to extend the displays
to some related settings, but that falls
outside the scope of this note.

More figures of the Top Gear data

in the main text was

in the first display.

and interactions in the formula we run

\texttt{fit = lm(1/MPG }\sim\texttt{ accel + log(weight) + accel:torque, data = cars)}

displays below. Interactions between
categorical regressors, or between a numerical
and a categorical variable, are visualized in
