Association of motoric cognitive risk syndrome with depression in older adults: a meta-analysis and systematic review of cross-sectional and cohort studies
Authors: Shi Zhou, Niansi Ye, Xueting Liu, Yucan Li, Yating Ai, Xiaotong Wang, Pengjun Zhou, Hui Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn an aging society, depression has become a public health challenge that can lead to many adverse health outcomes; evidence addressing the link between depression and motor cognitive risk syndrome (MCR, a novel syndrome that effectively predicts dementia) is still lacking.MethodsA PRISMA checklist was used to systematically review the relevant peer-reviewed literature for the primary data analysis. A computer search of CNKI, Wan Fang Data, CBM, VIP, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Ovid databases, all from creation to March 15, 2023. A meta-analysis was conducted using Stata 17.0, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale instrument (NOS), and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) for quality appraisal. We used Q and I2 statistics to assess heterogeneity and random effects models to pool estimates. Egger’s regression tests and adjustments were made using the trim and fill test.ResultThirteen studies were included, including seven cross-sectional studies and six cohort studies, and data on the association between depression and MCR in the elderly were extracted. Moreover, the results of the meta-analysis showed that there was a significant correlation between depression and MCR in the cross-sectional study [OR = 2.43, 95% CI(1.31 ~ 3.54), P < 0.01]. In the cohort study, depression in the elderly was associated with the occurrence of MCR [HR = 1.19, 95% CI(1.08 ~ 1.30), P < 0.01]; Subgroup analysis by age, depression assessment tool, MCR assessment tool, and follow-up time (cohort study only) showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.01).ConclusionMeta-analysis of cross-sectional studies and cohort studies showed that depression in older adults was associated with the development of MCR, and depression increased the risk of MCR in older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05507-y.

Introduction

Depression is characterized by persistent low mood and loss of interest in usual activities [1] and severely impacts older adults’ physical and mental health. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are over 350 million people worldwide who suffer from depression [2]. Furthermore, related studies have shown [3–5] that depression increases with age. As the second largest killer of human health [6], depression has caused severe harm to the psychological, physical, and social aspects of the elderly [7]. It has become a critical health issue in the aging society.

Studies have found that older adults who suffer from depression are more likely to suffer from cognitive impairment [8–10]. As depressive symptoms increase, older adults will experience a broad range of cognitive impairments, including memory, attention, calculation, language, etc [11]. Current hypotheses of possible biological mechanisms between the relationship between depression and cognitive impairment mainly include inflammatory changes and increased cortisol and hippocampal atrophy. On the one hand, depression leads to increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF) [12] interferes with 5-hydroxytryptamine metabolism, and reduces synaptic plasticity, which in turn leads to reduced cognitive function [13]; on the other hand, depression activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which stimulates the production of glucocorticoids, followed by hippocampal atrophy, ultimately leading to cognitive decline [14]. Furthermore, Significant correlations between depressive symptoms and gait speed, stride length, and swing time have been demonstrated [15], and older adults with depression are significantly impaired in balance, gait, and stride length [16], confirming a bidirectional correlation between gait speed and depression [17].

Motor cognitive risk syndrome (MCR) [18] is a pre-dementia syndrome, which presents with both gait slowness and subjective cognitive impairment in the non-demented older population [19–22] and can lead to an increased risk of adverse health outcomes in older people, such as the onset of dementia, depression, falls, disability and even death [23–25]. Clarifying the impact of depression on the MCR in older adults may provide new avenues for tertiary prevention of the MCR and indirect ways to reduce the incidence of dementia. Currently, several studies have investigated the prevalence of MCR and confirmed the correlation between depression and MCR. However, some studies [26, 27] still suggest that depression was not associated with the occurrence of MCR. Many scholars have carried out several studies on the prevalence of MCR. However, there is no systematic analysis focusing on the specific issue of the association between depression and MCR in the elderly.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the relationship between depression and MCR in older adults to open up new ideas for caregivers to develop tertiary prevention strategies for MCR in older adults from a psychological perspective; in addition, to provide a basis for early prediction and precise intervention in people at risk of dementia, which is of great public health significance in today’s rapid aging process.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines [28]. The search strategy, study selection, data extraction, and analysis were performed according to a pre-defined protocol.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The included articles met the following criteria: (1)people over 60 years of age; (2)types of observational studies; (3)clear outcome indicators such as odds ratio(OR) relative risk(RR) or risk ratio(HR) were available; (4)studies reporting more detailed data were included if the data were published repeatedly.

The exclusion criteria were: (1)non-research articles: conferences, reviews, commentaries, etc.;(2)full text not available; (3)lack of valid and accepted assessments for depression and MCR; (4)effect indicators not reported or not extractable.

Literature search strategy

This systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO international prospective registration system review database (protocol number: CRD42023402384) [29]. Two investigators (SZ and NSY) independently performed a literature search in CNKI, Wan Fang Data, CBM, VIP, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Ovid databases from their inception to March 15, 2023. The database was searched using keywords/titles/abstracts searching for the keywords: “motoric cognitive risk”、“depressive syndrome” 、“depression”、“elder”、“senior” with a search method using a combination of subject terms and free words, matching truncation characters. No chronologic or other restrictions were introduced.

Literature screening and data extraction

The literature was screened, extracted, and cross-checked independently by two researchers (SZ and NSY). Any disagreement was resolved through discussion or negotiation with a third party(YCL). In order to determine whether to include the literature, we first read the title of the text after excluding irrelevant literature and then read the abstract and full text. Data extraction included author information, year of publication, country, age, sample size, MCR and depression assessment methods, and the odds ratio (OR) or hazard ratio (HR) of MCR with depression in older adults. It was also noted whether the OR or HR was adjusted and the risk factors used for adjustment. If the OR was not reported, it was calculated using raw data in each group.

Risk of bias evaluation of included studies

In each of the included studies, two independent investigators assessed the risk of bias and cross-checked the results. Referring to previous studies [30–32], the risk of bias was evaluated using NOS for the inclusion of cohort and case-control studies [33]; the cross-sectional study used the evaluation criteria recommended by AHRQ [34]. The third author (HH) resolved discrepancies regarding data collection and quality assessment regarding the original report.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Stata 17.0 software. Separate meta-analyses were performed for cross-sectional studies and cohort studies. An odds ratio (OR) or hazard ratio (HR) was used as the effect analysis statistic and provided a 95% confidence interval (CI). OR was used to assess the association between MCR and the risk of depression for cross-sectional studies, and HR was used for cohort studies. Adjusted OR and HR were also pooled to assess the impact of the adjustment on the association between MCR and the risk of depression. Random effect models were used. The amount of heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Subgroup analyses evaluated cohort studies with basic (limited to demographic characteristics) or more extensive adjustments. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to check the robustness of the pooled OR and HR by removing each study individually.

The test level for meta-analysis was set to α = 0.05. Publication bias was assessed by inspecting the funnel plot, Egger’s test, and the trim and fill method.

Results

Literature search

A preliminary electronic search generated 406 potentially pertinent articles. Among these articles, 69 were eliminated because of duplicate records. Based on a review of titles and abstracts, 272 articles were excluded as irrelevant. The remaining 65 potentially eligible articles were retrieved for full-text assessment, resulting in 51 articles being excluded due to insufficient data (n = 45), conference abstracts (n = 4), and reviews (n = 3). Finally, the remaining 13 studies were included in the final analysis. Depression and MCR were extracted from the 13 studies. Results of meta-analysis

Correlation between depression and MCR in elderly

Due to the limited number of included studies (7 cross-sectional studies [35–41], 6 cohort studies [42–47]), it was impossible to separate the crude or adjusted results for meta-analysis, so we usually prioritized the adjusted model results for inclusion in the meta-analysis.

We included seven cross-sectional studies, including a total of 20,321 individuals. Meta-analysis of adjusted OR demonstrated that depression in the elderly was associated with the occurrence of MCR [OR = 2.54, 95% CI (1.5 ~ 4.3), P < 0.01], and the difference was statistically significant (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis of the association between depression and MCR

Due to the high heterogeneity index in the reported studies that examined the association between depression and MCR, We analyzed subgroups of the literature separately for different study types. Age、assessment tools for MCR and depression were used in the cross-sectional study. The cohort study, on the other hand, also considered follow-up time.

In cross-sectional studies, we found that for different age groups of older adults, the OR for depression and MCR was relatively higher in studies with age < 75 years (OR = 3.63,95% CI:3.08,4.29, I2 = 90.2%, P < 0.01). In terms of choosing an MCR assessment tool, the use of GAITRite, a more scientifically based assessment tool, yielded higher OR compared to time reckoning (OR = 3.97,95%CI:1.98,7.95, I2 = 90.2%, P < 0.01). In the subgroup analysis of the depression assessment tool, then the OR between depression and MCR obtained using the GDS to assess depression was higher (OR = 3.97,95%CI:1.98,7.95, I2 = 90.2%, P < 0.01) (Table 1).

In the cohort study, we found that, as in the cross-sectional study, the OR for depression and MCR was relatively high in studies with age < 75 years (OR = 1.5,95% CI:0.92,2.46, I2 = 86%, P < 0.01). In contrast, in the subgroup analysis of the MCR assessment tool, a different result was obtained from the cross-sectional, with a higher OR using the time reckoning assessment tool instead (OR = 1.32,95%CI:1.05,1.66, I2 = 86%, P < 0.01). By subgroup analysis of follow-up time, the OR between MCR and depression was slightly higher in cohort studies of < 5 years (OR = 1.32,95% CI:1.05,1.66, I2 = 86%, P < 0.01) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed on cross-sectional and cohort studies by excluding individual studies one by one, and our results showed that there was no directional change in the results of the meta-analysis, suggesting that the meta-analysis results were relatively stable. After the single cross-sectional study was deleted, depression in the elderly was significantly associated with MCR [OR = 2.54, 95% CI (1.5 ~ 4.3), P < 0.01] (Fig. 4). In addition, among the included cohort studies, we found that the deletion of any studies did not change the significant association between depression and MCR in older adults [HR = 1.24, 95% CI (1.11 ~ 1.39), P < 0.01] (Fig. 5).

Publication bias

The inspection of the funnel plots (Supplementary Appendix Figure. S1 and Figure. S2) showed the presence of possible publication bias, especially concerning the cohort studies. However, to test for publication bias in this study, we used Egger’s linear regression test for publication bias. The results showed no outcome bias in the included cross-sectional studies (P = 0.98). Still, we found a certain amount of publication bias in the cohort studies (P = 0.002), which led to the use of the trim and fill method to correct publication bias in the included literature. We then used the trim and fill method to correct the publication bias of the included literature and remove the effect of publication bias on the combined effect. The trim and fill method is a nonparametric statistical method based on the assumption that publication bias causes asymmetry in the funnel plot [48]. Finally, after including data from 2 dummy studies, Meta-analysis was re-run for all studies, and the results showed a test for heterogeneity: Q = 71.928, P = 0.000, indicating that the results remained statistically non-significant and did not reverse, thus making the combined results robust(Supplementary appendix Figure. S3).

Discussion

The present meta-analysis, including data from 13 observational studies, confirmed the correlation between MCR and depression in cross-sectional and cohort studies, aligning with previous findings. Existing studies have shown that depression is an independent risk factor for MCR in community-dwelling older adults and that the prevalence of MCR is higher in patients with depression than in patients without depression [46]. In a cross-sectional survey of 9,683 older adults in the Japanese community, Doi et al. [36] found that older adults with depressive symptoms had an increased risk of developing MCR after adjusting for confounders (OR = 3.57). A longitudinal study of aging in Ireland showed [35] that antidepressant use was a significant risk factor for MCR (OR = 4.46). Sekhon et al. [49] conducted a 20-year prospective cohort study of 29,569 older adults by exploring the relationship between anxiety-depressive disorder (ADD) and depressive states. MCR in older adults showed that the prevalence of ADD and depression was higher in patients with MCR as compared to the group with no MCR in all age groups (P < 0.01). These studies suggest that there is a correlation between depression and MCR in older adults and that depression can be a risk factor for MCR in older adults and do not rely on correction for potential publication bias in individual studies or sensitivity analyses.

Given the observational design of included studies, causality, inverse causality, and confounding must be considered. However, Since adjustment factors were inconsistent among studies, their relative impact on the association between depression and MCR could not be individually assessed. Age, gender, education, race, chronic medical condition, and medication status were the most common potential confounders taken into account for adjustment, which may lead to similar pathogenic triggers between depression and MCR in older adults.

In the cross-sectional study, stratified by age, MCR, and assessment tools for depression, we found that depression and MCR were significantly associated with older adults and that age and MCR assessment tools may be potential sources of bias. In addition, cross-sectional studies covered more dispersed areas, which may have contributed to the heterogeneity of the studies. Meanwhile, follow-up time might be the primary source of heterogeneity in subgroup analyses of cohort studies. Unfortunately, subgroup analyses of depression assessment tools could not be performed in cohort studies because one paper contained multiple cohorts with different choices of depression assessment tools. In addition, the cohort studies were mainly from the United States, so the region was not included in the subgroup analyses.

Overall, we found less evidence on depression and MCR in older adults, and by analyzing the available studies, we found that depression and MCR in older adults are correlated and that depression can be an essential risk factor for the development of MCR in older adults. This is the first comprehensive meta-analysis to distinguish cross-sectional studies from prospective cohort studies to investigate the possible relationship between depression and MCR in older adults and further confirms that depression is a major psychological factor associated with MCR, which will provide caregivers with new ideas and avenues to develop tertiary prevention strategies for MCR in older adults, which can be constructed as effective preventive interventions from the perspective of depression to alleviate the symptoms associated with MCR.

Limitations

However, there are some limitations in our study; firstly, the Meta-analysis of data from observational studies cannot effectively control or exclude their intrinsic confounders, and the present study included some adjusted estimates by multivariate parameters and subgroup analyses to analyze the effect of possible confounders. However, uncertainty bias and confounders still exist, and the included studies were not adjusted for the same confounders, which may bias the results of this study. Secondly, there were situations such as a small number of literature and lack of detailed information in some of the initial literature, which resulted in the inability to make further subgroup analyses of the data and to look for sources of heterogeneity; thirdly, the different methods of diagnostic assessment of depression and MCR, the differences in the selection of depression scales, and the use of different tests, such as instrumented pavement, 4 m timed walking, and 6 m timed walking to determine the presence of gait slowness in the study participants. This led to more significant heterogeneity in the results of the included studies, suggesting that a standardized method of diagnostic assessment of MCR should be set in the future to improve comparability between studies. More large-sample, multicentre clinical studies are needed to validate the findings further.

Conclusion

This study aimed to reveal the relationship between depression and MCR in older adults, and a positive effect was found by meta-analysis. The findings suggest that depression can be an independent risk factor for the development of MCR in older adults and that depression is one of the reliable evaluation indicators for identifying MCR in older adults. It is recommended that screening for MCR be carried out in clinical practice for older adults with diagnosed depression to identify potential MCR patients and take targeted interventions to maintain the physical and mental health of older adults, which may also slow down the process of dementia.
