Multicenter study of trauma activation criteria and time-sensitive care in older versus younger adults
Authors: Samir M Fakhry, Yan Shen, Nina Y Wilson, Alessandro Orlando, The Geriatric Trauma Activation Criteria Research Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe applicability of existing trauma activation criteria (TAC) and the need for geriatric-specific TAC for this vulnerable population has been debated. The goal of this study was to determine whether existing TAC are equally effective at identifying the need for high-intensity time-sensitive (HITS) interventions in older versus younger adults and their relationship to outcomes.MethodsThis multicenter, retrospective, cohort study collected data (2017–2019) securely from participating centers as National Trauma Data Bank files with trauma activation (TA) status: full (fTA), partial (pTA), none (nTA). HITS were derived from Trauma Quality Improvement Program process of care measures as critical interventions requiring trauma expertise including emergent airway management/ventilation, hemorrhage control surgery, blood transfusion, intracranial pressure monitoring, chest tube placement, and angiography. Older adults (≥65 years) were compared with younger adults (<65 years) on demographics, injury characteristics, and HITS status by TAC status using χ2 tests.ResultsTwenty-five level I and 11 level II US TCs enrolled 216 960 patients (87.9% blunt, 18.7% fTA, median age: 54 years, median Injury Severity Score: 9, in-hospital mortality 3.3%). Overall, 10.3% of patients received a HITS intervention. Older adults were less likely to receive HITS compared with younger adults (fTA: 35.4% vs 39.3%, p<0.001; pTA: 5.1% vs 6.1%, p<0.001), but this difference was not significant in the adjusted analysis for fTA (adjusted OR: 0.97, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.02, p=0.25). Compared with non-HITS, HITS were associated with increased total mortality (in-hospital mortality+hospice) in both fTA (25.6% vs 4.3%, p<0.001) and pTA (15.2% vs 2.0%, p<0.001). Rates of undertriage were high in both younger and older adults (25% and 47%, respectively).ConclusionsMeeting current TAC for highest level activation identifies patients needing HITS equally for younger and older adults, but high undertriage and overtriage remain problematic in both groups. This research highlights opportunities to address significant deficiencies in the accuracy and/or application of TAC for both younger and older adults.Level of evidenceLevel III, therapeutic/care managementKeywords: triage, geriatrics

Background

Trauma activation criteria (TAC) allow trauma centers to effectively and efficiently prepare resources for the care of incoming trauma patients.1 These allow prospective identification of severely injured patients likely to require specialized, life-saving skills and resources. Among these skills and resources are high-intensity time-sensitive (HITS) interventions such as emergent airway management, hemorrhage control, emergent surgical intervention, and other Trauma Quality Improvement Program (TQIP) Process of Care interventions.2 Using TAC ensures a qualified trauma surgeon and trauma team are immediately available to intervene and improve patient outcomes.3,8

The American College of Surgeons (ACS) requires eight TACs be adopted by verified trauma centers.1 Many centers use two to three tiers of trauma activation (TA), with seriously injured patients receiving full TA (fTA) based primarily on deranged physiology, and less severely injured patients receiving partial TA (pTA) based primarily on mechanism of injury.9 As the number of injured older adults brought to trauma centers continues to increase,10 questions have arisen regarding the applicability of existing TAC for this vulnerable population.11,16 Studies suggest geriatric trauma patients may benefit from TA even if they do not meet conventional criteria when they have risk profiles not adequately captured by traditional criteria;11 17 18 but, the quality of research supporting this approach remains modest, which warrants additional research focused on this subgroup.14 19

The purpose of this research was to conduct a large, multicenter study to determine the prevalence of HITS interventions for fTA and pTA, compare the prevalence between younger and older adults, and study the interactions between TA, HITS interventions, and outcomes. The study hypothesis was that existing TAC are equally effective at identifying the need for HITS (a surrogate for trauma team involvement) in older versus younger adults.

Methods

Study design and study sample

This was a pragmatic, nationwide, multicenter, retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult trauma inpatients (≥18 years) from level I and II trauma centers admitted from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2019. Data were securely transferred from participating centers as NTDB files with trauma activation status [fTA, pTA, none (nTA)]. Patients were excluded if: (1) Missing both emergency department (ED) disposition and hospital discharge disposition; (2) Transferred to another acute care hospital from the ED; (3) Dead on arrival (death ≤1 hour of arrival and at least one of the following: systolic blood pressure of 0 mm Hg, heart rate of 0 beats/min, motor Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) Score of 1); or (4) Incomplete data submitted. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines were applied (online supplemental digital content 1).20 A preliminary a priori power analysis using the National Trauma Data Bank for the same time period to estimate the effect size showed 3854 patients were needed for each age group, assuming an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 80% (online supplemental digital content 2). Center recruitment, data sources, and data collection are described in online supplemental digital content 3. Research workflow and data stewardship framework can be found in online supplemental digital content 4.

Variable definitions

The primary exposure variable was age group (18–64 vs ≥65 years) and was stratified by TA level: fTA, pTA, and nTA (no activation, consults, and other). TA level was defined per individual center criteria.

The primary outcome was utilization of a HITS intervention, derived from surgeon-selected acute interventions and National Trauma Data Standard (NTDS) TQIP process of care measures defined as critical interventions requiring trauma expertise: (1) Placement of endotracheal tube within 4 hours of arrival (International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS) code 0BH17EZ); (2) Surgery for hemorrhage control within 24 hours of arrival (TQIP process of care measure); (3) Any blood product transfusion within 4 hours of arrival, including packed red cells, plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate (TQIP process of care measures); (4) Placement of intracranial pressure monitor anytime during hospitalization (TQIP process of care measure); (5) Mechanical ventilation in first 24 hours (ICD-10-PCS 5A1935Z, 5A1945Z, or 5A1955Z); (6) Chest tube placement in first 4 hours from arrival (ICD-10-PCS 0W9930Z or 0W9B30Z); (7) ED thoracotomy in first 4 hours from arrival (ICD-10-PCS 0WJB0ZZ or 0WJ90ZZ); and (8) Angiography with/without embolization in first 24 hours of admission (TQIP process of care measure).

Secondary outcomes included total mortality (in-hospital mortality or discharge to hospice) and early mortality (in-hospital mortality ≤24 hours of arrival).

Statistical analysis

Older adults (≥65 years) were compared with younger adults (18–64 years) on demographics, injury characteristics, and outcomes stratified by TA level using Pearson χ2 tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. To account for clustering of patients within hospitals and capture inherent interhospital variability, a random intercept was used in generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs), allowing each hospital to have its own baseline utilization rate of HITS interventions. The adjusted results from random intercept models were quantified using adjusted ORs (aORs) and 95% CIs. A GLMM evaluated the interaction of TA level on the association of age group and HITS interventions, and on secondary outcomes of early mortality and total mortality.

To assess how the predictive performance of fTA (vs no fTA) to identify HITS interventions differed between younger and older adults, traditional performance metrics were calculated using HITS as an alloyed gold standard (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV)). Undertriage was defined as the percentage of patients with HITS interventions who did not receive an fTA (e.g., 1−sensitivity). Overtriage was defined as the percentage of patients with an fTA who did not receive a HITS intervention (e.g., 1−PPV).

High-risk groups for mortality were identified using a composite variable of HITS intervention status (HITS ±) and TA level (fTA ±). Each group was compared with the reference group on secondary outcomes using random intercept models for all patients and by age groups. In addition to standard reporting of aORs and 95% CIs, areas under the curves and 95% CIs were generated via parametric bootstrapping using the bootMer function in the lme4 package in R with 100 simulations.21

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness of results for the primary outcome (details in online supplemental digital contents 5–7). All hypothesis tests were two-tailed, and values of p<0.05 were considered statistically significant. R software V.4.3.2 was used for all analyses.22 To enhance data quality and reproducibility, an independent biostatistician verified the accuracy of the data cleaning, analytic methods, statistical code, and results.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 36 trauma centers were enrolled: 25 level I and 11 level II centers. The majority were teaching hospitals (83.3%) in urban areas (69.4%; online supplemental digital content 8). Of the 244 250 patients’ details submitted, 216 960 patients met inclusion and exclusion criteria and were included in the final study sample (figure 1).

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram for selection of patients included in the study. ED, emergency department.

The sample included 18.7% fTAs and 32.7% pTAs (table 1). Across centers, the weighted mean (SD) percentages of patients who received an fTA or pTA were 18.7% (8.9) and 32.7% (20.1), respectively. The proportion of patients >65 years increased as the TA level decreased: 18.3% in fTA, 33.7% in pTA, and 48.8% without TA. The sample had a median age of 54 years (IQR 33–71), was mostly male (60.1%), White (72.2%), and sustained predominantly blunt injuries (87.9%). Compared with younger adults, older adults had fewer male patients, more White patients, and higher comorbidity counts at all TA levels (table 1, p<0.001 for all comparisons). Additionally, older patients had higher proportions of blunt injury, falls, lower proportions of motor vehicle crashes or assaults, had higher median Injury Severity Score (ISS), and lower median ED GCS.

HITS interventions by age group and activation level

Overall, 10.3% of patients received a HITS intervention (table 2). Across centers, the weighted mean (SD) percentage of patients who received a HITS intervention was 10.3% (3.4). The proportion of HITS interventions was significantly higher in fTA than pTA patients (38.6% vs 5.7%, p<0.001). Although older adults were less likely to receive HITS interventions compared with younger adults in unadjusted analyses, the adjusted analyses revealed no statistically significant difference in the odds of HITS interventions between the two age groups among patients with fTA (table 2). For pTAs and nTAs, older age had significantly reduced adjusted odds of receiving a HITS intervention, but these differences may not be clinically meaningful. Median ISS was significantly higher in HITS+ patients than HITS− patients, both in the entire sample (18 (10–27) vs 8 (4–10)) and at each level of TA and age group (p<0.001 for all comparisons, online supplemental digital content 9).

The performance of fTA to predict HITS status was assessed (figure 2, online supplemental digital content 10) . Overall, fTA had a PPV of 39%, NPV of 96%, sensitivity of 70%, specificity of 87%; overall undertriage was 30%, and overtriage was 61%. When comparing these performance metrics between younger and older adults, there were several similarities and differences. In both age groups, fTA had similar PPVs, NPVs, and overtriage but differed in sensitivity, specificity, and undertriage. Specifically, in older adults, fTA had a higher specificity but lower sensitivity. Consequently, compared with younger adults, older adults had nearly two-fold higher undertriage (47% vs 25%, figure 2).

HITS interventions and mortality

Early mortality was 1.2% and total mortality was 4.3% (table 3). Both univariate and adjusted analyses showed significantly higher odds of early mortality and total mortality in older than in younger patients; this association was modified by TA status or HITS intervention status (table 3, online supplemental digital content 11). Compared with HITS− patients, HITS+ patients had nearly 10-fold higher risk of total mortality (22.5% vs 2.3%, p<0.001), and 30-fold higher risk of early mortality (9.0% vs 0.3%, p<0.001). The unadjusted early mortality and total mortality were low for both pTA and nTA patients who were HITS− (table 3).

HITS status and TA level were combined to identify high-risk groups for mortality (table 4). Overall, compared with those who were fTA−/HITS−, the fTA+/HITS+ group had the highest adjusted odds of total mortality (aOR: 18.28, 95% CI 17.37 to 19.23, p<0.001); these results were more pronounced in younger adults. When early mortality was examined, all effect estimates were similar in direction, but more extreme. As shown in table 4, in the fTA+/HITS+ group, compared with fTA−/HITS− group, there was a 262-fold higher adjusted odds of early mortality in the subset of younger adults (95% CI 193.58 to 355.89, p<0.001), and a 95-fold higher odds of total mortality in the subset of older adults (95% CI 79.14 to 114.18, p<0.001).

Discussion

This large, multicenter study analyzed rates of HITS interventions in patients who have TA and compared rates among types of activations and between younger and older adult patients. Regardless of age, current TAC appears equally effective at identifying adult patients who received a HITS intervention and thus required immediate, expert trauma care. The frequency of HITS interventions was significantly higher in patients who had fTA (vs pTA), whereas differences in rates of HITS interventions between age groups were small. Patients who received a HITS intervention had substantially higher 24-hour mortality and overall mortality. The combination of fTA and a HITS intervention identified the highest 24-hour mortality and overall mortality risk. Rates of undertriage and overtriage were high in both older and younger adults.

The use of the HITS interventions concept is intended to facilitate identification of patients with a clear need for trauma team deployment and justify the resource allocation and costs of TA. Trauma team deployment provides specialized, life-saving skills and resources that are not typically available in a hospital’s ED and the advance notice provided by TAC allows timely involvement of the trauma team. If the primary goal of a TAC protocol is to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from activation, then patients who required HITS but did not receive fTA would represent a missed opportunity for the triage system (i.e., undertriage). Conversely, patients assigned to fTA using a center’s TAC but not receiving HITS may represent unnecessary resource utilization (i.e., overtriage). The relatively high rates of HITS interventions in the fTA group for both younger and older patients justify the high personnel and resource costs of TA, especially since the combination of fTA and HITS+ interventions was associated with the highest mortality risks. The substantially lower rates of HITS interventions in pTA and the correspondingly lower mortality risks suggest further research is needed to identify benefits to patients and trauma systems of these lower-level activations.

The rates of HITS interventions for older adults were not clinically meaningfully different from those for younger adults for both fTA and pTA (table 2). The HITS+ patients had significantly higher ISS than the HITS- patients and their 24-hour and overall raw and adjusted odds of hospital mortality were significantly higher, especially if they also had fTA. This was true regardless of age, suggesting receiving a HITS intervention—especially in patients who met current, age-agnostic TAC for fTA—serves to identify the patients at highest risk, including those in the geriatric age group, and would be useful in the assessment of triage efficiency. In view of the extremely high odds of early and overall mortality for patients with both fTA and a HITS intervention, they would be priority candidates for early referral for palliative care consultation as recommended in the ACS TQIP Palliative Care Best Practices Guidelines.23

A large majority of patients who did not receive a HITS intervention had extremely low rates of early mortality (table 3). This was true for both younger and older adults, suggesting opportunities for decreasing preventable early mortality through early, life-saving interventions may be limited. Older patients experienced high total mortality, suggesting opportunities for decreasing preventable deaths in these older adults may exist during the subsequent phase of hospitalization rather than at arrival to the trauma bay or within the initial 24 hours.

Patients who received HITS interventions but were not fTAs (table 4) had much higher raw and adjusted mortality rates compared with similar non-fTA patients who did not receive HITS. Their ISSs were significantly higher and consistent with major trauma (ISS >15), as compared with those who did not receive HITS (online supplemental digital content 9). These patients may represent cases of undertriage or failure to upgrade to fTA in the trauma bay and are candidates for review through the trauma performance improvement program. Overall, the relatively high rates of undertriage in both younger and older adults (patients requiring HITS interventions not being classified as fTA; tables3 4) raise concerns. This is consistent with previous findings that older adults are more likely to experience undertriage,17 18 but the high undertriage rates in younger patients suggest there may be deficiencies in the accuracy or application of TAC for both younger and older adults. An adequately powered study is needed to identify the individual criterion used to prompt TA for each specific patient and analyze which criteria are associated with the highest benefit to patients, including those requiring HITS interventions, those at high risk of early and later mortality, as well as those not likely to benefit from TA with the attendant resource commitment (overtriage).

Older adult trauma patients are at increased risk of complications and mortality compared with their younger counterparts and may benefit from geriatric-specific TAC.11 19 Despite efforts to develop and implement geriatric-specific TAC, convincing evidence demonstrating effectiveness of these age-specific criteria on patient care and outcomes remains scant.14 19 A 2012 practice management guideline from the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma evaluated age as an independent determinant of trauma triage decisions and found “insufficient Class I and Class II data to support any standards.”14 In a 2022 systematic review of prehospital TAC, Boulton et al found a “paucity of published real-world data examining the effect on patient care and clinical outcomes of elderly-specific triage criteria.”19 All studies reviewed were at risk of bias and graded as “very low” or “low” scientific quality using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) Methodology.24 The ACS Trauma Quality Programs Best Practice Guideline on Geriatric Trauma Management recommends geriatric-specific TAC for ground-level fall patients on antithrombotics, systolic BP <110 mm Hg, heart rate >90 beats/min, Shock Index >1, and ground-level fall patients not on anticoagulants with GCS <14 with signs of head trauma.25 Although there is some evidence to support modifying physiologic criteria for geriatric TA, there are few large, multicenter studies that have identified geriatric TAC that achieve the goals of low undertriage without high overtriage.19

The finding of significant overtriage rates for both age groups across activation levels (figure 2) underlines the continued struggle to balance acceptable undertriage and overtriage rates but may also reflect the overuse of TAC. The opportunity to add unlimited numbers of TAC with the ostensible goal of reducing undertriage may lead to significant variation in TAC protocols at trauma centers in addition to high overtriage rates. This may result in inefficient use of hospital resources, increase provider burnout, and lead to “activation fatigue,” all factors that may contribute to decreased efficiency of organized trauma care.926,29

The effect on outcomes and TAC performance of defining geriatric trauma using different age cut-offs was evaluated in several sensitivity analyses (online supplemental digital content 5–7). Notable is the gradual increase in undertriage and overtriage rates as the age cut-off increased from 55 years to 65 years to 75 years (online supplemental digital content 7). Although some patterns emerge, the results indicate that the majority of the study conclusions generally apply regardless of the age cut-off used, further reinforcing our conclusion that the TAC currently used at US trauma centers appear equally effective at identifying adult patients needing trauma team interventions (i.e., HITS). This approach may provide data to inform future investigations on the most appropriate age cut-offs to use in designing efficient and effective TA protocols for discrete age groupings.

This study has several important limitations, including those of retrospective data analysis.30 31 Although this was a very large sample from many hospitals, it remains a convenience sample and may not be entirely generalizable. Advance directives and code status can have a significant impact on outcomes, such as mortality, but their interactions with these outcomes are difficult to evaluate in large administrative data sets. Because few centers collect data on which individual criterion was used for activation for a specific patient, the relationship of individual criteria to specific patients and their associated outcomes could not be evaluated.

Strengths of this study include the use of the HITS concept, allowing objective assessment of the need for fTA and trauma team involvement. An additional strength of this study is its generalizability to other level I and II TCs in the USA, given its large sample size and the number of TCs from varied geographic locations. The use of random intercepts in GLMMs provided a statistically robust methodology to account for the clustering of patients within hospitals related to center characteristics such as numbers of TAC. This pragmatic study was thus a real-world evaluation of the use of existing TAC in current-day practice. To extend the findings of this research and provide evidence-based recommendations for the inclusion of specific, individual activation criteria in TA protocols, a large, multicenter study is needed that collects data on which individual TAC was used for each individual fTA and pTA patient and the associated patient and system outcomes. Such a study would provide an evidence-based rationale for recommending that specific TAC be included in activation protocols with the ability to calibrate acceptable undertriage and overtriage rates and optimize both patient outcomes and system resources.

Conclusions

Meeting current TAC for highest-level activation identifies patients needing HITS equally for younger and older adults, but high undertriage and overtriage rates remain problematic in both groups. The TAC for fTA were associated with higher rates of HITS interventions regardless of age group, and justify deployment of the highest levels of trauma resources and expertise. In the interest of optimizing patient outcomes and trauma team deployment, additional research is needed to address the significant deficiencies in the accuracy and/or application of TAC for both younger and older adults. Addressing the high rates of undertriage and overtriage in both age groups may be the most pressing priority in the ongoing development and application of TAC.

Media summary

This multicenter study finds current TAC equally identify HITS interventions in younger and older adults. The study also identified high undertriage rates in both groups using HITS methodology, an area for additional study.
