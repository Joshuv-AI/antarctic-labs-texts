Reidemeister torsion of two-bridge knots and signatures of TQFT
Julien Marché, Seokbeom Yoon

Abstract
. We establish an explicit relation between the adjoint Reidemeister torsion of the two-bridge knot K ⁡ ( p , q ) K(p,q) at any parabolic representation and the Frobenius algebra governing the signatures of SU 2 -TQFT vector spaces at the root ζ = exp ⁡ ( i ​ π ​ q / p ) \zeta=\exp(i\pi q/p) . As applications, (a) we prove that the inverse sum of torsions is constant (i.e., independent of p p and q q ); and (b) we show that along sequences of roots of the form ζ n = exp ⁡ ( i ​ π ​ a + b ​ n c + d ​ n ) \zeta_{n}=\exp\left(i\pi\tfrac{a+bn}{c+dn}\right) , the signatures have the same asymptotic behavior as the Verlinde formula.



1.
Introduction

Let
p
and
q
be coprime odd integers satisfying
0<q<p
and denote by
K(p,q)
the two-bridge knot with these parameters. Independently, setting
\zeta=\exp(i\pi q/p)
, one can consider the
\mathrm{SU}_{2}
-TQFT with defining root
\zeta
. For any genus
g
, it gives a Hermitian vector space
\mathcal{V}_{\zeta}(S_{g})
endowed with a projective unitary representation of the mapping class group
\mathrm{Mod}(S_{g})
. This article establishes a tight relation between, on the one hand, the space of parabolic
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-representations of
\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q))
and the adjoint Reidemeister torsion, and, on the other hand, a Frobenius algebra governing the signatures of the various vector spaces
\mathcal{V}_{\zeta}(S_{g})
. It completes the work initiated in
[
Mar23
]
and provides applications both to the computation of the Reidemeister torsion itself and to the study of the asymptotic behavior of the signatures for a sequence
\zeta_{n}
of roots converging to a given root
\zeta
.

In both the introduction and the main part of the paper, we have tried to separate, as much as possible, the knot-theoretic aspects from the TQFT aspects for the reader’s benefit. Before proceeding further, we caution that our proofs are computational in nature: we do not yet have a conceptual explanation for the relations we uncover. Finding such an explanation remains an interesting open problem.

1.1.
About two-bridge knots

We denote by
\mathcal{X}
the set of conjugacy classes of irreducible representations
\rho:\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q))\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
which send any (hence every) meridian to a non-trivial matrix with trace
2
. This set is a finite affine algebraic set defined over
\mathbb{Z}
, hence its coordinate ring
\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
is a finite commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra. Riley showed in
[
Ril72
]
that it is semi-simple.

Given
[\rho]\in\mathcal{X}
and any
n\geq 1
, we define
\rho_{n}
to be the composition of
\rho
with the
(n+1)
-dimensional irreducible representation of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. We refer to
\rho=\rho_{1}
as the tautological representation and to
\rho_{2}
as the adjoint representation.
By choosing appropriate generators of the twisted cohomology groups
\mathrm{H}^{*}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q),\rho_{n})
, one can define the Reidemeister torsion
\tau_{n}(\rho)
as a complex number depending on
[\rho]\in\mathcal{X}
. By construction, the map
\rho\mapsto\tau_{n}(\rho)
is conjugation-invariant and algebraic, yielding an element
\tau_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
. Throughout the paper, we consider
\tau_{n}
up to sign to avoid unnecessary complications.

It will be technically more convenient to replace
\mathcal{X}
with a ramified 2-cover
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
, and hence
\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
with the corresponding quadratic extension
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
. Topologically, this amounts to recalling that
K(p,q)
is doubly amphichiral, meaning that there exist two commuting involutions of
S^{3}
which preserve
K(p,q)
while reversing its orientation. Using this fact, we construct an orbifold quotient of
S^{3}\setminus K(p,q)
whose orbifold fundamental group
G
fits in the (split) exact sequence

0\to\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q))\to G\to Q_{8}\to 0

where
Q_{8}
is the usual quaternion group of order
8
. Note that similar groups have already been considered in
[
LS13
]
. Then
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
is the set of conjugacy classes of representations
\rho:G\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
whose restriction to
\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q))
defines an element of
\mathcal{X}
.

Concretely, we will show that there is an isomorphism
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]\cong\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1}(X))
where
P_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
is the sequence of polynomials given by

P_{0}=1,\ P_{1}=X\text{ and }P_{k}=\epsilon_{k}XP_{k-1}+P_{k-2}\text{ where }\epsilon_{k}=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{kq}{p}\rfloor}.

We set
V=\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
and
V^{+}=\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
to simplify notation, and denote by
x
and
\mathcal{P}_{k}
the image of
X
and
P_{k}
, respectively, in
V
. It turns out that
V^{+}
is spanned by
\mathcal{P}_{k}
for even
0\leq k\leq p-3
, and that
V
is the quadratic extension of
V^{+}
obtained by adjoining
\mathcal{P}_{p-2}
, which is a square root of
-1
in
V
.

The recurrence relation defining the sequence
P_{k}
is typical for orthogonal polynomials. Indeed, there exists a non-degenerate bilinear form
\eta:V\times V\to\mathbb{Q}
for which the basis
\mathcal{P}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{p-2}
is orthogonal. It is given by
\eta(x,y)=\epsilon(xy)
where
\epsilon:V\to\mathbb{Q}
is the linear form satisfying
\epsilon(\mathcal{P}_{0})=1
and
\epsilon(\mathcal{P}_{k})=0
for
0<k<p-1
.
The data of
\eta
, or equivalently of
\epsilon
, endows
V
with the structure of a commutative semi-simple Frobenius algebra.
At first glance, this appears unrelated to the topological definition of
V
, however, one of our main theorems is that this additional structure arises from the adjoint Reidemeister torsion.
To explain this, recall that for any Frobenius algebra
(V,\eta)
, one defines an element
\Omega=\sum x_{i}y_{i}
where
\eta^{-1}=\sum x_{i}\otimes y_{i}\in V\otimes V
. When
V
is semi-simple,
\epsilon
can be recovered from
\Omega
by the formula
\epsilon(x)=\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(\Omega^{-1}x)
.

Theorem A

(Theorems
2.8
,
2.14
and Equation (
31
))
.

Let
0<q<p
be coprime odd integers and
V
be the Frobenius algebra described above with associated element
\Omega
. Denoting by
\tau_{n}
the Reidemeister torsion of
S^{3}\setminus K(p,q)
with coefficients
\rho_{n}
, one has

\tau_{1}=\frac{2}{x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{2}=\frac{\Omega}{4x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}}

where
0<\ell<p
is the unique odd integer satisfying
q\ell\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
.

The appearance of
\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}
deserves some explanation. By the classification of two-bridge knots,
K(p,q)
and
K(p,\ell)
are isotopic. Denoting by
\mathcal{X}^{*},V^{*},\ldots
all the previous data associated to
K(p,\ell)
, it follows that
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
are isomorphic as algebraic varieties, hence
V
and
V^{*}
are isomorphic as algebras. More precisely, we will show that the isomorphism
V^{*}\leftrightarrow V
sends
x^{*}
to
x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}
and
x
to
x^{*}\mathcal{P}^{*}_{q-1}
.
As
\tau_{n}
is a topological invariant, the formulas provided in
V
and
V^{*}
should match. This is obvious for
\tau_{1}
, if one writes it as
\tau_{1}=\frac{2}{xx^{*}}
. In the case of
\tau_{2}
, we have instead:

\tau_{2}=\frac{\Omega}{4x(x^{*})^{2}}=\frac{\Omega^{*}}{4x^{*}x^{2}}.

This yields the reciprocity formula
x\Omega=x^{*}\Omega^{*}
, showing that the Frobenius algebra structures on
V
and
V^{*}
are indeed distinct. In addition, this formula will play a crucial role in our study of the asymptotics of the signature of TQFT.

As a first application of our formulas, we will prove the following two equations, where the latter extends the result of
[
Yoo22
]
to the parabolic case and has a quantum-physical motivation; see
[
GKY21
]
.
Note that the condition
q\neq 1
in the following theorem implies that the two-bridge knot
K(p,q)
is hyperbolic, and that the theorem fails when
q=1
.

Theorem B

(Theorems
2.16
and
2.17
)
.

Let
0<q<p
be coprime odd integers with
q\neq 1
and
\mathcal{X}
be the parabolic character variety of
S^{3}\setminus K(p,q)
. Then one has

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\tau_{1}(\rho)}=\pm 1\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\tau_{2}(\rho)}=0.

1.2.
About signatures of TQFT

Fix an odd integer
p
and let
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
be the cyclotomic field of order
2p
, where
\zeta
is a primitive
2p
-th root of unity. We set
\Lambda=\{0,1,\ldots,p-2\}
and
\Lambda^{+}=\{0,2,\ldots,p-3\}
which we use as “colors” indexing marked points. In this article, we consider the
\mathrm{SU}_{2}/\mathrm{SO}_{3}
-TQFT as a collection of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-vector spaces
\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g,n};\lambda)
where
S_{g,n}
is a surface of genus
g
with
n
marked points and
\lambda\in\Lambda^{n}
is a color of the marked points. These are endowed with an Hermitian form defined over
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
and an action of the pure mapping class group
\mathrm{PMod}(S_{g,n})
by projective isometries.
A key property is that these vector spaces satisfy compatibility conditions under gluing. If
S
is a surface and
\gamma\subset S
is a simple closed curve disjoint from the marked points, then a new (possibly disconnected) surface
S^{\prime}
is obtained by cutting
S
along
\gamma
, capping off the resulting boundaries with discs, and adding a new marked point in each disc. When
\gamma
is non-separating, the compatibility means an isomorphism

\mathcal{V}_{p}(S;\lambda)=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\Lambda}\mathcal{V}_{p}(S^{\prime};\lambda,\mu,\mu)

which is compatible with both the Hermitian structure and the mapping class group action. There is a similar statement for the separating case.

In this article, we embed
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
into
\mathbb{C}
by setting
\zeta=\exp(i\pi\tfrac{q}{p})
and define
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces as
\mathcal{V}_{\zeta}(S_{g,n};\lambda)=\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g,n};\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{C}
whose signature is denoted by

\sigma_{g}(\tfrac{q}{p};\lambda)=\Sign\mathcal{V}_{\zeta}(S_{g,n};\lambda).

Since any surface can be decomposed into pairs of pants, the aforementioned compatibility allows one to reduce the computation of the signature to the sphere with at most three marked points, by introducing the so-called signed Verlinde Frobenius algebra
[
DM22
]
. It is a
(p-1)
-dimensional vector space
V=\bigoplus_{k\in\Lambda}\mathbb{Q}e_{k}
equipped with a symmetric bilinear form
\eta:V\times V\to\mathbb{Q}
and a trilinear symmetric form
\omega:V\times V\times V\to\mathbb{Q}
, given by

\displaystyle\eta(e_{j},e_{k})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}(\tfrac{q}{p};\,j,k)=\delta_{jk}(-1)^{j}\epsilon_{j+1}\,,

\displaystyle\omega(e_{j},e_{k},e_{l})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}(\tfrac{q}{p};\,j,k,l)\in\{-1,0,1\}\,.

Here
\delta_{jk}
denotes the Kronecker delta.
It was shown in
[
DM22
]
that there is a unique structure of semi-simple algebra on
V
so that one has
\omega(x,y,z)=\eta(xy,z)
for any
x,y,z\in V
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
Mar23
]
that sending
e_{k}
to
\mathcal{P}_{k}
defines an algebra isomorphism from
V
to
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
. This is why we have already denoted it by
V
in the first part of the introduction. The initial motivation of this article was to extend the isomorphism
V\simeq\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
to an isomorphism of Frobenius algebras. To achieve this, one needs to give a topological meaning to the element
\Omega
. It was a pleasant surprise that the adjoint Reidemeister torsion turns out to play exactly this role.

One can recovers the signatures from the Frobenius algebra
V
by means of the following formula, which holds for any surface
S_{g,n}
colored by
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
:

\sigma_{g}(\tfrac{q}{p};\lambda)=\epsilon(\Omega^{g}e_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots e_{\lambda_{n}})=\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(\Omega^{g-1}e_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots e_{\lambda_{n}}).

There is a similar
\mathrm{SO}_{3}
-TQFT for which the set of colors is restricted to
\Lambda^{+}
. The corresponding Frobenius algebra is the subalgebra
V^{+}\subset V
consisting of even colors. As
\Omega\in V^{+}
, this subalgebra naturally inherits a Frobenius algebra structure. One often prefers the
\mathrm{SO}_{3}
-TQFT because it has smaller dimension and better properties, such as irreducibility and the existence of an integral basis. In this article, however, we restrict to the
\mathrm{SU}_{2}
-case, as their signatures only differ by a power of
2
.

1.3.
Asymptotics of the signature along roots of unity

Let us first recall that the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g})
is given by the famous Verlinde formula (we set
n=0
, i.e., we consider surfaces with no marked points from now on):

\dim\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g})=\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{g-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\sin\big(\frac{k\pi}{p}\big)^{2-2g}.

One can easily deduce from the formula the asymptotic behavior

\dim\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g})\underset{p\to\infty}{\sim}2\left(\frac{p^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\right)^{g-1}\zeta(2g-2),

which admits a beautiful interpretation; see, e.g.,
[
Wit91
]
.
For motivational purposes, we briefly recall this. Regarding the vector space
\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g})
as the geometric quantization of the character variety

\mathcal{M}(S_{g})=\Hom(\pi_{1}(S_{g}),\mathrm{SU}_{2})/\mathrm{SU}_{2},

one can endow
\mathcal{M}(S_{g})
with a complex structure and construct a holomorphic line bundle
L
over
\mathcal{M}(S_{g})
such that

\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g})\simeq\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathcal{M}(S_{g}),L^{p-2}).

Since
c_{1}(L)
represents the Atiyah-Bott-Goldman symplectic (Kähler) structure on
\mathcal{M}(S_{g})
, the higher cohomology groups vanish for sufficiently large
p
, and the Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch theorem yields

\dim\mathcal{V}_{p}(S_{g})=\int_{\mathcal{M}(S_{g})}e^{p\,c_{1}(L)}\operatorname{Todd}(X)\underset{p\to\infty}{\sim}p^{3g-3}\operatorname{Vol}(\mathcal{M}(S_{g})),

together with the non-trivial fact that the Verlinde formula is a polynomial in
p
.

It is then natural to ask whether a similar behavior occurs for the signature along sequences of roots of unity. Numerical experiments show that the answer depends strongly on the specific sequence of roots of unity. Our final main theorem provides an explicit asymptotic formula for the signatures.

The proof of the above theorem is rather involved, so we outline its main steps for the reader’s convenience.

Step 1:

One has
\sigma_{g}(q_{n}/p_{n})=\Tr_{V_{n}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}}(\Omega_{n}^{g-1})
where
V_{n}^{*}
is the Frobenius algebra associated with
q_{n}/p_{n}
. Recall that
V_{n}^{*}\simeq V_{n}
as algebras, hence the trace can be computed in
V_{n}
instead. Then, by construction, the pair
(p_{n},q_{n})
is reciprocal to the pair
(p_{n},d)
. It will be much more convenient to work in
V_{n}
, as
q_{n}
is replaced by the constant
d
.

Step 2:

We will show that in
V_{n}
, the polynomial
P_{p_{n}-1}
(and certain other polynomials used in the computation of the signature) arises as a specializations of a
2
-variable polynomial
Q
; explicitly, one has

P_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})(t-t^{-1})^{d}=i^{n+1}Q(t,t^{n}).

Step 3:

We consider the plane algebraic curve
\mathcal{C}=\{(u,v)\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}\,|\,Q(u,v)=0\}
. The signature can be expressed as the sum of a rational function to the power
g-1
over a finite set
\mathcal{C}[n]=\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,v=u^{n}\}
. We then study the distribution of these points as
n\to\infty
and show that the main contribution to the signature concentrates around the singular points
\{\pm 1\}^{2}\subset\mathcal{C}
.

Step 4:

We analyze the branches of
\mathcal{C}
at the singular points
(\pm 1,\pm 1)
. Under the assumption (
H
), exactly
d
branches pass through each singular point, and the points of
\mathcal{C}[n]
accumulate along each branch in a regular way. The sum of these contributions, raised to the power
g-1
, is accounted for in the term
\Tr_{W/\mathbb{Q}}\Omega^{g-1}
.

To end this section, we advise the reader that there is a parallel article which also study the asymptotic behavior of the signature function
\frac{q}{p}\mapsto\sigma_{g}(\frac{q}{p})
, but in a different regime. Contrary to the case of this article, the authors consider sequences
\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}
converging to a (very) irrational limit. The order of convergence is then
p_{n}^{2g-2}
instead of
p_{n}^{3g-3}
and the limit is expected to have modular properties, see
[
MM25
]
.

1.4.
Back to two-bridge knots

To end this introduction, let us explain how our last theorem arises naturally from the knot-theoretical side. We do not attempt to make the argument precise.

The sequence of two-bridge knots
K(p_{n},q_{n})
can be drawn in its Conway normal form starting from the continued expansion of
p_{n}/q_{n}
. It is known (see e.g.
[
Khi64
]
) that there are integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
(independent of
n
) such that for any
n

\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\cdots+\cfrac{1}{a_{k}+n-1}}}

We observe in Figure
1
that this sequence of knots can be obtained by performing
\frac{1}{m}
-surgery on the trivial component
L_{2}
of the link
L
, where
m=\frac{n-1}{2}
for odd
n
. In the figure, boxes indicate twist regions, and
L_{2}
is the trivial knot encircling the last twist region. This gives the key intuition for the last theorem.

Figure 1.
The Conway normal form of
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}]
with a trivial component.

We consider the character variety
\mathcal{X}_{L}
of representations
\rho:\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus L)\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
which send a meridian
m_{1}
of the non-trivial component
L_{1}
to a non-trivial parabolic matrix. Denoting by
m_{2}
and
l_{2}
the meridian and longitude of
L_{2}
, respectively, the knot group of the surgered knot is obtained by adding the relation
m_{2}l_{2}^{-m}=1
.
The character variety
\mathcal{X}_{L}
is expected to be 1-dimensional and to project birationally onto the character variety of the boundary
T_{2}
of a tubular neighborhood of
L_{2}
. The latter variety is parametrized by a pair
(u,v)\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
corresponding to a representation
\rho_{u,v}:\pi_{1}(T_{2})\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
given by

\rho_{u,v}(l_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&u^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho_{u,v}(m_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}v&0\\
0&v^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

The image of
\mathcal{X}_{L}
in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
is then a plane curve
\mathcal{C}
given by the vanishing of a polynomial analogous to the
A
-polynomial of a knot. The parabolic character variety
\mathcal{X}_{n}
of
K(p_{n},q_{n})
maps to the set of points
\mathcal{C}[m]\subset\mathcal{C}
satisfying
v=u^{m}
.

To end this rough picture, recall that one can define the Reidemeister torsion
\tau_{L}
of
S^{3}\setminus L
with coefficients
\rho_{2}
for
[\rho]\in\mathcal{X}_{L}
. This is a meromorphic function on
\mathcal{X}_{L}
which behaves well under surgery, meaning that there is a formula expressing the collection of elements
\Omega_{n}
in terms of the function
\tau_{L}
evaluated at points in
\mathcal{C}[m]
. This expression happens to have poles for
u,v=\pm 1
, and the resulting expression for the signature will localize on those poles.

It is then natural to expect that the Frobenius algebra
W
, explicitly described in Section
3
, can be described from the parabolic character variety of
L
, i.e., from representations which send both
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
to parabolic elements. We leave this for future investigation.

2.
Reidemeister torsions of two-bridge knots

2.1.
Topological setting

Let
0<q<p
be two coprime odd integers and
K(p,q)
be the two-bridge knot of parameters
(p,q)
. It is characterized by the property that its double branched cover is the lens space
L(p,q)
.
Precisely, the knot
K(p,q)
is defined as follows. Set

S^{3}=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\,:\,|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}=2\}

and let
R:S^{3}\rightarrow S^{3}
be the homeomorphism defined by

R(z_{1},z_{2})=(\zeta z_{1},\zeta^{q}z_{2}),\quad\zeta=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{p}\right)\,.

The lens space
L(p,q)
is the quotient of
S^{3}
by the free action of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
generated
R
.
The complex conjugation
C(z_{1},z_{2})=(\overline{z_{1}},\overline{z_{2}})
satisfies
CRC^{-1}=R^{-1}
and hence extends the
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
-action to an action of the dihedral group
D_{p}=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
This
D_{p}
-action is no longer free, and the resulting quotient is homeomorphic to
S^{3}
, with ramification locus given by the knot
K(p,q)
.

A standard way to visualize the above construction is to decompose
S^{3}
into two solid tori
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
defined by the inequalities
|z_{1}|\leq|z_{2}|
and
|z_{2}|\leq|z_{1}|
, respectively. Note that their common boundary is a torus given by
|z_{1}|=|z_{2}|=1
. The quotient of
T_{1}
by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is also a solid torus

T_{1}/(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})=\big\{(re^{i\theta},z_{2})\,\big|\,0\leq r\leq 1,\ -\frac{\pi}{p}\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{p},\ r^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}=2\big\}/_{\sim}

where
\sim
means that the discs bounded by
z_{1}=\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
z_{1}=\zeta^{-\frac{1}{2}}
are identified by
R
; see Figure
2
. If we further quotient
T_{1}/(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
by the conjugation
C
, we obtain a ball with some ramification. Precisely, the quotient of
T_{1}
by
D_{p}
is a ball
B_{1}
with ramification locus consisting of two arcs (blue arcs in Figure
2
):

(1)

\{(1,t)\,|\,-1\leq t\leq 1\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{(\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}},t\zeta^{\frac{q}{2}})\,|\,-1\leq t\leq 1\}\,.

Similarly, the quotient
T_{2}/D_{p}
is also a ball
B_{2}
containing two ramified arcs. Gluing two balls
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
yields the 3-sphere, in which the ramified arcs combine to form the knot
K(p,q)
.

Figure 2.
The quotients of
T_{1}
by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and
D_{p}
.

If we project the ball
B_{1}
onto a planar square so that the two ramified arcs in (
1
) are mapped to its vertical edges, the two ramified arcs in
B_{2}
are attached to the vertical edges with slope
q/p
, as illustrated in Figure
3
. The resulting diagram is called Schubert’s normal form of
K(p,q)
.
Letting
u
and
v
be the Wirtinger generators associated with the vertical edges, we obtain the following presentation of the knot group
G=\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q))
:

(2)

G=\langle u,v\,|\,wuw^{-1}v^{-1}\rangle,\quad w=u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u^{\epsilon_{p-2}}v^{\epsilon_{p-1}}

where
\epsilon_{k}=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{kq}{p}\rfloor}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and the relator in (
2
) is obtained by traversing the diagram once.

Figure 3.
Schubert’s normal form of
K(5,3)
.

By interchanging the roles of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, we obtain another presentation of
G
, which coincides with the presentation (
2
) of
\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,\ell))
. Here
0<\ell<p
is the unique odd integer satisfying
q\ell\equiv\pm 1
modulo
p
.
This follows from the fact that the exchange map
E(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{2},z_{1})
induces a homeomorphism between
L(p,q)
and
L(p,\ell)
when
q\ell\equiv 1
, and the composition
E\circ F
, where
F(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1},\overline{z_{2}})
, does the same when
q\ell\equiv-1
. In either case,
K(p,q)
and
K(p,\ell)
are isotopic, and thus their knot groups are isomorphic.

The knot
K(p,q)
is doubly amphichiral, meaning that there are two commuting involutions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
induced by the transformations
(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(z_{1},-z_{2})
and
(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(-z_{1},z_{2})
, respectively. Each
h_{i}
fixes a circle that intersects the knot in two points, and the two circles meet at two points. Choosing a base point of
G
at one of the intersection points of the two circles, the involutions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
induce automorphisms
h_{1}^{\ast}
and
h_{2}^{\ast}
of
G
, respectively, which commute with each other.
Explicitly, they are given by

(3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}h_{1}^{\ast}(u)=u^{-1}\\[2.0pt]
h_{1}^{\ast}(v)=v^{-1}\end{array}\right.,\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}h_{2}^{\ast}(u)=v^{-1}\\[2.0pt]
h_{2}^{\ast}(v)=u^{-1}\end{array}\right.\,.

It is not trivial from the presentation (
2
) that
h^{\ast}_{1}
and
h^{\ast}_{2}
are well-defined, one of the key properties of the sequence
\epsilon_{k}
is that it is palindromic in the sense that
\epsilon_{k}=\epsilon_{p-k}
for all
0<k<p
.

2.2.
Parabolic character varieties

In this section, we consider the character variety of irreducible representations

\rho:G=\pi_{1}(S^{3}\setminus K(p,q))\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

which are
parabolic
, by which we mean that each meridian is mapped to a non-trivial matrix with trace 2. It is well-known that this is an affine algebraic set
\mathcal{X}
defined over
\mathbb{Z}
whose complex points can up to conjugation be put in the form

\rho(u)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(v)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
X&1\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
X
is nonzero, as
u
and
v
are conjugate in
G
. The coordinate ring
\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
of
\mathcal{X}
is generated by
\Tr\rho(u)
,
\Tr\rho(v)
, and
\Tr\rho(uv)
; hence, it has the single generator
X
. More precisely, it is shown in
[
Ril72
]
that

\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}[X]/(R)

where
R\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
is called the Riley polynomial of the knot
K(p,q)
.

For the purpose of this article, it is more elegant to adopt a symmetric setting by instead taking

(4)

\rho(u)=\begin{pmatrix}1&X\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(v)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
X&1\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
X
in this setting is also non-zero, as meridians are sent to non-trivial matrices.
This symmetric setting can be understood conceptually in the following way.
Recall from Section
2.1
that
S^{3}\setminus K(p,q)
admits two involutions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
. The quotient of
S^{3}\setminus K(p,q)
by these involutions is an orbifold
\mathcal{O}(p,q)
, where its (orbifold) fundamental group fits into the exact sequence

0\to G\to\pi_{1}(\mathcal{O}(p,q))\to(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}\to 0\,.

The above sequence splits. In addition, it follows from Equation (
3
) that

(5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}h_{1}uh_{1}^{-1}=u^{-1}\\[2.0pt]
h_{1}vh_{1}^{-1}=v^{-1}\end{array}\right.,\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}h_{2}uh_{2}^{-1}=v^{-1}\\[2.0pt]
h_{2}vh_{2}^{-1}=u^{-1}\end{array}\right..

We then add an extra global
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
isotropy group to
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{O}(p,q))
by considering the fiber product

\hat{G}=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{O}(p,q))\!\underset{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}}{\times}\!Q_{8}\,.

Here
Q_{8}
is the standard quaternion group and
Q_{8}\to(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
is the abelianization map.
The group
\hat{G}
is a central extension of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{O}(p,q))
, and the involutions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
lift to elements
\hat{h}_{1}
and
\hat{h}_{2}
in
Q_{8}
, respectively, whose squares are equal to the central element of order 2.

Definition 2.1
.

The
parabolic character variety

\hat{\mathcal{X}}
of
\hat{G}
is the algebraic set of conjugacy classes of representations
\rho:\hat{G}\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that the its restriction to
G
is irreducible and parabolic, and
\rho(\hat{h}_{1}^{2})=\rho(\hat{h}_{2}^{2})=-I
.

For any
\rho
in
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
, the last condition implies that its restriction to
Q_{8}
is irreducible, because any reducible representation of
Q_{8}
satisfies
\rho(\hat{h}_{i}^{2})=I
. Hence, there is a unique conjugation so that
Q_{8}
has its usual irreducible representation in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
:

(6)

\rho(\hat{h}_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(\hat{h}_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(\hat{h}_{1}\hat{h}_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 2.2
.

The conjugations described in Equation (
5
) force
\rho(u)
and
\rho(v)
to have the symmetric form given in (
4
) up to exchanging the role of
u
and
v
.

Proof.

From the fact
\hat{h}_{1}u\hat{h}_{1}^{-1}=u^{-1}
, we deduce that
\rho(u)_{11}=\rho(u)_{22}
. Here
A_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-entry of
A
. Combined with the condition
\Tr\rho(u)=2
, this implies
\rho(u)_{11}=\rho(u)_{22}=1
. Since
\det\rho(u)=1
, it follows that either
\rho(u)_{12}
or
\rho(u)_{21}
is zero. The same reasoning applies to
v
, showing that
\rho(v)
is upper or lower triangular with ones on the diagonal.
Since conjugating with
h_{2}
changes
u
to
v^{-1}
,
\rho(u)
and
\rho(v)
can not be upper or lower triangular simultaneously, one must be upper and the other lower. Finally, the fact that the conjugation by
\hat{h}_{1}\hat{h}_{2}
exchanges
u
and
v
forces the non-zero off-diagonal entries of
\rho(u)
and
\rho(v)
to be equal. This results in Equation (
4
) up to exchanging the role of
u
and
v
.
∎

As with any character variety, the coordinate ring
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
of
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
is generated by trace functions
\Tr\rho(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\hat{G}
. As we have explicit generators of
\hat{G}
, it is easy to check that
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
is generated by
x
and
i
; for instance, we have

\Tr\rho(\hat{h}_{2}u)=-x
and
\Tr\rho(u\hat{h}_{1}\hat{h}_{2})=ix
.
In Proposition
2.4
below, we will prove that
i
can be expressed as a polynomial in
x
, implying that
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
has the single generator
x
.

Given formal variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k}
, we define the continuants

K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})=\det\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&-1&&&\\
1&X_{2}&-1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&X_{k-1}&-1\\
&&&1&X_{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}]\,.

The following are well-known properties of continuants. We omit the proofs, as they follow readily by routine induction arguments.

Lemma 2.3
.

The continuants
K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
satisfy

(7)

\displaystyle K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1})+K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-2}),

\displaystyle K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})

\displaystyle=K(X_{k},\ldots,X_{1}),

(8)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}X_{k}&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})&K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1})\\
K(X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})&K(X_{2},\ldots,X_{k-1})\end{pmatrix}

for any
k\geq 1
. In addition,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1})\,K(X_{i+k+2},\ldots,X_{i+j})

\displaystyle=K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i+j})K(X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{i+k})-K(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i+k})K(X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{i+j})

holds for any
i\geq 1
,
k\geq 0
, and
j\geq k+1
.

We set

(9)

P_{k}=K(\epsilon_{1}X,\ldots,\epsilon_{k}X),\quad Q_{k}=K(\epsilon_{2}X,\ldots,\epsilon_{k}X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]

for
k\geq 1
and extend both to all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
by using the recursion (
7
); for instance,

P_{0}=1,\quad P_{-1}=0,\quad Q_{0}=0,\quad Q_{1}=1.

Equation (
8
) says that for any
k\geq 1

(10)

\begin{pmatrix}P_{k}&P_{k-1}\\
Q_{k}&Q_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{1}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{k}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

Grouping matrices in the right-hand side in pairs, we have

\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{k}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{k+1}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\epsilon_{k}X\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\epsilon_{k+1}X&1\end{pmatrix}=\rho(u^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\epsilon_{k+1}})

for any
k\geq 1
, implying that

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}P_{2k}&P_{2k-1}\\
Q_{2k}&Q_{2k-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\rho(u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u^{\epsilon_{2k-1}}v^{\epsilon_{2k}})

as well as

(12)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}P_{2k}&P_{2k+1}\\
Q_{2k}&Q_{2k+1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\rho(u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u^{\epsilon_{2k-1}}v^{\epsilon_{2k}}u^{\epsilon_{2k+1}}).

In particular, for
w=u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u^{\epsilon_{p-2}}v^{\epsilon_{p-1}}
, one has

(13)

\rho(w)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{p-1}&P_{p-2}\\
Q_{p-1}&Q_{p-2}\end{pmatrix}.

It is shown in
[
Ril72
]
that the Riley polynomial is equal to
\rho(w)_{11}=P_{p-1}
. That is, the assignment
(
4
) defines an
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-representation of
G
if and only if
P_{p-1}(X)=0
.

Proposition 2.4
.

P_{p-2}
satisfies
P_{p-2}^{2}=-1
in
\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
.

Proof.

Combining Equation (
10
) for
k=p-1
with the fact that
\epsilon_{k}=\epsilon_{p-k}
for all
0<k<p
, we deduce that
\rho(w)
is symmetric, i.e.,
P_{p-2}=Q_{p-1}
. Together with the fact that
\rho(w)
has determinant 1, we have
P_{p-2}^{2}=-1
in
\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
.
∎

The above proposition shows that the complex number
i
in (
6
) can be replaced by the polynomial
P_{p-2}\in\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
. It follows that the coordinate ring
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
of
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
has the single generator
X
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The coordinate ring
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
.

Proof.

The coordinate ring
\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
of
\mathcal{X}
is generated by
\Tr\rho(u)
,
\Tr\rho(v)
, and
\Tr\rho(uv)
and is thus generated by
X^{2}
, subject to the relation given by the Riley polynomial. Namely,
\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]\simeq\mathbb{Q}[X^{2}]/(P_{p-1})
. On the other hand,
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
is obtained from
\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
by adding
X
and
i\in\mathbb{C}
, where the latter can be replaced by the polynomial
P_{p-2}
due to Proposition
2.4
. Hence
\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
.
∎

As Riley did in
[
Ril72
]
and the first author repeated in
[
Mar23
]
, by reducing modulo 2, one can prove that the polynomial
P_{p-1}
has no multiple roots. In other words,
\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
is a semi-simple
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra.

2.3.
Computation of the torsion

In this section, we compute the Reidemeister torsions of
K(p,q)
associated with representations
\rho_{n}
defined as below. The computation proceeds in
V=\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]=\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
, and we denote by
x
,
\mathcal{P}_{k}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{k}
the image of
X
,
P_{k}
and
Q_{k}
in
V
, respectively.

Recall from Section
2.2
that the polynomial
P_{p-1}
is designed to give a parabolic representation
\rho:G\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{2}(V)
.
The natural action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(V)
on the
n
-th symmetric power
\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2}
of
V^{2}
defines a representation
s_{n}:\mathrm{SL}_{2}(V)\to\mathrm{SL}(\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2})
. The composition
\rho_{n}=s_{n}\circ\rho
is a representation of
G
into
\mathrm{SL}(\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2})
and allows us to twist the usual cohomology of the knot complement by the local system
\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2}
. Under some cohomological conditions specified below, the Reidemeister torsion with respect to
\rho_{n}
is well-defined (see e.g.
[
Por15
]
), and we simply denote it by

\tau_{n}\in V/\{\pm 1\}\,.

Note that the torsion is defined up to sign. This sign ambiguity can be resolved by fixing a homological order of the knot complement, but we will not address it in this paper.

The fact that the torsion is invariant under simple homotopy allows us to compute the torsion
\tau_{n}
using the 2-dimensional complex associated with the presentation (
2
) of the knot group. Since this presentation has two generators
u
and
v
with a single relator
r=wuw^{-1}v^{-1}
, the corresponding chain complex is

(14)

\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2}\overset{D^{0}}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2}\oplus\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2}\overset{D^{1}}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2}\,.

The differentials
D^{i}
of the above complex are given as follows.
Let
F_{2}
be the free group with generators
u
and
v
, and let
\frac{\partial}{\partial u},\frac{\partial}{\partial v}
be the usual Fox derivatives, i.e, the unique endomorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}[F_{2}]
such that

\frac{\partial u}{\partial u}=1,\quad\frac{\partial v}{\partial u}=0,\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial v}=0,\quad\frac{\partial v}{\partial v}=1

and

\frac{\partial ab}{\partial u}=\frac{\partial a}{\partial u}+a\frac{\partial b}{\partial u},\quad\frac{\partial ab}{\partial v}=\frac{\partial a}{\partial v}+a\frac{\partial b}{\partial v}\quad\text{for any }a,b\in F_{2}\,.

Note that

(15)

\frac{\partial a}{\partial u}(u-1)+\frac{\partial{a}}{\partial v}(v-1)=a-1\quad\text{for any }a\in F_{2}\,.

Abusing notation, we denote by
\rho_{n}:\mathbb{Z}[F_{2}]\to\mathbb{Z}[G]\to\End(\mathrm{S}^{n}V^{2})
the map sending
a
to
\rho_{n}(a)
. Then the differentials in the chain compelex (
14
) read

D^{0}(\xi)=\Big(\rho_{n}(u-1)\xi,\ \rho_{n}(v-1)\xi\Big),\quad D^{1}(\xi,\eta)=\rho_{n}\!\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial u}\right)\xi+\rho_{n}\!\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial v}\right)\eta.

Note that
D^{1}\circ D^{0}=0
follows from Equation (
15
) together with the fact that
\rho_{n}(r)=I
.

Let us further compute
D^{1}
.
Recall that the word
w
is given by
w=u^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots v^{\epsilon_{p-1}}
. A striaghtforward computation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\begin{cases}u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots v^{\epsilon_{2k-2}}&\text{if }\epsilon_{2k-1}=1\\
-u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots v^{\epsilon_{2k-2}}u^{\epsilon_{2k-1}}&\text{if }\epsilon_{2k-1}=-1\end{cases},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\begin{cases}u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u^{\epsilon_{2k-1}}&\text{if }\epsilon_{2k}=1\\
-u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u^{\epsilon_{2k-1}}v^{\epsilon_{2k}}&\text{if }\epsilon_{2k}=-1\end{cases}.

Using Equations (
11
) and (
12
), one has

(16)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\!\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial u}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k-1}\,s_{n}\!\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{2k-2}&\mathcal{P}_{2k-2-\epsilon_{2k-1}}\\
\mathcal{Q}_{2k-2}&\mathcal{Q}_{2k-2-\epsilon_{2k-1}}\end{pmatrix},

(17)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\!\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial v}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\,s_{n}\!\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1-\epsilon_{2k}}&\mathcal{P}_{2k-1}\\
\mathcal{Q}_{2k-1-\epsilon_{2k}}&\mathcal{Q}_{2k-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Combining these equations with

(18)

\frac{\partial r}{\partial u}=(1-v)\frac{\partial w}{\partial u}+w,\quad\frac{\partial r}{\partial v}=(1-v)\frac{\partial w}{\partial v}-1\,,

which directly follows from
r=wuw^{-1}v^{-1}
, we obtain an explicit formula for
D^{1}
.

2.3.1.
Tautological representation

In this section, we compute the torsion
\tau_{1}
associated with the tautological representation
\rho_{1}
. Note that
s_{1}
is the identity map and thus
\rho_{1}=\rho
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Writing the differentials
D^{i}
in matrix form, we have

D^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&x\\
0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0\\
x&0\end{array}\right),\quad D^{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}0&D^{1}_{1}&D^{1}_{2}&0\\
0&D^{1}_{3}&D^{1}_{4}&0\end{array}\right)

where
D^{1}_{1}=\mathcal{P}_{p-2}
,
D^{1}_{2}=-1
,

D^{1}_{3}=\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}-x\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k-1}\mathcal{P}_{2k-2-\epsilon_{2k-1}}\,,\quad D^{1}_{4}=-x\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1-\epsilon_{2k}}\,.

Proof.

It is clear from

\rho_{1}(u-1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\,\rho_{1}(v-1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
x&0\end{pmatrix}

that the matrix form of
D^{0}
is given as in the claim.

Equation (
15
) for
a=r
says that
\frac{\partial r}{\partial u}(u-1)+\frac{\partial r}{\partial v}(v-1)=0
. Thus we can write a priori

\rho_{1}\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial u}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\
0&b\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho_{1}\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial v}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
d&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

It then follows from Equations (
18
) and (
13
) that
a=\mathcal{P}_{p-2}=D^{1}_{1}
,
c=-1=D^{1}_{2}
, and from (
16
) and (
17
) that
b=D^{1}_{3}
and
d=D^{1}_{4}
.
∎

The above lemma implies that the chain complex (
14
) is acyclic if and only if
D^{1}_{1}D^{1}_{4}-D^{1}_{2}D^{1}_{3}\neq 0
. In the acyclic case, the torsion
\tau_{1}
can be computed directly by using the Cayley formula (see, e.g.
[
GKZ08
, p.485]
). Precisely, we have

(19)

\tau_{1}=\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\Delta_{0}}\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta_{0}=x^{2},\ \Delta_{1}=D^{1}_{1}D^{1}_{4}-D^{1}_{2}D^{1}_{3}.

Theorem 2.7
.

The torsion of the two bridge knot
K(p,q)
in its tautological representation
\rho_{1}
is well-defined and given by

\tau_{1}=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}-x\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k-1}(\mathcal{P}_{2k-1}+\mathcal{P}_{2k-3})\right).

Proof.

The last equality of Lemma

implies that

\mathcal{P}_{p-2}\mathcal{P}_{k}=(-1)^{k}\mathcal{P}_{p-2-k}
for any
k
.
Hence we can rewrite

\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{p-2}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1-\epsilon_{2k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{p-1-2k+\epsilon_{2k}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{p-3}{2}}\epsilon_{2k+1}\mathcal{P}_{2k+\epsilon_{2k+1}}\,.

In the last equality, we have substituted
2k
with
p-2k-1
and used the symmetry
\epsilon_{2k+1}=\epsilon_{p-2k-1}
. Then two sums that appear in

\Delta_{1}=-x\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{p-2}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1-\epsilon_{2k}}+\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}-x\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k-1}\mathcal{P}_{2k-2-\epsilon_{2k-1}}

add to

. Combining this with Equation (

), we obtain the desired formula of

.

It remains to show that

is invertible to prove that the chain complex (

) is acyclic and the torsion

is well-defined. This follows from Theorem

below, saying that

is invertible with the explicit inverse

.
∎

2.3.2.
Simplication of

In this section, we show that the formula of

in Theorem

admits a remarkable simplification.

Theorem 2.8
.

One has

\tau_{1}=\frac{2}{x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}}

where

is the unique odd integer satisfying

modulo

Prior to the proof, we record some lemmas.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let

be a sequence of polynomials satisfying the recursion

We henceforth set

∎

Lemma 2.11
.

One has

for

∎

Lemma 2.13

For all

Proof of Theorem

.

First observe that if

, the above equality is equivalent to

(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2})S=2+\iota(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}).

On the other hand, using the symmetry

and
\epsilon_{2k-1}=-\epsilon_{-2k+1}
, we can write

where

for

odd,
\delta_{k}=-1
for
-p<k<0
odd, and
\delta_{k}=0
otherwise. As Lemma

states that
(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2})\mathcal{P}_{k}=\delta_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-2})
for odd

, we have

(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2})S=\sum_{k:\text{odd}}(\epsilon^{\prime}_{k}\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k}-\epsilon^{\prime}_{k}\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-2})=\sum_{k:\text{even}}(\epsilon^{\prime}_{k-\ell^{\prime}}-\epsilon^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}-2-k})\mathcal{P}_{k}.

In this formula,

if

, and
\epsilon^{\prime}_{k}=0
otherwise; the sums are taken over

. As

and

(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2})S=\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{p-3}{2}}(u_{k}+v_{k})\mathcal{P}_{2k}

•

For

\epsilon^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}=\epsilon^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}=-1

•

For

\ell^{\prime}>p

.

•

Similarly, one has

for

\ell^{\prime}-2-2k\equiv p

:

If
\ell^{\prime}<p
, then there is
k_{0}
such that
-2-2k_{0}-\ell^{\prime}=-p
, and one has

;

If
\ell^{\prime}>p
, then there is
k_{0}
such that
\ell^{\prime}-2-2k_{0}=p

We conclude that in both cases

∎

2.3.3.
Adjoint representation

In this section, we compute the torsion
\tau_{2}
associated with the adjoint representation
\rho_{2}=s_{2}\circ\rho
.

The symmetric power
\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2}
is 3-dimensional and the representation

is known to be isomorphic to the adjoint representation

We denote by

The torsion

is our main case of interest. However, it provides extra difficulties due to the fact that the chain complex (

is the knot complement of

and the coefficient

More precisely, it is well-known that

]
.

Let

be the boundary torus of

and let

be a meridian of

homotopic to

. The inclusion maps induce the following homomorphisms

It is well-known that when

is hyperbolic and

is a lift of the geometric representation, these two maps are isomorphism. In our case, this will follow from a computation.

Applying the Poincaré duality, combined with the invariant pairing of

, to the isomorphisms in (

\mathrm{H}^{1}(M,\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2})\simeq\mathrm{H}^{0}(m,\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2})^{*},\quad\mathrm{H}^{2}(M,\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2})\simeq\mathrm{H}^{0}(\partial M,\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2})^{*}\,.

By definition of the 0-th cohomology,
\mathrm{H}^{0}(m,\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2})
and

are generated by elements of
\mathrm{S}^{2}V^{2}
that are fixed by the restrictions

and

). With this choice of basis, the torsion

is well-defined.

\Delta_{1}
and keep the third entry of
D_{2}
to get
\Delta_{2}
. Then the Cayley formula (see, e.g.
[
GKZ08
, p.485]
) says that

(27)

\tau_{2}=\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\Delta_{0}\Delta_{2}}

where
\Delta_{0}=4x^{3}
,
\Delta_{1}=-2x(\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1}^{2})
, and
\Delta_{2}=\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}
. Here
\Delta_{1}
is computed from (see Equation (
18
))

\rho_{2}\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial v}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
2x&0&0\\
x^{2}&-x&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast&a\\
\ast&\ast&\ast\\
\ast&\ast&\ast\end{pmatrix}-I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\
\ast&\ast&-2xa\\
\ast&\ast&\ast\end{pmatrix}

where
a=-\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1}^{2}
, which follows from Equation (
17
). Substituting
\Delta_{i}
into the Cayley formula (
27
), we obtain the desired formula.

To show acyclicity of the cone complex, it is sufficicient to show that no
\Delta_{i}
vanish. The non trivial one is
\Delta_{1}
, and its relation with the invertible element
\Omega
in Section
2.5
will show that it is invertible; see Equation (
31
).
∎

2.4.
Comparing reciprocal pairs

Let
0<\ell<p
be the unique odd integer such that
\ell q\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
. Recall from Section
2.1
that the knots
K(p,q)
and
K(p,\ell)
are isotopic. It follows that the orbifolds
\mathcal{O}(p,q)
and
\mathcal{O}(p,\ell)
are homeomorphic, and the algebras
V
and
V^{\ast}
associated to
(p,q)
and
(p,\ell)
, respectively, are isomorphic.
It is useful to make this algebra isomorphism explicit for two reasons. First, it clarifies the denominator of the formula in Theorems
2.8
and
2.14
, providing a topological explanation for the discrepancy between the torsions and the element
\Omega
in Section
2.5
below that will be needed to compute signatures. Second, it provides one of the few ways to verify the formulas in these sections: since torsions are topological invariants, they should correspond under the isomorphism.

2.4.1.
Explicit isomorphism

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\ell
be the integer as above and let
V=\mathbb{Q}[X]/(P_{p-1})
and
V^{\ast}=\mathbb{Q}[X^{\ast}]/(P^{\ast}_{p-1})
the algebras associated to
K(p,q)
and
K(p,\ell)
, respectively. Then the maps

V^{\ast}\to V,\ x^{\ast}\mapsto x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad V\to V^{\ast},\ x\mapsto x^{\ast}\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{q-1}(x^{\ast})

are algebra isomorphisms, inverse to each other.

Proof.

Recall from Section
2.1
that we can decompose the 3-sphere into two ball
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
where the knot
K(p,q)
becomes two ramified arcs in each ball. Let us draw Schubert’s normal form of
K(p,q)
in the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-plane so that its vertical edges run from
(0,0)
to
(0,1)
and from
(1,0)
to
(1,1)
, respectively, as in Figure
4
. From Equation (
4
), we have
\Tr\rho(uv)=2+x^{2}
where
uv
is a loop in the ball
B_{1}
that encloses the two ramified arcs (the vertical edges of the normal form).

Figure 4.
Wirtinger generators in the normal form.

In the other ball
B_{2}
, the two ramified arcs appear as arcs of slope
q/p
. If we follow the diagram from
(0,0)
to
(1,1/p)
, i.e., to the crossing just above
(1,0)
, the associated Wirtinger generator begins as
v
and is conjugated whenever it crosses a vertical edge. Let us denote this sequence of Wirtinger generators by
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots
as in Figure
4
. Then we have

m_{k}=(u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots v^{\epsilon_{k-1}})^{-1}\,v\,(u^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots v^{\epsilon_{k-1}}).

When we arrive to the crossing at
(1,1/p)
, the corresponding Wirtinger generator is
m_{\ell}

and Equation (
11
) yields

\displaystyle\rho(m_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{Q}_{\ell-2}&-\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2}\\
-\mathcal{Q}_{\ell-1}&\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}&\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2}\\
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell-1}&\mathcal{Q}_{\ell-2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1-x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}&-x\mathcal{P}^{2}_{\ell-2}\\
x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}&1+x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Since the vertical edge from
(1,0)
to
(1,1)
is attached with the arc of slope
q/p
at
(1,0)
, the loop in
B_{2}
enclosing the two ramified arcs is given by
um_{\ell}
as depicted in Figure
4
, with

\rho(um_{\ell})=\begin{pmatrix}1-x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}+x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}&*\\
*&1+x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}\end{pmatrix}

and
\Tr\rho(um_{\ell})=2+x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}
. As the homeomorphism between the orbifolds
\mathcal{O}(p,q)
and
\mathcal{O}(p,\ell)
is given by interchanging the role of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, it swaps the loops
uv
and
um_{\ell}
, and thus exchanges their traces
2+x^{2}
and
2+x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}
. It follows that the map sending
x^{\ast}
to
x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}
gives an isomorphism from
V^{\ast}
to
V
.
Interchanging the role of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, we obtain its inverse, sending
x\in V
to
x^{\ast}\mathcal{P}_{q-1}^{\ast}(x^{\ast})\in V^{\ast}
.
∎

2.4.2.
Cusp shape

Let
m
and
l
be a meridian and a canonical longitude of
K(p,q)
, viewed as commuting elements in
G
and let
\rho_{\text{geom}}:G\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a lift of the geometric representation, provided that
K(p,q)
is hyperbolic. Up to conjugation, one can assume that

\rho_{\text{geom}}(m)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho_{\text{geom}}(l)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&c\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

The element
c
is called the cusp shape of
K(p,q)
: it is a geometric invariant of the knot, controlling the Euclidean structure at infinity.
By analogy, one can define the cusp shape associated to the tautological representation
\rho:G\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(V)
.
As the blackboard-framed longitude is given by
w^{\ast}w
(recall the proof of Theroem
2.14
), it follows from Equations (
13
) and (
26
) that

\rho(m)=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(l)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\iota\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}-2x\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\epsilon_{k}\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Here the sum of the epsilons in
\rho(l)
arises from the writhe correction.
We derive

(28)

c=\frac{2\iota\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}}{x}-2\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\epsilon_{k}\in V

which show a tight relation between
\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}
and the cusp shape of
K(p,q)
. Computing the same element for
K(p,\ell)
yields

\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}(x)}{x}=\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\ast}_{p-2}(x^{\ast})}{x^{\ast}}

where the isomorphism between
V
and
V^{\ast}
is used implicitly.
Note that the sum of the epsilons in (
28
) does not change when we replace
q
by
\ell
, since they are inverses of each other.
Substituting
x^{\ast}=x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}(x)
yields the following identity:

\mathcal{Q}^{\ast}_{p-2}(x^{\ast})=\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}(x)\in V.

We verified this identity by computer for many values of
p
and
q
.

2.4.3.
Invariance of the adjoint torsion

Let us comment the corresponding equation for the adjoint torsion
\tau_{2}
. Anticipating sligthly the next section, define
\Omega
so that we can write

\tau_{2}=\frac{\Omega}{4x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}}=\frac{\Omega^{\ast}}{4(x^{\ast})^{2}(\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{q-1})^{2}}

Here the second equality uses the isomorphism
V\simeq V^{\ast}
implicitly, with the fact that
\ell^{\ast}=q
. Substituting
x=x^{\ast}\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{q-1}
, the reciprocal of the relation
x^{\ast}=x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}
, we deduce that

(29)

\Omega^{\ast}(x^{\ast})=\Omega(x)\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}(x)^{-2}

This shows that the element
\Omega
transforms non trivially under the isomorphism between
V
and
V^{\ast}
, but in a similar way to
\mathcal{Q}_{p-2}
.

2.5.
Reidemeister torsions in Frobenius algebra

It is shown in
[
Mar23
]
that
V=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(P_{p-1})
has a natural semi-simple Frobenius algebra structure, meaning that it is endowed with a linear form
\epsilon:V\to\mathbb{Q}
, a bilinear symmetric non-degenerate form
\eta:V\times V\to\mathbb{Q}
, and an invertible element
\Omega\in V
related by the following equations:

(30)

\eta(x,y)=\epsilon(xy),\quad\epsilon(x)=\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(\Omega^{-1}x),\quad\Omega=\sum_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}}{\eta(e_{k},e_{k})}

where
x
and
y
are any elements of
V
, and
e_{k}
is an orthogonal basis for
\eta
.
In our case, the natural basis
\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{p-2}
of
V
is orthogonal and satisfies
\epsilon(\mathcal{P}_{k})=0
for
k>0
and
\epsilon(\mathcal{P}_{0})=1
. Moreover,
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{k})=(-1)^{k}\epsilon_{k+1}
so that

\Omega=\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}(-1)^{k}\epsilon_{k+1}\mathcal{P}_{k}^{2}.

Let us write
\Omega=\Omega^{+}-\Omega^{-}
by decomposing the sum over even and odd indexes of the epsilons. Using Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
, we have

\Omega^{+}=\sum_{k=1}^{(p-1)/2}-\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{2k-1}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{(p-1)/2}-\epsilon_{2k}\mathcal{P}_{p-2k-1}^{2}=-\sum_{k=0}^{(p-3)/2}\epsilon_{p-2k-1}\mathcal{P}_{2k}^{2}.

As
\epsilon_{p-k}=\epsilon_{k}
, it follows that
\Omega^{+}=-\Omega^{-}
, and hence
\Omega^{+}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega
.
By Theorem
2.14
, we obtain

(31)

\tau_{2}=\frac{\Omega^{+}}{2x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}}=\frac{\Omega}{4x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}}.

This relation between
\tau_{2}
and
\Omega
implies that the inverse sum of
\tau_{2}
vanishes; see Theorem
2.16
below. Note that a similar equation for non-parabolic representations was studied in
[
Yoo22
]
. See
[
GKY21
]
for the quantum-physical motivation behind such inverse sums of adjoint torsions.

Theorem 2.16
.

Fix coprime odd integers
p
and
q
satisfying
1<q<p
, that is, fix a hyperbolic knot-bridge knot
K(p,q)
and let
\mathcal{X}
be its parabolic character variety. Then

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\tau_{2}(\rho)}=0\,.

Proof.

For any
f\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]
, we have

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{X}}f(\rho)=\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}]/\mathbb{Q}}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]/\mathbb{Q}}(f)\,.

Therefore, it suffices to compute the trace of
1/\tau_{2}
in
V=\mathbb{Q}[\hat{\mathcal{X}}]
over
\mathbb{Q}
. From Equations (
30
) and (
31
), one has

\displaystyle\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(1/\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(4x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2}/\Omega)

\displaystyle=\epsilon(4x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1}^{2})

\displaystyle=4\eta(\epsilon_{\ell}x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1},\epsilon_{\ell}x\mathcal{P}_{\ell-1})

\displaystyle=4\eta(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2},\mathcal{P}_{\ell}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2})

As
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is orthogonal with
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{k})=(-1)^{k}\epsilon_{k+1}
, one has

\eta(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2},\mathcal{P}_{\ell}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell-2})=(-1)^{\ell}(\epsilon_{\ell+1}+\epsilon_{\ell-1})=0.

The last equality follows from the fact that
q\ell\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
, implying that
\epsilon_{\ell+1}
and
\epsilon_{\ell-1}
have different signs. This completes the proof.
∎

The above theorem motivates us to consider the inverse sum of
\tau_{1}
. Although we have no quantum-physical explanation for its simplicity—specifically, for its independence of
p
and
q
—Theorem
2.17
below shows that the inverse sum of
\tau_{1}
is
\pm 1
.

Theorem 2.17
.

Fix coprime odd integers
p
and
q
satisfying
1<q<p
, that is, fix a hyperbolic knot-bridge knot
K(p,q)
and let
\mathcal{X}
be its parabolic character variety. Then

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\tau_{1}(\rho)}=\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2}

where
0<\ell^{\prime}<2p
is the unique odd integer satisfying
\ell^{\prime}q\equiv-1
modulo
2p
.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
2.8
that

\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(1/\tau_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-1})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(x(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}})).

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{p-2}
form an orthogonal basis of
V
, and for
i
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}/2p\mathbb{Z}
,
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{i},\mathcal{P}_{j})=0
unless
i\equiv j
or
i+j\equiv 2p-2
, in which case this scalar product gives
(-1)^{i}\epsilon_{i+1}
.
The trace of an endomorphism
M\in\text{End}(V)
is given by

\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(M)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},M\mathcal{P}_{k})\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{k}).

If we let
M
be the multiplication by
x(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}})
, then for
0\leq k\leq p-2
,

\displaystyle M\mathcal{P}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}})x\mathcal{P}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}})\epsilon_{k+1}(\mathcal{P}_{k+1}-\mathcal{P}_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{k+1}(-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k+1}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-3}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k-1}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-1}).

Here the last equality follows from Lemma
2.13
.
Therefore, one has

\displaystyle\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(M)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\epsilon_{k+1}\,\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\,-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k+1}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-3}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k-1}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-1})\,\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}(-1)^{k}\,\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\,-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k+1}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-3}+\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k-1}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-1})\,.

Expanding the bilinear form
\eta
in the sum, we obtain four terms:
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k+1})
,
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-3})
,
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}+k-1})
, and
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-k-1})
.
We analyze the non-trivial contributions from each term individually. Recall that
\eta(\mathcal{P}_{k},\mathcal{P}_{i})\neq 0
if and only if
k\equiv i
or
k+i\equiv 2p-2\pmod{2p}
.

•

First term: if
k\equiv\ell^{\prime}+k+2\pmod{2p}
, then
\ell^{\prime}=-1
which we excluded. The only possibility is to have
k+\ell^{\prime}+k+1\equiv 2p-2\pmod{2p}
which appears only for
k=(2p-\ell^{\prime}-3)/2
. This contributes to the trace
-\epsilon_{k+1}=-\epsilon_{p-(\ell^{\prime}+1)/2}=-\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}+1)/2}
.

•

Second term: if
k+\ell^{\prime}-k-3\equiv 2p-2\pmod{2p}
, then
\ell^{\prime}
is congruent to
1
modulo
p
, which is impossible. If
k\equiv\ell^{\prime}-k-3\pmod{2p}
, then
k=(\ell^{\prime}-3)/2
. This contributes
\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2}
.

•

Third term: the case
k\equiv\ell^{\prime}+k-1\pmod{2p}
is impossible. The other case
k+\ell^{\prime}+k-1\equiv 2p-2\pmod{2p}
happens when
k=p-(\ell^{\prime}+1)/2
and contributes
\epsilon_{p-(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2}=\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2}
.

•

Fourth term: the case
k+\ell^{\prime}-k-1\equiv-2\pmod{2p}
is excluded. It remains the case
k\equiv\ell^{\prime}-k-1\pmod{2p}
which happens when
k=(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2
and contributes
-\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}+1)/2}
.

Collecting the non-trivial terms, we conclude that the trace of
M
is equal to
\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2}-2\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}+1)/2}
. If we write
q\ell^{\prime}=2ps-1
for some integer
s
, it follows that
\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}+1)/2}=(-1)^{s}
and
\epsilon_{(\ell^{\prime}-1)/2}=(-1)^{s+1}
, giving
\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(M)=4(-1)^{s+1}
. Combining all the above, one has

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\tau_{1}(\rho)}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(1/\tau_{1})=\frac{1}{4}\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(x(\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-2}-\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}}))=(-1)^{s+1}\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.18
.

For
q=1
, we have
\ell^{\prime}=2p-1
and
\mathcal{P}_{\ell^{\prime}-1}=\mathcal{P}_{-2}=1
. In this case, a direct computation shows that
\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(1/\tau_{1})=\Tr_{V/\mathbb{Q}}(x^{2})/2=2-p
.

3.
Asymptotics of the signature function

3.1.
Statement of the theorem

Let
\Gamma(2)
be the principal congruence subgroup of level 2 in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Fix
M\in\Gamma(2)
and define the sequence
(p_{n},q_{n})
for odd
n
by

(32)

\begin{pmatrix}q_{n}\\
p_{n}\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}1\\
n\end{pmatrix}\,.

Writing the matrix
M
as

M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma(2)\,,

we have odd integers
a,d
and even integers
b,c
with
q_{n}=a+bn
and
p_{n}=c+dn
.
As we want to study the asymptotics of signatures along this sequence
(p_{n},q_{n})
, we further impose that
0\leq b<d
. Then for
n
big enough,
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
are coprime odd integers satisfying
0<q_{n}<p_{n}
.
We observe that
\lim_{n}\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}=\frac{b}{d}
is always the quotient of an even number by an odd one. Moreover, up to a shift of
n
, the sequence
(p_{n},q_{n})
only depends on
b/d
. With such a shift, we can moreover assume
c>0
. We denote by
\Gamma_{S}(2)
be the subset of
\Gamma(2)
consisting of matrices with
0\leq b<d
and
c>0
.

To state the asymptotic of the signature
\sigma_{g}(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}})
, we construct an explicit Frobenius algebra
W_{M}
that depends on
M\in\Gamma_{S}(2)
as follows. Set

(33)

\alpha_{r}=\begin{cases}\lfloor\frac{rc}{d}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{(r-1)c}{d}\rfloor&\text{for }0<r<d\\[2.0pt]
\lfloor\frac{rc}{d}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{(r-1)c}{d}\rfloor-1&\text{for }r=d\end{cases}\,.

Note that
\alpha_{r}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=c-1
. We define

N(\alpha_{r})=i^{\alpha_{r}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{r}+1+x&i^{-1}(\alpha_{r}+x)\\
i^{-1}(\alpha_{r}+x)&-\alpha_{r}+1-x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[i][x])

and set

\begin{pmatrix}-iH_{1}\\
H_{2}\end{pmatrix}=N(\alpha_{1})\overline{N(\alpha_{2})}N(\alpha_{3})\cdots\overline{N(\alpha_{d-1})}N(\alpha_{d})\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,.

From the fact that
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=c-1
is odd, one checks that both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are integer polynomials of degree
d
.
Similarly, we define an integer polynomial
H_{3}
of degree
b
as

\begin{pmatrix}H_{3}\\
*\end{pmatrix}=N(\alpha_{1})\overline{N(\alpha_{2})}N(\alpha_{3})\cdots\overline{N(\alpha_{b})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Provided that

(H)

H_{1}
has no multiple roots and no common root with
H_{3}
,

we define the semi-simple Frobenius algebra
W_{M}
of degree
d
by

W_{M}=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(H_{1})\quad\text{with}\quad\Omega_{W_{M}}=-H_{1}^{\prime}H_{2}H_{3}^{-2}\,.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix
M\in\Gamma_{S}(2)
and let
(p_{n},q_{n})
be the sequence defined in (
32
). Then for any
g\geq 2
,

(1)

the signature
\sigma_{g}(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}})
is a polynomial in
n
, and

(2)

under the assumption (
H
), its asymptotic satisfies

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}2\left(\frac{n^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\right)^{g-1}\zeta(2g-2)\Tr_{W_{M}/\mathbb{Q}}\left(\Omega_{W_{M}}^{g-1}\right).

3.2.
Reciprocal Frobenius algebra

Fix
M\in\Gamma_{S}(2)
and
(p_{n},q_{n})
as in Theorem
3.1
. As
dq_{n}-bp_{n}=1
,
d
is the inverse of
q_{n}
modulo
p_{n}
.
Let us denote by
V^{*}_{n}
the Frobenius algebra associated to
(p_{n},q_{n})
and
V_{n}
the one associated to
(p_{n},d)
. Here we adopt the notations from Section
2.1
, except that the subscript
n
is added, and we attach
\ast
to the algebra associated with
(p_{n},q_{n})
. Recall from Section
2.4
that they are isomorphic as algebras and denoting by
x^{*},x
their generators, one has the relations:

x^{*}=x\mathcal{P}_{q_{n}-1},\quad x=x^{*}\mathcal{P}^{*}_{d-1}

together with (see Equation (

))

\Omega^{*}_{n}=\Omega_{n}{\mathcal{P}}_{q_{n}-1}^{-2}\,.

It follows that

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)=\Tr_{V^{*}_{n}/\mathbb{Q}}\left((\Omega^{*}_{n})^{g-1}\right)=\Tr_{V_{n}/\mathbb{Q}}\left((\Omega_{n}\mathcal{P}_{q_{n}-1}^{-2})^{g-1}\right).

Proposition 3.2
.

One has

\begin{pmatrix}P_{p_{n}-1}\\
P_{p_{n}-2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{d+1}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n+\alpha_{d}}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}-X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n+\alpha_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n+\alpha_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

and

\begin{pmatrix}P_{q_{n}-1}\\
P_{q_{n}-2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{b+1}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n+\alpha_{b}}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}-X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n+\alpha_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n+\alpha_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Here the integers
\alpha_{r}
, defined in (
33
), are independent of
n
.

Proof.

We first consider the
\epsilon
-sequence
\epsilon_{k}=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{dk}{dn+c}\rfloor}
for
1\leq k\leq p_{n}-1
associated to the Frobenius algebra
V_{n}
. It is a sequence of
d
alternating blocks of
1
s and
-1
s. Precisely, to find the last term of the
r
-th block for
1\leq r\leq d
, we compare
dk
and
r(dn+c)
:
writing
k=rn+s
, this amounts to compare
ds
and
rc
. The last term of the
r
-th block is then
k_{r}=rn+\lfloor\frac{rc}{d}\rfloor
. In particular, the last term is
k_{d}=nd+c=p_{n}
. As we want to stop the sequence at
p_{n}-1
, we set instead
k_{d}=p_{n}-1
. We also set
k_{0}=0
for notational convenience. Note that
k_{r}-k_{r-1}=n+\alpha_{r}
.

We now recall that
P_{k}=\epsilon_{k}XP_{k-1}+P_{k-2}
. Equivalently,

\begin{pmatrix}P_{k}\\
P_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{k}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P_{k-1}\\
P_{k-2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

By the definition of
k_{r}
, one has
\epsilon_{k}=(-1)^{r}
for all
k_{r-1}<k\leq k_{r}
. Since
k_{r}-k_{r-1}=n+\alpha_{r}
, we obtain the first formula.

On the other hand, as
ad-bc=1
, we have
\frac{bc}{d}=a-\frac{1}{d}
and thus

k_{b}=bn+\lfloor\frac{bc}{d}\rfloor=bn+a-1=q_{n}-1
. It follows that
P_{q_{n}-1}
is obtained from the same formula as
P_{p_{n}-1}
by taking only the first
b
blocks. This proves the proposition.
∎

Set
X=i(t+t^{-1})
for a formal variable
t
. Then a direct computation shows that

(34)

\begin{pmatrix}X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{k}=\frac{i^{k}}{t-t^{-1}}\begin{pmatrix}t^{k+1}-t^{-k-1}&-i(t^{k}-t^{-k})\\
-i(t^{k}-t^{-k})&-(t^{k-1}-t^{-k+1})\end{pmatrix}.

Changing the sign of
X
amounts to changing
i
to
-i
, hence

(35)

\begin{pmatrix}-X&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{k}=\frac{i^{-k}}{t-t^{-1}}\begin{pmatrix}t^{k+1}-t^{-k-1}&i(t^{k}-t^{-k})\\
i(t^{k}-t^{-k})&-(t^{k-1}-t^{-k+1})\end{pmatrix}.

Combining the above formulas with Proposition
3.2
, we obtain the following proposition, which produces three two-variable polynomials. We will need more precise information about these polynomials, which we defer to the technical Lemma
3.7
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exist polynomials
Q_{M},R_{M},S_{M}\in\mathbb{Z}[U^{\pm 1},V^{\pm 1}]
, depending only on
M\in\Gamma_{S}(2)
, such that

\begin{cases}P_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})(t-t^{-1})^{d}=i^{n+1}Q_{M}(t,t^{n})\\
P_{p_{n}-2}(it+it^{-1})(t-t^{-1})^{d}=i^{n}R_{M}(t,t^{n})\\
P_{q_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})(t-t^{-1})^{b}=S_{M}(t,t^{n})\end{cases}

for any odd
n
.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
3.2
and Equations (
34
), (
35
) that
P_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})
can be expressed as a polynomial in
t
and
t^{n}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, multiplied by
i^{\sum_{r}(-1)^{r+1}(n+\alpha_{r})}(t-t^{-1})^{d}=i^{n+\kappa}(t-t^{-1})^{d}
where
\kappa=\sum_{r}(-1)^{r+1}\alpha_{r}
. As
\kappa\equiv\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}=c-1\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, one gets
\pm i^{n+1}(t-t^{-1})^{d}
. Remaining powers of
i
appear exactly in non-diagonal coefficients, and this property is stable by product.
This proves the desired identity for
P_{p_{n}-1}
and
P_{p_{n}-2}
. For
P_{q_{n}-1}
, there is no term of the form
i^{n}
and the remaining power of
i
is
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{b}\equiv a-1\equiv 0
modulo 2.
∎

3.3.
The signature is a polynomial in
n

Let us prove in this section the first item of Theorem
3.1
.

From
[
Mar23
]
, one has
\Omega_{n}=-P_{p_{n}-2}P_{p_{n}-1}^{\prime}
, hence

(36)

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)=\Tr_{V_{n}/\mathbb{Q}}\left(\big(-P_{q_{n}-1}^{-2}P_{p_{n}-2}P^{\prime}_{p_{n}-1}\big)^{g-1}\right).

Recall from the Residue Formula (see e.g.
[
GH78
]
) that given any polynomial
P
with simple roots and any
Q\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
one has

\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}[x]/P}(Q)=\sum_{P(x)=0}Q(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{C}}\Res_{x}(\frac{QP^{\prime}}{P}dx)=-\Res_{x=\infty}(\frac{QP^{\prime}}{P}dx).

We cannot apply this directly to the computation of
\sigma_{g}
because we need to invert
P_{q_{n}-1}
, which creates new poles and invalidates the residue formula. To circumvent this problem, we use

\frac{x^{*}}{x}=P_{q_{n}-1}(x),\quad\frac{x}{x^{*}}=P^{*}_{d-1}(x^{*})

from which we deduce

P_{q_{n}-1}(x)^{-1}=P^{*}_{d-1}(x^{*})=P^{*}_{d-1}(xP_{q_{n}-1}(x)).

To simply the notation, let us set
Q(x)=P^{*}_{d-1}(xP_{q_{n}-1}(x))
.
In order to apply Proposition
3.3
, we perform the change of variables
x=it+it^{-1}
. Since this maps
t=0
to
x=\infty
and is non-ramified there, we can replace
x
by
it+it^{-1}
,
x=\infty
by
t=0
, and
dx
by
\frac{i(t-t^{-1})dt}{t}
. It follows that
\sigma_{g}(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}})
is equal to

\underset{t=0}{\Res}\left((-1)^{g}\frac{Q(it+it^{-1})^{2g-2}P_{p_{n}-2}(it+it^{-1})^{g-1}P^{\prime}_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})^{g}}{P_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})}\frac{i(t-t^{-1})dt}{t}\right).

On the other hand, from Proposition
3.3
, one computes

(37)

P^{\prime}_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})=i^{n}\left(\frac{t\frac{d}{dt}Q_{M}(t,t^{n})}{(t-t^{-1})^{d+1}}-\frac{d(t+t^{-1})Q_{M}(t,t^{n})}{(t-t^{-1})^{d+2}}\right).

Substituting this into the above residue expression of
\sigma_{g}(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}})
, we obtain

(38)

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)=\Res_{t=0}\left(\frac{R(t,t^{n})}{Q_{M}(t,t^{n})(t-t^{-1})^{m}}\dfrac{dt}{t}\right)

for some integer
m
independent of
n
and some
R\in\mathbb{Z}[U^{\pm 1},V^{\pm 1}]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
R
and
S\in\mathbb{Z}[U^{\pm 1},V^{\pm 1}]
be two polynomials and suppose that the lowest
V
-coefficient of
S
is
(U-U^{-1})^{\ell}
modulo
\mathbb{Z}[U^{\pm 1}]^{\times}
. Then

\Res_{t=0}\left(\frac{R(t,t^{n})}{S(t,t^{n})(t-t^{-1})^{m}}\frac{dt}{t}\right)

is a polynomial in
n
of order less than
m+\ell
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider
R(U,V)=U^{a}V^{b}
by the linearity of the residue. Let
d
be the lowest
V
-degree of
S
. Then, by the assumption, we can write

S(U,V)=\epsilon U^{c}(U-U^{-1})^{\ell}V^{d}(1-V\tilde{S}(U,V))

where
\epsilon=\pm 1
and
\tilde{S}\in\mathbb{Z}[U^{\pm 1},V][[U]]
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{R(t,t^{n})}{S(t,t^{n})(t-t^{-1})^{m}}=\frac{t^{a+nb-c-nd+m+\ell}}{\epsilon(1-t^{n}\tilde{S}(t,t^{n}))(t^{2}-1)^{m+\ell}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-1}t^{a-c+m+\ell+n(b-d)}\sum_{i,j\geq 0}t^{ni}\tilde{S}(t,t^{n})^{i}t^{2j}(-1)^{m+\ell}\binom{j+m+\ell-1}{m+\ell-1}.

We need to pick the constant coefficient in
t
: the key point is that only a finite number of
i^{\prime}s
can contribute. Fix a value of
i
and expand
\tilde{S}(t,t^{n})^{i}
. All terms have the form

C\sum_{j\geq 0}t^{A+Bn}t^{2j}\binom{j+m+\ell-1}{m+\ell-1}.

If
B<0
and
A+Bn
is even, the constant term of this series is
\binom{\frac{A+Bn}{2}+m+\ell-1}{m+\ell-1}
. This is a polynomial in
n
(which is assumed to be odd) of degree
m+\ell-1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
3.7
below shows that Lemma
3.4
can be applied to Equation (
38
). It follows that the signature
\sigma_{g}(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}})
is a polynomial in
n
, which proves the first part of Theorem
3.1
.

3.4.
The leading term of the signature

We now prove the second part of Theorem
3.1
, starting again from Equation (
36
) that we rewrite in the following form:

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)=\sum_{x\in Z_{n}}\left(\frac{-P_{p_{n}-2}(x)P^{\prime}_{p_{n}-1}(x)}{P_{q_{n}-1}(x)^{2}}\right)^{g-1}

where
Z_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,P_{p_{n}-1}(x)=0\}
. From the relation between
P_{p_{n}-1}
and
Q_{M}
in Proposition
3.3
, one sees a direct relationship between the zeros of
P_{p_{n}-1}
and the zeros of
Q_{M}
. To be more precise, we define

\mathcal{C}=\{(u,v)\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}\,|\,Q_{M}(u,v)=0\},\quad\mathcal{C}[n]=\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{\pm 1\}^{2}\,|\,v=u^{n}\}.

The map
\mathcal{C}[n]\to Z_{n}
given by
(u,v)\mapsto i(u+u^{-1})
is surjective and
2
-to-
1
: two preimages are related by the involution
(u,v)\mapsto(u^{-1},v^{-1})
.

Lemma 3.5
.

The set
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{C}[n]
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

This amounts to showing that points in
\mathcal{C}[n]
cannot accumulate to ideal points of
\mathcal{C}
. Recall the well-known fact that such ideal points correspond to zeroes of the side polynomials of
Q_{M}
, that is, the one-variable polynomials associated with the sides of the Newton polygon of
Q_{M}
. To see this, fix an ideal point of
\mathcal{C}
and take Puiseux coordinates of the form
u=s^{p}
and
v=s^{q}F(s)
, where
p,q
are coprime and
F
is a convergent series with
F(0)\neq 0
. As
Q_{M}(s^{p},s^{q}F(s))=0
identically, degree considerations imply that the linear form
(i,j)\mapsto pi+qj
should support a face of the Newton polygon of
Q_{M}
.

By Lemma
3.7
below, this polygon is an explicit parallelogram, so the only possibilities are
(p,q)\in\{(0,1),(0,-1),(d,-c),(-d,c)\}
. In the first two cases, the Puiseux coordinates have the form
u=s^{\pm 1}
and
v=F(s)
. As
F(0)\neq 0
, it is impossible to have
F(s)=s^{\pm n}
in a neighborhood of
0
. In the latter two cases, we have
u=s^{\pm d}
and
v=s^{\mp c}F(s)
. Again, the equation
v=u^{n}
forces
F(s)=s^{\pm nd\pm c}
, which is impossible for
s
sufficiently close to
0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Recall the formula

P_{p_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})(t-t^{-1})^{d}=i^{n+1}Q_{M}(t,t^{n})
of Proposition
3.3
. Differentiating it at
(t,t^{n})\in\mathcal{C}[n]
, one has

(39)

P_{p_{n}-1}^{\prime}(it+it^{-1})i(t-t^{-1})^{d+1}=i^{n+1}t\partial_{U}Q_{M}(t,t^{n})+i^{n+1}nt^{n}\partial_{V}Q_{M}(t,t^{n}).

Hence, setting

F_{n}(u,v)=(u-u^{-1})^{2b-2d-1}\frac{R_{M}(u,v)}{S_{M}(u,v)^{2}}\left(u\partial_{U}Q_{M}(u,v)+nv\partial_{V}Q_{M}(u,v)\right)

we can write

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\!\!\!\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}[n]}F_{n}(u,v)^{g-1}.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any neighborhood
W
of
\{\pm 1\}^{2}\subset\mathcal{C}
, there is a constant

such that
|F_{n}(u,v)|\leq Cn
for any
(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}[n]\setminus W
.

Proof.

Let us write
x=it+it^{-1}
. As
P_{p_{n}-2}(x)^{2}=-1
for any

(see Proposition
2.4
), this term can readily be neglected. As
(t,t^{n})
belongs to a compact subset of
\mathcal{C}
, the polynomials
U\partial_{U}Q_{M}(U,V)
and
V\partial_{V}Q_{M}(U,V)
are uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{C}[n]
. Moreover, by the third point of Lemma
3.7
, the set of
(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}
with

is reduced to
\{\pm 1\}^{2}
. This means that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}\setminus W
one has
|u^{2}-1|>\epsilon
. Combining these with Equation (
39
), we deduce that there is a constant
C>0
such that
|P^{\prime}(iu+iu^{-1})|\leq Cn
for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}[n]\setminus W
.

It remains to deal with
P_{q_{n}-1}(x)^{-2}
. If we first replace it with
P^{*}_{d-1}(xP_{q_{n}-1}(x))^{2}
, we are reduced to showing that
P_{q_{n}-1}(x)
is uniformly bounded.
To this aim, recall from Proposition
3.3
that
P_{q_{n}-1}(x)(t-t^{-1})^{b}=S_{M}(t,t^{n})
. The same argument as for

shows that
P_{q_{n}-1}(it+it^{-1})
is uniformly bounded for
(t,t^{n})\in\mathcal{C}[n]\setminus W
, proving the lemma.
∎

Since the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}[n]
is linear in
n
, from the above lemma, one has

\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}[n]\setminus W}\hskip-5.69046ptF_{n}(u,v)^{g-1}=O(n^{g})

which is negligible compared to the expected

for

. Therefore, henceforth we concentrate on
\mathcal{C}[n]\cap W
.

Let
W^{\pm}
be a neighborhood
(1,\pm 1)\in\mathcal{C}
. For parity reasons, the neighborhood of
(-1,\pm 1)
will contribute in the same way to the sum. The previous lemma implies that

\sigma_{g}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{p_{n}}\right)=\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}[n]\cap(W^{+}\cup W^{-})}\hskip-19.91684ptF_{n}(u,v)^{g-1}+O(n^{g})

Let
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
be the normalization of
\mathcal{C}
and suppose that the first projection
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{C}
is not ramified over
1
. This implies that there exist
d-1
branches of
\mathcal{C}
with Puiseux coordinates of the form
u=e^{s},v=e^{F_{j}(s)}
with
F_{j}(0)=0
and
F_{j}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
. By the symmetries of
Q_{M}
given in Lemma
3.7
, the branches over
(\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v})
are parametrized by

for

.

Recall that an element of
\mathcal{C}[n]
in the

\pm e^{F_{j}(s)}=\pm e^{ns}
. Equivalently,
F_{j}(s)=ns-i\pi k
for some

. As
F_{j}(s)
is close to
0
, there is a unique value of
k\neq 0
which can solve this equation, reciprocally given

of this equation that we can expand into powers of

Along such a sequence, we have

By the fourth point of Lemma

One can reduce to the case

3.5.
The main lemma

Here we state and prove the main lemma used in the last two subsections.

Lemma 3.7
.

The polynomial
Q_{M}
satisfies the symmetries

Q_{M}(-U,V)=Q_{M}(U,V)=-Q_{M}(U,-V)=-Q_{M}(U^{-1},V^{-1})

together with the following properties:

(1)

The terms with highest (resp. lowest) power of
V
are
\pm V^{d}(U+U^{-1})^{d-1}U^{c}
and
\pm V^{-d}(U+U^{-1})^{d-1}U^{-c}
respectively.

(2)

Its Newton polygon is the parallelogram of vertices
\pm(d-1,0)\pm(c,d)
.

(3)

Q_{M}(\pm 1,V)=\pm 2^{d-1}(V-V^{-1})^{d}
.

(4)

Q_{M}(e^{u},e^{v})=2^{d}u^{d}H_{1}(v/u)
modulo terms in
(u,v)
of total order
<d
.

Similarly, the polynomials
R_{M}
and
S_{M}
satisfy

(5)

R_{M}(e^{u},e^{v})=2^{d}u^{d}H_{2}(v/u)
modulo terms in
(u,v)
of total order
<d
.

(6)

S_{M}(e^{u},e^{v})=2^{b}u^{b}H_{3}(v/u)
modulo terms in
(u,v)
of total order
<b
.

Proof.

Before all, we provide an explicit formula for
Q_{M}
. Set
X=it+it^{-1},U=t,V=t^{n}
, and
N_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}i(t+t^{-1})&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{k}(t-t^{-1})
. We compute

\displaystyle N_{n+\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}i^{n+\alpha}t^{n+\alpha+1}-i^{n+\alpha}t^{-n-\alpha-1}&i^{n+\alpha-1}t^{n+\alpha}-i^{n+\alpha-1}t^{-n-\alpha}\\
i^{n+\alpha-1}t^{n+\alpha}-i^{n+\alpha-1}t^{-n-\alpha}&i^{n+\alpha-2}t^{n+\alpha-1}-i^{n+\alpha-2}t^{-n-\alpha+1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i^{n+\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}U^{\alpha+1}V-U^{-\alpha-1}V^{-1}&i^{-1}U^{\alpha}V-i^{-1}U^{-\alpha}V^{-1}\\
i^{-1}U^{\alpha}V-i^{-1}U^{-\alpha}V^{-1}&-U^{\alpha-1}V+U^{-\alpha+1}V^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

If we index the elements of these matrices by signs, say
\eta,\xi\in\{\pm 1\}
, the entry
\eta,\xi
of the matrices above are given by

\left(N_{n+\alpha}\right)_{\eta,\xi}=i^{n+\alpha-1+\frac{\eta+\xi}{2}}\Big(U^{\alpha+\frac{\eta+\xi}{2}}V-U^{-\alpha-\frac{\eta+\xi}{2}}V^{-1}\Big).

It follows that setting
\mathcal{N}=N_{n+\alpha_{1}}\overline{N_{n+\alpha_{2}}}\cdots\overline{N_{n+\alpha_{d-1}}}N_{n+\alpha_{d}}
, its entry
\mathcal{N}_{\xi_{0},\xi_{d}}
equals:

\sum_{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d-1}}i^{\sum_{r=1}^{d}(-1)^{r+1}(n+\alpha_{r}-1+\frac{\xi_{r-1}+\xi_{r}}{2})}\prod_{r=1}^{d}\Big(U^{\alpha_{r}+\frac{\xi_{r-1}+\xi_{r}}{2}}V-U^{-(\alpha_{r}+\frac{\xi_{r-1}+\xi_{r}}{2})}V^{-1}\Big)

We compute that the power of
i
reduces to
n+\kappa-1+\frac{\xi_{d}+\xi_{0}}{2}
where
\kappa=\sum(-1)^{r+1}\alpha_{r}
and the product can further be expanded as follows:

\sum_{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d-1}\in\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}\in\{\pm 1\}}\Big(\prod_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\Big)U^{\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}(\alpha_{r}+\frac{\xi_{r-1}+\xi_{r}}{2})}V^{\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}}.

Finally, we sum over
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d-1}
to obtain

(40)

\sum_{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}\in\{\pm 1\}}\Big(\prod_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\Big)U^{\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\alpha_{r}+\frac{\eta_{1}\xi_{0}+\eta_{d}\xi_{d}}{2}}\prod_{r=1}^{d-1}\Big(U^{\frac{\eta_{r}+\eta_{r+1}}{2}}+U^{-\frac{\eta_{r}+\eta_{r+1}}{2}}\Big)V^{\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}}.

To recover
Q_{M}(U,V)
from this formula, we simply take
\xi_{0}=\xi_{d}=+1
and multiply the result by the sign
i^{\kappa-1}
. It follows readily from this expression that
Q_{M}
contains only even powers of
U
and odd powers of
V
. The symmetry
Q_{M}(U^{-1},V^{-1})=-Q_{M}(U,V)
is clear from the change
\eta_{r}\mapsto-\eta_{r}
.

From this formula, one sees that the maximal power of
V
in
Q_{M}
is
d
and corresponds to putting
\eta_{r}=1
for all
r
: its coefficient is

i^{\kappa-1}U^{\sum\alpha_{r}+1}(U+U^{-1})^{d-1}=i^{\kappa-1}U^{c}(U+U^{-1})^{d-1}.

The case of the minimal coefficient is similar and gives
i^{\kappa-1}U^{-c}(U+U^{-1})^{d-1}
.

Let us prove the second statement, we consider the linear form
L:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{Q}
given by
L(i,j)=i-\frac{c}{d}j
. It takes the values
\pm(d-1)
on the sides of the parallelogram expected as the Newton polygon of
Q_{M}
. Hence one needs to prove that any non trivial monomial
U^{i}V^{j}
appearing in
Q_{M}
satisfies
|L(i,j)|\leq d-1
. It reduces to taking a sequence
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}\in\{\pm 1\}
and compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}L\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\alpha_{r}&+\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{d}}{2}+\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\pm\frac{\eta_{r}+\eta_{r-1}}{2},\ \sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\Big)\\
&=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\alpha_{r}+\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{d}}{2}+\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\pm\frac{\eta_{r}+\eta_{r+1}}{2}-(\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r})\frac{c}{d}.\end{split}

Let
S
be the set of indices
0<r<d
for which
\eta_{r}\neq\eta_{r+1}
: one has

\Big|\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\pm\frac{\eta_{r}+\eta_{r+1}}{2}\Big|\leq d-1-\#S

so that we are reduced to showing
|\sum\eta_{r}\alpha_{r}+\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{d}}{2}-(\sum\eta_{r})\frac{c}{d}|\leq\#S
. We do a discrete integration by parts in each sum.

On one hand:
\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}\alpha_{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}(\beta_{r}-\beta_{r-1})=\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}(\eta_{r}-\eta_{r+1})\beta_{r}+\eta_{d}(c-1)
where
\beta_{r}=\lfloor\frac{rc}{d}\rfloor
for
0\leq r<d
and
\beta_{d}=c-1
.
The sequence
\eta_{r}-\eta_{r+1}
vanishes if
r\notin S
so that this sum reads
2\sum_{r\in S}\eta_{r}\beta_{r}+\eta_{d}(c-1)
.

On the other hand:
\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}r(\eta_{r}-\eta_{r+1})+d\eta_{d}=2\sum_{r\in S}r\eta_{r}+d\eta_{d}
. We are then reduced to

\Big|2\sum_{r\in S}\eta_{r}(\beta_{r}-r\frac{c}{d})+\frac{\eta_{1}-\eta_{d}}{2}\Big|\leq\#S.

As
-1\leq\beta_{r}-\frac{rc}{d}\leq 0
and the sequence
\eta_{r}
is alternating, the sum of two consecutive terms does not exceed
1
in absolute value. If
\eta_{1}=\eta_{d}
,
\#S
is even and the result follows. When
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{d}
then
\#S
is odd: either the first or the last term of the sum has a sign opposite to
\frac{\eta_{1}-\eta_{d}}{2}
: again their sum does not exceed 1 in absolute value and the argument repeats and the second point is proved.
We also observe that in case of equality, the sequence of
\xi
s and the sequence of
\eta
s should be constant: this shows that the non trivial coefficient on the slanted sides occur only at vertices.

Let us prove the third statement, we simply put
U=1
in Equation (
40
). We also observe from the expression just above that powers of
U
in
Q_{M}
have the form
\sum_{r=1}^{d}\eta_{r}(\alpha_{r}+\frac{\xi_{r-1}+\xi_{r}}{2})
which is congruent modulo 2 to
\sum_{r=1}^{d}\alpha_{r}+\frac{\xi_{0}+\xi_{d}}{2}=c
. Hence
Q
has only even powers of
U
and the statement follows.

Finally the last three statements are clear once we have observed that writing
U=e^{u},V=e^{v}
one has

\displaystyle i^{n+\alpha}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}U^{\alpha+1}V-U^{-\alpha-1}V^{-1}&i^{-1}U^{\alpha}V-i^{-1}U^{-\alpha}V^{-1}\\
i^{-1}U^{\alpha}V-i^{-1}U^{-\alpha}V^{-1}&-U^{\alpha-1}V+U^{-\alpha+1}V^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=i^{n+\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}2\sinh((\alpha+1)u+v)&\frac{2}{i}\sinh(\alpha u+v)\\
\frac{2}{i}\sinh(\alpha u+v)&-2\sinh((\alpha-1)u+v)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=2i^{n+\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}(\alpha+1)u+v&i^{-1}(\alpha u+v)\\
i^{-1}(\alpha u+v)&-(\alpha-1)u-v\end{pmatrix}+\text{higher order terms}.

Taking a product of
d
such matrices, the first order will be given by the product of the first orders. The formulas
(4,5,6)
are direct consequences of the definition of
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}
.
