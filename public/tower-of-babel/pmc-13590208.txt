Association Between Charlson Comorbidity Index and Pressure Injury Stage: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jay Hyun Pak, Woo Sung Jang, Dae Won Kwon, Seung Hee Baek, Il‐Kug Kim, Joon Seok Lee, Kang Young Choi, Jung Dug Yang, Ho Yun Chung, Jeong Yeop Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPressure injuries (PIs) are a major clinical burden in patients with multiple comorbidities. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is a validated comorbidity measure; however, its quantitative association with PI severity remains poorly characterised. To evaluate the association between CCI scores and PI stage, and to identify individual CCI component diseases independently associated with higher PI stage. This retrospective cohort study included 454 PI patients at a tertiary university hospital (July 2023–December 2025). PI severity was classified into stages 1–4. Both the original and updated CCI were calculated from comorbidities only, without the Charlson age adjustment. Ordinal logistic regression assessed associations between CCI and PI stage, adjusting for age and sex. The median patient age was 73 years (range, 17–95); 63.2% were male. Stage 2 PIs were most prevalent (70.5%). Each 1‐point increase in original CCI was associated with 1.10‐fold higher odds of a more advanced PI stage (95% CI: 1.02–1.20; p = 0.015) after adjusting for age and sex. Among individual CCI components, rheumatic disease (OR = 2.20; 95% CI: 1.04–4.65; p = 0.039) and hemiplegia/paraplegia (OR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.07–2.77; p = 0.024) were independently associated with higher PI stage; moderate‐to‐severe renal disease showed an association of similar direction that did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.63; 95% CI: 0.99–2.69; p = 0.056). A higher CCI was associated with higher PI severity in this cohort, although the effect size was modest. In a mutually adjusted multi‐variable model, rheumatic disease and hemiplegia/paraplegia were independently associated with higher PI stage. These associations are observational and require prospective confirmation before any clinical application can be considered.Keywords: Charlson Comorbidity Index, comorbidity, logistic models, pressure ulcer, severity of illness index

Introduction

Pressure injury (PI), formerly designated as pressure ulcer, refers to localised damage to the skin and underlying soft tissue arising from sustained mechanical loading that compromises blood circulation, culminating in ischemia and tissue necrosis [1]. As the global population ages at an accelerating pace, the prevalence of chronic diseases among older adults continues to escalate, rendering PI prevention and management an increasingly critical clinical priority [2]. PIs predominantly afflict patients subjected to prolonged immobilisation or recumbency, characteristically developing over bony prominences exposed to sustained external mechanical forces [3]. Reported prevalence varies substantially across care settings, ranging from patients already affected at the time of hospital admission to those managed in tertiary inpatient and critical care populations [4, 5, 6].

PIs are categorised into four recognised stages of increasing severity. Stage 1 is characterised by non‐blanchable erythema of intact skin. Stage 2 encompasses partial‐thickness skin loss involving the epidermis and dermis, manifesting as blistering, erosion or shallow ulceration. Stage 3 denotes full‐thickness skin loss extending into the subcutaneous tissue without exposure of underlying muscle or bone. Stage 4, the most severe form, entails full‐thickness tissue destruction with exposure of muscle, bone or supporting structures, frequently necessitating surgical intervention [1]. Advanced‐stage PIs may precipitate life‐threatening sequelae, including sepsis; accordingly, early identification of high‐risk patients is of paramount clinical importance [7].

The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), developed by Charlson et al. in 1987, constitutes a well‐validated instrument for prognosticating mortality risk in patients harbouring multiple comorbidities [8]. It comprises 17 diagnostic categories, including myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, mild liver disease, diabetes mellitus (with and without complications), hemiplegia/paraplegia, renal disease, malignancy, metastatic solid tumour, moderate‐to‐severe liver disease and AIDS/HIV. Each condition is assigned a weighted score of 1, 2, 3 or 6 commensurate with its associated mortality risk, and the cumulative score quantifies the patient's overall comorbidity burden. Elevated CCI scores correlate with heightened 10‐year mortality. The Updated CCI, proposed by Quan et al. in 2011, recalibrated the original weightings to account for advances in medical therapeutics and contemporary mortality patterns [9].

Zhang et al. [10] examined the association between CCI and community‐acquired PI in older acute inpatients at a Chinese tertiary hospital. However, that investigation was subject to several methodological constraints: it employed a binary outcome (PI presence vs. absence) without differentiating among PI stages, the analysis was confined to patients aged 65 years and older and the study population was restricted to a single institution with distinct demographic characteristics. Moreover, prior investigations have largely neglected to examine whether a graded increase in comorbidity burden corresponds to a proportional escalation in PI severity across the full staging spectrum.

The present study was designed to address these knowledge gaps by employing ordinal logistic regression to delineate the graded association between CCI scores and PI Stage (1–4). By using CCI as a continuous variable and also analysing individual CCI component diseases, this study sought to elucidate the quantitative relationship between comorbidity burden and PI severity. Furthermore, both the original and updated CCI were concurrently evaluated to compare their respective associations with PI stage. No age restriction was imposed, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a single‐centre retrospective cohort study conducted at the Department of Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery of a tertiary university hospital in South Korea. The medical records of all patients admitted between July 2023 and December 2025 who received a diagnosis of PI either at admission or during the course of hospitalisation were systematically reviewed. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our hospital, and all data were de‐identified in accordance with institutional privacy protocols. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de‐identified data.

Participants

Eligibility criteria for inclusion were as follows: (1) admission to the plastic surgery department between July 2023 and December 2025; (2) diagnosis of PI at admission or during hospitalisation; and (3) availability of sufficient comorbidity information in the medical record to calculate the CCI. Patients were excluded if PI staging could not be clearly determined from clinical photographs, or if the wound was located on the buttock but was not a pressure‐related injury (e.g., moisture‐associated skin damage). Following application of these criteria, a total of 454 patients were enrolled in the final analysis.

Pressure Injury Staging and Charlson Comorbidity Index

PI severity was classified into Stages 1 through 4 based on clinical photographs taken at the time of diagnosis, in accordance with the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) staging system [1]. Of 568 patients initially screened, 114 with unstageable injuries or deep‐tissue pressure injuries that could not be assigned a definite stage were excluded, yielding the 454 patients analysed. A plastic surgery resident performed the initial assessment and every case was subsequently reviewed and confirmed by a board‐certified plastic surgeon with more than 10 years of experience. In cases where the initial and senior assessments differed, the senior surgeon's assessment was adopted as the final stage. Formal inter‐rater reliability was not quantified.

Comorbidities were ascertained through medical record review. Both the original CCI [8] and the updated CCI [9] were calculated for each patient. Both indices were derived from comorbidities only. In the original index, one point was assigned to myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, mild liver disease and diabetes without complications; two points to diabetes with complications, hemiplegia/paraplegia, moderate‐to‐severe renal disease and any malignancy; three points to moderate‐to‐severe liver disease; and six points to metastatic solid tumour and AIDS/HIV. For the updated index, the weights published by Quan et al. were applied without modification. In both indices the standard hierarchical rules were observed: mild liver disease was not scored in the presence of moderate‐to‐severe liver disease, diabetes without complications was not scored in the presence of diabetes with complications and any malignancy was not scored in the presence of metastatic solid tumour. The Charlson age adjustment was not applied to either index; age was instead entered as a categorical covariate in the regression models, together with sex. Conditions were scored only when they had been diagnosed within the preceding 1 year or were clinically active at the time of assessment.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. CCI scores were reported as medians, given that median values afford superior resistance to the influence of extreme observations and thus provide a more robust representation of the underlying data distribution.

The dependent variable was PI Stage (1–4), treated as an ordinal outcome. The independent variables were individual CCI component diseases (dichotomous), sex (dichotomous), age (categorical) and CCI scores (continuous). Ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds model) was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional odds assumption was assessed by a likelihood‐ratio test of the ordinal model against a generalised ordered logit model (p = 0.070 for the original CCI model and p = 0.046 for the updated CCI model); estimates from the updated CCI model are therefore reported with corresponding caution. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF values below 10 deemed acceptable. In the first model, all 17 individual CCI component diseases were entered simultaneously into a single multivariable ordinal logistic regression, mutually adjusting for one another and for age and sex; the resulting estimates therefore represent mutually adjusted associations. In a second model, the overall CCI score was entered as an independent variable, again adjusting for age and sex. In a sensitivity analysis, serum albumin was added to both models to assess whether the observed associations were robust to adjustment for nutritional status. The Braden scale was not included, as it is a prospective PI‐risk prediction instrument for patients without an existing injury and is conceptually inappropriate as a covariate for the severity of an already‐established PI; ICU status and functional mobility were not incorporated as separate covariates; as these factors may overlap with components already included in the CCI (e.g., hemiplegia/paraplegia), we acknowledge the potential for residual confounding.

All statistical tests employed a two‐tailed 95% CI, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Data analyses were performed using Stata/MP version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Patient Demographics and Comorbidities

The study cohort comprised 454 patients with PIs. The median age was 73 years (range, 17–95), with 287 (63.2%) male and 167 (36.8%) female patients. The most frequently observed comorbidities included hemiplegia/paraplegia (332 patients, 73.1%), cerebrovascular disease (205, 45.2%), renal disease (136, 30.0%), diabetes without complications (126, 27.8%), any malignancy (107, 23.6%), congestive heart failure (93, 20.5%) and peptic ulcer disease (89, 19.6%). The median original CCI for the entire cohort was 4 (IQR: 3–6) and the median updated CCI was 3 (IQR: 2–5). Individual CCI Component Analysis

Ordinal logistic regression analysis of individual CCI component diseases revealed that sex was not associated with PI stage (p > 0.05); among the age categories, only the 60–69‐year group differed significantly from the reference group (OR = 2.57; 95% CI: 1.01–6.51; p = 0.047), with no consistent gradient across the remaining categories. In this mutually adjusted multi‐variable model, two of the 17 CCI component diseases were significantly associated with a more advanced PI stage. Rheumatic disease exhibited the strongest association, with 2.20‐fold higher odds of a more advanced PI stage (OR = 2.20; 95% CI: 1.04–4.65; p = 0.039). Hemiplegia/paraplegia was associated with 1.72‐fold higher odds (OR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.07–2.77; p = 0.024). Moderate‐to‐severe chronic renal disease was associated with 1.63‐fold higher odds (OR = 1.63; 95% CI: 0.99–2.69; p = 0.056); although the point estimate was comparable in magnitude to those of the two significant components, the 95% CI included 1.00 and this association did not reach the conventional significance threshold. The remaining comorbidities were not significantly associated with PI stage (all p > 0.05). AIDS/HIV was excluded from the forest plot owing to the presence of only a single affected patient, which yielded an unstable point estimate with an exceedingly wide confidence interval (OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 0.03–134.78). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study encompassing 454 patients with PIs, the present analysis demonstrated that CCI‐quantified comorbidity burden was significantly and positively associated with PI severity. Among individual CCI component diseases, rheumatic disease (OR = 2.20) and hemiplegia/paraplegia (OR = 1.72) were independently associated with advanced PI stage in the mutually adjusted model, whereas moderate‐to‐severe renal disease showed an association of similar direction and magnitude (OR = 1.63) that did not attain statistical significance. The magnitude of the overall association was modest, as reflected by the small per‐point odds ratio, although median and mean CCI values were generally higher in Stages 2 to 4 than in Stage 1; these observational findings should be interpreted as indicating association rather than causation.

The robust association between rheumatic disease and PI severity may be ascribed to the prolonged administration of corticosteroids, which constitute the therapeutic cornerstone of rheumatic disease management [11]. Chronic corticosteroid exposure suppresses collagen biosynthesis, induces cutaneous atrophy and diminishes dermal resilience against external mechanical forces [12, 13]. The resultant cutaneous thinning augments susceptibility to PI development and progression, as the depth of tissue damage upon mechanical loading is amplified in structurally compromised integument, consistent with the central role of collagen and elastin integrity in cutaneous resilience and wound healing [14, 15]. Furthermore, rheumatic disease is typified by chronic systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation and a hypercoagulable milieu, which may collectively foster microvascular thrombosis and compromise tissue perfusion [16, 17]. Notably, the buttock and pelvic regions, where PIs most frequently develop, are endowed with a rich vascular network perfused by multiple arterial tributaries of the internal iliac artery [18]. This robust arterial supply with extensive collateral pathways may attenuate the vascular sequelae of systemic inflammation, suggesting that the predominant mechanistic pathway linking rheumatic disease to PI severity is corticosteroid‐induced cutaneous fragility rather than ischemic vascular compromise. Moreover, rheumatic disease per se contributes to sarcopenia and joint contractures, which progressively curtail patients' ability to change positions independently, thereby engendering a synergistic dual mechanism of cutaneous vulnerability and diminished mobility that compounds PI risk.

The association observed for hemiplegia/paraplegia may reflect the convergent effects of motor and sensory nerve impairment. Patients with hemiplegia or paraplegia exhibit profoundly restricted volitional movement, severely constraining their capacity for independent repositioning [19]. Concomitantly, sensory loss attenuates the perception of pain and discomfort engendered by sustained pressure, thereby diminishing the afferent stimulus to reposition [19]. These factors result in continuous pressure loading on dependent body parts, which is the primary mechanism for PI development and progression. Additionally, neurological injury distal to the level of the lesion disrupts autonomic vasomotor regulation, thereby attenuating the reactive hyperemia response that ordinarily restores perfusion following pressure release. Prolonged immobility further precipitates progressive muscular atrophy and subcutaneous adipose depletion overlying bony prominences, diminishing the soft tissue buffer that absorbs and dissipates externally applied pressure forces, underscoring the importance of dermal bulk and mechanical‐load tolerance in resisting pressure‐induced injury [20]. Critically, these structural and functional deficits are inherently irreversible and refractory to pharmacological remediation, which may account for the persistence of hemiplegia/paraplegia as a factor robustly and independently associated with higher PI stage.

Moderate‐to‐severe renal disease showed an association of similar direction to the two significant components but did not reach statistical significance. Several features of advanced nephropathy could plausibly contribute to more severe pressure injury, including protein‐energy wasting, chronic low‐grade inflammation, impaired tissue regenerative capacity, microcirculatory dysfunction intrinsic to the uremic state and uremia‐associated immune dysfunction [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. With 136 affected patients and a confidence interval only marginally overlapping unity, the present data cannot determine whether this reflects a genuine but smaller association or the absence of one, and adequately powered studies will be required to resolve this question.

The structural distinctions between the original and updated CCI merit consideration in the interpretation of our findings. In the updated CCI, five conditions—myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, peptic ulcer disease and uncomplicated diabetes—were assigned a weight of 0 and thus contribute nothing to the score. The weights of congestive heart failure (1 to 2), dementia (1 to 2), mild liver disease (1 to 2) and moderate‐to‐severe liver disease (3 to 4) were increased, whereas those of diabetes with complications (2 to 1), moderate‐to‐severe renal disease (2 to 1) and AIDS/HIV (6 to 4) were reduced; chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, hemiplegia/paraplegia, any malignancy and metastatic solid tumour retained their original weights [9]. Notwithstanding these recalibrations, both the original CCI (OR = 1.10, p = 0.015) and updated CCI (OR = 1.10, p = 0.039) showed similarly significant associations with PI stage, suggesting that the conditions most strongly associated with PI severity are among those that retain substantial weightings across both scoring systems. Collectively, these findings indicate that either version of the CCI may serve as a measure associated with PI severity in this cohort.

Of note, sex was not associated with PI stage in either analytic model, and no consistent age gradient was observed. This observation suggests that the aggregate comorbidity burden, as quantified by the CCI, constitutes a more salient determinant of PI severity than demographic characteristics alone. The clinical implication is that the CCI, as a standardised and readily quantifiable instrument, may provide an objective summary of comorbidity burden that is associated with PI stage in patients with established pressure injuries.

A noteworthy finding of the present study is that several conditions traditionally regarded as risk factors for impaired wound healing—including cardiovascular diseases (myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease) and diabetes mellitus—did not demonstrate significant associations with PI severity. One speculative possibility is a pharmacological offset: medications commonly used in these conditions may exert protective effects on skin and wound healing that counterbalance the tissue‐level consequences of the underlying diseases [28, 29, 30, 31]. However, because medication exposure was not available in this dataset, this remains an untested hypothesis, to be examined in future studies with detailed pharmacological data.

By contrast, for the conditions associated with higher PI stage, the corresponding treatments may be less able to offset tissue vulnerability—for example, corticosteroids used in rheumatic disease can themselves impair skin integrity. We emphasise that, in the absence of medication‐level data, these mechanisms are offered only as hypotheses for future investigation.

Several methodological limitations of the present study merit acknowledgment. First, the retrospective cohort design inherently precludes definitive causal inference. Information bias arising from retrospective data ascertainment and residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be entirely excluded. Second, as a single‐centre study drawn specifically from a tertiary plastic surgery service, the cohort may be subject to substantial selection bias, and the findings may not generalise to general medical, surgical, intensive‐care or community‐based populations [32]. This may yield elevated baseline comorbidity prevalence and PI severity, potentially constraining the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Third, PI staging was predicated on clinical photographs, and the inherent subjectivity of visual classification—including the distinction between partial‐thickness and full‐thickness injury—introduces the possibility of misclassification. Inter‐rater reliability was not formally evaluated. Fourth, the cross‐sectional ascertainment of CCI at a single time point fails to capture the temporal evolution of comorbidity burden in relation to PI development. Fifth, the present study did not incorporate several potentially salient clinical variables. Although serum albumin was examined in a sensitivity analysis, other variables—such as body mass index, functional mobility, wound location, moisture/incontinence, care setting and present‐on‐admission versus hospital‐acquired status—were not available and may represent unmeasured confounders of the observed associations. Several further caveats apply. The PI‐stage distribution was highly imbalanced (Stage 2, 70.5%), and several CCI components were of low prevalence (rheumatic disease, n = 39; moderate/severe liver disease, n = 6; AIDS/HIV, n = 1), yielding limited effective sample sizes and, for the rarest categories, unstable estimates. Because 17 components were examined, the analysis is subject to multiple‐comparisons concerns; the component‐level findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. The Braden scale was not analysed because it is a prospective risk‐prediction instrument for injury‐free patients and is not a valid covariate for the severity of an established PI, while ICU status and functional mobility were not incorporated as separate covariates, with the attendant potential for residual confounding acknowledged.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the present study offers several noteworthy contributions. First, to our knowledge, this represents the first investigation to systematically delineate the quantitative graded association between CCI and PI stage using ordinal logistic regression, thereby transcending the binary outcome (presence/absence) framework employed in prior studies. This ordinal analytic framework characterises how comorbidity burden relates to the severity of the injury observed at the time of diagnosis, rather than to the occurrence of injury alone. Second, the identification of rheumatic disease and hemiplegia/paraplegia as conditions independently associated with higher PI stage delineates where this association is concentrated and may inform the design of future studies addressing PI prevention, with particular relevance to patients with autoimmune conditions receiving prolonged corticosteroid therapy. Third, by quantifying the association between a standardised and readily available comorbidity measure and PI stage, the present study furnishes reference estimates against which prospective investigations—including those specifically designed to evaluate predictive performance—may subsequently be calibrated.

It should be noted that the CCI alone may have limited discriminatory power for predicting individual PI stages, given that PI severity is governed by a multitude of factors extending beyond comorbidity burden, including nutritional status, serum albumin concentration, functional mobility and local tissue perfusion [33]. Future predictive models that integrate these supplementary clinical variables in conjunction with the CCI may attain enhanced discriminatory accuracy. Multi‐centre prospective investigations are warranted to corroborate the observed associations across heterogeneous healthcare settings. Additionally, intervention studies examining whether CCI‐based risk stratification, combined with targeted prevention protocols for patients with rheumatic disease and hemiplegia/paraplegia, can ameliorate PI‐related clinical outcomes would furnish additional evidence for the clinical utility of this approach.

Conclusion

In this single‐centre retrospective cohort of 454 patients with established pressure injuries, a higher comorbidity burden quantified by the CCI was associated with a higher PI stage, although the effect size was modest. In a mutually adjusted multivariable model, rheumatic disease and hemiplegia/paraplegia were independently associated with higher PI stage. These associations are observational and hypothesis‐generating; prospective, multi‐centre studies incorporating nutritional, functional and wound‐level variables are needed before any role for CCI‐based assessment in clinical practice can be established.
