lnc122 RNA prevents liver tumorigenesis in MYC-driven liver cancer
Authors: Hagoon Jang, Feijie Zhang, Bo Kyoung Suh, Anh Le, Calvin J Stephens, Kerriann M Casey, Mark A Kay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsExtensive research has shown that the microRNA-122 (miR-122) plays an important role in liver homeostasis, and its dysregulation has been implicated in various liver pathologies such as liver cancer and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. The primary transcript for miR-122, long non-coding RNA 122 (lnc122) has traditionally only been considered an intermediate product for mature miR-122.MethodsWe used CRISPRi to inhibit lnc122 RNA expression in hepatoma cells and mouse livers with and without expression of mature miR-122 RNA to determine if a lnc122 RNA has a separate function. These models were used in combination of RNA-seq, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, RNA pulldown-mass spectrometry, and a CRSIRPi/CRISPRa mediated liver tumor model to establish the specific role of lnc122 in hepatic homeostasis.ResultsWe demonstrate that lnc122 RNA has a distinct tumor suppressive role that is separate from the antiproliferative function of miR-122. Specifically, lnc122 RNA promotes MYC protein degradation by stabilizing the MYC-UBR5 E3 ubiquitin ligase protein complex. Unlike other E3 ubiquitin ligases for MYC, UBR5 is co-amplified with MYC in >40% of human cancers because of their close location on the human genome and lnc122 is indispensable for efficient MYC degradation by UBR5. Paired patient liver and tumor samples showed decreased concentrations of lnc122 RNA in tumors. Furthermore, reducing the lnc122 RNA transcripts in mouse liver exacerbated MYC-driven liver tumorigenesis. Although lnc122 expression is restricted to the liver, exogenous expression of lnc122 in non-hepatic transformed cells also destabilized MYC protein.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that lnc122 RNA has a synergistic role with miR-122 in the surveillance and prevention of liver tumorigenesis and may represent a new target for treating MYC-driven human cancers.Impact and implicationsThe study demonstrates that lnc122 RNA not only is a precursor for miR-122, but also functions in forming a ubiquitination complex via UBR5, and responsible for limiting the half-life of MYC protein. Loss of lnc122 RNA expression promotes liver cancer perhaps categorizing it as tumor suppressor gene. Further studies to develop agents to enhance the activity of this complex may offer a new therapeutic approach to treat hepatocellular carcinoma.Keywords: lnc122, miR-122, MYC, UBR5, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

In humans, the majority of microRNAs (miRNAs) are derived from intragenic transcripts co-transcribed in functionally linked coding genes,1,2 whereas intergenic miRNAs are independently transcribed from their own promoters and produce their own primary transcripts. These primary transcripts are processed into pre-miRNAs and ultimately into mature miRNAs.

A substantial number of long non-coding (lncRNAs; >200 nucleotides in length) possess characteristics similar to mRNAs, such as Pol II-mediated transcription, elongation, splicing, 5′ methylated guanosine capping, and 3′ polyadenylation.3 LncRNAs are involved in numerous biological functions such as epigenetic control, transcriptional regulation, miRNA sponging, and can function via a structural scaffold mechanism.3 Recent studies established a few primary or precursor miRNA transcripts possess important biological roles as functional pre-miRNAs4 or lncRNAs during development and disease progression.[5], [6], [7], [8]

miR-122, one of the intergenic miRNAs, is derived from a long lnc122 primary transcript that is expressed at modest levels but results in ∼70% (∼50,000 copies per cell) of the total hepatic miRNA pool because miR-122 has a long half-life.9 The function of miR-122 has been intensively studied in liver lipid/cholesterol metabolism as well as regulating cell proliferation.[10], [11], [12] Germline or conditional knockout of the pre-miR-122 sequence from the lnc122 locus induces liver tumorigenesis long-term during aging, whereas reconstitution of miR-122 blocks liver tumorigenesis in this mouse model.11

lnc122 RNA exists as both spliced and unspliced forms in the human liver and is stably present even after microprocessor processing and formation of miR-122.13 We previously demonstrated that miR-122 knockout (KO) mice have increased lnc122 RNA transcripts lacking the miR-122 hairpin suggesting a possible feedback regulatory loop.9 Miravirsen (Santaris Pharma, Copenhagen Denmark) (an anti-miR-122 15-nt locked nucleic acid–phosphorothioate [LNA-PS] mixmer antisense oligonucleotide) has been reported to be relatively safe in animals and humans and binds to miR-122.14 This was shown to block both drosha/DGCR8 and Dicer processing of the primary and pre-miR-122 transcripts into mature miR-122 transcripts. As a result, the lnc122 RNA concentration increased in primary mouse hepatocytes,15 also suggesting feedback regulation or stabilization of lnc122 RNA transcripts. We previously reported that a 10% reduction in miR-122 biosynthesis induced by high small interfering RNA (siRNA) concentrations of almost any sequence delivered using adeno-associated virus (AAV)-transcriptional based short hairpin RNA (shRNA) delivery caused acute liver failure.16,17 The only shRNA expressed at high levels that did not cause toxicity was miR-122. Interestingly, unlike miR-122 KO mice or LNA-induced suppression of mature miR-122, there was no increase in the lnc122 RNA levels.16 These studies indicate reduction of miR-122 upregulates lnc122 levels in some, but not all, conditions. Moreover, the discrepancy in phenotype between germ line miR-122 KO and miR-122 ASO treated mice is further discrepant in lipid/cholesterol metabolism and is not well understood. For instance, various liver disease states including steatosis and tumorigenesis occurred in miR-122 germline KO with increased lnc122 levels,11,12,18 whereas liver steatosis and blood lipid profiles were dramatically improved by anti-miR-122 ASO treatment10 without any determination of lnc122 levels. Based on these discordant results, we hypothesized that the lnc122 RNA may have an independent function.

Here, we demonstrate that lnc122 RNA interacts with the UBR5 encoded E3 ubiquitin ligase to enhance MYC protein degradation making it a key regulatory factor in maintaining normal liver growth. Additionally, this mechanism provides the potential for the development of a new class of MYC-driven cancer therapies.

Materials and methods

Generation of stable cell lines and cell culture

Huh7, HepG2, HEK293T, HeLa, A549, MCF-7, and Hepa1-6 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with 2 mM L-glutamine, an antibiotic/antimycotic mixture, and 10% FBS. To generate dCas9-KRAB-expressing Huh7 cells, we plated 2 million Huh7 cells in 10-cm dishes and added 200 μl of dCas9-KRAB lentivirus. Two days later, cells were selected using 50 μg/ml of blasticidin for 7 days. For generation of lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cell lines, we performed combinatorial lentiviral transduction of sgSCR+shSCR and sglnc122#2+miR-122 in dCas9-KRAB-expressing Huh7 cells. Two days later, cells were selected using 5 μg/ml of puromycin and 500 μg/ml of hygromycin for 7 days and gene expression was confirmed. To generate the lentivirus, we cultured HEK293T cells in 15-cm dishes and transfected with 6 μg pMD2.G, 6 μg psPAX2, and 12 μg of dCas9-KRAB, sgSCR, sglnc122, shSCR, and miR-122 using the polycation polyethylenimine (PEI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. 23966) After 48 h, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and the virus was concentrated ten-fold using Lenti-X concentrator (Clontech). The final lentiviral volume was 0.5 ml. Guide RNA sequence information is provided in Table S1.

DNA constructs and transfection

wtlnc122, smlnc122, pAlnc122, and mouse lnc122 were inserted into pAAV-CAG-tdTomato which was digested by BamH1 & Mfe. The primers for PCR reaction of human lnc122, mouse lnc122, and miR-122 were ordered from IDT. PCR amplicons were inserted by In-Fusion enzyme (Clontech). For miR-122 construct, we used the pscAAV-CAG-GFP digested with Age1/Not1. We amplified the 363bp miR-122 genomic region from human genomic DNA which contains pre-miR-122 sequence centrally located, using the following primers: forward ATTGAGAGTGTTCAGCTCTT, reverse TGAGTGCAAAAGAGCCAGACTTTA primers. All subcloned fragments were sequenced by Sanger sequencing to confirm successful construction. Full sequences of all plasmids used in this study are available upon request. For transfection, 2 μg of DNA plasmid in six-well plates and 8 μg of DNA plasmid in 10-cm dishes were transfected with Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent and P3000™ Enhancer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For AAV-CRISPRi, we generated plasmid which contains HLP promoter-dSaCas9_KRAB-U6 promoter-single guide RNA cassette. For AAV-CRISPRa, we generated plasmid which contains HLP promoter-dSaCas9_VP64–U6 promoter-single guide RNA with MS2 loop.

Co-immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assay

Co-immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assay were performed as previously described.19 In brief, cells were treated with 20 μM MG132 (Abcam) for 4 h then lysis with IP-lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) including a 100 × of protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich), RiboLock RNase inhibitor (100 U/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 5 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) (Calbiochem). Total cell lysates were obtained by centrifugation at 13,400 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and 1–1.5 mg of lysates was used for immunoprecipitation. The lysates were incubated with 1 μg of anti-MYC (Cell Signaling Technology, 9402S) overnight at 4 °C to pull down endogenous MYC, followed by 1 h of further incubation with 10 μl of Dynabeads™ Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After washing three times with the lysis buffer, the immunoprecipitated proteins were recovered from the beads by boiling for 10 min in the sample buffer and analyzed by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA of cultured cells, mouse liver, and human liver and liver tumor samples obtained from the Stanford Hospital Tissue Bank was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen™) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, equal amounts of RNA were subjected to cDNA synthesis using Superscript IV (ThermoFisher Waltham MA USA) reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The relative amount of mRNA was evaluated by using a CFX real-time quantitative PCR detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and calculated following normalization to the level of GAPDH or β-ACTIN mRNA. The primer sequences used for the real-time quantitative PCR analyses are described in Table S2.

Small RNA qRT-PCR

A total of 0.5 μg of RNA was subjected to cDNA synthesis for miRNAs using mir-X miRNA first strand synthesis kit (Takara Bio). The miRNAs were polyadenylated using poly A polymerase and then reverse transcribed by SMART MMLV reverse transcriptase, both included in the mRQ enzyme mix in the kit. The relative amount of miR-122 was evaluated by using a CFX real-time quantitative PCR detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and calculated following normalization to the level of U6 snRNA. The U6 forward, U6 reverse, and common reverse primer set were included in mir-X miRNA first strand synthesis kit and sequence information is not disclosed. The primer sequences used for the real-time quantitative PCR analyses are described in Table S2.

Biotinylated RNA pulldown assay

For the biotinylated RNA pulldown assay, we modified a previously established protocol.20 In brief, human lnc122 sense and antisense DNA were synthesized with T7 promoter combined primer set. Using PCR products, we synthesized the lnc122 sense RNA and antisense RNA using in vitro transcription kit with biotin-UTP and then synthesized RNA were mixed with 0.5 mg of Huh7 nuclear extracts overnight, followed by RNA pulldown using Dynabeads™ Streptavidin Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After three washes, the binding proteins were eluted with SDS sampling buffer and run to 4–12% SDS–PAGE gel for 10 min. The gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue (Bio-Rad) and then cut and sent the stained band to Mass-spec facility (BGI, San Jose, CA, USA) to analyze binding proteins.

Preparation of recombinant AAV

HEK293T cells were transfected using polycation PEI with 3.75 μg of AAV8 capsid plasmid, 11.25 μg of pAd5 helper plasmid and 3.75 μg of each AAV plasmid, including CRISPRi_SCR, CRISPRi_lnc122, CAG-miR122, CRISPRa_SCR, CRISPRa_lnc122, and EF1a-MPH plasmid, per 15 cm dish. AAV pools were purified by double-CsCl gradient centrifugation, and the vector genome copy number was quantified using qPCR which is previously described.21 Guide RNA sequence information is provided in Table S1.

RNA immunoprecipitation

For RNA immunoprecipitation, we modified a previously established protocol.22 For formaldehyde fixation, 3 million cells per replicate were fixed with 0.1% formaldehyde for 10 min, and then quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min. For UV fixation, 3 million cells per replicate were fixed with 0.25 J/cm2 at 254 nm. RNA immunoprecipitation procedure were the same under native condition, formaldehyde fixation, and UV crosslinking. Three million cells per replicate were subjected to nuclear extraction using 500 μl of nuclear extraction buffer (1.28 M sucrose, 20 mM Tris-HCL pH 7.5, 20 mM MgCl2, 4% Triton X-100). Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 2,500 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in IP-lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with RiboLock RNase inhibitor (100 U/ml), 100 × protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma), and 5 mM NEM. After centrifugation at 13,400 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was precleared with protein G beads for 30 min at 4 °C and the beads were discarded. Then the supernatant was incubated with 1 μg of rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, 2729) or MYC (Cell Signaling Technology, 9402S) and rotated overnight at 4 °C. A total of 10 μl of protein G beads were added and rotated for 1 h at 4 °C and washed three times with IP-lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For fixed conditions, beads were incubated with 100 μl of proteinaseK buffer and enzyme mixture for 45 min at 55 °C then subjected to TRIzol (Thermofisher Waltham, MA, USA) for RNA extraction. For UV-fixed samples, to reduce indirect interaction, protein G beads were washed three times with fRIP wash buffer (1 × PBS, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% deoxycholate, 0.5% NP-40).

3′ RACE

We performed the 3′ RACE of human and mouse lnc122 according to previously described.13 To validate the 3′ end of lnc122, in vitro poly(A) tailing of nuclear RNA from Huh7 and Hepa1-6 was performed with poly(A)tailing kit (AM1350, Ambion) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then RNA purification and reverse transcription were performed with an oligo(dT)25V anchor primer and SuperScript IV (Invitrogen™). PCR was performed with common reverse primer with human primer 1F, human primer 2F, human primer 3F, mouse primer 1F, mouse primer 2F, and mouse primer 3F. PCR fragments were cloned into the pCR™2.1-TOPO™ TA vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for sequence verification. Sequence information of each primer is provided in Table S3.

RNA fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH)

We ordered 25 singly labeled oligonucleotides designed to selectively bind to 25 different regions of human lnc122 RNA transcripts and conjugated them with a CAL Fluor® Red 610 Dye (Biosearch Technologies). For detection of human lnc122 in HepG2 and Huh7 cells, 125 nM RNA FISH probes were prepared in the hybridization buffer with RiboLock RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Samples were washed twice for 10 min in 2 × SSCT at 37 °C, then 2 × SSCT for 10 min at room temperature, 0.2 × SSC for 10 min at room temperature, and twice with PBS for 5 min with DAPI. Then coverslips were mounted with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for confocal microscope image acquisition. The sequences of labeled oligonucleotides are in Table S4.

Measurement of cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was measured with a CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS assay; Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 5,000–10,000 cells were plated in 96-well plates followed by DNA transfection (100 ng/well) with lipofectamine 3000 (Thermofisher Waltham MA USA). At 72 h post-transfection, an MTS assay was performed. For lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells, we plated 10,000 cells in 96-well plates both lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells and control Huh7 cells, and performed an MTS assay at Days 0, 1, 2, and 3. Each independent experiment was performed in eight to 12 replicates.

Small RNA Northern blot

A sample of 5 μg of TRIzol-extracted RNA was loaded on a 15% Novex® TBE-Urea Gels to analyze miR-122 and U6, and the gel was transferred to a Hybond-N+ membrane (Amersham). miR-122 and U6 were detected using a 32P-labeled DNA probe. miR-122 probe sequence-CAAACACCATTGTCACACTCCA, sm-miR-122 probe sequence-CAAACACCATTGTCTGCTCTTA.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were prepared using IP-lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or SDS-lysis buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 30% glycerol) with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich) and 5 mM NEM (Sigma Aldrich). A sample of 10–20 μg of protein lysate was run on a 3–8% or 7% Tris-acetate gel and transferred to PVDF membrane using iBlot2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The membrane was incubated for 20 min at room temperature in a 4% BSA (Omnipur) solution or 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with one of the antibodies listed below. After washing and incubation for 2 h at room temperature with secondary antibody, the protein signal was detected using EMD Millipore Luminata Western HRP substrate (Forte or Crescendo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following primary antibodies were used for western blotting at the indicated dilution ratio. MYC (Cell Signaling Technology, 9402S, 1:1,000), UBR5 (Santa Cruz Biotech, sc-515494, 1:200), ubiquitin (Santa Cruz Biotech, sc-8017, 1:200), Flag (Sigma Aldrich, A8592, 1:5,000), and β-ACTIN (Sigma Aldrich, A3854, 1:10,000).

Establishment of mouse liver tumor model

For mouse experiments, 6-week-old male FVB/N mice were injected with AAV8-CRISPRi_SCR (1e12vg/mouse), AAV8-CRISPRi_lnc122 (1e12vg/mouse), AAV8-CRISPRa_SCR (1e12vg/mouse) + AAV8-MPH (5e11vg/mouse), AAV8-CRISPRa_lnc122 (1e12/mouse) + AAV8-MPH (5e11vg/mouse), AAV8-CRISPRi_SCR (1e12vg/mouse), and AAV8-CRISPRi_lnc122 (1e12vg/mouse)+AAV8-miR-122 (0.7e11vg/mouse). One week later, SB13 plasmid (2 μg/mouse) and T3-cMYC plasmid (20 μg/mouse) were hydrodynamically injected as previously described.23,24 Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation according to the timelines in Fig. 5A.

Histopathology

Mouse livers were immersion-fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 72 h. Formalin-fixed tissues were processed routinely, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with H&E. All tissues were blindly evaluated by a board-certified veterinary pathologist (KMC). A semi-quantitative ordinal histopathology grading scale (score 0–4) was used to determine the percentage of histologically evaluated liver occupied by tumors.

Immunofluorescent staining

Sterile 12-mm coverslips (Fisher Scientific, 12CIR-1) were placed into a 24-well plate and 50,000 Huh7 cells were plated per well. Twenty-four hours later, the media was removed, cells washed once with 1 × PBS, then fixed with 300 μl of 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature with rotation. The 4% PFA was then removed, cells washed four times with 1 × PBS, and blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% BSA with 0.3% Triton X in PBS. After three washes with PBS, primary antibodies anti-MYC (Cell Signaling Technology, 9402S) and/or anti-URB5 (Santa Cruz Biotech, CB-1D) was applied at 1:125 dilution in 1% BSA with 0.3% Triton X in PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, antibody was removed, wells washed three times for 5 min each, then secondary antibodies anti-rabbit conjugated to Alexafluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21206) and/or secondary anti-mouse Alexafluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21236) were applied at 1:500 dilutions in 1% BSA with 0.3% Triton X in PBS. After 1-h incubation at room temperature, antibodies were removed, wells were washed with PBS four times for 5 min each with rotation, and coverslips mounted on microscope slides with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Slides were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope with identical laser intensities and exposure times. Controls consisted of cells stained without primary antibodies (secondary only) or without any antibodies.

RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted using Zymo Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus Kit with on-column DNase digestion (Zymo Research). At least 100 ng RNA was used to prepare the RNA-seq library using NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (E6310L, New England BioLabs) and TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Cat# 20020594, Illumina) for each sample following the manufacturer’s instruction. The library was sequenced on an Illumina Nova-seq partial lane to generate 2 × 150-bp paired-end reads (Novogene, USA).

Computational analysis for RNA-seq

We performed four biological replicate experiments for ctrl and lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells. Reads were aligned to hg19 (ENSEMBL transcripts) using STAR with default parameters (--alignIntronMin 61 --alignIntronMax 265006) and sorted by samtools (option -n). Quantification of aligned reads was carried out by HTseq-count with parameters (-s reverse -m intersection-nonempty -r name -f bam). The DESeq2 package from R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used to identify differentially expressed genes. For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), we used the fgsea (Fast GSEA) package in R (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

GSE77509 processing and QC

Dataset GSE77509 was obtained from NCBI’s GEO database and comprises RNA-seq data from 19 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), each with matched adjacent normal tissue, primary tumor, and portal vein tumor thrombus samples. Each condition contains up to four or five technical replicates per patient. For this study, we analyzed only the primary tumor and adjacent normal samples. Sample libraries with poor alignment quality (mapping rate <50% or <8 million aligned reads) were excluded. After this filtering, four patients were removed from downstream analyses. Quality control of raw and quantified reads was performed using FASTQC. Adapter sequences and low-complexity reads were removed using FASTP during preliminary processing. After quality control, transcript-level quantification was performed using Salmon with GC-bias and sequence bias correction enabled. Two different Salmon indices were evaluated, which resulted in minor differences in absolute TPM values but did not affect overall trends or conclusions. The GRCh38.p14 transcriptome and genome were obtained from GENCODE and combined to generate a decoy-aware transcriptome for quantification. Gene-level abundance and count matrices were generated from Salmon output using the tximport and biomaRt R packages (R Fondation for Statistical Computing). For visualization of lnc122 expression, TPM values were log2-transformed. The plotting strategy was used that paired individual replicates. TPM data were visualized using Matplotlib and Seaborn in Python.

Statistics analysis

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. For in vitro experiments, data are presented as mean ± SD and represent at least three biologically independent samples (n ≥ 3) from three or more independent experiments. For in vivo experiments, data are presented as mean ± SEM to represent uncertainty in estimating the population mean. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Depending on the experimental design, data were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, paired two-tailed Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, or two-way ANOVA, as indicated in the figure legends. Exact p values are reported where applicable. A p value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

lnc122 negatively regulates Huh7 cell proliferation

We first studied basic properties of lnc122 RNA. The cellular localization was established using RNA FISH in Huh7 and HepG2 human hepatoma cells. Lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs were not present in HepG2 whereas lnc122 RNA was present only in the nucleus of Huh7 cells (Fig. 1A,B).13,25 3′ RACE identified both poly(A)+ and poly(A)- lnc122 transcripts from these human hepatoma cells (Fig. 1C–E), consistent with reports that transcription is terminated by drosha/DGCR8 processing.13 To estimate the relative amounts of poly(A)+ and poly(A)- lnc122 RNAs, we used oligo dT and a random hexamer to convert the cDNA from poly(A)+ population only and poly(A)+ and poly(A)- population together and quantified the lnc122 levels (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, the spliced form of lnc122 RNA had a similar portion of poly(A)+ and poly(A)- transcripts whereas unspliced lnc122 was mostly poly(A)- RNA.

To investigate the possible functional roles of lnc122, we modulated lnc122 transcript levels using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi). First, we generated a stable population of Huh7 CRISPRi expressing cells using a lentiviral vector26 and then transfected guide RNAs targeting the lnc122 promoter. Suppression of transcription at the lnc122 locus led to a decline in both lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs and resulted in enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 1G and Fig. S1A). To decouple the roles of lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs, we designed human lnc122 expression constructs with a mutated miR-122 seed sequence (smlnc122) or with a substitution of the miR-122 hairpin sequence with a poly(A) signal sequence (pAlnc122) (Fig. 1H and Fig. S1B). Overexpression of human wild-type lnc122 (wtlnc122), smlnc122 or pAlnc122 led to suppression of cell proliferation (Fig. 1H and Fig. S1C and D). However, overexpression of miR-122 alone also reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 1H), consistent with the reported antitumorigenic effect of miR-122 RNA. Lastly, we established a lnc122 RNA suppressed/miR-122 positive Huh7 cell line. To do this, we reconstituted the lnc122 CRISPRi induced suppressed cells with miR-122 (lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells) using lenti-sglnc122 and lenti-miR-122 expression cassettes (Fig. S1E and F). These cells still exhibited accelerated cell proliferation compared to control Huh7 cells (Fig. 1I). Together, these experiments allowed us to decouple the separate antiproliferative functions of miR-122 and lnc122 RNAs confirming that both RNAs have separate antiproliferative functions.

We also explored whether the murine and human lnc122 RNAs would functionally complement each other because unlike miR-122, which is identical in all vertebrates,27 lnc122 sequences are not well conserved between species. The murine lnc122 primary transcript consists of one long 5 kb exon (Fig. S2A), whereas human lnc122 has two exons and one intron (Fig. 1C). Despite a lack of sequence conservation, murine lnc122 displayed similar functional characteristics as human lnc122, including containing both poly(A)+ and poly(A)- transcripts, and antiproliferative functions (Fig. S2B–D, and Fig. 1H).

lnc122 regulates MYC protein stability

To investigate how lnc122 RNA regulates cell proliferation, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and GSEA using control and lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells. RNA-seq profiles showed no difference in the expression of miR-122 target genes including Aldolase A, a known miR-122 target compared with control Huh7 cells, indicating the lnc122 had no effect on the function of miR-122 (Fig. S3A). GSEA analysis revealed various cell cycle and tumor related pathways were activated by lnc122 suppression including the MYC targets (Fig. 2A,B). qRT-PCR quantification and RNA-seq analysis showed static levels of MYC mRNA in lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells, indicating MYC mRNA expression was not affected by lnc122 reduction (Fig. 2C and Fig. S3B). In addition, Huh7 cells that ectopically overexpress human wild-type lnc122 (wtlnc122), human seed mutant lnc122 (smlnc122), human poly(A) lnc122 (pAlnc122), or mouse lnc122 (mlnc122) RNAs also showed similar levels of MYC mRNA (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, we inhibited transcription using actinomycin D, and found similar MYC mRNA stability profiles in both control and lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells (Fig. S3C).

We next studied MYC protein stability in Huh7 cells overexpressing human wtlnc122, human smlnc122, human pAlnc122, miR-122, or mouse lnc122 RNAs by treatment with cycloheximide to arrest translation and measured the MYC protein levels over time. Ectopic expression of human wtlnc122, human smlnc122, human pAlnc122, or mouse lnc122 RNAs in Huh7 cells led to a more rapid loss in MYC protein concentrations compared to control cells whereas miR-122 overexpression had no effect on MYC protein stability (Fig. 2E,F). Furthermore, the concentration of MYC protein was more stable in lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells compared to controls, following inhibition of protein synthesis with cycloheximide treatment (Fig. 2G,H). Taken together, the presence of either human or mouse lnc122 RNAs in cells resulted in the de-stabilization of MYC protein, resulting in suppression of cell proliferation.

lnc122 physically interacts with MYC and UBR5

To examine how lnc122 RNA regulates MYC protein stability, we first checked for a physical interaction between lnc122 RNA and MYC protein. The endogenous MYC protein complexes in the nuclear fraction were pull-downed with an anti-MYC antibody and analyzed for lnc122 RNA enrichment. We found both spliced and unspliced forms of lnc122 RNA bound to the MYC protein (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, lnc122 RNA was enriched in non-crosslinked and after formaldehyde crosslinked, but not UV-crosslinked samples. These results imply that the interaction between lnc122 RNA and MYC might be mediated by a double-strand region of lnc122 RNA or through other RNA binding proteins (RBPs). To gain further mechanistic insight, we screened for lnc122 RBPs (Fig. 3B). To do so, we synthesized the sense strand of lnc122 RNA with biotin-UTP and used the antisense strand as a negative control, and then separately mixed these RNAs with Huh7 nuclear protein extracts. We pull-downed lncRNA-protein complexes using magnetic streptavidin agarose followed by mass spectrometry analysis.

Mass spectrometry data identified 288 candidate proteins, which selectively bound to lnc122 RNA and were then segregated into groups based upon their known functions, including protein degradation, RNA binding, transcriptional regulation, and DNA binding (Fig. 3C). One of the enriched proteins, UBR5 is an E3 (HECT) ubiquitin ligase for MYC28 and a candidate that might explain how lnc122 regulates MYC protein stability. To test this hypothesis, we first examined the physical interaction of endogenous UBR5 and MYC protein by performing a co-immunoprecipitation assay. As commonly observed, the short half-life of MYC protein (∼30 min) (Fig. 2F),29 required the addition of the MG132 proteasome inhibitor to increase the MYC protein half-life to allow for the detection of the endogenous UBR5 and MYC interaction (Fig. 3D).

lnc122 RNA assembles the MYC-UBR5 protein complex

We found MYC and UBR5 to be localized in the nucleus by immunocytochemistry (Fig. 4A), consistent with the nuclear localization of lnc122 RNA (Fig. 1B) and nuclear functionality of the complex. Depletion of UBR5 by siRNA-mediated knockdown resulted in increased MYC protein levels (Fig. 4B) and showed less ubiquitinated MYC protein following endogenous MYC immunoprecipitation (Fig. 4C).

The physical interaction of MYC and UBR5 by lnc122 RNA was established by multiple experiments. First, the interaction of these two proteins was reduced when the cell lysates were treated with RNase during the co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 4D,E). Second, we performed the co-immunoprecipitation and an in vivo ubiquitination assay of endogenous MYC protein in control Huh7, lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7, and lnc122-/miR-122 Huh7 with lnc122 complementation. The MYC-UBR5 interaction and MYC ubiquitination were substantially reduced in the lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 compared with control Huh7 cells whereas complementation with lnc122 RNA intensified the interaction and polyubiquitination of MYC (Fig. 4F). Third, we established that overexpression of UBR5 in lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells did not enhance either MYC-UBR5 interactions or MYC polyubiquitination (Fig. 4G). Taken together, all these studies strongly support a pivotal role for lnc122 RNA in regulating MYC protein stability through intensifying the UBR5 and MYC interaction and polyubiquitination of the complex. Moreover, we analyzed RNA-seq data from control and lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells to examine the expression of previously reported MYC E3 ubiquitin ligases, including UBR5. However, we did not identify any known MYC E3 ligases whose expression was downregulated upon lnc122 suppression, including UBR5. These findings suggest that lnc122 does not regulate MYC stability indirectly through modulation of MYC E3 ligase expression (Fig. S3D).

Finally, because the mouse and human lnc122 RNAs are not well conserved at the sequence level, we wanted to establish if they would function across species. Murine lnc122 RNA overexpression in Huh7 cells did not affect MYC mRNA levels (Fig. S3E) but destabilized MYC protein during cycloheximide treatment (Fig. 2E). Further, we also confirmed the physical interaction of mouse lnc122 RNA with MYC in human cells when murine lnc122 RNA was overexpressed in Huh7 cells (Fig. S3F). These data suggest that the lnc122-mediated molecular pathway of cell growth suppression was conserved in humans and mice, despite the lack of sequence conservation. Thus, the human and mouse lnc122 RNA’s ability to both physically and functionally interact with UBR5 suggests their commonality in structure rather than sequence is more important in its biological role in regulating MYC protein levels.

lnc122 inhibition exacerbates MYC-driven liver tumors in vivo

As MYC is known as a critical driver of cancer, we set up a MYC-driven liver tumor model in mice using the Sleeping-Beauty transposon (SB), to overexpress MYC after in vivo transfection and integration into the host genome.23 Because hydrodynamic injection of these plasmids results in integrated expression cassettes in about 15% of hepatocytes in the liver30 and AAV8 transduces over 90% of hepatocytes in vivo,31 we first injected AAV-CRISPRi or AAV-CRISPRa to inhibit or activate the lnc122 expression, respectively, and then injected the transposon plasmids, T3-MYC and SB13 1 week later (Fig. 5A). We measured the total tumor volume for each animal. AAV-CRISPRi_lnc122 suppressed both lnc122 and miR-122 by >80% in mouse livers (Fig. S4A) and led to accelerated tumor formation (Fig. 5B–D, p = 0.0023). Alternatively, activation of lnc122 expression by AAV-CRISPRa_lnc122 repressed liver tumor formation, which could also be explained by an increase in miR-122 (Fig. 5E–G and Fig. S4B, p <0.0001). To distinguish the role of lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs in vivo tumorigenesis, we co-injected AAV-CRISPRi_lnc122 and AAV-miR-122. The AAV-miR-122 construct contained ∼363 bp of genomic DNA encompassing the mature miR-122 RNA sequence allowing preservation of the endogenous processing of the pre-miRNA. This expression construct, allowed for the ability to produce murine livers that were lnc122(-)/miR-122(+) for induced liver tumorigenesis using the MYC transposon. Liver tumor formation was more prevalent in the lnc122-/miR-122+ liver group compared to liver injected with AAV-CRISPRi_SCR despite complementation of miR-122 function, indicating that lnc122 RNA had its own tumor suppressive role independent of miR-122 in vivo (Fig. 5H–J and Fig. S4C, p = 0.0239). Despite the marked induction in overall tumor number and size in sglnc122 + miR-122 rescue mice compared with sgSCR controls, immunohistochemical staining of established tumors revealed no substantial differences in MYC, Ki67, or cleaved caspase-3 levels between the two groups (Fig. S4E). In this model, MYC expression is stably maintained through SB–mediated genomic integration, whereas lnc122 suppression and miR-122 restoration which were achieved via AAV delivery, is temporary as the episomal AAV genomes are lost over time in proliferating tumor cells. Consistent with this, tumors that formed in both groups exhibited comparable expression of proliferation and apoptosis markers.

We were next interested in lnc122 RNA levels in human liver cancers, We directly analyzed patient-paired liver-normal/tumor samples using qRT-PCR and found lnc122, and miR-122 RNA expression was reduced in liver cancer compared to the paired normal liver tissue from individual patients (Fig. 5K and Fig. S4D). Moreover, using the rRNA-depleted RNA-seq data from GSE77509,32 which comprises paired primary HCC tumors and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues, we found that lnc122 expression was significantly reduced in human HCC (Fig. 5L), accompanied by increased MYC transcriptional activity (Fig. 5M,N and Fig. S4F). It is plausible that reduced expression from the lnc122 locus participates in the development of liver cancer in humans.

lnc122 RNA is functional in non-hepatic cells

As lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs are hepatic-cell specific, we examined if lnc122 RNA has an antiproliferative function in non-hepatic cells. First, we measured expression of MYC and UBR5 mRNA and protein in several different cell lines including HeLa, A549, MCF-7, and HEK293T (Fig. S5A and B). Among them HeLa cells showed the most abundant expression of MYC mRNA and protein (Fig. S5A and B). When human wtlnc122, human smlnc122, or human pAlnc122 RNAs were ectopically expressed in HeLa cells, all lnc122 expressing constructs had an antiproliferative function and similar MYC mRNA levels (Fig. 6A,B). As with the hepatoma cells, lnc122 overexpression led to diminished MYC protein levels in HeLa cells (Fig. 6C). The ability of lnc122 RNA to decrease MYC protein concentrations was abolished when UBR5 expression was suppressed by siUBR5 (Fig. 6D). We also observed that ectopic expression of lnc122 enhanced the interaction between endogenous UBR5 and MYC, resulting in accelerated endogenous MYC polyubiquitination in HeLa cells (Fig. 6E). As in the hepatoma cells, UBR5 and MYC had a physical interaction in HeLa cells in the absence of lnc122 RNA and this interaction was reduced with RNase treatment (Fig. 6F,G) suggesting the RNAs other than lnc122 may exist in other cells to promote the UBR5-MYC protein interaction. Collectively, ectopic expression of lnc122 in non-hepatic cells has an antitumor effect and finding other RNAs that have a similar ability to facilitate UBR5-mediated MYC protein degradation requires further investigation.

Discussion

lnc122 RNA transcription and processing results in high level concentrations of miR-122 but no function to this primary RNA had been revealed to-date.13 Here we provide evidence that lnc122 RNA facilitates the UBR5-MYC protein interactions to modulate MYC protein levels in the liver (Fig. S5C). Although lnc122 RNA provided a similar function when ectopically expressed in non-hepatic cells, this suggests there may be other long non-coding RNAs that have a similar function in controlling MYC protein levels in non-hepatic cells.

There are other post-translational modifications of MYC protein, including phosphorylation and ubiquitination that have been extensively investigated because of the protein’s short half-life. Specifically, various E3 ubiquitin ligases for MYC have been validated. FBXW7, SCFFBXL14, UBR5, and SCFSPOP are associated with MYC protein degradation, whereas HUWE1, RNF4, and FBXL16 stabilize or activate MYC protein by ubiquitination.33,34 Alterations in the expression or function of MYC E3 ubiquitin ligases for MYC protein degradation have been reported in various types of cancer. For example, mutations or loss of function of FBXW7 are frequently found in T-cell lymphomas, gastric cancer, and other malignancies, resulting in the accumulation of MYC protein and promoting oncogenesis.35,36 Similarly, decreased expression of SCFFBXL14 has been associated with poor prognosis in glioma stem cells37 and the SCFSPOP loss of function mutation has been found in >15% of prostate cancers.38,39

Unlike these MYC E3 ubiquitin ligases, UBR5 was recently identified through CRISPR screening and is in close proximity to MYC in the human genome at chromosome 8q24 and 8q22, respectively, and co-amplified with MYC in >40% of all cancer cells regardless of type. TCGA data analysis demonstrates that MYC expression levels significantly correlate with UBR5 levels.28,40 As such, one might expect high UBR5 protein levels to have an antitumor effect, as UBR5 degrades MYC, yet this does not appear to be the case in MYC-driven cancers.28,40 Here, we suggest that lnc122 RNA, and potentially other factors, are necessary for UBR5-dependent MYC destabilization and may even represent a rate-limiting component in maintaining MYC protein levels during cellular homeostasis. This model may also explain why genomic co-amplification of UBR5 and MYC fails to arrest tumor cell proliferation, as UBR5-mediated MYC degradation appears to require lnc122 RNA as an essential cofactor. More broadly, UBR5 remains a molecule of considerable debate, alternately described as a tumor suppressor or oncogene depending on context.[41], [42], [43], [44], [45] As an E3 ubiquitin ligase, its biological impact is likely dictated by substrate selectivity within a given cellular environment—and among its substrates, MYC is one of the most well-characterized. How UBR5 selectively recognizes MYC, distinguishes among multiple substrates, and how these interactions shape tumor phenotypes are critical questions that warrant further investigation. Nevertheless, our findings provide a mechanistic basis for this paradox and identify a potential therapeutic axis applicable across diverse MYC dysregulation mechanisms—whether driven by copy number amplification, RNA stability, super enhancer activation, or protein stabilization.46 Given that MYC dysregulation occurs in at least 30% of liver cancers and up to 70% of all human cancers,[46], [47], [48] lnc122-based strategies may offer broad anticancer applicability.

Previous reports describing lnc122 gene transcriptional termination,13 showed most of the lnc122 transcripts in human Huh7 hepatoma cells were poly(A)-. When we evaluated our genetically modified cells, we found for the unspliced form, more poly(A)- compared to poly(A)+ lnc122 RNA was detected. However, when studying both the spliced and unspliced forms or total lnc122 RNA transcripts, we found similar amounts of poly(A)+ and poly(A)- lnc122 species because of the instability of the unspliced lnc122 RNA (Fig. S3C). Similarly, the mouse lnc122 also showed relatively similar amounts of poly(A)+ and poly(A)- lnc122 transcripts (Fig. S2D). While our studies suggest multiple forms of the lnc122 RNA can interact and affect MYC protein concentrations, the importance of the various poly(A)+, poly(A)-, spliced and unspliced forms in regulating MYC, miR-122 or lnc122 RNA concentrations or perhaps other functions is not yet clear. Notably, the mouse lnc122 transcript lacks intronic sequences, whereas the human lnc122 contains introns. Apart from the pre-miR-122 region, primary sequence conservation between mouse and human lnc122 is minimal. However, limited sequence similarity does not preclude functional conservation in lncRNAs. For example, XIST, HOTAIR, and NEAT1 retain conserved biological functions across species despite substantial sequence divergence.49 Consistent with this paradigm, we demonstrate that both mouse and human lnc122 interact with MYC and promote MYC protein degradation, indicating that its functional role in MYC destabilization is evolutionarily conserved despite low primary sequence conservation.

It is noteworthy that lnc122 expression shows diurnal fluctuation in day/night cycle whereas miR-122 concentrations are unchanged because of its long stability.50 The half-life of lnc122 is around 2 h and miR-122 is over 1 week.9,13 Because the RNA-seq data in lnc122-/miR-122+ Huh7 cells (Fig. 2A) showed changes in the expression of genes in metabolic pathways including fatty acid/cholesterol metabolism, which also has been extensively studied in cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and prognosis,[51], [52], [53] it is plausible that lnc122 RNA might play a direct role independent of those regulated by miR-122 in lipid/cholesterol metabolism. This would be consistent with the circadian pattern of lipid/cholesterol metabolic gene regulation.54

During oncogenic conversion of hepatocytes, there is a gradual loss of homeostatic cellular states including a reduction in the expression of the HNF transcription factor family, morphological changes, as well as increased expression of liver cancer stem cell genes including EpCAM, CD90, and CD133.55 HNF4a is a major regulator of hepatic genes including lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs and its expression is steadily lost during the development of HCC. This is associated with an increase in lnc122 promoter DNA methylation, likely further reducing lnc122 and miR-122 RNA concentrations.56 Additionally, miR-122 levels slowly increase during normal mouse liver development and reach their highest expression in adults.57 Although miR-122 and lnc122 RNAs appear to regulate cell proliferation through independent molecular pathways, it is becoming apparent that maintaining high levels of hepatic lnc122 and miR-122 RNAs are critical for suppression of uncontrolled growth and cancer generation in the liver. In total, we have shown that lnc122 and miR-122 levels are decreased in paired liver cancer samples, and it can be easily assumed that decreased lnc122 RNA leads to stabilization of MYC protein resulting in acceleration of tumorigenesis. Precise regulatory circuits that control growth are likely especially important in a highly regenerative organ such as the liver. At this time, it is not yet clear if lnc122 RNA serves as a physical conduit by binding both UBR5 and MYC proteins or acts to alter the structure of one of these proteins allowing for enhanced functional interactions between these two proteins. It is also possible that additional proteins are involved in the interactions (see model in Fig. S5C).

Finally, lnc122 RNA may offer a class of new anticancer agents in MYC-driven tumors. The identification of the minimal structural components of lnc122 may result in the design short lnc122 RNA mimics (aptamers or antisense oligonucleotides) or even small molecules that can bind to MYC and promote its interaction with UBR5 and ultimate enhance degradation of the MYC protein in cancer.
