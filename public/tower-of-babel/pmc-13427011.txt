ABSTRACT
Optimum intake of calcium is fundamental for skeletal health and additionally plays a role in cardiovascular health, haemostasis, synaptic transmission, and several enzyme-driven metabolic reactions. In several countries, including India, dietary calcium intake is poor, and it is necessary to ensure adequate calcium intake for optimizing musculoskeletal health. Not many guidelines focus on regions where calcium in the diet is suboptimal, and supplementing calcium may be specially relevant. In view of this, the Endocrine Society of India (ESI) Skeletal Task Force has developed clinical practice guidelines on calcium supplementation. The statement emphasizes that food sources should be the preferred modality of meeting calcium needs, and supplements should be used when dietary intake is insufficient or when physiological demands are higher, such as in pregnancy, lactation, or after menopause. Additionally, calcium should be supplemented in bone mineral disorders, such as osteoporosis, osteomalacia, hypocalcaemic rickets, and hypoparathyroidism. Special precautions are necessary in chronic kidney disease and in the presence of renal stone disease, but safety in cardiovascular disease is reinforced unless the total intake crosses the recommended daily intake. The statement recommends ensuring vitamin D sufficiency but strongly recommends against supplementing activated forms of vitamin D except in special situations. This ESI statement aims to provide rationalized guidance to ensure adequate calcium supplementation while avoiding the risks associated with injudicious use.

INTRODUCTION

Calcium is essential for numerous bodily functions beyond the skeletal system. Low consumption of calcium-rich foods can result in inadequate calcium intake, necessitating supplementation. This is particularly relevant for middle and lower-income countries, including India, where the dietary calcium intake is suboptimal in more than 50% of the population.[1] The ESI Skeletal Health Taskforce, therefore, presents an evidence-based consensus statement on calcium supplementation (refers to adding calcium preparations orally) in the Indian context.

CALCIUM HOMEOSTASIS

The adult human body contains about 1 kg of calcium, with 99% stored in bones and teeth, and the remaining 1% present in blood, extracellular fluid, and soft tissues. In plasma, approximately 47% of calcium is ionized, 8–10% is bound to organic and inorganic acids, and around 40% is attached to proteins. Calcium homeostasis is maintained through the coordinated actions of the intestines, bones, and kidneys, and is regulated by hormones, including 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitonin, and fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23). PTH raises serum calcium by mobilizing calcium from the bone through receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) ligand-mediated osteoclast activation and by increasing renal calcium reabsorption in the distal tubule. At the same time, it induces phosphaturia by reducing proximal tubular phosphate reabsorption, which prevents calcium–phosphate precipitation and helps maintain ionized calcium, while also supporting magnesium reabsorption.[2] Additionally, PTH stimulates renal 1-α hydroxylase, to increase calcitriol formation, which in turn raises intestinal absorption of both calcium and phosphate. Although phosphate absorption increases, the phosphaturic action of PTH offsets this rise.[3] Other factors, such as sex hormones, glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, and pH variations, also modulate calcium levels. Calcitonin, produced by thyroid C cells, reduces blood calcium by inhibiting osteoclast activity. This complex system keeps serum calcium levels within the normal range.

Physiological role of calcium

Calcium is fundamental to bone health, as it forms hydroxyapatite, the mineral complex that provides skeletal strength and rigidity. It supports bone remodelling, where osteoblasts deposit calcium and osteoclasts reabsorb it, maintaining bone density and preventing conditions like osteoporosis and fractures, especially in aging populations. Calcium is also essential for muscle contraction, nerve impulse transmission, and blood clotting. It acts as a second messenger in cellular signalling, regulating hormone release, enzyme activity, and gene expression. Apart from its primary role in preserving musculoskeletal health, calcium supplementation has been proposed to decrease premenstrual symptoms, blood pressure, pre-eclampsia, and recurrent colorectal adenomas.[4] A systematic review demonstrated that maternal intake of calcium resulted in a reduction of 1.92 mm Hg in systolic blood pressure at age one to nine years in the offspring.[5] Low calcium intake can paradoxically raise intracellular calcium in vascular smooth muscle, promoting sustained vasoconstriction, thereby increasing peripheral resistance. Over time, this contributes to vascular remodelling and elevates the risk of hypertension and myocardial infarction (MI) in adults.[6]

Recommended daily intake of calcium

Recommendation 1

We advise that daily calcium intake follow the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) across all age groups.

Recommendation 2

We suggest assessing calcium intake through diet recall and encourage the consumption of calcium-rich foods, particularly dairy products, to meet the RDA.

The RDA of calcium across different age groups is shown in Table 1. The Indian Council of Medical Research–National Institute of Nutrition 2020 guideline emphasizes that the Indian calcium intake pattern is distinct. Low habitual calcium intake (300–500 mg per day), limited dietary diversity due to socioeconomic constraints, low dairy consumption, and a higher intake of cereals, particularly among rural communities, contribute to this. Associated widespread vitamin D deficiency may further lower the calcium absorption.[78] To enhance dietary calcium intake, we recommend consumption of widely available and affordable calcium-rich foods. These include milk, milk-based products (such as yogurt and cheese), green leafy vegetables (drumstick leaves, curry leaves, amaranth leaves, broccoli), pulses (horse gram, bengal gram, rajma, and soyabean), seeds (gingelly seeds), and millets (ragi and bajra).[7] The main sources of calcium in the Indian diet are milk and milk-based products, with 200 mL of cow milk providing approximately 250 mg of elemental calcium. Ragi is a rich source of calcium, providing 344 mg in every 100 gms and can be a useful source for those who do not consume dairy products. However, like with most grains, the presence of phytates can interfere with calcium absorption. This interference can be partially overcome by consuming sprouted ragi.[9]

Indications for calcium supplementation in physiological and diseased states

Recommendations 3

If dietary intake cannot meet the RDA, we recommend calcium supplementation in conditions where physiological demand is high, such as pregnancy, lactation, and after menopause. We also encourage supplementation whenever the RDA of calcium cannot be met by diet alone.

Recommendation 4

We recommend supplementation in pathological conditions that require optimal calcium intake, including osteoporosis, osteomalacia, rickets, and hypoparathyroidism.

Recommendation 5

We recommend supplementation in conditions that limit dietary calcium intake, such as lactose intolerance, or disorders that reduce calcium absorption, such as malabsorption syndromes.

The indications for adding calcium in tablet form include physiological or pathological conditions of increased demand where dietary intake alone is unlikely to fulfil the daily requirement. These include physiological conditions, such as pregnancy and lactation. Pathological conditions, such as osteoporosis, osteomalacia, hypocalcaemic rickets, and hypoparathyroidism, warrant long-term calcium supplementation.[10]

Type of calcium preparation

Recommendation 6

The selection of an appropriate calcium preparation should take into account its bioavailability, optimal timing of administration, the clinical context, associated comorbidities, and cost considerations. Calcium carbonate and calcium citrate are the two most commonly used preparations when pharmacological supplementation is necessary

Recommendation 7

We recommend calcium citrate tablets for individuals on proton-pump inhibitors or with achlorhydria and in patients with renal stone disease. Calcium citrate can be taken irrespective of meals, although multiple tablets are often needed to meet daily requirements.

Recommendation 8

We do not recommend calcium supplements containing other salts due to higher cost, the need for multiple doses (in the absence of slow-release preparations), and the absence of evidence supporting their routine use.

Calcium intake recommendations pertain to the milligrams of elemental calcium present in the supplement. Calcium carbonate comprises 40% elemental calcium by weight, while calcium citrate includes 21%. The prescriber or consumer should review the supplement facts panel for the elemental calcium content to ensure optimal supplementation.

The most commonly available calcium salts are shown in Table 2. The two most common forms are calcium carbonate and calcium citrate. The percentage of calcium absorbed depends on the total amount of elemental calcium consumed at one time[11]; with maximum absorption occurring at 500 mg or less. Gastrointestinal side effects, such as bloating or constipation, may occur; these can often be mitigated by switching formulations, dividing doses throughout the day, or taking the tablets with meals.[12]

Associated vitamin D supplementation

Recommendation 9

We recommend correcting associated vitamin D deficiency to ensure optimal calcium absorption. We advise against injectable vitamin D unless a malabsorptive disorder is present.

Recommendation 10

We recommend maintaining a daily vitamin D intake of 1000–2000 units through daily or 60,000 units monthly supplementation.

Recommendation 11

We recommend against using calcium supplements that contain active forms of vitamin D, such as calcitriol or alphacalcidol, except in chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypoparathyroidism, or genetic disorders like vitamin D-dependent rickets.

The administration of moderate daily doses of vitamin D and calcium to elderly nursing home residents with dual deficiencies significantly decreased their risk of hip fractures and all non-vertebral fractures over an 18-month duration.[13] In a community-dwelling elderly cohort with borderline levels of vitamin D and calcium, dual treatment resulted in moderate enhancements in bone mineral density (BMD) and a reduction in fractures. Administration of high levels of vitamin D in those who are vitamin D sufficient has not been shown to reduce fracture risk. Growing evidence indicates that both insufficient consumption of vitamin D and calcium, as well as excessive intake of vitamin D, adversely affect the risk of falls and fractures. Data indicate that a 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] range from 20 to 40 ng/mL is correlated with the lowest risk of falls and fractures.[14] A major portion of the Indian older adult population is deficient in both calcium and vitamin D, with calcium intakes below recommended levels and serum 25(OH)D levels below the optimum threshold. An estimated 46.5% of available Indian calcium preparations contain calcitriol, but their use should be limited to specific clinical scenarios such as CKD, hypoparathyroidism, pseudohypoparathyroidism, and vitamin D-dependent rickets. Calcitriol supplementation is associated with a higher risk of hypercalciuria and renal stone formation.[15]

Calcium supplementation in special situations

Recommendation 12

Cardiovascular disorders (CVDs): Individuals with coronary artery disease or other forms of CVD should be encouraged to maintain calcium intake at the RDA through dietary or additional supplementation if necessary. Intake above 2000 mg per day should be avoided as higher doses may increase the risk of coronary and vascular calcification.

Current evidence on calcium supplements and CVD is mixed. Observational studies raise a possible association of high-dose supplements (not dietary calcium) with coronary calcification and MI, but randomized trials and meta-analyses have not definitively confirmed a harmful effect. Most analyses find that dietary calcium intake is neutral or protective for heart health, whereas calcium pills (especially without vitamin D) may carry a slightly higher risk when taken in large amounts over the years. Mechanistically, dietary calcium is absorbed more gradually and accompanied by other nutrients, whereas calcium pills cause transient serum spikes. Such spikes could promote vascular calcification.[16] A recent meta-analysis reported that calcium alone at a mean dose of 1 g/day was not associated with risk of MI, cardiac heart disease or death due to cardiac heart disease, and stroke.[17] Two older meta-analyses showed a modest increase in MI risk with calcium alone compared to placebo, but the studies had heterogeneous outcomes in terms of CVD data.[1819] Of note, among those already consuming adequate dietary calcium, adding supplements increased the heart attack risk, while in those with lower baseline dietary calcium intake, the risk was not increased.[18] Major cardiovascular and bone-health guidelines therefore recommend obtaining calcium from food and keeping supplements within recommended limits (<1,000 mg/day total).[20]

Recommendation 13

Renal stone disease: We suggest maintaining the RDA of calcium through dietary sources in individuals with active or past renal stone disease. If supplementation is required to achieve a total intake of 1000–1200 mg/day, administer calcium supplements with meals and monitor for stone recurrence. In individuals with enteric hyperoxaluria, calcium supplementation with meals is recommended to help prevent stone formation.

Balanced dietary intake of calcium prevents the formation of oxalate renal calculi by inhibiting intestinal oxalate absorption.[21] There is also an increased risk of renal stones with excessive supplemental calcium but not dietary calcium.[22] The risk is higher in individuals with a personal or family history of kidney stones or hypercalciuria. Consuming calcium supplements alongside meals and optimizing magnesium intake can help mitigate the risks.[23] Citrate salts are widely used in the management of nephrolithiasis, owing to their inhibitory effect on kidney stone formation, particularly calcium oxalate stones, which account for 70–80% of all renal calculi. Calcium citrate may therefore confer an added therapeutic benefit beyond calcium supplementation alone in renal stone disease.[24]

Recommendation 14

CKD: We recommend a daily calcium intake of 800–1000 mg in individuals with CKD to maintain calcium in the normal reference range. We suggest optimizing calcium intake primarily through diet, while balancing phosphate restrictions. For individuals requiring a phosphate binder with low dietary calcium, a calcium-based binder, such as calcium acetate, may be preferred.

As the kidney disease progresses, the ability of the kidney to regulate urine calcium excretion is lost, thereby eliminating a crucial safety mechanism preventing calcium overload. 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D levels decline with progressive CKD, affecting intestinal calcium absorption. A neutral calcium balance should be targeted, as calcium deficiency may result in significant bone loss and secondary hyperparathyroidism, whereas calcium excess could promote vascular and soft tissue calcification. Studies indicate that a neutral calcium balance is maintained when consuming 800–1000 mg/day. There is no evidence to suggest that reducing dietary calcium intake is beneficial and may cause harm by inducing secondary hyperparathyroidism and impairing bone health. If calcium intake is insufficient, the aim should be to maintain an intake of 800–1000 mg/day through calcium-rich foods, supplements and calcium-based phosphate binders.[2526] Dietary calcium is generally safer and better regulated. Supplemental calcium results in higher acute intestinal absorption and serum calcium spikes, posing greater calcification risk.[27]

Calcium intake during osteoporosis treatment

Recommendation 15

Calcium and vitamin D should be used as adjuncts to osteoporosis therapies

Most guidelines recommend supplementing calcium and vitamin D during osteoporosis treatment. Adequate calcium and vitamin D help maintain normal bone turnover and prevent secondary hyperparathyroidism, which can reduce the effectiveness of both antiresorptive and anabolic therapies. This is particularly relevant for agents, such as denosumab, where low calcium stores could precipitate hypocalcaemia, and for anabolic drugs, where calcium and vitamin D deficiency would blunt the expected BMD gains. In clinical trials, the amount of calcium supplementation has typically ranged from 500 to 1500 mg/d. Supplementation should be used to ensure that the individual’s total calcium intake from diet and supplements meets the RDA and should not exceed 1000 mg/day.[20] Similarly, vitamin D sufficiency should be targeted, In patients receiving treatment for osteoporosis, there is general consensus among expert groups about the optimum 25(OH)D level being >20 ng/mL, with some suggesting a level of >30 ng/mL.[2028] We recommend maintaining serum 25(OH)D levels between 20 and 40 ng/mL, which is achieved with approximately 2000 IU/day of vitamin D in most Indian adults.

Calcium intake and drug interaction

Recommendation 16

Several medications can cause hypocalcaemia, and calcium tablets can impair absorption of agents, such as quinolones, tetracyclines, iron, dolutegravir, and levothyroxine, which should be appropriately time-separated when co-administered.

Drug-induced hypocalcaemia can occur with drugs, such as bisphosphonates, cisplatin, and some of the anti-epileptics.[29] The common drugs causing hypocalcaemia are shown in Table 3. However, calcium tablets can interfere with the absorption of certain drugs. For example, quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics, iron supplements, and dolutegravir should be taken 2 hours before or 6 hours after the calcium tablet, while the interval between levothyroxine and calcium supplementation should be at least 4 hours.[30]

CONCLUSION

Calcium is a key nutrient for bone strength and for several other physiological functions. In India, where many people consume far less calcium than recommended and vitamin D deficiency is common, clear guidance on when and how to supplement is important. This statement emphasizes that food sources should remain the first option, and supplements be used when dietary intake is insufficient or when physiological demands are higher, such as in pregnancy, lactation, or specific disorders. Calcium carbonate is most widely used, while citrate is useful in the presence of renal stone disease or conditions that impair gastric acidity. Concurrent vitamin D sufficiency is necessary to ensure effective absorption. In special situations, including cardiovascular disease, renal stone disease, and CKD, supplementation must be individualized to avoid excessive intake. During osteoporosis therapy, calcium and vitamin D serve as necessary adjuncts to maintain treatment efficacy. Attention to drug interactions and appropriate timing of doses supports safe and effective use. These recommendations aim to guide safe and practical use of calcium in routine care.
