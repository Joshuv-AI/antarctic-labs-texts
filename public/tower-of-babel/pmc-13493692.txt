Integrating genomic selection into potato breeding: A comparison of genotyping platforms and cross‐environmental predictions
Authors: Rakshya Dhakal, Marco Antonio Peixoto, Leo Hoffmann, Bonny Michael Oloka, Mark Clough, Gabriel de Siqueira Gesteira, Lincoln Zotarelli, Jeffrey B Endelman, G Craig Yencho, Marcio F R Resende
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic selection (GS) is a powerful tool for accelerating genetic gain in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) breeding, particularly for complex traits. In this study, three practical aspects of GS implementation in a potato breeding program were examined. First, the predictive ability of GS models was evaluated for three key traits (total yield, marketable yield, and specific gravity) using two elite potato populations with shared ancestry, tested across seven location‐year environments. Two cross‐validation strategies were used to reflect practical breeding scenarios: predicting unphenotyped lines in known environments and predicting clonal performance in unknown environments. Four models were evaluated, two of which included genotype‐by‐environment interactions. Tuber specific gravity showed higher and more consistent prediction accuracy across environments, supporting the evidence that it is a more stable trait. Second, the impact of genotyping platforms and marker density on GS performance were examined, as the two populations were genotyped using two different targeted sequencing platforms: Flex‐seq (22K loci) and DArTag (4K loci), sharing ∼4K common loci, that allowed direct comparison. Prediction accuracies were comparable across platforms, indicating that both are suitable for GS implementation, with the choice depending on breeding goals, cost, and throughput considerations. Finally, the long‐term impact of GS on genetic gain was assessed through stochastic simulation of a 30‐year breeding pipeline, comparing conventional phenotypic selection with GS‐assisted selection scenarios. GS scenarios achieved higher long‐term genetic gains, though practical deployment should consider both cost and breeding objectives. Our findings for the three aspects of this study support the integration of GS into potato breeding programs, while highlighting key considerations for its effective implementation.

INTRODUCTION

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is one of the most important staple crops worldwide, valued for its high yield potential (Haverkort & Struik, 2015), nutritional significance, and critical role in ensuring future food security (Devaux et al., 2014). Despite its importance and decades of breeding efforts, genetic gains for yield and other complex traits have progressed slowly, and many older varieties continue to dominate commercial production (Bachem et al., 2019; Douches et al., 1996). To meet year‐round consumer demand, the US potato market relies heavily on production in subtropical southeastern and mid‐Atlantic states, such as FL and NC. However, potato production in these environments presents substantial challenges, particularly high temperatures and humidity, which accelerates critical growth phases, disrupts assimilate partitioning, and hinders tuber initiation and bulking, ultimately limiting yield potential (Gautam et al., 2024). In addition, the dominant cultivars produced in these regions were largely developed in northern environments under different climatic conditions and are often poorly adapted to the stresses imposed by current and emerging environmental conditions present in the southern regions of the United States. As a result, there is an urgent need to develop climate‐resilient, heat‐tolerant potato varieties capable of thriving not only in traditional temperate regions but also in increasingly warmer climates. Added to this complexity is the biology of the crop itself. Cultivated potato is a highly heterozygous autotetraploid (2n = 4x = 48) crop that suffers severe inbreeding depression (Aalborg & Nielsen, 2024; Ortiz & Mihovilovich, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019). These characteristics, among others, have negatively impacted breeding efforts and have made genetic improvement through conventional methods especially difficult. Breeding approaches that integrate modern tools and account for genotype‐by‐environment (G × E) interactions may be an important approach to support the development of heat‐tolerant potato varieties with stable performance across diverse conditions, providing a practical solution to sustain and expand production. In this context, genomic selection (GS) offers a solution by enabling the early and accurate identification of superior genotypes, making it a valuable tool for enhancing breeding efficiency in potato.

GS utilizes genome‐wide markers to capture the effects of loci across the genome, enabling more accurate selection, especially for complex traits that are difficult to measure in the early stages of breeding (Meuwissen et al., 2001). By identifying promising genotypes early, GS allows breeders to shorten the generation interval and guide crossing decisions. It has now become an integral part of many modern breeding programs and is being widely adopted in many major crops (Merrick et al., 2022; Peixoto, Leach, et al., 2024; Werner et al., 2023). However, GS remains relatively underutilized in potato breeding. Breeding for a temperate crop like potato in subtropical environments presents unique challenges, as most elite varieties and genotypes have been developed focusing on temperate adaptation, where the majority of production occurs. When these genotypes are tested in subtropical states, G × E interactions can be more pronounced not solely due to environmental stress but because the genetic material is not well‐adapted to the target environment, amplifying the interaction effects. The effectiveness of GS in stressed environments depends on the genetic architecture of the trait and breeding populations, as well as the ability of the models to capture G × E interactions. These G × E interactions significantly influence trait performance (Bakare et al., 2022; de Leon et al., 2016; Elmerich et al., 2023), and therefore should be accounted for in prediction models. In this study, we investigate the application of GS in potato breeding, considering the G × E component and integrating environmental covariates (ECs) such as temperature, rainfall, and solar radiation. Publicly available weather databases provide these covariates, allowing models to characterize G × E components more effectively and enhance their stability across growing environments (X. Wang et al., 2018).

While GS offers significant potential, breeders must balance its implementation with cost considerations, particularly in resource‐limited breeding programs. Genotyping costs vary by platform, and although advancements in sequencing technologies have reduced these costs (T. Liu et al., 2020), resource optimization is a constant need. In addition to cost, factors such as data turnaround time, bioinformatics services, reproducibility, the number of yielded DNA markers, and DNA sample requirements influence the choice of platform (Rasheed et al., 2017; Sipowicz et al., 2025). Optimal technology depends on program goals and is not always straightforward. Commonly used platforms in potato breeding include the Infinium single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array and DArTag. This study utilizes a new, recently developed panel, namely, the potato Flex‐seq panel, which was designed for compatibility with the Infinium SNP array. It features 22K probes that optimize genome coverage while targeting key traits and have not been tested before for potato. In this study, we utilize the two genotyping platforms, namely, DArTag and Flex‐seq, to compare the prediction accuracies.

With GS being an integral component of modern breeding programs, several tools and strategies have been developed and implemented to enhance selection efficiency and accelerate genetic gain (Crain et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2021; S. Wang et al., 2019). Breeding programs have begun to adopt new strategies through simulations to compare alternative breeding strategies and evaluate potential outcomes before practical implementation, as reported in previous studies (Peixoto, Coelho et al., 2024; Sabadin et al., 2022; Tessema et al., 2020). Compared with field validation, computer simulations provide a cost‐effective and time‐efficient approach to assess breeding strategies by enabling replicated experiments across diverse scenarios, allowing breeders to evaluate potential genetic gains, risks, and trade‐offs associated with different decisions before committing substantial resources to long‐term field evaluations (Hassanpour et al., 2025; Katiyar et al., 2025). Using stochastic simulation with genomic prediction implemented explicitly, we compare conventional phenotypic selection against GS applied at different genotyping intensities and assess the resulting genetic gains, prediction accuracies, and trade‐offs in genetic diversity achievable through early GS implementation.

In this study, the use of GS in potato breeding was explored in the context of subtropical heat‐prone production environments, with particular attention to G × E interactions, genotyping platform choice, and the long‐term value of early GS implementation. Several GS models were evaluated under different training and validation strategies to enhance prediction accuracy across multiple traits and environments. The impact of genotyping platforms on genomic predictions was also explored. In addition, the breeding program was simulated to assess genetic gain over time with GS. These efforts bridge advanced genomic tools with practical breeding objectives, advancing potato breeding for hot and stress‐prone environments. The specific objectives of this study were to (1) estimate the prediction accuracy of three important traits in elite breeding lines using GS models and different cross‐validation (CV) schemes; (2) compare the prediction accuracies from two genotyping platforms to obtain insights into cost‐effective GS implementation in breeding programs; (3) understand the genetic performance of key traits and use their stability patterns to guide breeding strategies; and (4) investigate the benefits of early deployment of GS in potato breeding programs in terms of genetic gains.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and experimental design

The population used in this study consisted of 611 elite varieties developed through a collaborative effort between the potato breeding programs at North Carolina State University and the University of Florida (UF). Two related breeding populations of autotetraploid chipping clones were evaluated, referred to as Cycle 1 (C1) and Cycle 2 (C2). The C1 population included 212 genotypes, while the C2 population comprised 399 genotypes, totaling 611 genotypes. These populations were not direct parent‐progeny groups but shared ancestry through multiple parental lines. Specifically, three full‐sib families were shared by both populations. In addition, six parents were shared between C1 and C2, resulting in six half‐sibling families between the two populations.

Field evaluations were conducted at three sites: Hastings, FL; Tidewater Research Station, NC (TRS); and Black Gold Farm, NC (BG), over a 2‐year period (2023 and 2024). The different trials were labeled as a unique combination of location, population, and year. For example, the C1 population planted in FL in 2023 is referred to as FL‐C123. In total, seven environments were evaluated, with two corresponding to the C1 population and five to the C2 population (Table 1).

The experimental design for the trials in NC (TRS and BG) was an unreplicated row and column design with an augmented representation of checks. The cultivars Atlantic and Snowden were used as checks in the 2024 trials, while only Atlantic was used as a check in 2023. The trials in FL were also unreplicated, with the Atlantic cultivar interspersed regularly across the field as a check to account for spatial heterogeneity and to enable performance comparison. Plots consisted of 8 hills without replication, consistent with a preliminary yield trial design, as seed tuber availability is limited at this stage.

All genotypes were tested for three traits: total yield (TYLD; Mg ha−1), marketable yield (MYLD; Mg ha−1), and tuber specific gravity (SG; unitless). The tubers with defects or culls (heat sprouts; chain tubers; knobs; including irregular shapes and secondary growths; growth cracks; green heads; soft rots; insect damage; and cut tubers) and small‐sized tubers (grades C and B; < 48 mm) were categorized as non‐marketable tubers, whereas defect‐free tubers of good size (grades A1–A4; > 48 mm) were regarded as marketable tubers. The TYLD trait was a measure of all tubers, including those classified as marketable, culls, and small tubers. The SG of tubers was evaluated by comparing the weight of 10 marketable tubers in air and water using the formula: specific gravity = [weight in air/(weight in air − weight in water)].

Genotypic data

For the genomic analyses, populations C1 and C2 were genotyped using two distinct platforms: Flex‐seq for C1 and DArTag for C2. Leaf tissue collected during the 2023 growing season was used for genotyping on the corresponding platforms.

Flex‐seq genotyping

The C1 population was genotyped using the Flex‐seq panel (https://www.biosearchtech.com/services/genotyping‐services/targeted‐genotyping‐sequencing), a recently developed high‐density panel by the community. This high‐throughput targeted genotyping panel was designed for backwards compatibility with the potato Infinium SNP array. A total of 15K SNPs were selected from the Infinium SNP array based on their ability to discriminate tetraploid allele dosage. An additional 12K SNPs were selected from genic regions to improve uniformity of genome coverage, and finally, 25 markers targeting 12 specific traits were added. Based on results from the pilot experiment with 27K probes and 96 samples, a final set of 22K probes covering 23K target SNPs was selected.

DArTag genotyping

The C2 population was genotyped using the mid‐density Diversity Arrays Technology sequencing (DArTseq) (https://www.diversityarrays.com/services/targeted‐genotying) genotyping platform, with the V2 DArTag panel, consisting of 4K targeted regions distributed across the potato genome. This panel was specifically designed for potato, providing comprehensive genome coverage and integrating markers for important agronomic traits. The genotyping process was conducted at Diversity Arrays Technology Pty Ltd. Details on the panel's development and application are available in (Endelman et al., 2024).

Genotypic data curation

Curation of the genotypic data involved filtering out markers with a minor allele frequency below 5% and markers with missing data of more than 20%. Across all targeted regions, a total of 535K SNPs were initially available from the Flex‐seq platform (including additional SNPs from flanking or nearby regions commonly captured in targeted sequencing), and 3092 SNPs were from the DArTag platform. After filtering, 105K high‐quality SNPs from Flex‐seq and 2910 from DArTag were retained. These curated marker sets were subsequently used to estimate the genomic relationship matrix between the lines, following VanRaden method 1 (VanRaden, 2008), modified to accommodate polyploids, following Endelman (2023). GS models

To evaluate predictive performance, genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) models were implemented under four different modeling frameworks. These included (i) M1, a baseline model including only random additive genetic effects; (ii) M2, a model including random additive genetic effects and fixed environmental effects; (iii) M3, a G × E interaction model including random additive genetic effects, fixed environmental effects, and random G × E interaction effects; and (iv) M4, a genotype‐by‐environmental (G × W) covariate interaction model including random additive genetic effects, fixed environmental effects, and random G × W covariate interaction effects. These models were used to predict three traits, and prediction accuracy was assessed under various CV scenarios that reflect the structure of the breeding program. A broad set of ECs used in M4, characterizing growing conditions for each environment, was derived from daily weather and soil data using the envirotypeR package (Costa‑Neto et al., 2021). For each environment, ECs were summarized using weekly intervals across the growing season to capture temporal dynamics relevant to crop growth and development. Imputation and comparison of the DArTag and Flex‐seq genotyping platforms

To compare prediction accuracy between genotyping platforms of differing marker densities and genome coverage, two different approaches were used: (1) imputing the 4K DArTag platform to the higher‐density Flex‐seq platform using information from Flex‐seq; and (2) extracting the overlapping 4K region from the Flex‐seq panel, allowing a direct comparison of the same 4K regions across both DArTag and Flex‐seq platforms. For the imputation, the “polybreedR” package (Endelman et al., 2024) was used. The imputation requires that at least one observation (one clone in our case) be genotyped twice, once with each platform, to bridge a connection between populations/platforms (for more details, please see [Endelman et al., 2024]). Since no genotypes were shared between the two main populations, an additional set of 162 genotypes that had been genotyped with both DArTag and Flex‐seq platforms was used as a bridging panel. The Flex‐seq data from this additional panel had been previously analyzed and filtered to a total of 18,566 SNPs, one per polymorphic probe. Across these bridging samples, 2905 SNP markers were shared between the two platforms (same physical position in both platforms). These bridge samples were merged with the C2 and C1 populations, which were genotyped using DArTag and Flex‐seq, respectively, providing 162 common genotypes between the datasets. Then, the random forest method was used to impute from the 4K DArTag to the Flex‐seq panel (∼19K polymorphic) using the “impute L2H” function. As a result, genotypic data for the C2 population at ∼19K SNP resolution were obtained as well.

For the second approach, since the two genotyping platforms shared the 4K region, the overlapping regions from the Flex‐seq panel were extracted to enable a direct comparison of prediction accuracy between models using only the shared 4K regions and those using the full set of markers from Flex‐seq. The C1 population, originally genotyped with the Flex‐seq platform, was subsetted to retain only SNPs corresponding to this overlapping 4K region. As a result, genotypic data for the C1 population at 4K SNP resolution were obtained as well.

After quality checks on the SNPs (following the criteria explained in Section 2.2.3), almost 10K SNPs were retained from the ∼19K SNPs and ∼3K from 4K SNPs. Details of this process can be seen in Figure 2.

To evaluate the predictive performance of the models, three different CV schemes were explored, reflecting potential breeding‐related prediction scenarios. These included within‐environment prediction, prediction in untested environments, and prediction under varying marker densities and genotyping platform. Within‐environment and untested‐environment prediction scenarios followed the CV1 and CV0 terminology, respectively, as defined by Jarquín et al. (2017) to distinguish within‐ and across‐environment CV. All prediction accuracies were calculated as the correlation between the estimated breeding values (EBVs) and the BLUEs obtained from the first stage of analysis for each trait. Application of GS in the breeding program

As GS is used in breeding programs to accelerate genetic gain and assist selection decisions, it was hypothesized that its deployment would increase long‐term genetic gain in a potato breeding program. To evaluate this hypothesis, a stochastic simulation framework implemented in AlphaSimR package (Gaynor et al., 2021) was used.

Trait architecture and base population

The simulation was conducted for two quantitative traits, mimicking yield and SG. The initial base population was generated using the runMacs() function with 200 individuals with 12 quadrivalent chromosomes (ploidy equals to 4). The species argument was set to “GENERIC.” Each trait was assigned an independent set of 300 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) per quadrivalent, resulting in a non‐pleiotropic genetic architecture. This choice was supported by the negligible phenotypic correlation between yield and SG in our field trials (r = 0.08), which provided no evidence of shared genetic control between the traits. Traits were modeled as additive using an additive architecture with G × E (the additive‐G × E, AG, model) in AlphaSimR.

The additive genetic mean was set to 32 Mg ha− 1 for yield and 1.050 for SG, and the corresponding additive genetic variances were 5 Mg ha− 1 and 4.36 × 10−5, respectively. Environmental variance (varE) was set to 30 Mg ha− 1 for yield and 4.58 × 10− 5 for SG. G × E interaction variance was set to 1.25 for yield and 4.36 × 10− 5 for SG. These variance values were informed by variance component estimates for FL obtained from a multi‐environment trial dataset such that the simulated phenotypic data reflected levels of experimental variation observed in potato breeding trials in FL.

Phenotype simulation and heritability parameterization

Phenotypic records at the replicated clonal trial stages (clonal evaluations 2 and 3, official trials 1 and 2) were simulated using a constant varE, which was defined once and held fixed across all years. Under this parameterization, heritability decreases naturally over time as genetic variance is depleted through selection, reflecting the expected behavior of a closed breeding program. At the initial field evaluation stage, heritability was set to a fixed per‐plot value, as this unreplicated single‐plant stage is expected to have inherently low phenotypic precision regardless of the population's genetic composition. The specific heritability values assigned at each stage are described within each scenario in Section 2.7.4. Realized per‐stage heritabilities for both traits and all scenarios are reported in Table S4.

Breeding pipeline and selection criteria

A burn‐in phase of 20 years of phenotypic selection was used to simulate a conventional breeding program and to establish the starting point for comparison among the selection scenarios. The conventional breeding program began with 80 different crosses and 200 progenies per cross, resulting in a total of 16,000 F1 individuals. Except at the first clonal generation, selection of top‐performing individuals throughout the pipeline was based on a standardized additive selection index assigning equal weight (0.5) to yield and SG. Trait values were standardized to a mean of 0 and a variance of 1 before index calculation so that both traits contributed equally despite their different scales. At the first clonal generation, however, F1s were evaluated in an unreplicated trial with limited reliability, trait heritability was assumed to be low, and SG was not phenotyped; consequently, selection at this stage was based exclusively on yield. From the second clonal generation onward, the top 10% of clones were selected using the selection index applied to replicated trial data. Selection was applied at each subsequent step until one or two top‐performing clones were identified for release as varieties, thereby emulating the current pipeline of the potato breeding program (Figure 3).

Selection scenarios

Four scenarios were evaluated: a conventional phenotypic selection scenario (hereafter Conv) and three GS‐assisted scenarios (hereafter Strategy 1, Strategy 2, and Strategy 3). All scenarios shared the same burn‐in and differed only in the 30‐year future period. In the GS‐assisted strategies, GS was simulated explicitly (Section 2.7.6): a genomic prediction model was trained each cycle and used to compute EBVs for selection candidates at the early evaluation stage.

In Conv, selection was based solely on phenotypic evaluation. Because the early stages of the breeding pipeline relied on visual selection of unreplicated single‐plant plots, very low heritability was assumed for yield in the first clonal generation (h 2 = 0.05), reflecting limited phenotypic precision under early‐stage selection. SG cannot be phenotyped and therefore was not selected for at this stage. Parents were selected from clonal evaluation 3.

In the GS‐assisted strategies, parent selection incorporated overlapping generations: the top 12 individuals from clonal evaluation 3, the top 6 from official trial 1, and the top 3 from official trial 2 were combined into a parent pool of 21 individuals each year. Mating via random crossing allowed inter‐cohort crosses, reflecting the practice in modern potato breeding programs where elite material from different testing stages is recycled into the crossing block.

For Strategy 1, 650 F1 individuals were selected at the initial field evaluation based on phenotypic evaluation (yield). These individuals were genotyped, and 200 superior clones were retained using the GS index (EBVs). These 200 clones were evaluated in six replications, and 60 were selected; these 60 were evaluated in 15 replications, and the top 20 were retained; and the 20 were evaluated in 30 replications, from which the top three were selected as final candidates.

For Strategy 2, 1600 F1 individuals were selected at the initial field evaluation instead of 650, while maintaining a 10% selection intensity, and these individuals were genotyped, with 200 clones retained using the EBVs. The remainder of the pipeline was identical to that of Strategy 1.

For Strategy 3, all 16,000 F1 individuals were genotyped, bypassing the initial field evaluation and advancing directly to replicated clonal trials, resulting in a faster but more resource‐intensive scheme. More details of the scenarios are presented in Table 3.

Genomic prediction

In the GS‐assisted strategies, GS was simulated explicitly within the simulation framework. A genome‐wide array of 1000 SNP markers per chromosome, totaling 12,000 SNPs, was defined on the simulated genome, and candidate individuals were genotyped at the early evaluation stage. A Bayesian whole‐genome regression model (bWGR) (Xavier et al., 2019) was fitted to a training population comprising phenotypic and genotypic records from the replicated clonal trial stages, assembled using a sliding window that retained the most recent cycles. The model was retrained every cycle so that marker effects were re‐estimated as the population evolved, and separate single‐trait additive models were fitted for yield and SG. The resulting EBVs were combined in the selection index to advance candidates. Because the model was retrained each generation, prediction accuracy remained relatively stable over time rather than decaying, consistent with the continual recalibration used in operational genomic breeding programs.

Simulation design and statistical analysis

Each scenario was replicated 30 times, and their performance was assessed by estimating the mean of parent genetic values at each year. To statistically evaluate differences among scenarios, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey HSD (Honestly Significant Difference) pairwise comparisons was performed on parent genetic mean at three time points post‐burn‐in (+10, +20, and +30 years).

Computational implementation

The statistical analysis of phenotypic data (first stage) was carried out in the R package ASREML 4.0 (Gilmour et al., 2015). The subsequent genomic prediction models (second stage) were implemented using the R package BGLR (Pérez & de los Campos, 2014). The Bayesian models used 30,000 iterations, burn‐in of 3000, and a sampling interval of 10, totaling 2700 iterations. To obtain the additive relationship matrix (G), R package AGHmatrix (Amadeu et al., 2023) was used, and for ECs, R package envirotypeR (Costa‑Neto et al., 2021) was used. Imputation was performed using the R package PolyBreedR (Endelman et al., 2024), and to run the simulations of the breeding program, R package AlphasimR (Gaynor et al., 2021) was used.

The descriptive analysis of phenotypic traits TYLD and MYLD showed higher variation across environments (TYLD: 20.2–64.6 Mg ha−1, MYLD: 8.77–49.9 Mg ha−1) while the results for SG were relatively stable across the seven environments (Table S5). This suggests that SG is a more consistent trait under varying environmental conditions. For all traits, the empirical distribution was reasonably symmetric (Figure S1).

Out of the eight spatial models, model selection was based on the lowest AIC. For SG, the best fitting model was model 1, which included only clone as a random effect and assumed independent residuals across plots, with no spatial correlation in either row or column direction. For TYLD and MYLD, model 4 was selected, which models spatial correlation across both columns and rows.

Broad‐sense H 2 values ranged from 0.25 to 0.75, reflecting the varying degrees of genetic control and environmental influence for each trait (Figure 4A ; Table S5). Among the traits, SG had the highest heritability in several environments (TRS‐C223, TRS‐C224, and BG‐C224), while all traits tended to show lower estimates in the FL environments, likely due to greater environmental variability, including heat stress and less uniform field conditions. Comparison between platforms: Flex‐seq SNP density and DArTag SNP density

Prediction accuracy using Flex‐seq 4K versus 105K SNP density

The two platforms used in this study have an overlap of 4K loci (in other words, all 4K loci of DArTag are completely contained within 22K loci of Flex‐seq). Therefore, the overlapping 4K regions (∼3K SNPs) from Flex‐seq were selected and compared against the full Flex‐seq dataset (∼105K SNPs) for prediction via CV (as described in Section 2.6, Prediction Scenario 3) to assess prediction accuracy for three traits. The prediction accuracies for all three traits showed slightly higher mean prediction accuracy with 105K markers (Figure 7A). However, the absolute differences were small, generally within 0.01–0.04 across traits and environments, meaning that the smaller SNP set (∼3K SNPs) provided prediction accuracies comparable to the high‐density set (∼105K).

Prediction accuracy via imputation of DArTag into Flex‐seq

The polybreedR package supports two random forest‐based imputation strategies, namely, regression‐based and classification‐based imputation. Both approaches were benchmarked using mean RMSE values per tree and marker. The classification model demonstrated consistently lower average RMSE across all trees and fewer high‐error markers (Figure S3). Based on this evidence, the classification model was selected for final imputation.

Using the imputed dataset, genomic prediction was performed under a CV1 scheme to compare predictive abilities at ∼3K and ∼10K SNP densities across five environments in the C2 population. Results for TYLD, MYLD, and SG are presented in Figure 7B. For TYLD and MYLD, the 3K SNP set showed slightly higher prediction accuracies in several environments, compared to the 10K SNP set, but the absolute differences were minor. The difference generally ranged from 0.02 to 0.03 for TYLD and 0.01 to 0.06 for MYLD. For SG, higher prediction accuracy with 10K SNPs was observed in environments such as BG‐C224 and TRS‐C224, while in other environments, it was comparable to the 3K SNP set, with absolute differences mostly around 0.01–0.02. These results suggest that increasing marker density via imputation should be evaluated on a case‐by‐case basis but that predictive performance may be sufficient with a mid‐density panel (e.g., 4K panel in our case).

In contrast, Strategy 3, which included full genotyping of all F1 individuals, achieved the highest parental mean, followed by Strategy 2 and Strategy 1, with gains increasing as genotyping intensity increased.

For yield, a similar overall pattern was observed. All the GS‐assisted scenarios outperformed phenotypic selection, but differences among the three GS scenarios were less distinct. Although parental means under GS were consistently higher than under Conv selection, the trajectories among Strategies 1–3 were relatively close, indicating that yield responded to GS with less differentiation among strategies than SG did.

The statistical test for comparing scenario performance via one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD pairwise comparisons also validated the trends/patterns. Significant differences among scenarios were detected at all time points for both yield (p < 0.001) and SG (p < 0.001) (Table S6; pairwise comparisons in Table S7).

For yield, all three GS‐assisted strategies significantly outperformed Conv at every time point (p < 0.01). The advantage of GS strategies increased over time, with the mean difference between Strategy 3 and Conv growing from 5.5 Mg ha− 1 at +10 years to 13.75 Mg ha− 1 at +30 years. At +20 years, Strategy 3 was also significantly higher than Strategy 1 and Strategy 2 (p < 0.05). By +30 years, however, differences among the three GS strategies were no longer statistically significant, suggesting that the marginal benefit of increased genotyping intensity diminishes over the long term. Strategies 1 and 2 were statistically indistinguishable from each other at all time points.

For SG, the response to GS was more immediate and the separation among strategies more pronounced. All GS strategies were significantly superior to Conv at every time point (p < 0.001). The ranking was consistent, with Strategy 3 > Strategy 2 > Strategy 1 > Conv, and the magnitude of the differences increased over time. Strategy 3 showed the largest gain, with the difference relative to Conv growing from 0.0218 at +10 years to 0.0415 at +30 years. All pairwise comparisons were significant at all time points (Table S7).

These simulation results demonstrate that GS accelerated genetic gain for both yield and SG compared to conventional phenotypic selection. However, the strength and clarity of the response depended on the trait. SG showed a more pronounced and consistent response to different GS strategies, while yield showed weaker differentiation among GS strategies.

DISCUSSION

PCA and heritability

The phenotypic results for TYLD, MYLD, and SG, as well as their heritabilities, varied substantially across environments, reflecting a wide range of environmental effects on trait expression. High heritability for a trait indicates that most of the phenotypic variance is under genetic control, and selection could be used to make genetic progress. Heritability was consistently lower in FL compared to NC. Both yield and SG are sensitive to environmental fluctuations (Sood et al., 2020), and our results reflect this, with heritability estimates falling in the lower range for all three traits. This trend may also be explained by the fact that the clones evaluated in this study were primarily bred in NC, making them less adapted to the stress‐prone microenvironments of FL. As a result, trials conducted in FL likely expose these genotypes to suboptimal growing conditions, further elevating environmental effects and masking genetic differences.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports of strong G × E interactions in FL. For instance, Schmitz Carley et al. (2019) found that FL was an outlier in terms of genetic correlations for yield and vine maturity, showing substantially lower across‐location genetic correlations compared to other states, including NC. In contrast with the findings of Ticona‑Benavente and da Silva Filho (2015) and Sood et al. (2020), which reported high heritability for SG and low heritability for tuber yield, our study found all three traits to exhibit similarly low heritability values, especially in FL. This further reinforces the complexity of trait inheritance under variable environmental conditions and the need for multi‐environment testing in breeding programs.

Implementation of CV schemes

The GS models used two different CV schemes. The CV1 scheme conducts the prediction of newly developed, untested genotypes by removing all phenotypic data for a subset of individuals across environments, using only the remaining genotyped and phenotyped individuals for model training. Moderate prediction accuracies were observed for all three traits when predicting within the environment. This is comparable to past studies (Endelman et al., 2018; Sood et al., 2020; Sverrisdóttir et al., 2018). At the FL sites in 2024 and the TRS site in 2023, the prediction accuracy was lower (0.25–0.29), and more comparable with the values reported by Yusuf et al. (2024). The same pattern of prediction accuracy has been observed for MYLD as well.

The CV0 scheme predicts genotype performance in entirely new environments where no phenotypic data are available. It reflects real‐world breeding scenarios where genotypes have been tested in some environments but not in the target environment. We tested different models and compared prediction accuracies on all three traits. For all traits, the differences between the prediction models were minimal. Our results showed that the prediction accuracy of the simplest GBLUP model (i.e., M1) was comparable to complex models (M3 and M4) for all traits in most of the environments. Similar results have been reported in other studies (Habyarimana et al., 2017; Sverrisdóttir et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2021). Wilson et al. (2021) concluded that there is little difference in prediction accuracy between modeling strategies, and the use of an additive GBLUP model would be sufficient for GS in auto‐tetraploids, which is reflected by our study as well. One noteworthy observation was that when models were trained in six environments to predict the remaining one environment, FL consistently showed lower prediction accuracy across all models and traits. However, for SG, the predictions were still acceptable (0.4 and higher) except for one environment. This could be attributed to the fact that SG is a relatively stable trait and shows little G × E interaction (Endelman et al., 2018; Haynes, 2008; Y. Wang et al., 2017) compared to yield. Our results showed that the G × E variance component was negligible for SG, again validating the stable nature of this trait (Figure 4C). SG is a very important trait for chippers, and this prediction accuracy can enable breeders to select early for SG.

Although the low‐density platform is technically nested within the high‐density platform, the two were developed and genotyped independently. The observed differences in prediction performance between platforms likely reflect differences in marker representation, probe chemistry, or allele dosage estimation rather than biological differences among individuals. Therefore, platform‐specific analyses provide a more appropriate comparison of genomic prediction performance while avoiding potential biases introduced by combining marker datasets generated from different platforms.

Comparison of two platforms, marker density, and imputation on prediction

Targeted sequencing technologies such as DArTag and Flex‐seq offer advantages over genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS), including lower rates of missing data and consistent targeting of the same markers across all samples. The Flex‐seq panel yielded more markers than DArTag, as it targets more genomic regions (22K probes) and can detect multiple variants within a single probe region. Despite this difference in marker yield, both platforms produced comparable genomic prediction results in our two breeding populations.

Our results demonstrated that a medium‐density SNP genotyping platform performed nearly identically to a high‐density SNP platform in terms of prediction accuracy. In this study, a marker density of ∼3K SNPs was as effective as using ∼105K SNPs for predicting yield and SG using genomic models. This equivalence suggests that both platforms likely captured genotypic variance well enough to reconstruct near‐identical relationship matrices, which ultimately drive genomic prediction. The imputation analysis pointed the same way: imputing DArTag up to 10K markers did not improve the accuracy over the original ∼3K. Similar findings have been reported across multiple crop species, where comparisons among GBS, SNP arrays, and sequencing‐based platforms resulted in comparable genomic prediction accuracies (Elbasyoni et al., 2018; C. Liu, Sukumaran, et al., 2020; Sipowicz et al., 2025; Sleper et al., 2025). Collectively, these results indicate that once marker density is sufficient to capture genetic relatedness among individuals, adding markers provides little improvement in genomic prediction accuracy for the population evaluated here.

Future research should evaluate the performance of these models over time, as higher density platforms are expected to maintain model accuracy for more generations. Because lower‐density panels are considerably cheaper, they are an attractive choice for routine GS deployment. Overall, the results from this study suggest that lower marker density would be sufficient for GS applications in potato.

GS in the breeding program

The introduction of GS in the first year of clonal selection is expected to have a significant impact on the efficiency of a potato breeding program. At this stage in the potato breeding cycle, accurate phenotyping is challenging because clones are typically evaluated as unreplicated single‐plant plots with limited seed availability. Without sufficient replication, the error variance associated with individual phenotypic estimates is high, resulting in low heritability and less reliable selection decisions. Breeders, therefore, typically limit selection at this stage to traits where visual selection provides adequate accuracy (e.g., tuber shape, freedom from important diseases). Many key breeding traits, such as tuber yield and SG, cannot be accurately assessed until later vegetative generations, where more tubers from the same clone are available for planting in the field.

In this study, the potential of GS‐informed selection was evaluated by comparing four strategies through stochastic simulation over a 30‐year future period. Compared to the conventional breeding scenario, GS‐assisted selection consistently resulted in higher genetic gain for both yield and SG. Under conventional selection, phenotypic evaluations at the early unreplicated stages are limited in their ability to discriminate among genotypes due to low heritability, leading to less effective identification of superior individuals. In contrast, incorporating genomic information at this stage, as implemented in the GS‐assisted strategies, was shown to improve selection accuracy, enabling the identification and advancement of superior genotypes earlier in the pipeline. This earlier identification also allowed superior genotypes to be recycled as parents sooner, resulting in higher parental mean and accelerated genetic gain across years (Gorjanc et al., 2016; Peixoto, Coelho et al., 2024; Vieira et al., 2025).

It is important to highlight that this simulation is an oversimplification compared to real conventional breeding programs, since only two traits were considered. In practice, potato breeders evaluate a substantially larger number of traits prior to advancing a selection. Nonetheless, the ability to select for SG early in the breeding program using GS is expected to translate into meaningful genetic gains, particularly given the high selection intensity typically applied at the initial stages.

Several features of the simulation should be considered when interpreting these results. First, prediction accuracy is not constant across the breeding cycles, as recombination can erode the linkage between markers and QTL over generations; accuracy is expected to decline unless the model is recalibrated. Here, the genomic prediction model was retrained each cycle, which kept accuracy relatively stable over the 30‐year horizon (Figure S4); a mild decline was nonetheless evident, most pronounced for SG and for Strategy 3 (where we genotype all F1s). Second, genomic prediction applies different shrinkage across families, favoring those better represented in the training population; combined with the higher selection intensity of the GS strategies, this accelerated the loss of genetic diversity relative to conventional selection, and the rate of decline in genetic variance was greatest under the most intensive strategy (Strategy 3), followed by Strategy 2 and Strategy 1, and slowest under Conv (Figure S5). This diversity cost could be mitigated by incorporating cross‐selection approach that explicitly balance genetic gain and the maintenance of diversity, such as those implemented in SimpleMating (Peixoto et al., 2025), rather than selecting the top individuals solely on EBVs. Finally, parent sources differed among scenarios. The conventional program recycled parents across multiple overlapping generations, whereas the GS strategies did not, so generation structure is therefore partially confounded with selection strategy, and the diversity comparisons should be interpreted with this in mind rather than as a pure effect of GS.

To effectively integrate GS into a conventional breeding pipeline, it is essential to optimize the inputs used in GS. Although implementing GS can be expensive, it has been shown to be more cost‐effective and time‐efficient than conventional breeding methods when applied strategically (Slater et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2023). The results of this study demonstrated that a medium‐density genotyping platform can achieve predictive performance comparable to a high‐density platform, allowing breeding programs to allocate resources more efficiently. In addition to genotyping costs, conventional phenotyping incurs both tangible and intangible costs, including labor, time, and field resources. When these factors are considered, genotyping a smaller subset of individuals, as proposed in Strategy 1 or Strategy 2, may prove to be a more efficient and practical solution, depending on the program's budget and goals. Strategy 3, while delivering the highest genetic gain over time, involves genotyping all F1 individuals, making it financially infeasible for most breeding programs. Therefore, striking a balance between genetic gain and implementation cost is key when deciding on the most suitable strategy.

CONCLUSIONS

This study evaluated the potential of GS to improve TYLD, MYLD, and SG in elite tetraploid potato germplasm evaluated under challenging production environments. The observed heritabilities, prediction accuracies, and variance components underscore the trait‐dependent performance of GS and highlight the critical role of G × E interactions for complex yield traits. While total and MYLD exhibited strong environmental sensitivity and reduced predictability, SG emerged as a comparatively stable trait with higher and more consistent prediction accuracy. These results suggest that GS is particularly well suited for traits with greater genetic stability, enabling effective early‐stage selection even when phenotypic data originate from environments that differ from the target production region. In contrast, traits strongly influenced by environmental variation require selection strategies that explicitly account for local adaptation and G × E effects. This distinction has important implications for designing GS pipelines, emphasizing the need for trait‐specific implementation rather than a one‐size‐fits‐all approach.

The study also demonstrated that multiple genotyping platforms could support effective genomic prediction in potato breeding. Both DArTag and Flex‐seq provided comparable predictive performance, offering breeders flexibility to choose platforms based on cost, turnaround time, marker density, and programmatic constraints without substantially compromising selection outcomes. Such flexibility is particularly valuable for routine implementation of GS in resource‐limited breeding programs.

Finally, simulation of a GS‐assisted breeding pipeline illustrated the potential for increased long‐term genetic gain when genomic prediction is integrated into selection cycles. Together, these findings provide encouraging evidence that GS can be a viable and impactful tool for selecting potato cultivars, especially when supported by affordable genotyping and robust selection strategies.
