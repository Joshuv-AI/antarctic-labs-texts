On zero-divisors and units in group rings of torsion-free CAT ( 0 ) (0) groups
Manisha Garg, Igor Mineyev

Abstract
. This paper addresses two of Kaplansky’s conjectures concerning group rings K ⁡ [ G ] K[G] , where K K is a field and G G is a torsion-free group: the Zero-Divisor Conjecture, which asserts that K ⁡ [ G ] K[G] has no non-trivial zero-divisors, and the Unit Conjecture, which asserts that K ⁡ [ G ] K[G] has no non-trivial units. While the Zero-Divisor conjecture still remains open, the Unit Conjecture was disproven by Gardam in 2021. The search for more counterexamples remains an open problem. Let m m and n n be the length of support of two non-trivial elements α , β ∈ 𝔽 2 ​ [ G ] \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[G] , respectively. We address these conjectures by introducing a process called left-alignment and recursively constructing the combinatorial structures associated to ( m , n ) (m,n) which would yield counterexamples to both conjectures over the field 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} if they satisfy conditions 𝖳 1 − 𝖳 4 \mathsf{T}_{1}-\mathsf{T}_{4} . Such combinatorial objects are called oriented product structures of type ( m , n ) (m,n) . We also present a computer-search that can be utilized to search for counterexamples of a certain geometry by significantly pruning the search space. We prove that a class CAT(0) groups with certain geometry cannot be counterexamples to these conjectures. Moreover, we prove that for 1 ≤ m ≤ 5 1\leq m\leq 5 and n n any positive integer, there are no counterexamples to the conjectures such that the associated oriented product structures are of type ( m , n ) (m,n) . With the aid of computer, we prove that, in fact, there are no such counterexamples of the length combination ( m , n ) (m,n) where 1 ≤ m , n ≤ 13 . 1\leq m,n\leq 13.

Introduction

In 1956, I. Kaplansky
[
Kap57
,
Kap70
]
presented a list of several algebraic conjectures concerning group rings
K[G]
, where
K
is a field and
G
is a torsion-free group. Our paper focuses on two of these conjectures:

(1)

The
Zero-Divisor Conjecture
:
K[G]
has no non-trivial zero-divisors.

(2)

The
Unit Conjecture
:
K[G]
has no non-trivial units.

The unit conjecture has been studied extensively, with its origins traced back to Higman
[
Hig40
,
Hig40a
]
, who, in his doctoral thesis among other results, proved that all locally indicable groups satisfy this conjecture. In 2021, Gardam
[
Gar21
]
discovered the first counterexample to the unit conjecture over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, which was subsequently extended to fields of prime characteristic by Murray
[
Mur21
]
.

The unit conjecture implies the zero-divisor conjecture (see
[
Pas77
]
, Lemma 13.1.2) and both conjectures are known to hold true for a large class of groups. For torsion-free supersolvable groups
[
For73
]
and polycyclic-by-finite groups
[
FS76
,
Bro76
]
, the zero-divisor conjecture is true. Both conjectures hold true for the class of unique-product groups
[
Coh74
]
. A group
G
is called a
unique product group
if for any two finite sets
A,B
, there exists an element
g\in A\cdot B
such that
g
can be uniquely expressed as
g=ab
, where
a\in A
and
b\in B
, that is,
g\neq a^{\prime}b^{\prime}
where
a^{\prime}\neq a
or
b^{\prime}\neq b
. Examples of unique-product groups include linearly ordered groups and torsion-free abelian groups. Thus it might be fruitful to look for counterexamples among torsion-free non-unique product groups.

Constructing torsion-free non-unique product groups has been a challenging task. In 1987, Rips and Segev
[
RS87
]
constructed the first torsion-free non-unique product group using small cancellation theory. A year later, Promislow
[
Pro88
]
, gave a simpler example of a non-unique product group
P
given as

(1)

P=\langle x,y\mid(x^{2})^{y}=x^{-2},(y^{2})^{x}=y^{-2}\rangle

This group is known by several names in literature including Promislow group, Passman and Hantzsche–Wendt group. It is supersolvable and in particular, it is polycyclic.
P
contains the subgroup
H=\langle x^{2},y^{2},[x,y]\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, extended by the Klein Four group
V_{4}=C_{2}\times C_{2}\cong\langle xH,yH\rangle
. Apart from
[
Ste15
,
AS23
,
GMS15
,
Soe18
]
very few examples of non-unique product groups are known to us. Gardam’s counterexample to the unit conjecture comes from Promislow group
P
given in (
1
). However,
P
is known to satisfy the zero-divisor conjecture, since it is supersoluble (see
[
CP13
]
).

Several combinatorial and computational approaches have been utilized to search for counterexamples of these longstanding conjectures (for instance, see
[
Sch13
,
Soe18
,
Car14
,
BJ24
,
AT18
,
AJ19
]
). The second author in Theorem
4
,
[
Min24
]
presents a list of combinatorial conditions which are sufficient to find counterexamples of these conjectures.

In this paper, we address the zero-divisor conjecture and the unit conjecture by introducing a process called ‘
left-alignment
’ and constructing an algorithm based on four combinatorial conditions
\mathsf{T}_{1}
through
\mathsf{T}_{4}
to search for counterexamples in the group ring
K[G]
, where
K=\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
G
is a CAT(0) group constructed in
[
Min24
]
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[G]
be elements expressed as
\alpha=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}
and
\beta=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}
. Recall that the support of an element
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[G]
, denoted as

, is the set
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
. Let

and

. Since the field is

, we can assume that all

are distinct and all

are distinct and thus

and

. The product structure associated with

\Pi
provides a counterexample to either the zero-divisor conjecture or the unit conjecture. The sufficient conditions provided in Theorem
4
relate the structure of
\Pi
to properties such as girth and non-degeneracy of the associated combinatorial object.
In this paper, we prove that one of the conditions never holds for an oriented product structure:

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two finite sets and
\Pi=(A,B,P)
be an orientable even or odd product structure with associated middle-link graph
\mathsf{L}_{1}
. Then the girth of

is at most 4.

A counterexample is said to be of type
(m,n)
if there exists an even or odd product structure

where

,

, and the partition

through
\mathsf{T}_{4}

serves as a counterexample to the unit conjecture. We also prove:

.

There are no counterexamples of type
(m,n)
or
(n,m)
where

and
n
is a positive integer.

For larger values of

and

, the combinatorial arguments to prove the existence or non-existence of counterexamples of type
(m,n)
become tedious as the number of cases grow exponentially with size. Thus it is beneficial to employ a computer-search. We introduce a process called left-alignment which is similar in spirit to the adequacy condition employed in
[
Sch13
]
. We develop an algorithm for a fixed length combination

and utilize the left-alignment condition and the combinatorial conditions

to prune the search space for the counterexamples significantly. We obtain the following strengthened variant of Theorem

Theorem 3

(Computer-assisted)
.

There are no counterexamples of type
(m,n)
or
(n,m)
where

(1)

1\leq m,n\leq 13
, or,

(2)

m\in\{6,7\},1\leq n\leq 200

Since these conjectures are known to hold for a large class of groups, a new direction towards identifying counterexamples was inspired by Gardam’s example, which involves elements with a support length of 21. Extensive work has been done to rule out cases with smaller support lengths; see, for instance,
[

]
. However, since the number of potential cases grows exponentially with the product

In this paper, we effectively prune a significant number of pairs, narrowing the search space further to look for counterexamples. The Algorithm
1
is scalable to incorporate any topological or geometric condition that can be realized combinatorially. Furthermore, it can be generalized to facilitate searches in higher-dimensional CAT(0) cell complexes which might be the right space to look for counterexamples.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, we will consider the case where the field is
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[G]
such that
\alpha=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}
and
\beta=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}
. Recall that for an element
\alpha=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}
, the support of
\alpha
, denoted
\text{supp}(\alpha)
, is the set
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
. Since the field is
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, we may assume that
|\text{supp}(\alpha)|=m
and
|\text{supp}(\beta)|=n
.

Now, let
\alpha\beta=0
and
mn
be even. This implies

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{j}=0.

Since all terms in
\alpha\beta
cannot be distinct, at least two terms must be identical. Without loss of generality, assume
a_{1}b_{1}=a_{2}b_{2}
. This implies
a_{1}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{2}=0
. By induction on the number of terms in the sum, it follows that all terms in
\alpha\beta
must occur in pairs of identical elements.

As a consequence, the product
\alpha\beta
can be represented by a complete bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}(A,B)
, where
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
and
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
are two disjoint vertex sets. The edge set of
\mathcal{G}(A,B)
is given by
\{(a,b)\mid a\in A,b\in B\}
, which corresponds to the Cartesian product
A\times B
. Each term in the product
\alpha\beta
(
2
) corresponds to an edge in
A\times B
. A pair of two identical terms corresponds to the pairing of two vertical edges. Note that if
a_{i}b_{j}=a_{i^{\prime}}b_{j^{\prime}}
then
a_{i}\neq a_{i^{\prime}}
and
b_{j}\neq b_{j^{\prime}}
, because otherwise either
|\text{supp}(\alpha)|<m
or
|\text{supp}(\beta)|<n
. Thus, in a pair, no two edges share the same vertex. We will call each pair a cell.

Similarly, for
mn
odd, consider the equation
\alpha\beta=1
. This would lead to pairing of
mn-1
edges in the complete bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}(A,B)
. For the sufficiency of the existence of non-trivial zero-divisors and units in torsion-free group rings, there are additional combinatorial properties and structures associated with the bipartite graph and the pairing. In this section, we will describe the additional combinatorial constructs and the conditions that are sufficient for the existence of counterexamples to the zero-divisor conjecture and the unit conjectures.

Product Structures and Substructures

Disjoint vertex condition

if each cell
C_{i}
satisfies the disjoint vertex condition. Finally, a

is a triple
\Pi=(A,B,P)
, where:

•

A
and
B

•

By a

Let

3.
Our approach

Throughout this paper, we focus only on even and odd product structures. While the approach can be extended to more general product structures, such extensions fall outside the scope of this work.

3.1.
Decreasing Conditions

The goal is to search for counterexamples of the conjectures by building a product structure recursively by starting from an empty product substructure. This subsection outlines how the combinatorial conditions facilitate the recursive process.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
be two finite graphs such that
V_{1}=V_{2}
and
E_{1}\subseteq E_{2}
, then
\text{girth}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\geq\text{girth}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
.

Proof.

Since
E_{1}\subseteq E_{2}
,
\mathcal{G}_{1}
can be viewed as a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
with the same vertex set and possibly fewer edges.
We consider two cases.

Case 1:

\mathcal{G}_{1}
has no cycles. In this case we will say that
\text{girth}(\mathcal{G}_{1})=\infty
. The girth of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is finite (if it contains a cycle) and infinite otherwise.

Case 2:

\mathcal{G}_{1}
contains cycles. Any nontrivial edge-loop in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
is a nontrivial edge-loop in
\mathcal{G}_{2}
. Hence minimum of their lengths is larger for
\mathcal{G}_{1}
.
In both cases, we have shown that
\text{girth}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\geq\text{girth}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\Pi=(A,B,P)
and
\Pi^{\prime}=(A,B,P^{\prime})
be two product substructrues such that
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
. Let
\mathsf{L}_{AB}
and
\mathsf{L}_{AB}^{\prime}
be horizontal graphs associated to
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\prime}
, respectively, as defined in (
6
). Then,
\text{girth}(\mathsf{L}_{AB}^{\prime})\leq\text{girth}(\mathsf{L}_{AB})
.

Moreover, if
\mathsf{L}_{1}
and
\mathsf{L}_{1}^{\prime}
denote middle links for
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\prime}
, respectively, then
\text{half-girth}(\mathsf{L}_{1}^{\prime})\leq\text{half-girth}(\mathsf{L}_{1})
.

Proof.

Since
A
and

are fixed finite sets for both

and

,
\mathsf{L}_{A},\mathsf{L}_{A}^{\prime}
are graphs on the same vertex set

are contained in the set of edges for

. Similarly,

are graphs on the same vertex set

such that edges of
\mathsf{L}_{B}
are contained in the set of edges for
\mathsf{L}_{B}^{\prime}
. In particular,
\mathsf{L}_{AB}
and
\mathsf{L}_{AB}^{\prime}
are graphs on the same vertex set

such that edges of

are contained in the set of edges for
\mathsf{L}_{AB}^{\prime}

, the result follows.

Let
\mathsf{L}_{1}

be the middle link graphs for

and

This lemma shows that as we create a larger subpartition of

by adding more cells, we decrease the girth of the horizontal graph and of the middle link.

Definition 3.1

(Decreasing condition)
.

Let
A
and
B
be two finite sets, and let
\Pi=(A,B,P)
and
\Pi^{\prime}=(A,B,P^{\prime})
be product substructures such that
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
. A condition
T
is said to be decreasing if the following holds:
If
T
fails for
\Pi
, then
T
must also fail for
\Pi^{\prime}
.

Equivalently,
T
satisfies the hereditary property in reverse: If
T
holds for
\Pi^{\prime}
, it must also hold for
\Pi
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A
and
B
be two finite sets, and let
\Pi=(A,B,P)
and
\Pi^{\prime}=(A,B,P^{\prime})
be product substructures such that
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
. The conditions
\mathsf{T_{1}}-\mathsf{T_{4}}
are decreasing conditions, that is, if the conditions
\mathsf{T_{1}}-\mathsf{T_{4}}
are satisfied by
\Pi^{\prime}
then they are also satisfied by
\Pi
.

Proof.

Let
\Pi=(A,B,P)
and
\Pi^{\prime}=(A,B,P^{\prime})
be two partitions such that
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
.

\mathsf{T_{1}}
,
orientation
: Assume that
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies the orientation condition
\mathsf{T_{1}}
. This means there exists an orientation function
O
(as defined in Definition (
2.1
)) that provides a consistent orientation for the partition
P^{\prime}
. Since
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
, the orientation function
O
, which orients the elements of
P^{\prime}
, is orients the elements of
P
, as all cells in
P
are contained within
P^{\prime}
.

Thus, if
\mathsf{T_{1}}
is satisfied by
\Pi^{\prime}
, it must also be satisfied by
\Pi
, because the orientation function
O
remains valid for the smaller partition
P
. Conversely, if
\mathsf{T_{1}}
is not satisfied by
\Pi
, it implies that no orientation function exists for
P
. Since
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
, the orientation function
O
cannot exist for
P^{\prime}
either.

\mathsf{T_{2}}
,
no-fold
: Assume that
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies the no-fold condition, meaning there is no fold at any vertex in the taiko for the partition
P^{\prime}
. Since
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
, all edges in
P
are also present in
P^{\prime}
. If there were a fold at some vertex in
P
, this would imply that two horizontal edges in
P
have the same color and the same direction at a common vertex
v
. However, since these edges are also part of
P^{\prime}
(as
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
), this would contradict the fact that
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies the no-fold condition. Therefore, no such fold can exist in
P
if
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathsf{T_{2}}
.

Conversely, if
\mathsf{T_{2}}
is not satisfied by
\Pi
, meaning a fold occurs in
P
, then this fold would also be present in
P^{\prime}
because
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
, which implies that
\mathsf{T_{2}}
cannot be satisfied by
\Pi^{\prime}
.

\mathsf{T_{3}}
,
no-pattern
: Assume that
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies the no-pattern condition
\mathsf{T_{3}}
, meaning no repeating pattern occurs in the partition
P^{\prime}
. Since
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
, any pattern occurring in
P
is also a pattern in
P^{\prime}
. If there were a repeating pattern in
P
, then that same repeating pattern would also occur in
P^{\prime}
, as all edges and vertices in
P
are also present in
P^{\prime}
. This would contradict the assumption that
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies the no-pattern condition. Therefore, no repeating pattern can occur in
P
if
\Pi^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathsf{T_{3}}
.

Conversely, if
\mathsf{T_{3}}
is not satisfied by
\Pi
, meaning a repeating pattern occurs in
P
, then this pattern must also be present in
P^{\prime}
, as
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
. Hence,
\mathsf{T_{3}}

4.2.
Counterexamples of type
(m,n)

Although it is well known in the literature that if
\alpha\beta=0
in a group ring
R[G]
then
|\text{supp}(\alpha)|>2
and
|\text{supp}(\beta)|>2
. For instance, see
[
Sch13
]
. We show a rather obvious result to demonstrate the combinatorial technique to prove such statements for counterexamples of type
(m,n)
.

Theorem 6
.

There are no counterexamples of type
(2,n)
or
(n,2)
where
n
is a positive integer.

Proof.

The cases for
(2,n)
and
(n,2)
are identical by symmetry, therefore, we will only prove the result for
(2,n)
. When
n=2
, by Example
2
, the condition
\mathsf{T}_{1}
fails.

If
P
is a partition of
A\times B
then
|P|=n
. Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
denote
n
2-cells of
P
. By the left-alignment condition, we can assume that
C_{1}=\{(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\}\in P
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
(a_{1},b_{2})\in C_{2}
. By left-alignment,
(a_{2},b_{3})\in C_{2}
. Now consider the cell containing the edge
(a_{2},b_{1})
. We can assume it is
C_{3}
. Any edge
(a_{1},b_{j})
will yield a horizontal edge
(b_{j},b_{1})
in
\mathsf{L}_{B}
which leads to girth
2
in the associated middle-link graph,
\mathsf{L}_{1}
.
∎

Theorem 7
.

There are no counterexamples of type
(3,n)
or
(n,3)
where
n
is a positive integer.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
6
. Again, we will only work with the case when
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
and
|B|=n\geq 3
. Note that by Theorem
1
, we cannot have a cycle in
\mathsf{L}_{A}
because otherwise
girth
(L_{AB})=3
and
girth
(\mathsf{L}_{1})\leq 4
and the subpartition would fail the triple-girth condition. We have two cases:

(1)

Both edges in
\mathsf{L}_{A}
are of the same color, that is, there is only one equivalence class of horizontal edges. The proof is identical to the proof of Theorem
6
.

(2)

Both edges are of different color. For a subpartition of cardinality
3
, the only possibilities upto permutation are
P_{115}
and
P_{123}
by Lemma
7
. For a child
P^{\prime}
of
P_{115}
, there will be horizontal edge
(b_{3},b_{j})
for some
j\notin\{1,2,3\}
such that it belongs to the same equivalence class as
(b_{1},b_{2})
and
(b_{2},b_{3})
. Thus,
girth
(\mathsf{L}_{1})=2
for
P^{\prime}
.
Similarly, consider the child
P^{\prime}
of
P_{123}
so that
(a_{2},b_{4})\in C_{k}
for some
C_{k}\in P^{\prime}
. Then there is horizontal edge
(b_{j},b_{4})
for some
j\notin\{1,2,3,4\}
. The edges
(b_{1},b_{2}),(b_{2},b_{3}),(b_{1},b_{4})
and
(b_{j},b_{4})
belong the same horizontal class thus leading to
girth
(\mathsf{L}_{1})=2
.

∎

Computational Results

The Algorithm
1
generates all product substructures of size
(m,n)
upto isomorphism such that the product substructures satisfy conditions
\mathsf{T}_{1}
through
\mathsf{T}_{4}
. The algorithm is similar in spirit to the canonical labeling algorithm in
[
Sch13
]
. However, instead of choosing canonical representations in each iteration, we choose canonical representations of the cells we construct by using the left-alignment condition. To list all product structures of size
(m,n)
, we build a product structure recursively by starting from an empty product substructure. In each step we check if the disjoint vertex condition and the combinatorial conditions are satisfied. Since the conditions are decreasing as described in Section 3, if a condition is not satisfied by a parent then it cannot be satisfied by any of its descendants, thus the conditions help us to prune the computations significantly.

The algorithm builds a tree with the root of the tree representing the empty product substructure and
k^{\text{th}}
level of the tree consists of all valid subpartitions (and hence, all associated product substructures) upto isomorphism of cardinality
k
. Each node at height
k
represents a subpartition of cardinality
k
.

The algorithm records the height of each node. For instance, at height 1, there is only
1
child of empty subpartition as discussed in Lemma
5
and for height
2
, there are only three subpartitions by left-alignment in Lemma
6
. Tree for upto first three iterations is depicted in Figure

Remark
.

For a pair
(m,n)
of positive integers, if the highest height recorded is
h

\mathsf{T}_{1}-\mathsf{T}_{4}
such that
m^{\prime}\geq m
and
n^{\prime}\geq n
. We refer to this as
no-example criterion 1
.

.

Another similar criterion is: for a pair
(m,n)
, if the highest height recorded is
