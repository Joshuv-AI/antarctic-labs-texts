A cross-sectional study based on the Comprehensive Model of Information seeking: which factors influence health information-seeking behavior in patients with periodontitis
Authors: Yuxin Xu, Mengli Wang, Li Bao, Zijian Cheng, Xiaojun Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the current status of health information-seeking behavior (HISB) of periodontitis patients, and to identity its main influencing factors using the Comprehensive Model of Information Seeking (CMIS).MethodsIn total, 274 periodontitis patients were recruited from a specialized dental hospital in Hangzhou by purposive sampling method for a cross-sectional study. Demographics, direct experience, salience, beliefs, characteristics and utility were 6 variables of CMIS. Data were collected from the patients by using the general information questionnaire, the Health Information Seeking Behavior Scale, the Self-Efficacy Scale for Self-care (SESS) for measuring belief, the Short Form of Health Literacy Dental Scale (HeLD⁃14) for measuring direct experience, and the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) for measuring salience. Univariate analysis and regression analysis were utilized to determine the factors influencing the HISB.ResultsThe HISB score of periodontitis patients in this study was 3.68 ± 0.40. The low level of HISB was negatively associated with multiple factors, including age of 40 ~ 59 (odds ratio [OR] 0.041, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.006–0.299), age of 18 ~ 39 (OR 0.053, 95%CI 0.008–0.364), low level of understandability of information (characteristic) [OR 0.317, 95%CI 0.119–0.840] and low level of satisfaction of information (utility) [OR 0.027, 95%CI 0.008–0.089]. However, low level of HISB was positively correlated with medium self-efficacy level [OR 3.112, 95% CI 1.463–6.747] and low self-efficacy level [OR 8.061, 95% CI 1.981–32.807].ConclusionsAccording to the CMIS model, we identified several factors influencing health-seeking behaviors (HISB). Lower levels of HISB are closely associated with older age and lower level of understandability and satisfaction of information. Conversely, higher self-efficacy may encourage patients to seek health information more actively. Therefore, it is essential to focus on elderly patients and assess their information expectations and needs in a timely manner, while also working to enhance their self-efficacy to promote more effective access to health information.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05068-x.Keywords: Periodontitis, Health information-seeking behavior, Influencing factors, Illness perception, Self-efficacy

Introduction

Periodontitis is a common chronic inflammatory disease of the oral cavity [1], which has various degrees of negative physical and psychological effects on patients [2–5]. The economic burden of periodontal disease is far-reaching globally, with the indirect costs of severe periodontitis estimated at more than $50 billion [6], making it one of the major public health problems worldwide [7].

Health information seeking behavior (HISB) is described as an active or purposeful behavior by an individual to find health information [8]. This behavior promotes patient participation in healthcare decision-making, may facilitate behavioral change, and may also promote psychosocial adaptation to illness [8–11]. Individuals who seek health information are more likely to develop healthier habits, such as regular exercise [12]. Dentists often struggle with promoting compliance with oral hygiene instructions [5]. In this context, HISB could play a key role. HISB can help patients with periodontitis better understand and follow the doctor’s advice, and thus have a positive impact on the outcome of periodontal treatment.

Johnson and Meischke proposed a comprehensive model of information seeking (CMIS) [13] by synthesizing the health belief model [14], the uses and gratifications theory [15], and a model of media exposure and appraisal [16]. The model suggests that health-related factors provide motivation for information-seeking actions, which are shaped by information carrier factors, as shown in Fig. 1. This study does not intend to examine the model, as it is quite comprehensive. We use the CMIS as a theoretical framework [13], combined with relevant literatures, to conduct operational definitions of variables in the model. Direct experience refers to the experience and knowledge of the personal and social network about the disease, including the individual’s disease status, family history, health literacy, and so on. Salience refers to the significance of health information to an individual in relation to the degree of perceived health threat, such as illness anxiety or self-perception. Beliefs are an individual’s confidence to solve problems, such as self-efficacy. Information carrier characteristics refer to the characteristics of the content of the information, such as the " level of understandability of information “. The term utility is derived from economics and is used to measure the degree of consumer satisfaction [17]. Information utility is an evaluation of the benefits, problem solving and value objectives obtained by the user [18], and this study focuses on measuring the " level of satisfaction of information”.

The Comprehensive Model of Information Seeking (CMIS)

There has been a proliferation of studies related to HISB in the diseases, such as osteoporosis [19], shoulder pain [20], endocrine disease [21] and cancer [22, 23], however, little is known about the HISB of patients with periodontitis. Since there are few studies on HISB in patients with periodontitis, this study intends to investigate the level of HISB of Chinese periodontitis patients, and to explore the relevant factors that may influence patients’ seeking behavior based on the CMIS.

Methods

Study design

Participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in the Department of Periodontics of the Stomatology Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine in China from March 2023 to December 2023. All the participants were selected by purposive sampling methods. All questionnaire entries were answered anonymously. Informed consent was signed by all individuals who agreed to participate in this study. In this study, four periodontal specialists working for more than 5 years were selected to examine the patients and diagnose periodontitis with reference to the AAP/EFP consensus [24]. Eligibility criteria included: (a) ≥ 18 years old, (b) diagnosis of periodontitis based on the consensus of 2017 World Workshop: interdental clinical attachment loss (CAL) is detectable at ≥ 2 non-adjacent teeth, or buccal or oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with probing pocket depth (PD) > 3 mm is detectable at ≥ 2 teeth [24] (c) patients having the ability to read and communicate normally. Exclusion criteria included: (a) presence of severe somatic dysfunction and psychiatric disorders, (b) comorbidity with multiple severe systemic diseases. Kendall advocated the estimation of sample size for multivariate analysis, and the number of observations was at least 10 times the number of variables [25]. A total of 18 independent variables were included in this study, which were gender, age, residence, marital status, education level, health insurance, income, internet access, time of diagnosis of periodontitis, stage of periodontitis, family history, number of other oral diseases, experience of seeking information, understandability of information, satisfaction of information, self-efficacy, health literacy, illness perception. A total of 288 questionnaires were collected and 31 incomplete response questionnaires were excluded. Finally, we get 274 effective response questionnaires, which is more than the minimum sample size standard of 180.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatology Hospital affiliated to Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IP 2023(024)) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013. All patients were informed and consented.

Measurements

General information and oral health status

The general data collection form was designed by the researcher with reference to the literatures and group discussion, which consisted of a total of 15 questions including gender, age, marital status, education level, health insurance, income, internet access, time of diagnosis of periodontitis, stage of periodontitis and etc.

Health information seeking behavior

HISB was measured by the Health Information Seeking Behavior Scale, which was developed by Zamani et al. [26] initially and localized into Chinese by Qiuzi Sun et al. in 201927. The scale consists of 4 dimensions and 43 items. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (very unimportant = 1, unimportant = 2, average = 3, important = 4, and very important = 5). Items 36 to 43 are reverse scoring questions and the rest are forward scoring questions. The final score is the average of all the items, with higher scores representing higher level of HISB. Cronbach’s α coefficients of HISB was 0.925 in this study. The median score of all patients was used as the cutoff, above which was the “high-level” group and below part was the “low-level” group.

Self-efficacy

The Self-efficacy Scale for Self-care (SESS) was developed by Kakudate et al. [28] for assessing the self-efficacy for patients of periodontal disease and modified for Chinese by Wu Di et al. [29]. It uses a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1(not confident) to 5(completely confident) and consists of 15 items assessing self-efficacy. According to the authors of the source scale’s pre-existing grading cut-off, a total score of 15 to 53 indicates a low level of self-efficacy, 54 to 59 indicates a moderate level of self-efficacy, and 60 to 75 indicates a high level of self-efficacy. This survey verified SESS reliability for internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.912).

Health literacy

The health literacy of patients with periodontitis was measured by the Short Form of Health Literacy Dental Scale (HeLD-14) [30], which was widely used in oral diseases. It was adapted by Jones et al. from the full version of the HeLD-29, which consists of 7 dimensions and 14 items. Entries were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 meaning “not difficult at all” and 5 meaning “cannot do it at all”. When analyzing the scores, they were converted to calculate the total score, for example, 5 to 0, 4 to 1, etc. According to Jones ‘s pre-existing grading cut-off, a total score of 14 to 35 indicates a low level of health literacy, 36 to 46 indicates a moderate level of health literacy, and 47 to 70 indicates a high level of health literacy. Wen Yan et al. localized to Chinese in 2021 [31]. This survey verified HeLD-14 reliability for internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.959).

Illness perception

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) was developed by Broadbent et al. in 2006 [32]. It has been used to assess patient perceptions in a variety of chronic conditions. Yaqi Mei et al. modify it for Chinese in 2015 [33]. The scale consists of 9 items with 3 dimensions: cognition (items 1, 2, 3, 4, 5), emotion (items 6, 8), and comprehension (item 7), and the 9th item is a causality question about the cause of the disease (“Please list in order the 3 most important factors that you believe have contributed to your disease. The most important cause for me is __”). Each entry is scored on a 0–10 points range (with reverse scoring for entries 3, 4, and 7), with higher scores representing more negative perceptions and more severe symptoms of the disease. The original scale did not establish predefined cut-off scores for the ratings. However, based on the data from this study, the analysis using standard deviation methods suggests the following classification: a total score ranging from 0 to 29 indicates a low level of disease self-awareness, a score between 30 and 47 reflects a moderate level, and a score from 48 to 80 signifies a high level of disease self-awareness. Cronbach’s α coefficients for this scale was 0.691 in this study.

Data collection

Data were collected by Xu Y.X., Wang M.L. and Bao L., who had received the centralized training before the formal collection of questionnaires to minimize bias in the interpretation of scale entries. Unified instructions were provided to explain the questionnaire’s content and completion guidelines, allowing respondents to complete the questionnaire independently. For patients who had difficulty understanding any part of the questionnaire, clarifications were offered in a neutral manner. For those unable to complete the questionnaire themselves, the investigator recorded responses as dictated by the respondent. All questionnaires were collected on-site, with patients reminded to promptly correct and supplement any incorrect or missing items.

Data Analysis

SPSS (Win version 26.0) was used to analyze the data. The normality of the study variables was tested with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between groups were made using the χ2 test. For variables that did not meet the application conditions of the χ2 test, Fisher’s exact probability method was used. Regression analysis was performed using a binary logistic regression model, with level of HISB as the dependent variable to analyze the factors influencing the “low level” of HISB and were expressed as ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The questionnaire completion rate for this investigation was 95.1% (274/288). Table 2 presents the general characteristics of the study population. The mean age of the respondents was 41.4 ± 13.3 years old and the sex ratio was balanced (149 male/125 female). Most of respondents were in their middle age (50.7%), married (74.1%), living in towns (79.9%) and having a bachelor’s degree or higher (63.5%). Only 5.1% of respondents do not have health insurance, and 79.1% of respondents have income of family more than 5,000 yuan monthly.

Oral health status

As shown in Table 2, the largest part patients (42.7%) have been diagnosed with periodontitis for more than 3 years in this study. Stage I of periodontitis accounted for 42.7%, Stage II accounted for 34.3%, while Stage III and IV accounted for 43.8%. Most of the patients (62.4%) had other concomitant oral diseases. Patients without family history of periodontitis were in majority (59.5%).

Health information-related content

As shown in Tables 2 and 50.4% of the participants had experience of seeking information for their families or friends. 89.1% of the participants had access to the internet and the majority of the participants had higher level of understandability (71.9%) and satisfaction (70.1%) of the health information.

The average score of the HISB scale was 3.68 ± 0.40 in this study. Discussion

Poor adherence in patients with periodontitis has been a major concern in the area of dentistry. It is clear from the above that HISB is a prerequisite for behavioral change in health promotion [8]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of HISB in periodontitis patients, with the aim of investigating the level of behavior and the associated influencing factors. The results showed that the HISB level of periodontitis patients in present study is acceptable but still needs to be improved. We found that HISB of periodontitis patients was associated with age, self-efficacy, understandability and satisfaction of the information.

The HISB score of the patients in this research is higher than the level of patients with systemic chronic diseases [27, 34], which may be due to the lower proportion of elderly people in the present study, and the younger patients were more concerned about their oral health. According to the mean scores of four dimensions above, we can see that patients have a positive attitude and strong demand for health information related to periodontitis, which are good signs. The average scores were 4.24 and 4.40 (maximum score = 5), respectively.

Doctors, nurses and healthcare organizations ranked as top 3 sources of HISB in present study (Table 1), which was consistent with the results of previous study conducted by Diviani et al. [35]. But the point is not just about trust, but also about behavioral change. Patients’ desire for information and participation in healthcare decision-making may require longer interaction times to be reflected in information-seeking communication behaviors [36]. With the current high outpatient attendance in the department of periodontics in China [37], the high percentage of time set aside for treatment, and the concomitant understaffing of nursing staff and assistants, additional interaction time is a challenge. HISB consists of two forms, online and offline. The Internet has become the primary medium for online HISB [8]. It is gradually replacing media such as paper-based information, television and radio. The Internet penetration rate in China reached 76.4% [38]. However, in this survey, the score of using Internet was ranked sixth, which may be due to the credibility or limitations in online health information that makes people confused and worried [36]. Also, ineffective hyperlinks and poor navigation make online health information inaccessible to the patients [39]. In order to solve the above difficulties, tele-dentistry can be developed for internet health information provision and patient management. As in the case of the promotion of Internet hospitals and the upgrading of their functions, it is important to take into account the convenience of operation and the specialization of information resources.

Compared to the patients with systemic chronic diseases in previous study, barriers to seeking information in our study are less [26, 34]. It may be related to the fact that more of the study subjects in this study suffered from periodontitis for more than 1 year. Regular follow-up visits give patients a better understanding of medical terminology related to periodontitis, and patients would trust the information provided by the hospital, which they would perceive as authoritative and reliable, and will not be uncertain about the information.

By using a binary logistic regression analysis, our study indicated that higher level of HISB were positively associated with younger age, higher self-efficacy, higher level of understandability and satisfaction of the information. The role of age in the HISB is controversial. Diviani et al.. did not find a correlation between age and HISB [35] and but Powell et al.. found increased information-seeking behaviors of women in 56–65,compare other age bands [40]. In this study, HISB level were higher in patients of other age groups compared with those over 60 years of age. The higher level of HISB in the 18 ~ 39 age group compared to patients aged 40 ~ 59 years suggests that younger participants were more likely to search for information. Middle-aged individuals tend to prioritize the practicality and reliability of information when processing health-related content. Older adults may encounter difficulties in processing health information, potentially due to declines in cognitive function, hearing, and responsiveness associated with aging. In contrast, younger individuals are generally more adept at processing information and translating it into action. As to the aspect of information carriers, we conclude from the results that the level of understandability and satisfaction of information are significant predictors of HISB for patients with periodontitis. Characteristics and utility of information carriers do affect patients’ behavior [34, 41]. This suggests that there is a need to provide health information that is understandable and needed. In present study, self-efficacy significantly and positively influences patients’ HISB. Patients with a high level of self-efficacy are more likely to exhibit elevated levels of HISB, whereas patients with low self-efficacy demonstrate lower HISB levels compared to those with medium self-efficacy. In clinical practice, self-efficacy denotes “how certain a patient feels about his/her ability to take the actions necessary for improvement of symptoms and maintenance of health” [42]. Individuals with high self-efficacy are more inclined to seek health-related information due to their strong beliefs. Furthermore, patients with elevated HISB levels tend to be proactive in acquiring information and adjusting their plans to address challenges in long-term oral health care. These patients are more likely to recognize the necessity and significance of treatment, cooperate actively with healthcare providers, achieve better treatment outcomes, and subsequently reinforce their self-efficacy.

In this study, there was no correlation between health literacy and HISB. In contrast to our results, previous surveys found that people with higher health literacy were more active information seekers [34, 43]. It is might be explained by the insidious onset of periodontitis. Patients who were able to actively seek medical care were already among those who were more concerned about their oral health. This study was conducted in a developed city in China, where healthcare resource saturation may be higher. This abundance of resources could obscure the relationship between health literacy and health-seeking behaviors (HISB). For instance, the prevalence of public health initiatives and accessible dental clinics might lead residents to encounter sufficient oral health information passively, diminishing the need to actively seek it out and thus weakening the expected correlation [44]. Additionally, as previously noted, limited health literacy tends to be more common in areas with lower socioeconomic conditions. The population in this study, drawn from a developed city, likely has different priorities and access to health information. The fast-paced urban lifestyle may further mask the influence of health literacy on HISB, as individuals might prioritize convenience and quick solutions offered by local healthcare services over actively pursuing in-depth health knowledge.

Based on the CMIS model, illness perception did not show a correlation with HISB in our study, suggesting that patients with periodontitis are still not sufficiently sensitized to the threat of the disease. In our study, the self-perception score, as measured by the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), was 38.24 ± 8.82, indicating a low to moderate level of self-perception. This finding may be associated with the relatively mild and non-life-threatening nature of the pain linked to the physical discomfort caused by periodontitis. As Machado et al. found that patients with periodontitis tend to hold a devalued view of their illness in comparison to other chronic diseases [45].

There are several limitations of this study that must be recognized. The population of this study was from a single center and the behavior was examined at a single time point and longitudinal observations were lacking. In future, it is necessary to design multicenter and longitudinal studies to observe HISB of patients with periodontitis over an extended period of time. It has been noted in the literature that HISB predisposes patients to self-diagnosis, leading to nonadherence to treatment [46]. The present study did not track patients’ late follow-up visits due to the time of observation, but loss of visits could be considered as an observational outcome in later studies.

Conclusion

The level of HISB among Chinese periodontitis patients remains to be improved, and it is crucial to encourage patients to be proactive in learning health information. HISB were significantly associated with age, self-efficacy, degree of understandability and satisfaction of the information carriers. More attention should be paid to aged patients and those with low self-efficacy level. It is essential to provide them with health information that is easily understandable and tailored to their specific requirements.
