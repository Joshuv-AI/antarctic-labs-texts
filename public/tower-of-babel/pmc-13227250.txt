A case of severe Legionella pneumonia treated with omadacycline and nemonoxacin
Authors: Tingrui Zhao, Yukai Chen, He Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe Legionella pneumophila pneumonia carries high mortality, and treatment is challenged by emerging resistance to conventional fluoroquinolones/macrolides and diagnostic delays. Novel agents such as omadacycline and nemonoxacin show theoretical promise, yet robust clinical evidence in legionellosis is lacking. We report a 59-year-old man with severe community-acquired pneumonia(sCAP) who initially received empiric ceftazidime-avibactam plus nemonoxacin. Respiratory failure did not improve, and the inflammatory markers did not decline. Subsequent bronchoalveolar lavage fluid metagenomic next-generation sequencing(BALF-mNGS) and urinary Legionella antigen confirmed Legionella pneumonia. We then switched to dual therapy with omadacycline and nemonoxacin. The combination led to rapid improvements in inflammatory markers, hypoxemia, and creatine kinase levels. This case provides a clinical rationale for using omadacycline plus nemonoxacin as salvage therapy in severe Legionella pneumonia when conventional regimens fail.

Introduction

Legionella pneumonia is increasingly recognized as a significant public health threat, with rising incidence and substantial mortality, particularly in vulnerable populations. Its nonspecific clinical and radiographic features often delay diagnosis, while conventional diagnostic tools remain suboptimal [[1], [2], [3]].

Antibiotic therapy is the cornerstone of treatment, with fluoroquinolones and macrolides recommended as first-line agents [4]. However, treatment is increasingly challenged by emerging drug resistance—reported in up to 16.3%—along with concerns over adverse effects and drug-drug interactions [5].

Here we present a 59-year-old male with severe Legionella pneumonia who failed initial empirical therapy. Following pathogen confirmation, treatment was switched to a dual regimen combining omadacycline and nemonoxacin, which resulted in rapid clinical and laboratory improvement. This report provides real-world evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of this combination in severe, refractory Legionella pneumonia.

Case description

A 59-year-old male was admitted to the hospital with the chief complaint of "fever accompanied by generalized weakness for two days". Two days prior to admission, the patient suddenly experienced generalized weakness and limb paralysis without obvious case. He was sent to the emergency room, where his body temperature was measured at 40.5 °C, accompanied by chest tightness and shortness of breath. There was no cough, expectoration, dizziness, headache, nausea, or vomiting. On admission, his vital signs were as follows: body temperature 36.6 °C, heart rate 85 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, and blood pressure 123/69 mmHg. Wet rales were heard in both lungs. No other significant abnormalities were noted. The patient had no genetic disorders or chronic diseases such as hypertension or diabetes.

Admission laboratory findings were as follows: white blood cell count (WBC) 16.17 × 109/L, neutrophil percentage (NE%) 95%, lymphocyte percentage (L%) 2.7%, lymphocyte count (L#) 0.37 × 109/L, absolute neutrophil count 11.59 × 109/L, hemoglobin (Hb) 112 g/L, platelet count (PLT) 185 × 109/L. C-reactive protein (CRP) was 200.74 mg/L, and serum amyloid protein was >288.00 mg/L. Serum potassium was 3.42 mmol/L, sodium was 137.8 mmol/L, and chloride was 102.3 mmol/L. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 64 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was 155 U/L, albumin was 28.8 g/L, creatinine was 72 μmol/L, and creatine kinase (CK) was 4667 U/L. Hepatitis C antibody was positive, while hepatitis C virus RNA was below the detection limit. Anti-PL-7 antibody (Anti-threonyl-tRNA synthetase (PL-7) antibody, Hunan Hao Oubo Biomedical Co., Ltd), anti-PM-Sc110 antibody(Autoimmune Myositis Antibody Profile Detection Kit, Hunan Kangqing Biotechnology Co., Ltd) were positive, and antinuclear antibody was 1:100 weakly positive, . KL-6 was 218 U/mL. The patient's blood gas results indicated hypoxemia (Table 1). Urine routine showed yellow and clear urine, with 4+ urine occult blood and negative urine leukocyte esterase. Chest Computered Tomography(CT) revealed bilateral lung inflammation, more prominent in the lower lobes (Fig. 1). Nucleic acid tests for novel coronavirus, influenza A virus, influenza B virus, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, mycoplasma, and chlamydia were all negative. Initial empirical treatment with nemonoxacin 0.5g ivgtt and ceftazidime-avibactam 2.5g q8h ivgtt for anti-infection was ineffective. The patient's respiratory failure did not improve (Table 1), and the inflammatory markers did not decline.

Bronchoscopy with alveolar lavage under general anesthesia was performed on July 24, along with pathogenic examinations of the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid(BALF). The bronchoscope showed a patent trachea, a sharp carina, and no obvious secretions. A small amount of yellow purulent sputum was seen gushing out from the lateral segment of the left lower lobe. The bronchi of all levels in the right lung were patent, and no obvious secretions were found. The cytology of the alveolar lavage fluid showed a colorless and transparent appearance, the nucleated cell count was 120 × 106/L, with neutrophils accounting for 65% and lymphocytes for 25%. No acid-fast bacilli were found in the concentrated smear, and the cultures for bacteria, fungi, and tuberculosis were all negative. BALF-mNGS (MGISEQ-200, Wuhan MGI Tech Co., Ltd) detected 2530 sequences of Legionella pneumophila DNA. The Legionella antigen (Legionella Urinary Antigen Rapid Test Kit, Binax NOW) in the midstream urine was positive. The final diagnosis was severe pneumonia (Legionella pneumophila). The anti-infection regimen was adjusted to nemonoxacin + omadacycline. After the adjustment, the peripheral blood inflammatory markers decreased (Table 2), the blood gas analysis showed improvement in hypoxemia (Table 1), the creatine kinase returned to normal, and the bedside chest X-ray showed absorption of the lesions (Fig. 2). On August 5, a reexamination of the chest CT revealed multiple patchy and flocculent density-increasing shadows in both lungs (Fig. 1), suggesting a radiological possibility of organizing pneumonia secondary to infection. Therefore, methylprednisolone sodium succinate for injection 40 mg qd ivgtt was added for anti-inflammatory treatment. Discussion

We report a case of severe Legionella pneumonia that was successfully managed with omadacycline plus nemonoxacin after initial empiric therapy failed. Although fluoroquinolones and macrolides remain the recommended first-line agents, the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance [6,7]—along with suboptimal response to monotherapy in some severely ill patients, underscores an urgent need to validate novel antibiotics in clinical practice.

The patient met criteria for sCAP per Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines(IDSA) [8]: community-onset fever, inflammatory infiltrates on chest CT, PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≤250, multilobe involvement on CT, and confusion at admission. According to IDSA guidelines, hospitalised patients with sCAP should receive combination therapy, i.e., a potent beta-lactam plus either a macrolide or a respiratory fluoroquinolone—the latter being particularly suitable for sCAP caused by Legionella.

Traditionally, bacterial pathogens causing CAP are divided into typical and atypical groups. Typical pathogens include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, and aerobic Gram-negative bacilli. Atypical pathogens include Legionella species, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Chlamydia pneumoniae [9]. In China, macrolide resistance exceeds 90% in non-meningitis S. pneumoniae and 80% in M. pneumoniae [10,11]. Fortunately, penicillins, cephalosporins, and quinolones still work well against S. pneumoniae, and quinolones/tetracyclines remain active against M. pneumoniae [[10], [11], [12], [13]].

Nemonoxacin is a non-fluorinated quinolone with broad-spectrum activity, covering multidrug-resistant organisms including penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae, methicillin-resistant S. aureus, H. influenzae, M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, and Legionella pneumophila [14,15]. A multicentre randomised controlled trial has further demonstrated that nemonoxacin is non-inferior to levofloxacin for the treatment of CAP, with high activity against common and atypical pathogens [16]. Mortality in sCAP remains daunting—exceeding 30% when patients require invasive mechanical ventilation plus vasopressor support [17]. Given that the patient was critically ill and there was a possibility of multidrug-resistant bacteria and atypical pathogens, we initially selected a broad-spectrum treatment regimen that included a novel beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor and a novel non-fluoroquinolone antibiotic. However, this initial empiric therapy failed, as defined by persistent fever, worsening oxygenation, and rising inflammatory markers 48 hours after pathogen-directed treatment.

Concurrent aetiological workup eventually identified L. pneumophila by both BALF-mNGS and urinary Legionella antigen. Legionella pneumophila is frequently associated with artificial water environments, commonly isolated from air conditioning cooling water, shower water, hot springs, and similar sources. Sporadic cases or outbreaks predominantly occur during summer and autumn. The patient denied any contact with Legionella-infected individuals or recent travel. However, there was a record of using an air conditioner at home, which may have been the source of infection.

Two questions then arose: why did nemonoxacin monotherapy fail against Legionella, and how should we adjust therapy? Theoretically, nemonoxacin as a non-fluorinated quinolone has a lower MIC90 for Legionella than levofloxacin or azithromycin [14,15], and clinical trials have confirmed its non-inferiority to levofloxacin in CAP [16]. Nevertheless, in vitro susceptibility doesn't guarantee clinical cure, especially in critically ill patients. Some experts have suggested that for severe or refractory Legionella pneumonia, a fluoroquinolone plus azithromycin may be considered [3]. Yet we were concerned about the potential for QT prolongation, elevated liver enzymes, and drug-drug interactions associated with adding a macrolide. At this point, we turned to omadacycline. Compared with traditional tetracyclines, omadacycline has low plasma protein binding, high tissue penetration, and achieves markedly elevated concentrations in alveolar macrophages and epithelial lining fluid. It is not affected by the two major tetracycline resistance mechanisms (efflux pumps and ribosomal protection), and no dose adjustment is required in patients with hepatic or renal impairment [18,19]. The efficacy of omadacycline in Legionella pneumonia has been documented in several case reports [[20], [21], [22]]. Although successful monotherapy with omadacycline has been described, given that our patient had severe disease and had already failed initial nemonoxacin, we ultimately opted for dual therapy with nemonoxacin plus omadacycline. The subsequent clinical response was rapid: inflammatory markers, oxygenation, and creatine kinase levels all improved promptly.

The use of glucocorticoids involved careful deliberation. Chastre et al. reported that among 20 patients with Legionella infection, 5 developed pulmonary fibrosis changes, with 4 deaths and post-mortem confirmation of fibrosis [23]. Other reports have described interstitial lung changes following Legionella infection, including organizing pneumonia and acute fibrinous organizing pneumonia [24]. This may be related to Legionella-induced activation of alveolar macrophages or epithelial cells, resulting in extensive fibrin-rich alveolar exudates or fibroblast activation, ultimately leading to interstitial changes and fibrosis [25]. Beyond that, the benefit of glucocorticoids in sCAP has been substantiated by clinical studies such as the CAPE COD trial [26]; moreover, multiple meta-analyses of RCTs on glucocorticoids in sCAP have been published in recent years [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. The 2024 Society of Critical Care Medicine guideline and the 2025 American Thoracic Society CAP guideline have expanded the indication for glucocorticoids to all patients with sCAP (excluding influenza pneumonia), no longer restricting it to those with septic shock [32,33]. A 5- to 7-day regimen of either hydrocortisone (<400 mg/d) or methylprednisolone (40–80 mg/d) is a recommended treatment option [34]. Patients with high oxygen requirements and elevated inflammatory markers are particularly likely to benefit. Therefore, irrespective of whether the patient had radiological suggestion of organizing pneumonia secondary to infection or severe pneumonia per se, glucocorticoid use was considered justified.

The patient tested positive for anti-PL-7 and anti-PM-Sc110 antibodies, but there was no Raynaud's phenomenon, mechanic's hands, arthritis, rash, or typical muscle weakness. Only on the 5 August chest CT were there subtle interstitial changes, and the patient did not meet the 2011 Solomon criteria for antisynthetase syndrome. Creatine kinase had already declined from 4667 U/L to normal with antibiotics alone (before any glucocorticoids), whereas antisynthetase syndrome-associated myositis is typically steroid-dependent. KL-6 remained normal throughout. Consequently, we are inclined to interpret these antibodies as a transient immune response triggered by severe infection, a phenomenon also observed in COVID-19 [[35], [36], [37]]. Finally, after treatment with methylprednisolone, the patient's pulmonary infiltrates resolved significantly. On later outpatient follow-up, the patient's autoimmune-related antibodies had all turned negative. This strongly suggests that the earlier positive anti-PL-7 and anti-PM-Sc110 antibodies were most likely caused by infection.

Finally, we must honestly acknowledge several limitations. First, the initial use of ceftazidime-avibactam was not strongly indicated. Second, We could not determine whether Legionella was resistant to nemonoxacin, as we were unable to culture the bacteria and perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Third, we did not test omadacycline alone after nemonoxacin failed, so we cannot confirm whether combination therapy was superior to omadacycline monotherapy. Forth, it is unclear whether the radiological improvement came from adequate antibiotics or from added glucocorticoids. For sCAP, clinicians should balance severity, resistance risk, and atypical coverage—avoid over-reliance on broad-spectrum agents or unnecessary combinations, and use novel beta-lactamase inhibitors only when clearly indicated. In the future, More studies are needed to compare omadacycline alone versus combined with nemonoxacin in severe Legionella pneumonia.

Summary

This case suggests that for severe Legionella pneumonia that fails initial treatment, the combination of omadacycline and nemonoxacin may be a viable salvage option. More importantly, when deciding on the initial anti-infective regimen for sCAP, we need to stick closely to solid evidence-based medicine and a thorough assessment of bacterial resistance risk. That's the only way to avoid both over-treatment and the mistake of failing to cover the likely pathogens. Meanwhile, glucocorticoids may be beneficial for infection-related organizing pneumonia as suggested by radiology, but timing and dosage must be carefully individualised. Our aim in reporting this case is not to promote any ‘novel therapy’, but rather to share a real-world clinical decision-making process that encompasses successes, errors, and uncertainties.
