Abdominal pain as the sole initial manifestation of ANCA-positive EGPA with myocarditis: a case report and literature review
Authors: Wang Zhongkai, Li Ruijie, Liu Liping, Wu Changyong, Sun Huang, Peng Yunzhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) typically presents with asthma, but we report an anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-positive EGPA case with an unusual presentation: isolated abdominal pain as the initial manifestation of systemic vasculitis, which progressed rapidly to life-threatening cardiac involvement in the absence of overt respiratory symptoms at onset.Case presentationA 70-year-old female presented with abdominal pain and was found to have eosinophilia with elevated cardiac biomarkers. Despite normal coronary angiography, cardiac magnetic resonance revealed left ventricular hypoperfusion and an apical thrombus, leading to a diagnosis of eosinophilic myocarditis and vasculitis by biopsy. Symptoms and thrombus resolved with glucocorticoids without anticoagulation. Cyclophosphamide was added for persistent cardiac dysfunction.ConclusionsANCA-positive EGPA can present atypically with isolated abdominal pain and rapid cardiac involvement. Therefore, this triad warrants urgent evaluation and early immunosuppression. Thrombus resolution without anticoagulation further supports an inflammation-driven pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05380-6.

Introduction

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis, characterized by asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis (often with nasal polyps), eosinophilia and granulomatous or vasculitic involvement of several organs [1]. It is crucial to recognize that while EGPA is classified as an ANCA-associated vasculitis, only a subset of patients (approximately 30–40%) are ANCA-positive, typically exhibiting perinuclear (P)-ANCA targeting myeloperoxidase (MPO). This serological dichotomy correlates with distinct clinical phenotypes. ANCA-positive patients more frequently present with features of systemic vasculitis, such as glomerulonephritis and peripheral neuropathy. In contrast, ANCA-negative patients demonstrate a stronger association with tissue eosinophilia [2, 3]. Notably, cardiac involvement, which is a key prognostic factor linked to severe outcomes such as heart failure, arrhythmias, and intracardiac thrombosis, is observed more frequently in ANCA-negative patients and accounts for approximately half of all EGPA-related deaths [4, 5].

The diagnosis of cases lacking initial respiratory symptoms, while uncommon, is often challenging. This is particularly true for ANCA-negative patients, in whom the absence of a specific serological marker can contribute to diagnostic delays. Conversely, although cardiac involvement is more frequently observed in ANCA-negative disease, its occurrence as the primary manifestation in ANCA-positive patients represents an atypical presentation. Such a deviation from the classic clinical phenotype has the potential to be diagnostically misleading. The role of multimodal cardiac assessment, including advanced imaging and biopsy, remains critical yet insufficiently explored in such scenarios [6]. Here, we report ANCA-positive EGPA in a 70-year-old woman presenting solely with abdominal pain, who developed dyspnea/fatigue from eosinophilic myocarditis without a documented history of asthma or chronic rhinosinusitis. This underscores that unexplained abdominal pain concomitant with eosinophilia and troponin elevation should raise immediate suspicion for a systemic vasculitis like EGPA, warranting urgent investigation for both gastrointestinal and cardiac involvement.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old female patient had a 2-year history of gastric ulcer and no other diseases presented with postprandial bloating for 7 days, incomplete symptom relief after defecation, and significant worsening of bloating accompanied by upper abdominal pain, difficulty breathing, and fatigue over the past 2 days. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed isolated eosinophilia (1.05 × 10⁹/L, 20.00%), with normal white blood cell count and no other hematologic abnormalities (see Table 1). Cardiac-specific biomarkers were significantly elevated, with high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs-cTnI) at 0.83 ng/mL and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) at 110.08 pg/mL. Coagulation studies identified a hyperfibrinolytic state characterized by D-dimer elevation to 1.91 mg/L and increased fibrin degradation products at 8.14 mg/L, while inflammatory markers were notably elevated with C-reactive protein (CRP) at 37.39 mg/L. 12-lead electrocardiography displayed sinus rhythm with diffuse ST-segment depression (0.10–0.30 mV) and symmetrical T-wave inversions in the anterior and lateral precordial leads. Cross-sectional abdominal imaging demonstrated significant gaseous distension throughout the abdominal cavity accompanied by thickening of the gastric antral wall, though without radiographic evidence of mechanical obstruction or other structural abnormalities; concurrently, a plain chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed scattered faint ground-glass opacities in both upper and lower lung lobes, suggesting possible poor pulmonary ventilation-perfusion with slight exudation. Additionally, coronary CT indicated possible moderate stenosis in the anterior descending branch, while showing no significant abnormalities in the thoracic aorta or pulmonary artery CT (Fig. 1A-E). Empirical therapeutic measures including high-dose proton-pump inhibitors, antispasmodic therapy, and prokinetic agents failed to yield significant clinical improvement. A gastroscopic examination was initially planned to investigate the gastric wall thickening and abdominal pain. However, the procedure was postponed due to the need to first rule out acute coronary syndrome, as sedation or anesthesia posed a significant risk in the context of her progressively elevated cardiac troponin levels. Following coronary angiography, which definitively excluded acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the patient’s general condition remained poor (Fig. 1F). After the risks and necessity of the procedure were explained, she declined the proposed gastroscopy and colonoscopy due to her fragile state. High-resolution chest CT, while revealing scattered ground-glass opacities, showed no evidence of bronchial wall thickening or other structural changes suggestive of chronic airway disease. The patient’s persistent fatigue and abdominal pain precluded her ability to cooperate with pulmonary function testing, including fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) measurement, to objectively exclude obstructive impairment during the acute phase. Even after clinical stabilization, her profound fatigue and the primary focus on managing cardiac involvement limited the feasibility of additional elective procedures such as sinus CT. However, the absence of wheezing on auscultation, the lack of a clinical history of asthma or chronic sinusitis, and the aforementioned unremarkable CT findings collectively made the presence of typical asthmatic airway disease at the time of presentation less likely.

On hospital day 3, laboratory reassessment showed progression of peripheral eosinophilia, hs-cTnI (peaking at 3.10 ng/mL), and BNP (see Table 1). Fecal occult blood testing was positive. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated multiple pathological features including poor perfusion of the mid-to-distal left ventricular subendocardium with focal subendocardial enhancement and a small thrombus at the left ventricular apex (Fig. 2). Given the patients high bleeding risk (concurrent antiplatelet use, gastric ulcer history, and positive fecal occult blood), therapeutic anticoagulation was withheld. The treatment strategy prioritized addressing the inflammatory etiology of the thrombus with immunosuppression over anticoagulation. Comprehensive serological evaluation was notable for a positive ANCA screen by indirect immunofluorescence, showing a perinuclear-ANCA (P-ANCA) pattern at a titer of 1:10 (reference range < 1:10). Concurrently, antigen-specific immunoblot testing confirmed strongly positive results for anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) antibody (graded as 3 + according to the institutional reference standard). Additionally, anti-Ro52 antibody and antinuclear antibody were also positive. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was significantly elevated at 42 mm/h, prompting immediate rheumatological consultation and initiation of systemic vasculitis workup.

Transthoracic echocardiography performed on the fifth hospital day revealed significant structural abnormalities including pronounced left ventricular endocardial thickening, severe diastolic dysfunction classified as grade III according to current guidelines, and globally impaired myocardial deformation with severely reduced systolic function (Fig. 3). Subsequent right ventricular septum endomyocardial biopsy obtained on the sixth hospital day demonstrated extensive interstitial infiltration by eosinophils (≥ 50 cells per high-power field) accompanied by focal myocyte necrosis, interstitial edema, inflammation, mucoid degeneration, hyaline degeneration, and loss of vascular integrity. Critically, examination revealed definitive evidence of vasculitis, characterized by inflammatory disruption of the integrity of small venules (Fig. 2). Masson staining further highlighted the presence of collagen fibers, indicative of fibrotic changes. These histopathological findings, in conjunction with negative comprehensive evaluation for parasitic infections and absence of evidence for clonal hematopoiesis, conclusively established the diagnosis of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis with predominant cardiac involvement.

High-dose (80 mg daily) methylprednisolone started on seventh hospital day led to marked clinical improvement in abdominal pain and respiratory symptoms; thereafter, it is gradually reduced to 60 mg per day over the next 4 days, and then further reduced to 40 mg per day after another 3 days, and serial blood tests documented an even faster fall in peripheral eosinophils, which dropped sharply to < 0.01 × 10⁹/L (< 0.01%) after 2 days of glucocorticoid therapy. During tapering of the steroids, testing was repeated, where the values were on rebound up to 0.01–0.04 × 109/L (0.2%−0.7%). Cardiac biomarkers were declining progressively, with hs-cTnI decreasing from the peak concentration of 3.10 ng/mL to 0.69 ng/mL after 10 days of glucocorticoid treatment. Further, values were on decline until 0.19 ng/mL on the sixteenth day at Hospital. The BNP level demonstrated a characteristic pattern of transient elevation peaking at 973.84 pg/mL following endomyocardial biopsy procedure before progressively decreasing to 749.72 pg/mL with continued immunosuppressive therapy. On the thirteenth day at hospital, serum total immunoglobulin E (IgE) level was measured and found to be elevated at 215.0 IU/mL. During the treatment, the coagulation factors return to normal levels D-dimer down to 0.87 mg/L and fibrin degradation products down to 4.10 mg/L [Table 1].

Left heart acoustic perfusion ultrasound evaluation performed after 10 days of steroid therapy demonstrated complete resolution of the previously identified apical thrombus despite absence of therapeutic anticoagulation, accompanied by significant improvement in left ventricular systolic function (Fig. 3). In light of persistent echocardiographic evidence of myocardial involvement despite clinical improvement, a single intravenous infusion of 400 mg of cyclophosphamide was administered on the eighteenth hospital day. This reduced dose was chosen due to the patient’s advanced age and significant comorbidities (coronary heart disease and gastric ulcer) to minimize the risk of treatment-related complications. The patient tolerated this combined immunosuppressive regimen well, with no significant adverse effects other than a rapid decrease in platelet count from 280 × 10⁹/L to 149 × 10⁹/L. Following achievement of sustained clinical stability, the patient was discharged from the hospital on a maintenance regimen consisting of oral prednisone initiated at 40 mg daily with planned biweekly 5 mg dose reductions and pulsed intravenous cyclophosphamide administered at a dose of 200 mg every 14 days.

The patient returned for the first follow-up assessment 14 days after discharge to evaluate her response to glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive therapy. At this visit, she reported persistent fatigue but denied any other discomfort such as abdominal pain or dyspnea. Repeat laboratory tests showed improvement in complete blood count, cardiac necrosis biomarkers, and BNP levels compared to previous results while the platelet count remained at the previously low level of 149 × 10⁹/L. However, serum creatinine was noted to be mildly elevated (126.63 µmol/L), up from 110.94 µmol/L at the time of discharge. Liver function tests were within normal limits. It was decided to withhold the scheduled cyclophosphamide pulse for this visit. The patient was instructed to continue taking 40 mg of oral prednisone acetate daily and to return for a follow-up in another two weeks. The patient returned to the outpatient clinic as scheduled 15 days later. Her fatigue remained largely unchanged, with no other new symptoms. Repeated investigations confirmed further improvement in her complete blood count, cardiac necrosis biomarkers, and BNP. The platelet count showed a significant increase to 261 × 10⁹/L compared to the previous level. Notably, serum creatinine of the patient had continued to rise to 130.0 µmol/L. Liver function tests were within normal limits, while CRP and ESR had returned to normal (1 mg/L and 14 mm/h, respectively). Echocardiography demonstrated that the left ventricular endocardial thickening had improved compared to prior studies, with no significant deterioration in cardiac structure or global function. The treatment plan was adjusted as follows: the patient was to continue oral prednisone acetate, with the dose tapered to 35 mg daily, then further reduced to 30 mg daily after two weeks for maintenance therapy. Additionally, oral cyclophosphamide was initiated at a dose of 50 mg daily to address the persistent myocardial injury, as evidenced by ongoing troponin elevation and diastolic dysfunction, with a plan for subsequent comprehensive reassessment and transition to a less toxic agent. She was scheduled for the next follow-up evaluation in one month to reassess her immunosuppressive and glucocorticoid regimen.

Discussion

This case illustrates an exceptionally rare presentation of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, characterized by isolated abdominal pain as the initial symptom followed by rapid cardiac involvement, all in the absence of classic respiratory prodromes. Although direct pathological evidence of eosinophilic gastroenteritis was not obtained due to the patient’s acute cardiac condition and subsequent refusal of gastroscopy, the rapid resolution of abdominal pain following glucocorticoid initiation strongly suggests it was a manifestation of systemic vasculitis [7, 8]. This clinical course, coupled with radiographic gastric antral wall thickening, starkly contrasts with the traditional diagnostic paradigm that prioritizes asthma or chronic sinusitis. The abrupt progression to life-threatening eosinophilic myocarditis underscores the potential for EGPA to mimic gastrointestinal disorders, leading to diagnostic delay.

This atypical presentation, with gastrointestinal involvement as the sole initial feature, is exceedingly rare, occurring in fewer than 5% of EGPA cases [9]. The presence of ANCA positivity, specifically anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) antibodies, is traditionally linked to renal and neurological vasculitis [10]. The detection of MPO-ANCA positivity in this case of predominant cardiorespiratory involvement challenges phenotypic stereotypes. The detection of MPO-ANCA in this case is consistent with the ANCA-positive subset of EGPA. However, unlike in microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) and granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) where ANCA-mediated pathways are central, the pathogenesis of EGPA is predominantly driven by Th2 lymphocytes and eosinophils, with ANCA potentially playing a secondary or modifying role in a subset of patients [11]. Notably, cardiac involvement, a major cause of mortality, is more frequently reported in ANCA-negative EGPA [4]. However, our case, alongside other sporadic reports, confirms that severe, histologically-proven eosinophilic myocarditis and vasculitis can also occur in MPO-ANCA-positive patients. This underscores that a severe cardiac phenotype is not the exclusive domain of ANCA-negative disease and cannot be predicted by ANCA status alone.

The diagnostic process highlights the necessity of comprehensive multimodal cardiac evaluation in atypical presentations. Coronary angiography first excluded acute coronary syndrome. Echocardiography then raised suspicion of infiltrative disease, while CMR characterized tissue pathology, identifying dysfunction, hypoperfusion, late gadolinium enhancement, and an apical thrombus. Ultimately, endomyocardial biopsy confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating dense eosinophilic infiltration alongside definitive vasculitic changes, which definitively differentiate EGPA from alternative diagnoses [6, 12]. Additional features, including myocyte necrosis and interstitial edema, accounted for the acute clinical progression. Regarding the sampling strategy, we targeted the right ventricular septum rather than the left ventricular apex, adhering to guideline recommendations aimed at minimizing procedural risks, such as perforation and embolism [13]. The fact that vasculitis was captured in a right ventricular sample also underscores the diffuse nature of the cardiac involvement in this case.

The atypical presentation necessitated a rigorous exclusion of alternate causes of eosinophilia and multi-organ involvement. Idiopathic eosinophilic myocarditis (IEM), a more common entity, and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia were considered but could not account for the systemic features [14, 15]. Critically, the histopathological demonstration of vasculitis on endomyocardial biopsy is a hallmark of EGPA and is not a feature of these conditions [16]. This finding is particularly significant because direct evidence of necrotizing vasculitis on endomyocardial biopsy is far rarer than the observation of eosinophilic myocarditis alone. It was classically reported by Frustaci et al. in Churg-Strauss syndrome and provides unequivocal evidence of systemic vasculitis involving the heart [17]. Thus, while our case does not fully meet the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria due to the absence of typical respiratory symptoms, it remains a clinically diagnosable EGPA, with the pivotal pathological finding of vasculitis anchoring the diagnosis. Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) was excluded by the strongly positive MPO-ANCA and the biopsy-proven vasculitis, which are definitive for an ANCA-associated vasculitis. Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) was deemed unlikely given its ANCA-negative nature and preference for medium-sized vessels [9, 18]. The confluence of ANCA positivity, multi-organ eosinophilic infiltration, and biopsy-proven small-vessel vasculitis provided a definitive diagnosis of EGPA. This histopathological evidence satisfies the core histological component of the current criteria, even in the absence of classical fibrinoid necrosis or granulomas [12, 19].

The rapid resolution of peripheral eosinophilia within 2 days of high-dose glucocorticoid therapy is typical for EGPA and served as an early biomarker of response [20]. Concurrently, the apical thrombus resolved with immunosuppression alone, confirming its inflammatory nature and validating this approach when anticoagulation is contraindicated. A transient BNP elevation post-biopsy, consistent with a procedural injury response [21], and persistent regional wall motion abnormalities indicated ongoing cardiac involvement. Consequently, cyclophosphamide was added for this severe, life-threatening EGPA. This sequential approach, beginning with initial low-dose intravenous administration and transitioning to oral cyclophosphamide maintenance, was implemented based on evidence of persistent smoldering disease activity. It aimed to prevent progressive cardiac damage while recognizing the need for eventual transition to a safer maintenance agent. This strategy aligns with guidelines that prioritize cyclophosphamide for induction of severe vasculitic disease [22]. Further supporting the uniqueness of this phenotype, analysis of serum obtained prior to glucocorticoid therapy revealed normal levels of key cytokines (IL-5: 0.60 pg/mL [ref: 0–5.9.9]; IL-6: 3.39 pg/mL [ref: 0–7]; IL-10: 2.45 pg/mL [ref: 0–9.5.5]; TNF-α: 1.35 pg/mL [ref: 0–8.5.5]) despite severe clinical activity [6, 23]. This dissociation between systemic cytokine levels and tissue inflammation, along with only a mild elevation in total IgE, underscores the phenotypic heterogeneity of EGPA. It suggests that the inflammatory drive in this ANCA-positive variant may be particularly localized, reinforcing the primacy of clinical and histopathological findings in guiding therapy [24–26].

This case provides critical clinical implications. It demonstrates that the triad of unexplained abdominal pain, eosinophilia, and troponin elevation should prompt urgent investigation for cardiac vasculitis, even without respiratory symptoms. A hyperfibrinolytic state may be a further indicator of cardiac involvement [4, 5]. The critical part of the assessment is coronary angiography to rule out ACS, CMR to evaluate myocardial structure and function, and endomyocardial biopsy if deemed necessary [6, 12]. In terms of management, the complete resolution of the cardiac thrombus with immunosuppression alone underscores the primacy of controlling the underlying inflammation, particularly when anticoagulation is high-risk [1]. Furthermore, while the pathophysiological role of MPO-ANCA in cardiac disease requires further clarification, the centrality of IL-5 across all EGPA variants underscores the potential of anti-IL-5 agents (such as mepolizumab or benralizumab) to mitigate eosinophilic injury and facilitate steroid-sparing in suitable patients, as evidenced in both ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative cases [24, 27]. In conclusion, vigilance for atypical presentations, guided by serial biomarker monitoring and aggressive immunosuppression based on disease severity, is paramount to improving outcomes in EGPA with cardiac involvement.

Conclusions

This case presents a rare ANCA-positive EGPA variant. Initial isolated abdominal pain lacking a history of respiratory symptoms rapidly progressed to life-threatening eosinophilic myocarditis. It demonstrates that unexplained abdominal pain combined with eosinophilia and troponin elevation necessitates urgent vasculitis evaluation, including cardiac assessment. The diagnosis was confirmed by multimodal imaging and endomyocardial biopsy demonstrating eosinophilic infiltration and vasculitis, after coronary angiography excluded acute coronary syndrome. The management strategy highlighted the inflammatory nature of the disease: Glucocorticoid-induced resolution of symptoms and eosinophilia, coupled with apical thrombus dissolution without anticoagulation. However, cyclophosphamide was required for persistent cardiac dysfunction, reflecting severe involvement and the need for aggressive immunosuppression.
