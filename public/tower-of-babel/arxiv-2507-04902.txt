Brill–Noether loci in genus ≤ 12 \leq 12
Richard Haburcak

Abstract
. A refined Brill–Noether theory seeks to determine which linear series are admitted by a “general” curve in a particular Brill–Noether locus. However, as Brill–Noether loci are not irreducible in general, a coarse answer is given by the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci. Via an analysis of unstable Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles on K3 surfaces, we distinguish Brill–Noether loci and provide expectations for the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci in general. Together with classical results, the refined Brill–Noether theory for curves of fixed gonality and on Hirzebruch surfaces, and explicit constructions, we identify the relative positions of all Brill–Noether loci in genus g ≤ 12 g\leq 12 .

In his proof of the Brill–Noether–Petri theorem in

When

We extract the philosophy that K3 surfaces predict the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci, witnessing the behavior of “general” curves in a Brill–Noether locus. Namely, we expect K3 surfaces with fixed Picard group
\mathbb{Z}[H]\oplus\mathbb{Z}[L]

is a lift of a

on smooth irreducible curves

, to capture the Brill–Noether theory of

[

1.
Background

We recall some background on Brill–Noether loci, some classical constructions giving containments and non-containments of Brill–Noether loci, and Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles on K3 surfaces. In particular, we recall some useful results to distinguish Brill–Noether loci.

Recall that the
Clifford index
of a line bundle
A\in\Pic(C)
is defined as

\Cliff(A)\coloneqq\deg(A)-2(h^{0}(C,A)-1),

and the
Clifford index of a curve
is defined as

\Cliff{C}\coloneqq\min\{\Cliff{A}\ |\ h^{0}(C,A),h^{1}(C,A)\geq 2\},

and a line bundle is said to
compute the Clifford index
of
\Cliff(A)=\Cliff(C)
.

1.1.
Brill–Noether loci

The Brill–Noether–Petri theorem implies that when
\rho(g,r,d)<0
, the Brill–Noether locus
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
is a proper subvariety of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
, and we assume
g,r,d
satsify
\rho(g,r,d)<0
when considering Brill–Noether loci. We note that Serre duality gives
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}=\mathcal{M}^{g-d+r-1}_{g,2g-2-d}
, thus we freely assume
d\leq g-1
throughout.

The geometry of Brill–Noether loci remains mysterious in general; even basic questions, such as the dimension, are unknown. The expected dimension of
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
is
3g-3+\rho(g,r,d)
, any component having at least the expected dimension, as shown in
[
58
]
. Additionally, the existence of a component of expected dimension is known only when
\rho
is not too negative, see for example
[
9
,
37
,
56
,
57
,
59
]
. Less is known about equidimensionality of components, which is known in the range
-3\leq\rho\leq-1
, assuming additionally that
g\geq 12
when
\rho=-3

[
20
,
58
]
.

Similarly, little is known about irreducibility of Brill–Noether loci in general, though loci with
\rho=-1
are known to be irreducible by work of Eisenbud–Harris in
[
22
]
, and loci with
r=2
and
d
sufficiently large are known to be irreducible by work of Teixidor i Bigas and the author
[
30
, Proposition 4.2]
, where sharp bounds are obtained. Furthermore, Brill–Noether loci may admit components of larger than expected dimension arising from curves of constant gonality (see
[
30
]
and
[
56
, Appendix A]
) or Castelnuovo curves (see
[
56
, Remark 1.4]
). Complicating the picture further, there may be multiple components of the expected dimension, as shown in
[
30
]
, where for
r\geq 3
and
g=\binom{r+2}{2}
, it is shown that
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,g-1}
admits (at least) two components of expected dimension, one where the general curve does not admit a theta characteristic of dimension
r
, and the other where the
g^{r}_{g-1}
is a theta characteristic.

A refined Brill–Noether theory may instead seek to characterize the linear series for a general curve in each component of a Brill–Noether locus. However, as the number of components and a description of each component of Brill–Noether loci is currently unknown, for the time being we content ourselves by understanding the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci, seeking to describe the relative position of components when possible. We note that using chains of loops or chains of elliptic curves in
[
56
,
59
]
, there is a useful description of some components when
\rho
is not too negative.

There are many containments among Brill–Noether loci. For example, Clifford’s theorem implies the containments
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,2r}\subset\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,2}
. There are also the
trivial containments

\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d+1}
obtained by adding a base point; and
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{r-1}_{g,d-1}
when
r\geq 2
obtained by subtracting a non-base point, as observed in
[
23
,
45
]
. The refined Brill–Noether theory for curves of fixed gonality
[
55
,
36
,
40
]
and curves on Hirzebruch surfaces
[
41
]
provide many containments and non-containments of Brill–Noether loci for
r=1
and
r=2
, see
Section

1.3
for more detail on the former. Furthermore, non-containments of Brill–Noether loci, proven using limit linear series techniques, have been useful int he study of the Kodaira dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{g}
in work of Eisenbud–Harris in
[
21
]
and Farkas in
[
23
]
.

Since the work of Lazarsfeld in
[
43
]
, K3 surfaces have been a staple of Brill–Noether theory. Indeed, curves on K3 surfaces with prescribed Picard group have proven useful in distinguishing Brill–Noether loci. In
[
24
]
, Farkas studies the gonality of curves on K3 surfaces in

, distinguishing certain Brill–Noether loci and giving a new proof of the fact that the Kodaira dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{23}
is at least
2
. Work of Lelli–Chiesa in
[
46

on the Donagi–Morrison conjecture, which asks when Brill–Noether special linear series on a curve lift to the K3 surface, has yielded non-containments of Brill–Noether loci of the form
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,e}
and
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,e}
for a range of

, via an analysis of destabilizing subsheaves of Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles. However, similar non-containments
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
for

appear significantly more complicated, though some are obtained in
[
5
]
for
s=3
. Inspired by the known non-containments, in
[
5

, Auel and the author defined the

as those admitting no trivial containments, and conjectured that the expected maximal Brill–Noether loci should in fact admit no containments, except for the known exceptions in genus

. Limit linear series techniques and non-containments coming from the refined Brill–Noether theory for curves of fixed gonality in
[

[
6

via an analysis of Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles on K3 surfaces.

1.2.
Classical (non-)containments of (components of) Brill–Noether loci

We recall a few classical results on linear series on curves. We begin with classical results of Castelnuovo and Severi restricting the genus of curves.

Theorem 1.1

(Castelnuovo’s bound)
.

If
C
admits a basepoint free and birationally very ample
g^{r}_{d}
, then letting

m=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{r-1}\right\rfloor\text{ and }\epsilon=d-1-m(r-1),

we have

g(C)\leq\frac{m(m-1)(r-1)}{2}+m\epsilon.

Theorem 1.2

(Castelnuovo–Severi inequality)
.

Suppose
f_{1}:C\to C_{1}
and
f_{2}:C\to C_{2}
are two non-constant maps to curves of genus
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, respectively, of degree
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
, respectively. If
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
do not factor through a common map
\tilde{f}:C\to\widetilde{C}
, then

g(C)\leq(d_{1}-1)(d_{2}-1)+d_{1}g_{1}+d_{2}g_{2}.

We now give containments of Brill–Noether loci for curves with a divisor of Clifford index
1
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
g\geq 7
,
r\geq 2
, and
d\leq g-1
be positive integers. If
d-2r=1
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}=\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,2}
.

Proof.

Let
C\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
. We aim to show that
\Cliff(C)=0
, whereby Clifford’s Theorem shows that
C\in\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,2}
and we have
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,2}
. The result then follows from the fact that
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,2}
is contained in every Brill–Noether locus.

Let
D
be an effective divisor on
C
of type
g^{r}_{d}
. As
\Cliff(D)=d-2r=1
,
\Cliff(C)\leq 1
.
Suppose for contradiction that
\Cliff(C)=1
. Martens shows in
[
50
, 2.56]
that for an effective divisor
D
on
C
, with
\deg(D)\leq g-1
, and
\Cliff(D)=\Cliff(C)
odd, we have
h^{0}(C,D)<\Cliff(C)+2
, except if
g=3(\Cliff(C)+1)
. As
g\geq 7
, we must have
3\leq r+1=h^{0}(C,D)<3
, which is a contradiction. Thus
\Cliff(C)=0
, as desired.
∎

Unfortunately, such characterizations of Brill–Noether loci for linear series of higher Clifford index are not so simple.

In
[
38
]
, Lange shows that the moduli space
M(\gamma,k)
of curves with a degree
k
map to a curve of geometric genus
\gamma\geq 1
is
\dim M(\gamma,k)=2g-2-(2k-3)(\gamma-1)
. In particular, if
C\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
, from the determinantal nature of
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
, we must have

3g-3+\rho(g,r,d)\leq\dim\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\leq\dim M(\gamma,k)=2g-2-(2k-1)(\gamma-1),

which we restate below for reference.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
38
]
)
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
and suppose
C
admits a degree
k
map to a curve of geometric genus
\gamma\geq 1
, then

3g-3+\rho(g,r,d)\leq 2g-2-(2k-3)(\gamma-1).

Gonality will be a useful way to distinguish (components of) Brill–Noether loci, and we recall a few bounds on the gonality for different families of curves.

For a smooth plane curve of degree
d
, Max Noether’s theorem states that the gonality is
d-1
and is obtained via projection from a point on the curve. For a curve with a singular plane model, projection from a node gives a
g^{1}_{d-2}
. This gives a simple containment of Brill–Noether loci, which we summarize here for later reference.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
g,d
be positive integers with
d\geq 4
and
\rho(g,2,d)<0
. If
g<\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}
, then
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,d-2}
.

Proof.

For a curve
C\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,d}
either the
g^{2}_{d}
is not very ample, in which case subtracting points and trivial containments show that
C
admits a
g^{1}_{d-2}
; or the
g^{2}_{d}
is very ample, and the image of
C
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a curve with arithmetic genus
\frac{(d-1)(d-1)}{2}>g
, thus the image has a point of multiplicity
\geq 2
, and projecting from this point shows that
C
has a
g^{1}_{k}
for some
k\leq d-2
, hence also a

1.3.
Refined Brill–Noether for fixed gonality

As the Hurwitz space of degree

While

(

If

1.4.
Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles and stability on K3 surfaces

We briefly recall the definition and basic properties of Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles, and the notion of stability on K3 surfaces. For proofs and further details, we refer the interested reader to
[
2
,
43
]
and for stability to
[
33
]
.

Let S be a K3 surface and
C\subset S
a smooth irreducible curve of genus
g
. A basepoint free
g^{r}_{d}
on
C
, denoted by
(A,V)
with
A\in\Pic^{d}(C)
and
V\subseteq H^{0}(C,A)
an
(r+1)
-dimensional subspace, gives rise to the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E_{C,(A,V)}
on
S
, defined as the dual of the kernel of the evaluation map
V\otimes\cal{O}_{S}\twoheadrightarrow A
. We recall the following well-known properties of
E_{C,(A,V)}
:

•

\operatorname{rk}(E_{C,(A,V)})=r+1
,
c_{1}(E_{C,(A,V)})=[C]
,
c_{2}(E_{C,(A,V)})=d
;

•

\chi(E_{C,(A,V)})=g-d+2r+1
,
h^{1}(E_{C,(A,V)})=h^{0}(C,A)-r-1
,
h^{2}(E_{C,(A,V)})=0
;

•

E_{C,(A,V)}
is globally generated off a finite set;

•

\chi(E_{C,(A,V)}^{\vee}\otimes E_{C,(A,V)})=2(1-\rho(g,r,d))
. In particular, if
\rho(g,r,d)<0
, then
E_{C,(A,V)}
is non-simple.

Being a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle is an open condition. That is, a vector bundle
E
with
h^{1}(S,E)=h^{2}(S,E)=0
such that
c_{1}(E)
is represented by a smooth irreducible curve
C
is the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a linear series on
C
. More generally, as defined in
[
47
]
, a torsion free coherent sheaf
E
on
S
is called a
generalized Lazarsfeld–Mukai
bundle if
h^{2}(S,E)=0
, and either

(I)

E
is locally free and generated by global sections of a finite set, or

(II)

E
is globally generated.

Furthermore, if both (I) and (II) are satisfied, then
E
is the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle associated to a smooth irreducible curve
C
and a primitive linear series
(A,V)
, with
V=H^{0}(C,A)
when
h^{1}(S,E)=0
, as observed in
[
47
, Remark 1]
. This will be useful when constructing Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles as direct sums of line bundles and smaller Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles.

We now recall briefly stability of coherent sheaves on K3 surfaces, specifically Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles.

Let
H
be an ample line bundle and
E
be a torsion free sheaf on
S
. The slope of
E
(with respect to
H
) is defined as

\mu(E)\coloneqq\frac{c_{1}(E).H}{\operatorname{rk}(E)}.

We call
E

(slope)-stable
(resp.,
(slope)-semistable
) if for any coherent subsheaf
0\neq F\subset E
with
\operatorname{rk}(F)<\operatorname{rk}(E)
, one has
\mu(F)<E
(resp.,
\mu(F)\leq\mu(E)
). We recall that a simple sheaf is stable.

The Harder–Narasimhan filtration (HN filtration) is the unique filtration

0=HN(E)_{0}\subset HN(E)_{1}\subset\cdots\subset HN(E)_{k}=E,

with
HN(E)_{i+1}/HN(E)_{i}
a torsion free semistable sheaf, and

\mu(HN(E)_{i}/HN(E)_{i-1})>\mu(HN(E)_{i+1}/HN(E)_{i}).

Moreover, if
E
is a vector bundle, then the sheaves
HN(E)_{i}
are locally free and

\mu(HN(E)_{1})>\mu(HN(E)_{2})>\cdots>\mu(E).

For a semistable sheaf
E
, the Jordan–Hölder filtration (JH filtration) is a filtration

0=JH(E)_{0}\subset JH(E)_{1}\subset\cdots JH(E)_{k}=E,

such that
JH(E)_{i+1}/JH(E)_{i}
are torsion free stable sheaves with
\mu(JH(E)_{i+1}/JH(E)_{i})=\mu(E)
. While the JH filtration always exists, it is not unique.

Finally, we recall that the moduli space of stable coherent sheaves of rank
r
with fixed Chern classes
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
has dimension

(1-r)c_{1}^{2}+2rc_{2}-2r^{2}+2,

hence if
E
is stable, then

c_{2}(E)\geq\frac{(\operatorname{rk}(E)-1)c_{1}(E)^{2}}{2\operatorname{rk}(E)}+\operatorname{rk}(E)-\frac{1}{\operatorname{rk}(E)}.

1.5.
Curves on K3 surfaces

We will consider curves on quasi-polarized K3 surfaces
(S,H)
of genus
g
with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
, where
\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
is the lattice
\mathbb{Z}[H]\oplus\mathbb{Z}[L]
with intersection matrix

\begin{bmatrix}H^{2}&H.L\\
H.L&L^{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2g-2&d\\
d&2r-2\end{bmatrix}.

Denoting by
\mathcal{K}_{g}
the moduli space of quasi-polarized K3 surfaces, pairs
(S,H)
of a K3 surface
S
of genus
g
and
H\in\Pic(S)
a big and nef line bundle with
H^{2}=2g-2
. From Hodge theory, the Noether–Lefschetz locus of K3 surfaces with Picard rank
>1
is a countable union of irreducible divisors
\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
. For
g\geq 2
,
r\geq 0
, and
d\geq 0
, we denote by
\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
the quasi-polarized K3 surfaces
(S,H)\in\mathcal{K}_{g}
admitting a primitive embedding of
\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
preserving
H
, following notation from
[
5
,
6
]
. When
\Delta(g,r,d)\coloneqq 4(g-1)(r-1)-d^{2}<0
, such K3 surfaces exist by the surjectivity of the period map, see
[
52
, Theorem 2.9(i)]
or
[
53
]
. Furthermore, as in
[
6
, Section 1.2]
, we may assume that
H
is nef after acting by Picard–Lefschetz reflections. In fact, when
\Delta(g,r,d)<0
,
\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
is a non-empty irreducible divisor (see
[
54
]
), and the very general
(S,H)\in\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
has
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
.

In particular, for
g\geq 3
,
r\geq 1
, and
2\leq d\leq g-1
, smooth irreducible curves
C\in|H|
have genus
g
and carry a basepoint free complete
g^{r}_{d}
, namely
|\mathcal{O}_{C}(L)|
, see
[
6
, Lemma 1.7]
.

2.
Admissible assignments

We use curves on K3 surfaces to distinguish Brill–Noether loci. Specifically, we consider K3 surfaces with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
and the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
associated to a linear series
g^{s}_{e}
on
C\in|H|
with
\rho(g,s,e)<0
. As
\Pic(S)
is fixed, the determinants of the stable Jordan–Hölder subfactors of the Harder–Narasimhan subquotients of
E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
are constrained, and lead to many restrictions on
g^{s}_{e}
.

2.1.
Terminal filtrations

Throughout this section, let
(S,H)
be a polarized K3 surface of genus
g
, and
E
be an unstable sheaf on
S
with
c_{1}(E)=H
. Given
E
, it is natural to look at the HN filtration of
E
and the JH filtrations of the HN factors. By lifting each JH filtration, one obtains the
terminal filtration
of
E
, a filtration where each factor is stable and the slopes are “decreasing”. Here we set notation and recall the notions of terminal filtrations, and recall how a terminal filtration gives an expression for
c_{2}(E)
.

To arrive at a terminal filtration for
E
, begin with the HN filtration of
E

0=HN(E)_{0}\subset HN(E)_{1}\subset\cdots\subset HN(E)_{k}=E,

and the JH filtrations of each subquotient,
HN(E)_{i}/HN(E)_{i-1}
,

0\subset JH(HN(E_{i})/HN(E_{i-1}))_{1}\subset\cdots\subset JH(HN(E_{i})/HN(E_{i-1}))_{k_{i}-1}\subset HN(E_{i})/HN(E_{i-1}).

The subsheaves of the JH filtrations of each subquotient give subsheaves

HN(E_{i-1})\subset\overline{JH(HN(E_{i})/HN(E_{i-1}))_{1}}\subset\cdots\subset\overline{JH(HN(E_{i})/HN(E_{i-1}))_{k_{i}-1}}\subset HN(E_{i}),

which all together give a filtration of
E

0=E_{0}\subset E_{1}\subset\cdots\subset E_{n}=E,

where
E_{i}/E_{i-1}
is a torsion free stable sheaf, and the slopes satisfy
\mu(E_{i}/E_{i-1})\geq\mu(E_{i+1}/E_{i})
, and if
E
is a vector bundle then the slopes also satisfy
\mu(E_{1})\geq\mu(E_{2})\geq\cdots\geq\mu(E)
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
terminal filtration of length
n
and type
r_{1}\subset\cdots\subset r_{n}
of a sheaf
E
is a filtration

0=E_{0}\subset E_{1}\subset\cdots\subset E_{n}=E

of coherent sheaves with
\operatorname{rk}(E_{i})=r_{i}
such that
E_{i}/E_{i-1}
is a torsion free stable sheaf and the slopes satisfy

\mu(E_{j}/E_{i})\geq\mu(E_{k}/E_{i})\geq\mu(E_{k}/E_{j})

for
{0\leq i<j<k\leq n}
.
We say that a terminal filtration is
quotient non-negative
if
{c_{1}(E/E_{i})^{2}\geq 0}
for all
0<i<n
, and is
quotient slope-positive
if
\mu(E/E_{i})>0
for all
0<i<n
.

Remark 2.2
.

Given a terminal filtration, one obtains an expression for
c_{2}(E)
by recursively using the exact sequences

0\to E_{i-1}\to E_{i}\to E_{i}/E_{i-1}\to 0,

which gives

c_{2}(E_{i})=c_{2}(E_{i-1})+c_{2}(E_{i}/E_{i-1})+c_{1}(E_{i-1}).c_{1}(E_{i}/E_{i-1}).

In total, we obtain

c_{2}(E)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{2}(E_{i}/E_{i-1})+c_{1}(E_{i}/E_{i-1}).\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{1}(E_{j}/E_{j-1})\right).

Since
E_{i}/E_{i-1}
is stable for
1\leq i\leq n
, we have

c_{2}(E_{i}/E_{i-1})\geq(r_{i}-r_{i-1})-\frac{1}{r_{i}-r_{i-1}}+\frac{r_{i}-r_{i-1}-1}{2(r_{i}-r_{i-1})}c_{1}(E_{i}/E_{i-1})^{2}.

2.2.
Admissible assignments are Gelfand–Tsetlin patterns

Recall that a
Gelfand–Tsetlin pattern (of size
n
)
is a triangular array of entries
x_{i,j}
with
1\leq j\leq i\leq n

\textstyle{x_{1,1}}
\textstyle{x_{2,1}}
\textstyle{x_{3,1}}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{x_{n,1}}
\textstyle{x_{2,2}}
\textstyle{x_{3,2}}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{x_{n,2}}
\textstyle{x_{3,3}}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{x_{n,3}}
\textstyle{\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{-50.0}{$\cdots$}}}
\textstyle{\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{50.0}{$\cdots$}}}
\textstyle{x_{n,n}}

such that
x_{i,j}\geq x_{i+1,j+1}\geq x_{i+1,j}
.

Remark 2.3
.

The data of a terminal filtration of length
n
can be arranged into a triangle of size
n
with
x_{i,j}=E_{i}/E_{i-j}

\textstyle{E_{1}}
\textstyle{E_{2}/E_{1}}
\textstyle{E_{3}/E_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{E_{n-2}/E_{n-3}}
\textstyle{E_{n-1}/E_{n-2}}
\textstyle{E_{n}/E_{n-1}}
\textstyle{E_{2}}
\textstyle{E_{3}/E_{1}}
\textstyle{E_{4}/E_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{E_{n-1}/E_{n-3}}
\textstyle{E_{n}/E_{n-2}}
\textstyle{E_{3}}
\textstyle{E_{4}/E_{1}}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{E_{n}/E_{n-3}}
\textstyle{\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{-50.0}{$\cdots$}}}
\textstyle{\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{-50.0}{$\cdots$}}}
\textstyle{\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{50.0}{$\cdots$}}}
\textstyle{\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{50.0}{$\cdots$}}}
\textstyle{E_{n-2}}
\textstyle{E_{n-2}/E_{1}}
\textstyle{E_{n}/E_{2}}
\textstyle{E_{n-1}}
\textstyle{E_{n}/E_{1}}
\textstyle{E_{n}}

where the leftmost diagonal is simply the terminal filtration, and each subsequent diagonal is the quotient terminal filtration by
E_{i}
. Since
E_{1}\subset\cdots E_{n}
is a terminal filtration, we have

\mu(x_{i,j})=\mu(E_{i}/E_{i-j})\geq\mu(x_{i+1,j+1})=\mu(E_{i+1}/E_{i-j})\geq\mu(x_{i+1,j})=\mu(E_{i+1}/E_{i-j+1}),

hence taking the slopes gives a (rational) Gelfand–Tsetlin pattern, and in fact the slope conditions of a terminal filtration are equivalent to the Gelfand–Tsetlin conditions. The top row contains the stable subquotients of the terminal filtration, and the last North-East diagonal contains the quotients
E_{n}/E_{i}
.

For a fixed terminal filtration type (say
r_{1}\subset\cdots\subset r_{n}
), as the ranks are known, the slope data is completely determined by the first Chern classes of
E_{i}
, or equivalently by
c_{1}(E_{n}/E_{i})
, which will be slightly more convenient so work with when
E_{n}
is a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a sequence of integers
0=r_{0}<r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{n}
, we call a collection of line bundles
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{n}
a
slope admissible assignment
if
x_{i,j}=\frac{H.(L_{i}-L_{i-j})}{r_{i}-r_{i-j}}
is a (rational) Gelfand–Tsetlin pattern. We call
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{n}

quotient non-negative
if in addition
(L_{n}-L_{i})^{2}\geq 0
for all
0<i<n
, and
quotient slope-positive
if
H.(L_{n}-L_{i})>0
for all
0<i<n
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Given a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E
and a terminal filtration

E_{1}\subset\cdots\subset E_{n}=E,

the collection of line bundles
c_{1}(E_{1}),c_{1}(E_{2}),\dots,c_{1}(E_{n})
is a quotient non-negative quotient slope-positive admissible assignment.

Proof.

As the first Chern classes come from a terminal filtration, it is clear that they give a slope admissible assignment. It remains to show that
c_{1}(E/E_{i})^{2}\geq 0
and
\mu(E/E_{i})>0
. Let
M_{i}=E/E_{i}
. Since
M_{i}
is a quotient of
E
, it is globally generated off a finite set of points. Moreover, we have
{h^{2}(S,M_{i})=0}
, thus
h^{0}(S,\det M_{i})\geq 2
by
[
46
, Lemma 3.3]
as
M_{i}^{\vee\vee}
is globally generated off a finite set and
\det M_{i}=\det(M_{i}^{\vee\vee})
. As in
[
46
, Lemma 3.2]
, we see that
\det M_{i}
is base point free and nontrivial, thus
\mu(\det M_{i})>0
,
h^{2}(S,\det M_{i})=0
, and
\det M_{i}
is nef whereby
c_{1}(M_{i})^{2}\geq 0
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

To abbreviate the conditions, by an
admissible assignment
for any Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle we will always mean a quotient non-negative and quotient slope-positive admissible assignment.

Remark 2.7
.

Given an unstable Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle, one can consider all possible terminal filtration types, and consider quotient non-negative admissible assignments for each type. We note that the existence of such an assignment does not imply the existence of such a terminal filtration. Indeed, there are additional restrictions coming from the theory of (generalized) Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles for a (quotient non-negative quotient slope-positive) admissible assignment to arise from a terminal filtration. We give two such examples; the first coming from restrictions on Donagi–Morrison lifts of linear series, and the other coming from restrictions on generalized Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles arising as quotients of Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles.

The presence of elliptic curves gives admissible assignments which do not arise from a terminal filtration.

Example 2.8

(Non-terminal admissible assignment in genus
11
)
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{2}_{11,7}
. We note that
\Lambda^{2}_{11,7}
has no
(-2)
-curves, and has
(0)
-curves:
n(H-2L)
and
n(-H+5L)
.

There are a few admissible assignments of
E=E_{C,g^{3}_{e}}
giving a bound on
c_{2}
which is
\leq 10
(if the assignment gave
c_{2}>10
, then it would not be of a
g^{3}_{10}
). We note that
h^{0}(E_{C,g^{3}_{e}})=18-e
.

For a terminal filtration of type
1\subset 4
, there are two admissible assignments with
c_{2}(E)\leq 10
, which have
c_{1}(E_{1})=H-L
or
c_{1}(E_{1})=2L
, respectively. The former does not occur, as else the
g^{3}_{10}
would be contained in the
g^{2}_{7}
by
[
47
, Lemma 4.1]
. The latter does not occur as then

would be a generalized Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle with
c_{1}^{2}=0
and
[

shows
E/2L\cong\mathcal{O}_{S}(\Sigma)^{\oplus 3}
for an irreducible elliptic curve

, which is not stable. So this admissible assignments does not arise from a terminal filtration!

Similarly, there are two admissible assignments of type
1\subset 2\subset 4

Given a filtration

(2)

\displaystyle\mu(E_{i})\geq\mu(E)\geq\mu(E/E_{i})>0,

(3)

2.4.
Distinguishing Brill–Noether loci via admissible assignments

To determine the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci, it is generally easier to prove non-containments
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
, as it suffices to find one curve admitting a
g^{r}_{d}
and not admitting a
g^{s}_{e}
. To this end, we consider a K3 surface
S
with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
and a smooth irreducible
C\in|H|
, so that
C\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
. If
C
admits a
g^{s}_{e}
, then
S
carries an unstable Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
, which has an admissible assignment. Clearly, if no such assignment exists, then
C
admits no
g^{s}_{e}
, and more generally the admissible assignments give bounds on
c_{2}(E_{c,g^{s}_{e}})
showing that
C
admits no
g^{s}_{e}
for a range of
e
. Moreover, if there is an admissible assignment for some terminal filtration type, then one can in many cases use the admissible assignment to construct the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
. We note that the existence of admissible assignments it not sufficient, as there are examples of admissible assignments not arising from a terminal filtration, as in
Examples

2.8
and
2.9
.

When one can construct such a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle, then it is natural to ask whether there is in fact a containment of Brill–Noether loci. Using notation from
[
3
,
6
]
, let
\pi:\mathcal{P}_{g}\to\mathcal{K}_{g}
be the universal smooth hyperplane section, the fiber above
(S,H)
being the smooth irreducible curves in
|H|
, and let
\phi:\mathcal{P}_{g}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
be the forgetful map, and
\phi:\mathcal{P}^{r}_{g,d}\to\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
the restriction to the divisor
\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
, which has a natural map
\phi:\mathcal{P}^{r}_{g,d}\to\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
, by
[
6
, Lemma 1.7]
.

Definition 2.12
.

We call the potential containment
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\subset}}\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
of Brill–Noether loci
K3-expected
when
\phi(\mathcal{P}^{r}_{g,d})\subset\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
. That is, the potential containment is
K3-expected
if for a general K3 surface
(S,H)\in\mathcal{K}^{r}_{g,d}
and a smooth irreducible
C\in|H|
, we have
C\in\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
.

We will not dwell on all containments which are K3-expected, though many of the containments below were first seen to be K3-expected, after which the containment was verified. Hence K3-expected containments are a useful heuristic in determining the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci.

3.

We identify the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci in genus

–

4.
Brill–Noether loci in genus
\geq 11

In genus

, the previous methods for distinguishing Brill–Noether loci are no longer sufficient give a complete picture of all containments of Brill–Noether loci. For example, our strategy using K3 surfaces with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
fails when

, as the Hodge index theorem shows there are no K3 surfaces with such a Picard group where

4.1.

\leq 3
and bielliptic curves

For distinguishing Brill–Noether loci of small Clifford index, small degree covers of curves are quite useful. We collect a few results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose either that

•

r=2
and
g\geq 11
, or

•

r\geq 3
and
g\geq 7
.

If
d-2r\geq 2
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,3}
.

Proof.

By the trivial containments, it suffices to prove the result for
d-2r=2
.

Consider bielliptic curves
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2:1}}{{\longrightarrow}}E
, which exist by
Theorem

1.4
. We note that such curves
C
admit a
g^{e-1}_{2e}
for all
e\geq 2
, given by the composition

C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2:1}}{{\longrightarrow}}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g^{e-1}_{e}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{P}^{e-1}.

From
Theorem

1.2
, we see that
C
admits no
g^{1}_{3}
, as the
g^{1}_{3}
cannot factor through the map
C\to E
and
g(C)\geq 5
, whereby
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,3}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We note that the bielliptic curves above have
\Cliff(C)=2
. This follows directly from
Lemma

1.3
, which shows that if
C
had Clifford index
\leq 1
, it would admit a
g^{1}_{2}
or a
g^{1}_{3}
, and
Theorem

1.2
shows that bielliptic curves of genus
\geq 5
do not. However, as bielliptic curves admit all linear series with
\gamma=2
, bielliptic curves do not distinguish Brill–Noether loci with
\gamma=2
.

We note that there are always some containments in Clifford index
2
.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
g\geq 11
and
e\geq 4
, then

(i)

\mathcal{M}^{e-1}_{g,2e}\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,6}
and curves in
\mathcal{M}^{e-1}_{g,2e}
are either hyperelliptic or bielliptic, and

(ii)

\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,6}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{e-1}_{g,2e}
.

Proof.

We first prove (i). Let
C\in\mathcal{M}^{e-1}_{g,2e}
. One can easily check that
Theorem

1.1
shows that the
g^{e-1}_{2e}
cannot be basepoint free and very ample. If the
g^{e-1}_{2e}
has a basepoint, then from
Lemma

1.3
we see that
C\in\mathcal{M}^{e-1}_{g,2e-1}=\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,2}\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,6}
. Thus we may assume that the
g^{e-1}_{2e}
factors as

C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k:1}}{{\longrightarrow}}D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g^{e-1}_{2e/k}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{P}^{e-1}.

If
k\geq 3
, then we note that
\frac{2e}{k}<e-1
, hence
D
cannot admit such a
g^{e-1}_{2e/k}
. Hence we have
k=2
. If
g(D)\geq 2
, then Riemann–Roch shows that the
g^{e-1}_{e}
is special, contradicting Clifford’s theorem. Therefore
k=2
and
g(D)\leq 1
, and
C
is either hyperelliptic or bielliptic and
C\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,6}
, as claimed.

Statement (ii) follows from the fact that
\kappa(g,2,6)=3
, and using
Equation

1
one can readily verify that
\kappa(g,e-1,2e)=2
. Indeed, we note that
g\leq 2e+1
, as we assume that
d\leq g-1
. The case

follows easily, as then
\kappa(g,e-1,2e)=\left\lfloor\frac{2e}{e-1}\right\rfloor=2
for

. If
g=2e+1
, we show that
\kappa(2e+1,e-1,2e)=2
. Suppose for contradiction that
\kappa(2e+1,e-1,2e)\geq 3
; we compute

\displaystyle\kappa(2e+1,e-1,2e)\geq 3

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 2e+\left\lfloor-2\sqrt{-\rho(2e+1,e-1,2e)}\right\rfloor\geq 3

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\left\lfloor-2\sqrt{e^{2}-2e-1}\right\rfloor\geq 3-2e

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle-2\sqrt{e^{2}-2e-1}\geq 3-2e

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\sqrt{e^{2}-2e-1}\leq\frac{2e-3}{2}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle e^{2}-2e-1\leq\frac{4e^{2}-12e+9}{4}\text{ as both sides are positive}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle e\leq\frac{13}{4},

which is a contradiction as

. Thus
\kappa(g,e-1,2e)=2
. As
\kappa(g,2,6)=3
, (ii) follows from
Theorem

1.9
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

In particular, for
g\geq 11
and

In the spirit of

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

, or

\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,6}=\mathcal{M}^{3}_{g,9}

Proof.

We first show

.

or

\mathcal{M}^{2}_{11,7}\subset\mathcal{M}^{3}_{11,10}

5.
Conjectures on Brill–Noether loci

In general, due to the existence of multiple components of various dimensions, a complete picture of the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci in higher genus remains elusive. We motivate a few conjectures, using curves on K3 surfaces to provide numerical expectations, and give a few explicit examples in genus
100
to illustrate the numerical intuition.

5.1.
Irreducibility

When
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
has multiple components, it no longer makes sense to ask about the behavior of a general curve with a
g^{r}_{d}
. It would be appealing to identify a distinguished component of Brill–Noether loci that is well-behaved and whose Brill–Noether theory can be described.

There is no general expectation when Brill–Noether loci should be irreducible, though certainly there are many examples of reducible Brill–Noether loci. As pointed out in
[
56
, Appendix A.1]
, one way to find reducible Brill–Noether loci is from containments
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,k}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
. In
[
30
, Corollary 4.7]
, these containments in fact show that
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,d}
has at least two components for
\frac{g}{3}+3\leq d<\left\lfloor\frac{g+4}{2}\right\rfloor
and
g\geq 9
. It is natural to ask if this is perhaps the first origin of another component of Brill–Noether loci.

Question 1
.

Is
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
irreducible when
0>\rho(g,r,d)>\rho(g,1,\kappa(g,r,d))
?

Similarly, one is tempted to ask whether Brill–Noether are irreducible when
\rho
is not too negative.

5.2.
Gonality of curves in
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}

Turning the question above on its head, one may ask: Given a curve
C\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
, what is the gonality of
C
? When
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
is reducible, one may ask about the gonality of general curves of each component. In terms of the relative positions of Brill–Noether loci, it is natural to consider the smallest
k
such that all curves with a
g^{r}_{d}
admit a
g^{1}_{k}
, which is also the maximum gonality of the general curve in each component.

Definition 5.1
.

For a Brill–Noether locus
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
, we define

K(g,r,d)\coloneqq\min\{k\ \mid\ \mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,k}\}.

Clearly, we have
\kappa(g,r,d)\leq K(g,r,d)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{g+3}{2}\right\rfloor
. Moreover, for plane curves, the results of
[
14
,
15
]
show that
K(g,2,d)=d-2
in many cases.

Again, one may try to use curves on K3 surfaces to provide some expectation for
K(g,r,d)
. Let
S
be a K3 surface with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
and
C\in|H|
a smooth irreducible curve. When
g,r,d
satisfy certain numerical constraints,
[
24
, Theorem 3]
shows that we have

\gon(C)=\min\{d-2r+2,~\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}.

In some range, it is perhaps expected that
K(g,r,d)=\min\{d-2r+2,~\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
. However, already in genus
11
and
12
this does not hold. As observed in
4.6
and
4.14
,
\mathcal{M}^{3}_{11,9}=\mathcal{M}^{2}_{11,6}\subset\mathcal{M}^{1}_{11,4}
,
\mathcal{M}^{3}_{12,9}\subset\mathcal{M}^{1}_{12,4}
, and
\mathcal{M}^{4}_{12,11}\subset\mathcal{M}^{1}_{12,4}
, and for higher genus one has
4.6
and
4.18
.

This expectation does not hold on K3 surfaces in general. In particular, when
S
has elliptic curves, the gonality of smooth curves in
|H|
may be much lower than
\min\{d-2r+2,\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
. We give two examples. In both examples, the numerical conditions on
g,r,d
of
[
24
, Theorem 3]
are not satisfied.

Example 5.2

(Gonality of curves on K3s with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
)
.

Let
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{10}_{100,60}
. We note that there are no
(-2)
-curves, but there are
(0)
-curves, namely
n(H-3L)
and
n(-3H+11L)
. We show that for a smooth irreducible curve
C\in|H|
,
\gon(C)=18<42=\min\{d-2r+2,\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
. We consider all admissible assignments of a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a
g^{1}_{e}
with
\rho(g,1,e)<0
on
C
. There is a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of the form
(-H+6L)\oplus(2H-6L)
, which is the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a
g^{1}_{36}
, and
H.(2H-6L)=36
, so

is a lift of the

. In this case we have
\gon(C)=36<60-2\cdot 10+2=42
. There is also another Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of the form
(2H-4L)\oplus(-H+4L)
, also the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a
g^{1}_{36}
, but
H.(-H+4L)=42
, and the
g^{1}_{36}
is just contained in the
g^{1}_{42}
(
(-H+4L)
is a Donagi–Morrison lift of the

). Finally, there is another Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of the form
(3L)\oplus(H-3L)
, which is the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a
g^{1}_{18}
, and we note that
\mathcal{O}_{C}(H-3L)
is a
g^{1}_{18}
. Any other admissible assignment gives a larger lower bound on

, thus
\gon(C)=18<42
, as claimed.

On the other hand, if
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{10}_{100,61}
, then for a smooth irreducible curve
C\in|H|
, we have
\gon(C)=43=\min\{d-2r+2,\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
. We note that
\Lambda^{10}_{100,61}
has a
(-2)
-curve (
-H+4L
) and no
(0)
-curves. Indeed,
(H-L)\oplus L
is a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a
g^{1}_{43}
, and an explicit computation of the admissible assignments for a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle shows that all other admissible assignments give a higher

do not satisfy the numerical constraints of
[
24
, Theorem 3]
,
\gon(C)=43
.

With additional knowledge of the curves in a given component of
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
, one may provide additional bounds on
K(g,r,d)
, as in
[
30
, Lemma 5.5]
, which provides bounds on the gonality of curves in certain components of
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,g-1}
for
g=\binom{r+2}{2}
. Namely,
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,g-1}
has at least two components, one of curves where the
g^{r}_{g-1}
is a theta characteristic and another where it is not, and a lower bound on the gonality of curves in each component is provided by
[
30
, Lemma 5.5]
. Specifically, when
g=\binom{r+2}{2}
, we have
K(g,r,d)\geq 2r
. In particular, we have the following two examples where
K(g,r,d)\neq\min\{d-2r+2,~\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

For
r=3
or
r=4
and
g=\binom{r+2}{2}
, we have

K(g,r,g-1)\geq 2r>\min\{g-1-2r+2,\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}.

Proof.

From
[
30
, Lemma 5.5]
, we have
K(g,r,g-1)\geq 2r
, and one readily verify that

d-2r+2=\frac{r(r-1)}{2}+2<2r,\text{ and }g-1-2r+2<\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor.\qed

However, examples where
K(g,r,d)\neq\min\{d-2r+2,\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
seem fairly rare, occurring when
\rho(g,r,d)
is very negative and
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
is far from maximal. Hence it is natural to expect that
K(g,r,d)=\min\{d-2r+2,\left\lfloor(g+3)/2\right\rfloor\}
in some range.

5.3.
Conjectures on distinguishing Brill–Noether loci in large genus via secant varieties

We turn now to identifying a rough range where we expect to have no non-trivial containments of Brill–Noether loci. We start be identifying secant expected containments.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
r\geq 3
be odd. If
e\geq d-2r+2+\left\lfloor\frac{r+3}{2}\right\rfloor
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,e}
.

Proof.

Suppose
C\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
. A
g^{2}_{e}
would be obtained from a divisor of degree
(d-e)
imposing
(r-3)
independent conditions on the
g^{r}_{d}
. The expected dimension of such “secant divisors” is

(d-e)-(d-e-(r-3)-1)(r-(r-3))\geq-r+2\left\lfloor\frac{r+1}{2}\right\rfloor=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $r$ is even}\\
1&\text{ if $r$ is odd.}\end{cases}

As
r
is odd, there exists such a divisor, giving a
g^{2}_{e}
on
C
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

If
r
is even, then there may still be an effective “secant” divisor of degree
(d-e)
imposing
(r-3)
conditions. However, as the virtual class of such “secant” divisors becomes quite complicated, it is not immediate.

It is natural to ask whether
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
admits no containments on
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,e}
outside the range of secant expected containments.

Conjecture 1
.

For
g
sufficiently large, if
r\geq 3
,
d,e\leq g-1
, and
e<d-2r+2+\left\lfloor\frac{r+3}{2}\right\rfloor
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{2}_{g,e}
.

Remark 5.6
.

Computations of admissible assignments on K3 surfaces with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
for large genus support this conjecture. In
[
46
, Theorem 1.4]
, Lelli-Chiesa uses K3 surfaces of Picard rank
2
to verify this claim, with some mild restrictions on
g,r,d
to avoid
(-2)
-curves and to ensure nice K3 surfaces exist, for
e<d-2r+5
(which is equivalent to
\gamma(g^{2}_{e})\leq\gamma(g^{r}_{d})
).

More generally, we identify some range where there are secant expected containments.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
and
\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
be Brill–Noether loci such that
r\geq s+1\geq 2
. Suppose that
e\geq~d-2r+2+s+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor+k
for some non-negative integer
k
. If
r+\left\lfloor\frac{-r+1}{2}\right\rfloor s+ks>0
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
.

Remark 5.8
.

The assumption that
r+\left\lfloor\frac{-r+1}{2}\right\rfloor s+ks>0
forces
s
to be fairly small (depending on
k
).

Proof.

Let
\in\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
. Let
V=V^{r-s}_{d-e}(g^{r}_{d})
be the divisors of degree
(d-e)
imposing
(r-s-1)
independent conditions on the
g^{r}_{d}
.
If
V\neq\emptyset
, then
C\in\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
. We recall that

\displaystyle\dim V

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{exp}\dim V

\displaystyle=(d-e)-(d-e-(r-s-1)-1)(r-(r-s-1))

\displaystyle=r-s-(d-e-r+s)s.

It remains to show that
r-s-(d-e-r+s)s>0
.

As
e-d\geq s+2-2r+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor+k
, we have

\displaystyle r-s-(d-e-r+s)s

\displaystyle\geq r-s+\left(s+2-2r+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor+k+r-s\right)s

\displaystyle=r+\left\lfloor\frac{-r+1}{2}\right\rfloor s+ks.

By assumption,
r+\left\lfloor\frac{-r+1}{2}\right\rfloor s+ks>0
, whereby
V=V^{r-s}_{d-e}(g^{r}_{d})\neq\emptyset
and we obtain a
g^{s}_{e}
, as claimed.
∎

We note that similar results may hold with less restrictive assumptions on
r
and
s
. Outside of the range where there are secant expected containments, it is natural to ask the following question.

Conjecture 2
.

For
g
sufficiently large,
d,e\leq g-1
, if
r\geq s+1\geq 3
and
e<d-2r+2+s+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
.

Computations in large genus on K3 surfaces support this conjecture. We note that in more precise ranges of
r
and
s
, it may be possible to obtain stronger results.

In the range
s\geq r+1
, there may be additional non-trivial containments which are not secant expected. One source of examples of such non-trivial containments are the containments arising from the refined Brill–Noether theory for curves of fixed gonality of the form
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{g,k}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}
.

Question 2
.

Identify additional non-trivial non-secant expected containments of Brill–Noether loci.

Nevertheless, when there are no secant expected containments, it may be the case that there are in fact no non-trivial containments among Brill–Noether loci. It would be interesting to identify a range where Brill–Noether loci admit no non-trivial containments.

Question 3
.

For sufficiently large
g
, is there a range where there are no non-trivial containments of Brill–Noether loci? How does this range depend on
g
? Does it align with the range predicted by K3 surfaces or secant expected containments?

Roughly, is there a range of
r
and
d
, depending on
g
, where the only containments are secant-expected; and further, outside of this range (for larger
d
, perhaps), are there no containments among Brill–Noether loci? For instance, expected maximal Brill–Noether loci admit no containments for large genus, see
[
6
]
.

5.4.
On non-containments of the form
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
for
s>r
via K3 surfaces

Showing (non-)containments of the form
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
for
s\geq r+1
appears to be more difficult, as there are many admissible assignments when
d
is small. Here we explain the general trends coming from admissible assignments, and formulate an expectation (from K3 surfaces) for the behavior in general.

On a K3 surface with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{r}_{g,d}
, empirical evidence suggests that, in many cases, the lowest bound on
c_{2}
for an unstable Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
comes from admissible assignment of the form
(H-L)\subset E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
, and other admissible assignments allow larger values of
c_{2}
. However, in the range
s\geq r+1
,
E_{C,g^{s}_{e}}
cannot have a terminal filtration of type
1\subset s+1
with
c_{1}(E_{1})=H-L
, as then the
g^{s}_{e}
would be contained in
|L\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}|=g^{r}_{d}
, as observed in
[
47
, Lemma 4.1]
, which cannot occur as
s>r
.

However, there are other admissible assignments which potentially do occur. An example of this is given by Lelli-Chiesa–Knutsen in
[
47
, Remark 12]
, where the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E_{C,g^{3}_{d}}

Example 5.9
.

Suppose we want to find the smallest

such that
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{100,51}\subset\mathcal{M}^{3}_{100,e}
. To find some expectation for
e
, we consider a K3 surface with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{2}_{100,51}
. Checking admissible assignments for a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E=E_{C,g^{3}_{e}}
of rank
4
for smooth irreducible
C\in|H|
, we see that there is the admissible assignment
H-L\subset E
, but as noted above this does not come from a terminal filtration.

The admissible assignment giving the next smallest bound on
c_{2}(E)
is of type
2\subset 4
, giving
c_{2}(E)\geq 77

g^{3}_{77}
on

. To wit,
E_{H-L,g^{1}_{26}}\oplus E_{L,g^{1}_{2}}
is a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle of a
g^{3}_{77}
on

\mathcal{M}^{3}_{100,77}
is maximal as
\rho(100,3,77)=-4

\mathcal{M}^{2}_{100,51}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\subset}}\mathcal{M}^{3}_{100,77}

is not secant expected, though it is K3-expected, by the above, and perhaps the containment is valid.

For
d\geq 52
, there are in fact no containments. By computing admissible assignments, one can readily verify that on a K3 surface with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{2}_{100,d}
with
d\geq 52
, a smooth irreducible
C\in|H|
admits no
g^{3}_{e}
with
\rho(g,3,e)<0
. We note that for
d\geq 52
,
(S,H)
satisfies decomposition rigidity, see
[

admits a
g^{3}_{e}

. For

\kappa(100,2,52)=26=\kappa(100,3,77)
, and one checks the admissible assignments to show a non-containment. We note that for

For lower

We give some more detail when

•

for a terminal filtration of type

for a terminal filtration type

When

is small, the assignment of type
1\subset 2\subset s+1
seems to give the smallest bound on

. As

increases, the bound from the
1\subset 2\subset s+1

H-2L\subset E

Example 5.10
.

We work on K3 surfaces with
\Pic(S)=\Lambda^{2}_{100,d}
, and look for admissible assignments for the Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle
E=E_{C,g^{3}_{e}}
. As
Example

5.9
deals with
d\geq 51
, we focus on
d\leq 50
.

For
d\leq 19
, as
\Delta(100,2,d)>0
there are no such K3 surfaces.

For
d=20
, there are over
2000
admissible assignments, a few which give smaller lower bounds on
c_{2}(E)
than the above assignments, and we don’t comment on this further.

For
21\leq d\leq 36
, the admissible assignment of type
1\subset 2\subset 4
with
c_{1}(E_{1})=H-2L
and
c_{1}(E_{2})=H-L
gives the smallest lower bound, and one can check that
(H-2L)\oplus L\oplus E_{L,g^{1}_{2}}
gives a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle attaining the bound.

For
d=37
, the admissible assignments above of type
1\subset 4
and
2\subset 4
give the same lower bound,
c_{2}(E)\geq 70
, while the admissible assignment of type
1\subset 2\subset 4
above gives
c_{2}(E)\geq 72
. One can check that both
E_{H-L,g^{1}_{33}}\oplus E_{L,g^{1}_{2}}
and
(H-2L)\oplus L\oplus E_{L,g^{1}_{2}}
are Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles of a
g^{3}_{70}
on
C\in|H|
.

For
38\leq d\leq 50
, the admissible assignment of type
2\subset 4
gives the smallest lower bound on
c_{2}(E)
, and one obtains a Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundle as a sum of Lazarsfeld–Mukai bundles of expected gonality pencils, namely,
E_{H-L,g^{1}_{f}}\oplus E_{L,g^{1}_{2}}
, with

(the expected gonality of a smooth curve in
|H-L|
), giving
c_{2}(E)\geq d+f=52+\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor
, as in
[
47
, Appendix A]
. We note that in this range,
H
admits two flexible decompositions,
H=(H-L)+L=(H-2L)+2L

[
6
, Definition 2.1]
.

Thus, when
d
is not too small, one obtains K3-expected containments of the form
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subset\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
for
r<s
, and expects non-containments when
e
is smaller than the bound given by the admissible assignment for a terminal filtration of type
2\subset s+1
with
c_{1}(E_{1})=H-L
.

Conjecture 3
.

For
g
and
d
sufficiently large,
d,e\leq g-1
, and
2\leq r<s
,

•

if
e<d-2r+s+\frac{g-d+r+1}{2}+\frac{(s-2)(r-1)-1}{s-1}
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\nsubseteq\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
,

•

if
e\geq d-2r+s+\frac{g-d+r+1}{2}+\frac{(s-2)(r-1)-1}{s-1}
, then
\mathcal{M}^{r}_{g,d}\subset\mathcal{M}^{s}_{g,e}
.

The range of
g
and
d
are “sufficiently large”, as determining the precise range where only a few admissible assignments exist appears involved. Indeed, the existence of admissible assignments is deeply related to the existence of flexible decompositions of
H
, and, as in
[
6
]
, the range where is can be shown that
H
admits only one flexible decomposition can be quite intricate.

Of course, simply because curves on K3 surfaces seem to agree with this prediction does not mean the (non-)containments hold. Explicit constructions giving the predicted containments appear out of reach in general. Numerically, one can readily verify some non-containments by computing all admissible assignments.
