Impact of post stroke fatigue on saccadic eye movement control and learning through inflammatory mechanisms
Authors: Akram Jamali, Tourandokht Baluchnejadmojarad, Hajar Mehdizadeh, Seyede Zohreh Jazaeri, Soheila Fallah, Ghorban Taghizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to (1) examine the impact of post-stroke fatigue (PSF) on saccadic control, (2) assess the effect of PSF on saccade adaptation, and (3) explore the correlation between serum levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) with saccade control and adaptation in chronic stroke survivors. Fatigue was assessed in stroke survivors with high fatigue (HF-stroke, n = 13) and low fatigue (LF-stroke, n = 13) using the Fatigue Severity Scale and Fatigue Assessment Scale, alongside age-matched healthy controls (n = 13). The stroke groups were compared based on lesion volume and location. Eye-tracking recorded pro-saccade, anti-saccade, and saccade adaptation parameters. Serum levels of IL-6 and hsCRP were measured. HF-stroke survivors demonstrated significantly higher saccade latency, duration, and positional error, along with lower velocity, amplitude, and gain in pro- and anti-saccade tasks compared to the LF-stroke group (p < 0.05). HF-stroke survivors showed a lower adaptation rate (F(2,36) = 144.38, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.89) and adaptation after-effect (F(2,36) = 98.93, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.85) compared to other groups. The result revealed a significant main effect for group (F(2, 36) = 1108.30, p < .001, η2 = 0.98), BIN (F(9, 324) = 318.05, p < .001, η2 = 0.90), and group × BIN interaction (F(2, 324) = 2.72, p = 0.01, η2 = 0.13). Negative correlations were observed between serum levels of IL-6 and hsCRP and fatigue, saccadic velocity, adaptation rate and adaptation aftereffect in stroke survivors (p < 0.05). Both stroke groups showed significantly reduced gain in pro- and anti-saccade tasks compared to healthy controls, yet gain was not significantly correlated with IL-6 or hsCRP levels. The study demonstrated that fatigue significantly affects saccade control and adaptation in stroke survivors, with inflammation having a negative impact on these processes. Pro- and anti-saccadic gain was reduced in stroke participants but unrelated to inflammation, while adaptive gain during saccade adaptation correlated with inflammatory markers, suggesting a link between PSF, adaptation, and inflammation. Introduction

Post-stroke fatigue (PSF) is a common chronic symptom and a major unmet need, affecting approximately 29–68% of stroke survivors1–3. It is characterized by a persistent and overwhelming sense of physical and/or mental exhaustion that is disproportionate to exertion and not adequately relieved by rest4. Unlike transient fatigue, PSF often persists for months to years after stroke, acquiring a chronic nature that substantially limits functional outcomes and motor performance2,5–7.

PSF is thought to arise from multifactorial pathophysiological mechanisms, including neuroinflammatory responses, disruptions in cortico-subcortical neural connectivity, and dysregulation of key neurotransmitter systems—particularly the dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways5,8. Collectively, these alterations can disrupt brain networks responsible for motor planning, attention, motivation, and arousal. Progressive neurodegeneration has also been proposed as a contributing factor, potentially mediated by chronic neuroinflammation, secondary white matter degeneration, and altered neurotransmitter regulation9,10. These processes may result in a progressive course of fatigue, with symptoms gradually worsening during the chronic phase of stroke. Consequently, stroke survivors experiencing progressive neurodegeneration may be at increased risk of PSF, which can further impair motor control and learning by reducing the brain’s capacity to plan, execute, and adapt movements7,11,12.

Saccadic eye movements—rapid shifts in gaze essential for visual information gathering and daily interactions—provide a precise model for investigating how fatigue affects motor control and adaptive motor learning. Saccade dynamics, including velocity, amplitude, and duration, are sensitive to fatigue across different populations13–18. Their simplicity, with clearly defined start and end points, allows for precise error measurement, and their high frequency provides abundant data. Stroke can affect cortical and subcortical regions involved in saccades, leading to inattention, deficits in spatial awareness, balance and movement difficulties, and reading problems19. PSF can further exacerbate these impairments, compromising both the control and adaptive modulation of saccadic eye movements.

Fatigue may influence saccadic movements through neural mechanisms involving regions associated with motor control, attention, and alertness. It likely impacts the planning, execution, initiation, inhibition, and coordination of saccadic movements20,21. Although PSF has been linked to impaired motor performance in tasks such as balance and gait6,7, its effects on saccades remain unexplored. The association between PSF and saccade adaptation also remains inconclusive. While current findings do not directly address saccade adaptation, they suggest that fatigue may influence adaptation processes by altering the neural mechanisms involved in saccade generation and control15,20. Fatigue may affect saccade adaptation by altering activity in neural regions involved in adaptation or by affecting cognition, which interferes with the attention and processing resources needed for effective adaptation22–24.

Previous studies indicate an association between PSF and inflammation in both the acute and chronic stages of stroke4,5,25. One study highlighted that circulating levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) are significantly linked to PSF in the chronic stage of stroke25. Inflammation also plays a crucial role in impairing learning and recovery after a stroke, likely due to inflammatory cytokines and immune mediators that disrupt synaptic plasticity26. Therefore, PSF may impact saccade control and learning through inflammatory factors.

Therefore, this study has three objectives: (1) examine the impact of PSF on saccadic control, (2) assess the effect of PSF on saccade adaptation, and (3) explore the correlation between IL-6 and hsCRP serum levels with saccade control and adaptation in chronic stroke survivors.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twenty-six first-time stroke survivors with middle cerebral artery involvement (at least 6 months prior), confirmed by MRI and neurological examination, and thirteen age-matched healthy controls participated in this study. All participants could read and write and had no cognitive impairment (Montreal Cognitive Assessment score ≥ 24(. Exclusion criteria included psychiatric, neurological, rheumatological, diabetes, or orthopedic diseases; alcohol or drug abuse; depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Depression (HADS-D) score > 7); anxiety (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety (HADS-A) score > 7 ; Uncorrectable visual impairment, including issues with visual acuity, visual field, hemi-neglect, or ocular alignment, assessed using the Snellen chart, confrontation test, star cancellation test, or cover uncover test respectively; sleep problems (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) > 5 and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) ≥ 10); pain (Visual Analogue Scale- pain (VAS-P) > 3.4); apathy (Dimensional Apathy Scale (DAS) > 39); hearing problems; use of drugs affecting fatigue (e.g., fluoxetine, duloxetine, modafinil, sertraline, citalopram, tolazamide mesylate, vitamins B and C, acetylsalicylic acid, D2 agonists, synthetic serotonin, etc.); and inability to complete the most difficult experimental condition.

Stroke participants were categorized into high fatigue (HF-stroke) and low fatigue (LF-stroke) groups based on the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS)27 combined with Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS)28. Participants showing fatigue on only one scale were excluded. Scores ≥ 36 on FSS and ≥ 24 on FAS were classified as HF-stroke, while scores < 36 on FSS and < 24 on FAS were classified as LF-stroke. The healthy controls comprised individuals with a FSS score of < 36 and a FAS < 24, indicating low levels of fatigue. Both the FSS and FAS are reliable tools for screening fatigue in stroke survivors27,28 and Iranian population.

The stroke groups were compared based on lesion volume and location. By including only first-time MCA stroke survivors with comparable stroke type, lesion volume, and lesion location, we aimed to minimize stroke-related variability in oculomotor outcomes and better isolate the impact of fatigue severity on saccadic measures.

Sleepiness, state fatigue, state anxiety, executive function and visual attention, cognitive processing speed, and post-stroke disability were assessed using the Stanford Slee iness Scale (SSS), Visual Analog Scale-Fatigue (VAS-F), state-trait anxiety inventory (STAI) (Form Y) > 30, Trail Making Test, Digit Symbol Substitution Task, and Modified Rankin Scale, respectively.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of IUMS (Ethical Code: IR.IUMS.REC.1401.970), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study protocol

Clinical assessments and blood sampling were performed the day before the laboratory visit. The experimental sessions were conducted between 10:00 am and 2:00 pm, based on participants’ agreements regarding their physiological fitness. The pro-saccade and anti-saccade tasks were performed on one day (randomly), while the saccade adaptation task was conducted on a different, randomly selected day. The STAI, SSS, and VAS-F were completed at the beginning and end of each testing day.

The horizontal eye saccade movements during pro-saccade, anti-saccade and saccade adaptation tasks were collected using EyeLink® 1000 Plus eye tracker. Serum levels of IL-6 and hsCRP were measured using ELISA kit and a Fluorescence Immunoassay Analyzer, respectively (see Appendix A for more details about eye-tracking setup and experimental design).

Data analysis

The saccade recordings were analyzed offline using custom-written MATLAB code to calculate pro-saccade, anti-saccade, and saccade adaptation parameters (see Appendix B for more details).

Statistical analysis

A preliminary power analysis, using the standard deviation of saccade velocity from a pilot study, indicated that with a type I error probability of 0.05, type II error probability of 0.20, and 10% dropout rate, 13 participants per group were needed. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics. Group differences in demographic parameters were analyzed using one way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, and Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The Chi-square test assessed categorical variables.

Since no significant differences were found between leftward and rightward movements, or between 15- and 10-degree movements in pro-saccade and anti-saccade parameters across the different groups, comparisons were made using the mean values of 40 trials per subject for both pro-saccades and anti-saccades, regardless of direction or eccentricity. Pro-saccade and anti-saccade parameters were compared between groups using one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey tests, except for pro-saccade duration, which was non-normally distributed and analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni adjustments.

A logarithmic transformation was applied to the average gain change in each BIN to address non-normal distribution. Subsequently, a 3 × 10 mixed ANOVA compared average gain change across BINs (BIN1 to BIN10) among the groups (LF-stroke, HF-stroke, healthy), followed by post hoc Tukey tests. Adaptation rate and adaptation aftereffect were compared between groups using one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey tests.

IL-6 and hsCRP serum levels were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni adjustments. The correlation of IL-6 and hsCRP serum levels with pro-saccade, anti-saccade and saccade adaptation parameters and fatigue was assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients. Correlations were interpreted as: 0.10–0.39 (weak), 0.40–0.69 (moderate), and ≥ 0.70 (strong)29.Effect size was measured using partial eta squared (η2), classified as small (η2 ≥ 0.01), medium (η2 ≥ 0.06), and large (η2 ≥ 0.14)30. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Inflammatory factors

IL-6 and hsCRP serum levels differed significantly among the groups (χ2 = 7.42, p = 0.025; χ2 = 8.33, p = 0.016, respectively). Multiple comparisons showed the HF-stroke group had significantly higher levels of peripheral IL-6 and hsCRP compared to the LF-stroke and healthy groups (p < 0.05) (Table 2, Figs. 2C and D).

IL-6 and hsCRP showed a significant moderate negative correlation with mean and peak velocity in pro- and anti-saccades and a moderate positive correlation with pro- and anti-saccade latency (r = 0.41–0.57, p < 0.05). Additionally, pro-saccade mean velocity and duration had significant correlations with hsCRP levels in stroke survivors (r = − 0.65 and 0.48, respectively, p < 0.05). There was no significant correlation between saccadic gain and IL-6 or hsCRP. Althouch, IL-6 and hsCRP had a significant moderate negative correlation with adaptation rate and adaptation aftereffect (r = 0.41–0.50, p < 0.05) (Table 4, Fig. 3).

Discussion

PSF is a common condition that can persist for years after a stroke1 and may significantly impact motor control and learning, particularly saccadic control and adaptation. Saccadic movements are crucial for maintaining spatial awareness, balance, and coordination, helping to prevent falls and accidents, and are important for independence and quality of life in stroke survivors19. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to examine the effects of PSF on saccadic control and adaptation, as well as to explore the correlation between inflammatory factors (IL-6 and hsCRP) and saccadic control and adaptation in chronic stroke survivors. Our results show that PSF increases latency and reduces velocity in pro- and anti-saccade tasks, as well as decreases adaptation rates and aftereffects. Higher levels of IL-6 and hsCRP correlate with greater impairments in saccadic movements and adaptation in stroke survivors.

This study found that HF-stroke survivors exhibited significantly higher saccade latency, duration, and positional error, along with lower velocity in both pro- and anti-saccade tasks compared to LF-stroke survivors. These findings align with previous studies on the effects of fatigue on saccadic movements in individuals with multiple sclerosis and chronic fatigue syndrome13,18,31. The effect of PSF on motor control has also been confirmed in other tasks, such as balance and functional gait, in stroke survivors6,7. Fatigue likely leads to disruptions in saccadic movements by affecting the cognitive, motor, or perception and sensory components of saccade. From a cognitive perspective, activity of the frontal cortex influences the attentional and cognitive components of saccadic movements32. It is possible that PSF disrupts saccades through attentional and cognitive mechanisms by impacting frontal cortex activity4. From a motor perspective, the basal ganglia and superior colliculus play a crucial role in the control of saccades33,34. Fatigue may disrupt the function of these brain areas, affecting the smoothness, direction, amplitude, and overall performance of saccadic movements, as well as the motor commands required for executing saccades20. From a perception and sensory perspective, the parietal cortex is a primary region involved in the dorsal visual processing stream that processes visual information for spatial perception and action guidance35. PSF can influence visual perception by affecting parietal function36. On the other hand, according to sensory integration theory, saccadic movements are highly dependent on sensory integration, and disruptions in this process by fatigue can affect the oculomotor system and saccade control37.

This study found that IL-6 and hsCRP serum levels were higher in the HF-stroke group compared to both the LF-stroke and healthy groups. These findings align with previous research linking inflammatory markers to fatigue in stroke survivors during both the acute and chronic phases4,5,25. Although the exact mechanisms underlying PSF are not fully understood, strong evidence suggests that inflammatory factors can independently trigger sickness-like behaviors, including fatigue5,38. Thus, it is plausible that elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 or hsCRP, could contribute to PSF. Additionally, the results show a significant positive correlation between PSF and serum levels of IL-6 and hsCRP, as well as negative correlations between these inflamatory factors with saccade velocity in both pro and anti-saccade in stroke survivors. This suggest that inflammation may mediate the effects of PSF on saccade control. Peripheral inflammation in stroke survivors, along with disruption of the blood–brain barrier, may affect neural regions and neurotransmitter systems essential for saccade control, particularly basal ganglia, which are crucial for motor control39. Moreover, higher self-reported fatigue (FSS/FAS) was correlated with slower saccades, increased latency, greater positional errors, and reduced adaptive gain, as well as with elevated inflammatory markers. These findings provide an integrated perspective, linking subjective fatigue, objective saccadic performance, and post-stroke inflammation, highlighting how fatigue may be reflected in both behavioral and physiological measures.

Saccade adaptation gradually adjusts the saccade amplitude to ensure accuracy when saccadic eye movements become hypometric or hypermetric relative to the target. Our results indicate that the HF-stroke group had lower adaptation rates compared to the LF-stroke group. This result can likely be explained by the following reasons. The first possible reason could be related to a disturbance in the internal feedback mechanisms and error correction, which rely on the forward model. This model predicts the sensory outcomes of motor commands, crucial for accurate saccades and saccade adaptation40. PSF may lead to altered saccade adaptation by disrupting these internal feedback mechanisms20. Other potential reason could be due to the impact of fatigue on neuroplasticity, which may impair effective motor learning. This is significant because the relearning of motor skills after a stroke is associated with neuroplastic changes in the brain. Previous studies have shown that fatigue induces long-lasting detrimental effects on motor-skill learning in healthy individuals11. Another study demonstrated that fatigue-controlled physical training positively influenced neuroplasticity in the striatum of rats following a stroke41. Plastic changes occur during saccade adaptation, particularly in the firing patterns of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum42. These alterations are crucial for fine-tuning motor responses and contribute to the overall adaptation process. Indeed, saccadic adaptation primarily depends on the cerebellum for computing and correcting motor errors with contributions from the frontal eye fields and posterior parietal cortex that provide signals for fine-tuning saccadic accuracy40. In our study, lesions were predominantly located in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes, as well as in the basal ganglia and corona radiata, which may indirectly disrupt cerebellar function through fronto-cerebellar disconnection. Such lesions can alter networks involved in motor planning, attention, and sensorimotor integration, thereby contributing to both impaired saccade control and reduced adaptation performance even in the absence of direct cerebellar damage. An additional possible explanation could be the correlation between fatigue and cognitive function. Cognitive processes play a crucial role in acquiring, refining, and adapting motor skills43. Fatigue can reduce cognitive flexibility and impair the ability to concentrate and process information effectively. This occurs because brain resources are diverted toward managing fatigue rather than focusing on tasks. Therefore, stroke survivors with PSF may be more affected by the cognitive load of the saccade adaptation task.

Additionally, the results show a significant negative correlations between serum levels of IL-6 and hsCRP with saccadic adaptation in stroke survivors. This suggests that inflammation may mediate the effects of PSF on saccade adaptation, similar to its role in mediating saccade control. Inflammation can impair neuroplasticity, a process essential for motor learning as it involves refining motor skills through repeated practice. In stroke patients, inflammation-related oxidative stress and neuroplasticity changes, as indicated by serum biomarkers like IL-6, are linked to motor function improvements following rehabilitation44. On the other hand, elevated levels of inflammatory markers, such as IL-6 and CRP, have been associated with cognitive deficits45, which can affect saccade adaptation. Our results highlight a distinction between pro- and anti-saccade gain and adaptive gain modulation during saccade adaptation (adaptation rate and aftereffect). While both were impaired in chronic stroke survivors compared to healthy controls, only adaptive gain measures correlated with inflammatory markers. This suggests that post-stroke inflammation may selectively affect neural mechanisms underlying adaptive motor control, emphasizing the importance of considering adaptive processes when assessing motor deficits associated with PSF.

While this study offers valuable insights into the impact of PSF on saccadic control and adaptation, several limitations exist. The sample size was relatively small, necessitating larger studies to confirm these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms in more detail. Although all patients had MCA involvement and stroke type, as well as lesion characteristics, were compared between groups, more advanced lesion–symptom mapping analyses could provide deeper insights into how subtle differences in lesion topography contribute to saccadic performance. PSF may, at least in part, result from progressive neurodegeneration. Although the inclusion criteria and clinical assessments in our study were designed to minimize the influence of degenerative processes, the study was not specifically designed to directly evaluate progressive neurodegeneration. Subtle neurodegenerative changes over time cannot be entirely excluded. Future longitudinal studies employing advanced structural and functional imaging are needed to clarify the role of progressive neurodegeneration in the development and progression of PSF. Although none of our participants had direct cerebellar infarcts, impaired saccadic adaptation may reflect secondary or indirect cerebellar dysfunction due to disrupted connectivity between the fronto-parietal regions and the cerebellum. Future studies using functional connectivity or diffusion tensor imaging could further clarify this mechanism. Another potential limitation of this study is that saccadic adaptation was assessed only in the rightward direction, potentially overlooking directional asymmetries. Future research would benefit from assessing both directions to provide a more comprehensive understanding of this phenomenon. Future research should also explore the efficacy of various interventions aimed at reducing fatigue and inflammation to improve motor control and learning in stroke survivors. It is also recommended that future studies consider using predictive models to explore the potential of inflammatory factors and saccade indices in predicting fatigue. According to the results obtained in this study, any pharmacological treatment that can control inflammation appears to play a role in improving fatigue, as well as motor control and learning, in chronic stroke survivors. Therefore, it is suggested that future studies examine the effects of anti-inflammatory drugs on these factors. Given the impact of fatigue on motor control and learning, it seems that more specialized treatment approaches from a rehabilitation perspective should be considered for stroke survivors with PSF compared to those without PSF.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant impact of PSF on saccade control and adaptation, with inflammation playing a key mediating role. The findings emphasize the need for a comprehensive assessment and management of fatigue in therapeutic interventions for stroke survivors. Addressing these factors could enhance recovery outcomes and improve the quality of life for stroke survivors.
