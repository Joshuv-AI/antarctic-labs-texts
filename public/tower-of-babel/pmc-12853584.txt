Exploration of the associations between violent and aggressive behaviors risk and NHHR, TyG in patients with schizophrenia: an SHAP interpretable machine learning prediction and FAERS pharmacovigilance analysis
Authors: Guangwei Qing, Hanggai Taorigen, Yuxin Zhou, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchizophrenia (SCZ) patients have high violent and aggressive behavior (VAB) prevalence, causing harm and stigma. The non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) and triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) show promise for psychiatric disorder prediction, but their links to SCZ patients’ VAB risk remain understudied.MethodsA cross-sectional study included 102 hospitalized SCZ patients from May 2023–October 2024, stratified by Broset Violence Checklist (BVC) into low/medium/high VAB risk groups. Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) quantified psychiatric severity; NHHR and TyG were calculated from venous biochemical indices. Analyses included multivariate logistic regression, mediation analysis (PANSS Scores as mediator), 7 machine learning (ML) models with SHAP interpretation, and FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) disproportionality analysis.ResultsIn fully adjusted models, NHHR and TyG were both negatively associated with VAB risk (NHHR: OR = 0.43, 95%CI:0.20–0.92, p = 0.0285; TyG: OR = 0.29, 95%CI:0.12–0.67, p = 0.0038); further analysis revealed an atypical inverted U-shaped correlation between NHHR and VAB risk, with the inflection point measured at 2.98. Meanwhile, PANSS scores partially mediated the associations between NHHR, TyG and VAB, and TyG had higher mediation proportions (54.2% for BVC scores, 57.8% for VAB risk) than NHHR. In ML-based VAB prediction, LASSO Logistic Regression was the optimal model (Accuracy = 0.8065, highest F1 Score), and SHAP analysis confirmed NHHR and TyG as core predictive features. Additionally, FAERS analysis identified positive metabolic abnormality signals for olanzapine (early-onset adverse events) and quetiapine (late-onset adverse events).ConclusionNHHR and TyG are potential VAB risk biomarkers for SCZ patients, with psychiatric severity mediating their effects. ML and FAERS provide clinical prediction and medication monitoring references.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02799-w.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) constitutes a chronic, severe mental disorder characterized by a gradually developing and persistent disease trajectory [1]. Patients primarily exhibit severe distortions in thought processes, often accompanied by psychotic symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions [2, 3]. Among SCZ patients, particularly those with severe illness, the incidence of violent and aggressive behaviors (VAB) is relatively high, ranking among the highest across all mental disorders [4]. VAB in patients with severe mental disorders is a major issue of significant social concern. Such behaviors not only may cause severe harm to others but also exacerbate the social stigma attached to patients, leading many to be involuntarily hospitalized and even requiring long-term institutionalization [5]. A comprehensive meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of VAB among individuals diagnosed with SCZ and hospitalized ranges from 15.3% to 53.2% [6]. Therefore, identifying novel biomarkers for assessing VAB risk in individuals with SCZ is crucial for optimizing prevention and treatment strategies.

In studies related to metabolites and VAB, previous and recent evidence has suggested that dysregulation of lipid and glucose metabolism induces various pathological changes [7]. These changes are not only associated with normal brain development and function but may even affect VAB and other impulsive behaviors [8]. A previous prospective study of hospitalized SCZ patients identified distinct VAB trajectories and observed significant triglyceride level variations across different trajectory categories, as a decrease in triglycerides associated with increased VAB [9]. Separately, the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR)—a new composite lipid index—has shown valuable predictive potential in gauging the risk of diverse diseases, ranging from coronary artery disease and diabetes to mental health conditions such as depression and suicidal ideation [10–12]. Although there is a lack of empirical studies examining the relationship between NHHR levels and VAB, a large number of literatures have explored the associations between VAB and traditional lipid indicators. Within a transverse study conducted by Anjana Rao Kavoor et al. that included 60 SCZ patients, TC, LDL, and TG levels were significantly negatively correlated with impulsivity scores; TC and LDL also correlated negatively with suicidal tendency scores [13]. Another study by Klara Suneson included 52 recently suicidal inpatients without psychotropic treatment. TC and LDL levels were significantly negatively correlated with State Aggression, and LDL additionally correlated negatively with Trait Aggression, with this association potentially mediated by anxiety symptom severity [14]. A cross-sectional study by Dora Herceg et al., focusing on 120 female SCZ patients, found HDL levels were significantly lower in the aggressive group; abnormal LDL was ~ 3x more common in the aggressive group [15]. Additionally, among numerous indicators for evaluating insulin resistance (IR), the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) is an emerging composite lipid metabolism index that not only accurately assesses IR but also serves as an alternative to traditional predictive indicators for this condition [16]. Previous studies have demonstrated that it has exhibited robust predictive utility in both cardiovascular and neurological diseases [17], and even holds significant implications for the prediction and assessment of psychiatric disorders such as cognitive impairment and major depression with suicide attempts [18, 19].

Taken together, novel biomarkers such as the TyG and NHHR have demonstrated significant application potential in assessing the risk of multi-system diseases in SCZ patients and even in the psychiatric domain [20, 21]. The present study focuses on SCZ patients, aiming to deeply dissect the intrinsic associations between the risk of VAB and the NHHR and TyG. Presently, the majority of related research utilizes conventional statistical analysis methods, while our use of machine learning (ML) could prove more efficient at precisely outlining the correlations among NHHR, TyG, and VAB [22]. Mediation analysis will also be carried out to examine the possible mediating role of SCZ severity on these relationships [23]. Given that cross-sectional studies struggle to rule out the confounding effects of antipsychotic medications on patients’ metabolic indicators, this study utilizes the FDA’s Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database to conduct a real-world disproportionality analysis on glucose and lipid metabolic abnormalities induced by SCZ treatment medications [24]. It intends to identify high-risk drug signals for metabolic disorders, further explore the potential association between such medication-induced metabolic abnormalities in SCZ treatment, and provide real-world evidence for auxiliary revelation whether the association between metabolic indicators and VAB may be influenced by high-risk medications [25].

Through these studies, we aim to provide more personalized and efficacious treatment regimens for SCZ patients, reduce the incidence of VAB, and boost the quality of life for them [26]. Furthermore, the identification of these biomarkers offers novel perspectives and tactics for preventing and treating SCZ [27].

Materials and methods

Study population

The study initially enrolled 176 hospitalized SCZ patients at Jiangxi Mental Health Center. These patients were admitted between May 2023 and October 2024 and met the inclusion criteria. After excluding the subjects who met the exclusion criteria, 102 patients were eventually included in the analysis. Patients were classified into three groups on the basis of their BVC scale scores: the low-risk group (score = 0), the medium-risk group (scoring 1–2), and the high-risk group (scoring 3–6) (Fig. 1 A for details) [28].

Research Framework for VAB in SCZ patients. Panel A: Categorizing Schizophrenia Inpatients by Violent Aggression Risk. Panel B: Research Process and Analytical Methods of Biochemical Indicators. Panel C: Drugs Induced Adverse Reactions from FAERS Data

Eligible participants met the following criteria [29]: (1) aged 18 to 60 years; (2) met the criteria for diagnosis about SCZ as specified in the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), with the diagnosis confirmed jointly by at least two physicians with the title of attending physician or above; (3) were able to cooperate with and complete the scale assessments required for this study; (4) had adequate comprehension ability and audio-visual function; (5) had obtained informed consent from themselves and their family members for participation. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Confirmed diagnosis of other mental disorders like bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder; (2) Diagnosis of severe neurological diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, or epilepsy(3) Severe organ dysfunction diseases involving the heart, kidneys, or liver; (4) Current use of medications that may impact blood lipid levels; (5) Pregnant or lactating women; (6) Symptoms of malnutrition or eating disorders, which may affect the stability of metabolic indicators; (7) Participation in other clinical studies or intervention programs that may interfere with the results of this study.

Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at Jiangxi Mental Hospital (Ethical Registration Number: V1.0,2023.12.13.032), and the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients and their family members voluntarily consented to participate in this study and signed the corresponding informed consent forms.

Collection of general information

On the day of admission, patients’ basic information was collected using a self-designed general information questionnaire, including: age (years); gender (male/female); marital status (unmarried, married, divorced, widowed); educational level (Illiterate, elementary school, junior high school, high school, vocational high school, junior college, bachelor’s degree and above); smoking (yes/no); alcohol consumption (yes/no); hypertension (yes/no); diabetes (yes/no); body weight (kg); and body mass index (BMI).

Assessment of violent and aggressive behaviors

On the day of admission, the Chinese version of the Broset Violence Checklist (BVC) was used to assess patients’ risk of VAB. The BVC is one of the most widely applied short-term violent risk assessment tools in psychiatric care, designed to predict VAB within an 8–24-hour window by evaluating six risk factors: confusion, irritability, noisiness, verbal threats, physical threats, and aggression toward objects [30]. We excluded patients with documented VAB episodes within 72 h before admission to avoid confounding the assessment of baseline VAB risk. Specifically, the BVC reflects patients’ risk level via scores (total possible score = 6, a score of 0 indicates low risk, scores of 1–2 denote moderate risk, and scores of 3–6 represent high risk), with scores positively correlated to their future VAB risk; assessors should implement targeted prevention strategies based on scores [31]. The Chinese version of the BVC was translated by Yufei Wu, who also ascertained this tool’s psychometric properties: internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.74) and content validity (0.93, with optimal validity for the 8-hour prediction window) [32].

Assessment of psychopathological symptoms and severity

In strict accordance with the ICD-10 criteria, a systematic, structured clinical interview was conducted for each SCZ patient who fulfilled the inclusion criteria for this research. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) served to quantitatively assess the classes and severity of patients’ psychopathological symptoms [33]. Developed by Kay and his research team in 1987 [34], validated studies have shown that the Chinese version of the PANSS exhibits excellent reliability and validity, which renders it appropriate for quantifying symptoms in SCZ patients [35].

Appraisal of suicide risk

The Nurses’ Global Assessment of Suicide Risk (NGASR) was used to assess patients’ suicide risk. Developed by British researchers, this internationally recognized tool is specifically designed for nurses to evaluate the suicide risk of psychiatric patients [36]. The NGASR comprises 15 items, with a total score calculated via a weighted scoring method. Each item uses dichotomous “yes/no” responses, where a “yes” response scores 1 point, resulting in a maximum total score of 25 [37].

Determination of NHHR and TyG index

Venous biochemical blood samples were collected from patients within 24 hours of admission. A series of biochemical parameters was measured using the Japanese Beckman Coulter automatic biochemical analyzer (model: AU680), including total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and blood glucose (BG). Fasting venous blood samples (collected after 8–12 hours of overnight fasting) were used to measure TG and BG for TyG index calculation,” to clarify the measurement protocol. All levels of cholesterol, triglycerides, and blood glucose were expressed in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL) [38]. The non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) to HDL-C ratio was used to calculate NHHR, with the formula: NHHR = (TC - HDL-C)/HDL-C [11]. The formula for the TyG index was: TyG = Ln [fasting triglycerides × fasting blood glucose/2] [39](Fig. 1B).

FAERS database source and collection

We initiated a retrospective pharmacovigilance evaluation and performed an analysis of adverse events associated with lipid and glucose metabolism disorders during SCZ treatment, using data from the FAERS database covering Q1 2004 to Q2 2025. This study was carried out in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

These sources encompass demographic and administrative details (DEMO), adverse drug reaction records (REAC), patient outcome data (OUTC), drug-specific information (DRUG), therapy duration records (THER), reporting entity details (RPSR), and indications for medication use (INDI) [40]. The collected information was leveraged to classify adverse events (AEs) related to individual patients’ drug exposures. Adverse events were coded using preferred term (PT) codes from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA). These codes are organized into a hierarchical framework comprising system organ class (SOC), high-level group term (HLGT), and high-level term (HLT) [41]. To ensure analytical rigor, duplicate reports were removed. To secure analytical rigor, duplicate reports were excluded. Within the DEMO table, for entries with matching CASEID and FDA_DT, the record featuring the most recent FDA_DT was kept [42]. In cases where no match existed, the entry with the higher PRIMARYID was chosen [43].

In total, 22,775,812 AE reports were obtained from the FAERS database. Among these, 1,186 reports were related to drugs inducing lipid metabolism disorders in the treatment of SCZ, 4,593 reports were associated with drugs inducing glucose metabolism disorders in the treatment of SCZ, and 115,007 reports involved drugs inducing VAB as adverse reactions. Furthermore, among the reports of drugs inducing VAB as adverse reactions, 935 were attributed to drugs inducing VAB in the treatment of glucose metabolism disorders, and 315 were due to drugs inducing VAB in the treatment of lipid metabolism disorders. Our study’s methodology is detailed in the flowchart provided in Fig. 1C.

Statistical analysis

This study was analyzed using R 3.4.3 and Empower software. For continuous variables, data with normal distribution were expressed in the form of mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s) and subjected to t-test analysis; non-normal variables were presented as median and interquartile range [M(Q1, Q3)] and tested via Mann-Whitney U test for heterogeneous variance.

Categorical data were shown as frequencies and percentages [n(%)], with a chi-square test for inter-category associations. Multivariate logistic regression examined the independent associations of NHHR, TyG with VAB risk in SCZ patients across three models: Model 1 (no covariate adjustment), Model 2 (adjusted for gender, age), and Model 3 (further adjusted for marital status, educational level, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, PANSS score; additional adjustment for fasting blood glucose when analyzing NHHR).

Penalized spline smoothing curves and a weighted generalized additive model (GAM) regression explored non-linear relationships between NHHR, TyG, and VAB risk. Subgroup analyses used stratified multivariate regression (stratified by gender, age, educational level, and marital status), with a log-likelihood ratio test for model comparison. Missing data in the original dataset were handled by multiple imputation.

To examine the mediational role of SCZ severity in the relationship between VAB risk and TyG, as well as NHHR, we performed causal mediation analysis via the ‘mediation’ R package to quantify direct, mediating, and total effects. Importantly, a valid mediator must be associated with both exposure and outcome [44]. In the present study, TyG and NHHR functioned as independent variables, VAB risk as the outcome variable, and PANSS scores indicating SCZ severity as the mediator. This is consistent with the causal mediation framework, decomposing the impact of TyG and NHHR on VAB risk into direct effects and indirect effects mediated by SCZ severity [23]. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Construction of the machine Learning(ML) model

Our final dataset comprised 102 entries, split into two partitions: 70% (n = 71) were used for training and validation, and the remaining 30% (n = 31) were set aside for testing. A random splitting approach was adopted to ensure unbiased distribution between the two partitions. Six machine learning (ML) algorithms were utilized to predict NHHR and TyG in SCZ patients with VAB using the training set: LASSO Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, Naive Bayes, and XGBoost Decision Tree. These models were executed via EasyR software [45], chosen for their unique characteristics and adaptability to varied datasets. Multiple evaluation metrics were employed to assess the model, including Sensitivity, Specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), Precision, Recall, F1 Score, Balanced Accuracy, and Accuracy. To enhance model robustness and generalizability, 3-fold cross-validation (splitting training data into three subsets for iterative validation) and grid search were adopted to optimize hyperparameters through systematic exploration of value ranges. To confirm model stability, we repeated the random train-test split 5 times, and the average Accuracy and F1 Score of LASSO Logistic Regression across splits were 0.79 ± 0.02 and 0.81 ± 0.03, respectively,” to support the reliability of the optimal model.

Application of the shapley additive explanation (SHAP) method for model interpretability and predictor importance assessment

To improve model interpretability, the SHAP method was applied to evaluate the relative importance of predictor variables [46]. For each sample in the prediction, the model generates a predicted value. A visual display of the SHAP values for each feature across all samples is provided, where purple dots stand for higher feature values and yellow dots denote lower ones, and this is presented in the SHAP Summary plot.

Detection of potential drug safety signals based on the FAERS database

We compared target events and target drugs with other events and drugs from FAERS database via contingency table calculations, and investigated the observed frequency ratios between drug-exposed and non-exposed groups with the use of a four-cell table to screen for potential positive signals (Supplementary Table 1). Meanwhile, we conducted disproportionality analyses to identify potential safety signals, and employed the Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN), and Multinomial Gamma Poisson Shrinkage (MGPS) to assess the elevated risk of drug-related adverse events (AEs). If an adverse event exceeded the positivity threshold via any of the aforementioned methods, it was classified as a potential adverse drug reaction [47]; higher values indicated stronger signals, and this reflected a more significant association between the suspect drug and the adverse event (Information on the methods and thresholds for the disproportionality analyses is outlined in Supplementary Table 2).

For SCZ patients who reported lipid and glucose metabolism abnormalities, we performed descriptive analyses: we compared the onset times of drug-related metabolic abnormalities and classified the signal strength of relevant drugs based on ROR values. To comprehensively evaluate the spectrum of drug-related adverse events and clarify the potential association between metabolic abnormalities and VAB, we further analyzed VAB induced by drugs and the drugs of VAB caused by lipid and glucose metabolism abnormalities, as potential positive signals.

Results

General clinical data

In total, 102 SCZ patients were admitted into this study, of whom 52.94% were identified as having VAB risk; the distribution of participants’ BVC scores is shown in Fig. 2A. Regarding VAB risk stratification, the low-risk group included 38 SCZ patients, having a mean age of 36.53 ± 11.55 years and consisting of 26 males and 12 females; the medium-risk group was composed of 43 patients, with an average age of 34.70 ± 9.79 years (21 males, 22 females); and the high-risk group consisted of 21 patients, with an average age of 36.71 ± 9.50 years (11 males, 10 females). Disparities were found in NHHR level distribution among different risk groups: it was relatively concentrated in the high-risk group but more widespread in the low- and medium-risk groups. For TyG, the distribution characteristics also varied across risk groups, with the TyG levels in the high-risk group being more concentrated than those in the medium-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 2B). Compared with the medium-risk (HDL: 22.34 ± 3.57 mg/dL, TG: 22.94 ± 18.39 mg/dL) and low-risk groups (HDL: 22.12 ± 3.07 mg/dL, TG: 31.30 ± 18.50 mg/dL), the high-risk group had a higher HDL level (26.05 ± 9.50 mg/dL), a lower TG level (15.16 ± 5.97 mg/dL), and lower NHHR (2.35 ± 0.43 vs. 2.82 ± 0.63 vs. 3.12 ± 0.78) and TyG levels (6.48 ± 0.40 vs. 6.84 ± 0.70 vs. 7.21 ± 0.65), all of which showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). The distribution and clustering of different groups in terms of NHHR, TyG, and BVC scores are shown in Fig. 2C. Figure 2D displays a correlation matrix heatmap that visualizes the pairwise correlations between multiple metabolic and anthropometric indicators. Except for HDL-C, TG, TyG, and NHHR, no statistically significant differences were found among the three groups regarding other covariates, and detailed data are provided in Table 1.

Correlations of NHHR and TyG with VAB risk

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the associations of NHHR and TyG with VAB risk. For NHHR: Although correlation coefficients weakened across progressively adjusted models from Model 1 to Model 3, the fully adjusted model still revealed a negative correlation with VAB risk. Specifically, each 1-unit increase in NHHR was associated with a 57% lower risk of VAB in Model 3 (OR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.20–0.92; p = 0.0285). Sensitivity analysis using NHHR tertiles in Model 2 showed the highest tertile had a 66% lower risk than the lowest tertile (OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.12–0.93; p = 0.0364), but this trend was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (Table 2). For TyG: A significant negative correlation with VAB risk was observed in the fully adjusted model—each 1-unit increase in TyG was linked to a 71% reduced risk (OR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.12–0.67; p = 0.0038). Tertile-based sensitivity analysis in fully adjusted model indicated the highest TyG tertile had an 89% lower risk than the lowest tertile (OR = 0.11; 95% CI: 0.03–0.41; p = 0.0009), and a significant progressive downward trend was observed across all models (see Table 3).

Non-linear analysis of the associations between NHHR, TyG and VAB risk

In this study, the smoothed curve fitting method was used to conduct an in-depth analysis of the non-linear relationships between NHHR, TyG levels and the risk of VAB in SCZ patients, with the results presented in Fig. 3A. As clearly shown in the figure, there was an obvious atypical inverted U-shaped relationship between NHHR levels and the risk of VAB: when NHHR levels were in the low range, the risk of VAB decreased as NHHR levels increased; however, after NHHR levels exceeded a certain value, a further increase in NHHR levels led to a subsequent rise in the risk of VAB. Threshold analysis identified the inflection point of this curve at 2.98 (see details in Supplemental Table 3). When NHHR is below 2.98, VAB risk decreases as NHHR increases, and clinical attention may be paid to the positive regulation of metabolic indicators within this range; when NHHR exceeds 2.98, the risk may rebound, requiring enhanced monitoring. In exploring the relationship between TyG levels and the risk of VAB, it was found that overall, the risk of VAB showed a downward trend as TyG levels increased. However, this trend was not a strict linear relationship (Fig. 3B). Specifically, the change in TyG levels and the reduction in VAB risk did not follow a fixed proportion or linear pattern; instead, there existed a certain degree of fluctuation and uncertainty. To identify potential differences in the associations between the risk of VAB and NHHR, TyG levels across different populations and clinical subgroups, we performed subgroup analysis to determine influencing factors or markers that facilitate stratification (Fig. 3 C). However, no statistically significant associations were observed, which suggests that these associations may be consistent across different population settings.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analyses were conducted to explore whether PANSS scores mediated the associations of NHHR and TyG with BVC scores or VAB risk. For NHHR and BVC scores (Fig. 4 A), NHHR had a significant direct effect on BVC scores (DE = −0.483, 95%CI: −0.807 - −0.175, p = 0.002), and PANSS scores mediated this relationship (ME = −0.289, 95% CI: −0.587 - −0.012, p = 0.034), accounting for 37.4% of the total effect. For TyG and BVC scores (Fig. 4B), TyG exhibited a significant direct effect on BVC scores (DE = −0.339, 95% CI: −0.618 - −0.041, p = 0.024), with PANSS scores showing a stronger mediation effect (ME = −0.402, 95% CI: −0.715 to −0.185, p < 0.001) that contributed to 54.2% of the total effect. Regarding VAB risk, although the direct effect of NHHR on VAB risk was non-significant, PANSS scores still exerted a significant mediation effect (ME = −0.091, 95% CI: −0.189 - −0.004, p = 0.034), explaining 42.4% of the total effect (Fig. 4 C). For TyG and VAB risk (Fig. 4D), despite the non-significant direct effect of TyG, PANSS scores mediated the association significantly (ME = −0.123, 95% CI: −0.230 to −0.056, p < 0.001), accounting for 57.8% of the total effect. Collectively, these results indicated that PANSS scores partially mediated the links between NHHR, TyG and VAB-related outcomes, underscoring the role of psychiatric symptom severity in the pathway from metabolic indicators to VAB risk.

Pharmacovigilance analysis from the FAERS database

Although most admitted patients had relapsed due to long-term drug discontinuation or were admitted for the first time without prior medication use, the limitations of the cross-sectional study prevented us from ruling out the interference of medication use. We further utilized the FAERS database to conduct pharmacovigilance analysis—aiming to make up for the insufficiency in excluding medication-related interference, and to auxiliary verify the potential associations between drugs for SCZ treatment and the occurrence of lipid/glucose metabolism abnormalities.

As shown in Supplementary Table 44, drugs showing positive signals for lipid metabolism disorders induced by SCZ included Olanzapine, Quetiapine, Fluoxetine, Gabapentin, Citalopram, Biperiden, and Bromperidol; these drugs were further categorized by psychiatric drug type in Fig. 6A. Among these, the two atypical antipsychotics had the highest number of AE reports. To evaluate the onset time of drug-induced AEs related to lipid metabolism disorders for these two atypical antipsychotics, Weibull distribution analysis was conducted (see Fig. 6B for specific probability change patterns). Specifically, Olanzapine had an estimated shape parameter (β) of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.41–1.02); this β value indicated a decreasing trend in AE occurrence over time, a pattern typically associated with early-onset adverse reactions. In contrast, Quetiapine had an estimated β of 1.07 (95% CI: 0.91–1.22), which suggested an increasing trend in AE occurrence over time—consistent with a pattern often linked to late-onset adverse reactions.

We further conducted an analysis of drug withdrawal-related adverse reactions of VAB induced by medications. The top 30 drugs with the highest number of positive reports are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Among these, varenicline (a smoking cessation agent) had the highest number of reported cases (n = 5102, ROR = 11.7) among drugs with positive signals. Montelukast exhibited the strongest signal intensity (n = 2683, ROR = 23.3). Antidepressants represented the largest category of drugs with positive signals: among selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), paroxetine(n = 3157, ROR = 14.87) and fluoxetine(n = 1035, ROR = 7.08) ranked highest in both case counts and signal strength, consistent with their serotonergic activity potentially modulating aggressive behavior [48]. Additionally, we performed a detailed analysis focusing on indications related to glucose and lipid metabolism abnormalities, with specific results presented in Fig. 6E and F and Supplementary Tables 88 and 9. In dyslipidemia patients, drugs with statistically significant positive signals exhibited substantially elevated ROR for VAB: for example, Simvastatin (ROR = 4.29) and Lovastatin (ROR = 6.93) showed particularly strong associations. Other lipid-lowering agents like Atorvastatin (ROR = 1.91) and Rosuvastatin (ROR = 1.97) also demonstrated significant positive signals, indicating their VAB-related adverse event. Notably, non-lipid agents such as aripiprazole (ROR = 262.58) and esomeprazole (ROR = 525.17) within this subgroup also presented extreme positive signals, suggesting potential off-target effects contributing to VAB. In patients with abnormal glucose metabolism, several drugs were identified with robust positive signals for VAB: pregabalin (ROR = 6.02), gabapentin (ROR = 9.46), and duloxetine (ROR = 15.49) ranked among the top in signal strength. Additionally, first-line glucose-lowering agents like metformin (ROR = 3.12) and insulin human (ROR = 1.51) also showed significant positive signals, highlighting the need for vigilance in VAB monitoring during their clinical use.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study involving 102 adult patients with SCZ admitted to Jiangxi Mental Hospital, we found that individuals with lower levels of NHHR and TyG were more likely to exhibit VAB. This association was validated in multiple subgroup analyses and showed consistency. We also observed an atypical inverted U-shaped relationship between NHHR and the risk of VAB, with 2.98 being the inflection point of this curve. Additionally, we developed a ML model to further demonstrate the clinical value of NHHR and TyG as important predictors of VAB occurrence. To further explore the intervention effect of drug treatment for SCZ on this relationship, we undertook a pharmacovigilance analysis via the FAERS database to support the study’s findings.

Relationship between VAB risk and NHHR

Based on current understanding, this study marks the preliminary exploration of the relationship between NHHR and VAB risk in SCZ patients. A growing body of evidence supports that NHHR is a superior indicator of the risk of lipid-related disorders [49, 50].

While prior findings do not provide direct evidence, they indirectly support the presumption concerning a negative correlational link between NHHR levels and VAB. The results of our study are broadly consistent with earlier research, and we found a statistically significant link between elevated NHHR levels and reduced risk of VAB in SCZ patients. Notably, the presence of a critical breakpoint in this association provides a practical risk stratification tool for psychiatrists managing SCZ patients.

There are multiple mechanisms linking lipid metabolism to VAB. One widely accepted theory is that serotonin exerts an inhibitory effect on VAB in the central nervous system (CNS) [49]. The cholesterol content in brain synaptic membranes is closely associated with the number of serotonin receptors—an elevation in cholesterol content brings about a corresponding elevation in the number of serotonin receptors [50].Conversely, cutting down on serum cholesterol brings down the cholesterol ratio and lipid microviscosity in brain cell membranes, and in the meantime lessens the exposure of serotonin receptors on the membrane’s surface [51]. These changes result in reduced uptake of circulating serotonin, followed by a decrease in serotonin entering brain cells [49], which in turn impairs serotonergic function in the brain. Excessively low cholesterol levels further reduce serotonin receptor activity, weakening the inhibitory effect on VAB and ultimately increasing an individual’s aggressiveness and impulsivity.

Beyond the aforementioned theory, there is also the inflammation-related hypothesis. A randomized controlled trial conducted by Emil F. Coccaro showed that compared with healthy controls, patients with intermittent explosive disorder who exhibited VAB had significantly elevated plasma levels of CRP and IL-6 [51]. Based on the mechanism of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and inflammatory balance, an increased ratio of n-6 to n-3 PUFAs induces a pro-inflammatory state [52]. Penttinen et al. noted that an increase in cytokines leads to elevated total cholesterol, reduced serum HDL, impaired melatonin release, and increased impulsivity and suicide risk in individuals [53].

Relationship between VAB risk and TyG

Our study yielded a critical finding in SCZ patients: TyG was significantly negatively associated with VAB risk. Notably, TyG has been proven to be a simple and convenient method for assessing IR [54].There is a close correlation between it and the results of the euglycemic hyperinsulinemic clamp test, the “gold standard” for IR assessment, and its effectiveness in evaluating IR is comparable to that of the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) [55]. Earlier studies have demonstrated that TyG exhibits clinical predictive value in research on multiple systemic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, kidney stones, stroke, and lung diseases [56–59]. Recent studies further indicate that TyG also plays an important predictive role in psychiatric and psychological research—its predictive value is particularly significant when assessing the risk of mental health conditions such as cognitive impairment, depression, and suicide attempts [60–62]. Though research on IR and VAB risk remains nascent, existing studies linking abnormal glucose metabolism to violence provide key insights. For example, a prospective study included 69 habitual violent offenders with recent serious violent crimes. Results showed low non-oxidative glucose (NOG) metabolism predicted higher violent offending risk in men with antisocial personality disorder—offenders who committed new violent crimes later had significantly lower NOG levels than healthy controls and non-recidivist offenders [63]. Separately, a 1990 cross-sectional study by Gottfredson et al. included 112 U.S. adult volunteers and found a positive correlation between diabetes severity and VAB (r = 0.49). This suggests that glucose metabolism capacity may affect an individual’s self-control ability, which in turn is related to the regulation of aggressive impulses [64].

When explaining the relationship between glucose metabolism and impulsive VAB behavior, a key perspective to adopt is that of self-control ability. Self-control denotes the capacity of an individual to inhibit their impulses, thoughts, and habitual tendencies in accordance with personal or socially accepted standards of appropriate behavior. From a psychophysiological standpoint, self-control is a limited psychological resource—when individuals exert self-control, they consume corresponding psychological energy, a phenomenon known as ego depletion [65]. Notably, after initial self-control exertion, an individual’s ability to inhibit aggressive impulses decreases significantly [66, 67]. This is because inhibiting aggressive impulses is highly dependent on self-control energy; once this energy is depleted, individuals are more prone to displaying aggression [68]. Glucose—a key blood chemical produced by metabolizing ingested nutrients—acts as a key energy source for brain physiological activities. Importantly, self-control is a complex process involving inhibiting strong impulses that relies in part on glucose for energy and consumes relatively large amounts of it [69, 70].

These hypotheses provide theoretical support and a biological mechanism for our findings. Our conclusion aligns with existing research, which shows that IR is not only closely linked to various cardiovascular diseases and metabolic dysfunctions yet may further raise the risk of VAB by disrupting brain metabolism and normal brain function.

The mediating mechanism linking metabolic abnormalities to VAB risk in SCZ patients

The results of the mediation analysis demonstrate that the impact of metabolic abnormalities on the risk of actual VAB in patients with SCZ is likely primarily achieved through the “bridging pathway” of exacerbating psychiatric symptoms, rather than through a direct effect [71]. Insulin resistance directly impairs glucose metabolism in brain regions critical for symptom regulation and impulse control, where glucose is the primary energy source [72, 73]. Reduced glucose availability in these regions can exacerbate psychotic symptoms (e.g., by disrupting dopamine and serotonin neurotransmission) and weaken self-control over aggressive impulses—creating a stronger “metabolism→symptom→VAB” cascade than lipid-only abnormalities [74–77]. By contrast, NHHR primarily reflects lipid profile imbalance, which exerts more indirect effects on brain function via altering synaptic membrane cholesterol content and thus shows a weaker mediating link through psychiatric symptoms [78, 79]. Furthermore, the phenomenon that the mediation proportion of TyG is generally higher than that of NHHR further suggests that insulin resistance—compared with simple lipid metabolism indicators—may be a more core metabolic target linking psychiatric symptoms to VAB risk [80]. This mediation effect not only validates the theoretical pathway of ‘metabolic abnormalities → exacerbated psychiatric symptoms → increased VAB risk’ but also provides dual clinical intervention directions: when managing VAB risk in SCZ patients, optimizing metabolic indicators and targeted control of psychiatric symptom severity may both block this association chain [81].

Machine learning analysis supporting clinical value verification for VAB prediction

In the machine learning analysis for VAB prediction, the key findings further support the clinical value of this study. From the perspective of model performance, LASSO logistic regression has been confirmed as the optimal prediction framework, and this balanced performance aligns perfectly with the clinical core requirement for risk stratification tools, which minimizes missed diagnoses and misdiagnoses [82]. More importantly, through SHAP interpretability analysis, we confirmed that NHHR and TyG occupy a core position consistently among all predictive features, and their predictive importance significantly outperforms that of traditional indicators [83]. This result is not merely a model output; instead, it closely corresponds to the mechanism elaborated earlier, that “abnormal lipid metabolism and insulin resistance are linked to VAB by affecting neurological function.” Although educational level ranks highest in predictive importance, this aligns with prior studies linking educational attainment to VAB [84, 85]. Epidemiological studies show lower education is often tied to higher VAB risk. Because education not only boosts problem-solving via knowledge transmission but also strengthens impulse control and empathy during long-term socialization—key psychological resources for inhibiting VAB [86, 87].

Pharmacovigilance analysis of metabolic risk of SCZ medications and implications of drug-induced VAB

The pharmacovigilance analysis using the FAERS database does not fully resolve the core limitation, the inherent inability to completely rule out medication interference, but rather supplements this limitation by providing complementary real-world evidence. Additionally, it uncovers several key findings highly relevant to the study’s core logic and clinical practice [88].

First, among commonly used drugs for SCZ treatment, olanzapine, quetiapine, and other medications were detected with positive signals for lipid and glucose metabolism abnormalities [89]. This provides real-world evidence for the “medication-metabolic abnormality” association in SCZ patients and explains why some patients exhibit abnormal metabolic indicators, with medication factors being one of the key triggers [90]. This is closely related to the pharmacological mechanisms of these two drugs: From the perspective of shared mechanisms, both olanzapine and quetiapine exert potent antagonistic effects on histamine H1 receptors [91]. This action inhibits the regulatory function of the hypothalamic feeding center, leading to excessive appetite and energy overconsumption in patients [92]. Subsequent fat accumulation and weight gain then occur, and abnormal weight is a critical predisposing factor for insulin resistance and dyslipidemia [93]. Meanwhile, the antagonistic effect of both drugs on 5-HT2C receptors weakens the inhibitory signals of serotonin on feeding behavior, further exacerbating the tendency for overeating and forming a cascade reaction [94]. From the perspective of drug-specific mechanisms, olanzapine exerts a more prominent blocking effect on muscarinic M3 receptors [95]. This effect directly inhibits the insulin-secreting function of pancreatic β-cells, resulting in elevated fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels [96]. Additionally, olanzapine interferes with hepatic lipoprotein metabolism, reducing HDL synthesis and increasing TG and LDL levels—making it one of the atypical antipsychotics with the highest metabolic risk [97].

Notably, Weibull distribution analysis revealed that olanzapine and quetiapine exhibit significant differences in the pattern of metabolic AEs: olanzapine is associated with a decreasing trend in metabolic AE risk over time, while quetiapine shows an increasing trend. This difference has direct implications for clinical monitoring: when using olanzapine, it is necessary to intensify metabolic indicator screening in the early stages of treatment; when using quetiapine, sustain long-term monitoring to avoid missing late-onset metabolic abnormalities.

Unlike previous reports, among drugs most likely to induce VAB, varenicline outpaces antidepressants of the SNRIs and SSRIs classes—specifically duloxetine and paroxetine—and accounts for the highest number of reports [98]. This phenomenon is closely linked to its unique pharmacological mechanisms and interference with neurotransmitter systems: As a partial agonist of nAChR, the core mechanism by which varenicline induces VAB lies in its bidirectional disruption of brain reward pathways and emotional regulation networks [99]. On one hand, its partial activation of the α4β2 subtype of nAChR promotes dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens [100]. While this relieves cravings caused by nicotine withdrawal, the intensity of this activation is far weaker than that of nicotine, leading to abnormal fluctuations in dopamine levels—especially in patients without a smoking history [101]. These unstable signals disrupt the prefrontal cortex’s inhibitory control over impulsive behaviors, increasing hostility and aggressive tendencies [102, 103].

Study strengths and limitations

This research explored a number of indicators associated with the risk of VAB in individuals with SCZ. These indicators are easily accessible and cost-effective in routine clinical practice, providing clinicians with a means to identify patients who may exhibit violent behavior at an early stage and implement timely interventions to reduce the occurrence of violent incidents. However, this study does have some limitations. First, on account of using a cross-sectional design, we fail to establish causal relationships among the variables, but only observe the associations among them. This design limits our understanding of the continuity and developmental nature of VAB, as such behavior is a complex, multi-stage process. Second, the time span of this study restricts our observation of the long-term changes and impacts of the indicators; Subsequent research should employ a longitudinal design to resolve this limitation. In addition, since all participants were recruited exclusively from the same medical center, the sample in this study may have selection bias, a factor that could limit the generalizability of the study results.

In future, patient groups with different characteristics should be included, while more potential influencing factors should be explored in depth. Moreover, multi-center collaboration should be adopted to develop a more universal and predictive model, so as to more accurately predict and intervene in VAB in SCZ patients.

Conclusion

This research examined the link between metabolic indicators and VAB risk in patients with SCZ, finding that NHHR and TyG were negatively correlated with VAB risk, with their effects primarily mediated by exacerbated psychiatric symptoms and TyG showing a stronger mediating role. Machine learning analysis confirmed LASSO Logistic Regression as the optimal VAB prediction model, with NHHR and TyG as core predictive features, while FAERS-based pharmacovigilance revealed that olanzapine and quetiapine had positive metabolic abnormality signals, with distinct AE time patterns. However, further verification through multi-center longitudinal studies is needed to confirm their generalizability and long-term predictive efficacy in clinical practice. Future research could further explore the impact of the interaction between NHHR/TyG and the dosage of antipsychotic drug on VAB risk.
