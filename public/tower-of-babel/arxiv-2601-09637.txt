Light-induced Magnetization by Quantum Geometry
Hiroki Yoshida, Takehito Yokoyama

Abstract
We propose a mechanism for the inverse Faraday and the inverse Cotton–Mouton effects arising from quantum geometry, characterized by the quantum metric quadrupole and the weighted quantum metric. Within a semiclassical framework based on the Boltzmann transport theory, we establish a general formalism describing light-induced magnetization in electronic systems as a second-order response to the electric field of light. Using continuum and tight-binding models, we discuss the symmetry constraints on these effects and estimate the magnitudes of the resulting magnetizations. Our results highlight a direct manifestation of quantum-geometric quantities in nonlinear magneto-optical responses and suggest a viable pathway for experimental detection.



I
Introduction

The Faraday effect is one of the most prominent magneto-optical phenomena, characterized by the rotation of the polarization plane of light as it propagates through a magnetized medium
Faraday 1846
. Its counterpart, the inverse Faraday effect (IFE), refers to the generation of magnetization in a material in response to circularly polarized light and has been both theoretically predicted
Pitaevskii 1961
;
Pershan 1963
;
Hertel 2006
;
Taguchi and Tatara 2011
;
Battiato et al. 2014
;
Sharma and Balatsky 2024
;
Cardoso et al. 2026
;
Tazuke et al. 2025
and experimentally confirmed
van der Ziel et al. 1965
;
Kimel et al. 2005
;
Kirilyuk et al. 2010
. Similarly, another form of light-induced magnetization is known as the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect, in which linearly polarized light generates magnetization; this effect has also been theoretically predicted
Pershan et al. 1966
and experimentally verified
Zon et al. 1987
. These phenomena have attracted considerable attention as mechanisms for the optical manipulation of magnetic order, or, more broadly, for imprinting information onto quantum states using light
Yeh et al. 2025a
;
Yeh et al. 2025b
;
Aeppli et al.
.

One of the most intriguing aspects of quantum states is their quantum geometry
Provost and Vallee 1980
;
Yu et al. 2025
. The Berry curvature
Berry 1984
;
Simon 1983
;
Aharonov and Anandan 1987
, which corresponds to the imaginary part of the quantum geometric tensor
Fubini 1904
;
Study 1905
, is known to play a pivotal role in phenomena such as the anomalous velocity of electron wave-packets
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Sundaram and Niu 1999
and the properties of topological materials. The real part of the quantum geometric tensor is known as the quantum metric. Recent studies have shown that the quantum metric contributes to Hall viscosity
Avron et al. 1995
;
Read and Rezayi 2011
, the Drude weight
Resta 2018
, and the superfluid weight
Peotta and Törmä 2015
;
Julku et al. 2016
;
Liang et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Julku et al. 2020
;
Törmä et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2023
. Moreover, the dipole moments of the Berry curvature (Berry curvature dipole) and the quantum metric (quantum metric dipole) are known to give rise to nonlinear Hall effects
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Das et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2023
. In addition to transport phenomena, quantum geometry also manifests itself in nonlinear optical responses. A paradigmatic example is the bulk photovoltaic effect
Kraut and von Baltz 1979
;
Belinicher and Sturman 1980
;
von Baltz and Kraut 1981
;
Aversa and Sipe 1995
;
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
;
Fridkin 2001
, in which a dc photocurrent is generated as a second-order response to the electric field, and the corresponding nonlinear conductivity can be expressed in terms of geometric quantities
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016a
;
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016b
;
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Ahn et al. 2022
. Although these studies have revealed close connections between optical responses and quantum geometry, the relationship between optically generated magnetization and quantum geometry remains unclear.

In this paper, we unveil a mechanism for light-induced magnetization in electronic systems mediated by quantum-geometric effects. We consider spatially nonuniform light, characterized by an electric field with explicit position dependence. Such spatial variation modifies the semiclassical equations of motion for electrons
Lapa and Hughes 2019
, which in turn alters the second-order current response obtained from the Boltzmann transport equation. The resulting current, arising from the spatial inhomogeneity of the electric field, can be interpreted as a magnetization current, enabling us to derive an expression for light-induced magnetization as a second-order response to the electric field. We find that the induced magnetization originates from two contributions: one associated with the quantum metric quadrupole
Liu et al. 2025
and the other related to the weighted quantum metric
Jain et al. 2025
;
Yoshida and Takehito
;
Ren and Barrero
;
Ren
. The latter contribution does not appear in transport currents but is essential for the calculation of the magnetization current. We further show that, in general, these two contributions give rise to magnetization under both linearly polarized light (LPL) and circularly polarized light (CPL), and are expected to be present in a wide range of materials (See Fig.
1
for schematic illustration of light-induced magnetization by quantum geometry).

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we provide a general theory of light-induced magnetization using the Boltzmann equation and derive general formulae for both LPL and CPL. Then, in Sec.
III
we introduce continuum and lattice models and numerically evaluate the size of the induced magnetization. We conclude this paper in Sec.
IV
.

II
General theory

In this section, we develop a general theoretical framework for light-induced magnetization. We calculate the magnetization current induced by light using the semiclassical equations of motion for electrons combined with the Boltzmann transport theory. We show that the magnetization, as a second-order response to the electric field, can be expressed in terms of quantum-geometric quantities.

II.1
Equation of motion

We begin with the semiclassical equations of motion for an electron in a spatially nonuniform electric field. Throughout this paper, we neglect the magnetic-field component of the light and focus exclusively on the electric field. The oscillating electric field is expressed in terms of a complex field
\tilde{E}
as

\displaystyle E_{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\real\left[\tilde{E}_{a}(\bm{r})e^{i\omega t}\right]\quad\left(\tilde{E}_{a}\in\mathbb{C}\right),

(1)

where
a=x,y,z
and the electric field is assumed to be oscillating in a single frequency
\omega
. Using the scalar potential
\phi(\bm{r},t)
and vector potential
\bm{A}(\bm{r},t)
, this electric field can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{E}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\phi(\bm{r},t)-\frac{\partial\bm{A}(\bm{r},t)}{\partial t}.

(2)

The Hamiltonian of the system under this electric field is

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{0}\left[\hat{\bm{q}}-Q\bm{A}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)\right]+Q\phi(\hat{\bm{r}},t),

(3)

where
\hat{H}_{0}(\hat{\bm{q}})
is a static Hamiltonian including a periodic potential and
Q=-e
is the charge of an electron. The semiclassical wave-packet dynamics of a system with this Hamiltonian is studied in Refs.
Sundaram and Niu 1999
;
Gao and Xiao 2019
. The semiclassical equations of motion is given as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{r}_{a}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}-\Omega^{\prime qq}_{ab}\dot{q}_{b}-\Omega^{\prime qr}_{ab}\dot{r}_{b}\\
\dot{q}_{a}=\frac{Q}{\hbar}E_{a}(\bm{r},t)+\Omega^{\prime rq}_{ab}\dot{q}_{b},\end{array}\right.

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}
is the energy of an
n
th band including the effect of an external electric field,
\Omega^{\prime qq}
and
\Omega^{\prime qr}
are the Berry curvatures of the
n
th band defined as

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime qq}_{ab}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-2\imaginary\left[\left\langle\partial_{q_{a}}u_{n}^{\prime}\middle|\partial_{q_{b}}u_{n}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right],

(6)

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime qr}_{ab}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-2\imaginary\left[\left\langle\partial_{q_{a}}u_{n}^{\prime}\middle|\partial_{r_{b}}u_{n}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right].

(7)

Here,
\Omega^{\prime qr}_{ab}=-\Omega^{\prime rq}_{ba}
and these quantities are defined using the perturbed state

\displaystyle\left\lvert u_{n}^{\prime}(\bm{r},\bm{q})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=|u_{n}\rangle+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{QE_{a}(\bm{r},t)(A_{mn})_{a}}{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}|u_{m}\rangle,

(8)

where
|u_{n}\rangle
is the unperturbed periodic part of Bloch state and
(A_{mn})_{a}=i\langle u_{m}|\partial_{q_{a}}u_{n}\rangle
is the Berry connection.

Below, we evaluate each quantity appearing in Eq. (
II.1
). As will be shown in the following sections, to achieve our final goal of deriving the magnetization response proportional to
\tilde{E}\tilde{E}^{*}
, it is sufficient to retain terms up to the order of
\tilde{E}\partial_{r}\tilde{E}^{*}
in the equations of motion. We begin by computing the mixed Berry curvature
\Omega^{\prime qr}
. Since the spatial derivative in Eq. (
7
) acts on the electric field in Eq. (
8
), we obtain, without further approximations,

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime qr}_{ab}

\displaystyle=-\frac{Q}{2\hbar}G_{aj}\partial_{r_{b}}E_{j}-2Q^{2}\imaginary\left[\sum_{m\neq n}\partial_{q_{a}}\left(\frac{\left(A_{nm}\right)_{i}}{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}\right)\frac{\left(A_{mn}\right)_{j}}{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}+i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\neq n\\
l\neq n\end{subarray}}\frac{\left(A_{nm}\right)_{i}\left(A_{ml}\right)_{a}\left(A_{ln}\right)_{j}}{(\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n})(\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{n})}\right]E_{i}\partial_{r_{b}}E_{j},

(9)

where

\displaystyle G_{aj}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 4\hbar\real\left[\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\left(A_{nm}\right)_{a}\left(A_{mn}\right)_{j}}{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}\right]

(10)

is a quantity called weighted quantum metric
Jain et al. 2025
;
Yoshida and Takehito
. This quantity appears in electric polarization induced by a uniform electric field
Gao et al. 2014
and nonadiabatic dynamics of electrons
Ren
;
Yoshida and Takehito
;
Ren and Barrero
. Then, by recursive substitution (see Appendix
A
for detail), we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{a}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{Q}{\hbar}E_{a}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}E_{b}\partial_{r_{a}}E_{j}.

(11)

We now turn to the calculation of
\dot{r}_{a}
. The correction to the group velocity can be obtained by evaluating the expectation value of the energy of the wave-packet
Gao and Xiao 2019
. The full expression for
\dot{r}
, including terms up to the order of
\tilde{E}\partial_{r}\tilde{E}^{*}
and contributions from
\Omega^{\prime qq}
, is presented in Appendix
A
. On the other hand, for the purpose of calculating the current up to the order of
\tilde{E}\partial_{r}\tilde{E}^{*}
, it is sufficient to retain
\dot{r}
only up to the order of
\partial_{r}\tilde{E}
, since the distribution function in metals contains at least first order in
\tilde{E}
, as discussed in Sec.
II.2
. In this case, the calculation follows the same procedure as in Ref.
Gao and Xiao 2019
. The resulting expression is

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{a}

\displaystyle\approx v_{a}-\frac{Q}{\hbar}\Omega^{qq}_{ab}E_{b}+\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\left(-\partial_{q_{a}}g_{bj}+G_{aj}v_{b}\right)\partial_{r_{b}}E_{j},

(12)

where
v_{a}=\partial_{q_{a}}\varepsilon_{n}/\hbar
is the group velocity of an electron in the
n
th band, the Berry curvature
\Omega^{qq}
is defined in terms of the unperturbed states, and
g_{ij}\coloneqq\real\left[\sum_{m\neq n}\left(A_{nm}\right)_{i}\left(A_{mn}\right)_{j}\right]
is the quantity known as the quantum metric. The derivative of this quantity is referred to as the quantum metric dipole. The term containing the quantum metric dipole in Eq. (
12
) originates from the correction to the group velocity,
\partial_{q_{a}}\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}/\hbar
, in Eq. (
II.1
), as also discussed in Refs.
Lapa and Hughes 2019
;
Gao and Xiao 2019
.

II.2
Electric current

We next calculate the electric current induced by
E\partial_{r}E
. Since we focus on phenomena that do not vanish upon time averaging, we consider the direct current proportional to
\tilde{E}\partial_{r}\tilde{E}^{*}
.

The electric current flowing in the
a
direction can be calculated as

\displaystyle j_{a}

\displaystyle=Q\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]D\dot{r}_{a}f(\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{q})),

(13)

where
\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]=\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}
with
d
denoting the dimensionality of the system, and
D
is the modified density of states
Xiao et al. 2005
, given by

\displaystyle D=1+\tr\Omega^{\prime qr}.

(14)

Here,
f(\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{q}))
is the distribution function. The nonequilibrium distribution function can be obtained from the Boltzmann transport equation within the relaxation-time approximation,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{t}+\dot{\bm{q}}\cdot\partialderivative{f}{\bm{q}}=-\frac{f-f_{0}}{\tau},

(15)

where
f_{0}
is the equilibrium distribution function and
\tau
denotes the relaxation time.

We derive the distribution function from Eq. (
15
) using
\dot{q}
that is also obtained (Eq. (
11
)) in the previous section. We write
f
as a Fourier series of complex functions
\tilde{f}_{m}
:
f(\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{q}))=\real\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}_{m}(\varepsilon_{n}(\bm{q}))e^{im\omega t}\right]
. By substituting this into Eq. (
15
), we obtain each
\tilde{f}_{m}
. We note that,
\dot{q}_{a}
includes a term proportional to
E\partial E
and thus, there is a term proportional to
E\partial E
in the distribution function, different from calculations in previous studies (Ref.
Sodemann and Fu 2015
, for example). We show the details of the calculation in Appendix
B
. The results up to
E\partial_{r}E
are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{0}

\displaystyle\approx f_{0}+\frac{\tau}{4}\left(\frac{Q}{\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial q_{a}}\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{r_{a}}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*},

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{-1}{1+i\tau\omega}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{\hbar}\right)\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial q_{a}}\tilde{E}_{a},

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{1+2i\tau\omega}\frac{\tau}{4}\left(\frac{Q}{\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial q_{a}}\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{r_{a}}\tilde{E}_{j},

(18)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{m}

\displaystyle\approx 0\qquad(m\geq 3).

(19)

In this paper, we focus on metallic systems. Therefore, for our calculation of the current Eq. (
13
) we only use Fermi surface terms. Also, as we are interested in dc response, what we use in the following are
\tilde{f}_{1}\,\left(\propto\tilde{E}\right)
and the second term in
\tilde{f}_{0}\,\left(\propto\tilde{E}\partial_{r}\tilde{E}^{*}\right)
.

By substituting Eqs. (
9
), (
11
), (
12
), (
14
), (
16
), and (
17
) to Eq. (
13
), we obtain a direct current proportional to
\tilde{E}\partial\tilde{E}^{*}
as

\displaystyle j^{0}_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau Q^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}}\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]\left[G_{bj}v_{a}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial q_{i}}+\frac{1}{1+i\tau\omega}\left(\partial_{q_{a}}g_{ij}+G_{ij}v_{a}-G_{aj}v_{i}\right)\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial q_{b}}\right]\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{r_{i}}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}+(\mathrm{c.c.}),

(20)

where
c.c.
represents the complex conjugate of the first term.

II.3
Magnetization

We derive the magnetization induced by light using the obtained expression for the electric current in Eq. (
20
).

The magnetization current
\bm{j}^{M}
is defined as the curl of a position-dependent magnetization
\bm{M}
,

\displaystyle\bm{j}^{M}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{r}\times\bm{M},

(21)

where
\nabla_{r}
denotes the derivative with respect to position. We focus on a static magnetization that remains nonzero after time averaging. Since the electric field is oscillatory, such a static magnetization should be at least second order in the electric field and can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{a}^{0}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{abc}\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}^{*}_{c},

(22)

where
M_{a}(\bm{r},t)=\real\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tilde{M}^{m}_{a}e^{im\omega t}\right]
. By taking the curl of the nonoscillating component of the magnetization,
M^{0}_{a}:=\real\left[\tilde{M}^{0}_{a}\right]
, we obtain the static magnetization current
\tilde{j}^{M,0}_{a}=\varepsilon_{aic}\left(\sigma_{cbj}+\sigma^{*}_{cjb}\right)\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{r_{i}}\tilde{E}^{*}_{j}+\mathrm{c.c.}
. By comparing this expression with Eq. (
20
), we identify
\sigma
in Eq. (
22
) as

\displaystyle\sigma_{abc}+\sigma^{*}_{acb}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau Q^{3}}{16\hbar^{2}\left(1+i\tau\omega\right)}\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]\varepsilon_{aij}\left(\partial_{q_{i}}g_{jc}+G_{jc}v_{i}-G_{ic}v_{j}\right)\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial q_{b}}.

(23)

This result is, coincidentally, analogous to the expression of the current proportional to
\partial_{r}E
in insulator obtained in Ref.
Gao and Xiao 2019
. The first term originates from the correction to the energy, whereas the latter two terms in the brackets result from the correction to the state (Eq. (
8
)). For the transport current, only the first term contributes
Lapa and Hughes 2019
;
Gao and Xiao 2019
, and the magnetization current is discarded. In contrast, in our case, the latter two terms play an essential role.

We now return to our original motivation of deriving the magnetization induced by light and consider its dependence on the polarization of the light. Physically, the magnetization is obtained by taking the real part of Eq. (
22
). For LPL,
\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}^{*}_{c}\right]
is nonzero and symmetric under the exchange of indices
b
and
c
. In contrast, for CPL,
\operatorname{Im}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}^{*}_{c}\right]
is nonzero and antisymmetric with respect to the indices (see Appendix
C
). Accordingly, we define
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}
for LPL as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{abc}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{\sigma_{abc}+\sigma_{acb}}{2}\right]=\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{acb},

(24)

and
\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}
for CPL as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{abc}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\operatorname{Im}\left[\frac{\sigma_{abc}-\sigma_{acb}}{2}\right]=-\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{acb}.

(25)

For a general polarization of light, both coefficients contribute to the observable magnetization. By performing integration by parts, these coefficients can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{abc}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\tau Q^{3}}{64\hbar^{2}\left(1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\right)}\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]\varepsilon_{aij}\left[\partial_{q_{b}}\partial_{q_{i}}g_{jc}+2\partial_{q_{b}}\left(G_{jc}v_{i}\right)+\left(b\leftrightarrow c\right)\right]f_{0},

(26)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{abc}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\tau^{2}\omega Q^{3}}{64\hbar^{2}\left(1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}\right)}\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]\varepsilon_{aij}\left[\partial_{q_{b}}\partial_{q_{i}}g_{jc}+2\partial_{q_{b}}\left(G_{jc}v_{i}\right)-\left(b\leftrightarrow c\right)\right]f_{0}.

(27)

This is our main result.

The coefficient
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}
represents the magnetization induced by LPL, corresponding to the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect, while
\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}
describes the magnetization induced by CPL, corresponding to the inverse Faraday effect. In both cases, the effects are governed by a quadrupole-like quantity of the quantum metric
\int[\mathrm{d}\bm{k}]f_{0}\partial\partial g
, which we refer to as the
quantum metric quadrupole
. This quantity has previously been studied in the context of third-order electric responses
Liu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2025
. In addition to the quantum metric quadrupole, these responses also include terms involving the weighted quantum metric
G
. These terms originate from perturbations to the Bloch states and do not appear in the transport current
Gao and Xiao 2019
;
Lapa and Hughes 2019
.

III
Model Calculation

In this section, we calculate
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}
for two-band models and evaluate their magnitudes with an eye toward experimental detection. In the following, we focus on two-dimensional materials. We first introduce an effective continuum model to elucidate the general features of the induced magnetization. We then consider a lattice model and estimate the size of the effects.

Before introducing the specific models, we discuss the role of symmetries in two-dimensional systems. Here, we summarize the main results, while the detailed analysis is provided in Appendix
D
. For a response of the form given in Eq. (
22
), the simultaneous presence of symmetries under a mirror operation with respect to a mirror plane perpendicular to the system
m_{\perp}
and a
n
-fold rotational symmetry around an axis perpendicular to the system
C_{nz}
with
n\neq 2
enforces
\sigma_{zab}^{\mathrm{LPL}}=0
. In contrast, neither mirror nor rotational symmetries constrain the response to CPL. Therefore, in order to observe responses to both LPL and CPL, the system should break either mirror or rotational symmetries.

III.1
Continuum model

We consider a two-dimensional massive Dirac model with anisotropic quadratic corrections, described by

\displaystyle H(q_{x},q_{y})

\displaystyle=\hbar v\left[\left(q_{x}+\alpha q_{x}^{2}\right)\sigma_{x}+\left(q_{y}+\beta q_{y}^{2}\right)\sigma_{y}\right]+M\sigma_{z},

(28)

where
\sigma_{i}\ (i=x,y,z)
are Pauli matrices acting on a two-component internal space, and
M
is the mass-gap parameter.
We have presented expressions of quantum metric quadrupole and weighted quantum
metric terms in Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
) for general two-level systems in Appendix
E
, which we apply to our model Hamiltonian here.
For
\alpha=\beta
, we find
\sigma_{zaa}=0
due to the mirror symmetry (see Appendix
D
) and
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}=0
by the direct calculation. Hence, we here focus on the case with
\alpha\neq\beta
.

It should be also noted that the electric current transforms as a polar vector under spatial symmetry operations. For mirror reflections with respect to planes normal to the
x
and
y
directions, denoted by
m_{100}
and
m_{010}
, respectively, the corresponding representations are given by

\displaystyle U_{m_{100}}=i\sigma_{y},\qquad U_{m_{010}}=i\sigma_{x}.

(29)

In the absence of quadratic corrections
(\alpha=\beta=0)
, the Hamiltonian is isotropic in momentum space and thus possesses a continuous rotational symmetry
C_{\infty z}
. Since this isotropic Dirac model has both mirror
m_{\perp}
and rotation
C_{\infty z}
symmetries, the response to LPL is forbidden.

One may also construct a model with an anisotropic Dirac cone without quadratic terms,
H=\alpha_{x}q_{x}\sigma_{x}+\alpha_{y}q_{y}\sigma_{y}+M\sigma_{z}
with
\alpha_{x}\neq\alpha_{y}
. Since this system breaks rotation
C_{\infty z}
symmetries, one would then expect a nonzero response
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}
. However, as shown by explicit calculation in Appendix
F
, this model is still too simple: the contributions cancel upon momentum integration, resulting in
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}=0
. Similarly, for a system described by the Hamiltonian
H=\alpha_{x}q_{x}^{2}\sigma_{x}+\alpha_{y}q_{y}^{2}\sigma_{y}+M\sigma_{z}
, the response
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}
also vanishes. Therefore, Eq. (
28
) constitutes the minimal model that yields nonvanishing responses to both LPL and CPL.

By integrating these quantities, we obtain the coefficients

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a general theory of light-induced magnetization in electronic systems originating from quantum-geometric effects. By considering spatially nonuniform electric fields within a semiclassical framework based on the Boltzmann transport equation, we established a unified formalism in which the magnetization arises as a second-order response to the electric field.

We identified two distinct quantum-geometric contributions to the magnetization: the quantum metric quadrupole and weighted quantum metric. While the former has been previously discussed in the context of transport currents under nonuniform electric field, the latter originates from perturbations to the Bloch states and does not appear in conventional transport currents. Our results demonstrate that both contributions are essential for a complete description of light-induced magnetization and that they naturally give rise to responses under both LPL and CPL. These effects correspond to the inverse Cotton–Mouton and the inverse Faraday effects, respectively.

We further analyzed symmetry constraints in two-dimensional systems and clarified the conditions under which responses to LPL and CPL are allowed. Using both a continuum massive Dirac model with anisotropic quadratic corrections and a tight-binding model on the hexagonal lattice, we explicitly demonstrated the emergence of nonvanishing magnetization responses when either mirror or rotational symmetries are broken. In particular, the tight-binding model of anisotropic gapped graphene allowed us to estimate the magnitude of the effect and showed that the induced magnetization is experimentally observable, although it may not constitute the dominant contribution to the overall inverse Faraday and inverse Cotton–Mouton effects.

Our work establishes optically generated magnetization as a direct and experimentally accessible manifestation of quantum geometry in solids. Beyond providing a unified description of magneto-optical effects, the present framework opens new avenues for probing quantum-geometric quantities through nonlinear optical measurements.

Appendix B
Distribution function

We write the distribution function by a Fourier series up to the terms containing
E\partial E
.

We write the distribution function
f(\bm{q},t)
using a complex representation as

\displaystyle f(\bm{q},t)

\displaystyle=\real\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}_{n}(\bm{q})e^{in\omega t}\right]\qquad\left(\tilde{f}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\right).

(49)

The Boltzmann equation (
15
) gives

\displaystyle f(\bm{q},t)

\displaystyle=f_{0}(\bm{q})-\left(\tau\dot{q}_{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{a}}+\tau\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)f(\bm{q},t),

(50)

where
f_{0}(\bm{q})
is an equilibrium distribution function,
E_{a}=\real\left[\tilde{E}_{a}e^{i\omega t}\right]
is the electric field, and
\tau
is the relaxation time. By substituting Eq. (
49
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}_{n}(\bm{q})e^{in\omega t}+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle=\left[f_{0}-\tau\dot{q}_{a}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}-\tau\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}in\omega\tilde{f}_{n}e^{in\omega t}\right]+\mathrm{c.c.}.

(51)

We want to obtain
\tilde{f}_{n}
neglecting terms with
E^{2}
and higher orders of
E
. We employ a result Eq. (
36
) for
\dot{q}_{a}
:

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{a}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\left(\tilde{E}_{a}e^{i\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}\right)E_{a}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\left(\tilde{E}_{b}e^{i\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}\right)\partial_{r_{a}}\left(\tilde{E}_{j}e^{i\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}\right).

Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}_{n}(\bm{q})e^{in\omega t}+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle\approx\left[f_{0}-\tau\left(\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\left(\tilde{E}_{a}e^{i\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}\right)E_{a}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\left(\tilde{E}_{b}e^{i\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}\right)\partial_{r_{a}}\left(\tilde{E}_{j}e^{i\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\times\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}-\tau\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}in\omega\tilde{f}_{n}e^{in\omega t}\right]+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle=\left[f_{0}-\tau\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\left(\tilde{E}_{a}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{i(n+1)\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{i(n-1)\omega t}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\tau\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\left(\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}+\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}e^{2i\omega t}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}-\tau\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}in\omega\tilde{f}_{n}e^{in\omega t}\right]+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle=\left[f_{0}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\left(\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right)\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n}}{\partial q_{a}}-in\tau\omega\tilde{f}_{n}\right\}e^{in\omega t}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\left(\tilde{E}_{a}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n-1}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n+1}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\left(\tilde{E}_{b}\partial\tilde{E}_{j}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n-2}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}+\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}\partial\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\sum_{n=-2}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{n+2}}{\partial q_{a}}e^{in\omega t}\right)\right]+\mathrm{c.c.}

(52)

By equating terms at each order of
e^{i\omega t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{0}

\displaystyle=f_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\frac{\partial\left(\tilde{f}_{0}+\tilde{f}_{0}^{*}\right)}{\partial q_{a}}-\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{1}^{*}}{\partial q_{a}}++\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{2}}{\partial q_{a}},

(53)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\tilde{E}_{a}\frac{\partial\left(\tilde{f}_{0}+\tilde{f}_{0}^{*}\right)}{\partial q_{a}}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\frac{\partial\left(\tilde{f}_{1}+\tilde{f}_{1}^{*}\right)}{\partial q_{a}}+\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{1}^{*}}{\partial q_{a}}\right]-i\tau\omega\tilde{f}_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt-\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{2}}{\partial q_{a}}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{3}}{\partial q_{a}},

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}\frac{\partial\left(\tilde{f}_{0}+\tilde{f}_{0}^{*}\right)}{\partial q_{a}}-\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\tilde{E}_{a}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{1}}{\partial q_{a}}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\tilde{E}_{b}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\frac{\partial\left(\tilde{f}_{2}+\tilde{f}_{2}^{*}\right)}{\partial q_{a}}-2i\tau\omega\tilde{f}_{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt-\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\tilde{E}_{a}^{*}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{3}}{\partial q_{a}}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\tau Q}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}G_{bj}\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}\partial_{a}\tilde{E}_{j}^{*}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}_{4}}{\partial q_{a}},

Appendix C
Polarization of light

To make this paper self-contained, here we briefly summarize the relationship between polarization of light and second-order responses of its electric field.

Without loss of generality, we consider an electromagnetic wave propagating in the
z
-direction with frequency
\omega
. The, the electric field can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{E}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{\bm{E}}e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\right],

(61)

where a complex vector

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{E}}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{0}}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\left(\begin{matrix}a\\
be^{i\theta}\\
0\end{matrix}\right)e^{i\phi}

(62)

determines the polarization of the light. Here,
E_{0},a,b,\theta,\phi
are all real values. When
\theta=n\pi\ (n\in\mathbb{Z})
,
x
and
y
components oscillate without any phase difference and represent LPL. On the other hand, for
\theta\neq n\pi
, the light is elliptically polarized. When
\theta=(2n+1)\pi/2
, polarization is perfectly circular.

Let us consider the following products of two electric fields:

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{x}\tilde{E}_{x}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{a^{2}+b^{2}}E_{0}^{2},

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{y}\tilde{E}_{y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{2}}{a^{2}+b^{2}}E_{0}^{2},

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{x}\tilde{E}_{y}^{*}+\tilde{E}_{y}\tilde{E}_{x}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{ab}{a^{2}+b^{2}}E_{0}^{2}\cdot 2\cos\theta,

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{x}\tilde{E}_{y}^{*}-\tilde{E}_{y}\tilde{E}_{x}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{ab}{a^{2}+b^{2}}E_{0}^{2}\cdot\left(-2i\right)\sin\theta.

Therefore, for LPL (i.e.
\theta=n\pi
),
\operatorname{Im}\left[\tilde{E}_{x}\tilde{E}_{y}^{*}\right]=0
and for CPL (i.e.
\theta=(2n+1)\pi/2
),
\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{x}\tilde{E}_{y}^{*}\right]=0
.

Assume that a physical quantity
\bm{X}
is expressed in complex representation as
\bm{X}=\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\right]
and it is a second order of the complex electric field,

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{a}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{abc}\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}.

(63)

Then, the physical quantity can be calculated by taking the real part of both sides as

\displaystyle X_{a}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\alpha_{abc}\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\alpha_{abc}\right]\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right]-\operatorname{Im}\left[\alpha_{abc}\right]\operatorname{Im}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right].

(64)

When we consider LPL, the real part of the coefficient
\alpha_{abc}
contribute to the final response. Furthermore, since
\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right]
is symmetric with respect to the exchange of indices
b
and
c
, only the symmetric part of
\alpha_{abc}
contribute to the response to LPL. It is now natural to define

\displaystyle\alpha^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{abc}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{\alpha_{abc}+\alpha_{acb}}{2}\right]=\alpha^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{acb}.

(65)

Similarly, for CPL, only the imaginary and antisymmetric part of the coefficient contribute to the physical phenomena. Thus, we define

\displaystyle\alpha^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{abc}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\operatorname{Im}\left[\frac{\alpha_{abc}-\alpha_{acb}}{2}\right]=-\alpha^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{acb}.

(66)

Using these quantities, we can decompose
X
to two parts, representing responses to LPL and CPL as

\displaystyle X_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Re}\left[\alpha_{abc}\right]\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right]+\operatorname{Re}\left[\alpha_{acb}\right]\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{c}\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}\right]}{2}-\frac{\operatorname{Im}\left[\alpha_{abc}\right]\operatorname{Im}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right]+\operatorname{Im}\left[\alpha_{acb}\right]\operatorname{Im}\left[\tilde{E}_{c}\tilde{E}_{b}^{*}\right]}{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{abc}\operatorname{Re}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right]+\alpha^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{abc}\operatorname{Im}\left[\tilde{E}_{b}\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}\right].

(67)

Appendix D
Restrictions by crystalline symmetries

We discuss the restrictions imposed by the mirror symmetry and rotation symmetry of the system on the magnetization induced by second-order of electric field. In our formulation, magnetization can be induced only in the
z
-direction and thus, we focus on the response

\displaystyle\tilde{M}^{0}_{z}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{zab}\tilde{E}_{a}\tilde{E}^{*}_{b}\qquad(a,b\in\left\{x,y\right\}).

(68)

D.1
Mirror symmetry

First of all, symmetry with respect to the mirror plane perpendicular to the
z
-axis does not impose any restrictions as this mirror operations keeps the form of Eq. (
68
). Next, we consider a mirror operation
m_{\perp}
whose mirror plane is perpendicular to the system. Suppose that the angle between this mirror plane and the
x
-axis is
\phi
. Then, the electric field is transformed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{E}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\tilde{E}_{x}\\
\tilde{E}_{y}\end{matrix}\right)\to\left(\begin{matrix}\cos 2\phi&\sin 2\phi\\
\sin 2\phi&-\cos 2\phi\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\tilde{E}_{x}\\
\tilde{E}_{y}\end{matrix}\right).

(69)

On the other hand,
m_{\perp}
changes the sign of
\tilde{M}^{0}_{z}
. Therefore, this operation transforms Eq. (
68
) as

\displaystyle\tilde{M}^{0}_{z}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{E}}^{T}\sigma_{z}\tilde{\bm{E}}^{*}

\displaystyle\to-\tilde{M}^{0}_{z}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{E}}^{T}\left(\begin{matrix}\cos 2\phi&\sin 2\phi\\
\sin 2\phi&-\cos 2\phi\end{matrix}\right)^{T}\sigma_{z}\left(\begin{matrix}\cos 2\phi&\sin 2\phi\\
\sin 2\phi&-\cos 2\phi\end{matrix}\right)\tilde{\bm{E}}^{*}.

(70)

Then, the invariance of the system under this symmetry imposes the condition for
\sigma_{z}:=\left(\begin{matrix}\sigma_{zxx}&\sigma_{zxy}\\
\sigma_{zyx}&\sigma_{zyy}\end{matrix}\right)
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{matrix}\cos 2\phi&\sin 2\phi\\
\sin 2\phi&-\cos 2\phi\end{matrix}\right)\sigma_{z}\left(\begin{matrix}\cos 2\phi&\sin 2\phi\\
\sin 2\phi&-\cos 2\phi\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{zxx}C^{2}+\sigma_{zyy}S^{2}+(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx})SC&\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)SC-\sigma_{zxy}C^{2}+\sigma_{zyx}S^{2}\\
\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)SC+\sigma_{zxy}S^{2}-\sigma_{zyx}C^{2}&\sigma_{zxx}S^{2}+\sigma_{zyy}C^{2}-(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx})SC\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\sigma_{zxx}+\sigma_{zyy}\right)+\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)C^{2}+(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx})SC&\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)SC+\left(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}\right)S^{2}\\
\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)SC+\left(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}\right)S^{2}&\left(\sigma_{zxx}+\sigma_{zyy}\right)-\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)C^{2}-(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx})SC\end{array}\right)=0,

where we used shorthand notations
C:=\cos 2\phi
and
S:=\sin 2\phi
. This indicates that

\displaystyle\sin 2\phi\neq 0,\cos 2\phi\neq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sigma_{zxx}=-\sigma_{zyy},\sigma_{zyy}=\frac{\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}}{2}\tan 2\phi,

(75)

\displaystyle\sin 2\phi\neq 0,\cos 2\phi=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sigma_{zxx}=-\sigma_{zyy},\sigma_{zxy}=-\sigma_{zyx},

(76)

\displaystyle\sin 2\phi=0,\cos 2\phi\neq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sigma_{zxx}=\sigma_{zyy}=0.

(77)

Therefore, if the system has
m_{100}
or
m_{010}
symmetry,
\sigma_{zaa}=0\ (a=x,y)
. If the mirror symmetry is
m_{110}
or
m_{1\bar{1}0}
, then
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}=0
and
\sigma_{zxx}=-\sigma_{zyy}
. Otherwise, the existence of the mirror symmetry requires
\sigma_{zxx}=-\sigma_{zyy}
and relationship among remaining three components but does not force them to be zero.

D.2
Rotation symmetry

Next we consider the effect of rotation symmetry around an axis perpendicular to the system. Rotation of an angle
\theta
around the
z
-axis changes the electric field as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{E}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\tilde{E}_{x}\\
\tilde{E}_{y}\end{matrix}\right)\to\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\tilde{E}_{x}\\
\tilde{E}_{y}\end{matrix}\right).

(78)

In contrast to the mirror operation with respect to the plane perpendicular to the system, rotation operation around the
z
-axis keeps
\tilde{M}^{0}_{z}
unchanged. Thus, the invariance under this rotation operation requires

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{matrix}\right)^{T}\sigma_{z}\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)S^{2}+\left(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}\right)SC&\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)SC+\left(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}\right)S^{2}\\
\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)SC+\left(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}\right)S^{2}&-\left(\sigma_{zxx}-\sigma_{zyy}\right)S^{2}+\left(\sigma_{zxy}+\sigma_{zyx}\right)SC\end{matrix}\right)=0,

(79)

where
S:=\sin\theta
and
C:=\cos\theta
. This indicates that

\displaystyle\sin\theta\neq 0,\cos\theta\neq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sigma_{zxx}=\sigma_{zyy},\sigma_{zxy}=-\sigma_{zyx},

(80)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\neq 0,\cos\theta=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sigma_{zxx}=\sigma_{zyy},\sigma_{zxy}=-\sigma_{zyx},

(81)

\displaystyle\sin\theta=0,\cos\theta\neq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{Always\ satisfied.}

(82)

Therefore,
C_{2z}
-symmetry of the system does not constrain the response. Other rotation symmetries force
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}=0
and
\sigma_{zxx}=\sigma_{zyy}
.

D.3
Rotation and mirror symmetry

Combining two results discussed above, in the presence of both a mirror symmetry with respect to a plane perpendicular to the system
m_{\perp}
and a rotation symmetry
C_{nz}\ (n\geq 3)
, response to LPL is forbidden. On the other hand, these crystalline symmetries do not pose any conditions for the response to CPL.

Appendix E
Quantum metric quadrupole

Here we present expressions of quantum metric quadrupole and other terms in Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
) for general two-level systems. Then, we apply them to our model system.

In general, Hamiltonian of a two-level system can be written as

\displaystyle H(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=h_{0}(\bm{q})\sigma_{0}+\bm{h}(\bm{q})\cdot\bm{\sigma},

(83)

where
\sigma_{0}
is an identity matrix and
\bm{\sigma}
is a vector of Pauli matrices. Then, for the lower band, quantum metric can be given as

\displaystyle g_{ab}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\left(A_{01}\right)_{a}\left(A_{10}\right)_{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4h^{2}}\left(\partial_{a}h_{i}\partial_{b}h_{i}-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h\right),

(84)

where
h=\absolutevalue{\bm{h}}
,
\partial_{a}=\partial/\partial q_{a},\,(a=x,y,z)
, and the sum over repeated indices is taken. The quantum metric dipole density and quantum metric quadrupole density are

\displaystyle\partial_{c}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{c}h}{2h^{3}}(\partial_{a}h_{i}\partial_{b}h_{i}-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h)+\frac{1}{4h^{2}}\partial_{c}(\partial_{a}h_{i}\partial_{b}h_{i}-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h),

(85)

\displaystyle\partial_{d}\partial_{c}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\partial_{c}h\partial_{d}h}{2h^{4}}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h)-\frac{\partial_{d}\partial_{c}h}{2h^{3}}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\partial_{c}h}{2h^{3}}\partial_{d}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h)-\frac{\partial_{d}h}{2h^{3}}\partial_{c}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h)+\frac{1}{4h^{2}}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{b}h).

(86)

For the calculation of
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}
, we need an anti-symmetric part of the quantum metric quadrupole;

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mu ca}\partial_{d}\partial_{c}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\mu ca}\left[\frac{3\partial_{c}h\partial_{d}h}{2h^{4}}\partial_{a}h_{i}\partial_{b}h_{i}-\frac{\partial_{d}\partial_{c}h}{2h^{3}}\partial_{a}h_{i}\partial_{b}h_{i}-\frac{\partial_{c}h}{2h^{3}}\partial_{d}\left(\partial_{a}h_{i}\partial_{b}h_{i}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\left.-\frac{\partial_{d}h}{2h^{3}}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{c}\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{c}\partial_{b}h)+\frac{1}{4h^{2}}\partial_{d}\quantity(\partial_{a}h_i\partial_{c}\partial_{b}h_i-\partial_{a}h\partial_{c}\partial_{b}h)\right].

(87)

The symmetric part of Eq. (
87
) with respect to the exchange of indices
d\leftrightarrow b
contributes to
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}
and the anti-symmetric part to
\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}
.

For the
\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}
, we also need a term containing the weighted quantum metric. In a two-band system described by Eq. (
83
), we have
G_{ab}=2\hbar g_{ab}/h
. Thus,

\displaystyle\partial_{d}\left(G_{ab}v_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\partial_{d}g_{ab}\frac{\partial_{c}\left(h_{0}-h\right)}{h}-g_{ab}\frac{\partial_{d}h\partial_{c}\left(h_{0}-h\right)}{h^{2}}+g_{ab}\frac{\partial_{d}\partial_{c}\left(h_{0}-h\right)}{h}\right).

(88)

Appendix F
Anisotropic massive Dirac model

Let us assume a Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{x}q_{x}\sigma_{x}+\alpha_{y}q_{y}\sigma_{y}+M\sigma_{z},

(89)

which is an effective model of an anisotropic, massive, and two-dimensional Dirac cone. For this system, the quantum metric, metric dipole density, and metric quadrupole density are

\displaystyle g_{ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}}{4h^{2}}\left(\delta_{ab}-\alpha_{b}^{2}\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{h^{2}}\right),

(90)

\displaystyle\partial_{c}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4h^{2}}\left(\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\delta_{bc}q_{a}+\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\delta_{ca}q_{b}+\alpha_{b}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\delta_{ab}q_{c}+\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\delta_{ab}q_{c}\right)+\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\frac{q_{a}q_{b}q_{c}}{h^{6}},

(91)

\displaystyle\partial_{d}\partial_{c}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4h^{2}}\left(\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\delta_{bc}\delta_{ad}+\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\delta_{ca}\delta_{bd}+\alpha_{b}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}+\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha_{d}^{2}}{h^{6}}\left[\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\left(\delta_{bc}q_{a}q_{d}+\delta_{ac}q_{b}q_{d}+\delta_{cd}q_{a}q_{b}\right)+\alpha_{b}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\left(\delta_{ab}q_{c}q_{d}+\delta_{ad}q_{b}q_{c}\right)+\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\left(\delta_{ab}q_{c}q_{d}+\delta_{bd}q_{a}q_{c}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-6\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\alpha_{d}^{2}\frac{q_{a}q_{b}q_{c}q_{d}}{h^{8}},

(92)

where
h=\sqrt{(\alpha_{x}q_{x})^{2}+(\alpha_{y}q_{y})^{2}+M^{2}}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle G_{ab}v_{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{a}\alpha_{c}}{2h^{4}}q_{c}\left(\delta_{ab}-\alpha_{b}^{2}\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{h^{2}}\right),

(93)

\displaystyle\partial_{d}\left(G_{ab}v_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}}{2h^{4}}\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}+\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}}{2h^{6}}\left(4\alpha_{d}^{2}\delta_{ab}q_{c}q_{d}+\alpha_{b}^{2}\left(\delta_{ad}q_{b}q_{c}+\delta_{bd}q_{a}q_{c}+\delta_{cd}q_{a}q_{b}\right)\right)-3\alpha_{a}^{2}\alpha_{b}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\alpha_{d}^{2}\frac{q_{a}q_{b}q_{c}q_{d}}{h^{8}}.

(94)

Using these quantities, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxx}

\displaystyle\propto\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]f_{0}\left[-\frac{10\alpha_{x}^{4}\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{x}q_{y}}{\left(\alpha_{x}^{2}q_{x}^{2}+\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{y}^{2}+M^{2}\right)^{3}}\right],

(95)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}

\displaystyle\propto\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]f_{0}\left[\frac{5\alpha_{x}^{2}\alpha_{y}^{2}}{\left(\alpha_{x}^{2}q_{x}^{2}+\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{y}^{2}+M^{2}\right)^{3}}\left(\alpha_{x}^{2}q_{x}^{2}-\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{y}^{2}\right)\right],

(96)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zyy}

\displaystyle\propto\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]f_{0}\left[\frac{10\alpha_{x}^{2}\alpha_{y}^{4}q_{x}q_{y}}{\left(\alpha_{x}^{2}q_{x}^{2}+\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{y}^{2}+M^{2}\right)^{3}}\right],

(97)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{CPL}}_{zxy}

\displaystyle\propto\int\left[\mathrm{d}\bm{q}\right]f_{0}\left[\frac{5\alpha_{x}^{2}\alpha_{y}^{2}\left(\alpha_{x}^{2}q_{x}^{2}+\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{y}^{2}-M^{2}\right)}{2\left(\alpha_{x}^{2}q_{x}^{2}+\alpha_{y}^{2}q_{y}^{2}+M^{2}\right)^{3}}\right].

(98)

By introducing new variables
u:=\alpha_{x}q_{x}
and
v:=\alpha_{y}q_{y}
, the integrands of
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxx}
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zyy}
are odd functions of
u
and
v
and thus, these integrations give zeros. Similarly, the integrand of
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}
is odd under the exchange of
u
and
v
, indicating
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zxy}=0
. Therefore,
\sigma^{\mathrm{LPL}}_{zab}=0
for the simplest massive Dirac model.
