Introduction

Left ventricular pseudoaneurysm (LVP) is a rare complication of myocardial infarction that often has a delayed time to diagnosis. We report a chronic, massive LVP that was missed on routine imaging by mimicking normal anatomy.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with exertional intolerance for 2 weeks. Besides mild hypoxia that was corrected with 2 litres of oxygen, vitals and physical exam were unremarkable.

The patient had a history of chronic total occlusion of the right coronary artery, inferior wall ST-elevation myocardial infarction (MI) treated with percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, and medically managed mitral valve endocarditis 4 years before this presentation. Other comorbidities include peripheral arterial disease, chronic kidney disease stage two, diabetes mellitus type 2, and hypertension.

High-sensitivity troponin was within the normal reference limits, and no changes were noted on the electrocardiogram. She had an elevated D-dimer, prompting a computed tomography (CT) pulmonary artery angiogram. There were no pulmonary artery filling defects, but the study revealed a 9.0 × 8.8 cm density adjacent to the left ventricle (Figure 1). Cardiology was consulted immediately and, per their recommendation, subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) established the diagnosis of LVP of the basal inferior wall. The LVP measured 10.0 × 9.0 × 8.0 cm with an opening of 1.0 × 3.0 cm (Figure 2).

The timing of pseudoaneurysm formation was unclear. The patient has had no known interval cardiac insults since her MI 4 years prior. Given her increased risk for silent ischaemia, a coronary angiogram was performed. Catheterization demonstrated stable coronary anatomy, suggestive of chronic LVP.

A multidisciplinary heart team of clinical cardiology, cardiac imagers, and cardiovascular surgery retrospectively reviewed prior cardiac imaging to further stratify the risk of rupture. Two years earlier, a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) with CT stress test were obtained for dyspnoea. The TTE was initially interpreted without significant abnormalities. One set of images demonstrates what appears to be an apical five-chamber view (see Supplementary material online, Video S1A, Supplementary material online, Figure S1). Colour Doppler shows flow from the LV as expected in what was originally interpreted as the LVOT. However, this flow continues into an adjacent chamber instead of the aorta, suggesting the images are not an apical five-chamber but of an LVP and its neck (see Supplementary material online, Video S1B, Supplementary material online, Figure S1). The non-contrast CT portion of the SPECT study showed a large density adjacent to the LV (Figure 3). Without the use of contrast, it was initially thought to likely represent a hiatal hernia, but with the overlooked TTE findings, these studies support that an LVP was present 2 years ago.

Retrospective computed tomography portion of stress test demonstrating left ventricular pseudoaneurysm. Non-contrasted computed tomography portion of single photon emission tomography stress test demonstrates large density inferior to the left ventricle (arrow) which, in retrospect, was a left ventricular pseudoaneurysm.

Through shared decision making with the patient, urgent surgery was pursued. Because of its proximity to the mitral valve apparatus, a preoperative transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) (Figure 4) of the LVP was obtained, and it was determined no mitral valve intervention was necessary. Intraoperatively, the LVP was visualized (Figure 5A) and pseudoaneurysm resection (Figure 5B) with subsequent patch Dor repair of the neck was successful.

There were no perioperative complications, and the patient was discharged home 4 days after the operation. During outpatient follow-up, she reported symptom resolution.

Discussion

The incidence of MI-related LVP—ventricular free wall rupture contained by adherent pericardium—has drastically declined owing to contemporary reperfusion strategies.1

However, the risk of progressing to complete rupture remains as high as 45%,2 underscoring the importance of timely diagnosis. The median time between MI and LVP detection can be as long as 7 months,3 potentially owing to the largely asymptomatic nature of LVPs.4

MRI and cardiac CT are the gold standard for diagnosing LVP. MRI has the additional advantage of distinguishing LVP from true aneurysms with sensitivity near 100%.5 TEE also has good sensitivity (75%).6 Unfortunately, these modalities can have increased costs, have limited availability, and—regarding TEE—is invasive. As a result, they are not ordered routinely for cardiac patients unless there is a high suspicion of LVP.

On the other hand, TTE is a highly accessible modality used after MI to provide prognostication and determine guideline-based treatment strategies.7 Unfortunately, the sensitivity for diagnosing LVP may be as low as 26%,6 possibly because of the posterior wall—the most obscured wall on transthoracic imaging—being the most frequent site for LVP development (39%)8 and, as demonstrated in this case, LVP can mimic normal anatomy. Risk factors for LVP are well defined.9 In our patient, there was increased age, female gender, prior transmural infarction, and a single-artery blood supply to the posterior wall. Heightened awareness of these risk factors during retrospective imaging interpretation led to detection of LVP on TTE; however, the same cognizance at the time of image acquisition may increase the power of TTE to detect LVP or at least find abnormalities that hasten more definitive. This is support by a landmark analysis of LVP studies where TTE was useful in the diagnostic pathway for 91% of patients.9 In the proper context, these findings may prompt additional imaging: Abrupt myocardial discontinuity, intracavitary thrombus, globular contour of the LV, epicardial clots, loculated pericardial effusion,10 and as seen in this case bidirectional doppler flow between the LV and an adjacent structure (see Supplementary material online, Video S1A). Ultrasound enhancing agents may further improve detection, and it is possible that their earlier use in this patient would have allowed for an earlier diagnosis.11

SPECT with non-contrasted CT for attenuation correction is another highly utilized test in patients with prior MI and new cardiac symptoms. Although the non-contrasted CT portion is not as powerful as a contrasted cardiac CT for defining LVP, it is another common opportunity to find abnormalities, as highlighted in this case, which may facilitate definitive imaging.

Surgery is the standard treatment for acute LVP as procedural risk has drastically decreased because of modern surgical techniques, and risk for rupture is high. Benefits of surgical intervention for chronic LVP (>3 months) are less defined. There are small studies that support nonoperative strategies for chronic, asymptomatic LVP, with some cohorts having no observed rupture.4,12 Factors that portend rupture include anterior LVP, LVP greater than 3 centimetres, and LVP less than 3 months.11 Our multidisciplinary team thought these reports did not reflect our patient as their chronic LVPs rarely reached massive size. One notable exception is Sulman et al. describing a known LVP existing 24 years managed nonoperatively because of prohibitive surgical risk. While their patient did survive multiple decades, there was high morbidity with heart failure and transient ischaemic attacks.13 Our patient’s non-contrasted CT suggested stability in LVP size; however, the truly massive size, new exertional symptoms, and the patient’s overall low surgical risk (EuroSCORE II predicted 3.4% perioperative mortality) led to our recommendation of surgical intervention.

LVP is a rare consequence of MI that is considered a surgical emergency. However, diagnosis is often delayed because its presentation is often non-specific, and the gold standard tests for diagnosis, cardiac CT and MRI, are ordered late. Highlighted by this case, traditionally less sensitive but more readily available imaging, such as TTE and SPECT/CT, may serve a complementary role as a sentinel, prompting cardiac imagers with heightened suspicion to pursue more definitive testing for more timely diagnosis.
