Abstract
. We study a critical problem for an operator of mixed order obtained by the superposition of a Laplacian with a fractional Laplacian. The main novelty is that we consider a mixed operator of the form − Δ − γ ​ ( − Δ ) s -\Delta-\gamma(-\Delta)^{s} , namely we suppose that the fractional Laplacian has the “wrong sign” and can be seen as a nonlocal perturbation of the purely local case, which is needed to produce a nontrivial solution of the critical problem.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with
n\geq 3
) be an open and bounded set with smooth enough (say Lipschitz) boundary
\partial\Omega
.
We consider the following problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Delta u=\gamma\,(-\Delta)^{s}u+|u|^{2^{\ast}-2}u&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u\not\equiv 0&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{array}\right.

The operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
is the fractional Laplacian, defined, for
s\in(0,1)
, as

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,dy.

The parameter
\gamma
is assumed to be positive
as suitably small (namely,
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
, with a notation that will be clarified here below).
As usual,
2^{\ast}:=\tfrac{2n}{n-2}
is the Sobolev critical exponent. Our aim is to prove that under the previous assumption on
\gamma
, problem (
1
) admits a nontrivial weak solution in the appropriate variational space.

-
Assumptions and motivations
.
It is known, see e.g.
[
8
]
, that the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
continuously embeds into the fractional Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
s\in(0,1)
, i.e. there exists a positive constant
C>0
(depending on
s
and
n
) such that

(1.4)

C\,[u]^{2}_{s}\leq\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for every
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Here and in what follows, we use the notation

[u]_{s}:=\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\right)^{1/2}

for the Gagliardo seminorm of
u
.

Together with the classical Poincaré inequality, formula (
1.4
) shows that there exists
C>0
(depending on
\Omega
as well) such that

(1.5)

C[{u}]^{2}_{s}\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for every
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Hence, we can define

C_{emb}:=\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}:u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\,[\hat{u}]_{s}^{2}=1\big\}.

The request
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
can now be explained: we are interested in dealing with a local and nonlocal operator
with the wrong sign
, namely

(1.6)

-\Delta-\gamma\,(-\Delta)^{s},

but at the same time we need some restrictions on the coefficient
\gamma
so that the operator is positive definite.

The main motivation behind the study of problem (
1
) comes from the recent works
[
2
,
1
]
. The starting point is the following simple observation: let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set and
s\in(0,1)
, then there exists a constant
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)>0
such that

(1.7)

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u]^{2}_{s},

for every
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This is clearly a consequence of the classical Sobolev inequality.

In principle, the constant
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)
could depend on
n
,
s
and
\Omega
, but this is actually not the case (see
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
), indeed

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=S_{n}

where
S_{n}
is the best constant for the classical Sobolev inequality, achieved by the Aubin-Talenti functions.

Moreover, the energy functional

(1.8)

E(u):=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{2}[u]^{2}_{s}

exhibits a lack of scaling invariance, namely

(1.9)

E(u_{t})=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{t^{2s-2}}{2}[u]^{2}_{s},

where
u_{t}(x):=t^{(n-2)/2}u(tx)
is the rescaled function which preserves the
H^{1}_{0}
-norm of
u
. This second simple remark allows to show that the best constant in the local-nonlocal Sobolev inequality (
1.7
) is never achieved, see
[
2
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Since the first variation of the energy functional
E
in (
1.8
) is given by the local-nonlocal operator

-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s},

it becomes natural to attack the study of Sobolev critical problems in bounded domains, with leading operator
-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
. The first results in this direction are contained in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
and are all inspired by the seminal paper
[
6
]
, where the authors showed the importance of adding some kind of perturbation (linear, superlinear, sublinear) when looking for positive weak solutions. In all the mentioned papers
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
the main difficulties (and differences from the purely local case) come once again from the lack of scaling invariance in (
1.9
), especially when one has to compute the fractional seminorm of a suitably localized version of the Aubin-Talenti functions, see e.g. (iv) in Lemma
3.2
below.

A natural question (raised to us by S. Terracini) is what happens when one considers a Sobolev critical problem with a leading operator of the form (
1.6
). This could also be interpreted in the spirit of
[
6
]
as a nonlocal perturbation of the purely local problem.
Our main result reads as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set with Lipschitz boundary.
Then, the following assertions hold:

(1)

(
High-dimensional case
)
If
n\geq 5
, for every
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
there exists a nontrivial
weak solution
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
to problem (
1
).

(2)

(
Low-dimensional case
)
If
n=3,4
, there exists
\gamma^{*}\in(0,C_{emb})
such that
problem (
1
) has a nontrivial
weak solution
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
for every
\gamma\in(\gamma^{*},C_{emb})
.

We postpone to Section
2
, and in particular to (
2.5
), the precise definition of the function space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is contained in Section
3
.

, as

(2.2)

V_{\varepsilon,y}(x):=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{(n-2)/2}}{(\varepsilon^{2}+|x-y|^{2})^{(n-2)/2}}.

We will avoid to write the dependence on

when
y=0
.

We now set the adequate functional setting to study problem (
1
). We refer to e.g.
[
2
, Section 2]
for more details.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and let
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. We set

(2.3)

[u]_{s}:=\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\right)^{1/2},

and we refer to
[u]_{s}
as the
Gagliardo seminorm
of
u
(of order
s
).

Let
\varnothing\neq\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(here
n\geq 3
would suffice) be an open set, not necessarily bounded. We define the function space

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
as

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega):=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\,\,\rho(\cdot)}

i.e. as the completion
of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to the norm

(2.4)

\rho(u):=\left(\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u]^{2}_{s}\right)^{1/2}.

We stress that the norm
\rho(\cdot)
is induced by the scalar product

\langle u,v\rangle_{\rho}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy,

and therefore
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space.

For our purposes, we are interested in the case
\Omega
bounded. Recalling the continuous embedding given by (
1.4
), and combining it with the classical Poincaré inequality, we find that

\rho(\cdot)
and the full
H^{1}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

are actually equivalent on the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, and hence

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\,\,\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}

\displaystyle=\big\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)$ and
$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\big\}.

Remark 2.1
.

On the account of (
2.5
), and taking into account that the norm
\rho(\cdot)

is equivalent to both the full
H^{1}
-norm
and the
H_{0}^{1}
-norm on
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
(see (
1.5
)), we derive the following useful facts.

i)

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is
continuously embedded into
L^{p}(\Omega)
for every
1\leq p\leq 2^{*}
; moreover, if
1\leq p<2^{*}
, this embedding is
compact
.

ii)

Taking into account the continuous embedding in (
1.4
),
and since this embedding
is actually compact
(see, e.g.,
[
9
, Theorem 1.3]
),
we get

\text{$\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$ is compactly embedded into $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$}.

iii)

Moreover, it is easily seen that

(2.6)

C_{emb}=\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}:\,u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega),\,[u]^{2}_{s}=1\big\},

(recall that
\rho(\cdot)
is equivalent to the
H_{0}^{1}
-norm), and this infimum is achieved, that is, there exists a function
\Phi_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

[\Phi]^{2}_{s}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\|\nabla\Phi_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C_{emb}.

We end this section by giving the precise definition of

weak solution
to (
1
).

Definition 2.2
.

We say that a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
weak solution

to problem (
1
) if it satisfies the following properties:

1)

u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

2)

for every test function
v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{\rho}=\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2^{*}-2}uv\,dx.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We follow the ideas presented in
[
6
]
combined with the crucial expansion provided by Proposition
3.4
.
To this aim, let us first define the functional

(3.1)

Q_{\gamma}(u):=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx-\gamma\,[u]^{2}_{s},

and let us consider the following constrained minimization problem

(3.2)

S(\gamma):=\inf\big\{Q_{\gamma}(u):u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega),\|u\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=1\big\}.

Obviously, by formally choosing
\gamma=0
, the above minimization problem reduces to finding the best Sobolev constant
S_{n}
in
\Omega
, which is known to be never achieved; as a consequence, we have the following upper bound for
S(\gamma)
:

S(\gamma)\leq S_{n}\quad\text{for every $0<\gamma<C_{emb}$}.

We also notice that, since we are assuming
0<\gamma<C_{emb}
(recall that
C_{emb}
is the constant defined in (
2.6
)), for every

u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle Q_{\gamma}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx-\gamma\,[u]^{2}_{s}\geq\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{C_{emb}}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{C_{emb}}\right)S_{n}>0.

Gathering the last two formulas in display, we conclude that

(3.3)

0<\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{C_{emb}}\right)S_{n}\leq S(\gamma)\leq S_{n}\quad\text{for every $0<\gamma<C_{emb}$}.

Now, the key tool for the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
S(\gamma)<S_{n}
, the minimum in (
3.2
) is achieved.
In this case, if
u_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
achieves this minimum, then

(3.4)

\widetilde{u}=S(\gamma)^{1/(2^{*}-2)}u_{0}

is a nontrivial weak solution to problem (
1
).

Proof.

To ease the readability, we split the proof into two steps.

Step I):
We first prove that, if
S(\gamma)<S_{n}
, then the minimum in (
3.2
) is achieved.
For this, let
u_{j}
be a minimizing sequence of functions in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. In particular, it holds that
\|u_{j}\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=1
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
In this way, we have that

(3.5)

\lim_{j\to+\infty}Q_{\gamma}(u_{j})=S(\gamma).

Moreover, since
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
, it follows that

(3.6)

\left(1-\dfrac{\gamma}{C_{emb}}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\,dx\leq Q_{\gamma}(u_{j}).

From this and (
3.5
),
we deduce that
u_{j}
is a bounded sequence in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Therefore, up to subsequences and using Remark
2.1
-ii), we infer the existence of
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

•

u_{j}\to u
weakly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

•

[u_{j}-u]_{s}\to 0
;

•

u_{j}\to u
a.e. in
\Omega
,

as
j\to+\infty
. We stress that the latter implies that
\|u\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}\leq 1
.

Now, since
\|u_{j}\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=1
for very
j\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\,dx\geq S_{n},\quad\textrm{ for every }j\in\mathbb{N},

and hence, recalling (
3.5
), we get

\displaystyle\gamma\,[u]^{2}_{s}=\gamma\,\lim_{j\to+\infty}[u_{j}]^{2}_{s}=\limsup_{j\to+\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}\,dx-Q_{\gamma}(u_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{j\to+\infty}\left(S_{n}-Q_{\gamma}(u_{j})\right)=S_{n}-S(\gamma)>0.

This shows that
u\not\equiv 0
.

Set now
v_{j}:=u_{j}-u
and notice that, as
j\to+\infty
,

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|v_{j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}-\gamma[u]^{2}_{s}\\
&\quad=\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|u_{j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}-2\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla u_{j}\,dx-\gamma[u]^{2}_{s}\\
&\quad=2\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+Q_{\gamma}(u_{j})+\gamma\big([u_{j}]^{2}_{s}-[u]^{2}_{s}\big)-2\left(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+o(1)\right)\\
&\quad=S(\gamma)+o(1).\end{split}

Moreover, since
v_{j}\to 0
weakly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
(hence,
v_{j}
is bounded in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
), it follows that
v_{j}
is bounded in
L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)
as well. This allows to use the Brezis-Lieb Lemma in
[
5
]
to get that,
as
j\to+\infty
,

1=\|u_{j}\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=\|u+v_{j}\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=\|u\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+\|v_{j}\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+o(1).

This yields that, as
j\to+\infty
,

\begin{split}1&=\Big(\|u\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+\|v_{j}\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+o(1)\Big)^{2/2^{*}}\\
&\leq\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+\|v_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+o(1)\\
&\leq\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+\dfrac{1}{S_{n}}\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{j}|^{2}\,dx+o(1).\end{split}

Recalling that
S(\gamma)>0
, we find that, as
j\to+\infty
,

S(\gamma)\leq S(\gamma)\,\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+\dfrac{S(\gamma)}{S_{n}}\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{j}|^{2}\,dx+o(1)\quad\textrm{ as }j\to+\infty,

and thus, utilizing (
3.7
),

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|v_{j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}-\gamma[u]^{2}_{s}\leq S(\gamma)\,\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+\dfrac{S(\gamma)}{S_{n}}\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{j}|^{2}\,dx+o(1).

From this, and exploiting that
S(\gamma)<S_{n}
, we get
that, as
j\to+\infty
,

(3.8)

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}-\gamma[u]^{2}_{s}\leq S(\gamma)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}+o(1).

Now, since
u\not\equiv 0
, we can define

u_{0}:=\frac{u}{\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}

and we obtain from (
3.8
) that

S(\gamma)\leq Q_{\gamma}(u_{0})\leq S(\gamma).

This says, that
u_{0}
achieves the minimum in (
3.2
).

Step II):
We now turn to show that, if
u_{0}
achieves the minimum in (
3.2
), then there exists a nontrivial
solution of
problem (
1
), given by (
3.4
).

To this end, we first observe that, if
u_{0}
achieves the minimum in (
3.1
),
by
applying the Lagrange Multiplier Theorem we can infer the existence of
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that,
for every
\psi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx

\displaystyle-\gamma\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y))(\psi(x)-\psi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\Omega}|u_{0}|^{2^{\ast}-2}u_{0}\psi\,dx.

In particular, we can choose
\psi:=u_{0}
in (
3.9
) and this yields

Q_{\gamma}(u_{0})=\mu\int_{\Omega}|u_{0}|^{2^{\ast}}\,dx=\mu,

which, recalling that
Q_{\gamma}(u_{0})=S(\gamma)
, shows that

\mu=S(\gamma).

Setting now
\widetilde{u}:=ku_{0}
, with

k:=S(\gamma)^{1/(2^{\ast}-2)}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widetilde{u}\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx

\displaystyle-\gamma\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(\widetilde{u}(x)-\widetilde{u}(y))(\psi(x)-\psi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=S(\gamma)^{1/(2^{*}-2)+1}\int_{\Omega}|u_{0}|^{2^{\ast}-2}u_{0}\psi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{u}|^{2^{\ast}-2}\widetilde{u}\psi\,dx,

for every
\psi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
and this shows that
\widetilde{u}
solves (
1
).
∎

In view of Proposition
3.1
, the path toward establishing the existence of a weak solution to problem (
1
) is laid out: indeed, it remains to show that

(3.10)

S(\gamma)<S_{n}.

To prove this, we need to distinguish between two cases,
the high-dimensional case
n\geq 5
and the low-dimensional case
n=3,4
, which we now deal with separately.

1)  The high-dimensional case
n\geq 5
.
In this first case,
we are able to prove that (
3.10
) actually holds

for every
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
by using an
ad-hoc
competitor function
in (
3.2
). To begin with, we have the following technical results.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
0\in\Omega
. Let
\delta>0
be
such that
B_{4\delta}\Subset\Omega
. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},[0,1])
be such that
\varphi\equiv 1
in
B_{\delta}
and
\varphi\equiv 0
in
B^{c}_{2\delta}
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, let

(3.11)

U_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varphi(x)\,V_{\varepsilon}(x),\quad{\mbox{for all }}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
V_{\varepsilon}
is as in (
2.2
) with
y=0
.

Then, the following holds:

(i)

\dfrac{\|\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\|V_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}}=S_{n}
;

(ii)

\|\nabla U_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=K_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2}),\textit{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
;

(iii)

\|U_{\varepsilon}\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=K_{2}+O(\varepsilon^{n}),\textit{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,
where
\dfrac{K_{1}}{K_{2}^{1-2/n}}=S_{n};

(iv)

[U_{\varepsilon}]_{s}^{2}=O(\varepsilon^{2-2s}),\textit{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

The validity of (i) follows from the classical result by Talenti
[
11
]
. For (ii) and (iii) we refer to
[
6
, Lemma 1.1]
, while (iv) is contained in the proof of
[
2
, Lemma 4.10]
.
∎

We stress that the assumption
n\geq 5
is actually not needed in the previous Lemma
3.2
: indeed, the typical term which exhibits different behaviours according to the dimension
n
is
\|U_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. The restriction on
n
becomes important in the upcoming estimate.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 5
,
C_{0}>0
and
v\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that

|v(x)|+|x||\nabla v(x)|\leq C_{0}\,\min\left\{1,\dfrac{1}{|x|^{n-2}}\right\},\quad\textrm{ for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

Then, there exists
C=C(n,C_{0},s)>0
such that

(3.12)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B^{c}_{R}}\dfrac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq\dfrac{C}{R^{2s}}\quad\textrm{ for all }R\geq 10.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\left(0,\frac{R}{2}\right]
. We remark that,
if
y\in B_{R}^{c}
and
z\in B_{\rho}(y)
,

|z|\geq|y|-|z-y|>|y|-\rho\geq|y|-\frac{R}{2}>\frac{|y|}{2},

and therefore, if
x\in B_{\rho}(y)
, we have that

\displaystyle|v(x)-v(y)|\leq\sup_{z\in B_{\rho}(y)}|\nabla v(z)|\,|x-y|\leq\sup_{z\in B_{\rho}(y)}\frac{C_{0}\,|x-y|}{|z|^{n-1}}\leq\frac{C\,|x-y|}{|y|^{n-1}},

where we will take the liberty of freely renaming
C>0
from line to line.

Consequently,

\begin{split}&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{R}^{c}}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\leq\iint_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{R}^{c}}\atop{\{|x-y|\leq\rho\}}}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy+2\iint_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{R}^{c}}\atop{\{|x-y|>\rho\}}}\frac{|v(x)|^{2}+|v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\leq C\iint_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{R}^{c}}\atop{\{|x-y|\leq\rho\}}}\frac{|x-y|^{2-n-2s}}{|y|^{2n-2}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad+C\iint_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{R}^{c}}\atop{\{|x-y|>\rho\}}}\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{|x|^{2n-4}}\right\}\frac{dx\,dy}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\\
&\qquad+C\iint_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{R}^{c}}\atop{\{|x-y|>\rho\}}}\frac{dx\,dy}{|y|^{2n-4}|x-y|^{n+2s}}\\
&\leq\frac{C\rho^{2-2s}}{R^{n-2}}+\frac{C}{\rho^{2s}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{|x|^{2n-4}}\right\}\,dx+\frac{C}{R^{n-4}\rho^{2s}}\\
&\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2s}}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{R^{n-2}}+1+\frac{1}{R^{n-4}}\right).\end{split}

In particular, choosing
\rho:=\frac{R}{2}
, the left-hand side of (
3.12
) is controlled by

\frac{C}{R^{2s}}\left(\frac{1}{R^{n-4}}+1\right)

and the desired result follows.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 5
and let
U_{\varepsilon}
be as in (
3.11
). Then,

[U_{\varepsilon}]^{2}_{s}=\varepsilon^{2-2s}\,[V_{1}]^{2}_{s}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\quad\textrm{ as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

Proof.

The proof follows the line of
that of
[
2
, Lemma 4.10]
.
Recall that
\delta>0
is such that
B_{4\delta}\subset\Omega
and define the sets

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:x\in B_{\delta},y\in B^{c}_{\delta}\,\textrm{ and }|x-y|>\delta/2\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:x\in B_{\delta},y\in B^{c}_{\delta}\,\textrm{ and }|x-y|\leq\delta/2\right\}.

With this notation, we get that

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{B_{\delta}\times B_{\delta}}\dfrac{|V_{\varepsilon}(x)-V_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+2\iint_{\mathbb{D}}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+2\iint_{\mathbb{E}}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+\iint_{B^{c}_{\delta}\times B^{c}_{\delta}}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.

Following the computations of
[
10
, Proposition 21]
, one can infer that
the last three integrals of the above identity behaves like
O(\varepsilon^{n-2})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. See also
[
7
]
for similar computations with
p\neq 2
.

Let us now improve the estimate performed in
[
2
]
for the first integral (recall Lemma
3.2
-(iv)).
We perform the change of variables
x:=\varepsilon\xi
and
y:=\varepsilon\eta
to see that

(3.13)

\iint_{B_{\delta}\times B_{\delta}}\dfrac{|V_{\varepsilon}(x)-V_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy=\iint_{B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\times B_{\delta/\varepsilon}}\dfrac{|V_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\xi)-V_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\eta)|^{2}}{|\varepsilon\xi-\varepsilon\eta|^{n+2s}}\,\varepsilon^{2n}\,d\xi\,d\eta.

We also observe that, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

V_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon z)=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{(n-2)/2}}{(\varepsilon^{2}+|\varepsilon z|^{2})^{(n-2)/2}}=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{-(n-2)/2}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{(n-2)/2}}=\varepsilon^{-(n-2)/2}V_{1}(z).

Plugging this information into (
3.13
), we infer that

(3.14)

\iint_{B_{\delta}\times B_{\delta}}\dfrac{|V_{\varepsilon}(x)-V_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy=\varepsilon^{2-2s}\iint_{B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\times B_{\delta/\varepsilon}}\dfrac{|V_{1}(\xi)-V_{1}(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{n+2s}}\,d\xi\,d\eta.

Moreover, we notice that

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus\left(B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\times B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\right)}\dfrac{|V_{1}(\xi)-V_{1}(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{n+2s}}\,d\xi\,d\eta\leq 2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B^{c}_{\delta/\varepsilon}}\dfrac{|V_{1}(\xi)-V_{1}(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{n+2s}}\,d\xi\,d\eta.

Now, since
V_{1}
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
, choosing
R:=\delta/\varepsilon
, it follows that there exists
\widetilde{C}>0
(depending on
\delta
but independent of
\varepsilon
) such that

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus\left(B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\times B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\right)}\dfrac{|V_{1}(\xi)-V_{1}(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{n+2s}}\,d\xi\,d\eta\leq\widetilde{C}\varepsilon^{2s}.

From this and (
3.14
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\iint_{B_{\delta}\times B_{\delta}}\dfrac{|V_{\varepsilon}(x)-V_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2-2s}\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|V_{1}(\xi)-V_{1}(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{n+2s}}\,d\xi\,d\eta-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus\left(B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\times B_{\delta/\varepsilon}\right)}\dfrac{|V_{1}(\xi)-V_{1}(\eta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\eta|^{n+2s}}\,d\xi\,d\eta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2-2s}[V_{1}]_{s}^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Combining all the estimates, we get the desired result.
∎

Thanks to the previous results, we can now prove that the family
U_{\varepsilon}
decrease the energy of
Q_{\gamma}(\cdot)
below the threshold
S_{n}
, that is,
(
3.10
) holds.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 5
, and let
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
. Then, there exists a function
v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
Q_{\gamma}(v)<S_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
U_{\varepsilon}
be as in (
3.11
). By Lemma
3.2
and Proposition
3.4
, it follows that, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle Q_{\gamma}\left(\frac{U_{\varepsilon}}{\|U_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}}\right)=\frac{1}{\|U_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla U_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx-\gamma\,[U_{\varepsilon}]^{2}_{s}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{K_{2}^{{2}/{2^{\ast}}}+O(\varepsilon^{n})}\left(K_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2})-\gamma[V_{1}]^{2}_{s}\varepsilon^{2-2s}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\frac{1}{K_{2}^{{2}/{2^{\ast}}}}+O(\varepsilon^{n})\right)\left(K_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2})-\gamma[V_{1}]^{2}_{s}\varepsilon^{2-2s}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=S_{n}-\frac{\gamma[V_{1}]^{2}_{s}}{K_{2}^{{2}/{2^{\ast}}}}\,\varepsilon^{2-2s}+o(\varepsilon^{2-2s}).

This entails the desired result. ∎

With Proposition
3.5
at hand, we can immediately prove the first assertion in our main Theorem
1.1
.

Proof (of Theorem
1.1
- (1)).

Assume that
n\geq 5

and let
\gamma\in(0,C_{emb})
. Owing to Proposition

3.5
, we know that
S(\gamma)<S_{n}
; hence, by Proposition

3.1
we conclude that
S(\gamma)
is achieved, and
there exists a weak solution of
problem (
1
).
∎

2)  The low-dimensional case
n=3,4
.
In this second case, even if we can still rely on Proposition
3.1
(which holds for every
n\geq 3
), we
cannot use
the estimate in Proposition
3.4
; rather, we use a more
implicit
argument to show that

S(\gamma)<S_{n}\quad\text{for every $\gamma^{*}<\gamma<C_{emb}$},

for some (not explicit)
\gamma^{*}\in[0,C_{emb})
.

To this end, we consider
S(\cdot)
as a
function of
\gamma
, defined
in the interval
\mathcal{J}:=(0,C_{emb}
). Owing to the definition of
S(\gamma)
in (
3.2
), we see that

(3.15)

S(\lambda)\leq S(\mu)\quad\text{for every $0<\mu\leq\lambda<C_{emb}$},

that is,
S(\cdot)
is non-increasing on
\mathcal{J}
.

Moreover, we have the following:

Lemma 3.6
.

The function
S(\cdot)
is continuous on
\mathcal{J}
. Furthermore,

(3.16)

\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}S(\gamma)=S_{n}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\lim_{\gamma\to C_{emb}}S(\gamma)=0.

Proof.

To ease the readability, we split the proof into two steps.

Step I):
We begin by proving the continuity of
S(\cdot)
in
\mathcal{J}
. To this end we first observe that, since
S(\cdot)
is non-increasing, for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}
we have

\exists\,\,\,{S}(\gamma-):=\lim_{\mu\to\gamma^{-}}{S}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad\exists\,\,\,{S}(\gamma+):=\lim_{\mu\to\gamma^{+}}{S}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}.

We now fix
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{J}
and
r>0
such that
[\gamma_{0}-r,\gamma_{0}+r]\subseteq\mathcal{J}
and we show that
{S}(\cdot)
is
continuous from both the left and the right at
\gamma_{0}
, that is,

{S}(\gamma_{0}-)={S}(\gamma_{0}+)={S}(\gamma_{0}).

To prove the left-continuity, we proceed as follows. First of all,
given any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
by the definition of
{S}(\gamma_{0})
we infer that there exists

u=u_{\varepsilon,\gamma_{0}}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

(3.17)

\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad{S}(\gamma_{0})\leq{Q}_{\gamma_{0}}(u)<{S}(\gamma_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

From this, using the monotonicity of
{S}(\cdot)
, for every
\gamma\leq\gamma_{0}
we obtain

(3.18)

\begin{split}&0\leq{S}(\gamma)-{S}(\gamma_{0})\leq{Q}_{\gamma}(u)-{S}(\gamma_{0})\\
&\qquad=\big({Q}_{\gamma_{0}}(u)-{S}(\gamma_{0})\big)+(\gamma_{0}-\gamma)[u]^{2}_{s}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(\gamma_{0}-\gamma)[u]^{2}_{s}.\end{split}

Moreover, exploiting (
2.6
),

Q_{\gamma_{0}}(u)=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\gamma_{0}[u]^{2}_{s}\geq\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{C_{emb}}\right)\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, using again (
2.6
) and recalling (
3.17
),

\begin{split}&[u]^{2}_{s}\leq\frac{1}{C_{emb}}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{C_{emb}}\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{C_{emb}}\right)^{-1}Q_{\gamma_{0}}(u)\\
&\qquad<\frac{1}{C_{emb}-\gamma_{0}}\left(S(\gamma_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\leq\frac{S(\gamma_{0})+1}{C_{emb}-\gamma_{0}}.\end{split}

From this and (
3.3
) we thus obtain that

(3.19)

[u]^{2}_{s}<\frac{S_{n}+1}{C_{emb}-\gamma_{0}}\leq\frac{S_{n}+1}{C_{emb}-\gamma_{0}-r}=:\Theta(\gamma_{0}).

Using this estimate together with (
3.18
),
we find that

0\leq{S}(\gamma)-{S}(\gamma_{0})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(\gamma_{0}-\gamma)\Theta(\gamma_{0}).

As a consequence, setting

(3.20)

\delta=\delta_{\varepsilon,\gamma_{0}}:=\min\left\{r,\frac{\varepsilon}{2\Theta(\gamma_{0})}\right\}>0,

we conclude that,
for every
\gamma\in(\gamma_{0}-\delta,\gamma_{0}]
,

0\leq{S}(\gamma)-{S}(\gamma_{0})<\varepsilon

and this proves that
{S}(\cdot)
is continuous from the left at
\gamma_{0}
.

As regards the continuity from the right, we proceed essentially as above,
but we exploit in a more crucial way the uniform estimate (
3.19
).
First of all, given
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma\in[\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}+r]
, we let

u=u_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
be such that

\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad{S}(\gamma)\leq{Q}_{\gamma}(u)<{S}(\gamma)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

From this, by the monotonicity of
{S}(\cdot)
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq{S}(\gamma_{0})-{S}(\gamma)\leq{Q}_{\gamma_{0}}(u)-{S}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\qquad=\big({Q}_{\gamma}(u)-{S}(\gamma)\big)+(\gamma-\gamma_{0})[u]^{2}_{s}\quad<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(\gamma-\gamma_{0})[u]^{2}_{s}.

Furthermore, by arguing exactly as above
to obtain the estimate in (
3.19
) (with
\gamma_{0}
here replaced by
\gamma
), we obtain that

[u]^{2}_{s}<\frac{1}{C_{emb}-\gamma}\left(S(\gamma)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\leq\frac{S_{n}+1}{C_{emb}-\gamma}<\frac{S_{n}+1}{C_{emb}-\gamma_{0}-r}=\Theta(\gamma_{0}).

As a consequence, taking
\delta
as in (
3.20
),
we conclude that, for every
\gamma\in[\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}+\delta_{\varepsilon})
,

0\leq{S}(\gamma_{0})-{S}(\gamma)<\varepsilon.

We then conclude that
{S}(\cdot)

is also continuous from the right at
\gamma_{0}
, and thus, by the arbitrariness of
\gamma_{0}
,
S(\cdot)\in C(\mathcal{J})
.

Step II):
We now prove the limits in (
3.15
). The limit of
S(\cdot)
as
\gamma\to 0^{+}
is a direct consequence of
(
3.3
), hence we now focus on the limit as
\gamma\to C_{emb}
.

We recall Remark
2.1
-iii) and we take
\Phi_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{,12}(\Omega)
such that

[\Phi_{0}]_{s}^{2}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\nabla\Phi_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C_{emb}.

Then, we have that, for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}
,

\displaystyle 0\leq S(\gamma)\leq Q_{\gamma}(\Phi_{0})=(C_{emb}-\gamma)[\Phi_{0}]^{2}_{s}=C_{emb}-\gamma.

Hence, by letting
\gamma\to C_{emb}
in the above estimate, we obtain the desired limit. This ends the proof.
∎
