Time-Periodic Non-Radial Solutions Near Monotone Vortices in Linearized 2D Euler
Ángel Castro and Daniel Lear

Abstract
. We study the linearized 2D Euler equations around radial vortex profiles. Previous works have shown that the strict monotonicity of the vorticity profile leads to axisymmetrization and inviscid damping of non-radial perturbations. Given any strictly decreasing radial vortex, we construct arbitrarily close (in low Hölder norms C α C^{\alpha} , with 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 ) radial profiles that are merely non-increasing, for which non-radial, time-periodic solutions to the linearized equation exist. This shows that both axisymmetrization and inviscid damping are not robust under small, low-regularity perturbations of the background profile that violate strict monotonicity.

1.
Introduction and main result

In this manuscript, we consider the 2D Euler equation for an incompressible and inviscid fluid, expressed in vorticity form:

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\omega+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\omega

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

in the full domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where the velocity field is recovered through the scalar vorticity by

(2)

\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega,

with
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{2},\partial_{1})
and

(3)

\Delta^{-1}\omega(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|)\,\omega(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}.

The main objective of this paper is to study the dynamics around radially symmetric vortex profiles that are monotone decreasing. To this end, we consider linearized 2D Euler equation around a radial vortex
\varpi(r)
, which takes the form

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+u_{r}[w]\partial_{r}\varpi+\frac{u_{\theta}[\varpi]}{r}\partial_{\theta}w=0,

where we use polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
and where
u_{r}[f]
and
u_{\theta}[f]
are defined by

\displaystyle u_{r}[f](r,\theta):=\frac{\mathbf{e}_{r}}{2\pi}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{(r\cos(\theta)-s\cos(\beta),\,r\sin(\theta)-s\cos(\beta))^{\perp}}{r^{2}+s^{2}+2rs\cos(\theta-\beta)}f(s,\beta)sd\theta ds,

\displaystyle u_{\theta}[f](r,\theta):=\frac{\mathbf{e}_{\theta}}{2\pi}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{(r\cos(\theta)-s\cos(\beta),\,r\sin(\theta)-s\cos(\beta))^{\perp}}{r^{2}+s^{2}+2rs\cos(\theta-\beta)}f(s,\beta)sd\theta ds.

In
[
4
]
, Bedrossian, Coti-Zelati and Vicol established that strict monotonicity (up to at zero and infinity) of the vorticity profile
\varpi
leads to inviscid damping and axisymmetrization. In this work, we show that strict monotonicity is essential. Specifically, we exhibit a strictly monotone radial profile
\varpi(r)
for which, arbitrarily close to it in
C^{1^{-}}
, there exists another radial profile
\overline{\varpi}(r)
that is monotone but not strictly monotone, and for which the linearized equation (
4
) admits non-radial, time-periodic solutions with
m
-fold symmetry for any
m\geq 2
.

In this paper, we work with the profile
\varpi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1}
. This choice is made for simplicity; however, much more general radial profiles could also be considered, such as
(1+r^{2})^{-k}
with
k=2,3,\dots
, or
e^{-r^{2}}
.

1.1.
Motivation and background

The stability of vortices is one of the most fundamental problems in the theory of hydrodynamic stability and has been considered by many authors, starting with Kelvin
[
31
]
and Orr
[
38
]
, and continuing to the present day in both mathematics and physics.

Over the years, a combination of experimental observations, computer simulations, and formal asymptotics (see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
8
,
48
,
49
]
and references therein) suggests that a vortex subjected to a sufficiently small disturbance might return to radial symmetry as
t\to\infty
in a weak sense. This weak convergence is referred to as
vortex axisymmetrization
, and is thought to be relevant to understanding coherent vortices in 2D turbulence
[
19
]
, atmospheric dynamics
[
39
,
40
]
, and various settings in plasma physics
[
42
,
43
]
.

From a theoretical perspective, Arnold’s variational principle provides a foundational framework for studying the stability of vortices. The treatment in
[
1
]
develops the general variational framework and establishes sufficient conditions for Lyapunov stability based on monotonicity properties of the vorticity, while the more recent work of Gallay and Šverák
[
20
]
applies these ideas to specific vortex configurations, illustrating Lyapunov stability, for example, in the cases of the Gaussian vortex and the algebraic vortex.
Nevertheless, the precise dynamics near these steady states remain subtle and largely open.

In this regard, we also highlight the work of Wan and Pulvirenti
[
44
]
, who established the Lyapunov stability of the characteristic function of a circle in

L^{1}
for circular bounded domains; see also
[
41
]
for results concerning the whole space. This result has been further generalized for monotone smooth vortices in stronger norms by Choi and Lim, see
[
13
]
.

Early studies of vortex dynamics focused on point vortex, which are
\delta
-functions centered at points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Such solutions provide models of vorticity sharply concentrated in small neighborhoods, and have been studied by many authors, including Kirchhoff
[
32
]
and C.C. Lin
[
33
]
and the books
[
36
,
35
]
for more references. Building on this, Ionescu and Jia
[
27
,
28
]
studied the asymptotic stability of these highly concentrated configurations in the full 2D Euler equation, showing how non‑radial perturbations evolve and ultimately axisymmetrize.

Results for the linear stability of smooth, radially symmetric vortices have recently begun to emerge. In particular, Bedrossian, Coti Zelati, and Vicol
[
4
]
proved that strict monotonicity of the vorticity profile leads to vortex axisymmetrization, inviscid damping, and vorticity depletion for the linearized 2D Euler equation. Their work provides the first general mechanism explaining how non-radial perturbations decay in time around a monotone radial vortex.

To understand the significance of our work, let us examine the result of
[
4
]
, which is the main motivation of this paper. There, the authors study a
radial vorticity profile
\varpi(r)
satisfying

(V1)

0\leq\varpi(r)\leq(1+r^{2})^{-3}
,

(V2)

|(r\partial_{r})^{j}\varpi(r)|\leq C_{j}(1+r^{2})^{-3}
, for all
j\geq 0
,

(V3)

(Strict monotonicity)
\partial_{r}\varpi(r)>0
,
r>0
,

and the equation (
4
) with initial data

w(r,\theta,0)=\sum_{n\neq 0}w^{\text{in}}_{n}(r)e^{inx},

and with the extra assumption

(5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{\text{in}}_{\pm 1}(r)r^{2}dr=0.

Roughly speaking, they are able to prove inviscid damping, meaning that

\displaystyle\|u^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\text{weight}}}\lesssim(1+t^{2})^{-1/2},\qquad\|u^{\theta}\|_{L^{2}_{\text{weight}}}\lesssim(1+t^{2})^{-1},

where
L^{2}_{\text{weight}}
denotes an
L^{2}
space with a suitable radial weight. Their result is in fact much stronger, providing sharper estimates for the decay of the velocity, vortex axisymmetrization, and vorticity depletion near the origin.
The existence of time-periodic solutions for (
4
) would clearly contradict these conclusions.

Condition (
5
) is not merely technical. Indeed,

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{\infty}w_{\pm 1}(r,t)\,r^{2}\,dr=0,

which prevents the decay of
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pm 1}
.
Identity (
6
) arises from the translational invariance of the 2D Euler equation:
the translation of a radial function is again a stationary solution, and at the linear level,
this implies the existence of these neutral modes. To avoid such solutions, we will restrict
our attention to modes with
|n|\geq 2
.

Another important result we would like to discuss is
[
15
]
(see also
[
51
]
). There, Coti-Zelati and Zillinger prove stability and inviscid damping for (
4
) under some assumptions on
\varpi(r)
, which allow non monotone profiles. The most important condition (H1 in section 1.3), as stated by the authors, is the following (we translate it into our language):

(H1) There exists
0<\gamma_{0}<1
such that

\displaystyle n^{2}|\partial_{y}U(y)|-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{sgn}(\partial_{y}U(y))\partial^{3}_{y}U(y)\geq\gamma_{0}n^{2}|\partial_{y}U(y)|,\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R},

where

(7)

\displaystyle U(y):=\left.\frac{u^{\theta}[\varpi](r,\theta)}{r}\right|_{r=e^{y}}.

We will see in Remarks
1.6
and
2.3
that our profiles do not satisfy (H1); thus, the existence of time periodic solutions is compatible with
[
15
]
.

We emphasize that this problem shares many features with the study of the nonlinear stability of shear flows. Significant progress has been achieved in this direction, beginning with the inviscid damping breakthrough of Bedrossian and Masmoudi
[
5
]
in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
, and subsequently extended to the finite periodic channel
\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
through the independent works of Ionescu and Jia
[
25
,
26
]
and Masmoudi and Zhao
[
37
]
.

Motivated by these developments, the linearized equations around more general shear flows have been intensively investigated in recent years. See, for example,
[
4
,
7
,
16
,
17
,
23
,
29
,
30
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
52
,
53
]
and the references therein for a representative, though not exhaustive, list of contributions.

Earlier foundational results on stability were established by Lin and Zeng
[
34
]
, who proved that nonlinear inviscid damping fails for perturbations of the Couette flow in
H^{s}
when
s<3/2
. They also showed the nonexistence of nontrivial traveling waves close to Couette in
H^{s}
for
s>3/2
. More specifically, they identified Kelvin’s cat’s eyes steady states in a neighborhood of the Couette shear flow. In fact, the dynamics at this level of regularity are even more intricate: traveling waves of order
O(1)
speed and zero circulation also arise, as demonstrated by the authors in
[
10
]
and in
[
11
]
for the Taylor–Couette setting.

The question of whether nontrivial invariant structures, such as steady states or
traveling waves, may exist near the Poiseuille flow has attracted significant attention. For
the quadratic profile
U_{P}(y)=y^{2}
in
\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
, Coti Zelati–Elgindi–Widmayer

[
14
]
proved that there are no nontrivial traveling waves (TW) arbitrarily
close to Poiseuille in
H^{5^{+}}
, establishing the first high-regularity rigidity result around
this shear flow. Complementing this, Gui–Xie–Xu
[
24
]
showed in their classification
theorem that any
traveling wave
\omega
satisfying

\|\partial_{y}\omega+2\|_{L^{\infty}}<2

must necessarily be a shear flow, thereby ruling out nontrivial TW within this regime.

A different perspective was recently provided by Drivas–Nualart
[
18
]
, who proved
that Poiseuille is isolated in
the
C^{1}
topology from non-shear steady states. These results collectively illustrate a rigidity framework near Poiseuille at high regularity.

In contrast to this rigidity, our previous work
[
12
]
shows that such phenomena break down at lower regularity.
More precisely, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we construct nontrivial Lipschitz
traveling wave solutions
\omega
satisfying

\|\omega+2y\|_{H^{3/2^{-}}}<\varepsilon.

These solutions also satisfy

\|\omega+2y\|_{C^{1^{-}}}<\varepsilon.

Moreover, our construction saturates the Gui–Xie–Xu criterion since the solutions satisfies

\|\partial_{y}\omega+2\|_{L^{\infty}}=2.

This optimality was also established in
[
18
]
, where the authors additionally proved the existence of smooth stationary states near the Poiseuille flow in the
C^{1^{-}}
topology. In fact, Drivas and Nualart go further and demonstrate the existence of smooth stationary states near the shear flow
y^{k}
in the
C^{(k-1)^{-}}
topology (at the vorticity level).

Remarkably, the existence of rotating solutions for the (full) 2D Euler equations near strictly monotone vortices, in the
L^{\infty}
topology, can be recovered from Theorem 1.1 in the work of García, Hmidi, and Mateu
[
21
]
(see also
[
22
]
). Indeed, if one takes, for example,
\varpi_{R}(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1}\chi_{[0,R]}(r)
(which corresponds to
f_{0}(r)\mathbf{1}_{[0,R]}(r)
in the notation of
[
21
]
), then Theorem 1.1(ii) (abundance case) can be applied. To verify this, one simply replaces
\varpi_{R}
by
-\varpi_{R}
and observes that, although the authors work with
R=1
, an analogous result remains valid for any
R>0
. Consequently, the existence of rotating solutions bifurcating from
\varpi_{R}
follows. In addition,

\displaystyle\|\varpi_{R}-(1+r^{2})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}=(1+R^{2})^{-1},

and
\varpi_{R}
is as close as we want in
L^{\infty}
to
(1+r^{2})^{-1}
by making
R
large enough. However, this distance, in smoother spaces, such as for example
C^{\alpha}
, is infinite.

Finally, we remark that our strategy to prove the main result of this paper comes from the construction of our solutions in
[
12
]
.

1.2.
Statement of the main result

Our goal is to find time-periodic solutions for (
4
) of the form
w(r,\theta,t):=W(r,\theta-\lambda t)
. The previous ansantz give us

(8)

\displaystyle-\lambda\partial_{\theta}W+u_{r}[W]\partial_{r}\varpi+\frac{u_{\theta}[\varpi]}{r}\partial_{\theta}W=0,\quad(r,\theta)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}.

Now, we take

(9)

\displaystyle W(r,\theta):=W_{n}(r)\cos(n\theta),

with
n\geq 2
, by the parity of the cosine.

Substituting (
9
) into (
8
) yields

(10)

\displaystyle(\lambda+c(r))W_{n}(r)-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{n}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)W_{n}(s)ds=0,\quad r\in[0,\infty),

with

(11)

K_{n}(r):=\frac{1}{2r}\left\{\begin{aligned} r^{-n}\qquad&r>1,\\
r^{+n}\qquad&r\leq 1.\end{aligned}\right.

and

(12)

c(r):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varpi^{\prime}(s)K_{1}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)ds=-\frac{u_{\theta}(r)}{r}.

The computations required to go from (
8
) to (
10
) follow steps similar to those
in Section 3 of
[
9
]
. For the sake of completeness, we provide all the details in the
appendix.

To solve (
10
), we next introduce the ansatz

(13)

\displaystyle W_{n}(r):=h_{n}(r)\partial_{r}\varpi(r)

in (
10
), which yields the following equation for
h_{n}
:

\displaystyle\left((\lambda+c(r))h_{n}(r)-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{s}\varpi(s)K_{n}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)h_{n}(s)ds\right)\partial_{r}\varpi(r)=0.

Therefore, it will be sufficient to solve

(14)

\displaystyle(\lambda+c(r))h_{n}(r)-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{s}\varpi(s)K_{n}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)h_{n}(s)ds=0,\quad r\in\text{supp}(\partial_{r}\varpi),

with a function
h_{n}:\supp(\partial_{r}\varpi)\to\mathbb{R}
.

The main result of the paper is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough and
0<\alpha<1
. For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\varpi_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}
,
h^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\emph{supp}(\partial_{r}\varpi))
and
\lambda^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying (
14
) and such that

\displaystyle\|(1+r^{2})^{-1}-\varpi_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{\alpha}}\leq\varepsilon.

Remark 1.2
.

The reason why we take
n
large enough is quite technical, and we could actually take
n\geq 2
. See Remark
4.11
for more details.

Remark 1.3
.

The profile
\varpi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1}
is strictly monotone, in the sense that

\partial_{r}\varpi(r)>0
for all
r\in(0,\infty)
, as required in (V3).
In contrast, the approximating profile
\varpi_{\varepsilon}
does not retain this
strict monotonicity: by construction, one has

\partial_{r}\varpi_{\varepsilon}(r)=0\quad\text{for some interval }I\subset(1/2,2).

Remark 1.4
.

The profiles
\varpi(r)=(1+r^{2})^{-1}
and
\varpi_{\varepsilon}(r)
do not satisfy (V1)
(although both are positive). Nevertheless, one could equally work with other profiles, such as
e^{-r^{2}}
, which do satisfy (V1) and (V2), and establish an analog
of Theorem
1.1
for them. We simply choose
(1+r^{2})^{-1}
because it leads to
simpler computations.

Remark 1.5
.

The profile
\varpi(r)
used in
[
4
]
is smooth (see condition (V2)).
In our case, while
(1+r^{2})^{-1}
is smooth, the approximating profile

\varpi_{\varepsilon}
is only in
W^{1,\infty}
. We believe that it should be
possible to construct time-periodic solutions associated with a
C^{k}
profile as
well, although at the cost of significantly more involved computations

Remark 1.6
.

In our case,
\partial_{y}^{3}U(y)
, with
U(y)
as in (
7
), involves
\partial_{r}^{2}\varpi_{\varepsilon}
and

\partial^{2}_{r}\varpi_{\varepsilon}(r)=a\delta_{r_{1}}(r)-b\delta_{r_{2}}(r)+\text{low order terms},

where
a,b,r_{1},r_{2}>0
and
\partial_{y}U(\log(r_{1}))
,
\partial_{y}U(\log(r_{2}))
have the same sign. This fact makes it impossible for (H1) to hold. We provide more details in Remark

.

3.1.
The problem as a system of integral equations

As we are working in a disjoint domain, it will be useful to use the notation

f(r):=\begin{cases}f_{L}(r)\qquad r\in[0,1-\varepsilon/2],\\
f_{R}(r)\qquad r\in[1+\varepsilon/2,+\infty).\end{cases}

Thus, for

-\frac{(+n)}{x}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{x}^{1}(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1-\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{-n}\frac{H_{L}(s)}{\lambda+c_{L}(s,\varepsilon)}ds\\
-\frac{(+n)}{x}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{1}^{+\infty}(1+\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1+\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{-n}\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{1-\varepsilon/2}\right)^{-n}\frac{H_{R}(s)}{\lambda+c_{R}(s,\varepsilon)}ds=0.

By introducing the auxiliary notation

(44)

A(x):=\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1-\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{n}\frac{H_{L}(s)}{\lambda+c_{L}(s,\varepsilon)}ds,

(45)

B(x):=\frac{1}{2n}\int_{x}^{1}(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1-\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{-n}\frac{H_{L}(s)}{\lambda+c_{L}(s,\varepsilon)}ds\\
+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{1}^{+\infty}(1+\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1+\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{-n}\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{1-\varepsilon/2}\right)^{-n}\frac{H_{R}(s)}{\lambda+c_{R}(s,\varepsilon)}ds,

we can write (
41
) and (
43
) in a more compact form as follows

\displaystyle H_{L}(x)-A(x)-B(x)=0,

\displaystyle\frac{x}{n}H_{L}^{\prime}(x)+A(x)-B(x)=0.

Thus, we have the following relations:

(46)

H_{L}(x)+\frac{x}{n}H_{L}^{\prime}(x)=2B(x),\qquad H_{L}(x)-\frac{x}{n}H_{L}^{\prime}(x)=2A(x).

Now, taking another derivative in (
43
) we obtain taking into account that the boundary terms do not cancel out and give us now an extra term that

H_{L}^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{(-n)(-n-1)}{x^{2}}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1-\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{n}\frac{H_{L}(s)}{\lambda+c_{L}(s,\varepsilon)}ds\\
-\frac{(+n)(+n-1)}{x^{2}}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{x}^{1}(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1-\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{-n}\frac{H_{L}(s)}{\lambda+c_{L}(s,\varepsilon)}ds\\
-\frac{(+n)(+n-1)}{x^{2}}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{1}^{+\infty}(1+\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(s(1+\varepsilon/2))\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{-n}\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{1-\varepsilon/2}\right)^{-n}\frac{H_{R}(s)}{\lambda+c_{R}(s,\varepsilon)}ds\\
+\frac{1-\varepsilon/2}{x(1-\varepsilon/2)}\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1-\varepsilon/2))H_{L}(x)}{\lambda+c_{L}(x,\varepsilon)}=0,

which can be simplified using the notation introduced above as follows

H_{L}^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{(-n)(-n-1)}{x^{2}}A(x)-\frac{(+n)(+n-1)}{x^{2}}B(x)+\frac{1}{x}\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1-\varepsilon/2))H_{L}(x)}{\lambda+c_{L}(x,\varepsilon)}=0.

Finally, using (
46
) and simplifying, we arrive at

H_{L}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}H_{L}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(n^{2}-x\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1-\varepsilon/2))}{\lambda+c_{L}(x,\varepsilon)}\right)H_{L}(x)=0,

for
x\in(0,1).

We now repeat the same procedure by taking derivatives at (
42

H_{R}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}H_{R}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(n^{2}-x\frac{(1+\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1+\varepsilon/2))}{\lambda+c_{R}(x,\varepsilon)}\right)H_{R}(x)=0,

for
x\in(1,+\infty)
.

3.3.3.
The boundary conditions of the ODE

From (
16
) together with (
39
), (
40
) and

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}c_{L}(r,\varepsilon)=-\frac{\varpi_{0}(0)}{2}-\frac{\varpi_{0}(1+\varepsilon/2)-\varpi_{0}(1-\varepsilon/2)}{2},\qquad\lim_{r\to+\infty}c_{R}(r,\varepsilon)=0,

we deduce

3.4.
The ODE system

After all these calculations, we have relaxed our problem from a system of coupled integral equations to a system of ODEs. That is, we have shown that a smooth solution of (
41
)-(
42
),
H=(H_{L},H_{R})
, satisfies the following system of uncoupled ODEs:

(47)

\left\{\begin{aligned} H_{L}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}H_{L}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(n^{2}-x\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1-\varepsilon/2))}{\lambda+c_{L}(x,\varepsilon)}\right)H_{L}(x)&=0,\quad x\in(0,1),\\
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{1-n}H_{L}(x)&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

and

(48)

\left\{\begin{aligned} H_{R}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}H_{R}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(n^{2}-x\frac{(1+\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1+\varepsilon/2))}{\lambda+c_{R}(x,\varepsilon)}\right)H_{R}(x)&=0,\quad x\in(1,+\infty),\\
\lim_{x\to+\infty}x^{n-1}H_{R}(x)&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

The process to find a solution to (
41
) and (
42
) will consist of three main steps:

(1)

Solve the system of ODEs for general
\varepsilon>0
and the limiting system obtained for
\varepsilon=0
.

(2)

Study the difference between the solution for general
\varepsilon>0
and the limiting one obtained for
\varepsilon=0
. Here, we require a precise control of the difference in terms of the parameter
\varepsilon
.

(3)

Using the above, we reduce the problem of solving the original system of integral equations to solving a single equation for the only remaining unknown at that point, namely the parameter
\lambda
.

4.
Existence and uniqueness

In this section we shall study equations (

).

It is important to emphasize that

, and

implicitly depend on the parameter

.
We note that at this time, both systems are decoupled. The only relationship between them is the eigenvalue

that appears in both.

In addition, we note that the boundary values

and

1

and

and

With this normalization, the general solution to (

, where

are free parameters.

In addition, we denote by
\mathring{h}
the solution of the limiting problem of (
47
)-(

) resulting from making
\varepsilon\to 0

\mathring{h}

\mathring{h}_{L}
and
\mathring{h}_{R}

That is,
\mathring{h}

For the remainder of this section, and in order to simplify notation, we will denote

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(x):

\displaystyle=-\frac{x\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x)}{\lambda_{0}-\mathring{c}(x)}=\frac{2x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}\frac{1}{\frac{\log(2)}{2}-\frac{\log(1+x^{2})}{2x^{2}}}.

The function

is real analytic in

. Moreover, it has a pole of order 1 in

and discontinuous branches at

4.1.
The limiting ODE in the right

In this paragraph, we are going to study

(51)

\mathring{h}_{R}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}\mathring{h}_{R}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(n^{2}+\Psi_{0}(x)\right)\mathring{h}_{R}(x)=0,\qquad\text{for }x\in(1,+\infty),

with

(52)

\lim_{x\to 1^{+}}\mathring{h}_{R}(x)=1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{x\to+\infty}x^{n-1}\mathring{h}_{R}(x)=0.

The main result of this section is the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

The system (
51
)-(
52
) admits a unique solution
\mathring{h}_{R}\in C([1,+\infty))\cap C^{\infty}((1,+\infty))
. Moreover, this solution satisfies:

(1)

\mathring{h}_{R}(x)>0
for all
x\in[1,+\infty)
.

(2)

\|x^{n}\mathring{h}_{R}\|_{L^{\infty}((1,+\infty))}<\infty
.

(3)

\mathring{h}_{R}(x)
behaves as
1+O(|x-1|\log|x-1|^{-1})
as
x\to 1^{+}
.

(4)

\|\mathring{h}_{R}\|_{C^{2}([a,+\infty))}<+\infty
, with
a>1
.

Now, we have to work with the infinite domain
(1,+\infty).
To handle it, it will be convenient to introduce the auxiliary variable
z:=1/x\in(0,1)
and the auxiliary function
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z):=\mathring{h}_{R}(1/z)
. Then,

\mathring{h}_{R}^{\prime}(x)=-z^{2}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime}(z),\qquad\mathring{h}_{R}^{\prime\prime}(x)=2z^{3}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime}(z)+z^{4}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime\prime}(z),

and (
51

(53)

\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\frac{1}{z}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime}(z)-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\left(n^{2}+\Psi_{0}(1/z)\right)\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=0,\qquad\text{for }z\in(0,1),

with the boundary conditions (

(54)

\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}z^{1-n}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=1.

4.1.1.
Analysis of (
53
)

We first consider

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(z)+\frac{1}{z}h^{\prime}(z)-\frac{n^{2}}{z^{2}}h(z)=g(z),\qquad\text{for }z\in(0,1).

Since the solutions of the homogeneous part are
Az^{n}+Bz^{-n}
, we can write

\displaystyle h(z)=Az^{n}+Bz^{-n}+\frac{z^{n}}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{-n+1}g(y)dy-\frac{z^{-n}}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{n+1}g(y)dy,

where it is assumed that
g(y)
has enough cancellation at zero for the first integral to be convergent. Since we need
h(0)=0
we pick
B=0
. Thus, we look for solutions to (
53
) through the integral equation

(55)

\displaystyle\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=Az^{n}+\frac{z^{n}}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{-n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(y)dy-\frac{z^{-n}}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(y)dy.

Here we remark that

\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}=\frac{4}{(1+y^{2})^{2}\left(\log 2-y^{2}\,\log\!\left(1+\frac{1}{y^{2}}\right)\right)},

and then
\Psi_{0}(1/y)/y^{2}\in C([0,a])
for any
a<1
. We write
C(a):=\|\Psi_{0}(1/y)/y^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,a])}
. We take
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=z^{n}f(z)
. Then, (
55
) reads

(56)

\displaystyle f(z)=A+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}f(y)dy-\frac{z^{-2n}}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{2n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}f(y)dy.

Let us fix
A=1
. To solve (
56
) we can apply a classical contraction argument. We define the space
X_{\alpha}:=\{f\in C([0,a]):\|f\|_{X_{\alpha}}<+\infty\}
with the norm
\|f\|_{X_{\alpha}}:=\sup_{z\in[0,a]}|e^{-\alpha z}f(z)|
,
\alpha>0
. Thus the application

F[f](z):=1+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}f(y)dy-\frac{z^{-2n}}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{2n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(1/y)}{y^{2}}f(y)dy,

satisfies

\displaystyle\|F[f]\|_{X_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{C(a)}{2n}\|f\|_{X_{\alpha}}\left(\sup_{z\in[0,a]}\left(e^{-z\alpha}\int_{0}^{z}ye^{\alpha y}dy\right)+\sup_{z\in[0,a]}\left(e^{-\alpha z}z^{-2n}\int_{0}^{z}y^{2n+1}e^{\alpha y}dy\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{C(a)}{2n}\|f\|_{X_{\alpha}}\frac{C}{\alpha}.

We then have that
F
is a linear contraction for
\alpha
large enough. This last fact gives us that there exists a solution
f(z)
,
z\in(0,1)
, which belongs to
C([0,a])
for any
a<1
, of (
56
). By a bootstrapping, we get that this solution is smooth in
(0,a)
for any
a<1
, since
\Psi_{0}(1/z)/z^{2}
is smooth in
(0,a)
. Also, since
\Psi_{0}(1/z)/z^{2}\in C^{1}([0,a])
, we have
f\in C^{2}([0,a])
for any
a<1
.

Thus we have proven the existence of a solution

(57)

\displaystyle\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=z^{n}f(z)\in C^{2}([0,a]),

of (
53
), smooth in
(0,a)
, for all
a<1
and satisfying the first boundary condition of (
54
).

It remains to study the behavior of this solution as
z=1
, and to check the second boundary condition in (
54
). Since we have fixed
A=1
in (
56
), the function
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)
defined in (
57

\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(1^{-})=1
. However, we will show that
0<\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(1^{-})<+\infty
. Therefore, by simply normalizing the function via
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)/\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(1^{-}),

we obtain a solution to (
53

54
).

Remark 4.2
.

The same argument works for other profiles, as for example, either
e^{-x^{2}}
or

, with

, since the corresponding

also satisfies that

with

(actually it is better than that).

4.1.2.
Regularity of
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}
at
z_{0}=1

The only point we need to study, in order to get the regularity of
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}
in the closed interval
[0,1]
, is
z_{0}=1
.

Following the notation in
[
50
]
and using the classical Frobenius method, we can write (
53
) as

\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime\prime}(z)+p(z)\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}^{\prime}(z)+q(z)\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=0,

with

p(z)=\frac{P(z-z_{0})}{z-z_{0}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(z)=\frac{Q(z-z_{0})}{(z-z_{0})^{2}}.

Regular-singular point at
z_{0}=1
:

Here, we have

P(z-z_{0})=\frac{z-z_{0}}{z},\qquad Q(z-z_{0})=-(z-z_{0})^{2}\,\frac{n^{2}+\Psi_{0}(1/z)}{z^{2}},\qquad z_{0}=1.

That is,

P(z)=\frac{z}{z+1},\quad Q(z)=-\left(\frac{z}{z+1}\right)^{2}\left(n^{2}+\Psi_{0}\left(\frac{1}{z+1}\right)\right),

with

\Psi_{0}\left(\frac{1}{z+1}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{1-2\log(2)}\frac{1}{z}+O(1)\right),\qquad\text{as }z\to 0,

and we have that the indicial polynomial

I(r)=r(r-1)+P(0)r+Q(0),

is given by

I(r)=r(r-1),

with the roots
r=1
and
r=0
.

Therefore, we have two linearly independent solutions:

•

Associated to the larger root
r=1
we have an analytic solution on (0, 1), which we denote by
q_{1}(x)
, which can be obtained from the expansion

q_{1}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{1}^{[k]}(z-1)^{k},

where
q_{1}^{[k]}\in\mathbb{R}
. We choose, without loss of generality,
q_{1}^{[1]}=1
.

•

If the roots differs by a integer number, as in our case, then the second solution can be found through the expression

\displaystyle q_{2}(z)=q_{2}^{[-1]}q_{1}(z)\log(1-z)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{2}^{[k]}(z-1)^{k},

where
q_{2}^{[k]}\in\mathbb{R}
for
k=-1,0,1,...
, with
q_{2}^{[0]}\neq 0
(see
[
50
]
, pag. 200-201).
The series converges for
z\in(0,1).

Here, it is crucial to determine whether the logarithmic term appears. To that end, we observe that, for our equation, the coefficient
q_{2}^{[-1]}
is given by

q_{2}^{[-1]}=\frac{\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(1)}{(\partial_{x}c_{R})(1,0)}\frac{q_{2}^{[0]}}{q_{1}^{[1]}}\neq 0,

which implies that
q_{2}(x)
has a singularity at
z=1
of the form
(z-1)\log|z-1|
.

Thus we have that
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)
in (

(58)

\displaystyle\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)=\alpha q_{1}(z)+\beta q_{2}(z),\qquad\text{for }z\in(0,1),

for some
\alpha,\,\beta\in\mathbb{R}.
In particular, this shows that

\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}|\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)|<+\infty.

Next we show that
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)(1^{-})>0
. This fact is just an application of the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f
be a solution of (

\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}z^{1-n}f(z)=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}z^{-n}f(z)>0.

Then, it satisfies

for all

Proof.

A more general proof of this fact has been postponed to Section

, see Lemma
4.12
.
∎

Then we can redefine
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)
as
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)/\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(1^{-})
to produce a solution of (
53
) which satisfies (
54

q_{1}(1)=0
, the coefficient

in (

) has to be different from zero. Thus,
\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}(z)
has a singularity of the type
(z-1)\log(1-z)
at

.

Uniqueness follows from Lemma

and expression (

between them satisfies the equation with boundary conditions

.
Therefore, by Lemma

, we would find that

.

4.1.3.
Back to the original variable

We now revert the change of variables and return to the original formulation of the equation. That is, for
x\in(1,+\infty)
, we define

\mathring{h}_{R}(x):=\mathring{\mathsf{h}}_{R}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{g}_{1}(x):=q_{1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right).

Then, taking the Wronskiano

To close this section, we establish a uniform estimate for

that characterizes its asymptotic behavior as

Lemma 4.4
.

The function
\tilde{g}_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|x^{-n}\tilde{g}_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}([1,+\infty))}\leq C,

where
C
denotes a universal constant.

Proof.

We first notice that
\|\tilde{g}_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}([1,x_{0}])}\leq C(x_{0})
for a finite constant
C(x_{0})
and
x_{0}>1
. The function

satisfies (

\displaystyle g(x)=Ax^{n}+Bx^{-n}+\frac{x^{n}}{2n}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}z^{-n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(z)}{z^{2}}g(z)dz-\frac{x^{-n}}{2n}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}z^{n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(z)}{z^{2}}g(z)dz,

for
x_{0}>1
and some constants
A=A(x_{0})
,
B=B(x_{0})
(
|A|,\,|B|<\infty
for
x_{0}<\infty
). Let us take

We obtain that

\displaystyle G(x)=A+Bx^{-2n}+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}z\frac{\Psi_{0}(z)}{z^{2}}G(z)dz-\frac{x^{-2n}}{2n}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}z^{2n+1}\frac{\Psi_{0}(z)}{z^{2}}G(z)dz.

Thus

\displaystyle\|G\|_{L^{\infty}([x_{0},+\infty))}\leq|A|+|B|+\|G\|_{L^{\infty}([x_{0},\infty))}\left\|x\frac{\Psi_{0}(x)}{x^{2}}\right\|_{L^{1}([x_{0},+\infty))}.

Since
\Psi_{0}(x)/x
is integrable in

for

, we have that
\left\|x\frac{\Psi_{0}(x)}{x^{2}}\right\|_{L^{1}([x_{0},+\infty))}
is

when

goes to infinity. Thus, taking

large enough, we can bound

.
∎

4.2.
The limiting ODE in the left

In this paragraph, we are going to study

(60)

\mathring{h}_{L}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}\mathring{h}_{L}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(n^{2}-\frac{x\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x)}{\lambda_{0}-c_{L}(x,0)}\right)\mathring{h}_{L}(x)=0,\qquad\text{for }x\in(0,1),

The main results of this section are the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.5
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
0\leq\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and let

that satisfies

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{1-n}f(x)=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{-n}f(x)>0.

Then, taking

big enough, it satisfies
f(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,x_{L}^{\ast}].

Proof.

In order to proceed, we start writing the above in a more convenient way as follows

f^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{x}f^{\prime}(x)-\frac{n^{2}}{x^{2}}f(x)=\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(x)}{x^{2}}f(x),

where (we write
\lambda\equiv\lambda(\varepsilon)
to make the dependence on
\varepsilon
explicit)

\Psi_{\varepsilon}(x):=-\frac{x(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(x(1-\varepsilon/2))}{\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(x(1-\varepsilon/2))}.

We find that the general solution is given by

f(x)=Ax^{n}+Bx^{-n}-\frac{1}{x^{n}}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}{z^{2}}f(z)z^{1+n}dz+x^{n}\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}{z^{2}}f(z)z^{1-n}dz.

Because the boundary condition at zero, and since
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)/z^{2}=O(1)
, the first integral behaves like
x^{2n+2}
and the second one like
x^{2}
. Therefore,
B
has to be equal to
0
. We can then write

(65)

\displaystyle f(x)=Ax^{n}+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}{z}f(z)\left[\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n}\right]dz,

where
A>0
to get
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{-n}f(x)>0
. Let us take
A=1
for simplicity.

Iterating once this formula, we have the following expression for
f(x)
:

\displaystyle f(x)=x^{n}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}{z}z^{n}\left[\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n}\right]dz

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}{z}\left(\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(y)}{y}f(y)\left[\left(\frac{z}{y}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)^{n}\right]dy\right)\left[\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n}\right]dz.

Since
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(x)<0
for all
x\in(0,x_{L}^{\ast}]
, and

\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n}\geq 0,\qquad 0<z\leq x,

we observe that the last integral term on the right-hand side is non-negative whenever
f(x)>0
.

We know by hypotheis that
f
is positive near the origin. Thus, as long as
f(x)>0
, which is guaranteed near the origin and for
x\in(0,x_{L}^{\ast}]
, we can neglect the last (complicated) term in the expression for
f
to obtain a simpler lower bound:

(66)

\displaystyle f(x)>x^{n}+\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}{z}z^{n}\left[\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n}\right]\,dz.

This reduced inequality no longer depends on
f
, and its right-hand side is an explicit expression that is considerably simpler to analyze. Therefore, the proof reduces to verifying that this lower bound remains strictly positive for
x\in(0,x_{L}^{\ast}]
.

Recalling the definition of
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
, we have to check that

x^{n}-\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{z(1-\varepsilon/2)\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(z(1-\varepsilon/2))}{\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(z(1-\varepsilon/2)}z^{n-1}\left[\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n}\right]dz>0,\qquad\text{for }x\in(0,x_{L}^{\ast}].

Firstly, we consider the change of variable
z(1-\varepsilon/2)=\bar{z}
and introduce the auxiliary notation
x(1-\varepsilon/2)=\bar{x}
. Thus, we can just write (after multiplying all the expression by
(1-\varepsilon/2)^{n}
)

\bar{x}^{n}-\frac{1}{2n}\frac{1}{\bar{x}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\bar{x}}\frac{\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(\bar{z})}\left(\bar{x}^{2n}-\bar{z}^{2n}\right)d\bar{z}>0,\qquad\text{for }\bar{x}\in(0,(1-\varepsilon/2)x_{L}^{\ast}].

By definition,
x_{L}^{\ast}
is a simple zero of
\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(\cdot(1-\varepsilon/2))
. Thus, introducing
z_{L}^{\ast}:=(1-\varepsilon/2)x_{L}^{\ast}
gives us

\frac{\bar{z}-z_{L}^{\ast}}{\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(\bar{z})}\leq C_{\varepsilon},

with
C_{\varepsilon}=O(1)
. This implies (using also that
(\bar{z}-z_{L}^{\ast})^{-1}\geq(\bar{z}-\bar{x})^{-1}
as
\bar{z}\in(0,\bar{x})
)

0\leq\int_{0}^{\bar{x}}\frac{\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(\bar{z})}\left(\bar{x}^{2n}-\bar{z}^{2n}\right)d\bar{z}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\bar{x}}\frac{\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\bar{z}-\bar{x}}(\bar{x}^{2n}-\bar{z}^{2n})d\bar{z}.

Moreover, using the rough but sufficient estimate
0\geq\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{z})\geq-2/3
and the change of variable
\bar{z}=\xi\bar{x}
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\bar{x}}\frac{\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\bar{z}-\bar{x}}(\bar{x}^{2n}-\bar{z}^{2n})\,d\bar{z}\leq\frac{2}{3}\bar{x}^{2n}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-\xi^{2n}}{1-\xi}\,d\xi\leq\frac{2}{3}\bar{x}^{2n}\left(\log(2n)+\gamma+O(n^{-1})\right),

where in the last step we have used an upper bound for the
2n
-th harmonic number, and where
\gamma
denotes the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

All the above, allows us to say that there exist
n^{\ast}\gg 1
such that for all
n>n^{\ast}
we have

\bar{x}^{n}-\frac{1}{2n}\frac{1}{\bar{x}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\bar{x}}\frac{\varpi_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{z})}{\lambda(\varepsilon)+c(\bar{z})}\left(\bar{x}^{2n}-\bar{z}^{2n}\right)d\bar{z}\\
\geq\bar{x}^{n}\left[1-\frac{C_{\varepsilon}}{n}\left(\log(2n)+\gamma+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\right]>0,\qquad\text{for }\bar{x}\in(0,(1-\varepsilon/2)x_{L}^{\ast}].

∎
