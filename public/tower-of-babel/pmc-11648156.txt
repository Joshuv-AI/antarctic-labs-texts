Orthopantomogram teeth segmentation and numbering dataset
Authors: Niha Adnan, Fahad Umer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the digitization of radiographs, vast amounts of data have become accessible, enabling the curation and development of extensive datasets. Among radiographic modalities, Orthopantomograms (OPGs) are widely utilized in clinical practice. The integration of automated diagnostic processes into routine clinical practice holds great potential as an adjunct for dentists.Various OPG datasets exist, however their limitations affect the robustness of Artificial Intelligence (AI) models trained on them. This paper introduces an OPG dataset specifically designed for training AI algorithms in teeth segmentation and numbering tasks. A key feature of this dataset is its dual annotation, which allows for individual tooth segmentation by class, as well as numbering according to the Fédération Dentaire Internationale system.This dual-annotated dataset enhances the existing pool of OPG datasets and can be leveraged for further training of pre-trained algorithms or the development of new ones. Moreover, it offers researchers to carry out annotations tailored to their respective research objectives, thereby facilitating the development of AI models capable of addressing diverse diagnostic tasks.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Dataset, Healthcare industry, Big data, Healthcare research, Panoramic radiography, Annotation

Background

For robust AI models to be developed in healthcare, especially dentistry, certain shortcomings need to be addressed. One such need is that of large-scale, fully annotated datasets to enable training of AI models in dental radiographic diagnoses [1]. Researchers in the field of computer vision recognize this challenge, and over the years, several open-sourced OPG datasets have been made available to facilitate the advancement of AI [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]].

A limitation of existing datasets lies in the type of annotations used to localize anatomical structures. Image annotation can be performed using various techniques, with the three most common being object detection, semantic segmentation, and instance segmentation [7,8]. In the object detection method, bounding boxes are employed, which inadvertently include background pixels within the region of interest [7]. To address this issue, segmentation is employed, using polygons to precisely delineate regions of interest [9]. Segmentation can be further categorized into semantic and instance segmentation techniques [7]. In the former, all teeth are grouped under a single label, such as "tooth," without distinguishing between different types. Instance segmentation resolves this limitation by allowing each tooth to be classified according to its specific type, such as molar, premolar, central incisor, etc. [7].

Various large-scale OPG datasets are available for aspiring researchers to leverage their AI algorithms, however they are limited by certain shortcomings. The datasets curated by Muramatsu et al., Tuzoff et al., and Beetul Oktay et al, for example, use bounding boxes to annotate teeth or jaw segments, inadvertently incorporating redundant background pixels [4,5,10]. Koch et al. and Silva et al. addressed the issue of background inclusion by employing semantic segmentation technique [11,12]. This method uses polygons to distinguish teeth from the background; however, these datasets do not classify teeth into distinct categories. The datasets by Jader et al. and Silva et al. address the issue of classification by employing the instance segmentation technique, which identifies each tooth as a distinct class [2,13]. Nevertheless, both datasets rely on images obtained from a single machine, resulting in a lack of variation in the dataset. Pinheiro et al. introduced a dataset that employs instance segmentation while also identifying teeth according to the FDI numbering system. However, as this dataset builds upon the existing dataset by Silva et al., the issue of limited heterogeneity persists [11,14].

To address the limitations of existing datasets, the authors combined images from some publicly available datasets with self-sourced images. The teeth in these images were annotated using the instance segmentation technique, enabling identification based on both the FDI numbering system and tooth class. Further details on this approach are provided in the following sections.

Data Description

OPGs of the permanent dentition were included in the dataset, while images with mixed dentition were excluded. The annotations include instance segmentation of teeth using polygons with the labels according to tooth class such as molar, premolar, canine, lateral incisor and central incisor. Additionally, every tooth has also been annotated according to the FDI numbering system. This dual annotation approach provides researchers with the flexibility to use either label type, depending on the specific task at hand.

The dataset, hosted on Zenodo (external source: GitHub), contains three folders with a total of 250 anonymized OPGs, sourced from three datasets. Folders 1–3 are organized by image resolution and each contains subfolders with the corresponding OPGs and their annotations. All images in the dataset feature permanent dentition only. The annotations were performed using the LabelMe annotation software [15]. Annotations for all images are provided in JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) format, stored in a separate folder with corresponding identifiers. This widely accepted format is commonly used for training AI models and can easily be converted to other formats as needed. Detailed information on the annotation types is beyond the scope of this paper and can be found elsewhere [7].

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The curation of a heterogenous dataset is a resource-intensive task [16]. There is a growing need for widely accessible datasets with optimal annotations to enable researchers to focus their cognitive resources on conducting experiments that yield impactful results. The dataset presented in this paper was curated to fulfill this need and serve as an enhancement to existing open-sourced datasets. In addition to this primary use, this dataset can also be employed for pre-training algorithms with new initialization parameters. It can be used to evaluate the performance of other pre-trained algorithms, with the weights from these models being imported to further refine existing algorithms. Furthermore, the dataset is open to additional annotation, allowing researchers to customize it for specific needs or explore new findings.

The authors utilized this dataset to train a multi-model for teeth segmentation and numbering, as outlined in a previously published paper [17]. Initial results were based on a smaller dataset of 50 OPGs, but an additional 250 OPGs have since been incorporated, and the models were re-trained using the expanded dataset [18]. A U-Net algorithm was trained to accurately segment teeth according to their respective tooth classes, while a Faster R-CNN algorithm was employed to correctly number teeth according to the FDI numbering system, making use of the dual annotations. These algorithms were then combined to perform both tasks simultaneously [18]. The authors referenced this preliminary dataset of 300 radiographs to support researchers aiming to implement AI in dental diagnosis, providing annotations for models capable of segmenting and numbering teeth in OPGs. Segmenting and numbering teeth is a crucial first step in developing an AI model that can analyze all findings within an OPG, potentially leading to the deployment of the model as a software application.

Future Work

The authors recommend expanding the current dataset by incorporating additional OPG images from various other sources. Furthermore, they envision this dataset being used to annotate other findings on OPGs, such as decay, fillings, crowns, etc. in order to train an AI model capable of identifying all potential findings within these images. To mitigate the limited size of the current dataset, the authors suggest applying augmentation techniques [9,17,18].

Limitations

The limitations of the dataset include its relatively small size and the lack of annotations for other findings on OPGs. Additionally, the authors resized all images to 512×512 pixels to minimize the computational resources required for training.
