Development and internal validation of a clinical nomogram incorporating quantitative CT features for predicting malignancy in pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm
Authors: Yingding Ruan, Yujie Ma, Jianwei Han, Chuan Long, Wenjun Cao, Aiming Yang, Peng Sun, Ting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate differentiation between benign and malignant pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm remains a clinical challenge. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a clinically interpretable nomogram integrating clinical variables and quantitative computed tomography (CT) features for predicting malignancy in pulmonary nodules.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 1,419 patients with pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm who underwent surgical resection between January 2012 and July 2025 with pathologic confirmation. The cohort was randomly divided into a training set (n = 994) for model development and a validation set (n = 425) for internal validation. Clinical data, conventional imaging findings, serum biomarkers, and quantitative CT measurements from preoperative thin-section CT were collected. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify variables associated with malignancy and construct the nomogram.ResultsAmong the 1,419 nodules, 1,150 (81.0%) were malignant and 269 (19.0%) were benign. The final nomogram incorporated seven variables: suspicious radiologic features, nodule size, sex, symptoms at detection, consolidation-to-tumor ratio, minimum CT attenuation, and age. Age was retained in the final model on clinical grounds despite lacking statistical significance in multivariable analysis. Suspicious radiologic features (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 6.61, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 4.51–9.84; P < 0.001), nodule diameter > 2 cm (aOR = 4.07, 95% CI: 2.16–7.62; P < 0.001), female sex (aOR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.23–2.33; P = 0.001), asymptomatic presentation (aOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.34–0.69; P < 0.001), consolidation-to-tumor ratio > 0.50 (aOR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.61; P = 0.005), and minimum CT attenuation per 100-HU increase (aOR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.74–0.92; P < 0.001) were independently associated with malignancy. The nomogram showed good discrimination, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values of 0.809 in the training set and 0.782 in the validation set. Calibration analysis showed agreement between predicted and observed risks, and decision curve analysis supported usefulness.ConclusionsWe developed and internally validated a clinical nomogram incorporating quantitative CT features for malignancy risk estimation in surgically resected pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm. The model showed good discrimination, calibration, and potential utility in a malignancy-enriched preoperative cohort. External validation in broader, less selected, screening-detected, incidental, and multicenter populations is warranted before routine clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02490-1.Keywords: Pulmonary nodules, Computed tomography, Nomogram, Malignancy prediction, Risk stratification

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, primarily due to delayed diagnosis at advanced stages [1, 2]. The widespread implementation of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) for lung cancer screening has markedly increased the detection of indeterminate pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm, presenting a critical diagnostic challenge [3, 4]. Accurate discrimination between malignant and benign nodules at this early stage is critical for facilitating curative interventions for early-stage malignancies while avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures for benign lesions.

Conventional diagnostic approaches largely depend on subjective interpretation of morphological features, such as spiculation, lobulation, and solid components, guided by frameworks like the Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) [5]. However, these methods are constrained by inter-observer variability and limited accuracy in individualized risk stratification [6, 7]. To address these limitations, increasing attention has been directed toward quantitative image analysis and computer-assisted approaches for pulmonary nodule evaluation. Recent studies suggest that these methods can provide more objective imaging assessment and may improve malignancy risk stratification in selected settings [8–10].

Despite these advances, currently available prediction models remain heterogeneous in design, input variables, and validation quality, and many have not yet been translated into tools that are readily applicable in routine clinical practice [11, 12]. In addition, some models incorporating advanced imaging features have been derived from relatively small cohorts or are not readily implemented in routine practice. Furthermore, systemic factors, such as diabetes and chronic inflammation, have been implicated in carcinogenesis [13–15], yet their incremental diagnostic value in distinguishing malignant from benign small pulmonary nodules remains controversial and inadequately explored [16, 17]. To address this gap, we conducted a comprehensive analysis incorporating both imaging and systemic clinical data. Specifically, we evaluated the diagnostic contributions of diabetes status, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and inflammatory biomarkers, including the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), within an integrated predictive modeling framework.

Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to develop and validate a clinically applicable nomogram that integrates key clinical variables with quantitative CT features to provide individualized malignancy risk estimation for pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted at the First People’s Hospital of Jiande. We reviewed consecutive patients who underwent surgical resection for pulmonary nodules between January 2012 and July 2025. Accordingly, the study population represents a surgically treated cohort with pathologic confirmation rather than an unselected screening population. All patients underwent preoperative high-resolution chest CT with a slice thickness of 1.25–1.5 mm, performed in the supine position during full inspiratory breath-hold. Throughout the inclusion period, thin-section CT acquisition was required for eligibility, and only scans with a slice thickness of ≤ 1.5 mm obtained within one month before surgery were included to reduce heterogeneity in image acquisition. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of First People’s Hospital of Jiande (Approval No. 20251021-KY-04-001). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective design.

Eligible patients were between 18 and 80 years of age, had a definitive histopathological diagnosis following resection, and possessed thin-slice (≤ 1.5 mm) CT scans acquired within one month prior to surgery. Complete clinical records and preoperative peripheral blood samples obtained within seven days before surgery were also required. Patients were excluded if they had received preoperative anti-tumor therapies (such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or ablation), had active systemic infections or autoimmune diseases, were pregnant or lactating, underwent multiple or repeat thoracic surgeries within a one-month interval, or had histopathological confirmation of non-primary lung malignancies, such as metastatic lesions.

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and the presence of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, were extracted from electronic medical records. Peripheral venous blood samples collected within seven days prior to surgery were used to calculate systemic inflammatory biomarkers. Specifically, the NLR, LMR, and PLR were derived from absolute cell counts. The SII was calculated using the formula: SII = (platelet count × neutrophil count) / lymphocyte count.

CT image acquisition and interpretation

All preoperative CT images were reviewed in DICOM format. Quantitative CT measurements, including nodule volume, mass, and maximum and minimum Hounsfield unit (HU) attenuation values, were extracted using the Infervision platform (InferRead, Beijing, China). This platform was used solely to obtain predefined quantitative CT measurements and was not developed, retrained, or validated as a malignancy prediction model in the present study. In addition, all images were independently reviewed by a thoracic surgeon and a thoracic radiologist, both blinded to pathological outcomes and clinical/laboratory information, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Qualitative imaging features were annotated based on definitions provided by the Fleischner Society [18, 19].

Nodule classification and morphologic assessment

Nodule density

Pulmonary nodules were classified into pure ground-glass nodules (pGGNs), part-solid nodules (PSNs), and solid nodules (SNs) according to established radiological criteria [18, 20]. pGGNs were defined by focal, hazy, increased attenuation without obscuration of underlying bronchial structures or pulmonary vessels. PSNs contained both ground-glass and solid soft-tissue components. Solid nodules (SNs) exhibited homogeneous soft-tissue attenuation that completely obscured the underlying lung parenchyma.

Consolidation-to-tumor ratio

Consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) was categorized based on previous studies linking this metric to pathological invasiveness [21, 22]. CTR was classified as < 0.01, 0.01–0.50, and > 0.50 for analysis.

Morphological features

Key morphological features were assessed. Lobulation was characterized by rounded, scalloped margins. Spiculation was identified as linear strands radiating from the nodule margin into adjacent lung parenchyma. Pleural retraction was defined by a linear connection from the nodule to the pleural surface, resulting in pleural indentation. Cavitation was recorded when an air-filled space was identified within the nodule [5, 23]. For the present analysis, “suspicious radiologic features” were defined as the presence of one or more of the following findings: lobulation, spiculation, pleural retraction, or cavitation. Definitions of these suspicious radiologic features are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 22.0) and R software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The entire cohort was randomly divided into a training set (70%) for model development and an internal validation set (30%) using stratified randomization. The stratification was based on pathological diagnosis (malignant or benign) to ensure a balanced outcome distribution between the two sets.

To assess collinearity among predictors, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was conducted, with a VIF > 10 indicating severe multicollinearity. Nodule radiographic appearance was excluded from multivariable analysis due to high collinearity with CTR (VIF: 22.24).

Candidate predictors with a P-value < 0.10 in univariate logistic regression were considered for inclusion in multivariable modeling. Age was additionally retained in the final multivariable model a priori because of its established clinical relevance in pulmonary nodule risk assessment and to preserve model interpretability. To enhance clinical interpretability and stabilize the regression coefficients, several continuous variables were scaled to clinically relevant units: Max and Min CT Attenuation (per 100 HU), Nodule Volume (per 500 mm³), Nodule Area (per 200 mm2), Nodule Mass (per 200 mg), SII (per 100 units), and PLR (per 10 units). Variables were categorized only when clinically established thresholds or radiologic classification schemes were available (e.g., nodule size and consolidation-to-tumor ratio); otherwise, continuous variables were modeled after scaling to clinically interpretable units.

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA). To further assess clinical interpretability, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy were calculated at selected risk thresholds of 30%, 50%, and 70%, as well as at the Youden-derived threshold determined in the training cohort. The final nomogram was derived from the multivariable logistic regression model and internally validated in the randomly split validation cohort. Because eligible patients were required to have complete data for the variables of interest, no imputation for missing data was performed. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 1,419 patients with pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm who underwent surgical resection at the First People’s Hospital of Jiande between January 2012 and July 2025 were enrolled in this study. The overall malignancy rate was 81.0% (n = 1,150), while the remaining 19.0% (n = 269) were benign based on histopathological diagnosis. The cohort was randomly divided into a training set (n = 994, 70.0%) and a validation set (n = 425, 30.0%) for model development and internal validation, respectively. The mean age of the entire cohort was 59.9 ± 11.0 years, and females comprised 61.9% (n = 879) of the study population. Most nodules (77.9%, n = 1,105) were incidentally detected in asymptomatic individuals. Baseline demographic, clinical, and radiological characteristics were comparable between the training and validation sets (Table 1).

Comparison of clinicopathological characteristics between benign and malignant pulmonary nodules [n (%), Mean ± SD, M(P25, P75)]

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics: Malignant nodules were significantly more common among female patients (64.5% vs. 50.9%; P < 0.001) and in individuals who were asymptomatic at the time of detection (80.4% vs. 66.9%; P < 0.001). Additionally, the median time interval from detection to surgical resection was significantly shorter in the malignant group (P = 0.001).

Radiological Characteristics: Malignant nodules more frequently exhibited ground-glass (37.0% vs. 23.1%) and part-solid (37.3% vs. 20.5%) appearances, whereas benign nodules were predominantly solid (25.7% vs. 56.5%; P < 0.001). A CTR > 0.50 was more commonly observed in benign lesions (63.9% vs. 36.5%; P < 0.001). Moreover, suspicious radiologic features were significantly more prevalent among malignant nodules (64.3% vs. 35.3%; P < 0.001).

Inflammatory Biomarkers: Patients with malignant nodules had lower median SII values (422 vs. 490, P < 0.001) and NLR values (2.4 vs. 2.7, P = 0.002) compared with those with benign lesions.

Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses of predictors of pulmonary nodule malignancy

To identify predictors of malignancy in pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm, a logistic regression analysis was conducted using a structured, multi-phase approach. Initial screening for candidate variables was performed in the full cohort (n = 1,419), followed by model development in the training set and evaluation in the validation set, respectively.

Variable screening in the entire cohort

Initial univariate logistic regression analysis of the entire cohort (n = 1,419) identified numerous variables associated with malignancy (Supplementary Table S2). To ensure a robust selection of predictors, only variables with a P-value < 0.10 were considered candidate predictors for multivariable modeling, while age was additionally retained a priori on clinical grounds. The following variables met this criterion: female sex (odds ratio [OR] = 1.75; 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 1.34–2.29; P < 0.001), asymptomatic presentation (OR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.37–0.66; P < 0.001), presence of suspicious radiologic features (OR = 3.29; 95% CI: 2.50–4.36; P < 0.001), nodule diameter > 2 cm (OR = 2.37; 95% CI: 1.60–3.55; P < 0.001), CTR > 0.50 (OR = 0.35; 95% CI: 0.25–0.48; P < 0.001), and minimum CT attenuation per 100 HU increase (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.74–0.84; P < 0.001).

These candidate variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Collinearity among predictors was evaluated using VIF analysis, and the following variables were independently associated with malignancy in the multivariable model (Supplementary Table S2): the presence of suspicious radiologic features (adjusted OR [aOR] = 6.61; 95% CI: 4.51–9.84; P < 0.001), nodule diameter > 2 cm (aOR = 4.07; 95% CI: 2.16–7.62; P < 0.001), female sex (aOR = 1.69; 95% CI: 1.23–2.33; P = 0.001), asymptomatic presentation (aOR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.34–0.69; P < 0.001), CTR > 0.50 (aOR = 0.20; 95% CI: 0.06–0.61; P = 0.005), and minimum CT attenuation per 100 HU increase (aOR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.74–0.92; P < 0.001).

Model development in the training set

Logistic regression analysis in the training set (n = 994) was conducted to identify independent predictors of malignancy in pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm (Supplementary Table S3). In univariate analysis, malignancy was significantly associated with female sex (OR = 1.93; 95% CI: 1.40–2.66; P < 0.001), the presence of suspicious radiologic features (OR = 3.14; 95% CI: 2.27–4.39; P < 0.001), and nodule diameter > 2 cm (OR = 2.71; 95% CI: 1.67–4.53; P < 0.001). Conversely, asymptomatic presentation (OR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.35–0.70; P < 0.001), minimum CT attenuation per 100 HU increase (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.74–0.84; P < 0.001), and a CTR > 0.50 (OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.23–0.50; P < 0.001) were significantly associated with benign pathology.

Significant variables from the univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. After addressing multicollinearity through VIF analysis, the final multivariable model included sex, age, symptoms at detection, suspicious radiologic features, CTR, minimum CT attenuation, and nodule size (Supplementary Table S3). Independent predictors of malignancy included female sex (aOR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.29–2.78; P = 0.001), asymptomatic presentation (aOR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.28–0.67; P < 0.001), presence of suspicious radiologic features (aOR = 5.83; 95% CI: 3.75–9.25; P < 0.001), and nodule diameter > 2 cm and ≤ 3 cm (aOR = 6.26; 95% CI: 2.72–14.04; P < 0.001). A CTR > 0.50 was a strong independent negative predictor (aOR = 0.16; 95% CI: 0.04–0.66; P = 0.011). Although age did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.213), it was retained in the final model in accordance with the prespecified modeling strategy.

Validation in the validation set

The final model was evaluated in the independent validation cohort (n = 425) (Supplementary Table S4). Univariate associations were largely consistent with those observed in the training set. In the multivariable model, the presence of suspicious radiologic features remained the most powerful predictor of malignancy (aOR = 11.40; 95% CI: 5.28–26.80; P < 0.001). Age showed a significant association in the validation multivariable model (aOR = 1.03 per year; 95% CI: 1.00–1.06; P = 0.033); however, age was retained in the final model primarily on the basis of the prespecified clinical rationale rather than validation-set significance alone.

Final prediction model

A final multivariable logistic regression model was constructed based on key predictors identified in the training set, with selection guided by both statistical significance and clinical relevance (Table 3). The model included the following variables: female sex (aOR = 1.90; P < 0.001), asymptomatic presentation (aOR = 0.43; P < 0.001), CTR > 0.50 (aOR = 0.20; P < 0.001), presence of suspicious radiologic features (aOR = 6.54; P < 0.001), and nodule diameter > 1 cm and ≤ 2 cm (aOR = 1.65, P = 0.027) and > 2 cm and ≤ 3 cm (aOR = 7.55, P < 0.001). Although not statistically significant in the training set, age was retained in the final model to preserve clinical interpretability and consistency with the prespecified modeling strategy. Minimum CT attenuation was also included in the final model (aOR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.69–0.88; P < 0.001).

Model fit and overall performance

The prediction model was derived using the training cohort (n = 994; 805 malignant, 189 benign). Model fit was statistically significant, with a likelihood ratio chi-square value of 222.45 (degrees of freedom = 9, P < 0.001). Performance metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. The model achieved a generalized R² of 0.322 and a Brier score of 0.112, indicating good explanatory power and prediction accuracy.

Discrimination and validation

The model exhibited high discriminatory performance in both training and validation sets. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis yielded an AUC of 0.809 (95% CI: 0.772–0.847) in the training set and 0.782 (95% CI: 0.721–0.842) in the validation set (Fig. 1). The concordance index (C-index) of 0.809 and Somers’ Dxy of 0.619 further supported the model’s ability to differentiate malignant from benign pulmonary nodules.

Model calibration assessment

The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted malignancy probabilities and observed outcomes (Fig. 2). The bias-corrected curve, representing the model’s performance on new data, closely aligned with the ideal reference line across the full range of predicted probabilities. The mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and observed probabilities was 0.025, indicating good calibration accuracy and minimal deviation between predicted and observed risks.

Clinical utility assessment

DCA was performed to evaluate the net clinical benefit of the model across a range of high-risk thresholds (Fig. 3). The prediction model outperformed both the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies at threshold probabilities between approximately 10% and 70%. These findings support the model’s potential value in clinical decision-making scenarios where malignancy probability influences intervention thresholds.

The nomogram’s decision curves consistently yielded greater net benefit than the default treat-all or treat-none strategies within the clinically relevant range of approximately 10% to 70%, supporting its potential clinical utility

Threshold-based diagnostic performance

To improve clinical interpretability, threshold-based diagnostic performance was further evaluated at selected risk thresholds. As shown in Supplementary Table S6, at the 50% risk threshold, the nomogram achieved a sensitivity of 97.5%, specificity of 36.0%, PPV of 86.6%, NPV of 77.3%, and accuracy of 85.8% in the training cohort. In the validation cohort, the corresponding values were 97.4%, 25.0%, 84.8%, 69.0%, and 83.8%, respectively. At the Youden-derived threshold of 0.780, sensitivity and specificity were 81.4% and 68.8% in the training cohort and 80.9% and 62.5% in the validation cohort, respectively.

Clinical implementation nomogram

To facilitate individualized risk estimation in clinical practice, we developed a points-based nomogram based on the final multivariable model (Fig. 4). This nomogram incorporated key predictors, including patient sex, age, symptom status, CTR, radiologic features, minimum CT attenuation, and nodule diameter. Each predictor was assigned a weighted point value, and the total score corresponds to an estimated probability of malignancy, ranging from < 5% to > 90%. This tool enables individualized malignancy risk estimation.

Nomogram for predicting malignancy risk in pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm. This nomogram provides a practical tool for estimating the probability of malignancy in pulmonary nodules. To use the tool, the clinician first identifies the patient’s value for each predictor and locates it along the corresponding axis. A vertical line is then drawn from each predictor value to the Points line to determine the individual score. After assigning scores for all predictors, the points are summed to obtain a total score, which is then projected downward to the Predicted Probability axis to estimate individualized malignancy risk

Discussion

Accurately distinguishing malignant from benign pulmonary nodules measuring ≤ 3 cm remains a significant clinical challenge with direct implications for subsequent management strategies and patient outcomes. In this study, we developed and internally validated a clinically applicable nomogram that integrates clinical variables with quantitative CT features to predict malignancy risk in these nodules. Using a large cohort of 1,419 patients who underwent surgical resection, we identified multiple independent predictors of malignancy, including suspicious radiologic features, nodule diameter > 2 cm, female sex, asymptomatic presentation, CTR > 0.50, and lower minimum CT attenuation. The final nomogram demonstrated good discriminative performance, with AUCs of 0.809 in the training set and 0.782 in the validation set. Calibration analysis showed good alignment between predicted and observed risks (slope = 1.000, intercept = 0.000), while DCA confirmed net clinical benefit over a wide range of probability thresholds. Taken together, these findings suggest that the nomogram may provide a practical tool for individualized malignancy risk estimation in surgically selected patients with small pulmonary nodules.

A key strength of our model lies in the incorporation of objective, software-extracted quantitative features, notably the minimum CT attenuation. This variable, which independently predicted malignancy (aOR = 0.82 per 100 HU increase), represents a dimension of radiologic information not reliably captured by conventional visual assessment. Its inclusion underscores the potential of quantitative CT analysis to supplement traditional features with objective, reproducible image-based biomarkers. By quantifying objective imaging characteristics such as tissue density, the model addresses known limitations in inter-observer variability in lung nodule interpretation [6, 7] and aligns with current evidence highlighting the value of quantitative imaging features in pulmonary nodule assessment [8, 9, 24]. The robust performance of minimum CT attenuation as a malignancy predictor also reinforces its relevance in distinguishing invasive behavior in both solid and subsolid nodules [21, 24]. Integrating this metric into a clinician-friendly nomogram bridges the divide between quantitative image measurement and practical, point-of-care clinical decision-making, a translational step whose clinical impact is increasingly being recognized [25].

The robust predictive performance of our nomogram is underpinned by several key independent predictors, each with a plausible biological and clinical rationale. The most influential predictor was the presence of suspicious radiologic features (aOR = 6.54), a composite variable encompassing spiculation, lobulation, and pleural retraction. These morphological characteristics are widely recognized markers of tumor invasiveness and angiogenesis, as defined by the Fleischner Society [18, 19]. The critical value of such qualitative assessment is further corroborated by their inclusion in validated risk models, such as the Brock University model, which relies on similar reader-assigned features for malignancy prediction in pulmonary nodules [6]. The CTR also emerged as a significant radiologic predictor. A CTR > 0.50 was independently associated with a lower likelihood of malignancy (aOR = 0.20), which contrasts with findings from Suzuki et al. [21], who demonstrated that higher CTR values were linked to invasiveness in adenocarcinomas. In our broader, histologically heterogeneous cohort, a high CTR may also reflect benign inflammatory or organizing processes, such as focal organizing pneumonia, that are known to radiologically mimic lung cancer [26]. This diagnostic overlap is clinically relevant, as highlighted by Archer et al. [27], who found that a significant number of benign nodules resected in screening settings exhibited imaging features typically associated with malignancy. Among clinical variables, female sex was independently associated with increased malignancy risk (aOR = 1.90), consistent with emerging epidemiological data indicating a rising incidence of lung cancer in women and suggesting possible biological and exposure-related differences in risk profiles [1, 13]. In contrast, symptomatic presentation at the time of detection was inversely associated with malignancy (aOR = 0.43), reflecting findings from low-dose CT (LDCT) screening trials, such as NLST [3] and the NELSON trial [28], which predominantly detect asymptomatic, early-stage malignancies. The integration of clinically and radiologically meaningful variables with quantitative CT measurements may improve risk estimation while reducing subjectivity in image interpretation [4, 8].

When contextualized within the landscape of existing pulmonary nodule risk prediction models, our nomogram demonstrates several key methodological improvements. Existing models, such as the Brock University model, provide a crucial foundation for malignancy risk stratification [6], but their reliance on reader-dependent semantic features inherently limits objectivity and reproducibility. More recently, computer-assisted and machine-learning-based models have shown promising predictive performance. For instance, Piskorski et al. [29] compared a deep-learning model directly with multiple established statistical models and found a significant performance advantage. Similarly, Li et al. [30] specifically evaluated an AI model (Sybil) against the Brock model in patients with persistent nodules, confirming the incremental predictive value of AI-based imaging analyses. Beyond standalone performance, the synergy of combining quantitative imaging with clinical models presents a particularly promising pathway. Nevertheless, many such models remain constrained by narrow diagnostic scopes, limited interpretability, or insufficient clinical usability. For example, Wang et al. [31] developed a deep‑learning system for predicting nodule progression on serial imaging, and Wu et al. [24] reported a high‑accuracy radiomic classifier for invasive adenocarcinoma, but neither was presented as a general-purpose, clinically accessible nomogram for overall malignancy risk assessment. Hunter et al. [32] demonstrated that a radiomics-based decision support tool significantly improved diagnostic accuracy when used in combination with the Herder score, a finding that supports an integrative approach to malignancy risk assessment. Our model contributes to this integrative paradigm by combining validated clinical variables with quantitative CT measurements in a point-based nomogram. Its main advantages include focusing on the diagnostically challenging subset of nodules ≤ 3 cm, improving measurement objectivity through standardized quantitative image extraction, and enabling rapid estimation of individualized malignancy probability at the point of care [4, 8].

The clinical performance of our nomogram suggests potential utility in improving patient management and supporting guideline-concordant care. DCA demonstrated a high net clinical benefit across a range of relevant risk thresholds, supporting its utility in reducing unnecessary invasive procedures—a burden previously quantified by Archer et al. [27], who reported that a substantial proportion of resected pulmonary nodules ultimately proved benign. Similarly, Maldonado et al. [33] found that radiomic risk stratification using the BRODERS classifier could have avoided 36.4% of benign resections without compromising diagnostic sensitivity. Beyond procedural reduction, our model standardizes malignancy risk assessment, thereby mitigating practice variation across providers. Adams et al. [25] demonstrated that computer-assisted decision tools altered clinical management in over 30% of cases, underscoring their potential to accelerate the diagnosis of early-stage lung cancers, where timely intervention is critical for improving survival outcomes. Moreover, the psychological morbidity associated with indeterminate nodules—often termed “scanxiety”—is increasingly recognized as a clinically relevant outcome. Studies have shown that patients often experience significant anxiety and uncertainty due to unclear risk communication and limited understanding of their condition [34]. Effective, quantitative risk communication has thus been advocated as a key strategy to mitigate this anxiety and improve patients’ understanding of their management options [35]. Therefore, our nomogram addresses multiple domains of clinical need: it enables consistent, evidence-based risk estimation to guide biopsy or surveillance decisions [5, 20] and may help alleviate patient psychological distress during the diagnostic process. Future prospective, multicenter implementation trials are warranted to assess its real-world impact on clinical workflow, cost-effectiveness, and patient-reported outcomes across diverse healthcare systems.

Despite the strong performance of our predictive nomogram, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study based on surgically resected nodules with pathological confirmation. As a result, the cohort was enriched for malignant lesions, with an overall malignancy prevalence of 81.0%. The absolute risk estimates generated by the model should therefore be interpreted with caution, and the model may be most applicable to patients with small pulmonary nodules who are already being considered for surgical management rather than to unselected screening-detected or incidentally detected nodules. Generalizability may also be limited by the single-institution Asian cohort and by the complete-case design, which may introduce selection bias related to the exclusion of patients with incomplete clinical, laboratory, or imaging data. Second, model validation was limited. Internal validation was based on a single random split, and neither external validation nor resampling-based approaches such as bootstrap or cross-validation were performed; therefore, model stability, optimism, and potential overfitting may not have been fully characterized. Direct head-to-head comparison with established models, such as the Brock or Herder models, was also not performed because several required predictors were unavailable or not consistently defined in this retrospective dataset. Third, this study did not develop or retrain an artificial intelligence model; instead, predefined quantitative CT measurements extracted using a commercial software platform were incorporated into a conventional multivariable logistic regression model. Thus, the findings should be interpreted as supporting the incremental value of software-assisted quantitative imaging variables rather than as validation of a novel AI algorithm. Fourth, although definitions of suspicious radiologic features were provided and summarized in Supplementary Table S1, qualitative image assessment may still be affected by reader experience and institutional practice. In addition, formal reproducibility testing of software-derived quantitative CT measurements was not performed. Potential variability related to segmentation verification, scanner models, acquisition protocols, and reconstruction parameters across the long inclusion period could not be fully assessed. Although thin-section CT acquisition was required for inclusion, scanner upgrades and reconstruction changes over time may have affected quantitative CT measurements. Future multicenter studies should include external validation and, if necessary, recalibration in broader and less selected cohorts, protocol harmonization, interobserver and interscanner reproducibility assessment, comparison with established prediction models, and evaluation of clinical implementation, cost-effectiveness, and patient-reported outcomes [10, 36].

This study presents several notable strengths. First, we developed a prediction model that integrates clinical variables with quantitative CT features, extending models based solely on semantic or quantitative features. Second, the use of a large, surgically confirmed cohort (n = 1,419) combined with 7:3 stratified randomization provided separate cohorts for model development and internal validation. Third, the complexity of the algorithm was translated into an immediately actionable, points-based nomogram, facilitating point‑of‑care risk assessment, supporting shared decision‑making, and aligning with guideline‑concordant workflows. Fourth, although systemic inflammatory biomarkers did not enter the final model, their comprehensive profiling establishes a valuable reference data set for future research in nodule malignancy risk modeling. Ultimately, the nomogram achieved high discrimination (AUC = 0.809 in the training set and 0.782 in the validation set) and good calibration. Its net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds further supports its potential clinical utility.

In conclusion, we developed and internally validated a clinical nomogram for malignancy risk stratification in surgically selected patients with pulmonary nodules ≤ 3 cm. By combining clinical and quantitative CT features into an accessible points-based tool, the model showed good discrimination, calibration, and net clinical benefit in this malignancy-enriched preoperative cohort. The nomogram may support individualized risk discussion for patients already being considered for surgical management. However, its generalizability to screening-detected or incidentally detected pulmonary nodules remains uncertain. Prospective multicenter validation and, if necessary, recalibration in broader and less selected populations are essential before routine clinical application.
