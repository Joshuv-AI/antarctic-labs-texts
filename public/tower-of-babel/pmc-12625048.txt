Intimate partner violence and postpartum depression: a moderated mediation model of fear of birth and antenatal care
Authors: Mengmei Yuan, Zining Zhu, Tieying Zeng, Ke Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntimate partner violence is a serious social problem affecting maternal mental health and has been confirmed to be associated with postpartum depression. However, the mechanisms through which intimate partner violence influences postpartum depression remain unclear, particularly the mediating role of fear of birth and the buffering effect of antenatal care, which require further investigation.MethodsThis research adopted a cross-sectional design and surveyed 562 postpartum women from a tertiary hospital in Hubei Province, China. Data were analyzed to examine the effects of intimate partner violence on postpartum depression, the mediating role of fear of birth, and the moderating effect of antenatal care attendance.ResultsThe results showed that intimate partner violence significantly predicted postpartum depression, and fear of birth partially mediated this relationship. Additionally, antenatal care significantly moderated the association between fear of birth and postpartum depression. Among those who attended antenatal care, the impact of fear of birth on postpartum depression was weaker, whereas this effect was more pronounced among women who did not attending at antenatal care.ConclusionsThis study shed light on the mechanisms through which intimate partner violence influences postpartum depression, with a particular emphasis on the mediating role of fear of birth and the moderating role of antenatal care in alleviating maternal psychological stress. These findings provide novel theoretical grounding for clinical interventions targeting maternal mental health. The results suggest that healthcare providers should strengthen screening for intimate partner violence, deliver targeted psychological interventions for women experiencing fear of birth, and enhance the accessibility and quality of antenatal care services to reduce the risk of postpartum depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25255-x.

Background

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a common mental health concern. A meta-analysis showed that 17.22% of women worldwide report symptoms of postpartum depression [1]. Postpartum depression can lead to impaired short-term memory, decreased milk production, reduced willingness to interact with mother and baby, and even serious adverse behaviours such as self-harm, suicide, or infanticide [2, 3]. Studies have shown that many factors influence the development of postpartum depression. Physiologically, there are dramatic fluctuations in maternal hormone levels after childbirth, which can lead to increased psychological vulnerability [4]. At the same time, psychological factors such as stress during pregnancy, fear of birth, and discomfort with role transition may elevate the likelihood of developing postpartum depression. Furthermore, increasing research attention has been directed toward the critical role of socio-environmental factors in postpartum depression. Inadequate family support, financial stress, marital relationship problems, and occupational conflict are all potential contributors to the risk of postpartum depression [5, 6]. Among them, intimate partner violence (IPV) is considered one of the important social risk factors affecting postpartum depression, which has received extensive attention from scholars in various countries [7].

Intimate partner violence refers to threatening or abusive behavior between intimate partners, including physical, sexual, psychological, and controlling acts. As a global public health concern, a Lancet study across 161 countries found that 27% of women had experienced IPV in their lifetime [8]. During pregnancy, IPV prevalence ranges from 1.8% to 13.5% across different countries, with variations potentially explained by recall bias, social norms, or stigma [9]. IPV during pregnancy can compromise women’s emotional well-being and parenting capacity. Affected women are at increased risk of Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety, and tend to report lower mother–infant attachment and breastfeeding rates [10]. IPV is also associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as miscarriage, stillbirth, premature rupture of membranes, and low birth weight [11]. Although pregnancy is often perceived as a protective period against IPV, research findings on this issue remain inconsistent. Some studies suggest that IPV decreases during pregnancy, possibly due to perpetrators’ increased restraint or concern for fetal well-being, particularly in relationships with a prior history of abuse [12]. However, other studies indicate that pregnancy may act as a trigger for heightened violence, fueled by factors such as unwanted pregnancy, financial strain, or increased partner control. Notably, approximately 50% of abused women report experiencing IPV for the first time during pregnancy [13]. Moreover, the form of violence may shift during this period, from overt physical assaults to more subtle emotional abuse or coercive control [14]. Additional risk factors, including unintended pregnancy, lower socioeconomic status, and being unmarried, have also been identified as predictors of IPV during pregnancy.

Fear of birth (FOB) is a common negative emotional state experienced by women before or during labour. Fear of birth manifests itself in uncertainty about the childbirth process, intense worry about pain, anxiety about medical interventions, and excessive concern about her own and her baby’s health [15], and in severe cases may even lead to an increase in elective caesarean Sects [16, 17]. It has been found that FOB can cause pregnant women to become negative about childbirth and is a predictor of postpartum depression [18]. Meanwhile, a growing body of research has focused on the relationship between FOB and IPV during pregnancy. A study by Qgurlu et al. [19] showed that intimate partner violence during pregnancy had a significant negative effect on maternal FOB. Studies also indicated that women who suffered from physical IPV, particularly those experiencing their first pregnancy, are more likely to develop a fear of childbirth [20]. This may be because intimate partner physical violence may have direct harmful effects on the unborn child, and the risk to the health of the foetus is the most important cause of childbirth fear in women [21–23].

Antenatal care (ANC) is an important external support resource for pregnant women. By providing expert health education, psychological support, and personalised care guidance, antenatal care can help pregnant women better manage the physical and emotional demands of pregnancy and build positive expectations for childbirth [24]. At least one ANC visit has been associated with a 1.04% point reduction in neonatal mortality and a 1.07% point reduction in infant mortality [25]. ANC attendance has also been linked to reduced fear of childbirth and to promoting active participation in the childbirth process [26]. FOB may act as a psychological mechanism linking IPV to PPD, and the strength of this pathway may be influenced by available external support. As a form of psychosocial care, ANC may buffer the effects of FOB and, in turn, reduce the risk of PPD. Compared with directly intervening in the association between IPV and PPD, which is often complicated by the hidden nature of IPV, focusing on ANC’s moderating role in the link between FOB and PPD offers greater clinical feasibility. FOB is more easily identified and addressed within ANC settings through education and emotional support. Investigating whether ANC moderates the relationship between FOB and PPD may help clarify the underlying psychological processes and support the development of targeted strategies for high-risk women, particularly those affected by IPV.

Resource Conservation Theory provides an important theoretical framework for this study [27]. The theory suggests that psychological resources are depleted when an individual is exposed to a stressor, which can lead to increased psychological vulnerability and subsequent negative mental health outcomes. Concurrently, the theory emphasizes that external supportive resources can buffer the negative impact of stressors on mental health [28]. Within this framework, IPV can be conceptualized as a major external stressor that depletes women’s psychological resources. FOB represents a negative emotional response reflecting this resource depletion, which in turn increases the likelihood of adverse outcomes. PPD can be understood as a downstream manifestation of cumulative resource loss and heightened vulnerability. In contrast, ANC attendance serves as an external supportive resource that can help preserve or replenish psychological resources, thereby buffering the effects of IPV on FOB and ultimately reducing the risk of PPD.

The aim of this study was thus to investigate whether antenatal care moderates the mediating role of fear of childbirth on the relationship between intimate partner violence and postpartum depression. The study was conducted in two steps: first, to examine (1) whether intimate partner violence affects postpartum depression through fear of childbirth (mediation model); and second, to examine (2) whether the strength of the mediating effect through fear of childbirth changes as a result of attendance in antenatal care (moderating mediation model) (see Fig. 1). We hypothesized that intimate partner violence would be positively associated with postpartum depression and that this relationship would be partly explained by the enhancing effect of fear of childbirth on postpartum depression. Attendance in antenatal care would have a moderating effect on of FOB on PPD. With this study, we hope to provide a new rationale for maternal health management to help mothers better cope with psychological stress and improve maternal and child health.Fig. 1Study conceptual model

Methods

Participants and procedures

This study utilized a cross-sectional design. The study was conducted between August to October 2024 at a tertiary hospital in Hubei Province, China, using convenience sampling to select participants. Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) within one week after childbirth, (2) age ≥ 20 years, (3) agreement to take part in this study and signing the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included: (1) with serious complications and comorbidities of pregnancy; (2) with severe psychological or psychiatric disorders; and (3) stillbirth.

The questionnaire was collected face-to-face in the maternity ward. The researcher identified participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria by reviewing medical records, then provided a detailed explanation of the study to them. Women who agreed to join the study were asked to sign a consent form after receiving an explanation of the research. All questionnaires were collected through the Questionnaire Star platform. The researcher provided the women with the Quick Response code of the questionnaire so that the participants could fill in the questionnaire online. During the process of completing the questionnaires, the researcher temporarily left the ward and returned after a while to confirm and check whether the participants had completed the questionnaires and to answer any confusion that existed in the mothers. At the end of the investigation completion, a small gift was given to each participant as a token of appreciation. In total, 607 questionnaires were distributed during the study, and after removing incomplete responses, 562 valid questionnaires were retained, achieving a response rate of 92.6%.

Measures

Intimate partner violence during pregnancy

The Abuse Assessment Screening Questionnaire was used in this study to assess intimate partner violence during pregnancy. The questionnaire was developed by McFarlane in 1995 and later translated into Chinese by the Family Harmony Research Group of the University of Hong Kong [29]. It consists of eight items that identify common abusers, such as the respondent’s husband, ex-husband, boyfriend, and stranger. Each item is answered with either “yes” or “no.” For Question 5, “Since you’ve been pregnant, have you been emotionally hurt by someone?” and Question 6, “Since you’ve been pregnant, have you been physically hurt by someone?”, participants were considered to have experienced intimate partner violence during pregnancy if they answered “yes” to either question and identified the perpetrator as an intimate partner (e.g., husband, ex-husband, or boyfriend). Those who reported violence solely from strangers or other non-intimate individuals were excluded from the IPV classification. The internal consistency coefficient for this questionnaire was 0.646, indicating acceptable internal reliability.

Fear of childbirth

Fear of childbirth was assessed using the Fear of Birth Scale (FOBS), developed by Haines et al. in 2015 [30], which includes two dimensions: anxiety and fear. The scale uses two 10 cm visual analogue lines ranging from 0 (no anxiety/fear) to 10 (extreme anxiety/fear), in response to the question: “How do you feel about your upcoming labour?” The average of the two scores was taken as the overall fear of childbirth score. In previous studies, a cutoff score above 5.4 has been used to indicate clinically significant fear. The Chinese version of the FOBS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high translation equivalence (0.833–1.000), content validity (0.800–0.933), internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.897), and test-retest reliability (r =.860) [31]. In the present study, the FOBS was administered within one week postpartum as a retrospective measure of antenatal fear. Internal consistency in our sample remained excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.957).

Postnatal depression

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) was used in this study to assess maternal postnatal depression. The tool was developed by Cox et al. in 1987 [32] and consists of 10 items. The Chinese version of the EPDS was first introduced in 1998 and later revised by Wang et al. in 2009 to better reflect Chinese linguistic and cultural norms [33]. It has demonstrated good psychometric properties in Chinese populations, with an internal consistency coefficient of 0.76 and a content validity index of 0.93. The scale is rated on a four-point Likert scale (“as before”, “not as before”, “definitely less than before”, and “not at all”). All items are scored from 0 to 3, except for items 1 and 2, which are reverse-scored from 3 to 0. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In our study, the EPDS demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.857.

Antenatal care attendance

Antenatal care attendance was measured by answering the multiple choices question ‘Have you attended antenatal care?’. Options included: receiving eugenics counseling, pregnancy check-ups, nutritional guidance during pregnancy, mental health guidance during pregnancy, hygiene guidance during pregnancy, exercise guidance during pregnancy, or other health services. Selecting any one or more of these options, except ‘none’, was considered participation in maternal health.

Socio-demographic information

Socio-demographic information included age, education level, household Income (RMB/per month), number of pregnancies, parity, and whether the pregnancy was planned. Among these variables, age was treated as a continuous variable, while the others were categorical. Their categories were defined as follows: education level: postgraduate, college/university, high school, junior high school and below; household income: < 5000, 5000 ~ 10,000, >10,000; number of pregnancies: none, one, two and above; parity: none, one, two and above; and planned pregnancy: yes, no.

Data analysis

Data distribution and tests for multicollinearity showed that the kurtosis coefficients and skewness coefficients of the main variables (intimate partner violence, fear of birth, postpartum depression, and antenatal care attendance) were less than ± 1, indicating that the variables conformed to a normal distribution. The variance inflation factor (VIF) values for intimate partner violence, fear of childbirth, and ANC attendance were between 1.00 and 1.02, indicating that there was no multicollinearity in the data. Harman’s one-way method was applied to examine common method bias, and the results indicated that the eigenvalues of the two factors exceeded 1 and the maximum variance explained by a single factor was 37.921%, which is below 40%, indicating that common method bias was not significant [34].

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS version 26.0 and PROCESS version 3.5 [35]. Correlations between the study variables were tested using Pearson’s correlation, Point-Biserial correlation, Phi coefficient, and Spearman’s correlation. Starting with a simple model, more complex models were constructed progressively as suggested by Klein [36]. Model 4 [35] was first used to test whether the effect of intimate partner violence on postpartum depression was partially attributable to the mediating effect of fear of childbirth. Due to the relatively large sample size, a Sobel test was conducted to determine the statistical significance of the indirect effect [37]. Following validation of the simple mediation model, a moderated mediation model was run using Model 14 [35]. As intimate partner violence and ANC attendance were categorical variables, only fear of childbirth was centered before the creation of interaction terms. To further understand the moderating role of ANC attendance, we conducted a simple slope analysis. The statistical significance of the mediating effect was assessed by bootstrapping confidence intervals with bias correction.

Ethics consideration

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, and Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval number: TJ-IRB20230832). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were given both oral and written information about the study, and the legality and anonymity of data storage were assured. All participants provided informed consent and were notified of their right to withdraw at any point without any repercussions.

Testing the mediating role of fear of birth

After controlling for sociodemographic variables, including maternal age, educational level, household income, number of pregnancies, parity, and planned pregnancy, PROCESS Model 4 was used to examine the potential mediating role of fear of birth in the association between intimate partner violence and postpartum depression. The results showed that intimate partner violence was positively associated with postpartum depression (β = 3.570, Boot 95% CI: [2.154, 4.986]), and fear of birth partially mediated this association (indirect effect = 0.796, Boot 95% CI: [0.233, 1.369]). The total effect of intimate partner violence on postpartum depression was also statistically significant (total effect = 4.366, Boot 95% CI: [2.857, 5.875]). Since all confidence intervals excluded zero, the indirect effect of fear of birth was considered statistically reliable, supporting a partial mediation model (see Table 3).

Bootstrapping analysis (5,000 samples) further confirmed the presence of a statistically significant moderated mediation effect. The conditional indirect effect of intimate partner violence on postpartum depression via fear of birth was statistically significant among participants who did not attend antenatal care (indirect effect = 1.360, Boot SE = 0.547, 95% CI: [0.328, 2.502]) and also among those who did attend, though with a reduced magnitude (indirect effect = 0.703, Boot SE = 0.262, 95% CI: [0.186, 1.235]). The index of moderated mediation was − 0.658 (Boot SE = 0.365, 95% CI: [–1.494, –0.068]), indicating a significant difference in the strength of the indirect effect across levels of antenatal care attendance.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate how intimate partner violence is associated with postpartum depression based on the Conservation of Resources Theory, and to further examine the mediating role of fear of birth and the moderating effect of antenatal care attendance. The findings suggested that intimate partner violence not only had a direct association with increased risk of postpartum depression, but also exerted an indirect effect through elevated fear of birth. Furthermore, antenatal care attendance moderated this pathway by attenuating the impact of fear of birth on postpartum depressive symptoms. These results highlight the complexity of maternal mental health challenges, underscoring the importance of addressing both psychosocial stressors and available support resources in clinical interventions. The study offers valuable theoretical and practical insights for optimizing maternal health management strategies during the perinatal period.

The results of this study reinforce the evidence that intimate partner violence is strongly associated with an increased risk of postpartum depression, in line with prior research findings. Numerous studies have shown that intimate partner violence is linked not only to depression, but also to anxiety, PTSD, and other psychological conditions [38–40]. Women who experience violence during pregnancy are often subjected to ongoing emotional distress, which can persist into the postpartum period and negatively impact parenting and mother-infant attachment [41]. The Conservation of Resources Theory offers a compelling framework to interpret these findings [27]. According to the theory, prolonged exposure to stressors leads to a depletion of psychological resources, thereby increasing vulnerability to mental health problems. These findings highlight the importance of IPV screening in routine prenatal care, and underscore the need for timely psychological support for women exposed to interpersonal violence.

Additionally, this study identified the mediating role of fear of birth in the relationship between intimate partner violence and postpartum depression, highlighting fear of birth as a key psychological mechanism linking intimate partner violence to maternal mental health outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated that heightened fear of birth is often associated with adverse birth experiences, postpartum mood disorders, and negative infant health outcomes [42, 43]. The present findings further suggest that intimate partner violence may erode a woman’s sense of safety and emotional stability, which may increase fear of birth and subsequently heighten the risk of postpartum depression. These results are consistent with prior literature indicating that experiences of interpersonal violence can undermine women’s self-esteem and self-efficacy during pregnancy, thereby aggravating childbirth-related fears [44]. Given the mediating role of fear of birth, screening for this psychological factor among IPV-exposed women may offer an effective and feasible strategy to identify those at highest risk for postpartum depression. Unlike IPV, which is often underreported, fear of birth can be readily assessed in clinical settings and is responsive to intervention. Incorporating FOB screening into perinatal care may thus enable more targeted psychological support for this vulnerable population.

Furthermore, the study found that antenatal care attendance significantly moderated the relationship between fear of birth and postpartum depression. Previous research has shown that antenatal care can offer not only professional medical advice, but also valuable psychological support and targeted health education, helping women better understand the childbirth process, build self-efficacy, and reduce fear of birth [45, 46]. Consistent with these findings, our study further demonstrated that for women who had experienced intimate partner violence, antenatal care attendance helped buffer the adverse effect of fear of birth on postpartum depression. However, it should be noted that although antenatal care showed a protective buffering effect, it did not fully eliminate the negative impact of fear of birth. This may be attributed to factors such as variations in care quality, individual psychological resilience, and the availability of social support [47]. To enhance the effectiveness of antenatal care, future research should explore the specific mechanisms by which different types of services impact maternal mental health, thereby informing more precise and personalized intervention strategies to improve maternal well-being.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary hospital using convenience sampling, which may introduce cultural, regional, and selection biases and limit the generalizability of the results. Future research should recruit more diverse and representative populations to improve external validity. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and temporal interpretation among IPV, FOB, and PPD. FOB and PPD were measured concurrently within one week postpartum, which may introduce recall bias and be influenced by actual birth experiences. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the direction and stability of these associations. Third, IPV was assessed using only two items addressing emotional and physical abuse. Other forms, such as sexual violence, coercive control, and stalking, were not included. This may have led to underestimation of IPV prevalence and missed distinct psychological impacts, especially those related to bodily autonomy and childbirth fear. Finally, ANC attendance was assessed as a binary variable, which may not capture the diversity of antenatal care experiences. Future studies should explore how different types and frequencies of ANC services influence maternal mental health.

Conclusion

This study explored the impact of intimate partner violence on postpartum depression and revealed the mediating role of fear of birth as well as the moderating effect of antenatal care attendance. The results indicated that intimate partner violence was associated not only with an increased risk of postpartum depression, but also with an indirect psychological pathway through heightened fear of birth. Moreover, antenatal care attendance helped mitigate the influence of fear of birth on postpartum depressive symptoms, demonstrating its protective value in supporting maternal mental health. This study provides important empirical evidence for understanding both the development and intervention of maternal mental health problems, offering new insights into optimizing perinatal care strategies and enhancing maternal and infant well-being. On the one hand, early screening and psychological support should be provided for women with a history of intimate partner violence, helping them effectively manage fear of birth. On the other hand, the protective role of antenatal care should be maximized to strengthen maternal confidence and self-efficacy during the perinatal period, thereby reducing the risk of postpartum depression. Future research should further investigate other psychosocial mechanisms through which intimate partner violence may influence postpartum mental health. In addition, longitudinal study designs are needed to establish temporal and causal relationships and to better understand the long-term psychological trajectories associated with intimate partner violence.
