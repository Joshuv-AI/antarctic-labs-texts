Put purpose before process: lessons from evaluating patient involvement in governance structures in health research organisations
Authors: Kristina Staley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Many health research organisations make strong commitments to involvement in their organisational strategies. This commitment is often operationalised via an involvement strategy, and evidenced by high quality, successful involvement within areas such as making research funding decisions, or policy development. One area that can prove more challenging is involvement in governance structures. Little has been written about impact or best practice in this context. This article reflects the wealth of knowledge and experience from Board members, staff and involved patients that has been shared during evaluations of involvement. Regretfully these people cannot be named as these evaluation reports have not been made public. The intention is to draw out general lessons to support wider discussion in this area, and share valuable insights from some of the more challenging experiences. With little guidance available, it is unsurprising that organisations tend to approach involvement in governance in the same way as other forms of involvement, leading to a focus on process – how to recruit, prepare and support patients to join a Board. The learning suggests that having a clear and shared understanding of the purpose of involvement at this strategic level may also be critical to its success. It seems that there are many different understandings of what this involvement is for and these are not often clearly defined. Involvement is also commonly confused with stakeholder engagement. Stakeholder engagement requires people to speak on behalf of others and to be representative of a wider community. In contrast, during involvement, patients contribute their experiential knowledge to decision-making as an expert in their own right. When making any kind of strategic decision people will refer to their values to inform their views. Involved patients are likely to hold different values to people who are an integral part of the health research system. This can lead to value conflicts which are best resolved by allowing considerable time for discussion and through skilled support from facilitators. Being prepared for these kinds of conflict to arise and having strategies and resources to deal with them, may be another important requirement for meaningful and impactful involvement in governance.

Background

Over the last ten years, I have conducted a number of evaluations of patient involvement in health research organisations, organisations that fund research and/or support research or researchers in other ways. (I use the term patient to refer to people with relevant lived experience in this context). The evaluations included formal interviews and group discussions with Board members, staff, involved patients and other stakeholders, as well as observing key meetings. Many of the lessons and experiences have been much the same. This led me to conclude that the learning this work has generated might be useful to other organisations experiencing similar challenges. This is the intention behind writing this article.

In writing this, I am reflecting on the wealth of knowledge and experience interviewees have shared with me, who regretfully cannot be named because the internal evaluation reports and strategy documents have not been published. Some of their anonymised quotes are included in quotation marks (with their permission). I am sharing the learning in a generalised, anonymised way with the goal of encouraging discussion amongst the wider involvement community and with deep gratitude to those who have been working hard at the coalface.

Involvement in governance structures in health research organisations

Many health research organisations make strong commitments to involvement as part of their wider organisational strategies, making statements to the effect that they will be “patient-centred”, or “put patients at the heart of everything they do”, or “ensure meaningful involvement in all aspects of their work”. If they fund research, they also state that they will ensure there is involvement in this research. Many of these organisations are also charities with wider remits to provide services and support. In this article I am focused on patient involvement in research functions. Charities have an extra dimension to their governance in terms of meeting the requirements of charity law. This is beyond the scope of this commentary.

Often health research organisations develop involvement strategies to operationalise their strategic commitment and very successfully involve patients within specific areas of work, e.g. on research funding panels or on projects to develop health policy. This kind of involvement is often conducted to a very high standard, underpinned by general best practice guidance [1].

One area of involvement that can prove more challenging, is involving patients within the governance structures of health research organisations. Governance refers to the rules and processes by which an organisation is given strategic direction, as well as being controlled and held accountable. In this article, I am particularly interested in how patients can contribute to organisational strategy, rather than overseeing executive functions or ensuring public accountability.

Involvement at strategic level is thought essential to meet organisational commitments (see above), and can be seen as symbolic of positive culture change within the organisation. It is thought to indicate a genuine intention to share power with patients, because they are involved “at the top” of the organisation, and are publicly recognised as members of a Board (a group of people who are given responsibility for the governance of an organisation). In the discussion, I explore the potential for involvement at this level to promote a much wider systemic culture change, through influencing high level research policy.

While there has been extensive research exploring the mechanisms and impact of public and patient involvement in healthcare governance [2–6], there has been little published about patient involvement in health research governance. Similarly, there are few benchmarks or related practice guidance in this context. A brief review of the guidance that is available, revealed that overall it lacks clarity and precision, confusing involvement with other forms of decision-making processes e.g. democracy or stakeholder engagement, and using terms such as ‘patient representative’ and ‘public and patient voice’ without clear definitions [7–9]. Furthermore, the involvement and engagement of citizens, public and patients are often described collectively as PPIE (patient and public engagement and involvement) in the literature, without explaining that these groups can make distinct contributions to very different kinds of processes [9, 10]. This is symptomatic of the wider issue around inconsistent use of language in this field [11].

In the absence of specific guidance, it is unsurprising that organisations tend to approach involvement in governance structures in much the same way as other forms of involvement. This can lead to a focus on the process – how to recruit, prepare and support patients to join a Board. Terms of Reference are commonly agreed around the expectations and limits of a patient role, but these documents also typically have a process focus. Much less attention is given to the purpose of involvement, and more importantly how this might need to change in different contexts. Previous evaluations suggest that having a clear and shared understanding of the purpose of involvement at a strategic level may also be critical to its success.

Having a clear consensus about the role of patients on a Board

In evaluation interviews with Board members, (health professionals, researchers, staff and patients), interviewees described their expectations of the role of patient Board members. These were very varied. The expected roles included: observer, outsider, contributor of the patient voice, expert or involvement champion, similar to those described by Crocker et al. [13]. During the evaluations, other interviewees commented on the challenges or uncertainties around these roles, illustrating a lack of consensus. Observer

Some interviewees believed that simply having patients in the room while a Board meeting takes place is sufficient to make a difference, on the basis that Board members will be more likely to consider patients’ concerns and interests in their physical presence. The expectation seemed to be that patients will contribute very little to the actual discussions. However, patients reported being unhappy with this approach, because they want to be able to influence decisions. They may be unwilling to give their time to such meetings, because this experience can feel like tokenism.

Outsider

Patient members of Boards are sometimes expected to offer an independent, outsider perspective that will critically challenge the culture of the organisation and any ‘group-think’ of like-minded professional Board members. As Crocker states, outsiders are people who can feel freer to express their views without fear of consequences, and often think ‘outside the box’ to bring fresh ideas [13]. One concern about this view is that outsider in this context means anyone who is not part of the organisation. It does not explain why patients’ input would be of particular value, nor which patients should be involved.

Contributor of “the patient voice”

Some interviewees were committed to the principle that patients have a right to express their opinions at Board meetings and that other Board members have a moral obligation to hear “the patient voice”, appealing to values of democratic decision making [7]. However, holding this expectation does not help to identify who can claim to be the voice of patients [6], which can create doubts about the legitimacy of the views of the patients who are around the Table [8]. Despite these concerns, along with uncertainty about what it means in practice to “hear the patient voice” [6], it can become a matter of principle to keep working with patients in this way. With this moral purpose, Board members felt they could not critique this practice, even if they had concerns about whether it was making a valuable difference.

Expert

Some interviewees expected that patient members of a Board would also bring some kind of professional expertise that might be useful to the work of the Board, for example, through having a legal background or experience as a Chief Executive in other types of organisations. This may undoubtedly be valuable, particularly to the management and accountability functions of governance [5]. From the patient perspective, it can be difficult to wear two hats. It can leave patients less clear about what kinds of contributions they are expected to make. They sometimes described experiencing internal conflict, when these two different perspectives are at odds with each other, and find that their ‘professional’ contributions are welcomed, while their ‘patient’ contributions may be less so. The expertise that patients can bring based on their lived experience, may not be recognised as such [8].

Involvement champion

When organisations have a strategic commitment to patient involvement, patient members of Boards can sometimes be assumed to be the people who will keep the organisation on track and remind them of this commitment. Patients sometimes saw this as their responsibility to be there to “push the involvement agenda”. In contrast, staff and other Board members have sometimes stated that this should not be the patients’ role. In line with good practice guidance [14], they concluded this championing is better done by senior managers or other Board members (non-patients), as they have more authority and influence with their peers. Some reflected that if patient members of Boards routinely ask for more patient involvement, there is a risk this can be perceived as self-serving.

It is of note that inviting patients to become Trustees is a common mechanism by which patients become members of a Board, which is most relevant to charities that fund and/or support research. A Trustee is a very specific role defined by law [15] and examination of this role is beyond the scope of this article. However, in common with the above reflections on different roles, it is not always clear whether patient Trustees are expected to contribute anything distinct from Trustees with other kinds of experience (Table 1).

In summary, there are many different expectations of patient members of a Board in a health research organisation. This can lead to confusion, especially if different Board members hold different views, or are in disagreement with each other. Starting with these expectations does not always precisely define who needs to be involved or why [8]. This also creates some uncertainty as to when patients should be asked to contribute. Some people believed patients should be offering their views to every decision, while others believed they should only contribute to certain decisions that are somehow determined as being those most relevant to patients. This opens up a challenging question around who decides which decisions are relevant for patient input.

By way of contrast, there are two more clearly defined roles for patient members of Board: a stakeholder representative or an involved patient. These two activities are distinct, but in practice are sometimes confused, as discussed in the next section.

Knowing when stakeholder engagement or patient involvement is required

Patients can be stakeholders in an organisation (people with an interest in the success of that organisation) and also experts who can offer knowledge and wisdom to inform decision-making [8]. Both are valuable to inform organisational strategy development. However, they most usefully contribute to different kinds of decisions in different ways. For example, in the context of making strategic decisions about the governance of a health database, developing agreed criteria for access to data and priorities for access, might be best supported by a process of stakeholder engagement, working with patients and researchers. Making decisions about data protection mechanisms and organisational policies on data leaks would need expert input. This could include hearing from people with lived experience of a harmful data breach, a form of patient involvement.

The key distinction between an engaged stakeholder and an involved patient is that the former relies on a mechanism of representation and the latter on expertise [8] (Table 2). However, previous evaluations have shown there is considerable confusion around this, and involved patients are very often mistakenly referred to as ‘patient representatives’. More importantly, the legitimacy and authority of the contributions made by involved patients may be undermined or dismissed by other Board members because of concerns about lack of representativeness [8]. Past studies of patient involvement in healthcare governance similarly report frequently unresolved tensions between whether a patient is there to provide the collective voice, or the input of an individual [5]. Such tensions and confusion might be resolved through greater clarity around the differences between these two roles and activities, as discussed in more detail below.

Stakeholder engagement

Stakeholder engagement by a Board is a way to ensure that the views of the various groups with an interest in an organisation’s success are somehow represented on the Board. This representation can take various forms, such as direct membership or indirect input (e.g. via advisory groups, surveys and engagement events). It ensures the Board has a clear understanding of the various stakeholder perspectives and engages with them meaningfully. The goal is to improve decision-making, and to enhance the organisation’s long-term sustainability, reputation and social performance.

In the context of health research organisations, patients are only one such stakeholder group. These organisations might also wish to engage with researchers, health professionals, policymakers, funders or internal staff, depending on the nature of the decision being made. The final decision-making then requires balancing the interests of the different stakeholder groups, and finding solutions that address their various concerns, at the same time as achieving the organisations’ intended goals. Stakeholder representation can mean asking questions such as “What do our stakeholders think about us doing x?” and “Could stakeholder representatives help us with getting buy-in from their peers to do x?” It is sometimes perceived as a way that stakeholders can enable the organisation’s work. Board members have sometimes expressed concerns that patients are “coming with an agenda”, if they perceive patients to be critical of the organisation’s decisions.

Patient involvement

Patient involvement provides a mechanism by which patients can contribute their experiential knowledge to decision-making, acting as critical friends. Experiential knowledge can be defined as truth learned from personal experience. It is based on the individual’s actual experience which is unique and limited to them, but nonetheless is often similar to the experience of others, who have the same challenges [16]. Experiential knowledge turns into expertise, when it can be used to resolve problems that other people are encountering [16].

This is one way of conceptualising how involvement works [8]. Because Board members may lack experiential knowledge in a given area, they may sometimes find they are working with gaps in their knowledge or are holding false assumptions, that are only revealed through involvement. Board members might therefore need the expertise and foresight of patients, to identify problems with their proposals and to find acceptable solutions. Acting without patients’ experiential knowledge runs the risk of making ill-informed decisions which can lay waste to considerable amounts of wastage, in terms of money, time and effort [17].

Implications for practice

It is important to note that the processes of stakeholder engagement and patient involvement could look identical. They could both result in patients being invited to join a Board. However, because the purpose of these two activities is distinct, their contributions could be very different. It also means recruiting different people to those distinct roles, and providing them with different kinds of support. In order to be genuinely representative, engaged stakeholders need to somehow be chosen or elected on these grounds, and/or to have a wider group of patients to consult. This is not relevant to involvement, where patients need to recruited on the basis of having knowledge and expertise that is relevant to the decisions being made [8].

Importantly, implementing either stakeholder engagement or patient involvement takes time, resources, a commitment to hearing what people have to say and a willingness to be influenced by their contributions. In both instances, attention must be given to power and how it is being shared, for example, whether involved patients or engaged stakeholders are to be given voting powers on a Board. There can be little value in investing in either activity, if there is no willingness or intention to make changes in response.

Preparing for value conflicts

When making any kind of strategic decision about priorities, direction and goals for an organisation, it is inevitable that people will refer to their own values to inform their views (what’s most important to them), or sometimes the accepted values of the organisation. For patients involved in any organisation within the research system (i.e. the set of institutions, policies, people, culture and funding that manage, deliver and disseminate research), it is likely that their values will differ from the values of other people who are an integral part of the system. This is because values are partly informed by experiences of life and work, as well as social and cultural backgrounds, ethnicity and education [18]. It is to be expected and natural that value conflicts (deep-seated disagreements or tensions arising from differences in beliefs or principles), will arise when patients and researchers/ health professionals work together, because these disagreements are always likely when diverse people work together [19] (see Example 1). Value conflicts can happen any time during involvement in research, but may be more frequent at a strategic level, when the consequences of any decision can be more far reaching.

Example 1: An example of a value conflict: deciding what makes good quality research evidence

In deciding what makes good evidence, researchers are likely to value characteristics such as robustness of methods, objectivity, and quantifiable outcomes. In contrast, patients might value real-life relevance and the usefulness of the findings generated. Finding common ground and resolving this value conflict, for example, when making decisions about how to communicate research evidence, might need to accommodate these different values to ensure the needs and interests of both parties are met.

Value conflicts affect personal interactions as well as individual decisions. They can be particularly complex to resolve because they often touch on moral, ethical, or personal preferences that define a person’s identity – who they are, rather than what they do. These kinds of conversations can become deeply uncomfortable. It can seem as if people are on either side of a chasm, “pitching one world view against another, without there being a way for either to cross over to the other side”.

However, value conflicts can be resolved, more easily if all parties are: (1) aware this is happening, (2) are prepared and willing to engage with each other, and (3) are supported in ways that help them find a resolution [20]. The learning from previous evaluations, is that organisations have not often prepared for these kinds of interactions, and can find them very difficult to manage when they arise. Furthermore, organisations usually find that Board meetings are not always the best place to hold these types of conversation, because it can take a long time to find common ground, and often only with skilled and neutral facilitation [19]. This might be why Board away-days often seem to create better spaces for more constructive dialogue between patients and other Board members, than the actual Board meetings themselves.

Involved patients have sometimes described experiences of value conflicts whenever they are involved in research and seek to challenge the values of the research system. The capacity to keep challenging the system constructively, particularly at a strategic level, is a highly developed skill. Patients describe putting in enormous efforts to become “the acceptable face of challenge”, people who can be taken seriously, and be heard and understood. However, always being on the outside of the system, and often having their contributions questioned on the basis of their “lack of representativeness” or from “bringing an agenda”, can lead some to feel rejected and unheard. This has been described as taking “a heavy emotional toll”, sometimes to the extent that patients become less willing to be involved.

In summary, value conflicts are an inevitable and natural consequence of diverse people working together. Despite this being the norm, it seems that organisations are not often prepared when these emerge, and have not always developed robust strategies to manage them.

Discussion

Past evaluations have revealed the importance of being very clear about the purpose of involvement in governance in health research organisations, and to distinguish between stakeholder engagement and patient involvement. Some key questions are then: What is the purpose of involving patients as Board members in this context? How does involvement add value to the decisions made by these kinds of Boards? Whose experiential knowledge is relevant? Some initial responses to these questions are explored below, with the intention of stimulating further debate on this issue.

In the context of a research organisation with a specific health condition focus, involving people with lived experience of that condition may be relevant at the strategic level. If strategic decisions are being made about prioritisation and direction based on assumptions about patients’ lives, then in theory the expertise of those patients might very well serve to correct that. For example, if decisions are made to about research priorities based on an assumption of what’s important, then patients can correct that and identify steps to find out about people’s genuine priorities. However, this kind of involvement often takes place at other levels within the organisation, before such decisions reach the point of being ratified by a Board. This raises questions about how to ensure there is involvement at every level where there is potential to influence strategy and to ensure these processes all join up. It also begs the question as to whether there is extra benefit from asking involved patient Board members about issues that may have already been addressed by high-quality involvement elsewhere.

In the context of a health research organisation whose mission is to support health research more broadly, the lived-experience of any particular health condition may no longer be relevant. Instead, what patients could bring to general research strategy, is their lived experience of being involved in a research system that does not always share their values, and the barriers this creates to meaningful influence.

Whenever patients are involved in research, they can experience systemic barriers to their involvement, which arise from the current research system and culture, rather than any individual working in it. These barriers can limit the extent and impact of their involvement. Examples of such barriers include: a lack of resources and infrastructure to fund relationship-building outside of research projects, or to work with diverse communities [21]; and, a lack of clarity on how to document and assess impact [22]. The knowledge that patients gain through extensive experience of involvement means they are able to identify these barriers, while others who are an integral part of the system may not be able to do so, or might not feel able to voice their concerns [12].

Therefore organisations with a strategic commitment to involvement in research more generally could usefully involve patients in decisions about strategy and direction in order to bring about constructive and supportive changes to the research system. This is a complex and very challenging objective. It is very likely to lead to value conflicts which will likely require a specialist approach for resolution, a great deal of preparation, and willingness for change from all involved.

Being involved in this kind of strategic work, requires patients who have extensive knowledge and experience of the research system through having been involved in multiple contexts e.g. on grant panels in funding organisations, as well as working on research projects. Rather than being the ‘usual suspects’, or people who have lost their ability to provide ‘lay input’, these kinds of experiences are part of the essential skills and experiences that equips patients to take on high-level, strategic and leadership roles.

Conclusion

In conclusion, it seems that involvement in governance structures within health research organisations is embarked on with the very best of intentions and often diligently supported to best practice involvement standards by staff within those organisations. However, sometimes a lack of a clear and agreed purpose for this activity and/or a lack of preparation for the challenges of value conflicts, can limit the potential for this involvement to be impactful and meaningful. In worst case scenarios, it can lead to confusion and conflict.

In planning this work, there can be a tendency to emphasise organisational structures and processes – the need to include patients on a Board. Instead it might be more beneficial to start with questions about purpose, such as: Why do we want to involve patients? What do we want patients to contribute, and to what end? Do we need to engage stakeholders or do we need the expert input of patients? It is essential that this is discussed and clarified before patients are invited to become Board members. Everyone on the Board needs to be in agreement about what is required as well as signed up and prepared for working in this way.

For genuine patient involvement in strategy development, the emphasis might be better placed on identifying the decisions that could benefit from patients’ expertise, mapping where these decisions are actually made, and then exploring whether there is a genuine interest in change. Then it is possible to develop processes that are fit for purpose and that maximise the potential for influence. This might require a more flexible approach, e.g. inviting different patients with different expertise to contribute to different aspects of strategic level decision-making at different times – rather than a static, standard process of Board membership that expects one or two patients to do it all.

Putting purpose before process, and being certain about when involvement is needed or when some other kind of activity such as stakeholder engagement is required, would lead to the most relevant people being involved, in the best way, and at the right time. It would reduce the potential for confusion, and help to ensure that any involvement that does takes place is impactful and not tokenistic. Genuine involvement at a strategic level in health research organisations is likely to require value conflicts to be addressed boldly and openly. This is especially true, if this involvement is hoped or expected to bring about the system-wide culture change needed to make all involvement genuine and meaningful.
