Development, expert validation, and pilot testing of digital mental health literacy program for Filipino college students
Authors: Mark Allan C. Mananggit, Ungsinun Intarakamhang, Kanu Priya Mohan, Amir Ledesma, Joel Collado, Klara Patricia Laureta, Joan Katrina Balmilero-Pelagio, Rochelle Ann V. Pararuan, Ferlyn F. Reyes-Colar, Irene G. Bustos, Jay C. Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing number of mental health problems globally and in the Philippines, particularly among young adults, demands not just low-cost but effective and science-based interventions. This was especially needed in places where traditional mental health interventions were not easily accessible. This study reported the conceptualization, expert validation, and piloting feasibility and usability of a digital mental health literacy program for Filipino college students.MethodsThe development of the program was composed of three distinct but connected phases using the methodological framework of the Theory of Change model. Phase 1involved the development of program through the collaboration of university-based mental health professionals. The program was grounded in a causal model of the relationship between mental health literacy, help-seeking behavior, and psychological well-being among Filipino college students, and the mental health literacy framework of Anthony Jorm [1]. In phase 2, experts in the field validated the program’s content using a researcher-made survey questionnaire. Phase 3 is pilot testing of the program to 10 participants; a focus group discussion was conducted to document user experiences, feasibility and usability of the program.ResultsThe program was a 12-module program with a 6-week duration and integrated three key digital components: psychoeducational videos, online group forums, and self-help modules. Thematic analysis was used in focus group discussions with 10 participants of the pilot study. Expert validation indicated high levels of content relevance and clarity, suggesting strong alignment with the objectives of the program. Three themes emerged in the thematic analysis of the focus group discussion: enhanced self-awareness, perceived program efficacy, usability, and barriers to engagement. Participants reported improved awareness and vocabulary regarding mental and emotional health due to its structured design. Participants also described the platform of the program as user-friendly, particularly appreciating its web-based and mobile app availability. On the other hand, recommendations for improvements were also identified, such as increasing the duration of the program, adding more personalized and interactive features, and ensuring the availability of peer support.ConclusionThe findings provided preliminary evidence on the feasibility and usability of the digital mental health literacy program for Filipino college students. The pilot results indicated strong potential for implementation and further testing. Future studies with larger samples and more rigorous research designs were needed to examine the effectiveness of the program on mental health outcomes such as help-seeking behavior and psychological well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04695-z.

Introduction

Recent years have witnessed increasing interest focusing on the well-being and mental health needs of college students [2]. Mental health issues among college students have entered greater awareness as an increasing number of students report experiencing distress, depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, addictive behaviors, and traumatic experiences [3–6]. Most mental disorders manifest during the developmental stages of childhood and adolescence [7]. College students are also at more risk for mental health problems than other age groups [8]. In 2023, approximately 76% of college students reported having various psychological issues. Of these, 36% experienced symptoms of anxiety, and 28% have reported symptoms of depression among students [9].

In the Philippines, according to the Department of Health (DOH), over 3.6 million Filipinos suffer from mental disorders, with more than 1.1 million suffering from depression and 1.3 million persons having substance use disorders, with more than 50% having co-occurring mental illness [10]. Even though suicide rates are rising in the Philippines, mental health prevention and services are not common [11]. At the onset of the pandemic, a significant segment of college students in the Philippines suffered from anxiety and depression, with approximately 25% reporting moderate to severe anxiety and 16% reporting moderate to severe depression [12]. In addition, there has been a concerning rise in suicide rates among Filipino adolescents, particularly college students, over the years [13]. Depression is the most prevalent condition, with 3.3 million cases, followed by anxiety with 3 million cases (WHO, 2017).

In designing effective, relevant, and reliable interventions, understanding the Filipino psycho-social context is a prerequisite. Identifying the protective factors and barriers that help and inhibit mental health help-seeking can be a formula for creating an effective intervention. In the Filipino culture, barriers such as social stigma, loss of face (amor propio), and feelings of shame (hiya) are important factors that should be examined, as they significantly impede help-seeking [14]. Moreover, Filipino values such as social conformity (pakikisama) and self-reliance add to the mental health stigma that discourages individuals from seeking help until the situation gets worse [14]. These Filipino values, together with awareness barriers, may impede Filipinos from pursuing psychological support and seeking help from informal or formal sources. Negative perceptions and derogatory labels contribute to the stigmatization of mental health issues [14–18]. Additionally, cultural factors such as guilt, stigma, and collectivism influence Filipinos’ preference for folk healers and lay ministers for mental disorder treatment [19]. Efforts to improve mental health care utilization in the Philippines have focused on changing public attitudes towards seeking professional help [20, 21].

The advent of a digital intervention (in which digital technology, for example, PCs or smartphones, can be accessed in the form of a program) presents a strategic way to overcome these cultural barriers [22]. The anonymity and privacy of digital learning provide students with the opportunity to attain critical mental health literacy (MHL) privately from the fear of social exposure or embarrassment of being noticed publicly in distress [23]. Researchers consider this secure, self-paced path to learning as essential to decreasing self-stigmatization long before the person is made to consider engaging in public search for help [24]. This is why its validation of high cultural sensitivity after implementation is so critical to the program’s success [22].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has the Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan for 2013–2030 in place, which aims for 80% of countries to have at least three operational national programs by 2030, which concentrate on the promotion and prevention of mental health problems [25]. Programs should address the specific needs of vulnerable populations, such as mental health literacy, anti-stigmatization initiatives, and school-based programs for the promotion and prevention of this health problem [25]. Strengthening mental health first aid and stigma reduction is the core objective of MHL [26–28]. MHL programs target improving individuals’ understanding and utilization of healthcare-related information, especially in the context of mental health illnesses, treatment adherence, and the ability to understand when and how to access help [29–31]. MHL programs are designed to improve individuals’ understanding and use of healthcare information, focusing on understanding mental health illnesses, enhancing treatment adherence, and promoting awareness regarding when and how to seek help. Enhancing MHL among adolescents can be a viable approach to address obstacles related to seeking help, effectively manage mental health, enhance the effectiveness of help-Seeking behavior (HSB), and swiftly intervene in deteriorating mental health conditions [32–35]. The educational institution has emerged as a promising environment for enhancing adolescents’ MHL. There is a limited availability of research on MHL among college students in the Philippines, along with obstacles to seeking assistance and implementing effective strategies.

This study aimed to conceptualize, develop, validate, and pilot test the Digital Mental Health Literacy Program (DMHLP) — a digital intervention grounded in empirical evidence and customized to address the cultural and contextual factors affecting mental health literacy, help-seeking behavior, and psychological well-being among Filipino college students.

The study is also a specific response to a global public health concern on mental health problems/issues among youth. WHO’s Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030 has offered a critical roadmap to member states [36]. This strategy plan, as broadened to align with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, emphasizes a life-course vision of mental health and retains its four main goals: effective governance and leadership, comprehensive and integrated care, promotion and prevention implementation, and information system strengthening [25]. As inspired and guided by this policy, DMHLP is intended to be an innovative, scalable, and community-based mental health intervention. The program directly assists the achievement of the WHO’s strategic priorities through a national, multisectoral approach involving guidance counselors, student affairs personnel, and school psychologists [36]. This study is an effort to implement and expand mental health services based on global health mandates in developing countries, where resources are often limited, especially for a high-risk group – Filipino college students [37].

Materials and methods

Research design

The study was a mixed-method study that comprised three distinct but connected phases. The theory of change (ToC), specifically the proposed steps of Vogel (2012) in Ecosystem Services for Poverty (ESPA) Alleviation Program [38], served as the foundation and guide in the development and evaluation of the digital mental health literacy program (DMHLP).

Phase 1: a. development and structure of the DMHLP

Filipino mental health professionals in a higher educational institution such as licensed psychologist, psychometrician, and guidance counselors, collaborated in developing the contents of the DMHLP. The contents of the DMHLP were developed using theoretical frameworks, existing mental health literacy models, empirical studies, national and international guidelines, and prior digital interventions as key references. The modules of DMHLP were based mainly on the mental health literacy framework of Anthony Jorm [1], and prior study of the causal relationship among of mental health literacy (MHL) help-seeking behavior (HSB) and psychological well-being (PWB) among Filipino college students [39]. Studies on Filipino college students’ cultural values, traits, and experiences were also used as references in the asynchronous and synchronous discussion [40–43]. The structure of the DMHLP consisted of three components: (1) psychoeducational videos, (2) group discussions led by facilitators in an online setting, and (3) online modules designed to assist individuals in seeking help electronically. The effectiveness these components was demonstrated in previous research studies [44–48]. An IT professional specializing in web development created a website (http://clsuosa-elevate.com) that served as the platform for implementing DMHLP. It was designed on user-friendliness, interactivity, and cultural sensitivity.

Phase 1: b. delivery of the DMHLP

The DMHLP intervention was available online through the clsuosa-elevate.com website and the Moodle mobile application. Both platforms offered the same content and functions. The participants accessed the modules asynchronously within a given time frame. The course included twelve chapters and ran for six weeks. Two chapters were posted each week, and participants studied both chapters during that week. Each chapter included a short video lecture lasting about seven to eight minutes, lecture notes, and a summary infographic. After every two chapters, the participants completed a reflection activity and a quiz. They needed a score of at least 80% on the quiz to move to the next set of chapters. Participants could not skip modules. They completed each module and pass the weekly assessment before they moved to the next two chapters on the following week. Two sessions of online group discussion were facilitated synchronously after the third and sixth week of the intervention. The discussions focused on the common issues encountered by Filipino college students and strategies to address these issues.

Phase 2: expert validation

Expert validation characterized Phase 2 to further validate the program’s goals and objectives alignment, cultural appropriateness, and educational adequacy. Experts used a researcher-made survey questionnaire to examine content, identify ambiguous or culturally mismatched aspects, and provide quantitative scores for revision. This step strengthened the validity, reliability, and theoretical underpinnings of the program, resulting in an expert-validated DMHLP ready for pilot implementation.

Phase 3: pilot testing

Phase 3 involved pilot testing and focus group discussion (FGD) with student participants to assess the usability, clarity, and appropriateness of the program. This led to revisions based on feedback regarding language, design, and delivery. The final product was a user-tested and culturally responsive DMHLP, which can be administered to larger sample, as follows in Fig. 1.

The ToC framework acted as a strategic roadmap for how and why a complex intervention was expected to work, and what conditions has to be met for success to occur. For the DMHLP, this architecture facilitated the description of how the inputs—the 12 modules delivered over 6 weeks through the Central Luzon State University Elevating Leadership, Engagement and Virtues for Academic and Transforming Excellence website (CLSU ELEVATE) — made sense to lead to the outcomes sought. The importance of the ToC framework meant that underlying assumptions concerning the operational context, for example, students having sufficient time and digital access to engage fully, became evident. The challenges in implementation, as shown by subsequent pilot findings, served as necessary feedback for refining the initial ToC assumptions, thereby confirming that this rigorous development process was needed.

Participants

In phase 1, the content of the DMHLP was developed in collaboration with the five registered guidance counselors and student affairs personnel, a behavioral scientist, a registered psychometrician, and a registered psychologist. An IT professional created the website that served as the platform for the implementation of the DMHLP. In phase 2, the validators of the DMHLP were three mental health professionals who were experts in program development and were affiliated in higher education institutions. They evaluated the structure and content of DMHLP using the researcher-made questionnaire.

In phase 3, participants were recruited through an advertisement/invitation posted on the Facebook page of the Office of Student Affairs of Central Luzon State University. Twenty-seven students were enlisted and took the MHL scale. Ten students, five 3rd year and five 4th year college students, were qualified to become participants of the study as they were vulnerable to mental health problems, as evidenced by their low level of MHL scale scores (below the 50th percentile). Face-to-face meeting/orientation with the participants was conducted by the research staff at the Office of Student Affairs before the implementation of the DMHLP. The researchers discussed the program, the access to the platforms, data privacy and their rights as participants. The participants received the printed version inform consent form and information about the study prior to their participation. An internet allowance was also provided to the participants of the study.

Instruments

The DMHLP, questionnaire and interview tool used in the study were specifically developed for this study and had not been used or previously published elsewhere (Supplementary material S1). The expert validation questionnaire was a researcher-made questionnaire based on Utilization-Focused Evaluation questionnaire of Patton [49]. The questionnaire focused on the usefulness of the intervention to its intended users. The expert validators assessed the content of the program and the platform using the following factors: (1) Content Accuracy and Relevance, (2) Educational Effectiveness, (3) Cultural Sensitivity and Inclusivity, (4) User Experience and Accessibility, (5) Engagement and Interactivity (6) Alignment with Program Goals and Objectives, (7) Privacy and Data Security (8) Feedback and Improvement (Supplementary material S2). The data gathered from this procedure were utilized to enhance the program.

In phase 3, an unstructured interview with follow-up and probing questions was the main instrument that was used in the FGD (Supplementary material S3). A consent form was given to the participants before the FGD to ensure that they understood the purpose, confidentiality, and voluntary nature of the discussion. A demographic information sheet was also administered to collect participants’ basic details such as age, gender, and course. The facilitator of the FGD used an audio recorder to record the full discussion and an accurate transcription, and used an observation notes sheet to record non-verbal cues and contextual factors.

Data collection

The DMHLP was developed in collaboration with mental health professionals affiliated with a university. The DMHLP was guided by the theory of change, empirical and theoretical studies. An IT professional specializing in web development created a website (http://clsuosa-elevate.com) that served as the platform for implementing DMHLP. It was designed with a focus on user-friendliness, interactivity, and cultural sensitivity.

Three experts in program development and supervision served as the validators and assessed the structure and content of DMHLP using the researcher-made questionnaire. The validators were given access to the website to personally experience and assess the DMHLP. Before the implementation of the DMHLP in a larger population, it was initially administered to a group of 10 participants who had the same characteristics as the sample for which the final program is designed. The trial run was intended to verify the instructions for administering and completing the module, as well as to assess the projected time allowances. Additionally, it helped uncover any ambiguities in the program content.

Data analysis

Following the study of Curran and Hussong, the current study used integrative analysis in phase 1 [50]. The content creators combined the data from the concept of Jorm [1] of mental health literacy (MHL) and the study of Mananggit et al. [39] on the causal relationship model of MHL, HSB and PWB to gain a more comprehensive understanding of MHL and eventually create the content for DMHLP. Descriptive analysis, such as mean and standard deviation, was used to analyze the data from the expert validators in phase 2. The data were interpreted using a weighted mean with a class interval of 0.80, following the descriptive interpretation for Likert-type scales [51]. Lastly, thematic analysis was used to analyze and interpret the patterns of meaning and theme from the data that were gathered in the focus group discussion in phase 3.

Results

The DMHLP was structured as a 12-chapter program delivered over 6 weeks via a newly created website, CLSU ELEVATE (https://clsuosa-elevate.com). Each week, participants were expected to complete two chapters. The program was designed to be user-friendly, interactive, and culturally sensitive. The first component included psychoeducational videos, with approximately 7 to 8 min per chapter. The approach of video-based interventions with some form of storyline was more engaging and better disseminated than traditional ones, hence serving as a promising tool to enhance MHL. Such videos communicated key concepts, theories, and examples in a way that the young adult group relates to well.

The second element of the program included online group discussions led by professionally trained experts holding master’s degrees in either psychology, and guidance and counseling. This component used a synchronous learning format where participants engaged in structured discussions. The discussion allowed the participants to establish social support networks, and share their experiences, reflection, ask questions and deepen their understanding on the different concepts of mental health literacy presented in the program. Online group discussion had two scheduled sessions during the end of the 3rd and 6th week of the intervention. The topics were focused on the common issues encountered by Filipino college students, and how to address them. This was intended to connect their mental health knowledge in real life student situations and build confidence in applying it to daily decisions.

The third component of the intervention was made up of online self-help modules, consisting of lecture notes, quizzes, infographics, and brochures. Matheson et al. demonstrated that web-based help-seeking through online support groups and self-help increased empowerment. These modules were designed to supplement, not replace, human-delivered sessions and were intentionally self-directed and self-paced to foster an empowering approach.

This multifaceted strategy for MHL in the context of knowledge acquisition, social skills, and self-efficacy development represented an important methodological strength of this intervention, as seen in Tables 1 and 2.

Phase 2 of the DMHLP was the expert validation process. This was conducted to evaluate the program’s quality, relevance, and methodological integrity. Results from assessments conducted by experienced guidance counselors and psychologists strongly endorsed the program’s design and content.

The assessment across 7 categories and 21 items resulted in high scores, with averages between 4.67 and 5.0 (excellent). The DMHLP obtained a perfect score of 5.0 regarding accuracy and relevance, and alignment with program goals, confirming its effectiveness in meeting targeted learning outcomes. The privacy and data security category got an average score of 4.89. Categories such as educational effectiveness, cultural sensitivity, and inclusivity, user experience and accessibility, and engagement and interactivity often surpassed the standards with a score of 4.67. Summary of emerging themes from the focus group discussion after DMHLP pilot testing in phase 3

Phase 3 was a pilot study of the DMHLP with 10 participants, followed by an FGD. It was analyzed using thematic analysis. The qualitative feedback provided valuable operational insights. This confirmed the benefits from the program while understanding key barriers to sustained engagement. There are three major themes were identified in the data.

The first emergent theme confirmed the positive impact of the MHL component. Participants highlighted increased ability to identify and communicate their mental and emotional states. The structured program provided a framework and vocabulary about MHL, mental disorders, self-treatments, different mental health professionals, as well as cultural barriers that challenged the pre-existing concepts of the participants. The second theme confirmed prior expert validation ratings regarding perceived effectiveness, relevance, and user-friendly design of the DMHLP. The accessibility and interactivity of the web format of DMHLP helped the participants to easily navigate the platform, allowing them to easily relate to the program modules. The last theme was barriers to engagement and future improvement. Overall, the participants were satisfied with the content and usability of DMHLP but experienced barriers to regular use. Time constraints from academic and extracurricular pressures are some of the major obstacles to sustaining interest in the program. The 6-week module structure of the DMHLP clashed with the participants’ availability, as they were also fulfilling their school responsibilities during the pilot study. This rigidity and inflexibility may pose challenges in the implementation of DMHLP on a larger scale and may threaten the effectiveness of the structural core of the program.

Discussion

In a resource-limited country like the Philippines, digital platforms were recognized as a practical and effective strategy in the proliferation of mental health education [52–54]. The development, validation, and pilot testing of the digital mental health literacy program (DMHLP) provided preliminary insights from a pilot feasibility and usability evaluation of a culturally sensitive and responsive mental health intervention for Filipino college students. The validation scores among experts showed strong acceptability and perceived relevance, which was also consistent with the previous studies that culturally modified mental health services improve engagement and outcomes among young adults [22, 32].

Knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors toward mental health care were the key factors of the mental health literacy (MHL) framework of Anthony Jorm [1], which served as the foundation of the DMHLP [26]. The program also utilized empirical studies on the relationship of MHL on help-seeking behavior (HSB) and psychological well-being (PWB), wherein increased MHL led to improved HSB and PWB [28, 35, 39]. The increased self-awareness and emotional vocabulary exhibited by participants suggested that the psychoeducational components of the program supported the ability to identify and label emotions, a key capability associated with emotional regulation and early intervention [55]. Such observations were consistent with research indicating that emotional expression was a mediator of adaptive coping and mental well-being [56, 57].

The second newly developed theme, program effectiveness and usability, embodied the worldwide development of digital mental health interventions as powerful tools that contributed to literacy and help-seeking. Previous research also supported that video-based education, online forums for group discussion, and self-help exercises enhanced engagement, knowledge retention, and self-efficacy [44, 46, 58]. Positive reception of the multimedia features of DMHLP was consistent with research, which showed that interactive and easily related formats facilitated cognitive and affective learning in adolescents [48]. However, participants’ stated difficulties at operationalizing what had learned in real-life stressors were consistent with previous findings that digital psychoeducation without structured behavioral components may limit the translation of knowledge into practice [59]. This aligned with a set of recommendations to embed skills-based practice and personalized feedback in digital literacy interventions [23, 60].

The third theme - barriers to engagement, presented a recurring issue in the implementation of digital mental health interventions. Time pressures, educational load, and limited social support were among the most abundant challenges for low adherence. Some studies found similar obstacles in many digital well-being programs [61, 62]. Flexibility and opportunities for interaction with fellow participants appeared to be important factors for engagement. Findings also indicated the importance of peer-driven forums or “online peer advocates” for adherence, for social support, and for reducing isolation among participants [24, 63]. Future applications of DMHLP woud benefit from adaptive pacing, personalized features, and structured peer-mentoring to increase completion rates.

Cultural responsiveness emerged as one of the strongest dimensions of DMHLP, corroborated by high expert ratings. Filipino attitudes toward mental health care were influenced by collectivism, religiosity, and stigma related to mental illness [19, 64]. Culturally familiar examples, Filipino-language features, and peer-related narratives were used to increase relevance and acceptability in accordance with best practices in cross-cultural program design [65, 66]. This domestic adaptation responded to evidence that Western models of MHL had little applicability in collectivist societies, where interpersonal harmony and shame avoidance influenced help-seeking behavior [14, 17].

The mixed-method design of the study allowed researchers to have a comprehensive view the program during its initial implementation. Quantitative evaluation established its technical as well as pedagogical credentials, and qualitative feedback provided an in-depth understanding of the use in the field. This approach was consistent with evidence that hybrid assessments offered insight into the feasibility and usability of digital interventions [67]. In addition, the cooperation of mental health professionals, IT developers, and students’ affairs personnel was aligned with the multidisciplinary perspective towards the development of digital health programs, a perspective promoted by the WHO in its Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030 [25].

Collectively, DMHLP offered a culturally specific digital intervention designed to promote mental health literacy among Filipino college students. The findings of this study were interpreted as preliminary given the small number of the participants in the pilot phase. The results did not provide conclusive evidence of effectiveness of the DMHLP. Instead, the findings highlighted the feasibility, usability, and potential scalability of the intervention within higher education settings.

Future large-scale implementation was recommended to utilize randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to study the effect of DMHLP on MHL, HSB, and PWB, as emphasized in digital intervention guidelines [68]. Analysis of adherence rates, time-on-platform, and interaction frequency was recommended to identify a model for optimal engagement. The integration of peer advocates and facilitators into DMHLP was suggested to strengthen participant engagement and program sustainability. Such developments allowed a stronger evaluation of DMHLP and assessed its potential as a scalable model for enhancing MHL in the Philippines and other Southeast Asian educational institutions.

Recommendation

The digital mental health literacy program (DMHLP) demonstrated preliminary promise as a culturally responsive mental health intervention for Filipino college students. Given the pilot nature of the study and the small sample, several barriers needed to be addressed before implementation in a larger population. First, the extension of the duration of DMHLP to 10–12 weeks, with the facility for self-paced modes of learning, was considered more useful for participants to engage in the program without compromising their school commitments. Second, engagement can be enhanced by promoting peer interactions within a more adaptable context. A hybrid approach employing trained peer advocates to facilitate a healthy conversation and monitor high risk participants may strengthen the feasibility and usability of the intervention. Third, full-scale administration of DMHLP focused on assessing the effectiveness of the intervention in enhancing the help-seeking behavior and psychological well-being of Filipino college students.

Lastly, the present findings were interpreted as preliminary and did not provide conclusive evidence of effectiveness. Future studies were recommended to involve a randomized controlled trial assessing the impact of knowledge enhancement and psychological empowerment on behavior change. In addition, intervention fidelity measures such as engagement, adherence, and completion rates should be systematically examined in subsequent evaluations.

Strengths and limitations

The DMHLP was developed as a culturally tailored mental health intervention for Filipino college students. The multi-phase mixed-methods design, encompassing expert validation and pilot testing, ensured the program’s content quality and contextual relevance. It promoted accessibility and user experience as it highlights digitalization in program intervention. Strengths and areas for improvement were documented using different feedback mechanisms. However, the small sample size in the pilot testing, the absence of a control group, a short time evaluation, potential selection bias, and assumptions about the digital platform were acknowledged as limitations. These limits the generalizability of the findings and made it difficult to attribute the outcomes to the intervention alone. The recommendation of the respondents to improve the personalization and interactive features of the program suggested that the current version may not yet be fully responsive to the needs of the users. Further improvement must be applied to the program based on its documented limitations and the suggestions of the participants. Larger-scale and controlled research may be needed to assess the efficacy of the program.

Conclusion

DMHLP aimed to fill an identified gap in mental health services through a culturally sensitive and responsive mental health literacy program for Filipino college students. It placed emphasis on the need for structured data, cultural sensitivity, and contextually relevant mental health knowledge. In addressing identified hindrances such as time constraints and the need for personalization, flexibility, peer engagement, and involvement, the DMHLP demonstrated preliminary feasibility and usability as an adaptable model for the promotion of mental health literacy in low-resourced higher educational institutions. The findings from this study were interpreted as preliminary due to the limited sample size and pilot nature of the evaluation. Nevertheless, the program shows potential for scalability and wider implementation and warranted further testing through larger and more rigorous evaluations to determine its effectiveness.
