Effectiveness of a video-assisted teaching program on menstrual cup usage among women (18–45 years age group) in selected colleges of Patna
Authors: K. Lakshmi Prasanna, Sangam Jha, Naveen Kumar Sharma, Jyoti Rani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Menstrual cups are a sustainable alternative to traditional menstrual products, yet their usage remains low in many regions. In India, only 0.3% of women use menstrual cups, representing a lack of knowledge and a negative attitude towards the use of menstrual cups. This study aimed to promote menstrual cup usage and develop the right attitude among women (18–45 years old) in selected colleges in Patna.Material and Methods:One group pre-test-post-test quasi-experimental study was conducted in selected colleges in Patna with a nonrandomized convenient sampling technique employed to select 100 participants. The intervention consisted of a 30-minute video session on menstrual cup usage, and a structured questionnaire administered pre- and post-intervention to assess knowledge and attitudes.Results:Post-intervention analysis showed a significant increase in knowledge and positive attitudes towards menstrual cup usage. The video-assisted teaching program was found effective in enhancing knowledge regarding menstrual cup usage, as evidenced by the mean knowledge score, which improved from 7.25 + 1.89 to 8.40 + 1.43, as well as the attitude mean score, which improved from 32.14 + 8.50 to 33.91 + 7.88.Conclusion:The video-assisted teaching program effectively enhanced knowledge and positively influenced attitudes toward menstrual cup usage among women in the targeted age group. This suggests that similar educational interventions could be beneficial in promoting sustainable menstrual hygiene practices. Many participants suggested that if it is added through government schemes, it will be more valuable in promoting the adoption of menstrual cups among women.

Introduction

Every month, millions of women around the world undergo menstruation. This transitional phase from childhood to womanhood is a fundamental aspect of female life. In lower and middle-income countries, many girls encounter feelings of fear, confusion, shame, and discomfort due to societal issues and a lack of guidance as they navigate their monthly periods.[12]

A menstrual cup is a reusable feminine hygiene product, typically a small, flexible funnel-shaped cup made of rubber or silicone. It is inserted into the vagina to collect period fluid. With a higher capacity to hold blood compared to other methods. Depending on individual flow, menstrual cups can be worn for up to 12 hours. Reusable cup brands include Keeper Cup, Moon Cup, Lunette Menstrual Cup, Diva Cup, Lena Cup, and Lily Cup. Additionally, there are disposable options available on the market, such as the Instead Soft cup.[345]

According to a national health survey (NFHS-5), just 0.3% of women aged 15–24 in India use menstrual cups. Formal education is seen as making little difference to women’s menstrual knowledge and beliefs. Awareness of menstrual cups (and other reusable products) is negligible not only in developing countries like India but also in developed countries like the UK.[6]

In India, the lack of widespread understanding about menstrual cups is largely due to their limited market reach, which has prevented them from becoming a mainstream product. This relatively small market size has discouraged multinational companies from introducing menstrual cup products, as they typically seek larger market opportunities before launching new products. Consequently, the modest demand for menstrual cups has hindered their broader availability and promotion in India.[57]

It’s worth noting that menstrual hygiene management (MHM) holds a crucial place within the Swachh Bharat Mission Guidelines (SBM-G). The MHM Guideline, issued by the Ministry of Drinking Water and Sanitation in December 2015, aims to provide support for adolescent girls and women in managing their menstrual hygiene effectively.[89]

As per the report of Water Aid India and the Menstrual Hygiene Alliance of India (2018), an estimated 12 billion sanitary napkins are being generated as menstrual waste annually by 121 million menstruating women using sanitary napkins for menstruation management. Sanitary pads are manufactured using materials such as super-absorbent polymers (SAP), plastic, glue, etc., which may take up to 500 to 800 years to decompose.[9]

Menstrual cups offer a significant reduction in menstrual waste as they are reusable, unlike traditional sanitary pads and tampons, making them an ecofriendly choice. Additionally, their long-term usability, spanning five years or more, renders them a more economical option.[810]

In the realm of women’s health, menstrual hygiene management stands as a cornerstone of well-being and empowerment. Yet, despite the array of options available, misconceptions and limited awareness often shroud potentially beneficial alternatives such as menstrual cups. In this context, the need for targeted educational interventions becomes evident. This study embarks on a journey to explore the efficacy of a video-assisted teaching programme aimed at enlightening women aged 18–45 about the remarkable benefits of menstrual cups. By instilling knowledge and fostering positive attitudes towards menstrual cups in this impressionable cohort, we aim not only to enhance women’s well-being but also to spark a ripple effect of informed choices and advocacy across wider communities.[1112]

In India, menstrual cups, despite their long existence, are not widely recognised compared to the widespread adoption of disposable sanitary napkins, particularly prevalent in developing nations. Constructed from medical-grade silicone, menstrual cups are primarily intended for multiple uses. Unlike traditional feminine hygiene products that absorb menstrual flow, menstrual cups function by collecting them, thereby reducing the risk of bacterial infections such as toxic shock syndrome.[10131415]

Educational interventions play a crucial role in promoting the adoption of menstrual cups by dispelling myths, providing accurate information, and addressing concerns. Video-assisted teaching programs have shown promise in health education by enhancing understanding and retention of information. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of a video-assisted teaching program on menstrual cup usage among women aged 18–45 in selected colleges in Patna.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This quasi-experimental study with one group pretest-post-test design was conducted to assess the effectiveness of a video-assisted teaching program on knowledge and attitude regarding menstrual cups among women aged 18–45 years from selected colleges in Patna. Bihar, India.

Participants

The study population comprised 100 women aged 18-45 years from selected colleges in Patna. Participants were recruited through a nonprobability convenient sampling technique. The inclusion criteria included women who had not previously used menstrual cups and were willing to participate in the educational intervention. Exclusion criteria included women with medical conditions contraindicating the use of menstrual cups.

Intervention

The intervention consisted of a 30-minute video-assisted teaching program that covered various aspects of menstrual cup use, including benefits, insertion and removal techniques, cleaning and maintenance, and myth-busting. The video was developed in collaboration with gynecologists and public health experts to ensure accurate and comprehensive content. The session was delivered in a classroom setting by trained facilitators.

The study was done from January 24, 2024, to April 27, 2024. The sample size calculation is based on the previous study done by Krushna S. et al.[16]

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered before (pretest) and after (post-test) the intervention through an online Google form after obtaining informed consent. The tool comprised 3 sections, which include sociodemographic variables (7 items), a knowledge questionnaire (10 items), and a 5-point Likert attitude scale (10 items). The questionnaire was validated by a panel of experts in women’s health. The reliability of the questionnaire was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85.

The video-assisted teaching program was delivered in the lecture hall. At the end of the session, doubts were clarified by the trained facilitators. On the 7th day after intervention, the post-test was conducted through an online Google form. Out of 100 participants, 7 dropouts and 93 participants filled out the online Google form in the post-test.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic data. Paired t-tests were conducted to compare pretest and post-test scores, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. The knowledge questionnaire has a maximum score of 10 and 50 on the attitude scale.

Ethical considerations and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee (Ref. No. AIIMS/Pat/IEC/2022/948), and informed consent was obtained from participants through Google Forms and assured of confidentiality.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

A total of 100 participants completed the pretest. The majority of participants were 18–24 (95%) years old, with more than 82% having Hindu religion, 96% being undergraduates, 95% being medical students, 88% being unmarried, a monthly family income between Rs. 5,000 and Rs. 10,000 (40%), and a source of health information from family and friends (53%) [Table 1].

Pre- and postinterventional levels of knowledge among participants

Participants reported 20 (20%) inadequate, 34 (34%) moderate, and 46 (46%) adequate level of knowledge before intervention. The level of knowledge reported was 4 (4.3%) inadequate, 13 (13.97%) moderate, and 76 (81.72%) adequate in postintervention. The mean pretest knowledge score was 7.25 + 1.89. Following the video-assisted teaching program, the mean post-test knowledge score significantly increased to 8.40 + 1.43. The paired t-test analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in knowledge scores (t = 4.143), indicating the effectiveness of the educational intervention [Table 2].

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight the effectiveness of video-assisted teaching programs in promoting awareness and acceptance of menstrual cups among women in college settings. By addressing knowledge gaps, dispelling myths, and providing practical guidance, educational interventions can empower women to make informed choices about menstrual hygiene management.

The study underscores the importance of implementing educational initiatives targeted at promoting sustainable menstrual hygiene practices, particularly in regions with limited access to resources and information. Future research should explore the long-term impact of educational interventions on menstrual cup adoption and menstrual health outcomes.

In our study, in the pretest, 46% of participants reported having adequate knowledge. The mean knowledge score is 7.25 + 1.89. About menstrual cups, 31% expressed an adequate attitude, the mean attitude score is 32.14 + 8.50 regarding menstrual cup use. In the post-test, 81.72% had adequate knowledge, and 38.71% reported an adequate attitude towards their uses. Post-test mean knowledge and attitude scores are (8.40 + 1.43) and 33.91 + 7.88, respectively.

The finding is supported by the study of Krushna Sone et al.[8] regarding the effectiveness of a video-assisted teaching program on knowledge regarding menstrual cups among girls. In the pretest, 18 (36%) girls had poor, 26 (52%) had average, and only 6 (12%) had good knowledge regarding menstrual cups, whereas in the post-test 7 (14%) had average and 43 (86%) had good knowledge regarding menstrual cups. The study showed that the mean post-test knowledge score (23.68) was higher than the mean pretest knowledge score (12.74). So, the study result highlighted that the video-assisted teaching program was highly effective in increasing knowledge and attitude among the participants.

A statistically significant association was found between younger age, educational status, socioeconomic status, marital status of females in the reproductive age group, and knowledge about the menstrual cup as per the study of Sudevan Devan GM et al.[17] on Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices Regarding Menstrual Cup Among Females in an Urban Setting of South Kerala. However, in our study, there is no association between the pretest knowledge score and all sociodemographic variables. So, we can tell that the knowledge of the participants does not depend on socioeconomic status. During menstruation, there is a greater chance of infection related to vaginal bleeding associated with long-term use of clothes, tampons or sanitary napkins. So, we can improve the knowledge of women regarding the use of menstrual cups during the menstrual phase, which is ecofriendly and also reduces the chance of genital tract infection. This ultimately results in a decrease in women’s morbidity and mortality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this study support the effectiveness of video-assisted teaching programs in enhancing knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding menstrual cup usage among women aged 18–45 in selected colleges in Patna. By providing evidence-based information and dispelling misconceptions, such interventions have the potential to promote the uptake of menstrual cups and contribute to improving menstrual hygiene management and women’s health outcomes.

In this digital era, the study suggests that there is a need to promote more video-assisted health education programs as it is beneficial to women, especially those who are too shy to speak and clear their doubts.[18]
