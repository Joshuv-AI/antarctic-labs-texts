Nudging provider adoption of clinical decision support: study protocol for a stepped-wedge cluster randomized, hybrid type III trial of an electronic health record-agnostic pulmonary embolism risk tool
Authors: Sundas Khan, Ynhi Thomas, Nidhi Garg, Nicholas Genes, Gregory W. Simon, Charles M. Cleland, Usman Mir, Katherine L. Dauber-Decker, Jeffrey N. Solomon, Amelia Shunk, Lynn Xu, Angela Mastrianni, Yuhan Cui, Natalie Henning, Michael A. Diefenbach, Thomas McGinn, Safiya Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbout one-third of the computed tomography (CT) scans ordered yearly to evaluate for pulmonary embolism (PE) in emergency departments (ED) in the U.S. are avoidable. Clinical guidelines recommend the use of validated PE prediction rules which reduce CT scan ordering without an increase in missed PEs, but there is low provider adoption. Clinical decision support (CDS) that incorporates these rules along with nudges (subtle, non-coercive influences on decision-making) may improve provider adoption. In our pilot trial of a PE risk CDS tool with a nudge at order entry, adoption was significantly higher (39.1%) than for the tool without nudges (20.7%). The tool was developed on an EHR-agnostic web-based platform, designed for dissemination to work with any EHR. The objective of this study is to evaluate the tool with a nudge in a multi-site, randomized trial.MethodsA hybrid type III, stepped-wedge, ED-level, cluster randomized trial will be conducted. Study settings include 12 EDs in three geographically diverse health care systems. The EDs will be randomized over 9 steps over 30 months, ensuring at least 3 months of intervention exposure for all EDs. We will randomize matched pairs of EDs based on patient volume in each stratum to early intervention or late intervention. All providers ordering CTs for the evaluation of PE in adult patients at the site EDs will be included. Our study will be guided by two complementary frameworks: Behavioral Change Wheel Framework and Proctor’s Implementation Outcomes Framework. Nudge implementation strategies in the CDS user interface will be a peer comparison of providers’ hit rates for CTs ordered for PE and salient messaging. We will use an EHR-agnostic, web-based platform to implement the tool. The primary outcome will be guideline-concordant CT ordering for PE.DiscussionThis trial will advance our understanding of the impact of behavioral strategies on improving provider adoption of CDS. Additionally, the trial will confirm the impact of guideline-concordant CT ordering on CT yield rates across a diverse patient population. The use of an EHR-agnostic platform helps maximize the dissemination potential of the core evidence-based practices it facilitates.Trial registrationNCT07249385; Registered 11/18/2025.

Background

A pulmonary embolism (PE) is a blood clot that typically originates in the leg, travels to the pulmonary artery and blocks blood flow to the lungs [1]. Perceptions of the risk of missing the diagnosis of PE and associated mortality have lagged behind the evidence, leading to over testing, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment [2]. PEs are rarely missed in the emergency department (ED) [3, 4]. Provider perceptions of risk of a missed diagnosis have largely been based on studies done in the 1940s with significant methodologic pitfalls and little relevance to modern emergency medicine [5]. Since the landmark Prospective Investigation of Pulmonary Embolism Diagnosis (PIOPED) II trial established computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CT) imaging as the diagnostic standard, CT utilization during emergency visits has increased with diagnostic yield declining [6]. Approximately one-third of all CT studies for PE are now estimated to be avoidable [7].

The use of clinical prediction rules, or PE risk scores, to assess pre-test probability before CT reduces testing by 25% without any missed PEs [3, 8]. Clinical prediction rules are validated tools that quantify the contributions the history, physical, and laboratory results make towards a diagnosis [9]. PE risk scores classify patients into low and/or intermediate and high probability of PE based on key elements of the history and physical exam, establishing pre-test probability before imaging. They recommend the use of a laboratory value, the d-dimer assay, to rule out PE in low and intermediate risk patients. Providers can consider stopping additional workup for imaging in these patients if the d-dimer is negative. Several professional society guidelines recommend their use for PE risk stratification, including the American College of Chest Physicians [2, 10–12].

In phase 1 of our preliminary work, we sought to develop and evaluate a clinical decision support (CDS) tool for PE evaluation that incorporated a well-validated clinical prediction rule, or PE risk score, the Wells’ Criteria for PE [13].13 Providers entering any electronic order for the diagnosis of PE (i.e., d-dimer, ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan, or CT) were routed to the tool if they answered yes to a dialog box asking, “Are you considering PE?”. For low-risk patients, the tool only allowed providers to order d-dimer testing and for intermediate or high risk of PE the tool allowed for d-dimer testing, V/Q scan, or CT imaging. The tool could be dismissed at any time and any order could be placed by the provider. The tool was found to be efficacious with an increase in CT yield from 8% to 12% (a 37% reduction in unnecessary CTs) among tool adopters (p < 0.001) [13]. However, the CDS tool did not increase the CT yield for the entire ED, as it was used by only 15% of providers.

In phase 2 of our preliminary work, we surveyed and interviewed ED providers and identified behavioral barriers as major contributing factors to the low CDS use [14]. Major behavioral barriers identified were: Cultural Norms, Time/Cognitive Load, Professional Identity, and Fear of Missing a PE [14]. Guided by the Behavior Change Wheel, barriers were classified by Capability Opportunity Motivation Behavior Framework domain, linked to the Theoretical Domains Framework, and then linked to intervention functions, which are evidence-based behavior change approaches [15]. In our subsequent pilot trial, two nudges were developed based on intervention functions to target identified barriers. A peer comparison nudge showed the ordering provider’s CT hit rate (the percentage of tests positive for PE in the last 4 months) in comparison to the median hit rate of ED providers in their department. A salient messaging nudge allowed the provider to input a section of pre-written text explaining that the provider had considered the risks and benefits of CT and that the risks outweighed the benefits for this patient into their note.

We additionally developed an algorithm for automatically calculating the PE Risk score (Wells’ Score), using data from the health information exchange and the electronic health record (EHR) [16]. The tool triggered only after a CT scan was ordered to evaluate for PE and the Wells’ Score was ≤ 4, classified as “PE Unlikely” by the 2-tiered score. The tool recommended ordering a d-dimer for patients with a Wells’ Score ≤ 4 (“PE Unlikely”) and a CT for patients with a Wells’ Score > 4 (“PE Likely”). Although the tool was only triggered for patients with a Wells Score ≤ 4, the providers could revise the score within the tool based on the clinical data. The Wells’ Criteria for PE CDS tool was displayed without nudges at ED #1 and with nudges at ED #2 [17]. Tool adoption rate was significantly higher in ED #2 (CDS tool + nudge), with an adoption rate of 39.1% compared to 20.7% in ED #1 (CDS tool without nudge) (p < 0.001). The phase 2 tool was developed on an EHR-agnostic, web-based platform to maximize its potential for dissemination.

The primary objective of this study (phase 3) is to evaluate the impact of the nudge implementation strategy (CDS tool + nudge vs. usual care) on guideline-concordant CT ordering for PE in a multi-site randomized clinical trial. This study will use a hybrid type III trial design to evaluate implementation (primary) and effectiveness (secondary) outcomes[18]. Trial results will secondarily confirm the impact of guideline-concordant CT ordering on CT yield rates across diverse patient populations. Achieving the project’s objectives will advance the science of implementation in emergency medicine and the application of nudges to provider decision-making.

Methods

Study aims and implementation frameworks

The conceptual model of the relationships between the evidence-based practice, nudge implementation strategy, influence of behavioral barriers, and implementation and effectiveness outcomes is illustrated in Fig. 1. Our study is guided by two complementary frameworks: The Behavioral Change Wheel Framework and Proctor’s Implementation Outcomes Framework [15, 19, 20]. Aim 1 of the trial is to evaluate the impact of the nudge implementation strategy on provider adoption of guideline-concordant CT ordering for PE. Guideline concordance will be measured as the percentage of all CTs ordered for evaluation of PE where either: (1) the Wells Score indicated “PE likely” (> 4) or (2) a d-dimer assay was ordered, and the result was above the upper limit of normal. Aim 2 of the trial focuses on evaluating the underlying causal mechanism of the nudge implementation strategy. We will determine the impact of the nudge strategy on key, previously identified, behavioral barriers to provider adoption. Finally, Aim 3 confirms the effectiveness of the adoption of guideline-concordant CT ordering for PE with respect to clinical outcomes, including CT order rate, CT yield, and readmissions for PE. These three aims will provide the data required to scale and disseminate our results and substantially improve the design and development of this CDS tool, as well as reduce inappropriate imaging in the diagnostic management of PE.

Overview of study design

We will conduct a 30-month, pragmatic, cluster-randomized, stepped-wedge trial across 12 tertiary care and community EDs to evaluate the effectiveness of the nudge implementation strategy (CDS tool + nudge) on adoption of guideline-concordant CT ordering for PE. During the study start-up period, we will onboard all sites, complete the procedures for the central institutional review board (IRB) protocol approval, and establish extensive EHR data reports. During the pre-implementation period we will establish connections between the EHR-agnostic CDS platform and each site’s EHR and collect baseline data. Integration of the tool will be via open communication protocols, including Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR), which is already supported by all EHRs in this study. Baseline data collection will establish pre-control and pre-intervention clinical outcomes. Baseline data will be collected for 12 months prior to beginning of trial, due to seasonality in CT yield, likely secondary to the increased incidence of respiratory infections during the winter and early spring [21].

During the implementation period (Year 2026 Quarter 1 to Year 2028 Quarter 2), we will conduct the stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial with randomization to an intervention onset time at the level of ED. The study lead biostatistician (CMC), who is not involved in participant enrollment or intervention implementation, will generate the random allocation sequence and assign 12 EDs to one of nine intervention onset times using computerized random-number generation. Characteristics of the EDs, variability in patient volume, and other metrics across sites are detailed in Table 1. No patient or public involvement will be included in the design, conduct, and reporting of the trial.

Restricted randomization will be used to account for 3 health systems participating in the trial (Baylor College of Medicine, Northwell Health, and New York University (NYU) Langone Health). One site within the NYU Langone Health system will be randomly selected to be the first ED where the intervention will be implemented. Because there are more EDs than unique onset times, three onset times will be randomly selected to be assigned to two EDs rather than one. To ensure each health system has ED sites with earlier and later intervention onset times, one NYU Langone Health site will be randomly selected from the remaining 3 for earlier onset, 3 of 6 Northwell sites will be randomly selected for earlier onset, and 1 of 2 Baylor sites will be selected for earlier onset. Within earlier and later intervention onset groups, sites will be assigned to specific intervention onset times by permuting the list of times. By the completion of the 30-month trial, all EDs will contribute a minimum of 3 months of data before and after intervention implementation. Figure 2 shows the proposed trial schematic.

Study setting

The study will take place in 12 community and tertiary care EDs nested within three health care systems (Baylor College of Medicine, Northwell Health, and New York University Langone Health) that are geographically diverse. The EDs have varying patient volumes, patient acuity, and baseline rates of CT ordering (Table 1). All patients aged 18 years and older presenting to a study site ED during the study period who are being evaluated for PE using CT will be included in the study. We will exclude patients with diagnoses of hepatitis, cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, and/or statin allergy or intolerance and, for each indication, those who did not meet age thresholds. All providers (physicians, physician assistants, nurse practitioners, fellows, and resident physicians) ordering CTs for the evaluation of PE in adult patients at the site EDs will be included in the trial. Additionally, all providers at each site will be eligible for web-based surveys.

Details on provider training, the CDS tool, and the implementation strategy

At the start of the control period, each site will receive brief academic detailing by webmail on the evidence behind and appropriate use of the PE risk score. The human-centered design process employed for the tool development will ensure that providers can use the CDS tool without training. Prior to the implementation of the tool, providers will receive 1-hr virtual training sessions during a pre-scheduled faculty meeting that includes: (1) an overview of the project; (2) explanation of how the peer-comparison nudge works including sources of its data and (3) explanation of the benefits and how to use the tool. The proposed training is based on our successful pilot study [17, 22].

The tool will trigger only after a CT scan is ordered to evaluate for PE and the automatically calculated Wells’ Score is ≤ 4. The CDS tool was developed using an algorithm for automatically calculating the Wells’ Score, using data collected during routine clinical practice in the EHR [16]. Briefly, we use a combinatorial keyword search method to query free-text chief complaint fields for the criteria “clinical signs and symptoms of DVT” and “hemoptysis.” We used diagnoses listed in past medical histories for the “history of PE/DVT,” “malignancy,” and “immobilization or surgery in the previous 4 weeks” criteria. The presence of brief operative notes with the use of general anesthesia, were used to identify patients who underwent recent surgery for the “immobilization or surgery in the previous 4 weeks” criterion. Vital signs taken during the ED encounter were used for the “heart rate greater than 100” criterion. All patients were given 3 points for the subjective criterion “an alternative diagnosis is less likely than PE”. During previous validation with respect to the classification of the patients as “PE likely,” the automated process achieved an accuracy of 92.1% when compared with the chart review.

The CDS tool was developed on a novel, cloud-based, EHR-agnostic CDS platform. Feasibility of this approach has been previously demonstrated [23]. The front-end interface consists of the provider-facing EHR and the integrated CDS tool. The front-end EHR is responsible for launching and running the CDS tool, which is a web-based application that can be displayed within a native EHR window to appear as if it is an integrated part of the EHR clinical or digital workflow. The web-based CDS tool was built using standard web technologies, including HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The data standard FHIR, created by Health Level Seven International, utilizes modern web-based methodologies allowing the web-based CDS to operate seamlessly within any EHR environment. This enables the creation of highly functional CDS tools on a modern, web-based platform, independent of any EHR without duplication of effort.

During the intervention period, nudge implementation strategies in the CDS user interface will be a peer comparison of providers’ hit rates for CTs ordered for PE and salient messaging. The peer comparison nudge will show the ordering provider’s individual CT hit rate, or the percent of their CT tests that are positive for PE, in comparison to all other providers in their department. The ordering providers’ CT hit rate will be calculated over the previous 4-month period and will only be displayed if the provider has ordered 10 or more CTs evaluating for PE during this period. The peer comparison nudge was designed to address the previously identified Cultural Norms and Professional Identity barriers to adoption. The salient messaging nudge details why ordering a d-dimer is the best, evidence-based decision for this patient and can include language detailing the hospital system’s support of that decision and allow them to click one button to automatically populate this into their note. Salient messaging was used to address the Time/Cognitive Load and Fear of Missing a PE barriers.

Study outcomes

The primary study outcome will be adoption (Table 2). Adoption is defined as “the intention, initial decision, or action to try or employ an innovation or evidence-based practice” [24]. Adoption in this study will be placement of a CT order that is guideline-concordant (% CT scan orders with Wells’ Score > 4 [“PE Likely”] and/or d-dimer above upper limit of normal). Current estimates have found that 32% of CT studies are not guideline-concordant[7]. We have defined a clinically meaningful difference in guideline concordance as an absolute 10% increase in guideline-concordant scans.

Secondary outcomes for Aim 1 include assessing variation in the intervention effect over time and the representativeness of tool adopters. Representativeness, or the characteristics of adopters vs. non-adopters, is a key implementation outcome as well as a key de-implementation outcome [20, 24]. The impact of nudges on providers by demographics (i.e., age, gender) and provider type has not previously been investigated. There is preliminary evidence that both age and biological sex may have an impact on provider CDS adoption rates where female providers were found to be more likely to accept CDS and have a positive emotional response to it [25, 26].

Additional secondary outcomes for Aim 1 will include acceptability, fidelity, and cost (as reflected by local programmatic costs as well as by cost-effectiveness for health care sector). Acceptability of the intervention will be measured using the system usability scale, a simple, validated, 10-item scale, which gives a quantitative global view of subjective assessment of perceived usefulness and ease-of-use related to direct experience with the tool [27]. Intervention fidelity is defined as the degree to which an intervention is delivered as it was intended by developers [20]. Fidelity assessment shortly after implementation of the nudge strategy will ensure the quality of the CDS tool delivery and will be important if there are unexplained variations in adoption across sites. Fidelity will be assessed during the first month after each ED converts to the intervention condition using provider interviews. A fidelity checklist will be developed by the study team to ensure that the tool is functioning as intended. Providers (n = 3 per ED) will be briefly interviewed about their experience with the tool by the site project manager at each health system. Implementation cost is typically described as the cost impact of an implementation effort [20]. We will conduct both a cost effectiveness analysis with evaluation of cost per health outcome as well as a budget impact analysis to assess local costs of implementing CDS tool + nudge. Assessment of contextual elements

In Aim 2, to evaluate contextual elements, we will use an explanatory sequential mixed methods approach informed by the Behavior Change Wheel [15] to determine the impact of the nudge implementation strategy on identified behavioral barriers. A web-based survey assessing the prevalence of major barriers will be distributed within two months after the start of the control period and within two months after the start of the intervention period. A subset of participating providers (n = 75), targeted by demographic data, will be emailed the survey from their medical director. Brief surveys will be adapted from our extensive previous work [14, 22]. Repeat surveys will be sent to the same providers. Results will inform the need for and the development of a semi-structured interview guide. Interviews, if needed, will target discrepancies between reports by health system or site, or unexpected results as compared to pilot data. Interviews will take place with providers at target sites within 2 months of the start of the intervention arm at each ED and be conducted by a project manager with expertise in qualitative interviewing.

Data collection methods and management

We will collect adoption outcomes data through EHR reporting systems at the respective health organizations as reported in Table 2. As this is a pragmatic clinical trial, we will use data routinely collected during clinical care and extracted from the EHR. Surveys will be used to assess acceptability and behavioral barriers. Fidelity will be measured with provider interviews and a fidelity checklist. Data for cost analyses will use diverse data sources (i.e., EHR, administrative data, reported training times, etc.).

The research team has extensive experience recruiting, enrolling, and retaining EDs in similar research projects. Participation does not require significant time investment from ED faculty or staff and as such, and based on our previous experience, we do not anticipate any difficulty retaining participants. We will use ongoing training, and organizational and peer support to continuously improve acceptability of the CDS tool.

Study participant data will be transmitted from clinical sites and stored at NYU Langone Health. This will not include the participant’s contact or identifying information. Rather, individual participants and their research data will be identified by a unique study identification number and this information will be kept separate from the research data in research electric data capture (REDCap). The study data entry and study management systems (i.e., REDCap) used by clinical sites will be secured and password protected. At the end of the study, all study databases will be de-identified and archived at NYU Langone Health.

Statistical analysis

For the primary adoption outcome, we will estimate the proportion of CT scan orders for evaluation of PE that are guideline-concordant in the intervention and control conditions, calculating 95% confidence intervals (CIs) while adjusting for clustering by site and time trend [28]. We will employ generalized linear mixed models commonly used in the analysis of stepped-wedge trials [29, 30]. Models will be fit using the glmmTMB package using R software [31, 32]. In Aim 1, the primary outcome will be adoption of guideline-concordant ordering practices among ordered CT scans, and the fixed-effects coefficient for the intervention effect, exponentiated, will indicate how the intervention increases the odds of concordance. This model can be expanded to consider patient and provider covariates (e.g., patient age, patient gender, patient race, physician vs. nurse practitioner vs. physician assistant provider) as well as changes in the intervention effect with greater exposure time [33]. For example, the addition of terms for a main effect of patient race/ethnicity allow us to estimate differences in guideline concordance among patient racial/ethnic groups, and the addition of interaction effects between patient race/ethnicity and the intervention effect allow us to detect intervention effect heterogeneity.

Additional outcomes from Aim 1 will include quantitative data on acceptability that will be summarized graphically and with descriptive statistics. In addition to means and standard deviations, descriptive statistics will include robust estimates of central tendency and variability such as medians and interquartile ranges. Tables of counts and percentages will summarize categorical data. Fidelity findings will be summarized into systematic summary reports that highlight any gaps. Results will inform tool revisions and be incorporated into our dissemination tool kit. Cost-effectiveness analysis is reported in terms of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) [34]. We will calculate the ICER as the difference in costs between the intervention and control periods divided by the difference between the intervention and control periods in guideline-discordant CT scans. We will include projection of life expectancy for comparison of the intervention’s population level cumulative costs and life expectancy against the common cost effectiveness threshold of 50 K/gain in life year and 100 K/gain in life year. In such an evaluation, savings overall to the health care sector while maintaining similar life expectancy is a cost-effective scenario.

In Aim 2, we will estimate the change in provider report of barriers in the intervention and control conditions to calculate 95% CIs while adjusting for clustering by site through surveys. The effect size, including a CI, will be calculated using a mixed effects regression model to account for repeated measures on providers and clustering of providers in sites. We will weight survey responses for non-response by provider characteristics.

Additionally, semi-structured interviews will be conducted until thematic saturation is met, and no significant additional issues arise. The interview guide will be developed based on survey results, informed by the Behavior Change Wheel, and revised based on the ongoing analysis of interview data. Interviews will be audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. A codebook will be developed reflecting emerging themes after iteratively reading the transcriptions. Using the constant comparative method, additional readings of the transcripts will lead to consolidation of these coding schemes until no further refinement is required. Two independent coders will read and code the transcripts. All discrepancies in coding will be resolved by discussion to achieve a consensus. We will perform a thematic analysis of the transcribed audio recordings as recommended by Boyatzis [35].

In Aim 3, clinical outcomes will be analyzed using generalized linear mixed models similar to those specified for the primary outcome analysis. Rates, such as CT ordering, CT scan yield, and missed PE will use a count mixed model (e.g., Poisson or negative binomial) with the natural logarithm of the relevant denominator included as an offset term. These models will estimate rate ratios with CIs. Similar to the plan for the primary outcome, patient and provider level covariates can be included, and patient level variables can interact with intervention conditions to explore moderators of intervention effects. Reviews will occur quarterly to monitor any missing data or data anomalies and will be resolved by study team members.

Sample size

Using Power Analysis and Sample Size Software 2023, we calculated the number of eligible CT scans needed at each site within each period to test our primary Aim 1 hypothesis that the CDS tool + nudge implementation strategy leads to greater guideline concordant ordering when compared to the CDS tool only. We use a conservative estimate of the intraclass correlation coefficient (0.04), which does not have a strong impact on power since all sites are observed under both intervention and control conditions. Power calculations show an increase from 68% concordance under the control condition to 78% concordance under the intervention condition can be detected with at least 85% power (alpha = 0.01) when at least 29 eligible CT scans are observed in each site and quarter (total number of eligible scans = 11*12*29 = 3828). We expect approximately 100 scans per site and period (harmonic mean), suggesting adequate power to detect an increase from 68% to 78% in concordance overall and in patient subgroups constituting at least one-third of all scans.

Data monitoring

Each site will be responsible for maintaining their data and maintaining security. A data monitoring committee is not required as the trial poses minimal risk to participants. The study team will conduct quarterly evaluations searching for any differences between intervention and control sites in terms of clinical safety outcomes including missed diagnoses of PE, ED and inpatient readmissions, and deaths. If significant differences emerge the team will investigate and comprehensively address any issues at the affected sites.

Study oversight

The trial was approved by NYU Langone Health’s IRB, and it will serve as the single IRB for all study sites, as detailed further in the “Declarations” section. All study activities will take place at Baylor College of Medicine, Northwell Health, and NYU Langone Health.

Discussion

In this paper, we present a protocol for a hybrid type III effectiveness implementation trial design to evaluate implementation (primary) and effectiveness (secondary) outcomes [18]. This design was chosen because of the very strong evidence base for the effectiveness and safety of a PE Risk Score – Wells’ Score – in the ED setting [3, 8, 36]. Evidence for CDS based on Wells’ Score is similarly strong [13, 37–42]. CDS has traditionally been designed using a rational model of provider behavior, seeking to provide the right information, to the right person, in the right intervention format, through the right channel, at the right time in workflow [43]. We will evaluate through a clinical trial the impact of the addition of nudges to address provider behavioral factors, a key barrier to provider CDS adoption [14].

This study’s strengths include evaluating the causal mechanism behind nudging provider adoption of guideline-concordant CT ordering practices for PE. To further elucidate the processes responsible for the impact of the nudge implementation strategy’s impact on provider ordering behaviors we assess its impact on barriers to PE risk score adoption identified in our previous work (i.e., cultural norms, time/cognitive load, professional identity, fear of missing a PE). Secondly, the study will use an EHR-agnostic, FHIR-compliant, web-based platform to create CDS designed for dissemination. CDS is typically built inside of a local EHR, vastly limiting dissemination capabilities. Code used to create these tools cannot typically be re-used or “copy-pasted” to recreate tools at other institutions. The flexibility of the EHR-agnostic platform – a web-based CDS application that can be designed a single time and then integrated into EHRs from different vendors at various locations – helps maximize the dissemination potential of the core evidence-based practices it facilitates.

A limitation that exists is accounting for temporal trends in stepped wedge. No sites have plans to employ any intervention directed at the uptake of evidence-based practices for PE diagnostic management. Though we believe that a temporal trend will not be observed we do include such effects in the generalized mixed effects models. Another limitation is the risk of contamination. To minimize this risk, we have intentionally randomized at the ED level to avoid potential contamination between providers within a site. Providers at each site do not typically rotate between EDs. EDs will only have access to the CDS tool + nudge after implementation, minimizing the risk of contamination.

In summary, this trial will advance evidence on behavioral implementation strategies using advanced technological methods, demonstrating how theory-informed nudges can improve guideline adherence and patient-centered care in the ED while reducing unnecessary testing. The use of modern technological methods allows for the development of CDS that is designed for dissemination and can be utilized with any EHR.
