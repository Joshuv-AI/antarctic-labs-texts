Patient-reported distress and anxiety during cone-beam CT imaging sessions with increased gantry rotation speeds
Authors: Atefeh Rezaei, Alex T Price, Theodore H Arsenault, Runyon Woods, Rojano Kashani, Angela Y Jia, Daniel E Spratt, Clair R Kueny, Lauren E Henke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Increased gantry rotation speed during imaging does not increase patient anxiety.•Patient anxiety decreased after multiple exposures to increased gantry speed.•Median number of breath holds for C-arm full trajectory CBCT was 2.0.Keywords: CBCT, Patient-reported anxiety, Patient-reported outcomes, Rapid gantry rotation, IGRT

Introduction

Cancer is commonly associated with significant psychosocial challenges, with many patients experiencing emotional and social strain as a consequence of disease progression [1]. While severity of psychological distress varies among individuals, it can often reach levels that are debilitating. Guidelines recommend distress screening at the initial oncology visit and at appropriate intervals throughout care [2]. Notably, a majority of patients with cancer will undergo radiation therapy (RT) at some point during their care course, which may uniquely affect the pattern and intensity of distress compared to other treatments such as surgery or chemotherapy [3]. Yet, research examining the dynamics of psychological distress specifically in patients undergoing RT remains limited [1]. Studies show elevated levels of patient distress are linked to poorer outcomes—including higher hospitalization rates, decreased adherence to RT, and reduced overall survival [4]. A cohort study of head and neck cancer patients undergoing radiation therapy found that patient-reported fears—such as fear of enclosed spaces or movement restriction—were associated with session disruptions due to anxiety. Approximately 11–24% of sessions experienced disruption, highlighting the potential value of early screening to identify patients who may benefit from prophylactic interventions [3]. Additionally, it has been noted that increased speeds related to robotic arms or other mechanical systems in industrial professions have increased worker anxiety and stress [5], [6].

Recent advances in radiation oncology technology have led to the development of novel imaging solutions aimed at improving treatment precision and efficiency. One such innovation is the HyperSight (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) cone-beam computed tomography (HS-CBCT) solution, implemented on various Varian platforms, including the TrueBeam (TB) system, which offers enhanced image quality combined with a faster gantry rotation speed. Notably, the gantry rotation power source was upgraded to enable a faster gantry rotation speed of 9.0 degrees per second (1.5 rpm), compared to 6.0 degrees per second (1 rpm) in earlier models [7], [8]. While this increase in gantry speed has the potential to reduce imaging time, minimize motion-induced image artifacts, and enhance patient throughput, it also raises important questions regarding patient experience, particularly in relation to anxiety during imaging with increased gantry rotation speed. Given the established body of literature indicating that factors such as immobilization, confined spaces, and treatment environment can exacerbate distress among patients undergoing radiation therapy, there is a theoretical concern that the rapid, dynamic movement of the gantry could contribute to heightened anxiety.

As the field continues to prioritize both technical excellence and patient-centered care, understanding the psychosocial impact of such technological innovations is critical. However, to date, no studies have systematically evaluated whether the increased gantry speed associated with the HS-CBCT system affects patient anxiety levels during radiation therapy. In this prospective study conducted as part of an imaging clinical trial on a research HS-CBCT system, we hypothesized that increased gantry rotation speed would increase patient acute state-based anxiety during image acquisition. Additionally, we assessed the impact of the increased gantry rotation speed on the number of breath-holds needed to acquire full images.

Materials and Methods:

Patients who participated in this study were enrolled in a prospective imaging study evaluating the TB-HS-CBCT solution (NCT05975619). In this prospective imaging study, four cohorts of ten patients from four different anatomical regions were imaged: head and neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. Patients were either imaged on the same day as computed tomography (CT) simulation or on any treatment day, depending on patient availability and potential research questions, based on the timepoint and image acquired. The patients were imaged using a research TB-HS CBCT separate from their clinical treatment and imaging. Patients were eligible to participate in up to 5 imaging sessions to minimize patient burden and extra imaging radiation dose. Not all patients received 5 imaging sessions. Patients were not excluded if they had received prior radiation. For the thorax and abdomen cohorts, motion management techniques such as breath-hold, 4DCBCT, passive-gated, or compression could be utilized to match treatment setup. While 6 deg/s (1.0 rpm) C-arm gantry rotation speed or a closed-bore CBCT was used for all clinical imaging during standard therapy, in the separate imaging session, gantry rotation speed was chosen on a per-acquisition basis. The slower 6 deg/s speed was used when a direct image quality comparison with the faster speed, 9 deg/s (1.5 rpm), was needed. However, all sessions included at least one acquisition at the increased gantry rotation speed image to measure the impact on acute state-based anxiety. Not every session included an image acquired at a slower speed.

Participating patients were invited to complete the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory questionnaire before and after both the first and final imaging sessions conducted for the study to assess their change in state-based anxiety. Within each imaging session, patients received the questionnaire immediately before entering the treatment vault and again immediately after exiting the vault, with the imaging session (including an image at increased gantry speed) taking place while they were inside. The questionnaire was administered to assess their perception of state-based anxiety, specifically after exposure to the high rotation speed (1.5 rpm) gantry imaging solution. Participation in the anxiety assessment was voluntary and conducted in a manner consistent with the ethical guidelines and consent procedures of the parent study.

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) is a validated and widely adopted tool for measuring anxiety in clinical and research settings [9]. The STAI is composed of two distinct scales: the State Anxiety Scale (S-Anxiety), which measures a patient’s immediate feelings of anxiety in response to a specific situation, and the Trait Anxiety Scale (T-Anxiety), which assesses a patient’s general predisposition toward anxiety across various situations [10], [11], [12]. The inventory consists of self-report items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety. In this study, we specifically focused on the 6-item State-Anxiety Scale (STAI-S-6) to capture the acute emotional response of patients after undergoing radiation therapy imaging with a higher gantry rotation speed. Patients were not informed of the increased gantry rotation speed during the study period. The STAI-6 has previously been shown to correlate well with the full STAI inventory [13].

State-based anxiety scores were analyzed both within a given session (comparing pre- and post-imaging responses) and longitudinally by comparing responses between each patient’s first and final imaging sessions. The six responses within the STAI-S-6 were summed together to give a composite state-based anxiety score. Three answers (questions 2, 4, and 5) were inverted due to the positive response from the item opposite of an anxiety state. Composite scores were then prorated by 20/6 to reflect the standard STAI anxiety scale. Scores of 20-37, 38-44, and 45-80 represented low anxiety, moderate anxiety, and high anxiety, respectively[13], [14]. For patients undergoing breath-hold imaging, the number of breath-holds required to complete a single full-trajectory scan was recorded and analyzed to assess the feasibility and tolerability of the imaging protocol. Descriptive statistical analyses were performed using Spyder 6 Python 3.11. Variables were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Changes in patient-reported state-based anxiety responses before and after imaging sessions, as well as between the first and final sessions, were assessed using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test due to the data being non-normal as determined by a Shapiro-Wilk test. The association between the number of breath-holds required and post-imaging state-based anxiety scores was analyzed using a Pearson correlation coefficient. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A separate analysis was performed for patients who did not take anti-anxiety medication. The results of this analysis, reported in the supplementary material, indicate minimal differences as compared to all patients.

Results:

A total of 40 patients were enrolled and contributed data to this analysis. Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 67 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 59–73), and the median body mass index (BMI) was 28.0 kg/m2 (IQR: 25.2–31.7). The majority of patients were male (60.0%), white (90.0%), and non-Hispanic (97.3%). Tumor locations were evenly distributed among the head and neck/brain, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. Only 2 of the 40 patients were on anti-anxiety medications.

For the first imaging session, the median decrease in patient-reported levels of state-based anxiety remained unchanged with a value of 0.0 [IQR: -4.2 – 0.0] from pre- to post-imaging. This was not a significant reduction in state-based anxiety values (W = 64.5, p = 0.359). The effect size was small (r = 0.23). In the patients who completed the final imaging session inventory, the median change in the patient-reported levels of state-based anxiety during that session was also 0.0 [IQR: 0.0 – 0.0]. However, the changes in the distribution of reported state-based anxiety scores at the last imaging session, compared to the distribution at the first imaging session, were significant (W = 0.0, p = 0.027). There was a medium effect size (r = 0.45). Finally, when comparing the post imaging session survey from the final imaging session to the first survey before the first imaging session, there was a larger decrease in patient-reported state-based anxiety with a median change of -3.3 [IQR: -10.0 – 0.0]. This change in reported state-based anxieties was significant (W = 3.0, p = 0.001) and the effect size was large (r = 0.76). Changes in reported state-based anxieties are illustrated in Fig. 2. Of all imaging sessions in which pre- and post-imaging state-based anxiety scores were reported, 19 (30.7%) reported decreases in state-based anxiety, 38 (61.3%) reported no change in state-based anxiety, and 5 (8.1%) had an increase in reported state-based anxiety.

Moreover, among the imaging sessions where breath-hold was utilized, the median number of breath-holds required to complete the scan was 2 (IQR: 2 – 3; range: 1–6), with an average of 2.22 breath-holds per session (SD: 0.72). A total of 77 breath-hold sessions were recorded (27 of which had associating surveys collected), amounting to 171 breath-hold instances overall. There was no observed association between breath-hold imaging and increased patient-reported state-based anxiety, as state-based anxiety levels remained low across sessions regardless of breath-hold use (r = 0.24, p = 0.219).

Discussion:

In this study, we found that patients imaged with the HS-CBCT system, despite its significantly faster gantry rotation speed compared to conventional CBCT imaging solutions, reported minimal levels of state-based anxiety following imaging sessions. Across 127 survey encounters, the majority of patients consistently indicated feelings of calmness, relaxation, and contentment, with very few instances of reported distress, tension, or worry.

Distress has been increasingly recognized as a critical dimension of cancer care, often referred to as the “sixth vital sign” [15]. It encompasses a multifactorial emotional experience that includes psychological, social, and spiritual suffering, and can interfere with a patient’s ability to cope effectively with cancer and its treatment [2]. Given its prevalence and potential impact on clinical outcomes, national guidelines such as those from the NCCN recommend routine distress screening for all patients with cancer, beginning at diagnosis and continuing throughout the trajectory of care [16]. Studies have shown that 37% to 62% of individuals with cancer report clinically significant levels of distress [17], [18], [19], [20], regardless of cancer type or treatment modality, including surgery, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, immunotherapy, and radiation therapy [21], [22], [23], [24].

Despite widespread acknowledgment of the importance of distress screening, research specifically addressing distress among patients undergoing radiation therapy remains sparse. Even when radiation-specific data are available, patients are often undergoing multimodal treatments, making it difficult to attribute distress levels specifically to the experience of radiation therapy itself. This paucity of focused research highlights the importance of investigations like ours, which aim to directly assess psychosocial state responses to innovations in radiation delivery technology, such as the introduction of a high-speed gantry rotation CBCT system. Understanding how new treatment environments and technologies affect acute patient distress is critical for ensuring that advances in technical precision are matched by a continued commitment to patient-centered care.

In our study, we specifically utilized the STAI-S-6 to assess patient-reported acute state-based anxiety following imaging with the HS-CBCT system. The STAI-S-6 is particularly well-suited for use in oncology populations, as it distinguishes between transient procedure-related anxiety and baseline anxiety traits, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the patient’s psychological experience from treatment procedures. Its strong psychometric properties, including high reliability and validity across diverse populations, make it a robust instrument for detecting subtle changes in anxiety levels associated with new treatment technologies. Before and after an imaging session, patients completed the STAI-S-6 questionnaire, providing a direct and standardized assessment of change in their emotional state. Across 127 completed questionnaires from 38 patients, the overwhelming majority of responses indicated low levels of state-based anxiety. This pattern reinforces the conclusion that the introduction of a high-speed imaging solution did not adversely affect acute patient emotional well-being during the imaging session.

Our findings also suggest that the imaging experience with the high-speed HS-CBCT did not impact patient’s state-based acute anxiety levels for the majority of patients during an imaging session. In the comparison of pre- and post-session responses (Table 2), we observed more decreases in state-based anxiety scores than increases in state-based anxiety, indicating that actual exposure to the increased gantry speed rarely resulted in increases in state-based anxiety and that potentially patients were experiencing an anticipatory state-based anxiety before the first research imaging session due to it being in a separate treatment vault and research-based. Moreover, when comparing responses from each patient’s first and last imaging session (Table 2), there was a significant decrease in state-based anxiety scores between the very first and final timepoint. This pattern suggests that familiarity with the imaging process and treatment course may further reduce state-based anxiety, with patients becoming more comfortable as treatment and the imaging sessions progressed. Collectively, these results reinforce that while patients may enter treatment sessions with uncertainty or apprehension, the reality of the imaging experience is generally well-tolerated and does not increase levels of state-based anxiety.

Moreover, the feasibility of completing high-quality CBCT imaging with a minimal number of breath-holds is a notable advancement in radiation therapy delivery. In this study, the median number of breath-holds required per full-trajectory scan was only two, with an average of 2.2 breath-holds per session. This efficiency represents a significant improvement compared to prior C-arm CBCT systems, which often required multiple breath-hold cycles (Østergaard et al. and Hoffman et al. reported 3-4 breath-holds and Ottosson et al. reported a median of 2 breath-holds but a higher standard deviation of 1.6 for full trajectory acquisitions) and prolonged setup times, limiting their widespread use of breath hold in routine clinical practice [25], [26], [27]. Importantly, the majority of breath-hold sessions in our cohort were performed at end-exhalation, a phase that may be more reproducible for patients. These findings suggest that with the enhanced gantry speed and image reconstruction capabilities of the HS-CBCT system, breath-hold imaging becomes a more viable and patient-friendly option, without contributing to increased state-based anxiety.

Limitations of our study include not every patient completing a pre- and post-imaging session survey during their first and last imaging sessions. This may impact the conclusions drawn from this study. Additionally, not being able to measure state-based anxiety during the actual rotation of the gantry limits the true understanding of the impact of gantry rotational speed on patient state-based anxiety levels, but our investigation noted that the overall patient state-based anxiety decreased after the total imaging session experience. Additionally, causes of or changes in anxiety are multi-faceted with confounding factors that interplay with the patient’s baseline anxiety and reactionary state-based anxiety that is challenging to isolate within this study For example, in the first imaging session, the head and neck cohort experienced an increase in state-based anxiety, possibly due to the use of less familiar immobilization equipment or because they were in direct line of sight with the faster moving gantry. Therefore, we acknowledge that there are other possible reasons that impacted acute patient state-based anxiety that were not captured within this study.

In conclusion, higher gantry speed associated with the HS-CBCT system does not translate into a clinically significant increase in patient-perceived anxiety, supporting the safe integration of this advanced imaging technology into routine clinical practice from a psychosocial standpoint.
