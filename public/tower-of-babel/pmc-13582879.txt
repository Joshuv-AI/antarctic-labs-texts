A multimodal epilepsy dataset of paired 3-Tesla and 7-Tesla MRI and intracranial EEG
Authors: Tamjid Imtiaz, Alfredo Lucas, Mariam Josyula, Nina Petillo, Joost Wagenaar, Joel M. Stein, Sandhitsu Das, Nishant Sinha, Kathryn A. Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is an increasing need to integrate multimodal datasets in epilepsy research, particularly to correlate electrophysiology with imaging in patients with refractory epilepsy. We present a multimodal paired 3T and 7T MRI dataset acquired from 30 drug-resistant focal epilepsy patients (18 females, 38.8 ± 11.7 years) who underwent T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR), and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI). In addition to the raw data, we release preprocessed anatomical and functional data, along with various quality control and clinical metadata files. For participants who subsequently underwent intracranial EEG (iEEG) (n = 15), curated ictal and interictal epochs are also included. We demonstrate a potential application of this paired 3T and 7T data by training a deep learning model capable of synthesizing high-field 7T T1w MR images from the 3T equivalents. We anticipate that this dataset will facilitate future multiscale analyses in epilepsy.

Background & Summary

Epilepsy is a complex network disorder where precise identification of epileptic lesions is necessary for good surgical outcomes. While 3T MRI is a critical tool for detecting structural lesions in epilepsy, a substantial proportion of patients remain MRI-negative at 3T, posing challenges in pre-surgical evaluations1. Emerging evidence suggests that ultra-high-field 7T MRI, with its superior spatial resolution and soft tissue contrast, can uncover subtle lesions not visible at 3T, particularly in cases of Focal Cortical Dysplasia (FCD)2 and mesial temporal sclerosis (MTS)3. Additionally, functional MRI (fMRI) provides a non-invasive approach to delineate epilepsy networks where 7T fMRI sequence demonstrates improved delineation of epileptic networks4 and seizure onset lateralization5 compared to 3T, offering a more informative view of the functional organization of the brain in epilepsy patients.

Conversely, the high temporal resolution of intracranial EEG (iEEG) provides insights into seizure dynamics to complement the high spatial resolution of 3T and 7T MRI and fMRI6. It has been widely applied in epilepsy surgeries for pre-operative identification of the seizure onset zone (SOZ)7. It is also used to understand the relationship between eloquent cortex and the epileptogenic zone to predict cognitive and motor outcomes after resection surgery8.

Multimodal datasets in epilepsy research are crucial for understanding seizure networks, understanding brain connectivity patterns, and aiding clinical decision-making. Structural MRI, including T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) imaging, has widespread applications in epilepsy research, such as the detection of FCD9,10, localization of the SOZ11,12, distinguishing epilepsy subtypes13,14, and surgical planning15. Resting-state fMRI provides a detailed understanding of functional network disruptions in the brain and is increasingly utilized in epilepsy research. It has been applied to a wide range of studies, including SOZ lateralization, localization, and surgical outcome prediction16–20.

iEEG, on the other hand, provides precise localization and confirmation of seizure onset, aiding clinical decision-making. Key applications of iEEG in clinical settings include seizure prediction21–24, seizure characterization25,26, and SOZ localization27–29. A multimodal approach combining the high temporal resolution of iEEG with the superior spatial resolution of MRI has recently gained popularity in the research community. In one study, the predictive value of seizure recurrence was assessed using EEG and fMRI30. In another study, multimodal MRI data including T1-weighted, T2-weighted, FLAIR intensity, and diffusion tensor imaging, were integrated to classify regions as SOZ or non-SOZ31. These studies highlight the potential of multimodal datasets in epilepsy research, ultimately paving the way for clinical applications.

It is well established that in patients with pharmacoresistant epilepsy and well-characterized lesions on 3T MRI, 7T MRI provides superior visualization of the extent and multifocal nature of abnormalities32. The ultra–high-field strength of 7T offers enhanced signal-to-noise ratio and tissue contrast, enabling the detection of subtle cortical and subcortical abnormalities that are often missed at 3T33. Previous studies have also demonstrated that 7T MRI improves the delineation of focal cortical dysplasias, hippocampal sclerosis, and microstructural lesions34, while 7T fMRI offers better characterization of functional network alterations and SOZ lateralization5. Nevertheless, the acquisition of 7T MRI data remains costly and is often impractical for smaller clinical centers due to limited availability and technical demands. Therefore, the development of multimodal datasets combining 3T and 7T MRI represents a valuable resource for advancing computational and generative modeling approaches capable of predicting 7T-quality synthetic images from standard 3T acquisitions and thus improving accessibility to ultra–high-resolution imaging insights in broader clinical settings.

Given the utility of multimodal data in epilepsy research, we acquired paired 3T and 7T structural and functional MRI datasets from 30 patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Additionally, 15 of these patients underwent intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings; ictal and interictal epochs from these patients are included in the current release. The anonymized raw data, adhering to Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS)31 standards, are made publicly available, accompanied by quality control metrics and clinical metadata. In a previous study, we utilized a subset of the paired rsfMRI data of this cohort and directly compared 3T and 7T rsfMRI for seizure lateralization5. Moreover, we also utilized this 7T data in characterizing the volumetric atrophy patterns across hippocampal subfields and thalamic nuclei in drug-resistant focal epilepsy35. In this study, as a possible use case of this dataset, we developed a deep learning framework using the structural T1w MRI data to generate synthetic 7T images from the corresponding 3T images. We also evaluated the quality of the generated images in terms of volumetric measurements, including cortical and small subcortical regions like the thalamus and hippocampus, which are crucial epilepsy network regions. We anticipate that our dataset and framework will serve as valuable resources for the research community.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively enrolled 30 patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (18 females, 12 males; mean age: 38.8 ± 11.7 years) from the Penn Epilepsy Center at the University of Pennsylvania, all of whom were evaluated for surgical candidacy. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation, and the study was approved by three institutional review boards at the University of Pennsylvania (IRB#819126 for 3T, IRB#818407 for 7T, and IRB#821778 for iEEG data). participants consented to the retrospective review of de-identified clinical data, including sex, handedness, age at seizure onset, seizure type, frequency and localization, MRI lesion localization and lateralization, and structural and functional MRI and intracranial EEG (iEEG) data.

Of the 30 participants, 16 had left-sided, 9 had right-sided, and 1 had bilateral temporal lobe epilepsy, as determined by a multidisciplinary surgical conference; the remaining 4 had unclear epilepsy lateralization. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including seizure onset zone (SOZ) lateralization, lesional status, and surgical outcome, are summarized in Table 1. To protect participant privacy, all images were de-identified prior to sharing, including defacing of MRI scans and removal of protected health information (PHI) such as name, date of scan, medical record number, and date of birth. Each participant was assigned a randomized subject ID to ensure anonymity. Data Acquisition

Structural MRI

3T data acquisition

For the 3T protocol, T1w images were acquired with a sagittal, 208-slice magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence using the following parameters: echo time (TE) = 2.24 ms, repetition time (TR) = 2400 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1060 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm, flip angle = 8°, and a voxel size of 0.8 mm isotropic. T1w images were also acquired with a sagittal, 208-slice MPRAGE sequence using the following parameters: TE = 4 ms, TR = 1850 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm, flip angle = 9°, and a voxel size of 1 mm isotropic. T2w images were also acquired with a sagittal, 208-slice 3D sequence using the following parameters: TE = 564 ms, TR = 3200 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm, and a voxel size of 0.8 mm isotropic.

7T data acquisition

For the 7T protocol, T1-weighted images were acquired with three different sequences. First, they were acquired with a 176-slice MPRAGE sequence, TE = 4.4 ms, TR = 2800 ms, TI = 1500 ms, flip angle = 7.0° with a 0.8 mm isotropic voxel size. Second, T1w images were acquired with a 160-slice MP2RAGE sequence using these parameters: TE = 1.86 ms, TR = 5000 ms, TI = 2700 ms, FOV = 242 mm, flip angle = 5.0°, and a voxel size of 1 mm isotropic. Finally, T1w images were acquired with a multi echo MP2RAGE sequence using these parameters: TI = 2700 ms, TE = 6.33 ms, TR = 5000 ms, flip angle = 5.0°, and a voxel size of 1 mm isotropic. Additionally, T2-weighted turbo spin-echo images were acquired, with 224 oblique coronal slices oriented perpendicular to the long axis of the hippocampus, TE = 388 ms, TR = 3000 ms, 6.16-ms echo spacing, and a 0.4 × 0.4-mm in-plane resolution, with a 1-mm slice thickness.

Resting State Functional MRI (rs-fMRI)

3T rs-fMRI data acquisition

Two different acquisition protocols were followed at 3T. In the first protocol, BOLD fMRI images were acquired with the following parameters: 3.0 mm isotropic voxel size, TE = 30 ms, TR = 500 ms, a multiband factor of 6 and a 7-min acquisition time. In the second protocol, fMRI images were acquired with the following parameters: 2.0 mm isotropic voxel size, TE = 37 ms, TR = 800 ms, a multiband factor of 6 and a 9-min acquisition time.

7T rs-fMRI data acquisition

Two different acquisition protocols were followed at 7T as well. In the first protocol, fMRI images were acquired with the following parameters: 2 mm isotropic voxel size, TE = 23.6 ms, TR = 1000 ms, a multiband factor of 6 and a 7-min acquisition time. In the second protocol, fMRI images were acquired with the following parameters: 2 mm isotropic voxel size, TE = 30 ms, TR = 1000 ms, a multiband factor of 6 and a 9-min acquisition time.

Intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG)

For iEEG, data were recorded at sampling rates of 500, 512, or 1024 Hz using electrocorticography (ECoG) grids and strips, stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) depth electrodes, or a combination of both. In each patient, iEEG recordings were segmented into ictal and interictal categories. Ictal epochs were identified based on clinical notes from the attending EMU epileptologist; whereas, Interictal epochs were randomly selected while meeting the following criteria: (1) recorded during awake periods to ensure consistency across patients, (2) occurring more than 72 hours post-implantation to minimize implantation effects, (3) at least 2 hours before documented seizures, and (4) at least 2, 6, and 12 hours after subclinical, focal onset, and focal-to-bilateral seizures, respectively. SEEG contacts located in white matter, outside the brain, or affected by noise were excluded from all recordings. Post-implant CT scans were obtained after iEEG implantation to localize the electrode contacts37. Both bone and tissue windows were obtained with the following parameters: 120 kV, 300 mA, axial slice thickness = 1.0 mm and were finally converted to NIFTI format.

Data Preprocessing

MRI Preprocessing

MRI processing was performed using fmriprep 20.2.338 which is based on Nipype 1.6.139. The same pipeline was used for both 3T and 7T images.

Structural Image processing

The structural T1w images were corrected for intensity non-uniformity (INU) with N4BiasFieldCorrection40 and skull stripped using antsBrainExtraction.sh workflow from ANTs 2.3.341 using OASIS30ANTs as target template. Segmentation of brain tissues - cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white-matter (WM), and gray-matter (GM) - was performed on the brain-extracted T1w images using FAST42(FSL). Brain surfaces were reconstructed from the subject’s T1w reference map using Freesurfer’s recon-all, and the brain mask estimated previously was refined with a custom variation of a method introduced in Mindboggle43. Volume-based spatial normalization to one standard space was performed through nonlinear registration with antsRegistration (ANTs 2.3.3), using brain-extracted versions of both T1w reference and the T1w template. The following template was selected for spatial normalization: ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical template version 2009c (MNI152NLin2009cAsym)44.

Functional Image processing

The following preprocessing steps were performed for a single BOLD run of each subject. First, a reference volume along with its skull stripped version were generated using fmriprep. A B0-nonuniformity map was estimated based on a phase-difference map calculated with a dual-echo GRE (gradient-recall echo) sequence, processed with a custom workflow of SDCFlows inspired by the epidewarp.fsl script45. The fieldmap was then co-registered to the target EPI (echo-planar imaging) reference run and transformed into a displacement field map using FSL’s fugue and other tools from SDCflows, making it compatible with registration tools like ANTs. Using the estimated susceptibility distortion, a corrected EPI reference was generated to improve alignment with the anatomical (T1-weighted) image. The BOLD reference was then aligned to the T1-weighted image using FreeSurfer’s bbregister, which applies boundary-based registration with six degrees of freedom46. Head motion relative to the BOLD reference was estimated using mcflirt (FSL 5.0.9), yielding transformation matrices and six motion parameters (three translations and three rotations)47. Slice-timing correction was applied to the BOLD runs using AFNI’s 3dTshift48. Finally, the BOLD time series (including any slice-timing correction) were resampled in native space by applying a single, combined transform that corrects both head motion and susceptibility distortion to get the preprocessed BOLD time series in the native space. The BOLD time series were resampled into standard space, producing preprocessed runs in MNI152NLin2009cAsym space. A reference volume and its skull-stripped version were first generated using fMRIPrep’s custom workflow. Several confounding time series were then computed from the preprocessed BOLD data, including framewise displacement (FD), DVARS, and three global signals extracted from CSF, white matter (WM), and whole-brain masks. FD was calculated using two methods: Power’s formulation (absolute sum of relative motion) and Jenkinson’s approach (relative root mean square displacement between affine transformations), both implemented via Nipype39. DVARS was similarly computed for each functional run. Physiological noise components were modeled using CompCor49. After applying a high-pass filter (discrete cosine filter with 128 s cut-off) to the BOLD signal, principal components were extracted for both temporal (tCompCor) and anatomical (aCompCor) variants. tCompCor components were identified from the top 2% most variable voxels within the brain mask. For aCompCor, probabilistic masks for CSF, WM, and a combined CSF + WM region were generated in anatomical space. For each CompCor decomposition, the top components (those with the largest singular values) were retained until they explained at least 50% of the variance within the nuisance mask (CSF, WM, combined, or tCompCor). Remaining components were discarded. Head-motion estimates from earlier correction steps were included in the confounds file. Additional regressors were derived by expanding motion parameters and global signals with their temporal derivatives and quadratic terms (per Satterthwaite et al., 2013). Time points with FD > 0.5 mm or standardized DVARS > 1.5 were flagged as motion outliers. All spatial transformations—head motion, susceptibility distortion correction, and co-registrations—were combined and applied in a single interpolation step. Volumetric (gridded) resampling was conducted using antsApplyTransforms (ANTs) with Lanczos interpolation to reduce smoothing artifacts. Surface (non-gridded) resampling was carried out using FreeSurfer’s mri_vol2surf.

iEEG data preprocessing

To preprocess the data, a 60 Hz second-order infinite impulse response (IIR) notch filter (quality factor: 30) was applied in each channel to remove power line noise, and a third-order Butterworth filter (1–120 Hz) was used for detrending and high-frequency noise removal. Both filters were applied in forward and backward directions to prevent phase distortion.

Electrode localization

For electrode mapping, we manually selected the electrodes and then used iEEG-recon37 to map the electrodes from post-implant CT scans to preimplant MRI data. Visualization of electrodes in the MRI space helps to maintain quality assurance and correct any residual rotation between scans within the iEEG-recon graphical user interface. We also assign electrodes to several atlas (e.g. Automated anatomical labelling atlas50, Lausanne Atlas51, Desikan–Killiany–Tourville Atlas52) ROIs for each of the patients with iEEG and make these results available.

Application: Synthesis of 7T MRI using deep Learning Model

In order to generate synthesized 7T images from 3T MRI images, we implemented a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) architecture. Our proposed framework consists of two parts. The first part utilized the slices collected from three orthogonal planes (axial, coronal, and sagittal) and trained three separate parallel pix2pix layers while the corresponding 7T images are considered as the target images. The images were min-max normalized and reshaped to 256 × 256 pixels before training the network. The network was trained using an Adam optimizer at a fixed learning rate of 2 × 10-4 for 200 epochs with a batch size of 4. The model was trained using a 10 fold cross validation technique. The generator and discriminator part and all the remaining hyper-parameters of this architecture is similar to the pix2pix network54. The second part of the framework is a postprocessing step consisting of the removal of horizontal or vertical stripes55 and an anisotropic diffusion filter56 for smoothing out noise components and preserving fine scale details.

Usage Notes

The dataset shared via Pennsieve provides paired 3T and 7T structural and functional MRI, along with intracranial EEG data. This unique combination of spatial and temporal information can be helpful for identifying the structural-functional brain network organization, diagnosing and monitoring epilepsy and a wide range of machine learning applications.

A promising application of this paired dataset is 3T-to-7T image synthesis, which we demonstrate in this manuscript. Further studies could incorporate advanced deep learning approaches, such as diffusion models, which offer promising solutions to address data scarcity in medical image analysis58. A major challenge during this synthesis process is the potential introduction of artifacts and false lesions, which should be considered in future studies.

Due to the high temporal resolution of the iEEG data, another valuable application of this dataset is to combine iEEG with whole-brain fMRI data. Although these modalities are technically unrelated, they provide complementary spatial and temporal information, which can be highly informative in characterizing human brain activity59,60.

This multimodal dataset enables the fusion of structural and functional data, a technique frequently used in medical data analysis. Combining different data modalities allows researchers to extract meaningful information and explore relationships between brain anatomy and function by aligning structural and functional brain imaging data30,61.

Finally, we advise users to carefully consider the relatively modest sample size of this dataset when designing and interpreting downstream analyses. Our 3T-to-7T synthesis results demonstrate that meaningful inferences are achievable even with this modest dataset; however, independent validation remains essential for any analyses conducted using this resource. Where greater statistical power is required, combining this dataset with comparable high-quality imaging data from other cohorts represents a viable strategy. Nonetheless, we emphasize that this dataset, comprising paired 3T and 7T structural and functional MRI alongside iEEG recordings, constitutes a valuable and carefully curated resource, particularly for early-stage model development, hypothesis generation, and the exploration of multimodal brain structure–function relationships in epilepsy.
