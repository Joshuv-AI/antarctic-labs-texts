In Vitro Sonodynamic Therapy Using a High Throughput 3D Glioblastoma Spheroid Model with 5‐ALA and TMZ Sonosensitizers
Authors: Priyankan Datta, Nan Sook Lee, Sreejesh Moolayadukkam, Rakesh P Sahu, Xi Yu, Tianze Guo, Qifa Zhou, Y Wang, Ishwar K Puri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSonodynamic therapy (SDT) administered using low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound and sonosensitizers is an emerging, minimally invasive, targeted deep‐tissue therapy for solid tumors such as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). Initial clinical trials show promising outcomes for SDT treatments of GBM. A crucial aspect of SDT is the sonosensitizer that interacts with ultrasound, facilitating energy transfer to the tumor, thus inducing therapeutic efficacy. Current in vitro methods for determining the therapeutic efficacies of sonosensitizers are time‐consuming and expensive. A novel high‐throughput magnetically printed 3D GBM model is used to overcome this challenge. The hypothesis is that the use of two sonosensitizers, one a chemotherapeutic drug, enhances SDT efficacy through their additive chemical interactions. The GBM model is used to evaluate the effectiveness of two sonosensitizer molecules, 5‐aminolevulinic acid (5‐ALA) and theU.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved chemotherapeutic drug Temozolomide (TMZ). It is confirmed that implement high‐throughput GBM models to evaluate sonosensitizer combinations and their efficacies is feasible and, for the first time, show that the combined effect of both sensitizers, 5‐ALA and TMZ, is superior for preventing spheroid growth than employing each molecule separately. This finding is relevant for future clinical trials of GBM treatment with SDT.Keywords: 5‐ALA, Glioblastoma, high‐throughput spheroid platform, Sonodynamic therapy, TMZ

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive, common, and lethal glioma, with a mortality rate of ≈95% during the first five years after diagnosis.[ 1 , 2 ] The standard treatment for GBM involves surgical resection followed by a combination of radiation and chemotherapy.[ 3 ] Due to the rapid diffusion and infiltration of glioma cells within the surrounding healthy brain parenchyma, complete surgical removal of the tumor tissue is virtually impossible. Further, the blood‐brain barrier (BBB) deters chemotherapeutic molecules (>0.5 kDa) from entering the tumor tissue,[ 3 ] making standard chemotherapy challenging. Here, sonodynamic therapy (SDT), a minimally invasive, targeted cancer therapy that uses low‐intensity (0.125 – 3 W cm−2) pulsed ultrasound, offers an alternative.[ 4 , 5 ] Since ultrasound is able to deliver energy through the skull to a target within deep brain tissue (>10 cm)[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] and open the BBB,[ 3 , 9 , 10 , 11 ] SDT is considered a potential alternative for GBM therapy.[ 12 ]

The critical component for SDT is the sonosensitizer molecule, a specific nontoxic drug molecule that accumulates selectively within tumor cells due to their high metabolic rate. Sonosensitizers can also take advantage of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) of the tumor tissue when they are administrated through a drug delivery system. They are chemically stable and activated either by ultrasound or light.[ 6 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] While the fundamentals of how sonosensitizers work are unclear,[ 14 , 16 ] it is known that reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated when a sonosensitizer molecule is activated by ultrasound. Based on pre‐clinical in vitro and in vivo investigations of rat glioma, SDT therapy is attributed to the ROS‐mediated cytotoxic effect on tumor cells.[ 4 , 14 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ]

Initial clinical trials of SDT to treat recurrent GBM and diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma report that the 5‐aminolevulinic acid (5‐ALA) sonosensitizer molecule has promising outcomes.[ 12 , 16 , 21 ] In the trials (NCT05123534, NCT06039709, NCT05370508), the sensitizer was activated repeatedly with ultrasound (at 220 kHz, 200‐ 800 J) after 4–6 h of intravenous or oral administration that allows 5‐ALA uptake by tumor cells, and its conversion into protoporphyrin IX (PpIX).[ 12 , 16 ] A promising Phase 1 clinical trial (ChiCTR2200065992) to treat recurrent GBM used a multimodal approach, where SDT was followed by chemotherapy.[ 22 ] Here, the hematoporphyrin sensitizer molecule was stimulated by ultrasound in the 0.8–1 MHz and 1–1.3 W cm−2 range, followed by administration of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved drug temozolomide (TMZ). These clinical trials show that SDT is safe for GBM treatment, but its efficacy requires improvement.

Since the interaction of ultrasound and sensitizer and the energy transfer mechanism between them leading to ROS generation is not yet precisely understood, sensitizer selection remains an important consideration.[ 16 ] Due to their high selectivity in targeting tumor cells, fluorescence ability, low cytotoxicity toward healthy cells, and tunability of their side chains,[ 23 ] most preclinical SDT applications for GBM treatment use porphyrin precursors, such as 5‐ALA,[ 4 , 24 , 25 ] its derivatives like sinoporphyrin sodium (DVDMS),[ 26 , 27 ] hematoporphyrin monomethyl ether (HMME),[ 28 ] and xanthenes, such as Rose Bengal[ 29 ] and fluorescein.[ 14 ] Multimodal SDT treatments primarily use porphyrin sensitizers and TMZ/celecoxib as the drug.[ 24 , 30 ] A recent preclinical investigation found that TMZ also acts as an ultrasound sensitizer,[ 31 , 32 , 33 ] opening the possibility of simultaneously combining TMZ with other sensitizers to enhance SDT efficacy.

Most previous pre‐clinical in vitro SDT studies on GBM have been performed with 2D monolayer cultures, which do not mimic the 3D solid tumor microenvironment (TME) that is heterogeneous and contains oxygen, metabolite, and nutrient gradients extending from the 3D tumor necrotic core to its proliferative periphery. These gradients are instrumental in determining sensitizer accumulation and chemotherapeutic drug diffusion within the tumor.[ 34 ]

Three‐dimensional cellular structures of roughly 200 µm dimension are more capable of representing the spatial organization of an in vivo avascular tumor than a 2D cell culture. The cell‐cell/cell‐extracellular matrix interactions facilitated in 3D without external cues, such as the addition of a specific growth factor, are a better proxy for a tumor than cell‐cell interactions in 2D.[ 35 , 36 , 37 ]The cell‐cell interactions of a spheroid lead to the secretion of an extracellular matrix, which induces the formation of a physical barrier. This barrier prevents drug and sonosensitizer transport into the spheroid in a manner similar to that for in vivo solid tumors.[ 38 ]3D spheroids also exhibit hypoxic conditions in an inner zone,[ 39 ] where gene expression is altered by stabilization of the transcription factor, Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1 α), similar to what occurs in tumor xenografts in vivo.[ 35 , 39 ] The hypoxia occurring in spheroid models is clinically relevant in the context of the resistance acquired against drug molecules[ 40 ] during a multimodal therapy such as SDT followed by chemotherapy. Spheroid models are also cost‐effective and provide a simple, high‐throughput platform for investigating and analyzing drug‐induced therapeutic effects in real‐time as the spheroid size is measured, either by imaging or performing ATP end‐point assays.[ 41 , 42 , 43 ]

However, the literature reports only a single SDT experiment for a 3D GBM spheroid[ 44 ] with a continuous ultrasound (0–0.6 Wcm−2, 100% duty cycle for 3 min). The method used in that study to form spheroids is time‐consuming (48 h) and not scalable. Another SDT investigation on a 3D neurosphere (an organoid structure) took 21 days and is also challenging for scalability.[ 45 ]

Hence, we investigate SDT in vitro on a rapidly produced 3D glioblastoma model in the presence of two organic sensitizer molecules, where we use a new high throughput, label‐free cell printing technology[ 46 , 47 , 48 ] to form glioblastoma spheroids within 6 h with the U‐87 MG cell line. Our hypothesis is that the presence of the two sonosensitizers, one a chemotherapeutic drug, has an additive effect that enhances SDT efficacy. Our results show for the first time that two sonosensitizers, 5‐ALA and the drug TMZ, have a nearly additive effect that enhances tumor cell death in response to low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound stimulation.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Chemicals

5‐Aminolevulinic acid hydrochloride (99% purity, molecular weight 167.59 g mol−1, A16942‐ME) Temozolomide (98%, molecular weight 194.15 g mol−1, T2577‐25MG), Invitrogen Cell ROX Green reagent (C10444), Trypan Blue (0.4%, Thermofisher Scientific, USA), Invitrogen ready probes cell viability imaging kit, Blue/Green (R37609), GLPBIO Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8), Promega CellTiter‐Glo (R) 3D cell viability assay (G9681), Gibco DPBS (14 040 133), Gibco DMEM (11 995 040), are purchased from Fisher Scientific, USA, and Dimethyl Sulfoxide (for molecular biology, D8418), Millipore Sigma, USA.

Cell Line and Cell Culture

U‐87 MG cell lines were purchased from ATCC, USA, and cultured in a complete medium of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium, supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (ATCC, USA, Cat. No. 30–2020) and 1% Penicillin‐Streptomycin (Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 5% CO2 and 37 °C.

Cloning of Lentiviral Vector Containing the Hypoxia Reporter

Gene templates of the hypoxia reporter used in this work are constructed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the complementary DNA of 5 repeat Hypoxia Response elements (5XHREs) connected 5′ to a minimal CMV promoter, UnaG (a green fluorescent protein whose fluorescence was resistant to hypoxia), and PEST (5XHREs‐pCMV‐UnaG‐PEST, called as HRE‐UnaG). PCR was performed using synthesized primers (Integrated DNA Technologies) and Q5 DNA polymerase (NEB, M0491). The amplified gene elements (HRE‐UnaG) were cloned into the modified pHR lentiviral vector (pHR‐PGK‐mCherry), a gift from Wendell Lim (Addgene plasmid # 79 124) digested using EcoRI and NotI, using Gibson Assembly (NEB, E2611L).

U‐87 Hypoxia Responsive Cell Line Generation

The U‐87 Hypoxia reporting cell line (U‐87‐HRE‐UnaG) was generated by the lentiviral transduction (infection). The lentiviruses were produced from Lenti‐X 293T cells (Clontech Laboratories, #632 180) co‐transfected with a pHR contained hypoxia reporter (HRE‐UnaG), and the package plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2 using the ProFection Mammalian Transfection System (Promega, Cat. No. E1200). Virus particles were collected by using a 0.45 µm filter (Sigma‐Millipore) 48 h after transfection, then concentrated by Lenti‐X Concentrator (TAKARA, Cat. #631 232). For creating a stable cell line, U‐87 MG cells (ATCC, #HTB‐14) were infected with the viruses (MOI = 1) in the ratio of 0.1m: 1µL. Cells containing hypoxia reporter (U87‐HRE‐UnaG) were sorted by FACS (Sony, SH800) based on the constitutive reporter (mCherry) in the cells 2–3 days after infection.

Spheroid Printing

Cell spheroids were printed using a high‐throughput, magnetic field‐guided, label‐free method.[ 46 , 47 ] Briefly, U‐87 MG cells were cultured in a complete cell culture medium in a 10‐cm tissue‐cultured treated dish (Figure 1a). After achieving confluency, cells (≈105 cells mL−1) were then harvested using 0.25% Trypsin‐EDTA solution (ATCC, USA, Cat No. 30–2101) and seeded in an uncoated, flat, clear bottom, square 96‐well plate (Cat. No. 89 621, ibidi, USA) with 5000 cells/well in a culture medium containing the paramagnetic agent Gadovist. The paramagnetic agent concentration in the culture medium was maintained at 25 mm.[ 47 ] The well plate was placed on an in‐house designed magnetic array (Figure S1, Supporting Information) containing cube permanent magnets (K J Magnetics, N52‐grade neodymium with dimensions 4.5 × 4.5 × 4.5 mm). Ultrasound Stimulation and Experimental Setup

Figure 1 contains a schematic diagram of the experiment. Experiments are performed with U‐87 MG cells cultured as 2D monolayers to optimize the exposure time and intensity. For this purpose, cells (5000 cells/well) were seeded in tissue culture‐treated 96 well plates (GenClone 25–109) into eight different groups (n = 3 for each group). 48 h after cell confluency, the old media was replaced with the fresh media, and the sensitizers (5‐ALA and TMZ) are added and further incubated for 4 h. The ultrasound transducer was fixed through an in‐house designed support stage, and the wells were stimulated from the bottom via a 3 cm diameter 1 MHz transducer (deLta Digi Sound) head (Figure 1b). An ultrasound transmission gel (Aquasonic) was used between the transducer head and the well plate to minimize the acoustic impedance mismatch, similar to the previous in vitro SDT experimental studies.[ 4 , 44 , 50 , 51 ] In all the experiments, stimulation was carried out at an intensity of 1 W cm−2, 25% duty cycle, and 5 m duration.

For spheroid experiments, 6 h after spheroid formation, 96 well plates containing spheroids were further incubated for 48 h. The old media was replaced by the fresh media containing sonosensitizers and incubated for 4 h. Well plates were then placed on the transducer head, and the stimulation was carried out on eight different groups (n = 3 for each group) with the ultrasound parameters and stimulation conditions held identical to 2D experiments.

Cellular Uptake of 5‐ALA and Intra‐Cellular PpIX Conversion

A 10 mm stock solution of 5‐ALA was prepared in the complete DMEM culture media. After their formation, spheroids were incubated for 24 h, and the old media was replaced with 5‐ALA‐containing media at different concentrations (100–300 µg mL−1). To determine the cellular uptake of 5‐ALA at different times, treated spheroids were incubated at 37 °C and protected from light. Confocal images were taken using a Leica microscope system at two different intervals (4 and 24 h) to evaluate the fluorescence intensities. Intracellular conversion of 5‐ALA to PpIX was confirmed through the fluorescence emission (red, maximum wavelength 635 nm) against a laser excitation of 405 nm (maximum).[ 52 , 53 ] The 5‐ALA stock solution was stored at 4 °C and protected from light for further use.

IC50 Determination of TMZ

To determine the optimum dose for TMZ addition, the IC50 value was determined for U‐87 MG spheroids. After printing the spheroids, they were incubated for 48 h, after which the old medium was carefully replaced without disturbing the spheroid integrity. A stock solution (2500 µm) of TMZ in DMSO was prepared and added to the cells at different concentrations (0, 10, 100, 500, 1000, and 1500 µm). For all conditions, the maximum DMSO concentration in the culture media was held at 0.4% to avoid the cytotoxic effect of the DMSO itself. The cells were incubated in the presence of TMZ for 5 days. After treatment, the old medium was aspirated, and 100 µL of fresh medium was added. CCK‐8 (10 µL) solution[ 32 ] was added in each well and incubated for 2 h. Finally, absorbance was measured using a plate reader (Synergy H4) at 450 nm, and the IC50 value was evaluated using the GraphPad Prism 10 software. For 2D culture, an IC50 value of TMZ was also determined similarly as in 3D culture in this work. Briefly, cells were seeded (5000 cells/well) in tissue culture treated 96 well plates. After 48 h, cells become confluent, and the old media was replaced with new media. TMZ at different concentrations was added from its stock, and cell viability is determined, from which IC50 was evaluated.

Cell Viability: ATP‐Based Luciferase Assay

After SDT experiments, cell viability was assessed using Promega CellTiter‐Glo 3D cell viability assay according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, after ultrasound stimulation with (or without) the presence of sensitizers, spheroids (or 2D cultured cells) are incubated for 24 h. Following the old medium aspiration, the assay reagent was added to the fresh culture medium at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio in each well of the 96‐well plates and mixed vigorously for 5 min to induce cell lysis. After mixing, the plate was kept at room temperature for 25 min, and a dark condition was ensured. Finally, the supernatant was collected into a white opaque tissue culture‐treated 96‐well plate (353 296, Becton Dickinson Labware, USA) to minimize signal loss. The luminescence was measured (integration time 0.01 s) using a plate reader (Synergy H4). Cell viability was evaluated using GraphPad Prism (version 10) software.

Oxidative Stress Quantification

Oxidative stress generation following SDT was quantified using the Invitrogen Cell ROX Green reagent according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, after SDT, Cell ROX green was added to each group and incubated for 30 m at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS three times and imaged with a fluorescence microscope. The excitation and emission wavelengths were 485 and 520 nm, respectively. Images were analyzed with FIJI, and mean fluorescence intensities were plotted using GraphPad Prism software to quantify the data.

Live‐Dead Assay

To assess the live and dead cells following SDT, an Invitrogen ready probes cell viability imaging kit was used according to the manufacturer's protocol. After SDT, one drop of NucBlue live, and NucGreen dead are added simultaneously into each group of spheroids (as well as the 2D cultured cells) and incubated for 30 m at 37 °C. Confocal and fluorescence images were taken with a Leica microscope for spheroids and 2D culture. The standard DAPI (360/460 nm) and FITC (504/523 nm) filters were used for live and dead cell nuclei imaging, respectively.

Spheroid Area Calculation

Brightfield images were captured at regular intervals during a treatment period of 120 h using a Leica microscope to evaluate the spheroid area during TMZ treatment, and images were processed using FIJI. The ferret diameters (in x and y directions) were measured at a specific optical plane (fixed z), and the spheroid area was evaluated thereon.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. The two‐way ANOVA method (Šídák's multiple comparisons tests), one‐way ANOVA method (Tukey's multiple comparison test), and Student's t‐test method are used to find out the significant differences among different groups. p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed in triplicates at least.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Gadovist on Long‐Term Cell Viability

We have previously shown that a 25 mm Gadovist concentration in the cell culture medium leads to a sufficient magnetic susceptibility difference (Equation 1) to produce a net magnetic force that forms intact 3D cellular structures.[ 47 , 48 ] Gadovist toxicity can vary depending on the cell type, and its long‐term effect on U‐87 MG cell lines has not yet been reported. Therefore, we investigate U‐87 MG cell viability during a long‐term culture (7 days) with Gadovist in the culture medium. U‐87 MG cells are cultured as 2D monolayers in tissue culture‐treated 96 well plates. The cell viability is assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‐8, described in Section 2.8, at intervals of 24, 72, 120, and 168 h.

The effect of Gadovist on U‐87 MG cell viability is shown in Figure 2 . Even with Gadovist continuously present in the cell culture medium up to 168 h (7 days), there is no significant change between the treated and control groups. Thus, a 25 mm Gadovist concentration is used for all spheroids produced.

Effect of Gadovist (25 mm) on U‐87 MG cell viability up to 7 days (168 h). Cell viability is assessed with the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8). Observation shows that even after continuous culture in the presence of 25 mm Gadovist, no significant change in cell viability is found compared to the control group. Thus, in all experiments the Gadovist concentration is maintained at 25 mm in the cell culture medium. Our label‐free magnetic spheroid printing method is more rapid and scalable compared to any other glioblastoma spheroid formation approach reported in the literature.[ 44 , 45 , 54 ] Therefore, this novel in vitro platform can be used to evaluate the efficacy of the processes and molecules used to treat solid tumors.

Hypoxia Assessment via Tumor Spheroid Model

Experiments are conducted to confirm that the morphology obtained with our spheroid printing method is adequate in replicating hypoxic conditions in the tumor inner zone, as in an in vivo TME. Cells respond to hypoxia via HIF1 activation to promote transcription of hypoxia‐regulated genes. Utilizing this response, previous investigators[ 55 , 56 ] isolated the consensus binding region of HIF1, namely Hypoxia‐responsive elements (HRE), and combined it with a minimal CMV promoter to produce a hypoxia promoter (5XHRE‐pCMV). The hypoxia promoter can be used to detect the response of cells to hypoxia by combining it with oxygen‐independent fluorescence proteins, for example, UnaG.[ 57 ] This enables visualization of the cellular hypoxia response via live‐cell fluorescence imaging.

This hypoxia reporter in U87‐HRE‐UnaG cells drives the expression of UnaG via the hypoxia promoter. The expression of UnaG indicates hypoxic conditions within the spheroid. Therefore, we visualized the hypoxic zone through live‐cell fluorescence imaging, where the increase in green fluorescence intensity correlates with a decrease in oxygen levels (higher hypoxia).

Figure 4 reveals that the GBM spheroids formed with the hypoxia‐responsive U‐87 MG cell line exhibit a naturally occurring hypoxia zone 12 h after printing. A confocal image (Figure 5 ) at different optical planes is also captured 36 h after spheroid printing. The green fluorescence signal from the core confirms UnaG expression. This confirms that the spheroids that are formed using our system can adequately simulate physiological conditions, such as the O2 concentration gradient, making our spheroids suitable models for studying cancer and TME.

UnaG expression is obtained with a standard GFP filter. These results establish the reliability of our spheroid printing method in mimicking an O2 gradient similar to that in the in vivo TME.

Cellular Uptake of 5‐ALA at Different Time Intervals

The incubation time following 5‐ALA addition to the culture medium is important since the intracellular enzymatic conversion of 5‐ALA to PpIX ultimately drives the PpIX concentration and, thereby, SDT efficacy due to its ability to participate in the sonochemical reactions.[ 3 ] Therefore, we investigate the cellular uptake of 5‐ALA at two different times after its addition to the cell culture medium. For this purpose, spheroids are printed, and the old medium is replaced with a fresh medium that contains varying concentrations of 5‐ALA (100–300 µg mL−1) followed by 4 and 24 h of incubation. Treated spheroids are imaged with an excitation wavelength of 405 nm and an emission wavelength window between 600 and 695 nm (Figure 7 ) according to the PpIX maximum excitation and emission wavelength reported in the literature.[ 53 ]Image analysis is performed with FIJI, and the mean fluorescence intensities are measured. For all groups, the same ROI is considered during intensity measurement.

Cellular uptake of 5‐ALA by the U‐87 MG spheroid at different times after treatment. (a) Spheroids are treated with 5‐ALA with a varying concentration of 100–300 µg mL−1, incubated, and imaged at 4 and 24 h. Mean fluorescence intensity measurements show decreasing intensity with increasing incubation time. However, the fluorescence intensity increases with an increase in dosage. For all groups, the same ROI is considered for intensity measurement. The error bar shows the mean and standard deviation for n = 3; (b) The left column represents live cell nuclei for control (untreated), 100 and 300 µg mL−1 after staining with NucBlue live‐ready probes at the optical plane with the highest fluorescence intensity. The middle and last columns represent the PpIX signal for 4 and 24 h incubation with 5‐ALA, respectively. Following excitation with a 405 nm wavelength, a reddish signal in the emission wavelength window of 600–695 nm confirms the conversion of intracellular ALA to PpIX for the treated groups.

For the groups treated with 5‐ALA, the mean fluorescence intensity decreases with incubation time. The highest intensity is observed after 4 h of incubation in the presence of 5‐ALA (Figure 7a). 5‐ALA is a precursor in the heme biosynthesis pathway in mitochondria. In cancer cells, anaerobic glycolysis is activated over oxidative phosphorylation and tricarboxylic acid cycle, known as the Warburg effect.[ 59 ] This effect produces a scarcity of electrons, reducing the activity of Ferrochelatase (FECH), which typically converts PpIX to heme. The downregulation of FECH, therefore, leads to a decrease in heme conversion. Consequently, PpIX concentration temporarily increases during exogenous administration of 5‐ALA, leading to an increased fluorescence intensity at 4 h.[ 60 ]

This observation is also supported by the confocal images in Figure 7b, where a reddish fluorescence signal confirms 5‐ALA to PpIX intracellular conversion for both treated groups. This result is in good agreement with the previous reports of 5‐ALA‐based glioma surgery.[ 61 ] The mean fluorescence intensity increases with increasing 5‐ALA dosage (Figure 7a). However, for the control group, no change in the mean fluorescence intensity is observed during the 24 h incubation period. Thus, for all SDT experiments, a 5‐ALA concentration of 300 µg mL−1 is selected with incubation over 4 h before ultrasound stimulation.

US: Ultrasound, T: TMZ, A: 5‐ALA.

For a 2D culture, Figure 9a shows that in the absence of ultrasound, TMZ (T) or 5‐ALA (A) alone do not significantly influence tumor cell viability (%) after 24 h of treatment compared to the untreated (control) group. However, in the presence of ultrasound (US + T, US + A), both TMZ and 5‐ALA have a significant influence on cell viability (US+T: 59.7 ± 14.9%, US + A: 57.9 ± 2.9%). Ultrasound alone (US) also produces a significant change in cell viability (70.8 ± 15.2%). Maximum therapeutic efficacy is observed when TMZ and 5‐ALA are added together in the presence of ultrasound (US + A+ T: 37.6 ± 1.1%).

For 3D spheroids, Figure 9b shows that, like the 2D culture, in the absence of ultrasound, TMZ (T) or 5‐ALA (A) do not cause a significant change in the cell viability 24 h after treatment compared to the control group. Ultrasound stimulation alone (US) also does not induce a significant change in cell viability compared to the control group. Here, mechanical stimulation alone (1 W cm−2, 1 MHz, 25% duty factor) is insufficient to cause a physiological change within the compact 3D cellular structure. Administration of 5‐ALA or TMZ alone with ultrasound (US + A or US + T) is more effective compared to the control group. However, the efficacy of US + A is lower (71.1 ± 14.8%) in the 3D GBM model as compared to the 2D culture. This is attributed to the larger 2D culture surface area available for a given mass, which allows higher 5‐ALA adsorption and, consequently, cellular uptake through membrane permeabilization for that mass. Furthermore, in a 2D monolayer culture, cells are exposed uniformly to mechanical (pressure) waves during ultrasound application. This results in the uniform activation of the sonosensitizer over the cell population. However, in the case of 3D spheroid, the cellular uptake of 5‐ALA is determined by its diffusion through the periphery to the core of the spatially heterogeneous spheroid, which is a slower phenomenon that results in lower efficacy. Also, because of the spatial heterogeneity, varying sonosensitizer activation levels are expected due to uneven ultrasound energy transfer within the tumor spheroid.

On the other hand, TMZ exhibits dual features.[ 32 ] It exhibits antitumor activity through DNA base (Guanine) methylation[ 62 ] and also acts as a sonosensitizer with ultrasound stimulation due to the low energy gap between its singlet and triplet state (Δ EST = 0.1 eV).[ 32 ] Therefore, TMZ induces mitochondria‐independent necroptosis, producing tumor cell death by increasing ROS when ultrasound is applied. Literature also reports that transient pores with a resealing time of ≈120s form in response to the low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound, a phenomenon called sonoporation.[ 63 ] The sonoporation facilitates drug and sonosensitizer uptake by the tumor cells depending on ultrasound energy transfer, which differs for 2D cultures and 3D spheroids due to differences in their exposures as monolayer/cellular aggregates to the applied ultrasound field. We found a similar significant efficacy for TMZ alone with ultrasound (US + T: 49.7 ± 2.2%) for both the 2D and 3D models compared to the control group. TMZ and 5‐ALA used together in the presence of ultrasound (US +A +T) have the highest SDT efficacy (41.5 ± 8.6%) among all groups in the spheroid model.

It is well established that the primary cell death pathway for 5‐ALA‐based SDT is due to ROS‐mediated damage to mitochondria followed by the release of cytochrome c, which triggers cellular apoptosis.[ 3 ] However, TMZ‐based SDT triggers ROS‐mediated necroptosis. Thus, the presence of both sonosensitizers involves mitochondrial pathways for cellular apoptosis and cellular necroptosis (mitochondria independent), leading to greater SDT efficacy (in terms of % cell viability) irrespective of the GBM model.

This finding is further supported by the oxidative stress assessment in 2D (see Figure S4, Supporting Information) following SDT. We used Invitrogen Cell ROX green reagent, which exhibits weak fluorescence at its reduced state. Following SDT, it is oxidized by reactive oxygen species and exhibits a bright green fluorescence signal. This reveals that the maximum oxidative stress (normalized with respect to the control group) is generated for the ALA‐TMZ‐treated group. We also perform a live‐dead assay following SDT in the 2D and 3D GBM models (see Section S5, Supporting Information). The live and dead cell nuclei for two groups (control and US +A+T) in 2D and 3D models are shown in Figure S5 (Supporting Information).

An analysis is conducted based on our experimental observations (Figure 9) to investigate the effect of the simultaneous presence of TMZ and 5‐ALA in response to ultrasound. For this purpose, we have used the Bliss independence approach (see Section S6, Supporting Information)[ 64 ] as the best fit for our work and evaluated the combination index (CI). The evaluated combination index (CI = 1.04 for 2D culture and 1.10 for 3D spheroid) implies a nearly additive effect[ 65 , 66 ] in the simultaneous presence of two sonosensitizers for both 2D and 3D GBM models. This nearly additive effect results in the highest cell death.

Conclusion

We investigate an in vitro 3D glioblastoma model using sonodynamic therapy (SDT) using a new high‐throughput, label‐free, scalable, magnetic field‐guided method to create GBM spheroids more rapidly than other existing spheroid printing approaches. For the first time, we used two organic sonosensitizers and evaluated their efficacy in a GBM spheroid model. The outcomes of this work are:
