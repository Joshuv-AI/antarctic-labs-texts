Qualitative study of barriers and facilitators to Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) on the Caribbean island of Montserrat
Authors: Bill Berners-Lee, Nadine Duberry, Dorothea Hazel-Blake, Christian Mallen, Thomas Shepherd, Anna S Tirion, Dale Weston, Sharra Greenaway-Duberry, Riinu Pae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertension is the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, with high prevalence in the Eastern Caribbean. Dietary modification is central to prevention and management, with the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet supported by strong clinical evidence. DASH promotes increased consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, nuts, seeds and pulses while limiting salt, sugar, high-fat meats and dairy, and refined carbohydrates. Although promoted in primary care in Montserrat, adherence may be limited by structural, social and cultural barriers. We aimed to explore the barriers and facilitators experienced by Montserratians attempting to follow the DASH eating plan.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with 16 adults in Montserrat diagnosed with hypertension recruited through primary healthcare providers. Purposive sampling ensured representation across sex and country of birth. Interviews lasting 20–40 min were conducted between June and August 2025, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and anonymised. Using a topic guide developed with local and UK experts, participants discussed dietary practices, barriers, facilitators and suggested interventions. Data were analysed using the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) through deductive thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants (aged 40–79) identified five themes shaping DASH adherence: understanding of hypertension and DASH, access to healthy foods, skills and self-efficacy, cultural practices and readiness to change. While hypertension was seen as urgent, cost, availability and seasonality of fruits, vegetables and fish limited adherence. Cooking and cultivation skills supported adherence, though food label literacy was low. Cultural practices and family dynamics both enabled and constrained adherence, and taste preferences often posed barriers. Suggested interventions included nutrition education, financial support, agricultural coordination and peer support.ConclusionsMontserratian adults with hypertension are motivated to improve their diets, but DASH adherence is constrained by financial, logistical, cultural and knowledge barriers. Effective interventions must integrate structural supports with culturally appropriate strategies, developed collaboratively with stakeholders, to ensure feasibility, acceptability and sustainability.Keywords: Community Health, Hypertension, Public Health

Introduction

Hypertension, or high blood pressure, is the greatest modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, which is estimated to cause 17.8 million deaths every year and an annual loss of 388 million disability-adjusted life years.1 2 Hypertension is also an important risk factor for chronic kidney disease and dementia.3 A recent analysis from the Eastern Caribbean Health Outcomes Network Cohort Study (ECS) reported a hypertension prevalence of 57.9% among participants aged 40 years and older.4

Hypertension can be prevented and treated with dietary interventions. Among these, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) is supported by a wealth of evidence.5 6 There is also evidence that DASH provides further benefits, including a reduced risk of cancer7 and all-cause mortality.8 DASH is made up of a set of broad recommendations that include reducing consumption of sodium, sugar and high-fat dairy or meat, as well as increasing consumption of whole-grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, seeds and pulses.9 DASH also recommends the use of cooking oils high in unsaturated fats, such as olive oil or avocado oil, rather than products high in saturated or trans fats, such as coconut oil or butter.9 While DASH was designed to be applicable across cultures and cuisines at a low cost, low levels of adherence have reduced its effectiveness in the past.10 Several dietary patterns, including DASH and the Mediterranean diet, are recommended for the prevention and management of hypertension and cardiovascular disease. Although dietary patterns differ in their specific prescriptions, many share a common foundation: high intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts and fish, alongside restrictions on red meat, saturated fat and sodium.11 Given this overlap, barriers to adherence are unlikely to be unique. DASH was therefore used in this study as a well-defined and clinically recommended exemplar, but many of the barriers identified are likely to be relevant to dietary guideline adherence more broadly. DASH is also the diet primarily used to inform the advice provided by primary care professionals in Montserrat.

There are a number of factors that could explain low adherence to DASH in a specific population. A review of previous studies in a different population (low-income African-Americans) highlighted material, social and cultural barriers to DASH adherence.12 Material barriers involved the economic cost of and limited access to healthier foods, and insufficient material resources (refrigeration and cooking equipment) to store and prepare these foods.13,15 Social barriers included competing preferences from other household members,13 while cultural barriers included: limited exposure to the principles of the DASH diet, confusion around portion sizes and sodium content, and a lack of confidence in adapting traditional dishes in line with DASH recommendations.14

While evidence of barriers to DASH adherence exists for other populations experiencing a high burden of diet-related disease, we are not aware of any specific study among the Montserratian population. Montserrat may face particular challenges due to its recent and colonial history. Following a series of major volcanic eruptions between 1995 and 2013, Montserrat’s population declined sharply—from 10 639 in 1991 to 4396 in 2023.16 An exclusion zone now prohibits access to much of the island’s arable land, and transport links to the island remain severely restricted. These factors have had implications for both the cost and availability of food in Montserrat. Montserrat also has a unique food culture, shaped by its history and diverse population. The country’s food culture is influenced by its African and Irish heritage, as well as by more recent migration from other Caribbean, South American and North American countries (as of 2023, 44% of the Montserrat population were born outside of the country).16

In light of this, we aimed to explore the potential barriers and facilitators experienced by Montserratians attempting to follow the DASH eating plan. We also invited participants to suggest public health interventions they would find helpful. This analysis represents a first step toward co-developing effective public health strategies with, and for, Montserratian individuals who are managing hypertension.

Methods

Participants

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 16 participants aged 40–79 who had received a diagnosis of hypertension in Montserrat; no participants withdrew at any stage. Participants were recruited through primary healthcare services at the island’s health centres, where a nurse, doctor or nutrition officer introduced them to the study. Those who expressed interest were subsequently contacted by telephone and invited to attend an interview at a time and location convenient to them.

Purposive sampling was used to ensure representation across sexes and nationality. Given that a high proportion of Montserrat residents were born elsewhere in the region (38.9% at the 2023 census16), we also aimed to include both Montserratian nationals and Caribbean-born residents. However, recruitment challenges meant we were unable to achieve our targets of an equal sex split or 40% Caribbean-born participants, though representation across both groups was achieved (table 1).

Characteristics of interviewees

Age was not prespecified as a sampling dimension. Given Montserrat’s small population and the practical constraints of recruiting through primary healthcare services, formal age quotas were not set. The final age distribution of the sample—with fewer participants in the 40–49 and 70–79 age groups (table 1)—reflects patterns of health service access rather than a deliberate sampling strategy. While age may be a source of heterogeneity in DASH adherence behaviour, the TDF-based analytical framework was considered sufficient to capture the range of relevant behavioural influences across the age groups represented.

Sample size was determined using an information power approach.17 Given the specificity of the research question, the purposive sampling strategy and the use of a structured deductive framework (the Theoretical Domains Framework; TDF), a relatively small sample was considered sufficient. Data were analysed in parallel with collection, and by the time recruitment ceased—which was ultimately for pragmatic reasons—no new TDF domains or substantively new barriers or facilitators had emerged across the final interviews, providing confidence in the adequacy of the dataset.

Procedure

Interviews were conducted by an epidemiologist (WBL, male) between 6 June 2025 and 6 August 2025. The interviewer had undertaken relevant qualitative research training and was professionally interested in nutrition. WBL had a pre-existing professional relationship with one participant, but no relationship with any other participants. The topic guide was structured around the tenets of the DASH eating plan and specific questions were mapped against the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) framework.18 It was refined with assistance from a behavioural scientist at the UK Health Security Agency (AT, female) and a nutrition officer in the Montserrat Ministry of Health and Social Services (MT, female) before data collection (see online supplemental appendix 1 for topic guide with each question mapped against its corresponding COM-B domain). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the aims and rationale for the research were explained in full before the interview. Interviews all lasted between 20 and 40 min. All interviews were conducted in person, in a private room at a location of the participants’ choosing (either the participant’s workplace or a health centre), with only the interviewer and interviewee present. At the end of the interview, participants were signposted to online resources relating to hypertension and diet.

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service (The Transcription Company). The transcripts were fully anonymised before being returned by the professional transcription service. Given the small population size, anonymisation was double-checked by a long-term resident of Montserrat to ensure no individual could be recognised from their responses.

Analysis

Transcripts were coded using Microsoft Excel by at least one researcher. Initially, three team members (WBL, male epidemiologist; ND, female nurse practitioner; DHB, female director of primary healthcare) coded the same three transcripts to ensure consistency, meeting regularly to resolve uncertainties. Only WBL had experience of conducting thematic analysis. The transcripts were initially coded according to the TDF,19 through deductive thematic analysis.20 Themes were then refined and consolidated by the wider team. Deductive thematic analysis was preferred over content analysis as it retains interpretive depth while enabling systematic, theory-driven coding—an approach recommended when a validated framework such as the TDF is applied a priori.19 20

TDF was chosen as the analytical framework for this study, given its broad applicability to drivers and barriers to behaviour change and its established utility in structuring qualitative interview data.19 Rather than relying on a single theory, the TDF synthesises 128 theoretical constructs from 33 behaviour change theories into 14 domains, providing comprehensive coverage of the cognitive, affective, social and environmental influences on behaviour—thereby reducing the risk of important determinants being overlooked.19 The TDF can be understood as a more granular expansion of the COM-B model,18 which proposes that behaviour arises from the interaction of three conditions: capability, opportunity and motivation. Each of the 14 TDF domains maps onto one of these three COM-B components, as summarised in figure 1.19 While COM-B offers a parsimonious overarching model of behaviour, the TDF elaborates its components in greater detail, making it particularly well-suited to exploratory qualitative research where the range of influences on a behaviour is not known in advance.18 19

Public and patient involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in developing the research question, study design, recruitment or conduct of the interviews. Findings will be disseminated via a stakeholder consultation including patient contributors with hypertension, representatives from the Ministry of Health and Social Services and relevant community and business organisations, with the aim of translating results into feasible public-health interventions.

Results

16 Montserrat residents with a diagnosis of hypertension participated in the study (6 males and 10 females). Most participants were born in Montserrat, with four born elsewhere in the Caribbean. The age of participants ranged from 40 to 79 years old (mean: 58.9, SD: 9.9). The characteristics of the interviewees are reported in table 1.

The analysis identified 5 themes and 10 subthemes, corresponding to 10 of the TDF domains. Additionally, three subthemes were identified among the public health interventions suggested by participants. These themes and subthemes are reported, along with illustrative quotes, in online supplemental appendix 2.

Awareness and understanding of hypertension and the tenets of DASH

Participants’ beliefs about the consequences of uncontrolled hypertension provided a strong motivation to change their diets. Hypertension was perceived as an urgent, tangible threat rather than an abstract diagnosis, with participants linking dietary decisions to severe outcomes. One explained that it “could cause me a stroke” (participant 1), while another recalled that “my sister died from hypertension … she died at the age of 30” (participant 3). These accounts framed DASH as a means of preventing a known risk.

Practical access to healthy foods

Environmental context and the availability of resources strongly influenced participants’ perceived ability to follow DASH. Cost and availability were recurring barriers, particularly when buying fruits and vegetables, with one participant noting that “you can plant your vegetables, but buying the fruit sometimes it might be a bit expensive” (participant 2) and another observing that “the seasonality [makes it] a bit of a challenge” (Participant 8). Access to fresh fish was also uneven due to geography and supply—“I hardly get fish … sometimes [the fishermen] don’t go in the water. Where I’m living, I’m not close to them … by the time I reach them they are already gone” (Participant 5)—and compounded by price, as one participant explained: “you can get fish [in the shops], but it’s expensive … the common man can’t do that” (participant 3).

Skills and self-efficacy for following DASH

Participants often felt they had the necessary practical skills to enable DASH-aligned behaviours, frequently highlighting cooking and cultivation capabilities. One participant explained, “I prefer growing. Try and get a piece of ground and try and plant” (participant 1). Others described seasoning food without salt—“No, [I don’t add salt] … We all know how to season” (participant 4)—preparing drinks with less sugar than those sold in shops—“I make my own drinks … I add a bit of mango and guava … I [only] add a little bit of sugar” (participant 7)—and growing local staples such as lentils, which “could be grown right in your garden” (participant 8). Some also reported expanding their skills through informal learning, for example, by trying recipes found on YouTube (participant 4).

Participants described self-devised strategies for regulating their diets, such as diluting sweet drinks (participant 5), measuring sugar with a small spoon (participant 7) and carefully controlling the quantities of stock cubes used in cooking (participant 7).

However, knowledge gaps were also evident. Self-reported label literacy was limited—“most of the time I [only] read the label to check expiry dates” (participant 9)—and several participants expressed a desire for more education in this area, noting that “we need to have more education in that regard … reading labels” (participant 6). Others addressed their self-reported knowledge gaps using information sources such as phones, the internet or local radio (participants 6, 4, 2).

Cultural barriers and facilitators to DASH adherence

Social influences, taste and sensory experiences of food, and cultural identities simultaneously supported and impeded adherence to DASH. Household dynamics sometimes conflicted with dietary intentions; one participant noted that his wife “adds a little bit of butter … and I have to cut that out” (participant 1), which at times led to parallel meal preparation that was described as “plenty challenging” (participant 1). Social temptations from peers could also impede adherence, although family support was often described as helpful, with relatives “trying to help” and “generally supportive” (participant 6).

Taste and texture were powerful barriers against healthier food choices. Sugar-free drinks were dismissed as having “no taste at all” (participant 4), imported fish was rejected for its texture (participant 9), white bread was considered “more tasty” (participant 8), brown rice “too hard” (participant 5) and fattier cuts of chicken—particularly wings—were often preferred (Participant 2). Some participants felt that these less healthy preferences were passed down within families. One participant described how “parents teach that to the children because they don’t eat vegetables either” (participant 4).

Traditional practices were viewed as protective. Practices such as trimming fat from chicken while ‘cleaning’ it (participant 3) and the long-standing inclusion of pulses in the diet (participant 9) were framed as health-promoting, with one participant noting that “if we practice all of the traditions [we will be] a bit more healthy” (participant 3). However, preferences acknowledged as less healthy, such as a preference for white rice, were also attributed to cultural norms (participant 6). Alongside conventional dietary strategies, participants expressed confidence in traditional remedies such as ‘bush tea’ and lime juice, which some believed were informally endorsed by healthcare professionals (participant 7).

Readiness to change and intentions

Intentions were framed as a matter of discipline and prioritisation amid constraints. Adherence was seen as effortful—“It takes a lot of discipline and commitment and time” (participant 6)—yet also as a rational investment—“it’s better to spend the money on food than give it to the doctor for a consultation” (participant 3). Participants balanced cost concerns with health goals—“[fresh vegetables] are expensive … but for your health you have to buy [them]” (participant 10); “They’re a little expensive but you can’t eat the money” (participant 7).

Intervention suggestions

Participants proposed pragmatic solutions to several self-identified barriers. To address knowledge gaps, they called for simple, actionable tools—such as “a table with the charts, ‘You could eat this, you could eat that’” (participant 1)—and more intensive community-facing communication, with one suggesting that “they usually do radio programmes but … need to intensify it or maybe call like a town hall meeting” (participant 9). To ease environmental and resource constraints, participants recommended financial assistance to offset food costs (participant 10) and greater coordination with the agriculture department to prioritise the importation of healthier items (participant 8). To counter social influences, peer support was suggested in the form of “more group settings for open discussion … like support groups” (participant 6).

Discussion

Main findings

Our results support several broad conclusions about the perceived facilitators and barriers to DASH. First, the participants interviewed in this study perceive both the availability and cost of healthier foods—such as fruits, vegetables and fish—to be a barrier to DASH adherence. Second, there was a general preference for locally produced food and traditional dietary practices. This included a preference for local meats, fish, fruits and vegetables, as well as for ‘local’ drinks and traditional cooking methods—such as ‘washing’ meat with lime juice and rehydrating dried pulses. Third, participants generally reported strong self-efficacy in preparing and producing food, but low self-efficacy in understanding nutritional information on food packages. Among our participants, readiness and intention to change diet were strong. While this may partly reflect bias from opportunistic sampling, it is consistent with the view that some Montserratians are motivated to improve their health but are constrained by structural barriers.

Findings in the context of current literature

These findings are consistent with the COM-B model of behaviour change, which emphasises that motivation alone is insufficient for sustained dietary change without the presence of structural supports.18 In this respect, our results parallel previous studies of barriers and facilitators to DASH adherence,12 which similarly highlight the interplay between individual readiness and environmental constraints. The primacy of cost barriers in the present study is also concordant with prior research12; however, in the Montserratian context, this takes on specific forms, particularly the consistently high price of fruits, vegetables and fish. Moreover, although cultural influences on DASH adherence have been identified elsewhere,12 the barriers reported here—such as the strong preference for locally produced foods and specific traditional preparation practices—are distinctive to this population. This underscores the importance of culturally tailored interventions that adapt DASH principles to align with local food cultures and practices, rather than applying externally derived models in a prescriptive manner.

Strengths and limitations

Some limitations of this research should be noted. We used an opportunity sample and included only participants with a hypertension diagnosis. Excluding participants with undiagnosed hypertension limits the generalisability of the findings. In the ECS study, 34.8% of participants with hypertension were unaware of their condition, highlighting the sizeable proportion of undiagnosed cases in the population.4 This study is also limited by focusing solely on perceived rather than objective barriers. While similar studies have previously conducted systematic surveys of food environments to complement their qualitative findings,14 this was beyond the scope of our study. As such, the barriers identified should be understood as perceptions that shape dietary decision-making, rather than as definitive indicators of objective environmental constraints.

This study focused on patient perspectives; logistical constraints precluded recruitment from other groups. Future studies would benefit from including key informants such as health workers and government officials, who may offer complementary insights.

Our study was also limited by its focus on a single risk factor for hypertension. Future research into other contributing factors, such as physical inactivity, would be beneficial and could yield additional public health intervention suggestions.

Implications for policy and practice

Our findings support the development of structural, social, and educational interventions. Barriers relating to the cost and availability of healthy foods could be mitigated through financial and logistical measures, while social influences and knowledge gaps may be addressed through peer support and targeted education. Participant accounts of limited access to fresh food are substantiated by data from the Government of Montserrat Statistics Department: in 2022, imported produce outweighed domestically grown produce, with imports accounting for roughly 81% of total vegetable and fruit supply by weight.21 Local production of fish and livestock has also declined sharply, with total fish landings falling from 38 303 lbs in 2019 to 34 022 lbs in 2022, and livestock production contracting by two-thirds over the same period21—noting that inclusion of cereals, dairy and processed foods would make overall import dependency considerably higher.

Participant accounts about the relatively high cost of food were also substantiated by official statistics. Income data from the 2020 Labour Force Survey illustrate the constrained financial circumstances of many residents: approximately three-quarters of employed people in Montserrat earned less than EC$4,000 per month.22 When set against retail prices for basic staples recorded in December 2022—including tomatoes at EC$11.58/lb, onions at EC$5.89/lb and eggs at EC$18.53/dozen—these income levels suggest that a substantial proportion of residents face meaningful financial barriers to maintaining a healthy diet.21

Together, these structural and economic conditions point to the need for logistical and financial initiatives to improve access to affordable fruits, vegetables and fish, alongside educational programmes emphasising nutritional label literacy. These barriers are not unique to Montserrat: a systematic review of nutrition-based interventions across Small Island Developing States (SIDS) identified declining local agricultural production, import dependency and resource scarcity as dominant constraints on dietary behaviour across the region, applicable across interventions targeting a range of conditions including obesity, diabetes and hypertension.23 More broadly, SIDS share structural food environment challenges that are likely to make the barriers and facilitators identified in this study resonate across comparable Caribbean contexts. The systematic review of dietary interventions in SIDS also highlighted that effective interventions must be informed by local cultures and contexts, and that evidence to guide such approaches remains limited23—a gap this study directly addresses.

We identified tradition and cultural identity as important determinants of dietary behaviour. The effectiveness and acceptability of dietary guidance may therefore be enhanced by aligning recommendations with traditional practices and foods that are consistent with the DASH approach. Similar strategies have been suggested in other contexts where local cultural practices reinforce evidence-based dietary guidance,24 and reviews of SIDS-based interventions have similarly identified cultural relevance as integral to effectiveness.23

Overall, these results provide a strong foundation for developing interventions to promote DASH adherence. However, interventions will require further refinement to ensure feasibility. The practical suggestions offered by participants represent a valuable resource for this process. Development should also be undertaken in consultation with a broad range of stakeholders to ensure interventions are affordable, practicable, cost-effective, acceptable, safe and equitable.

Conclusions

This study highlights the complex interplay between structural, cultural and individual factors influencing DASH adherence in Montserrat. While motivation to adopt healthier diets is present, it is constrained by financial, logistical and cultural barriers. Addressing these challenges will require interventions that are not only evidence-based but also culturally responsive and developed in collaboration with local stakeholders. With culturally appropriate structural support, there is significant potential to enhance the feasibility and sustainability of DASH in Montserrat. As discussed above, the structural and cultural barriers identified are not unique to Montserrat, and the findings are likely to have broader relevance across Caribbean and comparable SIDS contexts23 and to other dietary patterns that overlap with DASH.
