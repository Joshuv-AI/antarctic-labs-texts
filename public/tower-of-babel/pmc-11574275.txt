Sensory stimulation enhances visual working memory capacity
Authors: Indre Pileckyte, Salvador Soto-Faraco
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Visual working memory (vWM) plays a crucial role in visual information processing and higher cognitive functions; however, it has a very limited capacity. Recently, several studies have successfully modulated vWM capacity in humans using entrainment with transcranial alternate current stimulation (tACS) by targeting parietal theta in a frequency-specific manner. In the current study, we aim to expand upon these findings by utilizing sensory instead of electrical stimulation. Across six behavioral experiments (combined N = 209), we applied rhythmic visual and auditory sensory stimulation at 4 Hz and 7 Hz, aiming to modulate vWM capacity. Collectively, the results showed an overall robust improvement with sensory stimulation at either frequency, compared to baseline. However, contrary to our prediction, 7 Hz stimulation tended to slightly outperform 4 Hz stimulation. Importantly, the observed facilitatory effect was mainly driven by the low-capacity sub-group of participants. Follow-up experiments using the Attention Network Test (ANT) and pupillometry measures did not find evidence that this effect could be directly attributed to modulation of phasic or tonic arousal. We speculate that our results differed from those obtained with tACS due to targeting functionally different theta oscillations, or the modulation of participants’ temporal expectations.

Introduction

Visual working memory (vWM) underlies our ability to maintain visual information in an active state1,2, which is fundamental for sampling the environment3,4, object search5,6, and various higher cognitive functions, including decision-making7–9, goal-directed behavior10,11, and mathematical abilities12,13, among others. One of the most prominent features of visual working memory is its severely limited capacity, typically around three to four simple items2,14,15, making it a significant bottleneck for many cognitive functions16,17. Consequently, individual differences in visual working memory capacity have been shown to explain a significant portion of variability in a range of cognitive measurements and aptitude scores2. Moreover, extensive research has shown that visual working memory capacity is reduced in a number of psychiatric diseases and neurological conditions, such as schizophrenia18,19, Parkinson’s disease20,21, or following a stroke22. Therefore, vWM capacity estimates can be used as important markers of cognitive impairment in various patient populations. Considering its wide-reaching significance for both healthy and patient populations, there has been a growing scientific effort to better understand the physiological mechanisms underlying visual working memory and its capacity and, eventually, to search for ways of improving them23–28.

Non-invasive stimulation-based interventions using electrical or magnetic currents have aimed to improve vWM performance by targeting brain regions and inter-regional networks underlying visual working memory, previously identified with neuroimaging techniques29–35. These studies have demonstrated that rhythmic brain stimulation targeting the posterior parietal cortex36–39 or the prefrontal cortex40–43 can significantly enhance visual working memory in young adults36–39,42,44–46, the elderly40,47–52, and various patient populations41,53–56 (but see refs. 57,58 for conflicting findings). Such stimulation has been applied at theta38,39,44,51,59–61, alpha52,62,63, beta46, and gamma45,64–66 frequencies, as well as using cross-frequency patterns67,68, indicating that not only the stimulation site but also the stimulation frequency may play a crucial role in improving vWM performance. This has led to the elucidation of the functional roles of different neural oscillations in visual WM and the formulation of theoretical models such as the theta-gamma, or oscillatory multiplexing buffer, model28,69,70 for working memory (see Fig. 1a). The model proposes that individual items in visual working memory are encoded by local neural ensembles firing at a gamma frequency, with the order of firing of each ensemble coordinated by the phase of a slower oscillation at theta frequency. The theta-gamma model of working memory has motivated the development of stimulation-based interventions that directly targeted visual working memory capacity, as opposed to overall visual working memory performance. Specifically, the model predicts that if entrainment slows down the relevant endogenous theta frequency, thus lengthening its duty cycle, then more gamma cycles can be embedded within the theta period, effectively increasing vWM capacity. In contrast, speeding up the endogenous theta frequency would shorten its duty cycle, reducing the number of gamma cycles that can be embedded and, consequently, decreasing vWM capacity. Indeed, these predictions have been tested by several studies that utilized transcranial alternate current stimulation (tACS) to entrain the endogenous theta frequency39,59–61. For example, Wolinski et al. 59 demonstrated that stimulating the right parietal cortex at a low theta frequency of 4 Hz improved visual working memory performance and capacity for stimuli presented in the contralateral visual hemifield. Conversely, stimulation at a higher theta frequency of 7 Hz, presumably above the individual theta frequency for most participants, reduced visual working memory capacity for contralateral stimuli.

While these studies provide compelling evidence that vWM capacity can be entrained and modulated, transcranial alternate current stimulation comes with certain drawbacks71. Most importantly, tACS is not easily applicable in more ecologically valid real-world situations outside the laboratory or clinic, thus limiting its generalization and potential transferability. Additionally, tACS produces large stimulation artifacts, which restrict the exploration of online entrainment effects and oscillatory changes in the brain71–74, and it does not allow for the use of arrhythmic stimulation conditions as controls. An alternative technique to circumvent these drawbacks is sensory entrainment75,76. It has been shown that trains of rhythmic sensory stimuli across different modalities can be used to entrain endogenous delta77, theta78–84, alpha83,85–90, beta91, and gamma92,93 oscillations, as well as to up- and down-regulate their frequency94,95.

Despite its potential advantages over transcranial electric stimulation, sensory entrainment has primarily been used to modulate performance in visual83,86,89,94,96 and auditory97,98 perception tasks, attention99–101, as well as language102,103 and music104–106 processing. However, its potential to modulate some other higher cognitive functions such as working memory is still poorly understood (but see ref. 81). Unlike other non-invasive stimulation techniques that typically produce localized cortical effects directly over identified cognitive targets, sensory entrainment responses originate and have been primarily measured over sensory cortices107–109. While there is substantial evidence that sensory stimulation in visual, auditory, or cross-modal modalities can induce phase-resetting with functional implications for higher cognitive functions (see refs. 90,110 for a review), the mechanisms by which these effects propagate from sensory cortices to other cortical regions remain poorly understood. Additionally, the potential confounding effects on multiple cognitive systems are still not well explored. Furthermore, sensory stimulation may also act as background noise and could influence task performance by modulating participants’ level of arousal111, with potential positive or negative effects depending on the task type and participants’ traits112–116. Therefore, while sensory entrainment holds promise for expanding research and practical applications in working memory, there is currently insufficient evidence to determine whether it could provide an alternative to electrical stimulation.

In this study, we report the results of six behavioral experiments (combined N = 209) measuring visual working memory capacity under visual and acoustic sensory entrainment at 4 Hz and 7 Hz frequencies. Based on the oscillatory multiplexing buffer model28 and previous tACS studies39,59, the initial hypothesis was that 4 Hz sensory stimulation would downregulate the endogenous theta frequency, thereby increasing memory capacity. Conversely, 7 Hz sensory stimulation would upregulate the endogenous theta frequency and reduce vWM capacity (see Fig. 1c).

Methods

Task

We employed a whole-display change-detection task, adhering to the parameters set in original task14. In this task, participants were required to memorize an array of briefly presented visual stimuli, specifically colorful squares, for subsequent comparison with a probe array. Each trial began with a 0.5-s display of a fixation cross, immediately followed by a 0.1-second presentation of the memory array (referred to as the “target”). A blank screen, serving as a “delay,” followed the memory array for 0.9 s. Subsequently, a test array, labeled as the “probe,” appeared on the screen and remained visible for 2.5 s or until a response was made (Fig. 1b). The trials were separated by a 1-s inter-trial interval. The memory and test arrays comprised colored squares (0.65° × 0.65°) displayed within an imaginary rectangle measuring 9.7° × 7.3°, centered on the computer monitor. The squares’ positions were randomized within the imaginary display, maintaining a minimal center-to-center distance of 2°. Each stimulus in the memory array was assigned a unique color from a predefined set of colors. The size of the color set was from seven to twelve colors, depending on the experiment (Table 1). In half of the trials, the test array mirrored the memory array, except that one square was assigned a different unique color. In the remaining trials, memory and test arrays were identical. Participants had to respond by pressing designated keys (F/J) on the keyboard to indicate whether a change occurred or not. The assignment of response keys was counterbalanced across participants. The task was implemented in Matlab (version R2018a), utilizing the Psychophysics Toolbox extensions117. General procedure

Each of the six experiments employed a within-subject design with sensory entrainment condition at two theta frequencies, 4 Hz and 7 Hz, alongside a baseline condition. One stimulation condition lasted throughout the entire task block, and the order in which the conditions appeared was randomized for each participant. Within each block, trials with different memory loads, as well as change and no-change trials, were shuffled. All experiments began with a training session consisting of three blocks, each containing six trials. Specific variations in the modality of sensory entrainment and other design elements were introduced based on the particular hypothesis being tested in each experiment, and they are outlined in the methods of the respective experiments.

For each experiment, we recruited independent samples of participants. In on-site experiments, we used the participant database at the Center for Brain and Cognition (CBC), Pompeu Fabra University (UPF), remunerating participants at a rate of 10 EUR per hour. For online experiments, participants were recruited using the prolific.co data collection platform and were compensated at a rate of 5 GBP per hour.

All participants signed informed consent before the experiment, and the study proposal and protocol were approved by the Institutional Committee for Ethical Review of Projects (CIREP) ethics board at UPF.

Visual working memory capacity index K

The principal dependent variable in all experiments was the memory capacity index K118. This index is widely employed to assess working memory capacity in change-detection tasks (e.g., 2,59,119). Data preprocessing was performed with customized MATLAB scripts (Version R2018a and R2020a, The MathWorks, Inc., MA, USA), while statistical analysis was performed in R120 (v 4.0.2., © Free Software Foundation, Inc) and RStudio (Version 2022.07.2, © 2009-2022 RStudio, PBC).

Statistical analysis

In the meta-analysis, we employed a fixed-effect model with inverse variance weighting to calculate a pooled effect from individual experiments. Statistical calculations were performed using customized scripts in R, and forest plots were created using the meta package121 in R (v 7.0.0).

For each statistical test, the normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. For ANOVAs, the sphericity assumption was tested with Mauchly’s test, and if it was violated, we used the Greenhouse-Geisser adjustment for p-values. For tests involving multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction to adjust the resulting p-values.

Methods of individual experiments

The main differences between the individual experiments are summarized in Table 1.

Experiment 1: Visual

Rationale and hypothesis

In this experiment, our aim was to establish the frequency-specific effect of visual stimulation at theta frequency on visual working memory capacity. Specifically, we expected that 4 Hz visual flicker would downregulate the frequency of endogenous theta activity, consequently increasing vWM capacity, while 7 Hz flicker would upregulate endogenous theta frequency, reducing vWM capacity. The rationale, hypothesis, estimated sample size, methods, and planned statistical analysis for this experiment were pre-registered on the Open Science Foundation before data collection (pre-registered on May 17, 2019, at https://osf.io/j9vmh).

Participants

We recruited 41 participants (25 women, 16 men, 39 right-handed, on average 25.15 (SD = 3.17) years old). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and self-reported normal color vision. The sample size was calculated a priori based on the effect sizes reported by Wolinski et al. 59, using G*Power v. 3.1.9.2122. One participant was excluded due to highly variable normalized K values (>3 SD), and another one due to the hit rate and false alarm rate being equal at baseline under load 4, making it impossible to normalize other K values under the same load. The remaining 39 participants were included in the statistical analysis.

Task and procedure

In this experiment, the task load was four, five, or six squares, drawn from a set of seven colors (red, blue, green, yellow, purple, white, and black). We used a 2 × 2 + 1 design with five conditions: two stimulation frequencies (4 Hz and 7 Hz) by two rhythmicity modes (rhythmic and arrhythmic), alongside no flicker baseline condition. Each condition was repeated three times, resulting in 15 blocks. The order of blocks was shuffled for each participant. Each block consisted of 42 trials, 630 in total. The total duration of the experiment was approximately 1 h.

Sensory stimulation

Visual stimulation was achieved by altering the contrast of the outer frame of the display. The outer frame measured 4° on the horizontal axis (i.e., left and right sides of the display), and 2.3° on the vertical axis (i.e., upper and lower sides), constituting 10% of the display dimensions in the corresponding axes. The flicker was created by a 50% contrast change in the frame for a duration of 1 frame (10 ms), with the change occurring at specific intervals determined by the frequency of stimulation. For the 4 Hz rhythmic condition, the visual flash (i.e., change in frame’s contrast) occurred every 250 ms, and for the 7 Hz rhythmic condition, it occurred every 140 ms. In arrhythmic conditions, the flash occurred at time intervals sampled from exponential random distributions with means corresponding to the inter-stimuli interval at 4 Hz and 7 Hz. During the baseline condition, the outer frame of the display stayed at 50% contrast throughout the entire task block.

Experiment 2: Binaural (i)

Rationale and hypothesis

In our initial experiment involving visual flicker, the observed results contradicted our expectation, as 7 Hz entrainment significantly outperformed 4 Hz, and there was no significant evidence of a rhythmicity effect. We interpreted these findings to suggest that the visual nature of the stimulation might have interacted and interfered with the visual nature of the task, possibly affecting attentional and arousal systems. Consequently, we chose to uphold our original hypothesis but opted for sensory stimulation of a different modality, specifically, acoustic stimulation of more subtle nature, such as binaural beats. This experiment was not pre-registered before data collection.

Participants

Forty-eight participants (23 women, 25 men, 45 right-handed, with an average age of 24.19 years, SD = 4.07) were recruited for this experiment. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, self-reported normal color vision, and normal hearing. The sample size was calculated using the same method as in the first experiment (Visual). Five participants were excluded due to near-ceiling behavioral performance, defined a priori as accuracy exceeding 95%. Additionally, two participants were excluded because their accuracy did not exhibit the expected inverse relationship with the task’s difficulty (i.e., accuracy did not decrease with increasing load). The remaining 41 participants were included in the statistical analysis.

Task and procedure

The task in current experiment was the same as in Experiment 1 (Visual), but with several modifications in the procedure. This experiment consisted of two rhythmic entrainment conditions (4 Hz and 7 Hz binaural beats) and a baseline condition (pure tone). Each condition block was repeated twice, resulting in a total of 6 blocks, the order of which was shuffled for each participant. Each block was initiated with 3 min of passive listening to the binaural beats (or pure tone in the baseline condition) while looking at the fixation cross. This was followed by a visual alert cue (a change in the size and color of the fixation cross), signaling the imminent start of the task. The task block then comprised 72 trials, during which participants continued to listen to the same acoustic stimuli without interruption. After completing the task block, the acoustic stimuli stopped, and participants listened to pink noise for 1 min while passively looking at the fixation cross to mitigate any potential carry-over effects between blocks. The total duration of the experiment was ~1 h and 15 min.

Sensory stimulation

In this experiment, we used binaural beats at 4 Hz and 7 Hz as auditory stimuli to entrain endogenous theta activity. The binaural beats were created using 220 Hz carrier frequency with 4 Hz and 7 Hz interaural frequency difference. For instance, a 4 Hz binaural beat was created by playing a sinusoidal waveform at 220 Hz in the left ear of the participant while at the same time playing a sinusoidal waveform of 224 Hz in the right ear. The baseline condition consisted of a continuous pure tone sinewave at 220 Hz played simultaneously in the left and right ears. All stimuli had added 10% pink noise to make them more pleasant for the participants and were created at the sampling rate of 44.1 kHz. We used Audacity ® (Version 2.3.3) to generate all acoustic stimuli.

Experiment 3: Monaural (i)

Rationale and hypothesis

In our second experiment, Binaural (i), 4 Hz acoustic stimulation improved vWM capacity more than 7 Hz acoustic stimulation, in line with our original hypothesis. This finding, however, contrasted with the results from Experiment 1, which employed visual stimulation. We considered that the discrepancy might have arisen from differences in entrainment modality (visual vs. acoustic) or the shape of stimuli. Specifically, in Experiment 1, the visual flash was a sharp-edged pulse, while in Experiment 2, the acoustic beat had a smooth sinusoidal shape. To further investigate, we conducted an additional experiment introducing two different stimulus shapes, sinusoidal and pulsed, within the acoustic modality. As binaural beats can only take a sinusoidal shape, in this experiment we used monaural sounds for greater flexibility in delivering sensory stimulation. The rationale, hypothesis, estimated sample size, methods, and planned statistical analysis of this experiment were pre-registered on the Open Science Foundation before the data collection (pre-registered on November 8, 2019, at https://osf.io/9yjat).

Participants

We recruited 22 participants (13 women, 9 men, 20 right-handed, on average 22.55 (SD = 4.43) years old). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, self-reported normal color vision, and normal hearing. One participant was excluded due to low accuracy (<65%), and another was excluded because their accuracy did not scale with the task’s difficulty. The final sample for statistical analysis consisted of 20 participants. This sample size represented only half of the initially planned recruitment, determined using the same method as in Experiments 1 and 2. Originally, we intended to collect behavioral data from the first half of the sample and, if the preliminary outcome confirmed the results of Experiment 2, proceed by adding participants with EEG recordings for the second half. However, as the preliminary results did not follow our predictions, we halted data collection at half of the intended sample size to revise our hypothesis.

Task and procedure

The task in this experiment was the same as in Experiments 1 and 2 with two modifications. Firstly, we increased the memory load by one, resulting in 5, 6, and 7 squares. Secondly, we expanded the pool of stimulus colors from 7 to 12, incorporating five additional colors (cyan, teal, olive, violet, and pink). These modifications aimed to enhance task difficulty compared to Experiment 2, as we observed many participants performing with very high accuracy, suggesting that the task might have been too easy.

Similar to Experiment 1, we employed a 2 × 2 + 1 design with five conditions: two stimulation frequencies (4 Hz and 7 Hz) paired with two stimuli shapes (pulsed and sinusoidal), alongside the pure tone baseline condition. Contrary to Experiment 2, there was no acoustic stimulation at the start of the block prior to the task. Each condition was repeated three times, resulting in 15 blocks, whose order was shuffled for each participant. Each block comprised 42 trials, totaling 630, and the experiment lasted ~1 h.

Sensory stimulation

Sinusoidal entrainment stimuli were generated by multiplying a 220 Hz sinusoidal carrier waveform with 4 Hz and 7 Hz sine waves, all sampled at 44.1 kHz. Pulsed stimuli were created by multiplying a segment of the carrier frequency (220 Hz), corresponding to the duration of one cycle of the entrainment frequency, by a Gaussian envelope with a width of 0.01. The resulting pulse was replicated and concatenated into 4 Hz and 7 Hz sequences. Finally, 10% of pink noise was added to both the sinusoidal and pulsed stimuli. These stimuli were generated using Matlab (version R2018a), and the pink noise was created using Audacity ® (Version 2.3.3.) and then added to the Matlab. The baseline condition consisted of a pure tone at 220 Hz with added pink noise, same as in Experiment 2.

Experiment 4: Binaural (ii)

Rationale and hypothesis

In our previous Experiment 3, Monaural (i), we observed the same tendency of 7 Hz outperforming 4 Hz stimulation, as in Experiment 1, and there was no evidence for a difference between the two stimulus shapes (i.e., sinusoidal and pulsed). Therefore, we speculated that the different results of Experiment 2, Binaural (i), could have been caused by either the different nature of the acoustic stimulation (i.e., the subtle binaural beats) or by differences in the procedure. Therefore, we decided to conduct an experiment which had the same procedure as Experiment 1 and 3, but used sensory stimulation with binaural beats as in Experiment 2. This experiment was not pre-registered before data collection.

Participants

We recruited 21 participants (13 women, 8 men, 19 right-handed, on average 23.76 (SD = 3.42) years old) for this experiment. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, self-reported normal color vision, and hearing. One participant was excluded because of a priori set criteria for too high accuracy (>90%), and two more participants were excluded because their accuracy did not scale with the task’s difficulty. The remaining 18 participants were included in the analysis.

We did not perform sample size calculation for this experiment, but based on the results from Experiments 1 to 3, we expected that 20 participant sample should be sufficient to show any clear tendency in the results.

Task and procedure

In this experiment, we utilized a task with memory load of 5, 6, and 7 squares, selected from a pool of 8 distinct colors (red, green, blue, yellow, white, black, purple, and cyan).

We included two rhythmic entrainment conditions (4 Hz and 7 Hz binaural beats) and a baseline condition (pure tone), all presented in a blocked fashion. Each condition was repeated three times, resulting in 9 blocks, the order of which was shuffled for each participant. There were 42 trials in each block, amounting to 378 trials in total. The total duration of the experiment was ~45 min.

Sensory stimulation

The 4 Hz and 7 Hz binaural beats and the pure tone baseline condition were the same as in Experiment 2, Binaural (i).

Experiment 5: Binaural (iii)

Rationale and hypothesis

In our previous experiment, Binaural (ii), once again we observed a tendency of 7 Hz stimulation to outperform the 4 Hz stimulation, same as in Experiments 1 and 3. We speculated that the discrepant results in Experiment 2 from the other experiments so far could have been caused either by differences in procedure (e.g., initial exposure period to binaural beats), or by statistical chance. Therefore, we decided to conduct an additional experiment replicating the same procedure as in Experiment 2. Successful reproduction of the results of Experiment 2 would indicate that binaural beats need a prolonged exposure to produce the entrainment effects; alternatively, a failed replication would support the possibility that the results of Experiment 2 were likely due to statistical chance. This experiment was not pre-registered before data collection.

Participants

Twenty-three participants (17 women, 6 men, 22 right-handed, on average 22.30 (SD = 4.72) years old) were recruited for this experiment. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, self-reported normal color vision, and normal hearing. One participant was excluded due to a priori set accuracy criterion being too low (<65%), and two more participants were excluded because their accuracy did not scale with task’s difficulty. The remaining 20 participants were included in the analysis. Similar to Experiment 4 (Binaural (ii)), we did not perform a sample size calculation; however, based on data from Experiments 1 to 4, we expected that a sample size of around 20 participants would be sufficient for detecting any trends in the results.

Task and procedure

In this experiment, we used the change detection task with memory load of 5, 6, and 7 squares, drawn from eight unique colors (same as in Experiment 4). The protocol was the same as in Experiment 2.

Sensory stimulation

The 4 Hz and 7 Hz binaural beats and pure tone baseline conditions were the same as in previous two experiments with binaural beats, Binaural (i) and Binaural (ii).

Experiment 6: Monaural (ii)

Rationale and hypothesis

In four out of five preceding experiments, we observed a tendency of 7 Hz stimulation to outperform 4 Hz stimulation. In addition, the last experiment with binaural beats, Binaural (iii) casted doubt on the discrepant results that were obtained in Experiment 2. Therefore, we decided to run an additional online experiment with a large and more heterogeneous sample of participants, to confirm the observed effect. In this experiment, we revised our hypothesis expecting 7 Hz sensory stimulation to outperform 4 Hz sensory stimulation, attributing the effect to the modulation of participants’ arousal. The rationale, hypothesis, estimated sample size, methods, and planned statistical analysis of this experiment were pre-registered on the Open Science Foundation before the data collection (pre-registered on October 13, 2020, at https://osf.io/k8bt2).

Participants

Using the prolific.co data collection platform, we recruited 99 participants (32 women, 67 men, on average 23.89 (SD = 4.49) years old). All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, normal color vision, normal hearing, and proficiency in English (ensuring comprehension of instructions). Following pre-established exclusion criteria, eight participants were excluded due to either too low or too high-performance accuracy (<60% or >90%), and additional nine participants were excluded because their accuracy did not scale with the task’s difficulty. In addition to the predefined exclusion criteria, we excluded three more participants due to highly variable normalized K values (>2 SD), as well as eight more participants who obtained negative K values because their false alarm rate exceeded the hit rate. The exclusion of all participants was performed during the preprocessing stage before data analysis. The remaining 71 participants were included in the final dataset.

The sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power v. 3.1.9.7122, and was based on a one-tail paired-sample t-test with 80% power, a 5% α level, and an effect size of 0.3 (Cohen’s d). The effect size was chosen based on the results of our Experiment 1, which had the largest sample size (N = 39), and, consequently, a more stable effect size estimate (Cohen’s d = −0.27), among all experiments showing an advantage of the 7 Hz entrainment condition over 4 Hz (i.e., Experiments 1 and 3–5).

Task and procedure

In this experiment, the change detection task had a memory load of 5, 6, and 7 squares, drawn from a set of eight unique colors (same as in Experiments 4 and 5). The online version of the experiment was implemented using PsychoPy v. 2020.1.3123 and hosted on Pavlovia.org. The code of the experiment is available at https://gitlab.pavlovia.org/IndreP/chd_v61.

We employed two experimental conditions (4 Hz and 7 Hz monaural beats) and one baseline condition (pure tone), presented in a blocked fashion. Each condition was repeated three times, resulting in nine blocks, the order of which was shuffled randomly for each participant. There were 30 trials in each block, 270 trials in total. The decision to reduce the number of trials in this experiment was motivated by the recommendation to keep the experiment duration as short as possible in online settings124. The number of trials was determined through Monte Carlo simulations using data from our previous experiments with acoustic stimuli to find the minimal number of trials that ensured stable estimates of vWM capacity index K.

The overall flow of the online experiment was as follows: once participants accepted the experiment on the Prolific.co platform, they were redirected to a Google Forms document containing informed consent and a short demographic questionnaire. After submitting these documents, they were prompted with a link to Pavlovia.org, where the actual experiment was hosted. Participants first underwent visual and sound calibration. Visual calibration implied asking participants to adjust the size of the rectangle on the screen to the size of a standard credit card (by placing it on top of the drawn rectangle). This step allowed us to scale the task elements to the individual screen resolution of each participant. Afterward, participants were asked to adjust their sound to a comfortable level while listening to a 3 Hz beat, and this adjustment was used to scale the auditory stimuli. Following the calibration, participants were given written instructions and proceeded to perform three training blocks while listening to the 3 Hz acoustic stimulation. Both training and experimental blocks were followed by an attention check task that started immediately after each block. The task consisted of a variable number of beeps (from 0 to 5), and participants were asked to report the number of beeps. This task served to verify that participants were paying attention and that their sound was functioning correctly. Finally, after completing the task, participants were automatically redirected to the Prolific.co platform for payment. The entire experiment took ~25 min.

Sensory stimulation

The 3 Hz, 4 Hz, and 7 Hz beat stimuli were generated by combining 220 Hz sinusoidal waves with 223, 224, and 227 Hz sinusoidal waves, respectively. The pure tone baseline condition was a 220 Hz sinusoidal wave. Furthermore, all four acoustic stimuli had 10% of pink noise added. For attention checks, we utilized individual beeps available at default settings in Audacity. All auditory stimuli were created using Audacity® v. 2.3.3.

Control experiment 1: Sensory entrainment of attentional networks

Rationale and hypothesis

In our preceding behavioral experiments on vWM capacity modulation, we observed that 7 Hz stimulation tended to outperform 4 Hz stimulation. We speculated that the effect could be driven by the potential of supra-threshold sensory stimuli to increase arousal in the participants, making them more focused on the task. To investigate the effects of sensory stimulation on arousal, we decided to use the Attention Network Test (ANT), which allows the isolation of three attentional networks: arousal, orienting, and executive. We hypothesized that sensory stimulation at 7 Hz would increase overall arousal in the participants more than 4 Hz stimulation, leading to a frequency-specific difference in alerting scores between these two conditions. The rationale, hypothesis, estimated sample size, methods, and planned statistical analysis of this experiment were pre-registered on the Open Science Foundation before data collection (pre-registered on September 1, 2021, at https://osf.io/rwhvq).

Participants

We recruited 116 participants using prolific.co data collection platform. Following a priori set exclusion criteria, 11 participants were excluded for failing attention checks, one participant was excluded because their average accuracy was below 85%. In addition, four more participants were excluded due to technical issues (the experiment crashed before uploading the results). The data from the remaining sample of 100 participants (50 women, 50 men, 91 right-handed, on average 24.61 (SD = 4.59) years old) was included in the final analysis.

We did not perform sample size calculations before the experiment, as we did not find reliable effect sizes to base our calculations on. However, after considering our available resources and modeling possible power outcomes with different sample sizes, we decided to aim at collecting a sample of around 100 participants. This would allow us to obtain 80% power for average effect size (d = 0.3).

Task and procedure

We employed the short version of the ANT task125. The task comprised three cue conditions (no-cue, central-cue, spatial-cue) and two target conditions (congruent and incongruent), resulting in six different conditions. The target stimuli consisted of a horizontally aligned set of five black arrows presented above or below the fixation cross (0.67°) against a gray background. Each arrow measured 0.48° in length, separated by a 0.14° gap; consequently, the entire array occupied 2.95° in total. The target was the central arrow of the five arrow array, pointing either to the left or right, while the remaining four arrows functioned as congruent (pointing in the same direction) or incongruent (pointing in the opposite direction) flanker stimuli. The target was displayed for 1700 ms or until the response, whichever event occurred first. Participants were asked to indicate the direction of the central arrow as fast as possible by pressing a corresponding arrow key on the keyboard. Furthermore, the target was preceded by a no-cue, central-cue, or spatial-cue, each presented for 50 ms. The spatial-cue appeared above or below the fixation cross and was always valid. The time interval between the cue and the target was 450 ms. The intertrial period was random between 400 and 600 ms (Fig. 2a). Control experiment 2: Sensory entrainment effects on tonic arousal

Rationale and hypothesis

In our first control experiment, we observed no significant evidence of a frequency-specific effect of sensory stimulation at 4 Hz and 7 Hz on the arousal network, as estimated with the Attention Network Test. We speculated that perhaps we were probing the wrong type of arousal. Indeed, the alerting score in the ANT task is most indicative of phasic arousal, or transient increases in arousal due to the cue effect. However, our sustained sensory stimulation throughout the change detection task might have modulated tonic arousal, or the general level of activation of the participants. One way to measure tonic arousal would be via physiological markers of arousal, such as changes in pupil diameter. Therefore, we decided to conduct an additional control experiment with a modified change detection task and concurrent recording of pupillometry while the participants were exposed to auditory stimulation at 4 Hz and 7 Hz monaural beats. The rationale, hypothesis, sample size estimation, methods, and planned statistical analysis for this experiment were pre-registered on the Open Science Foundation before data collection (pre-registered on February 12, 2021, at https://osf.io/dtkef).

Participants

We recruited 61 participants (45 women, 16 men, 7 left-handed, with an average age of 24.08 (SD = 3.93)) for this experiment. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, self-reported good color vision, and normal hearing. According to the a priori rejection criteria, six participants were excluded due to accuracy either being too low (<65%) or too high (>90%). An additional five participants were excluded because their accuracy did not scale with the task’s difficulty. Two more participants were excluded based on criteria not determined a priori but identified during data preprocessing: one for excessively variable memory capacity estimates (>2 SD) and one for excessive blinking. The remaining 48 participants were included in further analysis.

Pupil size recording

We recorded participants’ pupil size using Tobii T120 eye-tracker with a 120 Hz sampling rate. Before the experimental session, we conducted a 5-point calibration with the participant seated 65 cm away from the tracker. We checked and readjusted the participant’s position relative to the eye-tracker before every task block. The luminance of the room was set to 4.3 EV (equivalent to around 50 Lux) at the participant’s eye level.

Pupil data preprocessing

To preprocess participants’ pupil data, we initially used linear regression to fill in missing values when data points were available for one eye but missing for the other. Subsequently, we applied a low-pass filter at 5 Hz to eliminate high-frequency noise. Linear interpolation was employed to estimate missing signal chunks caused by blinks. The data was then segmented around the onset of the target array (−2000 ms to 2300 ms) and visually inspected for artifacts. Finally, we normalized the pupil size time-series within each participant by subtracting the mean pupil value from each data point and dividing it by the standard deviation, expressing it in z-scores. This normalization facilitated the comparison of changes in pupil sizes during the fixation (baseline) period. Results

In the six reported experiments, participants (combined N = 209) performed a whole-display change detection task14 (see Fig. 1b). In each trial, a memory array consisting of 4 to 7 colored squares briefly appeared on the screen for 100 ms, followed by a delay period of 900 ms. After the delay, participants were presented with a probe array that featured the same number of the squares in identical locations and, in half of the trials, in the same original color. On the other half of the trials, one square differed in color from the memory array (designated as a “change” trial). Participants were asked to indicate, via a key-press, whether any color had changed or not. While performing the task, participants were exposed to continuous visual stimulation (Experiment 1) or acoustic stimulation (Experiments 2–6) at 4 Hz or 7 Hz frequencies, with one type of stimulation persisting throughout the entire duration of a block. Additionally, all experiments included a baseline condition with static visual (Experiment 1) or pure tone (Experiments 2–6) stimulation. The order in which sensory stimulation (or baseline) blocks were presented was randomized for each participant. In all experiments, memory capacity was estimated using a behavioral index K118, which was normalized for the 4 Hz and 7 Hz conditions against the baseline condition. All experiments employed a within-subject design with independent samples of participants.

Frequency-specific stimulation effect

The main goal of this study was to establish a frequency-specific modulatory effect of sensory stimulation at low (4 Hz) and high (7 Hz) theta frequencies on visual working memory capacity. In our first experiment, Visual, we utilized visual flicker for sensory entrainment. In addition to the frequency effect (4 Hz vs 7 Hz), we also examined the rhythmicity effect; therefore, the 4 Hz and 7 Hz sensory stimuli were presented either in rhythmic or arrhythmic fashion (see Methods). Our principle variable was memory capacity index K variation from the baseline condition. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a main effect of stimulation frequency, F(1, 38) = 4.60, p = 0.039, ηp2 = 0.108, CI = [0; 0.301], with 7 Hz stimulation (M = 8.41%, SD = 26.3) outperforming the 4 Hz stimulation (M = 4.67%, SD = 23.64), in terms of increasing vWM capacity from the baseline. However, there was no significant evidence of an effect of stimulus rhythmicity, F(1, 38) < 0.01, p = 0.988, ηp2 < 0.001, CI = [0; 1], or interaction between the frequency and rhythmicity, F(1, 38) = 2.01, p = 0.164, ηp2 = 0.050, CI = [0; 0.224]. This outcome did not support the initial hypothesis. In our second experiment, Binaural (i), we aimed to reduce possible interference of visual flicker on the visual memory task by opting for auditory entrainment of a subtler nature, namely, binaural beats. As binaural beats can only be sinusoidal and do not allow an arrhythmic control condition, we only investigated the effect of stimulation frequency on vWM capacity. A two-tailed paired-sample t-test on the normalized capacity index K revealed no evidence for a difference between the 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation, t(40), p = 0.094, d = 0.24, CI = [−0.20; 0.68]; however, descriptively, 4 Hz stimulation outperformed 7 Hz stimulation in terms of increasing vWM capacity from the baseline (M = 6.07%, SD = 20.57; M = 3.28%, SD = 20.50, respectively), thus in line with the initial hypothesis. We attempted to address the seemingly discrepant results between the first two experiments in our third experiment, Monaural (i), which employed sinusoidal and pulsed rhythmic stimuli at 4 Hz and 7 Hz frequencies. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed no evidence for a significant difference between the two stimulation frequencies, F(1, 19) = 3.89, p = 0.063, ηp2 = 0.170, CI = [0; 0.433], stimulus shape, F(1, 19) = 0.41, p = 0.530, ηp2 = 0.021, CI = [0; 0.242], or their interaction, F(1, 19) = 0.16, p = 0.692, ηp2 = 0.008, CI = [0; 0.202]. However, descriptively, 7 Hz stimulation showed a tendency towards outperforming the 4 Hz stimulation in terms of increasing vWM capacity from the baseline (M = 16.55%, SD = 69.28; M = 10.40%, SD = 63.65, respectively). So far, binaural and monaural beats have produced contrasting results. Therefore, in our following experiment, Binaural (ii), we once again used binaural beats with some changes in protocol (see Methods), aiming to better understand the effects of this type of auditory stimuli. A two-tailed paired-sample t-test showed no evidence for a difference between the two stimulation frequencies, t(17) = −1.25, p = 0.227, d = −0.32, CI = [−1.00; 0.36], but once again, 7 Hz stimulation (M = 7.30%, SD = 45.09) descriptively outperformed 4 Hz stimulation (M = −0.16%, SD = 36.81). In our next experiment, Binaural (iii), we aimed to replicate the results of the original binaural beats experiment (i.e., Binaural (i)), to establish whether seemingly discrepant results were caused by statistical chance. A two-tailed paired-sample t-test revealed no evidence for a difference between the two frequencies, t(19) = −1.77, p = 0.094, d = -0.26, CI = [−0.90; 0.38], but again, 7 Hz stimulation descriptively outperformed 4 Hz stimulation (M = 8.19%, SD = 45.95; M = 1.06%, SD = 36.16, respectively). Finally, in our last experiment, Monaural (ii), we decided to confirm the observed tendency of stimulation at 7 Hz to outperform the stimulation at 4 Hz in a larger sample online experiment. The one-tailed paired-sample t-test revealed that 7 Hz stimulation (M = 14.13%, SD = 68.70) indeed significantly outperformed 4 Hz stimulation (M = 6.83%, SD = 48.84), t(70) = −1.90, p = 0.031, d = −0.21, CI = [−0.54; 0.12]. The evidence thus far does not support the initial hypothesis; instead, it appears to overall indicate an advantage for faster entrainment frequencies. Next, we used inverse variance weighting and a common effect model to combine the effects of the difference between the two stimulation frequencies obtained from the individual experiments, creating a single frequency-specific effect of sensory stimulation. This allowed us to confirm that the combined effect from the six experiments demonstrated that 7 Hz stimulation significantly outperformed 4 Hz stimulation, on average by 2% (CI = [−4.00; −0.01], p = 0.048). Next, we investigated whether the observed significant effect between the 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation was related to the rhythmic component of our stimuli or was purely sensory in nature. To disentangle these two potential effects, we subtracted the arrhythmic condition from the rhythmic one in our first experiment, Visual, and compared the resulting two frequency conditions. This indicates that the difference between the two conditions, as well as their difference from the baseline, was likely driven by the sensory component of the stimuli, but not its rhythmic component.

Overall effect of sensory stimulation

While we were unable to establish the hypothesized advantage of 4 Hz stimulation over the 7 Hz stimulation frequency, nor even a frequency-specific difference at all in most of our experiments, we observed an overall reliable increase in vWM capacity with both 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation, compared to the baseline. To further explore this sensory effect of stimulation, for each experiment we combined the 4 Hz and 7 Hz conditions into a single stimulation condition and compared its effect on vWM capacity against the baseline condition. A two-tailed one-sample t-test on the combined normalized vWM capacity index K values revealed a significant increase in vWM capacity with visual stimuli (Visual), t(38) = 3.33, p = 0.002, d = 0.53, CI = [−0.13; 1.19], as well as with monaural beats (Monaural (i) ans Monaural (ii)), t(19) = 2.11, p = 0.048, d = 0.47, CI = [−0.48; 1.42]; t(70) = 3.02, p = 0.003, d = 0.36, CI = [−0.12; 0.84], respectively. Additionally, there was a significant increase in vWM capacity in the first experiment with binaural beats (Binaural (i)), t(40) = 2.93, p = 0.006, d = 0.46, CI = [−0.18; 1.10]. However, the other two experiments with binaural beats, Binaural (ii) and Binaural (iii), did not show significant evidence of stimulation effect, t(17) = 0.82, p = 0.426, d = 0.19, CI = [−0.80; 1.19]; t(19) = 0.83, p = 0.414, d = 0.19, CI = [−0.75; 1.12], respectively. Importantly, these two experiments not only utilized milder sensory stimuli, namely binaural beats, but also had the smallest sample sizes.

To estimate the joint effect of sensory stimulation on vWM capacity from the six experiments, we applied a common effect model with inverse variance weighting. The analysis revealed a significant overall increase in vWM capacity from the baseline by 6.12% (CI = [3.94; 8.30], p < 0.001), when the 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation conditions were combined. Moreover, the experiments showed overall substantial homogeneity in their effects, I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, Q(5) = 4.37, p = 0.497, resulting in a robust prediction interval (see Fig. 4b).

Low- and high-capacity participants

So far, we have observed a strong facilitating effect of sensory stimulation on vWM capacity, evident for both visual and acoustic stimuli, independent of rhythm and rhythmicity. We hypothesized that the continuous stimulation protocol used here could have served as facilitating background noise, aiding participants in sustaining their attention and focus on the task. While background noise overall is considered to affect cognitive performance negatively126–130, in moderate amounts it has been shown to have a beneficial effect under certain circumstances on some tasks requiring vigilance112, creativity131, episodic memory132 and critically, also working memory113,114,133. Additionally, background noise has been shown to specifically aid populations with lower attentional capacities, such as inattentive children111,134 or children133,135–137 and young adults137 with ADHD. According to the moderate brain arousal model138, these populations require more noise for optimal cognitive performance due to lower dopamine levels. Therefore, if our sensory stimulation indeed functioned similarly, as background noise modulating participants’ arousal levels, we would expect the effect to be more pronounced in participants with lower vWM capacity, which has often been linked to suboptimal attentional mechanisms, such as filtering efficiency139–141 and attentional control142–144. To explore this possibility, we divided our participants into low- and high-capacity sub-groups based on their median vWM capacity index K in the baseline condition (see Fig. 5). We observed a significant group effect (i.e., low- versus high-capacity) or a significant interaction between the group and frequency in all experiments except for Binaural (iii), where neither group nor frequency effect reached significant levels (refer to the Table 2 for precise statistical values). That is, in low-capacity participants, stimulation produced an improvement in vWM capacity for at least one of the stimulation frequencies, if not both. Sensory stimulation effect on phasic and tonic arousal

We have observed a modality-independent facilitating effect of sensory stimulation on vWM capacity, with a slightly stronger effect observed at a higher stimulus rate (i.e., at 7 Hz compared to 4 Hz). We found no significant evidence that this facilitatory effect depends on stimulus rhythmicity, and it was mostly driven by low-capacity participants. It has been recently suggested, that background white noise and background speech can improve visual working memory performance by increasing participants’ arousal113. These findings align with the moderate brain arousal model138, which would predict that background noise will have a beneficial effect on low-capacity participants due to their impaired dopamine signaling145,146. Guided by these findings, we formulated the hypothesis that sensory stimulation increased participants’ arousal, with faster stimulation (7 Hz) leading to a greater increase compared to the slower one (4 Hz), due to higher stimulus rate. This heightened arousal was postulated to be the driving factor behind the improvement in vWM capacity. To test this hypothesis, we conducted two control experiments.

The first control experiment employed the Attention Network Test125,147 (Fig. 2a) to investigate the impact of 4 Hz and 7 Hz acoustic stimulation on participants’ average reaction time and alerting score, which is a proxy for arousal. A repeated measures ANOVA with a within-subject factor of stimulation frequency (three levels: 4 Hz, 7 Hz, and pure tone baseline) and a within-subject factor of cue type (three levels: no-cue, central cue, and spatial cue) on average participants’ reaction time revealed a significant main effect of stimulation frequency, F(2, 198) = 10.11, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.093, CI = [0.026; 0.170], and, as expected, cue type, F(2, 198) = 2000.2, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.953, CI = [0.941; 0.960], but no significant interaction between the two variables, F(4,396) = 0.33, p = 0.860, ηp2 = 0.003, CI = [0; 0.011]. A pairwise paired-sample t-test analysis with Bonferroni correction further clarified that average reaction time was significantly different for all stimulation frequencies, with the fastest reaction times for 7 Hz stimulation condition (M = 540.29 ms, SD = 60.83), followed by the 4 Hz stimulation condition (M = 543.03 ms, SD = 61.14), and the slowest in pure tone baseline condition (M = 546.19 ms, SD = 61.99). However, the differences in the average reaction times did not transfer to the differences in the arousal levels: a two-tailed paired-sample t-test revealed no evidence for a difference between the 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation on the baseline-normalized average alerting score, t(99) = −0.41, p = 0.683, d = −0.04, CI = [−0.32; 0.23]. Furthermore, as indicated by one-sample t-test, neither the 4 Hz, t(99) = 0.68, p = 0.500, d = 0.07, CI = [−0.33; 0.46], nor the 7 Hz, t(99) = 1.14, p = 0.259, d = 0.11, CI = [−0.28; 0.51], stimulation significantly increased the alerting scores from the baseline (see Fig. 2b). However, it is important to note that alerting score in the Attention Network Test is likely more sensitive to phasic changes in arousal, induced by brief cue stimuli, while our continuous sustained sensory stimulation might have influenced the tonic arousal of the participants. Therefore, we conducted an additional control experiment more suitable for probing changes in tonic arousal.

The second control experiment employed a change detection task (Fig. 3a), similar to the one in the primary six experiments, this time including continuous recording of participants’ pupil sizes as a proxy for the activation of the arousal system148. The primary analysis for tonic arousal focused on the baseline pupil diameter during the fixation period of the task, occurring before the presentation of any task-related stimuli. We anticipated that 4 Hz and 7 Hz acoustic stimulation would differentially impact the baseline pupil diameter in this analysis window, with a larger difference in the low-capacity participant group than the high-capacity group, indicating varying levels of tonic arousal. Importantly, our hypothesis regarding differences in pre-trial pupil diameter was two-tailed, following the arousal dysregulation hypothesis149. Specifically, we predicted that low-capacity participants would exhibit suboptimal arousal levels, potentially being in either a hypo-arousal or hyper-arousal state, with 7 Hz stimulation possibly leading to a more optimal arousal level. Low- and high-capacity groups were defined by splitting all participants into two groups according to the median memory capacity index K in the pure tone baseline condition. First, we checked the effects of sensory stimulation on vWM capacity to confirm that the results are in line with those from the main six behavioral experiments. A mixed-design ANOVA with a within-subject factor of stimulation frequency (two levels: 4 Hz and 7 Hz) and a between-subject factor of participant group (two levels: low- and high-capacity) on baseline normalized memory capacity index K revealed a significant main effect of participant group, F(1, 46) = 11.73, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.203, CI = [0.035; 0.386], but no significant evidence of a main effect of stimulation frequency, F(1, 46) = 1.91, p = 0.174, ηp2 = 0.040, CI = [0; .190], nor an interaction between the group and frequency, F(1, 46) = 0.25, p = 0.621, ηp2 = 0.005, CI = [0; 0.109]. These findings were consistent with the results from the main experiments, indicating that, on average, low-capacity participants (M = 10.95%, SD = 43.59) improved more with sensory stimulation than high-capacity participants (M = −1.80%, SD = 20.72). The behavioral findings were supported by pupillometry results: A mixed-design ANOVA with a within-subject factor of frequency (two levels: 4 Hz and 7 Hz) and a between-subject factor of participant group (two levels: low- and high-capacity) on average pre-trial pupil diameter revealed a significant group effect, F(1, 46) = 4.27, p = 0.045, ηp2 = 0.085, CI = [0; 0.256], but no significant evidence of an effect of stimulation frequency, F(1, 46) = 1.51, p = 0.226, ηp2 = 0.032, CI = [0; 0.175], or an interaction between the two variables, F(1, 46) = 0.97, p = 0.329, ηp2 = 0.021, CI = [0; 0.153] (see, Fig. 3b, c). As an additional control, we also used a linear mixed-effects model to examine the possible carry-over effects of the previous trial on the average baseline pupil diameter of the following trial, while accounting for variability across participants. The model included load (three levels: 5, 6, or 7 squares, with load 5 as the reference category), response (two levels: correct or wrong, with correct as the reference category), and trial type (two levels: change or no-change, with no-change as the reference category) as fixed effects and participant-specific intercepts as random effects. However, there was no significant evidence of the effect of load (β = 0.007, SE = 0.024, p = 0.755; β = 0.013, SE = 0.024, p = 0.591, for loads 6 and 7, respectively), response (β = −0.005, SE = 0.025, p = 0.941), or trial type (β = 0.038, SE = 0.020, p = 0.053) on the average baseline pupil diameter of the consecutive trial. The variance component for the random intercept of participants was 0.025 (SD = 0.158). While these findings support the prediction that low- and high-capacity groups had different arousal levels during the pre-trial period, we could not establish the hypothesized frequency-specific effect of sensory stimulation.

Discussion

In six initial behavioral experiments, we employed visual and auditory sensory entrainment at 4 Hz and 7 Hz frequencies to down- and up-regulate participants’ endogenous theta frequency with the intention to modulate their vWM capacity in a frequency-specific manner. Our hypothesis was grounded on the theta-gamma model for working memory capacity28,70 and findings from prior studies which successfully utilized tACS for the same purpose39,59–61. We predicted that 4 Hz stimulation would increase vWM capacity, while 7 Hz stimulation would decrease it. Contrary to the predictions, when we combined the results from six experiments, 7 Hz stimulation overall outperformed the 4 Hz stimulation, by ~2%. However, due to high inter-study heterogeneity, the frequency-specific effect was not robust. Additionally, sensory stimulation at both 4 Hz and 7 Hz had an overall effect of increasing vWM capacity compared to the no-stimulation baseline. After combining the two stimulation conditions and pooling results from the six experiments, we observed a robust improvement of over 6%, indicating an overall facilitating effect of sensory stimulation on vWM capacity. Importantly, exploratory analysis revealed that this effect was mostly driven by the low-capacity participant group. Our interpretation of this effect was that sensory stimulation increased participants’ arousal levels and, consequently, aided sustained attention, which is crucial for performing a change detection task. However, after conducting two control experiments, we could not find significant evidence of the frequency-specific modulation in either phasic (i.e., indexed by alerting score) or tonic (i.e., indexed by pupil size) arousal as a consequence of sensory stimulation.

Several potential reasons may explain why entrainment with sensory stimulation in our study yielded different outcomes compared to those in studies39,59,61 using non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, such as tACS, to modulate vWM capacity. While tACS can be applied directly above relevant brain structures, resulting in a more local stimulation150,151, sensory entrainment arises in primary sensory cortices107–109 and relies on functional brain pathways to traverse multiple cortical areas with intricate projections to other brain structures110,152. For example, auditory entrainment has been shown to influence structures in the basal ganglia, such as the dorsal striatum and putamen, which are likely involved in encoding predictive aspects of temporal sequences110. Although the mechanisms by which sensory entrainment propagates from sensory cortices to other brain areas are not yet fully understood, it is likely that sensory entrainment exerts a more global effect on the brain compared to tACS, potentially impacting multiple cognitive systems beyond the target one. Of relevance here, sensory stimulation of different modalities has been shown to influence the arousal system through redundant sensory projections onto the mesencephalic reticular formation153,154. To investigate this possible confounding effect of sensory stimulation on arousal, we included two control experiments, one measuring phasic alerting with the ANT task125,147, and the other measuring tonic arousal using pupillometry; however, these experiments did not reveal the predicted frequency-specific modulation of participants’ arousal levels. Despite these experiments being inconclusive as to the impact of entrainment on specific measures of arousal, this does not preclude the possibility that overall changes produced in other brain networks might have interfered or counteracted the potential entrainment of brain networks relevant for vMW.

Another, perhaps related, possibility is that sensory entrainment failed to target the same functionally relevant theta frequency than that targeted by tACS in previous studies. Brain oscillatory activity at theta frequency is not a unitary phenomenon, and it serves at least two broad functional roles155. Frontal-midline theta has been associated with cognitive control156–158 and cognitive effort159–161, and it plays a role in top-down inter-regional control across various cognitive tasks, including working memory63. In contrast, parietal-occipital theta has been linked to processes related to episodic and working memory maintenance and retrieval162–164. Working memory relies on both of these functionalities of theta oscillations, as it combines both control and maintenance components, but these two theta oscillations might have different spatio-temporal profiles165. Previous studies employing tACS have predominantly targeted the parietal-occipital theta, demonstrating frequency-specific modulation of vWM capacity39,59–61. However, other studies have shown a more general effect of stimulation at theta frequency on working memory performance by targeting frontal-midline theta166,167 or fronto-parietal theta-based connectivity168,169. If sensory entrainment had a stronger effect on frontal-midline theta then we would not expect the frequency-specific modulation as postulated by theta-gamma model28, and this could explain the lack of robust differences between 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation frequencies. Moreover, frontal-midline theta has been speculated to have a higher peak frequency170, which could clarify why, overall, 7 Hz stimulation tended to outperform 4 Hz stimulation. However, across the six experiments we did not employ control frequencies outside the theta range, neither obtained concurrent EEG recordings, therefore, further research is necessary to explore the hypothesis that sensory stimulation indeed entrained functionally different theta frequency.

It is important to note that, while we framed our hypothesis based on the theta-gamma model for working memory capacity28, sensory entrainment may also influence other oscillatory brain mechanisms potentially related to vWM performance, such as theta-phase dependent synaptic plasticity171. Indeed, it has been shown that entraining visual and auditory stimuli at phase-matched theta frequency can enhance associative memory between static images78 or movies79 and accompanying sounds. Significant improvements in memory performance have also been achieved through multisensory gamma stimulation in Alzheimer’s disease patients172 by targeting related pathological processes, including amyloid plaque deposition, neurofibrillary tangle formation, and neuronal loss173. However, the effects of unimodal or crossmodal sensory stimulation at gamma frequency in healthy participants are mixed. Some studies have reported improvements in verbal and visuospatial working memory174,175, while others have found no significant effect176,177. Therefore, it is crucial to consider that multiple oscillatory brain mechanisms may underlie working memory, which can be uniquely targeted by sensory entrainment and may interact in complex ways.

If sensory stimulation finally proved to target frontal-midline theta more effectively than parietal-occipital theta, it could also elucidate the observed difference between the low- and high-capacity groups. Previous studies have associated low capacity with attention and control processes of participants, such as a failure to suppress distractors139,141,178. This aligns well with the idea that strengthening frontal-midline theta, and thus overall control over the task, provided more benefit to the low-capacity participants. In line with this, a recent study has shown that 4 Hz and 7 Hz stimulation targeted distinct components of visual working memory, specifically its precision and overall recall rate, affecting low- and high-capacity participants differently61. Therefore, it is plausible that sensory stimulation had a stronger effect on those task components that were more limiting for the low-capacity participants. Importantly, an alternative explanation could be that we were merely increasing arousal in low-capacity participants; however, our control experiments did not reveal significant evidence of a frequency-specific stimulation effect on phasic arousal (see Fig. 2b), nor did they show a frequency or group effect on tonic arousal (see Fig. 3c).

It is worth noting that, in the first experiment (Visual), we did not observe significant evidence of a rhythmicity effect, suggesting that the observed improvement in vWM capacity could have been caused by mechanisms other than entrainment to periodic streams of stimuli. We hypothesize that such mechanism could involve the modulation of temporal expectations rather than periodic entrainment. Indeed, it has been argued that both rhythmic and arrhythmic stimuli can alter participants’ temporal expectations179,180, which, in turn, can align endogenous neural oscillations in a top-down fashion to a more optimal phase for information processing179. It is possible that, in the case of arrhythmic stimulation conditions in the first experiment, participants’ temporal expectations and sequential adjustment of neural phase were based on the passage of time (i.e., hazard rate function)181, with more optimal adjustment occurring at higher average stimulation rate (i.e., 7 Hz). Further research would be necessary to separate the bottom-up effects of periodic neural entrainment from the top-down phase-reset caused by temporal expectations on visual working memory capacity and performance.

Finally, it is important to consider that sustained stimulation acted as performance-aiding noise, which can be compared to the effects observed in studies using white noise in both animal and human research. Indeed, white noise has been shown to improve spatial working memory performance in monkeys, as compared to performance during silence or classical music182. This improvement has been suggested to be due to white noise masking potential acoustic distractors and aiding attention. The effects of white noise on healthy adults are mixed: while there is a general consensus that noise decreases cognitive performance126,128,129, an increasing number of studies show that, under certain circumstances, noise can have a positive impact on sustained attention, task performance, and creativity183, and particularly on working memory113,114,131. Furthermore, white noise has been shown to specifically aid inattentive children111,114,133 and children with ADHD111,133,135,137, while decreasing performance in typically developing children111,133. This indicates that white noise stimulation might be particularly beneficial for individuals with suboptimal attentional control. These findings align well with the neurocomputational Moderate Brain Arousal (MBA) model138,184, which postulates that abnormally low levels of extracellular dopamine in ADHD lead to exaggerated phasic responses and hypersensitivity to environmental stimuli. At the same time, stimulus-impoverished environments cause low arousal and lead to hyperactivity. The optimal response to stimuli can, therefore, be achieved in environments with moderate levels of stimulation, which can be attained through appropriately chosen levels of white noise. This positive effect of white noise is explained by the statistical phenomenon of stochastic resonance185, where random noise added to the neural system can aid signal detection and improve performance. While our findings that sensory stimulation predominantly aided low-capacity participants are consistent with the MBA model, it is noteworthy that our control condition consisted of a pure tone with added pink noise, rather than silence. Additional research is necessary to elucidate the effects of sustained sensory stimulation (both rhythmic and arrhythmic) compared to silence and white noise. This would help determine if such stimulation functions similarly to white noise in enhancing attention and working memory, particularly in populations with deficient or lower attentional control.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge several limitations in the current study. First, the initial hypothesis was based on the theta-gamma model28, leading us to focus solely on the theta range. However, it would be important to establish whether the observed effects are specific to theta oscillations or extended to other frequency bands. Indeed, as we did not observe significant evidence of a rhythmicity effect in our first experiment, but WM performance was higher with higher rate of stimulation, it would suggest that this effect can possibly extend beyond the theta range. Therefore, it would be informative to explore the boundaries and optimal stimulation frequency range that could produce the robust improvement in vWM capacity, as observed across our six experiments (see Fig. 4b). Moreover, future studies should include an arrhythmic condition to differentiate effects caused by rhythmic stimulation, or neural entrainment, from those resulting from sensory or aperiodic stimulation, which may modulate temporal expectations. Second, obtaining concurrent electroencephalographic (EEG) or magnetoencephalographic (MEG) data would help establish potential changes in theta activity in frontal-midline and parietal-occipital regions. Furthermore, concurrent M/EEG would enable frequency-specific entrainment tailored to the individual frequency profile of each participant. Thirdly, this study did not take into account potential individual differences, both objective and subjective, in the effectiveness of sensory entrainment. While sensory entrainment across different modalities can be used to influence relevant brain oscillations186, it remains unclear whether significant individual differences exist in the effects of certain sensory modalities compared to others. Fourth, in this study, we used sensory entrainment during the task as opposed to the resting period before the task, following other studies that targeted vWM capacity with tACS stimulation39,59–61. While it has been shown that vWM capacity is only increased during online tACS stimulation60, it would be insightful to explore whether sensory entrainment can influence vWM when applied during the resting period before the task, and whether the stimulation effects are task-dependent187. Finally, despite these data establishing a differential effect on low-capacity participants, there is a need for a better understanding of why. We have suggested some hypothesis, based on our findings, but it would be necessary to conduct further studies to explore how sensory stimulation influences attentional and control processes, which are associated with lower working memory capacity139, providing a better understanding of the complex effects of sensory stimulation across multiple cognitive systems.

Conclusions

In contrast to previous studies using tACS, sensory stimulation at 7 Hz outperformed the stimulation at 4 Hz. Yet, we found that sensory entrainment (both at 4 Hz and 7 Hz) produced an overall improvement in vWM capacity. This effect was predominantly driven by the low-capacity participant group, although it could not be conclusively attributed to the modulation of arousal. Therefore, sensory stimulation shows clear promise for modulating visual working memory, although its effects cannot be directly extrapolated from those found when using transcranial electrical stimulation. Further studies are therefore needed to garner this potential by gaining a better understanding of sensory entrainment effects on the different components of working memory.
