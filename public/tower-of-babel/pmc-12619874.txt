Primary hemangioblastoma of rectum: a rare case report and review of literature
Authors: Aiping Zheng, Shaojuan Zhang, Qiang Ma, Wenxu Yang, Hualiang Xiao, Xinyu Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHemangioblastoma is an uncommon tumor of uncertain histogenesis, primarily found in the central nervous system. However, extraneural cases have been reported in visceral organs such as the kidneys, pancreas, peritoneum, and liver. Hemangioblastoma occurring in the gastrointestinal tract is extremely rare, with only 4 cases can be retrieved. Here, we presented a case of rectal hemangioblastoma.Methods A 59-year-old woman sought medical attention for altered stool consistency and the colonoscopy revealed a submucosal lesion in the rectum. Subsequently, she underwent endoscopic submucosal dissection. Grossly, the excised lesion was a broad-based polypoid mass measuring 1.2 × 0.8 × 0.8 cm, with a grayish-white appearance and no signs of hemorrhage or necrosis. Histologically, the tumor consisted of a rich network of thin-walled blood vessels interspersed with vesicular neoplastic cells, with minimal mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry found that neoplastic cells were positive for D2-40, and β-catenin showed normal membranous staining pattern. No pathogenic mutations in the VHL gene were detected by NGS in this sample. However, we found genetic alterations in other genes potentially associated with the pathogenesis of hemangioblastoma, such as TSC, SDH and PTEN.ResultsBased on the above findings, the diagnosis of hemangioblastoma was made. The differential diagnosis included hemangioma, lymphangioma, neuroendocrine tumor, metastatic renal cell carcinoma, perivascular epithelioid cell tumor, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, and well-differentiated liposarcoma.ConclusionThis case underscored the importance of considering hemangioblastoma in the differential diagnosis of rectal submucosal lesions. It highlighted the need for a thorough diagnostic approach that integrates colonoscopic evaluation with histopathological examination, particularly in patients presenting with altered stool consistency or melena. Furthermore, the NGS results implicated that TSC, SDH and PTEN may contribute to hemangioblastoma development. These alterations could constitute a novel diagnostic signature, a premise that warrants definitive investigation through larger, multi-institutional studies.Keywords: Hemangioblastoma, Rectum, Histopathological examination, TSC, SDH, PTEN

Introduction

Hemangioblastoma (HB) is a rare benign tumor of uncertain origin, primarily affecting adults aged 20 to 50. It most commonly arises in the cerebellum, followed by the brainstem and spinal cord (Frank et al. 1989). Histologically, HB is characterized by neoplastic cells with clear cytoplasm and vacuolation, interwoven with an abundant vascular network. Approximately 30% of HB cases are associated with von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome, and 80% of individuals with VHL develop HBs during their lifetime (Iliopoulos et al. 2024). Less frequently, HB occurs in peripheral organs, including the kidneys, peritoneum, pancreas, liver, skin, and lungs (Lindholm and Moran 2020; Gnarra et al. 1994; Rojiani et al. 1991). Gastrointestinal involvement is exceptionally rare, with only four cases can be retrieved (Doyle and Fletcher 2014; Basave et al. 2015; Casadei Gardini et al. 2013; Takamura et al. 1975). Here, we report a case of rectal HB and provide a comprehensive review of its clinicopathological and molecular genetic features in the context of existing literature.

Case presentation

Clinical features

A 59-year-old woman presented with a three-month history of altered stool consistency of unknown cause, accompanied by mild anal discomfort and tenesmus, without abdominal pain or melena. Colonoscopy at a local hospital revealed a submucosal lesion in the rectum (Fig. 1A). Cranial MRI showed no abnormalities. Laboratory examination revealed no abnormalities. The patient subsequently underwent endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) under general anesthesia. During the procedure, a 2 cm protruding lesion was identified in the rectum and was completely resected for pathological examination.

Pathological features

Grossly, the tumor was a broad-based polypoid lesion measuring 1.2 × 0.8 × 0.8 cm. The cut surface appeared homogeneous and pale gray, with no macroscopic signs of necrosis or hemorrhage. Histologically, the tumor was polypoid and primarily located in the submucosa (Fig. 1B). It consisted of polygonal epithelioid cells of varying sizes interspersed with abundant blood vessels. The neoplastic cells had moderately eosinophilic cytoplasm, with some exhibiting clear cytoplasmic vacuolation. The nuclei were oval to polygonal, hyperchromatic, and showed minimal atypia, with no mitotic figures. A branching network of thin-walled capillaries with irregular lumina was interwoven with tumor cells, alongside larger-caliber vessels with both thin- and thick-walled configurations (Fig. 1C). Immunohistochemistry showed that the neoplastic cells were positive for D2-40 (Fig. 1D), and β-catenin showed normal membranous staining pattern. They were negative for CD117, CDK4, CK (Fig. 1E), Camta1, Desmin, DOG-1, HMB45, S-100, STAT6, SYN, TFE3, CA9, PAX8, NSE, GLUT1 and α-inhibin, while Rb expression was retained. CD31, CD34 (Fig. 1F), ERG, FLI-1, and SMA highlighted the intricate intratumoral capillary network.

Molecular genetics

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) revealed no MDM2 gene amplification. No pathogenic mutations in the VHL gene were detected by next generation sequencing (NGS) in this sample. However, we found genetic alterations in other genes potentially associated with the pathogenesis of HB, such as TSC, SDH and PTEN (Fig. 2). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of hemangioblastoma.

Follow-up

At the 6-month follow-up visit, there was no evidence of recurrence or distant metastasis.

Systematic literature review

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science using the keywords "hemangioblastoma", “gastric” and "intestine". Our search retrieved only four reported cases of gastrointestinal HB in the literature (Doyle and Fletcher 2014; Basave et al. 2015; Casadei Gardini et al. 2013; Takamura et al. 1975). Of these, one case was included in broader analyses of peripheral HBs, but detailed clinical and pathological data were unavailable. Based on previous reports and our case (Table 1), we propose that gastrointestinal HBs share several pathological characteristics. The tumor primarily affects adults, without significant gender predilection in its occurrence. Clinical symptoms are nonspecific and may include abnormal bowel movements, anemia, abdominal pain, nausea, hematemesis and melena. Endoscopic examination typically reveals smooth-surfaced submucosal lesions. Grossly, intestinal HBs appear as well-circumscribed polypoid tumors, ranging from 1.2 to 5.0 cm in diameter. The cut surface is gray-white and lacks necrosis. Microscopically, the tumors consist of round or polygonal epithelioid cells interspersed with abundant blood vessels. The neoplastic cells have lightly eosinophilic cytoplasm with prominent microvacuolation, suggesting lipid content. Abundant blood vessels traverse the neoplastic cells, dividing them into clusters. Both large thin-walled and thick-walled vessels are commonly observed. Mitotic figures are rare, and there is no evidence of necrosis, lymphatic invasion, or vascular infiltration. The immunohistochemical profile of HBs is nonspecific. However, positive expression of inhibin, neuron-specific enolase (NSE), smooth muscle actin (SMA), and D2-40 may assist in diagnosis. Immunostaining for PAX-8, MDM2, and HMB45 is useful in differential diagnosis. Current evidence suggests that intestinal HBs have a favorable prognosis. Complete resection appears curative, with no reported cases of local recurrence or distant metastasis.

Discussion

HB is a rare benign tumor, comprising less than 2% of all central nervous system tumors but approximately 11% of posterior fossa tumors. Around 30% of HB cases are associated with VHL syndrome, which results from mutations in the VHL gene located on chromosome 3p25-26 (Seizinger et al. 1988). The pathogenesis primarily involves the loss of VHL gene function, leading to the abnormal accumulation of HIF-1α. This, in turn, activates downstream genes such as VEGF and erythropoietin, promoting angiogenesis and tumor growth. Additional signaling pathways, including EGFR, Notch, and TGF-α (Kaelin 2009), further contribute to tumor development. Somatic mutations or epigenetic inactivation of the VHL gene occur in approximately 78% of sporadic HB cases (Shankar et al. 2014). Environmental factors, such as ionizing radiation and chemical exposure, may also contribute to sporadic HB by inducing genetic mutations (Yoda and Cimino 2022). Using the TargetSeq® liquid-phase probe hybridization capture technology, we performed NGS on DNA extracted from paraffin-embedded tumor tissue. This analysis interrogated a comprehensive panel of 122 genes but detected no mutations in the VHL gene. Our findings are consistent with molecular genetic reports from previous cases of hemangioblastoma. In previously reported cases, investigations employing polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis (Wang et al. 2012; Raja et al. 2023), loss of heterozygosity (LOH) analysis (Kuroda et al. 2015; Muscarella et al. 2018), FISH (Jiang et al. 2013) and NGS (Wang et al. 2022; Gao and Li 2025) have consistently failed to detect either VHL gene mutations or deletions of chromosome 3p.

Notably, our NGS analysis also identified mutations in additional genes. The top 20 most frequently mutated genes in this sample were: RHOA, NF1, CDK12, PDGFRA, AR, SDHD, ARID1A, SDHC, PTEN, MYCN, KDM5C, ERBB2, TSC1, BRCA2, PALB2, DICER1, CASP7, BRIP1, APC, and ALK. KEGG pathway analysis of these frequently mutated genes revealed significant enrichment in signaling pathways including EGFR and mTOR (Fig. 3). The TSC gene, associated with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), encodes the TSC1 and TSC2 proteins, which form a functional complex that negatively regulates the mTOR signaling pathway, thereby preventing aberrant cellular proliferation and growth. Previous studies have documented TSC mutations in 11 cases of renal HB and 3 cases of renal cell carcinoma exhibiting HB-like features. Moreover, Perez, Rachel L et al. previously reported the occurrence of a pancreatic HB in a patient with TSC (Perez et al. 2024). Integrating these prior literature findings with our current sequencing results, we hypothesized that dysregulation of the TSC/mTOR signaling pathway may be implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of HB. Mutations in SDHC or SDHD can inactivate and destabilize the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) complex, resulting in intracellular succinate accumulation. This process may drive tumorigenesis either through the stabilization of HIF or by inducing apoptotic pathways. Roh, Tae Hoon et al. found the evidence of SDH deficiency in approximately 80% (28/35) of HB cases (Roh et al. 2019), indicating that SDH inactivation could be a pivotal alternative mechanism in HB development. The PTEN gene acts as a tumor suppressor across various cancers. Its inactivation can lead to the upregulation of HIF-1, thereby contributing to tumor angiogenesis and vasculopathy. Chen et al. (2010) developed a mouse model resembling human liver hemangioblastoma through the concurrent conditional knockout of VHL and PTEN, suggesting a potential role for PTEN in the pathogenesis of HB. Although alterations in genes such as PDGFRA (Taher et al. 2023), BRCA2 (Sobottka et al. 1998), ALK (Taher et al. 2023), and APC (Wang et al. 2022) have been previously documented in HB cases, their mechanistic roles remain unexplored. In contrast, genomic changes in RHOA, NF1, CDK12, AR, MYCN, KDM5C, ERBB2, PALB2, DICER1, CASP7, BRIP1 have not been previously reported in HB. Our sequencing data may contribute to a better understanding of the molecular profile of gastrointestinal HB.

Although the WHO recognizes inhibin positivity as a typical histochemical feature of hemangioblastoma, it also emphasizes that diagnosis should integrate morphological features, immunohistochemical markers, and molecular background. In our case, despite the absence of inhibin expression, the tumor exhibited classic histological features, and strong D2-40 positivity supported its stromal cell origin. Additional immunohistochemical tests ruled out other morphologically similar tumors. Previous studies reported the sensitivity and specificity of inhibin-α for hemangioblastoma at 88% and 79%, respectively (Weinbreck t al. 2008). The result underscored its diagnostic value, while also highlighted its limitations, reinforcing the need for comprehensively evaluation in combination with other biomarkers. Furthermore, there may be heterogeneous expression of hemangioblastomas at different sites, and some previous studies have reported inhibin-negative cases (Doyle and Fletcher 2014; Basave et al. 2015; Weinbreck et al. 2008; Epari et al. 2014). Therefore, the lack of inhibin expression cannot deny the diagnosis.

The pathological diagnosis of intestinal HB requires differentiation from various tumors, including intestinal hemangioma, lymphangioma, neuroendocrine tumor, metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (CCRCC), perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa), gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), and well-differentiated liposarcoma. Histologically, intestinal HB is distinguished by a dense network of thin-walled blood vessels and vacuolated neoplastic cells, necessitating its differentiation from metastatic CCRCC. While both tumors share a clear cell morphology and rich vascularization, CCRCC cells contain cytoplasmic glycogen (PAS-positive), whereas HB cells contain lipids (oil red O-positive). Immunophenotypically, CCRCC expresses CA9, PAX-8, EMA, and CD10, while HB is negative for these markers but positive for α-inhibin and D2-40. Given HB’s extensive vascular network, it must also be distinguished from hemangioma and lymphangioma, which lack vacuolated neoplastic cells and lipid-laden vacuoles. Additionally, hemangiomas typically do not express D2-40, aiding in differentiation. Similarly, intestinal neuroendocrine tumors may exhibit vascular-like architectures, but they express chromogranin A (CgA) and synaptophysin (Syn) while lacking D2-40, differentiating them from HB. The vacuolated neoplastic cells in HB may resemble lipoblasts in atypical lipomatous tumors (ALT)/well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDL). However, ALT/WDLs typically contains atypical lipoblasts and myxoid stroma, contrasting with HB’s highly vascularized architecture. Immunohistochemically, ALT/WDLs expresses MDM2 and CDK4, whereas HB cells are negative for these markers. Additionally, the gold standard for diagnosis of ALT/WDLs is the detection of MDM2 amplification, usually by FISH (Machado et al. 2022). PEComas may also present epithelioid cells with clear cytoplasm, but they characteristically express HMB45 and Melan-A, which are absent in HB. Tumors arising in the intestinal mucosa, such as GIST, should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. Unlike HB, GIST exhibits spindle-shaped or epithelioid cell morphology and expresses CD117 and DOG1.

In conclusion, diagnosing intestinal HB is challenging due to its rare occurrence in this location. The key to an accurate diagnosis lies in recognizing its biphasic histological features—microvesicular tumor cells interwoven with a capillary network-long with α-inhibin and/or D2-40 positivity and potential VHL gene alterations. Misdiagnosis often arises from over-reliance on a single morphological feature, such as clear-cell appearance, without integrating immunohistochemical and molecular findings. Furthermore, the NGS results implicated that TSC, SDH and PTEN may contribute to hemangioblastoma development. These alterations could constitute a novel diagnostic signature, a premise that warrants definitive investigation through larger, multi-institutional studies.
