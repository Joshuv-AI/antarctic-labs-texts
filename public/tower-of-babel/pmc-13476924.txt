Whole-genome resequencing with multidimensional annotation revealed pathogenic networks in sirenomelia
Authors: Zefeng Zhu, Wanting Hao, Ziyu Wan, Tianhao Ma, Yao Xu, Yu Wang, Wei Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSirenomelia is a rare and lethal congenital malformation representing the most severe form of caudal dysgenesis, with a stillbirth rate of up to 53%. The genetic basis of sirenomelia remains poorly understood. Consequently, in this study, we aimed to comprehensively characterize genomic variants and explore potential pathogenic mechanisms in sirenomelia.MethodsWhole-genome resequencing was performed on peripheral blood mononuclear cells from one patient with sirenomelia and one matched healthy newborn. Variants were identified and prioritized using the gnomAD and ClinVar population databases. single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP)/InDel and structural variants were further analyzed using multidimensional functional enrichment approaches.ResultsA total of 116 candidate genes harboring rare and potentially deleterious SNP/InDel variants were identified, including BMP5 and BMP6. Structural variant analysis revealed 549 sirenomelia-specific variants involving genes such as TWSG1 and Retinoic acid receptor beta (RARB). Functional enrichment revealed that these genes were predominantly involved in key developmental pathways, including BMP, WNT and TGF-β signaling. Integrated analysis indicated convergent disruption of signaling networks regulating posterior axis development, particularly those associated with kidney formation, cloacal partitioning, and limb patterning.ConclusionThese findings support a multilayered pathogenic model in which genetic variants collectively disrupt the core developmental pathways involved in posterior axis formation, vasculogenesis, and organogenesis. Our findings provide a genomic framework for understanding the molecular basis of sirenomelia and identify potential targets for future research and clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02307-5.Keywords: Sirenomelia, Whole-genome resequencing, BMP signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, WNT signaling pathway

Introduction

Sirenomelia is a rare and fatal congenital malformation that represents the most severe form of caudal dysgenesis (CD) [1]. Characterized by fused lower limbs resembling those of a mermaid, this condition is commonly referred to as Mermaid syndrome. Sirenomelia is usually accompanied by severe malformations of the urogenital [2, 3] and gastrointestinal systems [4]. In addition, sirenomelia is associated with substantial phenotypic variability, including abnormalities in the musculoskeletal system [5], central nervous system [6] and cardiopulmonary systems [7].

Cases of sirenomelia occur across all racial groups, with an incidence of approximately 1 to 3 per 100,000 live births [8, 9] and are more prevalent in twin pregnancies compared to singleton pregnancies [10]. Maternal diabetes and a maternal age under 20 years are recognized as considerable risk factors for the occurrence of sirenomelia. In almost 50% of cases, the sex of the affected individual cannot be determined of the cases where sex is confirmed, there is no statistically significant difference in the male-to-female ratio. The live birth rate for sirenomelia is approximately 47%, whereas the stillbirth rate is alarmingly high at 53%, which includes approximately 28.5% of spontaneous fetal deaths and 24.5% of elective pregnancy terminations due to fetal anomalies (ETOPFA) [8]. Currently, there are two established hypotheses to explain the pathophysiology of sirenomelia. The vascular steal hypothesis suggests that this malformation results from an abnormal origin of the single umbilical artery (SUA) [3, 4]. As a large anomalous vessel originating high on the abdominal aorta, the SUA diverts substantial blood flow to the placenta, resulting in severe developmental abnormalities in caudal embryonic structures, including the lower limbs, pelvic organs, and kidneys, due to inadequate blood supply and nutrition [11–13]. An alternative hypothesis suggests that this malformation arises from developmental defects in the caudal mesoderm during the gastrula stage [13, 14]; this phenomenon overlaps with the VACTERL association and caudal dysgenesis (CD) phenotypes, thereby classifying this condition as a caudal developmental spectrum disorder [6, 8].

Clinically, sirenomelia is characterized by a broad range of lower limb fusion phenotypes, from soft-tissue fusion to the formation of a single lower extremity. It is frequently associated with bilateral renal agenesis or renal dysplasia, absent or hypoplastic bladder, ambiguous or absent external genitalia, imperforate anus, anorectal atresia, sacral agenesis, vertebral defects, and pulmonary hypoplasia secondary to severe oligohydramnios. Cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and central nervous system anomalies may also occur [3, 8, 13, 15]. Because renal agenesis and pulmonary hypoplasia are common, most affected fetuses or neonates have a poor prognosis.

Clinical diagnosis is mainly based on prenatal ultrasonography and postnatal physical examination. Prenatal ultrasound may reveal fused lower limbs, severe oligohydramnios or anhydramnios, bilateral renal agenesis, absent bladder, a single umbilical artery, abnormal pelvic development, and anorectal or genital abnormalities [3, 4, 15]. Color Doppler ultrasound may help identify abnormal umbilical and iliac vascular patterns, whereas three-dimensional ultrasound and fetal magnetic resonance imaging can improve anatomical assessment, particularly when severe oligohydramnios limits conventional sonographic visualization [16, 17]. After birth, diagnosis is usually established by the characteristic external phenotype and the presence of associated urogenital, anorectal, vertebral, and visceral anomalies.

There is currently no standardized curative treatment for sirenomelia. Disease management depends on gestational age at diagnosis, the severity of associated malformations, parental preference, and local ethical and legal considerations. When severe lethal anomalies, such as bilateral renal agenesis and pulmonary hypoplasia, are identified prenatally, multidisciplinary counseling is essential, and termination of pregnancy may be discussed where legally and ethically permitted [3, 15]. For live-born infants, treatment is primarily supportive and requires coordinated care from neonatology, pediatric surgery, urology, nephrology, orthopedics, radiology, and genetics. Potential interventions include respiratory support, assessment of renal function and urinary tract anatomy, management of anorectal malformations such as colostomy, reconstruction of urogenital anomalies, and staged orthopedic procedures for limb separation in rare survivors with preserved renal function [18, 19]. However, survival remains exceptional and is largely determined by the severity of renal, pulmonary, and vascular malformations.

Previous cytogenetic and genomic studies have provided preliminary evidence for the genetic contribution to sirenomelia.An X-chromosome translocation t(X;16)(p11.23;p12.3) was identified in a female patient with sirenomelia through cytogenetic analysis (GTG banding) of 20 midterm fetal cells combined with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [20]. Another study reported a triploid karyotype (69,XXX/46,XX mosaicism) in a fetus with sirenomelia following karyotype analysis through chorionic villus sampling (CVS) [14].

Recent genetic studies have provided important clues to the molecular basis of sirenomelia, particularly through genes involved in posterior axis patterning and retinoic acid (RA) homeostasis. Whole-exome sequencing has identified variants in CDX2 in a sirenomelia case [21]. CDX2, located on chromosome 13q12-q13, encodes caudal-type homeobox transcription factor 2, a nuclear transcription factor containing a conserved homeodomain. The human CDX2 gene is transcribed into CDX2 mRNA and encodes a 313-amino-acid protein that binds DNA and regulates downstream developmental transcriptional programs [22]. Functionally, CDX2 is best known for its role in intestinal epithelial differentiation and hindgut development, but experimental evidence also supports its involvement in axial elongation, posterior body patterning, and caudal mesoderm specification [23, 24]. Its expression is prominent in the posterior embryonic region and later in the intestinal epithelium, consistent with its role in posterior gut and caudal body development [25]. Genotype-phenotype studies have shown that disruption of CDX2 can impair posterior axis formation, and the identification of CDX2 variants in human sirenomelia suggests that altered CDX2-mediated transcriptional regulation may contribute to caudal developmental defects [21, 26].

The RA metabolic pathway is another important genetic axis related to sirenomelia. CYP26A1, located on chromosome 10q23-q24, encodes cytochrome P450 family 26 subfamily A member 1. CYP26A1 mRNA encodes a retinoic acid-metabolizing cytochrome P450 enzyme localized mainly to the endoplasmic reticulum. Structurally, CYP26A1 belongs to the microsomal P450 enzyme family and contains conserved P450 catalytic motifs required for substrate recognition and heme-dependent monooxygenase activity. Its major function is to hydroxylate and inactivate all-trans retinoic acid, thereby maintaining appropriate spatial and temporal RA gradients during embryogenesis [27, 28]. Because RA functions as a morphogen that regulates Hox gene expression, axial elongation, limb bud development, and organogenesis, abnormal RA accumulation can severely disrupt embryonic patterning. Experimental studies have demonstrated that loss or downregulation of CYP26A1 leads to excessive RA signaling, suppression of BMP pathway activity, and defects in tail mesoderm and limb bud development, producing phenotypes that overlap with sirenomelia [21, 29]. These findings support a genotype-phenotype relationship in which impaired RA degradation caused by CYP26A1 dysfunction may promote caudal regression-like or sirenomelia-like malformations.

POR, located on chromosome 7q11.23, encodes cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase, a membrane-bound flavoprotein of approximately 680 amino acids. The human POR locus contains one non-coding exon and multiple protein-coding exons, and its mRNA encodes a protein containing an N-terminal membrane-anchoring region and conserved FMN-, FAD-, and NADPH-binding domains [30]. Functionally, POR serves as the obligatory electron donor for microsomal cytochrome P450 enzymes, transferring electrons from NADPH through FAD and FMN to P450 catalytic partners. Therefore, POR is required for multiple metabolic processes, including steroid hormone biosynthesis, xenobiotic metabolism, and retinoid metabolism [31]. In the context of embryonic development, POR may influence RA homeostasis indirectly by regulating the activity of CYP enzymes, including RA-metabolizing enzymes such as CYP26A1. Disruption of POR in experimental models has been linked to abnormal RA accumulation, reduced BMP signaling output, and defects in tail mesoderm and limb bud development [29]. Although POR has not been definitively established as a causative gene for human sirenomelia, its functional connection with CYP26A1-mediated RA metabolism provides biological plausibility for its involvement in the RA-BMP developmental axis.

Together, CDX2, CYP26A1, and POR converge on regulatory networks that control posterior axis development. CDX2 mainly participates in caudal transcriptional patterning, whereas CYP26A1 and POR regulate RA metabolism and thereby influence RA-dependent developmental gradients. Excessive RA signaling can inhibit BMP pathway activity, and disruption of BMP signaling is strongly associated with sirenomelia-like phenotypes in animal models. Within the BMP signaling pathway, Bmp7/Shh [32] and Bmp7/Tsg [33] compound mutants, as well as conditional knockout of Bmp4 [34] has been shown to induce sirenomelia-like phenotypes in mice. These findings suggest that dysregulation of the CDX2–RA–BMP developmental network may represent an important mechanistic framework for understanding the genetic pathogenesis of sirenomelia.

Although studies have revealed associations between certain genetic mutations and the sirenomelia phenotype, our understanding of the genetic basis of this disease remains poorly defined, with the molecular mechanisms underlying many cases still unclear. In this study, we employed whole-genome resequencing (WGRS) to conduct whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on peripheral blood samples from a child diagnosed with sirenomelia, followed by differential analysis. Our aim was to investigate the pathogenesis and genetic mutations of sirenomelia, comprehensively screen for potential pathogenic mutations, to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this disease, and to provide a theoretical foundation for future prenatal diagnosis and familial genetic risk assessment.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The newborn’s mother was a 36-year-old multiparous woman with a history of two previous cesarean deliveries. She was admitted at 37 weeks of gestation because of vaginal fluid leakage for approximately 4 h. The pregnancy was complicated by advanced maternal age, a scarred uterus, premature rupture of membranes, and severe oligohydramnios. Emergency cesarean section was performed after obstetric evaluation. During the operation, the amniotic fluid was almost depleted.

The newborn exhibited classic features of sirenomelia immediately after delivery. The lower body showed a fish-like appearance from the waist to the hips. The pelvis was malformed, and the external genitalia were absent, making sex determination impossible. No urinary or fecal orifices were observed. Only a single lower limb (presumed to be the left thigh) was present, distally curved to the right, and lacking the knee joint, ankle joint, and foot. A soft tissue mass resembling a tail was visible at the base of the spine, posterior to the fish-like limb. A superficial depression was present posterior to the tail, which did not represent an anus or a spinal deformity (Fig. 1).

Child with Sirenomelia

The newborn weighed 2020 g at birth and presented with extreme weakness. Apgar scores were 9 at 1 minute and 3 at 5 minutes. Despite neonatal assessment and supportive care, the infant died within a short period after birth. Two milliliters of peripheral blood were collected from the newborn with sirenomelia and from a normal newborn, respectively, and whole genome resequencing (WGRS) testing was performed by Guangzhou Gidiou Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

DNA library construction

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the CTAB method. Optical density (OD) values for the DNA samples were measured with a NanoDrop micro-spectrophotometer, confirming an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. After verifying DNA integrity by agarose gel electrophoresis, DNA fragmentation was performed using an ultrasonication method. Subsequently, fragmented DNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and the ligation of sequencing adapters. The adapter-ligated DNA fragments were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads, with fragments within the 300–400 base pair (bp) range selected for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification to construct the final sequencing library. Following library construction, initial quantification was conducted using a Qubit 2.0. The insert size was then assessed using an Agilent 2100. Upon confirming the expected insert size, the effective concentration of the library (2 nM) was precisely quantified using Q-PCR to ensure quality. After passing quality control, sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

Data filtering

To ensure data quality, it was imperative to conduct quality control on raw data prior to information analysis and employ data filtering to minimize noise. We implemented more stringent filtering of the clean reads following sequencing to obtain high-quality clean reads for subsequent information analysis. The filtering steps were as follows: (1) adapters were removed while retaining the remaining reads; (2) reads with an “N” content exceeding 10% were removed; (3) reads composed entirely of A nucleotides were removed; and (4) Eliminate low-quality reads, defined as those where bases with a quality score of Q ≤ 20 make up over 50% of the entire read, were eliminated.

After filtering, high-quality clean reads were used for subsequent alignment and variant detection. The quality control parameters included the total number of raw reads, the number and proportion of high-quality clean reads, adapter-containing reads, low-quality reads, and N-containing reads. The clean read ratio was used to evaluate whether the sequencing data were suitable for genome-wide variant analysis.

Genome alignment, post-alignment processing, and coverage assessment

Genome alignment was conducted using the BWA alignment software (version 0.7.16a-r1181) with the mem algorithm, filtering reads that were aligned to the human reference genome (Ensembl release 113). The alignment parameters were set to -k 32-M. SAMtools (version 1.16.1) was utilized for sorting and marking duplicate reads, while Bedtools (version 2.30.0) was employed to calculate read depth and coverage.

The quality control metrics for genome alignment included total reads, mapped reads, unmapped reads, paired mapped reads, uniquely mapped reads, multiple mapped reads, perfectly matched reads, mismatch reads, low mapping-quality reads, duplicate reads, average sequencing depth, and the proportion of genomic bases covered at 1×, 4×, 10×, 20×, and 30 × . Samples with high mapping rates, adequate unique mapping rates, acceptable duplicate rates, and sufficient genome coverage were considered suitable for subsequent variant detection. Variants located in regions with insufficient read support or poor mapping quality were not prioritized in downstream analysis.

SNP and InDel calling, filtering, and annotation

For variant detection, the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) was utilized to perform single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) and insertion-deletion mutations (InDel) variant detection in the BAM files generated from the genome alignment results. Only variants with adequate sequencing support and acceptable variant quality were retained for further analysis. Low-confidence variants were excluded when they showed insufficient read depth, low genotype quality, low mapping quality, or evidence of unreliable alignment. Variants failing the internal quality filters were not included in the candidate variant set.

In the variant annotation phase, ANNOVAR software was employed to annotate the detected SNPs, InDels, and other genomic variants against human genome databases(Ensembl_release113). This process enabled the determination of the locations, frequencies, protein pathogenicity, genotype heterozygosity, and functional pathways of the variants.

Variant frequency annotation was conducted utilizing several key databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project (http://www.internationalgenome.org/), HAMAP (http://hamap.expasy.org/), ESP6500 (https://esp.gs.washington.edu/drupal/), dbSNP (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/SNP/), gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), and ClinVar (http://www.clinvar.com/). In addition, the prediction of variant harmfulness was based on a comprehensive set of databases including SIFT, Polyphen-2, MutationTaster, CADD, LRT, Fathmm, PROVEAN, DANN, MutationAssessor, fathmm-MKL, MetaSVM, GERP++, phyloP, phastCons, and SiPhy.

Candidate Mutation screening:The screening of genomic variants, including SNPs and InDels was applied to yield candidate mutations that are prioritized as high-risk. A mutation was classified as high-risk if categorized as either a frameshift or nonsense mutation, and if its frequencies in the 1000 Genomes dataset, the East Asian population of the 1000 Genomes, the gnomAD population, the East Asian population of gnomAD, or the ESP6500 were ≤ 0.01. In addition, a site was deemed harmful if predicted by at least three software tools (such as SIFT, Polyphen, MutationTaster, MutationAssessor, FATHMM, and CADD) with a population frequency ≤ 0.01, or if it was listed as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in the ClinVar database with a population frequency ≤ 0.01.

Finally, candidate variants identified in the sirenomelia patient were compared with those identified in the healthy newborn control. Variants shared by both samples were excluded from the final patient-prioritized candidate set. The remaining variants were considered prioritized candidate variants for downstream functional interpretation. Similar next-generation sequencing-based strategies combining variant calling, population frequency filtering, in silico pathogenicity prediction, and candidate variant prioritization have been used in previous genetic studies [35–37].

Structural variant detection, filtering, and annotation

Structural variants (SVs), including deletions (DEL), duplications (DUP), inversions (INV), insertions (INS), and translocation breakends (BND), were identified from the whole-genome sequencing data using the structural variant detection module of the sequencing analysis pipeline. SV detection was based on abnormal paired-end read signals, split-read evidence, local read-depth changes, and breakpoint-level mapping patterns where applicable. Candidate SVs with insufficient read support, unreliable breakpoint information, or low mapping confidence were excluded. SVs located in low-complexity or repetitive genomic regions were interpreted cautiously to reduce false-positive calls.

The retained SVs were annotated according to genomic coordinates, SV type, SV size, genotype, affected gene, and overlap with coding or regulatory regions. For structural variant prioritization, SVs identified in the sirenomelia patient were compared with those identified in the healthy newborn control to reduce background variation unrelated to the disease phenotype. The prioritization procedure consisted of two sequential steps. First, SV events that were detected in the sirenomelia patient but absent from the healthy newborn control were retained. Second, the genes affected by SVs in both samples were compared, and genes uniquely affected by SVs in the sirenomelia patient were selected. Genes affected by these patient-prioritized SVs were then subjected to functional annotation and enrichment analysis.

Candidate gene functional annotation and enrichment analysis

Genes harboring prioritized SNP/InDel variants and genes affected by prioritized SVs were analyzed separately.Disease annotations associated with variants were sourced from ClinVar (http://www.clinvar.com/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), and COSMIC70 (http://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic). Gene pathway annotations for variant-associated genes were identified using the Gene Ontology (GO) database (http://www.geneontology.org/), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), Disease Ontology (DO) database (https://disease-ontology.org/), and Reactome databases (https://reactome.org/). Enrichment analysis was used to identify biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, disease categories, and signaling pathways overrepresented among the candidate genes. Enriched terms were ranked according to statistical significance and Rich factor, and the top enriched terms were visualized using bubble plots.

Candidate genes were further interpreted by integrating variant type, population frequency, predicted pathogenicity, disease database evidence, enrichment results, known developmental functions, and phenotypic relevance to sirenomelia. Particular attention was given to genes and pathways involved in posterior axis development, caudal mesoderm formation, kidney development, cloacal partitioning, limb bud patterning, vascular development, retinoic acid metabolism, BMP signaling, WNT signaling, TGF-β signaling, and SHH signaling.

Results

Genome-wide Re-sequencing sample quality and depth analysis

Systematic quality control and statistical classification were conducted on the raw sequencing reads to evaluate the quality and filtering efficacy of the whole-genome sequencing data. The results indicated that after filtering, high-quality clean reads predominated in the frequency distribution of the both normal newborn (NC) and sirenomelia patient samples, with values significantly higher than those of other categories. In contrast, adapter sequences, low-quality reads, and sequences containing “N” accounted for extremely low proportions (Fig. 2a). The proportion of high-quality clean reads approached 100% in both sample (Normal control (NC): 96.65%; affected child: 96.3%) (Fig. 2b), thus suggesting that the preprocessing steps effectively eliminated low-quality and redundant sequences, thereby yielding a highly reliable dataset that met the quality requirements for subsequent bioinformatics analysis

Next, we employed GATK software to identify SNP and InDel sites. Using ANNOVAR software, we annotated the detected genomic variations, including SNPs and InDels, against external databases (Supplementary Table S1). We also quantified the genomic locations (Figs. 3A and 3B) and the coding status of variants (Figs. 3C and 3D), in addition to providing information relating to transpositions and inversions (Table 3).We also counted the number of homozygous and heterozygous SNP-InDel variants in each sample; detailed numerical data are presented in Supplementary Tables S2–S6. After intersecting the two candidate mutation sets and excluding eight identical mutations, we ultimately identified 116 mutation genes associated with SNP-InDel candidate mutations in the sirenomelia patient (Fig. 3E). Candidate mutation SNP-InDel enrichment analysis

Based on whole-genome sequencing results from the child diagnosed with sirenomelia, we conducted enrichment analysis (GO, KEGG, DO, and Reactome) on genes associated with candidate mutation SNPs and InDels. Our analysis revealed marked molecular functional abnormalities, disruptions in biological processes, and impairments in signaling pathways that are closely correlated with the disease phenotype. These findings were primarily concentrated in areas related to lysosomal homeostasis, transmembrane transport, steroid metabolism, and the regulation of developmental signaling.

In the GO cellular component category, significant enrichment was observed for the lysosomal lumen, lysosomal membrane, and lysosomal body (Fig. 4a). Coupled with an enrichment in ATP-binding and ATPase-coupled transmembrane transport activities (Fig. 4b), these findings suggest abnormalities in energy-dependent ion and substrate transport. At the biological process level, significant enrichment was detected in entries related to aldosterone and mineralocorticoid biosynthesis (Fig. 4c), indicating a disruption in the regulation of water-electrolyte homeostasis. Given that sirenomelia is frequently associated with bilateral renal agenesis and severe oligohydramnios, these findings support the pathological sequence of renal developmental impairment, fluid regulation abnormalities and alterations in embryonic microenvironment.

Concurrently, multiple muscle structure entries, including I-band, Z-disk, sarcomere, myofibril, and sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ion transport, all exhibited significant enrichment. This finding suggests that the dysregulation of sarcomere assembly and contraction may contribute to lower limb fusion and abnormalities in motor structure. KEGG analysis further indicated enrichment in the TGF-β and Hippo pathways. When combined with abnormalities in extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interactions and glycosaminoglycan metabolism (Fig. 4d), these results imply the disruption of ECM remodeling and morphogenesis regulation, which may affect cloacal partitioning and posterior axis separation. In addition, Reactome enrichment analysis demonstrated the most significant enrichment in chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate metabolism and associated degradation pathways (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, DO enrichment analysis revealed significant associations with disease entries, including neuromuscular disorders, myopathies, muscular atrophy, neurological disorders, and intellectual disabilities (Fig. 4f), which were consistent with the sacrococcygeal abnormalities observed in certain cases [6].

Overall, SNP-InDel variants primarily affected organelle homeostasis and ion transport. Through the disruption of matrix and signaling pathways, these variants were found to be associated with renal defects, cloacal malformations, and limb fusion abnormalities.

Screening of candidate structural variants

In this study, we examined chromosomal structural variations (CSVs) in the genomes of a normal newborn and patient diagnosed with sirenomelia (Supplementary Table S10). For the normal newborn, we identified 468 chromosomal translocation breakpoint mutations, 3,871 structural deletions, 325 structural tandem repeats, 347 structural insertions, and 114 structural inversion mutations. In contrast, the sirenomelia patient exhibited 463 chromosomal translocation breakpoint mutations, 3,720 structural deletions, 314 structural tandem repeats, 350 structural insertions, and 105 structural inversion mutations (Fig. 5a and Table 4). By comparing CSVs between the patient with sirenomelia and the healthy newborn, we identified 549 CSV sites and their associated genes that were unique to the patient with sirenomelia (Fig. 5b).

Candidate structural variation enrichment Analysis

Based on whole-genome sequencing results from the child diagnosed with sirenomelia, enrichment analysis was performed on genes associated with candidate structural variants (GO, KEGG, DO, and Reactome). Analysis indicated that genes associated with structural variants were predominantly enriched in developmental signaling pathways and the regulation of cell adhesion and migration.

GO cellular components exhibit significant enrichment in cell membranes, cell junctions, and axon-related structures (Fig. 6a). Molecular function analysis indicates substantial enrichment in β-catenin binding, ion channel activity, and cell adhesion molecule binding, suggesting abnormalities in the WNT/β-catenin axis (Fig. 6b). Biological process analysis highlights considerable enrichment in the morphogenesis of anatomical structures, formation of neuronal projections, and axon guidance (Fig. 6c). KEGG analysis identified significant enrichment in Wnt, Hedgehog, and axon guidance pathways, which are crucial for post-embryonic axis elongation, limb bud patterning, and cloacal separation. These data suggested that structural variants may directly influence post-axial tissue fusion and organ differentiation by disrupting developmental signaling gradients and cell migration (Fig. 6d). Reactome enrichment analysis revealed the most significant enrichment in oxidized cholesterol binding to the ABCA1 gene and the dissociation/expression of its regulatory complex (Fig. 6e). Furthermore, DO enrichment analysis identified significant associations with conditions such as leukemia, hematological cancers, cancers of the immune system, and neuromuscular diseases (Fig. 6f).

Both types of variants demonstrate hierarchical regulatory patterns along the pathway of renal developmental defects, cloacal partitioning abnormality, and limb fusion, collectively supporting the notion that sirenomelia arises from systemic disruptions in the developmental program of the posterior axis.

Based on SNP-InDel variant annotations derived from whole-genome sequencing, we employed a hierarchical strategy to prioritize candidate sites according to the criteria of rarity, functional impact, prior evidence, and phenotypic consistency. Initially, we retained variants with low frequencies in population databases (≤0.01% in 1000 Genomes, gnomAD and its East Asian subpopulations and ESP6500) to minimize interference from common background polymorphisms. Subsequently, we prioritized variant types that were likely to cause substantial alterations in protein function, including frameshift mutations, nonsense mutations, and splicing-related variants. Missense variants were subjected to a comprehensive evaluation using multiple pathogenicity prediction tools while concurrently referencing evidence of pathogenicity or potential pathogenicity reported in authoritative databases such as ClinVar. Building on this foundation, we further integrated core sirenomelia phenotypes, which included abnormalities in posterior axis or tail development, urogenital malformations, and embryonic developmental defects. Concurrently, we conducted literature searches and assessed the biological plausibility of genes implicated by the variants, considering the vascular hypothesis and the embryonic developmental defect hypothesis of sirenomelia. Ultimately, we selected five SNP candidate sites and their corresponding genes, which were highly consistent with the phenotypic mechanism and supported by robust functional and evidence-based rationale, for in-depth discussion (Fig. 7 and Table 5).

BMP5 and BMP6

The BMP signaling pathway is a critical component of the TGF-β superfamily and plays a central role in various processes of embryonic development, including the establishment of the dorsal-ventral axis, mesoderm induction, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and the formation of bone and cartilage [38–41]. BMPs activate SMAD proteins via their receptors, thereby regulating the expression of downstream target genes and controlling cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [38]. BMP5 and BMP6 are two notable members of this family, sharing highly similar amino acid sequences and signaling mechanisms [42–44].

The characteristic phenotype of sirenomelia suggests that its etiology likely stems from severe developmental abnormalities in the caudal mesoderm during early embryogenesis. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the genetic factors responsible for these primary developmental defects is crucial. Previous studies have demonstrated that BMP5 exhibits multifunctionality during embryonic development; mutations in the BMP5 gene, such as those observed in the short-eared mouse model, result in morphological and size abnormalities in various skeletal and cartilaginous structures, including shortened external ears and defects in ribs and vertebrae. This mechanism involves the regulation of chondrocyte differentiation and proliferation [45–48]. During limb development, the expression of BMP5 in interdigital zones induces mesenchymal cell apoptosis by synergistically activating the SMAD and p38 MAPK signaling pathways, thereby shaping phalangeal morphology [42]. This suggests that this process is critical for normal limb separation. The reactivation of BMP5 during adult tissue repair occurs through its unique injury response element (IRE) within its locus, enabling specific expression following fractures or soft tissue injuries to promote regeneration [48]. This establishes BMP5 as a pivotal molecule that bridges embryonic development and adult repair, suggesting a potential continuum between developmental processes and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis.

BMP6 plays a crucial role in various biological processes, including angiogenesis, skeletal development, differentiation, mineralization, and mineral metabolism [49–51] and is particularly essential for the development of the kidneys and cloaca, as evidenced by the renal developmental abnormalities observed in BMP6-deficient mouse models [43]. In addition, studies in zebrafish have demonstrated that BMP6 deficiency impairs osteoblast development while promoting osteoclast differentiation within the myotome, consequently inhibiting intermuscular bone formation [52].

Recent evidence suggests that specific mutations in BMP genes are implicated in the development of the sirenomelia phenotype. Mice featuring BMP7 knockout, exhibit renal, ocular, and skeletal abnormalities; however, these mice do not typically present with sirenomelia. Notably, the absence of BMP7, when combined with the loss of another gene that regulates BMP signaling, frequently leads to the manifestation of sirenomelia. In experimental models, compound mutations involving BMP7-/- and twisted gastrulation (TSG-/-; BMP7-/- or TSG+/-; BMP7-/-) have been shown to result in the sirenomelia phenotype. This phenomenon is believed to be associated with a reduction in mesodermal tissue in the dorsal region, which subsequently causes the fusion of the body cavity and hindlimb buds [33]. Similarly, in mice, compound mutations of BMP7 (BMP7-/-) combined with Sonic hedgehog (SHH) mutations (BMP7-/-; SHH-/- or BMP7-/-; SHH+/-) also result in sirenomelia. This phenotype is characterized by hypoplasia of the hindgut and caudal aorta, leading to midline structural defects that subsequently cause abnormal fusion of limb buds [32]. Studies indicate that mice with a heterozygous BMP4 and homozygous BMP7 double compound mutation exhibit hindlimb fusion and bladder agenesis and anorectal malformations, similar to those observed in conditional BMP4 knockout mice; this suggests that BMP4 is a key gene in tail development and that a genetic interaction exists between BMP4 and BMP7 [34]. These models imply that sirenomelia may not be triggered solely by the absence of a single BMP member but rather depends on the significant overall downregulation of the BMP signaling network.

Studies indicate that BMP genes are redundantly expressed during embryonic development [53, 54] and that the loss of a single BMP allele may be compensated by other BMP molecules. However, compound mutations diminish this compensatory capacity [32, 33]. BMP6 and BMP5 likely overlap with other BMP family genes in regulating tail mesoderm development. Consequently, the missense variants detected in BMP5 and BMP6 in this study suggest that they may contribute to susceptibility to the sirenomelia phenotype by impairing effective BMP signaling output. This hypothesis warrants further validation in larger sample or family-based datasets.

ENG

The ENG gene encodes the Endoglin protein, a crucial coreceptor predominantly expressed on the surface of vascular endothelial cells which functions as a key component of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily signaling pathway, particularly within the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP9/BMP10)-ALK1–SMAD1 signaling cascade [55–57]. This pathway is essential for angiogenesis, vascular remodeling, and the maintenance of vascular integrity.

Classic loss-of-function mutations in the ENG gene are responsible for the autosomal dominant disorder hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia type 1 (HHT1). Patients with this condition present with vascular developmental abnormalities, including cutaneous and mucosal telangiectasia, as well as visceral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) [55, 58–60]. Importantly, the pathology of HHT follows a two-hit model: germline mutations (the first hit) lead to haploinsufficiency, whereas localized somatic mutations or environmental factors (the second hit) result in complete ENG dysfunction at specific vascular sites, culminating in focal vascular lesions [61]. Research indicates that homozygous ENG knockout mice exhibit macroscopically observable developmental abnormalities following VEGF stimulation, supporting the hypothesis that disease onset necessitates the loss of both alleles. This highlights the indispensable role of the ENG gene in vascular system development and emphasizes the characteristic of haploinsufficiency [62].

The vascular steal hypothesis for sirenomelia proposes that its primary pathogenesis is attributed to an abnormally enlarged umbilical artery, often a single umbilical artery, which diverts blood supply from the tail region. This diversion leads to ischemia, hypoxia, and hypoplasia in downstream tissues [15]. As ENG is a critical regulator of vascular development, ENG haploinsufficiency (the first hit at the germline level) may compromise the foundational integrity of the embryonic tail vascular network. Under specific environmental influences or random somatic mutations (the second hit), vascular endothelial cells in the tail region may experience a complete loss of ENG function, triggering severe local angiogenesis and remodeling defects. This scenario may increase the risk of abnormal development of the umbilical-iliac artery system and local perfusion insufficiency, thereby providing a mechanistic explanation for lower limb fusion and multiple organ malformations. Consequently, the ENG frameshift mutation identified in this study represents a notable avenue for research into vascular-related mechanisms.

LMBRD1

LMBD1 is a predicted lysosomal cobalamin transporter that plays a crucial role in the intracellular transport and metabolism of vitamin B12. Mutations in this gene can lead to cblF-type methylmalonic acidemia [63]. Although LMBRD1 is primarily associated with metabolic disorders, studies utilizing mouse models indicate that its homozygous deletion results in embryonic lethality, mesodermal structural defects, and the disruption of dorsal–ventral axis formation. During early embryogenesis, LMBRD1 is expressed in both the ectoderm and mesoderm. The absence of LMBRD1 disrupts the BMP4 and Nodal signaling pathways, thereby compromising dorsal–ventral patterning and gastrulation [64, 65]. Thus, sirenomelia, which represents an extreme phenotype of mesodermal defects, may be correlated with failed gastrulation due to LMBRD1 mutations.

CREBBP

CREBBP (CREB-binding protein) is a transcription coactivator with histone acetyltransferase activity that regulates the expression of multiple developmental pathways, including TGF-β, nuclear receptor pathways, Hedgehog, and WNT [66]. CREBBP modulates its nuclear localization and transcriptional activity through methylation, such as CARM1-mediated methylation at arginine residues 714 and 742, thereby exerting spatiotemporal specificity in somite formation and myogenesis [67–69]. Studies in zebrafish indicated that CREBBPA was highly expressed in the caudal region, whereas CREBBPB was enriched in the anterior region. The tail-end expression of CREBBPA suggests that its transcriptional regulation may depend on Notch, WNT/β-catenin, RA, or fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling pathways, all of which are known to regulate somite formation [70]. Thus, CREBBP likely modulates the transcriptional programs essential for normal somite and muscle development through the downstream mechanisms of these major signaling pathways. Sirenomelia is characterized by the dysregulation of multiple morphogenetic pathways. The loss of CREBBP function may result in the impairment of SMAD-mediated BMP signaling transcription, thereby exacerbating limb pattern defects [69]. Furthermore, mutations in CREBBP are known to cause craniofacial and limb malformations in Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome, which partially overlaps with the sirenomelia phenotype, suggesting a potential association [71].

Based on tthe comparison of structural variations comparison between the two samples, we concentrated on genes uniquely affected by SVs in the sirenomelia patient, having excluded identical segment variations at the same loci found in the normal newborn controls. Subsequently, we performed systematic annotation and functional prioritization assessments of the involved genes.

During the screening process, we prioritized structural variation events with clear potential functional consequences. These events included deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations that may alter gene dosage, disrupt gene structure, or interfere with key regulatory elements. We integrated quality metrics and annotation information at variant sites to minimize false positives resulting from complex genomic regions. Building on this foundation, we cross-validated genes associated with structural variations against enrichment results from the GO, KEGG, DO, and Reactome databases, alongside existing evidence from developmental biology. We focused on signaling networks closely linked to processes such as posterior axis/tail development, vasculogenesis, and urogenital system differentiation. By integrating the vascular hypothesis and the embryonic developmental defect hypothesis for sirenomelia, we ultimately identified seven key candidate structural variants and their corresponding genes (Fig. 7 and Table 6). These variants provide robust functional support and were strongly aligned with the underlying phenotypic mechanisms. TGFBR3 and ACVR1B

TGFBR3 (betaglycan) and ACVR1B (ALK4) are pivotal receptors within the TGF-β signaling pathway, playing essential roles in embryonic development, germ layer formation, and organogenesis. TGFBR3 functions as a transmembrane proteoglycan that enhances signaling by binding TGF-β ligands, such as TGF-β2, and facilitating their presentation to type II receptors, including TGFBR2 [72]. In zebrafish embryos, TGFBR3 promoter activity demonstrates an anterior–posterior gradient expression pattern within somites, preferentially labeling slow-twitch muscle precursor cells, thereby indicating its involvement in myogenesis and somite patterning [73]. Furthermore, TGFBR3 deficiency in mouse models results in cardiac and hepatic defects as well as embryonic lethality, thus highlighting its critical role in early developmental processes [74].

ACVR1B, a type I transmembrane serine/threonine kinase receptor, has been demonstrated to form heterodimeric complexes with type II activin receptors, thereby mediating activin signaling [75]. ACVR1B and its ligands are implicated in various functions during vertebrate development, including endoderm-mesoderm induction, neurulation, and axial and left–right orientation [76]. Previous research indicates that let-7 microRNA directly targets the 3´UTR of ACVR1B, suppressing its expression and consequently diminishing Nodal signaling responsiveness, which promotes the differentiation of blastocyst cells toward ectoderm and mesoderm fates [77]. Further investigations revealed that the FGF/ERK signaling pathway antagonized the capacity of Tar*/ACVR1B to induce endoderm formation at the animal pole [78]. Conditional mouse models have also demonstrated that ACVR1B knockout leads to embryonic lethality [76]. Furthermore, ACVR1B plays a crucial role in both ectoderm and extraembryonic cells, mediating signaling essential for ovary column formation and gastrulation [75].

In summary, the deficiency or inhibition of TGFBR3 or ACVR1B in animal models leads to disorders in germ layer development and can even result in lethality. Chromosomal translocation breakpoints at the TGFBR3 locus, along with structural deletion mutations in ACVR1B, may disrupt the spatial confinement of Nodal signaling by diminishing receptor expression. This reduction could lead to abnormal limb field signaling and fusion malformations, ultimately resulting in embryonic defects that resemble sirenomelia.

WLS and WNT7B

WNT7B, a member of the WNT family, functions as a secreted signaling molecule that regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and tissue patterning during embryonic development, playing a particularly crucial role in limb and skeletal development [79]. In mouse embryos, WNT7B is expressed in the limb ectoderm and exhibits functional redundancy with WNT7A during limb development; both molecules jointly regulate skeletal growth and patterning through GPR124 and RECK-mediated signaling pathways [80]. A deficiency in WNT7B leads to limb shortening and digital deformities [79]. Furthermore, WNT7B is highly expressed in hypertrophic chondrocytes, where it promotes endochondral ossification by activating the canonical WNT-β-catenin pathway, and its absence impairs bone formation [81]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) indicated that WNT7B, as a member of the WNT family, resides in a genomic region associated with bone density, suggesting that mutations in this gene may impact skeletal development [82].

WLS (Wntless), a transmembrane protein, serves as a dedicated chaperone for WNT ligands, facilitating the transport of lipid-modified WNT proteins from the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane and thereby mediating all WNT secretion [83, 84]. Structural studies suggest that WLS forms complexes with WNT8A or WNT3A, binding to the palmitoylated modification of WNT through a hydrophobic cavity to ensure appropriate transport [83]. Furthermore, WLS is essential for the secretion of multiple WNTs during limb development, with mutations in WLS leading to multiorgan developmental defects and limb malformations. In mouse models, the deletion of WLS specifically in limb mesenchyme results in distal limb growth arrest and skeletal dysplasia [85]. Additional studies in WLS knockout mice revealed cystic medullary hydronephrosis, a severe reduction or absence of the tail bud, a reduced number of caudal vertebrae, and syndactyly [84]. In humans, biallelic mutations in WLS result in Zaki syndrome, which is characterized by microcephaly, spina bifida, and limb abnormalities such as syndactyly [86]. WLS deficiency in cartilage leads to delayed chondrocyte differentiation, disorganized cellular arrangement, and impaired endochondral ossification. In contrast, WLS deficiency in bone tissue does not affect chondrocyte differentiation or proliferation but does result in reduced bone mass and shortened growth plate proliferation zones, potentially by regulating osteoclast-mediated cartilage and bone resorption processes [87]. GWAS data further confirmed the association between the WLS locus and osteoporosis, thus highlighting its importance in skeletal homeostasis [82].

WNT7B and WLS are essential for limb and skeletal development, primarily through their regulation of the WNT signaling pathway. Chromosomal structural deletion mutations can result in loss-of-function, which may lead to limb malformations. The structural deletion mutations identified in WNT7B and WLS in this study indicate that they may contribute to limb development abnormalities by disrupting WNT ligand secretion or downstream signaling, potentially connecting to the sirenomelia phenotype.

GLI2

The GLI2 gene encodes a family of zinc finger transcription factors that act as crucial mediators in the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway [88, 89], regulating limb patterning during embryonic development [90]. Truncating mutations in GLI2 are linked to various congenital anomalies, including pituitary malformations, polydactyly, and facial deformities [91, 92]. In vitro functional assays and studies using animal models demonstrated that GLI2 deficiency impaired SHH signaling. Pattern formation in the ventral hindbrain, skeletal muscle development, and the establishment of limb anterior–posterior polarity all rely on the redundant functions of GLI2 and GLI3. Both proteins are highly expressed during gastrulation and the formation of the neuroectoderm [93, 94]. Further studies indicate that establishing complete axial positional identity in limbs necessitates the integration of spatial distribution, temporal regulation, and dosage effects of GLI2 and GLI3 activators and inhibitors [95]. In animal models, limb malformations observed in GLI2 knockout mutants partially resemble the hindlimb fusion phenotype seen in sirenomelia [96]. Thus, sirenomelia is evidently a multi-factorial disorder that potentially involves interactions with other genes or environmental factors, such as prenatal alcohol exposure [97].

RARB

RARB is a pivotal member of the nuclear receptor superfamily. As a vital transcription factor within the RA signaling pathway, RARB regulates downstream gene expression by heterodimerizing with retinoic acid X receptor (RXR) to bind to retinoic acid response elements (RAREs). Consequently, RARB plays a central role in embryonic development, cell differentiation, and the maintenance of homeostasis [98]. Research has shown that de novo missense mutations in RARB lead to a syndrome known as microphthalmia with congenital anomalies of the eye, diaphragmatic hernia, and cardiac defects (MCOPS12), which is characterized by ocular developmental abnormalities, diaphragmatic hernia, cardiac defects, and dystonia [99]. Furthermore, the intronic variant c.157+1895 G > A, located within the noncoding region of RARB affects the regulation of transcription by altering the conserved intronic regulatory region CR1. This alteration leads to hyperproliferation and downstream gene dysregulation, ultimately resulting in ocular and systemic malformations [100]. During somite development, RARB plays a crucial role in establishing bilateral symmetry by regulating the RA gradient. Studies have shown that RERE forms complexes with P300, NR2F2, and retinoic acid receptors, which subsequently recruit to the RARE region of the RARB promoter to exert regulatory effects [101]. Further research indicated that RA signaling regulates body axis elongation by directly inhibiting the transcription of FGF8. The RA-dependent recruitment of RERE occurs at the RARB RARE region, while the RA-dependent release of RERE occurs at the FGF8 RARE region, thus suggesting that RERE influences somite formation by upregulating RARB and by directly regulating FGF8 [102]. Transcriptomic analysis of RALDH2 knockout mice confirmed that RA deficiency impacts multiple signaling pathways, with specific genes in the FGF, Notch, BMP, Hedgehog, VEGF, and WNT pathways being dysregulated [103]. Numerous studies on the genetics of sirenomelia in mice have revealed that the abnormal accumulation of RA leads to sirenomelia phenotypes by impairing BMP signaling activity, thus resulting in defects in tail mesoderm and limb bud development [21, 29].

Whole-genome sequencing results have enabled identification of structural deletion mutations in the RARB gene among patients with sirenomelia. As a core regulator of RA signaling, abnormalities in the structure or expression of RARB may disrupt somite formation and axial development, potentially linking these anomalies to the sirenomelia phenotype.

TWSG1

TWSG1 (Twisted gastrulation) is an evolutionarily conserved secreted glycoprotein encoded by the TWSG1 gene, which plays a crucial role in embryonic development and the regulation of cellular signaling across various organisms [104, 105]. The TWSG1 protein consists of two distinct domains: the N-terminal domain facilitates binding to BMP ligands, while the C-terminal domain interacts with members of the Chordin family, thereby modulating the BMP signaling pathway [106, 107]. The functionality of TWSG1 is contingent upon its glycosylation modifications; mutations at glycosylation sites significantly impair its binding capacity to BMP, thus leading to disruptions in signal regulation [108].

As a key extracellular regulator of the BMP signaling pathway, TWSG1 exhibits dual roles as it can function as a BMP antagonist and as an agonist, depending on the cellular environment and interacting molecules [105, 109–112]. As an antagonist, TWSG1 forms a complex with Chordin, thereby enhancing the capacity of Chordin to inhibit BMP by preventing BMP-receptor binding and subsequently suppressing BMP signaling [105, 106, 109]. Conversely, under specific conditions, TWSG1 promotes the cleavage of Chordin, releasing the BMP ligand and amplifying BMP signaling, thus exhibiting agonist activity [110, 111]. Studies have revealed that the overexpression of TWSG1 suppresses BMP-induced SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation, while TWSG1 deficiency enhances BMP signaling, leading to abnormal cellular differentiation [112–114].

During embryonic development, TWSG1 plays a critical role in dorsal–ventral patterning by regulating BMP gradients. The loss of TWSG1 expression is associated with various malformations, including craniofacial defects and skeletal abnormalities [115, 116]. Research on sirenomelia has shown that the dual deletion of TWSG1 and BMP7 in mouse and African clawed frog models led to a deficiency in the posterior ventral mesoderm. This deficiency resulted in limb bud fusion along the posterior midline, which manifested as the sirenomelia phenotype. These findings suggest that TWSG1 deficiency disrupts BMP signaling, ultimately leading to abnormal posterior mesoderm patterning [33]. Furthermore, mutations in TWSG1 increase susceptibility to the teratogenic effects of retinoic acid exposure [115].

The chromosomal structural variation sites identified in the sirenomelia patient herein encompass RARB and TWSG1, further supporting their roles as key mutation genes associated with sirenomelia malformations. Through the combination of sequencing and enrichment analysis, TWSG1, a crucial regulator of BMP signaling, demonstrates increased genetic susceptibility via haploinsufficiency or complete loss, interacting with environmental factors (e.g., retinoic acid) to induce severe malformations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study included only one sirenomelia patient and one healthy newborn control. Although sirenomelia is an extremely rare and frequently lethal congenital malformation, the use of a single control represents a major limitation and restricts the ability to distinguish disease related variants from individual background variation. Therefore, the variants identified in this study should be regarded as prioritized candidate variants, potential susceptibility loci, or hotspot regions that require further validation in independent cases and larger control cohorts. Second, due to the lack of parental samples, we were unable to determine the inheritance pattern of the candidate variants or assess de novo occurrence and segregation within the family. This limitation prevents a more definitive interpretation of the genetic origin and pathogenic relevance of the identified variants. Third, functional validation was not performed in the present study. In addition, Sanger sequencing validation could not be carried out because the available DNA sample from this rare neonatal case had been exhausted after whole-genome sequencing and related analyses, and no additional biological material was available.

Although this study is limited by the lack of functional validation and family-based genetic data, such constraints are common in studies of ultra-rare congenital disorders. Importantly, the use of whole-genome sequencing enabled the comprehensive detection of coding and noncoding variants, as well as structural variations, thus providing a more complete genetic landscape compared to exome-based approaches. Furthermore, the consistency between variant-level findings, pathway enrichment results, and known developmental biology markedly strengthened the reliability of our conclusions and supports the biological plausibility of the candidate genes and pathways identified in this study. Future studies should include larger numbers of sirenomelia cases, ethnically matched control samples, parental samples, Sanger sequencing or breakpoint PCR validation, and functional assays to confirm the pathogenic relevance of these prioritized candidate variants and to clarify their roles in posterior axis development, vasculogenesis, and organogenesis.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically elucidated the potential molecular mechanisms underlying sirenomelia by employing whole-genome sequencing data in conjunction with multidimensional enrichment analyses. SNP/InDel and structural variant analyses consistently converged on core developmental pathways, including BMP, WNT, and TGF-β signaling. These pathways are critically involved in posterior axis development, and their disruption may underlie a unified pathogenic cascade linking renal defects, cloacal malformations, and limb fusion.

Furthermore, by integrating whole-genome sequencing results with two major pathogenic hypotheses for sirenomelia, this study prioritized a set of candidate genetic events with high biological plausibility and literature support. Genes harboring SNP/InDel variants include: BMP5, BMP6, ENG, CREBBP, and LMBRD1. Genes affected by structural variants (SVs) include: WLS, TGFBR3, TWSG1, ACVR1B, GLI2, WNT7B, and RARB.
