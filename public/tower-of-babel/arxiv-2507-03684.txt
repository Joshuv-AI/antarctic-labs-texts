Bilinear quadratic output systems and balanced truncation Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Heike Faßbender, Serkan Gugercin, Till Peters

Abstract
Dynamical systems with quadratic outputs have recently attracted significant attention. In this paper, we consider bilinear dynamical systems, a special class of weakly nonlinear systems, with a quadratic output. We develop various primal-dual formulations for these systems and define the corresponding system Gramians. Conditions for the existence and uniqueness of these Gramians are established, and the generalized Lyapunov equations they satisfy are derived. Using these Gramians and their truncated versions, which are computationally more efficient, we construct a balanced truncation framework for bilinear systems with quadratic outputs. The proposed approach is demonstrated through two numerical examples.



keywords

Model order reduction, balanced truncation, bilinear systems, linear quadratic output systems,
Gramians, generalized Lyapunov equations

Introduction

We investigate the so-called bilinear quadratic output (BQO) dynamical systems described in the state-space form as

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}x(t)u_{k}(t)+Bu(t),\qquad x(0)=0,

(1a)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=Cx(t)+\begin{bmatrix}x(t)^{T}M_{1}x(t)\\
\vdots\\
x(t)^{T}M_{p}x(t)\end{bmatrix},

(1b)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n},N_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
,
M_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
for
j=1,\dots,p
, and
t\in T:=[0,\infty)
. In (
1
),
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
describes the state,
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the inputs, and
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the outputs of the system. It can be assumed without loss of generality that
M_{j}
is symmetric since
x(t)^{T}M_{j}x(t)=x(t)^{T}M_{j}^{T}x(t)=\frac{1}{2}x(t)^{T}(M_{j}+M_{j}^{T})x(t)
.

The BQO systems represent a generalization of bilinear systems (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
42
]
) as well as of linear quadratic output (LQO) systems (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
). More specifically,
in
eq.

1b
, setting
M_{1}=\dots=M_{p}=0
leads to the output
y(t)=Cx(t)
, recovering the bilinear dynamical systems. On the other hand,
setting
N_{1}=\dots=N_{m}=0
in
eq.

1a
leads to the state equation
\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)
,
recovering the linear quadratic output (LQO) systems.

Bilinear state equations naturally appear in various applications or a result of Carleman bilinearization of nonlinear dynamics, see, e.g.,
[
33
,
25
,
21
]
. In recent years, dynamical systems with quadratic outputs as in
eq.

1b
have gained significant attention as they appear in the modeling of the variance of a quantity of interest of a stochastic model or in measuring the energy of state; see, e.g.,
[
30
,
29
,
41
,
32
,
38
]

and the references therein.
Moreover, in the context of port-Hamiltonian dynamical systems, bilinear port-Hamiltonian dynamical systems
naturally appear with a state-space form

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=(J-R)Qx(t)+\left(B+Nx(t)\right)u(t),

(2a)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=\left(B+Nx(t)\right)^{T}Qx(t),

(2b)

where
J,R,Q,N\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
with
J=-J^{T},R=R^{T}\geq 0,Q=Q^{T}\geq 0
, see
[
24
,
39
]
. (For simplicity, here we only considered a single-input single-output bilinear port-Hamiltonian system.) Note that
eq.

2
is a special case of
eq.

1
.

In most cases, such as those where the dynamical system arises from the spatial discretization of an underlying PDE, the dimension of the resulting BQO system is significantly large, and the task of model order reduction arises.
Thus we seek a BQO system of similar structure but with a significantly smaller dimension which should accurately describe the same input-output behavior. More precisely, we look for the reduced BQO system
of order
r\ll n
described as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}\hat{x}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\hat{N}_{k}\hat{x}(t)u_{k}(t)+\hat{B}u(t),\qquad\hat{x}(0)=0,

(3a)

\displaystyle\hat{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{C}\hat{x}(t)+\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}(t)^{T}\hat{M}_{1}\hat{x}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\hat{x}(t)^{T}\hat{M}_{p}\hat{x}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(3b)

where
\hat{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\hat{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m},\hat{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
,
\hat{N}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
for
k=1,\dots,m
,
\hat{M}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
for
j=1,\dots,p
,
t\in T
. In
eq.

3
,
\hat{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
describes the reduced state and
\hat{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the reduced output of the system. The goal is to construct
eq.

3
such that
the reduced output
\hat{y}(t)
provides a high-fidelity approximation to the original output
y(t)
.

In this manuscript, we employ
balanced truncation
(BT), a model order reduction method that is well established for linear systems (originally in
[
26
,
27
]
, see also
[
3
,
6
,
13
,
20
]
), or
[
9
, Section 7 and 8]
). BT transforms the original system into a state-space representation where the reachability and observability Gramians are equal and diagonal. The diagonal elements are called the Hankel singular values. The states corresponding to the smallest Hankel singular values are then removed because they have the least influence on the system’s behavior. In general, this results in a reduced model that provides a good approximation of the original system with a lower dimension. Theoretical foundation of balancing and balanced truncation for nonlinear systems has been introduced in
[
35
]
which requires solving Hamilton-Jacobi partial differential equations.
This task is computationally challenging for large-scale systems and the reduced system, in general, will not inherit the original structure due to the state-dependent model reduction bases. For example, in our setting, this means that the reduced system will not retain the structure
eq.

3
. As an alternative, one seeks algebraic Gramians, which are generalizations of the linear case and solve generalized Lyapunov equations. One of the main contributions of this work will be the development of proper and relevant algebraic Gramians for BQO systems.

BQO systems and their model order reduction have been briefly discussed in the master’s thesis
[
28
, pp. 25–42]
. In particular,
[
28
, Section 3.1]
highlights a transformation of BQO systems into bilinear systems with linear output via lifting transformations. While this approach enables the application of existing bilinear system theory and reduction techniques, it comes at the significant cost of increasing the system matrix size from
n\times n
to
(n+n^{2})\times(n+n^{2})
- an increase in the dimension that is clearly impractical for large-scale systems. Therefore, instead,
[
28
]
avoids this lifting technique and aims to develop reduction tools directly on the original BQO structure. To achieve this goal in the BT setting,
one needs the Gramians. In
[
28
]
, the reachability Gramian of (
1
) is taken to be the same as in the bilinear case,
that is, (
1
) with
M_{1}=\dots=M_{p}=0
. Similarly, the construction of the observability Gramian of (
1
) is motivated by the observability Gramian for the bilinear case.
The analysis in
[
28
]
is restricted to single-input single-output (SISO) configurations (
m=p=1
).

In contrast, our work addresses the more general case of multi-input multi-output (MIMO) BQO systems and focuses on developing the reachability and observability Gramians following the approach for quadratic bilinear systems from
[
10
]
. In quadratic bilinear systems, (
1a
) has an additional quadratic summand
H(x(t)\otimes x(t)),
while in (
1b
) all matrices
M_{j},j=1,\dots,p
are zero. Therefore, with
H=0
, the derivation of the reachability Gramian given in
[
10
]
can be adopted here.

In
[
10
, Section 2.2]
, it is suggested to define the observability Gramian for quadratic bilinear systems as the reachability Gramian of
the dual system
, analogous to what is known for linear and bilinear systems. We will follow this approach in our derivations. As we will show in
Section

3.2
,
there are multiple ways to write the BQO system (
1
) as a primal system.
This allows us to introduce multiple variants of the observability Gramian, each satisfying a different (generalized) Lyapunov equation.
Interestingly, although the observability Gramian in
[
28
]
is derived using a different approach than the one considered here,
it satisfies the same Lyapunov equation as one of the observability Gramians derived here. As we will show, since this equation has a unique symmetric positive semidefinite solution under certain conditions, the two Gramians must be identical.

The structure of the paper is as follows: Section
2
introduces preliminaries. In Section
3
, we derive the reachability and observability Gramians for BQO systems. Section
3.1
summarizes the derivation of the reachability Gramian for quadratic bilinear systems from
[
10
]
adapted to the case of BQO systems. In Section
3.2
, the primal and the dual systems for the BQO system (
1
) are discussed, showing that multiple
ways exist to write the primal system, which leads to different dual systems. The corresponding observability Gramians are derived in Sections
3.2.1
,
3.2.2
, and
3.2.3
.
Section
4
provides the theoretical foundations, including existence and uniqueness results for the Gramians and their Lyapunov equations. In Section
5
, we discuss Gramian truncation for code acceleration and analyze the resulting Lyapunov equations. Section
6
applies balanced truncation to BQO systems, comparing the interpretability of different observability Gramians and introducing a tailored algorithm. Finally, Section
7
presents numerical results using various Gramian combinations.

3
Reachability and Observability Gramians

We seek to apply model reduction to BQO systems using balanced truncation, that is, by balancing
the system and then truncating the unimportant states afterward. This will require properly defining the Gramians. Toward this goal, we will define and use generalized Gramians.
Because BQO systems (
1
) are a generalization of bilinear systems and of linear quadratic output systems, the Gramians derived here
will cover the Gramians of those systems as a special case.

3.1
Reachability Gramian

We begin by deriving the reachability Gramian
P
of the BQO system
eq.

1
. This has already been discussed in a more general setting in
[
10
, Section 2.1]
in the context of quadratic bilinear systems (see, also,
[
2
, Section II]
). For quadratic bilinear systems, the state equation (

) has an additional term in the form of

. In the following, we give the equations and definitions from
[
10
]
relevant for our further discussion for the case

The concept of Volterra series expansion plays the fundamental role in deriving the algebraic reachability Gramian for nonlinear systems. To simplify the notation appearing in these expansions (see below), we define
u^{(t_{1},\dots,t_{l})}_{k}(t):=u_{k}(t-t_{1}-\dots-t_{l})

As shown in

]

1a

\displaystyle x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{At_{1}}Bu^{(t_{1})}(t)dt_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{t}e^{At_{1}}N_{k}x^{(t_{1})}(t)u^{(t_{1})}_{k}(t)dt_{1},

(5)

which can be rewritten as

\small x_{i}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t-t_{i}}\dots\int_{0}^{t-t_{i}-\dots-t_{2}}\bar{P}_{i}(t_{1},\dots,t_{i})\left(u^{(t_{i})}(t)\otimes\dots\otimes u^{(t_{i},\dots,t_{1})}(t)\right)dt_{1}\dots dt_{i},

where, using

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{1}(t_{1})

\displaystyle=e^{At_{1}}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},

(7a)

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=e^{At_{2}}\mathcal{N}(I_{m}\otimes\bar{P}_{1}(t_{1}))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m^{2}},

(7b)

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{i}(t_{1},\dots,t_{i})

\displaystyle=e^{At_{i}}\mathcal{N}(I_{m}\otimes\bar{P}_{i-1}(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1}))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m^{i}}.

(7c)

Now, we are ready to define the reachability Gramian

(Reachability Gramian

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

To conclude, we adapt

3.2
Observability Gramian

Next we derive the observability Gramian of a BQO system as the reachability Gramian of the dual system using an approach similar to the one used in
[
10
]
for bilinear quadratic dynamical systems. For the
primal system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(x,u,t)x(t)+\mathcal{B}(x,u,t)u(t),\quad x(0)=0,

(13a)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(x,u,t)x(t)+\mathcal{D}(x,u,t)u(t),

(13b)

the associated
dual system
is given by

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(x,u,t)x(t)+\mathcal{B}(x,u,t)u(t),\quad x(0)=0,

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{A}^{T}(x,u,t)z(t)-\mathcal{C}^{T}(x,u,t)\hat{u}(t),\quad z(\infty)=0,

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}^{T}(x,u,t)z(t)+\mathcal{D}^{T}(x,u,t)\hat{u}(t),

where
z(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
describes the dual state,
\hat{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the dual input and
\hat{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the dual output; see, e.g.,
[
17
]
.

We have
(at least) two ways to write the BQO system (
1
) as a primal system of the form (
13
).
The somewhat straightforward choice is to consider the bilinear part of the state equation (
1a
) as part of
\mathcal{A}(x,u,t)
, in other words, we choose

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=A+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}u_{k}(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x,u,t)=B,

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=C+\begin{bmatrix}x(t)^{T}M_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x(t)^{T}M_{p}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x,u,t)=0.

A distinctly different possibility is to consider the bilinear part as part of
\mathcal{B}(x,u,t)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=B+\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}x(t)&\dots&N_{m}x(t)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x,u,t)=A,

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=C+\begin{bmatrix}x(t)^{T}M_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x(t)^{T}M_{p}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x,u,t)=0.

One could also use a mixture of (
15
) and (
16
) and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=A+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\phi_{k}N_{k}u_{k}(t),

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=B+\begin{bmatrix}(1-\phi_{1})N_{1}x(t)&\dots&(1-\phi_{m})N_{m}x(t)\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x,u,t)

\displaystyle=C+\begin{bmatrix}x(t)^{T}M_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x(t)^{T}M_{p}\end{bmatrix},\text{~~~and~~~}\mathcal{D}(x,u,t)=0

for given constants
\phi_{k},k=1,\dots,m
(or even matrices
\phi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
). While choosing
\phi_{k}=1
yields the primal system
eq.

15
, choosing
\phi_{k}=0
yields
eq.

16
.

These variants of the primal system lead to different dual systems, and thus to different observability Gramians. This is explained in more detail in

and
3.2.2
for the choices (

) and (

\displaystyle\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(t_{i}+t_{i-1}+\dots+t_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{S}}^{i-1}\left((i-1)\left\lVert BB^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert\right).

This yields

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q^{S}\right\rVert\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bar{Q}^{S}_{i}\left(\bar{Q}_{i}^{S}\right)^{T}\right\rVert dt_{1}\dots dt_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(t_{i}+t_{i-1}+\dots+t_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{S}}^{i-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot\left((i-1)\left\lVert BB^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert\right)dt_{1}\dots dt_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma_{Q^{S}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}\Gamma_{Q^{S}}}{2\alpha}\right)^{i}\left((i-1)\left\lVert BB^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert\right).

Since

\beta^{2}\Gamma_{Q^{S}}/(2\alpha)<1
(or, equivalently
\Gamma_{Q^{S}}<2\alpha/\beta^{2}
) by the assumption,
this term is finite due to the convergence of the geometric series, completing the proof for the existence of
Q^{s}
. The statement (c) for the existence of
Q^{A}
follows analogously to the proof for
Q^{S}
.

For the proof of the existence of
Q^{P}
, we first recall the definition of the bilinear observability Gramian from
eq.

27
and the expressions

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{1}^{B}=e^{A^{T}t_{1}}C^{T},\quad\bar{Q}_{i}^{B}=e^{A^{T}t_{i}}\mathcal{N}_{T}(I_{m}\otimes\bar{Q}_{i-1}^{B}).

Analogously to before, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{1}^{B}\left(\bar{Q}_{1}^{B}\right)^{T}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}e^{-2\alpha t_{1}}\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert,~\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{i}^{B}\left(\bar{Q}_{i}^{B}\right)^{T}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(t_{i}+\dots+t_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{i-1}\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert,

which lead to

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q^{B}\right\rVert\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bar{Q}^{B}_{i}\left(\bar{Q}_{i}^{B}\right)^{T}\right\rVert dt_{1}\dots dt_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(t_{i}+t_{i-1}+\dots+t_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{i-1}\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert dt_{1}\dots dt_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma_{Q^{P}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}}{2\alpha}\right)^{i}\left\lVert C^{T}C\right\rVert.

Hence,
Q^{B}
exists under the assumption on
\Gamma_{Q^{P}}
. Next, recall

28

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{1,j}=e^{A^{T}s_{1}}\mathcal{M}(I_{p}\otimes\bar{P}_{j}),\quad\bar{Q}_{i,j}=e^{A^{T}s_{i}}\mathcal{N}_{T}(I_{m}\otimes\bar{Q}_{i-1,j}).

Now, we estimate the following terms as before:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{1,j}\left(\bar{Q}_{1,j}\right)^{T}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}e^{-2\alpha s_{1}}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}\left\lVert\bar{P}_{j}\bar{P}_{j}^{T}\right\rVert,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{i,j}\left(\bar{Q}_{i,j}\right)^{T}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(s_{i}+\dots+s_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{i}\left\lVert\bar{P}_{j}\bar{P}_{j}^{T}\right\rVert.

Then, using
eq.

36
for
i,j=1,2\dots

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{i,j}\left(\bar{Q}_{i,j}\right)^{T}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(s_{i}+\dots+s_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{i}\beta^{2j}e^{-2\alpha(t_{j}+\dots+t_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{j-1}\left\lVert BB^{T}\right\rVert.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}Q_{i,j}\right\rVert\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{i,j}\left(\bar{Q}_{i,j}\right)^{T}\right\rVert ds_{1}\dots ds_{i}dt_{1}\dots dt_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta^{2i}e^{-2\alpha(s_{i}+\dots+s_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{i}ds_{1}\dots ds_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta^{2j}e^{-2\alpha(t_{j}+\dots+t_{1})}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}^{j-1}\left\lVert BB^{T}\right\rVert dt_{1}\dots dt_{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}\Gamma_{Q_{P}}}{2\alpha}\right)^{i}\cdot\frac{1}{\Gamma_{Q^{P}}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}\Gamma_{Q^{P}}}{2\alpha}\right)^{j}\left\lVert BB^{T}\right\rVert.

As a preparatory step, we recall
the following theorem from

,

on the uniqueness of the solutions to the generalized Lyapunov equations. See also
[

Theorem 15
.

[
16
, Theorem 4.1]
[
7
, Theorem 3.9]

Consider
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and linear operators
\mathcal{L}_{A},\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
so that
\mathcal{L}_{A}(X):=AX+XA^{T}
and
\Pi
is nonnegative, i.e.,
\Pi(X)\geq 0
if
X\geq 0
. The following are equivalent:

1.

For every
Y>0
, there exists an
X>0
such that
\mathcal{L}_{A}(X)+\Pi(X)=-Y
.

2.

There exist
Y>0
and
X>0
such that
\mathcal{L}_{A}(X)+\Pi(X)=-Y
.

3.

There exist
Y\geq 0
such that
(A,Y)
is reachable and
X>0
such that
\mathcal{L}_{A}(X)+\Pi(X)=-Y
.

4.

\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{A}+\Pi)\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
.

5.

\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{A})\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
and
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi)<1
.

The work
[
7
]
also states that the stability of
A
, the reachability of
(A,B)
, and some sufficiently small
N_{k}
’s guarantee a positive definite solution of the associated Lyapunov equation.

With this result, we can state and prove a theorem concerning the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the Lyapunov equations (
10
) for
P
and (
23
) for
Q^{S}
.

Theorem 16
.

Given is the BQO system
eq.

1
with stable
A
as in
Theorem

13
.

(a)

If
\Gamma_{P}<2\alpha/\beta^{2}
, then
there exists a unique symmetric positive semidefinite solution
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of
eq.

10
, i.e.,
AP+PA^{T}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}PN_{k}^{T}+BB^{T}=0
.
Furthermore, if
(A,B)
is reachable, then
P
is positive definite.

(b)

If
\Gamma_{Q^{S}}<2\alpha/\beta^{2}
, then there exists a unique symmetric positive semidefinite solution

of
eq.

23
, i.e.,
A^{T}Q^{S}+Q^{S}A+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}^{T}Q^{S}N_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{j}PM_{j}+C^{T}C=0
.
Furthermore, if
(A^{T},C^{T})
is reachable (equivalently if
(A,C)
is observable), then

is positive definite.

Proof.

We start with proving the statements for
P.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{A}(X)=AX+XA^{T}
.
The fact
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{A})\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
follows directly from the stability of
A
.
The choice
\Pi(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}XN_{k}^{T}

guarantees
\Pi(X)\geq 0
for
X\geq 0
.
The equation
\mathcal{L}(X)+\Pi(X)=0
can be rewritten as a fixed-point equation
X=-\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}(\Pi(X))
, which has a unique solution
X
if
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi)<1
. Since
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi)\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi\right\rVert
, it is sufficient to show that
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi\right\rVert<1
holds under the assumptions. Now, for
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}(\Pi(X))=Y
, we have

Y=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{At}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}XN_{k}^{T}\right)e^{A^{T}t}dt.

Using the techniques from the proof of
Theorem

13
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert Y\right\rVert\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert e^{At}\right\rVert\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}XN_{k}^{T}\right\rVert\left\lVert e^{A^{T}t}\right\rVert dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\alpha}\left\lVert\mathcal{N}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert X\right\rVert.

Hence, it holds that
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi\right\rVert\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\alpha}\left\lVert\mathcal{N}\right\rVert^{2}
. Due to the assumption on
\Gamma_{P}
, this term is smaller than
1
and thus
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi)<1
holds.
Using the facts
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{A})\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
and
\rho(\mathcal{L}_{A}^{-1}\Pi)<1
and Theorem
15
, it follows that
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{A}+\Pi)\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
. Hence, the equation
(\mathcal{L}_{A}+\Pi)(P)=-BB^{T}
has a unique solution which can be computed by a fixed point iteration
eq.

12
.

The solution
P
is symmetric since
0=(\mathcal{L}_{A}(P)+\Pi(P)+BB^{T})^{T}=\mathcal{L}_{A}(P^{T})+\Pi(P^{T})+BB^{T}
.
It is well known that, see, e.g.,
[
23
]
, assuming
A
is stable, the solution
P
of
AP+PA^{T}+F=0
is symmetric positive semidefinite
if
F\geq 0
.
With this result in mind, we investigate the recursive definition of
P
in
eq.

11
. We see directly that
P_{1}\geq 0

if
BB^{T}\geq 0
.
Hence, we can use a factorization
P_{1}=L_{1}L_{1}^{T}
in the equation for
P_{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=AP_{2}+P_{2}A^{T}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}P_{1}N_{k}^{T}

\displaystyle=AP_{2}+P_{2}A^{T}+\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}L_{1}&\cdots&N_{m}L_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}L_{1}&\cdots&N_{m}L_{1}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

Thus,
P_{2}
is symmetric positive semidefinite if

is symmetric positive semidefinite and, by induction, so are
P_{i}
and
P=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P_{i}
.
Thus, if
BB^{T}
is positive semidefinite, then
P_{i}
and

are positive semidefinite. If now additionally
(A,B)
is reachable,

is even positive definite due to
theorem

15
.
The results for

follow analogously with
\mathcal{L}_{A}=A^{T}X+XA
and

As we showed in
Section

and

solve the same generalized Lyapunov equation

Corollary 17

Let

and
Q^{P}
exist under the conditions of

Approximation and truncation of Gramians

The computation of the reachability Gramian
P
and the observability Gramian

(or

\displaystyle A^{T}Q^{S}+Q^{S}A+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}^{T}Q^{S}N_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{j}PM_{j}+C^{T}C

(40)

(41)

where

is the solution of

.
These results also follow easily from the proof of

3
. Since

such that

6
Balanced truncation of BQO systems

Now that we have defined the Gramians for BQO systems, we can apply
balanced truncation in a similar way as in the linear case. In this process, the original system is transformed such that the reachability and the observability Gramians are diagonal and equal.
By keeping only the most important states (i.e., easiest to reach and easiest to observe), we obtain a reduced-order model. Theory for linear balancing and balanced truncation can be found, e.g., in
[
3
,
6
,
13
,
20
]
, or
[
9
, Section 7 and 8]
.

To do this, we first need a suitable definition of (un)reachable/(un)observable states.
The energy functionals for nonlinear systems are no longer quadratic as in the linear case (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Section 2]
). The reachability functional for a nonlinear system
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t),\,y(t)=h(x(t))
is defined as

\displaystyle L_{c}(x_{0})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in L^{2}(-\infty,0],\\
x(-\infty)=0,\,x(0)=x_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert^{2}dt

(43)

with
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the observability functional is defined as

\displaystyle L_{o}(x_{0})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in L^{2}[0,\infty)],\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\alpha\\
x(0)=x_{0},\,x(\infty)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert y(t)\right\rVert^{2}dt

(44)

with a fixed parameter
\alpha\geq 0
; see, e.g.,
[
19
, Definition 2.1]
. We call states
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
L_{c}(x_{0})=\infty
unreachable and with
L_{o}(x_{0})=0
unobservable, respectively. The choice of
\alpha
in
eq.

44
allows for different approaches to study observability of BQO systems. For
\alpha>0
, we investigate the so-called
homogeneous BQO system
corresponding to
eq.

1
, namely

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}x(t)u_{k}(t),\qquad x(0)=0,

(45a)

\displaystyle y(t)

(a)

Consider the BQO system

and let

.

1
with zero input

and let
P\geq 0
and
Q^{S}\geq 0
exist and be solutions to
eqs.

10
and

. If
P>0
and
x_{0}\in\ker(Q^{S})
, then
x_{0}
is unobservable regarding the observability functional in
eq.

44
with
\alpha=0
.

(d)

Consider the BQO system
eq.

1
with zero input
u\equiv 0
and let
P\geq 0
and

exist and be solutions to

10
and

P>0
and

Proof.

(a)

This fact has already been proven for bilinear systems in
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
and can be applied here directly.

(b)

The proof of this statement follows the one of

[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Let
x_{0}\in\ker(Q^{S})
. Multiplying
eq.

23
from both sides by
x_{0}^{T}
and
x_{0}
, respectively, yields

0=x_{0}^{T}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}^{T}Q^{S}N_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{j}PM_{j}+C^{T}C\right)x_{0}.

Hence,
x_{0}^{T}C^{T}Cx_{0}=0
, which implies
\ker(Q^{S})\subseteq\ker(C)
.
Similarly, we see that
N_{k}\ker(Q^{S})\subseteq\ker(Q^{S})
and
M_{j}\ker(Q^{S})\subseteq\ker(P)
holds. Moreover, multiplying
eq.

23
only from the right side with
x_{0}
yields
A\ker(Q^{S})\subseteq\ker(Q^{S})
.
It follows for the homogeneous system
eq.

45
that if
x(t)\in\ker(Q^{S})
, then

Q^{S}\dot{x}(t)=Q^{S}Ax(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}Q^{S}N_{k}x(t)u_{k}(t)=0,

i.e.,
\dot{x}(t)\in\ker(Q^{S})
. Thus,
\ker(Q^{S})
is invariant under the dynamics, i.e.,
x_{0}\in\ker(Q^{S})\Rightarrow x(t)\in\ker(Q^{S})
. With the assumption
P>0
and
M_{j}\ker(Q^{S})\subseteq\ker(P)
,
M_{j}\ker(Q^{S})=\{0\}
holds. Due to this fact and the fact
\ker(Q^{S})\subseteq\ker(C)
, it follows that
y(t)=0
for
x(t)\in\ker(Q^{S})
.

(c)

This statement follows analogously to the proof of (b) above using
Bu\equiv 0
.

(d)

Let
x_{0}\in\ker(Q^{A})
. Multiplying
eq.

34
from both sides by
x_{0}^{T}
and
x_{0}
, respectively, yields

0=x_{0}^{T}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{j}PM_{j}+C^{T}C\right)x_{0}.

As in the proof of (b) above we have
\ker(Q^{A})\subseteq\ker(C),M_{j}\ker(Q^{A})\subseteq\ker(P)
and
A\ker(Q^{A})\subseteq\ker(Q^{A})
, but in general
N_{k}\ker(Q^{A})\not\subseteq\ker(Q^{A})
. Hence, the importance of

becomes apparent because now

holds for

.
The rest of the proof follows as before.

In contrast to
[
28
]
, Theorem
18
is formulated and proved here for the more general MIMO case.
In addition, we emphasize the subtle ambiguity concerning the observability functional, and we provide an extended statement regarding
Q^{A}
as well as the homogeneous case. Furthermore, we note that a formulation as for
Q^{S}
in (b) of

is not possible for

. This can be seen from the proof of (d) of
Theorem

18
where in general
N_{k}\ker(Q^{A})\not\subseteq\ker(Q^{A})
. If we now use the homogeneous system
eq.

45
with a nonzero input
u
, we only obtain

\displaystyle Q^{A}\dot{x}(t)=Q^{A}Ax(t)+\sum_{k=1}Q^{A}N_{k}x(t)u_{k}(t)=\sum_{k=1}Q^{A}N_{k}x(t)u_{k}(t)\neq 0

for
x(t)\in\ker(Q^{A})
using
A\ker(Q^{A})\subseteq\ker(Q^{A})
.

The result of this theorem can be interpreted as follows. The observability Gramian
Q^{A}
does not yield a statement regarding the observability functional in
eq.

44
when
\alpha>0
. So, it is not as versatile as
Q^{S}
which is also applicable to the observability functional in
eq.

44
when
\alpha>0
. Hence,
Q^{A}
does not contain as rich of an information as
Q^{S}
for the balancing steps, which can also be observed numerically in
Section

7
.
For similar analysis for linear systems with quadratic outputs and for bilinear systems with linear outputs, we refer the reader to, respectively,
[
11
]
and
[
8
]
.

Using the result of
Theorem

18
, we observe that the states that lie in
\ker(P)
or
\ker(Q)
have no significant influence on the system dynamics. This insight allows us to transfer the balancing approach from linear systems to BQO systems.
In particular, theoretically we diagonalize the Gramians in a similar way and then truncate them to eliminate the less important states.

However, in practice, one does not perform full balancing (simultaneous diagonalization) followed by truncation since this requires performing ill-conditioned operations. Instead, one constructs the reduced BT model directly without full balancing. We follow the same approach here. To begin, we compute the so-called square-root factors of the Gramians as
P=UU^{T}
and
Q=LL^{T}
, using, for example, Cholesky decompositions or suitable low-rank approximations. In practice, one directly computes
U
and
L
without ever forming
P
and
Q
. Based on these square-root factor, one performs balanced truncation as sketched in
Algorithm

5
.

Algorithm 5

BT_BQO
(P,Q)
, Balanced Truncation for BQO systems with Gramians
P
and
Q

1:

A,B,C,N_{k},M_{j}
as in (
1
),
P=UU^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
Q=LL^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a reduced order
r\leq n

2:

\hat{A},\hat{B},\hat{C},\hat{N}_{k},\hat{M}_{j}
as in (
3
)

3:

Compute the SVD

\displaystyle U^{T}L=Z\Sigma Y^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&\\
&\Sigma_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}^{T}\\
Y_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}

with
Z_{1},Y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
\Sigma_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
.

4:

Construct
W^{T}=\Sigma_{1}^{-1/2}Y_{1}^{T}L^{T}
and
V=UZ_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1/2}
.

5:

Compute
\hat{A}=W^{T}AV,\hat{B}=W^{T}B,\hat{C}=CV,\hat{N}_{k}=W^{T}N_{k}V
and
\hat{M}_{j}=V^{T}M_{j}V
.

Remark 19
.

Algorithm

5
uses the SVD of
U^{T}L
with the square-root factors of the Gramians,
P=UU^{T}
and
Q=LL^{T}
. We now compare
Q^{S}
in
eq.

21
with
Q^{A}
in
eq.

33
and their Lyapunov equations
eq.

23
and
eq.

34
to observe that
Q^{S}\geq Q^{A}
in the sense that
Q^{S}-Q^{A}\geq 0
, i.e.,
Q^{S}-Q^{A}
is symmetric positive semidefinite. Hence, starting from factorizations
Q^{S}=L_{S}L_{S}^{T}
and
Q^{A}=L_{A}L_{A}^{T}
, we have
Q^{S}=Q^{A}+\tilde{L}\tilde{L}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{A}\tilde{L}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}L^{T}_{A}\\
\tilde{L}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(U^{T}L_{A})\leq\sigma_{i}\left(\begin{bmatrix}U^{T}L_{A}&U^{T}\tilde{L}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\sigma_{i}(U^{T}L_{S}),

for all the Hankel singular values (HSVs)
\sigma_{i}(U^{T}L_{A})
(the diagonal entries of
\Sigma
in
Algorithm

5
). Hence, we obtain smaller Hankel singular values if we use the observability Gramian
Q^{A}
instead of
Q^{S}
. An analogous result can be shown for the truncated Gramians, where
Q^{S}_{T}\geq Q^{A}_{T}
and
Q^{P}_{T}\geq Q^{A}_{T}
.

As pointed out in

rescaling the input variable

In

by one with scaled input

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\gamma N_{k})x(t)\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}u_{k}(t)\right)+(\gamma B)\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}u(t)\right)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{N}_{k}x(t)\tilde{u}_{k}(t)+\tilde{B}\tilde{u}(t).

\displaystyle A^{T}\tilde{Q}^{S}+\tilde{Q}^{S}A+\gamma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}N_{k}^{T}\tilde{Q}^{S}N_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{j}\tilde{P}M_{j}+C^{T}C=0,

\displaystyle A^{T}\tilde{Q}^{A}+\tilde{Q}^{A}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}M_{j}\tilde{P}M_{j}+C^{T}C=0.

Conclusion

In this paper, we studied BQO systems, generalizations of both bilinear and LQO systems. We derived and compared different reachability and observability Gramians by a new interpretation of the formulation for the dual system, which leads to alternative observability Gramians. These new observability Gramians can also be applied to bilinear systems in a similar way. In comparison to

To address this issue, we proposed truncated variants that reduce the computational burden by requiring only a few standard Lyapunov equations to be solved.
We also introduced a balancing algorithm, analogous to that used for linear systems, and tested it with various Gramian combinations on two numerical examples, one SISO and one MIMO. The results show that the algorithm performs well in both cases. The truncated Gramians approximate the full ones effectively and offer improvements in computational efficiency.
Among the different observability Gramians, balanced truncation using the standard Gramian
Q^{S}
outperforms the use of
Q^{A}
. For higher-order systems, the truncated Gramian
Q_{T}^{P}
may not fully capture the system dynamics, making
Q_{T}^{S}
a better alternative.
