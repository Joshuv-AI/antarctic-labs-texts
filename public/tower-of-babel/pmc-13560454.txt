The 30-day perioperative outcomes of sigmoid volvulus surgery in resource-limited setting in Northcentral Ethiopia
Authors: Atalel Fentahun Awedew, Assefa Fentahun Emrie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSigmoid volvulus remains a leading cause of intestine obstruction in low middle income countries, particularly in volvulus belt countries. The objective of this study to explore the morbidity and mortality after sigmoid volvulus surgery in Northcentral Ethiopia.MethodsHospital-based retrospective cohort study employed in Debre Tabor comprehensive specialised hospital Northcentral Ethiopia. A medical chart reviews were conducted from Jan1, 2020 to June 30,2026. The collected data checked manually for completeness and consistency. The final data coded and entered to SPSS version 27 for data processing and analysis. Univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analysis conducted to identified attributable factors for morbidity and mortality after sigmoid volvulus surgery. The result of the final model expressed in terms of adjusted Odd Ratios (AOR) and 95% CI and statistical significance declared if the p-value is less than 0.05.ResultThree hundred sixty-two patients were analysed. Mean age was 56.4(SD14.5), with male predominance (321/362,88.7%) and most patients had anesthesia low risk (ASA grade 2, 230/362,63.5%). Emergency procedures were performed in 187/362(51.7%). Gangrenous sigmoid was found for 139/362 (38·4%) patients, 139/187(74.3%) of patients who underwent emergency surgery. The overall 30-day complication rate was 80 (22.1%) of 362, and mortality was 19/362(5.5%). 30-day postoperative complications were significantly associated with ASA IV (adjusted odds ratio AOR = 13.1, 95%CI:12.6–66.3; p = 0.002), presence of comorbidity (AOR = 4.4, 2.0-9.9; p < 0.0001), emergency procedures (AOR = 11.7, 3.6–37.9; p = 0.025), and gangrenous bowel intraoperative viability (AOR = 2.5, 1.2–5.7; p = 0.25).ConclusionMore than half of patients underwent emergency surgery, and two-thirds of these had intraoperative gangrenous bowel. Perioperative complication rates were high among patients undergoing emergency surgery and among those with comorbidities and higher ASA grades, despite most patients being classified as low surgical risk. Improving access to bellwether surgery, strengthening processes of care, and building surgical capacity and health-system delivery could help achieve safe, equitable surgical care for sigmoid volvulus.Keywords: Sigmoid volvulus, Ethiopia, Debre Tabor

Background

Sigmoid volvulus remains a leading cause of large bowel obstruction and contributes substantially to the global burden of emergency-related hospitalizations, morbidity and mortality [1]. It disproportionately affects peoples in low-income and middle-income (LMICs) particularly in the so-called volvulus belt, which extending across South Asia, Africa, and South America [2]. Sigmoid volvulus accounts 50–80% of large bowel obstruction cases in these regions, compared with approximately 4% in USA, where colorectal cancer and diverticular disease are the predominant in western countries [3].

Sigmoid volvulus surgery is one of bellwether emergency procedures that should be accessed in a timely and equitable manner achieve safe, equitable and affordable surgical care, to in line with universal health coverage (UHC) and the health-related SDGs [4]. Despite this, surgical care has been neglected from the global health agenda. This began to change in 2015, when the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCoGS) published Global Surgery 2030 [5], establishing surgical, obstetric, and anaesthesia (SOA) care as an indivisible component of UHC. That same year, the World Health Assembly unanimously adopted resolution WHA68.15, recognising the strengthening of emergency and essential surgical care and anaesthesia as a necessary component of UHC [6]. This was followed by decision WHA70.22 in 2017, which mandated biennial progress reporting by member states, and resolution WHA76.2 in 2023, which further embedded surgical care within the 2030 Sustainable Development agenda [4].

Perioperative mortality rate (POMR) is one of the six core indicators proposed by Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCoGS), which every country is expected to track by 2030 despite no specific number target has been set [5]. In addition, Dakar Declaration on access to equitable, affordable, and quality surgical, obstetric, and anaesthesia care in Africa by 2030, adopted by African Heads of State in May 2022, commits to track the perioperative outcomes and invest in bellwether procedures such emergency care for sigmoid volvulus, including 2-hrs access and prevent financial hardship after sigmoid volvulus surgery [7].

Some studies have explored the clinical profile and in-hospital mortality after sigmoid volvulus surgery in Africa [8] and Ethiopia [9, 10]. However, most of studies report only in hospital perioperative outcomes, which likely underestimate the true burden. In addition, there are scarce data on perioperative outcome in the study area. We therefore aimed to assess the 30-day perioperative outcome and to investigate factors associated with these outcomes after sigmoid volvulus surgery.

Methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, in northcentral Ethiopia. It is one of the oldest public hospitals in Ethiopia, located in Debre Tabor city, South Gondar zone, Amhara regional state, 666 km from Addis Ababa and 103 km from Bahir Dar. In 2025, the hospital provided clinical services to approximately 336 000 inpatients and more than half a million outpatients, drawn from a catchment population of approximately 3·5 million. Data were collected from Jan 1, 2020, to June 30, 2026. The hospital has 51 beds dedicated to general surgery and three operating tables (two for elective and one for emergency procedures).

Study design

A hospital-based retrospective cohort study conducted in Debre Tabor Comprehensive specialized hospital among patients who had undergone sigmoid volvulus surgery in the study period. The demography, clinical presentation, and duration of illness, surgical procedures, intraoperative finding, postoperative complications, and management outcome were collected.

Sample size calculation and sampling

The sample size required to assess 30-day perioperative outcomes (morbidity and mortality) after sigmoid volvulus surgery was calculated using a single-proportion formula. Because the proportion of 30-day perioperative morbidity after sigmoid volvulus surgery in Ethiopia was unknown, we assumed a proportion of 50%, with a margin of error of 5% and a 95% CI, giving a maximum sample size of 384 participants. However, complete data were obtained for 362 patients, who were included in the study period; no specific sampling technique was employed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged older than 14 years, of either sex, who were diagnosed with sigmoid volvulus intraoperatively during emergency surgery, or who underwent elective surgery for a redundant sigmoid colon, were eligible for inclusion. Patients whose medical charts were missing or contained incomplete data, and paediatric patients (aged 14 years or younger), were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection and analysis

A structured data-collection tool was developed after reviewing previous studies in this area. Medical charts were reviewed to extract data on clinical presentation, progress notes, radiology reports, operative notes, and postoperative complications up to 30 days. Patients were followed up through the surgical referral clinic, and those discharged from follow-up before postoperative day 30 were contacted by telephone to complete data ascertainment. Cases were identified using ICD-10 code K56.2 (volvulus of the intestine). The collected data were checked for inconsistency, code, and enter SPSS version 27 for data processing and analysis. Normality of distribution was assed using Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms. Quantitative variables were represented as mean (SD) for normally distributed data, and median (IQR) for skewed data. Univariable binary logistic regression was used to assess the association each factor and 30-day postoperative complications. Factors with p < 0.25 in univariable analysis were entered to multivariable binary logistic regression model to control for potential cofounding, Results of the final model were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% CI, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study provides the sociodemographic characteristics, treatment pattern, and perioperative outcomes of sigmoid volvulus in resource-limiting setting in Ethiopia. More than half of sigmoid surgery was emergency procedure, with two-third of these patients presenting with intraoperative gangrenous bowel, resulting in high perioperative mortality and complications rates.

In this study, we found that sigmoid volvulus had male predominance although sex was not associated with postoperative complication in this study. This finding is consistent with in several area studies, including those from Turkey (6:1) [11], west Africa (14:1) [12], Ethiopia (5:1) [9], Uganda (5:1) [13], Iran (5:1) [14], and Pakistan (12:1) [15]. The exact cause of this sex discrepancy remains unclear, but proposed explanations include a more spacious female pelvis, which allows a greater likelihood of spontaneous detorsion, and a wider mesocolon in women [11].

Sigmoid volvulus remains a surgical emergency that predominantly affects elder population. The mean age in our study (56 ± 14.5 years) was lower that reported in previous studies from Turkey (60.61 ± 14.81) [11], in Ethiopia (69years) [9], in Pakistan (60years) [15], but comparable to findings from Uganda (52.2 ± 15.98) [13]. Studies from developed countries have generally reported a mean age of onset between 60 and 70 years [16–18]. This discrepancy in age distribution across settings may reflect underlying differences in dietary patterns and colonic anatomical predisposition, such as a longer sigmoid loop and mesentery, which have been proposed as contributing factors to earlier presentation in volvulus belt populations.

More than half of patients underwent emergency procedures, while the remaining were admitted electively following successful rectal tube deflation. Notably, among those who underwent emergency procedures, two-third patients had intraoperative gangrenous bowel, a significantly high proportion. This might be attributed to patient-and health system related factors, including surgical accessibility and infrastructure. Although the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery recommends access to emergency surgical care within 2 h, most patients in LMICs [19], including in our study area, must travel long distances to access emergency laparotomy for sigmoid volvulus, resulting in delayed presentation, inequitable access, and poor perioperative outcomes.

In our study, approximately half of patients with sigmoid volvulus underwent emergency sigmoid volvulus surgery. This finding is consistent with an analysis from the NSQIP database, which reported an emergency sigmoid volvulus surgery rate of 48% [17], and with a large study from Turkey reported a rate of approximately 47% [18]. However, some studies reported as high as approximately 79%. This variation might reflect a shift in management of patients with sigmoid volvulus with endoscopic decompression, with preferences for elective surgery after deflation and bowel preparation. When surgery for sigmoid volvulus is performed in a nonemergent fashion, patients can be medically optimized which will contribute good perioperative outcome. Although there are no solid evidences on the optimal timing of elective surgery, most surgeons operate 5–7 days after decompression, once bowel edema subside [20].

Our results confirm findings from other similar studies showing that most patients with sigmoid volvulus undergo colectomy with primary anastomosis (two-third) when compared with colectomy with stoma [9, 16, 17]. Hartmann’s procedure was utilized more in emergent cases, which are prone to anastomosis leak such as hemodynamics instability, contamination, difficult to achieve tension free anastomosis, and high comorbidity [21]. Although several studies have compared these procedures, most found no significant differences in outcomes. One systematic review and meta-analysis involving 724 patients with gangrenous sigmoid volvulus found that overall mortality was 15% after resection with primary anastomosis and 19% after Hartmann’s procedure, with no statistically significant difference between the two [21].

This study found a 30-day perioperative mortality rate of approximately 5.2%. Previous studies have reported rates of 9% in the USA [17], 4.5% in Ethiopia [9], 16% in Uganda [13], and 8% in Pakistan [15]. These difference in POMR might be explained by patient-related factors such as comorbidity, high ASA, delay presentation as well as and infrastructure and human capacity experiences. Perioperative mortality rate (POMR) is a key indicator of the quality of surgical care and could be improved through greater accessibility and affordability of care, and through strengthening of the surgical workforce and infrastructure capacity.

Postoperative complications remain a major global contributor to mortality, morbidity, economic loss, prolonged hospital stay [22]. In this study, postoperative complications occurred in approximately one in five patients following emergency sigmoid volvulus surgery, most commonly surgical site infection and anastomosis leak.

This finding is broadly consistent with previous studies of sigmoid volvulus surgical outcomes, most of which report that approximately a quarter of patients have a poor perioperative outcome [9, 10].Postoperative complications in this study were associated with higher ASA grades, comorbidity, emergency procedures, intraoperative gangrenous viability and peritonitis at presentation. These findings are consistent with previous studies on perioperative outcomes of sigmoid volvulus surgery in Ethiopia [9, 10].While these patients related- factors are not readily modified, the associated burden of complications may still be reduced through strengthened evidence-based surgical site infection prevention protocols, improved access to timely emergency surgical care, and sustained capacity-building of the surgical workforce.

Conclusion

More than half of sigmoid volvulus surgeries were performed as emergency procedures and among these, two-third of patients had intraoperative gangrenous bowel. Perioperative mortality and postoperative complication following sigmoid volvulus surgery remained high and were consistently associated with higher ASA, comorbidity, emergency procedures, and intraoperative gangrenous viability. These findings underscore the critical importance of early recognition and timely surgical intervention in sigmoid volvulus, as delayed presentation appears to be a key driver of bowel non-viability and, consequently, poorer perioperative outcomes. Strengthening community awareness, improving referral pathways, and enhancing capacity for early diagnosis and prompt surgical management, particularly in resource-limited settings may substantially reduce the burden of gangrenous bowel and associated mortality. Future prospective, multicentre studies are warranted to validate these risk factors and to evaluate the impact of early intervention strategies on perioperative outcomes in this patient population.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Ethical Review Committee of Debre Tabor University and official permission to undertaken the study obtained from Debre Tabor Comprehensive specialized Hospital. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout the study. Data were extracted from medical records using a structured, anonymised data-extraction sheet without patient identifiers such as names or addresses. Each patient was assigned a unique code instead of their medical registration number, and access to the data was restricted to the investigators. All collected data were stored securely and used solely for the purposes of this study.

Because data were collected retrospectively from medical charts, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethical Review Committee of Debre Tabor University. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
