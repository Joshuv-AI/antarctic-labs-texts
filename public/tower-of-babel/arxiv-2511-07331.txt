Characterizing Mott Insulators in the Interacting One-Body Picture
Théo Nathaniel Dionne, Santiago Villodre, Mikel Iraola, Maia Vergniory

Abstract
We present a framework to characterize Mott insulating phases within the interacting one-body picture, focusing on the Hubbard diamond chain featuring both Hubbard interactions and spin-orbit coupling simulated within cellular dynamical mean field theory. Using symmetry analysis of the single-particle Green’s function, we classify spectral functions by irreducible representations at high-symmetry points of the Brillouin zone. Complementarily, we calculate the one-body reduced density matrix which allows us to reach both a qualitative description of charge distribution and an analysis of the state purity. Moreover, within the Tensor Network framework, we employ a Density Matrix Renormalization Group approach to confirm the presence of three distinct phases and their corresponding phase transitions. Our results highlight how symmetry-labelled spectral functions and effective orbital analysis provide accessible single-particle tools for probing correlation-driven insulating phases.



I
Introduction

Over the years, many approaches have emerged aiming to capture the essential physics of interacting electrons in more intuitive terms. On one hand, symmetry is a powerful organizing principle which prevails across much of the field of condensed matter
5
;
1
. On the other, constructing effective orbitals from the charge operator has been shown to reveal the microscopic ingredients that dominate the low-energy physics of a model
32
. Such orbital-based descriptions can serve as a bridge between formal Green’s function techniques and physically transparent pictures of electronic structure. Taken together, these developments point towards the possibility of a unified framework in which symmetry, electronic correlations, and emergent orbital degrees of freedom can be treated on equal footing, thereby opening new routes for the discovery and characterization of interacting phases. Additionally, it has long been known that one can induce
different
Mott insulating phases on the square lattice
34
, and it would be highly desirable to develop methods capable of distinguishing such phases in a way that can be directly applied to real materials.

Is it possible to distinguish distinct interacting phases from the perspective of single particle Green’s functions in a model-agnostic fashion? The strict emphasis on interacting single particle methods is motivated by recent advances in ab-initio matter modelling using a combination of density functional theory and cellular dynamical mean field theory giving ready access to the fully dressed electronic Green’s function
2
. Moreover, the single-particle quantities one can calculate with these methods are accessible by experimental means such as ARPES.

In this work, we study the interacting phases of a 1D chain (the Hubbard diamond chain)
16
;
28
with strong emphasis on phases induced both by Hubbard interactions and spin-orbit coupling. Initially, we verify the existence of three distinct correlated phases using Tensor Network (TN) methods. These phases are then characterized through two different avenues. First, the spectral function is decomposed into sectors corresponding to different irreps of the little group at high-symmetry points in the Brillouin zone. Second, we construct the one-particle reduced density operator enabling the construction of effective local orbitals on the level of the single particle Green’s function.

II
Theory

First, we present the symmetry-constrained form of the single-particle Green’s function (SPGF), highlighting how crystal symmetries restrict its structure. Next, we introduce the one-body reduced density matrix (1RDM) as a tool to analyze interacting systems from its Green’s function and demonstrate how effective one-body orbital charge configurations can be extracted from it. Finally, we describe the Hubbard Diamond Chain (HDC) model and provide a first principles derivation of the symmetry-allowed spin–orbit coupling (SOC) term.

II.1
Symmetry of the SPGF

In this section, we demonstrate that the symmetries of the system generate a unitary representation of the corresponding group, which in turn imposes rigorous constraints on the structure of the Green’s function. Upon incorporating crystalline symmetries, we see how the spectral function can be decomposed into symmetry sectors corresponding to the irreps of the little group.

II.1.1
Wigner’s theorem

Consider a unitary transformation
\hat{\mathrm{U}}
in the
N
-particle Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(N)}
which has a matrix representation
U_{\mu\nu}
on the set of fermionic creation and annihilation operators as
13
;
20
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\mu}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}U_{\mu\nu}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}U_{\nu\mu}^{*}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}^{\dagger}

(1)

One can see that the complex frequency Green’s function is invariant under the matrix transformation defined above as long as it is a symmetry of the many-body system (see annex
C
). Explicitly,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\beta}U_{\mu\alpha}G_{\alpha\beta}(z)U_{\beta\nu}^{*}=G_{\mu\nu}(z)

(2)

It is known
21
that if
\mathrm{U}
is a representation of a finite group, then one can find a basis such that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}(g)=\bigoplus_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{m^{i}}\otimes\mathrm{U}^{i}(g)

(3)

with
m^{i}
, the multiplicity of irrep
i
. Since the Green’s function in matrix form commutes with the full set of representation matrices (
2
), upon using Schur’s lemma and changing basis
17
;
21
, the Green’s function can be transformed into:

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}(z)=\bigoplus_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{d^{i}}\otimes\mathrm{G}^{i}_{m^{i}\times m^{i}}(z)

(4)

where
d^{i}
is the complex dimension of the associated irrep. This form indicates that the Green’s function possesses
m^{i}
generally distinct
d^{i}
-degenerate eigenvalues associated to the irrep labelled by
i
. This decomposition is valid for any frequency
z\in\mathbbm{C}
.

II.1.2
Application to crystalline space groups

In the case of a lattice, its symmetry group
\mathscr{G}
is called the space group
17
. A notable subgroup of
\mathscr{G}
is
T\subset\mathscr{G}
, the crystalline translation subgroup. Applying the results of the previous section (
II.1.1
) yields:

\displaystyle G(z)\sim\bigoplus_{\mathbf{k}\in\text{1BZ}}G(\mathbf{k},z)

(5)

which is how one denotes the common notion that crystalline translation invariance yields a SPGF as a function of crystal momentum in group theoretic terms. Within the subspace belonging to a given wavevector
\mathbf{k}
, the remaining symmetry group is called the little group
\mathscr{G}_{\mathbf{k}}
and is defined as
17
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{\mathbf{k}}=\{g\in\mathscr{G}\penalty\ |\penalty\ g\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}\penalty\ (\text{mod}\penalty\ \mathbf{G}\in\text{recip. latt.})\}

(6)

The irreps of these groups offer strong symmetry-based labels for the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the Green’s function off the basis of crystal symmetry. In practice, all irreps of the little groups for every space group are available on the Bilbao Crystallographic Server
8
.

II.2
One-body reduced density matrix

Here, we show how the one-body reduced density matrix (1RDM) can be used as a tool for analyzing average behaviour in a many-body system at the one-particle level. In particular, a scheme for analyzing average orbital charge distribution is laid out.

II.2.1
Generalities

The exact N-body density matrix for a system of interacting particles is generally impossible to calculate. However, some information about the system can still be obtained from the 1RDM
29
. Following
12
, the 1RDM is defined as the partial trace of the full density matrix over the degrees of freedom relative to
N-1
particles:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu\nu}=\bra{\mu}\operatorname{Tr}_{N-1}\left\{\hat{\rho}^{(N)}\right\}\ket{\nu}

(7)

where the Greek indices label the degrees of freedom of the remaining particle. It can be shown (as done in annex
F
) that this simply reduces to the expectation value of a pair of creation and annihilation operators. In turn, we know that this can be computed directly from the complex frequency Green’s function
25
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\mu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{<}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}G_{\mu\nu}(z)

(8)

an expression valid at zero temperature. In the case of this work, we readily have access to
\gamma_{a\sigma,b\sigma^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})
where latin indices indicate orbitals and
\sigma(\sigma^{\prime})
denote spin. It can be checked that the filling of the model is related to the trace of the 1RDM

\displaystyle n=\frac{1}{N_{\text{DoF}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\text{B.Z.}}\text{Tr}\{\bm{\gamma}(\mathbf{k})\}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{DoF}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\text{B.Z.}}\sum_{a\sigma}\left\langle\mathrm{n}_{a\sigma}(\mathbf{k})\right\rangle

(9)

where
N_{\text{DoF}}
is the number of lattice degrees of freedom. In general, all one-body operator expectation values can be obtained from the 1RDM
7

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathrm{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}t_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{c}_{\nu}\right\rangle=\text{Tr}\{\bm{t}\bm{\gamma}\}

(10)

Finally, it is intuitively clear that in the case of a
Slater determinant
type ground state

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{(N)}_{0}}=\bigotimes_{\mathbf{k}\in\text{B.Z.}}\ket{\psi_{0}(\mathbf{k})}

(11)

tracing out all wave-vectors except
\mathbf{k}
will yield a pure state for the resulting 1RDM. In fact, it is known that
12
:

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}=\gamma\penalty\ \Leftrightarrow\penalty\ \ket{\Psi^{(N)}_{0}}\text{ is a Slater determinant}

(12)

Therefore, the purity
\text{Tr}\{\gamma^{2}\}
can be seen as a measure of electronic interactions.

II.2.2
Effective one-body orbitals

In order to study the orbital distribution of charge in an N-body system, it is possible to trace out all degrees of freedom except for orbital and spin indices. In this case,
\gamma
can be expressed in terms of its eigenvectors and eigenvalues:

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{j}p_{j}\ket{\phi_{j}}\!\!\bra{\phi_{j}}

(13)

where
p_{j}\in[0,1]
are classical occupations and
\ket{\phi_{n}}
are effective one-particle orbitals.

II.3.2
Microscopic origin of SOC term

Although the SOC
\phi
parameter is
allowed
by symmetry (cf. annex
D
), it can be physically motivated by constructing the tight-binding model in a bottom-up approach. The physically relevant microscopic elements for deriving the form of the hopping terms are the kinetic and the SOC contributions to the Hamiltonian of a free electron
30
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{H}_{\text{kin}}+\mathrm{H}_{\text{SOC}}=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}+\kappa\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\nabla}U(\mathbf{r})\times\mathbf{p}

(17)

In order to construct the tight-binding basis, we need a unit cell index
i
, an orbital index
\alpha
and a spin index
\sigma
. Then, the matrix elements of these free terms are evaluated in the basis of the physical orbitals of the model. Here, we assume that every lattice site represents a positively charged ion with a perfectly radial charge potential
U(r)
. Furthermore, we only consider real s-like electronic orbitals based around every ion.

Starting with the kinetic term, the matrix elements can be written and evaluated as:

\displaystyle t^{\text{kin}}_{i\alpha\sigma;j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bra{i\alpha\sigma}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}\ket{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{2m}\int\text{d}^{3}r\,w_{i\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\nabla^{2}w_{j\beta}(\mathbf{r})

(18)

As expected,
\bra{i\alpha\sigma}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}\ket{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}=\bra{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}\ket{i\alpha\sigma}
in the case of real orbitals and thus
t^{\text{kin}}_{i\alpha\sigma;j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now, for the SOC term, the matrix elements are slightly more constrained by the geometry of the system. As a matter of fact, since both the gradient of the electrostatic potential and the average electron momentum is odd out-of-plane, only the
\sigma_{z}
term has any chance of surviving. Therefore,

\displaystyle t^{\text{SOC}}_{i\alpha\sigma;j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\bra{i\alpha\sigma}\kappa\sigma_{z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}U(\mathbf{r})\times\mathbf{p}\ket{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-i\hbar\kappa\bra{\sigma}\sigma_{z}\ket{\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{\mathbf{z}}\cdot\int\text{d}^{3}r\,w_{i\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\bm{\nabla}U(\mathbf{r})\times\bm{\nabla}w_{j\beta}(\mathbf{r})

(19)

In a similar fashion than for the kinetic term, it can be observed that the SOC term is purely imaginary. However, the geometry of the HDC renders the SOC contributions to
t_{2}
and
t_{3}
trivial since the gradient of the electrostatic potential and the gradient of the s-like orbitals are both spatially odd with respect to Wyckoff positions 1a and 1b. So, in the spin basis

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{2}

\displaystyle=t_{2}\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{3}

\displaystyle=t_{3}\mathbbm{1}

(20)

The relevant terms which do not have a geometric cancellation are the diagonal links pertaining to
t_{1}
. Since the kinetic term is purely real and the SOC term is purely imaginary, we can write:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{1}

\displaystyle=t_{1}^{\text{kin}}\mathbbm{1}+t_{1}^{\text{SOC}}\sigma_{z}

(21)

Focusing on the spin up sector, the contribution can be parametrized by

\displaystyle t_{1}^{\text{kin}}+t_{1}^{\text{SOC}}=t_{1}\text{e}^{i\phi}

(22)

where

\displaystyle t_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(t_{1}^{\text{kin}})^{2}+|t_{1}^{\text{SOC}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\text{arctan}\left(\frac{|t_{1}^{\text{SOC}}|}{t_{1}^{\text{kin}}}\right)

(23)

which allows us to make contact with the form derived via symmetry:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\text{e}^{i\phi}&0\\
0&t_{1}\text{e}^{-i\phi}\end{pmatrix}

(24)

Furthermore, the SOC phase parameter
\phi
can be further interpreted by considering an electron circulating along a closed loop around a diamond in the chain (cf. fig.
1
).

On one hand, hopping counterclockwise on the diamond acts as
\text{e}^{i\phi}\sigma_{z}
in spin space (
14
), showing already that the phase winds in opposite directions for each spin projection as imposed by the SOC term containing
\mathrm{L}\cdot\mathrm{S}
. Moreover, since the chain is confined to the
xy
plane, it only has orbital angular momentum along
\hat{\mathrm{z}}
, thus justifying
why
there is no spin mixing in this chain.

On the other hand, the total phase accumulated from a full rotation will be
\text{e}^{\pm 4i\phi}
, hence, the non-redundant phase angles are contained within
\phi\in[0,\pi/2]
due to angles being equivalent modulo
2\pi
. It follows that the effects of spin orbit coupling are maximal when
\phi=\pi/2
which corresponds naturally with the definition of the angle (
23
).

III
Methods

III.1
Tensor Networks

Tensor Network (TN) methods provide a powerful and efficient framework for simulating quantum many-body systems
23
;
3
. Among these, the Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) is the most widely employed variational algorithm for studying low-dimensional strongly correlated systems
26
;
33
.

In this work, we use DMRG to identify the three distinct phases reported in
17
for the model introduced in section
II.3
. The simulations were performed using the TenPy Tensor Network library for Python
14
;
15
, a dedicated platform for TN simulations in condensed matter physics. Within this framework, the lattice and Hamiltonian were explicitly constructed as a Matrix Product Operator (MPO), while the many-body wavefunction was represented as a Matrix Product State (MPS) optimized through the two-site DMRG algorithm.

To determine the energy gap, we first compute a well-converged approximation to the ground-state wavefunction and subsequently perform a second DMRG calculation in which the new target state is explicitly constrained to be orthogonal to the ground state. The energy difference between these two states then provides the excitation gap of the system.

The expectation value of the mirror operator
M_{x}
is calculated by applying a site permutation to the ground-state MPS, followed by a spin-flip transformation using the
S_{x}
operator. The overlap between this transformed MPS and the original wavefunction yields the desired expectation value.

This procedure is systematically repeated across a grid of
U
and
\phi
values to generate the data shown in figure
3
. Further technical details on the Tensor Network simulations are provided in annex
E
.

III.2
Cellular Dynamical Mean Field Theory

In this part, a brief overview of Cellular Dynamical Mean Field Theory (CDMFT) is given before elaborating how the technique is used to approximate the Green’s function of the HDC.

III.2.1
Overview

CDMFT is the cluster extension of the well known dynamical mean field theory
11
;
19
. This method is typically used when local physics dominates the overall behaviour of the system. Additionally, CDMFT can be viewed as an add-on to cluster perturbation theory (CPT)
7
which improves the Green’s function of the cluster by coupling it to a non-interacting bath configured to mimic the presence of the remaining lattice beyond the cluster. A pedagogical review of the theory underpinning both methods is found in
24
.

III.2.2
Cluster and bath setup

To apply CDMFT via the use of exact diagonalization (ED), a choice of cluster, baths and hybridizations must be made
7
. Since the phases studied in the model arise from single diamond physics
16
;
27
, it is intuitive to partition the lattice into single diamond clusters. The effective Anderson impurity model (AIM) was constructed following the irreps of the cluster symmetry group
10
;
18
.

Due to the influence of SOC and the lattice, the effective symmetry of the cluster model we consider is C
2
. Therefore, only two irreps need to be accounted for. We choose four baths per symmetry sector bringing the total size of the ED problem to 12 as schematically illustrated in figure
2
.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have shown how the single particle Green’s function and thus the spectral function can be analyzed in terms of the irreducible representations of the little group at the high-symmetry points of the Brillouin zone. Furthermore, it was shown how the one-body reduced density matrix can be used to construct effective one-body orbitals which can be used to qualitatively assess the average behaviour of electronic charge in the orbital basis. Finally, the purity of the one-body reduced density matrix is shown to be discontinuous at the transition between Mott insulator and spin-orbit induced atomic insulator, allowing for a simple single-particle level tool for detecting the transition between these distinct phases.

The techniques presented here are based on the interacting single-particle level making them easily accessible to current single-particle Green’s functions simulation techniques and various experimental probes. The irrep-labelled spectral function gives valuable symmetry-based information in strongly interacting matter while the one-particle reduced density matrix provides effective one-body orbitals which are used to gain intuition about how charge behaves in strongly correlated phases along with the notion of state purity which can reveal phase transitions between correlated phases.

Appendix B
Finite temperature complex frequency Green’s function in Lehmann’s representation

We start from the definition of the spectral function in
7

\displaystyle A_{\mu\nu}(t)=\left\langle\left\{\mathrm{c}_{\mu}(t),\mathrm{c}^{\dagger}_{\nu}(0)\right\}\right\rangle

(25)

Focusing on the electron contribution
A_{\mu\nu}^{(e)}(t)

\displaystyle A_{\mu\nu}^{(e)}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}\left\{\text{e}^{-\beta\mathrm{H}}\text{e}^{i\mathrm{H}t}c_{\mu}\text{e}^{-i\mathrm{H}t}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{n}\bra{n^{(N)}}\text{e}^{-\beta\mathrm{H}}\text{e}^{i\mathrm{H}t}c_{\mu}\text{e}^{-i\mathrm{H}t}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{n,m}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\text{e}^{i(E_{n}^{(N)}-E_{m}^{(N+1)})t}\bra{n^{(N)}}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}

(26)

where
\mathcal{H}^{(N)}
is the subspace of
N
-particle states. The spectral function in frequency space is given by

\displaystyle A_{\mu\nu}^{(e)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}{t}\sum_{n,m}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\text{e}^{i(\omega+E_{n}^{(N)}-E_{m}^{(N+1)})t}\bra{n^{(N)}}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{n,m}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}2\pi\delta\left(\omega-E_{n}^{(N)}+E_{m}^{(N+1)}\right)\bra{n^{(N)}}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}

The electronic contribution to the Green’s function is then obtained

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}^{(e)}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\text{d}{\omega}}{2\pi}\frac{A_{\mu\nu}^{(e)}(\omega)}{z-\omega}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{n,m}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)}}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}

(28)

An analogous procedure can be performed on the hole contribution then resulting in the full complex-frequency single-particle Green’s function:

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{n}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\Bigg[\sum_{m}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)}}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}+\sum_{\ell}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)}}c_{\nu}^{\dagger}\ket{\ell^{(N-1)}}\!\!\bra{\ell^{(N-1)}}c_{\mu}\ket{n^{(N)}}}{z-E_{n}^{(N)}+E_{\ell}^{(N-1)}}\Bigg]

(29)

Note that the complex frequency Green’s function is a meromorphic complex function containing only simple poles on the real axis
13
which is clear from its above form. Moreover, It can be shown that the complex frequency Green’s function is the only one possessing the correct properties: giving the correct Green’s functions (retarded, advanced, Matsubara,
etc.
) and simultaneously obeying the appropriate boundary conditions

Appendix C
Symmetry of the general complex frequency SPGF

In this section, we show that the single particle Green’s function transforms like a fermionic bilinear under unitary symmetries. This derivation, in the interest of generality, is carried out for arbitrary frequency and temperature.
Note that this proof can be trivially extended to n-particle Green’s functions by grouping indices.

Given the representation of the unitary transform on the ladder operators (
1
), one can naturally define that the hamiltonian is symmetric under the transformation if it commutes with the representation of the entire group.

However, it is also well known
6
that a system of interacting particles can develop a ground state with lower symmetry than the hamiltonian. For this purpose, we also require that the symmetry preserves the subspace of Hilbert space containing the ground state with spontaneously broken symmetry. For example, if the system spontaneously develops magnetization along an axis, any rotation
around
that axis is preserved. Conversely, the other rotations are no longer generally a symmetry of the Green’s function.

Thus, we define
the system
to be symmetric under a unitary transformation as long as:

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\mathrm{H}},\hat{\mathrm{U}}(g)\right]=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{\mathrm{U}}:\mathcal{H}_{\text{SSB}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\text{SSB}}\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ g\in G

(30)

Now, we can examine the transformation of the Green’s function under the ladder operator representation. Starting from the electronic contribution (see annex
B
) of the LHS in (
2
), we can use the Lehmann form (
28
) to obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\beta}U_{\mu\alpha}G^{(e)}_{\alpha\beta}(z)U_{\beta\nu}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{nm}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)}}\sum_{\alpha}U_{\mu\alpha}c_{\alpha}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}\sum_{\beta}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}U_{\beta\nu}^{*}\ket{n^{(N)}}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\sum_{nm}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)}}\hat{\mathrm{U}}c_{\mu}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{m^{(N+1)}}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)}}\hat{\mathrm{U}}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)}}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}

(31)

Since the unitary transformation commutes with both the hamiltonian and the number operator, the action of the
\hat{\mathrm{U}}
on the set
\{\ket{n^{(N)}}\}
forms a unitary representation that is closed on the subspace of degenerate states (energy
E_{n}^{(N)}
) and the subspace of
N
particles. Precisely,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{U}}\ket{n^{(N)},i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\text{deg}(n)}\mathcal{U}_{ij}^{(N)}(n)\ket{n^{(N)},j}

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{U}\right]^{\dagger}\!\left[\mathcal{U}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\text{deg}(n)}

(32)

We can then show that (
31
) is indeed invariant. To see this, the sums over the fixed particle number spectrum are replaced by a sum over energies and a sum over degeneracies
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{H}^{(N)}|_{E_{n}}\\
m\in\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}|_{E_{m}}\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\text{deg(n)}\\
j\in\text{deg(m)}\end{subarray}}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)},i}\hat{\mathrm{U}}c_{\mu}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{m^{(N+1)},j}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)},j}\hat{\mathrm{U}}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)},i}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{H}^{(N)}|_{E_{n}}\\
m\in\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}|_{E_{m}}\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,a,b\in\text{deg(n)}\\
j,c,d\in\text{deg(m)}\end{subarray}}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)},a}\mathcal{U}_{ia}^{(N)}(n)c_{\mu}\mathcal{U}_{cj}^{(N+1)*}(m)\ket{m^{(N+1)},c}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)},d}\mathcal{U}_{jd}^{(N+1)}(m)c_{\beta}^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}_{bi}^{(N)*}(n)\ket{n^{(N)},b}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{H}^{(N)}|_{E_{n}}\\
m\in\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}|_{E_{m}}\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,a,b\in\text{deg(n)}\\
j,c,d\in\text{deg(m)}\end{subarray}}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\mathcal{U}_{cj}^{(N+1)*}\mathcal{U}_{jd}^{(N+1)}\mathcal{U}_{bi}^{(N)*}\mathcal{U}_{ia}^{(N)}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)},a}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)},c}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)},d}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)},b}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{H}^{(N)}|_{E_{n}}\\
m\in\mathcal{H}^{(N+1)}|_{E_{m}}\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,a,b\in\text{deg(n)}\\
j,c,d\in\text{deg(m)}\end{subarray}}\text{e}^{-\beta E_{n}^{(N)}}\delta_{cd}\delta_{ba}\frac{\bra{n^{(N)},a}c_{\mu}\ket{m^{(N+1)},c}\!\!\bra{m^{(N+1)},d}c_{\beta}^{\dagger}\ket{n^{(N)},b}}{z-E_{m}^{(N+1)}+E_{n}^{(N)}}=G^{(e)}_{\mu\nu}(z)

(33)

An identical derivation holds for the other term in (

Appendix D
Symmetry analysis of the Hubbard Diamond Chain

Here, the symmetry-allowed couplings for the Hubbard Diamond Chain are derived. We demand that the model be symmetric under space group Pmmm + time reversal. Knowing how fermions transform under reflections and time reversal, we can lay out the forms of the relevant operators:

\displaystyle M_{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
-i&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle M_{y}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle M_{z}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-i&0\\
0&i\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}

(34)

where the basis is taken to be the spin-1/2 projections along
z
and
\mathcal{K}
is the complex conjugation operator. All couplings are analyzed within this basis. First and foremost, given the orientation of the model running along
x
in the
xy
plane, all couplings must be invariant under
M_{z}
. So starting with a general hopping matrix along any given link expressed in the spin basis (where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
):

\displaystyle\tilde{t}=M_{z}\tilde{t}M_{z}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-i&0\\
0&i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
-c&d\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \tilde{t}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&d\end{pmatrix}

(35)

Now, time reversal symmetry can be used to further constrain the form of the couplings:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}=\mathcal{T}\tilde{t}\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}&0\\
0&d^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d^{*}&0\\
0&a^{*}\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \tilde{t}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&a^{*}\end{pmatrix}

(36)

At this stage, only one complex parameter remains for any possible coupling in the plane.

The purely vertical and horizontal links in the model are now shown to be real:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{\text{vert}}

\displaystyle=M_{x}\tilde{t}_{\text{vert}}M_{x}^{-1}\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \tilde{t}_{\text{vert}}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ a\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{\text{hor}}

\displaystyle=M_{y}\tilde{t}_{\text{hor}}M_{y}^{-1}\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \tilde{t}_{\text{hor}}=\begin{pmatrix}b&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ b\in\mathbb{R}

(37)

It is to be noted that the next-neighbour intra-diamond couplings have no need to be identical, we have simply chosen to do so out of simplicity in this work.

The diagonal links do not share this same symmetry and must be examined slightly more carefully. Consider the diagonal link that takes site 1 to site 2 (
\tilde{t}_{12}
). Upon mirror reflection
M_{x}
, the coupling is mapped to
\tilde{t}_{32}^{*}
. However, since
\tilde{t}_{32}^{*}=\tilde{t}_{23}
, we can establish that
\tilde{t}_{12}=\tilde{t}_{23}
. Through the use of similar arguments, it can be verified that
\tilde{t}_{12}=\tilde{t}_{23}=\tilde{t}_{34}=\tilde{t}_{41}
.

In summary, all couplings except for the diagonal ones are
real
and are identical no matter the spin projections:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{2}\sim\tilde{t}_{3}\sim\mathbbm{1}

(38)

Moreover, the diagonal links are all identical with respect to a given order around the sites of the diamond and specified by a single complex number:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}&0\\
0&t_{1}^{*}\end{pmatrix}

(39)

Once an appropriate site ordering is defined, we specify the physical properties of each site. For this purpose, we employ the
SpinHalfFermionSite
class, which assigns spin
1/2
fermionic degrees of freedom to each site and provides the corresponding operators (creation, annihilation,
S_{x}
,
S_{y}
,
Sz
, etc.). In addition, it allows us to define conserved quantities such as the total particle number. Enforcing these conservation laws significantly reduces the size of the accessible Hilbert space, improving the computational efficiency of the simulation.

Once the full model is properly defined and the required classes are initialized, we create a half-filled ansatz wavefunction
|\psi_{anzar}\rangle=|\uparrow,\uparrow,\downarrow,\downarrow\rangle
. With the Hamiltonian already constructed, we employ the
TwoSiteDMRGEngine
to perform the optimization under open boundary conditions.

Several key parameters determine the performance and accuracy of the DMRG engine, and here we highlight the most relevant ones. First, a mixer is introduced at the beginning of the simulation to help avoid convergence to local minima. Second, the diagonalization method employed is the Lanczos algorithm, which efficiently computes the lowest-energy eigenstates.
We also allow the bond dimension to reach values of up to approximately
300
; however, such large values are generally unnecessary due to the use of open boundary conditions, which naturally reduce entanglement near the edges. Finally, the convergence criteria are set to a maximum energy error of
\Delta E=10^{-8}
and an entropy change threshold of
\Delta S=10^{-5}
.

As explained in Section
III.1
, once a reliable approximation to the ground-state wavefunction has been obtained, we perform a series of operations on the MPS to compute the expectation value of the mirror operator. Since this operator is not predefined in TenPy, it must be constructed from a combination of other operations. First, after creating a copy of the ground-state MPS, we apply a permutation of the lattice sites corresponding to the exchange of site indices between the left and right sides of the system, as illustrated in Figure
10
. Next, we apply the

operator to flip the spins, which are originally defined along the

10

\langle\tilde{\psi_{0}}|M_{x}|\tilde{\psi}_{0}\rangle
.

:

Afterwards, starting again from the ground-state wavefunction, we consider several possible approaches to compute the first excited state and the corresponding energy gap. In this work, we choose to create a copy of the ground-state MPS and perform a second DMRG calculation using the same configuration as before, except for the inclusion of the additional argument

Appendix F
One-body reduced density matrix

Formally,
\hat{\rho}^{(N)}
is the density operator of the
N
-particle system. The one-body reduced density matrix is obtained by taking the partial trace over the
(N-1)
-particle subspace. This can be shown to reduce to the expectation value of a pair of fermionic ladder operators
12
;
29
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\bra{\mu}\operatorname{Tr}_{N-1}\left\{\hat{\rho}^{(N)}\right\}\ket{\nu}

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\bra{\mu,\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\hat{\rho}^{(N)}\ket{\nu,\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\bra{\mu,\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{\Psi^{(N)}_{i}}\!\!\bra{\Psi^{(N)}_{i}}\right)\ket{\nu,\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}\bra{\Psi^{(N)}_{i}}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\ket{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\!\!\bra{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\right)\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\mu}\ket{\Psi^{(N)}_{i}}\!\!

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\mu}\right\rangle

(41)

where
\sum_{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\ket{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}\!\!\bra{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{N}}=\mathbbm{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{(N-1)}
. In practice, one does not generally possess the density operator for the entire system. However, this information can be readily extracted from the single particle Green’s function. It is clear from it’s Lehmann form (
28
) that one can pick up the poles on the negative frequency axis by choosing an appropriate contour (
\mathcal{C}_{<}
) to obtain the above expectation value
25
;
7
:

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathrm{c}}_{\mu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{<}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}G_{\mu\nu}(z)

(42)

The problem can be made numerically tractable by deforming the contour into an infinite semicircle going up the imaginary axis and following an arc downwards at infinity in the half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{-}+i\mathbb{R}
provided one inserts a simple pole at
p\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
to cancel the contribution on the arc (see figure
11(b)
)
7
.

(a)

An example contour that can be used to pick up the negative frequency poles of the Green’s function.

(b)

The contour used to obtain an integral over imaginary frequencies. The red pole must lie strictly outside of the semi-circle as to not pick it up in the contour integration.

:
Contours used in the evaluation of the fermionic bilinear expectation values.

Explicitely, we can see that:

\displaystyle\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{<}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}G_{\mu\nu}(z)=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{semi}}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}\left[G_{\mu\nu}(z)-\frac{\delta_{\mu\nu}}{z-p}\right]

(43)

Since
\frac{1}{z-p}=\frac{1}{z}+\frac{p}{z^{2}}+\dots
around
|z|\to\infty
, this term perfectly cancels out the simple pole that the Green’s function has at infinity since it is known to go as
G_{\mu\nu}(z)\to\delta_{\mu\nu}/z
for large frequencies
25
. Conveniently, this implies that the contribution on the arc of the large semicircle is exactly zero when its radius is taken to be infinite since:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{arc}}}\frac{\text{d}z}{2\pi i}\left[G_{\mu\nu}(z)-\frac{\delta_{\mu\nu}}{z-p}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int\limits_{\pi/2}^{-\pi/2}\frac{R\text{e}^{i\theta}\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\left[G_{\mu\nu}(R\text{e}^{i\theta})-\frac{\delta_{\mu\nu}}{R\text{e}^{i\theta}-p}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int\limits_{\pi/2}^{-\pi/2}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\left[\delta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\delta_{\mu\nu}}{1-p/R\text{e}^{i\theta}}\right]=0

(44)

Hence, all that remains is an integration over the purely imaginary frequencies. Combining this result with (
41
) shows that:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu\nu}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\text{d}\omega}{2\pi}\left[G_{\mu\nu}(i\omega)-\frac{\delta_{\mu\nu}}{i\omega-p}\right]

(45)

which is how this is computed in practice from the complex Green’s function at
T=0
.
