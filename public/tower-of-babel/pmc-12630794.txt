Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics applied to the acoustic levitation of acoustically large objects
Authors: Mehdi Akbarzadeh, Benjamin Halkon, Sebastian Oberst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany studies on acoustic radiation forces, especially those applied to acoustic levitation, focus on characterizing the behaviour of acoustic fields. However, the dynamic response of the levitated objects, particularly those larger than the wavelength limit, remains relatively underexplored. Here, we look to bridge this gap by deriving nonlinear equations of motion for a spherical object trapped under acoustic radiation forces while subject to external excitation. For such a contemporary scenario, the otherwise elemental Gorkov formulation fails to provide accurate results. Using Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamical Systems (SINDy), first, we derive the corresponding nonlinear equation of motion from analytical time series data obtained through the Gorkov formulation and external excitation for acoustically small objects. This approach recovers the governing equation with less than 0.05% error in coefficient values when compared to the analytical solution. Second, we conduct experiments with the TinyLev levitator with external excitation applied via an external actuator to generate the required time series for an acoustically large object. SINDy is applied to reconstruct governing equations from experimental data, allowing for the study of how excitation amplitude affects acoustically large objects. All obtained coefficients change with excitation amplitude, and the coefficients in the dynamic equation of motion should not be treated as constants. Strong velocity-dependent terms emerged, indicating a complex relationship between viscosity and object response, which classical models do not predict. The bifurcation diagram obtained using the SINDy-derived equation of motion shows closer agreement with that obtained experimentally. These results demonstrate that SINDy can recover equations consistent with Gorkov’s formulation and extend beyond it, providing a pathway to derive analytical expressions directly from data for levitating and manipulating objects beyond the Rayleigh limit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24666-5.Keywords: Acoustic radiation force, Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamical systems, Acoustic levitationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Acoustic radiation force is that which is experienced by an object when placed in a sound field, resulting from momentum transferred by acoustic waves as they interact with and scatter off the object1–5. This force phenomenon has many practical applications and has been utilized for drug delivery6, non-contact excitation7–9, particle manipulation10, and vibroacoustic spectrography of imaging acoustic responses of internal body tissues to mechanical excitation11. The acoustic radiation force is mathematically derived by integrating the acoustic radiation stress tensor over the object surface. The stress tensor is obtained by applying perturbations to the Navier-Stokes equations, which result in non-zero second order terms. The time-averaging method cancels out the linear, first-order term due to symmetrical oscillation, leading to a zero contribution over time. However, the nonlinear, second order terms do not cancel and produce the non-zero, time-averaged acoustic radiation force, which pushes the object toward the minimum potential12–14. Gorkov used the time-averaged potential and kinetic energies of an acoustic field, conceptualized as the gradient of a potential function, to determine the density, compressibility, and the geometry as functions of the acoustic radiation force. However, this formulation treats the object as an ideal, rigid particle and assumes it is acoustically small—i.e., its dimensions are much smaller than the wavelength of the incident acoustic field. As a result, Gorkov’s theory fails to capture the more complex dynamical behaviour exhibited by acoustically large or deformable objects15. While several studies have investigated the acoustic wave-object interactions for object sizes larger than the wavelength limit16–20, more research is required to understand their dynamics better.

Analytical representations, such as Gorkov’s formulation, provide closed-form descriptions but are limited to acoustically small spherical objects; full numerical simulations extend to larger objects yet do not readily yield compact governing equations that reveal the underlying physical relationships. Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamical (SINDy) is a data-driven algorithm that uses optimization to construct models by identifying a sparse set of candidate functions that describe the observed system behaviour21–29. For acoustically large objects, SINDy is applied here for the first time to evaluate its ability to extract explicit equations of motion directly from time-series data, while retaining predictive power and physical interpretability, which we are going to evaluate in this study.

We collect theoretical time series data for acoustically small spherical objects using the Gorkov formulation and compare this with experimental time series data generated from acoustically large objects and compare their bifurcation diagrams. In the acoustically small objects regime, we deliberately use synthetic data from Gorkov’s formulation as an exact analytical reference to benchmark the accuracy of SINDy under controlled conditions. This ensures the method was validated before applying it to experimental data for large objects. Also, we limit our study to spherical objects to maintain consistency with Gorkov’s formulation and to provide a clear baseline for validating the SINDy approach. This simplification allows direct benchmarking between theory, data, and experiment. Then we use a statistical analysis to identify the average quantities of coefficients used for SINDy to study acoustically large objects using the extracted equations with the aim to offer a framework to better understand the dynamics of arbitrary levitated objects, beyond the limitations of classical particle-based theories.

Results and discussion

We apply SINDy to analyse the dynamics of acoustically levitated objects. First, we validate its ability to reconstruct the original differential equation from time series generated by an analytical model based on Gorkov’s formulation for acoustically small objects. We then extend the study to acoustically large objects, applying the analytical model and using SINDy to determine the governing equation. We implemented SINDy in MATLAB using the Thresholded Least Squares Algorithm (TLSA) as originally introduced by Brunton et al.21. This algorithm iteratively applies ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, followed by thresholding small coefficients to zero, thus promoting model sparsity. We used a fixed sparsity threshold of across all experiments and simulations, which provided reliable results for the identification of governing equations. The choice of was based on trial-and-error tuning to achieve a balance between model accuracy and interpretability.

The same extended function library, including polynomial terms in and up to fifth order, was used for both the small and large object cases. The differences in the final identified models are a result of the SINDy algorithm’s sparsity-promoting structure. For the small object, most coefficients were eliminated, indicating relatively simpler dynamics. For the large object, more terms were retained which represent its richer nonlinear behaviour. This consistent approach avoids bias and allows the results to reflect the underlying system response. To generate a formulation using SINDy that not only displays the average of the measured data but also includes uncertainty bounds, we can use the mean value for each coefficient, which covers 95% of the data for a normal distribution. This approach provides a more conservative and comprehensive representation of uncertainty (see Tables S1-S3, in Supplementary material, S5). When a coefficient shows a small box plot in one subfigure and a large box plot in another—corresponding to different excitation amplitudes—it indicates that the coefficient’s variability is sensitive to the excitation amplitude. The current uncertainty assessment, as depicted in Fig. 2, relies on statistical variation across repeated experimental runs and coefficient distributions. However, in the literature, there are other approaches to uncertainty quantification in sparse identification. One such approach, UQ-SINDy, is gaining traction47, which integrates probabilistic modelling into the identification process by estimating distributions over both the model structure and coefficients47. UQ-SINDy has the potential to yield confidence intervals directly on terms within the governing equation and support model selection under uncertainty. Although beyond the scope of this study, incorporating uncertainty-aware methods such as UQ-SINDy could enhance model reliability in future work, particularly under noisy or ambiguous experimental conditions.

Because the governing equations were identified directly from LDV-measured velocity time series, SINDy inherently incorporates hidden influences such as temperature variations, medium viscosity changes, and other secondary effects into the fitted coefficients. In this study, we did not isolate these effects in an independent analysis; instead, their impact is implicitly embedded in the reconstructed dynamics. A notable feature here is the presence of mixed nonlinear terms, which are not captured in classical formulations. All coefficients in Eqs. (9) and (10) admit a direct interpretation: the linear displacement coefficient sets the effective trap stiffness, odd displacement terms (e.g., cubic) produce amplitude-dependent hardening/softening, linear and higher-order velocity terms quantify dissipative losses, and mixed displacement–velocity terms indicate nonlinear coupling between restoring and dissipative dynamics. In this way, the reconstructed equation not only reproduces the measured responses but also highlights additional physical effects that warrant further investigation. The SINDy method reveals important nonlinear effects in acoustically levitated objects that classical models like Gorkov’s miss. Specifically, cubic and mixed nonlinear terms show complex interactions between acoustic forces, drag, and inertia at larger object sizes. These nonlinear terms depend on excitation amplitude, meaning the trap’s stiffness and damping change with vibration strength. Also, strong velocity-dependent terms for large objects indicate that energy loss grows at higher excitations—something hard to capture with traditional analysis. This new understanding of scaling and nonlinear behaviour can help improve control and characterization in future levitation systems.

Although we do not derive a closed-form normal form using analytical methods such as centre-manifold reduction, the SINDy-identified equations can be considered a data-driven normal form, where the external excitation amplitude acts as a bifurcation parameter. These results suggest that SINDy provides not only an interpretable model but also a practical tool for reconstructing nonlinear bifurcation behaviour in experimentally limited regimes. In our previous study33, we demonstrated that acoustically small spherical levitated objects exhibit harmonic oscillations under external excitation, with nonlinear behaviours such as Duffing-like responses and bifurcations. Building on this, the current study uses a data-driven approach to reconstruct the governing nonlinear equations from time series data, capturing amplitude-dependent and higher-order effects. Together, these studies provide complementary insights into the dynamics of levitated objects, ranging from small spheres under controlled excitation to large objects that exceed the limits of classical formulations, typically bound by the Rayleigh limit.

Conclusions

In this study, we applied the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamical Systems (SINDy) to reconstruct a spherical levitated object’s nonlinear differential equation of motion when trapped in an externally excited acoustic radiation force field, using theoretical and experimental time series. This study demonstrates a two-stage application of SINDy: first, as a validation tool using synthetic data extracted from Eq. (1) for the acoustically small object regime, and second, as a discovery tool applied to experimental data for acoustically large objects. We found that for the acoustically small object regime, the extracted coefficients differed by less than 0.05% from the exact analytical model. The experimental data used for the second stage corresponds to the system’s response under a period-1 oscillatory regime, which was the only stable and repeatable dynamic state achievable within the limits of our acoustic levitation setup. While this restricts experimental validation to a single regime, it still provides a rich basis for extracting the governing dynamics. Importantly, the structure of the identified model is not hand-tuned but emerges purely from the SINDy algorithm’s sparse regression process, using the same candidate library functions across both acoustically small and large object cases. This allows for a direct comparison: in the large-object regime, SINDy revealed significantly higher-order and mixed nonlinear terms that were absent in the small-object case, highlighting how the model captures physically meaningful transitions beyond the assumptions of classical formulations. Here, SINDy employed as a data-driven framework to bridge the gap between theoretical limits and experimental observations of nonlinear object dynamics, demonstrates that, even within a limited dynamic regime, the identified equations carry clear physical signatures and offer insights into amplitude-dependent dynamics not previously characterized in literature.

We used box plots to visualize distributions and to assist in identifying key coefficients in SINDy models, to reveal their sensitivity to changes in external excitation. For acoustically large objects, SINDy reveals nonlinear dependencies between the external excitation amplitude and the extracted coefficients in the governing equation. These relationships indicate that the system’s response is strongly amplitude-dependent. Although the chosen library may not be used to fully capture all aspects of the bifurcation behaviour, it provides valuable insights into the dominant dynamics of the system. The three datasets in Fig. 3 were restricted to period-1 responses at a fixed frequency, since only under these conditions could stable and interpretable governing equations be reliably extracted using SINDy. In nonlinear systems, a stable limit cycle is not just a simple periodic solution but also encodes the essential nonlinearities of the underlying dynamics, making it a suitable basis for model extraction. Although additional validation under different amplitudes or transient conditions would be valuable, this initial demonstration confirms that the reconstructed equations capture the essential dynamics, with systematic trends and bifurcation behaviour (Fig. 4) further supporting their generality. Beyond reproducing the measured responses, the SINDy coefficients serve as indicators of the balance between stiffness, damping, and nonlinear coupling, which shifts with excitation amplitude. This shows that mechanisms such as viscous losses, acoustic streaming, and secondary radiation leave measurable signatures in the governing equations. Applying the same approach to different object sizes and geometries can reveal how these mechanisms scale in nonlinear acoustic trapping.

Also, future research could apply SINDy to experimental measurements in the acoustically small-object regime to directly compare with Gorkov’s formulation. Although our study was restricted to geometrically spherical objects, it is worth noting that SINDy itself imposes no restrictions on object geometry and can be used for non-spherical objects, too, which could be an expansion of our current study. Further, SINDy could be applied to experimental measurements of also larger objects, using different levitator devices, to quantify more specifically the functional relationships of coefficients and nonlinear terms. This could provide a basis for using levitation as a more comprehensive dynamic testing platform, where variations in SINDy-derived coefficients can reveal material properties that cannot be measured directly using conventional methods.
