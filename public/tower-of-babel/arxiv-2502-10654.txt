Trees with non log-concave independent set sequences
David Galvin

Abstract
We construct a family of trees with independence numbers going to infinity for which the log-concavity relation for the independent set sequence of a tree T \displaystyle T in the family fails at around α ⁡ ( T ) ​ ( 1 − 1 / ( 16 ​ log ⁡ α ⁡ ( T ) ) ) \displaystyle\alpha(T)\left(1-1/(16\log\alpha(T))\right) . Here α ⁡ ( T ) \displaystyle\alpha(T) is the independence number of T \displaystyle T . This resolves a conjecture of Kadrawi and Levit. Keywords : Independent set, unimodality, log-concavity, tree.



1
Introduction

For a graph
\displaystyle G
denote by
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
the size of the largest independent set in
\displaystyle G
(here an independent set is a set of vertices no two of which are adjacent), and for
\displaystyle t\geq 0
denote by
\displaystyle i_{t}(G)
the number of independent sets in
\displaystyle G
of cardinality
\displaystyle t
. The sequence
\displaystyle(i_{t}(G))_{t=0}^{\alpha(G)}
is the
independent set sequence
of
\displaystyle G
.

A sequence
\displaystyle(a_{k})_{k=0}^{n}
is
unimodal
if there is an index
\displaystyle k
(the
mode
of the sequence) with

a_{0}\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{k}\geq a_{k+1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n};

it is
log-concave
if
\displaystyle a_{k}^{2}\geq a_{k-1}a_{k+1}
for all
\displaystyle 2\leq k\leq n-1
, and it is
real-rooted
if the polynomial equation
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}=0
has only real zeroes. Unimodality, log-concavity and real-rootedness are ubiquitous in combinatorics and algebra; see for example the surveys
[
9
,
10
,
24
]
. It is well known that for a positive sequence
\displaystyle(a_{k})_{k=0}^{n}
real-rootedness implies log-concavity which in turn implies unimodality, although none of these implications can be reversed in general (see e.g.
[
8
]
). Unlike its close relative the matching sequence (which is always real-rooted
[
17
]
) the independent set sequence of a graph need not even be unimodal; this fact was first observed by Alavi, Malde, Schwenk and Erdős
[
1
]
who also obtained the remarkable result that the independent set sequence of a graph can exhibit arbitrary patterns of rises and falls.

That observation naturally led them to wonder whether there are families of graphs that have unimodal independent set sequences. One such family is the collection of claw-free graphs (graphs without an induced
\displaystyle K_{1,3}
; these include line graphs). Unimodality of the independent set sequence of claw-free graphs was first established by Hamidoune
[
15
]
and the stronger real-rootedness property was established by Chudnovsky and Seymour
[
12
]
.

Alavi, Malde, Schwenk and Erdős proposed studying two other very natural candidate families — trees and forests. They asked
[
1
, Problem 3]
:

Question 1.1
.

For trees (or perhaps forests), is the vertex independence sequence unimodal?

This question, which is often presented in the literature as a conjecture, has received some attention, with numerous partial results (see for example
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
14
,
16
,
20
,
25
,
26
]
and references therein) but few very general results. For example while on the one hand it is easy to check that paths and stars (the trees with extreme diameters) have unimodal (even log-concave) independent set sequences — this provides a possible motivation for Question
1.1
— on the other hand the status of caterpillars is not known. Here a
caterpillar
is a path with some pendant edges dropped from its vertices (not necessarily the same number of pendant edges at each vertex). Caterpillars are a simple common generalization of paths and stars.

A tempting strengthening of Question
1.1
is to ask, is the independent set sequence of trees always log-concave? Note that log-concavity for trees immediately implies log-concavity for forests, since convolutions of log-concave sequences are log-concave (see e.g.
[
8
]
). This is not true for unimodality.

One reason that the strengthening to log-concavity is tempting is because it may be easier to prove. There are more approaches to log-concavity than there are to unimodality, in part because of the uniformity of the log-concavity relations (note that establishing unimodality often requires identifying the location of the mode, usually a non-trivial task). It is also tempting because computations run by Radcliffe
[
22
]
showed that all trees on 25 or fewer vertices have log-concave independent set sequences. However, when Kadrawi, Levit, Yosef, and Mizrachi extended these computations
[
19
]
they found two trees on 26 vertices that do not have log-concave independent set sequences. Further work by Kadrawi and Levit
[
18
]
exhibited infinite families of trees that do not have log-concave independent set sequences. In
[
23
]
Ramos and Sun used the AI architecture
PatternBoost
, developed by Charton, Ellenberg, Wagner, and Williamson
[
11
]
to find a plethora of essentially unstructured counterexamples to log-concavity on between 27 and 101 vertices.

Kadrawi and Levit raised the issue of where along the independent set sequence of a tree log-concavity can break down. Say that log-concavity of a positive sequence
\displaystyle(a_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
is
broken at
\displaystyle k
if
\displaystyle a_{k}^{2}<a_{k-1}a_{k+1}
. A feature of the two examples found by Kadrawi, Levit, Yosef, and Mizrachi, and of the infinite families of examples constructed by Kadrawi and Levit, is that log-concavity of the independent set sequence is broken only at the last possible place, namely at
\displaystyle\alpha(T)-1
(where
\displaystyle T
is the tree under consideration). But Kadrawi and Levit also gave an ad hoc example of a tree
\displaystyle T
whose independent set sequence is not log-concave, and for which log-concavity is broken slightly earlier, at
\displaystyle\alpha(T)-2
. They made the following natural conjecture:

Conjecture 1.2
.

For every
\displaystyle\ell\geq 1
there is a tree
\displaystyle T=T(\ell)
for which log-concavity of the independent set sequence is broken at
\displaystyle\alpha(T)-\ell
.

In other words, log-cancavity can be broken arbitrarily far from the end of the independent set sequence.

Here we confirm this conjecture, and show that log-concavity can be broken at a distance around
\displaystyle\alpha(T)/(16\log(\alpha(T)))
from
\displaystyle\alpha(T)
(the constant
\displaystyle 16
here is certainly not optimal).

Theorem 1.3
.

For every sufficiently large
\displaystyle t
there is a tree
\displaystyle T_{t}
with
\displaystyle\alpha(T_{t})=(1+t)[2^{t/16}]
, for which log-concavity of the independent set sequence is broken at
\displaystyle t[2^{t/16}]+2
.

Observe that

\frac{(1+t)[2^{t/16}]}{16\log\left((1+t)[2^{t/16}]\right)}=(1+o(1))[2^{t/16}]

justifying the assertion made immediate before the statement of Theorem
1.3
.
(Here and throughout all asymptotic (
\displaystyle o(\cdot)
) statements are to be interpreted as
\displaystyle t\rightarrow\infty
.)

Our construction of
\displaystyle T_{t}
is inspired by the constructions of Kadrawi and Levit.
In Section
2
we give the construction and proof, and we briefly discuss the result in Section
3
.

2
Construction and proof

We will start with a more general construction of a family of graphs
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
for integers
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle t
. The tree
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
is rooted. The root (call it
\displaystyle v
) has
\displaystyle m
children,
\displaystyle w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
. Each
\displaystyle w_{i}
has
\displaystyle t
children,
\displaystyle x_{i}^{1},\ldots,x_{i}^{t}
, and each
\displaystyle x_{i}^{j}
has one child,
\displaystyle y_{i}^{j}
. It is easy to check that
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
has
\displaystyle 1+m+2mt
vertices and that it satisfies
\displaystyle\alpha(T_{m,t,1})=(1+t)m
(the unique largest independent set is
\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}\cup\{y_{i}^{j}:1\leq i,j\leq t\}
). See Figure
1
for an example of this construction. Note that
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
is an instance of a
spherically symmetric
tree — a rooted tree in which the number of children a vertex has depends only on its distance from the root.

The tree
\displaystyle T_{4,3,1}
. The root is the topmost vertex.

In this section we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
\displaystyle t\leq m=m(t)\leq 2^{t/16}
we have that for large enough
\displaystyle t
the log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
is broken at
\displaystyle mt+2
; that is,

\left(i_{T_{m,t,1}}(mt+2)\right)^{2}<i_{T_{m,t,1}}(mt+1)i_{T_{m,t,1}}(mt+3).

Theorem
1.3
follows immediately on taking
\displaystyle m=[2^{t/16}]
.

As a simple special case of Theorem
2.1
we see that the tree
\displaystyle T_{t,t,1}
on
\displaystyle 1+t+t^{2}
vertices has independence number
\displaystyle t+t^{2}
and has log-concavity of the independent set sequence broken at
\displaystyle t^{2}+2
, so for large
\displaystyle t
we have that log-concavity is broken at
\displaystyle\alpha(T_{t,t,1})-(1+o(1)\sqrt{\alpha(T_{t,t,1})})
. Computation suggests that in fact log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T_{t,t,1}
is broken at
\displaystyle t^{2}+2
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 4
.

Proof.

(Proof of Theorem
2.1
)
Denote by
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(k)
the number of independent sets in
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
that do not include the root
\displaystyle v
, and by
\displaystyle h_{m,t}(k)
the number that do include
\displaystyle v
. Observe that on deleting
\displaystyle v
and all its neighbors
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
reduces to a perfect matching on
\displaystyle mt
edges, a graph that admits independent sets of size only up to
\displaystyle mt
. It follows that
\displaystyle h_{m,t}(k)=0
for
\displaystyle k\geq mt+2
, and that

i_{T_{m,t,1}}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}h_{m,t}(k)+f_{m,t}(k)&\mbox{if $\displaystyle k\leq mt+1$}\\
f_{m,t}(k)&\mbox{if $\displaystyle k\geq mt+2$}.\end{array}\right.

(1)

Further, we have
\displaystyle h_{m,t}(mt+1)=2^{mt}
. So our goal is to show that

\left(f_{m,t}(mt+2)\right)^{2}<(2^{mt}+f_{m,t}(mt+1))f_{m,t}(mt+3),

which is implied by

\left(f_{m,t}(mt+2)\right)^{2}<2^{mt}f_{m,t}(mt+3).

(2)

We will establish (
2
) by showing that for
\displaystyle t\leq m\leq 2^{t/16}
we have

f_{m,t}(mt+3)\geq\binom{m}{3}2^{mt-3t}~~~\mbox{and}~~~f_{m,t}(mt+2)=\left(1+o(1)\right)\binom{m}{2}2^{mt-2t}.

(3)

That (
3
) implies (
2
) for large
\displaystyle t
and
\displaystyle m
satisfying
\displaystyle m\leq 2^{t-1}
(not just
\displaystyle m\leq 2^{t/16}
) is easily established.

We get the lower bound on
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(t^{2}+3)
in (
3
) by considering those independent sets of size
\displaystyle mt+3
that include exactly
\displaystyle 3
vertices from
\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}
(and so necessarily don’t contain the root). There are
\displaystyle\binom{m}{3}
ways to select the three vertices. Once they are chosen the remaining
\displaystyle mt
vertices come from a graph that consists of
\displaystyle 3t
isolated vertices and
\displaystyle mt-3t
isolated edges, for which there are
\displaystyle 2^{mt-3t}
options.

For the asymptotic determination of
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)
in (
3
):

•

Arguing exactly as we did for
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+3)
, there are
\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}2^{mt-2t}
independent sets in
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
of size
\displaystyle mt+2
that include exactly
\displaystyle 2
vertices from
\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}
(and so necessarily don’t contain the root).

•

Next, there is no contribution to
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)
from independent sets that include exactly
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
vertices from
\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}
(the maximum size of such independent sets is
\displaystyle mt+1
).

•

The remaining independent sets that contribute to
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(t^{2}+2)
have
\displaystyle s
vertices from
\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}
, where
\displaystyle s
satisfies
\displaystyle 3\leq s\leq m
(
\displaystyle\binom{m}{s}
options). The remaining
\displaystyle mt-(s-2)
vertices come from a graph that consists of
\displaystyle st
isolated vertices and
\displaystyle mt-st
independent edges. To construct all such independent sets, we can first choose
\displaystyle\ell
of the isolated edges from which not to select a vertex, then choose a vertex from each of the remaining edges, and finally choose
\displaystyle(s-2)-\ell
isolated vertices to not select to complete the independent set. Here
\displaystyle\ell
satisfies
\displaystyle 0\leq\ell\leq\min\{s-2,mt-st\}
.

It follows from the discussion above that for each
\displaystyle 3\leq s\leq m
and
\displaystyle 0\leq\ell\leq\min\{s-2,mt-st\}
we have a term

\binom{m}{s}\binom{mt-st}{\ell}\binom{st}{(s-2)-\ell}2^{mt-st-\ell}

contributing to
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)
, and that these are the only terms contributing to
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)
aside from
\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}2^{mt-2t}
(which we hope to show is the dominant term). Since there at most
\displaystyle m^{2}
such terms, we complete the verification of the asymptotic expression for
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)
in (
3
) by showing that

\frac{\binom{m}{s}\binom{mt-st}{\ell}\binom{st}{(s-2)-\ell}2^{mt-st-\ell}}{\binom{m}{2}2^{mt-2t}}=o\left(m^{-2})\right)

or equiavlently

\frac{\binom{m}{s}\binom{mt-st}{\ell}\binom{st}{(s-2)-\ell}}{2^{(s-2)t+\ell}}=o(1).

(4)

Using (here and throughout) the simple bound
\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\leq n^{k}
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\binom{m}{s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle m^{s}=2^{s\log m},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\binom{mt-st}{\ell}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle(mt)^{s}\leq 2^{2s\log m}

(the latter using
\displaystyle\ell\leq s
and
\displaystyle t\leq m
) and (for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, and using
\displaystyle s,t\leq m
)

\binom{st}{(s-2)-\ell}\leq(st)^{s}\leq 2^{2s\log m}.

Lower bounding
\displaystyle(s-2)t+\ell\geq st/3
(recall
\displaystyle s\geq 3
) we get that the left-hand side of (

2^{5s\log m-(st)/3}

As long as
\displaystyle m\leq 2^{t/16}
this is
\displaystyle o(1)
.

This concludes the verification that
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)=(1+o(1))\binom{m}{2}2^{mt-2t}
, and so the verification that the log-concavity of
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
is broken at
\displaystyle mt+2
for sufficiently large
\displaystyle t
as long as
\displaystyle m\leq 2^{t/16}
.
∎

3
Discussion

There is a heuristic that explains why we might expect the log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
to be broken at
\displaystyle mt+2
, but not at any subsequent point. Recall from (
1
) that for
\displaystyle k\geq mt+2
we have
\displaystyle i_{k}(T_{m,t,1})=h_{m,t}(k)
. But
\displaystyle h_{m,t}(k)
is the number of independent sets of size
\displaystyle k
in the graph
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}-v
, which consists of
\displaystyle m
disjoint copies of a tree
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
, which in turn consists of
\displaystyle t
paths of length
\displaystyle 2
all sharing a common start vertex. We observe (see Lemma
3.3
below) that the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
is log-concave for all
\displaystyle t
, and so (by the fact that the convolution of log-concave sequences is log-concave, see e.g.
[
8
]
), the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}-v
is log-concave. So certainly log-concavity for the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
is not broken at
\displaystyle mt+3
or later. However, there is a chance that it might be broken at
\displaystyle mt+2
: we have
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)^{2}\geq f_{m,t}(mt+1)f_{m,t}(mt+3)
, but it may well be the case (and indeed is, as we have shown) that the boost given to the right-hand side by the addition of
\displaystyle h_{m,t}(mt+1)
to
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+1)
results in the reversed inequality
\displaystyle f_{m,t}(mt+2)^{2}<\left(f_{m,t}(mt+1)+h_{m,t}(mt+1)\right)f_{m,t}(mt+3)
.

We do not know how far further beyond
\displaystyle\Theta\left(\alpha(T)/\log\alpha(T)\right)
we can push the point where log-concavity can be broken.

Question 3.1
.

Is there a
\displaystyle c>0
such that there are trees
\displaystyle T
with arbitrarily large independence number
\displaystyle\alpha(T)
, for which the log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T
is broken at no later than around
\displaystyle(1-c)\alpha(T)
?

It was shown by Levit and Mandrescu
[
21
]
that the independent set sequence of a tree
\displaystyle T
is monotone decreasing from
\displaystyle\lceil(2\alpha(T)-1)/3\rceil
on. So in order to use points at which log-concavity is broken as an avenue to producing a tree with non-unimodal independent set sequence (and so answer Question
1.1
in the negative), it would be necessary to work with examples that answer Question
3.1
in the affirmative with
\displaystyle c>1/3
.

All of our computations show that
\displaystyle T_{m,t,1}
either has log-concave independent set sequence or that log-concavity is broken at one point only, namely at
\displaystyle mt+2
. Furthermore, all of the counterexamples to log-concavity of the independent set sequence of a tree found in
[
18
,
19
,
23
]
have log-concavity broken at one point only. After reading an earlier version of this paper
[
13
]
, Ferenc Bencs observed computationally that deeper spherically symmetric trees can exhibit non-log-concavity of the independent set sequence at more than one place
[
7
]
. Indeed, denote by
\displaystyle T(2^{m}1^{n})
the spherically symmetric tree in which all vertices at distances
\displaystyle 0
through
\displaystyle m-1
from the root have two children, and all vertices at distance
\displaystyle m
through
\displaystyle m+n-1
have one child. A
Mathematica
calculation shows that, for example

•

log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T(2^{4}1^{9})
is broken at two places;

•

for
\displaystyle T(2^{5}1^{15})
it is broken at three places;

•

for
\displaystyle T(2^{6}1^{17})
it is broken at eight places;

•

for
\displaystyle T(2^{7}1^{23})
it is broken at sixteen places; and

•

for
\displaystyle T(2^{8}1^{27})
it is broken at twenty-four places.

Question 3.2
.

For each
\displaystyle k\geq 1
is there an
\displaystyle m=m(k)
and
\displaystyle n=n(k)
such that the log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T(2^{m}1^{n})
is broken at more than
\displaystyle k
places?

In
[
4
]
Bautista-Ramos used a different family of trees to verify that indeed for every
\displaystyle k\geq 1
there is a tree
\displaystyle T_{k}
such that the log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle T_{k}
is broken at more than
\displaystyle k
places.

We end with the promised verification of the log-concavity of the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
\displaystyle t
, the independent set sequence of
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
is log-concave.

Proof.

The independence number of
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
is
\displaystyle t+1
. For
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq t+1
the number of independent sets of size
\displaystyle k
in
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
is

\binom{t}{k-1}+2^{k}\binom{t}{k},

the first term coming from independent sets that include the root of
\displaystyle S_{t,2}
(the vertex in common to all the paths of length
\displaystyle 2
) — in this case the remaining
\displaystyle k-1
vertices must be selected from
\displaystyle t
isolated vertices — and the second term coming from independent sets that don’t include the root — in this case all
\displaystyle k
vertices must be selected from
\displaystyle t
independent edges. So our task is to establish that for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq t
,

\left(\binom{t}{k-1}+2^{k}\binom{t}{k}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\binom{t}{k-2}+2^{k-1}\binom{t}{k-1}\right)\left(\binom{t}{k}+2^{k+1}\binom{t}{k+1}\right).

(5)

Expanding out and using

\binom{t}{k}^{2}-\binom{t}{k-1}\binom{t}{k+1}=\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{t}{k}\binom{t+1}{k}

and the equivalent identity with
\displaystyle k
everywhere replaced with
\displaystyle k-1
, after some rearranging (
5
) is seen to be implied by

\frac{2^{k}}{k+1}\binom{t}{k}\binom{t+1}{k}\geq 2\binom{t}{k-2}\binom{t}{k+1},

which in turn is equivalent to

\frac{2^{k}(t+1)}{k}\geq\frac{2(k-1)(t-k)}{t-k+2}.

(6)

We have
\displaystyle 2^{k}/k\geq 2
for
\displaystyle k\geq 1
, so (
6
) is implied by
\displaystyle t+1\geq(k-1)(t-k)/(t-k+2)
.
This is evident for
\displaystyle k=t
, and for
\displaystyle k<t
it is implied by
\displaystyle t+1\geq k-1
,
which is true for
\displaystyle k\leq t
.
∎
