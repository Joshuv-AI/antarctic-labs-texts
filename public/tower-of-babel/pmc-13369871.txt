White matter structure-function coupling in neonatal brain and its association with Autism-related traits in early childhood
Authors: Zhe Zhang, Chi Zhang, Xinlin Zhang, Junrui Zhang, Yongxuan Xu, Wenjie Dou, Mingyang Li, Weihao Zheng, Baoming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The neonatal brain undergoes a rapid maturation of white matter pathways, yet how structural refinement within these tracts drives concurrent functional reorganization remains poorly understood. Using multimodal MRI data from 399 infants (348 term-born and 51 preterm-born) from the Developing Human Connectome Project, here we characterize the developmental trajectories of white matter structure-function coupling (SFC) and examine its associations with myelination (indexed by the T1w/T2w ratio), prematurity, and later autism-related traits. We find that SFC strength and its temporal variance develop heterogeneously across major fiber tracts, in which the inferior longitudinal and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi exhibit a marked left-lateralized tendency, supporting the formation of nascent language and social cognition. The association between SFC and myelination is also dynamic, weakening over time in most tracts but strengthening selectively in the inferior longitudinal fasciculus. Preterm infants exhibit significantly reduced SFC across most tracts compared to term-born peers. Notably, reduced SFC in the right corticospinal tract partially mediates the association between prematurity and higher autism-related traits at 18 months. Furthermore, neonatal SFC predicts these traits only in preterm infants. These findings delineate a dynamic, tract-specific interplay between structure and function in early-life brain and establish neonatal SFC as a key link between prematurity and subsequent neurodevelopmental risk.

Introduction

The neonatal period is a critical phase of brain development, characterized by a rapid, experience-dependent maturation of white matter (WM) pathways that establish the fundamental anatomical framework for the emergence of cognitive and behavioral functions [1, 2]. This early stage is especially sensitive to biological and environmental factors, like premature birth, which greatly alters normal development of WM and is linked to a higher risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [3]. Advances in neuroimaging have greatly improved our ability to explore the structure and function of neonatal WM in vivo. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), for instance, reveals a progressive maturation gradient of WM microstructure, typically beginning in primary sensorimotor pathways before extending to higher-order association tracts [4, 5]. Meanwhile, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has shown that BOLD signals within WM are detectable and biologically meaningful rather than mere noise [6–8]. Recent studies have further identified intrinsic functional organization within neonatal WM itself [9], and have also shown a shift from local to more distributed network organization accompanied by increasing functional integration and segregation during this early developmental period [10]. Note that these studies have primarily characterized the structural or functional maturation of neonatal WM in isolation. How structural refinement of neonatal WM shapes its concurrent functional reorganization, and how this relationship is affected by adverse events like premature birth, is still not fully understood. Revealing the precise relationship between these two elements is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying early typical and atypical brain development.

The structural architecture of WM fundamentally constrains and facilitates its functional activities across multiple spatiotemporal scales [11–13]. Structure-function coupling (SFC), which reflects the intricate interplay between the structural architecture of the brain and its emergent functional dynamics, has increasingly been recognized as a more informative marker for cognitive resilience and performance than either structural or functional property alone [14, 15]. In neonates, SFC within gray matter (neocortex) exhibits an emerging hierarchical organization, typically stronger in unimodal cortices and weaker in transmodal cortices, potentially reflecting a fundamental principle of early brain organization [16–18]. Despite these useful insights, the patterns and developmental trajectories of SFC within the WM itself remain largely uncharted. Moreover, the neonatal WM undergoes rapid myelination, a critical developmental process that enhances the speed and efficiency of neural signal transmission along WM pathways [19, 20]. Recent evidence suggests that the progressive myelination substantially influences the relationship between structural wiring and corresponding functional communication [21]. However, it remains unclear how the spatiotemporal pattern of myelination drives the emerging heterogeneity in SFC across different WM tracts.

Moreover, the investigation of neonatal WM SFC is critically important for understanding neurodevelopmental disorders like ASD – widely conceptualized as a disorder of atypical brain connectivity – given that early disruptions in this coupling are hypothesized to underlie its developmental origins [22, 23]. Altered SFC, often manifesting as reduced coupling between structural pathways and functional dynamics, is a consistent feature in ASD and correlates with symptom severity, reflecting inefficient neural communication [24, 25]. Critically, antecedents of these disturbances emerge neonatally. Studies in infants at high familial risk demonstrate that subtle neonatal microstructural alterations in WM tracts essential for socio-emotional processing (e.g., cingulum, uncinate fasciculus) predict later ASD diagnosis and symptom severity [26, 27]. This converging evidence – linking established ASD to atypical SFC and tracing its origins to neonatal WM – supports the central hypothesis that early disruptions in WM structural and functional interplay may serve as preclinical biomarkers predicting ASD developmental trajectories.

The primary goal of the present study was to characterize the spatiotemporal trajectories of neonatal SFC within WM tracts. Utilizing multimodal MRI data from a large cohort of 399 infants (348 term-born and 51 preterm-born) from the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP), we employed a tract-wise analytical framework to quantify SFC, defined as the local consistency between functional correlation tensor (FCT) and diffusion tensor (DT) [28, 29]. We also investigated the contribution of myelination, indexed by the T1w/T2w ratio, to SFC within individual tracts. We compared SFC patterns between preterm-born and term-born infants to elucidate alterations associated with prematurity, a known risk factor for ASD. We further conducted mediation analyses to explore how neonatal SFC influences the relationship between prematurity and later neurodevelopmental outcomes at 1.5 years of age, specifically targeting autism-related traits as measured by the Quantitative Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (Q-CHAT). Finally, we evaluated whether neonatal SFC could serve as an early predictor of later autism-related traits. We demonstrated that neonatal SFC exhibited dynamic, heterogeneous developmental trajectories across WM fiber tracts and could serve as a sensitive predictor for early autism-related traits, particularly in vulnerable populations such as preterm infants.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study utilized minimally preprocessed data from the fourth release of the dHCP (http://developingconnectome.org/). Neonatal datasets that failed dHCP quality control procedures or exhibited significant imaging artifacts were excluded from the final analysis. The final analyzed cohort comprised 399 neonates, including 348 term-born and 51 preterm-born infants. Preterm birth was defined as gestational age (GA) at birth < 37 weeks; term birth was defined as GA ≥ 37 weeks. For term-born neonates, the median GA at birth was 40.36 weeks (interquartile range [IQR] = 2.43 weeks), and their median postmenstrual age (PMA) at scanning was 40.43 weeks (IQR = 4.00 weeks). For preterm-born neonates, the median GA at birth was 32.5 weeks (IQR = 6.14 weeks). They were scanned at term-equivalent age, with a median PMA at scanning of 41.14 weeks (IQR = 3.29 weeks). Details regarding the inclusion/exclusion criteria and attrition at each processing step are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1. We also created an age-matched subset of term-born infants (n = 51) for comparison with the preterm-born group. Specifically, for each preterm infant, one term-born infant with the closest PMA at scan was selected from the larger term-born cohort using one-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement. As a result, the matched term-born and preterm-born groups did not differ in PMA at scan (t = 0.06, p > 0.95). The study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards approved by the relevant institutional review board (UK National Research Ethics Authority, reference 14/LO/1169), and written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to participation. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

Neuroimaging data were acquired at the Evelina Newborn Imaging Centre (Evelina London Children’s Hospital) using a 3 Tesla Philips Achieva scanner equipped with a dedicated 32-channel neonatal head coil and infant imaging system [30]. Further details on acquisition protocols are available in the dHCP data release documentation.

Anatomical preprocessing was performed using the dHCP structural pipeline, including bias field correction, super-resolution reconstruction to 0.5 mm isotropic resolution, registration of T1w to T2w images, brain extraction, and tissue segmentation on T2w volumes. Myelination index maps (T1w/T2w ratio) were obtained directly from the dHCP preprocessed data release.

DWI preprocessing included four steps: (1) correction for susceptibility-induced distortions using fieldmap data ; (2) correction for eddy currents and subject motion, including outlier slice processing; (3) upsampling of DWI data to 1.5 mm isotropic resolution ; and (4) registration of corrected diffusion data to the subject’s T2w structural image using boundary-based registration.

Resting-state fMRI data were initially processed using the dHCP functional pipeline and then underwent further custom preprocessing using scripts integrating the DPARSF toolbox [31]. To mitigate motion artifacts, frames with frame-wise displacement (FD) > 0.3 mm, together with one preceding and two subsequent frames, were censored. After this procedure, infants retained on average approximately 70% of the total volumes (~1600 volumes) for analysis [32]. Following current recommendations for WM fMRI analysis [33], subject-specific WM masks were derived from the tissue segmentation and used to constrain all subsequent WM functional analyses to WM voxels in each infant’s native space. Selected resting-state fMRI volumes were registered to each infant’s native T2w image using FSL’s FLIRT. Nuisance regression was then performed to remove linear trends, signals derived from 24 motion parameters (Friston 24-parameter model) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), without regressing out the mean WM signal. Temporal bandpass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) was subsequently applied. Spatial smoothing was performed in native space using a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 2 mm). All subsequent WM functional analyses, including estimation of the FCT and derivation of SFC metrics, were conducted in each subject’s native space.

FCT-DT consistency calculation

Local functional architecture was characterized using the FCT approach [34]. The FCT for a given voxel describes the spatial profile of temporal correlations between that voxel’s BOLD time series and those of its immediate neighbors. Analogous to the DT capturing water diffusion directionality, the FCT captures the directionality of local functional correlations. FCT maps were computed for all WM voxels for each neonate using the preprocessed resting-state fMRI data. Consistent with previous studies, SFC at the voxel level was quantified as the consistency between FCT and DT using a tensor difference metric [29]. Temporal SFC variance was estimated using a validated tapered sliding-window approach following established protocols [35]. BOLD time series from each WM voxel were segmented into overlapping windows of 39.2 s (100 TRs) with a 3.92 s step size (10 TRs), yielding 160 windows per infant. This window length was selected based on prior evidence that durations between 30–60 s balance temporal resolution with reliable covariance estimation [36]. Within each window, FCT-DT consistency was computed to generate a time-resolved sequence of 160 SFC maps, enabling fine-grained assessment of SFC fluctuations relative to its mean over the scan duration.

Tract-wise structure-function coupling analysis

Major WM tracts were reconstructed using probabilistic tractography implemented in MRtrix3 [37]. Fiber orientation distributions (FODs) were estimated voxel-wise using multi-shell, multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD). Multi-tissue response functions (WM, GM, CSF) were estimated (msmt_5tt method with an FA threshold of 0.2) to guide WM response function selection in low-anisotropy neonatal tissue. Signal intensity normalization across subjects was performed. Probabilistic tractography was performed using the iFOD2 algorithm generating 2 million streamlines (parameters: step size = 0.75 mm, max angle = 45°, FOD amplitude cutoff = 0.05, min/max length = 20/200 mm). Individual fiber tracts were segmented using the baby automated fiber quantification (babyAFQ) toolbox [20], employing waypoint regions of interest defined in template space. Following iterative outlier streamline removal, metrics along the core trajectory of each tract were quantified by resampling each fiber into 100 equidistant nodes. Voxel-wise FCT-DT consistency maps and T1w/T2w ratio maps were further sampled at each node using spline interpolation. A total of 20 major tracts were analyzed: bilateral anterior thalamic radiation (ATR), corticospinal tract (CS), cingulum cingulate (CC), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), middle longitudinal fasciculus (MLF), inferior frontal occipital fasciculus (IFOF), superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), uncinate fasciculus (UNC), arcuate fasciculus (AF), forceps minor (FcMi), and forceps major (FcMa). The basic flow of studying tract-wise SFC is depicted in Figure S2.

To evaluate the developmental trajectories of SFC and T1w/T2w in each fiber bundle from 38–44 weeks of PMA, we calculated the overall tract profiles using the area under the curve (AUC) across the 100 nodes. K-means clustering and principal component analysis (PCA) were finally employed to identify common patterns of developmental change across the fiber bundles.

Prediction analysis

To assess the predictive utility of neonatal SFC for later neurodevelopment, we employed support vector regression (SVR) with a linear kernel. The target variable was the Q-CHAT score obtained at 1.5 years of age. Models included tract-wise SFC values (AUC) as predictors. To control for PMA at scan, sex, and mean FD while avoiding data leakage, nuisance regression was performed separately within each training fold of the 5-fold cross-validation procedure [38]. The regression model fitted in the training set was then applied to residualize the corresponding test set using the parameters estimated from the training data only. Predictive accuracy within this framework was quantified by the Pearson correlation coefficient between observed and predicted Q-CHAT scores across all validation folds. The statistical significance of the prediction accuracy was assessed using permutation testing (10,000 iterations) by repeatedly shuffling the Q-CHAT scores. Feature importance was assessed by calculating the average absolute coefficient (AAC) for each predictor across the cross-validation folds.

Statistical analysis

Demographic differences (age) between term and preterm groups were assessed using independent two-sample t-tests, while differences in sex distribution were assessed using chi-square tests. Group comparisons (term vs. preterm) of tract-wise SFC (AUC) were conducted using non-parametric permutation testing (10,000 iterations). Associations between tract-wise SFC and T1w/T2w were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Mediation analyses were conducted to test whether SFC mediated the relationship between PT and Q-CHAT score in preterm-born infants. PT, SFC, and Q-CHAT score were entered as independent, mediator, and dependent variables, respectively. Statistical significance for all tract-wise analyses involving parallel tests was set at P < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons across the 20 tracts using the false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Results

Tract-wise SFC patterns in term-born infants

We observed a pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity in SFC patterns across distinct WM fiber bundles in term-born neonates (Fig. 1a). Overall tract-wise SFC increased with PMA from 38–44 weeks across all tracts examined. The increase in the relative rate of SFC varied significantly across tracts: the bilateral ILF, right IFOF, and bilateral MLF exhibited the steepest increases, whereas the CS and the CC showed the slowest increase (Fig. 1b). Clustering analysis based on these PMA-related SFC trajectories identified three discrete developmental profiles (Fig. 1c). Profile 1 comprised bilateral ILF, left IFOF and left MLF, Profile 2 included bilateral CS and left CC, and Profile 3 contained most tracts, including the right IFOF, bilateral AF, bilateral UNC, bilateral ATR, bilateral SLF, right MLF, right CC, FcMi, and FcMa. Multidimensional characteristics defining these profiles are presented in Fig. 1d. Specifically, Profile 1 showed high change rates, strong linear slopes, and high temporal stability, with minimal nonlinear contributions and moderate curvature. Profile 2 displayed markedly higher nonlinear contributions and curvature, alongside moderate rates of change and stability. To characterize the moment-to-moment dynamics of SFC, we quantified its temporal variance across the duration of the resting-state fMRI scan. Unlike the strength of SFC, its temporal variance revealed substantial hemispheric asymmetry across bilateral WM pathways (Fig. 2a). We observed that the left CS, left CC, left ILF, left IFOF, and right UNC consistently exhibited higher temporal variance than their right or left-hemisphere counterparts throughout the PMA window. Associations between SFC and T1w/T2w in term-born infants

To investigate the biological correlates of SFC, we examined its associations with the T1w/T2w ratio, an index of myelination. The T1w/T2w ratio increased with PMA (38–44 weeks) across all white matter tracts examined (Figure S5). We then assessed the relationship between SFC and T1w/T2w in individual tracts. The strength of this correlation varied substantially across tracts (Fig. 3a), with the strongest correlations observed in the FcMi, bilateral ATR, and bilateral UNC, and the weakest ones in the bilateral CS and bilateral CC. Examination of the developmental trajectories of these correlations revealed dynamic changes with age. The correlation strength between SFC and T1w/T2w (SFC~ T1w/T2w) significantly decreased with advancing PMA in most tracts analyzed, including the bilateral ATR, bilateral CS, bilateral MLF, bilateral IFOF, bilateral UNC, right AF, FcMi, and FcMa (all p < 0.05, FDR-corrected). Conversely, a significant PMA-related increase in the SFC~T1w/T2w occurred only in the right CC and left ILF (p < 0.05, FDR-corrected; Fig. 3b). Similar patterns were observed in the relationships between temporal SFC variance and T1w/T2w (SFC variance~T1w/T2w) and between SFC variance~T1w/T2w and PMA as well (Figure S6). Tract-wise SFC changes in preterm-born infants

Nonparametric permutation testing revealed significant group differences in both tract-wise SFC strength and its temporal variance (Fig. 4 and S7). Compared to their matched term-born peers (n = 51), the preterm-born group exhibited significantly lower SFC in 18 out of the 20 tracts and reduced temporal SFC variance in 12 out of the 20 tracts (all p < 0.05, FDR-corrected). We further explored group differences in the correlation between T1w/T2w and SFC metrics. Several tracts showed stronger associations between T1w/T2w and both SFC and its temporal variance in the preterm cohort relative to term-born counterparts, suggesting that myelination may play an increasingly prominent role in shaping structure-function correspondence following preterm birth (Figure S8). To test whether tract-wise SFC changes account for the association between prematurity and subsequent neurodevelopment, we conducted a mediation analysis with PT as the predictor, either SFC or its temporal variance as the mediator, and the Q-CHAT score at 18 months as the outcome. Prediction of autism-related traits

We further investigated whether neonatal white matter SFC could predict autism-related traits at 18 months of age, which were assessed via the Q-CHAT. The SVR models used neonatal tract-wise SFC (AUC) from 20 tracts to predict Q-CHAT scores separately in the term (n = 348) and preterm (n = 51) cohorts, controlling for PMA at scan, sex and head motion during MRI scanning (mean frame displacement). Significant prediction accuracy (Pearson’s correlation between predicted and observed scores, evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation) was observed in the preterm cohort (r = 0.40, p = 0.009, permutation test; Fig. 6), but not in the term-born cohort (r = 0.05, p = 0.529). Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive characterization of SFC within WM pathways during the critical neonatal period. By analyzing a large, multimodal imaging cohort, we reveal four key findings. First, the maturation of SFC between 38 and 44 weeks PMA is a highly heterogeneous process, defined by distinct, tract-specific developmental trajectories and emerging hemispheric asymmetries. Second, the relationship between SFC and myelination is surprisingly dynamic, with a general trend of developmental “decoupling” that is punctuated by tract-specific exceptions. Third, premature birth leads to a widespread reduction of SFC at term-equivalent age. Fourth, these early alterations in SFC are clinically significant, as they partially mediate the impact of prematurity on later autism-related traits and hold predictive value for these traits specifically within the vulnerable preterm population.

Our findings reveal a complex, non-uniform maturational landscape for neonatal WM SFC that both supports and refines existing models of brain development [39]. Consistent with previous studies focusing separately on structural or functional maturation of the neonatal brain, we observed significant hemispheric asymmetry in SFC maturation [40, 41]. Specifically, the pronounced left-lateralized increase in the temporal variance of SFC within the ILF and IFOF may reflect the nascent specialization of these pathways for the dynamic integration of visual, linguistic, and social-emotional information—foundational processes for later-developing lateralized functions [42]. More importantly, while global SFC increases with PMA, the divergent trajectories across tracts underscore a complex interplay between structural refinement and functional reorganization during this critical period [43]. SFC development does not strictly follow simple spatial gradients (like posterior-to-anterior or bottom-up) derived from structural maturation alone. Instead, it reflects the emerging functional demands of the perinatal period, prioritizing the rapid enhancement of sensory processing capabilities while other networks follow distinct developmental timelines. Rapid and linear increases were prominent in long-range association tracts like the ILF and IFOF, pathways essential for integrating sensory inputs and supporting early cognitive functions such as language and perception [44, 45]. This pattern aligns with the “speed-up” hypothesis, suggesting that tracts less myelinated at birth undergo an accelerated postnatal myelination driven by experience-dependent mechanisms [2]. In contrast, tracts like the CS and CC exhibited slower and non-linear maturation. While the trajectory of the CS may reflect initial rapid development followed by activity-dependent refinement crucial for motor control [46], the trajectory of the CC likely indicates experience-modulated development of the circuits involved in emotional and social processing, domains implicated in ASD [47]. These patterns support the “spatial-gradient” hypothesis, where the WM maturation progresses along organized spatial gradients [20]. In addition, tracts showing relatively stable SFC changes, including the AF, UNC, ATR, FcMi, and FcMa, may have undergone a substantial maturation before the term-equivalent age or follow prolonged developmental timelines, consistent with the “starts-first/finishes-first” hypothesis [48]. Collectively, these findings suggest that neonatal SFC development is heterogeneous and shaped by emerging functional demands rather than reflecting a single maturational principle. This interpretation is conceptually aligned with recent evidence from adult psychiatric disorders showing that WM functional abnormalities are anatomically selective and could not be fully explained by structural alterations alone [49], further supporting the biological relevance of tract-level SFC phenotypes in brain development.

Although both SFC and T1w/T2w generally increased with PMA, their developmental and hemispheric patterns showed only partial overlap. This dissociation suggests that neonatal SFC maturation is influenced by a complex interplay of microstructural factors, with myelination being one of several contributing elements whose impact varies across different tracts and developmental stages [50]. The variability in correlation strength between SFC and T1w/T2w across tracts indicates regional differences in the primary drivers of SFC during this period [51]. Moreover, the relationship between SFC and T1w/T2w evolved with PMA. In most tracts, the correlation was weakened as PMA increased from 38–44 weeks, indicating a progressive decoupling between myelination and SFC during this late‑gestation window. Early SFC gains are likely driven by the onset of myelination, whereas later structural-functional tuning may rely more on other microstructural properties or activity‑dependent mechanisms [52]. Notably, exceptions to this trend were observed in the right CC and left ILF, which exhibited increasing correlations. These tracts may be undergoing distinct maturational phases where myelination become more closely coupled with structural-functional interaction [53]. The right CC, which is crucial for early default model network activity and future socio-emotional processing, faces significant demands as neonates adapt to the extra-uterine environment [54]. Similarly, the left ILF, connecting occipital visual areas with temporal lobe structures, is essential for object recognition, visual memory, and language precursors [55]. The strengthening of SFC-myelination coupling in these pathways suggests that myelination may serve as the primary biological basis driving early hemispheric specialized improvements in SFC within this developmental window. These findings indicate that neonatal SFC is not a static readout of myelination, but a dynamic marker of tract-specific white matter reorganization whose biological basis shifts across developmental stages.

Preterm infants exhibited significantly reduced SFC across multiple WM tracts, even when assessed at term-equivalent PMA. This reduction underscores the profound impact of premature birth on brain development, extending beyond mere developmental delays to reflect disruptions incurred during the critical late gestational period and the challenges associated with early extra-uterine exposure [56, 57]. Despite increased sensory stimulation in the extra-uterine environment, the extended postnatal experience of preterm infants does not normalize SFC patterns to those of term peers, highlighting the irreplaceable role of the intrauterine environment in orchestrating coordinated WM structure-function dynamics [58]. The observed SFC alterations provide a plausible early neural substrate underlying the heightened susceptibility of preterm infants to cognitive, language, and socio-emotional difficulties [59]. These findings highlight the need for interventions targeting disrupted SFC maturation in preterm infants. It is worth noting that our findings of reduced coupling in preterm infants contrast with at least one recent report that suggested an “overgrowth” of SFC in preterm brains at term-equivalent age. In that study, the authors measured SFC across the neocortex and found higher coupling values in preterm infants than full-term infants, interpreting this as a potentially compensatory or pathological increase [17]. The discrepancy with our tract-level findings may reflect both methodological and biological differences. Specifically, the previous study by Wang et al. quantified SFC in a cortical network framework, whereas our approach focused on major WM tracts and defined SFC as the FA-weighted local consistency between the FCT and DT. Biologically, prematurity may have different effects across cortical and WM systems: higher cortical SFC may reflect atypically increased or less differentiated coupling, whereas lower WM SFC may reflect impaired WM maturation, including delayed myelination and reduced microstructural integrity [1, 60]. Mediation analysis further revealed that reduced SFC in the right CS partially mediates the association between prematurity and autism-related traits at 18 months. This finding suggests that disrupted maturation of the right CS may serve as an early neural substrate linking premature birth to later motor atypicality. Given the prevalence of early motor difficulties in ASD [61], reduced SFC in this tract may reflect a developmental vulnerability in preterm infants that contributes to broader autism-related behaviors.

Our study reveals that neonatal SFC could predict autism-related traits in preterm infants, as measured by the Q-CHAT. Such association was not observed in term-born infants, suggesting that SFC may serve as a sensitive biomarker for neurodevelopmental vulnerability associated with prematurity [62]. The variability in SFC among preterm infants possibly reflects different degrees of developmental disruptions or neurological insults incurred during the critical late gestational period. These disruptions could have long-term consequences for behavioral outcomes, including traits associated with ASD [63]. In contrast, the absence of a predictive relationship in term-born infants may indicate that, within the typical developmental range, some other factors exert a more substantial influence on Q-CHAT scores, or that SFC variability is less clinically relevant for detecting ASD risk in this lower-risk population [64]. Notably, specific tracts, such as right CS, bilateral SLF, and right UNC, emerged as key predictors in the preterm group. These tracts are implicated in motor control, language and executive functions, and social-emotional processing, respectively [46, 48]. Alterations in these domains are common in ASD, supporting the biological plausibility of disrupted SFC contributing to Q-CHAT variation in preterm infants [65]. Collectively, these findings position neonatal SFC within WM tracts as a potential early biomarker for identifying preterm infants at heightened risk for atypical neurodevelopmental trajectories, particularly those involving autism-related traits. Future research should focus on longitudinal tracking of SFC development alongside detailed assessments of ASD symptomatology to refine its clinical utility and inform early intervention strategies.

Several methodological considerations warrant mention when interpreting the present findings. First, although the T1w/T2w ratio is a useful proxy for myelination, it is also influenced by factors such as iron, hydration, and cell density [66]. Future research should incorporate advanced quantitative myelin mapping techniques, such as magnetization transfer imaging or myelin water imaging, which would provide more direct assessment of myelin content [67]. Second, our SFC metric is based on the voxel-wise consistency between the FCT and the DT. The DT model, however, is limited in regions with complex fiber architecture because it estimates only a single principal diffusion direction per voxel [68]. In voxels exhibiting crossing or fanning fibers, the primary eigenvector may be ambiguous or poorly defined, rendering the FCT-DT consistency challenging to interpret and less reliable. Future studies should consider replacing DT with more advanced models, such as those estimating fiber orientation distributions, to better capture local structural complexity. Third, our examination of SFC dynamics was confined to measuring its temporal variance. Although informative, this approach does not fully capture state-dependent WM structure-function interactions [36]. Future research could benefit from employing more sophisticated, time-resolved methodologies, such as leading eigenvector analysis, to better capture transient fluctuations in SFC [69].

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of WM SFC maturation during the critical neonatal period, demonstrating marked spatiotemporal heterogeneity, hemispheric asymmetry, and a complex, dynamic relationship with myelination. Crucially, we established that SFC is significantly reduced following premature birth and serves as a specific mediator and predictor for later autism-related traits in the vulnerable preterm population. These findings offer a novel insight into the intricate choreography of neonatal WM development and underscore the potential of SFC as a sensitive early biomarker for identifying infants at risk of altered neurodevelopmental trajectories, particularly those associated with ASD vulnerability in the context of prematurity.
