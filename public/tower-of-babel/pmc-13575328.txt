Psychometric Validation of the Spanish Version of the Sheffield Preference‐Based Venous Leg Ulcer Questionnaire (SPVU‐5D) in Patients With Venous Leg Ulcers
Authors: Miguel Ángel Barbas‐Monjo, Eleuterio A Sánchez‐Romero, José Verdú‐Soriano, Julio Cabrero García, Juan Nicolás Cuenca‐Zaldívar, Rob Sillevis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVenous leg ulcers are a common manifestation of chronic venous disease and are associated with substantial morbidity and impaired health‐related quality of life (HRQoL). Patient‐reported outcome measures are essential for capturing the patient perspective of disease burden and treatment outcomes in wound care management. The Sheffield Preference‐based Venous Ulcer questionnaire (SPVU‐5D) is a concise preference‐based instrument designed to assess the quality of life in individuals with venous leg ulcers and to support health‐economic evaluation. However, its measurement properties have not been extensively examined in different linguistic contexts. This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D in patients with venous leg ulcers. A methodological validation study was conducted involving 132 patients (264 observations from pre‐ and post‐treatment assessments) with a mean age of 63.76 ± 10.02 years. Cross‐cultural adaptation followed the established guidelines for translation and cultural equivalence. Content validity was assessed using Aiken's V coefficient. Structural validity and reliability were examined using multilevel confirmatory factor analysis and measurement performance was further evaluated using a multilevel graded response model based on item response theory. Floor and ceiling effects, minimum detectable change and convergent validity with the RESVECH 2.0 clinical scale were also evaluated. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a unidimensional structure with excellent fit indices and satisfactory internal consistency. Item response theory analyses showed high item discrimination and well‐distributed difficulty thresholds for the items. The floor and ceiling effects were minimal, indicating adequate sensitivity across the latent trait continuum. The Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D demonstrates satisfactory validity, reliability and measurement precision and represents a useful instrument for assessing the health‐related quality of life in patients with venous leg ulcers.Keywords: item response theory, patient‐reported outcomes, psychometrics, quality of life, venous leg ulcers

Introduction

Venous leg ulcers (VLUs) are the most advanced clinical manifestation of chronic venous disease and constitute one of the most common forms of chronic wounds affecting the lower extremities worldwide [1]. Chronic venous insufficiency leads to sustained venous hypertension, which progressively alters microcirculation, triggers inflammatory cascades and ultimately results in tissue breakdown and ulceration [2].

Epidemiological studies consistently indicate that venous ulcers account for approximately 70%–80% of all leg ulcers, making them the predominant type of chronic wound affecting lower limbs [3]. Its incidence is estimated to range between 1.5 and 3 cases per 1000 inhabitants per year, with a higher prevalence among older adults and individuals with chronic venous insufficiency [4].

Venous leg ulcers are characterised by prolonged healing times, high recurrence rates and substantial healthcare costs, representing a significant burden on both healthcare systems and affected individuals [5]. Beyond their physical manifestations, these lesions are associated with persistent symptoms, such as pain, exudate, malodor and impaired mobility, which substantially affect daily functioning and overall well‐being [6].

Current clinical guidelines recommend a comprehensive approach to the prevention and management of VLUs that combines appropriate vascular and wound assessment, local wound care and treatment of underlying venous pathology when indicated. Within this framework, compression therapy remains the standard conservative therapy in patients without relevant contraindications, as it counteracts venous hypertension, reduces edema and promotes ulcer healing. Following ulcer closure, continued compression is recommended as a key strategy for reducing the risk of recurrence [7].

Despite the central role of compression therapy in guideline‐based care, its implementation in routine clinical practice remains suboptimal due to various factors. An analysis of 3604 VLU‐related encounters in Australian general practice found that compression therapy was documented in only 2.1% of encounters, whereas dressings were used in 76% of encounters, highlighting a substantial evidence–practice gap [8]. Qualitative evidence suggests that this gap is multifactorial and may reflect limited awareness of clinical practice guidelines, difficulties accessing and applying recommendations, insufficient resources and lack of skills or confidence in vascular assessment before initiating compression [9]. Moreover, patients and clinicians may hold differing perspectives on VLU management: patients have reported unmet needs regarding vascular assessment, compression therapy, pain management, education and recurrence prevention, whereas clinicians may rely more heavily on topical wound care and individual clinical judgement than on guideline‐recommended pathways [10].

Even when compression therapy is prescribed, its effectiveness in real‐world care depends on patients' ability and willingness to sustain complex self‐management behaviour. Health literacy may influence understanding of the condition, adherence to compression therapy, physical activity and other self‐management practices relevant to healing and recovery [11]. These challenges are particularly important in a chronic and recurrent condition in which approximately half of healed ulcers may recur within 12 months and the recurrence risk varies substantially according to individual clinical characteristics [12]. Consequently, evaluating VLU care requires attention not only to wound closure and other objective clinical outcomes but also to the symptoms, treatment burden, functional limitations and psychosocial consequences experienced by patients. Conceptual work involving both patients and clinicians has emphasised the need for clinically feasible patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) capable of capturing traditional health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) domains alongside VLU‐specific concerns, including daily wound care, compression bandaging and dressings [13].

The impact of venous ulceration extends far beyond the wound itself, affecting multiple domains of patients' lives, including physical capacity, psychological status and social participation [14]. Systematic reviews have demonstrated that individuals with chronic venous ulcers frequently report reduced mobility, sleep disturbances, social isolation and emotional distress, highlighting the multidimensional consequences of this condition [15].

Because of this complex biopsychosocial burden, traditional clinical outcomes, such as wound size or healing rate, are insufficient to fully capture the impact of the disease [16]. Consequently, increasing attention has been directed towards HRQoL as a fundamental outcome in the evaluation of patients with chronic wound [17].

According to the World Health Organization, quality of life is defined as an individual's perception of their position in life within the cultural and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns [18]. Within healthcare research, this concept has evolved into the construct of health‐related quality of life, which reflects the perceived impact of disease and treatment on physical, psychological and social functioning [19].

To quantify these patient‐centred outcomes, numerous PROMs have been developed to assess HRQoL in chronic diseases. In the field of venous disease, several disease‐specific instruments have been proposed, including the Charing Cross Venous Ulcer Questionnaire (CCVUQ), the Chronic Venous Insufficiency Questionnaire (CIVIQ) and other disease‐specific scales designed to capture the patient perspective of venous disorders [20].

Among these instruments, the Charing Cross Venous Ulcer Questionnaire (CCVUQ) has historically been one of the most widely used disease‐specific measures for evaluating HRQoL in patients with venous ulcers. The CCVUQ evaluates multiple domains of patient experience, including social interaction, activities of daily living, emotional well‐being and cosmetic concerns and has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties across different populations [21].

However, despite its clinical utility, the CCVUQ contains a relatively large number of items, which may limit its feasibility in certain clinical or research contexts in which shorter instruments are desirable [22]. In response to this limitation, the Sheffield Preference‐based Venous Ulcer questionnaire (SPVU‐5D) was developed as a concise preference‐based instrument designed to assess the health‐related quality of life of patients with VLUs while also enabling health economic evaluation [23].

The SPVU‐5D comprises five dimensions capturing the physical, psychological and social aspects of the disease and incorporates preference‐based weighting derived from population valuations [24]. This design allows the instrument to quantify the perceived burden of disease and facilitate cost‐utility analyses and comparisons with other health outcomes [25].

Despite its potential clinical and research relevance, the measurement properties of the SPVU‐5D have not been sufficiently investigated across different populations and languages [26]. Previous studies have emphasised the need for additional psychometric validation using robust methodological approaches capable of assessing construct validity, reliability and measurement precision in patients with leg ulcers [27].

Furthermore, advances in psychometric methodology, particularly the application of item response theory (IRT) and multilevel structural modelling, provide powerful tools for evaluating the latent structure of health outcome instruments and improving the accuracy of measurements in longitudinal clinical studies [28].

Given these considerations, a rigorous psychometric evaluation of the SPVU‐5D across different linguistic and cultural contexts is necessary to ensure its validity and precision of measurement. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D in patients with VLUs using multilevel CFA and IRT models.

Methods

Study Design

This methodological investigation assessed the cross‐cultural adaptation and psychometric properties of a patient‐reported outcome measure (PROM) for assessing the HRQoL in patients with VLUs. The study was conducted in accordance with internationally accepted recommendations for observational studies and measurement instrument validation. In particular, the reporting of the study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [29], while the methodological procedures applied for the evaluation of the measurement properties were aligned with the recommendations of the Consensus‐based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) [30].

The research process comprised two main methodological phases: First, a cross‐cultural adaptation of the Sheffield Preference‐based Venous Leg Ulcer questionnaire (SPVU‐5D) was performed to obtain a linguistically and culturally appropriate Spanish version of the instrument for use in the Spanish population. Subsequently, the content validity of the adapted version was evaluated through an assessment of item clarity and relevance by a panel of experts in wound care and venous ulcer management.

Participants were recruited from two clinical units located within a single study centre, Hospital Asociado Universitario Guadarrama, in Guadarrama, Community of Madrid, Spain: the vascular surgery and difficult‐to‐heal wounds units. The hospital is located in the Sierra de Guadarrama and serves patients from across the Community of Madrid, providing a mixed urban and rural catchment context. The study centre was selected for convenience because the research team had direct access to the eligible population through these clinical services, particularly the Difficult‐to‐Heal Wounds Unit, which was coordinated by a member of the study team at the time of recruitment. Data were collected between January 2022 and March 2024. Eligible participants were adults diagnosed with VLUs who were receiving clinical management for their condition and who agreed to participate in the study. A convenience sampling approach was used in the accessible clinical population. Potentially eligible patients were identified among individuals receiving clinical care at the two participating units. Eligibility was assessed based on predefined study criteria and eligible patients were approached in person during routine clinical care and invited to participate by members of the clinical research team involved in the study. Questionnaire data were collected in person during routine clinical care; online data collection procedures were not used. The completion time was not systematically recorded; therefore, the mean completion time could not be calculated.

Cross‐Cultural Adaptation of the SPVU‐5D

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years with a clinical diagnosis of venous leg ulcers confirmed by healthcare professionals. Participants were required to read and understand Spanish and provide informed consent. Patients with cognitive impairment, severe psychiatric disorders, or other conditions that could interfere with questionnaire comprehension were excluded.

The cross‐cultural adaptation of the SPVU‐5D questionnaire was performed to ensure that the Spanish version maintained conceptual, semantic and cultural equivalence to the original English instrument. The methodological procedure followed internationally accepted guidelines for the translation and cultural adaptation of health‐related quality of life instruments. Specifically, the process followed the cross‐cultural adaptation framework described by Beaton et al. which includes forward translation, synthesis, expert review, back‐translation and evaluation of semantic, conceptual and cultural equivalence [31].

Initially, the original English version of the SPVU‐5D was independently translated into Spanish by two professional translators whose native language was Spanish and who were fluent in English. The objective of this stage was to generate two independent forward translations, allowing potential linguistic discrepancies and culturally inappropriate expressions to be identified and corrected.

Following the translation stage, the Spanish versions generated during the translation and reconciliation process were systematically reviewed by the research team and a multidisciplinary panel of healthcare professionals with expertise in venous ulcers and chronic wound management. The panel comprised two vascular surgeons, seven clinical nurses specialising in wound care and one academic researcher with experience in venous ulcer research. The expert review process comprised six evaluation sessions, during which five candidate versions arising from the translation and reconciliation process were examined. The experts assessed conceptual equivalence with the original instrument, linguistic clarity and cultural appropriateness for the Spanish‐speaking population. Particular attention was paid to ensuring that the wording of the items accurately reflected the construct of the health‐related quality of life in patients with venous ulcers.

The principal disagreement within the multidisciplinary expert panel concerned Item 5 (social activities), as the experts considered that the meaning and interpretation of social activities could differ between Anglo‐Saxon and Spanish cultural contexts. Alternative formulations were discussed until consensus was reached on wording that preserved the conceptual meaning of the original instrument while remaining culturally appropriate and readily understandable for Spanish‐speaking patients. Following discussion and reconciliation, the consensus version was approved by all members of the multidisciplinary expert panel with no residual disagreement.

To further assess the comprehensibility and cultural appropriateness of the translated instrument, cognitive interviews were conducted with 10 patients diagnosed with VLUs. A purposive sampling strategy was used in this phase. Patients were selected based on their ability to participate meaningfully in the cognitive assessment, including the absence of cognitive impairment that could interfere with questionnaire comprehension and were personally invited to participate by the investigator responsible for recruitment.

Participants were presented with the alternative Spanish versions generated during the translation and expert review process and asked to compare their comprehensibility. Specifically, they were asked which version was easier to understand, whether any item or response option was difficult or ambiguous and whether either alternative better reflected how the concept would ordinarily be expressed and understood in Spanish.

The main difficulty identified during the patient interviews concerned Item 5 (social activities), which generated initial uncertainty. After considering the alternative formulations presented to them, however, the patients did not identify clearer or more appropriate wording than that proposed by the translators and the multidisciplinary expert panel. Therefore, their feedback supported the retention of the consensus formulation rather than the introduction of new wording. No additional substantive modifications to the remaining items were suggested.

Based on the feedback obtained from both experts and patients, the research team produced a consensus version of the questionnaire that incorporated the most appropriate linguistic formulations while preserving conceptual equivalence to the original instrument. This preliminary version was subsequently circulated among experts for additional review before proceeding to the back‐translation phase.

The consensus Spanish version was independently back‐translated into English by two bilingual translators whose native language was British English. The back‐translators were unaware of the original questionnaire and did not participate in the preceding stages of the adaptation process. Back‐translation allowed us to verify that the translated instrument preserved the meaning and conceptual structure of the original questionnaire [31].

Finally, the back‐translated versions were compared with the original English SPVU‐5D instrument. Any discrepancies identified during this comparison were discussed by the research team and resolved through consensus to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence between the two versions of the manual. Following this procedure, the final Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D questionnaire was developed [31].

Content Validity

Once the cross‐cultural adaptation process was completed, the content validity of the Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D was evaluated. Content validity refers to the degree to which the items of an instrument adequately represent the construct being measured, ensuring that the instrument includes an appropriate set of items to capture the relevant dimensions [32].

Content validity was evaluated by a distinct panel of 10 nurses with expertise in geriatric care. Each expert independently assessed the clarity and relevance of the questionnaire items using a four‐point ordinal scale ranging from 1 (‘not clear or not relevant, requires major revision’) to 4 (‘highly clear and highly relevant, no revision required’). Experts were also encouraged to provide comments and suggestions regarding the item wording when necessary.

This content validity panel was distinct from the multidisciplinary expert panel involved in the preceding cross‐cultural adaptation process. During the content validity assessment, four of the 10 raters assigned lower scores to Item 4 (smell), reflecting concerns regarding the inherently subjective nature of patients' perception of ulcer‐related odour and the clarity with which the response options captured its perceived frequency.

Content validity was quantified using Aiken's V coefficient, a statistical index used to estimate the degree of agreement among experts regarding the relevance and adequacy of the questionnaire items [33]. The coefficient was calculated based on the ratings assigned by the experts, the lowest possible rating on the scale and the number of response categories. To provide a more robust interpretation of the results, confidence intervals for Aiken's V were estimated following the approach proposed by Penfield and Giacobbi [34].

Aiken's V values above 0.60 were considered indicative of acceptable content validity. An agreement index was calculated to complement the evaluation of content validity. This index was defined as the proportion of experts assigning each item a score of 3 or 4, with values greater than 0.80 considered indicative of adequate agreement on item clarity and relevance.

Ethical Considerations

All participants involved in the study, including experts and patients who participated in the cognitive interviews, provided informed consent prior to their participation. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects [35].

Sample Size Considerations

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed specifically for the psychometric analyses. The study included 132 participants, each assessed on two occasions, yielding 264 repeated observations. Sample size requirements for latent‐variable models are model‐dependent and should be considered in relation to the specific characteristics of the model under investigation rather than against universal numerical thresholds or simple rules of thumb [36]. Similarly, simulation research on graded response models indicates that parameter recovery depends not only on sample size but also on the characteristics of the instrument and model, including test length and model structure [37]. Therefore, the achieved sample was considered in the context of the highly parsimonious structure of the SPVU‐5D, which comprises only five items and the specific analytical framework applied.

Pre‐ and post‐treatment assessments provided responses across different levels of the latent construct and were jointly analysed within a multilevel framework. Importantly, the resulting 264 observations were not treated as statistically independent or interpreted as being equivalent to 264 independent participants. Dependence arising from repeated measurements within the same participants was explicitly accommodated through multilevel modelling, with measurement occasions nested within individuals.

Accordingly, although no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, the achieved sample was considered appropriate in relation to the parsimonious structure of the instrument, the characteristics of the fitted models and the explicit modelling of within‐participant dependence. Nevertheless, replication in larger and more heterogeneous samples would provide additional evidence regarding the stability and generalisability of the estimated measurement properties of the scale.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Quantitative variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables are expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages.

Pre‐ and post‐treatment differences in the SPVU‐5D and RESVECH 2.0 scores were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired samples. The effect size was calculated using the nonparametric statistic r, obtained by dividing the standardised test statistic by the square root of the sample size. Effect sizes were interpreted according to the conventional criteria of small (r≈0.10), medium (r≈0.30) and large (r ≥ 0.50).

Given the limited variability observed in responses to several SPVU‐5D items at individual measurement occasions, pre‐ and post‐treatment assessments were jointly analysed to provide responses across different levels of the latent constructs. As both assessments were obtained from the same participants, the resulting observations were not treated as statistically independent. Within‐participant dependence was explicitly accommodated using multilevel modelling approaches, with measurement occasions nested within individuals.

A multilevel modelling framework was selected to account for the hierarchical structure of the data, with repeated measurements nested within individual participants.

Specifically, Multilevel Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MCFA) and a Multilevel Graded Response Model (MGRM) based on Item Response Theory (IRT) were applied. The graded response model, originally proposed by Samejima [38], is particularly suitable for analysing ordinal response categories such as those used in Likert‐type health outcome instruments. The multilevel specification allowed explicit modelling of intraclass correlations by partitioning the variance attributable to individual participants and measurement occasions, thereby accounting for the nested structure of repeated observations.

For CFA, the model was estimated using the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted estimator (WLSMV), which is recommended for ordinal variables derived from Likert‐type response formats. Model fit was evaluated using standard fit indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR). RMSEA values < 0.06, SRMR values < 0.08 and CFI and TLI values > 0.95 were considered indicative of an adequate model fit. The validity of the indicators was examined through the statistical significance of standardised factor loadings (λ), considering values above 0.30 and standard errors below 50% of the loading magnitude as acceptable. The internal consistency of the scale was assessed using Cronbach's alpha (α) and McDonald's omega (ω).

Within the IRT framework, item discrimination (a) and difficulty (b) parameters were estimated using a multilevel graded‐response model. Item discrimination reflects the ability of each item to differentiate between different levels of the latent construct, whereas difficulty parameters represent the severity thresholds required for respondents to transition between the response categories. Local dependence between items was assessed using Yen's Q3 statistic and internal validity was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ρ subject), which estimates the proportion of variance attributable to stable differences between participants.

Additional psychometric analyses included the examination of corrected item–total correlations, item characteristic curves (ICCs), test information function (TIF) and Wright person–item map, which were used to evaluate the measurement precision of the instrument across the continuum of latent traits. Information‐based reliability was also calculated to assess the model's internal consistency. Differential item functioning (DIF) was explored by comparing nested models using likelihood ratio tests, incorporating sex as a potential predictor of item discrimination and difficulty parameters.

Floor and ceiling effects were assessed using the minimum and maximum possible scores on the SPVU‐5D scale obtained at the first measurement. The magnitude of these effects was interpreted as excellent (no floor or ceiling effect), adequate (< 20%), or poor (> 20%) according to the commonly accepted criteria for health measurement instruments.

The minimum detectable change (MDC) was calculated to estimate the smallest change that could be interpreted as a real change beyond the measurement error.

Finally, the association between the SPVU‐5D and RESVECH 2.0 scores was examined using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, given the ordinal nature of the variables.

Results

A total of 132 patients participated in the study, contributing 264 observations corresponding to pre‐ and post‐treatment assessments, with a mean age of 63.76 ± 10.02 years and most patients were women. Significant differences were observed between the pre‐ and post‐treatment assessments for both the RESVECH 2.0 scale and the global and item‐level SPVU‐5D scores.

All scores showed a reduction following treatment, with large and statistically significant effect sizes for both instruments.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

Multilevel confirmatory factor analysis (MCFA) showed excellent goodness‐of‐fit indices. At the within‐subject level, all factor loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.05) with precise standard error.

Item 5 showed the highest standardised loading (λ = 0.962), explaining the largest proportion of variance (R 2 = 0.926), whereas Item 4 presented the lowest loading (λ = 0.324), although it remained above the minimum acceptable threshold and was statistically significant.

The replication of these loadings at the between‐subject level confirmed the structural stability of the construct across the measurement levels. Internal consistency was satisfactory, with Cronbach's α = 0.792 and McDonald's ω = 0.797, indicating adequate reliability of the scale.

Item Response Theory Analysis

The multilevel graded response model (MGRM) demonstrated an excellent overall model fit and high information‐based reliability.

Regarding item discrimination parameters, very high discrimination values were observed, particularly for Items 2 (a = 16.720) and 5 (a = 9.000), indicating a strong ability to detect changes in the latent construct of quality of life.

Regarding the item difficulty parameters, the thresholds were distributed progressively across the latent trait continuum (from −4.21 to 3.51). Item 1 showed the lowest threshold values (b 1 = −4.212), indicating high sensitivity in detecting the early stages of quality‐of‐life deterioration and functioning as an early screening tool. In contrast, Item 4 showed the highest thresholds (b 2 = 3.517), suggesting that higher response categories were only reached in patients experiencing severe impairments in QoL.

The monotonic progression of thresholds across items indicates that the response categories were interpreted hierarchically and consistently by the participants. As expected in graded‐response models, the standard errors increased slightly for higher difficulty thresholds because of the lower number of observations at the extreme levels of the latent trait.

Local Independence and Internal Validity

The analysis of local independence using Yen's Q3 statistic yielded values ranging from 0.47–0.62. Although the multilevel specification explicitly accounted for the dependence arising from repeated measurements within participants, the observed Q3 values were considered when interpreting item‐level parameters.

The model estimated a random subject variance of σ 2 = 0.492. Internal validity indices indicated that approximately 70% of the variance in responses was attributable to differences between individuals, providing adequate stability for the longitudinal measurement of QoL during ulcer progression.

The corrected item–total correlations varied across items. The lower values observed for Items 2 and 5 were interpreted alongside their high discrimination parameters and differential performance across the latent trait continuum.

Item Characteristic Curves

An analysis of the item characteristic curves (ICCs) confirmed the scale's strong discriminative capacity. Items 2 and 5 displayed very steep slopes, reflecting their high discrimination parameters and abrupt transitions between the response categories. This pattern indicates that small changes in patient status may lead to rapid transitions between the levels of perceived impact.

In contrast, Items 1 and 4 showed curves distributed more gradually across the latent continuum (θ), supporting the role of Item 1 as an indicator of early impairment and Item 4 as a marker of severe impairment. Despite the high discrimination observed for some items, the monotonic progression of the curves indicated that the response categories were interpreted consistently by the participants.

Test Information Function

The test information function (TIF) showed that the SPVU‐5D scale reached its highest diagnostic precision around the central levels of the latent trait (θ≈0), where the standard error was markedly reduced.

The narrow and pronounced peaks observed in the information curve reflect high discrimination of the items, indicating that the scale is particularly effective in detecting subtle changes in patients experiencing moderate quality‐of‐life impairment.

Wright Map

The Wright map demonstrated a strong alignment between the distribution of patients and the location of the item difficulty thresholds. The highest density of participants was concentrated around the central region of the latent trait (θ≈0).

The dispersion of thresholds across the continuum indicates that the SPVU‐5D scale does not present significant measurement gaps and is capable of accurately assessing individuals across a broad range of quality‐of‐life levels, from minimal impairment to extreme impairment.

Floor and Ceiling Effects, MDC and Convergent Validity

The scale showed a ceiling effect of 3.79% and a floor effect of 9.09%, both well below the commonly accepted threshold of 15%, indicating that the instrument maintained adequate sensitivity across the full range of the construct.

The minimum detectable change (MDC) was estimated at 0.41, indicating that any change above this value could be interpreted as a true change beyond the measurement error.

The correlation between the SPVU‐5D and RESVECH 2.0 scale was negligible and not statistically significant (ρ = −0.09, p = 0.33); therefore, patients' subjective perception of quality of life does not necessarily correspond directly with the objective clinical severity of their ulcers.

Discussion

This study evaluated the psychometric properties of the Spanish version of the Sheffield Preference‐based Venous Leg Ulcer questionnaire (SPVU‐5D) in patients with VLUs using modern psychometric approaches, including multilevel confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and item response theory models. The findings of this study provide evidence supporting the structural validity, internal consistency, measurement precision and construct validity of the instrument when applied to Spanish‐speaking populations with leg ulcers.

Principal Findings

Overall, the results demonstrate that the Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D possesses strong psychometric properties for assessing the health‐related quality of life of patients with VLUs. The CFA showed excellent fit indices, with CFI and TLI values exceeding the commonly recommended thresholds and RMSEA and SRMR values within acceptable limits. These findings support the unidimensional structure of the instrument and indicate that the five items of the questionnaire adequately capture a common latent construct related to the impact of venous ulcers on QoL.

These findings are consistent with current methodological recommendations for the validation of patient‐reported outcome measures, which emphasise the systematic evaluation of content validity, structural validity and internal consistency to ensure the adequacy and interpretability of the measurement instruments used in clinical research [39].

According to the COSMIN framework, evidence of structural validity and internal consistency is a fundamental requirement for the adequate interpretation of patient‐reported outcome measures in clinical research [39].

The standardised factor loadings obtained in this study were statistically significant, indicating that each item contributed meaningfully to the underlying construct. The highest loading was observed for item 5, suggesting that this dimension may represent a particularly relevant component of perceived health status among individuals affected by VLU. The relatively lower loading observed for Item 4, although still above the recommended threshold, suggests that this item may capture a more specific dimension of patient experience; therefore, it may contribute differently to the latent construct.

These findings are consistent with the conceptual framework underlying disease‐specific quality‐of‐life instruments for venous disease, which aim to capture the multidimensional consequences of chronic venous insufficiency beyond purely clinical parameters. Previous research has demonstrated that venous ulcers affect multiple domains of patient functioning, including mobility, social participation and psychological well‐being, reinforcing the need for instruments capable of capturing these complex dimensions of the patient experience [14, 15].

Reliability and Internal Consistency

The internal consistency indices obtained in the present study indicate the satisfactory reliability of the SPVU‐5D Scale. Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega values were both close to 0.80, suggesting that the items measured a coherent construct while maintaining sufficient heterogeneity to capture different aspects of the patient experience.

These results are comparable to the reliability estimates reported for other disease‐specific quality‐of‐life instruments used in venous disease, including the Chronic Venous Insufficiency Questionnaire (CIVIQ), Charing Cross Venous Ulcer Questionnaire (CCVUQ) and Venous Leg Ulcer Quality of Life questionnaire (VLU‐QoL), which have previously demonstrated adequate internal consistency and construct validity across different clinical populations [20, 21, 40, 41].

However, unlike these instruments, the SPVU‐5D was specifically designed as a concise preference‐based instrument that enables both clinical assessment and economic evaluation. Shorter instruments may improve feasibility in clinical settings while maintaining adequate psychometric performance, particularly when the goal is to incorporate patient‐reported outcomes into routine clinical monitoring or health economic studies [42].

Item Response Theory and Measurement Precision

The application of the multilevel graded response model provided further insight into the measurement performance of the SPVU‐5D scale. The item discrimination parameters were particularly high for items 2 and 5, indicating a strong capacity to distinguish between individuals with different levels of perceived QoL impairment. The very high discrimination observed for some items may reflect the limited number of response categories and strong differentiation between response levels in the studied population.

High discrimination parameters suggest that relatively small changes in the underlying latent traits may lead to observable changes in item responses. This characteristic is particularly valuable in longitudinal clinical studies, in which subtle improvements or deterioration in patient status must be detected reliably.

The distribution of difficulty thresholds across the latent continuum also indicates that the scale captures a broad spectrum of disease severities. Item 1 appears to function as an indicator of early deterioration in perceived health status, whereas item 4 is associated with more severe impairment levels. Such a hierarchical ordering of thresholds supports the theoretical coherence of the response categories and suggests that patients consistently interpret the response options.

The Wright map further confirmed the appropriate targeting of the instrument, demonstrating good alignment between the distribution of patients and the location of the item thresholds. This finding indicates that the scale provides meaningful information across a wide range of QoL levels without major measurement gaps.

Modern psychometric approaches, such as item response theory, allow for a more precise evaluation of measurement properties than classical test theory by providing item‐level information regarding discrimination, difficulty and measurement precision. These methods have been increasingly recommended for the development and validation of health measurement instruments because they allow for more accurate modelling of latent constructs and improve the interpretability of patient‐reported outcome measures [28].

Floor and Ceiling Effects

The absence of substantial floor and ceiling effects observed in this study indicates that the SPVU‐5D scale maintains adequate sensitivity across the full range of constructs. Both effects remained well below the commonly accepted threshold of 15%, suggesting that the instrument can discriminate between patients with different disease severity levels.

Floor and ceiling effects are particularly relevant in quality‐of‐life instruments, as excessive clustering of responses in the extreme categories may limit the ability of the instrument to detect clinically meaningful changes over time. The low magnitude of these effects in the present study supports the suitability of the SPVU‐5D for longitudinal monitoring of patient outcomes during venous ulcer treatment.

Convergent Validity and Clinical Interpretation

The convergent validity analysis revealed a negligible and nonsignificant correlation between the SPVU‐5D scores and the RESVECH 2.0 clinical scale. At first glance, this finding might appear unexpected; however, it likely reflects the conceptual differences between the instruments.

The RESVECH 2.0 scale primarily assesses objective clinical parameters related to wound healing, whereas the SPVU‐5D captures the subjective perception of the disease burden from the patient's perspective. Previous research on chronic wound populations has demonstrated that objective clinical indicators do not always strongly correlate with patient‐reported quality‐of‐life outcomes, as individuals may perceive the impact of their condition differently depending on psychological, social and contextual factors [16, 17].

Similar discrepancies between clinical indicators and patient‐reported outcomes have been reported in studies validating disease‐specific instruments, such as the VLU‐QoL questionnaire, highlighting the importance of incorporating patient perceptions into the evaluation of venous ulcer burden [41].

This discrepancy highlights the importance of incorporating outcome measures alongside traditional clinical indicators when evaluating the effectiveness of interventions for VLUs.

This finding reinforces the conceptual distinction between clinical indicators of wound healing and patient‐perceived QoL, which represent complementary but not interchangeable outcome domains in wound care research.

Clinical Implications

The results of this study suggest that the Spanish version of the SPVU‐5D is a useful instrument for assessing the health‐related quality of life in patients with VLUs. Its concise structure and preference‐based design make it particularly suitable for use in both clinical practice and research settings, including health economic evaluations and cost‐utility analyses.

Given the growing emphasis on patient‐centred outcomes in healthcare research, the availability of validated PROMs adapted to different languages and cultural contexts is essential for improving the evaluation of treatment effectiveness and guiding clinical decision making [42].

Furthermore, the application of modern psychometric approaches, such as multilevel modelling and item response theory, contributes to a more robust evaluation of measurement properties and strengthens the methodological rigour of validation studies.

Strengths of the Study

This study had several methodological strengths. First, the validation process was conducted following internationally recognised methodological frameworks, including the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies and the COSMIN recommendations for evaluating the measurement properties of patient‐reported outcome measures [29, 30, 39].

The updated COSMIN guidance emphasises that content validity is the most critical measurement property of a patient‐reported outcome measure, followed by structural validity, internal consistency and other psychometric properties, which are evaluated using appropriate statistical approaches [39]. The present study adhered to this hierarchical framework by first conducting a rigorous cross‐cultural adaptation and expert‐based content validity assessment, followed by advanced psychometric analyses, including multilevel CFA and item response theory modelling.

To the best of our knowledge, no previously published independent Spanish translation or cross‐cultural adaptation of the SPVU‐5D scale has been identified. The cross‐cultural adaptation described in the present study constituted the first methodological phase of the same research program and was followed by content validity assessment and psychometric validation. Therefore, the present study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of the Spanish‐language version of the SPVU‐5D.

Furthermore, the application of multilevel modelling allowed for the explicit consideration of the hierarchical structure of repeated observations, thereby accounting for within‐subject dependency and improving the robustness of parameter estimation. The integration of item response theory provides detailed information on item discrimination, difficulty thresholds and measurement precision across the latent trait continuum, offering a more comprehensive evaluation of the scale than traditional psychometric approaches.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the study used a convenience sample that was drawn from a specific clinical population within a single study centre. All participants were adults with lower‐extremity ulcers of venous aetiology receiving care in the Vascular Surgery Unit or the Difficult‐to‐Heal Wounds Unit at Hospital Asociado Universitario Guadarrama, a medium‐stay hospital located in the Sierra de Guadarrama, Community of Madrid, Spain. Although the hospital serves patients from across the Community of Madrid and therefore has a mixed urban–rural catchment population, recruitment from a single centre may limit the generalisability of the findings to patients managed in other healthcare settings, geographical regions, or healthcare systems, as well as to populations with demographic or clinical characteristics different from those represented in the present sample.

Second, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed specifically for the psychometric analyses. Although the sample size was considered in relation to the parsimonious structure of the instrument and the characteristics of the analytical models, replication in larger and more heterogeneous populations would provide additional evidence regarding the stability and generalisability of the estimated measurement properties.

Third, this study focused primarily on structural validity, internal consistency and measurement precision. Additional research is required to explore other measurement properties, such as responsiveness and test–retest reliability in longitudinal clinical contexts.

Additionally, although the multilevel modelling approach addressed the dependence between repeated observations, future studies may benefit from prospective longitudinal designs that allow a more detailed evaluation of change trajectories in patient‐reported outcomes over time.

Finally, cultural and healthcare system differences may influence patient perceptions of QoL; therefore, the generalisability of the findings to other linguistic or cultural contexts should be interpreted with caution.

Future Research

Future research should replicate these findings in larger and more diverse populations and examine the responsiveness of the SPVU‐5D scale to clinical changes at different stages of venous ulcer treatment. Comparative studies evaluating the performance of the SPVU‐5D against other disease‐specific instruments, such as CIVIQ and CCVUQ, may also provide additional insights into the relative advantages of different quality‐of‐life measures used in venous disease research [20, 21, 40].

To the best of our knowledge, no published cross‐cultural adaptations or psychometric validations of the SPVU‐5D in languages other than English have been identified, which precluded the direct comparison of the present findings with other linguistic versions of the instrument. Therefore, future studies should validate the SPVU‐5D in additional languages, populations and healthcare systems to determine whether its factorial structure, reliability, item functioning and measurement precision are stable across cultural contexts.

Such studies would further contribute to the integration of patient‐reported outcomes into routine clinical practice and support the development of patient‐centred care strategies for individuals affected by chronic venous ulcers.

Conclusions

The findings of this study provide evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the Spanish version of the Sheffield Preference‐based Venous Leg Ulcer questionnaire (SPVU‐5D) for assessing the health‐related quality of life of patients with VLUs. The instrument demonstrated a stable unidimensional structure, satisfactory internal consistency and strong measurement performance when evaluated using modern psychometric approaches, including multilevel CFA and item response theory models.

The results also indicate that SPVU‐5D shows adequate sensitivity across the continuum of the latent construct, with minimal floor and ceiling effects and an acceptable minimum detectable change (MDC). The absence of a significant correlation with the RESVECH 2.0 scale highlights the complementary nature of patient‐reported outcomes and objective clinical indicators in the evaluation of chronic wounds.

Given its concise structure and preference‐based design, the Spanish SPVU‐5D represents a practical and efficient tool for assessing patient‐perceived quality of life in both clinical and research settings. The availability of a validated Spanish version may facilitate the incorporation of patient‐reported outcomes into routine clinical evaluations, longitudinal monitoring of treatment effectiveness and health economic analyses in populations with VLUs.

Future studies should further explore the responsiveness and longitudinal performance of this instrument in diverse clinical populations and healthcare settings.
