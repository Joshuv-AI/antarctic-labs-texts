A composite medium enabling co-culture of Lactobacillus spp. and Pseudomonas aeruginosa with application to surface growth studies
Authors: Natalya Harutyunyan, Marine Balayan, Anahit Manvelyan, Lena Malkhasyan, Susanna Mirzabekyan, Astghik Pepoyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Intercellular interactions among bacteria play a central role in shaping the structure, stability, and functionality of microbial communities [1,2]. Antagonistic interactions are particularly important, as they influence population dynamics, niche competition, and suppression of competing microorganisms [3]. Experimental investigation of such interactions is commonly performed using in vitro cultivation systems; however, outcomes are strongly dependent on the choice of culture medium [4]. An unbalanced growth environment may artificially favor one interaction partner, thereby distorting observed competitive behaviors and limiting biological relevance [5].

This challenge is especially pronounced in mixed cultures of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [6]. These groups differ substantially in cell wall structure, metabolic requirements, and stress tolerance, often resulting in asymmetric growth under standard laboratory conditions [7]. Lactobacillus spp. are typically cultivated in De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) medium, which supports fermentative metabolism but may inhibit other bacterial taxa due to its acidic nature and high carbohydrate content [8]. In contrast, Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibits optimal growth in Luria–Bertani (LB) or nutrient broth [9], media that do not adequately support Lactobacillus spp. [8].

Beyond supporting bacterial growth, the culture medium also influences regulatory systems governing intercellular communication [10]. Quorum sensing (QS) enables bacteria to coordinate collective behaviors through diffusible signal molecules in a cell-density-dependent manner [11]. QS-regulated processes contribute to biofilm formation, competitive fitness, and environmental persistence, and may be inconsistently expressed under non-optimized in vitro conditions [12].

QS-mediated regulation is particularly relevant in biofilm-associated phenotypes [13]. In Pseudomonas aeruginosa, QS networks comprising acyl-homoserine lactone–based and quinolone-based signaling pathways regulate the expression of genes involved in virulence, secondary metabolite production, biofilm maturation and motility [14]. In addition, QS also contributes to the enhancement of antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa. Pyocyanin, a phenazine pigment responsible for the characteristic blue-green coloration of Pseudomonas aeruginosa cultures, serves as a qualitative indicator of QS activity and biofilm-associated pathogenic potential [15].

In multispecies communities, QS-mediated behaviors may be modulated through interspecies interactions, including antagonistic interference with signaling pathways [16]. Previous studies have shown that probiotic bacteria can suppress pathogenic colonization through QS disruption rather than direct growth inhibition [17,18].

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a widely used model organism for QS research due to the complexity and robustness of its signaling networks [14]. However, reproducible induction of QS-associated biofilm formation in mixed-culture systems remains challenging, particularly in the presence of Gram-positive bacteria such as Lactobacillus spp. [19]. Standard media often bias interspecies competition or suppress QS-associated phenotypes, emphasizing the need for a balanced cultivation environment.

The present methodology addresses these challenges by providing a composite liquid medium that supports co-cultivation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Lactobacillus spp. while enabling observation of QS-associated biofilm-related phenotypic features. This approach facilitates reproducible investigation of antagonistic intercellular interactions under controlled in vitro conditions and provides a practical methodological tool for studying QS modulation and microbial competition in multispecies systems.

Method details

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The method was developed to investigate antagonistic intercellular interactions and quorum sensing–associated biofilm formation in mixed bacterial cultures. The reference strain used in this study was Pseudomonas aeruginosa NCTC 10662.

In addition to Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the co-cultivation framework was evaluated using probiotic and candidate probiotic Lactobacillus strains, including Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ZPZ [20,21], Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus Vahe, Lactobacillus delbrueckii IAHAHI [22], Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus strain 5-2 [23,24] and Lactobacillus acidophilus DDS®-1 [25].

The Lactobacillus strains originated from the institutional culture collections of the Scientific Research Institute of Food Science and Biotechnologies and the International Association for Human and Animal Health Improvement, Armenian National Agrarian University (Yerevan, Armenia), or were obtained from commercial sources, and have been described previously [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. All strains were maintained according to standard microbiological practices and subcultured prior to experimentation to ensure metabolic activity and reproducibility.

Inoculum standardization

For all antagonism and biofilm experiments, bacterial inocula were standardized using planktonic cultures. Fresh overnight monocultures were suspended in sterile physiological saline and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity using a spectrophotometer (StatFax 3300, Awareness Technology Inc., Palm City, FL, USA) at a wavelength of 600 nm, corresponding to approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Optical density–based standardization was applied exclusively to planktonic suspensions, as biofilm-associated cells cannot be reliably quantified using McFarland standards. Measurements of bacterial growth were performed every 60 min until the first decrease in OD was observed. In parallel, cocultures of the LAB strains and P. aeruginosa were cultured under the same conditions. Equal amounts of inoculum from each strain were combined for the co-culture. Cell concentrations were additionally verified by control plating after 24 h to ensure accuracy. Values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Composite medium formulation

The proposed liquid medium consists of a composite formulation combining MRS broth with either LB broth (Lennox modification) or nutrient broth. MRS broth was prepared using commercially available components (HiMedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India). LB broth (Lennox modification) (Sigma-Aldrich, Sant Luis, USA) and nutrient broth were obtained from Oxoid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Basingstoke, UK). All media components were prepared using distilled water and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min prior to use.

The final working formulation consisted of one part MRS broth and three parts LB broth or nutrient broth (1:3, v/v), selected to support balanced co-cultivation while preserving quorum sensing–associated phenotypes of Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Given that pH variation is a critical factor affecting bacterial growth during the stationary phase, pH values were measured at both the initial and final stages of cultivation. The initial pH of the composite medium (1:3) was 7.0.

Cultivation and incubation conditions

Standardized bacterial suspensions were inoculated into the composite medium and incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h. To evaluate the influence of medium composition on growth mode, both 1:3 and 1:2 (v/v) MRS-to-LB or nutrient broth ratios were tested. Cultures grown in the 1:3 medium exhibited surface-associated biofilm formation by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, whereas the 1:2 formulation primarily supported planktonic growth with uniform turbidity.

Control cultures were incubated in MRS broth, LB broth with Lennox modification, or nutrient broth alone under identical conditions. All experiments were performed in at least 30 independent replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Assessment of QS-associated phenotypes

To evaluate the preservation of quorum sensing–associated phenotypes, cultures grown in the composite medium were subcultured onto nutrient agar plates following incubation. Production of the characteristic greenish-blue pigment pyocyanin by Pseudomonas aeruginosa was used as a qualitative indicator of QS-linked, biofilm-associated phenotypic expression and was assessed visually under standard aerobic conditions.

Control conditions

Control experiments were conducted by incubating each bacterial strain separately in MRS broth, LB broth (Lennox modification), or nutrient broth under identical temperature and incubation conditions. These controls were used to assess baseline growth behaviour and to verify that observed phenotypes in the composite medium were not attributable to monoculture-specific effects.

Method validation

The proposed composite liquid medium was validated to confirm that it (i) supports reproducible co-cultivation of both interaction partners and (ii) preserves quorum sensing–associated, biofilm-linked phenotypes of Pseudomonas aeruginosa under standardized in vitro conditions. Validation experiments were conducted at 37 °C for 24 h using two medium formulations differing only in the ratio of MRS broth to LB (Lennox modification) or nutrient broth (1:3 and 1:2, v/v).

Compatibility of the composite medium with P. aeruginosa Following static incubation, cultures prepared in the 1:3 (MRS:LB or MRS:nutrient broth) formulation consistently exhibited surface-associated growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, visually distinguishable from the surrounding liquid phase. In contrast, cultures prepared in the 1:2 formulation predominantly displayed homogeneous turbidity, indicative of planktonic growth (Fig. 1).

Differential growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in composite media with 1:3 and 1:2 ratios after 24 h incubation. a-composite medium with ratio 1:3 (MRS:LB), b- composite medium with ratio 1:2 (MRS:LB) The arrow indicates the biofilm on the surface of the liquid medium.

These observations demonstrate that alteration of the medium ratio reproducibly shifts the growth mode of P. aeruginosa under otherwise identical environmental conditions.

Compatibility of the composite medium with Lactobacillus spp. growth

To verify that the composite formulation supports co-cultivation, Lactobacillus spp. were incubated under identical conditions in both 1:3 and 1:2 composite media. To confirm these observations, viable cell counts were assessed by plating serial dilutions on MRS agar, demonstrating the presence of culturable Lactobacillus populations under both medium conditions. Quantitative comparison of strain-specific growth levels was not the focus of this validation step. Growth was consistently observed as increased turbidity and formation of visible precipitate, indicating active proliferation in the composite medium (Fig. 2).

Growth appearance of Lactobacillus spp. in composite media following 24 h incubation. а- composite medium with ratio 1:3, MRS:LB, v/v; b- composite medium with ratio 1:2, MRS:LB, v/v.

This confirms that the composite medium does not suppress the Gram-positive interaction partner during mixed-culture experiments.

Quantitative analysis of bacterial growth revealed that final yields (CFU/mL) were strongly dependent on the specific Lactobacillus strain used in co-culture. Distinct strain-specific interaction patterns were observed. In co-culture with Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus Vahe, both strains maintained moderate growth levels, reaching (1.95 ± 0.38) × 10⁷ CFU/mL for the lactobacilli and (2.91 ± 0.26) × 10⁷ CFU/mL for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In contrast, co-cultivation with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ZPZ resulted in complete inhibition of P. aeruginosa (0 CFU/mL), while the lactobacilli reached (1.56 ± 0.42) × 10⁸ CFU/mL. An opposite interaction pattern was observed for Lactobacillus delbrueckii IAHAHI, where no viable lactobacilli were detected (0 CFU/mL), whereas P. aeruginosa exhibited substantial growth ((1.71 ± 0.31) × 10⁸ CFU/mL).

The two other Lactobacillus strains (L. rhamnosus strain 5–2and L. acidophilus DDS®-1) demonstrated interaction profiles comparable to that of L. rhamnosus Vahe, characterized by the coexistence of both species without complete inhibition.

Overall, the observed strain-dependent differences in growth dynamics indicate that the composite medium supports the manifestation of antagonistic interactions and is suitable for evaluating the strain-specific inhibitory potential of probiotic lactobacilli against P. aeruginosa.

Across all experimental conditions, including monocultures and co-cultures, the pH remained stable within the optimal range (6.5–7.0), indicating that the observed differences in growth dynamics were not attributable to pH variation but rather to strain-specific interactions.

To validate the composite medium, the optical density of planktonic growth of monocultures of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Lactobacillus spp. was measured during cultivation in standard and composite media.

As can be seen, the growth kinetics of the bacteria in the composite medium were generally comparable to those observed in the standard media.

Although a slight decrease in optical density was observed in the composite medium (approximately 0.1 OD units for both monocultures), bacterial cell counts remained comparable, with values on the order of 10⁹ CFU/mL. In the composite medium, counts reached (1.83 ± 0.41) × 10⁹ CFU/mL for Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ZPZ and (1.92 ± 0.38) × 10⁹ CFU/mL for Pseudomonas aeruginosa NCTC 10662, compared to (2.45 ± 0.35) × 10⁹ and (2.60 ± 0.32) × 10⁹ CFU/mL, respectively, in standard media.

Verification of QS-associated phenotype preservation

To assess whether quorum sensing–associated phenotypes were maintained, cultures grown in the composite medium were subcultured onto nutrient agar plates following incubation. Pseudomonas aeruginosa originating from the 1:3 composite medium consistently exhibited production of the characteristic greenish-blue pigment pyocyanin, whereas cultures derived from the 1:2 formulation did not show detectable pigment formation.

Pyocyanin production was used as a qualitative proxy for quorum sensing–associated phenotypic expression under the validated culture conditions (Fig. 5) rather than as a quantitative measure of quorum sensing activity. Quantitative assessment of quorum sensing signal molecules or gene expression was beyond the scope of the present method validation.

Reproducibility

All validation experiments were repeated in at least 30 independent experimental runs. Independent runs were performed using separate overnight cultures prepared on different days, demonstrating consistency of observed growth patterns and qualitative phenotypic indicators.

Limitations

The proposed method relies on the use of freshly prepared bacterial cultures, as prolonged storage or repeated subculturing may alter metabolic activity and quorum sensing responsiveness. Because Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an obligate aerobe, adequate oxygen availability is required; therefore, airtight incubation systems are not suitable, and breathable closures should be used to ensure consistent outcomes.

While the composite medium supports balanced co-cultivation and maintenance of quorum sensing–associated phenotypic features under controlled in vitro conditions, it does not reproduce the full physicochemical complexity of natural, environmental, or host-associated microbial ecosystems. Accordingly, observations obtained using this system should be interpreted within the context of simplified laboratory conditions.

The method was evaluated under static incubation at 37 °C for 24 h; its performance under alternative temperatures or agitation conditions was not assessed. Inference of quorum sensing–associated phenotypes was based on qualitative pigment production and does not represent a comprehensive analysis of quorum sensing networks.

Ethics statements

This study did not involve human participants or animal subjects. All experiments were conducted using bacterial strains under standard laboratory conditions. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required for this work.
