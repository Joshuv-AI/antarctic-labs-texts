Maternal Outcomes Among Periviable Births by Cesarean Delivery
Authors: Marie-Julie Trahan, Timothy Wen, Yongmei Huang, Uma M. Reddy, Eve Overton, Kristine E. Brown, Alexander M. Friedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines trends, risk factors, and adverse maternal outcomes associated with periviable vaginal vs cesarean delivery.

Introduction

Periviable birth is defined as occurring between 20 weeks 0 days and 25 weeks 6 days of gestation, representing approximately 0.5% of US births.1,2 Secondary to advances in obstetric and neonatal care, survival and neurodevelopmental outcomes have improved among periviable neonates.3,4,5 Consequently, resuscitation of periviable neonates has increased, and the gestational age (GA) threshold at which a trial of neonatal intensive care may be offered is as early as 22 weeks in some centers.6

There is limited data on maternal outcomes associated with periviable obstetric interventions, particularly prior to 24 weeks, as studies often exclude deliveries at this GA or include small denominators. Willingness to perform periviable cesarean delivery may be associated with increased likelihood of neonatal survival.7,8 However, in contrast to other obstetric interventions, such as antenatal corticosteroid or magnesium sulfate administration, that pose little maternal risk, cesarean delivery is associated with substantial immediate and long-term adverse maternal health consequences. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine (SMFM) do not support routine cesarean delivery for periviability alone; however, cesarean delivery for fetal indications (eg, abnormal fetal heart rate monitoring or malpresentation) may be considered as of 23 weeks.1 Because fetal malpresentation is common at periviable GAs, decisions regarding mode of delivery arise frequently. Prior to 26 weeks, a classical or vertical uterine incision is required in up to 50% of deliveries.9 Compared with a low-transverse incision, a vertical incision is associated with increased blood loss and postoperative pain as well as higher rates of transfusion and postoperative infections.10 In addition, the risk for uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancies increases with both prior vertical and very preterm low-transverse hysterotomy, increasing the risk of complications in subsequent pregnancies.11

Independent of hysterotomy type, periviable cesarean delivery is associated with increased risk of serious maternal complications.12 In the decision to perform periviable cesarean delivery, ACOG and SMFM recommend evaluating maternal health consequences of cesarean delivery in relation to potential neonatal benefits.1 Contemporary estimates of adverse maternal outcomes associated with periviable cesarean delivery are thus important for counseling and clinical decision-making. Given the clinical relevance of updated data, the objective of this study was to characterize risk factors, trends, and adverse maternal outcomes associated with periviable cesarean delivery at 22 weeks 0 days to 25 weeks 6 days of gestation.

Methods

The Columbia University Institutional Review Board deemed this cross-sectional study exempt from review and waived the informed consent requirement given the use of deidentified, publicly available data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Study Design

The primary end point of this repeated cross-sectional study was the risks for adverse maternal outcomes among periviable births by actual mode of delivery (cesarean or vaginal). Eight adverse maternal outcomes were compared: severe maternal morbidity (SMM) excluding transfusion (nontransfusion SMM), transfusion, a critical care composite, a wound complications composite, venous thromboembolism (VTE; deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), endometritis, and peripartum hysterectomy. There were 3 secondary end points: (1) demographic, hospital, and clinical factors associated with periviable cesarean delivery; (2) trends in the proportion of periviable cesarean delivery over the study period stratified by week of GA; and (3) trends in the proportion of periviable cesarean delivery associated with specific cesarean delivery indications, including malpresentation, labor disorders (protracted labor and labor arrest), and nonreassuring fetal status (NRFS).13

SMM was defined based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention composite measure,14 comprising 21 diagnoses and conditions including sepsis, transfusion, shock, stroke, heart failure, and other conditions identified by International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. Because transfusion is the most common diagnosis in the SMM composite, is unlikely to have long-term sequelae, may be a process measure, and is not necessarily representative of large-volume transfusion,15 transfusion was excluded from the composite. Critical care procedures included a composite of interventions representative of higher-level care requirement, including central monitoring, intubation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, mechanical ventilation, tracheostomy, and cardiac rhythm conversion. The wound complication composite included wound infection and disruption.16

Study Population and Data Collection

We identified periviable delivery hospitalizations using the National (Nationwide) Inpatient Sample (NIS) created by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project from 2016 to 2022. The NIS comprises approximately 20% of hospitalizations nationally and is one of the largest publicly available, all-payer inpatient databases in the US.17 Since 2012, the NIS has used a stratified systematic sampling design with proportionate representation of inpatient discharges in all hospitals to produce nationally representative estimates based on discharge weights; these weights were applied for this study.18 In 2015, NIS coding transitioned from International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) to ICD-10-CM coding. To ensure consistent diagnosis coding across the study period, we performed analyses from 2016 onward. Missing values were categorized as missing or unknown.

The current analysis was restricted to singleton delivery hospitalizations among pregnant individuals aged 15 to 44 years. Delivery hospitalizations were identified with ICD-10-CM codes using approaches that ascertain greater than 95% of deliveries.19,20 Periviable deliveries at 22 weeks 0 days to 25 weeks 6 days of gestation were identified with ICD-10-CM codes (eAppendix in Supplement 1). These codes have high validity compared with birth certificate best obstetric estimate of GA.21 The GA upper limit of periviability was defined based on criteria from a joint workshop sponsored by SMFM, the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and ACOG.1,3 Delivery hospitalizations were categorized based on the highest GA code associated with the hospitalization, and deliveries with a GA of 26 weeks or later were excluded. Multifetal pregnancies as well as delivery hospitalizations with a diagnosis of placenta accreta spectrum, vasa previa, and placenta previa were also excluded, as these diagnoses require cesarean delivery and are independently associated with SMM.

Exposures

The primary exposure of interest was periviable cesarean delivery vs vaginal delivery. The demographic factors evaluated included delivery year and maternal age, self-reported race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, or other [American Indian or Alaska Native; more than 1 race; other than Asian, Black, Pacific Islander, or White]), insurance payer (Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, no insurance, self-pay, or other), and median zip code–based income quartile. Race and ethnicity were included in this analysis as a proxy for social and structural factors to account for disparities in obstetric outcomes.

Hospital characteristics included location and teaching status (urban teaching, urban nonteaching, or rural) and geographic region (Northeast, Midwest, South, or West). Relevant clinical conditions identified with ICD-10-CM codes were obesity, asthma, pregestational diabetes, chronic hypertension, mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, and substance use disorder), prior cesarean delivery, GA at delivery (22 weeks 0 days to 22 weeks 6 days, 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days, 24 weeks 0 days to 24 weeks 6 days, or 25 weeks 0 days to 25 weeks 6 days), preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), preterm labor, fetal growth restriction (FGR), and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP).

Statistical Analysis

Demographic, hospital, and clinical factors were compared between patients with vaginal delivery vs cesarean delivery using the Rao-Scott χ2 test. Two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses used survey weights and procedures that properly accounted for the complex survey design of the NIS, producing design-adjusted variance and national estimates. Analyses were performed from March to December 2025 using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and the Joinpoint Regression Program, version 4.8.0.1 (National Cancer Institute). To maintain patient privacy, we did not report results for cells with 10 or fewer individuals, per the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project Data Use Agreement.

Primary End Point

For the primary end point, we performed unadjusted and adjusted survey logistic regression models for each of the adverse outcomes, with cesarean delivery as the exposure of interest. For the unadjusted model, odds for cesarean delivery associated with each of the adverse outcomes were determined. For the adjusted models, demographic characteristics, hospital characteristics, and clinical conditions were included. Both unadjusted and adjusted models accounted for the survey strata. The adjusted odds ratios (AORs) for adverse outcomes associated with cesarean delivery were then determined. Results are presented herein as unadjusted odds (ORs) and AORs with 95% CIs. We calculated the marginal effects of cesarean delivery for adverse outcomes in the primary end point using adjusted prevalence difference (APD) and 95% CI. The APD was calculated using prevalences estimated from the multivariable survey–adjusted weighted logistic regression, accounting for the same covariates. We estimated 95% CIs using bootstrapping methods with random resampling (replacement) for 1000 replicates.

Secondary End Points

To analyze factors associated with cesarean delivery, we performed unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models for the outcome of cesarean delivery. For the unadjusted models, odds for cesarean delivery associated with each demographic, hospital, and clinical factor were determined. Then, adjusted models with all of these factors accounting for survey strata were performed. A sensitivity analysis for the first secondary end point was performed, adding indications for cesarean delivery.

To analyze the proportion of deliveries occurring by cesarean delivery for each year of the study period, trends analysis was performed using the National Cancer Institute’s Joinpoint Regression Program, which uses linear segmented regression and logarithmic transformation to calculate the average annual percent change (AAPC) with 95% CI.22,23 Trends were ascertained for all deliveries and individually by GA. To analyze the proportion of cesarean delivery associated with specific indications, we analyzed trends over the study period with joinpoint regression.13

Results

After study exclusion criteria were applied, an estimated 98 240 periviable births were identified, of which 39 595 (40.3%) were by cesarean delivery and 58 645 (59.7%) were by vaginal delivery (eFigure in Supplement 1). The cesarean delivery group had a median (IQR) maternal age of 29.9 (24.1-33.4) ) years and comprised 7880 Hispanic (19.9%), 12 845 non-Hispanic Black (32.4%), and 13 575 non-Hispanic White (34.3%) individuals, with 3610 (9.1%) self-identifying as other race and ethnicity. The vaginal delivery group had a median (IQR) maternal age of 28.2 (23.3-32.9) years and included 11 275 Hispanic (19.2%), 17 950 non-Hispanic Black (30.6%), and 20 915 non-Hispanic White (35.7%) individuals, with 6000 (10.2%) identifying as other race or ethnicity.

Cesarean delivery rates increased with advancing GA: 13.1% (n = 2915) of births at 22 weeks 0 days to 22 weeks 6 days, 37.4% (n = 9030) of births at 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days, 52.1% (n = 13 795) of births at 24 weeks 0 days to 24 weeks 6 days, and 54.8% (n = 13 870) of births at 25 weeks 0 days to 25 weeks 6 days. Cesarean delivery was significantly more common in the setting of prior cesarean delivery, HDP, and urban teaching hospitals compared with rural hospitals and was significantly less likely in the setting of FGR, PPROM, and preterm labor. In adjusted analyses, prior cesarean delivery (AOR, 2.57; 95% CI, 2.35-2.82), HDP (AOR, 3.19; 95% CI, 2.88-3.53), and delivery at an urban teaching vs a rural hospital (AOR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.26-1.76) remained associated with increased odds of cesarean delivery (Table 1). Periviable deliveries with diagnoses of FGR (AOR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.42-0.53), PPROM (AOR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.46-0.54), and preterm labor (AOR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.68-0.91) were associated with lower adjusted odds of cesarean delivery. In adjusted analyses, deliveries at 22 weeks 0 days to 22 weeks 6 days (AOR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.12-0.15) and 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days (AOR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.48-0.57), but not at 24 weeks 0 days to 24 weeks 6 days (AOR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.86-1.01), were associated with decreased odds of cesarean delivery compared with deliveries at 25 weeks 0 days to 25 weeks 6 days.

Regarding the association of diagnoses with mode of delivery, cesarean delivery compared with vaginal delivery was associated with significantly higher rates of malpresentation (62.5% vs 25.1%; AOR, 7.66 [95% CI, 7.06-8.31], P < .001), NRFS (43.9% vs 11.9%; AOR, 4.65 [95% CI, 4.26-5.08], P < .001), and labor disorders (3.0% vs 1.7%; AOR, 1.60 [95% CI, 1.23-2.06], P < .001) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Cesarean delivery was associated with increased unadjusted odds of all the studied adverse maternal outcomes except for PPH (Table 2). In adjusted analyses, these associations were retained, with cesarean delivery associated with increased odds of nontransfusion SMM (AOR, 2.50; 95% CI, 2.10-2.97), transfusion (AOR, 2.44; 95% CI, 2.03-2.94), a critical care composite (AOR, 3.41; 95% CI, 2.25-5.17), wound infection (AOR, 31.13; 95% CI, 10.15-95.46), VTE (AOR, 2.44; 95% CI, 1.27-4.70), endometritis (AOR, 3.74; 95% CI, 2.84-4.93), and peripartum hysterectomy (AOR, 5.19; 95% CI, 2.64-10.19), but not PPH (AOR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.41-0.55). Nontransfusion SMM, transfusion, a critical care composite, and endometritis had the highest APD among cesarean delivery in comparison to vaginal delivery, with 47 318 (95% CI, 46 753-47 884), 33 305 (95% CI, 32 846-33 764), 6956 (95% CI, 6712-7199), and 6494 (95% CI, 6311-6677) more cases per 1 million periviable births, respectively (Table 3).

In joinpoint trends analysis, the proportion of periviable deliveries occurring by cesarean increased significantly over the study period (AAPC for all periviable deliveries: 1.6%; 95% CI, 0.7%-2.4%) (Figure 1; eTable 1 in Supplement 1). In analyses stratified by GA (in weeks) at delivery, a trend toward an increase in cesarean delivery was observed for deliveries at 22 weeks 0 days to 22 weeks 6 days (AAPC, 8.9%; 95% CI, −0.9% to 21.0%) and 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days (AAPC, 2.5%; 95% CI, −0.3% to 5.7%). There was a statistically significant increase in cesarean delivery at 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days between 2016 and 2020 (AAPC, 3.8%; 95% CI, 1.0%-6.9%). In joinpoint analysis of diagnoses associated with cesarean delivery, only NRFS increased significantly over the study period, from 36.7% of cesarean delivery in 2016 to 50.5% of cesarean delivery in 2022 (AAPC, 5.2%; 95% CI, 3.4%-7.3%) (Figure 2; eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

Main Findings

Over the study period, the overall proportion of periviable births occurring by cesarean delivery increased significantly. Specifically, cesarean delivery increased among births at 22 weeks 0 days to 22 weeks 6 days and 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days and among deliveries with NRFS. Periviable cesarean delivery was associated with increased odds of nontransfusion SMM, transfusion, VTE, wound complications, endometritis, peripartum hysterectomy, and a critical care composite. Specifically, cesarean delivery was associated with more than twice the odds of nontransfusion SMM, transfusion, and VTE. The increased risk for adverse maternal outcomes associated with periviable cesarean delivery found in our analysis was consistent with findings of prior studies.9,24

Clinical Implications

Factors associated with significantly increased odds for cesarean delivery included prior cesarean delivery and HDP. In the case of prior cesarean delivery, patients and clinicians may be less likely to pursue trial of labor, particularly if induction is required in the absence of spontaneous labor. While current data suggest a rate of vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC) under 28 weeks of almost 30%, specific VBAC data for deliveries before 26 weeks are lacking.25 In the setting of periviable HDP for which delivery is recommended because continuing the pregnancy could pose serious maternal risk,26 cesarean delivery may be more common due to reluctance to undergo a lengthy induction. HDP at periviable GAs may also be associated with conditions such as placental insufficiency, placental abruption, and NRFS, which increase cesarean delivery risk if fetal intervention is planned.

Factors associated with decreased odds of cesarean delivery included preterm labor, PPROM, and FGR. In the setting of PPROM and preterm labor, decreased likelihood of cesarean delivery may be due to spontaneous progression of labor and limited need for augmentation. The association between FGR and lower odds of cesarean delivery may be secondary to ACOG guidance stating that FGR does not represent an indication for cesarean delivery27 and that there is less likelihood of neonatal benefit from cesarean delivery in the setting of decreased neonatal survival with periviable FGR.28

The findings in this analysis broadly support that society recommendations for deliveries at 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days have diffused into clinical practice. In 2017, ACOG recommended that cesarean delivery as of 23 weeks should be considered for fetal indications if neonatal resuscitation is planned.1 Consistent with this updated guidance, we found a significant increase in cesarean delivery at 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days between 2016 and 2020. Improving clinical outcomes among periviable neonates4,29 may also play a role in increasing cesarean delivery trends at 23 weeks 0 days to 23 weeks 6 days and in the setting of NRFS. ACOG does not consider continuous electronic fetal heart rate monitoring as an intervention separate from cesarean delivery: “in most cases its use will be linked to plans regarding cesarean delivery for fetal indications”1(p7); thus, increasing cesarean delivery rates and increasing diagnosis of NRFS are likely associated with use of these interventions in tandem. Similarly, it is likely that periviable cesarean delivery occurs more frequently at urban teaching hospitals where neonatal intensive care for periviable infants can be offered. Periviable infants born by cesarean delivery are more likely to receive a trial of intensive care after birth, and there is considerable interhospital variability in treatment of periviable infants, particularly those born between 22 weeks 0 days and 23 weeks 6 days.30,31

For patient counseling, the most clinically relevant finding is the increased risk for a range of adverse maternal outcomes associated with periviable cesarean delivery. Specific, increased maternal risks associated with periviable cesarean should be discussed with patients at risk for periviable delivery and should be considered in clinical decision-making. This consideration is especially important for cases with a high likelihood of neonatal morbidity and mortality and in which neonatal benefit of cesarean delivery is uncertain. The benefit of cesarean delivery for malpresentation at extremely early GAs remains controversial,32 and data on the benefit of periviable cesarean delivery for NRFS are limited. A broad range of factors may affect patient counseling on the mode of periviable delivery and whether neonatal intensive care is offered or recommended, including hospital resources, physicians’ individual perspectives, use of assisted reproductive technologies, and pregnancy intendedness33,34,35; providing clear data on maternal outcomes can help facilitate informed decision-making. Our findings broadly support ACOG’s recommendation for individualized decision-making regarding cesarean delivery for fetal indications during the periviable period given the potential for maternal morbidity. Future research is needed to better identify correlates between maternal risk and likelihood of improved neonatal outcomes with periviable cesarean delivery, to aid patients and clinicians in clinical decision-making where complex tradeoffs in maternal and neonatal outcomes may be present in the setting of limited data.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including analysis of a nationally representative population, a range of meaningful clinical outcomes among deliveries in a relatively uncommon GA range, and 7-year trends. However, there are several important limitations to consider when interpreting this study and exploratory analyses in general. First, this study did not include clinical data. As a result, we could not evaluate how clinical management (surgical technique, antibiotic, and thromboembolism prophylaxis) may have affected maternal outcomes. We were not able to review clinical records, including operative notes to identify the primary indication for cesarean delivery, and multiple indications may coexist. Second, using administrative diagnosis codes has known limitations, including misclassification and underascertainment; therefore, some deliveries may have an inappropriate or misclassified GA code. Third, this study did not capture downstream health consequences of periviable cesarean delivery. Fourth, while we found that urban teaching hospitals were more likely to perform periviable cesarean delivery, we were not able to characterize hospital-level resources for caring for periviable neonates, specifically for those with 22 weeks’ gestation, as resuscitation at this extremely early GA is not universally offered. Gestational week–specific estimates of adverse maternal outcomes associated with periviable cesarean delivery represent an important area for future study, particularly as sample sizes in national databases increase over time. Fifth, while we were able to detect several associations and trends, this study cannot determine causation. Sixth, we were not able to link maternal delivery to newborn hospitalizations and thus were unable to evaluate neonatal outcomes. The association between periviable cesarean delivery and maternal outcomes should be subsequently evaluated with linked maternal-neonatal data. Further study evaluating maternal risk of periviable prelabor vs intrapartum cesarean delivery may also aid with counseling and clinical decision-making. Seventh, although a classical or vertical hysterotomy is frequently required at periviable GAs and is associated with greater maternal morbidity than a low-transverse incision, the NIS does not contain a distinct code to identify classical cesarean delivery; we were therefore unable to characterize hysterotomy type or assess its contribution to the observed outcomes. Eighth, because the NIS captures delivery hospitalizations rather than individual hospitalizations, patients who experienced more than 1 periviable delivery during the study period may be represented more than once, and we were unable to identify or account for such recurrences.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study found that, compared with vaginal delivery, periviable cesarean delivery was associated with an increased risk for adverse maternal outcomes and SMM. Periviable cesarean delivery is increasing, particularly at very early GAs and in certain clinical circumstances. Maternal risks and outcomes are an important consideration for patient counseling regarding mode of delivery for periviable birth, especially in cases where neonatal benefit of cesarean is uncertain.
