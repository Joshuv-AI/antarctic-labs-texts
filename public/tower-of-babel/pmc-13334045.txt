Permeability prediction of 3D complex fracture networks in deep underground based on fractal analysis of trace maps
Authors: Jing Zhang, Liyuan Yu, Qi Ping, Wei Li, Ding Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstimating the permeability of a three-dimensional (3D) discrete fracture network (DFN) based on its geometric properties can provide a useful alternative to the resource-intensive direct hydraulic computation of the 3D DFN. The analysis of the relationships between the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 3D model (K’ 3D) and its geometric properties was conducted to elucidate the linkage between permeability and geometric characteristics. The geometric characteristics encompass fracture density (ρ), the power-law exponent (a) governing the fracture length distribution in the 3D model, and the fractal dimensions of the 2D cutting planes (Df). A regression function with variables (ρ, a, and Df) was proposed to predict K’ 3D. The results show that the calculated K’ 3D is in good agreement with the values predicted by the regression function reported in the literature. The K’ 3D increases following a linear relationship with the increment of ρ and decreases linearly with the increasing a. For the fracture network with a = 2.0, flow paths are composed of a few long fractures, whereas with a = 4.5, the flow path is composed of numerous minor fractures. The Df follows an exponential relationship where it increases with increasing ρ and a linear one where it decreases with increment of a. The regression function with Df can estimate the permeability of the 3D DFN model despite whether there are continuous flow paths in cutting planes. Compared to the findings predicted by the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 2D cutting planes (K’ 2D), the anticipated outcomes from the regression function demonstrate a closer alignment with the simulated results.Keywords: Three-dimensional, Fracture network, Cutting plane, Geometric parameters, Fractal dimensionSubject terms: Civil engineering, Hydrology, Computational science

Introduction

Assessing the fluid flow within a three-dimensional (3D) fracture network at depth in the subsurface holds promise for reliable evaluations of CO2 storage1,2, seismic hazard3,4, and the storage of nuclear waste5,6. The fracture networks serve as the main conduits for fluid flow and transport within rock masses when the permeability of the matrix is significantly lower than that of the fractures7–12. The internal topography of a single fracture13–16 and the geometrical arrangement in complex networks17–21 would collectively contribute to the hydraulic complexity of natural fracture networks. The 3D discrete fracture network (DFN) models can capture the variability and heterogeneity present in real fractured rock masses, including the location, orientation, permeability and connectivity, which provide valuable insights for various engineering and environmental applications17,18,22,23. Simplifying 3D DFN models to two-dimensional (2D) cases is a practical approach for addressing computational challenges associated with high-density fracture networks10,24,25. The 2D model essentially represents a cutting plane of the original 3D fracture network, and as a result, some important aspects such as fracture orientation and connectivity in the 3D model are inevitably lost or simplified. Compared to the 3D fracture networks, the 2D fracture networks often have lower permeability26,10,12. Therefore, it should be valuable to predict the permeability of the 3D fracture network by combining the geometric characteristics of 3D and 2D models to avoid time-consuming direct hydraulic calculation of the 3D DFN.

Considerable research has concentrated on correlating the properties of 2D fracture networks with those in 3D cases27–32. Darcel et al.27 characterized the fracture-to-fracture correlation with fractal dimension and derived a stereological function that can give 3D distribution by 2D outcrop. Li et al.14 analyzed the 2D geometric features extracted from 3D fractured rock masses and proposed a fractal description model to evaluate the natural fracture network’s connectedness. Zhao et al.32 reconstructed the 3D model of sandstone through computed tomography (CT) images. Xu et al.25 presented an efficient set of methods to acquire statistical parameters of 3D fractured rock masses from the 2D trace maps. However, the above studies are limited to the analysis of geometric characteristics and neglect the permeability of 3D fracture networks. Permeability serves as a crucial parameter in the quantitative assessment of flow characteristics. In order to link the permeability of 3D fracture networks with their geometric properties, Lang et al.24 established a relationship between the fracture density observed in outcrops and the equivalent fracture density within 3D fracture networks. They yielded comparable maximum and minimum permeability. Huang et al.26 introduced a regression function aimed at estimating the permeability of three-dimensional discrete fracture networks (DFN) by utilizing the permeability of 2D cutting planes (outcrops) and the geometric parameters of the 3D model. McBeck and Renard2 derived 3D characteristics that govern the fluid flow of fracture networks. However, these approximations are inadequate for low-density fracture networks, where seepage may occur in 3D but not in the corresponding 2D outcrops. Although seepage may not occur in 2D outcrops, spatial organization of fractures in 2D fracture network, particularly the fractal dimension, can still be directly obtained from these outcrop29. Glazner and Mills33 establish the fundamental theoretical bridge by demonstrating that the fractal dimension of a 2D cutting plane through a 3D object quantitatively relates to its 3D fractal dimension. Lei et al.34 demonstrate the applied reliability of the method by using geometric and fractal parameters measured from 2D outcrops to generate statistically equivalent and larger-scale 3D fracture network models. Brabazon et al.35 confirm the physical significance of the 2D fractal dimension by showing that it successfully captures the 3D hydraulic complexity of a fracture void space in an analytical flow model. Wang et al.36 show the practical validity of this relationship, using the fractal dimension derived from 2D observations to successfully characterize the evolution of a complex 3D mining-induced fracture network. Stock and Frampton37 provide powerful inverse validation, proving that fractal parameters measured from 2D surface scans are sufficient to accurately synthesize realistic 3D fracture aperture fields. The spatial organization of fractures, governed by their mechanical formation processes, has been identified as a critical factor controlling connectivity and flow28,30,32. Maillot et al.38 demonstrated that fracture networks generated with simplified mechanical rules (featuring a high proportion of T-intersections) exhibit significantly higher flow channeling and lower overall permeability compared to Poissonian models with statistically equivalent but spatially random fracture distribution. This highlights that permeability predictions relying solely on statistical distributions without considering spatial structure may be inherently limited. The fractal dimension derived from 2D trace maps (Df) offers a potential pathway to implicitly capture such complex structural attributes, including connectivity and channeling, which are challenging to quantify directly in 3D models. While the aforementioned studies have made significant progress in predicting 3D permeability, this endeavor is fundamentally a stereological problem. Darcel et al.27 systematically elaborated the stereological rules for fractal fracture networks, demonstrating the mathematical relationships for inferring 3D properties (such as the length exponent and fractal dimension) from 2D observations. However, the predictive accuracy is constrained by the density and connectivity of the fracture network. Particularly in low-density networks where the 3D model is permeable but the corresponding 2D cutting planes are impermeable, traditional prediction methods relying on the permeability of 2D cutting planes fail. Therefore, exploring a predictive method that does not depend on the existence of continuous flow paths within the 2D cutting planes constitutes a primary objective of the study.

Statistical geometry parameters were employed to construct a series of 3D DFN models, and corresponding 2D cutting planes were taken out of the 3D models. Calculating the flow through 2D and 3D DFN models is done by equivalent pipe network (EPN) models. The simulation results are in strong agreement with the predictions from the regression function reported in the literature. The Df of all 2D cutting planes are analyzed. The effects of fracture density (ρ) and the power exponent of the fracture length distribution (a) and Df on the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 3D DFN models (K’ 3D) are quantified, and a multivariable regression function is proposed for predicting K’ 3D, taking into account ρ, a and Df. The multiple regression function is available for fracture networks with low densities when seepage occurs in the 3D fracture network but not in any of the 2D cutting planes and is more effective than the fitting function that respects the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 2D cutting plane (K’ 2D).

Geometric parameters of 3D DFN models

Natural discontinuities at depth in the subsurface have extensive scales that distribute from microscopic to engineering scales9,39,40. Since generally geometric parameters extracted from 2D outcrops or one-dimensional (1D) scan lines are available, it is difficult to generate real 3D fracture networks ideally. The DFN model is extensively employed for simulating natural fracture networks, particularly when the geometric properties of these networks, such as the length and spatial distribution of fractures, adhere to certain predefined probability distributions. Within the DFN framework, fractures are typically depicted as ellipses, circles, or convex polygons, as discussed in previous studies41–43. In the present study, all fractures are modeled as circular disks, with each fracture aperture uniformly set at 1 mm without considering the heterogeneity of the aperture. The fractures’ central location and orientations (dips and dip directions) are considered to be distributed in a random and uniform manner. Based on the assumptions made above, the seepage properties of DFN models are primarily governed by two key parameters including fracture length and density. The fracture density (ρ) denotes the number of fracture centers per unit volume of the 3D DFN model. Bour et al.44, through a detailed analysis of multi-scale fracture networks in the Hornelen Basin, validated the effectiveness of a first-order statistical model. This model successfully describes the geometric characteristics of fracture networks using the power-law exponent, the fractal dimension, and a density constant in their case study. Drawing on this classical model, our study employs a power-law distribution for fracture lengths and focuses on the fractal dimension, density, and exponent as the core parameters for characterizing the geometry of both the 3D fracture network and its 2D cutting planes. The sizes of the representative element volume (REV) of some engineering have been reported to be distributed between 10–30 m48,49,50,51. The model size of 20 m adopted in this manuscript exceeds the REV threshold of most rock masses and can effectively capture the connectivity and permeability of the fracture network. Therefore, we believe that the model size offers a practical tool for initial permeability estimation at this common engineering scale. Investigating the REV and scale effects across a wider range of model sizes is a critical and necessary direction for future research.

Flow simulation in fracture networks

Flow simulation in 3D DFN models

Although nonlinear Navier–Stokes equations are commonly used to analyze fluid flow in fractures, they are computationally intensive and often impractical for characterizing flow in complex fracture networks52. An open-source software, ADFNE, is utilized to derive the equivalent pipe network (EPN) model from the 3D DFN model to enhance computational efficiency45. The fracture network is reduced to pipes that link the centroids of fractures with their intersection points with other fractures. Dead-ends and isolated pipes are removed from the model to facilitate the evaluation of flow paths. The fluid flow is caused by the water head difference between the inlet and outlet boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The flow starts at the inlet nodes and traverses through the inner nodes to reach the outlet nodes. The water heads at inlet and outlet nodes correspond to the applied water heads at the respective boundaries. The hydraulic heads of the inner nodes are determined using the finite difference method53. The fluid flow through the 3D DFN was simulated using EPN model, the details of which are provided in Appendix A. The equivalent permeability K of each DFN realisation is first calculated from Darcy’s law (Eq. (A8)). And then K is normalised by the reference permeability K0, defining the dimensionless permeability K’ = K/K0. K0 is the permeability of a single, smooth fracture with a 1 mm aperture oriented parallel to the flow direction. Throughout this paper, K’3D and K’2D denote dimensionless permeability for the corresponding 3D and 2D DFN models, respectively.

Hydraulic boundary condition of 3D DFN model.

Considering different fracture length distributions, a sequence of 3D DFN models is generated with progressively increasing fracture density. The geometric parameters employed for constructing the 3D DFN models are identical to those tabulated in Table 1. A hydraulic water head of 1 m is applied across the yz plane at x = 0 m (where H1 = 1 m) and x = 20 m (where H2 = 0 m); the other boundaries remain impermeable. K’ 3D of the 3D DFN model is evaluated. The relationships between K’ 3D and a for various values of ρ is illustrated in Fig. 3(a). K’ 3D exhibits a linear decrease with increasing a. When ρ rises from 0.0125 to 0.0500 m−3, K’ 3D experiences an increase from 2.516 to 36.640 for a = 2.0 and from 0.536 to 15.569 for a = 4.5. For a = 2.0, the increase in K’ 3D (34.1240) is approximately 2.3 times greater than that for a = 4.5 (15.034). The differences in increment of K’ 3D for different a clearly show that a smaller a has a more significant impact on the variation of K’ 3D. This phenomenon is due to the proportion of large fractures in a fracture network with a small a is larger than that with a large a. A few large fractures make up the majority of the main flow pathways in the fracture network with a small a54. It is less likely for the small fractures to intersect and form flow pathways. Figure 3(b) depicts the relationships between K’ 3D and ρ for various a. The K’ 3D exhibits a linear increase with rising ρ. When other geometric parameters of the DFN model keep the same, a larger ρ is the same as more fractures which inevitably increases the probability of fractures intersecting with each other and constructs more flow paths. The phenomena shown in Fig. 3, where K’ 3D decreases linearly with increasing a and is more sensitive to changes in ρ for smaller a, can be interpreted through the lens of the percolation theory established by De Dreuzy et al.55 based on the length exponent. De Dreuzy et al. indicated that for a < 3, the permeability of fracture networks is dominated by long fractures. Specifically, in the regime where a < 2, network connectivity and permeability are almost entirely controlled by a few long fractures spanning the system. The range of a values in our study (2.0 to 4.5) spans the transition zone (2 < a < 3) and the edge of the short-fracture dominated regime (a > 3) defined in their theory. When a = 2.0, the system behavior is closer to the long-fracture dominated mode,an increase in fracture density ρ significantly increases the number of long fractures, efficiently constructing new flow paths and resulting in a substantial increase in K’ 3D. In contrast, when a = 4.5, the system comprises numerous shorter fractures, and its connectivity more closely resembles classical percolation theory, requiring more fracture intersections to form effective paths, thus leading to a more subdued response of K’ 3D to increasing ρ. Our results, from the perspective of 3D models, validate and extend the patterns observed primarily in 2D networks by De Dreuzy et al.55.

The efficiency of simulation results of 3D DFN models

A multi-variable regression function has been presented in the literature in order to evaluate the permeability of 3D DFN models, which have fracture densities ranging from 0.025 to 0.125 m−326. Huang et al.26 showed that, for relatively dense DFNs in which both the 3D fracture network and the corresponding 2D cutting planes are permeable, the 3D permeability can be predicted from geometric characteristics and the permeability of the 2D cutting planes. To assess the efficiency of the EPN results for dense fracture networks, the simulated K’3D values were compared with those predicted by the regression function of Huang et al.26, which relates K’3D to K’2D and geometric parameters of the 3D DFN (fracture density ρ and the power-law exponent a of the fracture length distribution) as tabulated in Table 2. Six power exponents of the fracture length distribution (a = 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, and 4.5) are taken into consideration for each fracture density. The simulated values of K’ 3D show a good consistency with predicted K’ 3D as illustrated in Fig. 7. The two K’ 3D sets have a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.922. This proves that the denser 3D fracture network’s permeability can be determined by the EPN method.

Prediction of the permeability of 3D models based on fractal analysis

Fractal analysis of 2D cutting planes

It should be noted that, in low-density DFN models where many cutting planes lack continuous flow paths, the combination of 3D fracture density ρ, the 3D fracture length-distribution exponent a, and the equivalent permeability of the 2D cutting planes K’2D cannot accurately predict the permeability K’3D of the 3D DFN models. In the present synthetic trace maps, Df is introduced as a compact descriptor of spatial organization for extracted 2D cutting planes, particularly when hydraulic indicators derived from the cutting planes are unavailable or uninformative because no continuous flow path exists44,56,30. The fractal dimensions are introduced to evaluate the permeability of 3D DFN models with impermeable cutting planes.

The original 2D cutting plane (Fig. 8a) is converted into a binary image (Fig. 8b) to meet the requirements of the FracLab software which defaults to counting white pixels on a black background. Figure 8(b) shows the reversal binary image of Fig. 8(a). The fractal dimension was calculated with the fractal analysis software Fraclab by the box-counting method. The binary image is subdivided into square meshes with subsequently varying sizes, as shown in Figs. 8(b) and 8(c). The square size is assumed to be 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64, 1/128, 1/256, and 1/512 m, which increase in the form of power-law growth.

The K’ 3D can be approximated using the proposed multi-variable regression function based on the 2D fractal analysis. The 2D fractal analysis requires less computational resources and is substantially faster than the direct simulation of 3D fracture networks. Particularly in cases where cutting planes lack a continuous flow path, the 2D fractal analysis can quantitatively describe these complex 2D cutting planes and give fractal dimensions for predicting K’ 3D. The geometric parameters of 3D fracture networks, including ρ and a, can be derived from initial 3D DFN models. The 3D DFN model can be constructed using statistical geometric data derived from geological surveys22,32,57,58. The contribution of this study is methodological rather than conceptual: compared with Huang et al.26, we propose the regression framework combining Df that remains applicable when the corresponding 2D cutting planes are non-percolating and K’ 2D is 0. Consequently, the proposed regression function can be used as a primary in-practice estimation to estimate the permeability of 3D fracture networks.

It is critical to note that Eq. (2) is an empirical regression that is fitted to specific units and the defined scale utilized. A fully nondimensional reformulation remains a subject for future work. A critical limitation of this study lies in the simplifying assumptions adopted to establish the relationship between geometric parameters and permeability and validate the predictive capability of the proposed regression function. The 3D DFN models assumed random and uniform fracture distributions, a constant aperture, and a fixed mode size restricts the direct applicability of the proposed regression function to heterogeneous natural systems. Compounding these issues is the omission of spatial correlation and fracture clustering, features inherent to natural fracture networks. Future investigations should therefore prioritise incorporating spatial heterogeneity, aperture distributions, and a rigorous analysis of scale effects across a broader range of sizes to enhance the model’s physical realism and general applicability.

Conclusions

A series of three-dimensional (3D) discrete fracture network (DFN) models were developed to conduct a systematic analysis of the impact of fracture density (ρ) and power exponent of fracture length distribution (a) on the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 3D model (K’ 3D). The computational efficacy of flow simulations in the fracture networks was enhanced by deriving the equivalent pipe network (EPN) models from the 3D DFN models. From each 3D DFN model, nine two-dimensional (2D) cutting planes were extracted, and the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 2D models (K’ 2D) was determined. The accuracy of K’ 3D determined by the EPN models was assessed by comparing the simulated results with those predicted using the regression function described in the literature. The fractal dimensions of the 2D cutting planes (Df) were computed, ρ, a and Df to predict K’ 3D.

The results show that K’ 3D increases linearly with the increase in ρand decreases with growing a. As ρ grows from 0.0125 to 0.0500 m−3, the increase in K’ 3D for a = 2.0 is approximately 2.3 times greater than that for a = 4.5. The amplitude of fluctuations of K’ 3D increases as the value of a decreases. This trend may reflect that fluid flow in networks with low power exponents (a = 2.0) is dominated by a few long fractures, which is consistent with classical percolation theory8,55. When a = 4.5, the contribution of minor fractures to the connectivity of the fracture network becomes more important. Compared to the dimensionless equivalent permeability of initial 3D DFN models, the dimensionless equivalent permeability of the 2D cutting plane is smaller. The calculated K’ 3D is well agreed with that predicted by the method reported in the literature, and the correlation coefficient is 0.922. The fractal dimension of the 2D fracture traces (Df) exhibits a linear reduction as a grows, demonstrating an exponential increase as ρ increases. The proposed regression function can efficiently predict K’ 3D with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.986. This approach can predict K’3D of the original 3D network even in situations where the network of 2D fracture traces is not percolating and the corresponding K’2D is 0. While full stereological inference of 3D parameters is still required, performing a 2D fractal analysis provides a mathematically constrained alternative for estimating permeability during stochastic 3D DFN modeling, particularly when extracted 2D planes lack direct hydraulic connectivity.

The scale effect’s impact on the magnitude of permeability was disregarded. No evaluation is conducted on the representative elementary volume (REV) of the 3D fracture network. It is possible that the REV of 3D DFN models will not be achieved. The scale effect will be considered in further studies.
