From Selective Immunoglobulin E (IgE) Deficiency to Primary Immunodeficiencies Coexisting with Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria: A Call for Immunological Survey
Authors: Murat Türk, İnsu Yılmaz
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Selective IgE deficiency is defined as a significant decrease in total IgE levels (<2 kIU/L) in patients whose other immunoglobulin levels are within the normal range. It is currently not accepted as a primary immunodeficiency (PID) in the current classification of inborn errors of immunity report,1 but low total IgE levels are commonly associated with other immunoglobulin or subclass deficiencies, particularly common variable immune deficiency.2 The exact prevalence and clinical features of selective IgE deficiency are poorly defined. Previous studies have shown that patients with selective IgE deficiency have a significantly higher prevalence of chronic sinusitis, cardiovascular diseases, recurrent infections, allergic diseases, malignancies, and autoimmune diseases.2 It has been shown that Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is the most frequent autoimmune clinical manifestation and that 17% of patients with this condition may present with chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU).2

Chronic spontaneous urticaria is a prevalent disease that affects approximately 1% of the general population across all age groups. Currently, the underlying pathophysiology of CSU is explained by at least two different endotypes, which appear to be linked to different phenotypic features and treatment responses. In type-I autoallergic CSU, autoreactive IgE antibodies directed against autoantigens degranulate mast cells. In type-IIb autoimmune CSU, mast cell degranulation occurs due to immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies against the high-affinity IgE receptor or IgE itself, and it is characterized by low total IgE levels, higher rates of immunoglobulin G anti–thyroid peroxidase antibody (IgG-anti-TPO) positivity, and higher rates of comorbid autoimmune diseases.3 It has been shown that 10% of patients with CSU have very low levels of total IgE,4 which appears to meet the definition of selective IgE deficiency. Type-IIb autoimmunity is expected to be the underlying endotype in these patients, with higher rates of autoimmune diseases and omalizumab resistance. A recent case series supported this hypothesis and demonstrated omalizumab resistance, even at higher doses, in six patients with selective IgE deficiency. Four of these patients had high IgG-anti-TPO levels and three had comorbid autoimmune diseases.5

The underlying reasons for the differentiation between type-I autoallergic CSU and/or type-IIb autoimmune CSU remain unclear. However, it is well established that the management of CSU involves physicians from various specialties, including primary care physicians, dermatologists, and allergologists/immunologists. International chronic urticaria guidelines recommend including total IgE and IgG-anti-TPO levels in the diagnostic workup of patients with CSU.3 The more frequently total IgE levels are measured, the more often selective IgE deficiency is likely to be encountered. Clinicians should be cautious when interpreting very low IgE levels and keep in mind that autoimmunity may mask an underlying PID. It is highly advisable to incorporate fundamental immunological assessments, such as a complete blood count with differential and quantitative immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgM, IgA, and IgE), in patients with autoimmune CSU to identify those with unrecognized primary immunodeficiencies. Early detection of PIDs in patients with autoimmune manifestations can lead to more targeted and effective treatment strategies, potentially improving long-term outcomes
