Patch-sampled contrastive learning for dense prediction pretraining in metallographic images
Authors: Mingchun Li, Yang Liu, Dali Chen, Jining Bao, Yan Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The shapes, sizes, and distributions of microstructures determine the mechanical properties of alloy products, necessitating identification of microstructures in metallographic images during the manufacturing process. Previous studies demonstrated the success of deep learning in analyzing such images. However, existing methods do not fully consider the high cost of annotations, which limits the application of deep learning-based methods. Considering the popularity of self-supervised learning for natural images, further research is required to design a microstructure-specific pretraining framework. To this end, we propose a novel patch-sampled contrastive learning (PSCL) method, considering the characteristics of metallographic image segmentation tasks. First, image- and patch-level contrastive learning frameworks are designed to help the model effectively capture visual features. The former captures global features, whereas the latter captures local features in greater detail. Second, a multiscale strategy is introduced to improve the adaptability of the subsequent microstructure segmentation. Finally, considering that patches may concurrently contain multiple microstructures, a sampling method based on feature similarity was designed to capture the discriminable features of different categories. In experiments, after model fine-tuning of only one annotated image, the proposed method achieved a Dice score of 0.6296 which is higher than those of existing self-supervised learning methods with identical model structure, proving the effectiveness of the method in microstructure segmentation. The source code and dataset of the proposed PSCL in this work are available at https://github.com/neulmc/PSCL.

Introduction

Metallographic images are microscopic images that reveal the microstructures of materials. They serve as an important tool for identifying and measuring these microstructures1. Considering the significance of microstructures in relation to mechanical properties of materials, analyzing metallographic images is an essential part of the manufacturing process2–4. In this study, the research object was an aluminum-magnesium-silicon alloy with good forming and processing properties5,6. The observed metallographic images after casting and homogenization heat treatments are shown in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Metallographic images and corresponding annotations for the three types of microstructures.

Metallographic images and corresponding annotations for the three types of microstructures.

In Fig. 1, three types of particles are observed: silicon particles, a magnesium-silicon phase, and an Fe-containing phase densely appearing in the aluminum matrix. In the grayscale images, the magnesium-silicon phase is the darkest, followed by the silicon particles, whereas the iron-containing phase is relatively light-colored. In addition, the proportion of silicon particles is the largest, and the distribution is relatively uniform, whereas the Mg–Si and Fe-containing phases are relatively small in proportion. These microstructures are of great significance for analyzing product quality. However, because the grayscale is relatively close, distinguishing between the three often requires material experts. Manually measuring and identifying these individually is a time-consuming and laborious task7.

To reduce the amount of manual labor, scholars have attempted to convert this into a computer vision problem, designing corresponding algorithms8–10. Early studies mainly adopted rule-based and traditional machine-learning methods. Typical methods used for metallographic image segmentation include watershed11, level sets12, and support vector machine13. Such methods14–18 are easy to implement and do not require large datasets. However, most of them can only be used to deal with relatively simple problems, such as foreground and background segmentation with significant inherent inter-class differences. When dealing with more complex problems, promising performance is often difficult to obtain.

Deep learning has become increasingly popular for natural images19. Its application to complex metallographic image analysis has also attracted considerable attention. Early researchers used deep models as feature extractors for traditional classifiers20. Gradually, several studies adopted an end-to-end approach for mainstream deep models to solve relevant problems. For example, in 2022, Luengo1 designed an image segmentation method that includes multiple convolutional networks, such as UNet21, DeepLabV322, and the feature pyramid network (FPN)23 to solve the problem of microstructure analysis of steel materials. Recently, vision transformer models have been used to solve specific problems24,25. Thus, methods26–30 based on deep learning have become the mainstream for solving metallographic image-related tasks.

However, most existing metallographic image analysis methods are designed under supervised conditions31,32. In particular, for segmentation problems that require pixel-level annotation, the upfront cost of acquiring deep models is typically high. Deep-learning methods typically outperform traditional methods. However, their drawbacks still limit practical applications. Unlike natural images where patches often contain distinct semantics (e.g., “cat” vs. “tree”), metallographic microstructures exhibit dense distributions with minimal inter-class variation (Fig. 1). This motivates our supervised-assisted sampling strategy to ensure meaningful contrastive pairs. Some scholars have attempted to introduce strategies such as semi-supervised, unsupervised and few-shot learning33–39. Recent works employ diffusion models to implement anomaly and defect detection, taking into account the extensive high-precision annotations in real-world scenarios40,41. However, allowing the model to capture effective visual representations in a metallographic context remains an open problem.

Fortunately, recent self-supervised learning has provided an effective pretraining paradigm42, capturing effective visual representations in advance without annotations through contrastive learning43–46 or masked image modeling47–49, following by fine-tuning the model using labeled data. For example, self-supervised learning brings the features of augmented views of the same image closer together. It also designs parameter updates to prevent degradation42. Such methods have attracted considerable attention in natural image-classification tasks. Subsequently, to improve the performance of self-supervised methods in dense prediction tasks, patch-based contrastive learning or generation methods were studied to strengthen local information50,51. When the cost of supervised information is high, this type of method is popular for solving natural imaging tasks.

Existing self-supervised methods do not entirely work on metallographic images. Natural images52 or other types of images53,54 often contain patches with discriminative semantic information. In contrast, metallographic images have densely distributed microstructures. For example, even without supervision information, it would be reasonable to assume that two randomly selected natural images or small patches contain different categories in many datasets. However, in metallographic images, the differences between the samples are very small. Distinguishing these instances with the same semantics may lead to negative effects. Therefore, the instances used for contrastive learning should be carefully selected. Considering that the actual segmentation task following pretraining is clear, an intuitive idea is to introduce supervised information in advance. To solve this problem, self-supervised learning methods for metallographic image segmentation require specific designs.

In this study, a novel contrastive learning method called patch-sampled contrastive learning (PSCL) is proposed for metallographic image segmentation tasks. Unlike the original self-supervised learning framework without manual annotations, a small number of labeled images are used to enhance the effect of pretraining. Unlabeled patches are sampled based on feature similarity with such supervised information, and instances with different semantics are carefully selected for contrastive learning. To adapt to microstructure segmentation tasks, our method applies contrastive learning to both image-level (global) and patch-level (local) features. We also introduce multiscale strategies to improve sensitivity to differently sized microstructures. In general, the contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

Methods

In this study, a contrastive learning approach based on supervised assistant patch sampling is proposed. This is a pretraining method for metallographic image segmentation. The exact process, which employs a type of UNET for microstructure segmentation, is illustrated in Fig. 2. To reduce the demand for manual labeling, a pretraining method was designed based on contrastive learning to capture task-related visual representations. This is achieved by making the extracted features of the two views of the same image closer. To meet the characteristics of microstructural analysis, both global image-level and local patch-level features with multiscale strategy were considered. Furthermore, given that microstructures are densely distributed in metallographic images, supervised information is introduced to enhance the discrimination between microstructures. In the following section, the proposed method is described in detail.

Image-level contrastive learning

Contrastive learning is a popular framework for self-supervised learning. In computer vision tasks, contrastive learning has better linear separability than masked image modeling, such as image reconstruction. Representative implementations include MoCo44 and SimCLR55, which have achieved significant success on natural image datasets. Considering the effectiveness of these methods in image classification tasks, we introduced contrastive learning to capture the global features of metallographic images.

Global contrastive learning enhances feature discriminability by minimizing distances between features from different augmented views of the same image. Simultaneously, it maximizes separation from features of other images in the embedding space. Specifically, image enhancement methods were used for a metallographic image, to obtain two instances. In the network shown in Fig. 2, these instances are fed into the encoder and projection head functions. The encoder is used to extract the global metallographic image features, which are retained for the subsequent fine-tuning stage. The head function is removed once the pretraining is completed. During the pretraining stage, two views are fed into the same encoder (SimCLR framework), or the respective networks with moment updates to avoid degradation (MoCo framework). The overall implementation process is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Patch-level contrastive learning

To analysis the microstructure quantitatively, a deep network is used to achieve a dense prediction of the metallographic images. Considering that image-level contrastive learning focuses more on capturing global visual representations, corresponding training strategies should be introduced to strengthen locality. Recently, scholars designed various patch-contrastive strategies based on transformer structures57 and convolutional neural networks (CNNs)50. Inspired by these studies, we propose a multiscale patch-level contrastive learning method for decoders. Specifically, for the UNet network shown in Fig. 2, a local projection head function is introduced. It is placed in the decoder and composed of activation function, convolution layer, and batch normalization layer. Supervised-assisted sampling strategy

The proposed model pretraining method for aluminum alloy metallographic image segmentation comprises two components: image-level contrastive learning and patch-level contrastive learning. The former is designed to capture global features by constructing image-level pairs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(q,{k}_{+}\right)$$\end{document}, while the latter aims to capture local features to facilitate subsequent semantic segmentation tasks by forming patch-level pairs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({p}_{i},{d}_{i+}\right)$$\end{document}, as illustrated in Fig. 3. In the upper section of Fig. 3, the objective of contrastive learning is to align the global features of the metallographic sample with its augmented instance, while distancing them from the features of other images through (1). This approach enables the model to acquire effective feature representations at the image level, such as differentiating metallographic samples subjected to varying process parameters.

During contrastive learning, positive samples and their augmented instances share the same semantics, while negative samples are derived from patch sampling that is more akin to supervised patches of other categories. To maintain class balance, Eq. The diversity of the selected patches is enhanced by comparing them individually. This approach offers two advantages. Firstly, it narrows the intra-class distance and widens the inter-class distance, capturing feature representations that aid in distinguishing different microstructures. Secondly, since only a subset of patches is sampled instead of all, contrastive learning can be applied to larger feature maps without increasing computational costs. This facilitates the model’s ability to capture effective features of some small-sized microstructures.

Pretraining and fine-tuning implementation

The segmentation of microstructures comprises two stages: pretraining based on contrastive learning and fine-tuning under a few supervised samples. Pretraining uses unlabeled data to learn visual representations. Fine-tuning then adapts these for segmentation tasks. In this section, the implementation details for pretraining and fine-tuning are introduced.

For the pretraining stage, we introduced image- and patch-level contrastive loss under the sampling strategy. The image-level contrastive loss captures effective global features by bringing different instances of a sample or production process closer to the encoder. Patch-level contrastive loss focuses on restoring the local information in the decoder and enhances the effect through multiscale sampling strategies. The loss function of the pretraining stage is as follows:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L=(1-\alpha\:){L}_{q}+\alpha\:{L}_{p},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} is a weighting parameter used for balancing the two loss functions.

Overall, a contrastive learning pretraining method was designed based on the characteristics of the microstructure analysis field. The proposed patch sampling strategy is not limited to specific contrastive learning frameworks. This work considers implementing the proposed strategy under the MoCo and SimCLR frameworks, which are two landmark works in the field of self-supervised learning. When constructing a negative sample set, the former uses the momentum encoder to generate and store in a feature queue. These negative samples do not undergo gradient updates during backpropagation. And SimCLR utilizes negative samples from the current mini-batch, and all of them are subjected to gradient updates during backpropagation. These two methods have achieved significant results on natural images and have a wide range of applications. In order to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed strategies, implementation algorithms based on these two existing frameworks were designed. Among them, the implementation details of MoCo are provided in Algorithm 1.

For our specific MoCo and SimCLR design, primarily a patch-level contrastive loss with a multiscale strategy was introduced. However, Algorithm 1 is not a completely self-supervised learning method. Supervised samples are introduced to improve the ability of the model to distinguish between different microstructures. Considering the dense distribution of microstructures in metallographic images, introducing supervised samples in advance can guide the model in capturing task-related visual representations. This design improves the adaptability of the method to microstructure image analysis.

The fine-tuning stage is performed after model pretraining. Specifically, the projection head function used for contrastive learning is removed, and the weights of the encoder and decoder are transferred to the segmentation network. Compared with random initialization, the pretrained model can capture effective visual representations. Results

In this section, the effectiveness of the proposed method is verified. First, a metallographic image dataset and evaluation metrics are introduced. Subsequently, the experiments conducted using different methods are compared using only one annotated image. Finally, ablation studies, parameter sensitivity analyses and analysis of pretraining effectiveness are presented.

Dataset

In this study, the experimental object was a homogenized aluminum alloy from a production line. A series of preheating-treatment microstructural images with different holding times were obtained using a metallographic microscope. These industrial data are private, but it could be available for non-commercial purposes through our public repository. In total, 198 metallographic images of size 376 × 376 were collected in this study. Among them, there were 66 images with holding temperatures of 15/30 min, 60 with holding temperatures of 45/60 min, and 72 with holding temperatures of 90/120 min. In the experiments, to evaluate the effectiveness of the method, 80% of the images were selected as the training set for pretraining and the remaining 20% constituted the testing set. In the pretraining stage, to obtain different views of the same sample, augmentation methods such as rotation, flipping, cropping, and changing contrast were used to build positive pairs, as shown in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Instances of different views of same metallographic sample.

Instances of different views of same metallographic sample.

Furthermore, only one image was randomly selected from the training set for annotation. The annotation was manually implemented at the pixel level under the guidance of material experts. The annotated labels included four categories: silicon particles, Fe-containing phase, magnesium-silicon phase, and an aluminum matrix (background). This supervised sample was used for both unlabeled patch sampling in the pretraining stage, and training the model in the fine-tuning stage. This process was repeated five times to more accurately evaluate the effectiveness of the pretraining method with a small number of annotations.

Discussion

In this study, a novel contrastive learning method called patch-sampled contrastive learning (PSCL) is proposed for metallographic image segmentation tasks. Our method constructs two contrastive learning frameworks: image-level and patch-level. Both use a feature-similarity-based sampling strategy. Conventional self-supervised contrastive learning exhibits inherent limitations in metallographic image analysis due to the densely distributed and visually similar microstructures (e.g., Si particle vs. Fe-containing phase shown in Fig. 1). Randomly sampled patch pairs might belong to the same phase category, leading to ineffective learning of discriminative features. Our supervised-assisted sampling strategy addresses this fundamental challenge by leveraging labeled patches as anchors to ensure meaningful negative pairs that span distinct phase categories while filtering out semantically ambiguous comparisons through feature-space similarity measurement (Eq. 6). This approach specifically focuses the contrastive learning on the most discriminative local characteristics, which is particularly crucial for microstructure analysis. The improvement in Dice coefficient compared to random sampling (Table 1) demonstrates the essential role of this design in analyzing densely packed microstructures. The following is a more detailed analysis and discussion.

First, for the microstructure segmentation results, compared with the existing models, the proposed method achieves higher scores. As shown in Table 1, PSCL-SimCLR achieved the highest Dice and ACC scores of 0.6296 and 0.9574, respectively. The prediction results shown in Fig. 5 more intuitively reveal this point. PSCL-MoCo and PSCL-SimCLR predictions align closely with expert annotations. This includes small or easily confused microstructures. Introducing multiscale patch-level contrastive learning enhances the capture of local information, leading to better prediction results.

Secondly, we conduct an ablation study on the designed patch-level contrastive loss, the multiscale strategy, and patch sampling in our method, as shown in Tables 2 and 3, and 4. Among these components, the patch sampling strategy has the most significant impact. For PSCL-SimCLR, the Dice score without a sampling strategy is 0.5584. For PSCL-MoCo, this metric drops to 0.5010, which is even lower than the 0.5134 achieved by random initialization in Table 1. This is because, in metallographic images, microstructures are densely distributed across the alloy matrix. The metallographic images studied in this work differ significantly from natural images. The high similarity between images limits the performance of MoCo and SimCLR based solely on image-level contrastive learning. An intuitive solution is to construct contrastive learning using patches, like DenseCL. However, this approach does not entirely resolve the issue. Patches are smaller than full images. However, semantically similar negative samples still occur. Different patches may contain the same particles, making it challenging for traditional patch-based contrastive learning methods to capture the semantic information of microstructures effectively. By adopting a sampling strategy designed with supervised information, the model can learn meaningful visual representations and discriminative deep features during the pretraining stage. As a result, the segmentation performance of this method is significantly improved.

Then, the performance of the model is verified for different numbers of annotated samples as shown in Fig. 7; Table 5. In general, regardless of the number of annotations, the methods with contrastive learning pretraining always performed better than the baseline. In the detailed images shown in the third row of Fig. 7, for fully supervised learning, the pretrained models predict more coherent regions for indistinct microstructures compared with baseline. For the quantitative analysis presented in Table 5, when the number of supervised images is five, the two proposed methods, PSCL-MoCo and PSCL-SimCLR, outperform the baseline method by 0.0156 and 0.0245, respectively. When the number of supervised images is 159, these two indices ​​become 0.0221 and 0.0238, respectively. This shows that even under fully supervised learning, the proposed pretraining method is still meaningful and effective.

Finaly, to better illustrate this point, we used the K-means61 method to directly perform unsupervised clustering analysis of the pretrained model, and the results are shown in Fig. 8. The cluster analysis presented in Fig. 8 was conducted before the model was fine-tuned. The definition of the terms “Scale-1,” “Scale-2,” and “Scale-3” are the same as in Tables 3 and 4, which represents the visualization results of feature maps with different scales. The number of clusters was set to four to correspond to the actual number of categories. Overall, even without fine-tuning, the pretrained model still captures the task-related visual representation. Through image- and patch-level comparative learning, the foreground, background, and corresponding contours can be effectively distinguished. Meanwhile, with an increase in the feature map size, the clustering results of Scale-2 and Scale-3 show more details than those of Scale-1. Using this sampling strategy, clustering results close to the actual segmentation ground truth are obtained. This reveals the potential reason why the method can achieve better performance in the fine-tuning stage.

The proposed pretraining method for microstructure segmentation tasks is based on contrastive learning, which significantly differs from semi-supervised learning and transfer learning approaches. First, unlike semi-supervised learning, the proposed method is a pretraining approach rather than directly training the model using labeled samples. Semi-supervised learning has two key limitations. It depends heavily on initialization parameters and requires high-quality supervised samples. This study considers the scenario where only one annotated image is available, making it more akin to a few-shot learning problem. If the supervised samples lack sufficient representativeness or the model initialization is poor, the generated pseudo-labels or constraints may fail, leading to the collapse of the semi-supervised strategy.

Furthermore, there are significant differences between our pretraining methods and transfer learning. The latter is based on the fundamental assumption that there is a meaningful correlation between the source domain data and the target domain data. However, a significant discrepancy exists between the latent distribution of natural image datasets (such as Pascal or MOCO) as the source domain and the target data (metallographic images). In our view, neither the “pretraining on natural images followed by fine-tuning on metallographic images” paradigm nor the domain adaptation paradigm is fully suitable for the current problem. Additionally, a practical challenge is that, compared to natural image datasets, publicly available metallographic image datasets are extremely scarce, and their data volume is insufficient due to the limited availability of industrial data.

Therefore, our solution is to perform pretraining directly on the metallographic image dataset. By leveraging a small amount of supervised annotations as guidance, the method aims to achieve close intra-class distances and distant inter-class distances. Unlike semi-supervised learning, it does not generate potentially unreliable pseudo-labels, nor is it a conventional unsupervised contrastive learning method. Instead, it focuses on pretraining to capture discriminative visual features. This is the most distinctive aspect of our method. Additionally, our pretraining approach remains valuable even in supervised learning scenarios. Even when all training images have corresponding annotations, our proposed pretraining can still enhance the method’s performance, further highlighting the fundamental differences between our approach and semi-supervised methods.

Conclusions

In this study, we proposed a pretraining method for microstructure segmentation task in metallographic image based on contrastive learning. To segment the microstructure, image- and patch-level contrastive learning methods were designed to help the model capture global and local features. Considering that the microstructure is densely distributed in the matrix, a sampling method based on feature similarity was also introduced to enhance the model’s ability to distinguish different types of microstructures. Through the specific design of metallographic images, the model effectively captured visual representations during the pretraining stage. In the experiments, compared with other existing methods, the proposed method displayed better computer vision performance indicators when only one image was annotated, thereby reducing manual annotation costs. Even in the fully supervised case, model pretraining is meaningful, which indicates the potential of the method in practical applications. However, some microstructures may have extremely small sizes, necessitating the use of smaller patches to capture their visual representations. This, in turn, increases computational power consumption. Additionally, processing noisy images should be considered to improve the method’s detection capability across diverse scenarios. For these reasons, future work in this area will focus on addressing extreme cases and noise-related challenges. Furthermore, developing effective model pretraining methods for other types of metal images and domains is also a promising direction for further research. To this end, broadening the applicability of pretraining methods and enhancing their practical deployment in extreme and complex production processes will constitute a pivotal direction of our future works.
