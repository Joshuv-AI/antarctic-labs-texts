Retrospective Validation of an AI Based Tool for Vault Prediction After Implantable Collamer Lens Surgery for High Myopia
Authors: Sanjana Molleti, Ethan J Lindberg, Hanna Pawlowski, Mina M Sitto, Nadim S Azar, Triston B Crook, Phillip C Hoopes, Majid Moshirfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOur study evaluated a cohort of patients who previously underwent EVO Implantable Collamer Lens (ICL) implantation to assess the retrospective performance of the ICLGuru™ artificial intelligence (AI) sizing tool in predicting postoperative vault. Additionally, biometric factors associated with vault prediction error were identified.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 104 eyes from 56 patients who underwent EVO V4c or V5 ICL implantation between May 2023 and June 2025. Preoperative ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) images, from patients who had already undergone ICL surgery, were retrospectively analyzed using ICLGuru to generate predicted postoperative vaults. Actual postoperative vault was measured at 1-, 3-, or 6-month follow-up visits, with 3-month measurements preferentially included for analysis. Mixed-effects regression models were used to evaluate associations between biometric variables and vault prediction error.ResultsMean absolute error was 135 ± 110 µm, indicating a tendency for ICLGuru to overestimate postoperative vault (p < 0.001). Among 16 eyes with actual postoperative overvault, ICLGuru retrospectively identified 15 cases in which a smaller lens size was predicted to achieve target vault, suggesting these outcomes may have been flagged. Out of 7 eyes with actual undervault, only two cases may have been flagged with use of ICLGuru, indicating that the tool is not as useful in preventing undervault. Mixed-effects regression demonstrated that greater iridocorneal angle and higher aRISE were significantly associated with lower vault prediction error (p < 0.05).ConclusionICLGuru demonstrated useful postoperative vault predictions with performance comparable to existing machine learning and UBM-based nomograms, though it appeared to overestimate longer term vault in our cohort. The tool may be valuable in identifying eyes at risk for excessive vault. aRISE and iridocorneal angle were significant predictors of vault prediction error, supporting their importance in future ICL sizing models.Keywords: ICLGuru, implantable collamer lens, EVO ICL, artificial intelligence, vault, phakic intraocular lens

Introduction

Posterior chamber phakic intraocular lenses (pIOLs) have expanded the scope of refractive surgery, increasing options for patients not considered candidates for laser‑assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) or photorefractive keratotomy (PRK), due to high degrees of myopia or myopia with astigmatism.1,2 The Visian EVO Implantable Collamer Lens (ICL) (EVO+ ICL, STAAR Surgical, Monrovia, CA, USA) was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2022 for the treatment of myopia up to −20.0 D and astigmatism between 1.0 and 4.0 D.3 Similar to LASIK and PRK, the ICL can also be utilized for mild myopia treatment. A critical preoperative consideration for ICL performance is selecting the optimized lens size. ICLs are currently offered in four sizes, which can impact the height of the postoperative vault, defined as the distance between the posterior surface of the ICL and the anterior surface of the crystalline lens. Inappropriate ICL sizing can lead to increased risk of patient complications. An undersized vault may cause the ICL to come in contact with the natural lens, increasing the risk of cataract formation, while an oversized vault may lead to elevated intraocular pressure, pupillary block, and acute angle closure glaucoma.4–6 To optimize lens sizing, several nomograms incorporating ocular biometrics have been developed, such as the FDA manufacturer’s nomogram, Optimized White to White, Parkhurst, and Dougherty-Rivera.7 Although currently available nomograms improve lens selection, incorrect ICL size remains one of the most common indications for ICL explantation.8,9 As a result, there is growing interest in additional variables and predictive models that may improve size selection.

Machine learning has emerged as a promising approach for predicting postoperative vault, enabling surgeons to have more information for sizing determination.10–12 ICLGuru™ (Revai Care, Newark, DE, USA) is an artificial intelligence (AI) powered software that uses ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) images, biometric inputs, AI algorithms, and mathematical models to predict the postoperative vault for each of the four ICL sizes.13 By generating predicted postoperative vault values for each available lens size, the platform may assist surgeons in selecting the lens best suited for a given eye. This tool has been evaluated in a prospective study by Zaldivar et al in populations from Argentina, Ireland, Belgium, and the Philippines, but has not been analyzed extensively in the United States.

Our study included patients who had previously undergone ICL surgery at a single center and evaluated discrepancy between retrospectively predicted vault by ICLGuru and actual postoperative vault. As a US‑based, single‑surgeon study, this retrospective validation avoids the performance inflation that may arise when a predictive tool is used prospectively, influencing the surgeon’s final lens choice. Additionally, we performed exploratory regression analyses to determine whether any biometric variables were associated with vault error.

Methods

This retrospective study evaluated eyes that had already underwent ICL implantation with the EVO V4c or EVO V5 ICL (Staar Surgical, Monrovia, CA, USA) by a single surgeon at a refractive surgery center in the United States between May 2023 and June 2025. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York Institutional Review Board (#A20-12-547-823) and adheres to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient data was deidentified and patients provided written informed consent prior to the study. Included patients were over 18 years of age, had previously underwent ICL implantation for myopia with or without astigmatism, had a preoperative UBM scan of sufficient quality for ICLGuru analysis, and at least one postoperative anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) evaluation at 1, 3, or 6-months. Eyes with uveitis, herpes zoster ophthalmicus, other ocular pathology, or prior ocular surgery were excluded.

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from patient records. Preoperatively, each patient received comprehensive slit lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundus exam. Preoperative manifest refraction (MR), uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), and best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) were collected. Biometric measurements including anterior chamber depth (ACD), aqueous depth (AQD), white to white (WTW), and central corneal thickness (CCT) were obtained from the LENSTAR LS 900 version i9.6.3.0 (Haag-Streit, Koeniz, Switzerland), OPD-Scan III (Nidek Co., Ltd), Galilei G4 (Ziemer Ophthalmic Systems AG), AS-OCT (iVue 80, Optovue Inc), and Pentacam HR (Oculus, Arlington, WA, USA). Additional measurements including sulcus to sulcus (STS), sulcus to sulcus lens rise (STSL), angle to angle (ATA), and iridocorneal angle were obtained with the VuMax UBM (Sonomed Escalon, New Hyde Park, NY).

Each patient received one of four available sizes of ICL implants (12.1 mm, 12.6 mm, 13.2 mm, or 13.7 mm). Size selection was determined using a combination of established ICL nomograms. ACD and WTW were applied to the FDA‑approved manufacturer’s nomogram and Optimized WTW nomogram.8 STS and STSL measurements were used in the Dougherty-Rivera and Parkhurst nomograms.8 Final lens size selection was based on consensus across these methods, with surgeon discretion applied in cases of disagreement. ICLGuru was not utilized for lens size selection preoperatively as this tool was not available at our health care center until October 2025, after the completion of this patient cohort. All ICL implantation surgeries were performed by a refractive surgeon (M.M.) at a single center. Surgical technique and postoperative care were standardized and have been described previously by our group.14

Postoperative evaluations were conducted at 1-day, 1-week, 1-month, 3-months, and 6-months after ICL implantation. Vault size was measured using serial AS-OCT scans. Measurements were not available at all postoperative time points for each patient. Prior literature has demonstrated that the postoperative vault achieves relative stability at approximately 3-months after ICL implantation.15,16 To create a primary analysis cohort for comparison with ICLGuru predictions, the 3-month measurement was preferentially included. If unavailable, 6-month measurements were used, followed by 1-month measurements when necessary. Seventy‑six (73.1%) eyes were from 3-month data, 8 (7.8%) from 6-month data, and 20 (19.3%) from 1-month data.

ICLGuru was used retrospectively, on patients who had already undergone ICL implantation, to generate predicted vault values based on their preoperative UBM. All UBM scans were performed by the same technician. UBM scans were obtained at the time of the patient’s preoperative workup, prior to the availability of ICLGuru at our institution. All scans were obtained in a room with standardized lighting conditions to account for pupil size and accommodation. As a result, images were not acquired using ICLGuru’s integrated device, which uses a standardized algorithm with automated quality checks to ensure optimal UBM scans. Instead, existing archived UBM images were processed using ICLGuru’s dynamic link library (DLL) to ensure minimum standards of sharpness, alignment, and consistency were met. For each eye, between 7 and 50 frames were automatically selected by the DLL for analysis by ICLGuru. From this subset, the frame with the highest alignment score was selected. ACD, ATA, and aRISE were measured for the chosen frame and input into the ICLGuru formula. For each lens size, predicted central vault, peripheral vault, and angle were generated, along with safety band calculations categorizing vault prediction by target, low, and high vault risk levels.

We define actual vault as the postoperative OCT measurement of vault in our patient cohort. Agreement between ICLGuru predictions and actual vault outcomes was assessed by comparing the implanted ICL size, the achieved postoperative vault, and the ICLGuru predicted vault. Target vault was defined as between 250 and 750 µm, while values below 250 µm and above 750 µm were classified as undervault and overvault, respectively.17 The primary outcome of interest in our analysis was vault error, defined as the ICLGuru predicted vault for the surgeon selected ICL size minus the actual postoperative vault. Overestimates show positive vault error (predicted > actual), while underestimates have negative vault error (predicted < actual). Mean error and mean absolute error were also calculated.

Statistical analyses were completed in Python, using the pandas, statsmodels, and scikitlearn statistical libraries, with a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Exploratory mixed-effects regression models were constructed to evaluate associations between individual biometric variables and vault error. Models included fixed effects for gender, eye laterality, and lens size, and a random intercept to account for inter-eye correlation. Missing data (0–11%) were assumed to be random and were addressed using mean imputation. Potential biometric predictors were first evaluated in partially adjusted single‑predictor mixed-effects regression models. Pairwise Pearson correlations were performed to assess multicollinearity among biometric variables and representative variables were selected for multivariable modeling based on statistical significance and colinearity. Results

A total of 104 eyes from 56 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis cohort. The majority of patients were female (62.5%), highly myopic (mean MRSE −9.12 ± 2.69 D), and in their 20s to 30s (mean 33.2 ± 6.9 years) at the time of surgery. Of the 104 eyes, 81 eyes (77.8%) achieved an actual vault within the target range (250–750 µm) and 7 eyes (6.7%) demonstrated extreme vault (<100 µm or >900 µm). No cases of clinically significant complications were observed and no explantation or revision surgery was required. The distribution of actual vault is shown in Figure 1. Of these 81 eyes, ICLGuru correctly predicted a vault within the target range for 64 eyes (79%). ICLGuru incorrectly estimated 3 target eyes (3.7%) to be undervaulted and 14 target eyes (14.3%) to be overvaulted. No differences in preoperative characteristics were noted in these cases.

Twenty‑three of 104 eyes resulted in an actual postoperative vault outside the target range, of which 16 eyes (15.4%) were overvaulted and 7 eyes (6.7%) were undervaulted (Figure 2). ICLGuru correctly predicted that 15 overvaulted eyes should have received a smaller lens size to be within the target vault range. In the remaining actual overvault eye, both the implanted size by the surgeon and ICLGuru prediction would have resulted in overvault. In the actual undervault group, ICLGuru correctly predicted that two eyes would result in undervault with the implanted lens size. Five of the 7 undervaulted eyes resulted in incorrect predictions by both ICLGuru and the surgeon. ICLGuru’s sensitivity for overvault was high at 93.8% in comparison to 79% for target vault and only 28.6% for undervault, indicating that while the device was excellent at predicting overvault, it had much more difficulty with undervault cases (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1). ICLGuru had higher specificity for undervault (96.9%) than both overvault (84.1%) and target vault (73.9%). PPV was highest for target vault at 91.4% with 51.7% and 40% for overvault and undervault, respectively. NPV was 50% for target vault, 98.7% for overvault, and 94.9% for undervault.

Discussion

This study evaluated the retrospective performance of the ICLGuru AI vault prediction tool in a cohort of patients who had already undergone ICL implantation at a single US center. A key feature of this study is the retrospective design, as ICLGuru was not available at our institution during the surgical period, and therefore did not play a role in lens size selection preoperatively. This design ensures that the predicted vaults generated by ICLGuru are completely independent of the surgeon lens selection and could not have influenced outcomes. Overall, our findings demonstrate that ICLGuru provided clinically meaningful predictions, particularly in eyes at risk for excessive vault.

ICLGuru demonstrated a tendency to overestimate vault with a mean error of +93 µm in the primary analysis cohort (Figure 3). Indeed, 14 out of 81 eyes (17.3%) which achieved an actual target vault were predicted to result in a vault over 750 µm. In contrast, Zaldivar et al found that predicted ICLGuru vaults were on average lower than actual vaults, with a mean vault error of −12 µm 1-day after surgery and +24 µm 1-month after surgery.13 This discrepancy may be due to the timing of vault measurement. In our cohort, vault decreased from 539 μm at the 1-month postoperative visit to 483 μm at the 6-month visit (Table 3). Consistent with our findings, previous studies have demonstrated a progressive decrease in vault over time, with the most significant changes occurring 3 to 6 months post-operatively.15,18 Our primary analysis cohort was predominantly composed of 3-month data points, while Zaldivar et al assessed vault in the early postoperative period. This suggests that ICLGuru may be optimized for vault prediction immediately after surgery, however, as the lens settles and vault decreases with time, the predicted value may be an overestimation of longer term vault, past the initial postoperative period. The Zaldivar study cohort also was primarily evaluating a highly myopic Asian population, while our cohort consists of a predominantly Caucasian population, which could have further contributed to our varied results. Additionally, our methodologies differed, as Zaldivar et al used ICLGuru in conjunction with UBM preoperatively to select an appropriate lens size, while our study retrospectively uploaded UBM images into the device after it became available at our health care center. As the ICLGuru-UBM device uses a standardized algorithm during the collection process to guide the practitioner and ensure that images are of sufficient quality, it is possible that the lower mean error in the Zaldivar et al study reflects improved UBM image quality and reduced inter-user discrepancies.

Mean absolute error found in our primary analysis cohort was 135 ± 110 µm. Machine learning models to predict postoperative vault, and thus ICL size, have been developed including XG Boost, Random Forest Regression (RFR), and linear regression (LR). Di et al note mean absolute error of 12170 ± 3.39, 123.13 ± 5.44, and 142.02 ± 8.53, for XG Boost, RFR, and LR, respectively, at one-week postoperatively.19 Despite the variation in vault measurement timing, ICLGuru appears to demonstrate absolute prediction errors within the range of these machine learning algorithms. When compared to one study evaluating currently utilized UBM‑based formulas, ICLGuru also demonstrated similar or better results as mean absolute errors of the KS, WH, ZZ, and Zhu formulas were 126.55, 131.45, 237.48, and 241.23, respectively.20 Overall, ICLGuru does not clearly outperform existing machine learning or UBM‑based formulas in these indirect comparisons. Additionally, it correctly predicted that the selected lens size would lead to target vault in only 79% of eyes that achieved actual target vault in our cohort. This suggests a continued need to evaluate multiple formulas and biometrics when selecting ICL size. ICLGuru may serve as a useful additional tool in cases where nomograms are unable to achieve a clear consensus or when anatomical features are outside the thresholds of nomograms.

More specifically, flagging of overvaulting may be a particular use for ICLGuru, as seen with its high sensitivity of 93.8% indicating that if a case of overvault occurred, ICLGuru was likely to predict it (Table 2). In 15 out of 16 cases of actual postoperative overvault in our cohort, ICLGuru could have correctly flagged the outcome and recommended an alternative smaller lens size that was predicted to achieve a target vault range, suggesting that ICLGuru use in the preoperative planning period may have flagged overvault outcomes (Figure 2). With the addition of a central port in the EVO ICL, historical concerns associated with undervaulting, such as cataract formation, have decreased, suggesting that the V4c and V5 lenses are more tolerant to low vault than previous generations.21–23 Therefore, ICLGuru’s propensity to anticipate overvaulting may be a beneficial strategy for safety in future EVO ICL cases. Additionally, operator variability is a known challenge of UBM‑based nomograms. Accordingly, ICLGuru’s ability to provide real‑time corrections when obtaining UBM data and to utilize a DLL to select the highest level of alignment may allow for improved quality of vault predictions. For example, in our cohort, mean ACD measured by LENSTAR and Galilei was different from that calculated by ICLGuru (3.21 vs 3.04 mm), likely due to the technician selecting a different frame from the one selected by ICLGuru. Therefore, ICLGuru’s selection process based on a standardized alignment score may optimize image quality and reduce inter-operator discrepancies historically associated with UBM‑based measurements.24,25

Our regression analysis provides additional insight into biometric predictors of vault error. Among all evaluated variables, aRISE and iridocorneal angle were significantly associated with vault error in the final multivariable model (Table 5). Both demonstrated inverse relationships with vault error, indicating that eyes with lower aRISE and smaller angles are more prone to overestimation of postoperative vault. aRISE, or lens rise, measures the amount of lens protrusion past the angle‑to‑angle line. Lens rise and iridocorneal angle have been noted to be important predictors of vault error in other studies, further validating the findings demonstrated in our analysis.26–28 In contrast, traditional sizing parameters including WTW, ACD, and STS were not independently associated with vault error in our cohort (Table 4). These findings have also been demonstrated in other studies, suggesting that the future of ICL sizing models may benefit from use of lens rise and angle measurements rather than currently utilized biometrics.29,30

The most significant limitation of this study is that preoperative UBM images were acquired using standard clinical protocols prior to the availability of ICLGuru at our institution, rather than through ICLGuru’s device with an automated quality control algorithm. As a result, the frame selected by ICLGuru’s DLL may not represent the same anatomical plane that would have been captured under ICLGuru’s guided protocol. This may have introduced additional variance into predicted vault values and may have contributed to the observed systematic overestimation. Using the most updated version of the software may have improved outcomes of the device. Additionally, the primary analysis cohort combined vault measurements from 1-month (19.3%), 3-month (73.1%), and 6-month (7.8%) postoperative visits. Although no statistically significant differences in vault error were identified across time points (Table 2), vault decreased progressively over time (539 µm at 1 month to 483 µm at 6 months), and the 20 eyes contributing 1-month measurements likely reduced the observed mean vault error relative to what a purely 3-month cohort would have shown. However, this would support our finding that ICLGuru consistently overestimates vault predictions. Notably, vault response is not only subject to ICL size but may also be caused by additional factors, such as age, lens compression/bowing, or ICL haptics not effectively engaging with the sulcus.31 Therefore, though ICLGuru may have provided alternative lens sizes predicted to be in range, these alternatives may also have not resulted in a target vault. The multivariable regression model required mean imputation for 0–11% missing biometric values, which may impact effect size estimates. All surgeries were performed by a single refractive surgeon at a single US institution, and lens size selection followed a consistent nomogram consensus approach. While this design controls for surgical technique variability and limits confounding, it also limits generalizability to other practice settings, surgeons with different nomogram preferences, or patient populations with different biometric distributions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ICLGuru demonstrates promising performance as a tool for predicting postoperative vault, particularly in identifying eyes at risk for excessive vault where it may support safer lens selection. Our mean absolute error of 135 µm appears to be similar to currently available UBM‑based and machine learning nomograms and suggests that ICLGuru consistently overestimates vault outcomes. Vault prediction error is significantly associated with aRISE and iridocorneal angle, supporting the transition away from traditional sizing parameters such as WTW, ACD, and STS. ICLGuru may be best utilized in conjunction with currently available nomograms to limit the impact of overvaulting errors or when attempting to decide between two lens options.
