The (in)congruence effects of organizational green compensation and employee green conscientiousness on pro-environmental behavior: evidence from China
Authors: Haiyan Zhang, Shuwei Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn past decades, the Chinese government has enacted a series of ecological policies to encourage organizations, the pivotal institutional agents implementing national policies, and employees, the crucial micro-actors engaging in ecological construction, to bring about employee pro-environmental behavior (EPEB) which is the foundation to realize nation’s ecological strategies. Yet, the effectiveness of a widely adopted organizational-level green management practice “organizational green compensation” (OGC) and a typical individual-level green personality trait “employee green conscientiousness” (EGC) have been explored alone, ignoring the prevalence of various OGC-EGC combinations and failing to clarify the potential influences of OGC-EGC (in)congruence on EPEB. Our research endeavors to address this limitation by resolving the following two problems: What are the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB in the Chinese context? And what is the underlying mechanism?MethodsStudy 1 surveyed EGC, OGC, and EPEB among 778 subordinate-supervisor dyads and sought to test two single effects and three sets of comparisons between and within the congruence and incongruence effects using the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis. Study 2 measured EGC, OGC, employee environmental commitment (EEC), and EPEB among 713 subordinate-supervisor dyads and attempted to verify the mediating role of EEC employing the block variable approach.ResultsStudy 1 found that OGC and EGC independently, positively promote EPEB (ß = 0.39, p < 0.001; ß = 0.24, p < 0.001), the OGC-EGC congruence relates to higher EPEB compared to the OGC-EGC incongruence (p11 = 3.77, 95% CI = [0.71, 23.04]; p10 = − 0.65, 95% CI = [− 25.80, 0.42]; [α3 −α4 +α5] = − 0.24, 95% CI = [− 0.41, − 0.07]), the EPEB level is higher when the OGC-EGC congruence is at a high rather than low level ([α1 +α2] = 0.51, 95% CI = [0.39, 0.62]), and the EPEB level under the high-low combination is lower than that under the low-high combination ([α1-α2] = − 0.20, 95% CI = [− 0.38, − 0.02]). Study 2 further confirmed that EEC plays a mediating role during the OGC-EGC-EPEB relationships (the indirect effect = 0.14, 95% CI = [0.08, 0.20]).ConclusionThis research substantiates the value of OGC-EGC (in)congruence to fully understand EPEB variations such that, EPEB will be boosted (hampered) when OGC is (in)congruent with EGC; a higher congruence between OGC and EGC leads to higher EPEB, the high-low combination of OGC and EGC results in lower EPEB compared to the low-high combination, and EEC plays a mediating role in the above relationships, offering the Chinese evidence and providing theoretical and practical implications for the optimization of the OGC-EGC combinations to strengthen EPEB.Keywords: Organizational green compensation, Employee green conscientiousness, Employee pro-environmental behavior, Employee environmental commitment, Polynomial regression

Introduction

Currently, the globe is undergoing major challenges from increasingly serious environmental issues such as climate change, pollution, natural resource depletion, energy shortage, ecosystem degradation, and biodiversity loss [1], most of which are anthropogenic [2] and could be mitigated by changing human behaviors [3]. Hence, the Chinese government highlights ecological civilization as a fundamental plan for the nation’s sustainable development and has led the whole nation in an arduous quest for ecological conservation: as early as in 1973, the environmental protection policy referring to “comprehensive planning, reasonable layout, comprehensive utilization, turning harm into profit, relying on the masses, everyone's hands, protecting the environment, and benefiting people” had been outlined; during the reform and opening up period, environmental protection had been established as nation’s basic policy and since then sustainable development has become a national strategy; since the 18th National Congress in 2012, ecological conservation has been of top priority, the “Innovative”, “Coordinated”, “Green”, “Open”, and “Shared” development philosophy has been enforced, and a range of strategies such as constructing a beautiful China and building a clean and beautiful world have been deployed.

As only through organizations' appropriate interpretation and systematical goal-setting could a nation’s strategic deployments in ecology be actually manifested as employees’ daily engagement in workplace pro-environmental behaviors, organizations are the key institutional agents carrying out a nation’s ecological strategies [4]. Likewise, since only through employees' subjective initiatives in behaving pro-environmentally could the nation’s ecological deployments come true, employees are the crucial micro-actors [5, 6] and their pro-environmental behaviors are the foundation for achieving the nation’s ecological strategies [7, 8]. Therefore, a variety of green management practices aiming at fostering employee pro-environmental behavior (EPEB) have emerged, including green support from organizations, supervisors, or colleagues [9–13], green transformational leadership [2, 14–16], spiritual leadership [17], Taoist leadership [18], and green human resource management (GHRM) [19–27]. The effectiveness of these organizational-level green management practices has attracted numerous scholars in the organization and management research domain and has accumulated insightful guidelines for EPEB improvement practices. For example, Zibarras and Coan found in 214 UK organizations of different sizes and from different industry sectors that, the most effective HRM practices in encouraging employees to become more pro-environmental primarily involve education and training via internal awareness raising campaigns such as a series of lectures, seminars, or debates for employees actively championed by senior management, induction programs highlighting environmental concerns, informal encouragement by line management, environmental behavior or targets appraisal, team-based and individual incentives or reward programs that encourage pro-environmental behaviors [21]. These HRM practices are also the most often used ones in organizations [21].

Meanwhile, the possible influences of individual-level green characteristics consisting of attitude, personal norms, perceived behavioral control [28], emotion and affect [29], intention [11], conscientiousness [30], motives [14, 15], knowledge [31], values [15], commitment and satisfaction [10, 12, 13], passion [2, 16, 17], and perceived resource scarcity and environmental unpredictability [32] have also attracted research attention. For example, according to the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions, Bissing-Olson et al. predicted the interactive effects of employees’ daily (un)activated positive affect and pro-environmental attitude on their daily task-related and proactive pro-environment behaviors [29]. Using a daily diary design among 56 employees, they found that employees’ pro-environmental attitude and daily unactivated positive affect such as feelings of contentment, feeling relaxed, and being at rest positively predict their daily task-related pro-environmental behaviors; daily activated positive affect such as feeling excited, euphoric, and enthusiastic only positively predict the proactive pro-environmental behaviors of employees with a less positive pro-environmental attitude.

Undoubtedly, green management practices at the organizational level and green characteristics at the individual level are both indispensable for EPEB enhancement. Yet, according to person-environment fit theory, they may further interact with each other through different combinations within the congruence and incongruence categories to exert differentiated effects on EPEB [33, 34]. Taking organizational green compensation (OGC), which is the most common and powerful means linking organizations’ environmental sustainability goals to employees’ interests [27, 35, 36] as well as the mostly favored GHRM practice in the Chinese organizations [26], and employee green consciousness (EGC), which is the most powerful personality trait predicting employee environmental attitudes and behaviors [30, 37], for example, as shown in Fig. 1, four typical combinations within the OGC-EGC congruence and incongruence categories prevail in organizations: the high-high combination corresponding to Quadrant 1 when organizations offer large green rewards for employees with strong green conscientiousness and the low-low combination corresponding to Quadrant 3 when organizations provide few green incentives for employees with weak green conscientiousness within the OGC-EGC congruence category, and the low-high combination corresponding to Quadrant 2 when employees manifest strong green conscientiousness but organizations provide little green compensation and the high-low combination corresponding in Quadrant 4 when organizations supply large green compensation for employees with faint green conscientiousness within the OGC-EGC incongruence category. Unfortunately, true consideration of the OGC-EGC (in)congruence in Fig. 1 has been terribly absent from the extant EPEB research. In an effort to scientifically guide organizations to engage in a beautiful China construction, our study is interested in the OGC-EGC (in)congruence and seeks to answer the following two research problems: (1) What are the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB in the Chinese context? And (2) What is the underlying mechanism?

Four typical combinations of OGC and EGC within the congruence and incongruence categories

Following recommendations from scholars exploring the (in)congruence effects in organization and management research field [38–41], we dedicate more attention to inferring the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB through making three sets of comparisons separately between the congruence and incongruence effects, within the congruence effect, and within the incongruence effect based on the insights of person-environment fit theory after predicting the single effect of OGC/EGC on EPEB. Person-environment fit theory claims that employee behaviors hinge on not only individual factors or environmental conditions but also the congruence or fit between these two different kinds of determinants [33, 34]. Accordingly, EGC, the most effective personality trait predicting EPEB, and OGC, the most common and powerful GHRM practice, may not only promote EPEB independently, the (in)congruence between them may also exert meaningful effects on EPEB such that the more congruent OGC and EGC are, the higher the EPEB level becomes; EPEB level tends to be higher when the OGC-EGC congruence is at a high rather than low level; EPEB level is likely to be lower when OGC level is higher than EGC level compared to that under the situation where EGC level is higher than OGC level. Besides, employee environmental commitment (EEC) tends to play a mediating role in the above OGC-EGC (in)congruence effects on EPEB based on interdependence theory and relevant commitment research [42–44]. Adopting the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis [38–41, 45–47], two empirical studies with questionnaire survey data separately from 778 and 713 subordinate-supervisor dyads in the Chinese organizations support our predictions.

Our study makes three main contributions to previous literature. First, it may be the first one to examine the interplay of organizational-level green management practices, i.e., OGC, and individual-level green characteristics, i.e., EGC, on EPEB, which has the potential to challenge current thinking about the antecedents and dynamics of EPEB. Second, it advances EPEB research methods by introducing the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis which is effective in exploring the (in)congruence effects and widely accepted by scholars in the organization and management research field. Third, it enriches person-environment fit theory by incorporating the roles of organizational-level “OGC” and individual-level “EGC” to predict EPEB. Our study also contributes to organizations’ EPEB enhancement practices in building a clean and beautiful world from the perspective of the optimization of the OGC-EGC combinations.

The remainder of our study is structured as follows. Section “Theory and hypotheses” has an in-depth, theoretical elaboration of the potential (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB to theoretically address our research problems by developing relevant research hypotheses. The following two sections seek to empirically resolve our research problems such that section “Method” details our empirical study design, including sample and procedure, measurements, and statistical analysis, while section “Results” reports hypotheses testing results. Finally, section “General discussion” presents the main conclusions regarding our research problems and discusses the theoretical and practical implications derived from our study, as well as the limitations and suggestions for future research.

Theory and hypotheses

The single effect of OGC/EGC on EPEB

In previous literature, AMO is a fundamental framework used to predict the behavioral and performance effects of (G)HRM practices from the perspectives of individuals’ (green) abilities (A), (green) motivations (M), or (green) opportunities (O) [25, 32, 48–51]. Put differently, as long as (G)HRM practices manage to consolidate one of the AMO elements, organizations will harvest their desirable employee (green) attitudes, behaviors, or performance. EPEB, which involves employee pro-environmental or green behaviors in the workplace supporting the environmental sustainability goals of organizations [30, 52], is exactly the desirable employee behaviors encouraged and motivated by all kinds of organizations’ environmental programs and practices. Although scholars prefer to differentiate EPEB into the voluntary category [53] and the involuntary category [7], EPEB here mainly encompasses employees’ daily basic pro-environmental behaviors that are required by organizations such as recycling reusable things and materials in the workplace or are at the discretion of employees such as attempting to prevent unnecessary printing [30]. OGC, a typical GHRM practice in which our study is interested, primarily refers to the development of reward programs by which organizations offer an incentive for employees to be involved and commit to organizations’ environmental sustainability goals [26, 27, 35, 36, 54]. Essentially, OGC aims at stimulating employees’ “extrinsic motivation”, a typical motivational element of AMO, through building a close linkage between employee pro-environmental behaviors/performance and financial rewards and connecting organizations’ environmental sustainability goals to employees’ interests [27, 36]. Usually, the higher the OGC level, the stronger employees’ extrinsic motivation to be environmentally proactive, and the higher the EPEB level. Thus, based on AMO theory, we speculate that OGC tends to encourage employees to behave more pro-environmentally. Following the insights of social exchange theory [55], we can also obtain the above inference. Specifically, OGC is a kind of transaction-based GHRM practice that emphasizes the economic exchange between employees and focal organizations separately for financial rewards and workplace pro-environmental behaviors [26, 55]. So, OGC may create a sense of reciprocity among employees, leading them to engage in workplace pro-environmental behaviors as a way of repaying focal organizations for their financial support. Previous studies that have confirmed a positive association between OGC and EPEB in the Chinese context [26] and the non-Chinese contexts [19–25] provides evidence for our inference. Therefore, regarding the possible, single effect of OGC on EPEB, we propose Hypothesis 1.

Hypothesis 1: OGC is more likely to promote EPEB.

Hypothesis 1: OGC is more likely to promote EPEB.

EGC mainly involves the conscientiousness trait that employees uphold in ecological protection [30], consisting of employee pro-environmental dependability, green achievement striving, and ecological self-discipline [56–58]. Under the framework of the big five personality traits [59], the existing literature has paid enough attention to the positive impacts of conscientiousness on work motivation [37], health behavior [60], innovation [61], job satisfaction, organizational citizenship behavior, and leader-member exchange relationship [62], work competence [63], and job performance [64], and the negative impacts on counterproductive work behaviors [65], of which the findings have laid theoretical foundation for us to speculate the potential effect of EGC on EPEB. Particularly, through a meta-analysis of 65 empirical studies, Judge and Ilies substantiated that conscientiousness is the largest positive predictor of employee work motivation [37]. Here, based on the above findings and the VBN (values, beliefs, and norms) theory, we contemplate that, individuals with strong green conscientiousness are more likely to hold intense environmental values and beliefs [66] and great green motivation [37], which in turn may drive them to get ahead in ecological protection [67] and proactively engage in workplace pro-environmental behaviors [68]. And, the stronger the EGC level, the more intense employees’ environmental values, beliefs, and intrinsic motivation to behave pro-environmentally, and the higher the EPEB level. Additionally, individuals with strong green conscientiousness may see themselves as environmentalists with a strong environmental self-identity [69–71], giving rise to their intense obligation-based intrinsic motivation and leading them to engage in workplace pro-environmental behaviors as a way of aligning their behaviors with their strong environmental self-identity [69]. So, EPEB enhancement is more likely to get benefits from EGC. Kim et al.’s study that utilized conscientiousness to predict employee voluntary pro-environmental behavior [30], offers evidence for our inference. Accordingly, regarding the possible, single effect of EGC on EPEB, we propose Hypothesis 2.

Hypothesis 2: EGC tends to facilitate EPEB.

Hypothesis 2: EGC tends to facilitate EPEB.

Comparison between the congruence and incongruence effects

The congruence effect mainly refers to the possible influence of the OGC-EGC congruence on EPEB while the incongruence effect largely involves the potential impact of the OGC-EGC incongruence on EPEB. Comparison between the congruence and incongruence effects mainly refers to comparing the EPEB levels separately corresponding to the OGC-EGC congruence and the OGC-EGC incongruence. We primarily draw the person-environment fit theory and theory of internal and external factors to infer the comparison result.

As an important manifestation of employee conscientiousness trait in green areas, EGC tends to motivate employees to seek or even create situations where they can fully demonstrate their strong conscientiousness in ecological protection [72]. At this moment, if organizations intensively implement green compensation practice, the high level of OGC exactly sets up an arena for employees to display their green conscientiousness through organizations’ well-established procedure of management by objectives. Specifically, the high level of OGC on the one side systematically decomposes organizations’ environmental sustainability goals into departmental tasks and then into individual employees’ objectives and responsibilities in a top-down style, and on the other side naturally entitles individual employees to self-manage their workplace pro-environmental behaviors through establishing a close linkage between their behaviors/performance and green financial rewards. Only when EGC aligns with OGC will individual employees experience less pressure and greater satisfaction and be more proactive and joyful with better adaptability [73], which thereby encourages them to self-engage in workplace pro-environmental behaviors stimulated by the high level of OGC. Thus, the OGC-EGC congruence tends to inspire employees to behave more pro-environmentally. This is also in line with the inference based on person-environment fit theory such that EPEB depends on not only the individual-level green conscientiousness trait or the organizational-level green compensation practice but also the congruence between them [33, 34]. Conversely, when the OGC-EGC incongruence occurs, such as in the situation where organizations afford large green rewards for individual employees with faint green conscientiousness or where organizations hardly provide green incentives for individual employees with strong green conscientiousness, EPEB level will be hampered due to the fact that individual employees’ weak green conscientiousness fails to utilize the large arena built by the high level of OGC or the arena set by the almost-none OGC is too small for them to display their strong green conscientiousness. Besides, compared to the OGC-EGC congruence situation, individual employees may go through cognitive dissonance [74, 75], which is an uncomfortable psychological state that arises from the conflict between their internally held environmental self-identity cultivated by EGC [69–71] and the external standard of workplace pro-environmental behaviors asserted by OGC [26] in the situation where EGC is not aligned with OGC. This cognitive dissonance may lead individual employees to disengage from workplace pro-environmental behaviors as a way of reducing their discomfort [26]. Therefore, the OGC-EGC incongruence is more likely to decrease EPEB.

According to theory of internal and external factors advocating that things development is a joint result of the internal and external factors, we can also obtain the above inference. In detail, OGC is essentially an external factor resulting in EPEB variations whereas EGC itself is an internal factor giving rise to differentiated levels of EPEB, and EPEB enhancement is exactly a joint result of OGC and EGC. Hence, only when the internal and external factors are both present at a not-so-low level and the gap between them are not large, that is, only when OGC matches EGC at an at-least-moderate level, could EPEB develop. Conversely, when there is a dearth of one or one of them is at an extremely low level, that is, when OGC does not align with EGC, EPEB development will be hindered. Taken together, we posit that the more congruent OGC and EGC are, the higher EPEB level gets, which is outlined as Hypothesis 3.

Hypothesis 3: Compared to the OGC-EGC incongruence, the OGC-EGC congruence tends to bring about a higher level of EPEB.

Hypothesis 3: Compared to the OGC-EGC incongruence, the OGC-EGC congruence tends to bring about a higher level of EPEB.

Comparison within the congruence effect

Comparison within the congruence effect mainly refers to comparing the EPEB levels separately corresponding to the high-high and low-low combinations of OGC and EGC. As these two combinations belong to the OGC-EGC congruence category, they are surely beneficial to EPEB enhancement, which can find evidence from the extant literature concerning the positive effects of person-environment fit or individual-organization congruence on employee positive attitudes, behaviors, and performance [47]. Here, we wonder, whether these two subtypes of the OGC-EGC congruence category give rise to different levels of EPEB. Unfortunately, we can’t find answers from extant literature since scholars heavily relied on “difference scores” to operationalize the congruence and directly examine the congruence effects [76], or prefer to use “interactive analysis” to explore the potential effects of two or more factors [23–26], failing to reflect the nuanced distinctions within the congruence and making it difficult to further contrast the congruence effects between the high-high and low-low combinations owing to the inherent limitations of “difference scores” or “internative analysis” [46].

Due to a high level of congruence, the high-high combination of OGC and EGC equals to a situation under which the internal and external factors simultaneously play roles at a high level. Similarly, because of a low level of congruence, the low-low combination of OGC and EGC is equivalent to a situation where the internal and external factors are jointly performing at a low level. It is well-established that the combination of internal and external factors is more conducive to things’ occurrence and development when it is at a high rather than low level. Hence, we predict that the high-high rather than low-low combination of OGC and EGC tends to bring about higher EPEB. Despite that almost no existing studies focus on the congruence effect of OGC and EGC, recent studies confirming that the high congruence in proactive personality traits between leaders and followers leads to a higher quality of LMX [38], the high leader-team congruence in power distance values tends to bring about team effectiveness [39], and the high alignment in LMX quality evaluation between superiors and subordinates results in a higher level of employee work engagement [40], provide indirect evidence to support our inference. Thus, we propose Hypothesis 4.

Hypothesis 4: Compared to the low-low combination of OGC and EGC, the high-high combination tends to engender higher EPEB.

Hypothesis 4: Compared to the low-low combination of OGC and EGC, the high-high combination tends to engender higher EPEB.

Comparison within the incongruence effect

Comparison within the incongruence effect primarily involves comparing the EPEB levels under the high-low and low-high combinations of OGC and EGC within the OGC-EGC incongruence category. As said earlier, EGC essentially is an internal factor giving rise to EPEB variations whereas OGC itself is an external factor. Therefore, EGC is the fundamental, internal condition that can largely determine whether EPEB comes up and tends to flourish while OCG is the secondary, external condition that can merely accelerate or decelerate EPEB improvement. Due to the fact that the external factors must be brought into play through the internal factors, i.e., only through the role of EGC could OGC exert impacts on EPEB, EGC is the first-ranked determinant and OGC is the secondary one. With the shift of the OGC-EGC combination from a moderate level of congruence separately to the high-low and low-high incongruence situations, the corresponding EPEB levels surely tend to decline because of the gradually larger misalignment between OGC and EGC. However, the completely opposite directions of the internal factor “EGC” in these two combinations will result in sharply differentiated declining rates of EPEB level and thereby will lead to distinctly declining levels of EPEB. Specifically, when the OGC-EGC combination moves to the low-high situation, which means the OGC level is reduced bit by bit while the EGC level is boosted gradually, the declining rate of the EPEB level will be relatively slow since the internal, first-ranked determinant “EGC” is becoming strong and the external, secondary factor “OGC” is getting weak. Comparatively, when the OGC-EGC combination moves to the high-low situation, which implies the OGC level is increased gradually while the EGC level is decreased bit by bit, the declining rate of the EPEB level will be relatively fast because the internal, first-ranked determinant “EGC” is getting faint and the external, secondary factor “OGC” is becoming strong. So, regarding the comparison result within the OGC-EGC incongruence effect on EPEB, we speculate that the high-low combination of OGC and EGC tends to lead to a lower level of EPEB compared to the low-high combination, which is concluded as Hypothesis 5.

Hypothesis 5: Compared to the low-high combination of OGC and EGC, the high-low combination is more likely to result in a lower level of EPEB.

Hypothesis 5: Compared to the low-high combination of OGC and EGC, the high-low combination is more likely to result in a lower level of EPEB.

The mediating effect of EEC

So far, we have elaborated on the potential (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB. If the above propositions hold up, what is the underlying mechanism? Based on interdependence theory, person-environment fit theory, and relevant literature on employee commitment, we speculate that "employee environmental commitment" (EEC) may be one latent mediator.

Drawing on interdependence theory, “commitment” mainly involves the desire or psychological attachment that individuals tend to stay in a relationship [42, 77]. There are a variety of commitments, such as employees' willingness and psychological attachment to stay in working organizations for a long time and maintain long-term cooperation, known as "organizational commitment" [43]. As a recurring theme in the green management field [78, 79], employee commitment to organizations’ environmental sustainability goals or responsibilities has been proffered as a construct of “employee environmental commitment” (EEC) [42, 79]. Conceptually, EEC refers to a kind of long-term responsibility and psychological attachment exhibited by individual employees towards focal organizations’ environmental sustainability goals [79]. When playing roles in reflecting individual employees’ desire to commit to focal organizations’ environmental sustainability and willingness to engage in workplace pro-environmental behaviors [80], EEC itself is an internal, obligation-based motivation stipulating individual employees to take actions in focal organizations’ green development [81, 82] and one key attitude variable predicting their workplace pro-environmental behaviors [44, 79, 83].

Person-environment fit theory maintains that individuals’ attitudes and behaviors are jointly shaped by their own characteristics and their surroundings [33, 34]. Accordingly, the OGC-EGC (in)congruence may not only affect EPEB, which mainly represents individual employees’ typical behavior encouraged by organizations’ green management practices but also influence EEC, which primarily represents individual employees’ typical attitude with good predictive power of workplace pro-environmental behaviors [30, 37]. Moreover, it is well-established that the attitude variables could effectively explain subsequent behavioral variables [29, 84], deriving from which we can further propose that the OGC-EGC (in)congruence may lead to EPEB variations through influencing EEC. Specifically, as discussed above, when the EGC level is strong, only in an environment with a high level of OGC will individual employees feel less stress and more satisfaction [73]. So, the OGC-EGC congruence, particularly at a high level, i.e., the high-high combination of OGC and EGC, is more likely to boost individual employees’ continual, long-term commitment to organizations’ environmental sustainability goals through intensifying their satisfaction [42], which consequently will drive individual employees to engage in workplace pro-environmental behaviors to fulfill their environmental commitment [79]. Social learning theory, which advocates that individuals learn by observing and modeling the behaviors of others surrounding them [85], can also help us acquire the above inference. In detail, when OGC aligns with EGC at a high level of congruence, it is more likely to create a positive social learning environment for workplace pro-environmental behaviors through individual employees’ mutually observing, modeling, and learning colleagues’ high level of EPEB encouraged by the high-high combination of OGC and EGC, making them more committed to focal organizations’ environmental sustainability goals and responsibilities and thereby leading to a higher level of EPEB. Generally, the higher the OGC-EGC congruence level, the more conducive it is to EEC enhancement, and the more likely the EPEB level can be boosted.

Conversely, when there is an OGC-EGC incongruence such as in the high-low situation where organizations afford relatively large green compensation for individual employees with feeble environmental conscientiousness and in the low-high situation where organizations offer relatively few green incentives for individual employees with intense environmental conscientiousness, it is not conducive to creating a good social learning environment and focal employees are more inclined to fail to find an organizational environment matching their environmental conscientiousness and thereby may undergo discomfort, pressure, and dissatisfaction, potentially hindering their commitment to organizations’ environmental sustainability goals and consequently weakening EPEB level. In particular, in the situation where organizations provide large green incentives to individual employees whose environmental conscientiousness is meager, i.e., the high-low combination of OGC and EGC, the high level of OGC may even incur focal employees’ aversion [26], leading to weaker EEC and ultimately resulting in lower EPEB.

To sum up, in light of the potential mediators underlying the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB, we posit that, compared to the OGC-EGC incongruence, the OGC-EGC congruence tends to engender higher EPEB by evoking stronger EEC; compared to the low-low combination, the high-high combination will result in higher EPEB after giving rise to stronger EEC; within two subtypes of the OGC-EGC incongruence, weaker EEC with subsequent lower EPEB will come true when OGC level is higher than EGC level. Briefly, EEC is one possible mediator transmitting the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC to EPEB. Accordingly, we put forward Hypothesis 6.

Hypothesis 6: The OGC-EGC (in)congruence tends to affect EPEB via the mediating mechanism of EEC.

Hypothesis 6: The OGC-EGC (in)congruence tends to affect EPEB via the mediating mechanism of EEC.

Method

As aforementioned, we launched two empirical studies to test our theoretical model pertaining to our two research problems: Study 1 mainly seeks to validate the single and (in)congruence effects whereas Study 2 primarily aims to test the underlying mechanism.

Sample and procedure

Choosing full-time employees working in our cooperative organizations in the service and manufacturing sectors in Jiangsu Province as samples, we conducted questionnaire surveys among them and their direct supervisors. Specifically, in each cooperative organization, we directly contacted the corresponding human resource manager, who has been cooperating with our team for a long time, to help us gather the subordinate-supervisor dyads, who had agreed to participate in our questionnaire survey with the written inform consent, in a large meeting room, then explained the purpose, procedure, and confidentiality issues to them, and finally asked them to leave their personal email addresses in the form of a subordinate-supervisor dyad. To facilitate our questionnaire survey, we produced two kinds of online questionnaires, namely employee questionnaire and supervisor questionnaire, on Wenjuanxing (www.wjx.cn) which is a public, commercial questionnaire survey platform in China, and then sent the employee questionnaire URL link(s) to each sample employee’s email box and the supervisor questionnaire URL link to her/his direct supervisor’s email box to invite them to complete the corresponding questionnaire(s) in a given time. Surely, we repeatedly informed participants that “our survey is for research purposes only, all personal information will not be disclosed, and please feel free to fill out the questionnaire” not only in email content but also in the introduction section of the questionnaire. In return for survey participation, we provided each participant with a random online red envelope from 5 to 10 yuan.

Since Wenjuanxing could help us store answers automatically when participants were answering each item online with no others’ witnesses and without the need to fill in names and submit the completed questionnaires to the management authorities, this data collection procedure succeeded in addressing the confidentiality concerns as well as avoiding manual data entry error. Hence, our two studies both employed it. Yet, Study 1 included one stage while Study 2 covered two stages. Specifically, in Study 1, sample employees were invited to self-evaluate their EGC levels and EPEB levels when their direct supervisors were requested to provide information about OGC to avoid the common method bias. In Study 2, to collect data on independent and dependent variables at different time points, in the first stage, sample employees were asked to self-evaluate their EGC levels and EEC levels and their immediate supervisors were invited to provide information about OGC; one month later, the second stage commenced, and sample employees were further requested to self-evaluate their EPEB levels. The data separately surveyed from subordinate employees and their direct supervisors were matched through the email addresses left previously in the form of a subordinate-supervisor dyad. From searching for the subordinate-supervisor dyads who agreed to participate in our questionnaire survey to the completion of the entire questionnaire survey, it took 3 and 5 months respectively for Studies 1 and 2 to collect data. It is noteworthy that our study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) at the School of Mathematics and Statistics, Xuzhou University of Technology. All procedures involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards with written informed consent from all subjects.

In Study 1, a total of 899 subordinate-supervisor dyads took part in our questionnaire survey. After excluding the incomplete, mismatched, or invalid ones, 778 questionnaires were eventually retained. Among 778 surveyed employees, 73.39% came from the service sector, 38.82% were male, 76.74% had received a college education or above, 51.28% were below 40 years old, 51.93% were from non-management positions, and 52.57% had worked for no more than 15 years. In study 2, a total of 802 subordinate-supervisor dyads participated in our questionnaire survey. After removing the incomplete, mismatched, or invalid ones, 713 questionnaires were finally acquired. Among 713 surveyed employees, 73.77% came from the service sector, 39.14% were male, 76.44% had completed their college education or above, 50.49% were below 40 years old, 52.31% came from the frontline positions, and 51.47% had worked for no more than 15 years.

Measurements

The measuring items of our research variables in Fig. 2 all come from the existing, authoritative English scales. Since our questionnaire survey was launched in Chinese organizations where most of the participants speak Chinese, we adopted the Brislin double translation procedure between English and Chinese [86], i.e., the initial translation from English to Chinese by two Chinese Professors in the green management research field, the back-translation from Chinese to English by two foreign MBA students whose native language is English, and the reconciliation of discrepancies between the original English version and the back-translated English version, to ensure the translation accuracy. Surely, we also invited experts in Chinese to ensure the translated items follow the expression habits of Chinese. The Likert five-point evaluating method (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) was utilized. The reliability and validity of all the translated items were guaranteed by a pre-test.

Organizational green compensation (studies 1 and 2)

Based on relevant scales [26, 87, 88], we invited each supervisor to evaluate the following 4 items to measure OGC: (1) when environmental programs go smoothly and well in our organization, employees will be rewarded; (2) employees who have achieved environmental objectives in our organization are rewarded with cash prizes or non-cash equivalents; (3) when departments’ environmental programs are improved, managers will reward subordinates; and (4) employees not complying with environmental regulations will be punished. Generally, the larger the evaluated score, the higher the OGC level.

Employee green conscientiousness (studies 1 and 2)

Drawing on the 4-item scale developed by Hannah et al. to operationalize individuals’ duty orientation to mission [89], we took the green concept into consideration and obtained the following 4 revised items to measure EGC: (1) I accept personal risk or loss in supporting our organization’s environmental goals, (2) I make personal sacrifices to serve our organization’s environmental goals, (3) I do whatever I can do to prevent our organization’s failure in environmental goals, and (4) I get environmental job done under the toughest conditions. Sample employees were requested to self-assess their EGC levels. Generally, the greater the self-rated score, the higher the EGC level.

Employee pro-environmental behavior (studies 1 and 2)

According to Kim et al.’s scale [30], sample employees were asked to self-appraise the following 6 items to evaluate their EPEB levels: (1) I prevent unnecessary printing to save papers, (2) I use personal cups instead of disposable cups, (3) I choose stairs instead of elevators when going from floor to floor within a building, (4) I reuse papers to take notes in the office, (5) I recycle reusable things in the workplace, and (6) I sort recyclable materials into appropriate bins when others do not. Generally, the larger the self-evaluated score, the higher the EPEB level.

Employee environmental commitment (study 2)

Adopting the measurement developed by Raineri and Paillé [79], sample employees were requested to self-evaluate the following 7 items to assess their EEC levels: (1) I am greatly concerned about our organization’s environmental issues, (2) I feel guilty when I fail to support our organization’s environmental efforts, (3) our organization’s environmental concern means a lot to me, (4) I feel a sense of duty to support our organization’s environmental efforts, (5) I really feel as if our organization’s environmental goals are my own, (6) I feel personally attached to our organization’s environmental concern, and (7) I highly value our organization’s environmental efforts. Generally, the greater the self-appraised score, the higher the EEC level.

Control variables (studies 1 and 2)

Given that individual employees’ socio-demographic variables such as gender, age, education, job tenure, job position, and industry sector potentially influence their EPEB levels [90], we included them as control variables, of which the operationalization details are as follows: gender (1-male, 0-female); age (1-8 separately represent less than 23 years old, 23 to 25 years old, 26 to 30 years old, 31 to 35 years old, 36 to 40 years old, 41 to 50 years old, 51 to 60 years old, and above 60 years old); education (1-high school and below, 2-vocational college, 3-bachelor degree, 4-master degree, 5-doctoral degree); job tenure (1-7 separately represent within 1 year; 2 to 5 years, 6 to 10 years, 11 to 15 years, 16 to 20 years, 21 to 25 years, and above 25 years); job position (1-frontline, 2-basic management, 3-middle management, 4-senior management); industry sector (1-service, 2-manufacturing).

Statistical analysis

We principally employed the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis [38–41, 45–47] to verify Hypotheses 3-5 regarding three sets of comparisons between or within the (in)congruence effects. Specifically, as shown in Polynomial Regression Equation 1, EPEB was regressed on five polynomial terms, i.e., OGC, EGC, OGC2, OGC × EGC, and EGC2, as well as six control variables (C1-C6). Notably, OGC and EGC should be mean-centered before calculating three second-order polynomial terms, namely OGC2, OGC × EGC, and EGC2, so as to eliminate the nonessential multicollinearity and improve the result interpretation. Based on the regression coefficients α0-α5, we continued to draw a three-dimensional response surface in which OGC and EGC were plotted on the perpendicular horizontal axes and EPEB on the vertical axis. After that, following the procedures for testing the (non)linear combinations of regression coefficients [46, 47], we estimated some crucial parameters depicting key features of the response surface to determine whether Hypotheses 3-5 hold up.

The OGC-EGC (in)congruence ratio

Following scholars’ recommendation [91, 92], it is necessary for us to compute the OGC-EGC (in)congruence ratio among our samples so as to ascertain the practical value of subsequent polynomial regression and response surface analysis. We therefore calculated the absolute gap between the standardized rated scores of OGC and EGC for samples one by one. We then clarified each sample according to the following rules [91]: if the absolute gap value is within 0.50, i.e., one-half standard deviation, the focal sample is classified into the OGC-EGC congruence category, otherwise, it falls under the OGC-EGC incongruence category. The calculation results demonstrate that the ratio of the OGC-EGC incongruence samples is as high as 47.04%, greatly exceeding the threshold criterion of 10% [92]. So, our subsequent polynomial regression and response surface analysis have great practical significance.

Hypotheses 1 and 2 posit the single, positive effects of OGC and EGC separately on EPEB. As presented in Model 2, after controlling for the possible impacts of EGC and C1-C6, OGC is significantly, positively related to EPEB (ß = 0.39, p < 0.001), suggesting that OGC independently promotes EPEB; similarly, after controlling for the potential effects of OGC and C1-C6, EGC is significantly, positively related to EPEB (ß = 0.24, p < 0.001), showing that EGC alone enhances EPEB. Hence, Hypotheses 1 and 2 are supported.

Pertaining to the comparison result between the congruence and incongruence effects, Hypothesis 3 argues that the situation where OGC is congruent with EGC is associated with a higher level of EPEB. The OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface plotted in Fig. 3 affords initial evidence for Hypothesis 3: an inverted U-shaped curve along the incongruence line is so apparent, indicating that EPEB is seemingly to be improved when there is an OGC-EGC congruence, but likely to be suffered when there is an OGC-EGC incongruence. We continued to inspect the estimates of some essential parameters depicting key properties of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface to acquire statistical evidence. According to the existing literature [38–41, 47], support for Hypothesis 3 mainly hinges on the first principal axis and curvature along the incongruence line. If the first principal axis of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface is exactly located along the congruence line such that its slope p11 is not significantly different from 1 and its intercept p10 is not significantly different from 0, and the curvature of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the incongruence line is significantly negative, Hypothesis 3 is then empirically established. As shown in Model 3, p11 is not significantly different from 1 since the 95% CI, i.e., [0.71, 23.04], of its estimate, i.e., 3.77, includes 1; p10 is not significantly different from 0 since the 95% CI, i.e., [− 25.80, 0.42], of its estimate, i.e., − 0.65, includes 0; and the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface significantly curves downward as the estimate of curvature along the incongruence line is significantly negative ([α3 − α4 + α5] = − 0.24 with 95% CI [− 0.41, − 0.07] excluding 0). So, the two conditions supporting Hypothesis 3 hold up. Thus, Hypothesis 3 is established.

Hypothesis 4 predicts an asymmetrical congruence effect concerning the EPEB variations under two typical situations within the OGC-EGC congruence category such that the EPEB level is higher when OGC is aligned with EGC at a high rather than a low level. The OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface plotted in Fig. 3 displays that the EPEB level corresponding to the high-high combination of OGC and EGC is seemingly higher than that under the low-low combination. The significantly positive slope of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the congruence line shown in Model 3 ([α1 + α2] = 0.51 with 95% CI [0.39, 0.62] excluding 0) provides further statistical support [38–41, 47]. So, the high-high rather than low-low combination of OGC and EGC is associated with a higher level of EPEB, supporting Hypothesis 4.

Hypothesis 5 proposes an asymmetrical incongruence effect pertaining to EPEB variations under two subtypical situations within the OGC-EGC incongruence category such that the EPEB level under the situation where the OGC level is higher than the EGC level, i.e., the high-low combination of OGC and EGC, will be lower than that in the situation where the OGC level is lower than the EGC level, i.e., the low-high combination of OGC and EGC. Support for Hypothesis 5 requires that the slope of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the incongruence line from the low-high combination of OGC and EGC to the high-low combination is negative [38–41, 47]. As shown in Fig. 3, there is an apparent distinction in EPEB levels corresponding to these two ends of the incongruence line, that is, the EPEB level under the high-low combination is seemingly lower than that under the low-high combination. Model 3 further reports that the slope of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the incongruence line is significantly negative ([α1-α2] = − 0.20 with 95% CI [− 0.38, − 0.02] excluding 0). Thus, Hypothesis 5 is supported.

The OGC-EGC incongruence ratio among our 713 samples achieves 46.00%, indicating that it is imperative to examine the joint relationships of OGC and EGC with EEC and EPEB [91, 92].

As demonstrated in Models 4 and 6, the five polynomial terms namely “OGC”, “EGC”, “OGC2”, “OGC x EGC”, and “EGC2” as well as the control variables have significant, predictive power for EEC and EPEB (R2 = 0.67, p < 0.001; R2 = 0.58, p < 0.001). Model 5 suggests that OGC is significantly, positively related to EPEB (ß = 0.35, p < 0.001) and EGC is significantly, positively related to EPEB (ß = 0.27, p < 0.001), supporting Hypotheses 1 and 2. The estimates of pivotal parameters illustrating the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface in Model 6 show that its first principal axis exactly has a slope that is not significantly distinct from 1 (p11 = 3.79 with 95% CI [− 6.08, 32.03] including 1) and an intercept that is not significantly divergent from 0 (p10 = − 0.41 with 95% CI [− 21.85, 0.56] including 0), and the curvature of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the incongruence line is significantly negative ([α3 − α4 + α5] = − 0.25 with 95% CI [− 0.43, − 0.07] excluding 0), supporting Hypothesis 3. Model 6 as well reports that the slope of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the congruence line is significantly positive ([α1 + α2] = 0.44 with 95% CI [0.33, 0.55] excluding 0), providing support for Hypothesis 4, and the slope of the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface along the incongruence line from the low-high combination of OGC and EGC to the high-low combination is significantly negative ([α1-α2] = − 0.20 with 95% CI [− 0.37, − 0.03] excluding 0), supporting Hypothesis 5. So, Study 2 also substantiates Hypotheses 1-5.

General discussion

Research findings

Green development is a necessity for building a high-quality modern economy as well as a fundamental solution to the pollution problem during the construction of a clean and beautiful world where humanity and nature can dwell together. To foster EPEB which is the basis for the achievement of ecological civilization, organizations at all levels have explored multiple green management practices. Although numerous studies have confirmed the effectiveness of organizational green management practices in promoting EPEB and laid the theoretical foundation for organizations to implement green management practices, the EPEB level hasn’t always been improved in situations where organizations have already launched green management practices. According to person-environment fit theory, EPEB enhancement largely depends on both organizational-level green management practices and individual-level green characteristics, particularly the congruence between them. It is a pity that almost no study takes into account the congruence, which is undeniably detrimental to organizational practices in fostering EPEB. Our study therefore struggles to bridge this research gap by focusing on OGC, a typical organizational-level green management practice widely adopted in Chinese organizations, and EGC, a representative individual-level green trait powerfully predicting employee pro-environmental attitudes and behaviors, and attempts to examine their (in)congruence effects on EPEB. We at first extracted four typical combinations of OGC and EGC within the congruence and incongruence categories in organizational practices, then inferred the single effects of OGC/EGC on EPEB, three sets of comparison results between and within the congruence and incongruence effects, and the mediation of EEC drawing on person-environment fit theory, theory of internal and external factors, interdependence theory, and relevant EPEB literature, and finally introduced the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis to test our predictions through two studies (Studies 1 and 2) based on our questionnaire survey data separately from 778 and 713 subordinate-supervisor dyads.

Four typical combinations of OGC and EGC within the congruence and incongruence categories universally exist in organizational practices: (1) the high-high combination when organizations provide large green rewards for employees with strong green conscientiousness and (2) the low-low combination when organizations hardly afford green incentives and employee green conscientiousness is relatively weak within the OGC-EGC congruence category, and (3) the low-high combination when employees manifest intense green conscientiousness but focal organizations provide little green compensation and (4) the high-low combination when organizations afford large green compensation for employees with faint green conscientiousness within the OGC-EGC incongruence category. Study 1 substantiates the single effects predicted by Hypotheses 1 and 2 and three sets of comparisons between and within the congruence and incongruence effects proposed by Hypotheses 3–5 such that OGC or EGC independently, positively predicts EPEB, EPEB level under the OGC-EGC congruence situation is higher than that under the OGC-EGC incongruence situation, EPEB level corresponding to the high-high combination of OGC and EGC is higher than that corresponding to the low-low combination, and EPEB level under the high-low combination of OGC and EGC is lower than that under the low-high combination. Study 2 once again verifies Hypotheses 1-5. More importantly, it reveals that EEC exactly plays a mediating role during the OGC-EGC-EPEB relationships, supporting Hypothesis 6. Accordingly, Hypotheses 1-6 developed by our study regarding our two research problems are empirically established and our research aim that seeks to shed light on the joint effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB in a subtle way and the possible influencing mechanism has been achieved.

Theoretical implications

Our study has three main theoretical implications for existing EPEB research. First, it concentrates on one typical organizational-level GHRM practice namely “OGC” and one key individual-level trait namely “EGC”, extracts their four combinations within the congruence and incongruence categories, and confirms that the OGC-EGC (in)congruence is of major significance for EPEB enhancement, which thereby enriches person-environment fit theory. OGC is a widely adopted GHRM practice in Chinese organizations [26] and it varies across organizations; EGC is a powerful individual-level personality trait that effectively predicts employee green attitudes and behaviors and it has individual differences [30, 37]. Hence, when organizations implement green compensation practice, the OGC-EGC congruence and incongruence are naturally present. Even when OGC is congruent with EGC, there are distinctions in the congruence levels; even in the case of the OGC-EGC incongruence situation, the OGC level may be higher or lower than the EGC level. The most typical OGC-EGC combinations therefore are the low-low congruence, the high-high congruence, the low-high incongruence, and the high-low incongruence. According to person-environment fit theory [33, 34], employee pro-environmental attitudes and behaviors are the joint results of organizational- and individual-level factors, and the OGC-EGC congruence and incongruence will inevitably exert divergent effects on EPEB. We are so interested in the potential (in)congruence effects that we compare EPEB levels under three sets of situations: the OGC-EGC congruence situation versus the OGC-EGC incongruence situation, the high-high combination versus the low-low combination within the OGC-EGC congruence situation, and the low–high combination versus the high-low combination within the OGC-EGC incongruence situation. Our two empirical studies find that the EPEB level under the OGC-EGC congruence situation is higher than that under the OGC-EGC incongruence situation, the EPEB level under the high-high combination is higher than that under the low-low combination, and EPEB level corresponding to the high-low combination is lower than that relates to the low-high combination. Thus, the OGC-EGC congruence and incongruence are substantiated to be of major value for EPEB reinforcement, providing support for the application of person-environment fit theory to the EPEB research field.

Second, our study is among the pioneers in exploring the (in)congruence effects of a typical organizational-level GHRM practice “OGC” and a representative individual-level green trait “EGC” on EPEB and unravel the black box from the perspective of environmental commitment, which therefore advances EPEB antecedent research. Pertaining to research on the potential factors resulting in EPEB variations, as concluded previously, numerous studies have dedicated attention and efforts to the organizational-level factors such as green support [9, 10, 12, 13], multiple leadership styles [2, 14–18], and single or bundle GHRM practices [19–27], some individual-level differences in attitude, personal norms, and perceived behavioral control [28], emotion and affect [29], intention [11], conscientiousness [30], motives [14, 15], knowledge [31], values [15], commitment and satisfaction [10, 12, 13], passion [2, 16, 17], and perceived resource scarcity and environmental unpredictability [32] have also been inspected. Yet, almost no study integrates organizational- and individual-level factors into the research framework. This is undoubtedly a limitation of extant EPEB antecedent research as it fails to reflect the fact that various combinations of organizational- and individual-level factors prevail in organizational practices. Against this theoretical and practical background, our study devotes close attention to OGC, a widely adopted GHRM practice, and EGC, a powerful green trait, and attempts to compare EPEB levels under four typical OGC-EGC combinations and unveil the possible mediating role of EEC in the OGC-EGC-EPEB relationships. To our knowledge, it is the first one to take OGC and EGC into consideration to examine EPEB variations. Hence, it contributes to enriching EPEB antecedents and advancing research maturity.

Third, our study may be the first one to introduce the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis to the EPEB research field, which thereby diversifies EPEB research methods. Regarding the potential effects of two or more factors simultaneously on EPEB, the extant literature heavily relies on “interaction analysis” to examine their interplay roles in affecting EPEB [23–26]. For instance, Zhang and Sun computed a product term of green compensation and green training to discuss their interactive effect on EPEB and found a neglected detrimental impact [26]. The overreliance on interaction analysis, however, constrains scholars from taking full advantage of empirical data since it merely generates the product term(s) and largely discards some potentially useful information [46]. Moreover, it cannot solve organizational managers’ practical puzzle about which situation on earth is the most conducive to EPEB enhancement since it fails to compare EPEB levels under different combinations. Fortunately, as more and more scholars are concerned about the (in)congruence effects in the organization and management research field, the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis has emerged and has been fully developed and widely recognized by scholars [38–41]. Compared with interaction analysis, this new methodology can vividly present three-dimensional relationships between OGC-EGC and EPEB and fully utilize empirical data to contrast EPEB levels under typical OGC-EGC combinations. Therefore, it is appropriate for our study to explore the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB in a subtle way. Thus, we introduced the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis to the EPEB research field, diversifying EPEB research methods.

Practical implications

Our study provides practical instructions for organizations to foster EPEB and implement green compensation practice in building a clean and beautiful world. First, in the situation where organizations have not implemented green compensation practice, they could achieve EPEB enhancement by strengthening EGC or EEC alone. As a widespread GHRM practice [26] and a typical personal green trait [37], OGC and EGC have been confirmed to facilitate EPEB individually [10, 19, 23–26, 30]. Our study also adds evidence. However, EGC, whether strong or weak, is an objective presence of personal conscientiousness trait in green development. That is to say, as long as individuals live in the world, their green conscientiousness trait does exist with strong or weak distinctions. Thus, as long as organizations initiate green compensation practice, the OGC-EGC combinations take place, which thereby will give rise to the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB. So, in the situation where organizations have not implemented green compensation practice, they can rely on EGC improvement to foster EPEB by leveraging EGC’s independent, positive role in EPEB enhancement. For example, organizations can select individuals with strong green conscientiousness when they recruit new employees or intensify EGC through some well-planned, long-term green training and development programs. Surely, in the situation without the implementation of OGC, organizations can also take advantage of the EEC’s role in boosting EPEB by consolidating EEC such as ensuring organizational or supervisors’ resource supply or support in the individual-level implementation of green activities or behaviors [10, 79], as EEC is found to facilitate EPEB (β = 0.22, p < 0.001, Model 7 in Table 6) during testing the mediating role in Study 2.

Second, when organizations intend to carry out green compensation practice, they should optimize the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB. As mentioned earlier, because EGC is an objective existence of individual conscientiousness trait in green development, organizations’ implementation of green compensation practice usually signifies the occurrence of OGC-EGC combinations. Further, owing to the Chinese organizations’ great preference for green compensation practice [26], the OGC-EGC combinations are already and will be more widespread. Regarding the possible (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB, our study has confirmed that different OGC-EGC combinations do exactly exert sharply divergent effects on EPEB. Specifically, the EPEB level under the OGC-EGC congruence situation is higher than that under the OGC-EGC incongruence situation, the EPEB level under the high-high combination of OGC and EGC is higher than that under the low-low combination, and the EPEB level under the low-high combination of OGC and EGC is higher than that under the high-low combination. So, when organizations plan to implement green compensation practice, they should carefully consider how to formulate an appropriate optimization plan according to the specific OGC-EGC combination they face. For example, if organizations are encountering a high-low combination of OGC and EGC, they should undermine the OGC level and simultaneously boost the EGC level so as to change the current combination to a low-high one according to EPEB level comparison results between these two combinations. Another example refers to the low-low combination of OGC and EGC confronted by organizations. Based on our findings regarding the EPEB level comparison result between these two typical combinations within the OGC-EGC congruence category, focal organizations should strengthen the OGC level and EGC level at the same time to optimize the current combination to a high-high one. Only through the above optimization paths, are organizations inclined to acquire desirable EEC and EPEB.

Limitations and suggestions for future research

The first limitation pertains to our uncomprehensive comparisons between and within the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB which deserves further research in the future. As aforementioned, our study primarily adopts the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis to compare three sets of OGC-EGC (in)congruence effects on EPEB, i.e., the congruence effect versus the incongruence effects, the low-low congruence effect versus the high-high congruence effect, and the low-high incongruence effect versus the high-low incongruence effect, which exactly correspond to the research problems that the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis can resolve. According to our study’s results in terms of these three sets of comparisons, i.e., the congruence effect > the incongruence effect, the high-high congruence effect > the low-low congruence effect, and the low-high incongruence effect > the high-low incongruence effect, it seems logical to deduce the following chain relationship: the high-high congruence effect > the low-low congruence effect > the low-high incongruence effect > the high-low incongruence effect. Yet, this seemingly logical deduction is untenable because it contradicts the OGC-EGC-EPEB response surface plotted in Fig. 3 which simply shows that the high-high congruence effect > the low-high incongruence effect > the low-low congruence effect > the high-low incongruence effect. More importantly, our study neither theoretically/empirically compares the low-low congruence effect with the low-high and high-low incongruence effects nor contrasts the high-high congruence effect with the low-high and high-low incongruence effects. Additionally, the congruence effect involved in Hypothesis 3 exactly refers to the EPEB level under the OGC-EGC congruence at a “moderate” level (see the intersection point of the congruence and incongruence lines on the OGC-EGC plane in Fig. 3). Therefore, the above two chain relationships simply deduced or visually displayed in Fig. 3 are severely lack of theoretical foundation and empirical evidence, requiring scholars to adopt or even develop appropriate research method(s) to launch relevant theoretical and empirical studies in the future.

The second limitation relates to our study’s flaw in exploring the possible boundary conditions and other potential mediating mechanisms for the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB. After comparing the OGC-EGC (in)congruence effects on EPEB, our study mainly unravels the probable mediating role of “EEC” from the perspective of employee commitment, neglecting the other possible mediators that have been found to have explanatory power of EPEB variations, such as employee intrinsic motivation [14, 15], employee pro-environmental goal clarity [16], employee affective commitment and satisfaction [10, 12], employee pro-environmental harmonious passion [2, 16], and employee green self-accountability [26]. Therefore, future research needs to ascertain their possible mediation in the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB. Regarding the possible boundary conditions, scholars have revealed that employee (green) knowledge sharing will make employees feel (pro-environmentally) confident when they communicate and share (green) knowledge and expertise with others [94, 95], employee green pursuit has preference differences in material or spiritual type which tends to play roles in EPEB improvement [26], and individual employees with a high need for autonomy may react negatively to a high level of OGC when they perceive it as controlling or manipulative under the situation where their EGC is also high [96, 97], which may in turn constrain the congruence effect of OGC and EGC on EPEB. In addition, the positive effect of high-high congruence on EPEB may be stronger in organizations with strong environmental culture or in industries where environmental sustainability is a key concern. Yet, our study did not consider the latent role of employee green knowledge sharing, employee green pursuit preference, employee autonomy need, environmental culture, or environmental concern as a possible boundary condition. Particularly, when we compared EPEB levels corresponding to the low–high and high-low combinations of OGC and EGC, employee geen pursuit preference is more likely to influence the comparison result. So, future research needs to clarify their potential moderating roles in the (in)congruence effects of OGC and EGC on EPEB.

The final limitation involves the external validity of our research conclusions. Our two empirical studies were mainly launched in the context of Chinese organizations, inevitably making our research conclusions be of distinct Chinese contextual characteristics and thereby impairing the generalizability of our findings. Thus, future research should carry out more similar studies under cross-cultural contexts to improve the external validity.

Conclusion

Drawing on person-environment fit theory, we focus on a widely adopted organizational-level green human resource management practice, i.e., “organizational green compensation” (OGC), and a typical individual-level green personality trait, i.e., “employee green conscientiousness” (EGC), and argue that the OGC-EGC (in)congruence exerts meaningful impacts on employee pro-environmental behavior (EPEB). Using the methodology of polynomial regression and response surface analysis of questionnaire survey data separately from 778 and 713 subordinate-supervisor dyads, two empirical studies substantiate our assumptions. Specifically, EPEB will be boosted when OGC is congruent with EGC, but suffer when they are incongruent; the higher the OGC-EGC congruence level, the greater the EPEB level; EPEB level under the high-low combination of OGC and EGC is lower than that under the low-high combination. Besides, employee environmental commitment plays a mediating role in the OGC-EGC-EPEB relationships. Consequently, to fully understand EPEB variations, the OGC-EGC (in)congruence should be considered.
