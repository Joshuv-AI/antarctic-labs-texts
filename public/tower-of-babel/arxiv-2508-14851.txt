Radosław Jan Rowicki, Adrian Francalanza, Alceste Scalas

Abstract
. Many software applications rely on concurrent and distributed (micro)services that interact via message-passing and various forms of remote procedure calls (RPC). As these systems organically evolve and grow in scale and complexity, the risk of introducing deadlocks increases and their impact may worsen: even if only a few services deadlock, many other services may block while awaiting responses from the deadlocked ones. As a result, the “core” of the deadlock can be obfuscated by its consequences on the rest of the system, and diagnosing and fixing the problem can be challenging. In this work we tackle the challenge by proposing distributed black-box monitors that are deployed alongside each service and detect deadlocks by only observing the incoming and outgoing messages, and exchanging probes with other monitors. We present a formal model that captures popular RPC-based application styles (e.g., gen_server s in Erlang/OTP), and a distributed black-box monitoring algorithm that we prove sound and complete (i.e., identifies deadlocked services with neither false positives nor false negatives). We implement our results in a tool called DDMon for the monitoring of Erlang/OTP applications, and we evaluate its performance. This is the first work that formalises, proves the correctness, and implements distributed black-box monitors for deadlock detection. Our results are mechanised in Coq. DDMon is the companion artifact of this paper. NOTE: This technical report is the extended version of a paper with the same title accepted at OOPSLA 2025: https://doi.org/10.1145/3763069

Introduction

Modern software applications are often concurrent and distributed in nature, as part of their implementations often includes ensembles of
(micro)services
that perform specific tasks, run concurrently across a network, and exchange information via
message passing
.
A design pattern prevalent to such systems is that of
remote procedure calls
(RPC),
in which a client sends a request to some service (which acts as an RPC server) and
awaits a response.
In order to compute such a response, the RPC server itself
might require further RPC interactions, thus acting as a client to other
services.
Various development frameworks support this style of
software organisation.
For instance, languages running on the Open Telecom Platform (OTP) like Erlang and Elixir support services based on
behaviours
(i.e., structures defining callback functions that a software module must implement) representing generic servers and state machines
(
Ericsson AB 2025
;
Erlang/OTP Team 2025a
;
Erlang/OTP Team 2025b
)
,
whereas
the Akka/Pekko frameworks support the development of RPC-based services as actors
(
Akka and Pekko developers team 2025
)
.
Some frameworks even assist programmers in exposing selected objects and APIs to web-based RPC via OpenAPI.

(Micro)service-oriented systems are often preferred to
monolithic systems, due to their flexibility, scalability, and modularity
(

.
By adhering to agreed-upon RPC APIs, services can encapsulate their internal logic, allowing them to evolve independently.
This decoupling fosters both modularity in design and greater adaptability.

.
For example, a commonly used computation can be refactored into a dedicated RPC call, making it reusable across services.
Likewise, frequently recurring interaction patterns involving sequences of requests and responses across different services can be abstracted behind a single RPC call — potentially coordinating further RPC interactions
in the background.

Deadlocks in RPC-Based Systems.

RPC-based systems are prone to
deadlocks
.
Concretely, a deadlock may occur when a service
S_{1}
needs an externally-computed result to serve a request: e.g.,
S_{1}
may send an RPC request to some service
S_{2}
, which in turn sends further RPC requests to other services, inducing a chain of dependencies where some service
S_{n}
sends an RPC request back to
S_{1}
itself, which is still waiting for
S_{2}
’s response.
Thus, the services
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
end up blocked in a dependency
cycle: see the depiction above, where the arrows represent requests,
and the numbers represent the order in which they are performed.
These deadlocks are more easily introduced when services are independently maintained and evolve over time.
To make matters worse, these deadlocks can be very hard to identify, understand,
and fix.
For instance, the user of an RPC system may only see a subset of the services involved; high amounts of
traffic could make the system temporarily unresponsive and some requests may be aborted after a timeout, making it easy to mistake a real deadlock for transient performance issues.
Moreover, defects such as deadlocks often arise in an unpredictable manner under specific conditions, and can be difficult to reproduce
(e.g. dependent on the contents and/or scheduling of RPC requests; we show one such example in
Figures

7
and
8
).
Even worse, other services may become blocked while awaiting responses from the deadlocked ones, thereby making it even harder to identify the real source of the deadlock.

In principle, the presence of deadlocks in a system could be detected via static
analysis. Several solutions have emerged, e.g., in the realm of process
calculi
(
Cristescu and Hirschkoff 2016
;
Dardha and Pérez 2022
;
Kobayashi 1998
;
Padovani 2014
;
Kobayashi 2006
;
Kobayashi and Laneve 2017
;
Ng and Yoshida 2016
)
.
These solutions are however limited.
Theoretically, the problem of deadlock-freedom is
generally undecidable, and any sound decidable static analysis must have false
positives, thereby rejecting systems that are deadlock-free.
Practically, static analysis methods require access to the entire source code for inspection, which is occasionally not possible (e.g. closed-source components).
Furthermore, many distributed systems use a variety of technologies and programming languages, some of which are hard to
statically analyse accurately (e.g. Erlang or Elixir, mentioned above).

The Challenge: Deadlock Detection in RPC Systems via Distributed Black-Box Outline Monitors.

Runtime monitoring for deadlock detection is an alternative to static analysis that avoids the aforementioned limitations.
In this work, we focus on the problem of designing
and developing monitoring processes that, when deployed in RPC-based distributed systems, can accurately identify
deadlocks at runtime. More precisely, we have
the following requirements for our monitors:

(1)

they must be deployed in a
distributed

fashion, alongside each service, without introducing any centralised components
(which
can become bottlenecks
in large-scale systems);

(2)

they must be
black-box
and

outline
, i.e., detect deadlocks by just observing the incoming and
outgoing messages of the service they monitor, without relying on
implementation details of the service internals, or invasive
modifications (
inlining
) in the service executable;

(3)

they must be
transparent
, i.e., the
presence or absence of monitors must not incorrectly alter the execution of the services;

(4)

they must be
precise
in their verdicts,
i.e., report a deadlock
if and only if
a deadlock occurs
in the system being monitored. Hence, the monitors must have
no false
positives (i.e., report non-existent deadlocks) or false negatives (i.e., miss the detection of actual deadlocks).

Requirements (
3
) and (
4
) are standard
notions of
monitoring correctness
in the field of runtime
monitoring
(
Francalanza 2021
)
.
Several algorithms have been presented in the literature for distributed
deadlock detection, starting in the early 1980s with the seminal work
of
(
Chandy et al. 1983
;
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)

and occasionally include proofs
of correctness reminiscent of our requirement (
4
).
Although these algorithms satisfy requirement (
1
) (distribution),
their deadlock detection logic is presented as an
inlined
part of a
potentially-deadlocking process, instead of being deployed as a separate process
that monitors the incoming/outgoing messages of an already-existing process.
Consequently, any formalisation of such algorithms (when available) cannot
satisfy requirement (
2
) (black-box outline
monitors) and does not permit one to state and prove
requirement (
3
) (transparency).
Still, their correctness results suggest that such algorithms are a good starting point towards
designing and developing distributed, black-box, outline monitors that
satisfy all our requirements (
1
)–(
4
).

Of course, the selection of requirements (
1
) and (
2
) above is a design choice based on trade-offs, and different approaches to deadlock monitoring lead to different trade-offs. For example, a centralised deadlock supervisor may be feasible for small systems (despite not being scalable) and it might take better
advantage of its immediate view of the global state. Offline deadlock detection (via log analysis) may be simpler to implement, but does not allow for immediate intervention upon deadlock detection. Non-black-box (inlined) monitors may better observe the system behaviour, at the cost of being more invasive and dependent on the service internals. In contrast to that, our requirements target large systems which may contain closed-source services which can only be verified at runtime and with weak assumptions (i.e., adherence to an RPC protocol).

Correctness Criteria for Deadlock Detection Monitors in Distributed Systems

In this section we establish the meaning of “correct monitoring” in the title of this paper: we define
general criteria for assessing the correctness of deadlock detection
monitors in distributed systems, based on widely-accepted standards for runtime
verification
(
Bartocci et al. 2018
)
. Some results akin to the
detection soundness

and
completeness
defined below can be found in earlier work on distributed deadlock detection
algorithms
(
Chandy et al. 1983
;
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)
but they are not presented in a
monitoring setup, i.e., as processes that can reach
verdicts
when
instrumented
on
a system, and act
transparently
.
This section systematises these concepts and aims to serve as a
blueprint for research in the field of deadlock monitoring. It also serves as an overview of our
approach and results (presented in the next sections).

Networks and Deadlocks.

In distributed systems, deadlocks span over multiple agents communicating across a network.
Therefore, we need a formal model of a network
N
equipped with semantics describing
executions as a series of transitions of the form
N_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha}N_{1}
; depending on the
formalisation style, the transition
\xrightarrow{\alpha}
can represent reduction semantics or LTS
semantics
(
Keller 1976
)
. We write
N_{0}\xrightarrow{\sigma}N_{1}
for a sequence of transitions
\sigma
,
which we call a
path
.
We also need to define when
N

has a deadlock
, i.e., a set of agents that are stuck waiting
for one another’s inputs: the specific formalisation depends on the specifics of the agents.

(We formalise our networks, agents (called
services
), and their deadlocks in
Section

3
.)

Monitors and Instrumentation.

Monitors are deployed by
instrumenting
them over a network. Therefore, we need some form of

instrumentation function
\mathscr{M}
that turns an unmonitored network
N
into a
monitored network
\widehat{N}
. Dually, the
deinstrumentation

\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N})
“strips off” the monitors of
\widehat{N}
and
returns an unmonitored network.
Instrumented networks have transitions
\widehat{N}_{0}\xrightarrow{\widehat{\alpha}}\widehat{N}_{1}
, extended over paths as

\widehat{N}_{0}\xrightarrow{\widehat{\sigma}}\widehat{N}_{1}
; notice that
\widehat{\alpha}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
may differ from the
unmonitored system’s
\alpha
and
\sigma
(e.g.,
\widehat{\alpha}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
may include additional
information or messages).

In principle, an instrumentation function
\mathscr{M}
can be applied to any state of a network — but in practice, the deployment of monitors is modelled by instrumenting a network in its initial state.
The details of instrumentation determine how, and to what extent, the monitors can observe the
network behaviour.

(We formalise our instrumentation of
black-box outline proxy monitors
in

Section

4
.)

Transparency.

A highly desirable property of monitor instrumentation is
transparency

(Criterion
2.1
below), ensuring that the addition of monitors does not
improperly change the behaviour of the system. Specifically, we formulate transparency as an

operational correspondence

(
Gorla 2010
)
requiring that all reachable
states of a network
N
are preserved after instrumentation (completeness), and all states reachable by
an instrumented network
\mathscr{M}(N)
are related to a reachable state of
N
(soundness). This ensures
that instrumentation retains all possible deadlocks of
N
, and does not introduce spurious
ones.
(We prove our transparency results in
Theorems

4.8
and
4.11
; recall
that
\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N})
is
\widehat{N}
with monitors “stripped off.”)

Criterion 2.1 (Monitor Instrumentation Transparency).

A monitor instrumentation
\mathscr{M}
is
transparent
for a network

N_{0}
if and only if the following clauses hold:

Completeness::

For any
N_{1}
and path
\sigma
,

N_{0}\xrightarrow{\sigma}N_{1}
implies
\exists\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{N}_{1}
such that
\mathscr{M}(N_{0})\xrightarrow{\widehat{\sigma}}\widehat{N}_{1}
and
\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N}_{1})=N_{1}
.

Soundness::

For any monitored network
\widehat{N}_{1}
and monitored path
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}
,
\mathscr{M}(N_{0})\xrightarrow{\widehat{\sigma}_{0}}\widehat{N}_{1}
implies that
\exists\widehat{N}_{2},N_{2},\widehat{\sigma}_{1},\sigma
such that
\widehat{N}_{1}\xrightarrow{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}}\widehat{N}_{2}
and
\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N}_{2})=N_{2}
and

N_{0}\xrightarrow{\sigma}N_{2}
.

Intuitively, transparency is achieved if a network
N
and its instrumented version

\mathscr{M}(N)
are mutually weakly similar; however, the operational
correspondence above allows for more flexibility in how the states of
N
and
\mathscr{M}(N)
are related
(as operational correspondences are typically weaker than simulation relations
(
Peters and van Glabbeek 2015
)
).
Notice that the
“soundness” clause of
2.1
allows the instrumented system to reach an
intermediate configuration
\widehat{N}_{1}
which, after some further steps
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
, aligns with an
unmonitored network state
N_{2}
. Depending on the instrumentation details, transparency may be
stronger (e.g., with
\widehat{N}_{1}=\widehat{N}_{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
always empty).

Preciseness.

2.2
below establishes that deadlock detection monitors must
be
precise
in their verdicts, i.e., they must be able to report all possible deadlocks of the
original unmonitored network (
completeness
), and all the deadlocks they report must be real
(
soundness
).
(We prove the preciseness of our approach in
Theorem

6.13
; recall
that
\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N})
is
\widehat{N}
with monitors “stripped off.”)

Criterion 2.2 (Preciseness of Deadlock Detection).

An instrumentation
\mathscr{M}
achieves
precise deadlock detection
for a network
N_{0}
if and only if,
for any monitored network
\widehat{N}_{1}
and monitored path
\widehat{\sigma}
such that
\mathscr{M}(N_{0})\xrightarrow{\widehat{\sigma}}\widehat{N}_{1}
, the following clauses hold:

Completeness::

If there is a deadlock in
\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N}_{1})
, then there are
\widehat{N}_{2},\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
such that

\widehat{N}_{1}\xrightarrow{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}}\widehat{N}_{2}
and
\widehat{N}_{2}
reports a deadlock.

Soundness::

If
\widehat{N}_{1}
reports a deadlock, then there is a deadlock in
\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\widehat{N}_{1})
.

Note that
2.2
does not require deadlocks to be reported immediately:
the “completeness” clause allows the monitored system to take some additional steps
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}

after a deadlock occurs; similarly, the “soundness” clause allows a deadlock to happen in a state
that precedes
\widehat{N}_{1}
(where the deadlock is reported). Also note that
2.2
is only effective in combination with

2.1
(transparency): e.g., a non-transparent instrumentation
\mathscr{M}(N_{0})

that blocks all transitions of
N_{0}
would trivially satisfy

2.2
for any not-yet-deadlocked
N_{0}
.
Finally, note that the “completeness” of
2.2
can be
strengthened by showing that, under suitable
fairness

assumptions
(
Glabbeek and Höfner 2019
)
, all deadlocks
will
be eventually
reported.
(We prove this stronger result in
Theorem

6.14
.)

3.3.
Locks and Deadlocks

In message-passing systems, an agent is generally
considered “locked” when it cannot progress because it is waiting to receive a specific message.
In this work we consider a service locked specifically when it is awaiting a response from another service,
as defined below.

Definition 3.6 (Lock).

Consider an SRPC service
S=\langle\penalty\ \!q^{i}\penalty\ \!\!\!\mid\penalty\ \!\!\!P\penalty\ \!\!\!\mid\penalty\ \!\!\!q^{o}\rangle\penalty\ \!
. We say that
S
is locked on name
n_{s}
when the following
clauses hold:

(1)

P\preccurlyeq\texttt{Locked}(n^{\bot}_{c},n_{s})

(i.e., the process in

S
is serving client
n^{\bot}_{c}
and awaits a response from
n_{s}
)

(2)

q^{i}\not\xrightarrow{{!}[n_{s}(\texttt{{R}})]}
(i.e., there is no response from
n_{s}
in
S
’s input queue)

(3)

q^{o}\not\xrightarrow{\alpha}
(i.e., the output queue of
S
is empty)

Clause
1
of
Definition

3.6
requires the service
process to await a response from the server
n_{s}
(as in
Definition

3.2
).
Moreover, by clause
3
, the output queue of the service must be empty:
hence, the service may
not
be considered locked as soon as its process becomes locked —
but the service
may
become locked if its output queue becomes empty before the awaited response
arrives on the input queue (by clause
2
).
Note that an SRPC service
S
locked on a service named
n_{s}
can still add incoming messages to its input queue (by
fig.

4
in
Figure

4
),
but cannot produce outgoing messages until it receives a response from
n_{s}
.
Using
Definition

we can now adapt the definition of deadlock from
(
Chandy et al. 1983
)
.

Definition 3.7 (Deadlock).

A non-empty set of service names
\mathcal{D}
is a deadlocked set in
N
when for every

n\in\mathcal{D}
, the service
N(n)
is locked on a member of
\mathcal{D}
.
We say that a
network
N
has a deadlock
if there is a deadlocked
set in
N
. We say that a
service named
n
is deadlocked in
N

when
n
belongs to a deadlocked set in
N
.

Note that
Definition

3.7
captures all “localised deadlocks,”

i.e.
, it does not require the entire system to be deadlocked. As
one would expect, deadlocks defined this way are persistent, by

.

Lemma 3.8 (Persistence of deadlocks).

If
\mathcal{D}
is a deadlocked set in
N
and
N\xrightarrow{\sigma}N^{\prime}
, then

\mathcal{D}
is also a deadlocked set in
N^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We first consider the case
N\xrightarrow{\alpha}N^{\prime}
(with a single action)
and show that if a service
n\in\mathcal{D}
is locked on
n^{\prime}
in

N
, then
n
is also locked on
n^{\prime}
in
N^{\prime}
. This comes from the fact that the
only way for
n
to unlock is to receive a response from
n^{\prime}
— but this cannot happen
because
n^{\prime}
is locked in

as well, hence
n^{\prime}
cannot send. We then extend this result to a path
\widehat{\sigma}
by induction over

and the statement holds by

3.7
.

4.
A Model of Generic Distributed Black-Box Outline Monitors

Generally speaking, a
monitor
is a process that observes a system under
scrutiny and potentially reaches a
verdict
reporting correct or
incorrect behaviours.
In this section, we introduce our model of
black-box outline monitors

that are deployed alongside each service
S
in a network
N
, and
observe the incoming and outgoing messages of
S
without depending on the
internals of

different application scenarios. In this work, we specify each monitor to act as a

that intercepts, examines, and
forwards the incoming and outgoing messages of the overseen service. Moreover, we allow our monitors to communicate directly with each other: this is
necessary for reaching deadlock verdicts (since a deadlock may involve an
arbitrary number of services across the network) while maintaining the

distributed
nature of the system (i.e., our monitors do not require any
centralised components).

We formalise monitored services and networks
(
Section

4.1
) and monitor instrumentation
(
Section

4.2
), proving that they guarantee our correctness

2.1
, i.e.,
transparency

(
Theorems

4.8
and
4.11
). Note that these results are independent from the specific deadlock detection
monitoring algorithm in use; we present and verify our algorithm in

Section

5
.

The basic idea of our algorithm is inspired by

Chandy et al. 1983

Mitchell and Merritt 1984

edge chasing

Moss 1985
;
Sinha and Natarajan 1985

monitor
\widehat{M}
receives a probe that it had itself produced and sent,
then (under some conditions) there is a dependency loop between locked services;
consequently,
\widehat{M}
can decidedly report a deadlock.
The intuition behind this approach is supported by
Proposition

5.1
below.

Proposition 5.1 (Equivalence between deadlocks and lock-on cycles).

A service named
n
is deadlocked in
N
(by
Definition

3.7

if and only if
n
is transitively locked on some service

that
is transitively locked on itself (by
Definition

3.6
).

We now address the dual problem w.r.t.

Theorem 6.14 (Eventual deadlock reporting).

Assume that the premises of

Proof.

From the monitor-flushing path constructed by

in a deadlocked monitored network (such as

(m,k)

8.
Related Work

8.1.
Distributed Deadlock Detection Algorithms Based on Edge Chasing

The state of the art in distributed deadlock detection is typically based on the notion of
edge chasing

(
Moss 1985
;
Sinha and Natarajan 1985
)
, used effectively by the seminal work of
(
Chandy et al. 1983
;
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)
.
These approaches send probes along the estimated edges of the
wait-for graph

(
Gligor and Shattuck 1980
)
of the system with the purpose of detecting cycles; different algorithms may send probes in the direction of the wait-for dependency chain or backwards.
The basic ideas and techniques have been improved in terms of correctness
(
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
;
Choudhary et al. 1989
)
, generality
(
Bracha and Toueg 1984
;
Chandy et al. 1983
)
and performance
(
Sinha and Natarajan 1985
;
Yeung et al. 1994
)
.
Compared to this body of work, we show that edge chasing can be leveraged in black-box monitoring, by producing probes and reaching deadlock detections based only on observing incoming and outgoing service messages (see
Figure

10
).

Our work distinguishes itself as it contributes:

(1)

formalised message-passing semantics of

(2)

black-box monitors for distributed detection, which we

(3)

prove sound and complete,

(4)

via an automated theorem prover.

None of the distributed deadlock detection algorithms we are aware of address requirement
2
.
Moreover,
(
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)
provide (non-machine checked) proofs for deadlock detection completeness but not
soundness, potentially allowing for false positives.
Neither of
(
Sinha and Natarajan 1985
;
Isloor and Marsland 1978
;
Choudhary et al. 1989
;
Gligor and Shattuck 1980
)
proves any correctness results, whereas the proofs in
(
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
;
Chandy et al. 1983
;
Badal 1986
;
Bracha and Toueg 1984
;
Srinivasan and Rajaram 2011
)
are not formal (and even less so mechanisable).
Such proofs are typically based on high-level graph-based arguments without any operational semantics such as ours.

The distributed deadlock detection algorithm closest to ours (
Definition

5.2
) is
(
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)
,
which propagates probes backwards similarly to us — but does not have an equivalent to our
waiting
set.
As a result, that algorithm requires clients to periodically poll for probe updates from the services they are locked on.

(
Chandy et al. 1983
)
presents two algorithms: one for
communication (OR) model
and the other for
resource (AND) model
, both of which differ from ours in that they use forward-moving probes.
In the
OR
model, a process (i.e., a service) can send requests to multiple processes and unlocks as soon as it receives a response from any of them; instead, in the
AND
model, a process can be locked on more than one resource and proceeds only when
all
of them are acquired.
The algorithm for the
OR
model is somewhat closer to ours: when a probe reaches its originator, its propagation is reversed and continues backwards along the wait-for graph (similarly to our
Definition

5.2
).
Instead, the algorithm for the
AND
model uses forward probes only. We initially tried to formalise a black-box deadlock monitoring algorithm with forward-moving probes inspired by this approach, but we found a subtle counterexample to its soundness, shown in
Figure

17
.

8.2.
Monitoring and Other Approaches to Deadlock Detection

There exists recent research on monitoring hyper-properties, although none of it addresses deadlock detection specifically.
For instance,
(
Finkbeiner et al. 2019
;
Aceto et al. 2022
)
provide frameworks for monitoring formulae in different variants of HML logic, but do not discuss correctness of particular applications.
In
(
Aceto et al. 2024a
)
, the authors synthesise decentralised monitors from hyper-property specifications and guarantee that these monitors are deadlock-free by construction.
Our work achieves this result through transparency (
Theorem

4.8
), but the main target is the detection of deadlocks in the
system being monitored
.

The GoodLock algorithm
(
Havelund 2000
)
is one of the first works to propose the detection of potential deadlocks of Java threads
at runtime
;
this influential work is extended in
(
Bensalem and Havelund 2005
;
Bensalem et al. 2006
)
and combined with static analysis techniques in
(
Agarwal et al. 2005
)
.
More recently,
(
Albert et al. 2016
)
combined static analysis and testing (often considered as a runtime analysis technique) for deadlock detection.
Neither of the above approaches are fully black-box. Moreover, no formal soundness or completeness guarantees are provided.
The authors of
(
Hilbrich et al. 2013
)
develop an algorithm for the runtime deadlocks detection of MPI programs.
The approach is mostly black box and its scalability is demonstrated through an extensive empirical evaluation.
The work
(
Asadollah et al. 2018
)
develops a runtime analysis tool to detect concurrency bugs in embedded software, with deadlocks being one of the classes of bugs detected.
The tool mainly relies on trace log analysis and, in that sense, the approach may be considered as black-box.
Both
(
Asadollah et al. 2018
;
Hilbrich et al. 2013
)
are however centralised and are
not
backed up by any soundness or completeness guarantees.

8.3.
Proof Mechanisation for Deadlock Detection and Prevention

There are efforts that mechanise correctness proofs for deadlocks in distributed systems, but these are considerably different from our work.
E.g.,
(
Wilcox et al. 2015
;
Hance et al. 2021
)
consider proofs for static deadlock prevention as opposed to deadlock detection at runtime.
More closely to our work,
(
Audrito and Haures 2023
)
present a Coq formalisations for monitors that do not communicate with each other, thereby limiting their analysis to protocol violations, not deadlock detection (which necessarily relies on probes).

The work of
(
Cogumbreiro et al. 2019
)
presents a method and tool for runtime deadlock detection for systems with distributed barrier synchronisations, such as X10
(
Charles et al. 2005
)
.
Their approach employs decentralised runtime entities called distributed barrier programs that can be seen as monitors, but it is unclear to what extent the implementation is (or can be made to be) black-box since this was not one of the requirements of the work.
Their deadlock analysis relies on a different abstraction to wait-for graphs called
task-event graphs
.
This is accompanied by mechanised proofs in Coq showing that detections based on task-event graphs are sound and complete with respect to actual deadlocks.
Despite the similarities, these proofs present graph-based arguments whereas our proofs are closer to the actual implementation, verifying the operational behaviour and verdicts of our decentralised monitors with respect to the deadlocks occurring in the network.
For instance, proofs of monitor transparency do not feature in the mechanised proofs of
(
Cogumbreiro et al. 2019
)
.

9.
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented the first formal theory of distributed black-box monitors for
deadlock detection in RPC-based systems. We introduced the general correctness

2.1
and
2.2
(transparency and detection
preciseness) and we proved that our monitors and algorithm
(
Definition

5.2
) satisfy them
(
Theorems

4.8
,
4.11
and
6.13
).
Our results are mechanised in Coq. We also presented and evaluated
DDMon
, a
practical prototype tool based on our theoretical results.

As future work, we plan to instantiate our generic monitoring framework
(
Section

4
) with other deadlock detection algorithms (e.g. having
probe propagation strategies closer
to
(
Chandy et al. 1983
;
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)
) investigating whether they are sound and
complete. If so, we can easily add such algorithms to
DDMon
, and evaluate
and compare their performance.
We plan to formalise and prove the correctness of the delayed probe emission strategy used in
Section

7.2.1
:
we do not expect conceptual difficulties, although the mechanisation may require a large number of small changes.
We also plan to mechanise
fairness of components

(
Glabbeek and Höfner 2019
)
and thus the final part of
Theorem

6.14

— although mechanising fairness appears challenging and we are not aware of other attempts in the literature.

We have preliminary results indicating that our algorithm can support the
AND

resource model of
(
Chandy et al. 1983
)
(i.e., sending multiple queries and then awaiting all responses)
with minimal adjustments. The
OR
model is more challenging: it is
not obvious how to correctly identify “
OR
queries” with a black-box monitoring
approach; after this design question is solved, its formalisation will likely
lead to different fundamental definitions and invariants w.r.t. the algorithm in
this paper. We have already tested some ideas and a preliminary implementation
of a deadlock monitoring algorithm (inspired by
(
Chandy et al. 1983
)
), but the formalisation is
future work.

We also plan to build upon our results to develop a theory for distributed
deadlock detection via
observing monitors
, which only receive

notifications
about messages sent/received by a service (akin to the

trace
mechanism in Erlang/OTP
(
Erlang/OTP Team 2025c
)
).
The advantages of this approach are potentially lower overhead w.r.t. our proxy monitors, as well as transparency by construction.
The disadvantage is that the delays introduced by notifications can
cause monitor states and probes to become subtly outdated, leading to false or
missed deadlock reports. We have already found counterexamples showing that our
algorithm (
Definition

5.2
) and
(
Mitchell and Merritt 1984
)
become
unsound if naïvely used in this setting. Therefore, we expect that
designing a distributed deadlock detection algorithm for observing monitors that
can be proven sound and complete will be a non-trivial effort.

Another topic worth investigating is how to handle RPC calls with timeouts. For that, we need to
address a key design question: how can a black-box monitor become aware of a timeout occurring
within the overseen service? If the monitor is somehow notified about the timeout, then we believe
our algorithm can be adapted and remain sound and complete (albeit its “black-box” nature might be
questioned); otherwise, fundamental definitions of “lock” and “deadlock” may need to be
changed, and sound and complete black-box monitoring may turn out to be impossible.

Appendix B
Implementing Replicated SRPC Services

Despite its streamlined design, our SRPC service model is sufficiently
expressive to capture
service replication
, where one logical

replicating server
distributes queries among a number of
workers
.
A client of such a server needs to first obtain the name of an available worker
by querying the server. The server replies with the first free worker in its
pool, and if all are busy, picks one of them. The client then queries the
received worker directly; after receiving a response, the client releases the
worker by sending a cast to the replicating server.

Appendix C
Proof of
Theorem

6.14

From the statement and proof of
Theorem

6.13
we
know that there is a monitor-flushing path
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
(constructed by

Lemma

6.9
) that begins with
\widehat{N}^{\prime}
and reaches a network
state where a monitor reports a deadlock; also, in
\widehat{N}^{\prime}
there is an
active probe
\widehat{p}
that causes a deadlock alarm as
\widehat{p}
reaches
that monitor through
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
. The monitor-flushing path
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
built
by
Lemma

6.9
has the shape:

\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\;=\;\widehat{\alpha}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\widehat{\alpha}_{m}\cdot\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}_{k}\qquad\text{where $\forall i\in 1..k$:}\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\;=\;n_{i}\mathbin{{-\!(}}\widehat{p}\mathrel{)\!\!\to}n^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\widehat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{1,i}\cdot\ldots\widehat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{j_{i},i}

where
\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
are monitor-flushing actions of the monitor that has the probe
\widehat{p}
in its
monitor queue (those actions do
not
forward
\widehat{p}
). In other words, the
path
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
says that the active probe
\widehat{p}
will be forwarded
k
times
(once per sub-path
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
, for
i\in 1..k
) after
m

monitor-flushing actions occur (which empty the monitor queue containing
\widehat{p}
); each
forwarding may be followed by more actions (denoted as
\widehat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{j,i}
above).
We can then define
\operatorname{length}({\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}})
as the pair
(k,m)
: intuitively, this represents the distance between the active probe
\widehat{p}
and the monitor that owns (i.e., created)
\widehat{p}
.

We then prove that such a distance never increases as the network
\widehat{N}^{\prime}
runs,
and can be decreased toward
(0,0)
. More precisely, for any
\widehat{N}^{\prime\prime}
reachable from
\widehat{N}^{\prime}
, if we take the
monitor-flushing path
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}
constructed by
Lemma

6.9

in
\widehat{N}^{\prime\prime}
, we have
\operatorname{length}({\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}})\leq\operatorname{length}({\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}})

by standard lexicographic ordering. Moreover, in any such
\widehat{N}^{\prime\prime}
where
\operatorname{length}({\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}})>(0,0)
,
there is always a monitor with an
enabled monitor-flushing action
\widehat{\alpha}
such that
\widehat{N}^{\prime\prime}\xrightarrow{\widehat{\alpha}}\widehat{N}^{\prime\prime\prime}
and, if we take the monitor-flushing path
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime\prime}
constructed
by
Lemma

6.9
in
\widehat{N}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, we have
\operatorname{length}({\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime\prime}})<\operatorname{length}({\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}})
.

Hence, assuming fairness of components
(
Glabbeek and Höfner 2019
)
, every
monitor that reduces the current monitor-flushing path constructed by

Lemma

6.9
will be eventually scheduled to execute an action.
Therefore, the length of the path constructed by

Lemma

6.9
will eventually reach
(0,0)
, and the
monitor that owns
\widehat{p}
will raise an alarm.

(Note: this last paragraph from “Hence…” is not part of our Coq mechanisation.)
