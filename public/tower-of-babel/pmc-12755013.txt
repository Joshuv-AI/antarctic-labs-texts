Influence of health empowerment on health outcomes of different socioeconomic status: evidence from Anhui, China
Authors: Jing Cheng, Wenjun Dai, Sixuan Chen, Lidan Wang, Ren Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth disparities across socioeconomic status (SES) remain a critical issue. Health empowerment, a promising strategy linked to improved health outcomes, remains under explored in terms of mitigating SES-related health disparities and its potential to counteract structural inequities. This study aims to evaluate how health empowerment varies across SES strata and its association with health outcomes to inform targeted health promotion strategies for mitigating health inequalities.MethodsThis study conducted a cross-sectional survey in five cities in Anhui Province via purposive sampling. Data on health empowerment (health literacy, health consciousness, and healthcare trust/fairness), health outcomes (self-rated health, chronic diseases), and SES were collected. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression were used for analysis.ResultsAmong the 1,364 participants, chronic disease incidence showed a clear SES gradient (24.1% in the low SES group, 15.1% in the middle SES group, and 14.0% in the high SES group), whereas health literacy levels were lower in the low SES group when both median and upper quartile thresholds were used. High health literacy (using the upper quartile as the threshold) was linked to lower chronic disease prevalence in the low- and middle-SES groups and better self-rated health across all SES strata; health awareness was positively associated with self-rated health only in the middle- and high-SES groups, while perceived trust/fairness showing an inverse association with self-rated health in the low-SES group. Logistic regression confirmed that health literacy using the upper quartile as the threshold was associated with lower odds of chronic diseases in the low SES group (OR = 0.453, P < 0.05), with both thresholds benefiting self-rated health across all SES groups.ConclusionsThis study confirms significant socioeconomic disparities in chronic disease incidence and health literacy, with higher health literacy levels showing a protective association against chronic diseases, particularly in the low-SES population. While health literacy is consistently associate with self-rated health across all socioeconomic strata, health awareness is associated with self-rated health only in middle- and high-SES groups, suggesting that structural barriers impede health behavior translation in vulnerable populations. The findings underscore the need for tiered health empowerment interventions targeting specific socioeconomic groups, with higher thresholds of health literacy potentially related to overcoming structural disadvantages in low-SES communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25514-x.Keywords: Health empowerment, Health outcomes, Socioeconomic status

Background

Health disparities across socioeconomic status (SES) remain a critical issue. Individuals with higher SES consistently exhibit significant advantages across health indicators, such as longer life expectancy, lower levels of depression and anxiety, higher levels of happiness and satisfaction, and better self-rated health status [1, 2], with such gaps widening over time. Regarding the causes of health inequalities, social causation theory has garnered extensive recognition [3, 4]. It posits that individuals in different socioeconomic positions have access to varying levels of living resources, healthcare services, work environments, and health risks. These disparities ultimately shape health outcomes through cumulative effects, enabling those in advantageous positions to sustain better health over the long term [5].

Social causation theory highlights structural resource disparities as the root cause of SES-based health inequalities, yet such disparities are often slow to address through systemic changes. Identifying modifiable factors to reduce these gaps is therefore particularly valuable. Health empowerment, which is hypothesized to influence health through habits, cognition, awareness, and lifestyle, has emerged as a potential solution in this context [6]. Extensive research has confirmed its close association with health behaviors and outcomes, such as improved self-management of chronic diseases and better quality of life [7, 8]. A recent review proposed an empowerment model as a better approach for managing menopause, which is centered on gaining knowledge, confidence and ability in managing one’s own care [9]. Another review investigated the effects of empowerment education on patients after percutaneous coronary intervention and suggested that a reasonable empowerment education model should be formulated to achieve a balance between the benefits of patients and the medical burden [10]. As a strategy adopted to improve health outcomes, health empowerment has gained importance in health education and promotion. However, the specific role of empowerment in reducing SES-related health disparities remains under explored.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines health empowerment as the process of increasing control over decisions and actions that affect health [11]. Scholars have proposed a variety of different interpretations because of vague conceptual content. Rappaport and Maunsell [6, 7] proposed it as an individual’s ability to control health-related aspects of their life and make informed decisions regarding health matters. Anderson [12] emphasized that empowerment enables individuals to gain greater control over their lives through resource mobilization while acknowledging structural barriers to equality. This notion of empowerment is inherently interwoven with discourses on personal responsibility and self-care. Prigge et al. [13] conceptualized empowerment as behaviors addressing the intrinsic needs of autonomy and competence, manifested in information seeking, knowledge construction, and decision-making participation. Castro et al. [14] distinguished empowerment from patient participation, framing it as a process of gaining control over health issues through enhanced knowledge and skills, with a focus on autonomy and social engagement. Agner et al. [15] identified core themes of empowerment via systematic review, including knowledge acquisition, decision-making involvement, and daily life autonomy. Despite these variations, we can still abstract some core elements that health empowerment can achieve through improving individuals’ knowledge and potential, raising health awareness, and fostering a healthier environment through social processes.

Building on these insights, this study operationalizes health empowerment as a three-dimensional construct through three layers, including health literacy (knowledge and skills) at the foundational level, health awareness (psychological motivation and efficacy) at the driving level, and perceived reliability and fairness (assessment of the social environment) at the systemic interaction level. Specifically, health literacy is the foundational element of health empowerment. Scholars have highlighted the critical role of knowledge acquisition and skill enhancement in individual health control. In discussions on empowering chronic disease patients, Europe has reached a certain degree of consensus, acknowledging that patients must possess knowledge and skills for disease management to achieve empowerment [16]. Second, health awareness constitutes one of the intrinsic motivations for health empowerment. Empowerment requires individuals to possess the motivation and confidence to actively participate in health decision-making, manifested as the awareness of ‘taking responsibility for one’s own health’. Finally, previous studies have noted that empowerment requires the healthcare system to support power transfer [14], such as by ensuring patients’ decision-making participation rights and providing accessible resources and services [17]. The perception of the reliability and fairness of the healthcare system serves as an external condition for the realization of health empowerment.

Our multidimensional health empowerment framework also aligns with China’s national health initiatives. These include the seven-decade Patriotic Health Campaign (PHC) and the “Healthy China” strategy, both aiming to enhance public health literacy and equity. The PHC has advanced health literacy, awareness and equity, and now integrates the “Healthy China” strategy to emphasize universal participation. Notably, China’s continuous monitoring shows residents’ health literacy rose significantly from 8.80% in 2012 to 31.87% in 2024 [18]. Emerging evidence suggests health literacy mitigates health disparities, but SES-related health research in China remains limited. Only Yu et al. reported health literacy’s impact on health outcomes for those in the lower and middle socioeconomic strata [19].​.

Against evolving PHC strategies, it is unclear if health empowerment differs across SES groups, or how well it counters structural inequities to promote health equity. As an integrated construct encompassing health literacy, health awareness, and perceptions of the fairness and reliability of the healthcare system, health empowerment offers a multidimensional analytical framework for elucidating health inequalities across social strata. We may identify key empowerment dimensions related to health improvement across SES groups. Findings can provide scientific evidence for the formulation of targeted health promotion strategies tailored to specific socioeconomic groups. Furthermore, exploring the associations between varying threshold levels of health literacy and health outcomes may yield empirical insights to inform precise interventions.

Methods

Research sites and participants

This study was conducted in Anhui Province, China. Five cities were selected as research sites via purposive sampling, including three that had already been recognized as National Health Cities (an honor authorized by the National Patriotic Health Campaign Committee) and two that were currently working toward this designation. Data were collected from individual samples of community residents in these five cities.

At each sample site, 4 specific survey points were chosen, including primary school entrances, vegetable markets, large shopping malls, and ordinary residential communities. At each of these points, 60 respondents were interviewed via the street intercept method. Additionally, two fixed groups were surveyed in each sample area, government agency staff and urban sanitation workers, with 50 participants in total for these two groups. Thus, each sample area included 290 respondents, resulting in a proposed total sample size of 1,450 people.

The inclusion criteria for survey respondents were as follows: (1) permanent residents of the sample site and (2) adults aged 18 years and older. People with behavioral or cognitive disabilities were excluded from the study.

Instruments and measurements

Health literacy

The Chinese version of the Health Literacy Scale Short Form (HLS-SF12) was used. Developed on the basis of the HLS-EU-Q47, the HLS-SF12 retains the original conceptual framework and is an optimized version for the fast, comprehensive and effective measurement of health literacy [20]. It comprises three dimensions: healthcare, disease prevention and health promotion. Each dimension covers four aspects, including accessing, understanding, evaluating and applying health information, with a total of 12 items. Each item is scored on a 4-point scale (1 = very difficult; 2 = difficult; 3 = easy; 4 = very easy). A formula is then used to calculate a standardized health literacy (HL) index ranging from 0 to 50, with higher indices indicating higher levels of health literacy. The formula is index = (mean − 1) * (50/3). The Chinese version of the HLS-SF12 has good reliability and validity and can serve as a simple and accurate tool for assessing health literacy in this population [21].

Health consciousness

Bergman developed 5 items to measure health consciousness among Americans and reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.72 [22]. Researchers have translated the original version, tested it among Chinese residents, and demonstrated good reliability and validity [23]. The present study applied the Chinese version of the health consciousness scale. The five items are as follows: “living life in best possible health is very important to me,” “eating right, exercising, and taking preventive measures will keep me healthy for life,” “my health depends on how well I take care of myself,” “I actively try to prevent disease and illness,” and “I do everything I can to stay healthy.” A five-point Likert scale was used to measure responses, with 1 to 5 representing “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree”, respectively. A higher score indicates a high level of health consciousness.

Perceived Trust and Fairness in Healthcare Services

It was measured by two items as follows: “Do you trust the services provided by medical institutions? “, “Do you think the medical services are provided fairly?” Responses were ranked from 1 (extremely distrust/unfair) to 5 (extremely trust/fair). A higher score indicates higher perceived trust and fairness levels.

Health status

It was measured by self-rated health status and whether the patient had at least one chronic disease diagnosed by a county-level or higher medical institution.

Demographic information

Demographic background variables, including sex, age, education, income level and occupation were collected.

Assignment standard

SES is a composite measure that reflects an individual’s relative social position, with education, income, and occupation widely regarded as its core indicators at the individual level [24]. There is no consensus among researchers as to which of these three indicators carries greater weight in SES assessment [25, 26], so this paper still adds these three variables with equal weight [19, 27, 28]. This is also a commonly adopted approach in China as it facilitating simplicity and rapid application in preventive and response strategy development [29, 30].

Specifically, this study measured SES by first categorizing education, income, and occupation into four levels with each level assigned a score from 1 to 4 [30]. These scores were then summed to produce a total SES score (range: 3–12). Based on the distribution of total scores within the study sample, cutoff points were set at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles. Total scores ≤ 8 (≤ 25th percentile) were classified as low SES, scores 9–10 (25th −75th percentile) as medium SES, and scores ≥ 11 (≥ 75th percentile) as high SES, thereby reflecting their relative social stratification in the study population.

Regarding classification of Health Literacy Levels, given the lack of explicit pre-specified health literacy thresholds in existing literature, we conducted an exploratory analysis using two distribution-based thresholds to categorize health literacy levels. Participants were classified into high- and low-health literacy groups using the median (50th percentile) and the upper quartile (75th percentile) of the health literacy index. This exploratory approach aimed to preliminary examine how associations between health literacy and other variables vary under different classification standards. Statistical methods

We used descriptive statistics to compare differences in the distribution of health empowerment and health outcomes across low-, medium-, and high-SES groups, as well as differences in health outcomes between individuals with lower versus higher health empowerment within each SES group. Categorical variables are expressed as proportions, and the chi-square test was applied to examine intergroup differences. Logistic regression models were further employed to analyze the associations between health empowerment and health outcomes across different SES groups; these models included the three dimensions of health empowerment and demographic variables such as age and gender. Statistical significance was determined using a p value threshold of less than 0.05. All analyses were performed via SPSS 22.0.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the participants

Between May and July 2024, a total of 1,386 participants were approached for the study. Following data screening, 22 participants were excluded because of incomplete responses, yielding a final analytical sample of 1,364 individuals. Within this sample, 23.2% (n = 368) were under 30 years of age, 30.9% (n = 422) were aged 31–40, 19.4% (n = 265) were 41–50, and 22.7% (n = 309) were aged above 50 years old. Female participants accounted for 67.1% (n = 915) of the survey sample. On the basis of SES scoring, 32.9% were categorized as having low SES, 38.9% as having middle SES, and 28.2% as having high SES. In terms of health, 17.7% reported at least one chronic illness, whereas 28.7% reported being in poor or fair health status.

There were also stratum differences in health literacy levels. In our exploratory analysis of health literacy thresholds, the health literacy index in this population exhibited an interquartile range (IQR) of [33.3, 38.6], with a median score of 36.1. When exploring the median threshold (≥ 36.1), the rates of high health literacy were 40.3%, 52.5% and 56.4% in the low, medium and high SES groups, respectively. The findings revealed that when the median threshold was used, chronic disease incidence did not differ significantly between the high- and low-health literacy groups. However, when the upper quartile threshold was applied, chronic disease incidence was significantly lower in the high-level literacy group among the low- and middle-SES groups (p < 0.05), with no such association evident in the high-SES group. Associations of health empowerment with health outcomes across SES groups

Univariate analysis initially revealed associations between health empowerment, chronic disease incidence, and self-rated health status across diverse socioeconomic status groups. To control for biological confounding variables (e.g., age and sex) and quantify the independent associations, logistic regression was employed for further analysis. The regression models were constructed with chronic disease incidence and self-rated health status as dependent variables separately and health empowerment along with control variables (age, sex) as independent variables. The analysis employed two distinct health literacy thresholds (median and upper quartile), with models estimated separately for each threshold (Tables 5 and 6). All models demonstrated statistical significance in likelihood ratio tests (P < 0.05) and acceptable goodness-of-fit values via Hosmer–Lemeshow tests (P > 0.05), indicating good model performance.

For chronic disease incidence, age and sex were significant predictors across all strata, with female sex and age < 30 years serving as protective factors. After adjusting for these covariates, only the upper quartile health literacy threshold revealed a significant protective association with chronic disease within the low-SES population (OR = 0.453, P < 0.05).

With respect to self-rated good health, age and sex effects were attenuated. However, high-SES women reported better health than men did. Both health literacy thresholds were positively associated with self-rated health across all SES groups. Health awareness was positively associated with self-rated health in the medium- and high-SES groups but not in the low-SES groups. Perceived fairness and trust were significantly associated with self-rated health only in the low-SES group, demonstrating a counter-intuitive inverse relationship (lower perceived fairness/trust correlated with higher self-rated health).

Discussion

Significant socioeconomic disparities in both chronic disease incidence and health literacy

In the literature on health determinants, low socioeconomic status is closely associated with a high disease burden [19, 31, 32], a finding that was again confirmed in this study of urban populations in Anhui Province, China. The incidence of chronic disease followed a distinct socioeconomic gradient, with 24.1% observed in the low SES groups, 15.1% in the moderate SES groups, and 14.0% in the high SES groups. This inverse relationship underscores the potential importance of implementing structural health policies, such as improving access to affordable healthcare and expanding health management services, to address systemic inequalities.

This study confirms that significant differences in health literacy exist based on socioeconomic status, regardless of the threshold used to determine health literacy levels. The lowest SES group had the lowest health literacy level. This suggests that low-SES groups also face a health literacy disadvantage. This may be because health literacy is knowledge dependent, meaning that individuals with higher levels of education can access and utilize health-related information more easily, making them better able to make healthy behavioral choices and decisions in medical settings and in their social lives [33]. Economic income is linked to health literacy through accessibility to resources. Low-income groups may have limited access to health information because of an inability to afford associated costs (e.g., subscription fees for professional medical information or the purchase of digital devices) [34]. Occupation represents access to social networks, which can affect the efficiency with which health information can be obtained [35]. This study categorized SES groups on the basis of education, income and occupation and confirmed the unequal distribution of health literacy across different socioeconomic strata. This suggests that, in addition to universal health education, a stratified intervention strategy for health education and promotion should be implemented. For example, digital platforms could be used to deliver personalized health management tools to populations with high health literacy and high SES, but face-to-face skill training would be more appropriate for low SES groups with lower health literacy.

Notably, self-rated health followed a similar SES gradient (67.7% low SES, 73.6% moderate SES, 72.5% high SES), although statistical significance was not reached. This lack of significance reflects the subjectivity of self-assessment. Individuals in low-SES groups may normalize suboptimal health or prioritize economic survival over health perceptions, whereas high-SES individuals may use stricter health standards. Despite the lack of statistical significance, the lowest self-rated health among the low-SES group is consistent with the possibility of potential unmet healthcare needs or reporting biases in vulnerable populations.

Health literacy across all social strata and health awareness in the middle and upper strata are associated with self-rated health status

In univariate group comparisons, health literacy and health awareness were both significantly associated with self-rated health status across all socioeconomic strata. This finding indicates that, regardless of socioeconomic status, an individual’s health literacy level and health consciousness are linked to their perceived health status. When adjusting for covariates, health literacy remained significantly correlated with self-rated health across all strata, whereas health awareness retained significance only in the middle- and high-SES groups. This suggests the universal significance of health literacy across all socioeconomic status groups, as even among resource-constrained low-SES populations, high health literacy may be related to counteracting structural disadvantages through knowledge and skills. Conversely, the loss of statistical significance for health awareness in the lower strata after adjustment indicates that its association with health cognition is mediated or confounded by other factors. Specifically, within lower socioeconomic groups, the association of health awareness with self-rated health may be less pronounced in the context of structural barriers such as economic pressures and competing life burdens, which can impede the translation of health intentions into actionable behaviors. Consequently, health awareness may fail to manifest as a discernible correlation with self-assessed health status in these populations.

A counter-intuitive finding emerged within the low-SES group, where lower perceived trust and fairness in the healthcare system was associated with better self-rated health. This may be understood through the lens of ‘critical consciousness’, wherein a realistic distrust of inequitable systems may motivate individuals to rely on their own agency for health management [36, 37]. This shift toward self-reliance and proactive behaviors can enhance perceived control over one’s health, potentially explaining the association with better self-rated health.

Threshold association of health literacy with chronic diseases in low-SES populations

To examine how different cutoff points for defining health literacy levels were associated with outcome variables, this study analyzed two thresholds. Univariate analysis revealed no significant difference in chronic disease incidence when the median was used as the cutoff. However, using the upper quartile as the cutoff revealed differences in chronic disease incidence among low- and middle-SES groups, with no such associations observed in high-SES groups. After controlling for confounding variables, lower health literacy thresholds showed no significant association with chronic diseases across all SES groups, whereas higher thresholds were associated with lower odds of chronic diseases in low SES populations.

These findings align with those of a prior study in China, which revealed that health literacy has a more pronounced association with chronic diseases in low-SES groups than in high-SES groups [19]. Our exploratory analysis extends this work by suggesting a threshold pattern, wherein health literacy may need to reach a relatively high level to demonstrate a discernible association with chronic disease outcomes in disadvantaged populations. This may be understood by considering two potential explanations. First, low SES groups face structural barriers, with limited access to health information and constrained economic/social networks for health behavior modification, potentially making higher levels of health literacy more critical for navigating these challenges. In contrast, middle-to-high SES groups, with greater access to quality healthcare and health management resources, may be less dependent on very high health literacy level alone. Second, health knowledge and skills may require cumulative acquisition to translate into effective self-management capabilities. For example, low SES individuals with basic health literacy (e.g., understanding simple medical advice) may still struggle with complex tasks such as lifestyle modification, whereas higher literacy bridges the “capability-demand” gap.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and the purposive sampling of National Health Cities limits the generalization of the findings to all Chinese urban populations. Secondly, the study applied three dimensions (health literacy, awareness, and perceived system fairness) to operationalize health empowerment. As the conceptualization of health empowerment evolves, future research should continue to explore the impact of health empowerment and its different dimensions on population subgroups, as this will be of greater practical importance for targeted interventions. Thirdly, the inverse association between trust/fairness and self-rated health in the low-SES group should be interpreted with caution. Despite the proposed theoretical explanations, the cross-sectional design and response bias prevent causal inference. Future research requires longitudinal designs and refined trust/fairness measures to clarify this relationship. Fourthly, this study focused on the association of SES and health empowerment with health outcomes, controlling for basic demographic factors (age and sex). Consequently, other potential covariates, such as marital status, health behaviors, and healthcare access, were not included. This limited set of variables is reflected in the model R-squared values, indicating that a portion of the variance remains unaccounted for. Future studies should expand the range of covariates to better elucidate the complex interactions between SES and other social determinants, to provide a more comprehensive explanation of the factors influencing health outcomes.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to explore the relationship between health empowerment and health outcomes across social economic status in the Chinese population. Our findings document significant socioeconomic disparities in both chronic disease incidence and health literacy. The low SES group was found to bear a heavier burden of chronic diseases and to have lower levels of health literacy. In examining the associations between different levels of health literacy and health outcomes, a threshold pattern was identified. Specifically, a higher level of health literacy (defined by the upper quartile cutoff in this study) was associated with lower prevalence of chronic disease among low-SES individuals, whereas a moderate level (defined by the median cutoff) showed no significant association with health outcomes across SES groups. Health awareness was positively associated with health outcomes in middle- and high-SES groups but showed no significant association in low-SES populations.Notably, despite the comparable distribution of health awareness across socioeconomic strata, this divergence suggests that low-SES individuals face structural constraints in translating health awareness into health improvements.

In summary, the socioeconomic gradients in health literacy and chronic diseases, coupled with the attenuated association of health awareness with health outcomes in the low-SES group, support the potential value of targeted efforts to enhance health literacy in this population. Furthermore, the observed threshold association suggests that achieving a critical level of health literacy may be particularly relevant for improving health outcomes among the socioeconomically disadvantaged.
