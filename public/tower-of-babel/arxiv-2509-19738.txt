Plasmon damping in a charged Bose-Einstein condensate model sarira@nucleares.unam.mx
José F. Nieves

Abstract
In this work we consider the calculation of the imaginary part of the dispersion relations of the propagating modes in a model of a charged scalar Bose-Einstein (BE) condensate, as well as the contribution to the imaginary part of the longitudinal component of the photon polarization tensor and the dielectric constant. In that model, two modes correspond to the transverse photon polarizations, while the other two modes are combinations of the longitudinal photon and the massive scalar field, which we denote as the ( ± ) (\pm) modes. The dispersion relations of the transverse modes have the usual form for transverse photons in a plasma, and we do not consider here any further. In a previous work we determined the real part of the dispersion relations of the ( ± ) (\pm) modes, as well as the real part of the longitudinal component of of polarization tensor and dielectric constant, which have some unique features. In the appropriate limit, those results reproduce the results obtained for the dielectric constant and dispersion relation in non-relativistic models of the BE condensation of charged scalars. Here we determine, in the same model, their imaginary part. The results can be useful in physical contexts involving the electrodynamics of a charged scalar BE condensate, and can serve as benchmark results for further exploration.



1
Introduction and motivation

The properties of a particle that propagates in a medium,
such as its dispersion relation, depend crucially on the
composition and conditions of the background.
Models that extend the standard electroweak theory usally include
additional scalar particles, neutral and charged,
that interact with the standard model particles. Such new
scalar particles and interactions can have implications in
nuclear, astrophysics, and cosmological plasma systems,
which may contain a background of the
scalar particles
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Thus, for example, the intrinsic properties of neutrinos
and photons propagating in such a background medium, such as the dispersion
relations and/or electromagnetic properties, can be modified
in special ways that lead to significant and observable effects.
Under the appropriate conditions, the scalar background
may form a Bose-Einstein (BE) condensate. This is the case,
for example, in the models involved in the hypothesis that the
dark-matter is a self-interacting BE
condensate
[
5
,
7
,
6
,
8
,
9
]
.

Since the BE condensate is a very special state of matter, the properties
of the particles propagating in such a background, such as the dispersion
relations, are different from those in normal matter. The
propagation of photons in a medium that contains a BE condenssate
has been studied using various approaches and in various contexts,
for example the electrodynamics of fermions and
charged scalar bosons at low temperature
[
10
]
,
heavy-ion collisions
[
11
]
, exciton-polaritons
[
12
]
,
the BE condensation of photons
[
13
,
14
]
and the
Cerenkov effect
[
15
]
, and the reduction of the photon
velocity
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
in atomic gases.

Motivated by these developments, in a previous work
[
19
]

we presented a model for the propagation of photons in a thermal
background that contains a BE condensate of a charged scalar.
The model is in turn based on a model we had considered earlier
[
20
]

to study the propagation of fermions in a BE condensate.
The field theoretical method we used in Refs.
[
19
]
and
[
20
]

to treat the BE condensate, which we use here, has been discussed by various
authors
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

In Ref.
[
19
]
we determined the dispersion relations of the
propagating modes after the symmetry breaking associated with the BE
condensation in the model. In that model, two modes correspond to the
transverse photon polarizations, while the other two modes are combinations
of the longitudinal photon and the massive scalar field, which we denote
as the
(\pm)
modes. While the dispersion relations of the transverse modes
have the usual form for transverse photons in a plasma, the dispersion
relations of the (
\pm
) modes reveal some unique features. In our
previous work cited above, we determined and analyzed the real part of the
dispersion relations of the
(\pm)
modes, as well as the real part of
the contribution to the longitudinal component of polarization tensor
and the corresponding contribution to the dielectric constant.
In particular, in the appropriate limit, the dispersion
relation of the
(-)
mode reduces to the expression
for the dispersion relation that has been obtained in
non-relativistic models of the BE condensation of charged
scalars
[
25
,
26
]
.

The focus of the present work is the calculation of
the imaginary part of the dispersion relations,
as well as the corresponding contributions to the imaginary part
of the longitudinal component of polarization tensor
and the dielectric constant, in the same model.
These parameters are important because they
determine the energy loss and absoption effects when the system
is subject to external perturbations.
The main result is a set of explicit formulas for
those quantities, obtained by the one-loop
calculations of the self-energy of the propagating (
\pm
) modes
in the context of TFT. These results can be usfeul in the
context of the various applications that have been considered
in the references mentioned above, and in the works related
to dark-matter searches using materials with special optical
properties
[
27
]
.

To illustrate some features of the results
we consider in some detail the long wavelength limit, including
specifically the high and low frequency limits, and obtained
simple explicit expressions for the damping rate as well as the
background contribution of the longitudinal polarization
tensor and dielectric constant in those cases. These results are obtained
in a general way, within the context of the model, but not tied to any
specific application, and as such can be useful and pave the way
for further development in various contexts and applications.
In the following sections we present the details of the calculations,

2
The BE condensate model

For reference we first summarize the essential features
of the scalar BE condensation model we use, and then
review the basic elements of the model with the coupling to the photon.

The scalar model for the
\phi
, discussed in detail in Ref.
[
20
]
,
is based on the Lagrangian

L_{\phi}=(d^{\mu}\phi)^{\ast}(d_{\mu}\phi)-V_{\phi}\,,

(2.1)

where

d_{\mu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}-i\mu u_{\mu}\,,

(2.2)

and

V_{\phi}=m^{2}\phi^{\ast}\phi+\lambda(\phi^{\ast}\phi)^{2}\,.

(2.3)

The parameter
\mu
is to be identified with the chemical potential
of the
\phi
, and
u^{\mu}
is to be identified with
the velocity four-vector of the medium,

u^{\mu}=(1,\vec{0})\,,

(2.4)

in the medium’s own rest frame.

As discussed in Ref.
[
20
]

the motivation for considering this model is based on the following result:
the calculation of the effective potential
V_{\text{eff}}(T,\mu)
for
\phi

can be carried out in TFT using
\mu=0
in the partition
(and/or distribution) function, but using the

\mu
-dependent Lagrangian density
L_{\phi}
defined in Eq. (
2.1
).
Then neglecting the
T
-dependent terms (that is, at zero temperature),

V_{\text{eff}}(0,\mu)
is simply the
U
potential,
given below in Eq. (
2.8
).
In other words, calculations with the
\phi
propagators,

\phi
Lagrangian density and partition function for
\phi
,

Z=e^{-\beta H+\alpha Q}\,,

(2.5)

give the same result as calculating them with the Lagrangian density
for
\phi^{\prime}
obtained by using

\partial_{\mu}\rightarrow D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-i\mu u_{\mu}
and using

Z=e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}\,.

(2.6)

A simple proof is given in Ref.
[
19
]
.

Expanding the
d
term in Eq. (
2.1
),

L_{\phi}=(\partial^{\mu}\phi)^{\ast}(\partial_{\mu}\phi)+i[\phi^{\ast}(v\cdot\partial\phi)-(v\cdot\partial\phi^{\ast})\phi]-U(\phi)\,,

(2.7)

where

U\equiv V_{\phi}-\mu^{2}\phi^{\ast}\phi=-(\mu^{2}-m^{2})\phi^{\ast}\phi+\lambda(\phi^{\ast}\phi)^{2}\,,

(2.8)

with

v_{\mu}=\mu u_{\mu}\,.

(2.9)

The case in which
\mu^{2}<m^{2}
corresponds
to a standard massive complex scalar with mass
m^{2}-\mu^{2}
.
On the other hand, if
\mu^{2}>m^{2}
, the minimum of the potential is
not at
\phi=0
. In this situation
\phi
develops a non-zero expectation
value and the
U(1)
symmetry is broken, which is identified as
corresponding to the BE condensation phase.

We assume the situation is such that

\mu^{2}>m^{2}\,.

(2.10)

Then putting

\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\phi_{0}+\phi_{1}+i\phi_{2}\right)\,,

(2.11)

where

\langle\phi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_{0}\,,

(2.12)

is the minimum of

U_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{2}-m^{2})\phi^{2}_{0}+\frac{1}{4}\lambda\phi^{4}_{0}\,,

(2.13)

gives

\phi^{2}_{0}=\frac{\mu^{2}-m^{2}}{\lambda}\,.

(2.14)

The Lagrangian density for the scalar excitations
\phi_{1,2}
,
obtained by substituting Eqs. (
2.11
) and (
2.14
) in Eq. (
2.1
), becomes

L_{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial^{\mu}\phi_{1})^{2}+(\partial^{\mu}\phi_{2})^{2}\right]+\phi_{2}v\cdot\partial\phi_{1}-\phi_{1}v\cdot\partial\phi_{2}-U(\phi)\,,

(2.15)

where

U(\phi)=-\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{2}-m^{2})[(\phi_{0}+\phi_{1})^{2}+\phi^{2}_{2}]+\frac{1}{4}\lambda[(\phi_{0}+\phi_{1})^{2}+\phi^{2}_{2}]^{2}\,.

(2.16)

Using Eq. (
2.14
), it follows that only
\phi_{1}
appears in
the quadratic part,

U_{2}=\frac{1}{2}m^{2}_{1}\phi^{2}_{1}\,,

(2.17)

with

m^{2}_{1}=2(\mu^{2}-m^{2})\,,

(2.18)

while
\phi_{2}
does not appear. However,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are mixed
by the
v^{\mu}
term.

A convenient way to diagonalize the bilinear part of
L_{\phi}
and
determine the modes that have definite dispersion relations,
is to use matrix notation and write

\hat{\phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{array}\right)\,.

(2.19)

In momentum space, the bilinear part of
L_{\phi}
can then be written in the form

L^{(2)}_{\phi}(k)=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\phi}^{\ast}(k)\Delta^{-1}_{\phi}(k)\hat{\phi}(k)\,,

(2.20)

where

\Delta^{-1}_{\phi}(k)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}k^{2}-m^{2}_{1}&2iv\cdot k\\
-2iv\cdot k&k^{2}\end{array}\right)\,.

(2.21)

The classical field equations are

\Delta^{-1}_{\phi}(k)\hat{\phi}=0\,,

(2.22)

and the dispersion relations of the eigenmodes are obtained by solving

(\omega^{2}-\kappa^{2})(\omega^{2}-\kappa^{2}-m^{2}_{1})-4\mu^{2}\omega^{2}=0\,.

(2.23)

In Eq. (
2.23
) we have used Eq. (
2.9
) and defined the variables

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u\cdot k\,,

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\omega^{2}-k^{2}}\,.

(2.24)

In the rest frame of the medium

k^{\mu}=(\omega,\vec{\kappa})\,,

(2.25)

with
\kappa=|\vec{\kappa}|
. The dispersion relations
can be written in the form

\omega^{2}_{\pm}(\kappa)=\kappa^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}_{\rho}\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}m^{4}_{\rho}+4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}\,,

(2.26)

where

m^{2}_{\rho}=m^{2}_{1}+4\mu^{2}=6\mu^{2}-2m^{2}\,.

(2.27)

It follows that

\displaystyle\omega_{+}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{\rho}\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{-}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,.

(2.28)

The combinations of
\phi_{1,2}
that have the definite dispersion
relations
\omega_{\pm}
can be obtained from Eq. (
2.22
),
but we will not need them here and therefore not discuss them further.
We mention that, while the mode corresponding to
\omega_{-}

is the realization of the Goldstone mode associated
with the breaking of the global
U(1)
symmetry,
its dispersion relation is not the usual
\omega=\kappa
,
but the one given by
\omega_{-}(\kappa)
in Eq. (
2.26
).

3
Charged BE condensate model

As discussed in Ref.
[
19
]
, to study the propagation
of photons in the BE condensate background, we extended
the model by considering a
\phi
field that is electrically
charged and interacts with electromagnetic field in the standard way.
The Lagrangian density for the model is then

L=-\frac{1}{4}F^{2}+L_{\phi A}\,,

(3.1)

where
F_{\mu\nu}
is the electromagnetic field tensor,

L_{\phi A}=(\hat{D}^{\mu}\phi)^{\ast}(\hat{D}_{\mu}\phi)-V_{\phi}\,,

(3.2)

with

\hat{D}_{\mu}=d_{\mu}+iqA_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-iv_{\mu}+iqA_{\mu}\,,

(3.3)

while
V_{\phi}
and
v^{\mu}
are defined in Eqs. (
2.3
) and (
2.9
), respectively.
Notice that, while it would seem that the presence of
\mu

in the Lagrangian can be eliminated by letting

A_{\mu}=A^{\prime}_{\mu}-\frac{1}{q}v_{\mu}\,,

(3.4)

this would actually restore the presence of
\mu
in the partition function,
which defeats the purpose of using the transformed field
\phi^{\prime}
.

For our purposes, it is more convenient to adopt the unitary gauge
and parametrize
\phi
in the form

\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_{0}+\rho)e^{i\theta/\phi_{0}}\,.

(3.5)

The field
\theta
does not appear in
V_{\phi}
, and
by a gauge transformation it disappears also from the kinetic term.
To indicate clearly that we are using the unitary gauge, we denote
by
V_{\mu}
the transformed vector potential

V_{\mu}=A_{\mu}+\frac{1}{q\phi_{0}}\partial_{\mu}\theta
.
With this notation,
L_{\phi A}
is written in the form

L_{\phi A}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial\rho)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(v-qV)^{\mu}(v-qV)_{\mu}(\phi_{0}+\rho)^{2}-V_{\phi}\,.

(3.6)

Proceeding as in the previous section, in particular assuming again that

\mu^{2}>m^{2}\,,

(3.7)

it follows that
\phi_{0}
is given as in Eq. (
2.14
).
Furthermore, the bilinear part of
L
, including the
F^{2}
term, is given by

L^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{4}F^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}_{V}V^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial\rho)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}_{1}\rho^{2}-2m_{V}(v\cdot V)\rho\,,

(3.8)

where

m^{2}_{V}=q^{2}\phi^{2}_{0}=\frac{q^{2}}{\lambda}(\mu^{2}-m^{2})\,,

(3.9)

and
m_{1}
is defined in Eq. (
2.18
).

The physical picture is that we end up with the two fields
V
and
\rho
.
The field
\theta
becomes the longitudinal component of
V
,
which is mixed with the scalar field
\rho
, by the term
\rho v\cdot V

in the Lagrangian. As a consequence of this,
the propagating modes with definite dispersion relations are
superpositions of the longitudinal component of
V
and the
\rho
.
Finding the dispersion relations, and the corresponding
mixing

combinations of the longitudinal component of
V
and the
\rho
was the
subject of Ref.
[
19
]
. Our goal here is to determine the imaginary part,
or damping term, of the dispersion relations.

For this purpose, below we summarize the salient features of the results
we obtained for the dispersion relations, and more importantly for the
details of the present work, the corresponding mixing parameters
that determine the propagating modes in terms of the fields
\rho

and the longitudinal component of
V
.

3.1
Mixing equations

In momentum space
L^{(2)}
becomes

L^{(2)}(k)=-V^{\ast\mu}[k^{2}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}-m^{2}_{V}g_{\mu\nu}]V^{\nu}+\rho^{\ast}(k^{2}-m^{2}_{1})\rho-2\mu m_{V}[(u\cdot V)^{\ast}\rho+c.c]\,,

(3.10)

where

\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}-\frac{k_{\mu}k_{\nu}}{k^{2}}\,.

(3.11)

The first two terms are the usual terms for a vector with squared
mass
m^{2}_{V}
and a scalar with squared mass
m^{2}_{1}
,
while the last term mixes the scalar with the longitudinal
components of
V^{\mu}
. We decompose
V^{\mu}
in the form

V^{\mu}=V^{\mu}_{T}+V_{L}e^{\mu}_{3}+\frac{k^{\mu}}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}V_{k}\,,\qquad V^{\mu}_{T}=\sum_{i=1,2}V_{i}e^{\mu}_{i}

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(0,\vec{e}_{1,2})\,,\qquad(\vec{e}_{1,2}\cdot\vec{\kappa}=0)\,,

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{u}^{\mu}}{\sqrt{-\tilde{u}^{2}}}\,,

(3.13)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\mu}=\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{\mu}-\frac{(k\cdot u)k_{\mu}}{k^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\sqrt{-\tilde{u}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\,.

(3.14)

Substituting Eq. (
3.12
) in Eq. (
3.10
), it follows that
the component
V_{k}
does not have a kinetic energy term, and therefore
it is not a dynamical variable. Eliminating it
from the Lagrangian by using its Lagrange equation,

V_{k}=\frac{2\mu(k\cdot u)}{m_{V}\sqrt{k^{2}}}\rho\,,

(3.15)

and substituting it in Eq. (
3.10
),

\displaystyle L^{(2)}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(k^{2}-m^{2}_{V})V^{\ast}_{T}\cdot V_{T}+(k^{2}-m^{2}_{V})V^{\ast}_{L}V_{L}+\left(k^{2}-m^{2}_{1}-\frac{4\mu^{2}(k\cdot u)^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\rho^{\ast}\rho

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mbox{}+2\mu m_{V}\sqrt{-\tilde{u}^{2}}\left[V^{\ast}_{L}\rho+c.c.\right]\,.

Thus, the transverse modes
V_{T}
have a standard degenerate
dispersion relation for the two polarizations, and we will not
consider them further.

The remaining terms mix the longitudinal mode

V_{L}
ith the scalar
\rho
. Introducing the notation

\xi=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\rho\\
V_{L}\end{array}\right)\,,

(3.17)

they can be written in the form

L^{(2)}_{\xi}(k)=\xi^{\dagger}\left(\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}\right)^{-1}\xi\,,

(3.18)

where

\left(\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}&\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\\[12.0pt]
\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}&k^{2}-m^{2}_{V}\end{array}\right)\,.

(3.19)

In writing Eq. (
3.19
) we have used the
relation
(k\cdot u)^{2}=k^{2}+\kappa^{2}
as well as Eq. (
2.27
).
The field equations, which determine
the dispersion relations and mixing parameters are

\left(\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}\right)^{-1}\xi=0\,.

(3.20)

3.2
Dispersion relations

From Eq. (
3.20
), the equaion for the dispersion relations is

(k^{2}-m^{2}_{V})(k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho})-4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}=0\,.

(3.21)

Writing the momentum of each propagating mode
(s=\pm)
as

k^{\mu}=(\omega^{(s)}_{r},\vec{\kappa})\,,

(3.22)

the dispersion relations are given by

\omega^{(s)}_{r}(\kappa)=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+\sigma_{s}(\kappa)}\,,

(3.23)

where

\sigma_{s}(\kappa)=\frac{1}{2}(m^{2}_{\rho}+m^{2}_{V})+\frac{s}{2}\sigma(\kappa)\,,

(3.24)

with

\sigma(\kappa)=\left[(m^{2}_{\rho}-m^{2}_{V})^{2}+16\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(3.25)

As mentioned in Section
1
, throughout this work we are assuming that

m_{\rho}>m_{V}\,.

(3.26)

In this case, at zero momentum,

\sigma(0)=m^{2}_{\rho}-m^{2}_{V}\,,

(3.27)

and therefore

\displaystyle\omega^{(+)}_{r}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{\rho}\,,

\displaystyle\omega^{(-)}_{r}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{V}\,.

(3.28)

This is an indication that, at
\kappa=0
, the
(+)
mode is a pure
\rho

while the
(-)
mode is a pure
V_{L}
, as we will confirm below.

In the limit

\displaystyle\propto

3.4
Polarization tensor and longitudinal dielectric constant

The contribution to the longitudinal dielectric constant can be
obtained is as follows. The procedure is to start from Eq. (
3.16
)
and integrate out the variable
\rho
, which is the tree-level analog
of calculating the one-loop self-energy,
leaving only the dynamical variables of
V
, and then identifying the
polarization tensor by comparing the result with

L_{eff}=-V^{\ast\mu}\left[(k^{2}-\pi^{(T)})R_{\mu\nu}+(k^{2}-\pi^{(L)})Q_{\mu\nu}\right]V^{\nu}\,,

(3.42)

where
R_{\mu\nu}
and
Q_{\mu\nu}
are defined by

\displaystyle Q_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{u}_{\mu}\tilde{u}_{\nu}}{\tilde{u}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}-Q_{\mu\nu}\,.

(3.43)

In terms of the variables
V_{T,L}
we have defined in Eq. (
3.12
),

L_{eff}=-(k^{2}-\pi^{(T)})V^{\ast}_{T}\cdot V_{T}+(k^{2}-\pi^{(L)}V^{\ast}_{L}V_{L}\,.

(3.44)

In general, when the thermal corrections are taken into account,

\pi^{(T,L)}
develop an imaginaty part, which we indicate by writing

\pi^{(T,L)}=\pi^{(T,L)}_{r}+i\pi^{(T,L)}_{i}\,.

(3.45)

Solving Eq. (
3.20
) for
\rho
and substituting the solution back
in Eq. (
3.16

3.44
) with

\pi^{(L)}_{r}\equiv m^{2}_{V}+\frac{4\mu^{2}m^{2}_{V}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}(k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho})-4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}\,.

(3.46)

The solutions of the equation

k^{2}-\pi^{(L)}_{r}=0\,,

(3.47)

reproduce the dispersion relations given in
Section
3.2
, as it should be.

For our current purposes, one merit of Eq. (
3.46
) is that it gives
the contribution to the longitudinal dielectric function.
Using
[
28
]
,

\epsilon_{\ell}=1-\frac{\pi^{(L)}}{k^{2}}\,,

(3.48)

we then obtain,

\mbox{Re}\,\epsilon_{\ell}=1-\frac{m^{2}_{V}}{k^{2}}\left[1+\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}(k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho})-4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right]\,.

(3.49)

As we discuss in some detail in Section
7
, in the
large
m_{\rho}
limit, stated precsily there, the dispersion
relation of the
(-)
mode, given by
\omega^{(-)}_{r}
in Eq. (
3.23
), as
well as the expression for
\mbox{Re}\,\epsilon_{\ell}
given above in
Eq. (
3.49
), reproduce the results obtained in non-relativistic
models of BE condensation of charged scalars
[
25
]
.

The imaginary part of the longitudinal polarization tensor, which we denote
by
\pi^{(L)}_{i}
, arises at one-loop, and it is the subject of the present work.
The corresponding contribution to the imaginary part of the
longitudinal dielectric constant is then given by

\mbox{Im}\,\epsilon_{\ell}=-\frac{\pi^{(L)}_{i}}{k^{2}}\,.

(3.50)

The details of their calculation are presented in the sections that follow.

4
Propagators

4.1
Zero temperature
(\pm)
propagators

The zero temperature propagators of the
s=(\pm)
modes
can be written in the standard form

\Delta^{(s)}_{F0}(k)=\frac{1}{k^{2}-\sigma_{s}(\kappa)+i\epsilon}\,,

(4.1)

where the
\sigma_{s}(\kappa)
is given in Eq. (
3.24
).
For notational purposes in the discussions that follow,
it is convenient to generalize the expression above
and write the propagator for any momentum vector (e.g.,
q
) in the form

\Delta^{(s)}_{F0}(q)=\frac{1}{q^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}(q)+i\epsilon}\,,

(4.2)

where

\hat{\sigma}_{s}(q)=\sigma_{s}(Q)\,,

(4.3)

and

Q=\sqrt{(q\cdot u)^{2}-q^{2}}\,.

(4.4)

4.2
Finite temperature
(\pm)
propagators

The damping of each mode
(s=\pm)
is obtained from the imaginary
part of the self-energy, which in turn is determined
from the
(\pi_{s})_{12}
component of the thermal self-energy of each mode,
calculated, in our case, to 1-loop.
One simplifying feature of the
(\pm)
propagators is the fact that

\sigma_{s}
is function of
Q
but not of
q\cdot u
,
and therefore the usual derivation of the thermal propagators can be applied
here as well. The relevant components of the thermal propagator matrices,
which enter in the 1-loop calculation of
(\pi_{s})_{12}
, are

\displaystyle\Delta^{(s)}_{21}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\delta(q^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}(q))\left[\eta_{B}(q)+\theta(q)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\Delta^{(s)}_{12}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\delta(q^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}(q))\left[\eta_{B}(q)+\theta(-q)\right]\,,

(4.5)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{B}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(k)n_{B}(x_{q})+\theta(-q)n_{B}(-x_{q})\,,

(4.6)

with

\displaystyle x_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta q\cdot u\,.

(4.7)

Further,

\theta(q)=H(q\cdot u)\,,

(4.8)

where
H(z)
is the step function, and
n_{B}
is the
boson distribution function

n_{B}(x)=\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}\,.

(4.9)

4.3
Zero temperature flavor propagators

From Eq. (
3.19
) it follows that the flavor propagator is given by

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}(k)=\frac{C^{(\xi)}}{D^{(\xi)}}\,,

(4.10)

where

C^{(\xi)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}k^{2}-m^{2}_{V}&-\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\\[12.0pt]
-\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}&k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}\end{array}\right)\,,

(4.11)

and

\displaystyle D^{(\xi)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k^{2}-m^{2}_{V})\left(k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)-\frac{4\mu^{2}m^{2}_{V}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k^{2}-m^{2}_{V})(k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho})-4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}\,.

The equation
D^{(\xi)}=0
gives us the dispersion relations that
we have already discussed, and in fact we can write

D^{(\xi)}=d_{+}d_{-}\,,

(4.13)

where

d_{s}=k^{2}-\sigma_{s}\,,

(4.14)

and
\sigma_{s}
has been defined in Eq. (
3.24
).

Using the relations

\displaystyle k^{2}-m^{2}_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{-}+\sigma_{-}-m^{2}_{V}\,,

\displaystyle k^{2}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{+}+\sigma_{+}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}\,.

(4.15)

and

\frac{1}{d_{+}d_{-}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{+}-\sigma_{-}}\left(\frac{1}{d_{+}}-\frac{1}{d_{-}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\frac{1}{d_{+}}-\frac{1}{d_{-}}\right)\,,

(4.16)

the propagator in Eq. (
4.10
) can be written in the form

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}(k)=\sum_{s}\Delta^{(s)}_{F0}(k)C_{s}\,,

(4.17)

where
\Delta^{(s)}_{F0}
is given in Eq. (
4.1
), and

\displaystyle C_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma+\sigma_{-}-m^{2}_{V}&-\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\\[12.0pt]
-\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}&\sigma_{+}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}\end{array}\right)\,

\displaystyle C_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}-\left(\sigma_{-}-m^{2}_{V}\right)&\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\\[12.0pt]
\frac{2\mu m_{V}\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}&\sigma-\left(\sigma_{+}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\end{array}\right)\,.

Although we are not indicating it explicitly here, it should be
kept in mind that the the matrices
C_{\pm}
are functions
of
\omega
and
\kappa
.

4.4
Finite temperature flavor propagators

Eq. (
4.17
) is the formula for the full flavor
propagators at zero temperature. Their finite temperature
counterparts are given by the same expression but replacing
the zero temperature
\Delta^{(s)}_{F0}
by their finite-temperature
counterparts, given in Eq. (
4.2
). Explicitly,

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{ab}(k)=\sum_{s}\Delta^{(s)}_{ab}(k)C_{s}

(4.24)

where
a
and
b
stand for the thermal indices 1,2.

For sufficiently small
\kappa
, the
C_{\pm}
matrices become
diagonal,

C_{+}\rightarrow\text{diag}(1,0),C_{-}\rightarrow\text{diag}(0,1)
,
and therefore

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}(k)\rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta^{(+)}_{F0}(k)&0\\
0&\Delta^{(-)}_{F0}(k)\end{array}\right)\,,

(4.25)

and correspondingly for the thermal propagator matrix,

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{ab}(k)\rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta^{(+)}_{ab}(k)&0\\
0&\Delta^{(-)}_{ab}(k)\end{array}\right)\,.

(4.26)

That is, the flavor propagator matrix becomes diagonal
with
\rho
identified with the
(+)
mode and
V_{L}
with the
(-)
mode,
as we have already anticipated.

4.5
One-particle approximation

The one-particle approximation consists in making the replacements

C_{s}(\omega,\kappa)\rightarrow C_{s}(\omega^{(s)}_{r},\vec{\kappa})\,,

(4.27)

in the expressions for the propagators in
Eqs. (
4.17
) and (
4.24
).
That is, aproximate the propagator by evaluating the numerators
at the dispersion relation. Thus, for example,

C_{+}\rightarrow C_{+}(\omega^{(+)}_{r},\kappa)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma+\sigma_{-}-m^{2}_{V}&-m_{V}\gamma_{+}\\[12.0pt]
-m_{V}\gamma_{+}&\sigma_{+}-m^{2}_{\rho}-\gamma^{2}_{+}\end{array}\right)\,,

(4.28)

and similarly for
C_{-}(\omega^{(-)}_{r},\kappa)
, where
\gamma_{\pm}

are given in Eq. (
3.3
). It is straightforward to confirm that

\displaystyle C_{+}(\omega^{(+)}_{r},\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Lambda\sigma}u_{+}u^{\dagger}_{+}\,,

\displaystyle C_{-}(\omega^{(-)}_{r},\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\Lambda+\gamma^{2}_{-})\sigma}u_{-}u^{\dagger}_{-}\,,

(4.29)

where
u_{\pm}
are the (flavor) spinors given in Eq. (
3.3
).
In other words, in the one-particle approximation, the zero-temperature
flavor propagators are given by

\left(\Delta^{(\xi)}_{F0}(k)\right)_{1p}=\frac{U_{+}U^{\dagger}_{+}}{k^{2}-\sigma_{+}+i\epsilon}+\frac{U_{-}U^{\dagger}_{-}}{k^{2}-\sigma_{-}+i\epsilon}\,,

(4.30)

where

\displaystyle U_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{\Lambda\sigma}}u_{+}\,,

\displaystyle U_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{(\Lambda+\gamma^{2}_{-})\sigma}}u_{-}\,.

(4.31)

The
U_{\pm}
factors defined above are analogous in the present context
to the normalization factors for plasmons propagating in a plasma
or fermions propagating in a thermal background
[
29
,
30
]
.
They in turn determine the properly normalized
transformation matrix between the
\rho,V_{L}
fields
and the fields of the eigenmodes. If we denote the latter by
\phi_{\pm}
, then

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\rho\\
V_{L}\end{array}\right)=U_{+}\phi_{+}+U_{-}\phi_{-}\,.

(4.32)

The thermal propagators given in Eq. (
4.24
)
can be expressed as

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{ab}(k)=\sum_{s}\Delta^{(s)}_{ab}(k)U_{s}U^{\dagger}_{s}\,,

(4.33)

in particular,

\Delta^{(\xi)}_{12}(k)=\sum_{s}\Delta^{(s)}_{12}(k)U_{s}U^{\dagger}_{s}\,,

(4.34)

and similarly for the (21) components, where the

\Delta^{(\pm)}_{12}(k)
and
\Delta^{(\pm)}_{21}(k)
propagators are given
explicitly in Eq. (
4.2
).
The spinors
U_{\pm}
that we have identified
above allow us to calculate the rate of processes such as

\phi_{+}\rightarrow\phi_{-}+\phi_{-}\,,

(4.35)

in a straightforward way once we know the vertices to use.
In turn, the thermal propagators allow us to calculate the thermal
corrections to such processes. The proper
vertices for such purposes are determined in Section
5.2
.

5
Imaginary part of the self-energy - Formulation of the problem

5.1
General formulas for
\pi_{i}

Denoting by
\pi^{(\pm)}_{r}
the real part of the self-energy of each
propagating mode, then from Eq. (
4.1
) they are simply

\pi^{(\pm)}_{r}=\sigma_{\pm}\,.

(5.1)

In general, including the loop corrections, the self-energy will develop
an imaginary part due to the interactions with the background.
Thus we write the total self-energy in the form

\pi^{(\pm)}_{eff}(k)=\pi^{(\pm)}_{r}(k)+i\pi^{(\pm)}_{i}(k)\,.

(5.2)

In the case of one scalar field, a convenient formula to determine
\pi_{i}
is

\pi_{i}(k)=\frac{i\pi_{12}(k)}{2n_{B}(x)}\,,

(5.3)

where
\pi_{12}(k)
is the 12 component of the thermal self-energy matrix.
The dispersion relation is the solution of

k^{2}-(\pi_{r}+i\pi_{i})=0\,.

(5.4)

Writing

\omega=\omega_{r}+i\omega_{i}\,,

(5.5)

to the lowest order

\omega_{i}(\kappa)=\frac{\pi_{i}(\omega_{r},\kappa)}{2\omega_{r}N}\equiv-\frac{\Gamma(\kappa)}{2}\,,

(5.6)

where
\omega_{r}
is the solution of

\omega^{2}_{r}-\pi(\omega_{r},\kappa)=0\,,

(5.7)

and

N=1-\frac{\pi^{\prime}_{r}}{2\omega_{r}}\,,

(5.8)

with

\pi^{\prime}_{r}\equiv\left.\frac{\partial\pi_{r}}{\partial\omega}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{r}}\,.

(5.9)

The quantity
\Gamma
defined in Eq. (
5.6
)
has the interpretation of the total decay rate;
the evolution amplitude of the propagating mode has the factor

e^{-i\omega t}=e^{-i\omega_{r}t}e^{-\frac{\Gamma}{2}t}\,.

(5.10)

and therefore the flux is damped by
e^{-\Gamma t}
.

In the spirit of the one-particle approximation,
similar formulas apply also to our case, for each propagating

(s=\pm)
mode. At this point it is useful to recall the fact
that
\sigma_{\pm}
, and whence
\pi^{(\pm)}_{r}
by Eq. (
5.1
), are independent
of
\omega
[Eq. (
3.24
)], and therefore the normalization factors
analogous to
N
above are 1. Thus we write their dispersion relation
in the form

\omega_{s}=\omega^{(s)}_{r}-i\frac{\Gamma_{s}}{2}\,,

(5.11)

with

\Gamma_{s}\equiv-\frac{\pi^{(s)}_{i}(\omega^{(s)}_{r},\kappa)}{\omega^{(s)}_{r}}\,.

(5.12)

The strategy now is to consider the cubic interaction terms
in the Lagrangian and write them in terms of the
\phi_{\pm}
fields
by means of Eq. (
4.32
). Then it becomes straighforward
to calculate the rate for processes such as

\phi_{+}\rightarrow\phi_{-}+\phi_{-}
, and related ones,
as well as the thermal corrections from the one-loop diagrams for
the imaginary part of the self-energy of each mode.

5.2
Vertices

The relevant interaction terms are obtained from Eq. (3.8) of Ref.
[
19
]
,

L_{\phi A}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial\rho)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(q\phi_{0})^{2}V^{2}+\frac{1}{2}q^{2}V^{2}\rho^{2}+q^{2}\phi_{0}\rho V^{2}-2q\mu(u\cdot V)(\phi_{0}+\rho)^{2}-U_{\phi}\,.

(5.13)

To write down the
V_{L}-\rho
interaction terms we must eliminate the
component
V_{k}
in the cubic and quartic terms, as we did it
for the bilinear terms, using Eq. (
3.15
).
Since that formula depends on
k
, it will produce vertices
that are momentum-dependent. We consider the relevant
interactions terms that follow from Eq. (
5.13
) one by one.
The terms that must be looked at are

L^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}q^{2}V^{2}\rho^{2}+q^{2}\phi_{0}\rho V^{2}-2q\mu(u\cdot V)\rho^{2}\,,

(5.14)

where

\displaystyle V^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{T}\cdot V_{T}-V^{2}_{L}-V^{2}_{k}\rightarrow V_{T}\cdot V_{T}-V^{2}_{L}-\frac{4\mu^{2}(k\cdot u)^{2}}{m^{2}_{V}k^{2}}\rho^{2}\,,

\displaystyle u\cdot V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{-\tilde{u}^{2}}V_{L}+\frac{(k\cdot u)V_{k}}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\rightarrow-\sqrt{-\tilde{u}^{2}}V_{L}+\frac{2\mu(k\cdot u)^{2}}{m_{V}k^{2}}\rho\,.

(5.15)

Therefore, the
V_{L}-\rho
interaction terms contained in Eq. (

)
are the following quartic and cubic terms,

\displaystyle L^{(4)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}q^{2}V^{2}_{L}\rho^{2}\,,

\displaystyle L^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-qm_{V}\rho V^{2}_{L}+2q\mu\sqrt{-\tilde{u}^{2}}V_{L}\rho^{2}\,,

(5.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-qm_{V}\rho V^{2}_{L}+q\gamma(k)V_{L}\rho^{2}\,,

where we have used

m_{V}=q\phi_{0}\,,

(5.17)

and defined

\gamma(k)=\frac{2\mu\kappa}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\,.

(5.18)

L^{(3)}

is

\phi_{+}\leftrightarrow\phi_{-}+\phi_{-}

V^{2}_{L}\rho^{2}
can contribute to the diagonal (
11
and
22
)

(or

L^{(3)}=-qm_{V}\rho V^{2}_{L}+q\gamma(k)V_{L}\rho^{2}\,,

\phi_{\pm}

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\rho\\

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\sum_{i}U_{2i}\phi_{i}\,,

\phi_{+}\phi^{2}_{-}

\displaystyle U_{2+}

\displaystyle U_{1-}

\displaystyle U_{2-}

(5.25)

Imaginary part of the self-energy - Calculation of
\pi^{(+)}_{i}

6.1
Expression for
\pi^{(+)}_{i}

From Fig.
2
,

-i(\pi^{(+)}_{12}(k))=(iA)(-iA^{\ast})\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}i\Delta^{(-)}_{21}(p)i\Delta^{(-)}_{12}(k+p)\,,

(6.1)

where
\Delta^{(-)}_{21}(q)
and
\Delta^{(-)}_{12}(q)
are given
in Eq. (
4.2
). Using the following identities

\displaystyle\eta_{B}(q)+\theta(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{x_{q}}n_{B}(x_{q})\epsilon(q)\,,

\displaystyle\eta_{B}(q)+\theta(-q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{B}(x_{q})\epsilon(q)\,,

(6.2)

where

\epsilon(q)=\theta(q)-\theta(-q)\,,

(6.3)

the propagators can be written in the form

\displaystyle\Delta^{(s)}_{21}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\delta(q^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}(q))e^{x_{q}}n_{B}(x_{q})\epsilon(q)\,,

\displaystyle\Delta^{(s)}_{12}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\delta(q^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}(q))n_{B}(x_{q})\epsilon(q)\,.

(6.4)

Then substituting Eq. (
6.1
) in Eq. (
6.1
), after
some straightforward algebra [see Appendix
A
] we arrive at

\displaystyle\pi^{(+)}_{i}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}|A|^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}P}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(P)}\frac{d^{3}Q}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(Q)}(2\pi)^{4}\times

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\Big\{\delta^{(4)}(k+p-q)(n_{-}(p)-n_{-}(q))

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\delta^{(4)}(k+q-p)(-n_{-}(p)+n_{-}(q))

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\delta^{(4)}(k+p+q)(n_{-}(p)+n_{-}(q)+1)

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\delta^{(4)}(k-p-q)(-n_{-}(p)-n_{-}(q)-1)\Big\}\,.

In this formula,

\displaystyle p^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega^{(-)}_{r}(P),\vec{P})\,,

\displaystyle q^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega^{(-)}_{r}(Q),\vec{Q})\,,

\displaystyle k^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega,\vec{\kappa})\,.

(6.6)

n_{s}(p)\equiv\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega^{(s)}_{r}(P)}-1}\,,

(6.7)

and similarly for
n_{s}(q)
with
P\rightarrow Q
.
The damping of the propagating
(+)
mode is then obtained by
Eq. (
5.12
).

This expression for
\pi^{(+)}_{i}
in Eq. (
6.5
) can be interpreted
in terms of the transition probablities for varios processes,

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(k)+\phi_{-}(p)

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\phi_{-}(q)\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(k)+\phi_{-}(q)

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\phi_{-}(p)\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(k)+\phi_{-}(q)+\phi_{-}(p)

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle 0\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(k)

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\phi_{-}(q)+\phi_{-}(p)\,.

(6.8)

Consider, for example, the first term. Using

n_{-}(p)-n_{-}(q)=n_{-}(p)(1+n_{-}(q))-n_{-}(q)(1+n_{-}(p))\,,

(6.9)

and remembering that the delta function fixes
k+p=q
,
the first term corresponds to the processes

\phi_{+}(k)+\phi_{-}(p)\longleftrightarrow\phi_{-}(q)\,.

(6.10)

Some processes are forbiden by energy conservation while others
supppresed by our specific assumptions about the background conditions.

The dominant process in the case we consider is the decay process

\phi_{+}(k)\rightarrow\phi_{-}(q)+\phi_{-}(p)
. For completeness
we also include its inverse, although it will be suppressed under the
background conditions that we assume. The corresponding contribution
to
\pi^{(+)}_{i}
is

\pi^{(+)}_{i}(k)=-\omega\left[\Gamma(\phi_{+}\rightarrow\phi_{-}\phi_{-})-\Gamma(\phi_{-}\phi_{-}\rightarrow\phi_{+})\right]\,,

(6.11)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma(\phi_{+}\rightarrow\phi_{-}\phi_{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega}|A|^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}P}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(P)}\frac{d^{3}Q}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(Q)}(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(k-p-q)(1+n_{-}(p))(1+n_{-}(q))\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\phi_{-}\phi_{-}\rightarrow\phi_{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega}|A|^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}P}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(P)}\frac{d^{3}Q}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(Q)}(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(k-p-q)n_{-}(p)n_{-}(q)\,.

(6.12)

In the limit that the
n_{-}
distribution is negligible, the
dominant contribution to
\pi^{(+)}_{i}
is due to the decay rate

\Gamma^{(0)}(\phi_{+}\rightarrow\phi_{-}\phi_{-})=\frac{1}{2\omega}|A|^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}P}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(P)}\frac{d^{3}Q}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega^{(-)}_{r}(Q)}(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(k-p-q)\,.

(6.13)

8
Conclusions and Outlook

The subject of the present work is the imaginary part of the dispersion
relations of the propagating modes in a model of a charged scalar
Bose-Einstein (BE) condensate.
In a previous work
[
19
]
we considered the real part of the dispersion
relations. In that model, two modes correspond to the transverse photon
polarizations, while the other two modes are combinations
of the longitudinal photon and the massive scalar field, which we denote
as the
(\pm)
modes. While the dispersion relations of the transverse modes
have the usual form for transverse photons in a plasma, the dispersion
relations of the (
\pm
) modes reveal some unique features. In our
previous work cited above, we determined and analyzed the real part of the
dispersion relations of the
(\pm)
modes, as well as the real part of
the contribution to the longitudinal component of polarization tensor
and the corresponding contribution to the dielectric constant. The focus
in the present work is the calculation, in the same model, of
the imaginary part of the dispersion relations of the (
\pm
) modes,
as well as the corresponding contributions to the imaginary part
of the longitudinal component of polarization tensor
and the dielectric constant.

The main result of the present work is an explicit formula for the
imaginary part of the dispersion relations,
as well as the corresponding contributions to the imaginary part
of the longitudinal component of polarization tensor
and the dielectric constant, obtained by the one-loop
calculations of the self-energy of the propagating (
\pm
) modes
in the context of TFT. To illustrate some features of the results
we considered in some detail the long wavelength limit, including
specifically the high and low frequency limits, and obtained explicit
simple explicit expressions for the damping rate as well as the
background contribution ot the longitudinal polarization
tensor and dielectric constant in those cases. This work and the results
obtained pave the way for further exploration of the
properties of the model, which can be useful for
aplications related to the optical properties of this kind
of system in various physical contexts, such as those mentioned
in the references already cited, and experiments related
to dark-matter searches using materials with special optical
properties
[
27
]
.
