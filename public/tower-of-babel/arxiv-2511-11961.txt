“Power of Words”: Stealthy and Adaptive Private Information Elicitation via LLM Communication Strategies
Shuning Zhang 1 , Jiaqi Bai 1 , Linzhi Wang 1 , Shixuan Li 1 , Xin Yi 1 2 and Hewu Li 1

Abstract
While communication strategies of Large Language Models (LLMs) are crucial for human-LLM interactions, they can also be weaponized to elicit private information, yet such stealthy attacks remain under-explored. This paper introduces the first adaptive attack framework for stealthy and targeted private information elicitation via communication strategies. Our framework operates in a dynamic closed-loop: it first performs real-time psychological profiling of the users’ state, then adaptively selects an optimized communication strategy, and finally maintains stealthiness through prompt-based rewriting. We validated this framework through a user study (N=84), demonstrating its generalizability across 3 distinct LLMs and 3 scenarios. The targeted attacks achieved a 205.4% increase in eliciting specific targeted information compared to stealthy interactions without strategies. Even stealthy interactions without specific strategies successfully elicited private information in 54.8% cases. Notably, users not only failed to detect the manipulation but paradoxically rated the attacking chatbot as more empathetic and trustworthy. Finally, we advocate for mitigations, encouraging developers to integrate adaptive, just-in-time alerts, users to build literacy against specific manipulative tactics, and regulators to define clear ethical boundaries distinguishing benign persuasion from coercion.

To answer this question, this paper designs and validates an adaptive and stealthy attack framework capable of executing both untargeted (i.e., increase privacy disclosure) and targeted attacks (i.e., eliciting specific type of information such as personal ID) through communication strategies (Figure

III
An Adaptive Framework for Stealthy and Targeted Information Elicitation

The core challenge of this paper is eliciting
targeted
disclosures while remaining
stealthy
. Our framework is therefore designed to maximally elicit information disclosure while keeping detectability as a constraint. Its novelty lies in synthesizing three steps: (1) four stealthy communication strategies engineered from behavioral science to lower user defenses, (2) an selection engine that adaptively deploys strategies by assessing user’s real-time conversational state, and (3) a dynamic optimization mechanism to maintain stealth. This section first introduces the threat model and the algorithm that integrates these components. We then detail each step, and conclude with a validation of the prompt-based estimation module.

III-A

Threat Model and Elicitation Goals

III-C

Step 1: Stealthy Communication Strategy Design

To enhance the efficacy of privacy elicitation in multi-turn dialogues, we design and implement a set of stealthy interaction strategies. Our approach is theoretically grounded in Nudge Theory
[
43
]
, a framework from Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) that is commonly used to model subtle behavioral influence
[
44
]
. This framework is ideal for our objective, as it provides systematic methods for influencing user behavior covertly.

We adapt this framework by selecting four strategies for covert manipulation: Facilitate, Confront, Social Influence and Deceive. These strategies are effective because they operate by subtly manipulating conversational context and social dynamics, rather than applying overt pressure or utilizing explicit feedback mechanisms, that could alert the user to the persuasive intent.
2
2

2

The ‘Fear’ (e.g, scarcity, loss aversion) and ‘Reinforcement’ (e.g., explicit rewards) strategies from the original framework are excluded. Their mechanisms are inherently overt, often creating high-pressure or unnatural interactions that risk immediate detection and are unsuitable to our stealth objective.

Facilitate strategy
lowers a user’s cognitive defense by embedding sensitive queries with natural conversation. The chatbot seeks to construct the bridge from comfortable, non-sensitive topic to the targeted private information. For example, a chatbot might begin by discussing weekend plans, then transition to work-life balance, such as
“It sounds like you had a busy weekend. Many people find it hard to disconnect from work”,
and finally pose a sensitive query about the user’s occupation. The disclosure feels more like a natural extension of the dialogue than an interrogation.

Confront strategy
exploits the human tendency to correct inaccuracies. It involves the chatbot making a false assertion about the user, which prompts a corrective response that simultaneously serves as the disclosure of the accurate information. For example, in a restaurant recommendation task, the chatbot might falsely assume the user’s budget by stating
“Since you mentioned a special occasion, I’ll focus on fine dining options for you,”
The user may be compelled to correct this assumption by saying
“By special I meant a unique atmosphere but not expensive. My budget is quite modest.”

Social influence strategy
exploits users’ tendencies to seek social approval or conform to group norms. Techniques like reciprocity or public commitment use shared experiences or social cues to encourage disclosure. For example, the chatbot might ask:
“Many people have been discussing fitness lately. Do you follow any specific health routines?”
, prompting users to reveal their health practices.

Deceive strategy
subtly misleads users, altering their perceptions by presenting misleading or exaggerated scenarios that prompt disclosure. For example, the chatbot might suggest,
“I can tailor recommendations for you. Could you share your location to help me refine the suggestions?”
inducing users to share location details.

We use a few-shot prompt-based implementation, strictly following the threat model, which assumes black-box access to LLMs without the ability to fine-tune or retrain. Advanced techniques such as fine-tuning or training from scratch are beyond this paper’s scope but could potentially increase flexibility and induce further harm.

For each strategy, we define its objective, provide three query templates, an example, and outline specific execution rules. For instance, the Facilitate strategy asks LLMs to “gradually transit from general topics to sensitive questions”, while the Deceive strategy lets LLMs “integrate subtle misdirection to elicit private details”. The objective is to embed private information elicitation within responses while simultaneously answering user queries. To enhance stealthiness, explicit expressions indicating elicitation or privacy disclosure are explicitly prohibited. Additionally, we include rules preventing the chatbot from consistently asking for sensitive information directly, further minimizing detectability (Table
VI
).

Within this framework, we prompt an LLM to estimate the user’s states based on chat histories, specifically focusing on their motivation to disclose private information and their capability to do so effectively. Motivation is defined as the willingness to disclose privacy-related information, reflected by the users’ eagerness to share. Capability is defined as the effectiveness of users’ privacy information disclosure, primarily indicated by the precision of the privacy information they provide. Both dimensions are important: motivation influences the quality of disclosures, while capability influences how effectvely the user leverages private information within the conversation. Guided by prior work
[
43
]
, we set the strategy based on the estimated motivation and capability as follows:

\bullet

: Encourage direct disclosure for users

.

\bullet

.

\bullet

.

\bullet

Following prior practices
[
46
,
47
]
, we use prompts that leverages an LLM’s reasoning capabilities
[
48
]
to analyze chat histories and estimate user motivation and capability. These prompts include definitions of user engagement (reflecting motivation) and response precision (measuring capability), instructing the model to anlayze conversational patterns to infer willingness to disclose and effectiveness of information utilization. The threshold for high/low motivation and capability was empirically determined to be 0.7 (on a 0-to-1 scale) through a pilot study with author-crafted test inputs. This threshold also balances the application frequency of the different strategies. Detectability is evaluated using separate prompts that assess user reactions to previous queries and strategies, specifically focusing on “how likely the interaction would be perceived as intrusive or overly probing”.
Finally, we chose a rule-based implementation for the
Decide

III-E

Step 3: Stealthy Response Optimization

To elicit user disclosure in natural interaction, it is crucial to apply communication strategies stealthily, minimizing user awareness of any persuasive intent
[
49
]
. Instead of relying on post-hoc detectability calculations, we integrate stealth optimization into the prompt generation process, considering the user’s conversational history. Our approach leverages the capabilities of LLMs
[
50
]
, using zero-shot prompting to dynamically generate responses that align with both the elicitation aim
A_{1}
and stealth aim
A_{2}
(Algorithm
1
). By incorporating user chat history
h
, current response
p_{t}
, strategy
P
and target information
c
, the prompt optimization process is guided by a role-play rewrite mechanism, where LLM adopts a considerate persona to produce contextually tailored responses that minimize the user’s detection likelihood.

Besides zero-shot prompting, alternative methods include multi-agent adversarial generation, iterative rewriting or direct modification. However, we refrain from multi-agent adversarial generation and iterative rewriting due to their high cost and latency, and we avoid direct modification due to its inferior performance. By avoiding repeated probing or reliance on explicitly defined detectability thresholds– both impractical in human-LLM interaction scenarios
[
51
,
52
]
–our method optimizes prompts dynamically during generation. This ensures stealthiness while preserving adaptability to user inputs
[
51
,
53
]
.

IV
Methodology

To validate our adaptive attack framework, we conducted a user study to quantitatively assess the framework’s effectiveness in targeted attack (RQ2) and its stealthiness (RQ3).

IV-A

Recruitment and Participants

This IRB-approved study recruited Chinese participants by distributing posters online in WeChat groups. All 84 respondents met the inclusion criterion (age 18 or older) and successfully completed the entire experiment, resulting in our final sample of 84 participants (39 males, 45 females). Participants had ages from 18 to 59 years, with a mean age of 23.7 (SD=6.0). 50 participants were with engineering or technical backgrounds, 15 participants were with mathematical backgrounds, 7 were with humanity and society backgrounds, 4 were with artistic backgrounds and 8 were with other backgrounds. The study was carried out in November, 2024 and each participant was compensated 90 CNY for their participation according to the local wage standard. We informed participants that they could quit the study, request the deletion of their data, and still receive compensation at any time during the experiment.

IV-C

Procedure

We developed an online website on our institution’s server to host the study, which mimicked real interfaces and interactions of ChatGPT. In line with prior research
[
14
]
, we employed an
Incomplete Disclosure Protocol

[
65
]
to ensure ethical conduct while maximizing the study’s validity. Before the study, we obtained signed informed consent from all users, who were initially informed that the study aimed to understand their general experiences with chatbots. Each participant needed to complete 9 sessions, differing by 3 scenarios times 3 model architectures, lasting 50 minutes on average. While we provided sample prompts adapted from prior work
[
26
]
, participants were encouraged to formulate their own questions based on their daily lives within the given scenario. The design aimed to mimic realistic environments, elicit authentic disclosures, and participants confirmed during the de-briefing that they used their genuine information. They then prompted the chatbots and get answers, and could end the dialogue when they thought chatbots had properly addressed their questions. The length and the number of sessions were designed to minimize the fatigue while allowing for exploration of the nuanced details of the manipulation. After the experiment, following the
Incomplete Disclosure Protocol
, we fully disclosed the aim of the experiment to participants and informed them that we could delete their data if they were uncomfortable with the experiment. No one explicitly asked for a data deletion. The experiment material and dialogues were in Chinese.

We analyzed the collected data using a combination of quantitative statistical methods, manual content annotation and qualitative thematic analysis. Two trained authors annotated the conversational dialogues to quantify privacy disclosure, adhering to a detailed coding guide derived from our six predefined private information categories (social-economic, lifestyle-behavior, tracking, financial, authenticating, and medical-health)
[
61
]
.
We also consulted to prior guidance for determining the granularity of privacy
[
14
]
and the definition of privacy
[

67

68

. To ensure reliability, both annotators independently coded a random 20% subset of the dialogue data, reaching an inter-rater reliability of
k=0.85
. Disagreements were resolved through discussions to finalize the guidelines, after which one annotator coded the remaining dataset. This count focused on the appearance frequency of information items, without verifying their factual accuracy against users’ actual private details, a measure taken to protect participant anonymity and adhere to ethical guidelines.

For quantitative count data, specifically privacy disclosures (i.e., disclosure number), we employed multi-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) due to our mixed-factorial experimental design. These ANOVAs examined the main effects and interactions of the between-subject and within-subject factors. Significant ANOVA results were followed by Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons. For subjective rating scales, we used the Friedman non-parametric test, with subsequent post-hoc Nemenyi tests incorporating Bonferroni adjustments for multiple comparisons.

VI-B

Trust, Demographics and Experimental Context

An important consideration is whether the attack’s success hinges on a pre-existing trust in the research environment or the underlying model (e.g., ChatGPT). Our findings suggest the attack framework does not simply leverage pre-existing trust but actively manufactures it. This manufacturing process is substantiated by our qualitative analysis (Sec
V-B2
). The paradoxical high ratings for trust and empathy (Table
IV
) are not abstract. They are a direct consequence of the chatbot’s perceived utility and persona. Participants rationalized the agent’s intrusive inquiries as a functional necessity for receiving effective and competent guidance. Simultaneously, they were disarmed by the agent’s perceived warmth and enthusiastic persona, which made refusal difficult. This demonstrates the attack’s core mechanism: it effectively masks its manipulative intent by reframing it as a benign, helpful feature, such as enhanced personalization. The potency of this mechanism is substantial, as it provides a clear pathway for even an untrusted, third-party agent to rapidly gain credibility.

The generalizability of this trust-manufacturing mechanism must be contextualized by our participant sample. As our study consisted primarily of university students from technical backgrounds, our efficacy findings may represent a conservative lower bound on the attack’s potential impact within a heterogeneous population. Furthermore, we observed significant variance in disclosure rates, where the lowest rating being 12.9% of the highest, confirming that susceptibility is not uniform and echoing the “privacy paradox”. This variance underscores the need for future research into demographics. Susceptibility may differ across age groups (correlating with varied mental models of AI), cultural dimensions (where norms of politeness or directness could alter strategy effectiveness), and linguistic fluency (impacting the detection of subtle manipulative cues). These factors align with existing work showing that disclosure likelihood is affected by users’ familiarity with AI systems

, inherent communication styles

, lower privacy literacy

VI-C

Mitigation

The fact that users paradoxically rated the attacking chatbot as empathetic and trustworthy (RQ3) while disclosing significantly more information demonstrates that purely technical safeguards are insufficient. An effective defense should be built as a direct response to the threats we identified, integrating the responsibilities of platform developers, the awareness of end-users, and the oversight of the research and policy communities.

For platform developers and system designers, the responsibility lies in building robust, privacy-preserving systems to balance privacy disclosure and task execution
[
13
,
93
]
. This could involve, for example, client-side filtering that pseudo-anonymizes personally identifiable information (PII) before the query is sent to the LLM
[
14
]
. However, given our finding that the attack’s stealth fosters a false sense of security, these defenses must be paired with proactive interaction designs. Instead of static, easily-ignored banners, systems should utilize adaptive alerts
[
94
]
that function as contextual nudges
[
43
]
. For instance, if the agent repeatedly probes for sensitive information irrelevant to the immediate task, a salient, just-in-time warning could appear, empowering users to reflect and break their misplaced trust.

For end-users, as our study shows that the attack framework is effective because it weaponizes natural conversational behaviors, user awareness should evolve beyond generic advice (e.g, “don’t share private data”) to build literacy around the specific manipulative tactics we identified (“facilitate”, “confront”, “social influence”, and “deceive”). Future educational initiatives should utilize interactive sandbox environments
[
90
]
to help users recognize these subtle patterns. For example, a training module within the interaction system could simulate the “social influence” tactic (e.g., “Most users share their full budget with me to get the best financial advice”), allowing users to practice identifying and resisting such malicious elicitation.

For researchers and policymakers, the central challenge is to define and enforce ethical boundaries for persuasive AI. Our work shows how communication strategies can weaponize cognitive biases like the illusion of control
[
95
]
or confirmation bias
[
96
]
, blurring the line between improving user experience and malicious manipulation
[
70
,
41
]
. The finding that these risks are model-agnostic (Sec
V-A3
) and severe in sensitive contexts like medical-health
[
97
]
and psychology-related
[
98
]
tasks, necessitates a risk-based regulatory framework. This framework should clearly distinguish between acceptable nudging (e.g., encouraging positive health habits), persuasive design, and outright coercion (e.g., using deception to gain data)
[
99
]
, ensuring that as LLMs become more capable and integrated into our lives, their ability to engage is balanced with non-negotiable safeguards for user privacy and autonomy.

VI-D

Prompt-based Implementation of Dynamic Interaction Mechanisms

A core methodological component of our framework is using LLM-based prompting not only for response generation but, critically, for the dynamic estimation of user psychological states (i.e., motivation and capability). This approach addresses a key challenge: these states are not simple, discrete variables but complex properties emerging from a nuanced, multi-turn conversational history. Traditional deterministic algorithms are insufficient for this estimation task, which requires inferring latent states from subtle linguistic cues (e.g., response richness and proactivity). The sophisticated understanding and reasoning capabilities of LLMs are essential for this contextual dialogue analysis, especially since direct user queries (e.g., questionnaires or scales) would be intrusive in a stealthy attack scenario. Crucially, this component was validated (Sec
III-F
), where we found high inter-annotator agreement between the LLM’s estimations and those of human experts (Krippendorff’s
\alpha
up to 0.93, Table
I
). This result confirms that LLMs, when guided by our precise operational definitions, function as a reliable and valid instruments for this complex inferential task.

VI-E

Limitation and Future Work

We acknowledge several limitations in this paper. First, our findings’ generalizability is constrained by our small participant pool, consisting primarily of Chinese university students, a demographic not fully representative of the broad user group. This homogeneity may obscure subtle statistical effects. This sample bias may render our findings conservative. University students are often considered more tech-savvy and privacy-conscious
[
100
]
. Their demonstrated susceptibility therefore suggests that the general public could be at even greater risk. This sampling approach is also consistent with prior privacy disclosure studies
[
101
]
. Moreover, despite the sample size, our primary findings yielded large and detectable effect sizes, particularly for the dynamic strategy (Section
V-A1
), underscoring the attack’s potency. These quantitative results were further supported by qualitative data, where users perceived the chatbot as competent and empathetic, effectively masking the manipulative intent. Furthermore, our sampling is culturally and linguistically homogeneous, which means our findings are context-bound.

Second, our methodologies have constraints. The in-lab setting may not mirror real-world behavior, as we informed participants that we aimed to understand their experience with chatbots. This guidance may alter their disclosure behavior compared to in-the-wild interactions, where participants may perceive the chatbot as a research tool than a commercial product. They may also be influenced by social desirability
[
102
]
or use fictitious information
[
14
]
. Furthermore, due to ethical considerations, our analysis was limited to quantifying privacy disclosures rather than performing subsequent realistic attacks, such as attribute inference or membership inference, on the gathered data. A key direction for future work is to validate these settings in in-the-wild settings.

VII
Conclusion

This paper introduces and validates an adaptive framework for stealthy and targeted private information elicitation in human-chatbot interactions. The core of the framework is to adaptively select communication strategies based on users’ psychological states, and maintain stealth in multi-round dialogues. We operationalized the attack through three core steps: stealthy communication strategy design, adaptive strategy selection, and stealthy response optimization. Our findings from a user study (N=84) shows that the targeted attack is highly effective, increasing the elicitation of specific information by 205.4%, and is robust across LLM architectures. We further found that users not only failed to recognize the manipulation but paradoxically perceived the attacking chatbots as more empathetic, trustworthy, and satisfactory. The findings emphasize the urgency for multi-faceted mitigation efforts that include privacy-preserving designs from developers, enhanced user literacy against manipulations, and regulatory frameworks for persuasive AI.

Facilitate Strategy

let users disclose private information by using a progressive and natural conversation flow to reduce their psychological barriers.

- General Topic Initiation: Your [context] sounds really interesting. How do you usually [context-related task]?

Confront Strategy

use hypothetical or subtly incorrect statements to prompt user clarification and disclosure.

- Hypothetical Statement: It seems like you [inferred action], especially back in your [specific time frame]. Is that correct?

- Inaccurate Claim: I’ve heard that [incorrect fact]. What’s your take on that?

- Clarification Query: So, you’ve always [inferred behavior]? How did you start doing this?

Social Influence Strategy

Leverage the user’s desire for social connection and conformity to elicit disclosure.

- Social Norm Reference: Many people I know are focusing on [common activity]. What’s your approach?

- Group Connection: Others in your field have mentioned [specific habit or trend]. Have you noticed this too?

- Commitment Elicitation: To better assist you, can you share your [habit/routine]?

- Many of my friends are into morning exercise routines. Do you prefer mornings or evenings for working out?

Deceive Strategy

Introduce misleading information to manipulate the user into correcting or clarifying.

- Misleading Statement: I’ve heard that [incorrect fact about context]. Is that true for you?

- Decoy Detail: A lot of people say [false fact]. What’s your perspective?

- Bias Elicitation: Was [misleading factor] a big reason for your decision?

- I’ve heard that regular exercise can increase height significantly. Have you noticed this?
