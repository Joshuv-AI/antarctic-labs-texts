Stromal ARHGEF15 Correlates With Inflammatory EMT and Stromal-Immune Crosstalk During Inflammatory Bowel Disease-To-Colorectal Cancer Progression
Authors: GuoliangLi, Xiangxiang Hao, Guoshuai Chen, Gaowen Huang, Yongshuai Wu, Kewei Jiang, Hongpeng Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) have an increased risk of colorectal cancer (CRC), but how chronic intestinal inflammation drives malignant transformation remains unclear. We retrospectively reanalyzed published single-cell transcriptomic datasets from intestinal biopsies of healthy individuals and patients with IBD; differential expression was assessed using independent t tests with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. We then integrated those single-cell findings with the Cancer Genome Atlas bulk transcriptomes and pharmacogenomic cohorts to trace stromal programs across the IBD-to-cancer continuum. ARHGEF15 emerged as a stromal gene enriched in CD74hi HLA-DRB1hi arterial pericytes within inflamed tissue. Its expression rose steadily from IBD to CRC and tracked with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) activity. In CRC, higher ARHGEF15 expression was associated with shorter overall and progression-free survival. These retrospective, in silico findings identify ARHGEF15 as an exploratory stromal biomarker associated with inflammatory EMT and stromal–immune remodeling during IBD-to-CRC progression. Prospective experimental and clinical validation is required to establish its prognostic or therapeutic relevance.Keywords: ARHGEF15, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, tumor microenvironment, immune infiltration, stromal remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, cancer-associated fibroblasts, pericytes, prognostic biomarker

1. Introduction

1.1. Background

IBD, including Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic, relapsing, and disabling inflammatory gastrointestinal tract disease, without curative treatment currently available.1,2 The pathogenesis and pathophysiological mechanisms of IBD are highly complex and multifactorial, involving factors related to genetics, gut microbiota, immunity, and the environment.3,4 Due to chronic inflammation and continuous oxidative stress promoting malignant transformation of normal mucosa, IBD patients have higher prevalence in CRC than general population, with increasing risk with disease duration.5,6 The cumulative incidence is approximately 5-8%, and exceeds 15% after 30 years. 7 While IBD and CRC have historically been regarded as distinct disorders, accumulating evidence strongly supports the existence of chronic inflammation-driven mechanistic continuity between the two diseases.8,9 Nevertheless, such pathological continuity does not negate the uniqueness of IBD-associated colorectal cancer as an independent disease subtype, which differs fundamentally from sporadic CRC in molecular pathways, clinical characteristics and therapeutic responsiveness.10,11 Thus, in-depth researches of the molecular mechanisms enabling illustrate the continuum from IBD to CRC are needed.

1.2. Knowledge Gaps

Recently published studies provided a large single-cell transcriptomic dataset, characterizing intestinal immune cell dynamics and therapeutic response.12,13 Whereas this public dataset also contains abundant underexplored information regarding stromal and perivascular cells. Notably, these stromal cellular features have not been comprehensively dissected, nor linked to inflammation-triggered EMT, multi-omic alterations and clinical traits during IBD-CRC carcinogenesis. The EMT-related markers evaluated in this study and the supporting literature for their selection are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomic technological revealed that non-epithelial stromal components play vital roles in chronic intestinal inflammation modulation and tumor generation.14,15 Fibroblasts, pericytes, and endothelial-related stomal cells contribute to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, immune cells recruitment, and vascular regeneration.16-19 These microenvironment features can be investigated via the high-resolution single-cell transcriptomic technology, thereby we are able to discover the dynamic microenvironmental modulation mechanism from IBD to CRC.

1.3. Study Objectives

To address these unresolved research gaps, we will perform a comprehensive re-analysis of this authoritative single-cell dataset, with a specific focus on chronic inflammation-associated stromal and perivascular cell compartments. By integrating this single-cell resource with multi-omics datasets encompassing transcriptional expression profiles, somatic mutation landscapes, immune infiltration patterns, and clinical prognostic information, we aim to screen and characterize key inflammation-related regulatory molecules that modulate EMT signaling and mediate the pathological continuum from progressive IBD lesions to colorectal malignant transformation, with ARHGEF15 as our core candidate gene for further mechanistic exploration.

2. Method and Materials

2.1. Selection of Public Single-Cell Datasets

This study was based on the single-cell transcriptomic dataset GSE282122, originally published in Nature Immunology and accessed via the OmniBrowser platform. The dataset comprises a longitudinal collection of human ileal and colonic tissue samples from healthy controls, UC patients, and CD patients, including paired samples collected before and after adalimumab treatment. For cross-cohort validation of epithelial transformation signatures driving IBD-to-CRC progression, we integrated a subset of unified single-cell profiles from the multi-tissue gut cell atlas published by Oliver et al (2024). 20 The dataset sources and their corresponding references are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. All raw sequencing data for this atlas were deposited across standard omics repositories as described in their data availability section, and preprocessed integrated single-cell objects are publicly available at https://gutcellatlas.org/pe’m’tangi.html. This comprehensive design offers a unique resource to assess the therapeutic effects of anti-TNF therapy in IBD. To ensure high data quality, raw sequencing reads were processed through an in-house bioinformatics pipeline. Initial quality control steps included the removal of low-quality reads and adapter sequences, followed by an assessment of sequencing depth and quality for each sample. For cell-level quality filtering, we excluded cells based on the number of genes detected, total unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, and the percentage of mitochondrial gene expression, thereby retaining a high-quality cell population for downstream analysis. To mitigate potential batch effects arising from different patients, tissue sites, and treatment time points, we integrated all samples using the Harmony algorithm. This approach effectively corrected for technical variations while preserving the underlying biological heterogeneity, ensuring robust and comparable analyses across the entire dataset and providing a solid foundation for subsequent biological investigations.

2.2. Single-Cell Transcriptomic Data Analysis

2.2.1. OmniBrowser Data Analysis

Our analysis was conducted on OmniBrowser (https://omnibrowser.abiosciences.cn/), an interactive platform designed for large-scale multi-omics single-cell data analysis and visualization. We queried IBD-related studies and utilized the integrated analysis modules for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis, cell type composition analysis, volcano plot visualization, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). All results were visualized directly on the platform.

2.2.2. Cell Clustering and Annotation

Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by cell clustering via the Louvain algorithm. Cluster identities were annotated based on canonical marker genes (e.g., CD8A, CD79A, MS4A1, LYZ) and visualized through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).We further stratified and annotated cells of the primary dataset via the OmniBrowser platform. After accessing the target dataset by searching its study title, two rounds of regrouping were implemented on the Dimensional Reduction panel. We first filtered cells by Organ Groups to preserve only small intestinal and colonic cells, labelling non-intestinal populations as undefined and discarding them from downstream work. Cells were then re-grouped by Disease status into control, crohns_disease, ulcerative_colitis and cancer_colorectal subgroups, with residual unclassified cells marked undefined. We manually revised subgroup labels to complete cell annotation for validation analyses.

2.2.3. Data Processing and Differential Expression Analysis

Discovery cohort: Cell Ranger v3.1.0 was used to generate feature-barcode matrices. High-quality cells were retained after removing doublets (Scrublet), cells expressing <500 genes, and cells with >60% mitochondrial reads (with subsequent confirmation that mitochondrial content was balanced across groups). Genes detected in <3 cells were excluded. For dimensional reduction and clustering, UMI counts were normalized, log-transformed, and scaled. BBKNN followed by Harmony were applied to correct batch effects for PCA and Leiden clustering (resolution adjusted per subpopulation). For differential expression analysis between inflamed and non-inflamed/non-IBD conditions, raw UMI counts were aggregated at the sample-level to generate pseudobulk profiles. Differential testing was performed using DESeq2 with the design ∼ Donor + Condition to rigorously control for inter-donor biological variability. Genes with an adjusted P ≤ 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 were considered significant. For differential abundance testing of cell subpopulations, we employed MASC with a nested random effects structure (1| Donor/Sample) to account for repeated measures, adjusting for covariates including age, sex, treatment, site, and mitochondrial percentage.

Validation cohort: Datasets were chosen from a literature search of IBD and CRC scRNA-seq studies. Available metadata from each sample were collated from various data repositories and harmonized for unified nomenclature. Metadata related to sample retrieval methods, tissue processing and cell enrichment methods were retrieved from the methods section of the original study. Each sample were run through quality control metrics, pooled together, further filtered using Scrublet, and then integrated. BBKNN and Harmony were used for sample batch correction. Leiden clustering was applied to derive broad cell populations. To annotate at fine resolution, we predicted broad lineages in the projected disease data using a label transfer method based on majority voting from k-nearest neighbour (kNN). To validate our findings, we harmonized public datasets and performed differential expression exclusively via pseudobulking, aggregating raw counts per donor-cell-type combination using Decoupler, followed by DESeq2 21 with Study included as a covariate to mitigate cross-dataset batch effects. Log2FC shrinkage was applied using the ashr estimator. Wilcoxon tests were only used for preliminary exploratory comparisons in the downsampled visualization (Scanpy) and were not used for formal hypothesis testing.

2.2.4. Boxplot Visualization

To systematically assess the expression distribution of target genes across various groups, boxplots were generated based on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. Expression levels of target genes were extracted and grouped according to pericyte and fibroblast subpopulations.

2.2.5. Volcano Plot Visualization

To characterize perivascular-specific expression profiles, differential expression analysis was performed between pericyte and other fibroblast subsets using t-tests. The thresholds were set to |log2FC| > 5 and FDR < 0.05. Volcano plots were generated with log2FC on the x-axis and −lg (FDR) on the y-axis, with each dot representing a single gene. Significantly upregulated genes were shown in red, downregulated in green, and nonsignificant in gray. The distribution of ARHGEF15 was highlighted in the plot.

2.2.6. PAGA Trajectory Analysis

To infer the developmental relationships and differentiation trajectories among cell populations, we employed the Partition-based Graph Abstraction (PAGA) algorithm, a graph-based trajectory inference method. The connectivity between cell clusters was calculated, and only strong connections (min_connectivity > 0.5) were retained to filter out low-confidence topological relationships. In the PAGA graph, edge thickness was proportional to connectivity strength, intuitively reflecting the robustness of differentiation trajectories among cell populations.

2.2.7. Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis

DEGs identified from each cell cluster were subjected to GO enrichment analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories. Enrichment results were visualized as bar plots to illustrate the biological functions associated with the differential expression patterns.

2.2.8. Visualization of Cellular Composition

Using the metadata function in OmniBrowser, cellular composition was analyzed across multiple clinical and inflammatory states. Bar plots were generated to depict differences in the proportions of cell types between remission and non-remission, as well as inflamed and non-inflamed conditions, providing a visual overview of cellular heterogeneity under various disease states.

2.2.9. Mutation Profiling and Clinical Correlation Analysis

The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSCA, https://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/GSCA) platform is an integrated platform for genomic, pharmacogenomic, and immunogenomic gene set cancer analysis. We used the GSCA platform to analyze the mutation frequency and types (e.g., missense, nonsense, frameshift) of the target gene, as well as its Copy Number Variations (CNVs), in COAD and READ. Furthermore, we visualized the gene’s expression profile according to clinical TNM stages using heatmaps and line plots to illustrate its expression trends in tumor progression.

2.2.10. Immune Microenvironment Analysis

Immune cell infiltration level was quantified with the association of single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis(ssGSEA) algorithm based on gene expression profiles from COAD or READ samples on the GSCA platform, covering 28 immune cell types. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations between target gene expression and immune cell infiltration levels. Correlations with |Rho| > 0.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

2.3. Construction and Validation of a Prognostic Model

2.3.1. Survival Analysis

The prognostic impact of ARHGEF15 expression, mutation status, and methylation level on OS and PFS was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis implemented in both the GSCA and UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) platforms. Statistical significance was determined using the log-rank test, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

2.3.2. Construction of Cox Proportional Hazards Model

With the association of the TIMER2.0 platform (https://timer.cistrome.org/), we constructed a Cox proportional hazards model which included variables like target gene expression quartiles (dichotomized by the median), immune cell infiltration levels (including CD4+ T cells, plasma cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, monocytes, NK cells, etc.), and clinical stage of colorectal cancer (Stage I-IV).

2.3.3. Drug Sensitivity Prediction

Drug response data were obtained from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp/) and the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) databases. Pearson correlation analysis was used to identify drugs significantly correlated with ARHGEF15 expression (|R| > 0.1, FDR < 0.05), focusing on clinically approved antitumor agents.

2.4. Statistical Analysis

For all bioinformatic analyses, t-tests or one-way ANOVA were applied depending on data distribution. Log-rank tests were used for survival analyses. Multiple hypothesis testing corrections were performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and p < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

3.1. ARHGEF15 Is Enriched in Inflammation-Associated Perivascular Fibroblasts in IBD

Quantitative comparison across disease states showed a marked expansion of CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericytes in inflamed tissues and in non-remission samples compared with non-inflamed and remission states (Figure 2B). Enrichment analysis of DEGs in CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericytes demonstrated significant over-representation of biological processes involved in angiogenesis, cell adhesion, migration, and vascular regulation (Figure 2C). We then identified ARHGEF15 among the most significantly upregulated genes within CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericytes subset (Figure 2D), implicating Rho GTPase-related cytoskeletal signaling in inflammation-associated stromal activation. PAGA analysis revealed robust developmental connectivity among fibroblast and pericyte subsets, indicating dynamic stromal remodeling rather than isolated lineage expansion during inflammation (Figure 2E).

To further explore the expression profile of ARHGEF15, we examined its distribution on the PAGA graph, where ARHGEF15 expression was selectively enriched in the CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericyte cluster (Figure 2F). Box plot quantification across all stromal subsets showed that ARHGEF15 levels were markedly higher in CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericytes than in other clusters (Figure 2G). Consistently, UMAP visualization revealed that ARHGEF15 expression was largely restricted to this subpopulation (Figure 2H). Together, these results confirm that ARHGEF15 is specifically expressed in CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericytes and suggest that ARHGEF15 may play an important role in IBD-associated inflammation and non-remission.

3.2. ARHGEF15 Shows Progressive Upregulation Along Colorectal Cancer Progression and Associates With EMT Activity

We characterized ARHGEF15 cell-type expression patterns on the basis of our original single-cell dataset (Figure 3C). ARHGEF15 was mainly enriched in stromal fibroblasts and endothelial cells, with minimal expression in immune cells. These patterns were consistent with tissue-level expression profiles derived from Human Protein Atlas data, and we found ARHGEF15 was mainly expressed in adipocytes and endothelial cells but not in immune cells(Figure 3D).Analysis of bulk transcriptomic data demonstrated a gradual increase in ARHGEF15 expression across pathological stages of colorectal cancer in both COAD and READ (Figure 3E), suggesting a stage-dependent involvement in tumor evolution. Pathway activity analysis showed that tumors with high ARHGEF15 expression exhibited significantly increased EMT activity in both COAD and READ (Figure 3F,G). In contrast, cell cycle related pathway activity was significantly reduced in ARHGEF15-high tumors in COAD (Figure 3H), indicating site-specific differences in downstream functional programs associated with ARHGEF15 expression.

3.3. Distinct Mutation Landscapes and Copy-Number Characteristics of ARHGEF15 in COAD and READ

Through integrating multi-omics public datasets, we systematically characterized the molecular regulatory features of ARHGEF15 during the transition from IBD to CRC. Genomic variation analysis revealed a significant tissue-specific discrepancy: samples with ARHGEF15 mutations in COAD ranked fourth among pan-cancer, whereas almost no significant mutations were detected in READ. Specifically, ARHGEF15 exhibited a mutation frequency of 4.42% in COAD, dominated by missense mutations, compared to only 1.34% in READ, indicating that ARHGEF15 may operate through distinct regulatory mechanisms across CRC (Figure 5A). Restricting our analysis to single-nucleotide variants alone, the heatmap demonstrates ARHGEF15 SNVs occur more frequently in COAD (18/407) than READ (2/149) (Figure 5B). Detailed mutation classification demonstrated that missense mutations represented the predominant mutation type in COAD samples, while READ showed only sporadic alterations (Figure 5C–F).

Copy number variation analysis indicated that heterozygous deletions constituted the main CNV pattern of ARHGEF15 in COAD, whereas CNV events were infrequent in READ (Figure 5G and H). Correlation analyses revealed weak associations between ARHGEF15 CNV status and mRNA expression levels in both tumor types (Figure 5I and J), suggesting that CNVs do not serve as the primary determinant of ARHGEF15 transcriptional upregulation.

3.4. ARHGEF15 Is Associated With Tissue-specific Immune Infiltration Patterns in CRC

In our analysis, systematic analysis of the TME in COAD and READ using the GSCA and TIMER2.0 databases revealed a unique regulatory pattern of ARHGEF15 in immune cell infiltration.

Comparative immune infiltration analysis demonstrated that COAD tumors harboring ARHGEF15 mutations exhibited significantly increased infiltration. Compared with the wild-type group, the mutant group exhibited a 2.1-fold increase in the infiltration of CD8+ central memory T cells (TCM) (p=0.0012) and a 4.7-fold expansion of NK cells (p=0.028). Furthermore, the activated NK cell subset was significantly elevated by 1.5-fold (p=0.00031), whereas plasma cells showed a 76% reduction (p=0.00074). (Figure 6A–E). This pro-inflammatory immune feature aligns with the cellular stress response potentially induced by ARHGEF15 mutations. Notably, in COAD, ARHGEF15 expression levels exhibit a robust positive correlation with the infiltration of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) across multiple immune deconvolution algorithms, including EPIC, MCPCOUNTER, XCELL, and TIDE (Figure 6F–I), highlighting a close association between ARHGEF15 expression and stromal activation.

These findings indicate that ARHGEF15 is linked to distinct immune-stromal remodeling programs depending on colorectal tumor subsite.

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses revealed that high ARHGEF15 expression was significantly associated with worse OS and PFS in both COAD and READ cohorts (Figure 7A–D).

Integrated survival analyses incorporating immune infiltration further showed that ARHGEF15-based risk stratification was modulated by immune context. In COAD, low ARHGEF15 expression combined with high CD4+ memory T cell infiltration was associated with improved survival, whereas high ARHGEF15 expression coupled with elevated NK cell infiltration predicted poorer outcomes (Figure 7E and F). ARHGEF15 mutation status displayed a stronger association with NK cell infiltration than expression alone (Figure 7G and H).

Finally, exploratory drug sensitivity analyses using GDSC and CTRP datasets demonstrated that ARHGEF15 expression was negatively correlated with sensitivity to most targeted agents but showed significant associations with increased sensitivity to DNA damage repair related drugs, including Poly (ADP-ribose) Polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) (Figure 7I and J), indicating a potential therapeutic vulnerability in ARHGEF15-high tumors. These in silico pharmacogenomic findings suggest a preliminary and correlative association between ARHGEF15 expression and PARPi sensitivity. Crucially, no mechanistic link to DNA repair deficiency has been established in the present study. Therefore, this therapeutic correlation must be interpreted strictly as a secondary exploratory observation that requires extensive prospective wet-lab validation (e.g., in vitro efficacy assays in ARHGEF15-overexpressing systems) before any clinical implications can be proposed.

4. Discussion

ARHGEF15 has been reported to possess Rho-specific GEF activity. ARHGEF15 deletion leads to delayed retinal vascular network extension, resulting in a reduction in total vascular area. 22 Meanwhile, overexpression of ARHGEF15 in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is associated with enhanced growth and motility of pancreatic cancer cells, leading to a significantly worse patient prognosis. 23 Recently, more studies have revealed that the Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor ARHGEF15 act its function in multiple diseases like cerebral small vessel disease, the disease Alzheimer, and tumors, yet its involvement in the IBD-CRC continuum has not been investigated.23-25

We found that ARHGEF15 exhibits a continuous upregulation trend across IBD inflammatory states, precancerous lesions, and CRC, indicating that ARHGEF15 isn’t only a marker of CRC but also serves as a bridging molecule in the inflammation-driven malignant transformation process from IBD to CRC.

During the IBD stage, single-cell analysis clearly identified ARHGEF15 enrichment in CD74hi HLADRB1hi arterial pericytes, which are recognized as critical nodes for antigen presentation, vascular remodeling,26,27 and immune cell recruitment in chronic inflammation. CD74 can interact with macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) expressed by macrophages that possesses pro-inflammatory properties like enhancing macrophage phagocytosis, and promoting TNF secretion.28,29 HLA-DRB1 is associated with endothelial dysfunction in auto-immunity disease.30,31 These high expression of the two genes in arterial pericytes likely associated with the pro-inflammatory features in IBD patients. GO enrichment results showed that these cells are highly involved in adhesion, migration, and vascular regulation.

An ARHGEF15-high stromal state may promote ECM reconstitution and tissue sclerosis, enhanced epithelial-stromal signaling, pre-programming of EMT programs during the tumor stage. This inference is highly consistent with our observation of a significant positive correlation between ARHGEF15 and EMT activity in the CRC stage.32-34 In other words, ARHGEF15 may establish a “pro-tumor niche” in the IBD stage via non-epithelial cell-dominated stromal remodeling, and further amplify EMT, immune remodeling, and invasive phenotypes after tumor formation.12,35,36

ARHGEF15 is primarily localized to stromal and vascular-related cells, rather than immune or epithelial cells, suggesting its function as a microenvironmental regulator rather than a traditional oncogene. This localization fits well with the classic model of “inflammation-stroma-epithelium” cascade dysregulation during the IBD-CRC transformation. Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between epithelial and mesenchymal marker expression in merged epithelial-mesenchymal cell populations, and the correlation coefficient gradually decreased from normal tissues (-0.3694) to IBD tissues (-0.3894) and CRC tissues (-0.5291). This finding suggests that epithelial characteristics are gradually diminished while mesenchymal traits are enhanced along the progression from intestinal homeostasis to inflammatory lesions and colorectal malignant lesions, which may reflect dynamic EMT alterations during intestinal pathological progression. The upregulated expression of ARHGEF15 occurs synchronously with this progressive EMT shift, which provides a fundamental pathological background for exploring the potential function of ARHGEF15. Epithelial cells exhibited distinct functional alterations across disease stages. In IBD, epithelial cells were mainly enriched in pathways associated with innate immune defense, including defense response to virus, antimicrobial humoral response and MHC class II protein complex, indicating that intestinal epithelial cells may participate in local immune modulation via antigen presentation and antimicrobial peptide secretion, rather than merely maintaining mucosal barrier integrity. In CRC, epithelial cells underwent metabolic reprogramming and proliferative activation, with enriched signatures of cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins, translation, RNA splicing and ribonucleoprotein complex assembly. Enrichment of focal adhesion and Wnt signaling pathways was also observed in CRC epithelial cells, which may facilitate the acquisition of proliferative and malignant phenotypes. Mesenchymal cells shifted from inflammatory stroma remodeling to tumor microenvironment reconstruction. In IBD, mesenchymal cells were enriched in TNF, IL-1, RANKL-RANK and p38 MAPK signaling pathways, which presumably participate in chronic intestinal inflammation and local tissue fibrosis. In CRC, mesenchymal cells were dominated by extracellular matrix remodeling and cytoskeleton rearrangement, with high enrichment of focal adhesion, ECM structural constituent, collagen and integrin binding terms, as well as elevated translational and ribosomal activity. These cells displayed properties similar to cancer-associated fibroblasts, which may support tumor invasion and metastasis via ECM secretion and cytoskeleton modulation. Endothelial cells also presented stage-dependent functional changes. IBD-derived endothelial cells were enriched in IFN-γ, IL-1 signaling and MHC class I-mediated antigen presentation, potentially mediating immune cell recruitment to inflamed sites. CRC endothelial cells were characterized by activated VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling, coupled with focal adhesion and ECM organization pathways, which may contribute to tumor angiogenesis and metastatic niche formation. In terms of intercellular crosstalk, impaired epithelial barrier function in IBD enables mesenchymal and endothelial cells to jointly regulate inflammation and tissue repair. The homeostatic inflammation-repair balance is disrupted in CRC, where epithelial cells gain malignant properties, and stromal cells are remodeled to form a pro-tumor microenvironment, partially supporting the transition from inflammation-driven to tumor-driven intestinal lesion progression. The progressive negative correlation between epithelial and mesenchymal markers may result from global transcriptomic shift of intestinal microenvironment, instead of EMT occurring solely in epithelial cells. Mild and reversible partial EMT emerges in inflamed IBD tissues, while irreversible malignant EMT dominates CRC lesions. Focal adhesion pathway is commonly enriched in three major cell types in CRC, which may serve as a candidate target for intestinal inflammation-cancer intervention. ARHGEF15 upregulation is correlated with mesenchymal polarization throughout disease progression. Its mild elevation in IBD may modulate stromal inflammatory response and tissue repair, whereas its high expression in CRC may interact with Wnt and focal adhesion pathways to regulate epithelial malignant transformation, stroma formation and tumor angiogenesis. Consistent with previous studies reporting inflammation-induced EMT and oncogenic roles of CAFs and VEGFA signaling in CRC, our transcriptomic results verify the reliability of the dataset.37-39 Different from most studies focusing on single cell type, this study quantifies epithelial-mesenchymal correlation dynamics, adding novel insights into microenvironment crosstalk during inflammation-cancer transition.

Exploratory drug sensitivity analysis shows a tentative correlation between elevated ARHGEF15 expression and increased susceptibility to DNA damage repair inhibitors (e.g., PARP inhibitors) and partial antimetabolites, which is the only drug-relevant finding obtained from the present cohort. Notably, no DNA damage repair, replication stress or genome stability-related transcriptomic signatures were enriched in our single-cell dataset. Combined with the core EMT progression features identified in this study, we propose a unified extrapolation rather than independent mechanistic assumptions: the stepwise EMT polarization driven by sustained ARHGEF15 upregulation throughout IBD-CRC transition, including gradual loss of epithelial identity, stromal cytoskeletal remodeling, and formation of pro-tumor stromal-epithelial crosstalk, may alter the overall adaptive stress status of malignant cells, which may secondarily modify tumor cellular drug responsiveness to DNA-targeted therapeutics, instead of direct modulation on DNA repair machinery. From the perspective of inflammatory cancer prevention and long-term intervention, repurposed pharmacological agents, including GLP-1 analogs, novel imidazo[2,1-b] quinazoline derivatives and thymidylate synthase conformer-selective inhibitors, have shown preliminary chemopreventive potency in gastrointestinal malignancies in prior researches, which deserves further validation in IBD-associated colorectal carcinogenesis models.40-42 Taken together, the observed drug sensitivity tendency is presumed to be attributed to ARHGEF15-related EMT and microenvironment remodeling. This ARHGEF15 expression signature may provide a preliminary stratification reference for inflammation-associated CRC patients with refractory tumor phenotype or limited therapeutic options.

The strengths of the present study lie in its systematic analytical design and stratified omics exploration. This study adopted validated longitudinal single-cell RNA-seq datasets covering normal intestinal, IBD and CRC specimens with complete clinical annotation, and performed integrative profiling combining transcriptomic phenotyping, cellular functional enrichment and exploratory pharmacogenomic analysis throughout the inflammation-cancer cascade.

5. Limitations and Future Perspectives

All conclusions in the present study are derived from bioinformatic mining, without supportive wet-lab evidence from in vitro functional assays or in vivo animal experiments. Additionally, predictive drug response outcomes are inferred from correlational analysis based on public pharmacogenomic datasets, which may be affected by inherent model discrepancies and sample selection bias. Consequently, prospective validation constitutes an essential next step for this research. Future research will prioritize multi-layered experimental verification, including immunohistochemistry and qPCR quantification using independent IBD and CRC clinical tissue cohorts, in vitro ARHGEF15 knockdown and overexpression assays in cultured cells and patient-derived organoids to clarify its regulatory effects on EMT progression and drug responsiveness, as well as in vivo evaluation of PARP inhibitor efficacy in colorectal animal models stratified by ARHGEF15 expression level.

6. Conclusion

ARHGEF15 is associated with IBD-to-CRC progression through tissue-specific molecular and immune mechanisms. Its prognostic value and enhanced sensitivity to PARPi highlight ARHGEF15 as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target requiring prospective experimental and clinical validation.
