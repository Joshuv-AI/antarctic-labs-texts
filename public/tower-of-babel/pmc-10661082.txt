Quantifying leadership in climate negotiations: A social power game
Authors: Carmela Bernardo, Lingfei Wang, Mathias Fridahl, Claudio Altafini, Sergey Gavrilets
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe consider complex multistage multiagent negotiation processes such as those occurring at climate conferences and ask ourselves how can an agent maximize its social power, intended as influence over the outcome of the negotiation. This question can be framed as a strategic game played over an opinion dynamics model, in which the action of an agent consists in stubbornly defending its own opinion. We show that for consensus-seeking opinion dynamics models in which the interaction weights are uniform, the optimal action obeys to an early mover advantage principle, i.e. the agents behaving stubbornly in the early phases of the negotiations achieve the highest social power. When looking at data collected from the climate change negotiations going on at the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, we find evidence of the use of the early mover strategy. Furthermore, we show that the social powers computed through our model correlate very well with the perceived leadership roles assessed through independent survey data, especially when non-uniform weights incorporating economical and demographic factors are considered.Keywords: network opinion dynamics, social games, climate change negotiations

Significance Statement

The article uses a network game to quantify the leadership role of the agents participating in a multiagent negotiation process. When all agents have the same weight, the solution of the game reveals a general principle, which we call “early mover advantage” (i.e. first movers get a head start). Evidence of early mover strategy can be found in the multilateral negotiations going on at climate conferences organized by the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). The leadership values produced by the model correlate well with the values of perceived leadership we obtain from independent surveys carried out at the UNFCCC meetings, especially when economical and demographic factors are included in the model.

Introduction

Complex multilateral negotiation processes such as those going on at climate conferences provide important case studies of how interacting agents influence each other’s opinions. Understanding the dynamics behind such multiagent “opinion forming” processes and unraveling the mechanisms by which influence propagates in these processes is an extremely challenging and largely unsolved problem. Consider for instance the case of climate change negotiations occurring in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Here, the agents are the contracting parties that have ratified the Convention (197 countries plus the European Union (EU)), the arena for the discussions is the United Nations Climate Change Conferences, which comprises annual Conferences of the Parties (COP) and subsidiary bodies. The matters of the discussion are all the aspects linked to the statutory function of the UNFCCC, whose ultimate objective is the “stabilization of greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system,” see (1), Article 2. Each party brings to the negotiation table its own viewpoint and formulates proposals that must take into account its national economical interests while at the same time contribute to the common good of limiting the effects of climate change on humanity. Each year, the ultimate goal of the COP is to find an agreement on matters of substance which is acceptable by all parties on some aspects of the problem, normally formulated as a cover decision and set of underlying decisions, and sometimes new treaty text or amendments to existing treaties. We refer to such decisions and treaties as annual accords, approved by consensus at the end of each COP session. The Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement are examples of treaties. In reality, the task of preparing a joint text to be approved by the COP assembly starts often earlier than the annual COP meeting, and it is a much longer and articulated process of multilateral negotiation. A draft document is first discussed by the subsidiary bodies, which try to forge agreements on specific topics and forward draft proposals to the COP. Only the COP can adopt a decision, and adoption is by consensus.

The questions that we pose ourselves in this article are: can we find a way to quantify which agents were most successful in the negotiation process and can we understand what strategy an agent should follow to orient the discussion and maximize its influence in the final outcome?

Mathematically, the double goal of finding an agreement approved by consensus while at the same time defending a party’s national interests is captured well by an opinion dynamics model called concatenated Friedkin–Johnsen (FJ) model (2, 3). An FJ model is a multiagent dynamical model in which the agents update their opinions according to an averaging rule similar to the basic DeGroot rule (4), but with the peculiarity that each agent is allowed to have a stubborn behavior, intended as a tendency to defend its own standpoint while discussing with the other agents (5, 6). The trajectory of an FJ model ends in a point in the convex hull of the initial conditions, i.e. the opinions at the end of the discussion are closer to each other than they were at the begin. A concatenated FJ model is an extension of this model useful to deal with sequences of discussion meetings (3). Each meeting is represented by an FJ model, while concatenation refers to the fact that the meetings are dealing with related topics and that the endpoint opinion of a meeting becomes the initial condition of the next meeting. In our UNFCCC example, the sequence of discussion meetings corresponds to the satellite meetings of the subsidiary bodies preparing for the final COP plenary, where the important decisions are made and the annual accord is approved. A useful property of concatenated FJ models is that opinions progressively approach a common value, and eventually consensus is achieved, which reflects well the spirit of the climate negotiations, with its aim to foster cooperation among the countries, as well as the decision-making modality applied by the UNFCCC.

In a concatenated FJ model, the behavior of the agents during the discussions influences the value of the consensus point eventually reached. In particular, an agent behaving stubbornly gives a large weight to its own opinion and tends to attract the opinions of the other agents towards its own, while an agent that is not stubborn applies instead a purely averaging rule when updating its own opinion. Also this feature captures well the inner working of the multilateral negotiations going on at the UNFCCC summits: parties that want to exert a leadership role must convince the audience of their arguments, and the best way to do so is to be vocal at the meetings, i.e. to engage in the discussion and to orient it with propositions, motions, support for similar statements, opposition to contrarian arguments, etc. All these actions are manifestations of a stubborn behavior. We therefore associate speaking up in a meeting with stubbornness, and quantify the stubbornness of an agent in terms of the number of speaking occasions utilized by the agent. Needless to say, it is in the nature of multilateral diplomacy that no single country can steal the show and have the monopoly of the conversation, which can be translated mathematically into an assumption that no agent can be totally stubborn, and that each agent has a “budget of speaking opportunities” (i.e. a cap on its stubbornness) to spend in the sequence of discussions.

Our question becomes then to understand how much a stubborn behavior translates into an effective leadership. The concatenated FJ model provides ways to quantify this through the notion of “social power,” which weights the importance of each agent on the final opinions achieved by the other agents (7–9). More properly, the question that we are posing ourselves in this article is the following: what strategy should an agent follow in allocating its own budget of speaking opportunities in order to maximize its social power? Clearly, the social power depends also on the strategy adopted by the other agents, and the optimization problem is naturally formulated as a strategic game. It is shown in the article that, when the interaction weights among the agents are chosen uniformly, for concatenated FJ models the optimal strategy for maximizing social power is for an agent to use all of its speaking opportunities as early as possible, strategy which we call “early mover advantage.” In particular, we show that a strategy in which all agents saturate their speaking opportunities as early as possible is a Nash equilibrium for the problem, and that the early mover strategy is dominant, i.e. it is optimal for an agent regardless of what the other agents do. The fundamental reason behind the optimality of the early mover strategy in the uniform weights concatenated FJ model is the presence of a diminishing return law for the social power: the changes in social power allowed by the model decrease as the meeting index increases (and as the opinions become closer to each other).

When the interaction weights are instead non-uniform, and in particular are highly skewed, the behavior of the concatenated FJ model is more complex and the early mover strategy alone may fail to maximize social power.

Screening through the data we collected for the UNFCCC meetings in the years 2001–2022, we see evidence of early mover strategy for many of the years. It appears that this strategy is most frequently used by the EU, which according to our model achieves the highest social power in all years up to the Paris Agreement in 2015. As a way to validate our findings, we show that the social power computed by our model over the years correlates well with the perceived leadership role reported in surveys carried out during the COP meetings (10, 11). The correlation becomes even higher (up to 0.85) when economical and demographic factors like gross domestic product (GDP) and population size are used to generate non-uniform weights in the interaction matrix.

Methods

Model

In order to represent the sequence of negotiation meetings occurring each year, we consider a model called the concatenated FJ model (3). Let s=1,…,M be the index of the discussion meeting occurring in a given year, and denote t=0,1,… the time axis within the sth meeting. where θi(s), 0≤θi(s)<1, is a stubbornness coefficient representing the reluctance of agent i to change opinion during the negotiation. The higher θi(s) is, the more stubborn agent i is in the sth meeting. The interaction between the agents is represented by a row-stochastic matrix W of entries wij: wij≥0 and ∑j=1nwij=1. In particular, the weight wij represents the influence that agent j has on agent i. We talk about uniform or non-uniform interactions depending on whether the weights wij are all equal (to 1/n) or different depending on the column index j (see Section 3.1 of the Supplementary Methods).

The vector of stubbornness coefficients θ(s)=(θ1(s),…,θn(s)) is the input to the model for each meeting s. The meetings are concatenated, in the sense that the final opinion of the sth meeting becomes the initial condition of the (s+1)st meeting: yi(s+1,0)=yi(s,∞). Social power

Row-stochastic matrices such as Q(s) have a dominant eigenvalue equal to 1. The associated right eigenvector characterizes the convergence of the opinion vector x(s) towards a consensus value. The left eigenvector is instead normally called eigenvector centrality (12) and used to characterize the importance of each agent in the resulting consensus value (7, 8). A strictly related concept is used in this article to define the notion of social power vector sp(s)=(sp1(s),…,spn(s)). More precisely, spi(s) corresponds to the influence accumulated by agent i over the other agents in the course of the first s discussions, and it is quantified in the model as the normalized sum of the ith column of the concatenation matrix Q(s):

In practice, similarly to a centrality measure, the social power can be used to summarize the cumulative effect of all opinion changes during all discussion events and to attribute them to the individual agents. In this way, it provides a measure of leadership for the agents: at the end of the M discussions, if an agent has a high spi(M), then its final opinion xi(M) does not differ much from its initial opinion xi(0). Agents with low spi(M) instead typically have their opinions significantly modified during the discussion process. The higher spi(M), the higher is therefore the leadership status assigned by the model to agent i (and, since spi(M) is a proxy for the eigenvector centrality, the higher is the centrality of agent i). Since spi(s)≥0 and ∑i=1nspi(s)=1, when the social power of an agent increases, that of the other agents must necessarily decrease.

Problem formulation: maximizing social power

The question that we pose and investigate in this article is how should an agent behave in order to maximize its social power, i.e. its influence over the final outcome of the negotiation? In order to answer this question, we need to understand what an agent can leverage upon. In a negotiation process like the one we are considering, the main actions consist in addressing the audience in plenary meetings. Speaking at a meeting can be an opportunity for an agent to present a stance, defend a position, support a motion, disagree with a resolution, etc. All these actions can be considered expressions of stubborn behavior. Since embedded in each P(s) is a stubbornness coefficient θi(s) for each agent, and such coefficient can be chosen at will by each agent, behaving stubbornly seems the simplest possible way that an agent has to increase its social power. If we associate the stubbornness coefficients with the amount of speaking opportunities and take the latter as strategic action of an agent, our question becomes then which strategy should the agent follow in allocating its speaking opportunities in order to maximize the social power? This is more precisely the problem investigated in the article.

Social power game

The problem can be formulated as a strategic game, in which the action of each player (i.e. agent) corresponds to the allocation of a certain number K of speaking opportunities across the sequence of M meetings in the best possible way.

We assume that each agent has a “basal” value of stubbornness 0≤α<1 (in climate negotiations, each country tends to defend its national interests), and that each speaking opportunity adds a fixed “token” of stubbornness 0<β≪1 to that agent, so that if agent i speaks ai(s) times at the sth meeting its stubbornness becomes

The number of speaking opportunities over the sequence of meetings, ai=(ai(1),…,ai(M)), is therefore the action of agent i in the strategic game. It is such that ai(s)∈{0,1,…,L} (the upper bound L on the number of speaking opportunities per meeting is chosen such that L≤1−αβ, to guarantee that in Eq. (4) the stubbornness coefficients do not exceed 1), and ∑s=1Mai(s)≤K (the sum of all speaking opportunities of an agent does not exceed K). These constraints guarantee that discussion meetings are not monopolized by a single actor but are indeed multilateral.

The payoff function of agent i is the social power accumulated at the end of the M meetings: ui(a)=spi(M), where a=(a1,…,an) is the collection of actions of all agents. The scope of each agent is therefore to allocate its speaking opportunities so as to maximize the payoff, which in the model coincides with the social power.

Results

Solving the social power game in the uniform weights case: early mover advantage

The solution of this n-player strategic game depends on the action of all players at all discussion meetings. In the uniform weights case, an explicit solution can be found, see details in the Supplementary Methods. Its main features are the following:

C1: If agent i invests more speaking opportunities than agent j in early meetings, then it will achieve a higher social power than agent j, regardless of what the other agents do.

C1: If agent i invests more speaking opportunities than agent j in early meetings, then it will achieve a higher social power than agent j, regardless of what the other agents do.

C3: The optimal strategy ai* is a dominant strategy, i.e. it holds no matter what the strategy of the other agents is.

The only Nash equilibrium for the game corresponds to all agents adopting the optimal strategy ai*, see the details in the Supplementary Methods, where a more technical formulation of the C1–C3 conditions is also provided. Notice that these conditions are rigorously valid when α and β are small enough, which translates into an assumption of stubbornness θi=α+βai which is small enough, but, as we show below, they are most often valid for any value of α and β such that α+βL<0.95. The upper bound 0.95 is meant to avoid totally stubborn agents (that would correspond to θi(s)=1 and to fixed opinion xi for agent i).

From C2, the optimal strategy ai* corresponds to an “early mover” strategy, and allows us to formulate an “early mover advantage” principle for the uniform weights social power game. When opinions tend to converge to a consensus, this principle has an intuitive explanation: in the early phases of the discussion the opinions are more diverse, and adopting early a stubborn behavior allows to steer them towards the stubborn agents’ opinions in a more effective way than when the stubborn behavior is applied at a later stage, where the opinions are more similar. This higher “opinion leverage” is what allows early movers to achieve a higher payoff. It can be formalized in terms of a diminishing return law, as done in the next paragraph.

For uniform weights, diminishing returns lead to early mover advantage

The averaging dynamics of Eq. (1) is a contracting dynamics (i.e. maxixi(s)−minixi(s) is decreasing in s). As shown in Section 3.3.1 of the Supplementary Methods, such contractivity property leads to a diminishing return law on the possible changes in the social powers of the agents: at each meeting the maximal change in social power over all possible speaking strategies is upper bounded by the product of the maximal stubbornness in each meeting thus far. The resulting diminishing return law can be interpreted as follows: as s increases, the possibility of an agent to steer the opinion of the other agents towards its own (and to gain social power in the process) decreases.

The early mover advantage rule is rigorously valid in the case of uniform weights, but when the weights change from agent to agent, the leverage effect given by stubbornness can be compensated (and beaten) by the heavier weight of an agent, even one with low stubbornness.

A small-scale example

As an example, we considered a small-scale network with uniform weights, illustrated in Fig. 1, with n=8 agents having K=20 speaking opportunities to be used in M=6 meetings with a maximum of L=8 speaking opportunities per meeting. The action of the agents (given explicitly in the Supplementary Materials) leads to the stubbornness profiles of Fig. 1b, where we have chosen the parameters α and β in the large stubbornness regime: α=0.2 and β=0.75/L=0.094. Even with large α and β, on a time horizon of M=6 meetings the concatenated FJ model reaches convergence, see Fig. 1c. Agent 1 makes use of an early mover strategy, and in fact it achieves the highest social power, see Fig. 1d. Agents 2 and 3 exchange their social power rankings at the third meeting: agent 3 starts the sequence of meetings with a higher stubbornness but then does not persist, and its high stubbornness in the last meeting comes too late to influence the balance of social powers. The same is true for agents 7 and 8 whose “late mover” strategies are not effective in terms of social power, as at around meeting 5 the opinions are already very close to each other (at consensus, in practice). In this example, some rearrangements of the rankings can be achieved by being stubborn in the mid-meetings, see e.g. agent 5, but the early mover advantage principle is always respected, i.e. ai* dominates all other strategies. On this example, the upper bounds on α and β given in the Supplementary Materials are very small, see Fig. S1a, meaning that the most realistic case is by far the “large” stubbornness regime, in which we have no theoretical guarantee that the three conditions C1–C3 are respected, nor that ai* is optimal. Indeed, for large stubbornness coefficients some sporadic violations of C1–C3 may appear and are sometimes visible in our running example. To quantify how frequent these violations are, we performed a systematic numerical comparison. We selected 100 values for the “basal” stubbornness α (chosen uniformly in [0,0.2]) and 100 different values of β, uniformly distributed in the interval [0,0.95−α]/L. In this way, we obtain 104 values of stubbornness θ=(θ1,…,θn) in the interval [α,0.95], i.e. spanning the whole range of possible stubbornness up the saturation value of 0.95. We simulate the concatenated FJ model using the same action profile a=(a1,…,an) with these 104 values of α and β. The resulting social power is compared with the social power obtained instead from small values of α and β obeying the bounds given in the Supplementary Materials, for which C1–C3 are rigorously true. The number of changes occurring between the two social power vectors can be estimated by looking at the corresponding ranks, using the Kendall τ rank correlation coefficient, see Supplementary Materials for the details. The resulting distribution of Kendall τ is shown in Fig. S1b. The computation is then repeated for 105 different action profiles a still comparing social powers for large and small (α,β) pairs, see Fig. S1c. The mean Kendall τ in both cases is around 0.95, which confirms that indeed a strategy profile a yields rather similar social power rankings in both small and large stubbornness regimes. In particular, the strategy ai* is optimal for all values of α and β. As shown in Fig. S2, analogous results are obtained when the number n of agents grows.

Application: UNFCCC negotiations as a social power game

A concrete setting in which the social power game can be tested and evaluated is the climate negotiations going on at the UNFCCC summits. Each year these negotiations lead to a set of formal decisions approved by consensus at the annual COP, usually occurring at the end of the year. Before the COP, a series of satellite meetings comprising of both permanent subsidiary bodies mandated to deal with implementation and scientific and technological advice, respectively, and ad hoc subsidiary bodies, with temporary mandates to deal with specific issues (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Materials for a list), is used to set the stage for the COP and to prepare draft decisions to be adopted by the COP. We collected the speaking occasions of all parties at these meetings, as available from the Earth Negotiations Bulletin website (https://enb.iisd.org/enb/vol12/). We considered the years 2001–2022, for a total of 201 meetings and 26,480 speaking occasions, see Fig. 2a and b. The speaking occasions are the actions a in our social power game. Their overall distribution across the 21 years we monitor is shown in Fig. 2b, and in Fig. 2c for a few significant parties (yearly totals are shown in Fig. 2, see Fig. 3 for values within a year). We assume for now that the interaction weights are uniform, i.e. that wij=1/n. Using the formula of Eq. (4), we associate to the parties the stubbornness coefficients θ and compute the corresponding social powers sp of Eq. (3) for the parties using the model of Eq. (1), according to the workflow in Fig. 1a. In Eq. (4), the values of α and β are chosen in the large stubbornness regime. In detail, we take α=0.2 and β=0.75/amaxs, where amaxs=maxiai(s) is the maximal number of speaking occasions of an agent at the sth meeting, leading to 0.2≤θi(s)≤0.95, with lower and upper bounds saturated for at least one agent for each s. The resulting stubbornness coefficients (yearly average) for a few parties are shown in Fig. 2d and the associated social powers in Fig. 2e. What stands out immediately is that the EU is the party achieving by far the highest social power in most years, essentially for all years up to 2016, see Fig. 2e. This is expected, as in the years 2001–2016 the EU has the largest action (i.e. the highest number of speeches per year), see Fig. 2c, and consequently the highest stubbornness, see Fig. 2d. If we lump together all speeches of a party in a year and correlate this number with sp (Fig. S4), then the average correlation (over the years) is 0.85, see Fig. 2f, meaning that having a stubborn behavior leads most likely to high social power, as expected. It is worth observing in Fig. 2f that sp and the dominant left eigenvector of Q(M) (denoted ξ) are nearly perfectly aligned for all years, which allows us to give to sp also the meaning of “practical” eigenvector centrality for the negotiation process modeled as in Eq. (1).

We can also investigate more in detail what happens during each year. Figure 3 shows the evolution of the actions and the resulting social powers for a few parties achieving the highest social power during a year, for three different years. The remaining years are shown in Figs. S5–S9. If we look at the actions (in Fig. 3, for simplicity, the associated stubbornness is shown), indeed we see that up to 2016 the strategy of the EU is to consistently use a high number of speaking opportunities throughout the year, including in the early meetings of each year, in greater quantity than any other party of the Convention. To evaluate if also the distribution of the speeches during the meeting sequence in a year matters (and therefore if the early mover advantage is indeed exploited in the UNFCCC negotiations), we compare the social power for the real EU action with that obtained from a null model constructed reshuffling the action of the EU during each year. In detail, we perform simulations in which in each year we randomize the order of the entries of the action of the EU, i.e. the same number of speeches is kept but associated to different meetings within the year. The result is shown in Fig. 2g. It can be appreciated that the social power of the EU nearly halves in most years after the reshuffling of the action. In particular, the temporal mean (over the years) of the social power of the EU for the real strategy is 0.064, while for the null model the temporal mean (of the mean over 100 reshuffled strategies) decreases to 0.038. If we look at Fig. 3 and Figs. S5–S9, indeed all the years in which one of the reshuffled strategies outperforms the real strategy corresponds to the EU not saturating its action in all the first meetings. These are however mostly minor differences, i.e. the real EU strategy is close enough to behave according to our early mover strategy. We can therefore conclude that, in comparison with the other parties, the EU indeed adopts an early mover strategy, and in our model it rips off the benefits in terms of social power.

The balance of social power starts to change after the Paris Agreement of 2015. In particular, in the years 2017–2022 the EU no longer applies an early mover strategy (see for instance the EU’s action in 2022 in Fig. 3) and consequently no longer achieves the highest social power. Such a rethinking of strategy appears to be systematic, as it occurs in all the last 5 years of our analysis.

As a way to validate the social powers we obtain from the concatenated FJ model for the UNFCCC negotiation data, we used questionnaire data from the International Negotiation Survey (INS) (see https://liu.se/en/research/international-negotiations-survey) that has gauged UNFCCC delegates’ views on leadership at the COP summits since 2008, for a total of 5530 respondents up to 2022. In one of the leadership-related questions of this survey, participants are asked to list which parties they perceive as leaders in the climate negotiations (10, 11). The social power computed from our model is compared to these perceived leadership responses in Fig. 4a. Interestingly, the correlation between these two ways to assess leadership is positive every year and very high on several years, see Fig. 4b. The mean correlation over the 14 years is 0.6. The main reason is that the top-ranking parties in the survey (the EU, the US, and China) are also those that our analysis usually identifies as having a high social power. These values are rather encouraging, given the completely different methodologies and contexts in which the two datasets originate. When we look at how perceived and model-based leaderships evolve over the years in Fig. 4c, then the results are a bit more mixed but still insightful. For instance, the social power of the EU has a correlation of 0.66 with the perceived leadership from the survey. Notice in particular how for the EU the decline of social power we deduce from our model is reflected well in the decline of perceived leadership in the survey data. For other parties, instead, the trend is of limited significance, see e.g. the US and China in Fig. 4c, or negative (for Australia, even though the extremely low percentages in the perceived leadership axis, see Fig. 4c, makes this correlation value rather unreliable when compared to the EU, the US, and China).

Beyond uniform interaction weights: assessing the importance of economical and demographic factors in the UNFCCC negotiations

So far we have considered the case of uniform weights in the interaction matrix W, i.e. wij=1/n. However, in the UNFCCC assemblies, certain parties carry a larger “specific weight” than others. This can be captured e.g. in terms of economical or demographic indicators. In our work, we use GDP and population size. Namely, in the interaction matrix W the columns are rescaled to be proportional to the GDP or population of a country, with the normalization constraint that W must be a row-stochastic matrix (see Supplementary Methods for more details). The analysis is then repeated using the new interaction matrices WGDP and Wpop, obtaining the vectors of social powers spGDP and sppop shown in Fig. 5c and d. When we compare spGDP and sppop with the perceived leadership of the INS survey, then the correlation improves significantly with respect to the uniform weights case, passing from 0.6 to 0.85 for spGDP and to 0.8 for sppop, see Fig. 5f and Figs. S10–S12. As shown in Fig. 5e and Figs. S13–S15, spGDP and sppop are more similar to each other than the uniform weight sp. The correlations between sp and spGDP and between sp and sppop are around 0.79 and 0.73, respectively, see Fig. 5e, and Figs. S14 and S15. Also the rankings in the three social power vectors change frequently, see Figs. S16 and S17. The main reason for these differences is that GDP and population vary of several orders of magnitude across different countries, implying that both WGDP and Wpop contain highly different weights (the jth column is proportional to the GDP/population of the jth party), see Fig. 5a and b for a few examples. Consequently, we can no longer expect that stubbornness alone is maximizing social power. Also the economical and/or demographic weight of the parties has an impact on the social power, see Fig. 5g and h. Therefore, the early mover strategy need not lead to the optimal payoff anymore, unless it is applied to a party having a large weight, such as the EU. In fact, as shown in Fig. 5c and d, the EU still dominates also in spGDP/sppop for most years. In our model among the “heavy weights” parties identified by GDP/population (namely the EU, the US, and China, plus India but only for sppop), the EU still has the competitive advantage given by the early mover strategy it adopts. This is confirmed by comparing spGDP and sppop resulting from the real and reshuffled actions, see Fig. S18.

Discussion

Problems related to the maximization of influence in social networks have been studied intensively in the literature from different perspectives. Approaches range from investigating the effect of the topology (analyzed through e.g. Bonacich centrality or eigenvector centrality (12, 13)) and of diffusion processes on the spreading through the network (14, 15), to optimally placing totally stubborn nodes so as to best manipulate the opinions, often formulated in a game-theoretical framework (16–19). In the setting of (20), the opinion influence game also leads to an early mover advantage strategy being optimal, but for a payoff function which is the opinion itself, not the social power. This viewpoint is also related to the basic game-theoretical interpretation (“best response”) that can be given to standard models such as the DeGroot model (21), the FJ model (22, 23), and the Hegselmann–Krause model (24). Many other influence maximization games over social networks have been investigated in the literature, see e.g. (25–28). See also (29–32) for more organic surveys.

The peculiarity of the social power game we introduce in this article is that the action is not related to the opinion of the agents, but rather to the stubbornness level of the agents in a discussion. Unlike the setting in which the agents are totally stubborn, which has been studied extensively (16, 17, 19, 20, 27, 33) (and which leads to rather different problems and models), the graded stubbornness case we consider in this article constitutes a so far little explored, and in our opinion rather natural, way that an agent has to influence the opinion-forming process. Furthermore, linking stubbornness to the act of speaking up makes it possible to quantify the stubbornness coefficients as we do for the UNFCCC case study, bypassing what has been so far the main obstacle to using FJ models in empirical contexts, namely, the difficulty of estimating θ. Similarly, the payoff we choose for our strategic game, the social power, is naturally associated to the “status” of an agent, and in fact a rich literature exists on interpreting and using social power in this perspective (5, 8, 9, 34). Having quantified stubbornness, we get a model-based quantification also for the social power.

The other big advantage of our model is that the strategic game is explicitly solvable. Such solution is rigorously valid under some technical assumptions, such as small stubbornness and complete uniformly weighted interconnection graph (all weights equal to 1/n). While we can show that numerically a concatenated FJ model behaves similarly for small and large stubbornness, understanding what happens when the edge weight distribution is nontrivial is a rather challenging (and so far unsolved) problem, as the edge weights enter nonlinearly in the computation of the row-stochastic matrix P(s) (see Eq. (S2)). Going beyond complete graphs is not required in our analysis of the UNFCCC negotiations (all meetings we consider are plenary and hence full connectivity is a good assumption), but already relaxing the assumption of uniform weights we find that an early mover strategy may fail to be optimal, at least when the weights are very skewed across parties, as it happens with GDP-based or population-based weights. In particular, a party with a low weight (weights in our model are assigned column-wise) will most likely fail to achieve a high social power even if it applies an early mover strategy. The best situation for a country is therefore to combine a high weight and an early mover strategy. In our data, this is the case for the EU, which maintains the highest social power also in the non-uniform weights settings we consider. This can be easily deduced by comparing sp in Fig. 2e with spGDP and sppop in Fig. 5c and d.

Both non-uniform weights cases we treat lead to a high concentration of social power in a few hands: while in sp no party exceeds a social power of 0.1, in both spGDP and sppop social powers of 0.5 for the EU are common, and the three parties with highest social power (the EU, the US, and China) make around 90% of the whole social power. In fact, in our model social powers are non-negative and sum to 1, meaning that the strategic game we use is basically a zero-sum game in which an agent increases its social power at the expenses of the others, and both stubbornness and non-uniform weights contribute to determine the social powers.

An extreme manifestation of this last property in our model is represented by agents that are totally stubborn and refuse to change their stance (i.e. θi(s)=1 for all s, for some i), which corresponds practically to blocking minorities or veto power, well-known complications of consensus negotiations. Our choice of putting an upper bound on the stubbornness coefficient (θi(s)≤0.95) is meant to avoid such deadlock situations.

The main feature of a concatenated FJ model is that it allows agents to manifest a stubborn behavior in their interactions with other agents, while at the same time using an averaging rule to update the opinions. This pattern is reasonable for the multilateral climate negotiations, where all participating parties have the right to express their opinion, and at the same time a constructive spirit is required, as climate change is a global issue affecting all countries. The need of a constructive approach (in our model captured by the averaging rule) is even more necessary when consensus is required, as in the case of the UNFCCC accords. In fact, as at the COP there is no agreed voting rule, decisions are adopted by consensus. Consensus is usually interpreted to mean that there is no stated objection to a decision, which is not quite the same as unanimity. For example, a party may choose not to object formally to a decision, but to ask for its concerns to be taken note of in a footnote or in the report of the session. For the purpose of our analysis, the two concepts “absence of formal objections” and “unanimity” are identified, and identified with all parties achieving an identical opinion, i.e. opinion consensus.

In the uniform weights case, the optimality of the early mover strategy is not surprising, given the properties of the model, especially in light of the diminishing return law discussed in the Results. The consequence of this law is in fact that early mover strategies allow to effectively steer the opinions of all agents towards those of the early stubborn agents, which in its turn provides a bigger leverage on the social powers than what can be achieved with a concentration of stubbornness on the later meetings. When the weights are non-uniform, however, this impacts also the updating of opinions and of social powers, see Eq. (S2). For highly skewed weight distributions (when considering GDP or population, the columns of W may differ by factors of ∼105, see Fig. 5a and b), then the leverage provided by an early move may vanish and can easily be reversed in favor of “heavy weight” nations.

For what concerns the investigation of the UNFCCC negotiation process, what stands out of our analysis is the consistent dominance of the social power of the EU throughout almost the entire time interval we consider. The leadership role of the EU in the climate change governance is well-recognized in the literature (11, 35–39), as is the EU’s preference for multilateral negotiation venues like the UNFCCC summits rather that through small fora such as “coalitions of the willing” or bilateral agreements (35). In particular, for the early years of our analysis, the failure of the US to ratify the Kyoto Protocol, the withdrawal of Canada from the Kyoto Protocol, and the absence of binding emissions reductions targets on developing countries made possible for the EU to become the undisputed leader in the multilateral climate negotiations (35, 37, 39). Setting the agenda and leading the discussion in the majority of the UNFCCC meetings is apparently one of the means that the EU had to exercise this leadership: we see in our data that the EU is constantly adopting an early mover strategy and making use of speaking opportunities for most meetings for basically all years of the first decade we monitor. Such strategy is unique to the EU: as shown in Fig. 2c, the cumulative number of speaking occasions exploited by the EU is significantly larger than for any other party. It is often commented in the literature that climate governance in these years was characterized by a top-down approach, essentially initiated and coordinated by the EU (35, 39–41).

However, when we look at our data, around 2017 we see a significant change of pattern: the EU seems to be no longer engaged nor intervening in all the meetings with the same intensity, and in fact its social power no longer dominates. This may be a consequence of the different emphasis that has characterized the climate negotiations since Paris: alongside emission cuts, other issues like adaptation and loss and damage have gained prominence in the agenda, and these are evidently better represented and promoted by other actors than the EU. In addition, the shifting balance of economic power over two decades has impacted also the global climate governance: actors like China, India, and other large developing countries can no longer be sidelined, with the result that the climate negotiations are as polycentric as they have ever been before. Perhaps even the shift from a top-down approach, with attempts to impose binding rules-based and science-motivated cuts on the states, to a bottom-up approach, with voluntary emission reduction pledges from the parties, might have played a role. All these factors may have conditioned the way the agenda of the satellite meetings is set and perhaps the importance of these meetings for the final COP summit, and the EU may have adjusted its strategy accordingly.1

A question that can be asked is whether the social power deduced by the model, obtained simply by monitoring the actions of the agents at the official UNFCCC meetings, provides a reasonable-enough measure of the leadership of an agent. The scholarly literature defines leadership in various ways, for instance as an “asymmetric relationship of influence in which one actor guides and directs the behavior of others towards a certain goal” (42) or as “the power to orient and mobilize others for a purpose” (43). Following (10), four major modes of leadership can be listed: structural (when an actor has the power and the capacity to deploy resource-based incentives), directional (leading by example), idea-based (ability to provide new ideas and solutions to common goals), and instrumental (problem-solving and negotiation skills). In the context of climate governance, assessing these four aspects of leadership requires to consider the behavior of the parties from a broader angle than the one represented in our simple uniform weight model. For instance, structural leadership relates a lot to soft power factors such as the population size of a nation, GDP, political alliances, etc. When we include these factors in our model, then the correlation between the social power produced by the model and the perceived leadership role of the INS surveys (in which leadership is evaluated from an all-round perspective) indeed increases and achieves a very high value for all years. This fact is encouraging and suggests that a model like ours, when properly tuned, may be a useful tool to understand the mechanisms at work in a complex negotiation process, and perhaps also for planning purposes.

One aspect that our current model cannot capture is the fact that several of the actions happening at climate meetings have an antagonistic character (e.g. disagreeing with a motion, proposing an alternative protocol, contesting a proposal, etc.), and therefore the opinion update should not be described by a weighted average, but perhaps through some other opposition-based rule (44, 45). Quantifying which speeches have an antagonistic connotation (and against whom) is however impossible with the data we currently have, but will be the subject of future research.
