Chimeric antigen receptor dendritic cells suppress melanoma growth in preclinical cancer models
Authors: Julia Minnee, Wallace ZH Wong, Benjamin S Russell, Jose LM Semana, Ruofan Li, Nathaniel R Landau, Takuya Tada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has been successful for the treatment of hematological cancers but less effective against solid tumors, a phenomenon that results from the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment. As a strategy to improve the treatment of solid tumors, we applied CAR therapy to dendritic cells (DCs) to generate CAR-DCs. The CAR targeted the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) which is overexpressed in breast cancer to defeat the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment.MethodsCAR-DCs were generated by lentiviral vector transduction of SAMHD1 knock-out murine bone marrow-derived DCs. The vectors coexpressed CD40L and a soluble form of programmed cell death 1 (PD-1), a checkpoint inhibitor. To increase the durability of CAR-DCs, a gene encoding the cytokine GM-CSF was introduced into the CAR vector. The CAR-DCs were injected into mice bearing B16.HER2 melanoma tumors. Tumor growth was measured, and T cell functionality was determined by IFNγ expression and in vitro cytolytic assay.ResultsCAR-DCs suppressed the growth of B16.HER2 tumors and induced the proliferation and activation of tumor-infiltrating cytolytic CD8+T cells. The PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor further augmented the antitumor response and prevented T cell exhaustion. Vectored expression of GM-CSF increased the durability of the antitumor response.ConclusionsCAR-DCs could be an effective strategy for therapies against solid tumors that should be further explored. The approach relies on the antigen-presenting ability of DCs and their role in T cell activation and can be coupled with checkpoint inhibition in place of monoclonal antibody treatment.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

While chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has been effective against lymphoma and leukemia, it has been less successful against solid tumors. The limited success of CAR-T therapies against solid tumors results from several factors, including limited trafficking to tumors, low persistence, and impaired function resulting from the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Some success has been shown in preclinical models with CAR-macrophages, which are phagocytic and capable of penetrating solid tumors. CAR-macrophages expressing an anti-HER2 CAR were reported to have phagocytic activity against a solid tumor in a model of mouse melanoma.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

The study shows that CAR-dendritic cells (DCs) may be an alternative approach to therapy for solid tumors. CAR-DCs generated using a lentiviral vector suppressed the growth of melanoma in a preclinical mouse model. The antitumor potential of CAR-DCs can be augmented by the coexpression of the CAR with CD40L, a cell surface receptor that causes signaling through CD40, or coexpression of a soluble form of PD-1 that prevents T cell checkpoint activation. CAR-DC durability can be increased by the vectored expression of GM-CSF.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

The development of CAR-DCs holds promise as a therapy for solid tumors which have been recalcitrant to CAR-T cells. Implementing the therapy will require additional development in small animal models to determine which tumor antigens can be targeted by CARs, the optimal CAR structure, and to further understand the mechanisms by which they suppress tumor growth.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell immunotherapy has been successful in the treatment of cancers such as B-cell lymphoma and myeloma1 2 but less so for solid tumors. In the treatment of B cell malignancies, CAR-T cells targeted to CD19 have resulted in as much as 70%–90% of patients showing significant improvement3 4 and their use has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration.5 6 In addition to CD8+T cells, CAR-natural killer (NK) cells and CARγδ T cells have shown promise.7 8 These benefit from a reduced risk of alloreactivity and alternative modes of cytotoxicity. The relatively low success rate of CAR T cell therapy for solid tumors is the result of several factors: the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), poor immune trafficking to the tumor, low CAR-T persistence, and loss of the CAR-targeted tumor antigen.1912 In addition, a complication of CAR-T therapy has been that of cytokine release syndrome, which has been problematic in some studies.13

Most CARs in current use are targeted to cell surface antigens that are expressed at increased levels on tumor cells such as CD19 and B-cell maturation antigen.14 15 The CARs consist of a single-chain immunoglobulin heavy chain variable fragment (scFv), transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling domain. Engagement by tumor-expressed antigen results in major histocompatibility (MHC)-independent T cell activation.16 17 In most studies, CAR expression has been mediated by lentiviral vector transduction of activated T cells. The vectors result in stable CAR expression in T cells and are generally not immunogenic.3 18

CAR-macrophages have been studied as a strategy to overcome the barrier to the treatment of solid tumors.1921 Unlike CAR-T cells, CAR-macrophages localize and persist within the TME where they influence surrounding immune cells towards a pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory state.22 A recent study found that CAR-macrophages can reduce the ratio of tumor-associated macrophages and can phagocytose tumor cells both in vitro and in vivo.19 CAR-macrophages have been found to promote antigen presentation and to enhance the cytotoxic effect of nearby T cells.23 24 Klichinsky et al studied CAR-macrophages armed with a CAR targeted to human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2),21 a receptor tyrosine kinase that is overexpressed in about 20% of patients with early stage of breast cancer.25 26 The CAR-macrophages exhibited an M1-like phenotype that was resistant to the M2 immunosuppressive effects of the TME. The antitumor effect of the CAR-macrophages appeared to be mediated by tumor cell phagocytosis. The strategy is currently in clinical trials.27 In that study, the CAR was delivered by an adenoviral vector, a vector that is inefficient in the transduction of macrophages and for which clinical grade vector production is expensive.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are professional antigen-presenting cells that capture antigen and migrate to secondary lymphoid organs to present peptide epitopes to CD8+and CD4+ T cells through MHC class I and class II. DCs are also professional phagocytes, although not as active as macrophages in the killing of infected target cells.28 DCs infiltrate the TME where they cross-present tumor-associated antigens and neoantigens to T cells. Their antigen-presenting function has been utilized in DC-based vaccines.29 In this approach, DCs are pulsed with peptide antigen and then reinfused. Alternatively, the antigen can be expressed by transduction with a viral vector. We previously reported the development of a DC-based therapeutic vaccine for HIV in which DCs were transduced with a lentiviral vector expressing an HIV epitope.30 31 The approach was also used to develop a DC vaccine for murine lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus infection31 in which DCs were transduced with a lentiviral vector expressing an MHC class-I restricted T cell epitope. The vaccine protected mice against infection and cured chronic lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus infection.31

In this report, we tested whether DCs armed with an anti-HER2 CAR would suppress the growth of melanoma tumors in a preclinical mouse model. The role of DCs as professional antigen-presenting cells and their ability to promote T-cell polarization could serve as a means with which to enhance the antitumor T cell response. The results show that HER2.CAR-DCs suppressed the growth of B16.HER2 tumors. The antitumor potency of the CAR-DCs was enhanced by incorporation of a vector-expressed checkpoint inhibitor based on PD-1 which reversed T-cell exhaustion and increased the antitumor cytotoxic T lymphocyte response. The incorporation of a GM-CSF gene into the vector caused the transduced monocyte precursors of the DCs to proliferate, further enhancing potency and durability of the CAR-DCs.

Results

Suppression of tumor growth by CAR-DCs

CAR-macrophages suppress tumor growth primarily through tumor cell phagocytosis. Unlike macrophages, DCs are professional antigen-presenting cells. They have the ability to infiltrate the TME and to activate antitumor T cells. Thus, arming DCs with a CAR directed at a cell surface tumor antigen could promote their interaction with tumor cells to stimulate the uptake of tumor antigen and increase antigen presentation, thereby enhancing the antitumor T cell response. To test this possibility, we constructed a lentiviral vector that expressed the HER2.CAR described by Klichinsky et al consisting of an anti-HER2 scFv, CD8α hinge and transmembrane domains, and CD3ζ cytoplasmic signal transduction domain.21 A second vector was constructed that coexpressed CD40L, a cell surface receptor that induces DC maturation and Th1 cytokine secretion and induces CD83 and CD86 (figure 1A). To generate CAR-DCs, we used bone marrow cells from SAM domain and HD domain-containing protein 1 knockout mice (SAMHD1 KO) mice. SAMHD1 acts to restrict lentiviral vector transduction of myeloid cells, and as a result, knock-out DCs are transduced with an approximate fivefold increase in titer by lentiviral vectors compared with wild-type.31 The knock-out bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) were transduced with the HER2.CAR and CD40L-HER2.CAR lentiviral vectors and injected the following day intratumorally or intravenously into C57BL/6 mice bearing B16.HER2 tumors that had been initiated 10 days earlier (figure 1B). The results showed that the HER2.CAR vector-transduced DCs had a modest effect on tumor growth. The addition of CD40L to the vector significantly increased the potency of the antitumor response. The CAR-DCs were similarly effective by intravenous and intratumoral injection (figure 1B). DCs expressing CD40L alone or untransduced had no effect on tumor size (online supplemental figure 1). To determine if CAR-DCs increased T cell functionality in the TME by i.t. CAR-DCs treatment, intracellular IFNγ levels in the CD8+and CD4+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were measured. The results showed that IFNγ levels increased in the CD8+TILs of mice treated with the HER2.CAR-DCs and CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs (figure 1C). IFNγ levels also increased in the CD4+TILs. To determine the life span of CAR-DCs in vivo, we injected and then inoculated them with B16.HER2 cells at increasing times post-treatment. The results showed that the CAR-DCs were active when injected up to 14 days prior to tumor inoculation but became ineffective by 60 days post-treatment, suggesting that they have a limited life span in vivo (figure 1D).

CAR-DCs tissue localization

To determine the tissue localization of the injected DCs, CAR-DCs were generated with a lentiviral vector encoding the HER2.CAR and containing a GFP.NLuc reporter cassette (CD40L-HER2.CAR-GFP.NLuc) or with a vector containing the reporter cassette alone (GFP.NLuc). The DCs were injected intratumorally or intravenously, and 3 days later, the mice were visualized by live imaging. The HER2.CAR and control vector DCs injected intratumorally primarily localized to the tumor while DCs injected intravenously localized to the spleen and liver, with a lesser portion (about 13-fold fewer in tumor compared with spleen per mass of tissue) localized to the tumor (online supplemental figure 3). While intravenously injected CAR-DCs were only partially localized to the tumor, they were effective at suppressing tumor growth, suggesting that they can act outside of the tumor to stimulate T cells. The localization of CD40L-HER2.CAR-GFP.NLuc and control GFP.NLuc DCs was similar, indicating that the incorporation of the HER2.CAR gene did not affect tissue localization.

Tumor suppression is mediated by CD8+ T cells

To determine the mechanism of tumor growth suppression, we tested the ability of the CAR-DCs to kill tumor cells or to stimulate cytolytic CD8+T cells. For this, we injected B16.HER2 tumor-bearing mice with CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs or control untransduced DCs (figure 2A). After 10 days, the tumors were excised and the TILs were separated into CD8+and CD8− subpopulations. To test their cytolytic activity, the CD8+and CD8− populations were incubated with a fixed number of CFSE-labeled B16.HER2 or control B16.OVA target cells over a range of E:T ratios (0:1, 1:1, 5:1 and 10:1). After 24 hours, the number of lysed target cells was quantified by flow cytometry. The results showed that the CD8- TILs from mice treated with CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs had minor cytotoxicity against B16.HER2, most likely due to either cytolytic CD4+cells or to residual CD8+cells, and lacked cytolytic activity against B16.OVA targets (figure 2B). The CD8+TILs from CD40L.HER2.CAR-DCs-treated mice were cytolytic against B16.HER2 targets, and the activity was greater than that of CD8+TILs from mice treated with untransduced DCs. The CD8+TILs had low-level activity against control B16.OVA targets, most likely the result of T cells specific for tumor antigens other than HER2.

To determine whether the CD8+T cells were sufficient to suppress tumor growth, we tested the CAR-DCs by adoptive transfer. CD8+ and CD8− TILs were isolated from CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs-treated or control mice and injected into tumor-bearing mice. Tumor size was measured over the next 10 days. The results showed that CD8+TILs from the CD40L-HER2.CAR-treated mice suppressed tumor growth, while those from control-treated mice were inactive (figure 2C). The CD8- population also had a modest effect on tumor growth, an effect that could have resulted from cytolytic CD4+cells or residual CD8+T cells. An analysis of the cytolytic effect of the CAR-DCs on CFSE-labeled B16.HER2 targets confirmed that the DCs were inefficient phagocytes (figure 2D).

Requirement for CAR signal transduction

The intracellular signal transduction domain of the HER2.CAR is derived from the CD3ζ chain and is thus optimized for signal transduction in T cells. Whether it would mediate signal transduction in DCs and whether signaling is required for antitumor activity was not known. To address these questions, we deleted the CD3ζ domain in the HER2.CAR lentiviral vector (HER2.CAR-ΔCD3ζ) (figure 3A). We then generated HER2.CAR-DCs and tested their antitumor activity. The results showed that CD3ζ-deleted HER2.CAR-DCs lacked antitumor activity (figure 3B). The results suggest that signal transduction by the HER.CAR is active in DCs and is required for antitumor activity.

CAR-DCs antitumor activity is MHC class I dependent

We hypothesized that CAR-DCs antitumor activity acts through MHC class I tumor antigen presentation to CD8+T cells. To test the hypothesis, we tested antitumor potential using DCs derived from β2 microglobulin knock-out mouse (β2 KO) bone marrow cells that lack cell surface MHC class-I. CAR-DCs were generated from control C57BL/6, SAMHD1 KO, and β2 KO mice and their antitumor potential was tested in B16.HER2 tumor-bearing mice (figure 4A). Transduction with a GFP-expressing lentiviral vector showed that 18%–19% of the C57BL/6 and β2 KO BMDCs and 38.4% of the SAMHD1 KO BMDCs were transduced (figure 4B). The results showed that the β2 KO CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs failed to suppress tumor growth, in comparison to those from control C57BL/6 mice and SAMHD1 KO (figure 4C). In addition, we isolated CD8+ and CD4+ TILs and tested their IFNγ levels and cytotoxicity against B16.HER2 targets. CD8+TILs from the β2 KO CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs treated mice did not upregulate IFNγ+ (figure 4D) and lacked detectable cytolytic activity against B16.HER2 cells (figure 4E). CD4+TILs from the β2 KO HER2.CAR mice also had reduced cytolytic activity as compared with the wild-type HER.CAR-DCs (figure 4E). The results suggest that the CAR-DCs cross-present tumor antigen to CD8+T cells to generate the antitumor response.

Synergy with a checkpoint inhibitor

Checkpoint activation of T cells in the TME results in T cell exhaustion that limits their ability to suppress tumor growth. We previously showed that a PD-1 microbody, in which the PD-1 extracellular domain was fused to a human IgG CH3 domain (figure 5A) reversed T cell exhaustion by preventing checkpoint activation through programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1).3032 To determine whether the checkpoint inhibitor would augment the antitumor activity of CAR-DCs, we constructed lentiviral vectors that coexpressed HER2.CAR, CD40L, and PD-1 microbody (figure 5B). To test their antitumor activity of vector-encoded checkpoint inhibitor and compare with a monoclonal antibody checkpoint inhibitor, an experimental group was included in which mice were multiply injected with anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody, with and without CAR-DCs treatment (figure 5C). We found that DCs expressing CD40L in combination with anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody had little effect on tumor growth (figure 5C). CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs had a moderate effect on tumor growth, and this was only slightly improved by anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody treatment. In contrast, CAR-DCs expressing CD40L and the PD-1 microbody strongly suppressed tumor growth. IFNγ levels were elevated to a similar extent in the CD8+T cells of mice treated with HER2.CAR-DCs co-expressing PD-1 microbody or treated with anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (figure 5D). IFNγ levels were also increased in the CD4+TILs. CTLA-4 and TIM-3 exhaustion marker levels were decreased in the CD8+and CD4+ TILs of the mice, an effect that was more pronounced for the PD-1 microbody vector compared with injected monoclonal antibody (figure 5E). The results suggest that checkpoint inhibition by the vector-encoded checkpoint inhibitor was effective in preventing T-cell exhaustion and augmented the antitumor effect of CAR-DC therapy.

Vector-encoded GM-CSF increases CAR-DC numbers

While CAR-T cells maintain the ability to expand their numbers in response to continuous antigen stimulation, CAR-DCs are terminally differentiated cells that do not replicate, limiting their numbers and half-life.33 A means to increase the number of CAR-DCs would increase their longevity and durability of the antitumor effect. The cytokine GM-CSF promotes the replication of immature DCs and their differentiation into cDC1s, improving their survival in vivo.34 35 GM-CSF has been used in several studies to stimulate antitumor responses. Melanoma cells engineered to secrete GM-CSF were found to stimulate the proliferation of antitumor CD4+and CD8+ T cells in mice through a DC-dependent pathway, increasing their antitumor potential,36 and iPSC-derived, GM-CSF-producing myeloid cells were found to promote CD8+T cell proliferation and tumor infiltration.35 Injection of mice with recombinant GM-CSF had little effect on DC abundance, but an adenovirus vector expressing GM-CSF caused a 200-fold increase in the number of splenic DCs.37 In light of these findings, we tested whether CAR-DC numbers could be increased by expressing GM-CSF in the lentiviral vector. To answer this question, we constructed a lentiviral vector that encoded GM-CSF and contained a GFP/luciferase reporter cassette (GFP.NLuc-GM-CSF) (online supplemental figure 4A). The vector caused high-level production of supernatant GM-CSF in transduced 293 T cells (online supplemental figure 4B). In mice injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with transduced DCs, luciferase luminescence increased over a 10-day period. In contrast, in mice injected with a control vector, luminescence decreased over the same time period (online supplemental figure 4C). Moreover, visual examination of the mouse spleen showed that the GM-CSF-encoding vector caused readily apparent splenomegaly (online supplemental figure 4D). The results suggested that the vector-encoded GM-CSF caused the DCs to proliferate.

To determine whether vector-encoded GM-CSF would enhance CAR-DCs’ antitumor activity, we introduced the GM-CSF gene into the CD40L-HER2.CAR and PD1.mb-CD40L-HER2.CAR lentiviral vectors (figure 6A). The vectors were used to generate CAR-DCs, which were then tested for antitumor activity by injection 10 days following tumor inoculation. The results showed that CAR-DCs expressing GM-CSF and CD40L-HER2.CAR had increased antitumor potential. CAR-DCs expressing GM-CSF, with or without the PD-1 microbody, also potentially suppressed tumor growth (figure 6B). In addition, we tested whether the CAR-DCs would be effective at controlling the growth of a tumor initiated 7 days post CAR-DCs injection. The results showed that CAR-DCs expressing GM-CSF gene, with or without the PD-1 microbody, significantly delayed the tumor growth for about 28 days (figure 6C) and then the tumors started to grow again (data not shown). It was unclear as to whether there was a significant difference for the vector expressing the PD-1 microbody with or without GM-CSF as both fully suppressed tumor growth; however, an analysis of survival showed that CAR-DCs expressing GM-CSF extended the lifespan of tumor-bearing mice. At 40 days post-tumor initiation, there was no mortality of mice treated with CAR-DCs expressing GM-CSF. In contrast, at 32 days, there was 50% mortality for mice treated with CAR-DCs not expressing GM-CSF (figure 6D).

Discussion

CAR-T cell therapy has been successful for the treatment of leukemias and lymphomas but less effective against solid tumors.1912 The findings reported here suggest that CAR-DCs could be an alternative therapy that will be more effective as a therapy for solid tumors. The findings of this study show that CAR-DCs armed with a CAR that targeted HER2.CAR suppressed the growth of HER2-expressing melanoma. The CAR-DCs were effective when injected either directly into the tumor or intravenously. Co-expression of CD40L skewed the T cell response to a proinflammatory Th1 phenotype, which increased the antitumor potential of the responding T cells. CD40L induces Th1 cytokines and induces CD83 and CD86, which are proteins that increase DC’s ability to activate T cells. GM-CSF, HER2.CAR, and PD-1 microbody did not have any effect on CD83 or CD86 (not shown). The CAR-DCs increased the number and functionality of antitumor CD4+and CD8+ T cells as judged by the numbers of intratumor IFNγ+ expressing CD8+ T cells. Co-expression by the CAR-DCs of a soluble PD-1 acted as a checkpoint inhibitor that prevented exhaustion of the responding T cells and enhanced their antitumor effect.

Antitumor activity was dependent on MHC class I expression by the DCs, suggesting that the antitumor effect required was dependent on antigen presentation by the transduced DCs to CD8+T cells on MHC class I. Analysis of the TILs showed that the CAR-DCs stimulated HER2-specific CD8+and CD4+ T cells. Most of the antitumor response was mediated by MHC class I-restricted CD8+T cells but there was also a significant CD4+T cell response. MHC class I-restricted CD4+T cells have previously been reported.38 Responding T cells also showed significant cytolytic activity against HER2-negative B16 cells, suggesting that epitope spreading to tumor antigens other than the HER2 targeted by the HER2.CAR had occurred. Evidence of epitope spreading has been reported in CAR-T cell therapy, but whether its level achieves a meaningful component of therapeutic efficacy is not clear.39 Epitope spreading in CAR-DCs therapy could play a significant role stemming from antigen uptake and presentation to T cells, which would be valuable as a means to counteract the selection of variant tumor cells that have lost the CAR-targeted antigen, a phenomenon that can be problematic in CAR-T therapy.40

The HER2.CAR used in this study has a CD3z signal transduction domain. In T cells, CD3ζ signals through ZAP70. In DCs, which do not express ZAP70, the domain signals through Syk.41 CD3ζ was functional in the CAR-DCs as deletion of the domain from the HER2.CAR resulted in the loss of antitumor activity, a finding consistent with results for CAR-macrophages.21 At least five generations of CARs have been described, each with different signal transduction domains. Which of these will be optimal in DCs is not yet known.

Coexpression of the PD-1 microbody checkpoint inhibitor in the CAR-DCs enhanced antitumor activity. The vector-encoded checkpoint inhibitor was more effective than multiple injections of anti-PD-L1 antibody. Previously, it was reported that the microbody binds PD-L1 with higher affinity than anti-PD-L1 antibody.30 42 It seems likely that sustained, localized expression also plays a role in synergizing the antitumor activity. Vectored expression of the checkpoint inhibitor may benefit from long-term, low-level production by the CAR-DCs. Release of the checkpoint inhibitor by the CAR-DCs as they interact with responding T cells may be a particularly effective means by which to activate T cells. Moreover, vectored expression of the checkpoint inhibitor obviates the need for multiple injections of large amounts of recombinant monoclonal antibody. We previously showed that the inhibitor is effective at enhancing the effectiveness of a DC-based vaccine against HIV in a humanized mouse model.30

In this study, we used CAR-DCs from SAMHD1 KO mice which are transduced with an approximate fivefold increase by lentiviral vectors.31 In the case of human DCs, lentiviral vector transduction efficiency can be similarly increased by the use of vectors that package Vpx, an HIV-2 and SIV accessory protein that degrades SAMHD14347 (online supplemental figure 4). We previously reported on the use of Vpx-containing lentiviral vectors in the humanized mice in the development of a DC-based vaccine for HIV-1.

An obstacle to the clinical use of CAR-DCs is their inability to proliferate in vivo, a problem not faced by CAR-T cells that continue to divide although terminally differentiated. Recombinant GM-CSF stimulates the proliferation of immature myeloid cells36 and has been found to have both antitumor and pro-tumor effects in preclinical models. Recombinant GM-CSF, which stimulates the proliferation of immature myeloid cells,36 has been found to have both antitumor and pro-tumor effects in preclinical models.48 As a strategy to increase CAR-DC numbers and durability, we introduced a gene encoding GM-CSF into the CAR vector. The vector increased the number of CAR-DCs in mice and increased the durability of the antitumor response. While the vector did not appear to affect the health of the mice, it caused splenomegaly, raising potential concerns for use of this strategy clinically. Strategies to silence the expression of lentiviral vectors are under development.49

With the success of CAR-T therapies for blood cancers, better treatments for solid tumors remain a high priority. CAR-macrophages and CAR-DCs present a possible means toward this end. While the findings of this study support the promise of this approach, future studies are needed to optimize CAR-T signal transduction, to evaluate effectiveness for different solid tumor types and for methods to increase cell numbers and durability.

Limitations

HER2 is not typically expressed on melanoma cells. It was used here on engineered B16 cells as these are a difficult tumor to suppress. This tested the potency of the CAR-DCs. It was possible that the mice made an antibody response to human HER2 on the B16 cells. While we did not test for such antibodies in the treated mice, the melanoma grew robustly, suggesting that there is no functional anti-HER2 antibody response. We also did not test for anti-CAR antibody, which could have limited the life span of the CAR-DCs. For use in humans, the CAR would not be immunogenic as it is of human origin. The CAR-DCs did not eliminate the tumor; they just delayed growth. In humans, tumors grow at a much slower rate than in mice models, and thus, the responding T cells might be able to control tumor growth over a longer time. GM-CSF-differentiated BMDCs are reported to consist of a mixture of macrophages and DCs,50 and we did not sort to purify contaminating macrophages; however, analysis of the differentiated DCs by flow cytometry showed that they have the phenotype of DCs with a small percentage of contaminating BMDMs (online supplemental figure 5).

Materials and methods

Animal studies

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Taconic Biosciences. B6.129P2-β2mtm1Unc/J (β2-microglobulin (β2M) KO) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. SAMHD1 KO mice were provided by Axel Roers (University of Technology, Dresden, Germany).

Plasmids

To construct pLenti.CD40L-HER2.CAR, a DNA fragment encoding murine CD40L was amplified from pLenti.CD40L31 32 with a forward primer containing a 5’ Xba-I site and a reverse primer encoding the P2A sequence. An amplicon encoding the HER2.CAR was amplified from pLenti.EF1α-HER2.CAR with a forward primer encoding the P2A motif and a reverse primer containing a 3’ Sal-I site. The CD40L and HER2.CAR amplicons were fused by overlap extension PCR using the external primers. The fused amplicon was then digested with Xba-I and Sal-I and ligated to pLenti.CMV.GFP.puro (Addgene). To construct pLenti.PD-1mb.CD40L.HER2.CAR, an amplicon was generated encoding the PD-1 microbody (PD1.mb) sequence with a 5’-XbaI site and 3’-His-tag and P2A motif. The amplicon was fused to a CD40L amplicon by overlap extension PCR. The fused amplicon was cleaved with Xba-I and Sal-I and ligated to cleaved pLenti.CMV.GFP.puro. To construct pLenti.CD40L.HER2.CAR-GM-CSF and pLenti.PD1.CD40L.HER2.CAR-GM-CSF, a GM-CSF encoding amplicon was generated by the amplification of with a forward primer containing a 5’ EcoR-I site and a reverse primer containing a 3’ Xho-I site. Murine GM-CSF/CSF2 cDNA ORF Clone (Sino Biological) was used as a template. The amplicon was cleaved with EcoR-I and Xho-I and ligated to cleaved pLenti.CD40L.HER2.CAR or pLenti.PD1.CD40L.HER2.CAR. The construct the EF1α-HER2.CAR-ΔCD3ζ vector, HER2.CAR-ΔCD3ζ was amplified with a forward primer containing a 5’ Xba-I site and a reverse primer containing a 3’ Sal-I site, cleaved with Xba-I and Sal-I and ligated to cleaved pLenti.CMV.GFP.puro. To generate pLenti.GFP.NLuc-GM-CSF, a GM-CSF encoding amplicon was generated by the amplification of GM-CSF with a forward primer containing a 5’ EcoR-I site and reverse primer containing a 3’ Xho-I site. The amplicon was cleaved with Xba-I and Sal-I and ligated to similarly cleaved pLenti.CMV.GFP.NLuc.31 32 All plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequence analysis. pMDL, pMDLchp6, and Vpx were previously described.30 43

Cells

293T and B16.HER2 cells were cultured in DMEM/10% FBS at 37°C in 5% CO2. B16.OVA cells were cultured in DMEM/10% FBS with the addition of 0.4 mg/mL G418. BMDCs were prepared as previously described.31 32 Briefly, bone marrow cells were harvested from mouse femurs. The cells were differentiated in RPMI/10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol containing 10 ng/mL murine GM-CSF. The medium was replenished and the non-adherent cells were harvested on day 8. Monocytes were purified by plastic adherence and cultured in medium supplemented with 110 U/mL GM-CSF and 282 U/mL IL-4 to generate MDDCs.

Lentiviral vector preparation

Lentiviral vector stocks were prepared as previously described.31 32 The virus was titered on 293 T cells by flow cytometry for the number of GFP+, CD40L+ (anti-CD40L-PE-Cy7) or CD340+ (murine anti-CD340-FITC (erbB2/HER-2) antibody (Biolegend).

Antibodies and flow cytometry

TILs were blocked by treating for 30 min at 4°C with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody and then stained with fixable viability dye eFluor 450 (eBioscience). Antibodies for cell surface staining were Alexa 700-anti-CD45 (BD Biosciences), PerCP Cy5.5 anti-CD8a, APC-Cy7 anti-CD4, PE anti-CTLA-4 and APC anti-TIM-3 antibodies (BioLegend). For intracellular staining of IFNγ, the cells were fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with PBS/0.1% saponin. The cells were then stained with PE anti-IFNγ antibody (Biolegend). The cells were analyzed on a CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Life Sciences, V.10).

Prophylactic and therapeutic vaccination

For therapeutic vaccination, C57BL/6 mice were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) with 2.5×105 B16.HER2 cells mixed 1:1 with Matrigel basement membrane mix (Corning). At 10 days postinoculation, the mice were injected intravenously or intratumorally with 1×106 BMDCs that had been transduced the previous day with lentiviral vector at MOI=5. For checkpoint inhibitor experiments, the mice were injected i.p. three times every 3 days with 50 µg anti-PD-L1 antibody (BioTek Instruments). For prophylactic vaccination, C57BL/6 mice were injected i.v. with 1×106 transduced BMDCs and 7 days later injected s.c. with 2.5×105 B16.HER2 cells in Matrigel basement membrane matrix. The mice were assessed daily for signs of overt illness and monitored for tumor growth. Animals were euthanized at experimental termination or when predetermined IACUC rodent health endpoints were reached.

TIL isolation

Tumors were harvested and minced using a razor blade in dissociation buffer (RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1 mg/mL Collagenase IV (Thermo Scientific) and 2 U/mL DNase I (Zymo Research). After a 30 min incubation at 37°C, the cells were passed through a 100 µm mesh filter. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1×ACK lysis buffer and resuspended in PBS or RPMI/10%FBS.

In vitro cytolysis assay

The cytolytic activity of CD8+and CD4+ TILs and transduced DCs was measured in a flow-cytometry-based assay. CD8+and CD4+ TILs were isolated from tumor samples on CD8 Microbeads and CD4 Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. B16.HER2 and B16.OVA target cells were stained with 5 µM CFSE (Invitrogen). A fixed number of target cells (5×104) was mixed with different numbers of effector TILs in the wells of a 96-well tissue culture plate. After overnight incubation, the cells were stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor450 (Invitrogen), and the number of live and dead target cells was quantified on an LSR-II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Adoptive transfer

C57BL/6 mice were inoculated s.c. with 2.5×105 B16.HER2 cells mixed 1:1 with Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix. 10 days postinoculation, the mice were treated with untransduced CD40L-HER2.CAR-DCs or control DCs. Seven days post-treatment, the tumors were harvested, and the TILs were separated into CD8+and CD8- populations on CD8 UltraPure Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec). Dead cells were removed with a dead cell removal kit (Miltenyi Biotec). The cells (1×106) were injected intratumorally into tumor-bearing recipient mice.

Luciferase activity assay

B16.HER2 tumor-bearing mice were injected with pLenti.CD40L-HER2.CAR-GFP.NLuc or pLenti.GFP.NLuc viruses (5×106 IU) or were injected intravenously or intratumorally with transduced BMDCs (1×106). The mice were euthanized 3 days later, and the organs were harvested and then homogenized in cold PBS at 10% weight/volume in lysing matrix D tubes (MP Biomedicals) using a Fastprep-24 5G homogenizer (MP Biomedicals). The homogenized tissue supernatant was mixed 1:1 with Nano-Glo luciferase assay reagent (Promega) in the wells of a black 96-well F bottom plate. After 3 min incubation at room temperature, luminescence was quantified on an Envision 2103 Multi-label plate reader (Perkin Elmer).

Statistics

Experiments were repeated two or three times, and representative results are shown. Statistical significance was determined by the two-tailed, unpaired t-test. Statistical significance was calculated with GraphPad Prism V.7.0e, based on two-sided testing or nonparametric analysis of variance test. CIs are shown as the mean±SEM. (*p≤0.05, **p≤0.01, ***p≤0.001, ****p≤0.0001).
