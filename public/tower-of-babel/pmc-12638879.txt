Ex vivo perfusion recellularization of acellular rat hindlimbs for vascular composite allotransplantation
Authors: Aisha Adil, Felor Biniazan, Valentina Shamoun, Golnaz Karoubi, Siba Haykal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular composite allotransplantation (VCA) is a promising reconstructive surgical option for severe tissue loss. Autologous reconstructive methods are limited by donor tissue availability and donor site morbidity. Long-term immunosuppression regimens have severe side effects that limit the clinical implications of VCA. Tissue engineered composite grafts using decellularization and recellularization represent a potential solution to this. We present ex vivo recellularization of decellularized rat hindlimbs using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and L6 rat myoblasts. HUVECs and L6 rat myoblasts were seeded in the vascular and muscle compartments, respectively and cultured up to 7 days ex vivo. We report cell engraftment in femoral vessels, small vessels located distally in muscle, and cell clusters in muscle, with phenotypic characterization in both tissue types. Formation of a neo-endothelium and nascent myotube formation was observed. Cell survival in both vessels and muscle was also demonstrated. These findings provide a foundational basis for a proof-of-concept small animal model for recellularization of composite tissues for VCA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25223-w.Keywords: Vascularized composite allotransplantation, Recellularization, Acellular scaffolds, Tissue engineeringSubject terms: Cell adhesion, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Tumor resections, traumatic injuries, severe burns, and congenital anomalies can result in large volumetric soft tissue loss leading to large tissue defects. Such defects can impede quality of life, cause functional deficits, and require complex surgical reconstruction1,2. Restoration of complex tissues is a longstanding challenge in reconstructive surgery. While the use of autologous tissues has allowed significant progress in the approaches for reconstruction, there are several limitations for its use in composite tissue reconstruction. Some of these include limited donor tissue availability, need for multiple surgical interventions, and subsequent donor site morbidity3,4. Vascular composite allotransplantation (VCA) is an emerging reconstructive avenue that holds strong promise in allowing the transplantation of multiple complex tissues (e.g. skin, vessel, nerve, muscle, bone) as a composite subunit between donor and recipient. VCA not only allows aesthetic restoration but also serves promising potential for functional restoration.

Despite its promise, VCA requires life-long immunosuppression regimens that have adverse side effects such as but not limited to opportunistic infections, malignancies, and organ toxicity4,5. The use of bioengineered tissues presents a potential solution. Perfusion decellularization and recellularization offers the use of decellularized scaffolds with retained extracellular matrix (ECM) that can be repopulated with recipient-specific cells. ECM-based scaffolds have been evidently effective given they retain endogenous native components5–7. Using decellularized scaffolds offers an advantage in this regard such as tissue-specific composition, relevant structural integrity, and biochemical cues from the retained matrix7. Being able to repopulate these scaffolds with recipient-specific cells can result in personalized transplants for recipients.

To achieve this scale of bioengineering composite tissues for VCA, we require foundational studies where our proof-of-concept perfusion decellularized rat hindlimb VCA model will be useful8. Building on our established and previously described ex vivo decellularization of a composite rat hindlimb, herein we report the first ex vivo recellularization of decellularized rat hindlimb scaffolds with endothelial and muscle progenitor cells using dynamic perfusion-recellularization up to 7 days.

Results

Perfusion recellularization of decellularized femoral vessels demonstrated partial intravascular cell engraftment

Ex vivo perfusion recellularization of decellularized composite rat hindlimbs was achieved after 7 days through recellularization of the vessels using 20 × 106 HUVECs via arterial perfusion. Relative to native vessels (Fig. 1b-d), decellularized vessels showed loss of nuclei from all tunica layers by H&E and DAPI staining (Fig. 1e-g). After recellularization, detection of cell engraftment in the femoral vessels was determined through H&E and DAPI staining after 7 days (Fig. 1h-j). The H&E staining demonstrated evidence of cells lining the intimal layer of vessels where flat cells were observed (Fig. 1h, j). Luminal coverage however showed some bare areas along the intimal layer of vessels. DAPI staining confirmed intraluminal cell engraftment found in H&E staining (Fig. 1i).

Small vessels show intravascular cell engraftment after 7 days of perfusion recellularization

Small vessels located distally in the muscle showed patterns of cell engraftment across five rat hindlimb recellularizations after 7 days. H&E staining showed cells lining in a monolayer along the intravascular layer of small vessels across all recellularizations (Fig. 2a, b). Cells were also found in clusters in some small vessels (Fig. 2c). DAPI staining confirmed these patterns where DAPI-stained nuclei were found both consistently lining the intimal layer and in cluster form (Fig. 2d-f).

Neo-endothelium formation observed in recellularized femoral and small vessels

Femoral vessels showed expression of CD31 along the intimal layer in select locations. CD31 expression was consistent in some locations of the intima (Fig. 3c, d). Several small vessels located distally showed consistent lining of CD31 along the intimal layer, indicating a neo-endothelium formation (Fig. 3d, f). Clusters of cells were also observed, similar to H&E and DAPI staining (Fig. 3e, f). In parallel, native vessels showed prominent CD31 expression (Fig. 3a) whereas decellularized vessels showed lack of CD31 staining (Fig. 3b).

Localization of cell engraftment demonstrates maintained perfusable arterio-venous loop after 7-day perfusion recellularization

To determine the extent of recellularization and whether cells were engrafting along the arterio-venous loop post-recellularization, evidence of cell engraftment across different vessel types was sought. Femoral arteries, femoral veins, arterioles, venules, and capillaries were histologically assessed from each recellularization. Each vessel type on average showed presence of cells throughout recellularizations (Fig. 4a-c). Cells were consistently present in arterioles (7.866 ± 2.804), venules (11.933 ± 3.061), and femoral veins (3.666 ± 0.837) with the least number of cells found in capillaries (0.733 ± 0.3231) and femoral arteries (1.133 ± 0.512) (Fig. 4a, b). Venules had significantly more cells than femoral veins (p = 0.0439), femoral arteries (p = 0.057), and capillaries (p = 0.0041) throughout the recellularizations (Fig. 4b).

Muscle recellularization shows cell clusters after 7 days ex vivo culturing

The muscle was recellularized simultaneously with the vascular recellularization described above whereby 20 × 106 rat myoblasts were suspended in medium and delivered via needle injections across multiple locations of the decellularized muscle in the rat hindlimb scaffold. H&E and DAPI staining shows aggregates of cells. Cells were densely populated together, and cell nuclei could be observed distinctively. Recellularized cells were consistently found to be located at the injection sites and clustered together (Fig. 5a-f).

Corresponding H&E and DAPI staining of native and decellularized muscle (n = 3) provided in Supplemental Fig. 3.

Nascent myotube formation detected in recellularized muscle

To determine the phenotypic state of the myoblasts after 7 days of ex vivo culturing, myosin heavy chain (MyHC) was assessed (Fig. 6a-f). MyHC expression was observed around some DAPI-stained nuclei within the clusters of repopulated cells. Morphologically, these MyHC-positive cells had DAPI-stained nuclei in close proximity together and expression of MyHC surrounding them. No elongated myotube morphology indicative of mature myotube formation was observed surrounding these MyHC-expressing cells (Fig. 6g-i).

Recellularized muscle and large and small vessels show relative cell survival after 7-day ex vivo culturing

We evaluated cell survival using TUNEL staining for apoptosis (Fig. 7a-d). The relative number of apoptotic cells to the total number of cells stained with DAPI were measured in both recellularized muscle and vessels. In recellularized muscle sections, apoptotic cells were observably dispersed in the DAPI-stained cluster of cells (Fig. 7a) and 38.50% ± 12.22% apoptosis was found (Fig. 7b). TUNEL-positive cells were observed in recellularized vessels, dispersed within the lining of cells along the intimal layer of vessels (Fig. 7c). Large femoral vessels showed 57.39% ± 11.29 apoptosis whereas small vessels showed 76.33% ± 5.94 across all recellularizations (Fig. 7d).

Discussion

Significant progress has been made in the bioengineering of decellularized scaffolds for reconstructive surgery applications yet, the next frontier remains challenging. We report the recellularization of acellular rat hindlimbs through an ex vivo perfusion bioreactor system as a proof-of-concept small animal model. Our findings offer several novel and key insights, providing a foundation for future work in the recellularization of composite tissues.

We detected cells seven days following recellularization in the femoral vessels, small vessels, and muscle compartments. Phenotypic characterization showed neo-endothelium formation in the vessels. Cells were also detected across the arteriovenous loop. Recellularized muscle showed clusters of cells and presence of nascent myotube formation. Through TUNEL, small vessels showed high apoptosis whereas cell survival was observed in large vessels and muscle compartments after seven days.

Decellularized scaffolds offer an ECM-scaffold with retained tissue-specific structure and composition that is otherwise difficult to synthesize. This scaffold can provide biochemical cues from the retained matrix during recellularization to aid cell adhesion, growth, and function5,7. This tissue engineering method therefore is appealing for complex, composite tissues used in VCA. We report this recellularization study using our previously established decellularization protocol that uses a lower concentration of SDS than typically employed for decellularization of composite tissues5,9. Our decellularization using 0.25% SDS has shown maintenance of tissue structure and major ECM proteins such as collagen and elastin (Supplemental Fig. 3, 4) across the different tissue compartments of the hindlimb8. Indeed, future work characterizing the retention of additional ECM components may provide more information on changes to the ECM and thereby, the resultant environment for cells during recellularization. It has also been recognized that SDS can be cytotoxic and damaging to the matrix, especially at higher concentrations and/or extended times of exposure, thereby causing cell death and preventing cell engraftment in downstream recellularization9. The present study however demonstrated cell engraftment after 7 days of recellularization, indicating a non-cytotoxic decellularized scaffold capable of supporting cells.

The recellularized femoral vessels showed flattened cells lining the intima with H&E and DAPI stains confirming the presence of these cells. Phenotypic characterization with CD31 positivity indicated a regenerated endothelial lining forming albeit incomplete in some areas of the femoral vessels. Interestingly, distal small vessels in the muscle showed consistent cell attachment, showing lining along the intima by H&E, DAPI, and CD31 staining. These findings represent the novelty of our work. Cells were detected and quantified across the arteriovenous loop with prominent and consistent cell detection in femoral veins, arterioles, and venules across all recellularizations in the present study. Recellularization literature has yet to show endothelial coverage distally given cells have only been detected proximal to the pedicle where cells were seeded10–12. Others have reported inconsistent and segmented cell engraftment, resulting in incomplete recellularization of composite tissues13,14. Additionally, maintaining the vasculature network is critical for the implantation of such bioengineered constructs10,15. This suggests evidence that venous outflow was potentially maintained after seven days of recellularization. Future work may entail incorporating fluorescent labelling of cells to extend on this finding. Further, to determine the functionality of the recellularized vasculature, the next step would constitute in vivo transplantations and survival studies over both short and long timepoints. Moreover, while we used a previously established cell-seeding metric, it may not be suited to the cell seeding numbers required given the difference in surface area and volume between the inner vasculature within the hindlimb16. Previous work in liver recellularization showed enhanced efficiency in cell engraftment and function using multi-step seeding relative to direct parenchymal or perfusion seeding6,17. Recellularization using both antegrade and retrograde perfusion or through both arterial and venous inlets may also allow for enhanced distribution and coverage18.

Despite the consistent cell presence and adhesion on the vascular intraluminal surface, the high rates of apoptosis may be due to a few reasons. Fluctuation of perfusion flow rates and pressures during vascular recellularization raises risks of affecting cell engraftment and potential negative effects of shear stress on the cells, especially with prolonged exposure19,20. While our perfusion parameters were based on our ex vivo decellularized hindlimb model, incorporating monitoring of flow and perfusion pressure during recellularization would be informative8,21,22. It is also possible that oxygen and nutrient delivery to sustain endothelial cells especially in small, distal vasculature became limited over time. Oxygen has limited diffusional distance which can impose hypoxic conditions and affect cell viability especially in central portions of a composite scaffold or in dense, thicker tissues19,23. Gassing the perfusion medium or incorporating oxygen-generating components as endogenous oxygen-generating supply may be helpful19,24. To support endothelial cells, growth factor supplementation for endothelial cells may also improve cell survival25. While the apoptosis findings indicate a limitation and subsequently, an area of future investigation, obtaining outflow and cell engraftment, especially consistently in distal small vasculature up to the level of capillaries and through to the venous vasculature after seven days of ex vivo culture represents a significant step forward in this field. This was previously recognized as one of the biggest challenges where cells were mostly prominent at proximal portions post- recellularization10,11,13,26. The current model can be used to further explore vascular recellularization strategies of composite tissues such as through additional cell types (e.g. vascular smooth muscle cells) or support cell populations (e.g. pericytes) across different time periods beyond 7 days.

Muscle recellularization in this rat hindlimb model also showed interesting findings. We observed clusters of cells aggregated at injection sites throughout the hindlimb. Previous work in human face, rat forelimb and genitourinary tissues across human cadavers and animal models that also used injection seeding reported similar findings where cells remained at their respective injection sites and in clusters26–30. Indeed, needle injections allow targeted cell delivery and ability to access hard-to-reach areas that may be difficult through static or perfusion seeding alone. The obvious aggregation of cells in respective injection locations may be due to high cell numbers in small injection volumes. Splitting the number of injections and reducing the cell numbers per injection may therefore result in less clustering and more distribution of cells across the decellularized muscle. Additionally, to improve the cell distribution from the clusters observed in this study, combining electrical stimulation with injection seeding may assist in the migration and maturation of the reseeded cells during recellularization, representing a potential future direction from this study31. However, in this study, an average of 38.50% of apoptotic cells were observed across clusters in recellularized muscle, indicating relative cell survival within the scaffolds. This is a significant finding in our study. Cell death may occur due to too high or too low cell densities, whereby too high cell densities may cause occlusion and too low cell densities may restrict cell-to-cell interactions that are inherent for cell survival and function6,32. Herein, luminal occlusion during vascular recellularization may have led to inadequate perfusion of media to the muscle. Monitoring perfusion pressure and flow rates during recellularization therefore remain important21.

Further, we report the expression of a muscle differentiation marker, MyHC in the clusters. It would be through further culturing and allowing more time and conditions for fusion of more myocytes that would show a mature myotube morphology, indicated by elongated tube-like structure with multiple nuclei as we did not find mature myotubes in the present study33. Previous studies that entailed seeding small sections of SDS-decellularized rat hindlimb muscle in 2D culture also showed similar MyHC expression patterns34. However, in our case of ex vivo recellularization of a whole rat hindlimb, it may be that the scaffold is sufficient to hold the cells, but additional parameters are needed to allow cells to fuse together and progress to a mature phenotype. These parameters can include enhancing the culture time, providing supplementation (e.g. growth factors) or incorporating exogenous stimulation (e.g. electrical stimulation) to mimic neuronal activity. Our ex vivo muscle recellularization findings in this model therefore have multiple exciting avenues to build upon. Our model shows potential in supporting muscle cell differentiation and therefore represents a 3D scaffold which may influence the maturation of the cells. This study can serve as a baseline for future, comparative studies assessing muscle cell differentiation, maturation, and function over longer periods of time and serve as a foundation for furthering muscle recellularization and re-functionalization studies.

Unique to VCA decellularization and recellularization studies is the multi-tissue composition of VCA grafts. Recellularizing multiple tissue compartments of such composite tissues is an existing, recognized challenge in the field but difficult to conduct given the need for multiple cell types, in relatively high quantities, and multiple forms of seeding methods/culturing to various tissue compartments at the same time. Further, different tissue compartments hold varying functional requirements which necessitate tailored reseeding and bioreactor environments. This points to the need of multimodal recellularization strategies to recapitulate the native composite tissues. Our study offers a strong starting point for repopulating different tissue compartments in a small animal model and can be used for future studies assessing long-term timepoints (7 days, 14 days) and addition of other cell types for other tissue compartments.

Rat hindlimbs have been a longstanding classical model in VCA however the advantages of using rat hindlimbs are an important consideration for its suitability as a small animal experimental model for tissue engineering VCA grafts. Rat hindlimbs contain larger vasculature, supplied by the femoral artery, which can be easier for microvascular anastomoses and replantation. Conversely, rat forelimbs contain smaller vessels, supplied by branches of axillary artery, and contain more fine functions that can result in more technically challenging procurement protocols relative to hindlimbs22. The rat hindlimb also offers larger skin and muscle compartments and has been used for muscle reinnervation and axonal regeneration studies35,36. This can be advantageous if recellularization strategies are to be tested and developed for multiple tissue compartments at the same time. Further, few rat forelimb models have been proposed37. Multiple, different rat hindlimb models have been reported with low post-operative mortality which could be adapted for such tissue engineering-based studies38. Previous comparative studies have also shown limitations in rat forelimbs such as higher perfusion pressure requirements and lower maximal flow rate tolerances relative to hindlimbs, two critical parameters in perfusion-based tissue engineering studies22.

Overall, this study presents a proof-of-concept tissue-engineered small animal model for VCA. We have demonstrated ex vivo recellularization of vessels and muscle in decellularized whole rat hindlimb. Our recellularization findings have built upon the many challenges that exist in this field. We identified cells within distal vasculature, shown maintenance of an arteriovenous loop after recellularization, and evidence of engraftment and phenotypic characterization of repopulated cells in the vessels. We also demonstrated muscle cell survival and differentiation. This can provide a foundational basis to continue developing and refining recellularization strategies for composite tissues.

Methods

Animal use

Cadaveric male Lewis rats (age 2.5–3.5 months, 300–430 g) were used for all experiments. Animals were obtained and used within 1-hour post-euthanasia from Toronto General Hospital Research Institute. Euthanasia was performed using general anesthetic (5% isoflurane) followed by carbon dioxide. All surgical procurements were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at University Health Network and Toronto General Hospital Research Institute (Toronto, ON Canada). The use of experimental animals reported in this study were performed in accordance with the animal care committee guidelines at University Health Network and Toronto General Hospital Research Institute. Reporting use of experimental animals in this study followed recommendations from ARRIVE guidelines. (Approval number #6015, January 2021)

Surgical procurement of rat hindlimbs

Surgical procurement of hindlimbs was performed as previously described8. Briefly, cadaveric rats were placed in supine position and prepared using standard sterile technique. An incision was made at the inguinal ligament and blunt dissection was performed to dissect through fat. The superior epigastric vessels were located and ligated. Through further proximal dissection, the femoral artery, vein, and nerve were located and dissected using fine forceps to obtain sufficient distance from bifurcation points. The femoral artery and vein were ligated separately using 6 − 0 sutures and circumferential dissection around the remainder of the hindlimb was subsequently performed. The femoral bone was transected mid-length. The femoral artery was then cannulated using a 24G angiocatheter (BD Angiocath IV catheters, BD) and flushed with heparinized saline until clear outflow from the femoral vein was seen.

Perfusion decellularization

Ex vivo perfusion decellularization was achieved using 0.25% SDS based on our previously described method (Supplemental Fig. 1)8. Briefly, upon constructing the ex vivo perfusion bioreactor system, cannulated hindlimbs were attached to the inlet port of the bioreactor. Once secured, the bioreactor was filled with 0.25% SDS to keep the hindlimb submerged and the bioreactor was covered with the air-tight lid. A 5 L reservoir of 0.25% SDS (Bioshop) in deionized water was made. Perfusion with 0.25% SDS was started at 1 mL/minute using a peristaltic pump (Ismatec). Perfusion was continued for 5 days with daily monitoring and replenishing of the bioreactor reservoir using the replenishing port (Supplemental Fig. 2a). After decellularization, hindlimbs were perfused with PBS (1X, supplemented with 1% antibiotic-antimycotic) for 2 days. 0.1% peracetic acid (Sigma) in 4% ethanol was then perfused for 2 h to perform sterilization. Under a biosafety cabinet, the hindlimbs were then placed in PBS (1X, supplemented with 1% antibiotic-antimycotic) and kept at 4 °C until further use. An overview of the process is depicted in Fig. 1a.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVECs) line (American Tissue Culture Collection/ATCC, USA) and L6 rat myoblasts (ATCC, USA) were used. HUVECs were maintained in culture using Endothelial Growth Medium (EGM-2) using SingleQuots Bullet Kit (Lonza, Switzerland) comprising of the following supplements: 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), hEGF, hydrocortisone, VEGF, hFGF-B, ascorbic acid, heparin, R3-IGF-1, and gentamicin/amphotericin-B. L6 rat myoblasts were cultured using DMEM and 10% FBS and kept at 60–70% confluency. Cell medium was replaced every 2 days and cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidified air.

Ex vivo perfusion bioreactor for recellularization

The bioreactor chamber construction was adapted from the design previously used for decellularization8. Changes to this construct included adjusting the circuit design to allow recirculation of culture medium (Supplemental Fig. 2b). The outlet port of the bioreactor was connected to a 3-way stopcock. A Luer connector was added to this stopcock and connected with silicone tubing. The silicone tubing was further connected with one 3-stop peristaltic pump tubing that was fitted into the peristaltic pump. Similar to the outlet tubing, the other end of the peristaltic tubing was connected with a silicone tube that connected to the 3-way stopcock at the inlet port. This resulted in a closed-loop circulation circuit design that allowed recirculation of culture medium from the bioreactor reservoir to perfuse the rat hindlimb. 300 mL of EGM-2 medium was added to the bioreactor chamber to keep the rat hindlimb submerged. This also allowed the outlet line to remain submerged and capable of extracting medium for recirculation without pulling air into the tubing circulation. The entire bioreactor circuit was perfused and therefore primed with medium prior to starting recellularization to prevent air bubbles in the circulation.

Recellularization of rat hindlimb

Using the previously established metric of seeding 1 million cells/cm3, 20 million HUVECs and 20 million myoblasts were used for recellularization16,39. Decellularized hindlimbs were pre-conditioned in an ex vivo perfusion bioreactor in an incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) with EGM-2 medium for 48 h before cell seeding. Medium was changed every 24 h. 20 million HUVECs were resuspended in 1.5 mL medium and perfused through the cannulated femoral artery. 20 million myoblasts were resuspended in 1 mL of medium and injected into muscle across the hindlimb using a 1 cc syringe. Injections were made across multiple, different locations of the decellularized muscle of the hindlimb, totalling to 10 injections. The recellularized hindlimb was connected back to the perfusion bioreactor and submerged in new medium. It was held in static culture for 2 h to allow cell attachment before beginning perfusion at 1 mL/minute. The hindlimb was maintained ex vivo in the incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidified air for 7 days, with medium changes every 24 h. Assessment for contamination and medium changes was done at 24-hour timepoints. After 7 days, biopsies of recellularized tissues were taken and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for further histological analyses.

Histology

Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h at room temperature and transferred to 70% ethanol until paraffin embedding. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, hemotoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining was performed on 5 μm sectioned paraffin slices to determine cell presence in recellularized vessel and muscle. Native and decellularized (n = 3) tissues were stained as controls. Sections were stained with DAPI where DAPI (1:5000, Abcam) was applied at room temperature for 5 min in a dark, humidified chamber, and mounted with anti-fade mountant (Abcam). Images were acquired on AperioScope CS2 Slide Scanner (Leica Biosystems, version 12.3.3.5048) for H&E and on Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Biosystems) for DAPI.

Immunostaining

5 μm sectioned paraffin sliced tissues were used for immunostaining. Primary antibodies included: myosin heavy chain (MyHC, 1:500, ab37484, Abcam), CD31 (1:100, ab28364, Abcam). Secondary antibodies included goat anti-mouse Alexa 488 (1:500, Invitrogen). Slides were warmed at 60 °C for 15–20 min prior to staining to allow adherence of tissues to slides. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, slides were washed with PBS-0.1% Triton X-100 (PBS-T). Slides were subjected to antigen retrieval using 1X Tris-EDTA buffer (ab93684, Abcam) for 10 min at 120 °C. Slides were cooled for 10–15 min, washed with PBS-T and blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Following this, sections were incubated with primary antibody, at 4 °C overnight in a moist, dark chamber. The sections were then washed with PBS-T the next day and treated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Negative controls were treated with secondary antibody only. Slides were counterstained with DAPI (1:5000) and mounted with anti-fade mountant (Abcam).

Cell nuclei quantification

Recellularized vessels from each of five recellularizations were classified by the following components of the arteriovenous loop: femoral artery, femoral vein, arteriole, venule, and capillary. Vessel diameter ranges were established using previous rat morphometric analyses for each vessel type as follows: femoral artery (450–800 μm), femoral vein (500–1200 μm), arteriole (20–150 μm), venule (40–250 μm), and capillary (< 10 μm)40–44. Images were viewed on AperioScope CS2 Slide Scanner (Leica Biosystems, version 12.3.3.5048) Three images from three different sections of each vessel type across each of the five recellularization experiments were taken and quantified based on nuclear staining. Data is presented as mean ± standard error of measure (SEM).

TUNEL staining

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase 2’-deoxyuridine 5’-triphosphate nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining was performed with the In Situ Cell Detection kit, using TMR red fluorescent marker (Roche). Staining was performed according to manufacturer instructions. Briefly, slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated using xylene and alcohol changes, respectively. Slides were incubated with Proteinase K for 20 min at 37 °C in a humidified chamber. The TUNEL reaction mixture was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. Positive control slides were treated with DNase I for 10 min at room temperature prior to adding TUNEL reaction mixture to induce DNA breaks. The negative control was treated with a label-only solution. DAPI (1:5000, Abcam) was added for 5 min at room temperature in a moist chamber and slides were subsequently mounted with an anti-fade mountant (Abcam). Images were captured on Leica SP8 confocal microscope with LAS X software. The number of apoptotic cells and DAPI-stained cells were quantified in recellularized vessel and muscle sections. Counts were conducted by 2 separate individuals and averaged. For the recellularized muscle, images from three different clusters from each of the five recellularized hindlimbs were taken. For the recellularized vessels, images from three different large vessels and three different small vessels from each of the five recellularized hindlimbs were taken. Apoptotic cells were expressed as a percentage relative to the total number of cells stained by DAPI. Data is presented as mean ± SEM.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 where data is presented as mean ± SEM. Unpaired one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was conducted for groups of more than two. Significance was considered with p value < 0.05.
