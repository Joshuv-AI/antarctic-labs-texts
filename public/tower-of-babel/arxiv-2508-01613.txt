On indecomposable involutive solutions to the Yang-Baxter equation whose squaring map is a p p -cycle
Marco Castelli, Arpan Kanrar

Abstract
. The pioneering work of Rump, which proved Gateva-Ivanova’s conjecture concerning the decomposability of square-free solutions to the Yang-Baxter equation, significantly motivated further research into the associated squaring map T T . This line of inquiry has yielded numerous decomposability theorems based on the underlying structure of T T . Two seminal questions, posed by Ramírez and Vendramin, ask about the existence of certain indecomposable involutive solutions whose squaring maps are transpositions or 3 3 -cycles. In this paper, we explore this problem in a more general setting by examining the case where T T is a p p -cycle, for an arbitrary prime number p p . Our results provide negative answers to the aforementioned questions under the assumption that the solution is latin or that its size is a prime-power. As a further application, we also present some decomposability theorems for solutions whose permutation groups are nilpotent.

Introduction

The quantum Yang–Baxter equation first emerged in theoretical physics, in a paper by C.N. Yang
[
31
]
, and in statistical mechanics, in the work of R.J. Baxter
[
2
]
. Later, it was also investigated from a mathematical perspective. In 1992, Drinfel’d
[
16
]
proposed the study of
set-theoretical
solutions, namely, solutions that act on a basis of the underlying vector space. Specifically, a
set-theoretic solution of the Yang-Baxter equation
on a non-empty set
X
is a pair
\left(X,r\right)
, where

r:X\times X\to X\times X
is a map such that the relation

(r\times id_{X})(id_{X}\times r)(r\times id_{X})=(id_{X}\times r)(r\times id_{X})(id_{X}\times r)

is satisfied. Set-theoretic solutions yield solutions to the Yang–Baxter equation via linearization. Writing a solution
(X,r)
as
r\left(x,y\right)=\left(\lambda_{x}\left(y\right)\rho_{y}\left(x\right)\right)
, with

\lambda_{x},\rho_{x}
maps from
X
into itself, for every
x\in X
, we say that
(X,r)
is
non-degenerate
if
\lambda_{x},\rho_{x}\in Sym_{X}
, for every
x\in X
, and
involutive
if
r^{2}=id_{X\times X}
.

The seminal papers by Gateva-Ivanova and Van Den Bergh
[
19
]
and Etingov, Schedler, and Soloviev
[
17
]
inspired many researchers to study involutive, non-degenerate set-theoretic solutions (which we simply refer to as
involutive solutions
). To understand and analyze an involutive solution, a successful approach is based on the notion of decomposable solution. Recall that an involutive solution
(X,r)
is said to be
decomposable
if
X
can be expressed as a disjoint union of two non-empty subsets
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
(that form a
decomposition

\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}
of
X
) such that
r(Y_{i}\times Y_{i})\subseteq Y_{i}\times Y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
(X,r)
is called
indecomposable
, otherwise. In some sense, indecomposable solutions are the fundamental building blocks of this theory (see
[
17
, Section
3
]
).

Indecomposability of solutions has been extensively studied in the last few years, and several results have been obtained by various techniques. According to
[
17
, Proposition 2.12]
an involutive solution is indecomposable if and only if the
associated permutation group
, i.e. the subgroup of
Sym(X)
generated by the maps
\lambda_{x}
, acts transitively on
X
. This fact is of crucial importance for several papers, in which indecomposable solutions whose associated permutation group belongs to a specific class have been extensively studied (see for example
[
11
,
20
,
21
,
23
]
). In
[
7
, Theorem 7]
, the indecomposability of solutions obtained by dynamical extensions was completely characterized, while in
[
6
]
and in
[
22
]
some sufficient conditions, involving the permutation group
\mathcal{G}(X)
, that guarantee the decomposability of an involutive solution were exhibited.
Several mathematicians have focused on the so-called
squaring map

T
, which is the permutation of
Sym(X)
given by
T(x):=\lambda_{x}^{-1}(x)
, for all
x\in X
. The success of this approach is due to the fact that this single permutation contains information about the decomposability of a solution. In this direction, the first paper was authored by Rump
[
26
]
, who showed that if the squaring map is the identity map, then the solution must be decomposable. In 2021, Ramírez and Vendramin provided several (in)decomposability theorems based on the cycle structure of
T

[
25
]
. In particular, they showed the following results:

1)

if
T
is an
n
-cycle, where
n=|X|
, then
(X,r)
is indecomposable;

2)

if
T
is an
(n-1)
-cycle, where
n=|X|
, then
(X,r)
is decomposable;

3)

if
T
is an
(n-2)
-cycle, where
n=|X|
and is odd, then
(X,r)
is decomposable;

4)

if
T
is an
(n-3)
-cycle, where
n=|X|
and is coprime with
3
, then
(X,r)
is decomposable.

A striking result, due to C. Mora and Sastriques
[
5
]
, provides an extension of the previous decomposability criteria and of Rump’s result. Indeed, in
[
5
, Theorem A]
it was shown that if the size of
X
and the order of the map
T
are coprime, then the solution must be decomposable. Further generalizations to non-involutive solutions are contained in
[
13
]
. However, the map
T
does not seem to be merely a tool for identifying the decomposability of a solution. In this regard, in a recent paper
[
14
]
Dietzel proved that involutive solutions of order
p^{k}
whose squaring maps are full cycles (and therefore these solutions are indecomposable) belong to the class of involutive solutions of finite
multipermutation level
if
p>2
, and he showed that involutive solutions of order
2^{k}
that are not of finite multipermutation level have an iterated-retraction of size
4
. Despite these interesting results, the relation between the structure of an indecomposable solution and its squaring map remains poorly understood. To partially fill this gap, in
[
25
]
Ramírez and Vendramin computed indecomposable solutions of size
\leq 10
and the cycle decompositions of their squaring maps. As noted in
[
25
, Section
3
]
, among small involutive solutions, there is only one indecomposable solution with a
3
-cycle as squaring map and two indecomposable solutions with a transposition as squaring map. Motivated by these pieces evidence, they asked if there are further indecomposable involutive solutions of this type (see Questions 3.18 and 3.19 of
[
25
]
). Moreover, they pointed out that, among the solutions of small order, the squaring maps of the indecomposable ones have “few” fixed points (see Question 3.17 of
[
25
]
and the comment below).

Motivated by this background, in this paper we explore the class of indecomposable solutions whose squaring map is a
p
-cycle, where
p
is an arbitrary prime number. Our strategy can be summarized as follows. First, we develop a theory of indecomposable solutions with a nilpotent permutation group, and then we use the combinatorial structure of these solutions to provide our classification results. As a consequence, our main theorems provide a negative answer to Questions 3.18 and 3.19 of
[
25
]
when we restrict to solutions with prime-power order or to latin ones. As a further application, we provide some decomposability theorems that allow us to detect decomposable solutions that are different from the ones considered in
[
5
,
6
,
22
]
.

1.
Basics on left braces and cycle sets

In this section, we provide basic definitions and results on left braces and cycle sets that are useful throughout the paper.

Following

We will indicate by

(resp.

.

1)

If

An useful tool to construct new cycle sets from a given one, introduced in

Specifically, in

2.
Indecomposable cycle sets with nilpotent permutation group

In this section, we consider indecomposable cycle sets whose permutation group
\mathcal{G}(X)
is a direct product of groups having relatively coprime orders. We will show that the structure of these cycle sets is influenced by the structure of the associated left braces.

We start with a lemma that provides a slight extension of
[
30
, Theorem 19]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(B,+,\circ)
be a left brace such that
(B,\circ)
is the direct product of subgroups
B_{i}
whose orders are relatively coprime. Then, each
B_{i}
is an ideal, and
B
is a left brace isomorphic to the direct product of
B_{i}
.

Proof.

We can assume
|B|=m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{r}
, where
|B_{i}|=m_{i}
. Let
A_{i}
be the additive subgroup of order
m_{i}
, then
A_{i}
is a characteristic subgroup, so a left ideal. Since by
[
17
, Theorem
2.15
]

(B,\circ)
is solvable,
A_{i}
is a conjugate of
B_{i}
, and the normality of
B_{i}
gives
B_{i}=A_{i}
. We obtain that each
B_{i}
is an ideal. Now, if
i,j\in\{1,...,r\}
with
i\neq j
, and
x,y\in B
with
x\in B_{i}
,
y\in B_{j}
, we have
x\circ y=x+\lambda_{x}(y)=\lambda_{x}(y)+x=\lambda_{x}(y)\circ\lambda_{\lambda_{x}(y)^{-}}(x)=\lambda_{\lambda_{x}(y)^{-}}(x)\circ\lambda_{x}(y)
and hence
\lambda_{x}(y)=y
. This gives
x_{1}\circ...\circ x_{r}=x_{1}+...+x_{r}
for all
x_{1}\in B_{1},
…,
x_{r}\in B_{r}
, and therefore the statement is proven.
∎

Below, we give the main result of this section, that extends Theorem
10
of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be a finite indecomposable cycle set such that the the permutation group is a direct product of
r
subgroups
B_{i}
whose orders are relatively coprime. Then
X
is isomorphic to the direct product of
r
cycle sets
X_{i}
such that
|X_{i}|>1
and
|X_{i}|
divides
|B_{i}|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
the left brace on
B:=\mathcal{G}(X)
is isomorphic to the direct product of the ideals
B_{i}
. By Proposition
1.12
, there exists
a\in B
such that
X
can be constructed as a cycle set on
B/K
, where
K
is a core-free subgroup of
(B,\circ)
contained in the stabilizer
B_{a}
of an element
a\in B
. Since the
\lambda
maps, the inverse operator and the
\circ
operation are defined component-wise on the ideals
B_{i}
, and the subgroup
K
is the direct product of subgroups
K_{i}
, with
K_{i}
a core-free subgroup of
B_{i}
, we obtain that
X
is isomorphic to the direct product of
r
cycle sets
X_{i}
. Since
K
is core-free, we cannot have
K_{i}=B_{i}
for some
i
, hence
|X_{i}|>1
and
|X_{i}|
divides
|B_{i}|
.
∎

Of course, the previous theorem applies when
\mathcal{G}(X)
is nilpotent and the

are the Sylow subgroups. In

the second author showed that in this case

. In this context, the following results provide additional information and a further decomposition criterion.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a finite indecomposable cycle set with nilpotent permutation group and

. Then,
\pi(o(\sigma_{x}))=\pi(|X|)=\pi(|\mathcal{G}(X)|)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}(X)
is nilpotent, the second equality follows by Theorem
2.2
(or by
[
22
, Corollary 3.10]
). Now, if
|X|=p^{n}
for some prime number

, then
\sigma_{x}
cannot be the identity by
[
25
, Theorem 3.2]
, and hence it must have order
p^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\pi(|X|)>1
, the statement follows by an easy inductive argument.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
X
be a finite cycle set with nilpotent permutation group and suppose that there is an element
x\in X
such that
\pi(o(\sigma_{x}))\neq\pi(|X|)
or
\pi(o(\sigma_{x}))\neq\pi(|\mathcal{G}(X)|)
. Then,

is decomposable.

In
[
22
]
, the second author showed that if a cycle set
X
has nilpotent permutation group and

, then

is decomposable. Proposition

and the previous corollary allow us to detect a larger class of decomposable cycle sets, as the following example shows.

Examples 2.5
.

Let
(X,\cdotp)
be the cycle set given by

,
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}:=(1,2)
, and
\sigma_{x}:=(3,4,5)
for all

. Then,

is the cyclic group of size

, but the solution is decomposable since

. Moreover, the decomposability of

does not follow from

because

.

3.
Indecomposable cycle sets with a p-cycle as squaring map

In this section, we study indecomposable cycle sets whose squaring map is a
p
-cycle, with an emphasis on the ones having prime-power size. In the first part, we present some general results and, in the core of the section, we establish the main classification results.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X
be an indecomposable finite cycle set such that the squaring map is a
p
-cycle, for a prime number
p
. Then,
X
is simple. In particular, if
|X|
is not a prime number, then
X
is irretractable and
Soc(\mathcal{G}(X))=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
X
is not simple. Let
Y
be a non-trivial epimorphic image of
X
and
f
be an epimorphism from
X
to
Y
. Then, the order of the squaring map
T_{Y}
of
Y
divides that of the squaring map
T_{X}
of
X
, hence
o(T_{Y})
is
1
or
p
.
In the first case,
Y
is square-free and by
[
26
, Theorem 1]
decomposable, a contradiction. In the second case, since by Lemma
1.9
the fibers of
f
are of the same size, we conclude that the squaring map of
X
cannot be a
p
-cycle since it moves at least
2p
elements, again a contradiction. Therefore,
X
is simple. By Proposition
1.15
if
|X|
is not a prime number then
X
is irretractable, and by
[
1
]

Soc(\mathcal{G}(X))=\{0\}
.
∎

A cycle set is said to be of
\pi
-type if
\pi(|X|)=\pi(|\mathcal{G}(X)|)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X
be a finite indecomposable cycle set. Then, there exists a cabled
(X,\cdotp_{l})
of
X
that is an indecomposable cycle set of
\pi
-type. Moreover, if
T
is a
p
-cycle for a prime number
p
, then the squaring map of
(X,\cdotp_{l})
is a
p
-cycle.

Proof.

Suppose that
|\mathcal{G}(X)|=l\cdotp t
for some number
l
such that
(l,|X|)=(l,t)=1
, and such that
\pi(t)=\pi(|X|)
. Let
(X,\cdotp_{l})
be the
l
-cabled of
X
. By Proposition
1.14
,
(X,\cdotp_{l})
is indecomposable and
\mathcal{G}(X,\cdotp_{l})=l\mathcal{G}(X)
, hence
|\mathcal{G}(X,\cdotp_{l})|=t
and the first part of the statement follows. The last part follows again by Proposition
1.14
and the fact that if
T
is a
p
-cycle with
p
prime, then
T^{k}
is again a
p
-cycle for all natural numbers

that are coprime to

.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
X
be an indecomposable finite cycle set such that the squaring map is a
p
-cycle, for a prime number
p
. Moreover, suppose that
Dis(X)
is nilpotent. Then,
|X|=p^{k}
for some natural number
k
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1

X
is simple and by
[
5
, Theorem A]

X
is a multiple of
p
. If
|X|=p
, the statement clearly follows. If
|X|\neq p
, by
[
3
, Corollary 41]
we have that
X
must have prime-power size, and the statement again follows.
∎

The following lemma, which is of crucial importance for our purposes, gives a nice combinatorial property of a (not necessarily indecomposable) cycle set.

Lemma 3.4

(Lemma 2.12,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
X
be a non-degenerate cycle set. Then, the map
M:X\times X\longrightarrow X\times X
,
(x,y)\mapsto(x\cdotp y,y\cdotp x)
is bijective.

The rest of this section is devoted to the description of indecomposable cycle sets whose squaring map is a
p
-cycle and with prime-power order. To this end, we have to show some preliminary results. From now on, we denote by
Fix(T)
the set of the fixed point under the squaring map.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be an indecomposable finite cycle set with nilpotent permutation group, and let
p
be the minimal prime that divides
|\mathcal{G}(X)|
. Then
X
has a system of blocks
I:=\{\Delta_{x}\}_{x\in X}
of size
p
and there exists
z\in X
such that the image of
\sigma_{z}
in
Sym(I)
is a
p
-cycle and if
x\in Fix(T)
then
\sigma_{x}
in
Sym(I)
is trivial.

Proof.

Since
X
is of
\pi
type, there exist two normal subgroups
H
and
K
of
\mathcal{G}(X)
such that
|H/K|=p
,
H
acts transitively on
X
and
K
acts intransitively on
X
. Then, the orbits of
K
induce a system of blocks
I
, and since
|H/K|=p
, we have that
|I|=p
. Now, consider the action of
\mathcal{G}(X)
on
I
. Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{G}(X),
the minimality condition implies
\sigma(\Delta)=\Delta
for all
\Delta\in I
, or it acts as a
p
-cycle on the elements of
I
. Therefore, if
x\in Fix(T)
, then
\sigma_{x}
in
Sym(I)
is trivial. Moreover, the image of
\mathcal{G}(X)
in
Sym(I)
, which we denote by
\mathcal{G}(X)_{I}
, is a
p
-group that acts transitively on
I
. Now, the result follows from the fact that
\{\sigma_{x}~|~x\in X\}
generates
\mathcal{G}(X)
.
∎

As noted in
[
25
, Section 3]
, inspection of small cycle sets suggests that if a cycle set
X
is indecomposable then the inequality
|Fix(T)|\leq|X|-|Fix(T)|
holds. Here, we are able to show a weaker inequality for cycle sets with a nilpotent permutation group.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be an indecomposable cycle set with nilpotent permutation group. Then,

|Fix(T)|\leq(|X|-|Fix(T)|)^{2}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.5
, there exists a system of blocks
I
of size
p
and an element
z\in X
such that
\sigma_{z}
acts as a
p
-cycle on
I
. If
|Fix(T)|>(|X|-|Fix(T)|)^{2}
by Lemma
3.4
, there exists
x\in Fix(T)
such that
x\cdotp z
and
z\cdotp x
are again in
Fix(T)
. By the cycloid equation, we have

\sigma_{z\cdotp x}\sigma_{z}=\sigma_{x\cdotp z}\sigma_{x}

and moreover
\sigma_{z\cdotp x}\sigma_{z}
acts as a
p
-cycle on
I
, while
\sigma_{x\cdotp z}\sigma_{x}
acts as the identity, a contradiction.
∎

The inequality of the previous theorem, that will be useful in the last part of this section, provides also a further decomposition criterion involving the fixed points of the diagonal map, as posed in
[
25
, Question
3.17
]
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
X
be a finite cycle set with nilpotent permutation group and

|Fix(T)|>(|X|-|Fix(T)|)^{2}.

Then
X
is decomposable.

By the previous corollary, examples of decomposable cycle sets distinct from the ones detected by Corollary
2.4
and
[
5
, Theorem A]
occur in abundance. Below, we show such an example.

Examples 3.8
.

Let
(X,\cdotp)
be the cycle set given by
X:=\{1,...,72\}
and
\sigma_{x}:=(1,2)(3,4,5)
for all
x\in X
. Then,
\mathcal{G}(X)
is the cyclic group of order
6
, hence
\pi(|X|)=\pi(|\mathcal{G}(X)|)=\pi(o(\sigma_{x}))=\{2,3\}
for all
x\in X
, and
(o(T),|X|)=6
. However,
X
is decomposable since
|Fix(X)|=66
and
(|X|-|Fix(X)|)^{2}=25.

Finally, we can present the desired classification results.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
X
be an indecomposable cycle set with a nilpotent permutation group and with squaring map
T:=(1\ldots p)
, for a prime number
p
. Then
|X|=p
if
p\neq 2
, and
|X|\in\{2,4\}
otherwise. Moreover, for each size, the cycle sets are unique.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
and Corollary
3.3
, we have that
|X|=p^{k}
for a natural number
k
and
\mathcal{G}(X)
is a
p
-group. Now, we show that
p^{k}\in\{p,p^{2}\}
. Suppose that
p^{k}\geq p^{3}
. Applying the inequality of Theorem
3.6
, we obtain
p^{k}-p\leq p^{2}
, hence
p^{k}\leq p(p+1)
that implies
p^{k-1}\leq p+1
, that is not possible. To obtain the first part of the statement, it remains to show that
p^{k}\neq p^{2}
if
p\neq 2
. Suppose that
p\neq 2
and
|X|=p^{2}
. By Proposition
3.1
,
X
is irretractable, and since
\mathcal{G}(X)
is a
p
-group by
[
15
, Theorem
3.9
]
we obtain that
X
is isomorphic to the cycle set on
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
given by
(a,x)\cdotp(b,y):=(b+x,y+\phi(b-a)
, for a suitable map
\phi
. In this way, the squaring map
T
moves at least
p(p-1)
elements and hence it cannot be a
p
-cycle, therefore the first part of the statement follows. The second part follows by
[
17
, Theorem 2.13]
and the inspection of the cycle sets of size
4
.
∎

In the previous result, the existence of a system of blocks of size
p
, with
p
a prime number dividing
|\mathcal{G}(X)|
, is of crucial importance. If we suppose the existence of such a system of blocks for the minimal prime dividing
|X|
, we can obtain a limitation on the size of
|X|
without assuming the nilpotency of
\mathcal{G}(X)
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
X
be an indecomposable cycle set, and
|Fix(T)|=|X|-m
for some positive integer
m
. Moreover, suppose that
\mathcal{G}(X)
has a system of blocks
I
of size
q
, where
q
is the minimal prime divisor of
|X|
. Then

m\leq|X|\leq k^{2}+k,

where
k:=|X|-|Fix(T^{k_{1}})|
, and
k_{1}
is the minimal positive integer such that
\pi(o(T^{k_{1}}))\subset\pi(|X|)
. In particular, if
T=(1,2)
, then
|X|\in\{2,4\}
.

Proof.

Assume
|X|>m
. First we consider the case, when
\pi(o(T))\subset\pi(|X|)
. In this case,
k_{1}=1
and
k=m
. By Proposition
3.2
, there is an integer
l
coprime with
|X|
such that
(X,\cdot_{l})
is an indecomposable cycle set of
\pi
-type and
Fix(T^{l})=Fix(T)
. Clearly,
I
is again a system of blocks of
\mathcal{G}(X,\cdot_{l})
. Since
q
divides
|X|
, thus
q
is a divisor of
|\mathcal{G}(X,\cdot_{l})|
. The squaring map of
(X,\cdot_{l})
is
T^{l}
and we have
|Fix(T^{l})|=|X|-m
. From this observation, we can assume
X
is of
\pi
-type.

The prime
q
becomes the minimal prime divisor of
|\mathcal{G}(X)|
, so for any
x\in Fix(T)
, we have that
\sigma_{x}
fixes each element of
I
. Let
X\setminus Fix(T):=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, therefore there is
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
such that
\sigma_{t}
acts on
I
as a
q
-cycle. Now, the map
a\mapsto(a\cdot t,t\cdot a)
is injective, so if
|X|-m>m^{2}
there exists
a\notin\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
such that
(t\cdot a,a\cdot t)\notin\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. By the cycloid equation, we have

\sigma_{a\cdotp t}\sigma_{a}=\sigma_{t\cdotp a}\sigma_{t},

which implies that the left-hand side fixes each element of
I
, but the other side permutes, which is absurd. Thus, we obtain
m\leq|X|\leq m^{2}+m
.

Now when,
\pi(o(T))\not\subset\pi(|X|)
, from the minimality condition of
k_{1}
, we get
(|X|,k_{1})=1
. Then
k_{1}
-cabled cycle set falls in the first case. Since the size of the cycle sets is fixed, we obtain
k\leq|X|\leq k^{2}+k
, also we have
k\leq m\leq|X|
. If
T=(1,2)
, by
[
5
, Theorem A]

2
must be a divisor of
|X|
and then
k_{1}=1
,

, and hence
2\leq|X|\leq 6
, and the last part of the statement follows by an inspection of the small cycle sets. The proof is complete.
∎

Using cabling, we can extend Theorem
3.9
to the class of indecomposable cycle sets of prime-power size without requiring the nilpotency of
\mathcal{G}(X)
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
(X,\cdotp)
be an indecomposable cycle set of order
p^{k}
and with squaring map
T:=(1\ldots p)
, for a prime number
p
. Then
|X|=p
if
p\neq 2
, and
|X|\in\{2,4\}
otherwise. Moreover, for each size, the cycle sets are unique.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
, there exists a cabling
(X,\cdotp_{l})
of
(X,\cdotp)
that is of
\pi
-type, hence
\mathcal{G}(X)
is a
p
-group, and again by Proposition
3.2
the squaring map of
(X,\cdotp_{l})
is a
p
-cycle. By Theorem
3.9
applied to
(X,\cdotp_{l})
,
|X|=p
if

and
|X|\in\{2,4\}
if

. The second part follows by
[

, Theorem 2.13]
and the inspection of the cycle sets of size

.
∎

We remark that the previous theorem answers negatively to Questions 3.18 and 3.19 of
[
25
]
when
|X|
is a prime-power. When we add an hypothesis on the left multiplications of

, we cannot have cycle set different from the one of size

, as we can see in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
X
be a finite indecomposable cycle set, and
x\in X
such that
T(x)=x
. Then
o(\sigma_{x})=o(\sigma_{x})_{+}=d
, where
d
is the Dehornoy class. Furthermore, when
(|X|,o(\sigma_{x}))=p^{s}
and

, for a prime number

, then

,
|X|=4
, and

is unique, up to isomorphisms.

Proof.

By the rules of the operations
+
and
\circ
of the left brace
\mathcal{G}(X)
, we have

\sigma_{x}^{-m}=\sigma_{x}^{-m+1}\circ\sigma_{x}^{-1}=\sigma_{x}^{-m+1}+\lambda_{\sigma_{x}^{-m+1}}(\sigma_{x}^{-1})=\sigma_{x}^{-m+1}+\sigma_{x}^{-1}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, by a standard induction on
m
, we obtain
\sigma_{x}^{-m}=m\sigma_{x}^{-1}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
X
is finite, we have that
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{x}^{-k}
for some

, therefore
\sigma_{x}^{n}=\sigma_{x}^{-kn}=kn\sigma_{x}^{-1}=n(k\sigma_{x}^{-1})=n\sigma_{x}^{-k}=n\sigma_{x}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence by the previous equalities and Proposition
1.17
the first part of the statement follows. Again by Proposition
1.17
, we have
\pi(|X|)\subseteq\pi(o(\sigma^{-1}_{x}))=\pi(o(\sigma_{x}))
, hence, when
(|X|,o(\sigma_{x}))=p^{s}
,
|X|
must be a
p
-power. Therefore, the last part of the statement follows by Theorem
3.11
.
∎

We close the section using the previous results to provide a further decomposition theorem.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
(X,\cdotp)
be a cycle set of order
p^{k}
, for an odd prime number
p
and a natural number
k>1
, and with squaring map

, where
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{s}
are disjoint cycles with

and
(p,o(\alpha_{j}))=1
for all

. Then,

is decomposable.

Suppose that
X
is indecomposable and let
l
be the product
o(\alpha_{2})\cdotp...\cdotp o(\alpha_{s})
. Then, the
l
-cabling of

is again indecomposable with squaring map equal to

. By Theorem
3.11

|X|=p
, but this contradicts

. Hence

is decomposable.
∎

4.
The latin case

As further evidence of the non-existence of non-trivial indecomposable cycle sets whose squaring map is a
p
-cycle, in this section we consider the class of latin cycle sets.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(X,\cdotp)
be a cycle set. Then,
X
is said to be
latin
if
X
is also a quasigroup.

Of course, latin cycle sets are always indecomposable and irretractable. For these cycle sets, we are able to show an inequality involving
Fix(T)
that is stronger than the one of Corollary
3.7
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
be a latin cycle set with diagonal map
T
. Then,
|Fix(T)|<\frac{|X|}{2}+1
.

Proof.

If
|Fix(T)|\geq\frac{|X|}{2}+1
then
|X|\geq 2(|X|-|Fix(T)|)+2
and this implies that if
x\in Fix(T)
, there exists
z\in Fix(T)
such that
z\neq x
and
x\cdotp z\in Fix(T)
. Then
(x\cdotp z)\cdotp(x\cdotp z)=x\cdotp z
, and
(x\cdotp z)\cdotp(x\cdotp z)=(z\cdotp x)\cdotp(z\cdotp z)=(z\cdotp x)\cdotp z
, therefore
x\cdotp z=(z\cdotp x)\cdotp z
. By the latinity of
X
we have
x=z\cdotp x
and since
x\cdotp x=x
, again by the latinity of
X
we obtain
x=z
, a contradiction.
∎

Surprisingly, there is only one latin cycle set whose
T
is a
p
-cycle. We show this in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
be a latin cycle set and suppose that
T=(1\ldots p)
, where
p
is a prime number. Then,
p=2
and
|X|=4
. Moreover,
X
is unique up to isomorphisms.

Proof.

By
[
5
, Theorem A]
,
X
is a multiple of
p
, hence there exist a natural number
k
such that
|X|=kp
. By the previous proposition, we have the inequality
|X|<2(|X|-|Fix(T)|)+2
and hence
kp<2p+2
. If
p>2
, we must have
k\in\{1,2\}
, hence
X
, being latin, would be an indecomposable and irretractable cycle set with
|X|
a square-free number, and this contradicts the main theorem of
[
12
]
. Therefore, we must have
p=2
and
|X|\in\{2,4\}
. By inspection of the small cycle sets, the only possibility is
|X|=4
, and there is only one such cycle set, up to isomorphisms.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
X
be a cycle set such that the diagonal map fixes more than
\frac{|X|}{2}+1
points. Then,
X
is not latin.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
4.2
.
∎
