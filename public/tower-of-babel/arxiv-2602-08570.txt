Approximate Cartesian Tree Matching with Substitutions
Panagiotis Charalampopoulos, Jonas Ellert, Manal Mohamed

Abstract
The Cartesian tree of a sequence captures the relative order of the sequence’s elements. In recent years, Cartesian tree matching has attracted considerable attention, particularly due to its applications in time series analysis. Consider a text T T of length n n and a pattern P P of length m m . In the exact Cartesian tree matching problem, the task is to find all length- m m fragments of T T whose Cartesian tree coincides with the Cartesian tree CT ​ ( P ) \textsf{{CT}}(P) of the pattern. Although the exact version of the problem can be solved in linear time [Park et al., TCS 2020], it remains rather restrictive; for example, it is not robust to outliers in the pattern. To overcome this limitation, we consider the approximate setting, where the goal is to identify all fragments of T T that are close to some string whose Cartesian tree matches CT ​ ( P ) \textsf{{CT}}(P) . In this work, we quantify closeness via the widely used Hamming distance metric. For a given integer parameter k > 0 k>0 , we present an algorithm that computes all fragments of T T that are at Hamming distance at most k k from a string whose Cartesian tree matches CT ​ ( P ) \textsf{{CT}}(P) . Our algorithm runs in time 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ m ⋅ k 2.5 ) \mathcal{O}(n\sqrt{m}\cdot k^{2.5}) for k ≤ m 1 / 5 k\leq m^{1/5} and in time 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ k 5 ) \mathcal{O}(nk^{5}) for k ≥ m 1 / 5 k\geq m^{1/5} , thereby improving upon the state-of-the-art 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ m ​ k ) \mathcal{O}(nmk) -time algorithm of Kim and Han [TCS 2025] in the regime k = o ⁡ ( m 1 / 4 ) k=o(m^{1/4}) . On the way to our solution, we develop a toolbox of independent interest. First, we introduce a new notion of periodicity in Cartesian trees. Then, we lift multiple well-known combinatorial and algorithmic results for string matching and periodicity in strings to Cartesian tree matching and periodicity in Cartesian trees.

Introduction

Pattern matching is a central topic in theoretical computer science and a ubiquitous task in practical applications.
In several real-world scenarios, such as stock price analysis and music analysis, one may wish to match a pattern with respect to the relative order between its sequential data points rather than their exact values.
For instance, consider the challenge of matching the performance of a stock in the past quarter to its historical performance in order to predict its future behaviour.
In this task, we are not concerned with the exact stock price but rather with its “ups and downs”.
Several notions of matching have been devised for capturing such scenarios, including Cartesian tree matching
[

Here, we focus on the Cartesian tree matching problem, which was introduced by Park et al.
[

A Cartesian tree
\textsf{{CT}}(S)
of a string
S
is a binary tree defined recursively as follows: the root of the tree corresponds to the index
i
of the minimum element in the sequence (under a fixed tie-breaking strategy); the left subtree of the root corresponds to the Cartesian tree of
S[1\mathinner{.\,.}i-1]
, and the right subtree of the root corresponds to the Cartesian tree of
S[i+1\mathinner{.\,.}\lvert S\rvert]
.
In what follows, we assume that ties are always resolved in favour of the leftmost elements.
The Cartesian tree of a string
S
can be built in linear time
[
2
]
.

We say that a string
U
CT-matches a string
V
if and only if
\textsf{{CT}}(U)=\textsf{{CT}}(V)
.
This notion of matching is well-behaved: it is a prime example of substring consistent equivalence relations
[
23
]
since, if
U
and
V
CT-match, then, for any

,
U[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
and
V[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
CT-match as well
[
29
]
.

In the Cartesian tree matching (CT-matching) problem, we are given a text
T
of length
n
and a pattern
P
of length
m
, and the goal is to compute all
m
-length fragments of
T
that CT-match
P
.
Park et al.
[
29
]
showed that this problem admits a linear-time solution.
An alternative linear-time algorithm, as well as practical solutions, were later presented in
[
32
]
.
Related problems such as dictionary CT-matching
[

32
]
and indexing for CT-matching
[

have also been studied.

Approximate Cartesian tree matching.

Similarly to the case of standard string matching, requiring exact CT-matches can be quite restrictive.
For instance, a single outlier value in the pattern (arising, for example, from an extreme but short-lived event or from noise in the data) may cause all relevant occurrences in the text to be missed.
To address this, one can instead seek approximate CT-matches of
P
in
T
, that is, fragments of
T
that are
close
to a string
X
such that
\textsf{{CT}}(X)=\textsf{{CT}}(P)
.
Here,
closeness
may be measured in terms of some distance measure such as the Hamming distance or the edit distance.
We consider the low distance regime, where an integer threshold parameter
k>0
is provided. Then, we have to report fragments of
T
at distance at most
k
from a string
X
with
\textsf{{CT}}(X)=\textsf{{CT}}(P)
.

Auvray et al.
[
1
]
recently presented an
\mathcal{O}(nm)
-time algorithm for the case of one difference (such as a swap or a substitution).
Kim and Han
[
21
]
studied the problem of computing the edit and Hamming distances of two strings under CT-matching.
They presented a dynamic-programming-based solution and, as corollaries, obtained
an
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}mk)
-time algorithm for approximate CT-matching under the edit distance
and an
\mathcal{O}(nmk)
-time algorithm for approximate CT-matching under the Hamming distance.
In this work, we focus on approximate CT-matching under the Hamming distance.

Technical overview.

It is a folklore fact that a pattern
P
of length
m
is either periodic or it has at most one exact occurrence in a text
T
of length
n\leq 3m/2

[
3
]
.
In approximate string matching, recent algorithmic breakthroughs
[
4
,
6
]
were obtained via analogous combinatorial results:
either
P
has
few
approximate occurrences in
T
, or
P
is
almost periodic
.

In the CT-matching model, it is not even clear what the right formalisation of periodicity is. Indeed, several notions of periodicity have been suggested and studied thus far
[
23
,
29
]
.
Here, we introduce a new notion of periodicity, namely
CT-block-periodicity
, that provides strong locality guarantees.
We then exploit these guarantees to show that we either have few approximate CT-matches or the pattern is almost CT-block-periodic.
Dealing with the almost CT-block-periodic case is the main technical challenge we overcome.
The workhorse of our approach for this case is a combinatorial result (see
\cref
lem:trim,lem:trim:nsv for precise statements) that allows us to compute
\textnormal{{CHd}}_{k}(T[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+m)\leadsto P)
very efficiently for almost all values of
i
when
P
is almost CT-block-periodic.
Essentially, we prove (under some extra assumptions) that, for equal-length strings
X=X_{1}Q^{r}X_{2}
and
Y=Y_{1}Q^{r}Y_{2}
with
\lvert X_{1}\rvert=\lvert Y_{1}\rvert
, the value of
\textnormal{{CHd}}_{k}(X\leadsto Y)
is the same for all choices of
r\geq 2k+1
.
1
1

1

We use
Q^{r}
for simplicity in this overview, noting that standard string periodicity implies CT-block-periodicity.
\cref
lem:trim,lem:trim:nsv are more general.

This allows us to trim aligned pairs of long CT-block-periodic fragments in
T[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+m)
and
P
, thus obtaining short strings
F
and
P^{\prime}
such that
\textnormal{{CHd}}_{k}(T[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+m)\leadsto P)=\textnormal{{CHd}}_{k}(F\leadsto P^{\prime})
, and compute the latter value in
\mathcal{O}(\lvert F\rvert\cdot k)
time.
In summary, in the case when the number of (candidate) approximate CT-matches may be large, the periodic structure allows us to verify almost all of them efficiently.

For integers

, we write

to denote

For a set

A string

over a finite alphabet
\Sigma

For

, the
i

is denoted by
X[i]
, and we use either of
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
,
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j+1)
,
X(i-1\mathinner{.\,.}j]
, and
X(i-1\mathinner{.\,.}j+1)
to denote the
substring

X[i]X[i+1]\cdots X[j]
of

.
If
i>j
, then
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
is the empty string.
We say that a string
Y
has an occurrence at position
i
of
X
if
i+\lvert Y\rvert\leq\lvert X\rvert+1
and
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+\lvert Y\rvert)=Y
.
We may refer to a substring
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
as a

if we want to emphasize that we mean the specific occurrence of
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
at position

. Two fragments are disjoint if they do not share any positions.

An integer
p>0
is a
period
of

if
X[i]=X[i+p]
for all
i\in[1,\lvert X\rvert-p]
.
The smallest period of
X
is referred to as

of

and is denoted by

.
A

in

is a fragment
Y=X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
that satisfies

and is inclusion-maximal; that is,

(or

).

Lemma 2

(Periodicity Lemma (weak version)
[
11
]
)
.

If a string
S
has periods
p
and

such that

, then

is also a period of

.

Throughout the paper, we assume that the alphabet
\Sigma
is totally ordered, and that an order comparison takes constant time.
For a string
X
, we define its

as the (positioned) character

, where

2.1
Cartesian Trees

In this work, consistent with the literature, we ensure that Cartesian trees are uniquely defined by resolving ties in favour of leftmost positions.

Definition 3
.

The Cartesian tree of a string
X
, denoted by
\textsf{{CT}}(X)
, is a rooted ordered binary tree with
\lvert X\rvert
nodes.
The Cartesian tree of an empty string is the order-zero graph.
For a non-empty string
X
,
\textsf{{CT}}(X)
is the tree obtained by creating a root node and (recursively) attaching to it
\textsf{{CT}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}j))
as a left subtree and
\textsf{{CT}}(X(j\mathinner{.\,.}\lvert X\rvert])
as a right subtree, where
X[j]
is the leftmost minimum of
X
.

Definition 4
.

We say that a string
X
CT-matches a string
Y
(or that
X
and
Y
CT-match) if and only if
\textsf{{CT}}(X)=\textsf{{CT}}(Y)
.
We denote that two strings
X
and
Y

X\approx Y
and that they do not CT-match by writing
X\not\approx Y
.

Fact 5
.

The CT-equivalence relation (
\approx
) is a transitive relation. That is, for any three strings
X
,
Y
, and
Z

X\approx Y
and
Y\approx Z
, then
X\approx Z
as well.

For strings
T[1\mathinner{.\,.}n]
(the text) and
P[1\mathinner{.\,.}m]
(the pattern), we say that

is a CT-occurrence of
P
in
T
(or that
P
CT-occurs in
T
at position

) if
P\approx T[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+\lvert P\rvert)
. The CT-matching problem consists in computing all CT-occurrences of
P
in
T

Lemma 6

(
[
29
]
)
.

Given a text

and a pattern

, all CT-occurrences of

Lemma 7

(
[

)
.

Given a text

For equal-length strings

Lemma 17

Consider a string

(1)

Lemma 19
.

Suppose that a fragment
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j)
has minimum CT-block-period

. Then, for any

such that

,

, and

, we have that

and that
X[a\mathinner{.\,.}b)
has minimum CT-block-period

.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
X[i]
is the leftmost minimum of
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j)
.
The complementary case when
X[j-1]
is the leftmost minimum can be handled in a symmetric manner using the analogues of the lemmas used below.

Fix

satisfying the conditions of the claim. If
a\neq i
, then the CT-border-period

of

implies that

X[a\mathinner{.\,.}b)\approx X[a-p\mathinner{.\,.}b-p)\approx\cdots\approx X[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+(b-a)).

Now, observe that
X[a]
is the leftmost minimum of
X[a\mathinner{.\,.}j)
by
\cref
lem:blockper_chain.
Additionally, we have
X[a\mathinner{.\,.}b-p)\approx X[a+p\mathinner{.\,.}b)
by
\cref
fact:subeq because

is a CT-border-period of
X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j)
.
Finally,

is a CT-block-period of

It remains to show that

is the minimum CT-block-period of

.

\cref

\textnormal{{PD}}(X)(a\mathinner{.\,.}b)
has a period

and
\textnormal{{PD}}(X)(i\mathinner{.\,.}j)
has a period

Since the length of the fragment is

\textnormal{{PD}}(X)(i\mathinner{.\,.}j)
also has period

.

Let

be a string.

is called a

if and only if

is not a CT-block-period of

(or

)

)
.

in a text

form arithmetic progressions with each such progression corresponding to a run with period

.

.

.

is of length

and has a CT-block-period

.
The leftmost minimum of

is at its first position.
The parent-distance array for

is

\textnormal{{PD}}(P)=[0,1,2,3,1,2]
.

Consider the text

of length

.
The parent-distance array for

is:
\textnormal{{PD}}(T)=[0,1,2,3,1,2,3,1,2,1]
.

There are two CT-occurrences of

in

:

at position

at position

Lemma 22
.

Let
P
be a pattern of length

with minimum CT-block-period

, and let

be a text of length

The exact CT-occurrences of

.

.

is at its first position. The proof for the complementary case, that is, when the leftmost minimum of

Let

.
Since

.

Consider a maximal arithmetic progression

of occurrences of

in
\textnormal{{PD}}(T)
. For

, denote

. Then, either

does not have any CT-occurrence at a position of

in

, or there exists an integer

such that both of the following hold:

in

and

is
\{d_{i}-1:i\in[\mu,s]\}

By the definition of

for all

. Hence, the

-match condition holds for all

Kim and Han
[

recently presented a dynamic programming algorithm that, given two strings

and

, computes the minimum cost of a sequence of edit operations (insertions, deletions, and substitutions) required to transform

into a string

such that

.
As noted in
[

, Section 6]
, a minor modification to their algorithm results in the following lemma for the special case when only substitutions are allowed.

Lemma 24

(see
[
21
]
)
.

Given two strings
T[1\mathinner{.\,.}m]
and
P[1\mathinner{.\,.}m]
, and an integer threshold
k>0
, we can compute
\textnormal{{CHd}}_{k}(T\leadsto P)
in
\mathcal{O}(mk)
time.

In
\cref

\cref

4.1
Analysing the Pattern

Definition 25
.

A set
\mathcal{S}
of equal-length strings is a
CT-rainbow if and only if there do not exist distinct
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S_{1}\approx S_{2}
.

Lemma 26
.

Let
m,\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
\Delta<\sqrt{m}/2
.
Given
\Delta
and a string
P[1\mathinner{.\,.}m]
, in
\mathcal{O}(m\Delta)
time, we can either compute a set of
\Delta
disjoint fragments of
P
, each of length
\lceil m/{(2\Delta)}\rceil
, that form a CT-rainbow or report a fragment of length at least
m/{(4\Delta)}
that has a CT-block-period less than
\Delta
, together with its minimal CT-block-period.

Proof.

Let
m^{\prime}=\lceil m/(2\Delta)\rceil
.
We process
P
in a left-to-right manner, attempting to identify
m^{\prime}
-length fragments to insert into an initially empty set
\mathcal{S}
,
which will remain a CT-rainbow throughout.
Whenever a fragment is inserted into
\mathcal{S}
, we find and mark all of its exact CT-occurrences in
P
in
\mathcal{O}(m)
time using
\cref
lem:exact.
For each inserted fragment, we use a unique symbol for marking.

We first insert fragment
P[1\mathinner{.\,.}m^{\prime}]
into
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
P[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+m^{\prime})
be the fragment that was most recently inserted into
\mathcal{S}
and assume that
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert<\Delta
(otherwise, we return
\mathcal{S}
and terminate the algorithm).
Our goal is to then find some
j\in[i+m^{\prime},i+m^{\prime}+\Delta)
such that
P[j\mathinner{.\,.}j+m^{\prime})
does not CT-match any element of
\mathcal{S}
.
To do this, it suffices to find, in
\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
time, the first unmarked position in
[i+m^{\prime},i+m^{\prime}+\Delta)
, if one exists.
If we can always find such a position
j
while
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert<\Delta
, then we eventually insert
\Delta
fragments into
\mathcal{S}
.
This holds because the total length of the fragments inserted into
\mathcal{S}
and the lengths of the gaps between them add up to at most
\Delta\cdot(m^{\prime}+\Delta-1)\leq{m/2}+\Delta^{2}\leq m
since the length of the gap between any two consecutive fragments is less than
\Delta
and
\Delta<\sqrt{m}/2
.
Inserting a fragment into
\mathcal{S}
and marking all of its exact CT-occurrences takes
\mathcal{O}(m)
time.
In the case when we can always find a fragment to insert into
\mathcal{S}
, the algorithm thus runs in
\mathcal{O}(m\Delta)
time.

Now consider the complementary case when, after inserting some fragment
P[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+m^{\prime})
into
\mathcal{S}
, we have
\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert<\Delta
and all positions in
[i+m^{\prime},i+m^{\prime}+\Delta)
are marked.
Then, by the pigeonhole principle, some fragment in
\mathcal{S}
has two CT-occurrences at positions
i_{1}<i_{2}
in
[i+m^{\prime},i+m^{\prime}+\Delta)
with
q:=i_{2}-i_{1}<\Delta
.
We find two such positions (marked with the same symbol) naively in
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\subseteq\mathcal{O}(m)
time. Let
G=P[i_{1}\mathinner{.\,.}i_{2}+m^{\prime})
.

G
has length
L=m^{\prime}+q
and CT-border-period
q
.
We apply
\cref
lem:period:border_to_block to trim
G
. Both conditions of the lemma are satisfied since
\Delta>q
,
m^{\prime}\geq m/(2\Delta)
, and
\Delta<\sqrt{m}/2
, and hence

L=m^{\prime}+q\geq m/(2\Delta)+q>\sqrt{m}+q\geq 2\Delta+q>3q.

Trimming
G
yields a fragment
F
with CT-block-period
q
and length

\lvert F\rvert\geq L-2q=m^{\prime}-q\geq{m/(2\Delta)}-\Delta>{m/(2\Delta)}-\sqrt{m}/2\geq m/(4\Delta).

Finally, we naively compute the smallest CT-block-period
p
of

by checking each integer in
[1,q-1]
in
\mathcal{O}(m)
time, for a total running time of
\mathcal{O}(m\Delta)
, and return the pair
(F,p)
.
∎

4.2
The Aperiodic Case

In this section, we deal with the (easy) case when the analysis of the pattern according to
\cref
lem:filtering_preprocess returns a large CT-rainbow of disjoint fragments.
We apply a standard marking trick: an exact CT-occurrence of a fragment

at a position

k

in
T

The following simple fact captures the key property we use: if

, then position

must receive at least
\Delta-k
marks.

Lemma 27
.

Let

with
\Delta\geq k
.
Consider strings
V,U
satisfying
{\textnormal{{CHd}}_{k}(V\leadsto U)\leq k}
.
Let
\mathcal{U}=\{U[a_{i}\mathinner{.\,.}b_{i}]:i\in[1,\Delta]\}
be a set of disjoint fragments of
U
. Then there exists a set

of size at least
\Delta-k
such that
V[a_{i}\mathinner{.\,.}b_{i}]\approx U[a_{i}\mathinner{.\,.}b_{i}]
for all
i\in I
.

Proof.

Let
V^{\prime}
be a string obtained from
V
by substituting at most
k
characters such that
V^{\prime}\approx U
.
Let

Set
I
is of size at least
\Delta-k
as the substitutions transforming
V
into
V^{\prime}
affect at most
k
fragments

.
For each

\cref
fact:subeq implies that
V^{\prime}[a_{i}\mathinner{.\,.}b_{i}]\approx U[a_{i}\mathinner{.\,.}b_{i}]
.
Moreover, for each

∎

In the following lemma, we use the
marking trick
to efficiently compute a small number of candidate positions that need to be verified, and verify them using

Lemma 28
.

Let

∎

with its minimum CT-block-period
p<\Delta
.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(m\Delta)
time.

We then apply the so-called standard trick to reduce the given instance of
Approximate CT-Matching with Substitutions
to
\mathcal{O}(n/m)
instances of the same problem with the same pattern
P
, the same threshold
k
, and the text being a fragment of
T
of length at most
3m/2
.
For
i\in[0,\lfloor 2n/m\rfloor]
, define

T_{i}:=T[1+\lfloor i\cdot m/2\rfloor\mathinner{.\,.}\min\{\lfloor(i+3)\cdot m/2\rfloor,n+1\})
.
It is readily verified that each
m
-length fragment of
T
appears in precisely one of the
T_{i}
s.

We then apply either
\cref
lem:aper_case or
\cref
lem:per_case (depending on the outcome of the analysis of the pattern according to
\cref
lem:filtering_preprocess) to solve an instance of
Approximate CT-Matching with Substitutions
separately for each
T_{i}
.
In the end, we merge the results.

Each instance is solved in
\mathcal{O}(mk^{4}\Delta+m^{2}k/\Delta)
time.
The total complexity over all
\mathcal{O}(n/m)
instances is therefore
\mathcal{O}((n/m)(mk^{4}\Delta+m^{2}k/\Delta))=\mathcal{O}(nk^{4}\Delta+nmk/\Delta)
.
We now distinguish between two cases:

•

If
k\leq\lfloor m^{1/5}/128^{2/5}\rfloor
, we balance the two terms in the complexity by setting
\Delta=\lfloor\sqrt{m}/(128\cdot k^{1.5})\rfloor
.
Since
k\Delta\leq\sqrt{m}/128
, and
2k\leq\Delta
, the time complexity is

\mathcal{O}\left(nk^{4}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}}{k^{1.5}}+\frac{nmk}{\sqrt{m}/k^{1.5}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n\sqrt{m}\cdot k^{2.5}).

•

Else, we have
k\in(\lfloor m^{1/5}/128^{2/5}\rfloor,\lfloor m^{1/4}/128\rfloor)
.
We set
\Delta=2k
, noting that
k\Delta=2k^{2}
. Since
k<m^{1/4}/128
, we have
k^{2}<\sqrt{m}/128^{2}
and hence
k\Delta=2k^{2}<\sqrt{m}/128
. Hence, in this case, the time complexity is

\mathcal{O}\left(nk^{4}(2k)+\frac{nmk}{2k}\right)=\mathcal{O}(nk^{5}+nm)=\mathcal{O}(nk^{5}).

Since
nk^{5}=\Theta(n\sqrt{m}\cdot k^{2.5})
for all
k\in[\lfloor m^{1/5}/128^{2/5}\rfloor,\lfloor m^{1/5}\rfloor]
, the statement holds.
∎

Lemma 37

(analogue of
\cref
lem:psv_equiv)
.

For equal-length strings
X
and
Y
,
\textsf{{CT}}(X)=\textsf{{CT}}(Y)
if and only if
\textnormal{{ND}}(X)=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y)
, or equivalently
\forall i\in[1,\lvert X\rvert]:\textnormal{{nsv}}_{X}(i)=\textnormal{{nsv}}_{Y}(i)
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
n=\lvert X\rvert
.

If
n=1
,
X
and
Y
both have the same Cartesian tree consisting of a single node.
Their
ND
representations are also identical (
\textnormal{{ND}}(X)[1]=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y)[1]=0
by definition).
Thus, the lemma holds for
n=1
.

Assume the lemma holds for strings of length
i
. We show it holds for length
i+1
.

(
\Rightarrow
:) Assume
X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1]\approx Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1]
. Two cases are to be considered:

•

X[i+1]
and
Y[i+1]
are not roots: Let
X[j]
be the parent of
X[i+1]
, and
Y[\ell]
the
parent of
Y[i+1]
. Since
X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1]\approx Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1]
, we must have
j=\ell
. Removing the node at index
i+1
results in
\textsf{{CT}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
and
\textsf{{CT}}(Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
. By the induction hypothesis,
\textnormal{{ND}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
.
Since
nsv
values represent the first smaller element to the right, and the structural position of
i+1
relative to its parent
j
is identical in both trees, it follows that
\textnormal{{nsv}}_{X}(z)=\textnormal{{nsv}}_{Y}(z)
for all
z\in[1,i+1]
. Specifically, the “new”
nsv
values for indices in
[1,i]
that were affected by the insertion of position
i+1
are determined solely by the tree structure, which is identical. Hence,
\textnormal{{ND}}(X)=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y)
.

•

X[i+1]
and
Y[i+1]
are roots: This implies
X[i+1]
and
Y[i+1]
are the minima of their respective strings. Removing these roots leaves
\textsf{{CT}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
and
\textsf{{CT}}(Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
as the left subtrees. Since
\textsf{{CT}}(X)=\textsf{{CT}}(Y)
, these subtrees are identical, and by the induction hypothesis,
\textnormal{{ND}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
. Because the root is at
i+1
, any
z\leq i
that had
\textnormal{{nsv}}_{X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]}(z)=i+1
will maintain
\textnormal{{nsv}}_{X}(z)=i+1
. Thus, all
ND
values remain equal.

(
\Leftarrow
:) Assume
\textnormal{{ND}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1])=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1])
. Since
\textnormal{{ND}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])=\textnormal{{ND}}(Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
is implied by the consistency of the
nsv
definition, the induction hypothesis gives
X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]\approx Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]
.

We can reconstruct
\textsf{{CT}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1])
from
\textsf{{CT}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
by considering
\textnormal{{nsv}}_{X}
. Let
z
be the largest index such that
z<i+1
and
\textnormal{{nsv}}_{X}(z)=i+1
.

•

If such a
z
exists,
X[i+1]
is inserted as the right child of
X[z]
, and the previous right subtree of
X[z]
becomes the left subtree of
X[i+1]
.

•

If no such
z
exists,
X[i+1]
becomes the new root, and the entire
\textsf{{CT}}(X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i])
becomes its left subtree.

Since
X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]\approx Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]
and the
nsv
values for the new position
i+1
are identical for both strings, the insertion process results in identical trees. Thus,
X[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1]\approx Y[1\mathinner{.\,.}i+1]
.
∎

Lemma 40

(analogue of
\cref

(2)

Further, by
\cref

\textnormal{{ND}}(X)[i\mathinner{.\,.}j)

\textnormal{{ND}}(X[i\mathinner{.\,.}j-p))

\textnormal{{ND}}(X)[1\mathinner{.\,.}j-p)

6
Conclusions and Open Problems

We have presented an efficient algorithm for
Approximate CT-Matching with Substitutions
.
Our algorithm can be seen as a reduction to several instances of computing
\textnormal{{CHd}}(X\leadsto Y)
for pairs of strings
X
and
Y
.
We note that any improvement upon the dynamic-programming approach of Kim and Han
[
21
]

(see also
\cref
app:compare)
for this comparison problem would directly yield an improvement over our main result.
Interestingly, no (conditional) lower bound is known for either the problem of computing
\textnormal{{CHd}}(X\leadsto Y)
or
Approximate CT-Matching with Substitutions
, and devising such a lower bound is another direction for future work.
Finally, we hope that our techniques will serve as a foundation for advancing the state of the art in approximate CT-matching under the edit distance.

Efficient Computation of Cartesian Tree Hamming Distance

First, we formally define a convolution problem on weakly monotone sequences and present a self-contained version of the efficient solution of Kim and Han

Max-Min Convolution for Non-Decreasing Sequences

Input:
Two non-decreasing sequences
A[i]_{i=0}^{i=z}
and
B[i]_{i=0}^{i=z}
.

Output:
A sequence
C[i]_{i=0}^{i=z}
such that
C[i]=\max\limits_{j\in[0,i]}(\min\{A[j],B[i-j]\})
.

Lemma 45
.

The
Max-Min Convolution for Non-Decreasing Sequences
problem can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(z)
time.

Proof.

We define, for each
i\in[0,z]
, breakpoint
\mathit{BP}[i]
as the largest integer
j\in[-1,i]
for which

A[j]\leq B[i-j]
.
Note that
\mathit{BP}
is non-decreasing due to the monotonicity of
A
and
B
.

In what follows, we first describe how array
\mathit{BP}
enables an efficient computation of array
C
and then present an
\mathcal{O}(z)
-time algorithm for constructing
\mathit{BP}
.

For convenience, we set
A[-1]:=-\infty
and
B[-1]=-\infty
.

Claim 46
.

For all
i\in[0,z]
, we have

C[i]=\max\{A[\mathit{BP}[i]],B[i-\mathit{BP}[i]-1]\}
.

{claimproof}

We can rewrite

C[i]=\max\limits_{j\in[0,i]}(\min\{A[j],B[i-j]\})
as

C[i]=\max\,\left\{\,\max\limits_{j\in[0,\mathit{BP}[i]]}A[j]\,\,,\,\,\max\limits_{j\in(\mathit{BP}[i],i+1]}B[i-j]\,\right\}.

The claim then follows by the monotonicity of arrays
A
and
B
.

The above claim implies that given array
\mathit{BP}
, we can compute
C[i]
for any
i\in[0,z]
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, and we can thus compute
C
in
\mathcal{O}(z)
time in total.

It remains to show that array
\mathit{BP}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(z)
time given
A
and
B
.
We first compute an auxiliary array
Z[0\mathinner{.\,.}z]
, where
Z[j]
is the number of the elements of
B
that are strictly smaller than
A[j]
, noting that
Z
is a non-decreasing sequence since
A
is non-decreasing.
Array
Z
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(z)
time by merging
A
and
B
in a merge-sort fashion.

We then initialise
\mathit{BP}[0\mathinner{.\,.}z]
as an array all of whose entries are set to
-1
and, intuitively, for each
j
from
0
to
z
, we then want to increment all entries in

BP[j+Z[j]\mathinner{.\,.}z]
by
1
.
We can do this in
\mathcal{O}(z)
total time using a left-to-right sweep to accumulate said increments, effectively computing prefix sums.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.
