Multiple fish diversity facets in the Nu-Salween basin: Unexpected bimodal patterns, environmental drivers, threats, and conservation implications
Authors: Yan Wang, Cheng-Zhi Ding, Sébastien Brosse, Nick Bond, Nyo Nyo Tun, Bang-Li Tang, Jie Wang, Liu-Yong Ding, Juan Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Nu-Salween River represents the second-longest river and one of the last major free-flowing systems in Southeast Asia, constituting a globally significant reservoir of freshwater biodiversity. However, basin-wide patterns of fish diversity across taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional dimensions, together with weighted endemism, remain poorly understood, and the environmental determinants shaping these patterns are not well resolved. To address this limitation, a basin-scale analysis was conducted using 26 998 georeferenced occurrence records encompassing 445 native freshwater fish species distributed across 382 subdrainages. Species richness, phylogenetic diversity, functional diversity, and weighted endemicity exhibited unexpected bimodal distributions along the longitudinal gradient, revealing two distinct peaks from headwaters to downstream reaches. Climatic and geographic gradients emerged as dominant drivers of spatial structure. Mean annual air temperature, distance to river mouth, elevation, and net primary productivity exerted strong influences on both species richness and phylogenetic diversity. Functional diversity increased primarily with mean annual air temperature and annual total precipitation, whereas weighted endemicity declined with increasing subdrainage area. Although the conflicts between anthropogenic disturbances and fish diversity patterns were weak, protection coverage across priority areas identified for different diversity facets remained critically low, ranging from 0.1% to 15.9%. Divergent spatial configurations among diversity dimensions, combined with inadequate representation within existing protected-area networks, highlight an urgent need for more targeted and context-specific conservation strategies. Effective management strategies must consider the atypical bimodal longitudinal structure of fish diversity and the multidimensional relationships among diversity facets to sustain ecosystem function and resilience under intensifying anthropogenic pressure.

INTRODUCTION

Freshwater fish are foundational components of inland water ecosystems, sustaining ecological integrity while simultaneously providing essential resources for human sustenance and livelihood (Feio et al., 2023; Su et al., 2021; Villéger et al., 2017). With more than 18 000 described species worldwide, freshwater fish account for nearly half of global fish diversity and more than one quarter of all vertebrates (Dudgeon et al., 2006; Froese & Pauly, 2024). Distributed across all major trophic positions, these taxa mediate interactions among producers, consumers, and decomposers, thereby regulating nutrient cycling, biomass transfer, and energy flux within and across aquatic ecosystems (Villéger et al., 2017). Beyond ecological function, freshwater fisheries represent a significant component of global fish production and constitute a critical source of dietary protein for millions of people (FAO, 2024).

Despite their ecological and socioeconomic importance, freshwater fish assemblages are undergoing accelerating degradation under multiple anthropogenic impacts, including overexploitation, pollution, hydropower development, biological invasions, and climate change (Arthington et al., 2016; Cheng et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025; Su et al., 2021; Tickner et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025a). These stressors have precipitated rapid fish population declines and widespread erosion of biodiversity (Su et al., 2021; Tao et al., 2023b), making freshwater fish among the most threatened vertebrate groups, with extinction risks exceeding those documented for many terrestrial taxa (Su et al., 2021; Tickner et al., 2020). Large river systems are particularly vulnerable, as hydrological modification, the introduction of non-native species, and overexploitation of native fauna collectively restructure habitat connectivity and ecological processes (Grill et al., 2019; Su et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). This vulnerability is especially concerning in Asia, where extensive dam construction, chronic overharvesting, and biological invasions have profoundly transformed freshwater ecosystems (Arthington et al., 2016; Dudgeon et al., 2006; Grill et al., 2019). Consequently, freshwater fish assemblages in Asia, together with the ecosystem functions they sustain, rank among the most imperiled globally (Tickner et al., 2020).

Halting the rapid decline in freshwater fish diversity requires a mechanistic understanding of spatial distribution patterns and the environmental gradients that drive them (Brumm et al., 2021; Côte et al., 2023). Such knowledge provides the foundation for high-resolution distribution mapping, quantitative assessment of spatial overlap between biodiversity and anthropogenic disturbances, and identification of underprotected regions within conservation networks (Tao et al., 2023b). Existing research has documented broad-scale diversity gradients in freshwater fish and their relationships with environmental variables (He et al., 2020; Oberdorff et al., 2011; Tao et al., 2023b), revealing the combined influence of historical contingencies and contemporary environmental constraints. These patterns are consistent with the “Area” hypothesis, emphasizing spatial extent and environmental heterogeneity, and the “Energy” hypothesis, underscoring the role of climate and ecosystem productivity in shaping biodiversity distributions (Field et al., 2009; He et al., 2020).

Despite these advances, investigations in large river systems have predominantly focused on taxonomic metrics, such as species richness, while largely neglecting phylogenetic and functional diversity dimensions, which underpin the mechanistic relationships between diversity and eco-evolutionary processes (Côte et al., 2023; Oberdorff et al., 2011). Phylogenetic diversity quantifies the evolutionary relationships among co-occurring taxa and is often correlated with patterns of resource utilization, lineage divergence, historical diversification processes, and potential adaptive capacity under environmental change (Faith, 1992). Functional diversity represents an array of distinctive morphological, physiological, and ecological traits within assemblages, thereby underpinning the functional architecture that governs ecosystem processes and resilience (Cadotte et al., 2011). Integrating these complementary dimensions can provide insights that extend beyond taxonomic diversity, which is crucial for understanding the functionality and stability of ecosystems in response to shifting environmental regimes (Cadotte et al., 2011; Gagic et al., 2015; Song et al., 2020). Nevertheless, comprehensive analyses that jointly explore multiple facets of fish diversity and their environmental drivers in large river systems remain scarce (Brumm et al., 2021).

The Nu-Salween (Thanlwin) River, the second-longest river and one of the last major free-flowing systems in Southeast Asia, represents a globally significant stronghold for freshwater biodiversity due to limited fragmentation and comparatively low levels of anthropogenic disturbance (Ding et al., 2023; Grill et al., 2019; Tao et al., 2023a). The basin supports an exceptionally diverse ichthyofauna, likely exceeding 400 freshwater species, and functions as a regional refuge for taxa extirpated from many heavily altered rivers elsewhere in Asia (Ding et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2023a). The longitudinal integrity of this megariver also provides a unique opportunity to investigate fish assemblages along the entire upstream-downstream continuum within an Asian context. Despite its global conservation significance (Ding et al., 2023), the Nu-Salween River faces imminent threats from prospective hydropower development and intensifying human activities, underscoring the urgency of resolving spatial biodiversity patterns and their environmental drivers (Ding et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2023a; Yang et al., 2022).

Here, a comprehensive occurrence database for freshwater fish in the Nu-Salween River was assembled through extensive long-term field surveys combined with systematic integration of multiple data sources. This synthesis enabled high-resolution mapping of fish diversity across the basin, providing a quantitative basis for evaluating spatial correspondence between biodiversity and anthropogenic pressures and for identifying conservation gaps (Tao et al., 2023b). Diversity was characterized using complementary facets, including species richness, functional diversity reflecting trait-based ecological roles, and phylogenetic diversity capturing evolutionary history or adaptive potential under environmental change (Brun et al., 2019; Pimiento et al., 2020). Spatial patterns, cross-facet congruences, and underlying environmental drivers were analyzed across the entire basin. Conflicts between diversity patterns and anthropogenic disturbances were subsequently assessed to detect conservation gaps. Integration of taxonomic, functional, and phylogenetic dimensions enables a mechanistic evaluation of eco-evolutionary processes shaping contemporary fish assemblages and allows explicit testing of competing explanations for spatial diversity structure. Given the comparatively moderate economic development and human population density within the basin, limited spatial correspondence between biodiversity hotspots and anthropogenic disturbance was anticipated. This study not only deepens our understanding of environmental gradients governing multidimensional fish diversity but also establishes an analytical framework for conserving the multifaceted value of freshwater diversity and sustaining ecological integrity in large river ecosystems, particularly the Nu-Salween River, the longest free-flowing megariver in Southeast Asia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area

The Nu-Salween River originates in the Tanggula Mountains on the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and flows through China, Myanmar, and Thailand before discharging into the Andaman Sea in southern Myanmar (Figure 1). Extending approximately 3 200 km with a drainage area of 325 000 km2 and an elevation range of over 6 000 m, the basin encompasses pronounced geomorphological heterogeneity, including high-elevation plateaus, deeply incised mountain valleys, and lowland plains. The climate is influenced by mid-latitude westerlies and the Asian monsoon system, generating substantial spatial variability in temperature and precipitation. Mean annual temperatures range from –10°C to 25°C, and annual precipitation varies from 400 to 4 800 mm (FAO, 2011; Myers et al., 2000; Yang et al., 2022). This environmental complexity sustains high biological diversity, including more than 350 freshwater fish species, 31 of which are endemic, as well as several threatened taxa such as the mottled eel (Anguilla nebulosa) and goonch (Bagarius bagarius).

Approximately 10 million people inhabit the basin, with population centers concentrated in the agriculturally productive delta floodplain, where livelihoods rely heavily on agriculture and fishing (Grill et al., 2019; Johnston et al., 2017). However, ongoing demographic expansion and regional economic development are intensifying anthropogenic pressures across the drainage network. Alterations in fish assemblage composition have been associated with the introduction of non-native species, including the Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) and Chinese false gudgeon (Abbottina rivularis), released to enhance fisheries production (Yang et al., 2022). Furthermore, 20 hydropower projects proposed along the mainstem pose substantial risks to longitudinal connectivity, habitat integrity, and migratory pathways. Combined with persistent stressors, such as overfishing, pollution, and climate change, these emerging drivers threaten fishery resources and undermine biodiversity across the basin (Tao et al., 2023a).

Fish-occurrence dataset

Although the Nu-Salween basin is recognized as a global biodiversity hotspot (Myers et al., 2000), basin-wide ichthyological surveys remain fragmented and spatially uneven (Ding et al., 2023). To address this limitation, comprehensive occurrence data for native freshwater fish species in the Nu-Salween River were compiled through integration of extensive long-term field records (Ding et al., 2019, 2023; Ji et al., 2022; Tao et al., 2023b; Yang et al., 2022) and systematically extracted records from published literature and authoritative databases. The dataset included strictly freshwater taxa and diadromous species that depend on freshwater habitats for growth or reproduction, such as anguillid eels. Literature searches were conducted across the Web of Science (WoS, https://www.webofscience.com), Scopus (https://www.scopus.com), and Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI, https://www.cnki.net) databases, supplemented by relevant books, technical reports, and global repositories, including FishBase (Froese & Pauly, 2024, https://www.fishbase.se), the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, https://www.gbif.org), and the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN, https://www.iucnredlist.org). The search encompassed publications from 1900 through August 2023. Screening and data extraction followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021; Supplementary Figure S1). Sources contributing occurrence data are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Occurrence records comprised two categories: survey-based records and expert-inferred records. Survey-based records included specific sampling georeferenced locations with associated temporal information, extracted from text, tables, figures, supporting materials, and online databases. These data were curated by removing duplicate entries, spatial outliers, and records with geographic uncertainty using the R package CoordinateCleaner v.3.0 (Zizka et al., 2019). Expert-inferred records were generated from estimated species distribution polygons available through the IUCN database to address spatial gaps in survey coverage. Distribution polygons were first intersected with subdrainage boundaries, and centroids of the overlapping subdrainages were designated as candidate expert-inferred records. The basin was partitioned into 382 subdrainages corresponding to level-8 hydrographic units in HydroBASINS (Lehner & Grill, 2013). Candidate records were retained only when consistent with documented climatic zones and habitat types, including freshwater and brackish systems, as described in FishBase. For a limited number of species documented in the literature as distributed in the Nu-Salween River but lacking survey points or estimated distribution ranges, occurrence data from their closest phylogenetic relatives were used as proxies. This integrative approach, combining survey data, distribution modeling, and taxonomic inference, has demonstrated effectiveness for high-resolution diversity mapping in large river systems (Tao et al., 2023b).

To exclude invalid species and synonyms, taxonomic validation was conducted by cross-referencing all species names with Eschmeyer’s Catalog of Fishes (Fricke et al., 2023). Conservation status was assigned according to the nine IUCN Red List categories: Extinct (EX), Extinct in the Wild (EW), Critically Endangered (CR), Endangered (EN), Vulnerable (VU), Near Threatened (NT), Least Concern (LC), Data Deficient (DD), and Not Evaluated (NE). Species categorized as CR, EN, or VU were classified as threatened taxa for subsequent analyses.

Functional traits and phylogenetic tree

Functional diversity was quantified using 11 traits representing key ecological functions related to trophic ecology, locomotor performance, and habitat use (Villéger et al., 2017). Nine traits described external morphology, including body elongation (Bel), vertical eye position (Vep), relative eye size (Res), oral gape position (Ogp), relative maxillary length (RMl), body lateral shape (BLs), pectoral fin vertical position (PFv), pectoral fin size (PFs), and caudal peduncle throttling (CPt). Morphological data were preferentially extracted from the FISHMORPH database (Brosse et al., 2021). For species lacking database records, measurements were derived from standardized lateral-view illustrations or high-resolution images of adult individuals obtained from gray literature, monographs, and curated online repositories. Two additional ecological traits, maximum body length (MBl) and trophic level (TL), were sourced from FishBase.

For 66 species (14.8% of 445 taxa) with incomplete trait information, missing values were estimated through imputation informed by available trait data and phylogenetic relationships (Rabosky et al., 2018). Phylogenetic relationships among species were reconstructed by generating a basin-level tree using the ggtree v.1.4.11 and ggtreeextra v.1.0.3 packages (Xu et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2017). Species absent from the global mega-tree framework (210 of 445 taxa) were incorporated using FishPhyloMaker based on genus- and family-level placement (Nakamura et al., 2021; Rabosky et al., 2018). The resulting phylogeny, combined with species composition data across subdrainages, was used to calculate phylogenetic diversity indices.

Environmental factor dataset

To identify key drivers influencing fish diversity patterns, environmental variables representing climatic, geographic, hydrological, and biological conditions were assembled. These predictors are widely applied in macroecological analyses and play significant roles in shaping large-scale patterns of aquatic biodiversity (He et al., 2020). A total of 12 environmental variables were initially compiled for each of the 382 subdrainages: longitude, latitude, mean annual air temperature, mean annual total precipitation, elevation, slope, subdrainage area, distance to river mouth (DRM), potential evapotranspiration, aridity index, mean discharge, and net primary productivity (NPP) (Supplementary Table S2). Data were derived from multiple authoritative sources and harmonized to the subdrainage level through spatial resampling to ensure consistency with biodiversity metrics (Tao et al., 2023b).

To minimize multicollinearity, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was conducted, and environmental predictors with high covariance (i.e., VIF>5) were excluded from subsequent analyses (Zhang et al., 2021). Although mean annual air temperature, elevation, and DRM exhibited VIF values above this threshold, these variables were retained to facilitate comparability with previous studies identifying them as important drivers of biodiversity structure (Supplementary Table S3). Spatial correspondence between fish diversity patterns and anthropogenic pressure was evaluated using the Human Footprint Index (HFI, Venter et al., 2016) following established approaches (Tao et al., 2023b). HFI values range from 0 to 1, representing minimal to maximal anthropogenic disturbance, respectively.

Mapping species distributions

Spatial distributions of native fish species in the Nu-Salween River were delineated using an occurrence-based buffering framework (Tao et al., 2023b; Wang et al., 2023). For each species, dispersal distances associated with individual occurrence records were determined from empirical trait-based relationships (Radinger & Wolter, 2014). Dispersal capacity was parameterized using MBl and caudal aspect ratio (CAr), the latter defined as the squared height of the caudal fin divided by caudal fin surface area and derived from standardized illustrations or high-resolution images. Each occurrence point was first relocated to the nearest river network using the “near analysis” toolbox in ArcMap v.10.4.1 (Environmental Systems Research Institute, 2016). River order for each location was then assigned using HydroRIVERS data (Lehner & Grill, 2013) to identify the highest river order occupied by each species. Species-specific dispersal distances were estimated based on MBl, CAr, and maximum occupied river order using the R package fishmove v.0.3-3 (Radinger & Wolter, 2014). Estimated dispersal distances were applied as buffer diameters around each occurrence record (Tao et al., 2023b; Wang et al., 2023). Buffers were merged for each species and intersected with the 382 subdrainages. Given that dispersal is constrained by hydrological connectivity, the overlapping areas were confined to the focal subdrainage and hydrologically connected upstream or downstream units (Wang et al., 2023). When two occupied subdrainages were separated by intermediate units, intervening subdrainages were considered occupied if hydrological continuity was present and separation distance did not exceed 10 times the estimated dispersal distance (Wang et al., 2023). Species distribution modeling was not implemented because occurrence data were insufficient for several taxa (Tao et al., 2023b) and because incorporating environmental predictors at this stage would introduce redundancy in subsequent analyses of diversity-environment.

Statistical analysis

Biodiversity metrics were quantified for 382 subdrainages across the Nu-Salween basin using only native species records. Calculated indices included species richness (SR), phylogenetic diversity (PD), functional richness (FR), weighted endemicity (WE), coldspots and hotspots (CH), and threatened species richness (TSR). SR, PD, WE, and CH were derived using the R package phyloregion v.1.0.8 (Daru et al., 2020). SR represented the total number of species recorded within each subdrainage. PD was computed as the minimum cumulative phylogenetic branch length spanning all taxa present in a subdrainage on the reconstructed phylogeny (Faith, 1992). FR was estimated using a multivariate trait-based framework. A functional trait matrix was constructed and reduced using principal component analysis (PCA), and the first four principal component (PC) axes were retained (Zhang et al., 2021). Species scores on these axes were combined with subdrainage-level species composition to quantify FR as the convex hull hypervolume in trait space using the R package geometry v.0.4.7 (Blonder & Harris, 2019). FR values were scaled between 0 and 1 to facilitate comparison among subdrainages. WE was calculated as the sum of the inverse of species range sizes within each subdrainage (Daru et al., 2020). Range size was defined as the total number of subdrainages occupied by a species within the basin. Consequently, species with restricted distributions contributed more strongly to WE than broadly distributed taxa. Hotspots and coldspots were identified as subdrainages within the upper and lower 5% of WE values, respectively (Prendergast et al., 1993). TSR was defined as the number of species classified as threatened within each subdrainage. Spearman correlation analysis was applied to evaluate relationships among SR, PD, FR, WE, and TSR, and to assess associations between diversity indices and the HFI. CH was excluded from correlation analyses because it represents a categorical variable.

Relationships between environmental variables and multiple diversity facets (SR, PD, FR, and WE) were assessed using structural equation modeling (SEM) to accommodate both direct and indirect effects. Environmental predictors were standardized to ensure comparability across variables with differing units (Oberdorff et al., 2019). To prevent distortion of overall patterns, subdrainages exhibiting extreme environmental values, such as exceptionally high annual rainfall (10 of 382 subdrainages, 2.6%), were excluded based on outlier detection using the R package performance v.0.10.4 (Lüdecke et al., 2021). Initial SEM structure was informed by predictors identified as significant in generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) and multi-model comparisons. GLMMs were fitted using the R package glmmTMB v.1.1.7 (Brooks et al., 2017), and models were ranked using corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). Parameter estimates were averaged across candidate models with ΔAICc<3 (Gross et al., 2017). Initial pathways were established and confirmed based on existing literature (Supplementary Figure S2) and further pathways refined using Shipley’s d-sep tests. Final model selection was based on minimum AICc values. Linear models were retained in both GLMMs and SEMs due to the absence of a clear nonlinear relationship between environmental factors and fish diversity (Supplementary Figure S3) and to preserve interpretability. To address the non-independence of fish diversity indices across subdrainages, random effects were defined hierarchically (1|06_basin/07_basin, Supplementary Table S4) in both GLMMs and SEMs. Analyses were performed using the piecewiseSEM v.2.1.2 package (Lefcheck, 2016), and variances explained by fixed effects (R2marginal) and fixed and random effects (R2conditional) were reported. Direct, indirect, and overall relative effects of each environmental factor were quantified; however, these relationships represent statistical associations rather than confirmed causal pathways.

Conservation representativeness was evaluated by identifying priority areas defined as the top 5% of subdrainages for each diversity index in addition to all hotspots and coldspots. Priority areas were intersected with protected area boundaries to estimate the proportion of high-value subdrainages currently under protection. For finer-scale assessment, the basin was subdivided into 1 km2 grids, and coverage was quantified by calculating the proportion of grids within priority areas overlapping with designated protected areas. Spatial data were obtained from the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA, https://www.protectedplanet.net) and World Database on Other Effective Area-Based Conservation Measures (WDOECM, https://www.protectedplanet.net) using the R package wdpar v.1.3.5 (Hanson, 2022).

RESULTS

Database and species richness

The final fish occurrence database comprised 26 998 records, including 17 865 survey-based points and 9 133 expert-inferred points, representing 445 native freshwater fish species across the Nu-Salween River basin (Figure 1). These species were distributed among 20 orders, 75 families, and 216 genera (Supplementary Table S5), with taxonomic composition dominated by Cypriniformes (230 species, 54.0%) and Siluriformes (82 species, 19.2%). According to IUCN assessments, 22 species (4.9%) were classified as threatened, with no cases of extinction or extinction in the wild (EX or EW) recorded within the basin to date (Figure 2). Only 17.8% of species exhibited broad geographic ranges, whereas 25.3% occurred in fewer than 10 subdrainages and were classified as rare. Geographic partitioning of diversity was pronounced. Myanmar supported the highest richness (386 species, 86.7% of the basin total), while China and Thailand harbored 201 and 221 species, respectively (Figure 2). Only 79 species were shared across all three countries, indicating limited cross-border congruence in assemblage composition (Supplementary Figure S4A). Similarly, substantial differences in species composition were found between major tributaries and the mainstem, as well as among tributaries (Supplementary Figure S4B, C).

Patterns of fish diversity

Taxonomic richness, phylogenetic diversity, functional diversity, and weighted endemicity generally increased along the upstream-downstream gradient (Figure 3). High diversity values were concentrated in the lower basin, particularly southeastern Myanmar and northwestern Thailand, and in the middle reaches spanning southwestern China. Areas with high SR, PD, and WE values were primarily recorded in Yunnan Province in China, Kayah, Kayin, and Mon States in Myanmar, and Mae Hong Son and Tak Provinces in Thailand (Figure 3A, B, D). Notably, the estuarine subdrainage exhibited an exceptionally high WE value (45.2) relative to the basin-wide mean (1.16±2.62), reflecting the concentration of brackish-freshwater transitional taxa, with 90 of 114 species in that subdrainage occurring across both habitat types.

FR displayed a partially overlapping but distinct spatial pattern, with elevated values in Yunnan Province (China), Shan and Kayin States (Myanmar), and Tak Province (Thailand) (Figure 3C). TSR was more spatially restricted, with pronounced concentrations in Shan and Kayin States in Myanmar and Mae Hong Son Province in Thailand (Figure 3F). Hotspots were predominantly located in Yunnan Province, Kayin and Mon States, and Mae Hong Son and Tak Provinces, and exhibited marked differences in species composition among regions (Supplementary Figure S4D). In contrast, coldspots were confined to headwater regions in the upper basin (Figure 3E). Across the basin, SR, PD, FR, WE, and TSR exhibited an unexpected bimodal distribution pattern, characterized by two distinct peaks within each diversity spectrum, most prominently for SR and PD (Figure 4). Subdrainages with both medium-low and medium-high values collectively comprised a substantial proportion of the basin. Congruence among diversity facets was variable (Spearman correlation, 0.72<r<0.97, P<0.001), with the strongest association observed between SR and PD (r=0.97) and comparatively weaker correspondence between FR and TSR (r=0.72) (Figure 4).

Drivers of fish diversity

Eight environmental predictors, including mean annual air temperature, mean annual total precipitation, NPP, mean discharge, subdrainage area, elevation, slope, and DRM, were retained for SEM (Figure 5) after appearing at least once in the model-averaged GLMMs (Supplementary Figure S5). SEMs accounted for a substantial proportion of variation across diversity dimensions (0.72<R2<0.99, Supplementary Tables S6–S9), indicating strong explanatory performance. SR was primarily impacted by temperature, elevation, NPP, precipitation, and area, with combined total effects explaining 94.5% of the observed variance. PD was significantly associated with DRM, temperature, elevation, NPP, and area, collectively accounting for 95.6% of variation. FR was predominantly influenced by temperature and precipitation, explaining 50.6% of variation, whereas WE was principally driven by subdrainage area, explaining 16.4% of variation. Temperature emerged as a major determinant of SR, FR, and WE, contributing 35.9%–40.4% of total effects across these facets. In contrast, DRM exerted a dominant influence exclusively on PD, with a total effect of 65.0%. Mean discharge and slope exhibited consistently weak and non-significant associations with all diversity metrics. Patterns identified in SEMs were generally consistent with those observed in model-averaged GLMMs (Supplementary Figure S5), while additionally showing indirect effects. Effects of NPP on diversity were exclusively direct. Indirect contributions of discharge were minimal, whereas temperature, precipitation, elevation, slope, area, and DRM exerted varying degrees of indirect influence. Specifically, temperature represented the strongest indirect driver of SR, PD, and FR, while elevation exerted the largest indirect effect on WE.

Human disturbance and conservation gaps

HFI values remained generally low across the entire basin (HFI<0.5; Figure 6A; Supplementary Figure S6), with higher values (0.4–0.5) restricted to 10 subdrainages (2.6%) located in the estuarine region and middle reaches of southwestern China. Spatial correlations between HFI and most diversity facets were not statistically significant, except for TSR (Spearman r=–0.16, P<0.005; Figure 4). A total of 58 protected areas were identified within the Nu-Salween River basin, collectively covering 24 442 km2, equivalent to 6% of total basin area (Figure 6B). Subdrainages designated as priority areas for one or more fish diversity facets encompassed approximately 91 000 km2, representing 28% of the basin and concentrated primarily in the middle reaches and estuarine region (Figure 6C; Supplementary Figure S7). Only 5% of these priority areas overlapped with protected areas. Protection coverage within priority areas varied substantially among diversity dimensions, ranging from 0.1% to 15.9% (Figure 6D).

DISCUSSION

Integration of long-term field investigations with the most comprehensive fish-occurrence dataset assembled to date enabled basin-wide evaluation of multiple dimensions of fish diversity across the Nu-Salween basin. This synthesis revealed at least 445 native freshwater fish species, establishing the river as one of the most species-rich systems in Southeast Asia, although overall richness remains lower than that of the Mekong, which supports more than 1 000 species (Froese & Pauly, 2024; Wang et al., 2025b). In contrast to the widely documented pattern of increasing downstream biodiversity across large river networks (He et al., 2020; Muneepeerakul et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2021), the Nu-Salween River displayed an unexpected bimodal distribution of biodiversity. This structure was evident not only for species richness but also for functional and phylogenetic dimensions, and persisted, albeit less strongly, when restricted to threatened (TSR) and endemic species (WE).

Conventional theory predicts a unimodal distribution of fish diversity within river basins, shaped by gradients in energy availability, environmental filtering, and habitat heterogeneity. Under this framework, environmentally constrained headwaters are expected to harbor relatively few, closely related species with overlapping functional attributes. Downstream reaches, characterized by greater habitat complexity, enhanced resource availability, and cumulative dispersal inputs, are predicted to sustain elevated diversity through mass effects and colonization from upstream sources (Muneepeerakul et al., 2008; Oberdorff et al., 2019). Intermediate subbasins should therefore exhibit moderate diversity, producing a single-peaked longitudinal pattern. However, the observed dual-peak configuration in the Nu-Salween River system deviated from this canonical expectation, indicating that additional ecological and historical mechanisms have shaped diversity along the river continuum.

The unexpected bimodal diversity configuration identified in the Nu-Salween River was characterized by a predominance of subdrainages exhibiting either low or high diversity, whereas intermediate diversity levels were comparatively underrepresented, resulting in two distinct peaks across the diversity spectrum. This pattern was closely associated with the pronounced dumbbell-shaped geomorphology of the basin, which separates the drainage into two contrasting regions defined by divergent elevations, climatic regimes, and productivity levels. Such environmental contrasts support ecologically and evolutionarily distinct fish assemblages. Upstream subbasins, characterized by cold, high-altitude climates and low primary productivity, harbored relatively few species, largely composed of cold-adapted taxa within Triplophysa and Schizothorax, which share similar functional attributes and close phylogenetic relationships. In contrast, downstream subbasins, situated in warm tropical to subtropical lowlands, were characterized by elevated temperatures and higher primary productivity, conditions that foster species coexistence and diversification (Oberdorff et al., 2019). Interestingly, overlap between upstream and downstream communities was minimal, consistent with classification into distinct freshwater zoogeographic regions, i.e., High Central Asia and South Asia, respectively (He et al., 2020). The marked divergence between upstream and downstream parts of the basin is explained by the unusual topography of the middle reaches, where the mainstem descends approximately 2 000 m over a 300 km segment. This abrupt elevation gradient likely constrained faunal exchange and accentuated ecological divergence, contributing to the emergence of bimodal diversity patterns. Thus, middle subbasins functioned as transitional zones linking high-elevation headwaters and lowland reaches and consequently exhibited intermediate species richness. Similarly, other fish diversity facets displayed analogous bimodal tendencies, consistent with partial dependence on underlying species richness patterns. Although survey coverage remained incomplete in several subdrainages, particularly in downstream regions (Figure 1), supplementary interpolation analyses indicate that this limitation does not alter the overall pattern (Supplementary Figure S8). Overall, the bimodal structure of fish diversity in the Nu-Salween River basin reflects the integrated influence of geomorphology, environmental heterogeneity, and spatial constraints on fish distribution.

The distinctive geomorphological configuration of the basin also impacted the mechanisms driving biodiversity patterns. Notably, climate and primary productivity emerged as dominant determinants of biodiversity (Figure 5; Supplementary Figure S9), highlighting the central role of the species-energy hypothesis in shaping upstream-downstream gradients (Field et al., 2009; He et al., 2020; Oberdorff et al., 2019). However, the relative importance of drivers differed among fish diversity facets (Figure 5). Variables associated with the species-energy hypothesis primarily governed taxonomic and functional diversity, whereas phylogenetic diversity and weighted endemicity were more strongly associated with historical legacy (Oberdorff et al., 2019) and habitat heterogeneity linked to the species-area hypothesis (Gross et al., 2017; Lehner & Grill, 2013; Nakamura et al., 2021). Reduced phylogenetic diversity in the upstream high-altitude region of the basin likely reflects historical constraints on dispersal and colonization, leading to assemblages composed of closely related taxa (Oberdorff et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). In contrast, peaks in endemic richness occurred in scattered downstream subdrainages defined by elevated habitat heterogeneity, as well as in select middle reaches characterized by unique environmental conditions that may promote localized endemism (He et al., 2020). Long-term stable systems, such as Inle Lake, are likely to preserve more endemic lineages by buffering against historical extinction events (Kano et al., 2016; Fuke et al., 2022, 2024), underscoring the importance of paleogeographic stability in shaping regional endemism. Conversely, the relatively low WE observed in the upper Nu-Salween River may reflect a historical river capture event involving the Red River system (Clark et al., 2004). Such geomorphological reorganization can restructure drainage connectivity, facilitate biotic exchange among formerly isolated catchments, and reduce opportunities for allopatric speciation and persistence of narrowly distributed taxa. However, our GLMM and SEM frameworks for WE incorporated only contemporary environmental variables because historical variables were not available, limiting inference regarding long-term processes. Future investigations integrating geological history, drainage reconfiguration dynamics, and molecular phylogenetic data will be necessary to clarify the enduring influence of historical events on present-day regional endemism. Clear recognition of facet-specific drivers is therefore essential for designing conservation strategies tailored to distinct components of biodiversity, particularly under accelerating global environmental change (Feio et al., 2023; He et al., 2020).

Our findings carry substantial implications for conservation of freshwater fish within this unique and second-longest free-flowing river system in Southeast Asia. The pronounced bimodal patterns of diversity and the distinct mechanisms underlying individual diversity facets warrant careful consideration when evaluating risks associated with climate change and anticipated hydropower expansion. Regional assessments consistently identify climate change as the dominant driver of distributional reorganization in freshwater fish (Comte et al., 2021; Nuon et al., 2024). Within the Mekong basin, projections indicate that more than 80% of species may shift northward along the river network, whereas approximately 25% may experience contraction of climatically suitable habitat (Nuon et al., 2024). In the Nu-Salween basin, vulnerability is spatially asymmetric. Notably, high-elevation headwaters support cold-water assemblages that are particularly sensitive to thermal increases, making upstream sectors especially vulnerable to climate warming (Ding et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025). In contrast, the downstream lowlands, which harbor the majority of regional biodiversity, face severe constraints on altitudinal redistribution. Upward range shifts are effectively impeded by the steep middle reaches, where pronounced elevation gradients limit longitudinal and vertical dispersal. This geomorphological barrier restricts climate tracking and may confine downstream assemblages to increasingly unsuitable thermal environments, consistent with broader patterns of constrained dispersal documented for freshwater fauna (Lenoir et al., 2020). Collectively, given the basin topography and projected climatic shifts, biodiversity in the Nu-Salween River basin is likely to exhibit pronounced sensitivity to continued climate warming.

Although recognized as one of the longest remaining free-flowing rivers in Southeast Asia, the Nu-Salween basin is subject to mounting hydropower development proposals intended to satisfy regional energy and water resource demands. Currently, over 20 large-scale hydropower dam projects have been proposed along the mainstem (Middleton & Lamb, 2019). While implementation has been temporarily suspended, post-pandemic economic recovery strategies may reignite interest in these projects and infrastructure expansion (Middleton & Lamb, 2019; Tao et al., 2023a). If realized, extensive dam construction would profoundly alter habitat connectivity, disrupt flow regimes, and fragment critical habitats, with cascading consequences for survival, dispersal, and reproduction of freshwater fish, particularly for migratory species such as eels (Barbarossa et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2026; Winemiller et al., 2016). Elevated levels of endemism in the middle reaches further amplify conservation concern, as localized habitat disruption may disproportionately affect range-restricted taxa. Comprehensive assessment of current and projected habitat fragmentation across the basin is essential for effective conservation and management of fish species under accelerating development pressures.

Coverage of freshwater biodiversity within existing protected areas remains limited, with less than 6% of total basin area currently under formal protection. Achieving the “30×30” target set forth in the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (Convention on Biological Diversity, 2022) will therefore require substantial expansion of conservation coverage. As different facets of fish diversity exhibited varied congruences in spatial distribution patterns, it is critical that the expansion of protected areas incorporates their complementary relationships to enhance overall conservation effectiveness (Tao et al., 2023b). Although present anthropogenic pressures remain comparatively moderate in parts of the basin, freshwater fish diversity is poorly represented within the existing protected area network, resulting in extensive conservation gaps. Such mismatches between protected areas and spatial biodiversity patterns require proactive expansion of protected areas throughout the river basin. Given that the Nu-Salween River traverses China, Myanmar, and Thailand, and that multiple conservation priority areas are situated near international borders, coordinated transboundary governance is essential. Development of cross-border conservation frameworks can facilitate data sharing, collaborative monitoring, and joint planning, while enabling integrated establishment and management of protected areas (Tao et al., 2023a). Such coordination is particularly important in highly connected river systems, where upstream activities can adversely affect the downstream basin. Basin-wide, cooperative conservation strategies will be critical to protect freshwater fish diversity and maintain habitat integrity throughout the entire drainage network (Sun et al., 2024).

As the second-longest and one of the last major free-flowing rivers in Southeast Asia, the Nu-Salween River represents a system of exceptional biodiversity and insufficiently recognized ecological value, with comprehensive evaluation of fish diversity across the entire basin lacking. The present basin-wide assessment revealed an unexpected bimodal diversity pattern associated with the distinctive geomorphological structure of the drainage. Diversity facets displayed only partial spatial congruence and were driven by multiple environmental factors, including temperature, precipitation, and primary productivity, each contributing in distinct proportions. The bimodal diversity peaks underscored pronounced ecological differentiation between upstream and downstream regions of the basin, which functioned as largely independent systems shaped by contrasting environmental regimes. This spatial decoupling heightened sensitivity to both climate warming and hydrological fragmentation, as responses to environmental change are unlikely to be uniform across the basin. Although current anthropogenic disturbance exhibited limited spatial conflict with diversity hotspots, the representation of freshwater fish in existing protected areas remains inadequate. Therefore, effective conservation planning requires explicit incorporation of multiple diversity dimensions to capture complementary patterns and maintain ecological integrity amid potential threats from climate change and prospective hydropower development. Coordinated transboundary action among basin countries, supported by international collaboration, is needed to enhance survey coverage and expand protection across priority areas.
