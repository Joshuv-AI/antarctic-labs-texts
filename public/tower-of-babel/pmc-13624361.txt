Lineage-resolved lifetime modelling reveals potential quiescence and synchronization among MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells
Authors: Mónica Suárez Korsnes, Ellen Marie Botne Quinsgaard, Siver Andreas Moestue, Reinert Korsnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents evidence of a previously unrecognised structured organisation of untreated cancer cell lineages and suggests an unexpected sensitivity to subtle experimental perturbations that is not readily explained by standard population models. These conclusions are based on exploratory analyses of cell-tracking data from untreated MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells cultured under standard conditions. The analyses indicate that lineage-tracking data can reveal multiple layers of heterogeneity, synchronisation, and ancestry, together with stable quiescent, senescence-like, or persister-like states, even in the absence of external perturbation. These findings are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory and are intended to motivate future studies designed to test these hypotheses. The analysis is based on 72-h recordings from one or two wells in each of 21 independent experiments, comprising trajectories of 4,000 initial cells and their 34,152 descendants. During observation, 238 (6%) of the initial cells died, whereas 39 (1%) survived without dividing. The lifetimes of initial cells that died are well described by a conventional mixed gamma distribution, but the observed number of non-dividing survivors exceeds model predictions, suggesting a subpopulation with prolonged quiescent or senescence-like behaviour. The study further explores information extracted from healthy pedigree trees, defined by three consecutive normal cell divisions from the start of recording and no subsequent cell death. Only 22% of the 4,000 pedigree trees satisfy these criteria; the remainder exhibit abnormal divisions, prolonged intermitotic times, or cell death. Basic renewal theory indicates that initial cells tend to delay their first division, consistent with systematic differences between first- and second-generation intermitotic times. Finally, comparisons of pooled and experiment-specific lifetime distributions identify information-theoretic “elements of surprise”. Unexpectedly pronounced local maxima in empirical probability densities suggest high sensitivity to experimental conditions and may also reflect cellular synchronisation through intercellular communication together with ancestry-dependent inheritance.

Introduction

The field of single-cell biology has become a major area of biomedical research, as differences between individual cells and their behaviour are increasingly recognised to have a significant impact on cellular function (Symmons and Raj, 2016). Genetically identical cells can vary widely in gene expression, mRNA and protein abundance, as well as metabolic profiles (Elowitz et al., 2002; Cai et al., 2006; Zenobi, 2013; Bendall et al., 2014; Buettner et al., 2015; Deconinck et al., 2021). This variability is central to many biological and medical contexts, including cancer and immunology, where clonal populations are often assumed to behave uniformly (Greaves and Maley, 2012).

One key driver of this heterogeneity is reversible cell-state transitions where epigenetic mechanisms generate substantial phenotypic diversity (Sharma et al., 2010; Shaffer et al., 2017; Rosano et al., 2024; Bürger et al., 2026). This non-genetic heterogeneity is particularly relevant in cancer, where resistant or rare subpopulations may arise through epigenetic reprogramming (Spencer et al., 2009; Gupta et al., 2011; Pisco and Huang, 2015; Symmons and Raj, 2016; Shaffer et al., 2017).

Cancer cells exhibit high phenotypic plasticity and often maintain stable subpopulations over many generations (Gupta et al., 2011). Lineage-tracing therefore helps to understand how this plasticity shapes population dynamics (Meacham and Morrison, 2013; Lawson et al., 2018; Stuart and Satija, 2019; Qin et al., 2023). Evidence suggests that the cell of origin can influence tumour subtype and aggressiveness (Nowell, 1976; Ding et al., 2021; Vinuesa-Pitarch et al., 2021). Characterizing clonal cancer cells through heritable traits can thus elucidate the genetic and phenotypic drivers of cancer progression, facilitating the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

Central to these lineage dynamics is the degree of similarity between closely related cells. When sister cells exhibit greater similarity to each other than to randomly selected cells, it suggests an internal heritable component, indicating a high degree of non-genetic inheritance (Koren et al., 2015; Korsnes and Korsnes, 2018; 2023; Qin et al., 2023; Quinsgaard et al., 2024; Laisné et al., 2025). Despite increasing evidence for such inheritance, rigorous testing of unperturbed control cells remains surprisingly rare.

This study therefore investigates whether such classifications hold consistently across experiments and whether specific inherited phenotypes predispose certain lineages to dominate. As datasets expand, we can more rigorously address how intercellular interactions shape phenotypic patterns. Implementing such analyses could provide critical insights, enhancing both the interpretability and clinical relevance of in vitro models.

A key motivation of the present work is to assess whether untreated clonal cancer cells already display sufficient phenotypic variation to enable informative drug testing assays without prior perturbation or selective pressure. If untreated cells spontaneously adopt distinct proliferative or quiescent states, these intrinsic subpopulations may provide useful models for studying variability in therapeutic response. Identifying such lineages through computationally supported behavioural analyses provides an important rationale for this study, since most studies lack a proper baseline because they ignore lineage-resolved dynamics in untreated cells.

To address these questions, we characterize the lineages of untreated MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells over 72 h using single-cell tracking and lineage tracing. The data set consists of 21 independent experiments including 38.152 cells, tracked across 4,000 lineages.

During proliferation, cells can propagate transcriptional states, epigenetic marks, protein abundances, and other molecular components to their daughters, producing lineage-specific phenotypes that can persist for multiple generations (Spencer et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2017; Davies et al., 2023; Laisné et al., 2025). This continuity enables lineage-resolved phenotyping rather than isolated single-cell measurements.

The work begins by presenting the joint probability distribution of intermitotic times for first- and second-generation sister cells. Consistent with previous observations, sister cells exhibit positively correlated intermitotic times (Konigsberg and Feister, 1986; Chakrabarti et al., 2018; Hughes et al., 2022), suggesting a partial inheritance of states related to the cell-cycle. However, the empirical distributions differed substantially between experiments. One possible explanation is that intermitotic time is sensitive to environmental factors, although this seems less likely in our case because the cells were untreated and exposed to relatively uniform conditions. An alternative hypothesis is that subtle interactions or synchronizing effects may emerge during experiments, potentially influencing lineage dynamics and population-level behaviour. If such interactions occur, they could have important implications for the design and interpretation of in vitro assays.

The data for the present work also revealed that the variability in intermitotic times increases following the first two divisions. This suggests that cells may require an adaptation period to the recording environment. While a mixed gamma distribution models the lifespan of non-dividing cells that die during the 72-h period, a subset of surviving non-dividing cells deviated from this model.

A key finding of this study is the identification of a rare non-dividing subpopulation of 39 cells, observed across 21 experiments during the 72 h recording period. The difficulty in identifying such rare cellular states at baseline without prior drug selection has historically presented a ‘needle-in-a-haystack’ dilemma in cancer biology. Because these spontaneous, non-dividing variants are exceptionally rare, their distinct molecular and behavioural signatures are entirely obscured in traditional bulk assays by the overwhelming signal of the actively proliferating majority (Sharma et al., 2010; Gupta et al., 2011; Liau et al., 2017; Guler et al., 2017).

These outliers may be biologically and clinically relevant, as rare non-genetic subpopulations within clonal cancer cell lines, since they have been shown to contribute to the emergence of drug-tolerant states following therapeutic exposure (Bell and Gilan, 2020; Goyal et al., 2023). The existence of pre-existing therapy-resistant or therapy-tolerant subpopulations prior to treatment has motivated efforts towards early identification and individualized therapeutic strategies with the goal of improving prediction of treatment response (Vendramin et al., 2021).

The use of long-term single-cell tracking was therefore essential for detecting these rare quiescent cells, as traditional bulk assays inherently obscure such variants within population-averaged measurements. Importantly, observing these cells in untreated in vitro conditions, in the absence of therapeutic or micro-environmental stress, supports the possibility that non-dividing subpopulations or persister-like phenotypes may arise from pre-existing intrinsic cellular states rather than exclusively from induced stress responses (Shen et al., 2020).

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The breast cancer MDA-MB-468, a triple negative epithelial cell line with amplified expression of the Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), was used in the present experiments. The cells were cultured in DMEM:F12 with HEPES (Gibco, 3133038), supplemented with 50UmL−1 penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco, 15070063), and 5% foetal calf serum (FCS) (Gibco, 10270).

Data set collection

The dataset for the present study is derived from 21 independent experiments originally conducted for two independent projects with objectives different from those of the present study. The first 17 experiments were conducted as part of the study by Quinsgaard et al. (2024), whereas the remaining four experiments were carried out as part of the master’s thesis by Wardy (2025). To ensure consistency, the analysis was restricted to cells maintained under standard basal culture conditions without exposure to experimental treatments. Although the video data were obtained from baseline control wells on plates that also contained treated experimental groups, the cells analysed in these wells remained entirely untreated. These baseline control data therefore satisfy the inclusion criteria for the present study.

Microscopy

Time-lapse recordings of the first 17 experiments were acquired using a Zeiss TIRF III fluorescence microscope. The cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified 5% C O2 atmosphere. The recordings were recorded with 10× magnification in phase-contrast mode, with images captured every 2.5 min for a total duration of 72 h. For each microscopy experiment, cells were seeded at a concentration of 200cells/mm 2 in 96-well culture plates with microscopy-grade µClear polymer bottoms (Greiner Bio-One, 655090). From each well, two fields of view with dimensions 1.33×1.33 mm2 were recorded.

The second time-lapse recordings of the remaining four experiments were captured using the Nikon Crest v3 Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope, equipped with dual Kinetix sCMOS cameras (2,720 × 2,720 pixels). To ensure physiological relevance, cells were maintained in a controlled environment using the integrated Tokai hit Warming Box incubation system, set to 5% C O2 atmosphere at 37 °C under humidified conditions. The imaging was conducted in differential interference contrast (DIC) mode using a ×10 magnification objective, with images captured every 3 min for a total duration of 72 h. Imaging fields of view were carefully centred in each well to optimise both cell density and trackability. Focus was maintained over the 72 h imaging period using a Perfect Focus System (PFS, Nikon) hardware autofocus, supplemented by manual adjustments as necessary.

Given the extended duration of image acquisition, a label-free imaging approach was used to enable continuous monitoring while minimising experimental perturbation. Accordingly, Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) microscopy was selected because it avoids photobleaching and phototoxicity associated with fluorescence-based techniques, thereby facilitating long-term observation of dynamic cellular processes while preserving cell viability and physiological function.

Time-lapse video microscopy

Video tracking of the MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cell line was used to monitor individual cell behaviour and trace lineage relationships. 21 independent experiments were conducted, each involving 72-h recordings of untreated control cells cultured in test plates under undisturbed conditions. This gave 76 recordings of cells cultured under comparable conditions. In total, over 38,152 control cells were tracked.

Cell tracking

Korsnes Biocomputing (KoBio) 1 conducted the actual video tracking of individual cells from the microscopy data using its specialised Kobio Celltrack software which in principle could be replaced by similar systems (Tinevez et al., 2017; Padovani et al., 2022; Aragaki et al., 2022) The initial stage involved localising all cells within a central rectangular region of the video frame at the start of the recording. This rectangle was defined with fixed margins relative to the edges of the field of view, precisely large enough to include 50 or 100 cells. Subsequently, the positions of these cells, as well as their progeny, were continuously monitored throughout the 72-h recording period. The identification of sisters during the second division is visualised using the KoBio cell tracking system (Figure 1).

The initial tracking phase generates raw positional coordinates that contain noise and identification ambiguities, potentially biassing subsequent calculations of track length and migration speed. To reduce these errors, Gaussian kernel smoothing (Wand and Jones, 1994) regularised the data (Korsnes and Korsnes, 2023) using a fixed bandwidth of 5 min. The programme system is also used for observer-guided registration of cell death and its classification into the two broad categories of necrosis and apoptosis. It is also used to collect data on multipolar cell division and cell fusion and re-fusion events (Korsnes and Korsnes, 2017).

The present single cell tracking demonstrates that, among the current 4,000 initial cells, a fraction of the 39 non-dividing survivors may withstand treatment by evading vulnerabilities specifically associated with active cell division. If they can reverse their quiescent state, this phenomenon may be of medical interest, particularly in relation to long-term clinical risk (Cai et al., 2006; Buettner et al., 2015; Cornwell et al., 2023; Davies et al., 2023).

Delay of first cell division

Cells at the beginning of the present video recordings tend to exhibit delayed division. Renewal theory (Cox, 1962) helps to reveal this delay, assuming that the cells had grown under stable conditions over long time scales before the transition to time-lapse microscopy. Synchronisation among cells can, however, affect estimates of the interval lifetime or the forward recurrence time distributions. There is no particular reason to believe that this synchronisation is a consistent side effect of the present experimental protocol. Cells were simply transferred from routine culture and seeded in wells 24 h before recording.

Discussion

Evaluating cell behaviour under baseline conditions is crucial for understanding the intrinsic capacity of cell populations to generate non-genetic heterogeneity and variability between experiments. Traditionally, in vitro assays have followed a “drug-first” paradigm, in which cell populations are analysed only after exposure to therapeutic agents. The responses are then compared with the untreated control cells, which are often implicitly assumed to represent a homogeneous or “standard” baseline that is insensitive to small perturbations. This approach introduces a significant confounding factor because it can be difficult to distinguish behaviours induced by drug stress from those arising from pre-existing intrinsic cellular states (Hata et al., 2016; Goyal et al., 2023; Russo et al., 2024). The present study aims to overcome this limitation by motivating the systematic characterisation of untreated cell populations. This approach allows pre-existing heterogeneity to be studied directly, rather than inferred indirectly from responses to therapeutic perturbations.

The generality and simplicity of the present approach may pave the way for automated, standardised analyses of untreated control cells in the context of in vitro assays. The study should be straightforward to reproduce. It is based on tracking, a widely available cell line maintained under standard culture conditions. The primary observable is inter-mitotic time, a relatively objective measurement that serves as a proxy for the complex and sensitive biological processes governing cellular proliferation. Future microscope systems could integrate these standardised analyses directly into their acquisition or analysis software, enabling real-time monitoring and early detection of unexpected cellular behaviours. Such capabilities could be valuable for both basic research and biomedical applications.

We analyse 4,000 unperturbed MDA-MB-468 cell lineages and observe extensive phenotypic heterogeneity at the single-cell level. Only 22% of the initial cells generated “healthy” pedigree trees, defined as lineages that underwent at least three consecutive divisions without cell death. This unexpectedly low fraction suggests that sustained proliferative expansion is not the predominant outcome, even in the absence of therapeutic pressure. The remaining 78% of lineages fell outside these criteria, displayed various aberrant outcomes, including refusions, multipolar divisions, prolonged intermitotic times, and cell death (Table 2). These observations challenge the common assumption that established cancer cell lines maintained under standard conditions predominantly undergo stable proliferative expansion.

Non-genetic heterogeneity and sensitivity to small perturbations appear to be intrinsic features of baseline population dynamics, prior to therapeutic intervention, and may therefore contribute to variability in subsequent treatment responses. One possible source of such sensitivity is synchronisation or flocking behaviour within the cell population. Situations exhibiting this type of sensitivity cannot always be meaningfully counteracted through improved experimental control, but instead require statistical characterisation. The situation is analogous to throwing dice: individual outcomes cannot be predicted deterministically, whereas their statistical properties can be characterised with high precision.

A total of 238 initial cells died during the 72-h recording period, and their lifetimes are well described by a common survival model that predicts that virtually no cells should survive beyond the recording period (Figure 4). However, lineage tracking identified a distinct subpopulation of 39 cells (1%) that remained non-dividing throughout the entire observation period. These cells fall well outside the range predicted by the fitted survival model and therefore cannot be regarded as the long-lived tail of the same lifetime distribution. Instead, they constitute a statistically distinct component of the population. Unlike the transient delays in cell-cycle progression observed in other lineages, these cells exhibited persistent proliferative arrest throughout the duration of the experiment. This behaviour is consistent with a stable non-proliferative state and may reflect quiescent or senescence-like phenotypes within the untreated population. It may also reflect a persister-like state in which cells exhibit a pre-existing slow proliferative rate. These cells may exist before drug treatment and are then selected by treatment, following classical Darwinian rules (Shaffer et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2020).

Notably, the emergence of such cells in the absence of external perturbation suggests that substantial phenotypic stratification may already exist at baseline, encompassing both actively cycling and persistently non-proliferative cellular states. Although the origin of these long-term non-dividing cells remains unclear, their occurrence raises the possibility that entry into proliferative arrest is influenced by lineage history rather than arising solely through stochastic state transitions. Such lineage-dependent behaviour would be consistent with non-genetic inheritance of cellular state across generations (Coller et al., 2006; Spencer et al., 2009; Shaffer et al., 2017; Emert et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2020). Future analyses of pedigree structure may reveal whether ancestral cellular states predispose specific lineages to enter persistent non-proliferative states.

The presence of a persistent non-dividing subpopulation within an otherwise dynamically heterogeneous baseline population is consistent with a bet-hedging framework, in which phenotypic diversification enables a fraction of cells to adopt dormant-like states that may enhance survival under future environmental stress. Rather than reflecting rare stochastic failures, these states may constitute an intrinsic component of adaptive population dynamics in clonal cancer cell systems (Oren et al., 2021; Goyal et al., 2023; Iyer et al., 2025; Hayford et al., 2026). To support this interpretation, growing evidence suggests that persister-like cells can arise from actively proliferating cells prior to treatment, rather than exclusively from pre-existing quiescent subpopulations (Oren et al., 2021). Recent studies have also identified “memory genes” that may contribute to the inheritance and or maintenance of these pre-existing cellular states (Shaffer et al., 2020; Emert et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2025). Such non-genetic memory provides potential mechanisms by which reversible phenotypic transitions can nevertheless produce lineage dependent patterns of behaviour.

Our findings are also consistent with studies that demonstrate that cancer cells undergo reversible transitions between distinct phenotypic states through transient epigenetic regulation and stochastic fluctuations (Gupta et al., 2011; Sharma et al., 2010; Liau et al., 2017; Guler et al., 2017; Tirosh, 2022). Consistent with this framework, the lineage-resolved trajectories observed here, together with previous single-cell tracking studies, reveal extensive dynamic heterogeneity between cancer cell lineages (Korsnes and Korsnes, 2015; 2018; 2023; Quinsgaard et al., 2024; Korsnes et al., 2024).

The challenge of resolving outliers in untreated populations is compounded by the limitations of conventional bulk and endpoint-based assays, which cannot capture continuous single-cell dynamics. In this context, 72-h single-cell lineage tracking provides a non-invasive framework to resolve temporal trajectories at cellular resolution, enabling direct observation of state transitions without perturbation. This differs fundamentally from retrospective barcoding approaches, which infer pre-existing heterogeneity from endpoint distributions (Oren et al., 2021) but do not resolve dynamic behavioural trajectories. Similarly, while time-lapse imaging studies have demonstrated spontaneous and transient entry into quiescent states associated with endogenous replication stress (Arora et al., 2017; Pulianmackal et al., 2021), continuous lineage reconstruction allows these events to be placed within a full genealogical and temporal context (Hilsenbeck et al., 2016; Chakrabarti et al., 2018; Ulicna et al., 2021).

Figure 6 shows the empirical joint distribution of lifetimes for sister cells of generations I and II. Lifetimes for sister cells are clearly positively correlated, suggesting inheritance. The figure confirms the finding of Figure 5 that cells tend to postpone their division at the start of video recording. The distribution for generation II exhibits greater variation. This suggests that cells experience stress at the start of recording, proving that video tracking experiments should cover several cell divisions. Figure 6 also shows correlation of intermitotic times for sister cells. Different types of inheritance can explain this observation.

Intercellular interactions can trigger coordinated behaviours resembling flocking, potentially introducing substantial variability between experiments that might otherwise appear identical (Spencer et al., 2009; Stuart and Satija, 2019; Sharma et al., 2010; Shen et al., 2025). This collective behaviour may explain the differences illustrated in Figures 8–11. Alternatively, the observed differences may reflect traits inherited from a small number of ancestral cells that influence subsequent cell division. Horizontal exchange of genetic or cytoplasmic material between lineages could weaken these lineage-dependent effects. Consistent with this possibility, the present experiments recorded 295 cell fusion events (Table 2), and lineage tracking showed that some re-fusions and fused cells subsequently underwent multipolar divisions, directly linking these events to abnormal mitosis.

Additional mechanisms of inter-lineage exchange may include the uptake of apoptotic bodies, extracellular DNA released from dying cells, and biomolecules transferred via extracellular vesicles. These observations raise the possibility that apoptosis can contribute to the evolution of cancer cell populations by facilitating the horizontal transfer of biological material between lineages. Single-cell video tracking provides a direct means of observing such processes and may help elucidate the mechanisms underlying tumour evolution. In the longer term, improved understanding of these dynamic cellular interactions may contribute to the development of more specific prognostic and therapeutic strategies, bridging in vitro observations with in vivo cancer biology.

Concluding remarks

Characterising untreated cell populations is essential for understanding the intrinsic sources of non-genetic heterogeneity that may influence experimental reproducibility and therapeutic responses. Conventional in vitro assays typically focus on drug-treated cells, implicitly assuming that untreated controls represent a stable and homogeneous baseline. Our findings challenge this assumption by demonstrating substantial phenotypic diversity already present before therapeutic perturbation.

The proposed approach is simple, reproducible and based on the tracking of a widely available cell line under standard culture conditions. Inter-mitotic time provides an objective proxy for proliferative behaviour and could potentially be incorporated into automated microscope software for routine quality control and early detection of unexpected cellular dynamics.

Analysis of 4,000 untreated MDA-MB-468 cell lineages revealed extensive baseline heterogeneity. Only 22% of the initial cells generated healthy pedigree trees, while most lineages exhibited prolonged cell cycles, cell death, abnormal divisions, or fusion events. In addition, a small but distinct population of cells remained non-dividing throughout the 72-h observation period, suggesting the presence of stable quiescent- or senescence-like states even without external perturbation.

These observations indicate that non-genetic heterogeneity and sensitivity to small perturbations are intrinsic properties of untreated cancer cell populations. Such variability is unlikely to be eliminated entirely by improved experimental control and, instead, requires statistical characterisation. The coexistence of actively proliferating and persistently non-dividing cells is consistent with current models of phenotypic plasticity and bet-hedging, in which reversible cellular states enhance population adaptability. Our results therefore support the view that untreated cancer cell populations already contain dynamic behavioural diversity that can contribute to variability in subsequent treatment responses and motivate routine lineage-based characterisation of baseline cell populations before therapeutic intervention.
