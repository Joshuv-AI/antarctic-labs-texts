Heading direction perception in the horizontal plane is strongly driven by rotational stimuli in healthy and impaired in vestibular hypofunction
Authors: Sarah Hösli, Christopher J Bockisch, Giorgia Rita Di Ruggiero, Julia Dlugaiczyk, Dominik Straumann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-motion perception relies on the central integration of vestibular, visual and proprioceptive sensory inputs into a coherent percept of self-motion. To better understand the central integration of linear and rotational stimuli, we examined rotational and translational movements in the horizontal plane in healthy subjects (HS) and in patients with unilateral vestibular hypofunction (pwUVH). We established a novel heading discrimination paradigm using a motion platform to examine heading direction perception for translational movements with additional yaw rotations of − 1°, 0°, or 1°. We examined 26 HS and 26 pwUVH. Heading direction perception in HS and pwUVH was strongly driven by rotational stimuli. Among HS, adding 1° of yaw rotation to a translational movement resulted in an average shift of perceived straight-ahead translations to the opposite side of 2.15° (yaw to the right) and 2.30° (yaw to the left) respectively. pwUVH showed significantly higher discrimination thresholds (right UVH +3.3°, left UVH +6.4°) across all conditions than HS. We show that vestibular guided self-motion perception is strongly influenced by rotational movements, supporting central canal-otolith integration hypothesis. Introduction

In active and passive human locomotion, complex movements within all three body planes are indispensable and often take place subconsciously. The perception of these motions is multimodal with involvement of the vestibular, visual and proprioceptive system1. With respect to the vestibular system, there is sound understanding of motion sensing on a peripheral level, i.e. within the inner ear. It consists of otolith organs (detecting translation and tilt) as well as the semicircular canals (detecting rotation). Central integration of these signals over time enables self-motion perception of angular and linear displacement1,2.

When movements of translation and rotation are combined in a curved motion, absolute translation perception thresholds depend on simultaneous rotation velocity, while rotation perception is less influenced by translation3. Based on these findings and comparisons with model estimates, it has been suggested that motion perception does not equal the addition of separate translation and rotation estimates alone but seems to be the result of integrated and centrally reweighted signals.3–5. Subcortical integration of canal and otolith information has been demonstrated in animal models with subadditive and frequency-dependent signal integration in vestibular nuclei6.

Vestibular information is processed at subsequent central levels (vestibular cerebellum, thalamus and cortex) and in different cortical regions, combining vestibular cues with visual and somatosensory information1,7–9. While vestibular information is processed bilaterally, structural and functional lateralization to the non-dominant hemisphere and upper brainstem have been shown10–13. The question therefore arises, whether handedness plays a part in vestibular guided self-motion perception leading to a possible bias of motions in direction of the non-dominant hemisphere.

With most vestibular perceptual research concentrating on examination of threshold measurements of isolated linear or angular movements, there is a knowledge gap on combined supra-threshold movements that closely mirror our daily movement patterns.

We set out to examine central integration of combined movement patterns by looking at self-motion perception of supra-threshold combined passive movements. We set out to (i) establish a heading discrimination paradigm using a motion platform that lets healthy participants detect combined translational and rotational movements in the horizontal plane correctly; (ii) explore what impact rotational movements have on heading perception of translational movements in healthy subjects and whether based on hemispheric dominance of the vestibular system, there is any difference of self-motion perception depending on handedness; (iii) examine how self-motion perception of these combined movements is affected in patients with unilateral vestibulopathy.

Feasibility

Healthy subjects were able to distinguish rightward and leftward translation without visual and proprioceptive input. All subjects were able to conduct the full experiment procedure. (ii)Self-motion perception in healthy subjects

Self-motion perception in healthy subjects

To examine the effect of rotation on perceived straight-ahead, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted with gender, age, condition (0° yaw, +1° yaw and − 1° yaw) and handedness as fixed effects while accounting for repeated measures as random effect. There was a statistically significant difference in perceived straight-ahead between all three yaw conditions. Adding +1° yaw rotation led to a higher percentage of “rightward” answers and a mean shift of perceived straight-ahead to the left by 2.15° (95% CI − 3.54 to 0.76, p = 0.003). The same movement combined with − 1° yaw rotation led to a higher percentage of “leftward” answers and a mean shift of perceived straight-ahead by 2.31° (95% CI 0.92–3.69, p = 0.001) to the right (Fig. 2A). There seemed to be increased variability of responses in the “yaw − 1°” condition compared to “no yaw” and “yaw +1°” condition. A second model, examining the effect of rotation on discrimination threshold adjusting for age, gender and handedness, showed no significant difference between the different yaw conditions (p > 0.05, Fig. 2B).

(B) No significant differences observed between yaw conditions based on linear mixed-effects modelling (p > 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons), "n.s." = non-significant.

Perceived straight ahead and discrimination threshold did not show dependence on handedness (p > 0.05, Fig. 3). Male subjects showed a significant difference in perceived straight ahead of 2.60° to the right compared to female subjects (95% CI 0.50–4.82, p = 0.017), Fig. 3). Secondary sensitivity analysis did not show a yaw condition-dependent effect of sex (p > 0.05).

(iii)Self-motion perception in patients with unilateral vestibulopathy

Self-motion perception in patients with unilateral vestibulopathy

To account and adjust for group differences (mainly age as the patients were older than the healthy subjects) a linear mixed model was fitted with age, gender, condition and population (right vestibulopathy vs. left vestibulopathy vs. healthy) as fixed effects while accounting for repeated measures and subject as a random factor. Since the affected side of the UVH was expected to influence the yaw rotation towards the pathological side, interactions of the side of pathology were accounted for in each condition. As no effect of handedness had been observed in the healthy cohort, handedness was not assessed or included in the model. Significant main effects on perceived straight-ahead were found for condition, population and for gender, with no significant effect of age. Patients with vestibulopathy showed a general leftward shift of the perceived straight ahead compared to healthy subjects which was independent of the side of the pathology. Patients with UVH on the right had a shift of − 3.33° (95% CI − 6.65 to 0.00, p = 0.05, Fig. 4A) and patients with UVH on the left of − 4.90° (95% CI − 8.77 to − 1.04, p = 0.013, Fig. 4A). Interaction terms of pathological side and yaw condition were not significant, indicating that the yaw-related shifts were similar between left and right UVH patients and healthy subjects.

(A) Estimated marginal means for perceived straight-ahead across yaw rotation conditions in UVH patients and healthy subjects. (B) Estimated marginal means for discrimination thresholds across yaw rotation conditions in UVH patients and healthy subjects. Discrimination thresholds are reported as geometric means from log-transformed mixed models. As observed in healthy subjects, analysis of the whole population (vestibular patients and healthy subjects) again showed a significant effect of gender on perceived straight ahead of 2.01° compared to female subjects (95% CI 0.26–3.77, p = 0.025).

The effect of yaw rotation on discrimination threshold revealed significant effects between patients and healthy subjects, with patients showing higher discrimination thresholds across all conditions (Fig. 4B). Geometric mean discrimination thresholds were 5.48° (95% CI 4.08–6.87) for healthy subjects, 8.74° (95% CI 5.71–11.77, p = 0.034) for right UVH patients, and 11.89° (95% CI 7.44–16.34, p = 0.001) for left UVH patients. Patients with left UVH displayed higher discrimination thresholds than patients with right UVH. Five observations (4 left UVH, 1 right UVH) reached the 50° fitting boundary, indicating severely impaired discrimination within the tested stimulus range (± 10°). There was no significant change in discrimination threshold between the three yaw conditions.

Individual psychometric curves for UVH patient groups (Supplementary Fig. 2) and relationships between discrimination thresholds, age, sex, and pathology (Supplementary Fig. 3) are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Discussion

This study shows the systematic effect of rotational movements on self-motion perception in combined motion paradigms in healthy subjects and patients with unilateral vestibular hypofunction.

In healthy subjects, 1° of added rotation resulted in a mean shift of perceived straight-ahead of 2.3° (rotation to the left) and 2.1° (rotation to the right) respectively. Discrimination thresholds did not change significantly between different conditions. Our findings are comparable to previous work, where concurrent rotation and lateral displacements revealed a consistent bias of rotation on coarse direction discrimination, which in turn have shown to be correlated to heading discrimination14,15. Given the known functional lateralisation of vestibular processing on the non-dominant hemisphere, the effect of the subjects’ handedness was evaluated to detect a possible lateralisation bias. There was no observed effect of handedness on perceived straight-ahead or discrimination thresholds. However, male gender did show an effect on heading perception with a 2.1° shift to the right of perceived straight-ahead compared to women without any effect on discrimination thresholds. While direction recognition thresholds have not shown to be different in male and female healthy subjects16, sex-differences have been described in demanding balance tasks as well as in active navigation tasks17,18. Prior literature points to a male advantage in human navigation19, our results however suggest a deviation of PSA to the right in men in this direction discrimination task. The interpretation of our finding of sex-specific differences in heading perception is currently unclear but supports consideration of sex-specific recruitment and analysis for future studies.

Patients with unilateral vestibulopathy displayed significantly larger discrimination thresholds than healthy subjects (left UVH: +6.4°, right UVH +3.3°), reflecting the reduced capability of heading perception based on vestibular inputs alone in these patients. Prior studies on self-motion perception in patients with bilateral vestibular hypofunction have observed increased motion perception thresholds for linear and angular motion20,21. Rotational direction-discrimination thresholds are known to increase after unilateral vestibular loss22–24. To our knowledge, there is no data on heading discrimination thresholds for patients with unilateral vestibular hypofunction. Our data reveal an overall change in perceived straight-ahead by − 3.33° (right UVH) and − 4.90° (left UVH) respectively. No specific, lateralized effect towards the pathological side was seen. Prior studies have described a shift of the subjective straight ahead in chronic UVH patients towards the right independent of the side of the vestibular lesion and argued to resemble the effect of a spatial neglect as seen in patients after right hemispheric stroke25,26. As in our paradigm, a leftwards shift of PSA represents a higher amount of “rightwards” answers, our results are comparable to these prior findings, although there is a limitation in the interpretability of our data due to the simultaneously observed increased discrimination thresholds. Our data shows a clear increase in discrimination thresholds in UVH patients, reflecting poorer vestibular-guided self-motion perception. In addition, we observe a leftward shift of PSA, which may be comparable to earlier reports resembling spatial neglect, although this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Our results are in accordance with previous findings of Ivanenko27, Nooij4 and MacNeilage3 that overall self-motion perception cannot be explained by pure summation of the effects of translation (linear motion) and rotation (angular motion) alone. If linear and rotational stimuli were processed equally, we would expect a shift of the perceived straight ahead of around 1° in the conditions of combined rotational and translational movements compared to the baseline condition in healthy subjects. Our data shows a significant shift of the perceived straight ahead of 2.3° and 2.1° respectively, indicating the effect of the added rotation to lead to more than double the estimated effect. A confounding effect of rotation on linear otolith perception cannot be excluded based on our findings. However, if rotational signals were processed by the otoliths, we would expect the linear vector to be pointing towards the opposite side (i.e. the ear that is rotating forward). In this case combined rotational and translational movements would counteract each other and we would expect the shift of the perceived straight ahead to be smaller than in the linear condition. We therefore argue that the strong effect on the perceived straight ahead that we observe in conditions with combined motions, is an indicator of canal-otolith integration with central re-weighting and prioritization of rotational motions over pure translational motions in the horizontal plane.

There are several limitations to our study that need to be taken into consideration. First, the participant sample size is small. However, with a total of 52 subjects this is to our knowledge the largest explorative study on direction discrimination in vestibular guided self-motion perception. Further, our healthy subjects were significantly younger than the vestibular patient group. Since age dependency in thresholds for linear anterior–posterior movements has been shown before, we adjusted our model for age and did not observe a significant influence on our outcomes14,28.

To conclude, this study was able to analyse combined rotational and translational movements in healthy subjects as well as in patients with vestibular unilateral hypofunction. Vestibular guided self-motion perception in the horizontal plane shows to be heavily influenced by rotational movements. These findings support the hypothesis of central integration mechanisms of canal and otolith signals. Self-motion perception is strongly impaired towards both sides in patients with unilateral vestibular hypofunction. With this study we introduce a novel motion paradigm to further explore physiological and pathophysiological effects in self-motion perception.

Methods

Subject details

Twenty-eight healthy subjects were recruited for the experiment. Due to protocol maladherence, two subjects had to be excluded. Twenty-six healthy subjects (18 female, 8 male, mean age 31.0 STD ± 9.9) with no history of neurological or vestibular disease were included for the data analysis. Healthy subjects were screened for handedness based on self-reported hand preference in one-handed activities, such as writing, and recruited accordingly to ensure a balanced distribution of right- and left-handed individuals (17 right-handed subjects). All but two subjects were naive to the task.

Vestibulopathy patients were screened from the local clinic information system. Vestibular testing was performed as described below. Inclusion criteria were chronic unilateral vestibular hypofunction (UVH) of at least the horizontal canal in the video head impulse test (since we wanted to test rotation around the vertical axis). All but two patients also demonstrated otolith hypofunction of either saccule or utricle measured with oVEMPs and cVEMPs (Supplementary Table 1, P007 and P022). Vestibulopathy had to be chronic and persistent for at least 12 months. Exclusion criteria were neurological disease or use of medication affecting the vestibular system. Twenty-six patients were included (12 female, 14 male, mean age 56.3 years, STD ± 11.8). Patients were screened and matched for equal distribution of the side of the pathology (14 vestibulopathies on the right). The Zurich cantonal ethics committee approved the protocol (BASEC 2020-01569). Written informed consent was obtained by all participants and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Vestibular laboratory testing

Vestibular testing in patients was performed as described previously by our group29. Horizontal canal function was determined by the video head impulse (vHIT) test, while dynamic utricular and saccular function were assessed by ocular and cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (o- and cVEMPs). Horizontal canal (HC) hypofunction was generally defined by a gain < 0.8 plus the presence of refixation saccades30. For o- and cVEMPs, an asymmetry ratio (AR) ≥ 30% indicates asymmetric otolith function based on the normative values of our lab. Ocular VEMPs were recorded for 500 Hz bone-conducted vibration (BCV) stimuli at Fz. For cervical VEMPs, a 500 Hz air-conducted sound (ACS) stimulus was applied to the ipsilateral ear via headphones in the first place. In case of an asymmetric response, the measurement was usually repeated with a 500 Hz BCV stimulus at Fz, as even small air-bone gaps may compromise the cVEMP response to ACS31. Dynamic saccular hypofunction was diagnosed if both ACS and BCV cVEMPs showed asymmetric results. In patients where an air–bone gap was either visible in the pure tone audiogram (PTA) or could not be ruled out completely (e.g. due to deafness), dynamic saccular function was solely determined by BCV cVEMPs. Detailed vestibular testing results are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Experimental setup and procedures

Participants were seated and fastened with a 4-point safety belt on a chair placed on a motion simulator at the University of Zurich (motion platform with six degrees of freedom, E Cue 624-1800 motion system, FCS Simulator Systems, Schiphol, Netherlands). To prevent head movement a mouldable head-mask was individually fitted and attached to the back of the chair (Fig. 5). Experiments were conducted in complete darkness to exclude visual input. Padded vacuum cushions and music played through noise cancelling headphones (BOSE, QuietComfort® 20i) were used to minimize proprioceptive and auditory inputs from the platform. In front of the subjects (distance 1.5 m) three LEDs were mounted horizontally and turned on in between trials to re-orientate subjects. Response buttons were mounted on a handrail in front of the subjects and adjusted to their arm length.

(A) Experiment setup on motion platform. Subject seated on chair on motion platform with vacuum cushions and mouldable head mask. Participants were sat in darkness and reported perceived location in relation to the starting point via right or left button press. (B) Schematic motion profiles for all three conditions.

Participants were tested in three conditions: translation without yaw rotation (no yaw), translation combined with rotation of 1° to the right (yaw +1°) and translation combined with rotation of 1° to the left (yaw − 1°). Stimulus intensities were chosen based on pilot experiments to develop a task difficult enough for healthy subjects but still feasible for patients. Similar direction recognition thresholds for yaw rotations have been described before32. Each condition consisted of a minimum of 65 randomized trials. The experiment was divided in two blocks, first completing all “no yaw” trials (with randomized heading angles) and then completing all “yaw +1°” and “yaw − 1°” trials (again randomized for heading angle and rotation direction) with a short break after half of the trials to avoid fatigue. After reaching the final position in each trial, participants were instructed to indicate the perceived location in space relative to the starting position by pressing the right or left button. In case that they felt they were going straight ahead or in case of uncertainty, they were instructed to press a random button. All instructions were provided verbally in Swiss German by the same experimenter (SH). Verbatim instructions are provided in English translation in Supplementary Materials. For the conditions with added yaw components, participants were instructed to again report the perceived location in space relative to the starting position, not the direction in which the head or nose was pointing. The platform returned to the starting position for the next trial in two steps: first, a slow movement to the center position (20 cm in 4 s); then, after a 1-s delay, a further backward movement of 20 cm in 2 s. In each case, the movement profile followed a sinusoidal half-cycle. The next trial began after a 1-s pause, making the time between the end of one stimulus and the start of the next 8 s plus the subject’s reaction time.

Motion profiles

All motion components (forward and inter-aural translations, and yaw rotations) were one half-cycle of a sinusoidal profile. Displacement for translation was 40 cm in 1.5 s using a raised cosine profile (average velocity = 26.66 cm/s, peak velocity = 42 cm/s, peak acceleration = 88 cm/s2) and therefore above thresholds for anterior–posterior and lateral heading discrimination28,33,34. Lateral displacements also had a raised cosine profile; for the 5° stimulus, this means a displacement of 3.5 cm with a peak velocity of 3.7 cm/s, and peak acceleration of 7.6 cm/s2. For the 10° stimulus the displacement was 7 cm with a peak velocity of 7.3 cm/s and peak acceleration of 15 cm/s2. Yaw rotations were fixed to the head, about an axis approximately bisecting the inter-aural axis. Rotations had an average velocity of 0.67°/s, peak velocity = 2.1°/s, and peak acceleration = 4.4°/s2). Heading varied from − 10° (leftwards) to +10° (rightwards) in 5° steps (5° and 10° headings correspond to lateral displacements of 3.5 and 7 cm). An example motion profile illustrating these components is shown in Fig. 6.

Motion profile details. Example motion profile for a forward motion with a 10° deviation in direction, and a 1° yaw rotation. The translation components are shown in A (position), B (velocity), and C(acceleration), while the yaw component is show in D (position), E (velocity), and F (acceleration).

The motion profiles were created in Matlab 2020a, downloaded to the motion platform control computer and executed with an update frequency of 512 Hz by the software program Moog Replication 3.18.2, Moog, Inc.

Statistical analysis

Psychometric functions were calculated by fitting cumulative Gaussian distributions to the data by maximum likelihood estimation using the MatLab (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) function ‘fmincon’35,36 to minimize negative log-likelihood of the observed responses under the binomial model. The mean of the fitted function was taken as the point of subjective equality (PSE), representing perceived straight ahead (i.e., the stimulus at which 50% of responses were to the right and 50% to the left). The standard deviation of the function was used as a measure of discrimination sensitivity, labelled discrimination threshold, and was bounded between 0° and 50° during curve fitting. Discrimination thresholds were log-transformed prior to analysis to meet model assumptions, as initial model diagnostics revealed right-skewed residuals (Supplementary Fig. 1). All estimates and confidence intervals were back-transformed to the original degree scale and are reported as geometric means with 95% confidence intervals.

Statistical Analysis was conducted using R (R Core Team, 2022), RStudio (RStudio Team, 2022), with lme437 to perform linear mixed model analysis. Model parameters were primarily chosen based on the research question; model fit was evaluated using AIC (Akaike Information Criterion).
