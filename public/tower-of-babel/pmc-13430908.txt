Comparison of Johnsons, Inslers formula and ultrasound for estimating fetal weight
Authors: Khadijeh Nasiri, Farzaneh Rashidi, Nasrin Akbari, Ayda EzzatiYazdani, Alireza Afshari-Safavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate estimation of fetal weight is a crucial component of pregnancy management and plays a key role in reducing maternal and neonatal complications. Although ultrasonography is considered the gold standard, its limited availability and cost emphasize the need to assess alternative clinical methods.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 132 term pregnant women (37to 41 weeks) selected via non-probability sampling. Fetal weight was estimated prior to childbirth using three methods: ultrasonography (performed within the last two weeks of the third trimester to minimize the impact of rapid fetal weight gain closer to term), Johnson’s formula, and Insler’s formula and was compared with the actual birth weight immediately after childbirth. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24, with descriptive statistics and regression.ResultsThe mean ± SD of estimated fetal weights (EFW) obtained via third-trimester ultrasonography and based on the Johnson and Insler formulas were 3164 ± 382.95 g, 3161.27 ± 302.90 g, and 3309.35 ± 374.05 g, respectively. Regarding diagnostic accuracy, the Johnson formula showed a precision of 69.70% for neonates < 3000 g and 71.21% for macrosomia (> 3500 g), the Insler formula showed a precision of 75.76% for neonates < 3000 g and 68.94% for macrosomia (> 3500 g). In the multivariate regression analysis of estimated fetal weight using the Johnson formula, only fundal height remained a significant independent predictor (β = 149.43, P < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough ultrasound is the most accurate method for estimating fetal weight, with the smallest mean difference from the actual weight, clinical methods, particularly the Insler method for normal weight pregnancies and the Johnson method for ruling out macrosomia, have acceptable accuracy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Fetal weight estimation, Formula, Ultrasound, Birth weight

Introduction

Pregnancy is a unique and complex physiological process during which a woman undergoes numerous adaptive changes to support fetal growth and development [1–3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines maternal health as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period [4]. Promoting well-being throughout pregnancy improves outcomes for both woman and fetus, and ultimately benefits the broader community [5]. During pregnancy, nutritional and energy requirements increase to meet the demands of the developing fetus. Inadequate dietary intake, resulting in deficiencies in essential nutrients and energy, can adversely affect neonatal health and pregnancy outcomes [6]. Limited dietary diversity, in particular, has been associated with higher risks of preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal anemia [7]. Insufficient gestational weight gain may lead to preterm birth, low birth weight, and increased perinatal mortality, whereas excessive weight gain is linked to macrosomia, gestational diabetes, and birth-related injuries. Monitoring maternal weight during antenatal visits serves as an indirect indicator of fetal growth, which is influenced by genetic, maternal, fetal, and placental factors [8]. Fetal weight estimation is a fundamental part of routine obstetric assessment during the second and third trimesters [9]. It directly informs prenatal and postnatal care planning and plays a vital role in reducing perinatal morbidity and mortality [10]. Furthermore, estimating fetal weight in late pregnancy provides essential information for clinical decision-making, including determining the mode of delivery [11]. It also aids in identifying intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), thereby helping to reduce associated complications and mortality [12]. According to the WHO, newborns weighing less than 2500 g face a substantially increased risk of mortality within the first 28 days of life [13]. Hence, accurate fetal weight assessment is a key determinant of optimal growth, survival, and long-term health outcomes [14], particularly at weight extremes, both low birth weight and macrosomia. Neonates below the third percentile have a higher risk of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, and necrotizing enterocolitis [11]. In contrast, macrosomia increases the risk of shoulder dystocia, birth trauma, postpartum hemorrhage, and perinatal mortality [15]. Globally, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates the prevalence of low birth weight (LBW) at 15.5%, corresponding to approximately 20 million births each year [16]. The prevalence of macrosomia (birth weight ≥ 4000 g) is reported to range from 3% to 15% of all pregnancies worldwide [17]. Given the importance of accurate fetal weight assessment, current methods primarily include imaging-based approaches (such as ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging) and clinical approaches (such as abdominal palpation, Leopold’s maneuvers, and predictive formulas for estimating birth weight) [18]. The Johnson and Insler formulas estimate fetal weight using fundal height measurements. Fundal height assessment is a routine antenatal procedure that can be performed by any trained healthcare [19]. The Johnson formula, in particular, incorporates both fundal height and the degree of fetal head engagement within the maternal pelvis [20]. Measuring the distance from the symphysis pubis to the uterine fundus with a tape measure provides a simple, objective, and accessible clinical estimate of fetal weight [21]. Because this method does not require specialized skills, the use of the Johnson formula can facilitate timely referral of pregnant women to higher-level healthcare facilities, thereby helping to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [20]. Accordingly, these formulas are recommended as simple, safe, cost-effective, and reasonably reliable tools for fetal weight estimation [19].

In contrast, ultrasonography has significantly transformed obstetric care by reducing maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [22]. Fetal size assessment via ultrasound typically relies on biometric parameters such as abdominal circumference (AC), femur length (FL), biparietal diameter (BPD), and head circumference (HC). Evidence suggests that combining multiple parameters yields more accurate estimates than relying on one or two measurements alone. However, fetal growth assessment is influenced by various factors, including epidemiological, social, and ethnic characteristics, as well as maternal factors (e.g., height, weight, parity) and fetal factors (e.g., amniotic fluid volume, sex, presentation, and placental location) [23].

In view of the clinical relevance of accurate fetal weight estimation and the need to determine the most precise method, the present study was designed to compare the Johnson and Insler formulas with ultrasonography for estimating fetal weight in pregnant women during 2024–2025.

Method

Study design

This study was a cross-sectional study conducted in 2024–2025. A non-probability sampling method was used. During the study period, all pregnant women attending the antenatal clinic who met the inclusion criteria were recruited until the required sample size was reached. In non-probability sampling, participants are selected based on the researcher’s judgment, and the probability of selection for each member of the population is unknown. As a result, this method does not rely on probability principles, and its findings may not be generalizable to the broader population.

Sample size

Based on the findings of Mossayebnezhad et al. [20] and considering a significance level of 5% and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). The analysis was performed using t-tests—Means: Difference between two dependent means. The input parameters were: Tail(s) = Two, Effect size dz = 0.2300049, α error prob = 0.05, and Power (1–β error prob) = 0.8. The computed total sample size was ×132 participants, with an actual test power of 0.8018499.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a structured demographic and obstetric questionnaire, neonatal weight form, standard scale and measuring tape, and abdominal ultrasonography.

The demographic and obstetric profile form included the following variables: occupation, educational level, history of infertility, Gender of the newborn, number of pregnancies (gravida), history of abortion, Enjoyment of pregnancy period, maternal age (years), body mass index (BMI), fundal height (cm), abdominal circumference (cm), and findings of the first vaginal examination (cervical dilatation and effacement) (supplementary).

actual birth weight (g), neonatal length at birth (cm), head circumference at birth (cm), and chest circumference at birth (cm).

A German Beurer-JBY80 digital baby scale was used to measure weight. It consists of a flexible plastic tape measure. The GE Voluson 730 Expert device was used to examine fetal ultrasound indices.

Study procedure

After obtaining the official introduction letter and necessary institutional permissions, the researcher entered the study setting and selected eligible participants according to the inclusion criteria. The study objectives and procedures were explained in detail, and participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential and that all data would be analyzed and presented in aggregate form, without identifying individual participants.

A total of 132 healthy term singleton pregnant women were ultimately enrolled through non-probability sampling. Fetal weight for each participant was estimated using three methods: The Johnson formula, the Insler formula, and ultrasonography.

For clinical formulas, fundal height (FH) and abdominal circumference (AC) were measured for each woman using a dedicated tape measure, and fetal presentation and station were determined by an experienced midwife with more than 10 years of labor ward and teaching experience in fetal weight estimation. The measurements were then entered into the Johnson and Insler formulas to obtain estimated fetal weights.

For the ultrasound-based estimate, a scan performed within the previous two weeks by a single sonographer at a designated center was used, and fetal weight was adjusted considering the expected late-pregnancy fetal growth rate of approximately 30–35 g per day. Immediately after birth, the neonate’s actual weight was measured using the same childbirth-room digital scale. The actual birth weight was then compared with the estimated fetal weights obtained from the three methods.

Inclusion criteria were: healthy, live, singleton fetus in longitudinal lie with vertex presentation; absence of structural anomalies on ultrasound; and no congenital or acquired uterine abnormalities. Exclusion criteria included: multiple pregnancies (twins or higher-order gestations); documented fetal anomalies or congenital malformations; abnormal amniotic fluid volume (oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios); and maternal conditions that could affect the accuracy of fundal height measurement, such as obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²), large uterine fibroids, or a history of recent abdominal surgery.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using frequency (percentage) for qualitative variables and mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed quantitative variables. To evaluate the diagnostic methods, sensitivity and specificity were computed. The key assumptions of the multivariate regression model, including the independence of errors, were tested and confirmed using the Durbin–Watson statistic. A significance level of 0.05 was considered for all tests. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 24 and included descriptive statistics, multiple regression, and diagnostic accuracy indices such as sensitivity, specificity, and precision across different neonatal weight categories.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences (IR.NKUMS.REC.1403.127) and conducted in accordance with institutional and international ethical standards. Prior to enrollment, all participants received comprehensive information about the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent. Participation was entirely voluntary, no coercion was applied at any stage, and participants retained the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any impact on their care.

Discussion

The result of the present study indicate that both the Johnson formula and ultrasonography exhibited the smallest deviation from actual birth weight, whereas the Insler formula tended to overestimate fetal weight. These results are consistent with evidence reported in recent years. Combs et al. (2024) noted that third-trimester ultrasound-based fetal weight estimation, despite being operator-dependent, remains one of the most accurate methods for predicting birth weight—particularly when multiple biometric parameters are used simultaneously [9]. Similarly, Iyiola et al. (2024) demonstrated that clinically based methods relying on maternal characteristics and simple measurements can achieve accuracy comparable to ultrasonography when appropriately applied. These findings align with the present study, suggesting that the Johnson formula may serve as a simple, cost-effective, and practical alternative in settings with limited diagnostic resources [12].

In the multivariate regression analysis of the current study, fundal height emerged as the strongest predictor of estimated fetal weight in both the Johnson and Insler models, consistent with recent literature. Gifty et al. (2024), in a prospective study, reported a strong correlation between fundal height at term and actual birth weight, supporting its role as a reliable clinical indicator [22]. In contrast, within the Insler model, maternal body mass index (BMI) remained an independent and significant predictor. Silva et al. (2025) found that in women with elevated BMI, the accuracy of abdominal-based estimation methods decreases, as increased subcutaneous fat thickness may lead to overestimation of fetal weight. This finding may explain the overestimation observed with the Insler formula in the present study [23].

Regarding the identification of macrosomia, the Johnson formula demonstrated high specificity but limited sensitivity. This is in line with the findings of Xing et al. (2024), who reported that non-imaging approaches, despite their high specificity, may fail to detect all cases of macrosomia and can miss some high-risk pregnancies [15]. Conversely, the Insler formula showed greater sensitivity among fetuses with normal weight. Hadianti et al. (2023) similarly reported that the Insler method maintains acceptable accuracy in low-risk pregnancies with normal fetal weights and can function effectively as an initial screening tool [16]. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of selecting fetal weight estimation methods based on both the fetal weight category and the intended clinical application.

Bhat et al. reported that ultrasound measurement of fetal weight is more accurate than estimation using Insler’s or Dare’s formula [25]. Likewise, studies by Sherman et al. [3] and Titapant et al. [26] concluded that ultrasonography provides more accurate fetal weight estimates. However, other studies, including those by Hendrix et al. [27], Raman et al. [28], and Sherman et al. [3], have suggested that ultrasound may be less accurate than clinical assessment. Additionally, Amrita et al. (2023) demonstrated that both clinical and ultrasound methods show good agreement with actual birth weight. Notably, the clinical method using Insler’s formula was found to be more accessible, cost-effective, and sufficiently accurate in low-resource settings [24].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of pregnant women. Second, the single-center design may introduce selection bias and affect the representativeness of the results. Third, the interval between the last ultrasonographic assessment and delivery may have influenced the accuracy of fetal weight estimation. Comparative analysis is not equivalent in methodological terms. Although inclusion criteria required an ultrasound within the final two weeks of the third trimester, this interval—despite adjustment for fetal growth—may have reduced the precision of ultrasonography compared to clinical assessments performed at the time of childbirth.

Conclusion

Although ultrasound is the most accurate method for estimating fetal weight, with the smallest mean difference from the actual weight, clinical methods, particularly the Insler method for normal weight pregnancies and the Johnson method for ruling out macrosomia, have acceptable accuracy.
