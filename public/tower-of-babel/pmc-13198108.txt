Hypothermia With Prominent Electrocardiographic Changes Following Liver Transplantation
Authors: Jaideep Dey, Amita Garg, Amit Kumar Singhal, Pankaj Lohia, Abhideep Chaudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case SummaryHypothermia can complicate recovery of postoperative patients in the intensive care unit. Cardiovascular manifestations may be an early finding in these cases. We report about a middle-aged patient of orthotopic liver transplantation who developed moderate hypothermia postoperatively. Hemodynamic alterations and electrocardiographic changes resolved with accomplishment of euthermia. Etiologies of postoperative hypothermia must be considered to prevent this complication, which is associated with morbidity and prolongation of hospital stay.Take-Home MessagesHypothermia is a differential diagnosis during new-onset electrocardiographic changes in post–liver transplant patients. Electrocardiographic features of hypothermia may occur early before hemodynamic deterioration. During continuous renal replacement therapy, temperature monitoring and active warming of dialysis fluid are key measures to avoid hypothermia.

A 54-year-old male patient with chronic liver disease (Hepatitis B and D virus coinfection) and hepatorenal syndrome underwent living donor orthotopic liver transplantation. On second postoperative day during ventilatory support and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), bradycardia and hypotension developed, requiring increased inotropic support. Patient's electrocardiogram (ECG) during the event is shown in Figure 1A. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable with no cardiac troponin elevation. Echocardiography did not reveal any new left ventricular regional wall motion abnormality (Videos 1 to 5). Response to ECG Challenge

The patient's ECG shows features of hypothermia. Sinus bradycardia (heart rate: 58/min) with low-amplitude P waves, prolonged PR interval (240 ms), QRS duration (128 ms), and QTc interval of 490 ms were noted (Figure 1A). J point showed a prominent upstroke characteristic of Osborn wave. Diffuse T-wave inversion was present. Shivering artifacts were also noted in precordial leads. The patient's core body temperature was 29.8 °C (85.6 °F). These changes were new, compared to preprocedural ECG (Figure 1B), with a normal sinus rhythm (heart rate: 99/min), PR interval 176 ms, QRS duration 94 ms, and QTc interval 426 ms. Following gradual rewarming, ECG changes resolved over 24 hours (Figure 1C), with core body temperature reaching 36.8 °C (98.2 °F). Sinus tachycardia (heart rate: 103/min), PR interval 145 ms, QRS duration 89 ms, and QTc 421 ms were recorded.

Patient- and procedure-related factors predispose to hypothermia in liver transplant recipients.1,2 Our patient required 36 units of blood component transfusions (including 12 units of packed red blood cells) intraoperatively, which could have contributed to hypothermia. However, meticulous active warming strategies were ensured perioperatively. Surgery duration was not prolonged.

CRRT is also a risk factor for hypothermia in intensive care unit patients and has been reported in >50% of such cases.3 Despite active warming of dialysis fluid in our patient, a malfunction was noted in the temperature-regulating device of the CRRT machine which could have contributed to hypothermia. Corrective measures ensured achievement of euthermia.

New ECG changes in hemodynamically unstable liver transplantation patients must warrant an evaluation for cardiac causes as acute coronary syndrome and stress cardiomyopathy may complicate the perioperative course. J point elevation in ECG should alert towards pericardial causes, particularly acute pericarditis.

Hypothermia did not prolong intensive care unit stay of our patient; however, the overall hospital stay was prolonged due to a surgical site complication. He was discharged in a stable condition 35 days following surgery.

Osborn wave may indicate severity of hypothermia and propensity toward ventricular arrhythmias.4,5 Dispersion of the cardiomyocyte transient outward potassium channel current from epicardium to endocardium promotes phase 2 re-entry, predisposing to ventricular arrhythmias.4 Calcium overload and reduced activation of L-type calcium channel also encourages arrhythmogenicity.4 Progressive diminution of P-wave amplitude may be misinterpreted as junctional rhythm or slow atrial fibrillation, while an increased QT interval may be attributed to medications or dyselectrolytemia, common in critically ill patients.

During new ECG changes or hemodynamic deterioration in postoperative liver transplant patients, hypothermia must be considered as a differential diagnosis to avoid delay in initiating rewarming strategies which can complicate the clinical course.
