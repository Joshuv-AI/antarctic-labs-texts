Sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with non-adherence to stroke medication: an analytical, multi-hospital cross-sectional survey in Punjab, Pakistan

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and a significant contributor to disability. In 2016, over 5.8 million deaths were due to strokes, accounting for 10.2% of total global fatalities. Between 2007 and 2017, there was a notable increase in both the years lived with disability (43.6%) and disability-adjusted life years (15.7%) due to stroke.1 2 The WHO highlighted that annually, 15 million people worldwide suffer a stroke. This results in 5 million deaths and another 5 million individuals facing permanent disability, creating profound challenges for those affected and their families.3 The recurrence of stroke significantly increases the risk of mortality and disability, adding to the healthcare costs for patients. Within the first year after a stroke, there is a 14.7% to 17.1% chance of having another stroke, and the risk rises to 15% to 42% within 5 years.4 5 The mortality rate within 30 days of a second stroke is 41%, almost double the rate for a first stroke.6 Moreover, the likelihood of dying after a second stroke is 43% higher compared with a first stroke. The chance of developing dementia is also threefold higher after a second stroke.7

Medication adherence is key to successful treatment and better health outcomes, including lowering the risk of recurrence of stroke. Adherence is defined as ‘the extent to which a person’s behaviour in taking medication, following a diet and executing lifestyle changes corresponds with agreed recommendations from a healthcare provider’.8,10 Adherence to blood pressure medication can reduce the likelihood of a recurrent stroke in patients by 23%. Additionally, maintaining consistent use of prescribed medication decreases the risk of mortality by 48%.11 More than half of individuals with multiple health risk factors demonstrate significantly improved outcomes when they adhere to their medication regimen. The use of antiplatelet drugs can lower the chance of a recurrent stroke by 32%,12 while post-stroke administration of blood pressure medication can decrease the risk of mortality by 36% to 62%.13 Furthermore, the incorporation of statins can reduce the possibility of another stroke, heart attack or death by 7% to 26%.14 Diabetic individuals who have survived a stroke and adhere to diabetes medication can achieve a 48% reduction in mortality risk.11 However, if patients do not adhere to their medication plans, they are more likely to experience poor prognoses, resulting in the loss of previously described benefits and a reversal in health outcomes. It was observed that less than half of stroke patients adhere to their medication plans,15 and many stop taking crucial blood pressure medication within a year of their stroke.16 This non-adherence is a serious issue, as it hampers recovery and prevention efforts. Having several health conditions often means taking many different medications, which can complicate adherence to treatment plans. In addition, there are multiple factors contributing to medication non-adherence.17 18 Medication non-adherence is indeed a critical issue in the management of stroke survivors. Taking medication correctly is important for recovery and avoiding further health issues.19

Given that medication non-adherence is a recognised challenge in secondary stroke prevention globally, evidence from high-income countries does not fully capture the complexities faced by patients in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). Pakistan, one of the world’s most populous LMICs, presents an urgent case study. In 2021, stroke was the second leading cause of death nationally, responsible for 99 759 deaths, with an incidence of 153 per 100 000 and a staggering prevalence of 1088 per 100 000 population. Despite declining age-standardised rates since 1990,20 the absolute burden remains catastrophic, driven by a unique confluence of factors, including a fragmented, out-of-pocket healthcare system,21 where 85% receive care at primary or secondary facilities, but existing research has focused almost exclusively on tertiary hospitals. Understanding non-adherence in this context is essential not only for Pakistan but also for informing strategies in other LMICs with similar health system constraints.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence of medication non-adherence among stroke survivors in Punjab, Pakistan, and explored why some patients may not follow their stroke medication plans. This will help us to identify the reasons behind this non-adherence and suggest ways to help patients take their medication properly. Also, by understanding these factors, we can improve patient health and lessen the impact of strokes on the healthcare system. Our first objective was to measure the prevalence of medication non-adherence using a tool called the Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Scale (SEAMS), along with the pill counting method. Our second objective was to document and understand the social, economic and health-related reasons that can affect patients taking their medication to prevent secondary strokes. This is the first study of its kind at the provincial level in Pakistan, and it will provide important information that can help improve how strokes are managed in the province.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional survey was conducted to determine the prevalence of medication non-adherence and factors associated with it among stroke patients in public and private facilities across Punjab, Pakistan. In Punjab, stroke care is provided through a mixed health system. Public hospitals, particularly tertiary care teaching hospitals and district headquarters hospitals, provide acute management, though dedicated stroke units are rare and resources such as neuroimaging and thrombolysis are inconsistently available. Follow-up care is usually provided in general medical outpatient departments (OPDs), with limited rehabilitation infrastructure. Private hospitals and clinics, mainly in urban areas, are more likely to have neurologists and specialised follow-up services, but affordability remains a major barrier. This dual system explains why both medical and neurology OPDs were included as recruitment sites.

Participant sampling and recruitment

We employed multi-stage sampling to select stroke survivors. First, we identified 30 eligible hospitals through purposive sampling, including both private and public facilities with inpatient and outpatient capacity to treat stroke patients. Of these, 20 hospitals (13 private and seven public) were randomly selected, provided consent and were included in the study (figure 1). Recruitment took place between January and December 2024 in the OPDs of these hospitals. Stroke participants were selected from medical and neurology OPDs using a consecutive sampling technique. Eligible participants included adults aged 21–75 years with a confirmed diagnosis of ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, verified by attending physicians or neurologists based on clinical evaluation and supporting neuroimaging (CT or MRI) documented in hospital records. Participants were recruited from medical and neurology OPDs of selected hospitals in Punjab, Pakistan. In the public sector, stroke follow-up is commonly delivered in general medical OPDs due to the limited availability of specialised neurology services. In contrast, neurology OPDs, more frequently found in tertiary and private hospitals, provide specialised stroke care, particularly for complex cases and rehabilitation. Including both settings ensured recruitment captured the full spectrum of stroke survivors across public and private healthcare facilities. Attending clinicians first identified potentially eligible stroke patients. We ultimately enrolled 6538 stroke survivors aged 21–75 years (figure 1) who had been diagnosed with stroke for at least the last 6 months before recruitment, were prescribed one or more anti-stroke medications (ie, antihypertensive, lipid-lowering drugs, antiplatelet agents), had family/caretakers willing to participate, and were then approached by pairs of trained research assistants (RAs) (bachelor’s-level graduates). The RAs explained the study in Urdu or Punjabi, provided an information sheet and addressed participant questions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their caregivers. Those unable to provide informed consent due to psychiatric or cognitive impairment, severe disability, pregnancy or a history of cancer were excluded.

Outcome and exposure variables

The study’s main outcome was medication non-adherence in stroke survivors in Punjab, Pakistan, measured by self-reported pill count, and the SEAMS. The study examines a range of exposure variables associated with non-adherence to stroke medication. These include sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level and income status. Clinical factors considered are smoking status, health insurance coverage, the presence of comorbidities (including their number and specific types), stroke type, family history of stroke and ischaemic heart disease, memory loss and fear of dependence on treatment. Treatment-related factors include continuing physiotherapy, blood pressure control, disease duration, daily medication frequency and the number of medications prescribed. The distance to healthcare facilities assesses healthcare access and utilisation, while patient beliefs encompass the effectiveness of medications for stroke prevention and perceptions of medication side effects. Finally, the patient-provider relationship is evaluated through satisfaction with doctor-patient communication.

Data collection

Tool development

We developed a structured questionnaire in Urdu and English that included four sections: (1) Sociodemographic data, (2) SEAMS, translated into Urdu from English, (3) Pill count data and (4) Potential risks for non-adherence (eg, adverse effects, forgetfulness, financial concerns). SEAMS is a 13-item questionnaire with three response options (1=not confident, 2=somewhat confident, 3=very confident). Total scores range from 13–39, with higher scores reflecting greater self-efficacy and better adherence.22,24 In Pakistan, the SEAMS tool was translated using the usual ‘forward-backward’ method from English to Urdu (SEAMS-U). The translated SEAMS-U had good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.897). The test-retest reliability was (Spearman’s r = 0.686, p<0.001) with an intraclass correlation coefficient score of 0.814.

We relied primarily on standard self-reported pill counting, with SEAMs data used for triangulation. Medication adherence was assessed using a self-reported 7 day pill count method. Trained RAs asked participants a standardised question: ‘In the last 7 days, how many of the (X) doses of your prescribed medication did you miss?’ (where (X) referred to the total number of doses prescribed based on dosing frequency). For patients taking multiple medications, this question was repeated for each drug class, and an overall adherence score was calculated by dividing the number of doses taken by the total number prescribed. Count data included the quantity of prescribed pills, the number of pills used and the number not used in a specified timeframe.23 25 26 We piloted the data collection tool on 650 stroke patients (equivalent to 10% of the sample) at a non-participating hospital.

Enumerator training, calibration and quality assurance

Ten Bachelor’s degree students were hired as RAs to recruit participants and collect data. Working in pairs across the 20 facilities, they approached eligible stroke patients identified by clinicians in OPDs, explained the study, obtained informed consent and conducted audio-recorded interviews. All participants were assured of anonymity and their right to withdraw from the study at any time.

RAs were trained for 4 days by trained Medical Officers and an epidemiologist, guided by the first author. Training covered medication adherence, non-adherence patterns, counselling and administering adherence-related questionnaires. To reinforce learning, refresher training was provided for both RAs and MOs via Zoom, helping ensure consistent and ethically sound data collection.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using the structured questionnaire, offered in Urdu or English according to participant preference. Sociodemographic variables and self-reported adherence behaviours (pill count and SEAMS items) were obtained directly from patients. Stroke type (ischaemic vs haemorrhagic) was determined by patient self-report during the interview; participants were asked whether their physician had informed them of the specific stroke type, and responses were recorded accordingly. While self-reporting may introduce recall bias, only patients with a confirmed physician diagnosis of stroke were included, thereby reducing misclassification risk. Other clinical characteristics (eg, stroke type, comorbidities, medication regimen) were verified against hospital medical records when available.

In our study, we included a wide range of sociodemographic, clinical, behavioural and perceptual variables to provide a comprehensive understanding of non-adherence to secondary stroke preventive medications in Pakistan. Specifically, we collected data on age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, education, smoking status, income and health insurance to capture socioeconomic context; concomitant diseases, number and type of comorbidities, hospitalisation history, stroke type, family history of stroke and ischaemic heart disease, memory loss and blood pressure to account for clinical risk factors; and treatment-related and perceptual factors such as fear of dependence, satisfaction with the doctor, continuing physiotherapy, duration of disease, number and frequency of daily medications, activity level, distance to healthcare facility, belief in medication efficacy and concerns about side effects.

Data management and analysis

Data were double-entered with internal cross-verification, regular team meetings and spot checks by field supervisors (MOs) to ensure quality. Analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS V.26 and Python V.3.10.12. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise study variables: continuous data were expressed as means±SD, and categorical data as counts (percentages). Medication non-adherence was calculated as the ratio of pills taken to pills prescribed over 15 days, with adherence below 80% classified as non-adherent. Sociodemographic (eg, age, gender, education, income) and clinical factors (eg, comorbidities, disease duration, daily number/frequency of medication) individually associated with the non-adherence outcome at the p<0.05 level were first analysed using χ²/Fisher’s exact test and included in a generalised linear model for subsequent multivariable logistic regression analysis to determine adjusted associations with stroke medication non-adherence. OR plots were generated using Seaborn and Matplotlib, respectively.

Multivariable logistic regression models were adjusted for age, gender, marital status, education, income, health insurance, smoking status, presence and type of comorbidities, stroke type, disease duration, blood pressure control, number of medications, daily dosing frequency, physiotherapy continuation, perceived medication side effects and satisfaction with doctor-patient communication.

Ethics

Study approval was provided by the Institutional Ethical Review Board of the University of Lahore (Reference Number: REC-UOL-125/23), Rehmatul-Lil-Almeen Institute of Cardiology (Reference Number: RAIC-PESSI-963) and the Employers Social Security Tertiary Hospital Lahore (PESSI-1026).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical factors

Of 9154 invited, 6538 (71%) consented. All included participants had their stroke diagnosis confirmed by hospital medical records, including clinician notes and neuroimaging (CT/MRI). The sample was mostly male (75.5%), Punjabi or Saraiki (91.2%) and married (82.3%). Current smoking (61.6%), uncontrolled hypertension (54.9%) and diabetes (49.6%) were common. Nearly half used >5 medications daily (45.2%), with 43.5% on two times per day dosing. Ischaemic stroke predominated (81.4%); 44.3% reported a family history of stroke/ischaemic heart disease. Over half discontinued physiotherapy (53.5%) or reported side effects (53.9%), though 67.1% believed in the medication’s preventive role (online supplemental table S1).

SEAMS results on medication non-adherence

Participants showed high confidence in routine medication management (86% for multiple daily medications, 79% for multiple daily doses and 78% for changes made by doctors), but markedly lower confidence in challenging situations (40% with side effects, 55% when unsure of timing and 39% when pills appeared different). The mean SEAMS score was 31.3±7.7, with pill counting showing near-equal adherence (50.3%) and non-adherence (49.7%). Non-adherence based on pill count was observed in 49.7% of participants, while the mean SEAMS score was 31.31 ± 7.7, indicating moderate self-reported adherence (table 1).

Associations of sociodemographic and clinical factors with stroke medication non-adherence

In multivariable analysis, after adjustment for sociodemographic (age, gender, marital status, education, income, health insurance, smoking status), clinical (comorbidities, stroke type, disease duration, blood pressure control and treatment-related/behavioural factors (number of medications, dosing frequency, physiotherapy continuation, perceived side effects and doctor-patient satisfaction), several variables were independently associated with medication adherence. Women had substantially lower odds of non-adherence than men (AOR 0.31, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.35). Married participants showed higher odds of non-adherence compared with those with other marital statuses (AOR 1.22, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.45). Lower income levels were protective: participants earning 10 000–25 000 PKR (AOR 0.31, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.41) and 26 000–50 000 PKR (AOR 0.57, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.68) had significantly lower odds of non-adherence than those earning more than 100 000 PKR. Educationally, those with primary/secondary education had lower non-adherence odds than postgraduates (AOR 0.74, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.87). Clinically, controlled blood pressure strongly reduced non-adherence risk (AOR 0.66, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.73), as did longer disease duration (≥5 years: AOR 0.43, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.49) and simpler medication regimens (one time per day: AOR 0.25, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.29; <5 medications: AOR 0.50, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.58). Finally, patients not perceiving medication side effects showed slightly better adherence (AOR 0.98, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.00) (online supplemental table S2).

Figure 2 further illustrated that patients who were married, physically inactive, had ischaemic stroke, discontinued physiotherapy or lived within 30 min of a healthcare facility were more likely to be non-adherent. Conversely, those without perceived side effects (AOR 0.98, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.00) and with fewer comorbidities showed slightly better adherence. All other sociodemographic and clinical factors were statistically insignificant.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive examination of medication non-adherence among stroke patients in Pakistan, showing that nearly half (49.7%) of stroke survivors are not taking their medicines as prescribed. This high rate is greater than the global average of 35.9%27 but lower than the much higher rates reported from Saudi Arabia (84%), China (59.7%) and India (56.2%).28,30 Our findings show that poor adherence to secondary stroke medicines is a widespread challenge and not limited to Pakistan. By combining pill counting with the SEAMS questionnaire, our study offers a more reliable picture of patient behaviour and adds important evidence from a LMIC where such data are scarce.

Our findings underscore the critical role of adherence in the management of chronic conditions such as stroke. Adherence to secondary preventive therapies including antihypertensive, lipid-lowering agents and antiplatelets is essential for reducing recurrence and improving survival, as consistently reported in prior research.31,33 Conversely, non-adherence has been associated with increased risk of recurrent vascular events, disability and higher healthcare expenditures. By documenting adherence patterns and their determinants at the population level, this study also highlights the importance of maintaining systematic and detailed records. Such records allow clinicians and policymakers to monitor trends, identify high-risk subgroups and design targeted public health interventions. In LMIC settings where electronic health information systems are limited, structured adherence data collection can serve as a critical tool for evidence-based planning and resource allocation.

Comparison of adherence assessment methods

An important strength of this study is the use of two complementary methods for assessing medication adherence: a self-reported measure (SEAMS) and a pill count–based method. SEAMS scores (mean 31.3±7.7) reflected moderate self-reported adherence and showed a robust inverse correlation with non-adherence (r = –0.40), reinforcing its utility in identifying at-risk patients. Pill counting revealed that nearly half of the patients (49.7%) were non-adherent, offering a more objective estimate of actual medication-taking behaviour. While self-reports may overestimate adherence due to recall or social desirability bias, pill counts can underestimate it if patients discard or share medications. Using both methods together lends credibility to the findings and aligns with recommendations to triangulate adherence data rather than rely on a single measure.2334,36 This integrated approach enhances validity and provides a more nuanced understanding of adherence behaviour among stroke survivors in Pakistan.

In multivariable analysis, several demographic, clinical and behavioural factors were independently associated with medication adherence.

Demographic factors

The analysis, adjusted for sociodemographic, clinical and treatment-related factors, revealed females demonstrating substantially lower odds of non-adherence compared with males (AOR=0.312). This represents one of the strongest protective effects observed, suggesting women in this population are approximately 69% less likely to be non-adherent than men. Similar findings were observed in studies conducted in India and Saudi Arabia, where females were found to be more adherent to stroke medication compared with males.28 30 Marital status also emerged as significant, with married individuals showing 22% higher odds of non-adherence compared with those with other marital statuses (AOR=1.217). A Chinese study reported no impact of marital status on medication adherence.29 Education displayed a counterintuitive pattern: those with primary/secondary education had 26% lower odds of non-adherence than postgraduates (AOR=0.744), while graduates showed marginally higher odds of non-adherence than postgraduates (AOR=1.166). This result contrasts with the conclusions of previous studies, where authors reported that higher education levels were associated with better adherence scores.28,3037 Given that in our scenario, education level may not directly influence adherence behaviours, there may instead be underlying factors contributing to poorer adherence among individuals with higher education backgrounds. Age and ethnicity did not demonstrate significant associations with non-adherence.

Socioeconomic factors

Participants in the lowest income bracket (10 000–25 000 PKR) had 69% lower odds of non-adherence (AOR=0.308), and those in the 26 000–50 000 PKR bracket had 43% lower odds (AOR=0.567) compared with the highest income group (>1 00 000 PKR). Conversely, middle-income earners (50 000–1 00 000 PKR) showed 29% higher odds of non-adherence (AOR=1.289) than the highest income group. This finding contrasts with a study conducted in a developed country, which reported no significant impact of income on medication adherence among stroke survivors.38 Health card status approached significance, with those without health cards showing a trend toward higher non-adherence (AOR=1.119, p=0.07), though this did not reach statistical significance.

Clinical factors

Our findings identified several clinical and behavioural factors associated with medication adherence among stroke survivors. Controlled blood pressure emerged as a significant protective factor, reducing the odds of non-adherence by 34% (AOR=0.656), consistent with prior studies linking blood pressure control to improved adherence and reduced stroke recurrence.39 Longer disease duration (≥5 years) was also protective, with patients showing 57% lower odds of non-adherence (AOR=0.426), likely reflecting increased health awareness and established medication routines.40 Interestingly, patients with no concomitant disease had 56% higher odds of non-adherence (AOR=1.559), suggesting that comorbid conditions may prompt more consistent healthcare engagement. However, diabetes as a specific comorbidity was associated with 29% higher odds of non-adherence (AOR=1.293), aligning with literature that highlights the challenges of managing complex medication regimens.39 40 Stroke type also showed a modest association, with ischaemic stroke patients having 20% higher odds of non-adherence compared with those with haemorrhagic stroke (AOR=1.198), echoing limited evidence on differential adherence patterns. Additionally, discontinuing physiotherapy increased the odds of non-adherence by 15% (AOR=1.149), reinforcing the importance of integrated chronic care and rehabilitation in supporting long-term adherence. These findings underscore the multifaceted nature of adherence behaviour and highlight key targets for intervention in stroke care.

Medication-related factors

The number of daily medications was inversely related to non-adherence: patients taking fewer than 5 medications had 50% lower odds of non-adherence (AOR=0.496), and those taking 5–9 medications had 29% lower odds (AOR=0.713) compared with those taking 10 or more medications. Most recent evidence suggests that a higher number of daily medications is associated with increased odds of non-adherence among stroke survivors, though some meta-analyses report no significant association.41,43 The relationship may depend on patient population, medication types and healthcare context.

One time per day dosing regimens were strongly associated with improved medication adherence after stroke, with patients on one time per day schedules showing 75% lower odds of non-adherence compared with those on three times per day dosing (AOR=0.248). In contrast, experiencing or fearing medication side effects remained a key barrier, though not perceiving side effects was linked to slightly better adherence (AOR=0.976). These findings suggest that simplifying dosing schedules and proactively managing side effects are promising strategies to enhance adherence in this population.30

Other factors

Other factors showed mixed associations with non-adherence. Physical activity level had a modest effect: those with moderate activity (30–60 minutes/day) had 18% higher odds of non-adherence compared with those with vigorous activity (AOR=1.180). Living closer to healthcare facilities (<30 min) was associated with a small but significant increase in non-adherence odds (AOR=1.028). These results contrast with the broader literature, which emphasises the role of physical activity in stroke recovery and secondary prevention but rarely quantifies its direct impact on medication adherence. Most existing studies focus on adherence to physical activity itself, often linking it to self-efficacy, knowledge and social support, rather than comparing activity intensity in relation to medication-taking behaviour. Similarly, while geographic proximity is often discussed in terms of access to rehabilitation services and structured programmes, there is limited evidence directly connecting closer residence to increased medication non-adherence. Our study adds to this gap by suggesting that moderate activity and closer proximity may not always translate into better medication adherence, possibly due to complacency, reduced perceived urgency or competing behavioural priorities. These findings underscore the need for tailored interventions that address not just access and activity, but also motivation and perception in adherence behaviour.44 45

Factors such as smoking status, family history of stroke, memory loss, fear of dependence on treatment, satisfaction with doctor and belief in medication’s preventive role did not show significant associations with non-adherence.

This comprehensive analysis identifies several modifiable factors (eg, blood pressure control, medication regimen simplification) and demographic characteristics (eg, gender, income) that significantly influence medication adherence among stroke patients, providing targets for tailored interventions.

Clinical implications

Despite the lack of systematic counselling in a resource-constrained setting like Pakistan, doctors can at least direct patients toward reliable services, including pharmacies, health programmes and trustworthy online resources.23 46 47 A recent systematic review recommended additional studies with more sophisticated designs to determine predictors of non-adherence and efficient interventions to enhance adherence in cardiovascular diseases.48

Patients may not comply for various reasons, including practical, emotional or perceptual factors, and we cannot address these issues without investigating each patient’s individual circumstances. Furthermore, healthcare professionals should be especially concerned about patients with multiple chronic diseases, as this increases the probability that they will not adhere to their treatment plans. Each characteristic is linked to significant variations in non-adherence, even with differing measurements, allowing for tailored and adjusted therapy options for these patients. According to our research, there is considerable regional and provincial variance in the prevalence of medication non-adherence among stroke survivors in Pakistan. These findings indicate that specific interventions are needed to increase drug adherence, particularly in provinces and divisions where non-adherence rates are high. To reduce non-adherence, a recent study compared a novel mHealth approach to peer counselling.46

Therapeutic non-adherence can give rise to various issues on four levels: individual, institutional, social and national. Medication adherence affects health outcomes and is a crucial indicator of how well medication management is conducted. To assist patients in transitioning from the planning stage to the action stage, it is essential to recognise and address their unique adherence issues. Inquiring with patients about their concerns regarding therapy for stroke survivors is crucial, and it is important to identify what barriers lead to non-adherence to medication in this population.

Recommendations

Our research highlights the crucial need for mobile health interventions in improving medication adherence, particularly in regions where healthcare accessibility is limited, but mobile phone usage is widespread. Our findings identified several barriers to medication non-adherence that can be overcome through rehabilitation and lifestyle changes. A complex interplay of economic, social and behavioural elements impacts adherence, highlighting the need for a comprehensive approach to understanding and enhancing it. Future research should further investigate these factors, possibly through qualitative studies, to uncover the underlying motivations behind patient medication adherence. Moreover, maintaining detailed records of stroke incidence and prevalence across the country is strongly recommended to inform and improve public health strategies. Lastly, to create solutions that effectively address the problem of non-adherence, further research is required, integrated with digital health and artificial intelligence49 to investigate the elements that contribute to it. To meet the medical needs of this particular population group, policymakers, healthcare professionals and other service providers can use the baseline numbers from our studies to establish their future goals and objectives.

Limitations

It is imperative to acknowledge several limitations. Since self-reporting was used to gauge non-adherence, it is possible that drug compliance was underestimated. However, the SEAMS questionnaire was deemed suitable for individuals with limited reading skills, so we chose to use it.23 50 A similar correlation was found between enhanced measurement validity of adherence and the self-reporting pill-counting approach. While pill counts or pharmacy databases could be used to objectively assess adherence, these methods are not appropriate in Pakistan. Furthermore, data from pill counts and pharmacy databases cannot identify the specific types of non-adherence. Stroke type (ischaemic vs haemorrhagic) was determined by patient self-report of physician diagnosis rather than independent clinical verification, which may have introduced some misclassification. Similarly, comorbid conditions were primarily assessed through patient report, with cross-verification from hospital records when available, and this approach may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Lastly, although our research revealed several significant predictors of non-adherence, further investigation is needed into potential unmeasured factors that may also affect medication non-adherence among stroke survivors.

Conclusion

This study highlights the urgent challenge of medication non-adherence among stroke survivors in Pakistan, with nearly half (49.7%) failing to follow prescribed regimens, underscoring a critical gap in secondary prevention. Our findings reveal that adherence is shaped by a complex interplay of clinical, behavioural and socioeconomic factors. Regimen characteristics emerged as dominant predictors: one time per day dosing reduced non-adherence risk by 75%, and simplified medication schedules were strongly protective. Surprisingly, lower-income and less-educated patients demonstrated better adherence than their wealthier, more educated counterparts, suggesting that cultural or psychosocial dynamics may override conventional assumptions. Gender differences were striking, with women showing threefold better adherence despite men reporting stronger belief in medication benefits. Controlled blood pressure, longer disease duration and higher self-efficacy further supported adherence, while discontinuation of physiotherapy and presence of diabetes increased risk. These insights call for a shift from broad educational campaigns to targeted, evidence-based interventions such as regimen simplification, SMS reminders and gender-sensitive strategies. In Pakistan’s resource-constrained health system, prioritising these tailored approaches could significantly reduce stroke recurrence and improve long-term outcomes.
