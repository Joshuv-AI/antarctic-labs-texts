Multiple Polynomial Recurrence in Weyl Systems
Felipe Hernández

Abstract
In this work we give a full characterization of sets of multiple polynomial recurrence in Weyl systems, which are ergodic unipotent affine transformations on products of tori and finite abelian groups. In particular, we show that measurable and topological recurrence in Weyl systems coincide. Our analysis also yields a structure theorem for polynomial multicorrelation sequences in Weyl systems. These results stem from an in-depth study of the Weyl complexity of a set of polynomials and the introduction of a new concept: the Weyl polynomials generated by a set of polynomials.

2.2.  Characteristic factors and complexities

For our study of recurrence, it will be crucial to characterize recurrence in a system through recurrence in a simpler factor. To achieve this, it is crucial to understand the so-called

of a system, which correspond to the factors that control ergodic averages for polynomial patterns.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an invertible measure-preserving system and
(Y,\nu,T)
a factor of
(X,\mu,T)
. Let

be a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials. We say that

such that there is

,

\displaystyle\lim_{N-M\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N-M}\sum_{n=M+1}^{N}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}=0.

The following result describes the characteristic factor for the ergodic averages over a polynomial pattern.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
19
, Host & Kra]
,
[
26
, Leibman]
)
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be a set of essentially distinct polynomials. Then, there is
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every invertible ergodic system some characteristic factor for the averages over
P
is an inverse limit of
d
-step nilsystems

.

Let

be polynomials. We define the

of

as the minimal

-step nilsystems that is characteristic for the ergodic averages over the pattern

Definition 2.11

Let

It is possible to define Host-Kra complexity and Weyl complexity for each polynomial

3.  The Weyl Polynomials

3.1.  Definition and properties

While the Weyl complexity provides essential information about the characteristic factors for studying ergodic averages for a pattern given by a set of polynomials
P
, it does not give further insights about the limit itself. However, for our purposes we require a deeper understanding. To be more precise, we need a description on how the polynomial multicorrelation sequences

n\mapsto\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu

(1)

evolve in Weyl systems. This is particularly relevant in the
case
f_{0}=f_{1}=\cdots=f_{r}=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
, where the sequence in
Eq.

1
becomes

n\mapsto\mu(A\cap T^{-p_{1}(n)}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-p_{r}(n)}A).

In order to analyze this finer structure, we introduce a new object of study, the
Weyl polynomials
. Before doing so, we first need some preliminary definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},...,p_{r}\}
be a set of integral polynomials. We define for
k\in\mathbb{N}
the matrix with polynomial entries given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}(P)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}p_{1}&0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{r}&0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
p_{1}^{[2]}&p_{1}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{r}^{[2]}&p_{r}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{1}^{[k-1]}&p_{1}^{[k-2]}&p_{1}^{[k-3]}&\ldots&p_{1}&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{r}^{[k-1]}&p_{r}^{[k-2]}&p_{r}^{[k-3]}&\ldots&p_{r}&0\\
p_{1}^{[k]}&p_{1}^{[k-1]}&p_{1}^{[k-2]}&\ldots&p_{1}^{[2]}&p_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{r}^{[k]}&p_{r}^{[k-1]}&p_{r}^{[k-2]}&\ldots&p_{r}^{[2]}&p_{r}\end{array}\right).

If the set of polynomials
P
is clear for the context, we will just write
\Lambda_{k}
(or
\Lambda_{k}(n)
to evaluate the matrix in
n\in\mathbb{N}
).

It was shown in
[
5
, Section 4]
that the matrices
(\Lambda_{k}(P))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
play a special role describing the polynomial orbits of almost every point in a standard Weyl system. In fact, for a standard Weyl system
(X=\mathbb{T}^{d},\mu,T)
with rotation by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
and a point
x
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
whose coordinates and
\alpha
are rationally independent, we have that after reordering coordinates accordingly

\overline{\{(T^{p_{1}(n)}x-x,\ldots,T^{p_{r}(n)}x-x)^{T}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}=\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{d}).

(2)

Moreover, these matrices also determine the Weyl complexity of
P
.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
5
, Proposition 4.1]
)
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials. The Weyl complexity
W(P)
equals the minimal
k
for which

\dim{\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k}(P))}=\dim{\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1}(P))}+r.

(3)

Notice that we can realize
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})
as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})
via the identification
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})\cong\{0\}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})
where the later is contained in
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})
. Thus,
3.2
implies that if the step of the standard Weyl system is at least
W(P)
, then for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
the polynomial orbit in
Eq.

2
has reached a point of “oversaturation,” meaning that all the information about the orbit is encoded in its projection to the
W(P)
-Weyl factor of the system.

On the other hand, the condition in
Eq.

3
amounts to

\dim{\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})\cap(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp})}=r.

The advantage of this equivalent form of
3.2
is that the elements in
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})\cap(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp})
encapsulate the polynomial frequencies that
k
-step Weyl systems can generate but
(k-1)
-step Weyl systems cannot. To be more precise, if
\mathbf{e_{i}}
denotes the
i
-th canonical vector of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, then

\Lambda_{k}=\Lambda_{k}[\mathbf{e_{1}},\mathbf{e_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{e_{k}}]=[\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{1}},\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{2}},\ldots,\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{k}}]=[\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{1}},\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{0}\\
\Lambda_{k-1}\end{array}\right)]

where
\vec{0}
is the vector with
r
zeros, yielding

\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})^{\perp}=\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{1}})^{\perp}\cap(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp}).

This implies that the function

\xi:\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times(\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp})\big)\to\mathbb{R}[n],

\mathbf{v}\mapsto\xi(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{1}}

is injective. Indeed, for
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})\cap(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp})
if
\xi(\mathbf{v})=0
then

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\in

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k}\mathbf{e_{1}})^{\perp}\right)\cap\left(\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})\cap(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})=\{0\}.

Consequently, the space
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times(\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp})\big)
is in bijection with the image of
\xi
, which is a space of polynomials. These polynomials not only capture the gap in dimension between
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})
and
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})
, but also define important frequencies that appear in
k
-step Weyl systems. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.3

(Weyl Polynomials)
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},...,p_{r}\}
be a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials. We define the set of
k
-Weyl polynomials generated by
P
as

\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P):=\xi\left(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1}(P))^{\perp}\right).

When
k=W(P)
, we will just denote
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
and call it the
Weyl polynomials
generated by the set
P
.

Remark 3.4
.

In the definition of the Weyl polynomials, we omit the restriction
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})
as for each
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k}(P))^{\perp}
we have
\xi(\mathbf{v})=0
.

Remark 3.5
.

For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp}
the only coordinate of the product
\mathbf{v}^{T}\cdot\Lambda_{k}(P)
that is possibly nonzero is the first one.

We notice that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for each set of essentially distinct integral polynomials
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
,
\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-module. In particular, when
k=W(P)
, the space
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
has dimension
r
, as it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k}(P))\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times(\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1}(P))^{\perp})\big)
. Moreover, as the polynomials
P
are integral, it is possible to find a basis
Q=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\}
of
r
integral polynomials in
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
.

The following proposition is a corollary of
3.2
and can be seen as an alternative definition to Weyl complexity.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials. The sequence
(\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-decreasing (for the inclusion
\subseteq
) and constant starting exactly from
k=W(P)
.

Proof.

First, we show that the sequence is non-decreasing. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
. Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{kr}
such that
\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{k}(n)=(q(n),0,\ldots,0)
. Define
w=(\mathbf{v},\vec{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)r}
. We observe that
w^{T}\Lambda_{k+1}(n)=(\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{k}(n),0)=(q(n),0,\cdots,0)
and thus
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{k+1}(P)
. Therefore, we conclude that
\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)\subseteq\textup{{{WP}}}_{k+1}(P)
.

Second, we see that the sequence is constant from
k=W(P)
. Let
k>W(P)
,
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
, and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{kr}
such that

\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{k}(n)=(q(n),0,\ldots,0).

(4)

This implies that the vector
(v_{r+1},\ldots,v_{kr})^{T}
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{R}\text{-span }\Lambda_{k-1}
. Since
k-1\geq W(P)
, we have that
v_{i}=0
for all
i>W(P)\cdot r
thanks to
3.2
. In particular

(q(n),0,\ldots,0)=\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{k}(n)=\Big((v_{1},\ldots,v_{W(P)\cdot r})\Lambda_{W(P)}(n),0,\ldots,0\Big),

which implies that
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{W(P)}(P)
.

Finally, we see that for
k<W(P)
, we have
\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)\neq\textup{{{WP}}}_{W(P)}(P)
. Indeed, given that
k<W(P)
, by
3.2
there is
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k})^{\perp}
such that not all its last
r
coordinates are
0
. We choose one of such
\mathbf{w}
with maximal
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
satisfying that there exists
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\cdot j}
such that
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\in\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k+j})^{\perp}
(we know that such
j
exists by finiteness of the Weyl complexity, which follows from
2.9
).
Define
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{k+j+1}(P)
through
(0,\ldots,0,\mathbf{v}^{T},\mathbf{w}^{T})\Lambda_{k+j+1}=(q,0,\ldots,0)
. If
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
, then there should be
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k-1})^{\perp}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
(\mathbf{v}^{\prime T},\mathbf{w}^{\prime T})\Lambda_{k}=(q,0,\ldots,0)
. Then we will have that the vector
-(\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\mathbf{w}^{\prime},0,\cdots,0)+(0,\ldots,0,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})
is in
\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{k+j+1}(P))^{\perp}
and has its last
r
coordinates non-zero, which contradicts the choice of
\mathbf{w}
(i.e., the maximality of

), which implies that
q\notin\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
, concluding.
∎

For
k\leq W(P)
it is possible that
\textup{{{WP}}}_{k-1}(P)=\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
, as the following example shows.

Example 3.7
.

Consider
P=\{n,2n,n^{2}\}
. It can be shown that
W(P)=3
and that
\textup{{{WP}}}_{1}(P)=\textup{{{WP}}}_{2}(P)
is generated by
\{n,n^{2}\}
. Meanwhile,
\textup{{{WP}}}_{3}(P)
is generated by
\{n,n^{2},n^{3}-\frac{n^{4}}{2}\}.

We do not know exactly when
\textup{{{WP}}}_{k-1}(P)\neq\textup{{{WP}}}_{k}(P)
. A better understanding of this behavior could provide an answer for a conjecture of Bergelson, Leibman, and Lesigne
[

, stating that

.

We now list some additional useful properties of the space of Weyl polynomials

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
P
and
Q
be a sets of essentially distinct integral polynomials and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

1.

The set
P
is contained in
\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
.

2.

If
W(P)=1
then
P
is a basis of
\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
.

3.

If
P\subseteq Q
then
\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)\subseteq\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(Q)
.

4.

If
q
is an integral polynomial, then
\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P\circ q)=\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)\circ q
.

5.

If all polynomials in
P
are linear, then
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
is the set of integral polynomial of degree at most
|P|
.

Proof.

For proving
1.
, let
r=|P|
. For
i\in[r]
, if we take
\mathbf{v}
as the vector that has
0
in all coordinates but on the
i
-th coordinate, in which

then we have that
\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{d}(P)=(p_{i},0,\ldots,0)
, and thus
p_{i}\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
.

We note that
2.
follows directly from the definition.

For proving
3.
, if
P\subseteq Q
, then
\Lambda_{d}(P)
can be obtained as a submatrix of
\Lambda_{d}(Q)
(eliminating columns and arrows associated to

). Hence, if
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|P|\cdot d}
is such that
\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{d}(P)=(q,0,\ldots,0)
with

, then by extending
\mathbf{v}
with

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
we have that
\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{d}(Q)=(q,0,\ldots,0)
, getting that

.

In the case of
4.
, for
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}(\Lambda_{d-1}(P))^{\perp}
we have that

\mathbf{v}^{t}\Lambda_{d}(P\circ q)(n)\mathbf{e_{1}}=\mathbf{v}^{t}\Lambda_{d}(P)(q(n))\mathbf{e_{1}},

which implies that
\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P\circ q)=\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)\circ q
.

Finally, we prove

. We already know from
[
5
]
that if

consists of

. We prove the statement then by induction over

. For

. Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r+1}\}
a set of linear polynomials. The set
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
contains all polynomials of degree
r
, given that it contains
\textup{{{WP}}}(P\setminus\{p_{r+1}\})
by

. On the other hand, if
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
does not contain a polynomial of degree
r+1
, then we have that
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)=\textup{{{WP}}}_{r+1-1}(P)
which contradicts
3.6
. We conclude that
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
contains a polynomial of degree
r+1
which implies that contains all polynomials of degree
r+1
, given that
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-module.
∎

3.2.  Polynomial multicorrelation sequences in totally ergodic Weyl systems

Now we turn to the analysis for the structure of polynomial multicorrelation sequences in Weyl systems. We will provide a “polynomial Fourier expansion” of such sequences into functions of the form
n\mapsto e(q(n))
, revealing the natural frequencies
q
of the system. It turns out that these frequencies are precisely the Weyl polynomials. We start with standard Weyl systems and the case in which
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}
are characters of such.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
(X=\mathbb{T}^{d},\mu,T)
be a
d
-step standard Weyl system with rotation by
\beta\in\mathbb{T}
. Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials. For
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
denote
\psi_{v}(x)=e(v_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+v_{d}x_{d})
with
x\in X
. Then, for every
v^{0},\ldots,v^{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
if the sequence

n\mapsto\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu

is not always
0
, then there is
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu=e(q(n)\beta).

Proof.

We will denote the characters of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
generating the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
as

\psi_{v}(x)=\exp\left(2\pi\mathbf{i}\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}x_{i}\right),\penalty\ x\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\penalty\ v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Let
v^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for each
i=0,\ldots,r
. Denote

\mathbf{v}=(v_{1}^{1},\ldots,v_{1}^{r},v_{2}^{1},\ldots,v_{2}^{r},\cdots,v_{d}^{1},\ldots,v_{d}^{r})^{T}.

We compute the integral:

\displaystyle\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu

\displaystyle=\int e\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{d}v^{k}_{i}(p_{k}^{[0]}(n)x_{i}+\cdots+p_{k}^{[i-1]}(n)x_{1}+p_{k}^{[i]}(n)\beta)\Big)dm_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=e\Bigg(\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{k}^{[i]}(n)v_{i}^{k}\Big)\cdot\beta\Bigg)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{d}\int e\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{r}(p_{k}^{[0]}(n)v_{i}^{k}+p_{k}^{[1]}(n)v_{i+1}^{k}+...+p_{k}^{[d-i]}(n)v_{d}^{k})x\Big)dm_{\mathbb{T}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}e\Bigg((\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{d}\mathbf{e}_{1})\beta\Bigg)&\text{ if }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}{\Lambda_{d-1}}^{\perp}\text{ and }v^{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}v^{k}=0\\
0&\text{ if not}\end{cases},

where we used that the condition

\sum_{k=0}^{r}(p_{k}^{[0]}(n)v_{i}^{k}+p_{k}^{[1]}(n)v_{i+1}^{k}+...+p_{k}^{[d-i]}(n)v_{d}^{k})=0,\penalty\ \forall i\in[d]

is equivalent to

v^{0}=-\sum_{k=1}^{r}v^{k}\text{ and }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}\textup{{-span}}{\Lambda_{k-1}}^{\perp}.

Thus,
\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu
is either
0
or equal to
e(q(n)\beta)
with
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
.
∎

3.9
gives an alternative definition of the Weyl polynomials in terms of weighted ergodic averages.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
and
h
be essentially distinct integral polynomials. Then
h\notin\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
if and only if for every
d
-step standard Weyl system, irrational number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, and functions
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}
:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=0.

Proof.

Assume that
h\notin\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
and let
(X=\mathbb{T}^{d},\mu,T)
be a
d
-step standard Weyl system with rotation by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. Let
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
. For each
k\in\{0,\ldots,r\}
by writing
f_{k}
in its Fourier expansion we can assume without loss of generality that
f_{k}=\psi_{v^{k}}
for some
v^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. If the integral
\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu
is zero for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we are done. If not, by
3.9
there is
q\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
such that

\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu=e(q(n)\alpha).

Then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e((h(n)+q(n))\alpha).

Since
h\notin\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
, the integral polynomial
h-q
is not constant, so by Weyl’s equidistribution theorem we get that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=0.

For the other direction, assume that
h\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
. Let
(X=\mathbb{T}^{d},\mu,T)
be a
d
-step standard Weyl system with rotation by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. Consider
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that the vector

\mathbf{v}:=(v_{1}^{1},\ldots,v_{1}^{r},v_{2}^{1},\ldots,v_{2}^{r},\cdots,v_{d}^{1},\ldots,v_{d}^{r})^{T}

satisfies

(-h,0,\ldots,0)=\mathbf{v}\Lambda_{d}\mathbf{e}_{1}.

Set
v^{0}=-\sum_{k=1}^{r}v^{k}
. Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\int\psi_{v^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{v^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{v^{r}}d\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)e(-h(n)\alpha)=1,

concluding.
∎

3.10
is particularly useful to deduce algebraic properties of the
\mathbb{R}
-module of Weyl polynomials, such as the following.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r},h
be essentially distinct integral polynomials and
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

1.

We have that
\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})=\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(p_{1}-p_{r},\ldots,p_{r-1}-p_{r},-p_{r}).

2.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
h\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\})
if and only if

\frac{h(mn+i)-h(i)}{m}\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}\left(\left\{\frac{p_{1}(mn+i)-p_{1}(i)}{m},\ldots,\frac{p_{r}(mn+i)-p_{r}(i)}{m}\right\}\right).

Proof.

Statement
1.
comes from the fact that

\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=\int f_{r}T^{p_{1}(n)-p_{r}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r-1}(n)-p_{r}(n)}f_{r-1}T^{-p_{r}(n)}f_{0}d\mu.

For statement
2.
let us first prove the case when
m=1
. This case comes from noticing that for each
(X,\mu,T)
a
d
-step standard Weyl system, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=0

for all
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
if and only if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e((h(n+i)-h(i))\alpha)\int g_{0}T^{p_{1}(n+i)-p_{1}(i)}g_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n+i)-p_{r}(i)}g_{r}d\mu=0,

for all
g_{0},\ldots,g_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, as we can take
g_{0}=f_{0}
and
g_{j}=T^{p_{j}(i)}f_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and multiply the expression by
e(-h(i)\alpha)
.

To prove the general case, it is enough to prove the case when
i=0
. Notice that by
3.8

4.
,
h\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\})
if and only if
h(mn)\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(\{p_{1}(mn),\ldots,p_{r}(mn)\})
. On the other hand,
h(mn)\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(\{p_{1}(mn),\ldots,p_{r}(mn)\})
if and only if there is
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1}^{T},\ldots,v_{d}^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{Q}^{r\cdot d}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(h(mn),0,\ldots,0)=\mathbf{v}^{T}\Lambda_{d}(\{p_{1}(mn),\ldots,p_{r}(mn)\})

or equivalently

\left(\frac{h(mn)}{m},0,\ldots,0\right)=(v_{1}^{T},mv_{2}^{T},\ldots,m^{d-2}v_{d-1}^{T},m^{d-1}v_{d}^{T})\Lambda_{d}\left(\left\{\frac{p_{1}(mn)}{m},\ldots,\frac{p_{r}(mn)}{m}\right\}\right).

This implies that
h\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\})
if and only if

\frac{h(mn)}{m}\in\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}\left(\left\{\frac{p_{1}(mn)}{m},\ldots,\frac{p_{r}(mn)}{m}\right\}\right),

concluding the argument.
∎

The following result extends
3.9
to all totally ergodic Weyl system, giving
C
as a special case.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a connected
d
-step Weyl systems with Kronecker factor given by
x\in\mathbb{T}^{s}\mapsto x+\beta
, where
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{s})\in\mathbb{T}^{s}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials. Then for functions
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
there are a sequence
(c_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
with

\left\lVert(c_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})}\leq\prod_{k=0}^{r}||f_{k}||_{2}

and integral polynomials
\{q_{l,j}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N},j\in[s]}\subseteq\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
such that

\int_{X}f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}c_{l}\cdot e\left(q_{l,1}(n)\beta_{1}+\cdots+q_{l,s}(n)\beta_{s}\right).

(5)

Proof.

Using
Lemma

2.6
and possibly passing to an extension, it suffices to consider the case in which
(X,\mu,T)
is a product of
s
many standard
d
-step Weyl systems, with
X=\mathbb{T}^{sd}
and
T=S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{s}
where
(\mathbb{T}^{d},S_{j})
is a standard Weyl system such that for
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
,

S_{j}(x_{1},...,x_{d})=(x_{1}+\beta_{j},x_{2}+x_{1},...,x_{d}+x_{d-1}).

We emphasize that
\beta
may not correspond to the rotation
\beta^{\prime}
in the Kronecker system of the original system. However, by carefully examining the proof of
Lemma

2.6
, one can deduce that
\beta^{\prime}
can be obtained by multiplying
\beta
with an invertible matrix with rational coefficients, and as
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-module, the conclusion will remain unchanged by this fact.

Let
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{sd})
. We notice that
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{sd})
is generated by the orthonormal basis of functions

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{s}\mapsto\psi_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}(x)=\psi_{l_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots\psi_{l_{s}}(x_{s})

with
l_{1},\ldots l_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Thus, for each
k\in\{0,\ldots,r\}

f_{k}=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots l_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}\rangle\psi_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}.

In this way,

\int_{X}f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}^{k},\ldots,l_{s}^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
k=0,\ldots,r\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{r}\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1}^{k},\ldots,l_{s}^{k}}\rangle\right)\cdot\int\psi_{l_{1}^{0},\ldots,l_{s}^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{l_{1}^{1},\ldots,l_{s}^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{l_{1}^{r},\ldots,l_{s}^{r}}d\mu.

Observe that we have the following identity

\int\psi_{l_{1}^{0},\ldots,l_{s}^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{l_{1}^{1},\ldots,l_{s}^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{l_{1}^{r},\ldots,l_{s}^{r}}d\mu=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\int\psi_{l_{j}^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{l_{j}^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{l_{j}^{r}}d\mu,

and if this product is not zero, then by
3.9
there are
q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\in\textup{{{WP}}}(P)
such that

\prod_{j=1}^{s}\int\psi_{l_{j}^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{l_{j}^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{l_{j}^{r}}d\mu=\prod_{j=1}^{s}e(q_{j}(n)\beta_{j})=e(q_{1}(n)\beta_{1}+\cdots+q_{s}(n)\beta_{s}).

On the other hand, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}^{k},\ldots,l_{s}^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
k=0,\ldots,r\end{subarray}}\left|\prod_{k=0}^{r}\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1}^{k},\ldots,l_{s}^{k}}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=0}^{r}\left(\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{s}}\rangle^{2}\right|\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=0}^{r}||f_{k}||_{2}.

Therefore, rearranging the nonzero terms in the absolutely convergence series

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}^{k},\ldots,l_{s}^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
k=0,\ldots,r\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{r}\langle f_{k},\psi_{l_{1}^{k},\ldots,l_{s}^{k}}\rangle\right)\cdot\int\psi_{l_{1}^{0},\ldots,l_{s}^{0}}T^{p_{1}(n)}\psi_{l_{1}^{1},\ldots,l_{s}^{1}}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\psi_{l_{1}^{r},\ldots,l_{s}^{r}}d\mu

we conclude.
∎

3.12
allows to prove the following extension of
3.10
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(X,\mu,T)
be a
d
-step Weyl system. Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be a set of essentially distinct integral polynomials,
h
an integral polynomial such that
h\notin\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. If
g:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is Riemann integrable with
\int_{\mathbb{T}}gdm_{\mathbb{T}}=0
and
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(h(n)\alpha)\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=0.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that the functions
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}
are continuous and bounded by
1
. Since any Riemann integrable function can be approximated by above and below by continuous functions, and those in turn can be uniformly approximated by trigonometric polynomials by the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, we also assume that
g(t)=e(kt)
, for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, by replacing
h
by
kh
, one can assume that
k=1
.

We prove first the case in which
X
is connected. By
3.12
there is
\beta\in\mathbb{T}^{s}
, a sequence
(c_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
with
\left\lVert(c_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})}\leq 1
, integral polynomials
\{q_{l,j}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N},j\in[s]}\subseteq\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
such that for
\epsilon>0
there is
M\in\mathbb{N}
with

\left|\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu-\sum_{l=1}^{M}c_{l}\cdot e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_{l,j}(n)\cdot\beta_{j}\right)\right|\leq\epsilon.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}(T^{p_{1}(n)}x)\cdots f_{r}(T^{p_{r}(n)}x)d\mu(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{l=0}^{M}c_{l}\cdot e\left(h(n)\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_{l,j}(n)\cdot\beta_{j}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\sum_{l=0}^{M}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e\left(h(n)\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_{l,j}(n)\cdot\beta_{j}\right)\right|.

Notice that for each
l\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
,
h(n)k\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_{l,j}(n)\beta_{j}
is a polynomial with at least one irrational coefficient. Indeed, if
\{1,b_{1},\ldots,b_{s},\alpha\}
are rationally independent, then the statement is direct. If not, as
\{1,\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{s}\}
are rationally independent, we have that there are rational numbers
m_{0},m_{1},\ldots,m_{s}
such that

\alpha+m_{0}+m_{1}\beta_{1}+\cdots+m_{s}\beta_{s}=0.

If the polynomial
h(n)k\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_{l,j}(n)\beta_{j}
does not have an irrational coefficient, then

h(n)\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q_{l,j}(n)\beta_{j}=m_{0}h(n)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}(q_{l,j}(n)+m_{j}h(n))\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{Q},

which implies that for each
j\in[s]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

q_{l,j}(n)+m_{j}h(n)=0.

The fact that
h\notin\textup{{{WP}}}_{d}(P)
yields
m_{j}=0
for each
j\in[s]
, and thus
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
, which is a contradiction. Thus, by Weyl equidistribution theorem

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu\right|\leq\epsilon.

As
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, we conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu=0

finishing the proof in the connected case.

For the general case, if
X_{0}
denotes the connected component of
e_{X}
on
X
, then by compactness there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
T^{m}X_{0}=X_{0}
and the sets
\{T^{i}X_{0}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are pairwise disjoint. In particular, we have that
\mu=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}T^{j}\mu_{X_{0}}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N/m}\sum_{n=1}^{N/m}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}e(h(mn+i)\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(mn+i)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(mn+i)}f_{r}d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N/m}\sum_{n=1}^{N/m}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}e(h(mn+i)\alpha)\cdot\int T^{j}f_{0}T^{p_{1}(mn+i)}T^{j}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(mn+i)}T^{j}f_{r}d\mu_{X_{0}}.

Thus, it is enough to show that for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and for all
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(\mu_{X_{0}})
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e((h(mn+i)-h(i))\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(mn+i)-p_{1}(i)}f_{1}\cdots T^{p_{r}(mn+i)-p_{r}(i)}f_{r}d\mu=0.

(6)

Notice that the polynomials
\{\frac{p_{j}(mn+i)-p_{j}(i)}{m}\}_{j\in[r]}
are essentially distinct integral polynomials. If we denote the transformation
S=T^{m}
, then proving
Eq.

6
amounts to show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e\left(\frac{h(mn+i)-h(i)}{m}\cdot(m\alpha)\right)\cdot\int f_{0}S^{\frac{p_{1}(mn+i)-p_{1}(i)}{m}}f_{1}\cdots S^{\frac{p_{r}(mn+i)-p_{r}(i)}{m}}f_{r}d\mu=0.

(7)

On the other hand,
Eq.

7
follows from the connected case, as

\frac{h(mn+i)-h(i)}{m}\notin\textup{{{WP}}}(\frac{p_{1}(mn+i)-p_{1}(i)}{m},\ldots,\frac{p_{r}(mn+i)-p_{r}(i)}{m})

by
3.11
and
(X_{0},\mu_{X_{0}},S)
is a totally ergodic Weyl system by
2.4
.

4.  Recurrence in Weyl systems

In this section we prove

In the sequel, we extend these sufficient conditions to all Weyl system. For this, we will need to reduce to the connected case in order to use
Lemma

2.6
to lift the conditions from standard Weyl systems. Then, for proving that this condition is indeed necessary we show that every set of

-topological recurrence in Weyl systems is a set of

-topological recurrence in Kronecker systems, where

P
-measurable recurrence is a set of
P
-topological recurrence.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
different positive integers. By a simple computation, we get:

•

W(an,bn,cn)=3
and
\textup{{{WP}}}(an,bn,cn)=\{c_{1}n+c_{2}n^{2}+c_{3}n^{3}:c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
,

•

W(an,bn,cn^{3})=2
and
\textup{{{WP}}}(an,bn,cn^{3})=\{c_{1}n+c_{2}n^{2}+c_{3}n^{3}:c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
,

•

W(an,bn^{2},cn^{3})=1
and
\textup{{{WP}}}(an,bn^{2},cn^{3})=\{c_{1}n+c_{2}n^{2}+c_{3}n^{3}:c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
, and

•

W(n,2n,cn^{2})=3
and
\textup{{{WP}}}(n,2n,cn^{2})=\{c_{1}n+c_{2}n^{2}+c_{3}(n^{3}-\frac{n^{4}}{2c}):c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

In particular, we see that the recurrence schemes associated to
(an,bn,cn)
,
(an,bn,cn^{3})
, and
(an,bn^{2},cn^{3})
in Weyl systems are equivalent, but they are in general position with respect to
(n,2n,cn^{2})
in Weyl systems. Moreover, it is not hard to see that given
\alpha\in[0,1)
the sets

\Big\{n\mid||n^{3}\alpha||>\frac{1}{4}\Big\},\text{ and }\Big\{n\mid||\left(n^{3}-\frac{n^{4}}{2c}\right)\alpha||>\frac{1}{4}\Big\},

are sets of
(n,2n,cn^{2})
-recurrence and
(an,bn,cn)
-recurrence in Weyl systems respectively, that are not sets of
(an,bn,cn)
-recurrence and
(n,2n,cn^{2})
-recurrence in Weyl systems respectively.

It is not possible to generalize
4.11
to recurrence in all systems, as Griesmer
[
17
]
showed that there is a set of
\{n^{2}\}
-recurrence that is not a set of
\{n,2n\}
recurrence, where clearly
\textup{{{WP}}}(\{n^{2}\})\subseteq\textup{{{WP}}}(\{n,2n\})
. Nevertheless, there is still hope for the converse direction, which we conjecture true for recurrence in all system.

Conjecture 4.13
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},...,p_{k}\}
and
Q=\{q_{1},...,q_{l}\}
two sets of polynomials such that
\textup{{{WP}}}(P)\setminus\textup{{{WP}}}(Q)\neq\emptyset
. Then,
Q
-measurable recurrence does not imply
P
-measurable recurrence.
If in addition
\textup{{{WP}}}(Q)\setminus\textup{{{WP}}}(P)\neq\emptyset
then

Q
-measurable recurrence and
P
-measurable recurrence are in general position.

5.  Leibman’s conjecture and some consequences in recurrence

In this section, we will show how a conjecture from Leibman would imply
3.16
and
4.13
. We remark that a similar argument would also show that Leibman’s conjecture implies
3.17
.

We now describe Leibman’s conjecture: Let
(X=G/\Gamma,\mu_{X},T)
be a connected nilsystem, with transformation
T
induced by left translation by an element
\tau\in G
. As
X
is connected, we have that
G=G_{0}\Gamma
where
G_{0}
is the connected component of the identity in
G
. Write
\tau=\tau_{0}\gamma^{-1}
, with
\tau_{0}\in G_{0}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma.
For
\alpha\in G_{0}
define the sequence
g_{\alpha}(n)=(\alpha\gamma^{-1})^{n}\gamma^{n}
. Let
p_{0},\ldots,p_{r}
be essentially distinct integral polynomials. Define the subgroup of
G^{r}
given by

\hat{H}=\left\langle\Delta_{G_{0}^{r+1}},\begin{pmatrix}g_{\alpha}(p_{0}(n))\\
\cdots\\
g_{\alpha}(p_{r}(n))\\
\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{Z},\alpha\in G_{0}\right\rangle.

Leibman
[
28
]
conjectured the following.

Conjecture 5.1

(
[
28
, Conjecture 11.4]
)
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\hat{H}\cap(G_{0})_{r+1}=\hat{H}_{d}
.

Let us consider
p_{0}=0
from now onwards. This conjecture is easily verified when the polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
are linearly independent. Moreover, Leibman
[
25
]
proved that it also holds when the polynomial are linear. Other special cases have been established in the literature. For instance, Frantzikinakis
[
8
, Cf. Lemma 4.3]
proved that the conjecture holds when
r=3
,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
linearly independent, and
p_{3}=k_{1}p_{1}+k_{2}p_{2}
for some
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Nevertheless, it remains unknown whether the conjecture holds in general.

We will assume this conjecture to be true from now on. As Leibman points out in
[
28
, Section 11.4]
, this implies that
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X

\overline{\{\tau^{m}x,\tau^{m+p_{1}(n)}x,\ldots,\tau^{m+p_{r}(n)}x\}}_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}=\hat{H}\Gamma^{r+1},

and this nilmanifold is connected. We plan to use this conjecture along with the following result.

Theorem 5.2

(Cf. Leibman
[
27
]
)
.

Let
X=G/\Gamma
be a connected nilmanifold. Consider a polynomial sequence
(g(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
G
. Define
Z=G/\left(\left[G_{0},G_{0}\right]\Gamma\right)
and let
\pi:X\rightarrow Z
be the natural projection. Then for every
x\in X
, the sequence
\{g(n)x\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly distributed in
X
if and only if
\{g(n)\pi(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
Z
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assuming
5.1
,
3.16
holds.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that the system
(X,\mu,T)
is ergodic by decomposing
\mu
into its ergodic components and using dominated convergence theorem to extend the result from the ergodic case to the general case.

Since
g:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
is Riemann integrable is enough to prove the case in which
g(t)=e^{2\pi kt}
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. In particular, in this case
g
is bounded by
1
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}}gd\lambda=0
. In addition, by replacing
h
by
kh
we can also assume that
k=1
.

Let
r=HK(P)
be the Host-Kra complexity of the polynomials
P
and denote
Z_{r}
the inverse limit of
r
-step nilsystem which is characteristic for the ergodic averages over
P
. Since for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\Big|g(h(n)\alpha)\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu-g(h(n)\alpha)\int T^{p_{1}(n)}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}|Z_{r})\cdots T^{p_{r}(n)}\mathbb{E}(f_{r}|Z_{r})d\mu\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\int f_{0}T^{p_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdots\cdot T^{p_{r}(n)}f_{r}d\mu-\int\mathbb{E}(f_{0}|Z_{r})T^{p_{1}(n)}\mathbb{E}(f_{1}|Z_{r})\cdot\ldots\cdot T^{p_{r}(n)}\mathbb{E}(f_{r}|Z_{r})d\mu\Big|,

we can assume without loss of generality that
X=Z_{r}
. Moreover, approximating by
r
-step nilsystems reveals that it suffices to prove the statement assuming that
(X,\mu,T)
is an
r
-step nilsystem.

It is enough to show that for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
, the sequence

\{(T^{m}x,T^{m+p_{1}(n)}x,\ldots,T^{m+p_{r}(n)}x,h(n)\alpha)\}_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}

(9)

is uniformly distributed in
Y_{x}\times\mathbb{T}
, where

Y_{x}:=\overline{\{(T^{m}x,T^{m+p_{1}(n)}x,\ldots,T^{m+p_{r}(n)}x)\}}_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}.

As mentioned early, we have that
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
,
Y_{x}=\hat{H}\Gamma^{r+1}
and
Y
is connected. Let
Z=G/[G_{0},G_{0}]\Gamma_{H}
where
\pi:X\to Z
is the canonical projection. By
5.2
and
5.1
, it is enough to prove that the the sequence

\{(T^{m}\pi(x),T^{m+p_{1}(n)}\pi(x),\ldots,T^{m+p_{r}(n)}\pi(x),h(n)\alpha)\}_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}

is uniformly distributed in
\left(\hat{H}/((G_{0,2})^{r+1}\cap\hat{H})\Gamma^{r+1}\right)\times\mathbb{T}
. By
2.3
we can assume that
Z=\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
T
is an ergodic unipotent affine transformations on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. In this case, it enough to prove that for
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in L^{\infty}(Z)
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(h(n)\alpha)\cdot\int f_{0}(z)f_{1}(T^{p_{1}(n)}z)\cdots f_{r}(T^{p_{r}(n)}z)d\mu_{Z}(z)=0,

which follows from
3.13
.
∎

Now we are in position to show how
5.1
implies
4.13
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Assuming
5.1
,
4.13
holds.

Proof.

Let
p\in\textup{{{WP}}}(P)\setminus\textup{{{WP}}}(Q)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
and define

S:=\Big\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\{p(n)\alpha\}\in[1/4,3/4]\Big\}.

Clearly,
S
is not a set of
P
-recurrence because it is not a set of
P
-recurrence for Weyl systems. Let us see that
S
is a set of
Q
-averaging recurrence. Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be a measure-preserving system, and
A\subseteq X
with
\mu(A)>0
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\mathbbm{1}_{S}(n)\mu(A\cap T^{-q_{1}(n)}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-q_{r}(n)}A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\mu(A\cap T^{-q_{1}(n)}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-q_{r}(n)}A)>0,

where the positivity of the last expression comes the polynomial Szemerédi theorem

3
]
.
