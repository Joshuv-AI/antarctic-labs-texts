Efficient NK cell transduction with VSV-G-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors
Authors: Emmi Järvelä, Jan Koski, Farhana Jahan, Antti Tuhkala, Mira Saari, Manar Elmadani, Kari Salokas, Laurens Veltman, Lotta Andersson, Erja Kerkelä, Hekim Can, Marie Nyman, Seppo Ylä-Herttuala, Markku Varjosalo, Diana Schenkwein, Henrik Paavilainen, Kim Vettenranta, Matti Korhonen, Helka Göös
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe need for safe, allogeneic cell therapies for cancer is driving a growing interest in CAR-NK-based therapies, which, unlike CAR-T cell therapies, offer the potential for off-the-shelf administration. Lentiviruses pseudotyped with vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G (VSV-G) are commonly used for genetic modification of cell therapy products. Their use in NK cells, however, is limited by low transduction efficiency. This study explores the complexities of NK cell transduction using lentiviral vectors pseudotyped with VSV-G. We demonstrate that efficient transduction depends on multiple factors, such as NK cell activation, domain organization of CAR construct, lentivirus pseudotype selection, and the use of transduction enhancers. By optimizing these elements, we achieved effective transduction, facilitating the use of VSV-G-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors for therapeutic NK cell production. Our optimized workflow comprises NK cell activation with interleukins, followed by transduction with an NK-cell-specific CAR construct using VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs in the presence of BX795 and retronectin, resulting in excellent transduction efficiency (91% using GFP and 80% using CAR) without compromising NK cell phenotype, growth, or cytotoxicity. This allows for the use of a widely used gene transfer vector with an excellent safety record for producing therapeutic NK cell products.Keywords: natural killer cell, chimeric antigen receptor, CAR-NK, vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein, VSV-G, transduction, BX795, retronectin

Introduction

T cells equipped with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR-T cells) have yielded impressive results in the treatment of hematological malignancies. They are, however, associated with severe toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Additionally, the autologous nature of CAR-T therapies poses logistical and financial challenges. Conversely, allogeneic CAR-natural killer (NK) cells may be associated with fewer toxicities and be manufactured as off-the-shelf products.1 The FDA and EMA have also issued warnings about the risk of secondary malignancies following CAR-T cell therapies, highlighting the need for safer options.2,3 In contrast to T cells, however, CAR transfer to NK cells is highly challenging.4 Current approaches to combat this challenge include retro- or lentiviral transduction, electroporation, and oligomers.5

HIV-derived lentiviral vectors (LVs) pseudotyped with vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G (VSV-G) are widely used for the genetic modification of therapeutic cells6,7 and their safety and stability have been documented in clinical trials.6,8 VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs enter their target cells via the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR).9 Freshly isolated NK cells express LDLR poorly but upregulate its expression upon activation; however, even after activation, NK cells express significantly lower LDLR levels than activated T cells.10 Recently, Gong et al. reported that rosuvastatin upregulates the expression of LDLR on NK cells and can be harnessed to improve the transduction of NK cells with VSV-G-pseudotyped LV.11 Adding IL-2 and rosuvastatin to NK cells prior to transduction resulted in a transduction efficiency of 33.3% using VSV-G LVs carrying green fluorescent protein (GFP) as the transgene. However, a study by Sutlu et al. also demonstrates an interleukin-dependent increase in NK cell transduction even in the absence of rosuvastatin.12

In addition to pre-activation and the use of rosuvastatin, a variety of methods can enhance the transduction of NK cells with VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs. Notably, the inhibition of the TBK1/IKKε antiviral signaling pathway using compounds, such as amlexanox, MRT67307, or BX795, significantly enhances NK cell transduction. Chockley et al. demonstrated that combining these inhibitors with IL-2 pre-activation can result in an yellow fluorecent protein (YFP) expression of up to 47% in NK cells.13 Furthermore, agents like retronectin and Vectofusin-1 physically concentrate the vector on the cell surface.14,15 Retronectin is a recombinant fragment of human fibronectin and contains the binding domains for both the vector and target cell, thereby facilitating closer virus-cell interaction and enhancing transduction efficiency.14 Similarly, Vectofusin-1 aggregates the vector on the target cell through nanofiber formation. It also offers the advantage of not requiring attachment to the cell culture dish surface.15

In a recent publication, Hayal et al. assessed P407, a non-ionic amphiphilic molecule, alongside other transduction enhancers (including BX795, Vectofucin-1, and retronectin with or without spinfection) for lentiviral transduction of rhesus macaque (RM) and human NK cells.16 They reported that P407 alone achieved the highest CD20 targeting CAR transduction efficiency in RM NK cells (up to 60% in expanded cells) without compromising viability or growth. In contrast, human NK cell transduction remained lower, reaching approximately 35%–50% with GFP and around 10% with CD20-targeting CAR constructs, while cell viability and growth were unaffected. Additionally, Allan et al. transduced NK cells with various CAR+GFP constructs using BX795 as an enhancer on retronectin-coated plates, achieving average transduction rates of 33% for CD19+GFP+, 25% for CD4+GFP+, and 23% for CD34+GFP+ NK cells.17

In addition to VSV-G-pseudotyped lentiviruses, recent studies have successfully introduced ASCT2 targeting baboon envelope-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors (BaEV-LVs) for NK cell transduction.10,18 However, BaEV-LV production has been limited by lower titers compared to VSV-G LVs, and overcoming these bottlenecks while ensuring consistent transduction remains an active area of research.

Collectively, current protocols remain suboptimal, emphasizing the need for improved lentiviral methods for NK cell transduction. Despite recent protocol optimizations,19 BaEV titers are still significantly lower than those achieved with VSV-G, and VSV-G-based approaches and continue to yield relatively modest CAR transduction efficiencies (typically <50%). Achieving higher efficiencies is critical, particularly for introducing more complex, multicistronic constructs into NK cells.

To overcome the challenges in efficient NK cell transduction, this study aims to investigate various methods and their combinations to boost VSV-G LV transduction. Additionally, we examined the impact of CAR molecules on the transgene expression and studied the effect of NK cell freezing on transduction efficiency. This study presents a protocol that combines NK-cell-specific CAR transduction with IL-2 and IL-15 activation and leverages BX795 and retronectin to enhance lentiviral transduction of NK cells without compromising cell viability, phenotype, or expansion.

Results

NK cells are successfully transduced with VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs carrying GFP

To better understand the surface expression of LDLR on freshly isolated and on activated primary NK cells, we analyzed LDLR expression using flow cytometry (Figure 1A). First, we examined freshly isolated, non-activated NK cells and then analyzed them after a three-day activation with IL-2 (500 IU/mL), IL-15 (140 IU/mL), or their combination. As expected, freshly isolated NK cells did not express LDLR on their surface, whereas cytokine activation induced LDLR expression ranging from 18% to 24% across the cytokine conditions (means of three donors). There were no significant differences between the different cytokine groups, indicating that both cytokines increased LDLR expression on NK cells but did not act synergistically.

To assess the impact of these cytokines in transduction efficiency, we transduced the activated NK cells with VSV-G-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors carrying the GFP transgene (VSV-G LV-GFP) (Figure 1B). Consistent with LDLR expression, the resulting GFP expression was comparable between cytokine groups (ranging from 41% to 46%, means of three donors), indicating that cytokine activation did not differentially enhance viral entry. For all subsequent experiments, we used IL-2 and IL-15 in combination, consistent with our two-week, post-transduction, feeder cell-induced NK cell expansion protocol (Figure 1C).

Next, to screen within-donor differences in the efficacy of rosuvastatin, retronectin, BX795, amlexanox, MRT67307, and Vectofucin-1 in enhancing NK cell transduction, we transduced primary NK cells from two donors with VSV-G LV-GFP using these enhancers. The efficacy of each agent was studied separately; some of them, having different mechanisms of action, were subsequently combined to interrogate possible synergism (Figure 1D). For example, inhibition of intracellular TBK1/IKKε antiviral signaling by BX795 was combined with co-localization of target cells with LVs facilitated by retronectin. This combination was then supplemented with rosuvastatin, which enhances the cell contact further via a complementary mechanism, i.e., upregulating LDLR expression.

Before all transductions, NK cells were pre-activated with IL-2 and IL-15 for 3 days. Under these conditions, the basal transduction efficiency was 36% and 34% for donors A and B, respectively (Figure 1D). Of the individual transduction enhancement methods rosuvastatin (donor A: 43% and B: 46%), retronectin (51% and 59%), BX795 (50% and 65%), amlexanox (38% and 41%), and MRT67307 (47% and 51%, respectively) improved the transduction efficiency, while Vectofucin-1 (31% and 31%) and feeder cell-induced activation (15% and 13%) lowered it. Notably, feeder-activated cells in this experiment were the only condition receiving feeder cells prior to transduction. In all other conditions, feeder cells were added after removal of the viral supernatant to increase expansion (Figure 1C). Additionally, a combination of BX795 and retronectin resulted in a synergistic enhancement with a transduction efficiency of (87% for donor A and 95% for B. A triple combination of BX795, retronectin and rosuvastatin resulted in similarly high levels of transduction (85% for donor A and 92% for B).

In addition to GFP expression, the subsequent expansion (Figure 1E) and viability (Figure 1F) of the NK cells were followed during a feeder cell-induced cell expansion. The growth curves of all cultures were comparable (Figure 1E). However, because feeder-activated NK cells exhibited poor transgene expression, this group was excluded from further analyses. The initial viability of all cultures, except for the feeder cell-activated culture, was high prior to transduction on day 3 (90%–91%, mean range of two donors) (Figure 1F). In contrast, feeder activation before transduction reduced viability to 75% (mean of two donors), most likely due to the presence of dying feeder cells in the culture. All cultures showed decreased viability following transduction (day 4, Figure 1F), with values ranging from 51% to 84%. By day 10, all cultures had recovered from the transduction process, reaching viabilities of 84%–92%. Interestingly, the culture transduced without enhancers exhibited the largest drop in viability, decreasing from 91% on day 3–51% on day 4 (means of two donors). However, viability had already increased to 86% by day 7.

After the addition of a second batch of feeder cells (on day 14), the viability decreased in almost all cultures, reflecting the presence of dying feeder cells. However, at the end of the culture period all cultures had good viabilities ranging from 86% to 92% suggesting that the transduction enhancers did not have a major negative effect on NK cell expansion or viability.

As BX795 has been reported to impair NK cell expansion, we additionally evaluated its effect at a lower concentration (2.5 μM) to assess the impact on transduction efficiency, cell expansion, and viability. Results obtained with 5 μM BX795 (Figure 1D) were compared with those obtained with 2.5 μM BX795 (Figure S1A). We observed no major differences in transduction efficiency, overall fold expansion, or viability between the two concentrations. Additionally, we assessed the effect of 5 μM BX795 on NK cell growth in feeder-free cultures and in cultures with K562-based feeder cells (two doses) following BX795 treatment (Figure S1B). Given that 5 μM corresponds to the reported EC50 for BX795-mediated enhancement of VSV-G lentiviral transduction in primary human NK cells,13 and that 5 μM was well tolerated and did not compromise transduction, viability, or feeder-supported expansion in our system, we selected 5 μM BX795 for subsequent experiments.

NK cells are successfully transduced with VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs carrying CD19-targeting CARs

After establishing the most effective, single-agent transduction enhancers and their potent combinations using VSV-G LVs carrying GFP, we investigated whether they could be used to enhance transduction with VSV-G LVs encoding CARs (Figure 2A). In this analysis, we used a CD19-targeting CAR construct (FiCAR-NK1, Figure S2A). Cells were produced as described previously (Figure 1C) and the CAR expression analyzed on day 20. While all enhancers increased transduction relative to the no-enhancer condition, the combination of BX795 and retronectin with or without rosuvastatin yielded highest transduction efficiencies (means of 84% and 80%, respectively. The difference between these combinations was not significant (p = 0.813). As before, the transduction enhancers did not markedly impact the feeder-based cell expansion (Figure S2B). Although cell viabilities varied slightly during the expansion, CAR-NK cells had similar viabilities at the end of the process on day 20 (93%–95%, Figure S2C).

To investigate whether different transduction methods influence cell phenotype, we analyzed the surface expression of several markers, including activating and inhibitory receptors and exhaustion markers (CD16, CD56, CD57, LAG3, NKG2A, NKG2C, and TIGIT) on the CAR-NK cells after 21 days of expansion using flow cytometry (Figures 2B, 2C, and S2D). Aside from natural variation between donors, the cell phenotypes remained consistent across different transduction methods. For example, although TIGIT expression varied markedly between donors, it was not systematically affected by the methods employed (Figure 2C).

Taken together, these data indicate that the combination of BX795 and retronectin with or without rosuvastatin yields high transduction efficiency without compromising NK cell expansion, viability, or phenotype. However, because rosuvastatin did not provide a significant additional benefit, we excluded it from subsequent experiments to simplify the transduction protocol and make it more suitable for clinical applications.

In the previous set of experiments, transduction was performed on freshly isolated and activated NK cells. To determine whether similar results could be obtained using thawed NK cells frozen directly after isolation, we transduced NK cells from three donors either directly after isolation or after freezing and thawing and analyzed the CAR expression on day 20 (Figure 2D). No significant difference (p = 0.962) was seen in the transduction efficiency between the fresh or frozen NK cells (Figure 2D), indicating that both may be used as starting material. However, a fraction of NK cells is lost during freezing and thawing potentially reducing the amount of starting material.

We then examined whether pre-activation of NK cells with IL-2 and IL-15 (Figure 1C) is necessary for efficient CAR transduction. Freshly isolated NK cells were transduced either immediately after isolation or after a three-day activation with IL-2 and IL-15. Transduction was performed without enhancers or with a combination of BX795 and retronectin (Figure 2E). The resulting CAR expression was low (ranging from 10% to 11% on day 10) on non-activated NK cells transduced without enhancers. Pre-activation increased the CAR expression to 20%–37%. Transduction of non-activated NK cells with BX795 and retronectin resulted in 14%–22% CAR expression, whereas pre-activated cells transduced with enhancers had the highest transduction efficiency, resulting in a CAR expression of 79%–89%. Enhancers did not improve transduction efficiency in freshly isolated cells. These results suggest that high transduction efficiency is achieved only when both NK cell pre-activation and transduction enhancers are used.

Given the potency of retronectin and BX795 in enhancing NK-cell transduction, we examined whether these reagents could similarly improve T cell transduction. Although T cells are readily modified using standard methods, any increase in efficiency could reduce costs in cell-therapy manufacturing. We transduced T cells with a T cell-specific CAR at various multiplicity of infections (MOIs) without enhancers or with BX795, retronectin, or the BX795/retronectin combination (Figure S3A). BX795 alone or together with retronectin did not enhance T cell transduction and was instead inhibitory. Retronectin alone showed minimal and MOI-dependent effects, and the detrimental effect of BX795 may reflect toxicity, as T cell expansion was also impaired in BX795-treated cultures (Figure S3B).

Proteomic analysis reveals minimal changes between differentially transduced NK cells

After establishing efficient transduction conditions for NK cells, we employed proteomic analysis by mass spectroscopy to assess potential alterations in the cells resulting from the transduction enhancement. This analysis was included to confirm the safety of our optimized approach by ensuring that the combination of BX795 and retronectin does not cause unintended changes in NK cell biology. NK cells from three donors were transduced with VSV-G-pseudotyped LV carrying the CD19 targeting CAR construct (FiCAR-NK1). IL-2/IL-15-activated NK cells were transduced either without enhancers or using the combination of BX795 and retronectin. Untransduced NK cells served as controls. We evaluated the CAR expression using flow cytometry (Figure S4A) and assessed the functionality of the CAR-NK cells through a cytotoxicity assay against RS4.11 cells (Figure S4B). For proteomic analysis, we collected 10 × 106 unsorted CAR-NK cells (day 19) in four technical replicates. The cells were lysed and digested with trypsin into peptides. Half of the resulting peptide mixture was analyzed for total protein content by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS), while the other half underwent enrichment of the phosphorylated peptides using Ti4+ beads prior to analysis by LC-MS.

The total protein analysis identified and quantified 5,727 most abundant proteins expressed in the (CAR-)NK cells (Table S1A). To gain a more comprehensive understanding of protein expression variation across different sample groups, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) which uncovered greater variation between the donors than among differentially transduced NK cells (Figure 3A). We did, however, notice slight subclustering within individual biological donors based on different conditions (untransduced, transduced without enhancer, or transduced with BX795 and retronectin). Yet the order of these subclusters in the PCA plot did not align consistently across different donors. Hierarchical clustering yielded similar outcomes: biological donors formed distinct clusters, and within each donor, we observed subtle subclustering based on different conditions (Figure 3B). Taken together, these findings indicate that the variation in protein expression between biological donors far outweighs the variation attributed to the different transduction methods.

To analyze the differences in protein expression on a more detailed level, we calculated the q values and fold changes for each protein or protein group across different sample groups and visualized them using volcano plots (Figures 3C and 3D). A comparison between the untransduced cells and cells transduced without enhancers did not reveal any significantly differentially expressed proteins (fold change >2 and -log10 q value >2; Figure 3C; Table S1B). This indicates that low CAR expression (less than 20%) or lentiviral infection alone has no impact on the expression of these 5,727 proteins after a two-week expansion following transduction. Next, to assess the impact of BX795 and retronectin on the proteome of transduced NK cell, we compared cells transduced without enhancers to samples transduced with BX795 and retronectin (Figure 3D; Table S1C). This analysis revealed that only the expression of cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) was significantly lower in BX795 and retronectin-treated samples compared to samples transduced without enhancers (fold change >2 and -log10 q value >2).

While the differentially transduced NK cells appear to express similar levels of the over 5,700 most abundant proteins, the resulting protein identifications serve as a valuable resource for understanding the protein expression in the feeder cell-expanded CAR-NK cells (Table S1). To gain deeper insights into the CAR-NK cell proteome, we conducted several enrichment analyses on the proteins identified. These included Biological Process Gene Ontology (GO-BP) (Table S1D), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment (Table S1E), and REACTOME pathway enrichment analysis (Table S1F).

The GO-BP analysis revealed enrichment in several pathways related to mRNA processing, protein transport, cell cycle, and translation (Table S1D). KEGG analysis highlighted enrichment of various pathways, including metabolic pathways (p = 2.8E−15), oxidative phosphorylation (p = 4.98E−11), Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis (p = 5.7E−05), and natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity (p = 2.5E−04, Table S1F). A closer examination revealed that most proteins of the natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity pathway were detected in our analysis (Figure S4C). These proteins include, for example, all the NK cell surface receptors relevant to the pathway.

Given that BX795 is known to impact the intracellular signaling pathways of NK cells, we conducted a Ti4+ bead-based phosphoprotein enrichment followed by LC-MS analysis of the phosphorylated proteins. This analysis identified a total of 1,957 phosphorylated proteins (Table S2A). Notably, we observed no significant difference in the expression of these phosphorylated proteins between the untransduced and enhancer-free transduced NK cells (Table S2B) suggesting that low CAR expression (less than 20%) or viral infection itself did not affect the NK cell phosphoproteome. However, two proteins, endosulfine alpha (ENSA) and CD244 (also known as natural killer cell receptor 2B4), showed elevated phosphorylation in BX795/retronectin-treated cells compared to cells transduced without enhancers (Table S2C). Interestingly, the CAR construct utilized in our experiments, (FiCAR-NK1, Figure 3E), incorporates a signaling domain originating from 2B4. This led us to investigate whether higher expression of 2B4 could be attributed to an increased CAR expression on NK cells. A detailed examination of the enriched phosphorylated 2B4 revealed the presence of a singular phosphopeptide whose sequence aligns with the 2B4 signaling domain incorporated within the CAR construct (Figure 3E). Notably, this phosphopeptide contains two phosphorylation sites at positions S614 and S616 in the CAR- construct (S334 and S336 in 2B4), both of which exhibited augmented phosphorylation levels in cells treated with BX795 and retronectin (Figure 3F). However, although 2B4 phosphorylation appeared elevated in CAR-expressing samples, these data do not exclude the possibility that the detected phosphopeptide originates from endogenous 2B4 rather than the CAR construct.

Finally, a total protein analysis identified three peptides corresponding to the signal-regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα)-derived spacer segment of the CAR construct (Figure 3E). The expression levels of these peptides were also elevated in the BX795/retronectin-treated cells (Figure 3G). Of note, these peptides are not only exclusive to SIRPα but also shared with SIRPβ1 and were consequently excluded from the total protein analysis. Nevertheless, these findings imply that an increase in CAR expression results in the detection of the CAR domains in the proteomic analysis.

The CAR structure influences VSV-G-LV-mediated transduction in NK cells

Next, we asked whether CARs with varying structures can be expressed on NK cells with equal efficiency. Earlier, we have used the FiCAR construct to successfully modify T cells.20 The FiCAR backbone contains an extracellular spacer derived from the immunoglobulin-like domains of SIRPa and allows for the design of multiple structural variants of the CAR.21 It has been shown that SIRPα interacts with CD47 through its N-terminal V-set domain, whereas the remaining domains are constant C1-set domains.22 Our CAR constructs incorporate only the SIRPα C1-type 1 and 2 domains, which do not bind CD47.20 These SIRPα-derived peptides are not known to interact with CD47 or other cellular proteins.

For this study, we designed three novel NK-specific, CD19-targeting CAR constructs carrying signaling domains from 2B4 and CD3z involved in activating signal transduction of NK cells. Two of these novel constructs, FiCAR-NK1 and FiCAR-NK2, contain the SIRPa spacer (Figure 4A). The third, CAR-NK3, contains a CD8 alpha-based spacer (Figure 4A). In this context, NK- or T cell-specificity denotes whether the signaling domain 2 within the construct originates from a protein that is naturally expressed in NK cells or T cells.

As before, NK cells were pre-activated with IL-2 and IL-15, and the BX795/retronectin combination was used to enhance transduction. To achieve comparable CAR expression across constructs, vector doses were adjusted based on prior optimization experiments (data not shown). The mock control was transduced with an empty vector at MOI 10, which serves as our standard baseline for lentiviral exposure. All vectors were produced, and the virus titers were measured at the same time (Table S3). Transduction with the combination of BX795 and retronectin resulted in a high transduction efficiency and expression of NK-specific CARs on the cell surface (Figure 4B). Interestingly, the expression of T cell-specific FiCAR-T on NK cells was markedly lower compared to the expression of NK-specific CAR constructs despite an MOI more than 2-fold higher (Figure 4B). This was unexpected as the same FiCAR-T LVs have yielded high transduction efficiency in T cells.20

To examine whether the lower expression of FiCAR-T compared to that of the NK-specific CARs was the result of lesser integration of the CAR construct into the genome or impaired expression of the CAR protein from the integrated gene, we analyzed the vector copy number (VCN) in the cell product by digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) (Figure 4B). The results show that the VCN obtained with FiCAR-T was much lower (0.5–0.7 copies per cell) than with the NK-specific CARs (2.3–7.7 copies per cell) suggesting a more efficient integration of the NK-specific CAR. Of note, the VCN number is the average across the whole cell batch, and not of the CAR expressing cells only, which prevents direct comparison of VCN between CAR-transduced cells alone.

To investigate whether CAR expression changes during cell expansion, the surface expression was analyzed by flow cytometry on days 13 and 20 of the cell production. The percentage of CAR expressing cells seemed to remain stable during the NK cell expansion (Figure 4B) indicating that the expression neither favored nor inhibited the expansion of transduced cells over non-transduced cells.

Next, we studied the cytotoxicity of the different CAR-NK cells using a luciferase-based cytotoxicity assay. CAR-NK cells (on days 14 and 21) were cocultured with luciferase expressing acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) target cells (RS4.11 or NALM-6) for four hours and the remaining live target cells quantified by luminescence (Figure S5A). The RS4.11 target cells were highly resistant to the natural cytotoxicity of unmodified NK cells and only killed by the CAR-NK cells, whereas NALM-6 cells were killed equally well by MOCK- and CAR-NK cells. NK cells transduced with CARs induced similar cytotoxicity, indicating that the FiCAR-based, CARs are equally cytotoxic compared to the CD8-based CAR-NK3. There was no notable consistent donor-dependence in cytotoxicity (Figure S5B).

The only structural differences between the FiCAR-T and FiCAR-NK1/NK2 constructs are the transmembrane and signaling domains, which in the FiCAR-T are derived from CD28 and in NK-CARs from NKG2D, 2B,4 and CD8 (Figure 4A). This suggests that the differences in CAR expression are caused by the CD28 domains. To further study whether the CD28 signaling domain causes a reduced transgene expression, we transduced NK and T cells with a FiCAR-T construct in which the signaling domain was replaced with one from 41BB (FiCAR-T_41BB; Figure 4C). FiCAR-T (R) is a reactor scale version of the FiCAR-T VSV-G LV from a different manufacturer, and it shares the main domain organization of FiCAR-T but has a small modification in the hinge domain to increase stability (Koski et al. 2026, submitted). FiCAR-T (R) and FiCAR-T_41BB share the same hinge domain and differ only in the signaling domain 2 (Figure 4C). We first titrated the vectors using MOIs ranging from 0.1 to 500 (Figure S6) using CAR-NK3 as a positive control for transduction efficiency. Based on these results, we repeated transductions with FiCAR-T (R) and FiCAR-T_41BB at MOI 1 and MOI 10 in both NK and T cells using three additional donors. Combined data from these donors and the donor shown in Figure S6 demonstrated a significantly higher transduction efficiency with FiCAR-T_41BB compared to FiCAR-T (R) in NK cells at both MOIs (MOI 1: p = 0.045; MOI 10: p = 0.022), whereas no significant difference was observed in T cells (Figure 4D). Similar differences were seen in VCN measurements. In NK cells, FiCAR-T_41BB yielded significantly higher VCNs than FiCAR-T (R) at both MOIs (MOI 1: p = 0.044; MOI 10: p = 0.018) (Figure 4E). In T cells, VCNs were also higher with FiCAR-T_41BB at MOI 1 (p = 0.0485) but not at MOI 10.

To further confirm CAR protein expression independently of flow cytometry or VCN analysis, NK cells from two donors transduced with FiCAR T_41BB (Figures 4D and 4E) were analyzed alongside CAR NK3 transduced NK cells for CD3ζ using western blot (Figures S7A and S7B). In line with high CAR surface expression (87% and 87%, Figure S7C), MFI (15237 and 24023, Figure S7D) and VCN (6.7 and 7.6, Figure S7E) observed for CAR NK3, CD3ζ was detected at the expected molecular weight, particularly at MOI 10 (Figures S7A and S7B). In contrast, despite measurable surface expression by flow cytometry (50% and 54%) and detectable vector integration (1.4 and 1.7), FiCAR T_41BB-transduced NK cells showed detectable but not quantifiable CAR-derived CD3ζ signal by western blot (Figures S7B and S7F) suggesting that FiCAR T_41BB was expressed at levels below the detection limit of this bulk protein assay. Endogenous CD3ζ levels were comparable across conditions (Figure S7G).

Baboon envelope-pseudotyped LV transduction shows fluctuation in virus titers and CAR expression

Recently, Baboon envelope-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors (BaEV LVs) have been successfully used for NK cell transduction.10,18 Upon entering its target cell, the BaEV LV binds to the ASCT2 receptor expressed on activated NK cells.10 To test BaEV LVs in NK cell transduction, we produced a BaEV LV carrying GFP and transduced NK cells using two enhancement methods, Vectofusin-1 and spinoculation (Figure S8A). Both Vectofusin-1 and spinoculation enhanced transduction, and by using both, we could achieve a nearly 100% transduction efficiency at an MOI of 5. However, compared to the VSV-G LV production, the titers of the BaEV LVs were on average 2 logs lower (Table S3; Figure S8B).

To test the usefulness of BaEV LVs for the transduction of FiCAR-T, FiCAR-NK1, FiCAR-NK2, and CAR-NK3 constructs, we produced the lentiviral vectors using the same transfer plasmids as for the VSV-G production, only substituting the BaEV envelope plasmid for the VSV-G plasmid. Surprisingly, only the CAR-NK3 carrying BaEV lentiviral vectors were functional resulting in a high transduction efficiency (mean of 81%, MOI 5) (Figure 5A). Based on a VCN analysis, it seemed that the FiCAR-LVs integrated in NK cells (Figure 5A), but the CAR expression by all of them was practically absent (Figure 5A). This was unexpected as the same transfer plasmids were functional in the VSV-G production, and the production process of the LVs was successful as both the GFP and CAR-NK3 BaEV LVs were functional. The LV production process was repeated with identical results: functional GFP and CAR-NK3 BaEV LVs were produced, but the BaeV LVs carrying FiCAR-T and FiCAR-NK1/NK2 genes failed to transduce NK cells under identical conditions. This may indicate that the CAR construct composition plays a role in BaEV LV production and/or transduction. Subsequently, due to low titers and mixed transduction results, experiments using BaEV-pseudotyped LVs were not pursued further in this study.

Discussion

Recently, genetically modified lymphocytes such as CAR-T and -NK cells have garnered growing interest in cancer cell therapy. While some CAR-T cell therapies have already gained marketing approval, CAR-NK cell therapies are still in preclinical and clinical development. G-retroviruses and VSV-G-pseudotyped lentiviruses have been the traditional “workhorses” for genetic modification of T cells. Although both approaches have a good safety record in the manufacture of therapeutic immune cells, the former may carry more risk, perhaps due to a different integration profile into the target cell genome.23 While efficient transduction of NK cells has been achieved using G-retroviruses,24 transduction with VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs is challenging.10 To address this gap, we have optimized the use of the safe and widely used VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs for NK cell transduction.

NK cell transduction with VSV-G LVs can be enhanced through three main mechanisms: first, by NK cell activation and increasing the expression of the natural virus receptor, LDLR, prior to transduction; second, by facilitating the contact between the LV and NK cells either by promoting the colocalization of viral particles and NK cells or by neutralizing the electrostatic repulsion between them; and third, by inhibiting the intracellular antiviral signaling induced by VSV-G infection in NK cells. Our study explored various approaches of activating NK cells and increasing the expression of the LDLR (with IL-15, IL-2, rosuvastatin, and feeder cells), facilitating the virus-cell contact (retronectin and Vectofusin-1), and influencing antiviral signaling (including Amlexanox, MRT67307, and BX795).11,13,14,15,17 We investigated these approaches as either single agents and in different combinations aiming to optimize transduction efficiency of VSV-G LVs in NK cells.

Gong et al. demonstrated that Vectofusin-1 did not significantly enhance transduction of primary NK cells by VSV-G LVs which concurs with the findings in this study.11 Our experiments revealed that retronectin outperforms Vectofusin-1 in transduction enhancement (Figure 1D). However, a recent comparison of retronectin and Vectofusin-1 for transduction of primary NK cells with VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs indicated that they have similar efficacies.25 In addition, Vectofucin-1 is used in large-scale CAR-NK cell production using the CliniMACS Prodigy platform for clinical studies.26 In our experiments, Vectofucin-1 enhanced the transduction of primary NK cells with BaEV-pseudotyped LV (Figure S8A). Interestingly, when the NK92 cell line was used, Vectofucin-1 completely blocked transduction (data not shown). These results indicate that the effectiveness of Vectofucin-1 depends on the LV-pseudotype used, the specific cell line employed and possibly the method of transduction.

Rosuvastatin has been reported to significantly enhance the transduction efficiency of NK cells.11 In our experiments, using rosuvastatin with IL-15/IL-2-activated NK cells, we observed a similar improvement (Figures 1D and 2A). The mechanism underlying the improved efficiency is likely due to the upregulation of the LDLR.11 Because cytokine stimulation alone upregulated LDLR expression and enhanced transduction efficiency (Figures 1A and 1B), rosuvastatin may further augment this cytokine-induced LDLR increase, thereby contributing to the enhanced transduction efficiency. Notably, the lack of significant differences in LDLR expression across cytokine conditions (Figure 1A) was mirrored by the transduction outcome, as GFP expression also remained comparable (Figure 1B). This indicates that the cytokine regimens did not differentially influence receptor availability or lentiviral transduction efficiency. Importantly, VSV-G LVs can utilize other members of the LDLR receptor family as alternatives for cellular entry,27 which may explain why the frequency of GFP-positive cells exceeds the proportion of cells expressing LDLR prior to transduction. Although many NK cell expansion protocols utilize IL-2 alone, NK cells expanded with IL-15 have been shown to be more resistant to cytokine withdrawal,28 an attribute particularly relevant for clinical application. To leverage the effects of both cytokines on NK cell function, we used the combination of IL-2 and IL-15 in all subsequent experiments (Figure 1C).

NK cell transduction can also be enhanced by inhibiting intracellular antiviral signaling, as demonstrated by Chockley et al.13 In their study, they targeted the TLR4 signaling pathway using Amlexanox, MRT67307, or BX795. In a representative donor, YFP expression reached 36.1% with Amlexanox, 46.5% with BX795, and 47.3% with MRT67307. Similarly, in a representative donor, MRT67307 yielded transduction efficiencies of 43.6% for a CD19 targeting CAR and 53.8% for a HER2 targeting CAR.

Consistent with the findings of Chockley et al., Amlexanox yielded a lower transduction efficiency (39.5% using GFP) in our study. However, contrary to their results, BX795 led to the highest transduction rates (57.5% using GFP and 53.8% using CAR) without compromising the viability or expansion of NK cells (Figures 1E and 1F) and was therefore selected for further experiments in this study (Figure 1D). BX795 has also previously been reported to enhance NK cell transduction, with efficiencies varying from 20% to 50%.12

Next, we investigated whether combining the most efficacious enhancement methods facilitating virus-cell contact and interfering with intracellular signaling would synergize in enhancing transduction efficiency. Notably, we found that the highest level of transduction was achieved when the TLR4 signaling was impacted through TBK1/IKKε inhibition with BX795, and the virus-cell contact physically facilitated using retronectin-coated culture plates (Figures 1D and 2A). The addition of a third enhancer, rosuvastatin, to the combination did not further increase transduction efficiency. By combining IL-2/IL-15 activation, BX795, and retronectin, we could achieve a high transduction efficiency: 91% using GFP and 80% using CAR, (means of two and three biological replicates, respectively). This BX795 and retronectin combination in IL-15/IL-2 activated NK cells outperformed transduction efficiency reported in previous studies, demonstrating its potential for CAR-NK cell therapies.

To investigate the potential effects of transduction enhancers on NK cells, we closely examined the growth, viability, and surface marker expression of the CAR-NK cells following transduction with various enhancers. Notably, cell growth and viability were not markedly affected by the enhancers. Additionally, the expression of surface markers, such as CD16, CD56, CD57, LAG3, NKG2A, NKG2C, and TIGIT, exhibited greater variation among biological donors than between the different transduction methods.

These surface marker expression findings align with the results of the total protein analysis, in which we identified and quantified approximately 5,700 highly expressed proteins in differentially transduced CAR-NK cells using an MS-approach (Figure 3). Specifically, we compared the protein expression profiles of untransduced NK cells to those of cells transduced with or without enhancers (BX795/retronectin). Interestingly, among the proteins identified, most components of the natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity KEGG pathway (Figure S4C) were identified but showed no significant differences between transduction conditions. In fact, similar to the surface receptor analysis by flow cytometry (Figures 2B and S2D), the expression of these proteins exhibited greater variability among the donors than between the different transduction methods (Figures 3A and 3B).

We did not detect significant differences in protein or phosphoprotein expression between the untransduced NK cells and those transduced without enhancers. This suggests that low CAR expression or lentiviral transduction itself does not impact the expression of these proteins, many of which are involved in intracellular signaling, after a two-week expansion post-transduction. To investigate whether transduction using the BX795/retronectin combination affects the protein expression of expanded NK cells, we compared the proteomic profiles of enhancer-free, transduced CAR-NK cells to those transduced using BX795/retronectin. This analysis revealed that CDK4 exhibited a lower expression in the BX795/retronectin-treated CAR-NK cells (Figure 3D). BX795 is a potent inhibitor of PDK-1, which plays an important role in cell cycle progression by controlling the expression of cyclin D1.29 CDK4 is a key regulator of G1-to-S transition and functions within the cyclin D/CDK4 complex to drive cell-cycle progression.30 Therefore, reduced CDK4 expression in BX795/retronectin-treated NK cells may reflect a shift toward slower G1 progression or a reduction in proliferative drive. Consistent with this interpretation, BX795 inhibited cell growth, although this effect was only seen in cultures without feeder cells (Figure S1B). Additionally, ENSA and 2B4 showed increased phosphorylation in BX795/retronectin-treated CAR-NK cells (Table S2C). ENSA is a protein phosphatase inhibitor related to cell-cycle regulation,31 while 2B4 serves as an activating receptor for NK cells. Increased phosphorylation of ENSA suggests activation of the Greatwall-ENSA-PP2A/B55 pathway, which promotes mitotic entry.31 The reduction of CDK4 expression together with elevated pENSA in BX795/retronectin-treated CAR-NK cells may indicate a shift in the cell population toward cells that are more responsive to feeder-cell-derived proliferative stimuli or a shift in balance in cell-cycle pathway regulation. However, based on the current data, we cannot conclusively determine whether the observed differences in CDK4, pENSA, and p2B4 levels arise from variability in CAR expression rather than from the enhancers and these findings should be studied further.

Interestingly, we also identified three peptides corresponding to the SIRPα-derived spacer segment of the CAR construct (Figure 3E). The expression levels of these peptides were elevated in BX795/retronectin-treated cells (Figure 3G). The presence of CAR-derived peptides specifically in samples with high CAR expression not only validates successful CAR expression but also supports the overall reliability of the proteomic dataset, as CAR is detected only when biologically expected.

Considering that only minimal changes were detected in the NK cell surface markers, proteome, and phosphoproteome, we postulate that using BX795/retronectin in CAR transduction does not significantly impact the final CAR-NK product suggesting favorable safety of the product. Additionally, the resulting protein list from the total protein analysis, along with its various annotations (such as GO-BP, KEGG, and REACTOME), provides a valuable resource for understanding the protein expression patterns in feeder cell expanded CAR-NK cells (Table S1).

Activation with cytokines such as IL-2 and IL-15 is essential for an efficient transduction of NK cells with VSV-G LVs.12 However, to improve the cost-effectiveness of therapeutic cell production, transduction should be performed as early as possible to minimize LV usage. Previous studies have shown that a two- to three-day activation period increases transduction efficiency.12 Consequently, we chose to transduce the cells on day 3. As depicted in Figure 2E, activation plays a crucial role in NK cell transduction, and the effectiveness of the BX795/etronectin combination relies on NK cell activation. Additionally, the results shown in Figures 1A and 1B suggest that the impact of NK cell activation is likely due to increased LDLR expression in the NK cells.

The high VCN values observed in this study could raise concerns for clinical applications, as elevated integration may increase the risk of insertional mutagenesis, alter cell physiology, lead to uncontrolled activation (such as tonic signaling), and complicate manufacturing and regulatory approval. However, the lentiviral vectors used here were relatively simple CAR-expressing constructs. In contrast, clinical-grade CAR-NK manufacturing typically relies on larger and more complex lentiviral vectors that incorporate additional functional elements such as safety switches and cytokine-expression cassettes. These more elaborate constructs are generally more difficult to transduce and integrate efficiently, and therefore often yield lower VCNs under comparable conditions. Establishing conditions that generate higher VCNs with simpler constructs may therefore help define a transduction window suitable for upcoming, clinically relevant vectors with greater cargo size and complexity. In our study, NK-specific CAR constructs achieved moderate and consistent CAR surface expression across donors at VCN values of approximately 1–2 copies per cell, indicating that this range reflects an operational transduction window that supports robust expression without excessive vector integration. Nevertheless, the optimal MOI must always be determined empirically for each vector to ensure that VCN remains within a range that is both effective and clinically acceptable.

Cord blood units can be utilized as a source of gene-modified NK cells and are often stored in frozen form.24 Both in research and in clinical therapy, the use of frozen NK cells as starting material can be advantageous. To assess whether freezing the NK cells prior to transduction affects transduction efficiency, we isolated cells from three distinct buffy coats and performed transduction on either freshly prepared or freeze-thawed samples (Figure 2D). Remarkably, the transduction levels remained consistent, suggesting that frozen NK cells can indeed serve as suitable starting material. However, it’s important to note that a fraction of NK cells is likely lost during the freezing process.

Besides the impact on NK cell activation and the use of transduction enhancers, we discovered that the CAR composition also plays a role in efficient transduction or transgene expression by the cells. Surprisingly, CARs successfully used for T cell transduction (FiCAR-T and FiCAR-T [R]) resulted in lower transduction efficiency in NK cells, as observed from both the vector copy numbers and transgene expression data. In contrast, NK-cell-specific CAR-NK3 was expressed at high levels both on NK and T cells (Figures 4B and S6A). Our results suggests that a CD28-signaling domain, or its combination with the CD28 transmembrane domain, reduced the functionality of the FiCAR-LVs in NK cells, as replacing the CD28-signaling domain with one from 41BB markedly improved the transduction efficiency in NK cells (Figures 4D and 4E). Although a small but statistically significant increase in vector copy numbers was also observed in T cells at an MOI of 1 (Figure 4E), this effect was minor and did not translate into higher CAR expression (Figure 4D). Unfortunately, our approach does not allow us to dissect whether the reduced reduced vector functionality in NK cells was due to the presence of CD28 per se, or to the presence of the two CD28-derived elements within a SIRPa-based CAR structure. It is, however, unlikely that this phenomenon is caused by inefficient production of SIRPa/CD28-containing LV’s, given that the same viral preparations performed efficiently in the transduction of T cells (Figures 4 and S6). Furthermore, similar T-cell-specific SIRPa/CD28-containing CAR constructs have been used in our previous studied with high transduction efficiency.20 To clarify whether the observed effect reflects a CD28-specific incompatibility with NK cell signaling or a structural interaction unique to the SIRPα/CD28 combination, a logical future experiment would be to replace the 2B4-signaling domain in the CAR-NK3 construct with a CD28-signaling domain. Such an approach would help determine whether the reduced NK cell transduction efficiency observed here are attributable to CD28 itself or arise from an interaction between CD28 and SIRPα.

When titrated on T cell-specific FiCAR-T (R), the maximum proportion of NK cells expressing a CAR plateaued at approximately 40% (Figure S6B). This saturation point was not exceeded, even when the virus-to-target cell ratio was amplified to an MOI of 500. This observation could suggest that only a certain fraction of NK cells is capable of being transduced with FiCAR-T (R). One explanation might be that the SIRPa/CD28-containing transgene, which is also manifested on the LV membrane,32 interacts unfavorably with certain NK cell subpopulations. This adverse interaction does not occur with T cells and could result in inadequate virus entry, integration, and/or CAR expression.

This intriguing phenomenon necessitates further investigation for a more comprehensive understanding. However, we propose that the composition of the CAR influences the efficiency of NK cell transduction. This may partially account for the challenges commonly encountered in NK cell transduction. For a more detailed understanding, it would be beneficial to separately study the CAR positive and negative NK cell fractions. However, this was not the primary focus of this study and was not pursued further.

While CAR expression was primarily quantified by flow cytometry and vector copy-number analysis, western blotting of the CD3ζ domain was used as an additional, qualitative readout of CAR protein levels (Figure S7). The readily detectable CD3ζ signal observed for CAR-NK3 is consistent with its higher surface expression, median fluorescence intensity and vector copy number. The lack of a quantifiable CAR-derived CD3ζ signal for FiCAR-T_41BB in NK cells likely reflects the limited sensitivity of western blotting for low-abundance or heterogeneous transgene expression.

In addition to VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs, we tested the BaEV LVs for NK cell transduction.10,18 Our results showed that transduction of GFP and CAR-NK3 could reliably be achieved using BaEV LVs (Figures S8A and 5A). However, the FiCAR constructs containing the SIRPα spacer (FiCAR-T, FiCAR-NK1, and FiCAR-NK2) were not successfully transduced using BaEV LVs, even though the same transfer plasmids were used as in production of VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs (Figure 5A). It is possible that the lack of Sirpα spacer and/or the presence of CD8-transmembrane domain in CAR-NK3 influence the transduction efficiency in BaEV LVs specifically. However, NK cells might be more sensitive to different genetic constructs introduced via gene transfer than T cells, but to confirm this, further studies are needed. Due to the low virus titers obtained in BaEV production (Figure S8B), we decided not to pursue this approach any further.

In conclusion, this study highlights the challenges and complexities of NK cell transduction, emphasizing the need for careful consideration of various factors, such as NK cell activation, construct design, LV-pseudotype selection, and the use of transduction enhancers to modulate the virus-cell interactions and NK cell anti-viral signaling. By manipulating these elements, effective transduction can be achieved, enabling the use of VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs for therapeutic NK cell production. In our optimized workflow, NK cells are activated with IL-2 and IL-15 followed by transduction with an NK-cell-specific CAR construct using VSV-G-pseudotyped LVs in conjunction with BX795 and retronectin. This approach yields an excellent transduction efficiency without compromising the NK cell phenotype or growth.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

RS4.11 (CD19+ ALL cell line, CRL-1873) and NALM-6 (ALL, CRL-3273) were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). To generate the luciferase-expressing cell lines, RS4.11 cells were transduced with LUC2-EGFP (VB210329-1384uya, VectorBuilder, Germany) and NALM-6 with the IVISbrite Red F-luc-GFP (RediFect, PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA) LVs with MOIs 15 and 10, respectively. After transduction and expansion, the eGFP- and GFP-positive cells were selected using a Sony SH800 (Sony Biotechnology, San Jose, USA) cell sorter. Both RS4.11 and NALM6-target cells were grown RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA).

To efficiently expand NK and CAR-NK cells, K562-based feeder cells were added into the culture twice at a ratio 2:1. The K562-mbIL-15-41BBL feeder cell line used was generous gift from prof. Dario Campana.33 The transduction and expansion protocol are depicted in Figure 1C.

CAR structures and lentiviral vectors

The schematic structures of the CAR constructs used in the study are shown in Figures 4 and 5. The CAR designed for making CAR T cells (FiCAR-T) has been previously described20 (FiCAR 1). Briefly, all the CAR constructs target CD19 with an scFv derived from the mouse anti-human CD19-targeting antibody FMC63, consisting of the light and heavy chain variable domains connected with a (GGGGS)4-linker. Following the ScFv, there is an extended human IgG1-derived hinge and another G4S-linker. The extracellular spacer was derived either from SIRPα C1-type 1 and 2 domains (FiCAR-T, FiCAR-T_41BB, FiCAR-NK1, and FiCAR-NK2) or from CD8 (CAR-NK3). The transmembrane domains (TMs) varied between the constructs: FiCAR-T and FiCAR-T_41BB contain a CD28 TM domain, while NK-specific CARs comprise NKG2D (FiCAR-NK1), 2B4 (FiCAR-NK2), or CD8 (CAR-NK3) TM domains. The intracellular signaling domain is derived from CD3 zeta in all constructs and the co-signaling domain from CD28, 41BB, or 2B4.

VSV-G- and BaEV-pseudotyped third generation LVs for gene transfer were produced in the National Virus Vector Laboratory (NVVL) at the A.I. Virtanen Institute for Molecular Sciences (University of Eastern Finland, Kuopio, Finland) with the standard calcium phosphate transfection method in 293 T cells.34 VSVG.LVs were concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 68 600 × g at room temperature for 2 h. BaEV-LVs were concentrated by centrifugation with 3,000 × g at 4°C overnight, as described before.35 Concentrated vectors were aliquoted and stored at −70°C until use. The produced vectors were characterized for their p24 capsid protein using the Alliance HIV-1 p24 Antigen ELISA Kit (NEK050B001KT, Revvity). LVs with the EGFP transgene were functionally titered in HeLa cells (VSV-G-pseudotyped vectors) or 293 T cells (BaEV-pseudotyped LVs) with the standard serial dilution method.34 For LVs with other transgenes, a titer estimate was calculated based on the vectors’ p24-concentration that was related to the functional titer and p24 content of a control VSVG-pseudotyped vector (Table S3).

The FiCAR-T (R) lentiviral vector was produced at a GMP-competent contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO; VIVEbiotech, Spain) and FiCAR-T_41BB by SIRION Biotech (part of Revvity; Germany).

NK isolation and culturing

This study is conducted under the ethical approval of the HUS Regional Committee on Medical Research Ethics (HUS/107/2023). NK cells were isolated from random healthy buffy coats using the Human NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from one-day-old buffy coats provided by the Finnish Red Cross Blood Service under an institutional permit (FRCBS 178/6/2023). The PBMCs were separated using a Ficoll-based separation (Ficoll-Paque, Cytiva, Germany). After negative selection, the isolated NK cells were either directed for activation and transduction or cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen for further use.

The isolated NK cells were cultured and activated in an NK expansion medium consisting of NK MACS Basal Medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) supplemented with 5% human AB serum (Sigma), 500 IU/mL IL-2 (proleukin S), 140 IU/mL IL-15 (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 ng/mL streptomycin (penicillin-streptomycin, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA).

NK cell transduction

After three days of activation the NK cells were subjected to transduction using either the VSV-G- or BaEV-pseudotyped LVs. The transductions were performed in NK cell expansion medium, with a final cell density of 500,000 cells/mL, primarily using a MOI of 10. To enhance the transduction efficiency, we employed various enhancers and their combinations as follows:

Rosuvastatin (5 μM Cayman chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was added on day 0 (the day of NK cell isolation), and the cells activated in its presence until transduction on day 3. Before transduction, retronectin (Takara, San Jose, CA, USA) was coated onto the culture plates at a concentration of 25 μg/mL for 2 hours. Subsequently, the wells were blocked with 2% BSA (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA) and the plates used for transduction. Dextran (8 μg/mL, Sigma Aldrich, Merck, Germany) and the TLR4 inhibitors (1.1 μM MRT67307, 5 μM BX795 or 24.8 μM Amlexanox, all from MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) were added 30 min before the transduction on day 3 and washed away on day 4. Vectofusin-1 (10 μg/mL, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) was added simultaneously with the LVs on day 3. A subset of the NK cells was activated by co-culturing them with K562-based feeder cells (K562-mbIL-15-41BBL) in a 1:1 ratio from day 0 until the transduction on day 3. For the spinoculation, the NK cells were centrifuged at 400×g for 1–2 h at RT after adding the LV.

Following transduction, the LVs and transduction enhancers were washed away using PBS at 24 h after the transduction (day 4). Subsequently, the transduced cells were cocultured with feeder cells (K562-mbIL-15-41BBL) at a 1:2 NK:feeder ratio from day 4 until day 12. On day 12, an additional dose of feeder cells (also at a 1:2 ratio) was added. After 2 weeks of expansion, the CAR-NK cells were either analyzed or cryopreserved. A subset of the CAR-NK cells was also analyzed after 1-week expansion following day 12.

T cell isolation, culturing, and transduction

CAR-T cells were produced as previously described (Koski et al. 2025, submitted). Briefly, PBMCs were isolated from fresh buffy coats followed by T cell isolation by positive selection with CD4/CD8 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Next, the isolated T cells were activated by incubating them for 24 h with TransAct reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in a complete T cell medium (TexMACS medium supplemented with 12 ng/mL of IL-7 and IL-15 (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and 100 U/mL of penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA)). The activated T cells were transduced using VSV-G-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors carrying the specific CARs. After 24 h of transduction, the T cells were washed with medium without supplements and expanded in complete T cell medium for 9 days prior to the analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

The viability of the CAR-NK and -T cells was assessed by staining the cells with Zombie Green or Zombie Aqua (BioLegend, Germany) at a 1:600 dilution (10 min at room temperature, respectively). Following viability staining, the Fc receptors of the NK cells were blocked by incubating the cells with Fc receptor blocking solution (BioLegend, Germany) at a dilution of approximately 1:30 for 10 min at room temperature. The actual antibody staining was performed for 15 min at room temperature or 30 min at +4°C in FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 40 ng/mL EDTA and 5 mg/mL human albumin).

To detect lineage markers, the CAR-NK cells were stained with anti-CD56 (B339013, BioLegend, Germany), and CAR-T cells with anti-CD3 antibodies (300406, BioLegend, Germany). The CAR expression of both the CAR-NK and -T cells was assessed by staining the cells with an anti-FMC63 antibody (FM3-AY54P1, ACRO Biosystems, Newark, DE, USA). For phenotypic marker analysis, CAR-NK cells were stained with anti-CD16 (302048, BioLegend, Germany), -CD57 (393326, BioLegend, Germany), -LAG3 (369322, BioLegend, Germany), -NKG2A (130-113-567, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), and -NKG2C (130-123-037, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) antibodies.

The stained samples were analyzed using the Symphony A1 instrument (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with the relevant isotype, unstained, and untransduced control samples. The data analysis and compensations were performed using the FlowJo software (version 10, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and isotype controls or stained, untransduced biological controls were used for gating. Full gating strategies are shown in Figure S9.

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of the CAR-NK cells was evaluated using a luciferase-based assay. In brief, the luciferase expressing target cells (5000 RS4.11 or 20000 NALM6) were plated on a 384-well tissue culture plate in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Walthan, MA, USA). The CAR-NK cells were then added at an E:T ratio ranging from 0.12 to 10:1. After 4 h of co-culture, the cells were lysed, and luciferase activity of the remaining live target cells measured following substrate addition (One-Glo, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The luminescence signal was quantified using the VICTOR Nivo Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA). All measurements were performed in three technical replicates.

Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses

For the proteomic analyses, NK cells from three biological donors were transduced with the FiCAR-NK1 construct either without enhancers or using the BX795 and retronectin combination, as previously described. Untransduced NK cells served as controls. On day 19, 10 × 106 CAR-NK cell pellets were collected in four technical replicates per condition, pelleted, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Mass spectrometry

Pellets were lysed in 8.0 M urea (#U5378-500G, Sigma Aldrich) in 100 mM ammonium biocarbonate (NH4HCO3, #A6141, Sigma Aldrich) with sonication (2 × 10 min). Total protein concentration of the homogenates was measured with Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye (#5000006, Bio-Rad Laboratories). The urea concentration was diluted to 1 M with 100 mM NH4HCO3. All the samples were reduced with 5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (#20490, Thermo Scientific), alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide (#122271000, Acros Organics) at room temperature, and trypsin-digested at 37°C for 16 h using Sequencing Grade Modified Trypsin (V5113, Promega). After digestion, samples were acidified with 10% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, #85049.051, VWR) and desalted with BioPureSPN MACRO TARGA C18 columns (HMM S18R, Nest Group) according to manufacturer’s instructions. From the eluate, one-sixth was dried in a centrifuge concentrator (Concentrator Plus, Eppendorf) for total protein analysis. The rest was first enriched for phosphopeptides (the dried peptides were reconstituted in 30 μL buffer A [0.1% (vol/vol) TFA and 1% (vol/vol) acetonitrile (#83640.320, VWR) in HPLC grade water (#10505904, Fisher Scientific)]). 50 μg of total protein from each sample was taken for total protein analysis, and 250 μg were used for phosphopeptide enrichment.

For phosphopeptide enrichment, the peptide mixture was loaded onto stage-tips packed with TiO4-IMAC microspheres, prewashed with loading buffer (80% acetonitrile/6% trifluoroacetic acid, TFA). TiO4-IMAC microspheres selectively captured phosphopeptides, while nonspecific peptides were removed with sequential washes of 50% acetonitrile/6% TFA/200 mM NaCl and 50% acetonitrile/0.1% TFA. Phosphopeptides were eluted using 10% NH4OH, followed by centrifugation to collect the eluate. The eluate was dried in a centrifuge concentrator (Concentrator Plus, Eppendorf) and stored for subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis.

Peptide samples were resuspended in buffer A (0.1% FA, 1% ACN, 30 μL for total, 60 μL for phospho). The samples were then diluted into a final concentration of 5 ng/μL and 20 μL was loaded into Evotip PURE (EV2011, Evosep) according to manufacturer instructions. Samples were then analyzed using the Evosep One liquid chromatography system coupled to a hybrid trapped ion mobility quadrupole TOF mass spectrometer (Bruker timsTOF Pro 2, Bruker Daltonics) (Meier, Brunner et al., 2018) via a CaptiveSpray nano-electrospray ion source (Bruker Daltonics). An 8 cm × 150 μm column with 1.5 μm C18 beads (EV1109, Evosep) was used for peptide separation with the 60 samples per day method for total samples and 30 samples per day method for phospho. Mobile phases A and B were 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, respectively. The MS analysis was performed in the positive-ion mode with dia-PASEF method (Meier, Brunner et al., 2020) with sample optimized data independent analysis (dia) scan parameters. To perform sample-specific dia-PASEF parameter adjustment the default dia-short-gradient acquisition methods was adjusted based on the sample-specific DDA-PASEF run with the software “tims Control” (Bruker Daltonics). The following parameters were modified for each sample type: m/z range; mass steps per cycle; mean cycle time (for total: da 400–1258, overlap 0.5, mass width 25; steps 13; cycle time 1.48 s. Phospho: da 345–1321, overlap 1.0, mass width 26; steps 22; cycle time 2.44 s. The ion mobility windows were set to best match the ion cloud density from the sample-type–specific DDA-runs.

To analyze diaPASEF data, the raw data (.d) were processed with DIA-NN v18.1 (Demichev et al., 2020; Demichev et al., 2022) utilizing spectral library generated from the UniProt human proteome (UP000005640, downloaded 2.4.2024 as a FASTA file, 20410 proteins). During library generation following settings were used for total samples: fixed modifications: carbamidomethyl (C); variable modifications: acetyl (protein N-term), oxidation (M); enzyme:Trypsin/P; maximum missed cleavages:1; mass accuracy fixed to 1.5e−05 (MS2) and 1.5e−05 (MS1); Fragment m/z set to 200–1,800; peptide length set to 7–50; precursor m/z set to 300–1800; Precursor changes set to 1–4; protein inference not performed. For phospho data: fixed modifications: carbamidomethyl (C); variable modifications: acetyl (protein N-term), Phospho (STY); enzyme:Trypsin/P; maximum missed cleavages:1; mass accuracy fixed to 1.5e−05 (MS2) and 1.5e−05 (MS1); Fragment m/z set to 200–1,800; peptide length set to 7–50; precursor m/z set to 300–1,800; Precursor changes set to 2–4; protein inference not performed. All other settings were left to default.

Vector copy-number analysis

The absolute vector copy number in the cell population was determined using the QX200 ddPCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). One million cells were used to extract genomic DNA using the EZ1&2 DNA Tissue Kit for automated purification of DNA from cultured cells and the EZ2 Connect instrument according to manufacturer’s protocol (QIAGEN, Germany). DeNovix DS-11 FX Spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, USA) was used to determine the DNA concentration. The ddPCR assay was performed using the ddPCR Multiplex Supermix and probes detecting the vector sequence (HivPsi in FAM, assay ID: dEXD14812826) and housekeeping gene (RPP30 in HEX, assay ID: dHsaCP2500350) with the compatible restriction enzyme Hae III according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). To generate droplets, the QX200 Automated droplet generator was used, and PCR amplification performed on the C1000 Touch Thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The VCN was analyzed using the Auto DG QX200 droplet reader PCR system and QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Western blot

To analyze CD3ζ expression on NK cells, 2 × 106 cell samples were collected from NK cells to SDS sample buffer (Novagen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), boiled for 10 min and stored in −20°C for western blotting. The western blot analysis utilized anti-CD3ζ antibody (1:1000, 88083 S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) or anti-beta-actin antibody (1:1000, 13E5, 4970 T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) with anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:3000, 7074P2, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Signal intensity was analyzed using ImageJ.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.10.5.0). Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. For all analyses, n refers to the number of independent biological donors. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used. Untransduced control samples were included in figures for visual reference but were not incorporated into statistical analyses. For one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple-comparison correction. For two-way repeated-measures ANOVA evaluating the effects of activation and enhancer conditions, planned simple-effects analyses were performed, and Holm-Šidák correction was applied to adjust p values. Paired, two-tailed t tests were used for comparisons between two related conditions within the same donor (e.g., CAR expression or vector copy number between two MOIs within each cell type) and p values were adjusted using the Holm-Šidák method when multiple paired comparisons were performed. Comparisons between the different peptides and phosphopeptides were analyzed at the donor level. For each donor and condition, four technical replicates were averaged prior to statistical testing. To assess treatment effects, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted, and post hoc comparisons were restricted to within-peptide treatment effects and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Šidák method. Adjusted p-values are reported in each figure where applicable. Significance is indicated as ns, not significant, p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), and p < 0.001 (∗∗∗) and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

For proteomic analyses, protein-level intensity values were log2 transformed, and proteins that were not detected in at least 60% of samples of one sample group were discarded as low-quality identifications. Values were median normalized, and missing values were imputed using QRILC. Differential abundance analysis between sample groups was performed using a two-sample t test, and p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Proteins were considered significantly differentially abundant if they met two criteria: an FDR-adjusted q value below 0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change exceeding 1. Principal component analysis was used to visualize the differently abundant proteins. Functional enrichment was performed with DAVID.36
