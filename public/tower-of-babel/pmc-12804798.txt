High-frequency observations during Adriatic mucilage event reveal unique phytoplankton traits and diversity response
Authors: Ivan Vlašiček, Daniela Marić Pfannkuchen, Mirta Smodlaka Tanković, Ana Baričević, Tjaša Kogovšek, Nataša Kužat, Mia Knjaz, Lana Grižančić, Ivan Podolšak, Ariana Turković, Robert Šulc, Robert Kopal, Mario Špadina, Martin Pfannkuchen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMucilage aggregation is a striking phenomenon in the northern Adriatic Sea, reappearing massively in surface waters on the Istrian coast in 2024 after 20 years. Formed through polymerization of exuded sugars during microphytoplankton blooms, mucilage events are hard to capture with traditional monitoring. Here, we used real-time in situ sensors, satellite data and daily pulse-shape flow cytometry to analyze phytoplankton dynamics during this event. Mucilage formation was linked to rising temperatures and Po River-induced salinity drops. Microphytoplankton species like Cerataulina pelagica, Cylindrotheca closterium, Thalassionema spp., and Gonyaulax fragilis showed as important taxa in each different phase of the phenomenon. Aggregates consisted mainly of single cells. Mucilage periods featured low diversity and thicker, more complex cells, unlike autumn blooms which showed higher diversity, chain-forming colonies, and more pigments. Our findings highlight the key role of microphytoplankton and single cells in mucilage dynamics and the influence of environmental factors like temperature, wind and freshwater inputs on phytoplankton structure and biomass in coastal ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31369-4.Subject terms: Marine biology, Community ecology

Introduction

The northern Adriatic Sea is the shallow and northernmost part of the Mediterranean. It is under direct influence of one of the largest freshwater inputs into the Mediterranean. Meteorological and hydrological characteristics result in relatively long retention times of water masses in the northern Adriatic. The combination of the abovementioned renders the area one of the most productive areas in the Mediterranean with strong west to east gradient. The area is well known for high nutrient concentrations and phosphate limitation throughout the year1–3. Marine snow and larger mucous aggregates are also well-known phenomena for the area4,5. While marine snow and macroflocs exhibit a great morphological and functional diversity and play roles in vertical transport of organic matter6, mucilage aggregates are similar but different phenomena. Large mucilage aggregates of up to several meters can form in the water column and frontal surface and subsurface (false bottom) organic layers, from hundreds of meters to several kilometres long, may develop. The massive formation of these macroaggregates is referred to as the “mucilage phenomenon” in the recent literature, which is further explored in this study.

This phenomenon called “mare sporco” (dirty sea) first attracted scientific attention in 1872 in the Gulf of Trieste7, although the event was previously mentioned in a document of 17298. It was described successively in scientific journals 1729, (1744?), 1872, 1880, 1891,1903,1095, 1920, 1927, 1929, 1930, 1941. 1949, (1973), 1983,1984, 1986, 1988, 1989,1991,1997,2001, 2002,2003, 20047–31. Mucilage events are natural phenomena which were extensively studied in the past by many scientists in the region but also in other seas where they occur, like Tyrrhenian Sea32,33, Marmara Sea34,35 and New Zealand36,37.

The most recent marine mucilage outbreak in the Sea of Marmara in spring 2021 sparked renewed interest in this complex environmental phenomenon and in monitoring the delicate marine ecosystems of our planet34,35.

Over the past two decades, large-scale surface mucilage aggregates have been absent from the Northern Adriatic, particularly in its eastern part. However, mucilage events were documented in autumn 2006, winter 2007, 2014 and 2018 9–14,21) on a smaller regional scale by regional monitoring agencies. But in the late spring and summer of 2024, large mucilage formed over all the northern Adriatic38 (Fig. 1).

In this article we will use the term large mucilage aggregates without distinguishing between their different stages as “clouds”, “creamy surface layer” and “gelatinous surface layer as defined in literature39,40. In the mentioned literature “clouds” are amorphous submerged structures which can be several meters long. “Creamy surface layer” and “gelatinous surface layer” differ in their colour and consistency: the first one is white and directly below the surface while the second is floating at the surface and yellow or brown in colour. Transparent aggregations in the centimeter range in general further aggregate to larger aggregates, accumulate cells, debris and particles and gradually change colour depending on the accumulated material. As all of these forms were visible simultaneously during the summer 2024, we decided to use a single term for all of them: large mucilage aggregates.

Mucilage itself is a complex mixture of organic compounds like carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, amids, organosilicates and other polymerised in humin like matrix41–43. Inorganic compounds like carbonates and silicates have also a significant contribution41. The main source of organic precursors of mucilage are phytoplankton cells43. So far, there were attempts to pinpoint one or several groups of these organisms which are mostly responsible for mucilage formation and / or aggregation. In previous research diatoms were considered to be main producers of dissolved organic matter. Species like Thalassionema nitzschioides, Cylindrotheca closterium, Cerataulina pelagica or Pseudo-nitzschia delicatissima group were found associated with aggregates44–46. Among them, a diatom C. closterium appears to play some roles in the formation of the aggregates, or at least it helps in self-sustaining of already formed mucilage44,47. Other authors reveal an important role of dinoflagellate groups in mucilage aggregate formation, especially the species Gonyaulax fragilis48,49 or Tripos (Ceratium) furca45. Previous studies have shown that G. fragilis is capable of producing large quantities of mucilage, with a composition similar to that of aggregates observed in natural environments48,49.

In summary, large aggregate formation starts with high concentrations of dissolved organic matter, leading to the production of transparent exopolymer particles (TEP) as the first step in mucilage development. From the literature we know that many phytoplankton species are capable of producing TEP50,51 or mucilage as mentioned before. This organic matter is produced and accumulated in conditions where the temperatures rise sharply, nutrients are available and there are rather calm oceanographic conditions52.

Calm weather conditions and strong stratification appear to further promote the accumulation of exopolymers into larger aggregates53. Nutrient (phosphorus) limitation, a characteristic stressor for phytoplankton in this area1 can also induce production of mucilage by phytoplankton cells54.

Other factors like reduced grazing or bacterial and viral activity can play roles in increased polymer production. On the other hand, mechanisms of aggregation of polymers, and roles of cyanobacteria in these processes remain to be examined52.

As, prior to 2024, this phenomenon last occurred in 2004 on large scales, this last mass occurrence was the first opportunity to monitor the northern Adriatic with high resolution methods during a mucilagine event. Moored buoys can capture high frequency real-time data about physical and biological conditions in the sea. Beside this, taxonomic and functional composition of the phytoplankton community can be monitored faster by using e.g. flow cytometric and image-in-flow methods. CytoSense (CytoBuoy BV, The Netherlands) is such an instrument which can simultaneously perform both aforementioned analyses of the phytoplankton community55,56. One of the remarkable properties of this instrument is the ability to capture whole scans of phytoplankton cells (pulse-shapes). Pulse-shapes in different channels (forward scatter, sideward scatter, fluorescence of photosynthetic pigments) can be further used in calculation of numerous parameters. Those parameters enable a fine classification of phytoplankton functional groups based on optical properties of cells57. Thus, detailed information about abundance and other optical properties of those groups and the whole phytoplankton community is provided. Due to high sensitivity and robustness of the instrument, the whole size range of phytoplankton is covered as well: from picoplankton to large microplankton cells. Pulse-shapes parameters can be further used as the information about certain morphological traits such as size, shape, specific pigment content or chain morphology. This approach can be used to highlight certain adaptations of organisms to environmental pressures, and automatically analyse them58,59. These traits are useful information for understanding the mechanisms of phytoplankton community succession if coupled with other environmental parameters60,61. In addition, CytoSense can generate a large amount of images per sample which are a very valuable resource for taxonomic composition analysis of samples.

Here we combined results obtained from CIM ODAS II (moored buoy with biological, oceanographic and meteorological sensors) and Sentinel-2 data with daily cytometric analyses of the phytoplankton community in order to characterize the changes in phytoplankton communities during 2024, a year when large mucous aggregates reappeared, after 20 years of absence. We used this methodology in order to test the hypothesis that microphytoplankton is the main constituent of the phytoplankton community in the period of aggregate appearance. We used taxonomic information from CytoSense images-in-flow to test the differences in species composition and diversity through the year. Also, we analysed the morphological single cell traits and we hypothesize that they are different in mucous aggregates from those in other bloom events and that they are selected by environmental factors.

Methods

We combined in situ oceanographic and meteorological measurements, satellite remote sensing, and flow cytometric observations to characterize physical, chemical, and biological conditions during mucilage events in the northern Adriatic in 2024.

In situ oceanographic, biological, and meteorological measurements

For in situ oceanographic, biological and meteorological data we used sensor data on a DB8000 (Mobilis, France) moored buoy CIM ODAS II, located 6 miles offshore from the Rovinj (Fig. 1a).

An SBE16 CTD probe (Seabird Scientific, USA) recorded temperature (°C) and salinity at 1 m depth. An attached ECO fluorimeter measured chlorophyll a (µg L⁻¹), turbidity (NTU), and green backscatter (β (532), m⁻¹ sr⁻¹). Dissolved oxygen (mL L⁻¹) was measured with an SBE63 optical oxygen sensor. To evaluate sensor drift, dissolved oxygen was also determined via Winkler titration of discrete samples collected at the buoy site62. Wind speed (m s− 1) and direction were recorded with a GMX551 Compact Weather Station installed on the buoy.

Satellite-derived monitoring of Adriatic mucilage events

The area chosen for the study of mucilage aggregation at the level of northern Adriatic included the river Po delta and western coastline of Istria peninsula. CIM ODAS II buoy is included as well.

Remote sensing method of choice is analysis of multispectral optical imagery data from two sources, namely Sentinel-2 Multi-Spectral Instrument (MSI)* and Planet Labs’ PlanetScope.

In this study, optical satellite data from the Sentinel-2 mission (Level L1C) were used. A Sentinel-2 Level-1 C Collection 1 product used provides orthorectified Top-Of-Atmosphere (TOA) reflectance, with sub-pixel multispectral and multitemporal registration accuracy. The data were obtained via the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem platform and cover the period from June 28 to September 11, 2024. Only scenes with favorable visibility conditions and minimal cloud cover were included in the analysis to ensure precision in identifying mucilage aggregates and deriving higher-level products (e.g., Chl-a).

For each selected date, six Sentinel-2 tiles were used and subsequently merged into a mosaic to fully cover the study area of the northern Adriatic and the western coast of the Istrian Peninsula. This step ensured spatial continuity and eliminated edge discontinuities between adjacent scenes.

The imagery multispectral data were processed using a combination of atmospheric corrections method, designed and validated for Case I and II types of seas as in Northern Adriatic63–65. This enabled the extraction of bio-optical variables from the spectral data, including chlorophyll a concentration (Chl-a), red-edge reflectivity and others. These parameters served as indicators of phytoplankton biomass and the trophic status of the studied water body, as well as classifiers for the spatial distribution of mucilage aggregation.

All data were resampled to a spatial resolution of 20 m to ensure consistency across bands and improve the accuracy of spatial analysis. Data processing and visualization were conducted using SNAP (Sentinel Application Platform) and QGIS. A total of five scenes were selected for visualization in this article.

We used 25 PlanetScope surface reflectance images from the period 2024-05-24 to 2024-09-10., comprising 24 images per study site, to map Chl-a, as well as surface leaving reflectivity of spectral bands, spatial and temporal variations. Planet Labs PBC (2024), PlanetScope (SuperDove) imagery, Surface Reflectance, 3 m, 8-band multispectral data, acquired from 2024-05-24 to 2024-09-10, are accessed via Planet Explorer.

Atmospheric correction was applied during data processing66,67. The number of usable satellite scenes per site was determined by prevailing atmospheric and ocean surface conditions. Only imagery with minimal cloud cover, absence of sunlight and low sea surface roughness was selected, based on the Planet cloud cover threshold (< 5%) and visual inspection. Bio-optical variables were extracted as spatial averages over 1 × 1 km areas and also as mean values across nine (9) adjacent pixels. An area of interest of 1 km2 was activated for data retrieval in a square geometry over the location of the CIM ODAS II. These satellite-derived parameters were subsequently validated against in situ measurements from CIM ODAS II buoy sensor datasets68.

By leveraging the two different satellite imagery sources over the same sensing period both medium (20 m and 10 m from Sentinel-2) and high resolution imagery data (3 m per pixel from PlanetScope) we analysed the macro- and micro-level spatial and temporal inhomogeneities in water constituents in the Northern Adriatic. In particular, macro-level view obtained by medium spatial resolution Sentinel-2 serves for understanding the broader sea area of the western Istria peninsula, while micro-level view using high resolution imagery data PlanetScope serves to deduce spatial inhomogeneities on scales less than 100 m in length, and the area of interest is clustered around CIM ODAS II buoy to explain the water constituents signal variations measured by probes (see Supplementary note 1 for more details). In addition to calculating the mean concentrations over a 1 km² area centered on the CIM ODAS II buoy, we established a grid of virtual sampling stations (or satellite-derived microlocation data points) to investigate finer-scale spatial variability (see Supplementary Fig. 1.).

CytoSense data acquisition

Samples for flow cytometric analysis were collected daily in front of Rovinj during the mucilage phenomenon. Daily sampling began on 2024-06-09, a few days after the first reports of mucilage, and continued until 2024-11-16. In addition, we incorporate monthly data from our regular monitoring program, covering the period from the beginning of the year. Monthly samples were collected at station RV001, located between the ODAS II buoy and Rovinj, approximately 4 nautical miles from shore, using Niskin bottles to sample the 0.5 m-thick surface layer of seawater. The phytoplankton community in all samples was analyzed using the same flow cytometry protocol as for the daily samples.

When large mucilage aggregates were present at the sampling site, we recorded their occurrence and collected seawater from nearby. Because aggregates were widespread, it was often impossible to avoid them entirely; however, we attempted to avoid the densest patches to prevent clogging of the flow cytometer. Flow cytometry was done using the pulse shape recording flow cytometer CytoSense (CytoBuoy BV, The Netherlands). The instrument pumped samples with a peristaltic pump into a flow chamber where it was hydrodynamically focused with sheath fluid. Sheath fluid is produced inside the instrument by filtering the water from the sample through internal filters (pore size: 0.1 μm). Particles suspended in the water passed through laser beams (beam width: 5 μm). The instrument is equipped with two lasers: green (wavelength: 532 nm, power: 70 mW) and blue (wavelength: 445 nm, power: 70 mW). Pulse shapes of particles were recorded in six channels: left and right polarized forward scatter (FWS), sideward scatter (SWS), red fluorescence (Fl Red, wavelength: 665–750 nm), yellow fluorescence (Fl Yellow, wavelength: 570–600 nm) and orange fluorescence (Fl Orange, wavelength: 630–650 nm). Scatter signals are light scatter from cells while fluorescence signals are autofluorescence of cell pigments (Fl Red: chlorophyll a, Fl Yellow and Orange: accessory pigments).

For discriminating phytoplankton from other suspended particles trigger levels on several channels were set prior to measurement. The main trigger was SWS as this detector is more sensitive to light and in order to record whole particles. The second trigger was set to Fl Red channel in order to record only cells with chlorophyll. As the targeted phytoplankton cells cover a wide size range (2–1000 μm) two different protocols were used in each sample. First protocol (“pico”) used 3 mV SWS and Fl Red 2 mV trigger combination in order to capture picophytoplankton cells. Pump speed and duration of measurement varied depending on total event rate (it should be lower than 5000 events in second, in order to avoid coincidence and thus bad discrimination of separate particles). Thus, the analyzed volume was between 120 and 1000 µL. The second protocol (“nano”) used higher triggers (SWS: 30 mV and Fl Red: 15 mV) in order to avoid smaller cells and keep the final file size manageable. Analyzed volume varied between 1800 and 12,500 µL, depending on particle flow rate. The instrument is also equipped with the camera (magnification = 16×) which can capture images-in-flow of some cells. The trigger for imaging is the same as the trigger for flow cytometry and corresponding optical properties of the imaged cell are known. Data acquisition was performed with the CytoUSB5 software from CytoBuoy.

Gating of cytometric data

Flow cytometry samples were first analysed in the CytoClus4 software (CytoBuoy BV, The Netherlands). Data in this software are stored as raw pulse shapes, corresponding images (if taken) and numerous parameters derived from pulse shape properties in each channel such as Total (sum of pulse shape values), Maximum (largest value in pulse shape), Fill factor (deviation from square profile of pulse shape, an indicator of shape), Number of cells (number of peaks in pulse shape), Length (length of signal at the half of the height) etc. Detailed descriptions of parameters are available in CytoClus4 software. All these parameters allow a construction of many cytograms where different populations of cells, if existing, can be visualised and discriminated. Discrimination of groups (gating) was done on these scatterplots and several groups were found based on their pulse shape properties. Characteristics of groups were determined following standard flow cytometry vocabulary57. Later in text we used more descriptional names in order to emphasize taxonomic composition. In “pico” data we found 3 groups: RedPicoProk (Prochlorococcus like cells deep within noise with weak red fluorescence), OraPicoProk (Synechococcus like cells with strong yellow and orange signals along with red), RedPico (picoeukaryotes with strong red signal, smaller than 2 µm). In “nano” dataset we found more groups: RedNano (nanophytoplankton with red signal only), OraNano (cryptophyte like cells with strong yellow and orange signals, smaller than 20 µm), OraMicro (signal similar to cryptophytes but with cells longer than 20 µm, mainly mixotrophic dinoflagellates, ciliates and filamentous cyanobacteria), RedMicro (cells larger than 20 µm with strong red fluorescence signal, mostly diatoms and dinoflagellates) and HighSWSNano (coccolithophores with high SWS signal). Collection of images in certain “nano” groups were used as additional help in group identification, along with pulse shapes collection After gating, we exported the set statistics of abundances and other pulse shape parameters, which were thereafter analyzed. Phytoplankton abundances (cells L⁻¹) were calculated by multiplying the concentration of events per volume by the mean FWS number of cells, i.e., the mean number of peaks in the FWS pulse shape, which represents the number of cells in a chain for each sample.”

Analysis of images-in-flow

Images from the CytoSense flow cytometer were used to generate taxa presence–absence data for each sample. The relative abundance of taxa was visually estimated based on their proportion in the image collection for each sample. Taxa that appeared particularly dominant in the images were categorized as blooming or high-blooming taxa, extending the standard absent/present classification with additional dominance levels depending on the extent of their visual dominance. To simplify clustering and visualization, only taxa present in more than 80% of samples were retained, as preliminary analyses confirmed that the exclusion of rare taxa did not affect the overall clustering patterns. The resulting community data were hierarchically clustered using R software69 to reveal distinct community structures. These clusters were subsequently used as grouping variables in downstream analyses.

Cell morphology and optical properties of cytometric groups

In addition to particle abundances, several traits were derived from pulse shape parameters. Biovolume concentration was calculated as the sum of total forward scatter (FWS) signals from all particles divided by the analyzed volume, and is expressed in arbitrary FWS units per µL. Chain length was estimated from the mean FWS length parameter, defined as the signal width at half of its maximum height. The number of cells corresponds to the average number of peaks detected in the FWS signal, representing individual cells within a particle. Cell length was calculated by dividing the mean FWS length by the mean number of cells. Finally, asymmetry quantifies the deviation of the FWS signal from symmetry around its center, providing a measure of particle shape irregularity. Sphericity is calculated as 1 - fill factor. The fill factor shows the profile’s shape: it’s 1 if the pulse shape is a square and 0 if all values are zero. We use 1 - fill factor as the lowest value of the fill factor indicates the most non spherical shape58. Cell biovolume is the mean FWS total divided by the mean FWS number of cells. Structural complexity is the ratio between SWS total and FWS total: high ratio is due to more complex subcellular organisation. Cell chlorophyll is the ratio between Fl Red total and FWS total, amount of chlorophyll in cell biovolume. The carotenoid-to-chlorophyll ratio is calculated as the sum of FL Yellow and FL Orange fluorescence divided by FL Red fluorescence. This reflects the presence of accessory pigments, such as carotenoids, in the cells alongside chlorophyll.

Statistical analysis

In order to distinguish potentially different phytoplankton communities we performed hierarchical clustering (“ward.D2” method, R function “hclust”) on the distance matrix derived from estimated abundances of images-in-flow (“bray” method, R function “vegdist” from package “vegan”70). We used the resulting dendrogram from the R package “dendextend”71 to analyze sample groupings. The final clusters were defined by cutting the tree into distinct groups to illustrate their temporal dynamics. Species composition of samples was plotted using “pheatmap” R package72. In order to show associations between samples and different traits or environmental conditions or cytometric functional groups abundances we used a principal component analysis (PCA) on those three datasets. For plotting PCA results we used the “ggfortify” R package73. We showed clusters and aggregate occurrences on PCA plots as well. All data analysis was performed in the R software69.

Results

In situ oceanography and satellite derived data

The first half of the year was characterized by the absence of characteristic northeastern bora wind, while relatively strong southeastern (locally known as Jugo, or Sirocco) or southern winds prevailed (Fig. 2a). Salinity showed typical values, around 38 while sea temperature remained below 10 °C (Fig. 2b). In April salinity decreased down to 35 while a sudden rise of temperature could be recorded as well (Fig. 2b). After that, temperatures decreased again while salinity increased. In late April the temperature was rising again while salinity was steady. In the first half of June temperature suddenly increased up to 25 °C while salinity dropped down below 30. At that moment, there was an abrupt decrease in oxygen concentration. Interestingly, although the oxygen levels later increased, they never returned to their previous values (Fig. 2b). Comparison with Winkler titrated samples showed no sensor drift. Shortly after this, salinity rose again but values stayed around 35. Later in June the temperature slightly decreased and stayed around 25 °C. In July the temperature increased to 28 °C. This increase was also accompanied by a drop in salinity. A similar pattern was observed in early August where salinity decreased further to 30 while temperatures rose almost to 30 °C. This warm period lasted until the first half of September. In mid-September strong winds (> 6 m s− 1) occurred again, after a relatively calm summer with prevailing S SE wind directions. Finally, the bora wind in the middle of September cooled the sea down to around 22 °C (compare Fig. 2a and b). Other optical parameters from the SBE16 probe showed two peaks in backscatter green in February and March. For the rest of the observed period it can be noted that the events of rise in temperature / drop in salinity (freshwater influence) were accompanied with rise in chlorophyll fluorescence, backscatter red and backscatter green. Strongest peaks in optical parameters were in early June and early August (Fig. 2c).

Satellite observations from 2024 revealed complex spatiotemporal variability, with the highest chlorophyll a concentration detected predominantly in the western Adriatic Basin.

Accordingly, imagery with spatial resolutions of 20 m and 10 m from Sentinel-2, as well as 3 m per pixel from PlanetScope, was utilized. Medium resolution Sentinel-2 processed images showing chlorophyll a concentration results are presented in Fig. 3, while a micro-scale spatial analysis at resolutions finer than 100 m was conducted to assess localized distribution patterns. Results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2 to 6.

Medium resolution Sentinel-2 processed images visualised with in-house Coastal Intelligence software (SeaCras, Croatia, https://seacras.com/) showing concentration (upper row) and red edge reflectivity (lower row) on selected dates. Chlorophyll shows areas with high phytoplankton abundance while red edge reflectivity indicates accumulation primarily of polymers and partially phytoplankton.

In most cases, regions with high chlorophyll levels coincided with increased red-edge reflectivity, a proxy for scattering. During summer, long and narrow mucilage fronts were observed, while in September, such features were absent despite similarly high productivity. The mucilage structures formed complex patterns aligned with the circulation dynamics of the basin. Fronts typically developed along the edges of high-productivity zones and migrated eastwards, where they were halted by the Croatian coastline (Fig. 3). Red-edge reflectivity and the relative scattering index, both associated with particle-rich waters, consistently increased during mucilage events. In contrast to buoy derived chlorophyll (Fig. 2c), elevated chlorophyll concentrations were recorded only during the first event. Coloured dissolved organic matter (CDOM) remained relatively stable and did not vary in accordance with mucilage presence, as expected for large-scale mixtures of optical constituents (Supplementary Fig. 7). It must be noted that varying spatial resolutions were necessary to distinguish areas of elevated chlorophyll a concentration from mucilage-rich frontal zones.

Furthermore, our findings reveal that during abrupt mucilage events, the spatial heterogeneity of the aggregates can be on a scale of less than 100 m. This is evidenced by distinct “peaks” in calculated water constituent concentrations at the virtual stations, which differ significantly from the area-averaged concentrations (see Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). This observed sub-mesoscale spatial variability accounts for the signal drift detected by the in-situ chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) probe at the CIM ODAS II buoy.

Phytoplankton abundances measured by cytosense

Phytoplankton abundances measured by CytoSense showed complex dynamics with high variability, but several periods and peaks can be easily observed. In most samples, abundances varied between 1 * 106 and more than 10 * 106 cell L− 1. High abundance microphytoplankton blooms (> 4 * 106 cell L− 1, Supplementary Fig. 8) correspond to main peaks in SBE16 optical parameters after April and to rises in temperature / drop in salinity. In November, phytoplankton abundances were consistently high across all samples (Fig. 4a).

Most samples were dominated by pico sized cells in groups picoeukaryotes, Synechococcus and Prochlorococcus. The nanophytoplankton also showed high relative abundances, especially during summer. After summer, picoeukaryotes and Prochlorococcus dominated in the community. Diatoms and dinoflagellates showed three highest peaks: beginning of June, mid of July and beginning of August. These three peaks in abundances correspond to the appearance of large mucilage aggregates at the sampling site. From October to mid November, contributions of the diatoms / dinoflagellates group were higher than at the end of the summer (Fig. 4b). It is also visible that nano and picoplankton (Synechococcus and picoeukaryotes) also make higher contributions in periods when mucilage aggregates were present (Fig. 4d). Highest contribution of Synechococcus was observed in September (Fig. 4b). However, if relative biovolumes are considered, diatoms / dinoflagellates and nanophytoplankton peaks correspond to samples associated with mucilage (Fig. 4c and d). Taxonomic composition of phytoplankton

Dynamics of species is shown on Fig. 5, together with clusters resulting from hierarchical clustering (Supplementary Fig. 9) of samples and appearance of large mucous aggregates on the sampling site.

In most samples, the so-called “basal community” (Cluster 1, Figs. 4d and 5) was present with low microphytoplankton abundances (Fig. 4b). This community was defined in the base of clustering analysis. The first increase in microphytoplankton abundance was observed in April, and images in flow showed a mixture of Chaetoceros species and other diatoms like Pseudo-nitzschia spp. In June an extraordinarily dense bloom of Cerataulina pelagica developed where this species largely dominated in image-in-flow collection (Fig. 6a). In addition, our unpublished phytoplankton data show that the abundance of this species exceeded 10 million cells per liter. This coincided with the first appearance of large mucous aggregates. In the following days Gonyaulax fragilis, and other large dinoflagellates together with filamentous cyanobacteria were frequently found in samples (Fig. 6b). Alive Gonyaulax fragilis cells were spotted for the first time on date 12.6.2024. A similar community was observed in the second mucilage phase (mid of July), where a mixture of Cylindrotheca closterium, Thalassionema sp. and dinoflagellates was prevalent (Fig. 6c). In early August, mucilages appeared again, with a strong bloom of Thalassionema sp. (Fig. 6d). Until the end of September basal community was present, just like in periods between mucilage episodes. At the end of September a regular autumn phytoplankton bloom developed (Fig. 6e). During autumn, several communities replaced each other, but all of them were grouped together in cluster analysis. The beginning of October was marked by a community, where a mixture of species coexisted, most notably Chaetoceros spp., Pseudo-nitzschia spp. and other diatoms, similar to the April sample. After a few days, a slightly different community developed: Pseudo-nitzschia spp. bloomed with Chaetoceros vixvisibilis and Chaetoceros didymus while coccolithophores like Syracosphaera pulchra and Calciopappus caudatus were prominent. In the mid October coccolithophores decreased while the same diatom species as before stayed dominant. In November Pseudo-nitzschia spp. was still an important part of the community while Chaetoceros socialis and Neomoelleria (Eucampia) cornuta showed up as co-appearing species.

CytoSense traits and influence of environmental variables

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of morphological traits derived from pulse-shape scanning flow cytometry (Fig. 8a) explained 54.95% of the total variance. PC1, which accounted for 35.19% of the variance, clearly separated samples collected during mucilage events from those without mucilage. It was positively associated with cell granulation and length, and negatively associated with chlorophyll-a content, the carotenoid-to-chlorophyll-a ratio, and the number of cells in chain (NoCC). PC2 explained 19.76% of the variance and was positively associated with cell asymmetry, and negatively with sphericity and cell biovolume. Phytoplankton communities linked to mucilage events (clusters 4, 5, and 6) showed traits indicative of thicker, more structurally complex cells (greater granulation), which were more likely to be elongated and asymmetric and had lower pigment content. Long single cells dominated during this period. Interestingly, similar morphological traits were observed in the “basal community” present between mucilage events, suggesting a recurring structural signature. In contrast, multispecies bloom communities (clusters 2, 3, 7, 8, and 9) were characterized by chain-forming colonies, higher pigment content, and a greater prevalence of spherical cells.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of environmental variables (Fig. 8b) explained 74.3% of the total variance. PC1 accounted for 42.88% of the variance and was positively associated with the temperature. PC2 explained 31.42% of the variance and was positively associated with oxygen concentration and salinity while negatively with wind speed. Clustering revealed distinct environmental patterns among sample groups. Clusters 4, 5, and 6, corresponding to samples with aggregates, were generally positioned in the right part of the PCA space, indicating association with higher temperature, and lower wind speed. In contrast, Clusters 2, 3, 7, 8, and 9 were more broadly distributed but tended to occupy central to lower-left regions of the PCA space, overlapping with vectors for higher oxygen concentrations and wind speed.

PCA on the functional group’s abundances (Fig. 8c) accounted for 52.53% of the total variance. PC1 accounted for 28.32%% of the variance and it was negatively associated with abundances of most groups with Synechococcus as an exception. Strongest association with PC1 showed diatoms-dinoflagellates functional group. PC2 accounted for 20.81% of the total variance and was positively associated with Synechococcus, nanophytoplankton, coccolithophores and diatoms-dinoflagellates functional group abundances while negatively with Prochlorococcus, picoeukaryotes, OraMicro and cryptophytes functional group abundances. Mucilage associated samples (clusters 4, 5, and 6) were positively associated with PC2 while multispecies bloom communities (clusters 2, 3, 7, 8, and 9) were negatively associated with PC2 axis, showing clear distinction between those two types of samples.

Discussion

This study provides high-frequency, multi-method observations of phytoplankton dynamics during the 2024 northern Adriatic mucilage phenomenon, integrating daily in situ sampling, imaging flow cytometry, and satellite remote sensing. Our results show that the formation, persistence, and dispersal of large mucilage aggregates were closely linked to intense, often monospecific microphytoplankton blooms and associated environmental anomalies.

Meteorological and oceanographic data showed stronger south-easterly winds in early 2024, followed by reduced summer wind speeds, consistent with previous observations38. The mucilage phenomenon was a single event unfolding in several phases, each marked by a sharp temperature rise and pronounced salinity drop. The most intense phase began in early June, when surface temperatures climbed rapidly and salinity fell to values typical of the Po River mouth, an occurrence unexpected for the eastern Adriatic coast,74, despite the recent trend of rising regional salinity75,76. These anomalies reflected the advection of nutrient-rich, low-salinity Po River plume waters, arriving one to two weeks after peak discharges38. Transport was enabled by strong thermally driven stratification, reinforced by prolonged heat accumulation that kept surface waters unusually warm throughout the year38. Elevated salinity in deeper layers strengthened baroclinic gradients, enabling rapid eastward spreading of plume waters over denser resident waters. Higher turbidity in the plume enhanced surface warming, while calm, clear-sky conditions in late spring and summer reinforced stratification, restricted mixing, and favoured phytoplankton growth and mucus aggregation38. The resulting sequence of freshening–warming phases in June, July, and August coincided with successive appearances of large mucilage aggregates along the eastern Adriatic coast. Calm weather maintained aggregate persistence, whereas strong winds in late summer promoted their dispersion, as in earlier events such as those in 2000 and 200253.

Satellite observations confirmed the spatial extent and persistence of mucilage fronts along the eastern Adriatic, especially during calm conditions from June to August. High- and very-high resolution satellite data revealed that these fronts consistently formed at the boundaries of high-productivity areas and followed eastward trajectories, consistent with in situ salinity patterns. Furthermore, satellite images indicated the presence of closed circulation patterns and laminar flow, which likely helped to concentrate and sustain the mucilage fronts in specific regions. Red-edge reflectivity and scattering indices, derived from satellite measurements, provided clear spatial markers of mucilage presence that aligned closely with the timing and location of in situ anomalies. Phytoplankton and mucilage constituents exhibited highly heterogeneous spatial patterns, with clear differences in spectral signatures observable at sub-100-m scales. On specific dates, ‘macro-scale mucilage’ fronts spanned distances exceeding 20 km. At the height of the event (e.g., 06 June 2024), smaller patches on the order of ~ 20 m in length were likewise detectable and quantifiable.

We could confirm that each different phase of large mucilage aggregates in 2024 coincided with exceptionally intense microphytoplankton blooms, while cyanobacteria did not play a big role as they showed high contribution in all samples. As the RedMicro cytometric group represents diatoms and dinoflagellates57, we recorded peaks in their abundance and biomass at the beginning of almost each phase of mucilage event. Image-in-flow collections confirmed that a main constituent in the RedMicro group were diatoms. Moreover, it appears that each mucilage initiating bloom was largely monospecific. The first large bloom in April 2024 was a mixture of prevailing Chaetoceros species and other diatoms. This bloom did not initiate large mucilage aggregates, possibly due to strong winds which were still prevailing during that time, making this bloom similar to those which will occur later in autumn. However small and translucent aggregates were produced and observed in the water column (personal observation) but did not form into larger and more opaque aggregates with time. In June 2024 an extraordinarily dense bloom of Cerataulina pelagica happened, the strongest in this area after 200077–79. Interestingly, a sharp drop in dissolved oxygen concentrations was observed, suggesting that respiration became dominant in this period, possibly due to large biomass accumulation and degradation. We started with daily sampling on the day the first mucilage occurred in front of Rovinj. Microphytoplankton abundances were comparable to those in late 1980s78, when mucilage aggregates were present52,39. During this bloom first macroaggregates appeared. This was the first almost monospecific bloom in 2024. In the following days, the C. pelagica community was replaced by a dinoflagellate dominated community. C. pelagica is a typical summer species77,80 which usually occurs without aggregates, but it can also be a constituent of mucilage macroaggregates44,45,81. Here we report a similar situation where an exceptionally high bloom of this species marked the beginning of mucilage formation, as described in previous study82. Our previous study showed that years in which this species bloomed and dominated the samples coincided with the presence of mucilage aggregates77.

Here we can propose the idea that the C. pelagica was the species which released a vast amount of organic matter in the seawater, as observed in New Zealand bloom37. While these aggregates aged, different species like Cylindrotheca closterium or dinoflagellates inhabited them. C. closterium is a species which is associated with aged large mucilage aggregates45,46. This species can also produce organic mucilage and it seems that, as a benthic diatom, this species is preadapted to living in aggregates and even plays a role in their self-sustaining44,47. It is also possible that this species prefers mucus because it represents a source of nutrients that this mixotrophic species can utilize83. Gonyaulax fragilis, a dinoflagellate, is another species with similar ecological preferences, and a possible role in formation or sustaining of macroaggregates48,49. They were observed in all mucilage phases, especially in late June, when a new episode of large macroaggregates occurred and when intense bioluminescence was visible in aggregates if they were physically disturbed. Still it remains unclear whether this species promoted the mucilage formation or they inhabit already formed aggregates. In the first case scenario it is possible that we missed a G. fragilis bloom before the first episode of mucilage formation as this species tends to form cysts and sink after the mucilages have aged49. In an earlier described event, G. fragilis almost completely decomposed and diatom cells increased in number and became predominant49. Another frequent and large dinoflagellate we recorded during mucilage episodes was Tripos furca, also a known constituent of aggregates45. In July, we recorded an exceptionally high bloom of C. closterium in aggregates. Here it is possible that aggregates were already aged when they arrived on the eastern Adriatic coast. The last episode of large mucous aggregates was observed at the beginning of August with high blooms of Thalassionema sp. Identification of this species was difficult from CytoSense images since we saw only single cells and no characteristic chains which can help in exact species identification. Since the characteristics of these cells align with both Thalassionema nitzschioides and Thalassionema frauenfeldii, and both species are present in the study area84, we chose to classify them under the single term Thalassionema spp. Nevertheless, we can try to compare this event with those in the past where the species T. nitzschioides was found as an important constituent of mucilage aggregates in the 1997 event44,85.

The end of 2024 summer was characterised by strong winds. In the first half of September the “basal community” now lived in conditions of water column mixing (implied by sharp lowering of sea surface temperatures) and the mucilage phenomenon was definitely terminated. Diverse autumn communities were characterized by Pseudo-nitzschia spp. and other accompanying species: Chaetoceros spp. in early October, Chaetoceros vixvisibilis, Chaetoceros didymus and coccolithophores in following days. In November Chaetoceros socialis and Neomoelleria cornuta were the last blooming species in our study. Chaetoceros spp., Chaetoceros socialis and Pseudo-nitzschia spp. are regular autumn species in the northern Adriatic when mixing of water promotes rich multispecies blooms78,79,86.

In 2024, we found that summer phytoplankton blooms, those associated with mucilage aggregates, showed a tendency towards domination of few species in the community, contrary to autumn blooms where more species were blooming simultaneously and the biodiversity was higher. These findings are consistent with previous observations that the number of taxa decreases during the evolution of mucilage aggregates87, and that such declines are also typical of summer months88. To investigate potential drivers of these seasonal differences, we applied a trait-based analysis to identify possible adaptations of species to different environmental conditions. This analysis revealed a clear separation between autumn and summer communities based on morphological traits, environmental parameters, and functional group abundances. The autumn community was dominated by chain-forming species with high cellular chlorophyll content and elevated accessory pigment ratios. On the other hand, in summer communities large and single, thick cells dominated with low chlorophyll content per cell, but high cellular complexity (Fig. 8a). Cell size is considered a master phytoplankton trait due to its influence on resistance to grazing, sinking rate, nutrient uptake and storage89,90. Our results show that summer communities characterized by stratification¹ and reduced salinity (reflecting freshwater inputs with imbalanced N: P ratios)91, together with episodic nutrient-rich pulses, were dominated by large, elongated cells. Such environmental settings promote species with robust, elongate morphologies that capitalize on transient nutrient availability92 and maintain growth during later nutrient depletion due to superior uptake efficiency. This aligns with our findings, as single-celled C. closterium was present in aggregates, while Thalassionema sp. was mostly single-celled and rarely found in its characteristic chains. Here we can suggest that in the case of Thalassionema sp. an active adaptation to the environment most likely occurred, instead of pre-selection of an existing life form. Moreover, in aggregates were found mostly big cells. Larger cells also can contribute to mucilage formation as they show higher exudation rates90. As there is a possible connection between reduced grazing and accumulation of mucilage52, it is important to emphasize that larger cells are more resistant to grazing93. Some models suggest that both temporal environmental variability and grazing pressure drive the evolution of larger cell sizes in phytoplankton94. An open question is whether mucous aggregations might similarly function as a selective force, potentially substituting for predation in driving increases in cell size. Another interesting trait associated with communities found in mucilages is structural complexity, a ratio between SWS and FWS. This high ratio can be caused either if cells are possessing external processes (which is not the case, as can be seen in the images in flow acquired) or if internal structures of cells are more complex. High temperatures and nutrient limitations can indeed change the structure, functioning and composition of internal membranes95,96. High structural complexity and low cellular chlorophyll also suggest a potential adaptation of photosynthetic apparatus to summer stress conditions97. This aligns well with the idea that larger cells can withstand photoinhibition and fluctuating light conditions, especially in stress inducing environments98. Low cellular chlorophyll content could also be a reaction to low oxygen99 in early June, as indicated by our data.

The autumn community, on the other side, shows different traits which are useful in water mixing conditions. Now long chained colonies of phytoplankton can thrive in nutrient enriched seawater. Chain forming is a useful trait in grazing avoidance, reproduction100, nutrient uptake and enhanced light absorption101,102. As shown in our results, not only chain forming species but also smaller cells prevail in the autumn community as they are better competitors for light and nutrients92,93,103,104. Higher cellular chlorophyll content in our results also suggests an adaptation to autumn lower light conditions104,105.

The first phase of the mucilage phenomenon, in terms of phytoplankton traits, appears to differ from subsequent phases. In particular, the bloom of C. pelagica shares key traits with autumn communities, which are often dominated by competitive generalist species. We hypothesize that the C. pelagica bloom likely coincided with the onset of strong stratification, which then persisted through the rest of the summer, whereas other blooms developed under already stratified conditions. A similar pattern was observed during the 2004 mucilage event, which followed an exceptionally high bloom of C. pelagica triggered by stratification82. Moreover, years when C. pelagica dominates summer communities often coincide with the appearance of large mucilage aggregates77. Although the hypothesis that C. pelagica plays a role in mucilage formation is new, it is supported by recurring observations: in both 2004 and in our current dataset, mucilaginous aggregates developed immediately following dense blooms of this species82 The persistent temporal coupling of C. pelagica blooms with mucilage events, coupled with evidence of its ability to produce extracellular mucus elsewhere39 provides a compelling mechanistic rationale for its involvement. Given that C. pelagica is also a regular component of summer phytoplankton communities in the absence of mucilage, and our sampling began after aggregates had already formed, it is still unclear whether its presence contributes to aggregate formation or primarily reflects colonization of pre-existing aggregates.

Once the phenomenon is triggered, these aggregates become colonized by more specialized species that are well-adapted to the mucilage environment, high temperatures and already stratified waters. When pulses of nutrient-rich freshwater occur, robust species with large, elongated cells can thrive92. These species face minimal competition and are protected from sinking and predation within the mucilage, enabling them to reach high abundances quickly. In autumn, the long period of water mixing and nutrient availability favors more competitive, generalist species, leading to a multispecies bloom. Given the potential role of C. pelagica in initiating mucilage formation, further research into its exopolymer production, physiology and ecology is necessary, as it could serve as an early indicator for the occurrence of large mucilage aggregates. We can conclude that general models for phytoplankton succession and trait distribution were applicable even during a massive phenomenon of mucous aggregate formation in the water column.

We could demonstrate that the microphytoplankton biomass and abundance increased during advances of the mucilage toward the eastern Adriatic coast. Other important groups were nanophytoplankton and coccolithophores. Here we confirmed that nanoplankton is also an important constituent of mucilage communities as shown in previous research5, but their role in the whole phenomenon remains to be elucidated.

Among cyanobacteria, Synechococcus showed positive association with mucilage aggregates as well. The role of picocyanobacteria has already been investigated, and it has been shown that picoplankton abundances are usually higher in mucilage associated communities5,45,106. Nevertheless, here we showed that in terms of biovolume microphytoplankton dominated the community, as expected87. Still, the role of autotrophic and heterotrophic bacteria and other picoplankton remains to be elucidated. We recorded a remarkable drop of oxygen concentration in surface water during the beginning of the phenomenon which opens the question about the role of respiration in this process. Increased respiration should, in theory, lead to lower sugar concentrations107. However, the substantial release of organic matter could also result from the degradation of dead cells. Alternatively, mucilage may not be the primary energy source for respiration. Thus, heterotrophic organisms should be included in high frequency monitoring of this phenomenon in the future. Oxygen concentrations remained reduced during and after the mucillage phenomenon for the rest of the year. This observation might indicate increased importance of respiration during and after the event.

Salinity in the surface waters remained reduced until the end of 2024, indicating the continued influence of riverine waters in the northern Adriatic and a possible reduction in the influx of high-salinity waters from the southern Adriatic, which had been important in maintaining the phenomenon38, ultimately leading to its termination.

It is important to emphasize that this phenomenon was observed throughout the northern Adriatic, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions from monitoring at a single location. One major open question is whether this phenomenon represented a single large event with distinct phases and whether similar mechanisms operated across the entire area. It should also be noted that massive events can occur without the formation of extensive surface-floating aggregates. Although satellite observations are extremely useful, they are not sufficient to assess the true extent of large aggregate formation occurring below the surface. In addition, monitoring phytoplankton dynamics only in surface waters (0–1 m depth) provides an incomplete picture of the processes involved, and this limitation requires critical discussion. Therefore, further research on the evolution of the phenomenon, covering both surface and subsurface layers, is necessary to fully understand the underlying mechanisms across all relevant spatial and temporal scales.
