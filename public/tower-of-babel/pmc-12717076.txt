The effect of hepatic steatosis index on prediabetes: A large-scale retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaohang Huang, Haohan Zhai, Xintao Zhang, Xiaohua Pan, Peng Xiao, Qiu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) is a well-established metric for evaluating hepatic fat accumulation. Although prior studies have examined the effect of HSI on diabetes, its association with prediabetes remains incompletely delineated. This study aims to elucidate the effect of HSI on prediabetes within a Chinese population. We conducted a multicenter cohort analysis involving 75,750 baseline normoglycemic adults from the Rich Healthcare Group in China. To assess the effect of HSI on prediabetes, this study employed Cox proportional hazards regression, restricted cubic spline models, subgroups, and sensitivity analyses. Multivariable-adjusted models identified HSI as an independent predictor of prediabetes (HR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04–1.07 for females, HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05 for males). A nonlinear relationship between HSI and prediabetes risk was observed in both sexes, with an inflection point at 36.48 for females and 31.27 for males. Among females, a significant positive link with prediabetes emerged below the threshold of 36.48 (HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.06–1.10, P < 0.0001), while above this level the association was attenuated and failed to reach statistical significance (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.02, P = 0.7579). In contrast, hazard ratios for males were 1.02 (95% CI: 1.00-1.04) below and 1.04 (95% CI: 1.03–1.05) above their respective inflection point. Our analysis revealed a non-linear positive correlation between HSI and prediabetes across both sexes. Precise assessment of HSI facilitates early identification of subjects at heightened risk, thereby enabling the implementation of targeted preventive strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27888-9.

Introduction

The increasing incidence of prediabetes (Pre-DM), an important forerunner of diabetes, constitutes a substantial public health challenge. This metabolic condition is defined by blood glucose levels that exceed normal parameters but remain below the threshold for a diabetes diagnosis1. In China, the prevalence of prediabetes reached approximately 35.2% in 2017, translating to an estimated 357 million adults being affected2. Thus, the identification of dependable predictive factors for prediabetes is vital to support timely intervention efforts, which may help prevent progression to overt diabetes.

Hepatic steatosis, more commonly termed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), has drawn increasing attention due to its links with metabolic abnormalities, such as insulin resistance and diabetes3,4. Among the various diagnostic approaches, the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) stands out as a reliable, noninvasive metric for detecting liver fat accumulation; it utilizes indicators like alanine aminotransferase (ALT), body mass index (BMI), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)5,6. Although substantial evidence has established the association of HSI with the presence of diabetes7,8, its connection to prediabetes—especially among Chinese adults—remains less clear and warrants further investigation. To address this knowledge gap, the present study seeks to clarify the effect of HSI on prediabetes in a Chinese cohort.

Methods

Data source

The data applied in the research were sourced from the DATADRYAD repository, an openly accessible digital platform that hosts a broad spectrum of datasets for scientific investigation9. In line with Dryad’s usage policies, these data are available for secondary analysis to explore new scientific questions. As the initial project had secured ethical clearance from the Rich Healthcare Group Review Board, no further approval was necessary for the secondary evaluation performed here. Additionally, all research procedures were consistent with the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and conducted in accordance with all relevant regulatory and institutional requirements.

Study population

All medical information from Chinese people (n = 685,227) who were 20 years of age or older and who had examinations at Rich Healthcare Group between 2010 and 2016 was gathered for this dataset, which comprises 32 sites across 11 cities. Participants were excluded if they had missing baseline data on ALT, AST, weight, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), height, or gender; exhibited extreme BMI values (< 15 or > 55 kg/m²); had a follow-up interval of less than two years; had a prior diagnosis of diabetes mellitus or a baseline FPG level exceeding 5.6 mmol/L; had uncertain diabetes mellitus status, developed diabetes, or recorded an FPG level greater than 6.9 mmol/L during follow-up; or had HSI outliers. Data collection

All data were collected by trained staff within the medical assessment center. Information gathered comprised physical measurements (including height and weight), lifestyle habits (alcohol consumption and smoking), demographic characteristics (such as age and sex), blood pressure, as well as family history of diabetes. After participants completed at least an 8-hour fast, venous blood samples were drawn for laboratory evaluation. Biochemical parameters—including high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), FPG, serum creatinine (Scr), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), ALT, triglycerides (TG), AST, and total cholesterol (TC)—were assessed utilizing the Beckman 5800 analyzer under rigorously controlled laboratory conditions. Dyslipidemia was defined as any of the following: TC > 5.2 mmol/L, TG > 1.7 mmol/L, LDL-C > 3.12 mmol/L, or HDL-C < 1.03 mmol/L10. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg11. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was derived using the CKD-EPI equation adapted for individuals of Asian ancestry12. The computation incorporates age (years), sex, and serum creatinine (Scr, mg/dL) and is applied piecewise as follows:

Scr > 0.9 mg/dL in males,

eGFR = 149 × (Scr/0.9)–1.210 × 0.993age;

Scr ≤ 0.9 mg/dL in males,

eGFR = 149 × (Scr/0.9)–0.415 × 0.993age;

Scr > 0.7 mg/dL in females,

eGFR = 151 × (Scr/0.7)–1.210 × 0.993age;

Scr ≤ 0.7 mg/dL in females,

eGFR = 151 × (Scr/0.7)–0.328 × 0.993age;

HSI

The HSI was calculated using sex-specific formulas, whereby females were assigned HSI = 2 + BMI + 8× (ALT/AST), while the equation for males was HSI = BMI + 8 × (ALT/AST), as previously reported5.

Outcome measures

Pre-DM was defined by fasting plasma glucose concentrations falling between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L13.

Missing data processing

Missing information was observed among participants for several key variables: SBP (n = 7, 0.009%), DBP (n = 8, 0.011%), LDL-C (n = 33,525, 44.273%), HDL-C (n = 33,859, 44.691%), triglycerides (TG; n = 1,003, 1.323%), TC (n = 1,003, 1.323%), Scr (n = 2,981, 3.936%), BUN (n = 5,992, 7.910%), drinking status (n = 57,538, 75.966%) and smoking status (n = 57,538, 75.966%). To address these incomplete covariates, multiple imputation techniques were utilized. The variables included in the imputation model were family history of diabetes, age, sex, SBP, TC, LDL-C, drinking status, Scr, BUN, DBP, TG, BMI, smoking status, HDL-C, and FPG. The approach employed to address missing data was predicated on the assumption that the missingness mechanism was random (missing at random)14,15.

Statistical analysis

Subjects were categorized into quartiles based on the HSI levels. Normally distributed data were analyzed using the ANOVA test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and the chi-square test was used. Skewed distribution data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and expressed as median and interquartile range. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were employed to evaluate the effect of HSI on prediabetes. No covariates were excluded in the final multivariable Cox regression analysis because of substantial collinearity with additional covariates (Table S1). To improve the stability of the effect of HSI on prediabetes, this investigation performed sensitivity analyses by excluding subjects with hypertension and dyslipidemia. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to test potential nonlinear relationships between HSI and prediabetes. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to examine the interaction effects of confounding categorical variables. All results were reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. Empower-Stats software was used for all statistical analyses, and the significance threshold was set at a two-tailed P value of less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline features of the study population. In total, 75,750 eligible adults were analyzed, with a mean age of 43.71 ± 8.90 years. Among these, 41,917 (55.34%) were men. Compared to Subjects in the lower quartile (Q1), participants in the higher quartiles (Q2–Q4) tended to be male, older, and demonstrated increased levels of TG, FPG, AST, BMI, Scr, LDL-C, blood pressure, ALT, BUN, and TC. Conversely, higher HSI quartiles were characterized by lower HDL-C and eGFR and by higher prevalences of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and family history of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia compared with the lowest quartile.

The incidence rate of prediabetes

As detailed in Table 2, over a median observation period of 3.15 years, 9,859 individuals—representing 13.02% of the study cohort—developed prediabetes. The cumulative incidence per 1,000 person-years was 41.32 for the entire sample, while rates within the respective HSI quartiles were 23.12 in Q1, 33.76 in Q2, 46.92 in Q3, and 61.57 in Q4. Corresponding proportions of prediabetes onset were 13.02% overall, with quartile-specific rates of 7.33%, 10.67%, 14.73%, and 19.33% from Q1 to Q4. Notably, individuals classified in higher HSI categories demonstrated a marked increase in the risk of transitioning to prediabetes.

The effect of HSI on prediabetes

The effect of HSI on prediabetes was evaluated through multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and the results are presented in Table 3. HSI levels were strongly linked to an elevated risk of prediabetes, with an HR of 1.07 (95% CI: 1.07–1.07, P < 0.0001) in the unadjusted model (Model 1). After accounting for smoking status, age, gender, family history of diabetes, hypertension, drinking status, and BMI (Model 2), the effect of HSI on prediabetes remained significant, with an HR of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.03–1.05, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which further accounted for additional covariates, including BUN, dyslipidemia, eGFR, and FPG, the association persisted, with an HR of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.04–1.05, P < 0.001). The observed associations persisted following stratification by sex.

The results of the sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses enhanced the stability of the effect of HSI on prediabetes (Table 4). After excluding adults with hypertension (Model 4) or dyslipidemia (Model 5), HSI remained significantly associated with prediabetes risk. In adults without hypertension, the HR for HSI was 1.04 (95% CI: 1.03–1.05). Similarly, the significant effect of HSI on prediabetes was observed in participants without dyslipidemia (HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05).

Nonlinear correlation

Table 5; Fig. 3 display a nonlinear association between HSI and prediabetes. Using a recursive estimation approach, inflection points were identified at 36.48 for females and 31.27 for males. In addition, for females, HSI values below 36.48 were significantly associated with increased risk of prediabetes (HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.06–1.10, P < 0.0001), while no statistically significant association was observed above this threshold (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.02, P = 0.7579).

Discussion

This retrospective study demonstrated a significant effect of HSI on prediabetes. Additionally, a nonlinear relationship between HSI and prediabetes was observed in both males and females. Of particular note, the link between elevated HSI and prediabetes was especially pronounced in individuals with a BMI below 25 kg/m².

The HSI, recognized as a reliable surrogate for hepatic lipid accumulation, is widely utilized in the screening of MASLD5. Beyond its established role in identifying hepatic steatosis, accumulating evidence indicates that HSI is associated with a broader spectrum of metabolic and vascular conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and carotid atherosclerosis7,8,16–18. For instance, a retrospective cohort study of 22,025 Chinese adults demonstrated a significant effect of HSI on diabetes (HR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.41–1.89)7. Supporting this, an independent analysis of 14,443 subjects reported that each standard deviation increase in HSI translated to an 81% higher risk of incident diabetes (HR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.61–2.03)8. A cross-sectional assessment comprising 768 individuals further revealed a significant effect of HSI on carotid atherosclerosis16. HSI was independently linked to chronic kidney disease in another extensive cross-sectional study among 94,893 Japanese participants17. Additional investigations have established a linear association between HSI and the occurrence of both cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in the general population18. In our study, each incremental rise in HSI corresponded to a 4% increased risk of prediabetes. Notably, this relationship persisted in sensitivity analyses conducted among subgroups without hypertension and dyslipidemia. Collectively, these findings underscore the consistent association between HSI and prediabetes risk, suggesting that interventions aimed at lowering HSI may help mitigate the likelihood of developing prediabetes.

This investigation additionally delineated a curvilinear relationship between HSI and prediabetes in both sexes. Specifically, in males, when HSI was less than 31.27, each incremental unit corresponded to a 2% increase in prediabetes risk, whereas above 31.27, the risk rose by 4% per unit increment. In females, each unit increase in HSI below the inflection point of 36.48 was associated with an 8% elevation in prediabetes risk; above this threshold, the association was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that elevated HSI may function as a biomarker for increased susceptibility to prediabetes, highlighting the importance of implementing preventative lifestyle changes at an early stage to improve long-term metabolic health.

The precise biological mechanisms underlying the effect of HSI on prediabetes are not yet fully understood. However, several plausible pathways have been proposed, suggesting that hepatic steatosis contributes to the development of insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism. Firstly, excessive lipid deposition within the liver has been shown to interfere with insulin signaling cascades, resulting in diminished cellular glucose uptake alongside elevated hepatic glucose production19. Secondly, the heightened presence of inflammatory cytokines typically observed in hepatic steatosis can further impair insulin responsiveness, thereby promoting systemic insulin resistance20–22. Finally, the surplus accumulation of lipids in hepatocytes can induce oxidative stress through the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, which in turn disrupts insulin receptor substrate pathways21,23.

Our study presents several significant advantages. Most notably, we revealed a nonlinear association between HSI and prediabetes. In addition, the robustness of this association was demonstrated by its persistence after adjusting for a comprehensive set of established confounders. The research also benefits from its foundation in a large, multicenter cohort of the Chinese population, ensuring substantial and representative data. Lastly, a series of extensive sensitivity analyses further reinforced the methodological rigor and the credibility of our results.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, since our findings are based on a Chinese cohort, their generalizability to other populations and ethnic groups remains uncertain and warrants further investigation. Second, the absence of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test in the prediabetes diagnosis may have resulted in underdiagnosis of new cases. We are planning prospective research that will incorporate multiple diagnostic parameters, including FPG, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test results, and HbA1c measurements to provide a more robust evaluation of the relationship between HSI and prediabetes. Finally, the original dataset did not include waist circumference or information on antihypertensive and lipid‑lowering therapies; these variables can influence both hepatic metabolism and glucose homeostasis, potentially confounding the observed associations. In future work, we will collect a more comprehensive set of metabolic and clinical variables—including waist circumference and detailed data on antihypertensive and lipid‑lowering treatments—to further assess the robustness of the association between HSI and prediabetes.

Conclusion

Our findings establish a significant association between HSI and prediabetes, characterized by a nonlinear relationship. These results suggest that effective control of HSI levels may be essential for lowering the risk of developing prediabetes.
