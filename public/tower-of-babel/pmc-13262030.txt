Short Peptoid Capping Ligands Stabilize Dispersion of Gold Nanospheres in Aqueous Solutions
Authors: Hanna G. Goldberg, Isabella K. Matusich, Jack M. Petersen, Nikhila Raman, Joseph C. Hong, Gabriela Edler, Daniel K. Tanaka, Jwwad M. Javed, Amelia A. Fuller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface functionalization of colloidal gold nanoparticles with capping ligands is critical for controlling their dispersive stability and for introducing application-specific moieties. Peptoids (N-substituted glycine oligomers) are useful candidates for study as capping ligands owing to their straightforward preparation and their biocompatibility. In this work, we have prepared a library of five-residue peptoids and evaluated them as capping ligands for 10 nm gold nanospheres. Our findings revealed important sequence features of peptoids that stabilize nanosphere colloidal dispersions in a range of aqueous conditions that vary in ionic strength and pH. We have also characterized the sizes and surface charges of peptoid-capped nanospheres and made direct comparisons between peptide- and peptoid-capped nanospheres. Lastly, we observed that nanospheres capped with one of the peptoids studied here inhibit the enzyme chymotrypsin.

Introduction

Colloidal gold nanoparticles have been employed in wide-ranging studies in nanomedicine, biomolecular sensing, and catalysis. − In these applications, capping ligands are used to coat the gold nanoparticle surface in a densely packed layer to mediate interactions between the nanoparticle and its aqueous environment. As such, they decorate the gold surface with functionality that directs its application. ,, For example, gold nanospheres and nanoclusters that inhibit the enzymatic activity of chymotrypsin have been studied, , and different capping ligands have different effects on the enzyme kinetics and reversibility of the enzyme function. − Additionally, capping ligand structures can influence adsorbed proteins’ structure. When introduced during nanoparticle synthesis, capping ligands affect the nanoparticles’ morphology. − Moreover, the size of the gold nanoparticles, including their assembly and/or their colloidal dispersion, − can also be affected by capping ligand identity. Importantly, gold nanoparticle optical properties vary with their size and morphology; this relationship underpins many gold nanoparticle sensor designs. New capping ligands that accommodate varied functionality and that stabilize gold nanoparticle colloids, including those developed in this work, have the potential to expand the applications of this useful material.

The evaluation of combinatorial collections of capping ligands is an efficient approach to identify surface-modifying molecules that confer desired colloidal properties or functions to gold nanoparticles. For example, peptide capping ligands that stabilize colloidal gold nanospheres at high ionic strength have been identified from synthetic libraries. , Additionally, gold nanoparticles capped with peptides and with amino acid-functionalized capping ligands have been surveyed to characterize and compared their effects on enzyme activity and protein structure. − ,,, These studies yielded useful sequence-function relationships that guide the design of new capping ligands. Access to other capping ligand scaffolds that can be prepared as diverse libraries is needed to expand our understanding of the relationships between capping ligand structure and nanoparticle properties.

A common modular design has been applied in capping ligands previously studied in varied aqueous applications of gold nanoparticles (Figure ). These capping ligands include short peptides such as CALNN (cysteine-alanine-leucine-asparagine-asparagine) or other pentameric peptides, amino acid-derivatized alkylthiols, , and peptoids (N-substituted glycine oligomers). Each scaffold includes a thiol or a thioether to anchor the ligand to the nanoparticle surface through a strong Au–S bond. Ligands with this design can undergo a ligand exchange (also called a place exchange) reaction whereby an excess of thiol-containing capping ligands of interest displace the surface ligands installed during nanoparticle synthesis, typically, citrate or other alkylthiols (Figure B). The hydrophobic portion of the capping ligand extends away from the nanoparticle surface and may allow for interligand packing to form a “core” domain that shields the nanoparticle from the bulk solvent and its solutes. ,− Lastly, the hydrophilic domain on the ligand solubilizes the particles in aqueous solution. Frequently, one or more charged functional groups are included in this domain, allowing repulsion of charged particles. Alternatively, uncharged ethylene glycol repeats, including poly­(ethylene glycol) polymers, have also been widely used to solubilize particles in aqueous solutions. Such uncharged capping ligands stabilize colloidal dispersions through repulsive, steric interparticle interactions.

Peptoids have many advantageous features for further study as capping ligands. Monodisperse, sequence-specific peptoids of varied length can be readily prepared via the submonomer method. − This synthetic approach enables a modular, combinatorial design of varied molecules for study. ,, Moreover, peptoids’ potential molecular diversity is exceptionally high because the variable N-substituents are introduced via primary amines, thousands of which are commercially or synthetically available. Beyond the peptoid residues, other moieties can be appended to peptoids using bioconjugation strategies. Thus, peptoid capping ligands may be customized for specific applications, analogous to derivatization of CALNN peptide capping ligands for targeted biological functions. ,− Additionally, low energetic barriers to amide bond isomerism and the lack of backbone H-bonding give peptoids a conformational flexibility that may allow peptoid capping ligands to pack tightly to shield the nanoparticle surface from solutes. ,,, Lastly, peptoids are not degraded by proteolytic enzymes nor do they elicit immune responses; both of these qualities are attractive for application in aqueous biological systems.

Only a few examples of peptoid capping ligands for gold nanoparticles have been reported. Robinson et al. reported that 5 and 10 nm gold nanospheres remained dispersed in high salt concentrations when coated with 10-residue peptoid capping ligands. The capping ligand design used followed the general modular design (Figure A). In a few other cases, peptoid capping ligands for gold nanospheres with different designs have been successful in stabilizing aqueous nanosphere colloidal suspensions, including an 18-residue brush polymer-like design that employs multivalent attachment to the nanoparticle surface and nitroxide-terminated peptoids that exhibit bivalent binding to gold nanospheres. Most studies explored peptoid capping ligands with at least seven residues; the study of short peptoid capping ligandshere, pentamersis underdeveloped. Shorter peptoids offer the advantage of being more cost-effective to prepare. A limited number of studies employ peptoids as capping ligands introduced during in the synthesis of novel gold or silver nanoparticle structures. ,,− From these, we have learned that peptoid sequence and peptoid structural flexibility can influence nanoparticle shape , and assembly. − Lastly, gold nanospheres have been arrayed in peptoid nanosheets. − Given the relatively small number of examples, our systematic understanding of the peptoid capping ligand features that regulate gold nanoparticle colloidal stability is incomplete.

In this work, we report a systematic screen of a library of peptoid pentamers to identify new capping ligands for gold nanosphere colloidal stabilization and surface functionalization. Peptoid capping ligands identified stabilize colloidal dispersions of 10 nm gold nanospheres under a range of aqueous conditions, including salt concentrations as high as 1.5 M NaCl. We have characterized sizes, surface charges, and colloidal stability over time of peptoid-coated nanoparticles, and we have compared peptoid- and CALNN peptide-coated nanoparticles. Analogs of peptoid capping ligands identified in our screen have been prepared and examined to validate important peptoid sequence features that modulate nanoparticle colloidal stability. Lastly, we report that chymotrypsin adsorbs onto peptoid-capped nanospheres, and its enzymatic activity is reduced in the presence of peptoid-capped nanospheres. These studies expand the library of custom capping ligands available for further derivatization and application, including as potential modulators of enzymatic activity.

Experimental Section

Materials

Materials for peptoid and peptide synthesis (solvents, resins, synthesis reagents) were purchased from commercial sources and used without further purification. Citrate-coated 10 nm diameter gold nanospheres were purchased from Nanocomposix as solutions of ∼6.8 nM in 2 mM aqueous sodium citrate (exact concentration varies by lot) and used without dilution, as detailed below.

Peptoid and Peptide Synthesis and Purification

Peptoid synthesis, purification, and identification by mass spectrometry were accomplished following well-reported procedures. Synthesis modifications implemented to install specific residues and data demonstrating purity (Figure S1) and identity (Table S2) of compounds 1b, 1g, 4b, 4g, 5d, 5g, 11–13 are also provided in the Supporting Information

Survey of Library (1a–j···10a–j) Peptoid-Nanoparticle Interactions by UV–Visible Spectroscopy

Following purification of library peptoids, a stock solution of each peptoid was prepared by dissolving purified peptoids in 500 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock with a concentration of approximately 1 mM. For each peptoid, three wells of a 96-well clear acrylic plate (Corning, UV transparent bottom, 360 μL maximum volume per well) were each loaded with 200 μL dispersion of citrate-coated 10 nm gold nanospheres. To each well was added 13.2 μL of peptoid stock solution, and the ligand exchange reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for an hour. If no agglomeration was observed (i.e., color remained red), additional wells were loaded with nanospheres and peptoid for triplicate analysis of these mixtures at increasing salt concentrations. To each peptoid-nanoparticle suspension was added 8 μL of 0.5 M phosphate, pH 7, along with the appropriate volume of 5 M NaCl to a final concentration of 500 mM NaCl. UV–visible spectra of the mixtures in the wells were then immediately collected on a Molecular Devices SpectraMax i3x instrument. Spectra were collected from 450 nm −700 at 2 nm increments. The three triplicate spectra for each peptoid-nanoparticle-NaCl mixture were averaged. For solutions that remained red with 500 mM NaCl, additional peptoid-nanoparticle wells were prepared to evaluate the agglomeration or dispersion of peptoid-coated nanoparticles at 1 and 1.5 M NaCl, and UV–visible spectra were recorded. Figure S2 includes UV–visible spectra for all of the peptoids surveyed at varied NaCl concentrations.

Preparation of Stocks for Peptoids 1b, 1g, 4b, 4g, 5d, 5g, 11–15, and CALNN Peptide

After being purified and lyophilized, peptoid stock solutions were made by weighing approximately 2.5–3.0 mg of peptoid into a small glass vial and dissolving it in 2 mL of nanopure water. Peptoid stock solutions were sonicated for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution. Peptide CALNN stocks were prepared similarly, using DMSO as the solvent. Stock concentrations were determined via a colorimetric assay using 5,5-dithio-bis­(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (Ellman’s reagent).

Removal of Excess Peptoid or Peptide from Ligand-Coated Nanoparticles and Resuspension in Water

A volume of peptoid or peptide stock was combined with 600 μL 10 nm citrate-coated gold nanospheres to achieve a final concentration of 50–100 μM peptoid or peptide, and the solution was equilibrated for 1 h at room temperature. The peptoid/peptide-coated nanoparticles were then transferred to an Amicon Ultra centrifugation filter (100 kDa MWCO) and centrifuged for 1 h at 4500 rpm at 20 °C. The peptoid- or peptide-coated nanospheres were then resuspended in 600 μL nanopure water. This resuspension in nanopure water and centrifugation was repeated two times. Following the third centrifugation, the peptoid- or peptide-coated nanospheres were suspended in 600 μL of nanopure water, transferred to a microcentrifuge tube, and sonicated for 10 min prior to use in experiments (UV–visible spectroscopy, TEM).

Peptoid Concentration Effects on Nanosphere Dispersion

Into each of three wells on a clear acrylic 96-well plate, 200 μL 10 nm citrate-coated gold nanospheres and a volume of peptoid stock to achieve a final concentration of 1 μM, 2.5 μM, or 5 μM were added. After the mixture equilibrated for 1 h, 8 μL 0.5 M Tris buffer, pH 8, and 5 M NaCl were added to each well to give 1 M NaCl. UV–visible spectra were recorded, and the triplicate measurements were averaged as detailed above.

Dynamic Light Scattering and Surface Charge Measurement

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and surface charge measurements of sonicated peptoid- or peptide-coated nanoparticles were done on a Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern Panalytical). Solutions were prepared by adding 1000 μL of the nanosphere suspension to a glass vial, followed by the addition of the peptoid capping ligand to achieve a final peptoid concentration of 10 μM. The displacement reaction proceeded for at least 4 h. Then, an aliquot of 1 M NaCl stock solution was added to reach a final NaCl concentration of 10 mM, and the mixtures were equilibrated for at least 15 min and up to 24 h. The solutions were then transferred to disposable polystyrene cuvettes for DLS analysis using backscatter mode. Three measurements were taken and averaged. Zeta potential values (ζ) were subsequently measured by transferring the sample to a folded capillary zeta-cell. Three ζ measurements were taken for each sample and averaged.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Nanoparticles coated with peptoids 1b and 5g were prepared as detailed above, excess peptoid was removed, and nanoparticles were resuspended in nanopure water. A droplet of the peptoid-AuNP suspension was placed on a continuous carbon grid until the water evaporated. Any excess liquid was removed carefully by manually blotting with filter paper. The grid was then washed with deionized water once more, and blotted again. Images were collected by Covalent Metrology (Sunnyvale, CA) on a ThermoFisher Scientific Talos F200X (S)­TEM instrument. Particle sizes were measured using ImageJ software, and the size distribution of particles was tabulated in a histogram to determine the average particle size (Figure S7).

Concentration of Peptoid-Capped Nanospheres for Experiments with Proteins

Following the displacement reaction to exchange 1b for citrate capping ligands in a microcentrifuge tube, 0.5 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7 was added to a final concentration of 5 mM. The 1b-capped nanospheres suspension was subjected to centrifugation (1 h at 12,000 rcf) to pellet the 1b-capped nanospheres. Removal of the clear, colorless supernatant, then resuspension of the pellet in 5 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7 provided a more concentrated colloidal suspension for other experiments.

Evaluation of Protein Binding to Peptoid-Coated Nanospheres

α-Chymotrypsin (ChT) from bovine pancreas (Sigma-Aldrich) and bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) stocks were created by dissolving lyophilized enzyme in 5 mM sodium phosphate buffer. Solution concentrations were measured from the UV absorbance at 280 nm using molar extinction coefficient (ChT: ε280 = 5.1 × 104 M–1 cm–1; BSA: ε280 = 43,824 M–1 cm–1).

Into microcentrifuge tubes containing 90 μL of 51 nM 1b-capped nanospheres in 5 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7, was added a volume of BSA or ChT stock to achieve a final protein concentration of 1 μM. In parallel, a control was prepared to account for protein–protein aggregation and nonspecific protein adsorption onto the tube surface, in which 90 μL of 5 mM sodium phosphate buffer was substituted for the nanoparticle suspension and combined with the same amount of protein (1 μM). Both solutions were equilibrated at room temperature for approximately 17 h, then centrifuged at 12,000 rcf for 1 h at room temperature. From the clear, colorless supernatant, 80 μL was removed, and protein concentration was quantified using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay (Pierce). The experiments were collected in triplicate, then averaged. The difference between the protein concentration measured in the tubes containing nanospheres and those without nanospheres was calculated to determine how much protein was retained in the pellet of nanospheres.

Chymotrypsin Enzyme Activity Measurement

ChT activity assays were adapted from Fischer et al. A 200 μL solution of 400 nM ChT or 400 nM ChT + 40 nM 1b-cappped nanospheres in 5 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7 with 50 μ M EDTA was added into wells of a 96-well plate. Each solution was added to three separate wells so that experiments were run in triplicate. The solutions were equilibrated at room temperature for 2 h. To each well was added 11.1 μL of 300 μM N-benzoyl-l-tyrosine p-nitroanilide (BTpNA, ChT substrate) in DMSO at time points: 0, 5.6, 11.25, 22.5, 45, 90, 180, 360, and 1440 min until the final concentration of BTpNA was 100 μM. The temperature was kept constant at 25 °C throughout the assay. At each time point, the absorbance at 405 nm was monitored using a Spectramax M5 plate reader, sampling every 15 s for 5 min. Absorbance at each time point was averaged for the replicate samples and was corrected by subtracting out the absorbance of a solution of the same concentration of BTpNA or BTpNA/1b-capped nanospheres without ChT. The corrected absorbance for the ChT only samples after the final time point was normalized to an activity of 1. The histogram in Figure shows the normalized activity collected at the final time point.

Results and Discussion

We elected to prepare and evaluate peptoid capping ligands for this study that were functionalized to allow displacement of citrate ligands on gold nanosphere surfaces. This experiment design paralleled earlier studies of peptoid capping ligands , and an early combinatorial exploration of peptide capping ligands for gold nanospheres. Further, it allowed us to use water-soluble, commercially available, well-characterized nanospheres with narrow size distributions and uniform morphology. Gold nanospheres with a diameter of 10 nm were chosen for study because they were similar in size to particles evaluated in combinatorial screening of short peptides for their colloidal stabilization of 12 nm19 and 16 nm20 gold nanospheres. Peptoid capping ligands for 10 nm gold nanospheres have also been studied, and we sought comparisons to these systems. , Stabilizing larger gold nanospheres with peptides and peptoids introduced via ligand exchange has been a challenge, although longer peptides and peptoids have been successfully used with larger nanospheres.

We created a modular design for a 100-member peptoid pentamer library (Figure A) that would allow us to survey peptoids that may stabilize 10 nm gold nanospheres. The capping ligand design was derived from the CALNN peptide structure. As a capping ligand, CALNN stabilizes colloidal dispersions of 12 nm gold nanospheres in aqueous solutions with high ionic strength (up to ca. 500 mM sodium chloride). Although longer peptide capping ligands also conferred colloidal stability at high ionic strength as well, the pentamer oligomer length offered a large combinatorial space to explore. Similarly short peptoid capping ligands for nanoparticles have been only rarely reported. Nonetheless, shorter peptoids offer advantages of being easier and more cost-effective to synthesize. Since the inception of our study, additional pentameric peptides have been reported that also stabilize gold nanosphere colloids, further validating the utility of short sequences for these uses. Lastly, comparisons between similarly designed peptide and peptoid capping ligands have not been reported.

Inspired by the CALNN template, the N-terminal residue for all peptoids in our library was N-(2-mercaptoethyl)­glycine to provide the thiol group for binding to the gold surface. Analogous to the N-terminal cysteine of CALNN, the proximal amine was hypothesized to assist the thiol attachment to the gold in the ligand exchange: at neutral pH, the positively charged ammonium has electrostatic affinity for the negatively charged particle. The central two residues were varied to include mostly aliphatic, hydrophobic residues, analogous to the A-L sequence in CALNN. Because it was challenging to anticipate what influence these residues might have on colloidal stability, other neutral and amine-functionalized hydrophilic residues were also included in the library. Notably, peptoid residues in the library bear moieties that are not found in amino acids, including cycloalkanes, ethers, and tertiary amines, highlighting the functional diversity of this scaffold. The two C-terminal residues of our pentamer peptoids were N-(carboxymethyl)­glycine. These residues conferred water solubility and an overall net negative charge to the peptoid in the region we predicted would project into the bulk solvent. The C-terminal residue of the peptoids, bearing both the carboxylic acid and primary carboxamide groups, also mimicked the functionality of the C-terminal asparagine residue in CALNN.

Peptoids were prepared on solid support following the submonomer synthesis approach. Consecutive reactions were executed to bromoacetylate, then to install specific side chains via an amine displacement reaction. To minimize competing intramolecular cyclization reactions, residues bearing basic side chains were chloroacetylated rather than bromoacetylated, following procedures previously reported in the literature. , Peptoid pentamers were cleaved from the resin and side chain protecting groups were removed under strongly acidic conditions. Subsequent purification by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) and identification of peptoids by mass spectrometry allowed isolation of 93 of the 100 compounds in sufficient purity for further evaluation.

Peptoids were evaluated for their ability to stabilize 10 nm gold nanospheres in aqueous solutions with sodium chloride salt concentrations as high as 1.5 M (Table ). The addition of salt to nanoparticle dispersions has been a common way to evaluate colloidal stability and to compare capping ligands. ,,, Higher salt concentrations were expected to screen the repulsive charge–charge interactions between nanoparticles that contribute to their dispersion. Excess peptoid capping ligands were added to citrate-coated particles to effect a ligand exchange reaction, and visual and spectral observations were employed in parallel to judge particle agglomeration (Figure S2 shows UV–visible spectral data of peptoid-nanoparticles at varied salt concentrations). Visually, red suspensions were recorded as nonagglomerating, and blue or purple colors were recorded as agglomerated. The 32 peptoid-nanoparticle solutions that did not induce immediate agglomeration (i.e., were red) were titrated with NaCl up to 1.5 M, and the UV–visible spectra were recorded. Seventeen peptoids were found to resist nanoparticle agglomeration at least up to 500 mM NaCl. Of these, eight peptoid-nanoparticle solutions were still dispersed at the highest salt concentrations we tested, 1.5 M NaCl.

A number of trends from this data set revealed that colloidal dispersion is highly sensitive to sequence, despite that this region of the peptoid was not hypothesized to anchor it to the nanosphere surface nor mediate interactions with the aqueous solution. Both the identity and the ordering of the residues contributed to the stability of peptoid-coated nanosphere dispersions. For example, isomeric analogs 1b and 2d differ only in the order of the R 1 and R 2 residues, but only 1b stabilized the nanosphere dispersion in solution. We noted that installing a N-methyl glycine residue in position R 2 yielded ligands that promoted nanosphere agglomeration. Similarly, sequencing of residues was found to be important for colloidal stabilization by pentameric peptide capping ligands. , Nearly all of the peptoids that included amine-containing groups at R 1 or R 2 induced nanoparticle agglomeration at the ligand exchange step. We posited that the positively charged residues enabled agglomeration at this step via interparticle electrostatic interactions with negatively charged, citrate-coated nanospheres. This observation is consistent with a recent report that some pentamer peptide capping ligands comprising basic residues (lysine, arginine, histidine) promote nanoparticle agglomeration. To generate a stable dispersion of nanospheres capped with positively charged peptoid, we expect that displacement of a different surface ligand (e.g., a short alkyl thiol) would be required; this approach has been successful in making positively charged nanoparticles with other capping ligand scaffolds.

Six peptoids1b, 1g, 4b, 4g, 5d, and 5gwere selected for further study, and common structural features were scrutinized (Figure B). Initial structure–function trends emerged. We recognized that 1b is a very close analog of the CALNN peptide; this was not surprising given that the library design was based on CALNN. More broadly, many peptoids that resisted agglomeration included branched, aliphatic side chains at the third residue: (N-(3-methylbutyl)­glycine, N-(cyclohexyl)­glycine, or N-(1-methylethyl)­glycine). In most cases, smaller residues were present at the second residue (N-methylglycine and N-(2-methoxyethyl)­glycine), similar to the CALNN structure. Peptoid 5d was unique in this series in that the second residue, N-(3-methylbutyl)­glycine, was larger than the third, N-(1-methylethyl)­glycine.

Nanospheres were coated in each of the six selected peptoid ligands (1b, 1g, 4b, 4g, 5d, and 5g), isolated from excess peptoid, and characterized by UV–visible spectroscopy. Spectral features were compared to those observed for nanospheres coated with citrate or CALNN peptide (Figure ). The wavelength and intensity of the plasmon peak are sensitive to size, particle core composition, and surface coating. There was a red-shift of the surface plasmon band from 518 to 524 nm observed upon exchanging the citrate coating for either 1b or CALNN, confirming the ligand exchange. Gratifyingly, the spectra were similar for solutions of peptoid-coated nanospheres in water and those observed during the library survey, the latter of which contained an excess of the capping ligands (Figure S3). This confirmed that free peptoid in solution did not affect the spectral properties of the colloidal suspensions. Adding 1.5 M NaCl to nanospheres coated with 1b or the other selected peptoids caused no change in the spectral features (Figures C and S3). However, spectra of CALNN-coated nanospheres showed evidence of agglomeration in 1.5 M NaCl, as previously reported. The extinction intensity at λmax was diminished and there is a concomitant increase in intensity at longer wavelengths. The agglomeration factors were determined as the ratio of extinction intensities at 614 nm, where aggregates absorb, and 524 nm, the plasmon band. These values were the same within error for all peptoid-coated nanospheres studied, but the agglomeration factor was higher for the CALNN-coated particles (Figure D). The results of these experiments suggested that peptoid capping ligands conferred slightly better colloidal stability of the nanospheres than the peptide under higher salt conditions.

The stability of peptoid-coated nanosphere suspensions under other conditions was also evaluated by UV–visible spectroscopy (Figure ), and we noted that nanospheres coated with all of six selected capping ligands had similar properties to one another and to the CALNN peptide-coated particles. First, the stability over time of peptoid-coated nanospheres suspended in water at room temperature was evaluated, and some modest spectral changes were observed. After 1 week, the spectrum of 1b-coated nanospheres was slightly less intense at the 524 nm surface plasmon band, and the peak was broadened (Figure A). Spectral changes were similar for 1b-coated nanospheres stored at 4 °C over 1 week (data not shown). Measurement of the hydrodynamic diameter of the week-old 1b-coated nanospheres by dynamic light scattering showed no change within error to the size or polydispersity (data not shown). These changes were consistent with a low level of agglomeration over 1 week. Nanospheres coated with other peptoids exhibited an 8–12% loss of signal intensity as well as a 1–3 nm blue shift of the surface plasmon band after 1 week (Figure S4). The impacts of changing the solution pH were evaluated for peptoid-coated nanospheres as well. For peptoid- or CALNN peptide-coated nanospheres, some spectral intensity loss and peak broadening were observed at acidic pH (Figure S6), consistent with protonating the carboxylate-functionalized residues that likely contribute to particle dispersion through charge–repulsive interactions. ,

We carried out a functional evaluation to estimate the surface coverage of the peptoids on the nanospheres, expecting that incomplete surface coverage of the nanosphere would promote agglomeration in the presence of NaCl. We identified and compared the minimal concentration of each of our selected peptoids introduced during the ligand exchange reaction that allowed nanospheres to remain colloidally disperse in 1 M NaCl (Figure B). CALNN surface coverage was reported to be 1.8 ligands/nm , Using the manufacturer-reported values for nanosphere concentration (0.053 mg/mL) and nanosphere surface area (28.2 m2/g), we calculated that 4.5 μM CALNN would be required to coat these particles. Nanospheres coated with 1b remain disperse when 5 μM of 1b was added in the exchange reaction, but agglomerate when lower concentrations of the peptoid capping ligand were used. Therefore, we estimated the functional surface coverage of the 1b on the nanosphere surface to be close to the 1.8 ligands/nm reported for CALNN. Notably, this is a lower ligand surface density than was estimated for longer peptoid capping ligands previously reported (5 ligands/nm). Using other peptoids for the ligand exchange reaction, all agglomerated at 1 M NaCl when 1 μM peptoid was introduced. Some remained disperse when 2.5 μM peptoid was used for the ligand exchange, suggesting that they may have a slightly less dense functional surface coverage than 1b (Figure S5).

The sizes of nanospheres coated with peptoids or with CALNN were evaluated and compared. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) of peptoid- or peptide-coated nanospheres was used to measure the hydrodynamic diameters of all peptoid-coated and CALNN-coated nanospheres (Table ). The particle sizes were in a narrow range from 15.16 ± 0.08 to 16.53 ± 0.05 nm. Consistent with the presence of larger surface ligands, these sizes were slightly larger than the hydrodynamic diameter for the citrate-coated particles (14 nm), as reported by the manufacturer (Nanocomposix). Polydispersities of the peptoid and peptide-coated nanospheres were generally low (0.14–0.23), indicating that relatively uniform sizes were measured. Small populations of larger particle sizes were observed for nanospheres coated with three of the peptoids, 1g, 4g, and 5d. We posited that these were reversible aggregates; this interpretation is consistent with the UV spectral data, and reversible aggregates have also been reported for other peptoid-coated gold nanospheres. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of nanospheres coated with either of two representative peptoids, 1b or 5g, were obtained (Figure ). These images revealed no evidence of particle decomposition by the new surface ligand. Nanosphere sizes measured by TEM are 11.2 ± 0.95 nm for 1b-coated particles and 11.1 ± 1.7 nm for 5g-coated particles (Figure S7). These sizes were comparable to the commercial citrate-coated nanospheres, reported by the manufacturer to have a size of 10.9 ± 0.8 nm.

Electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) was used to measure surface charges (ζ-potential) of the peptide- and peptoid-coated nanospheres. These measurements ranged from −29.28 ± 0.64 to −33.04 ± 2.14 mV, compared to −38 mV reported by the manufacturer for the commercial citrate-coated particles (Table ). Given the strongly negative surface charge, we speculated that electrostatic interactions dominated the interparticle repulsive interactions, as predicted by the ligand design. At high concentrations of NaCl, we expect that charges are at least partially screened. Because the nanospheres do not agglomerate, some steric interparticle repulsive interactions may also contribute to particle dispersion. This observation is consistent with stable dispersions of nanospheres coated in longer, anionic peptoids at high ionic strength. Taken together with the UV–visible spectroscopy results, the size and surface charge measurements supported that peptoids are robust surface ligands that stabilize aqueous colloidal dispersions.

To explore the contributions of peptoid sequence features to their capping ligand properties, we designed and prepared a series of four analogs of 1b (Figure ). Two of these, peptoids 11 and 13, deviated from the modular design criteria articulated for the library by changing residue identities or ordering, but peptoid 12 preserved the same general features of the library. First, we prepared a ligand that substituted the N-terminal residue functionality from a thiol in 1b to an alcohol (peptoid 11). We expected that this substitution of an alcohol-containing residue in 11 for the thiol-containing residue in 1b would obviate its ability to form strong interactions with the nanoparticle, compromising its colloidal stability. Peptoid 12 substituted one of the N-(carboxymethyl)­glycine residues with nonionizable, polar N-(2-hydroxyethyl)­glycine. This substitution adjusted the peptoid’s predicted net charge at neutral pH, but preserved the thiol, core, and hydrophobic residue ordering that were common to 1b and other library peptoids. We reasoned that this analog would allow us to interrogate how many charged residues were required to stabilize aqueous suspensions of peptoid-coated nanospheres. Another analog that was isomeric with 1b, peptoid 13, was prepared to explore the importance of residue ordering in the capping ligand design.

Structures of 1b analogs prepared. Highlighted residues emphasize the modifications introduced.

Nanospheres coated with peptoids 11–13 were studied to evaluate the impacts of structure modifications on the dispersion and size of peptoid-coated nanoparticles. UV–visible spectra of the nanospheres coated in peptoids 11–13 were observed at NaCl concentrations of 0 and 1 M (Figure ). Interestingly, despite the absence of a thiol, peptoid 11 coated nanoparticles, and the dispersion was stable in buffer without added NaCl. By contrast, nanospheres coated in peptoid 13, which does contain the thiol group, agglomerated immediately. As NaCl was added, the nanospheres coated with 11 agglomerated. From these results, we speculated that the two carboxylate-containing residues allowed 11 to interact with the surface of the nanospheres in a manner similar to the citrate interaction with nanosphere surfaces. Like the citrate-coated nanospheres, added salt screened the surface interaction of 11 with the nanospheres. We reasoned that adding a thiol group and/or separating the carboxylate-containing residues in the sequence (as in 13) prevented this surface interaction. Together, the results from 11- and 13-coated nanospheres underscored the importance of both the thiol group and the specific ordering of residues in designing peptoid capping ligands that stabilize nanosphere dispersions at high salt concentrations.

UV–visible spectra of gold nanospheres coated with peptoids 11, 12, and 13 at 0 M and/or 1 M NaCl.

Nanospheres coated with peptoid 12 did not agglomerate even at NaCl concentrations as high as 1 M, and these dispersions were relatively stable over 1 week, similar to 1b-coated nanoparticles (see Supporting Information). Dynamic light scattering experiments revealed that nanospheres coated with 12 were slightly larger in size (hydrodynamic diameter 17.67 ± 0.08 nm) compared to 1b-coated nanospheres (15.54 ± 0.04 nm), and a narrow size distribution (PDI 0.10) was measured (Table ). The surface charge measurement of 12-coated nanospheres (−13.75 ± 1.19 mV) was lower than what was observed for the other peptoid-coated nanospheres studied (approximately −30 mV), consistent with the more positive net charge expected for 12 relative to the other peptoids measured. The experimental data for 12-coated nanospheres suggested that the peptoid capping ligand needs only one carboxylate group to provide the charge–charge repulsion needed for disperse nanospheres. Moreover, their dispersion stability at high ionic strength is consistent with the hypothesis that steric interparticle interactions also contribute to the colloidal stability of peptoid-coated nanospheres. Taken together, these analog studies reinforce the importance of our modular ligand design. Within this design, we expect that additional residue changes might be introduced without disrupting colloidal stability; this will enable future design of custom-functionalized peptoid capping ligands for specific applications of gold nanospheres.

To demonstrate the potential utility of peptoid-capped nanospheres to modulate enzyme activity, we evaluated the interactions of 1b-capped nanospheres with the protease enzyme chymotrypsin (ChT). Rotello and co-workers first reported that ChT activity could be inhibited upon binding to surfaces of 2 nm gold nanoparticles. Work since has demonstrated that the identity of the capping ligand can influence the thermodynamics and kinetics of inhibition as well as the reversibility of enzymatic function. − Nonetheless, studies to date have employed gold nanoparticles smaller than those studied here, and peptoid-capped gold nanospheres have not yet been evaluated as modulators of ChT. We hypothesized that the 1b-coated nanospheres would interact with ChT, but not with bovine serum albumin (BSA). This expectation was grounded in the difference in isoelectric points (pI) for the two proteins: ChT has a pI of 8.75, giving it a net positive charge that could interact with the negatively charged peptoid-capped nanospheres, whereas BSA has a pI of 4.7. To evaluate this, we equilibrated both proteins with 1b-nanospheres, then separated the nanospheres from the solution using centrifugation, and measured the change in solution concentration of protein. We observed no reduction in BSA concentration, consistent with our hypothesis that there is not a strong interaction between BSA and 1b-capped nanospheres. In contrast, 242 ± 2 nM of ChT was found to adsorb to 51 nM 1b-capped nanoparticles. This experiment provided preliminary evidence that peptoid-capped nanospheres may allow some degree of selective binding to proteins, presumably through electrostatic interactions.

To explore the interaction between 1b-capped nanospheres and ChT further, we measured the enzyme activity and evaluated the structure of ChT in the presence and absence of 1b-capped nanospheres (Figure ). Enzyme activity of ChT was measured spectroscopically by monitoring the formation of p-nitroaniline from the ChT-catalyzed hydrolysis of N-benzoyl-l-tyrosine p-nitroanilide (BTpNA). When 1b-capped nanospheres were added to ChT in a 1:10 nanosphere/ChT ratio, the enzyme activity was reduced by more than half. This inhibition is similar to that observed by Rotello and co-workers in their original report of ChT inhibition by 2 nm gold nanoparticles bearing a negatively charged capping ligand. Ongoing work will prioritize elucidating the mechanism of inhibition by peptoid-capped nanospheres, including how peptoid capping ligand structure may influence protein structure and enzyme activity.

Conclusions

The studies reported here reveal that pentameric peptoids are suitable surface ligands for aqueous dispersions of gold nanospheres. Peptoid-coated nanospheres are colloidally stable even at salt concentrations as high as 1.5 M NaCl, improving on the colloidal stability previously reported for the structurally similar peptide CALNN. Sequence-function studies have revealed that both the identity and the ordering of the R 1 and R 2 residues contribute to the ability of peptoid capping ligands to stabilize nanosphere dispersions. Excitingly, these are some of the shortest peptoid capping ligands reported, making their synthesis faster and more cost-effective than longer sequences. The successful stabilization of dispersions of nanospheres capped with a peptoid bearing only one carboxylate (12) supported the notion that many more peptoid capping ligands are yet to be discovered. Ongoing work in our laboratory will elaborate on how further changes to the modular peptoid capping ligand design, including changes to its length, changes to the hydrophilic groups, and changes to the surface-anchoring residue, alter its ability to stabilize gold nanospheres. Systematic studies of additional peptoid capping ligand analogs will enable deeper understanding of capping ligand sequence effects on nanosphere dispersion. Moreover, further exploration of how different peptoid capping ligands may modulate the selective binding of proteins and the activity of ChT or other enzymes will clarify structure–function relationships in these systems. We expect that future work will customize peptoid capping ligands to tailor them for a range of aqueous applications.
