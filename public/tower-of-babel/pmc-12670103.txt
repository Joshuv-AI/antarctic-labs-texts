Differential Interaction of High Microvascular Resistance With Coronary Flow Reserve Across Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction Endotypes
Authors: Ahmet Tas, Yaren Alan, Peter Damman, Tim P van de Hoef, Ilke Kara Tas, Elif Gül Türkmenoğlu, Niels van Royen, Divaka Perera, Sabahattin Umman, Kim H Parker, Jan J Piek, Murat Sezer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn contrast to reduced coronary flow reserve (CFR), increased minimal hyperemic microvascular resistance (hMR) lacks prognostic relevance in stable chronic coronary syndromes.ObjectivesThe aim of the study is to investigate the local and systemic hemodynamics, including diastolic rate constant (s−1) and arterial reservoir function (reservoir-excess pressure [Pr-Pxs] parameters), as they relate to coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD; CFR <2.5) and hMR.MethodsFunctional CMD (CFR <2.5; hMR <2.5, nvessel = 82, npatient = 77), structural CMD (CFR <2.5; hMR ≥2.5, nvessel = 160, npatient = 149), no CMD-low hMR (CFR ≥2.5; hMR <2.5, nvessel = 44, npatient = 42), and no CMD-high hMR(CFR ≥2.5; hMR ≥2.5, nvessel = 26, npatient = 26) endotypes were compared using generalized linear mixed models adjusting for the multiple coronary arteries within same patients.ResultsCMD endotypes were associated with a higher diastolic rate constant (s−1; functional-CMD: 2.0 [1.7-2.2], structural-CMD: 1.9 [1.7-2.1], no CMD-low hMR: 1.5 [1.3-1.7]; no CMD-high hMR 1.5 [1.4-1.6]; P = 0.006) and lower Pr/Pxs (functional CMD: 18.8 [17.6-20.1]; structural CMD: 19.6 [18.5-20.8]; no CMD-low hMR: 21.9 [20.1-23.8]; no CMD-high hMR: 23.8 [22.4-25.2], P < 0.001), suggesting a systemic relative hyperemic state, with lower vasotonus and arterial compliance irrespective of high hMR. Despite similar hMR, No CMD-high hMR endotype distinctively had the lowest Pxs (mm Hg) (4.0 [3.4-4.6]), whereas structural CMD (5.1 [4.8-5.4]) had the highest Pxs (P < 0.001). In no CMD, a milder increase in hMR accompanied higher basal microvascular resistance than CMD (β = 0.128 [0.106-0.149], P < 0.001; β = 0.167 [0.155-0.180], P < 0.001; P = 0.002). Higher hMR was associated with lower CFR in the CMD cases (β = −0.057 [−0.082 to −0.033], P < 0.001), but not in the no CMD group (β = 0.066 [0.013-0.118] P = 0.014).ConclusionsIn this hypothesis-generating study, patients with high hMR-normal CFR exhibit favorable characteristics of coronary and aortic hemodynamics and reservoir function.Key words: ANOCA, arterial reservoir pressure analysis, coronary microvascular dysfunction, Windkessel

Background

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is the leading cause of myocardial ischemia in the absence of hemodynamically significant obstructive epicardial coronary artery disease (CAD),1,2 referring to functionally and/or structurally diseased coronary microcirculation leading to reversible perfusion defects in the subtended myocardium.3 It can be verified by magnetic resonance imaging, myocardial perfusion imaging, or intracoronary electrocardiography.4 Current consensus-based diagnosis of CMD necessitates the proof of blunted vasodilatory capacity (coronary flow reserve [CFR]), CFVR (coronary flow velocity reserve), myocardial perfusion reserve and/or increased minimal microvascular resistance (index of microcirculatory resistance) or hyperemic microvascular resistance (hMR).5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11

While the impairment in these flow capacity or resistance-based indices categorically correlates with perfusion defects or cardiovascular deterioration,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 some critical discrepancies remain unsolved. In patients with stable CAD and ANOCA (angina with the absence of flow-limiting CAD), the prognostic value of CFR is proven. Yet there is a lack of clarity regarding the individual prognostic impact of increased hMR in chronic coronary syndromes.12,18,19 In contrast to the association between high hMR and adverse outcomes in acute coronary syndromes (ACS),20,21 high hMR with normal CFR seems to be associated with good long-term prognosis in patients with stable chronic coronary syndrome in the ILIAS (Inclusive Invasive Physiological Assessment in Angina Syndromes Registry) and DEFINE-FLOW (Combined Pressure and Flow Measurements to Guide Treatment of Coronary Stenoses) studies.18,19 In accordance with this clinical finding, it has also been reported that high hMR is not intrinsically associated with cardiovascular risk factors,22 endothelial dysfunction,16 or impairment of coronary wave energetics responsible for hyperemic acceleration.23 Therefore, in stable CAD and/or ANOCA patients, a straightforward attribution of increased microvascular resistance to the impairment of microarchitecture of capillary bed may not be correct.22 Nevertheless, these results, derived from the long-term clinical follow-up data, need to be justified based on an exploratory hemodynamic analysis.

Based on these observations and hemodynamical principles, we hypothesized that high hMR in the presence of a normal CFR might primarily be the reflection of increased basal microvascular resistance (bMR) to counterbalance the inflammation-induced widespread capillary overflow and relative hyperperfusion, which is a major factor in CMD,3,24 particularly in functional CMD with increased resting flow. In this framework, we aimed to interrogate the relevance of high hMR and its relationship with CFR in CMD and no CMD cases and describe local and systemic hemodynamic characteristics of patients with high hMR in the presence of normal CFR. To study this, we examined invasive coronary physiology parameters in conjunction with pressure/volume buffering capacity (reservoir behavior) of elastic arteries, assessed by reservoir-excess pressure analysis25 that modulates the flow by cushioning the pressure excess, preventing sudden overflow and pressure burst and ensuring a temporally better distributed flow (Figure 1).

Method

Study population

This study was conducted on the multicenter DEFINE-FLOW data,26 containing de-identified raw pressure and flow velocity signals, core laboratory, and on-site measurements for physiological measures (CFR, fractional flow reserve [FFR], instantaneous wave-free ratio [iFR], Pd/Pa, and so on), angiographic, as well as clinical and laboratory data from 455 patients with stable CAD enrolled in 12 centers in 6 countries in Europe and Japan. The detailed original study protocol including the catheter laboratory protocol and inclusion and exclusion criteria has been published in detail elsewhere.27 The present study has included the core laboratory-approved measurements excluding the recurrent measurements for the same vessels (according to the numerical order of measurement in the recurrent cases before the signal analysis) and the vessels with flow limiting CAD identified as FFR ≤0.80. The final number was 312 unique vessels from 258 patients. Figure 1 demonstrates the flow chart and the averaged hemodynamic signal analysis with extraction of reservoir and excess pressure.

Signal analysis

Simultaneously obtained raw pressure and Doppler derived flow velocity signals (ComboWire XT, Philips Volcano) were imported into MATLAB (v R2021b, The MathWorks). Sampling frequencies in the original data were 100 and 200 Hz for Doppler and pressure signals, respectively. The signals were filtered with a second-order Savitzky-Golay filter with a window length of 11 samples. The hyperemic and resting time windows were automatically selected in MATLAB following maximum mean changes and approved via visual inspection for the quality of envelopes and correctness of the beginning of the hyperemic stimulus. Then, pressure and flow (velocity) waveforms in the selected time-windows were ensemble-averaged to a single representative cardiac cycle. Time window selection was made with an attempt to take the widest frame with adequate signal quality when available but using no less than 8 cardiac cycles.

Measures and definition of CMD

The CFR and hMR were automatically calculated from the resting and hyperemic time windows, which are used to extract reservoir-excess pressure parameters. Mean baseline (bMR) and hyperemic (hMR) microvascular resistances were calculated per their definitions (bMR = mean Pd basal/bAPV (baseline average peak velocity) and hMR = mean Pd hyperemic/hAPV (hyperemic average peak velocity)) and (CFR = hAPV/bAPV). Vasodilator capacity of coronary microvasculature was evaluated with Doppler-derived CFR with adenosine administration as previously described. For CFR, the cutoff value of 2.5 was used to identify CMD. In the categorical analysis for hMR, 2.5 mmHg s.cm−1 was used.15,16 Vessels with CFR ≥2.5 + hMR <2.5 mmHg s.cm−1 were labelled as no CMD-low hMR (reference group). Those with CFR <2.5 + hMR <2.5 mmHg s.cm−1 were classified to have functional CMD, whereas blunted flow reserve with high resistance (CFR <2.5 + hMR ≥2.5 mmHg s.cm−1) is defined as structural CMD. Vessels with normal CFR with high hMR (CFR ≥2.5 + hMR ≥2.5 mmHg s.cm−1) were labelled as “no CMD-high hMR.”

Reservoir-Excess pressure analysis

During ventricular systole, the generated pressure (and ejected volume) does not directly and fully propagate forward but, to some extent, gets temporarily stored proximally via expansion of arterial walls (Figure 1). Historically, the extent of the pressure/volume stored can be calculated via Windkessel models. Recently, a clinically tested analysis with technical nuances named “arterial reservoir-excess pressure analysis” has been developed. Reservoir and excess pressure are the components of arterial pressure which are derived from its waveform morphology. Reservoir pressure is a Windkessel-like estimation of pressure-buffering capacity of elastic arteries, serving as a cushion to prevent sudden pressure burst and overflow. Arterial reservoir pressure analysis visualizes and quantifies the stored (reservoir) and excess subfractions of arterial pressure wave (Figure 1). The reservoir pressure (Pr) curve displays the fraction of the arterial pressure that is buffered by the compliance of elastic arteries, whereas the excess pressure (Pxs) curve displays the pressure exceeding the reservoir pressure throughout the cardiac cycle.28,29 Mean or integral of the Pr and Pxs (or IntPr and IntPxs) are the widely used quantitative parameters with reported pathophysiological and clinical value. Experimentally, we included Pr/Pxs and IntPr/IntPxs ratios.30,31 The arterial reservoir capacity depends on the compliance and stiffness of the arterial wall and is dynamically modified by the microvascular resistance/vascular tonus.25,32,33 The analysis quantifies the diastolic pressure decay rate (diastolic rate constant [DRC]). A faster DRC indicates attenuated cumulative peripheral resistance and lower arterial compliance in favor of a faster discharge of the reservoir pressure, whereas slower DRC indicates increased vasomotor tonus and higher compliance.28,29 Theoretical basis and rationale of the technique is well established and can be found elsewhere.29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34

The reservoir-excess pressure analysis was performed on the averaged resting pressure waveforms (Figure 1). The detailed mathematical explanation can be found elsewhere.32,33 The analysis was performed using the open-source code35 for MATLAB of Alun D Hughes and Kim H Parker, which has been tested in several prior studies including a CAFE (Conduit Artery Functional Evaluation) substudy of the ASCOT (Anglo-Scandinavian Cardiac Outcomes Trial) by Davies et al.36 After the extraction of ensemble reservoir pressure waveform, no further transformation via the diastolic pressure subtraction was made (Figure 1). Excess pressure was calculated by subtraction of reservoir pressure from ensemble aortic and coronary pressure waveforms. Considering its known proportionality with arterial resistance and compliance, the DRC (inverse of the time constant (tau) of the diastolic decay) has been calculated to investigate the changes in vasomotor response.29 We also report asymptotic pressure (P∞) and the zero-flow pressure (Pzf) calculated via the slope of pressure-velocity loops during the mid-diastole.

While the physiological relevance of reservoir parameters derived from the aortic pressure waveform is well established, with the Pxs being virtually analogues to the flow in proximal aorta, due to the distinct diastole predominant flow in coronary arteries, the meaning of Pr and Pxs are not straightforward. This study includes the Pr-Pxs derived from Pa as main parameters. Based on the partial local modification of pressure waveform in coronary arteries, we experimentally included the same Pr-Pxs parameters derived from the coronary pressure waveforms as well.

Statistical Analysis

To prevent bias and ensure standardization, whenever multiple recordings for the same vessel were included in the data base, the first recording with adequate signal quality (core-lab approved) was used. For each analysis, inaccessible missing data are reported alongside the remaining sample sizes, and pairwise deletion was performed. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or mean [95% CI]. Normality of variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk’s test. Due to the nested structure of the data (multiple coronary arteries per patient) violating the assumption of independence, the analyses were performed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM). These models were built with a gamma distribution and log-link function, acknowledging the nonlinear, skewed nature of the data and the observed violation of normality (as per the Shapiro-Wilk's test). Patient ID was incorporated as a random effect to account for within-patient correlation, and subsequent post-hoc tests were conducted. For comparison of mean values, estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals were used, and estimates with 95% confidence intervals for various confounders were compared across models. Included confounders are provided among related results.

All data were blindly analyzed offline using the built-in modules of the R-based JAMOVI statistical software (The Jamovi Project). Illustrations were made using MATLAB (v R2021b, The MathWorks) and JAMOVI (The Jamovi Project).

Confounders in GLMM for CFR as outcome

While assessing the relationship between hMR and CFR, sex;37,38 age;38 smoking;39 diabetes;24 hypertension;40 chronic renal disease;41 dyslipidemia;42 resting heart rate;43 use of calcium channel blockers,44 beta-blockers,45 renin angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors,46 or statins;46 the use of nitrates,42 ranolazine,47 ivabradine,48 nicorandil,49 and alpha-blockers50 diuretics; and body mass index were initially considered as clinical confounders. Of these, those resulting in model nonconvergence were excluded (eg, hypertension, with multicollinearity with antihypertensive drugs preventing model convergence). Variance inflation factor >5 was considered high.

Ethical review

Each local site and data-management center received approval from its applicable institutional review board (18 December 2014, METC AMC), and all subjects provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02328820). The present study was performed retrospectively on the open-access de-identified dataset; hence, additional review was not sought.

Results

The study population mainly included elderly people with male predominance and frequent cardiovascular risk factors (Table 1). In this nonobstructed coronary arteries study, the mean FFR was 0.89 ± 0.05 in the studied vessels, without significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.267). A total of 77.6% (n = 242) of the vessels had CFR <2.5. Of these vessels with CMD, 160 vessels (51.3% of all included vessels) were classified as structural CMD, and 82 vessels (26.3% of all included vessels) were classified as functional CMD included and respectively. Of those with no CMD, a total of 44 vessels had normal hMR, whereas 26 vessels had high hMR despite normal CFR (no CMD-high hMR endotype) (Figure 1).

Hemodynamic characteristics of CMD and no CMD endotypes

CMD had two major endotypes, which mutually had abnormal CFR due to different mechanisms. First, functional CMD was characterized by a relative hyperemic state at rest with lower bMR and higher baseline velocity (Tables 3 and 4). Second, structural CMD was characterized by a lower mean hyperemic velocity (hAPV) due to high minimal hMR (Tables 3 and 4). These two groups had similarly lower CFR values than reference vessels (Tables 3 and 4). Moreover, the two CMD endotypes mutually had lower bMR-to-hMR ratio (resistive reserve ratio: RRR = bMR/hMR) than non-CMD groups (Tables 3, 4, and Figure 2).

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively described the characteristics of the no CMD-high hMR endotype in terms of clinical features and local and systemic hemodynamics including arterial reservoir function. We demonstrated the differential relationship between high hMR and CFR, such that the increased hMR is not associated with lower values of CFR in the no CMD cases. Together with more pronounced arterial reservoir behavior, this may explain the good prognosis of patients with a normal CFR and high hMR. The main findings are as follows (Central Illustration).

1)Functional and structural CMD have a worse profile of local and systemic hemodynamics compared to no CMD endotypes regardless of high or low hMR, and they mutually exhibit worse Pr/Pxs and steeper diastolic pressure decay rates (DRC) at aortic waveforms, denoting an ongoing systemic hyperperfusion and overflow state with lower arterial compliance, based on close relationship between Pxs and aortic flow.28,29

Local and Systemic Hemodynamics in Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction and No-Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction Endotypes With High and Low hMR

Independent use of high hMR for the diagnosis of CMD should be avoided, as it may mirror a stronger increment in bMR favoring a preserved CFR, without impairment of hyperemic response. High hMR – preserved CFR endotype exhibits favorable coronary and aortic hemodynamics.

Coronary microvascular resistance in acute and chronic coronary syndromes

The hMR, despite its established place in the CMD/ANOCA guidelines,5,11 had no prognostic significance in the global studies, including ILIAS registry and DEFINE-FLOW study.18,19,51,52 The observations regarding the adverse prognostic impact of high hMR or index of microcirculatory resistance mainly come from the post-percutaneous coronary intervention or acute coronary syndrome settings,21 where the increase of hMR is a result of intraluminal obstruction (distal thromboembolism, intraluminal plugging), extravascular compression (edema and hemorrhage), or vasospasm due to released vasoactive substances.53,54 All these factors can cumulatively contribute to an acute and mechanistic increase in the minimal microvascular resistance54,55 which tend to attenuate during long-term follow-up.56

Functional CMD appears to develop due to components of metabolic syndrome, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, or other causes of chronic systemic low-grade inflammation (including inflammatory and autoimmune disease) leading to a chronic hyperemic state, which induces submaximal vasodilation at rest. This hyperemic state induced by chronic low-grade inflammation is characterized by an increased NOS activity and NO levels in the circulation3 and affects different vascular beds including the heart,24 kidneys,57 and retina,58 causing diminished bMR resulting in a high resting flow rate. This hyperperfusion may persist for a longer time without being counteracted by a sufficient autoregulatory response, as occurs in diabetes mellitus, and may lead to local changes in microarchitecture of coronary bed including perivascular fibrosis and capillary rarefaction resulting in structural CMD.24 This transmission was demonstrated by Sezer et al. (2016), where patients with prolonged diabetes mellitus had low CFR due to higher hMR (structural CMD), whereas those with shorter disease duration had more frequently functional CMD endotype with a high resting flow.24

Arterial Reservoir Pressure behavior and microvascular myogenic response

Potent adaptive microvascular myogenic mechanisms against pressure changes regulates the microvascular perfusion and protects the capillary milieu from trauma due to overflow or sudden pressure bursts. The increased bMR as a potential adaptive myogenic response to normalize this ongoing relative hyperperfusion and overflow may also lead to higher microvascular resistance at pharmacologically induced hyperemia in the absence of microvascular structural abnormalities. In parallel, pressure-flow regulating reservoir function of elastic arteries is modified by vascular resistance. We have shown that patients with CMD have a less pronounced arterial reservoir behavior marked by lower Pr/Pxs and steeper diastolic decay indicating a relative systemic hyperemic state with attenuated vasotonus and compliance than those with normal CFR. Importantly, to our knowledge, the present study has also demonstrated for the first time that, despite high microvascular resistance locally in coronary arteries, structural CMD also had high DRC with lower Pr/Pxs, suggesting the ongoing systemic relative hyperperfusion similar to functional CMD, based on the consistently demonstrated close resemblance of Pxs of aortic flow,28 despite different local hemodynamics in coronary arteries at rest. Vessels with no CMD-high hMR having nearly 75% increased bMR in conjunction with a simultaneously higher peripheral vasomotor response and higher arterial compliance characterized by a reduced DRC (gradual diastolic decay at aortic pressure) seem to operate interconnectedly to suppress this hyperemic state (high Pxs, DRC and increased baseline bAPV) at rest, while CFR remains preserved (Tables 3 and 4, Central Illustration). Our findings suggest that high hMR mirrors increased bMR, which favors preserved CFR in this particular endotype. Although the mean hMR values between the no CMD-high hMR and structural CMD endotypes were not substantially different, vessels with structural CMD did not have parallel alterations in the arterial reservoir–excess function compared to reference vessels, and they still had higher Pxs and steeper diastolic decay (higher DRC), suggesting a persistent systemic overflow state and lower arterial compliance.28,29 Gradual diastolic decay at aortic pressure waveforms observed in the no CMD-high hMR group also suggests the simultaneously higher peripheral resistance and more pronounced compliance enhancing the reservoir function against relative hyperperfusion, which diminishes Pxs. Unlike structural CMD, the high hMR in the vessels with normal CFR seems to reflect remaining increased tonus, related to a potential adaptive bMR increment as reflected by a high bMR/hMR ratio, termed RRR (a robust marker of autoregulatory capacity),12 with a stronger coupling marked by less-pronounced deviance from model estimate, where for each unit of increase in bMR, the increase in hMR is much milder in the no CMD group. Cumulatively, these observations support the conclusion that high hMR primarily reflects increased bMR. The lack of meaningful variance in P∞ or Pzf indicates that the observed differences are primarily due to the dynamic, pulsatile nature of ventricular-aorto-systemic-coronary coupling, rather than differences in the static state that would theoretically be reached if the heart stopped beating and the system equilibrated. Consistent with the entirety of present findings, the GLMM models revealed that higher values of hMR were not associated with a decrease in CFR in no CMD cases, but they are actually accompanied by higher CFR values. This observation supports the hypothesis that, high hMR in no CMD cases primarily reflects increased bMR (causing distinctively reduced bAPV) which serves to preserve the CFR via readjustment of resting hemodynamics in favor of an adequate vasodilatory response.

Core physiological and clinical observations

Either with low or high coronary bAPV, or low or high microvascular resistance, CMD patients have a relative hyperemic state at rest with high DRC and lower Pr/Pxs ratio, which may relate to a worse prognosis,29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36,59, 60, 61, 62 lead to end-organ dysfunction,63 and be associated with exacerbated local hemodynamic forces linked to endothelial dysfunction29 and an increased workload for ventricles.29 The higher DRC and lower Pr/Pxs may, therefore, help explain the deterioration in long-term ventricular function and development of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in patients with CMD,14 whereas lower (reference-level) Pxs in the no CMD-high hMR subgroup may contribute to the explanation of the very good prognosis of this group. Furthermore, relatively increased excess pressure may be a reasonable mutual therapeutic target in patients with CMD.

Clinical implications and a diagnostic update for CMD stratification

Patients with healthy or dysfunctional microcirculation can have high or low flow (velocity) and microvascular resistance values, within a spectrum covering structural and functional endotypes of CMD, as well as no CMD endotypes with high or low hMR, depending on the degree of compensation (Table 2). In the light of our findings, we proposed a new mode of physiological stratification for CMD in the setting of chronic coronary syndromes, stable CAD and ANOCA, with the normal CFR with high hMR group (= no CMD-high hMR) having favorable local and systemic hemodynamic characteristics. Considering that the high hMR reflects the higher bMR in this distinctive group (based on higher RRR (bMR/hMR) and coupling between bMR and hMR) and the lack of suppressive relationship between high hMR and CFR in no CMD cases, this may be the reflection of a potential, unique adaptive autoregulatory response, which is dictated by the augmented resting microvascular resistance accompanied by a parallel global vasomotor response and a more pronounced arterial reservoir behavior to counter the relative hyperemic state of CMD. This may explain, at least in part, the good prognosis of these patients despite a high hMR. Comprehensively interpreted, a high hMR has no unequivocal meaning, and its relevance in fact depends on its pathophysiological drivers in the clinical context. Critically, based on these findings, considering the persistent low-grade inflammation as the core pathophysiological mechanism in CMD, the ongoing pro-inflammatory systemic relative hyperemic state may be an appropriate mutual and objective therapeutic target in the treatment of patients with CMD.

Limitations and strengths

This is a hypothesis-generating study performed via hemodynamic signals from stable CAD patients of the multicenter DEFINE-FLOW study. The present study has included patients with an indication for invasive coronary angiography, so the patients with the healthy reference vessels may not fully represent a perfectly healthy population. The retrospective analysis of cross-sectional data limits attribution of observed patterns to potential compensatory mechanisms, exploration of which necessitates longitudinal designated research, with evaluation of the effects related to randomized interventions. The inclusion of comprehensive echocardiography data could enhance the interpretation of study findings, although as per the study protocol, there were no patients with severe left ventricular hypertrophy, a septal wall thickness at echocardiography of >13 mm, or a severe cardiomyopathy defined by a left ventricle ejection fraction <30%. No adjustment for multiple testing was performed, which increases the potential for Type I errors; thus, the results should be interpreted with caution. The utilization of GLMMs, necessitated by the nested structure of coronary arteries within patients, limited the inclusion of some clinical variables as confounders in multivariable analyses due to model nonconvergence. Nevertheless, these variables were still compared among groups using simple GLMMs, adjusted for patient ID. Several clinical variables with very rare or too frequent observations (drugs with too few patients etc.) yielded very low (0%) or very high (100%) estimated probabilities in GLMM (Table 2), which should be interpreted carefully, alongside the actual observations provided in Table 1. Furthermore, the endogeneity of CFR and CMD (defined by CFR <2.5) precluded the construction of a single model incorporating a CMD-hMR interaction. Although separate analyses yielded parallel results (unreported), combining them introduced convergence issues and model disruption due to the strong influence of the CMD variable, preventing the inclusion of additional clinical confounders. Out of several Pr-Pxs parameters, Pr/Pxs was included in the multivariable GLMM because 1) we consider it to be pathophysiologically relevant and generalizable variable, 2) DRC is a fitting parameter, whereas Pr and Pxs are final products, and 3) inclusion of Pr and Pxs together precluded convergence in GLMM. It would be valuable to test these parameters in a larger dataset individually. As strength, the prognostic study by Eftekhari et al. which has demonstrated the good prognosis of high hMR-preserved CFR has been performed on the same cohort (DEFINE-FLOW), enhancing the relevance of the present findings to prognostic outcome.

Conclusions

In this hypothesis-generating study, we found that the interaction between the microvascular resistance, systemic arterial reservoir capacity, and CFR helps improve our understanding of CMD. Using this information and an updated diagnostic stratification may help untangle the ongoing prognostic controversy regarding the meaning of high microvascular resistance in stable CAD and ANOCA. Patients with a normal CFR with high hMR have distinctively higher resting microvascular resistance accompanied by lower bAPV resulting in adequately preserved vasodilatory capacity. The favorable local and systemic hemodynamic characteristics, including better preserved vasotonus and arterial compliance, seen in this no CMD-high hMR endotype suggest the presence of potential adaptive mechanisms which may be responsible for increased microvascular resistance without a decrease of CFR. These findings do not endorse the independent use of high hMR for the diagnosis of CMD.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE AND PATIENT CARE: Patients with high hMR-normal CFR exhibit favorable characteristics of coronary and aortic hemodynamics, and reservoir function, in line with previously reported good prognosis. To avoid misdiagnosis, independent use of high hMR for the diagnosis of CMD should be avoided as it may mirror an increment in bMR favoring a preserved CFR, without impairment of hyperemic vasodilator response.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Coronary microvascular dysfunction endotypes mutually relate to a worse arterial reservoir behavior, indicating potential interference of proximal aorta and systemic hemodynamics with coronary autoregulation. This indicates a more complex pathophysiology beyond local perturbations, necessitating consideration of the systemic nature of disease both for diagnosis and treatment strategies. The influence of potential compensatory mechanisms and exploration of potential adaptive processes necessitate longitudinal designated research, with evaluation of the effects related to randomized interventions.
