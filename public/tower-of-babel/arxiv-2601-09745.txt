Enhancing Formal Software Specification with Artificial Intelligence
Antonio Abu Nassar, Eitan Farchi

Abstract

Formal software specification is known to enable early error detection
and explicit invariants, yet it has seen limited industrial adoption
due to its high notation overhead and the expertise required to use
traditional formal languages. This paper presents a case study showing
that recent advances in artificial intelligence make it possible to
retain many of the benefits of formal specification while
substantially reducing these costs.
The necessity of a clear distinction between what is controlled
by the system analyst and can highly benefits from the rigor of formal specification and what need not be controlled is demonstrated.
We use natural language augmented
with lightweight mathematical notation and written in
L
a
T
e
X
as an
intermediate specification language, which is reviewed and refined by
AI prior to code generation. Applied to a nontrivial simulation of
organizational knowledge growth, this approach enables early
validation, explicit invariants, and correctness by design, while
significantly reducing development effort and producing a correct
implementation on the first attempt.

0.1  Introduction

The benefits of formal software specification—such as early detection
of design flaws, explicit assumptions, and built-in invariants—are
widely acknowledged in both academia and industry. Nevertheless,
formal methods have seen limited industrial adoption
[
3
]
. The primary
barriers are the significant overhead involved in writing and
maintaining formal specifications, as well as the specialized
expertise required to use traditional specification languages
(e.g., Z, VDM). As a result, many systems continue to rely on informal
natural-language requirements, deferring correctness concerns to
later development and testing stages.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence raise a natural and timely
question:
can we obtain many of the benefits of formal
specification while avoiding or substantially reducing its
traditional costs
? This paper presents a case study demonstrating
that the answer is affirmative. We show that natural language, when
augmented with lightweight mathematical notation and written in

L
a
T
e
X
, can serve as an effective intermediate specification
language. When combined with AI-based review, reflection, and code
generation, this approach enables early validation, explicit
invariants, and a strong form of correctness-by-design—without the
burden of heavyweight formal methods.

Our case study focuses on a simulation of knowledge growth within an
organization. This domain was deliberately chosen because it is
sufficiently rich to require nontrivial structure, invariants, and
parameterization, while still being accessible and interpretable. The
simulation therefore serves as a realistic and demanding testbed for
evaluating AI-assisted specification and development.

A central goal of this work is to
shift validation left
as much
as possible in the development lifecycle. We leverage AI throughout
the specification, design, and validation process, using natural
language as the primary interface between the human and the system.
Core requirements and design decisions are refined through AI-driven
reflection and other agentic design patterns, with the aim of
producing a system that is inherently correct by construction rather
than corrected post hoc through debugging.

We examine two distinct methodologies for developing the simulation
from a natural-language specification. In the first approach, the
simulation is corrected iteratively based solely on its execution
results. In the second approach, AI is used to review and refine the
specification
before
any code is generated or executed.
Figure
1
illustrates these two
workflows. Empirically, the second approach required approximately

\frac{1}{6}
of the time needed by the first and produced a correct
simulation on the first code-generation attempt, highlighting the
practical value of early, AI-assisted specification review.

Figure 1:
Simulation methodology flow showing iterative connections
between specification, AI review, and execution.

The specification style used in this work can be viewed as an
intermediate representation between informal natural language and
fully formal specification languages. Natural language is enriched
with mathematical notation, enabling precise assertions that can be
reasoned about, proved at the specification level, and later
translated into runtime invariants.

An important observation from the study is that, while high-level
invariants were envisioned by the human designer, the AI often
proposed lower-level invariants that were both correct and useful.
This division of labor proved effective: the human focused on
conceptual intent, while the AI contributed detailed consistency
checks. However, we also encountered attention limitations. As the
specification grew beyond a few pages, the AI occasionally omitted
parts of it during generation. This reinforces the importance of
abstraction, modularization, and decomposition when working with
longer specifications.

Beyond faithfully implementing stated requirements, the AI frequently
suggested natural and valuable extensions that had not been explicitly
requested. In a business setting, such suggestions might include
internationalization or configurability. In our case study, one
notable example was the proposal to introduce Monte Carlo simulation
to ensure statistical stability of the results. These contributions
were welcomed, provided they could be validated at the specification
level.

Throughout the development of the simulation, the generated code
itself was not manually reviewed, as the combination of
specification-level reasoning and invariant checking proved
sufficient. The final system supports multiple experimental
configurations, automatically compares alternative strategies, and
produces succinct conclusions about their relative effectiveness.
Interestingly, prompting the AI to explicitly summarize experimental
conclusions required some repetition, suggesting an area for further
refinement of agentic workflows.

An additional experiment, described in Section
0.10
,
used summarization of the refined, carefully written specification of the business logic,
to obtain a version of the specification where the line between what the system analyst
wants to control and what the analyst does not want to control became blurred.
The experiment emphasizes the primacy of precision when writing
specifications for business logic, and the importance of a clear distinction between
areas of strict control and areas where control is not desired.

All experiments reported in this paper were conducted using AI.
The generated code is written in Python and is available via linked
Google Colab notebooks. The documented sequence of prompts and
responses can serve as a benchmark for comparing the capabilities of
different AI agents in specification-driven software development.

For readability, the reader is encouraged to first follow the
high-level development flow, skimming the prompts and AI responses,
before diving into the details. The table of contents provides a clear
overview of the interaction structure, and visual boxes are used
throughout the text to distinguish between human prompts (blue) and
AI replies (red).

0.4.1  Basic Shapely value prompt

Human Prompt

Prompt:

In the context of cooperative games, the "recommendation" game can be formulated as a cooperative game
(N,v)
, where each player
i\in N
is able to recommend a set of items
B_{i}
. The entire set of items being considered is denoted by
B=\bigcup_{i\in N}B_{i}
. The value of a coalition
S\subseteq N
is defined as:

v(S)=\left|\bigcup_{i\in S}B_{i}\right|

The Shapley value of a player
i
in this game is given by:

\phi_{i}=\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\frac{1}{|\{k\mid j\in B_{k}\}|}

This formulation is intuitive: if item
j
is recommended by player
i
(i.e.,
j\in B_{i}
), the contribution of
i
to the recommendation of
j
is weaker the more other agents also recommend
j
, that is, the larger the set
\{k\mid j\in B_{k}\}
.

In our context, each player corresponds to a vertex
v_{i}
in a graph, and each vertex has an associated set of knowledge items
B_{i}
. This naturally forms a cooperative game as described above. We generalize the current program to compute the Shapley value of each vertex/player and graphically represent its change during the simulation.

0.4.3  Ambiguities and Inconsistencies Concerning the Shapley Value

AI Reply

This section outlines key ambiguities and inconsistencies in the simulation specification with respect to the application of the Shapley value.

Mismatch Between Simulation Dynamics and Shapley Value Definition

The simulation involves multiple stages, temporary edge additions, random clique selection, and probabilistic knowledge creation. In contrast, the Shapley value formulation assumes a static cooperative game with a well-defined value function. It is unclear when the Shapley value is computed (e.g., after each stage or only at the end), and whether it reflects dynamic contributions or static outcomes.

Unclear Definition of Coalition Value
v(S)

The cooperative game defines the value of a coalition
S\subseteq N
as
v(S)=\left|\bigcup_{i\in S}B_{i}\right|
, yet the simulation does not explicitly form or evaluate coalitions. Clique selection is random and temporary, not strategic or player-driven, and there is no mechanism for evaluating the value of arbitrary subsets
S\subseteq N
during the simulation.

Role of
tg
in the Cooperative Game

It is ambiguous whether
tg
is a player in the cooperative game (i.e., included in
N
), an external facilitator whose reward is tracked separately, or a contributor to knowledge items. If
tg
is part of the game, its knowledge set and contribution to
v(S)
must be defined.

Knowledge Item Ownership and Duplication

The Shapley value formula depends on how many players own each item. It is unclear whether knowledge items are unique per player or shared. If a knowledge item is added to all clique members, duplication affects the denominator in the Shapley formula, potentially inflating or deflating contributions.

Clique Formation and Coalition Interpretation

Cliques are selected randomly and temporarily, but Shapley value requires evaluating all possible coalitions. The simulation does not explore all subsets
S\subseteq N
, nor does it track marginal contributions across permutations.

Temporal vs. Static Analysis

The simulation is dynamic (10 stages), while the Shapley value is typically computed for a single snapshot of the game. If the Shapley value is to be tracked over time, it must be recomputed after each stage, the evolution of
B_{i}
must be recorded, and the interpretation of changing Shapley values must be clarified (e.g., does it reflect influence, contribution, or centrality?).

0.4.4  Recommendations to resolve ambiguities

1.

Clarify the time of Shapley value computation (e.g., per stage or at the end).

2.

Define the role of
tg
in the cooperative game.

3.

Track knowledge item ownership precisely, ensuring unique identifiers and ownership sets.

4.

Align simulation with coalition evaluation by considering multiple subsets
S\subseteq N
.

5.

Add a mechanism to track marginal contributions for each player.

6.

Visualize Shapley values per player after each stage to show dynamic contribution.

What we learned.

The benefit from this experiment is twofold.

1. Controlled vs. not controlled aspects.

A system analyst will want to separate between what the model can modify and what it must preserve. In other words, the art in the process is to know where and how to draw the line between controlled and not controlled program aspects. The specification of a GUI and other peripheral elements is not core logic and can be modified or expanded by the model without prompting the system analyst. However, anything pertaining to the business logic should be controlled by the analyst; it must be returned to the analyst for refinement or confirmation, especially in cases where the intention is ambiguous. .

0.11  Related Work

Recent advances in software engineering have explored the use of
Large Language Models (LLMs) and agentic systems to support program
synthesis and multi-step reasoning tasks. Agentic systems leverage
reasoning-action loops and multi-agent coordination to generate,
verify, and correct code autonomously, providing capabilities similar
to those described in Section 1, while remaining dependent on human
supervision for reliability. VIB (Value-Intention-Behavior) programming
offers a complementary perspective, structuring agent behavior around
high-level goals, intentions, and concrete actions
[
2
]
, which
aligns well with the layered reasoning used in AI-assisted software generation.

Spec-Driven Development (SDD) provides a methodology for guiding both
human and AI developers from a structured specification to verified
code
[
1
]
. By defining
what
and
why
first,
SDD improves alignment, traceability, and maintainability of
AI-generated outputs. Our work builds on this concept by using natural
language augmented with mathematical notation as an intermediate
representation. This approach retains the clarity and precision of
formal specifications while significantly reducing the notation
overhead compared to full formal languages such as
Z
.

The
Z
specification language remains a foundational formal
method for mathematically precise modeling of system state, operations,
and invariants
[
4
]
. Unlike
Z
, our intermediate
representation trades some formal rigor for readability and ease of use,
enabling AI agents to interpret and act on specifications directly
without requiring deep mathematical expertise. In this way, our
approach can be seen as a simplification of formal specification that
balances precision, expressiveness, and AI accessibility, bridging the
gap between rigorous formal methods and practical AI-assisted software
development.

0.12  Conclusion

This work illustrates that AI-assisted software development can
benefit from structured specifications to guide code generation and
support maintainable designs, though several limitations remain. By
combining natural language with lightweight mathematical notation, our
approach captures some of the advantages of formal specification—such
as explicit invariants and reasoning about correctness—while reducing
the complexity and overhead of full formal methods like
Z
.
The intermediate representation helped guide AI agents in generating
code, tests, and supporting infrastructure, drawing on principles from
Spec-Driven Development (SDD) and VIB programming to improve
alignment and traceability. However, the simulation used as a case
study, while logically complex, represents only a small piece of
software, and challenges such as attention limits, specification
decomposition, and iterative refinement were encountered during
development.

Future research directions include extending these methods to fully
exploit natural language specifications without embedded mathematics,
exploring richer abstractions, modular interfaces, and
document-driven composition to simplify system specification. Such
investigations could further reduce the barrier to entry for
formal-specification-inspired practices, broaden the applicability of
AI-assisted software engineering, and provide more scalable solutions
for larger and more complex software systems.

(Correctness Invariant)
.

Let

After every stage of the simulation, we have

\left|\bigcup_{v}B_{v}\right|\;=\;noTGKnowldge\;+\;\sum_{\text{all facilitators }tg}r(tg),

where:

•

counts the number of knowledge items created in stages where the selected clique contained

For each facilitator

Hence, each created knowledge item is counted exactly once by exactly one of the two terms.
