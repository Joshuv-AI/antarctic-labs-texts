Kernel manifolds: nonlinear-augmentation dimensionality reduction using reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
Alejandro N. Diaz, Jacob T. Needels, Irina K. Tezaur, Patrick J. Blonigan

Abstract
This paper generalizes recent advances on quadratic manifold (QM) dimensionality reduction by developing kernel methods-based nonlinear-augmentation dimensionality reduction. QMs, and more generally feature map-based nonlinear corrections, augment linear dimensionality reduction with a nonlinear correction term in the reconstruction map to overcome approximation accuracy limitations of purely linear approaches. While feature map-based approaches typically learn a least-squares optimal polynomial correction term, we generalize this approach by learning an optimal nonlinear correction from a user-defined reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Our approach allows one to impose arbitrary nonlinear structure on the correction term, including polynomial structure, and includes feature map and radial basis function-based corrections as special cases. Furthermore, our method has relatively low training cost and has monotonically decreasing error as the latent space dimension increases. We compare our approach to proper orthogonal decomposition and several recent QM approaches on data from several example problems.



Keywords:
nonlinear dimensionality reduction, quadratic manifolds, kernel methods, surrogate modeling

1
Introduction

In recent years, data-driven surrogate modeling of complex physical systems has been an active research area in scientific machine learning (SciML).
A common challenge of constructing surrogate models is that data from simulations of partial differential equations (PDEs) are often high dimensional, with the dimensionality dictated by the discretization methods used and the fidelity of the corresponding mesh.
Consequently, surrogate modeling techniques typically involve a dimensionality reduction component to
extract low-dimensional features from high-dimensional simulation data, which can then be used to construct a low-dimensional, computationally efficient surrogate model.
Crucially, the dimensionality reduction technique used fundamentally limits the achievable accuracy of the resulting surrogate model.

Classical linear dimensionality reduction techniques, such as proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
19
,
21
]
, seek to identify a low-dimensional affine subspace that best captures the high-dimensional data.
POD identifies this subspace as the span of the leading left singular vectors of a representative set of high-dimensional snapshot data.
The POD basis induces an affine
encoder
map that projects high-dimensional snapshots onto generalized coordinates that lie in a low-dimensional
latent space
, which can then be mapped to an approximate reconstruction of the high-dimensional snapshot via an affine
decoder
map.
While POD is effective in numerous applications, it is also well-known to poorly approximate data from problems with slowly decaying Kolmogorov
n
-width
[
32
]
, such as transport-dominated problems or problems with sharp gradients.
In these cases, a large POD basis is required to accurately approximate the given data.
However, despite lying in a high-dimensional affine subspace, these data may still possess an inherent low-dimensional nonlinear structure. This assumption motivates using
nonlinear
dimensionality reduction for problems with slowly decaying Kolmogorov
n
-width.

A number of nonlinear dimensionality reduction approaches have recently been proposed to circumvent the Kolmogorov
n
-width barrier.
These techniques are often collectively referred to as
nonlinear manifolds
to distinguish them from linear dimensionality reduction,
and include autoencoders (AEs)
[
28
,
24
,
36
,
9
,
11
,
17
,
8
,
7
,
13
]
,
quadratic manifolds (QMs)
[
3
,
6
,
15
,
14
,
43
,
42
,
22
,
37
]
,
the projection-based reduced-order model + artificial neural network (PROM-ANN) technique
[
4
]
, and the recent Gaussian process (GP) and radial basis function (RBF) interpolation-based nonlinear closure approach
[
10
]
.
AEs consist of fully nonlinear encoder and decoder maps, which can offer substantial accuracy improvements over linear dimensionality reduction.
However, they can be difficult to train due to their dependence on the neural network architecture and large data requirements.
Furthermore, they often lack interpretable structure and can be slow to evaluate, which is detrimental in use cases requiring fast online evaluations, such as reduced-order modeling.
To address these issues, the QM, PROM-ANN, and GP/RBF-interpolation approaches instead augment linear dimensionality reduction with a nonlinear correction term in the decoder.
We characterize this class of approaches as
nonlinear-augmentation manifolds
.
QMs model the nonlinear augmentation term as a quadratic polynomial, while PROM-ANN models it as an ANN and the GP/RBF-interpolation approach models it as the mean function of a GP or as an RBF interpolant.
These approaches have interpretable structure and, with the exception of PROM-ANN, comparatively low training cost relative to AEs, while still providing improved accuracy over linear dimensionality reduction approaches.
However, QMs, and more generally, feature-map based nonlinear-augmentation manifolds, are typically limited to polynomial corrections, which can restrict the achievable accuracy of these methods, while becoming increasingly difficult to train as the polynomial order increases.
Although using GPs and RBF interpolants as in
[
10
]
can address these difficulties, this approach can be further generalized to improve the flexibility of nonlinear-augmentation manifolds.
In this paper we develop a nonlinear-augmentation dimensionality reduction technique using regularized kernel interpolation, which generalizes recent work on QMs and GP/RBF interpolation-based nonlinear closures, yielding greater expressivity in the nonlinear approximation, reduced training cost compared to QMs, and improved accuracy on out-of-sample data.

Regularized kernel interpolation is a generalization of RBF interpolation relying on mature mathematical theory from reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS)
[
39
,
40
,
30
,
46
]
.
While kernel methods are a common tool in classical data science and machine learning, they have become increasingly popular in SciML in recent years, with notable applications in operator and equation learning
[
5
,
29
,
23
]
, tensor decomposition
[
27
]
, and reduced-order and surrogate modeling
[
12
,
2
]
.
In our dimensionality reduction method, which we refer to as
kernel manifold
dimensionality reduction, one can model the nonlinear augmentation term with arbitrary nonlinearities by choosing an appropriate kernel, and includes QMs and GP/RBF-based closures as special cases.
Furthermore, unlike feature map-based nonlinear augmentations, regularized kernel interpolation allows one to model the nonlinear term using polynomials of arbitrarily high order without increasing the number of degrees of freedom in the learning problem.
We also note that our approach is distinct from kernel principal component analysis (KPCA)
[
41
]
and kernel POD (KPOD)
[
38
]
in that it augments classical POD with a nonlinear correction term, whereas KPCA/KPOD returns a nonlinear kernel-based encoder without an explicit decoder, thus requiring one to learn an approximate decoder function through, e.g., kernel interpolation or neural networks.
While we purely focus on dimensionality reduction in this paper,
we emphasize that our method is general-purpose, and can be used for standalone dimensionality reduction, intrusive model reduction as in
[
10
]
, or non-intrusive model reduction through integration with operator inference (OpInf)
[
16
,
25
,
34
,
35
,
14
]
or the recent kernel methods-based approach of
[
12
]
.

2
Review of feature map-based nonlinear-augmentation manifolds

This section reviews a class of nonlinear manifold dimensionality reduction that augments linear dimensionality reduction with a nonlinear correction term in the decoder map.
These approaches are typically applied to data from large-scale physics simulations.
We first discuss a unified problem setting in
Section

2.1
.
We then briefly review the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) in
Section

2.2
, since nonlinear-augmentation manifolds approaches use POD as a starting point. Lastly, we review the feature map-based nonlinear augmentation approach in
Section

2.3
.

2.1
Problem setting

Let
{\cal D}\subset\real^{p}
denote a set of parameters for a given physical system and let
{\cal O}:{\cal D}\to\real^{N}
denote an operator that maps a parameter
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in{\cal D}
to a discretized state of the physical system on a mesh of size
N
:

{\cal O}:{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mapsto\mathbf{q}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\in\real^{N}.

(2.1)

For example,
{\cal O}
can represent the solution operator of a discretized PDE.
We seek a low-dimensional representation of the solution space of the operator
{\cal O}
,

{\cal Q}={\cal O}({\cal D})\subset\real^{N}.

(2.2)

In particular, we wish to construct an
encoder
map

\mathbf{h}:{\cal Q}\to{\widehat{\cal Q}}\subset\real^{r}

and a
decoder
map

\mathbf{g}:{\widehat{\cal Q}}\to{\cal Q},

where
r\ll N
,
such that

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}))\approx\mathbf{q}\quad\forall\quad\mathbf{q}\in{\cal Q}.

(2.3)

In this setting,
{\cal Q}\subset\real^{N}
is referred to as the
full space
and
{\widehat{\cal Q}}\subset\real^{n}
is referred to as the
latent space
.
We will commonly use the
projection error

\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}))

(2.4)

to assess the quality of the encoder and decoder.
In the following sections, we review methods for constructing
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{g}
using data samples from the full space
{\cal Q}
.

2.2
Proper orthogonal decomposition

POD computes an affine encoder

\mathbf{h}
and affine decoder
\mathbf{g}

optimal affine subspace
\bar{\mathbf{q}}+{\cal V}\subset\real^{N}
that best approximates
{\cal Q}
,
where
\bar{\mathbf{q}}\in\real^{N}
is a fixed offset vector and
{\cal V}\subset\real^{N}
is a linear subspace of dimension
r\ll N
.
We review the essential details here and refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
21
,
19
]
for a more complete overview.

Let

\mathbf{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{N\times M}

be a data matrix consisting of columns
\left\{\mathbf{q}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset{\cal Q}
.
We then shift the data
\mathbf{Q}
by the offset vector
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}},

(2.5)

where
\mathbf{1}\in\real^{N}
is the vector of all ones.
The offset
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
can be chosen by the user, but is often taken to be the mean of the snapshots:

\bar{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbf{q}_{j},

(2.6)

or set to zero:
\bar{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}
. It can also be used to strongly enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions in a projection-based reduced-order model (ROM)
[
20
]
.
We then compute the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
,

\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathsf{T}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}},

and define the POD basis matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\real^{N\times r}
to be the first
r
leading left singular vectors of
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
,

\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{U}(\,:\,,1:r).

The subspace
{\cal V}
is taken to be the span of the columns of
\mathbf{V}
,
{\cal V}={\rm span}(\mathbf{V})
.
Furthermore, the basis matrix
\mathbf{V}
minimizes

\min_{\mathbf{V}\in\real^{N\times r},\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r}}\;\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(2.7)

and hence
\mathbf{V}
optimally reconstructs the shifted data
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
.
Lastly, the encoder
\mathbf{h}
and decoder
\mathbf{g}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}.

(2.8)

While POD can successfully reduce the dimensionality of a given dataset
\mathbf{Q}
in a number of applications with small projection errors,
it is well-known that it struggles to approximate data coming from transport-dominated problems or from problems with sharp gradients.
In these cases, a large basis size
r
is needed to reconstruct the data to achieve a desired accuracy.
This issue is known as the Kolmogorov
n
-width barrier
[
32
]
.
To overcome this barrier, a number of recent works have proposed that augment the affine decoder
\mathbf{g}
from equation
eq.

2.8
with a nonlinear correction term.
In particular, these include
quadratic manifolds (QMs)
[
3
,
6
,
15
,
14
,
43
,
42
,
22
,
37
]
,
the projection-based ROM + artificial neural network (PROM-ANN) approach
[
4
]
,
and recent work using Gaussian processes and radial basis function (RBF) interpolation to fit nonlinear closure terms in the latent space
[
10
]
.
We review a class of these approaches in the following section.

2.3
Feature map-based nonlinear-augmentation manifolds

The feature map-based nonlinear-augmentation manifold approach, which we will abbreviate to “feature map manifold” (FM), takes an affine encoder
\mathbf{h}
of the form
eq.

2.8
and augments the affine decoder map
\mathbf{g}
with a nonlinear correction term, resulting in a decoder of the form

\mathbf{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

(2.9)

where
\mathbf{V}\in\real^{N\times r}
is a
reduced basis
matrix,

\overline{\mathbf{V}}\in\real^{N\times m}
is an
augmenting basis
matrix,

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}:\real^{r}\to\real^{n_{\phi}}
is a nonlinear feature map,
and
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\in\real^{m\times n_{\phi}}
is a coefficient matrix.
The basis matrices
\mathbf{V}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}
are typically chosen beforehand,
while the coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
is learned by minimizing the norms of the projection errors
\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}_{j}))\right\|_{2}
of the given data
\left\{\mathbf{q}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset{\cal Q}
:

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\in\real^{m\times n_{\phi}}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\;\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\left(\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}})+\overline{\mathbf{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}))\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(2.10)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a regularization parameter.

If
\mathbf{V}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}
are chosen to be
orthonormal matrices satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{r},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{m},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(2.11)

and the projections of
\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}
by
\mathbf{V}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}
, respectively, are denoted as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}),

(2.12)

then one can show that
eq.

2.10
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\in\real^{m\times n_{\phi}}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\;\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(2.13)

With the data matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}
defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}&\dots&\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{r\times M},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{1}&\dots&\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{m\times M},

(2.14)

a minimizer
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\in\real^{m\times n_{\phi}}
can be computed by solving the normal equations

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}){\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n_{\phi}}\right){\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{\mathsf{T}},

(2.15)

where
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
applied to a matrix is understood to operate column-wise.

A typical choice for
\mathbf{V}\in\real^{N\times r}
is the same POD basis from Section
2.2
consisting of the first
r
leading POD modes,
with the augmenting basis
\overline{\mathbf{V}}\in\real^{N\times m}
chosen to be the subsequent
m
modes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}(\,:\>,1:r),

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}(\,:\>,r+1:r+m),

(2.16)

see, e.g.,
[
14
, Algorithm 1]
.
With this choice of
\mathbf{V}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}
from equation
eq.

2.12
corresponds to the projection of
\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}
onto the first
r
leading modes and
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}
corresponds to the projection onto the subsequent
m
modes.
Moreover, solving
eq.

2.13
is equivalent to constructing a function

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle:\real^{r}\to\real^{m},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\approx\widehat{\mathbf{p}},

(2.17)

that approximates a map from the lower order modal coefficients
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
to higher order modal coefficients
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}
.
We will leverage this interpretation in the kernel-based approach in Section
3
.

Remark 2.1
.

The feature map
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}:\real^{n}\to\real^{n_{\phi}}
is often chosen to be a quadratic feature map that omits duplicated entries,

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{2}&\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}&\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}^{2}&\dots&\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{n-1}&\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{n}^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\real^{n(n+1)/2}.

(2.18)

Using a decoder of the form
eq.

2.9
with the quadratic feature map
eq.

2.18

is referred to as quadratic manifold (QM) dimensionality reduction.

Remark 2.2
.

The approaches in
[
3
,
15
,
43
]
use
\overline{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
and construct
\mathbf{V}
using POD modes.
Since
\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\neq\mathbf{0}
in this case, the simplified minimization problem defined by Equations
eq.

2.12
and
eq.

2.13
no longer holds, and thus
eq.

2.10
must be solved directly.
While
[
3
,
15
]
use the
r
leading POD modes to construct
\mathbf{V}
, the greedy QM approach in
[
43
]
greedily selects (potentially high-order) POD modes, which can lead to substantial accuracy gains; see
[
43
]
for further details.

Remark 2.3
.

The nonlinear correction term
\overline{\mathbf{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
in the decoder
eq.

2.9
can also be interpreted as a closure modeling term, since
\overline{\mathbf{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
approximates the effect of discarded POD modes without introducing additional degrees of freedom.
However, it is important to note that an exact mapping
\mathbf{n}_{*}:\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\mapsto\widehat{\mathbf{p}}
from low-order modes to high-order modes is not well-defined in general.
Indeed, consider the case
r=m=1
and
\bar{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}
. Then
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{v}_{1}=\mathbf{U}(:,1)
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{v}_{2}=\mathbf{U}(:,2).
Suppose that
\mathbf{q}:=\mathbf{v}_{1}\in{\cal Q}
and
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{v}_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{2}\in{\cal Q}
.
Notice that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}=1,\;\widehat{\mathbf{p}}=\overline{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}=0\quad\implies\quad\widehat{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{n}_{*}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\mathbf{n}_{*}(1)=0.

However,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=1,\;\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}=\overline{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=1\quad\implies\quad\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{n}_{*}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})=\mathbf{n}_{*}(1)=1\neq 0.

Therefore
\mathbf{n}_{*}
is not well-defined.
Nevertheless, fitting an approximate map of the form
eq.

2.17
is useful for dimensionality reduction.

While the FM approach outlined in this section is relatively general, typically
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is chosen to be a low-order polynomial.
Extending the approach to higher-order polynomials results in an increasingly large linear system
eq.

2.15
that must be solved to compute the coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
.
Moreover, using a high-order polynomial for
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
would increase the evaluation cost of the decoder
\mathbf{g}
, which can be detrimental in use-cases where
\mathbf{g}
is evaluated many times in an online stage, such as reduced-order modeling.
In the next section, we further generalize the FM approach to a kernel-based nonlinear manifold to address these issues.

3
Kernel-based nonlinear-augmentation manifolds

In the kernel-based nonlinear-augmentation manifold approach, which we will abbreviate to “kernel manifold” (KM), we again use an affine encoder
\mathbf{h}
and augment the affine decoder with a nonlinear correction term.
We express the decoder as

\mathbf{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{V}\in\real^{N\times r}
is a reduced basis matrix and

\overline{\mathbf{V}}\in\real^{N\times m}
is an augmenting basis matrix as before,
and
\mathbf{n}:\real^{r}\to\real^{m}
is an
optimal
nonlinear function that is learned through regularized kernel interpolation.
We briefly review regularized kernel interpolation in Section
3.1
and present the details of our new approach in Section
3.2
.

3.1
Review of regularized kernel interpolation

Definition 3.1

(Positive-definite kernels)
.

A function
K:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
is a (real-valued)
kernel function
if it is symmetric, i.e.,
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=K(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\real^{d}
. A kernel function
K
is said to be
positive-definite
if for any
\mathbf{X}=[~\mathbf{x}_{1}~~\cdots~~\mathbf{x}_{n}~]\in\real^{n}
with pairwise distinct columns, the kernel matrix
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})\in\real^{n\times n}
with entries
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})_{ij}=K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
is positive semi-definite.
Moreover, if
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
is strictly positive-definite, then
K
is also said to be
strictly positive-definite
.

Definition 3.2

(Reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS))
.

Consider the pre-Hilbert space

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{K}^{0}(\real^{d})=\left\{f:\real^{d}\to\real~\bigg|~\exists\,n\in\mathbb{N},\,\mathbf{w}\in\real^{n},\,\left\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\real^{d}~\textup{such that}~f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x})\right\}

where
K:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
is a positive definite kernel.
The
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)

{\cal H}_{K}(\real^{d})
induced by
K
is the (unique) completion of
{\cal H}_{K}^{0}(\real^{d})
with respect to the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\cal H}_{K}(\real^{d})}\coloneqq\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{K}(\real^{d})}^{1/2}
induced by the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle f,f^{\prime}\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{K}(\real^{d})}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}w_{j}w_{k}^{\prime}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime}),

where
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x})
and
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}w_{k}^{\prime}K(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})
.

For a set of pairwise-distinct vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\real^{d}
, we use the notation
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})\in\real^{n\times n}
to denote the
kernel matrix
of
Definition

3.1
and define the vector
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{x})=[~K(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x})~~\cdots~~K(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x})~]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\real^{n}
.
To simplify notation, we write
{\cal H}_{K}
instead of
{\cal H}_{K}(\real^{d})
when it is clear that
K
is defined over
\real{}^{d}\times\real^{d}
.

While the RKHS
{\cal H}_{K}
is a Hilbert space of scalar-valued functions, one can easily construct an RKHS of vector-valued functions by taking the
p
-fold Cartesian product of
{\cal H}_{K}
, i.e.
{\cal H}_{K}^{p}={\cal H}_{K}\times\dots\times{\cal H}_{K}
, with inner product

\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{K}^{p}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\langle f_{i},f_{i}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{K}}
for all
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}),\mathbf{f}^{\prime}=(f_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,f_{p}^{\prime})\in{\cal H}_{K}^{p}
.
We now state the Representer Theorem, a key result from the RKHS literature underlying our methodology.

Definition 3.3

(Regularized kernel interpolant)
.

Let
\mathbf{f}:\real^{d}\to\real^{p}
,
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\real^{d}
be pairwise distinct, and denote
\mathbf{y}_{j}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
.
For an RKHS
{\cal H}_{K}^{p}
and regularization parameter
\lambda\geq 0
, a
regularized interpolant

\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{f}}^{\lambda}\in{\cal H}_{K}^{p}
of
\mathbf{f}
solves the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{s}\in{\cal H}_{K}^{p}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\mathbf{s}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{K}^{p}}^{2}.

(3.2)

Theorem 3.1

(Representer Theorem)
.

The minimization problem
eq.

3.2
has a solution of the form

\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{f}}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{x}),

(3.3)

where the coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\real^{n\times p}
solves the linear system

(K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})+\lambda\mathbf{I}){\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\mathbf{Y}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(3.4)

Moreover, if
K
is strictly positive definite,
s_{\mathbf{f}}^{\lambda}
is the unique minimizer.

This result is a straightforward extension of the Representer Theorem for scalar-valued functions (see, e.g.,
[
40
, Theorem 9.3]
).
A key observation from
Theorem

3.1
is that a solution to the infinite-dimensional minimization problem
eq.

3.2
can be obtained by solving the finite-dimensional linear system
eq.

3.4
.

3.2
Learning kernel-based nonlinear augmentations

For our KM approach, we form the decoder
eq.

3.1
where the nonlinear function
\mathbf{n}:\real^{r}\to\real^{m}
belongs to the RKHS
{\cal H}_{K}^{m}={\cal H}_{K}^{m}(\real^{r})
for a given positive-definite kernel
K:\real^{r}\times\real^{r}\to\real
.
In particular, we use the optimal
\mathbf{n}\in{\cal H}_{K}^{m}
that minimizes the norms of the projection errors
\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}_{j}))\right\|_{2}
of the given data
\left\{\mathbf{q}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset{\cal Q}
:

\min_{\mathbf{n}\in{\cal H}_{K}^{m}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\;\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\left(\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}})+\overline{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}))\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\mathbf{n}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{K}^{m}}^{2},

(3.5)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a regularization parameter.

For our approach, we select

\mathbf{V}\in\real^{N\times r}
to be the POD basis from Section
2.2
consisting of the first
r
leading POD modes and
the augmenting basis
\overline{\mathbf{V}}\in\real^{N\times m}
to be the subsequent
m
modes as in
eq.

2.16
.
Thus
\mathbf{V}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}
satisfy
eq.

2.11
.
As in
eq.

2.12
, again let

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}),

(3.6)

i.e.,
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\in\real^{r}
denotes the first
r
leading POD modal coefficients and
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}\in\real^{m}
the subsequent
m
modal coefficients.
One can again show that
eq.

3.5
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{n}\in{\cal H}_{K}^{m}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\;\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}-\mathbf{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\mathbf{n}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{K}^{m}}^{2}.

(3.7)

With the data matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}
from
eq.

2.14
,
we can apply
Theorem

3.1
to
eq.

3.7
and obtain the solution

\mathbf{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}K(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

(3.8)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\real^{M\times m}
solves

(K(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}},\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})+\lambda\mathbf{I}){\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(3.9)

We then obtain the desired nonlinear decoder

\mathbf{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

(3.10)

with
\mathbf{n}
given by
eq.

3.8
.

An important distinction between our KM approach and the FM approach in Section
2.3
is that the nonlinear function
\mathbf{n}
is optimal in the RKHS
{\cal H}_{K}^{m}
, whereas the coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\in\real^{m\times n_{\phi}}
from Section
2.3
is optimal in the corresponding vector space of matrices.
Secondly, the linear system
eq.

3.9
scales with the amount of data
M
, whereas the linear system
eq.

2.15
scales with the dimension of the feature map
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
.
This can be problematic when using high order polynomial feature maps
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, where
n_{\phi}
can be much larger than the amount of data
M
.
Moreover, in the FM approach, the structure of the feature map
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
must be explicitly defined, whereas the nonlinear term
\mathbf{n}
can have implicit structure enforced by different types of kernels.
Lastly, our KM allows one to construct a feature map-based reconstruction of the form
eq.

2.9
as a special case.

Remark 3.1
.

The projection-based ROM + artificial neural network (PROM-ANN) approach of
[
4
]
also considers decoders of the form
eq.

3.10
with
\mathbf{V}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}
consisting of POD modes, but instead models the nonlinear function
\mathbf{n}
as an ANN.
Furthermore,
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
suggest selecting
r
and
m
by first identifying
n_{\rm tot}:=r+m
satisfying the energy criterion

n_{\rm tot}:=\underset{\tilde{n}}{\arg\min}\left\{1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{n}}\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{{\rm rank}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}})}\sigma_{j}^{2}}\leq\nu\right\},

(3.11)

for some
\nu>0
where
\sigma_{j}
is the
j
th singular value of
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
, then setting
r
to some small fraction of
n_{\rm tot}
and
m
to
m=n_{\rm tot}-r
.

3.3
Kernel selection and equivalence to feature map approach

By the Moore–Aronszajn Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
39
, Theorem 3.10]
),
a positive-definite kernel
K
uniquely defines an RKHS
{\cal H}_{K}
.
Consequently, the choice of kernel determines what form
\mathbf{n}\in{\cal H}_{K}^{m}
can take and how accurately it can be used to approximate the data
\mathbf{Q}
.
We summarize several effective choices for kernels and show that specific types of kernels, i.e. feature map kernels, can result in nonlinear augmentations of the same form as
eq.

2.9
.

An effective general-purpose choice of kernel are radial basis function (RBF) kernels.
These kernels have the form

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\psi(\epsilon\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2})

(3.12)

where
\psi:\real_{\geq 0}\to\real
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\real^{n}
and
\epsilon>0
is a
shape parameter
, and hence an
RBF interpolant of the data
eq.

2.14
has the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\psi(\epsilon\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\right\|_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\psi(\epsilon\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{M}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\right\|_{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(3.13)

provides some examples of commonly used RBF kernels.

Another type of kernel is the polynomial kernel
K:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\left(c+\rho\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)^{\ell},

(3.14)

where
c,\rho\in\real
,
c\geq 0
,
\rho>0
, are hyper-parameters and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
is the order of the polynomial.
Typical values of
c
and
\rho
are
c=1
and
\rho=1/d
.
Using a polynomial kernel results in a polynomial nonlinear term
\mathbf{n}
.
Therefore, the resulting decoder
eq.

3.10
defines a
polynomial manifold
.
However, using a polynomial kernel only
implicitly
defines the polynomial structure of
eq.

3.10
.
In some use cases, such as reduced-order modeling, it may be beneficial to know the
explicit
polynomial structure.
In these cases, using a
feature map kernel
may be preferable.

A feature map
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}:\real^{d}\to\real^{n_{\phi}}
and a strictly positive definite matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\real^{n_{\phi}\times n_{\phi}}
induce a postive-definite kernel
K:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
defined as the
\mathbf{G}
-weighted inner product of two feature vectors:

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(3.15)

The resulting interpolant
\mathbf{n}
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}K(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}}_{:={\boldsymbol{\Xi}}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})={\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

(3.16)

and thus the decoder
\mathbf{g}
is of the form

\mathbf{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}).

(3.17)

Therefore, feature map-based nonlinear decoders of the form
eq.

2.9
can be obtained as a special case of the KM approach.
In particular, taking
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
to be the quadratic feature map
eq.

2.18
results in a QM.

Remark 3.2
.

Although the GP-based closure of
[
10
, Section 3.2]
relies on a probabilistic framework, the authors’ use of the GP mean for
\mathbf{n}
is computationally equivalent to our KM framework with an RBF kernel and regularization
\lambda
equal to the GP prior covariance
\sigma^{2}
.
Similarly, the RBF interpolation-based closure of
[
10
, Section 3.3]
is a special case of our KM approach using an RBF kernel.

Remark 3.3

(Input normalization)
.

Depending on the input data
\mathbf{X}
and choice of kernel
K
, the kernel matrix
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
may have poor conditioning, which can adversely affect the solve for the coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}.

Although increasing the regularization parameter
\lambda
may ameliorate the conditioning of the linear system
eq.

3.4
, it may also increase the error of the resulting kernel interpolant at the given training data.
Alternatively, one can improve the conditioning of
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
by normalizing the inputs
\mathbf{X}
.
This relies on the following observation: for an injective
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:\real^{d}\to\real^{d}
, if
K
is a positive definite kernel, then the function
K_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
defined by

K_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=K({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x}),{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))

(3.18)

is also a positive-definite kernel
[
39
, Prop. 2.7]
.
Therefore, choosing an appropriate
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
can improve the conditioning of
K_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
compared to
K(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})
.
An effective choice is the function
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, where
\mathbf{M}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{m})\in\real^{d\times d}
and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\real^{d}
are given by

\displaystyle m_{i}=\max_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})-\min_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{ij}),\qquad\bar{x}_{i}=\min_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{ij}),\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

(3.19)

which maps the components of each row of
\mathbf{X}
to the interval
[0,1]
.
With this choice of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, we set the weighting matrix
\mathbf{G}
in a kernel induced by a feature map
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}:\real^{d}\to\real^{n_{\phi}}
to
\mathbf{G}=(1/n_{\phi})\mathbf{I}
.

4.2
High-speed aerodynamics – analytic model of surface heating

The next problem considered is an analytic function developed to parametrically emulate prominent features of high-speed aerodynamic flowfields.
The physical interpretation for this model problem is
the variation in aerothermal quantities of interest over conical geometries at varying flight conditions.

Specifically, this model emulates the variation in heat flux over a flared-cone geometry, similar to the HIFiRE-1 test vehicle
[
26
]
, though with significant simplification of the underlying physical models and geometry.
The test function is defined so that a non-dimensional surface quantity,
s
, corresponding to the heat flux magnitude can be computed on a quasi-three-dimensional domain, defined as a function in cylindrical coordinates
(z,r,\theta)
, where
z\in[0,2]
is the axial coordinate,
r\in[0,R]
,
R>0
, is a radial coordinate, and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
is a circumferential angle measure.
We consider variation of the field in response to
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=[\mu_{1}\;\mu_{2}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\cal D}=[-10^{\circ},10^{\circ}]\times[0.4,0.8]
consisting of two parameters: the angle of attack,
\mu_{1}\in[-10^{\circ},10^{\circ}]
, dictating the pitch angle orientation relative to the freestream, and a laminar-to-turbulent transition location parameter,
\mu_{2}\in[0.4,0.8]
, controlling the location of turbulent transition (and associated increase in heat flux) on the cone surface, where the dimensionless field quantity
s:[0,2]\times[0,2\pi]\times{\cal D}\to\real
can be expressed as

\displaystyle s(z,\theta;{\boldsymbol{\mu}})

\displaystyle=\frac{100(1-5\sin(\mu_{1})\sin(\theta))}{(1+20z)^{2}}+50\sigma(z-\mu_{2}(1+2\sin(\mu_{1})\sin(\theta)))-50\sigma(z-1.2)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{75\sigma(x-1.6)(1-5\sin(\mu_{1})\sin(\theta))}{\delta+x}+50.

Here,
\sigma(x)
is a scaled sigmoid function modified to approximate a sharp discontinuity as

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-100x}},

(4.6)

and
\delta
is a small, nonzero value of
10^{-6}
.

The first term in
eq.

4.5
constitutes a rapid quadratic decay of
s
over the surface modulated by the angle of attack parameter.
The second and third terms in the equation represent the onset and end of a laminar-to-turbulent transition front on the conical portion of the vehicle.
An assumption is made that the transition is pulled forward on the windward surface of the geometry, and in this parameter field the transition front is constrained to the conical region.
The fourth term represents heating augmentation associated with shock-boundary layer at the collar compression corner, resulting in a large jump in

modulated by the angle of attack with a reciprocal decay.
It is important to note that this model is designed to qualitatively capture features of high-speed heating profiles, but is not based on first-principles physics.

4.3
Solid mechanics – 3D flexible bracket

The third example is a moderately sized 3D hyper-elastic solid dynamics problem, referred to herein as the flexible bracket.
The governing PDEs are the following equations of motion, written in terms of displacement vector
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, material density
\rho>0
, and the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}

\rho\ddot{\mathbf{d}}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(4.8)

In (
4.8

\ddot{\mathbf{d}}:=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{d}}{\partial t^{2}}
represents the acceleration.
Encoded into

is a constitutive model for the underlying material. Herein, we specify a Neohookean-type material model extended to the compressible regime, with Young’s modulus
E=200\times 10^{9}
GPa, Poisson’s ratio
\nu=0.25
and density
\rho=7800
kg/m
3

[
31
]
. We emphasize that the governing PDE (
4.8
) is nonlinear and contains generic (non-polynomial) nonlinearities, as the Helmholtz free-energy density corresponding to the Neohookean constitutive model is nonlinear.

\rho u\\
\rho v\\
\rho E\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\

5
Discussion and conclusion

This paper generalizes recent work on QMs and the GP/RBF-based nonlinear closure approach of
[
10
]
by developing nonlinear-augmentation dimensionality reduction relying on regularized kernel interpolation.
Like previous approaches, our method augments POD with a nonlinear correction term in the decoder map, which we model as an optimal nonlinear function lying in a given RKHS.
We leverage the Representer Theorem to fit a nonlinear mapping from low-order POD coefficients to high-order POD coefficients,
which enables one to fit nonlinear augmentation terms with arbitrary nonlinearities through the choice of kernel.
We show that feature-map based nonlinear augmentations, specifically QMs, as well as the GP/RBF approach, can be obtained as special cases of our method.

Our numerical examples compare the KM approach to POD and several recent state-of-the-art QM approaches on data from several example problems and show that KMs using RBF kernels can yield substantial accuracy gains in the small latent dimension size regime.
These results suggest that mappings from low-order POD modal coefficients to an optimal nonlinear correction often cannot be sufficiently approximated using quadratic nonlinearities, making RBF kernels an attractive alternative.
Additionally, the results on the double Mach reflection test case show that nonlinear-augmentation manifolds may only provide slight improvements over POD for especially difficult test cases.
This is because nonlinear-augmentation approaches still rely on an underlying POD, and therefore will at best match the projection error of linear dimensionality reduction with a large POD basis.
Thus if a large POD basis is still insufficient for approximating the given problem, a nonlinear-augmenation manifold will be as well.
Therefore, for sufficiently difficult problems, an alternative nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique, such as autencoders, may be preferable.

Future work will focus on improving the attainable accuracy in the KM framework.
In particular, we will explore other kernels, such as high-order polynomial kernels or sums and products of kernels, and examine their effect on the KM’s accuracy.
We will also investigate if a natural nonlinear form for the map
\mathbf{n}
mapping low-order POD coefficients to high-order POD coefficients can be deduced, thus informing the selection of an “optimal” kernel for use in the KM approach.
Lastly, we will incorporate KMs as the dimensionality reduction component in a ROM, such as the kernel-based ROMs of
[
12
]
.
