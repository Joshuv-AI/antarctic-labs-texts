Association between self-reported oral health issues and autoimmune diseases: evidence from UK biobank
Authors: Dongwon Yoon, Barbanente Ottavio, Choa Yun, Sun-Young Jung, May A Beydoun, Lenore J Launer, Minkyo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral microbiome dysbiosis may contribute to autoimmune disease development, but epidemiological evidence linking oral health and autoimmune disease risk is limited.ObjectivesTo evaluate the association between self-reported oral health issues by questionnaire—defined as the presence of painful or bleeding gums, mouth ulcers, toothache, dentures, or loose teeth—and the risk of incident autoimmune diseases in the UK Biobank cohort.Materials and methodsIndividuals reporting any oral health issues (painful/bleeding gums, mouth ulcers, toothache, dentures, and loose teeth) were classified as any self-reported oral health issues; others as no self-reported oral health issues. We estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for any autoimmune diseases and 39 types of autoimmune diseases using Cox regression models with Bonferroni correction. We investigated associations of the type and number of self-reported oral health issues with autoimmune diseases.ResultsAmong 451,404 participants (mean age: 56.4 years, 54.2% female), any self-reported oral health issues (N = 177,198; 39.3%) were associated with an increased risk of any autoimmune diseases (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.09–1.14). Painful gums showed the strongest association (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.31–1.47), followed by mouth ulcers (1.23, 1.18–1.27) and toothache (1.21, 1.15–1.27). Risk increased with the number of self-reported oral health issues (per 1–issue increase: HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.08–1.11, P-trend < 0.001). For specific autoimmune disease, primary biliary cholangitis (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.24–2.19), rheumatism (1.48, 1.23–1.77), lichen planus (1.35, 1.15–1.57), Sjögren’s disease (1.30, 1.11–1.52), pernicious anemia (1.28, 1.12–1.46), rheumatoid arthritis (1.18, 1.12–1.26), and psoriasis (1.16, 1.07–1.26) were identified.ConclusionsSelf-reported oral health issues were associated with an increased risk of incident autoimmune diseases, suggesting that oral health indicators may serve as clinically relevant markers of autoimmune disease risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09395-z.Keywords: Oral health, Autoimmune disease, Oral microbiome, Prospective cohort, Immune-mediated disease

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases, characterized by immune disturbances involving self-reactive antibodies targeting normal host constituents, have shown a rising incidence and increasing medical burden worldwide [1, 2]. The global rise in autoimmune diseases, with an estimated 19% annual net increase, reflects a substantial and growing public health burden [3], underscoring the urgent need for awareness and in-depth understanding. However, the clinical mechanisms behind the development or progression of autoimmune diseases are not fully understood due to their complex pathophysiology in nature. A wide range of factors, including genetic predispositions, environmental influences, and comorbidities, have been unveiling their association with autoimmune disease development, with increasing recognition of the role of microorganisms [4, 5].

Dysbiosis of the oral microbiome may contribute to the autoimmune diseases through several pathways, including microbial translocation, molecular mimicry, overproduction of autoantigens, and cytokine-mediated autoimmune responses [6–8]. In these pathways, poor oral health conditions, including periodontitis, bleeding/painful gums, mouth ulcers, may facilitate the systemic dissemination of oral microorganisms, potentially promoting autoimmune disease [9].

While several studies have suggested associations between poor oral health conditions and various types of autoimmune diseases [10–16], those studies were largely focused on specific types of bacteria or autoimmune diseases, lacked population-scaled evidence, and/or yielded inconsistent results due to small sample sizes or the use of cross-sectional designs. To address these gaps, we aimed to investigate the association between poor oral health conditions (i.e., self-reported oral health issues) and the risk of autoimmune diseases in a prospective cohort design across a wide spectrum of autoimmune diseases to provide more robust and reliable evidence at a population level.

Methods

Data sources and study design

We adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines in the design, analysis, and reporting of this observational study.

The UK Biobank is a large-scale biomedical database of approximately 500,000 middle- and old-aged adults in the UK, recruited between 2006 and 2010 [17]. Data collection included physical examinations, phenotypic assessments, biological samples, and self-reported information from touchscreen questionnaires and computer-assisted interviews, covering demographics, lifestyle, and medical history. Health outcomes were obtained from self-reports and linked electronic health records, including hospital inpatient records, primary care records, and death and cancer registries.

Among 502,389 study participants who consented to data use, we excluded individuals who had missing data for body mass index (BMI) or Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI) (n = 10,741), prior autoimmune disease diagnoses at baseline (n = 34,745), and those who declined to answer the oral health questionnaire (n = 5,499). After applying eligibility criteria, the final study population comprised 451,404 participants.

Assessment of oral health status

Oral health conditions were assessed at the baseline visit using the UK Biobank touchscreen questionnaire (Field ID: 6149). Overview of the questionnaire and validation are documented by UK Biobank [18]. Participants were asked to select all applicable dental and mouth-related issues from eight options, representing a multiple-response nominal scale: ‘mouth ulcers,’ ‘painful gums,’ ‘bleeding gums,’ ‘loose teeth,’ ‘toothache,’ ‘dentures,’ ‘none of the above,’ and ‘prefer not to answer.’ For this analysis, oral health status was dichotomized as ‘any self-reported oral health issues’ if they reported at least one of six conditions: painful gums (N = 13,249), mouth ulcers (N = 44,691), toothache (N = 20,009), dentures (N = 73,415), loose teeth (N = 19,407), or bleeding gums (N = 60,340), and ‘no self-reported oral health issues’ if they reported none (n = 274,206).

Outcome ascertainment

Autoimmune diseases were selected based on the previous literature and the Autoimmune Registry [6, 19, 20]. We identified 39 types of autoimmune diseases using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes from hospital inpatient data: Addison’s disease, ankylosing spondylitis, antiphospholipid syndrome, aplastic anemia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, autoimmune hepatitis, autoimmune thyroiditis, bullous disorders, Celiac’s disease, Crohn’s disease, dermato/polymyositis, discoid lupus erythematosus, Graves’ disease, Guillain-Barré syndrome, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, lichen planus, mixed connective tissue disease, multiple sclerosis, pernicious anemia, polymyalgia rheumatica, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, psoriatic arthritis, psoriasis, pure red cell aplasia, reactive arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, rheumatic fever/rheumatic heart diseases, rheumatism (unspecified), sarcoidosis, scleroderma (localized), Sjögren’s disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, systemic sclerosis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, ulcerative colitis, uveitis, vasculitis, and vitiligo (Supplementary Table 1). To ensure incident cases, only diagnoses occurring after baseline assessment were included, with the earliest diagnosis dates used for classification. The primary outcome was defined as a composite of any autoimmune diseases, and the secondary outcome included specific autoimmune diseases. Follow-up continued from baseline until the earliest of death, autoimmune disease diagnosis, loss to follow-up, or the study end (October 2021).

Potential confounders

We assessed several potential confounders, including socio-demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Socio-demographic variables were age at baseline, sex, race/ethnicity, income level, education, and TDI [21]. Lifestyle factors consisted of smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, and BMI. Clinical confounders consisted of baseline comorbidities such as cardiovascular diseases (a composite of atrial fibrillation, heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, transient ischemic attack), hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cancer. Comorbidities were identified based on the presence of the date of first occurrences for each disease, as recorded in the UK Biobank using mapped ICD-10 codes. These records were collected through self-reports at assessment centers, hospitalization records, primary care or death records.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Differences between oral health groups were assessed using absolute standardized mean differences. For missing variables, no imputation was performed. Participants missing body mass index or Townsend Deprivation Index were excluded at the eligibility stage, whereas categorical covariates with missing values (income, education, smoking, alcohol) were handled using a missing-indicator methods. Given our prospective cohort design with time-to-event data, we employed Cox proportional hazard regression models to estimate crude and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the primary outcome of any autoimmune disease. Models were adjusted for socio-demographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders. We assessed proportional hazards assumption using scaled Schoenfeld residuals, supplemented by graphical inspection of the residual plots. As this test has high power to detect minor, clinically unimportant departures in very large samples, its results were interpreted together with the magnitude of the time trend in the residual plots rather than on statistical significance alone. Cumulative incidence curves with log-rank tests compared survival between oral health groups. We further conducted analyses for specific oral health conditions and the number of oral health issues, with adjusted HRs estimated for participants reporting one to six issues compared to those with no self-reported oral health issues. Trend analysis was performed using P-trend tests.

For the secondary outcome, we applied adjusted Cox regression models for each of the 39 autoimmune diseases individually. To address multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction to control family-wise error rate, thereby minimizing the risk of type I errors. All statistical analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.3.3), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05, and P < 0.05 for interaction in subgroup analyses.

Subgroup, sensitivity, and post-hoc analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted by age (≥ 65 vs. <65), sex (female vs. male), and smoking status (never vs. ever), with interaction p-values assessing heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses were performed using 2-year and 5-year lag times to minimize reverse causality and detection bias [22]. As dentures may reflect prior tooth loss rather than an active pathological condition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding participants who reported only the condition ‘dentures’ from the analysis. Additionally, analyses were repeated excluding immunocompromised participants (those with cancer, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV], or prior organ transplantation) to ensure robustness of findings. Post-hoc analyses were additionally conducted for primary biliary cholangitis, which showed the strongest association with any self-reported oral health issues in the main analysis, to identify specific oral health indicators contributing to this relationship.

Results

Characteristics of study population

Among 451,404 participants in the UK Biobank database, 274,206 (60.7%) were classified as having no self-reported oral health issues, while 177,198 (39.3%) were classified as having any self-reported oral health issues (Table 1). Overall, the mean age at recruitment was 56.4 years, and 54.2% of the participants were female. The majority were White (94.3%), followed by Asian (2.2%), Black (1.6%), Mixed (0.6%), and other races (1.2%). Participants with no self-reported oral health issues had higher income and education levels, while current smoking was more prevalent in those with any self-reported oral health issues group (12.1%) compared to the no self-reported oral health issues group (9.3%).

Association between any self-reported oral health issues and autoimmune diseases

In this study, we identified 31,476 incident cases of autoimmune diseases among the study population, with a median age at diagnosis of 68.4 years and a median time to diagnosis of 7.8 years (Supplementary Table 2). We found that any self-reported oral health issues were significantly associated with developing autoimmune diseases (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.09–1.14) (Table 2). Cumulative incidence curves showed significant differences in survival between oral health groups (log-rank p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Among specific categories of oral health issues, painful gums had the strongest association with autoimmune diseases (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.31–1.47), followed by mouth ulcers (1.23, 95% CI 1.18–1.27), toothache (1.21, 95% CI 1.15–1.27), dentures (1.13, 95% CI 1.10–1.16), loose teeth (1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.16), and bleeding gums (1.08, 95% CI 1.04–1.12). Moreover, we identified an increasing trend of association between the number of oral health issues and the risk of autoimmune diseases (per 1–issue increase HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.08–1.11, P-trend < 0.001). Compared to participants with no self-reported oral health issues, those reporting one to six oral health issues had HRs of 1.08 (95% CI 1.05–1.10), 1.21 (95% CI 1.16–1.26), 1.35 (95% CI 1.25–1.45), 1.30 (95% CI 1.12–1.52), 1.58 (95% CI 1.22–2.05), and 1.90 (95% CI 1.21–2.99), respectively.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

In the subgroup analyses, significant heterogeneity was observed across age and sex. The association between any self-reported oral health issues and autoimmune diseases was stronger in participants under 65 years compared to those aged 65 or older (HR<65y 1.12, 95% CI 1.09–1.16; HR≥65y 1.09, 95% CI 1.05–1.14; p-for-interaction = 0.03), and in females compared to males (HRfemale 1.15, 95% CI 1.11–1.18; HRmale 1.07, 95% CI 1.04–1.11; p-for-interaction = 0.002) (Table 3). Results for sensitivity analyses showed the robustness of the findings, even after adopting a 2-year and a 5-year lag, excluding denture-only participants, and excluding immunocompromised patients.

Significantly higher associations were observed in participants under 65 years for vasculitis (HR<65y 1.27, 95% CI 1.12–1.44 vs. HR≥65y 0.99, 95% CI 0.84–1.18; p-for-interaction = 0.02) (Supplementary Table 3). In subgroup analysis by sex, stronger associations were observed for females in Crohn’s disease (HRfemale 1.40, 95% CI 1.16–1.69 vs. HRmale 1.06, 95% CI 0.86–1.30; p-for-interaction = 0.04), psoriasis (HRfemale 1.27, 95% CI 1.13–1.43 vs. HRmale 1.07, 95% CI 0.95–1.20; p-for-interaction = 0.04), and rheumatic fever/rheumatic heart diseases (HRfemale 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.18 vs. HRmale 0.99, 95% CI 0.93–1.05; p-for-interaction = 0.03) (Supplementary Table 4). For smoking status, we identified substantial heterogeneity for primary sclerosing cholangitis (HRnever smoker 0.98, 95% CI 0.82–1.18 vs. HRever smoker 1.32, 95% CI 1.13–1.55; p-for-interaction = 0.01) and vasculitis (HRnever smoker 1.29, 95% CI 1.12–1.49 vs. HRever smoker 1.08, 95% CI 0.93–1.25; p-for-interaction = 0.03) (Supplementary Table 5).

The findings of each individual autoimmune disease remained robust across several sensitivity analyses. First, the associations between oral health and individual autoimmune disease remained statistically significant after applying a 2-year lag (Supplementary Table 6). Second, when the definition of any self-reported oral health issues was refined to exclude participants whose only reported condition was ‘dentures’, the patterns for the specific autoimmune diseases identified also remained largely consistent, with minor variations in statistical significance (Supplementary Table 7). Third, after excluding immunocompromised participants, the observed associations were similar with our main findings, with HRs varying less than 10%. In post-hoc analyses for primary biliary cholangitis, several specific oral health indicators showed significant associations. The highest risks were observed for the presence of dentures (HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.36–2.73; p < 0.01), loose teeth (1.79, 1.01–3.18; p = 0.04), bleeding gums (1.72, 1.17–2.53; p = 0.01), and mouth ulcers (1.70, 1.10–2.62; p = 0.02), whereas painful gums and toothache were not significantly associated.

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study, we found a significant association between any self-reported oral health issues and the incidence of autoimmune disease at a population level. Regardless of the type, oral health condition was associated with an elevated risk of developing autoimmune diseases, with the strongest associations observed for painful gums, followed by mouth ulcers and toothache. We also identified an incremental association between the number of oral health issues and autoimmune diseases. Of the 39 autoimmune diseases assessed, any self-reported oral health issues were associated with seven types of autoimmune diseases, including primary biliary cholangitis, rheumatism, lichen planus, Sjögren’s disease, pernicious anemia, rheumatoid arthritis, and psoriasis. Subgroup analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity by age and sex, with higher risk observed in participants who are either under 65 years or female. Findings remained consistent across sensitivity analyses, including 2- and 5-year lag, refining exposure definition, and the exclusion of immunocompromised patients, underscoring the robustness of our findings.

Although our study did not include oral microbiome profiling, prior studies suggest that microbiota may influence immune regulation, including early-life immune development, innate and adaptive immunity, metabolite production, and pattern recognition [23–25]. Oral microbiome dysbiosis has been proposed to contribute to autoimmune disease development through several pathways, including microbial translocation, molecular mimicry, antigenic stimulation, and cytokine-mediated immune amplification [6]. In this context, self-reported oral health issues may serve as indirect markers of oral inflammatory burden rather than direct measures of specific microbial changes. For instance, conditions involving direct mucosal disruption, such as mouth ulcers and bleeding gums, may facilitate the systemic entry of oral pathogens or their byproducts, potentially contributing to immune dysregulation [7, 26]. In contrast, painful gums and toothache may be better interpreted as non-specific symptom-based indicators of active oral inflammation or overall oral disease burden rather than direct markers of mucosal barrier disruption. Therefore, although painful gums showed the strongest association among the six self-reported oral health indicators, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken to indicate a distinct causal pathway. The significant associations observed for ‘loose teeth’ and ‘dentures’ are likely to reflect different but related pathways. Loose teeth typically indicate advanced periodontal disease with established pathogen reservoirs and chronic inflammatory burden, which may contribute to sustained systemic inflammation. Denture use should be interpreted cautiously, as it may represent a marker of advanced prior tooth loss rather than an active inflammatory condition. Denture use has been associated with altered oral microbiota composition, including increased colonization by oral pathogens such as Candida species and reduced microbial diversity [27, 28], which may contribute to oral inflammatory burden in susceptible individuals. Importantly, our sensitivity analysis excluding participants who reported dentures as their only oral health issue yielded results consistent with the main analysis, suggesting that the observed association was not driven solely by denture use. In this study, we also observed a significant dose-response relationship between the number of oral health issues and autoimmune disease risk, suggesting that the cumulative burden of oral health problems may be associated with increased autoimmune disease development.

The strongest observed association was between any self-reported oral health issues and primary biliary cholangitis. In our post-hoc analysis, this association was found to be specifically driven by mouth ulcers, bleeding gums, and loose teeth. This finding is biologically plausible and is compatible with hypotheses involving mucosal disruption and microbial translocation along the oral–gut–liver axis [29]. However, as oral microbial profiles were not measured, these mechanisms should be interpreted as hypotheses rather than confirmed pathways derived from our data. Conditions like mouth ulcers and bleeding gums represent a direct breach of the oral mucosal barrier, which could facilitate the systemic dissemination of oral pathogens and their byproducts. P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and Streptococcus spp. have been reported to share epitopes with the autoantigen pyruvate dehydrogenase complex E2 (PDC-E2), a proposed trigger in primary biliary cholangitis pathogenesis [6]. Similarly, the oral-gut-skin axis may explain the link between poor oral health and skin-related autoimmune diseases such as psoriasis and lichen planus [30], suggesting the microbiome’s systemic influence across multiple body sites. As metabolites derived from gut microbiota, such as short-chain fatty acids and trimethylamine, are known to play an important role in autoimmunity [31], translocation of bacteria and their metabolic byproducts disrupts cutaneous homeostasis, affecting the development of autoimmune skin diseases [30]. Additionally, a meta-analysis showed significant associations for lichen planus (OR 1.90, 95% CI 1.06–3.40) [10] and psoriasis (OR 2.87, 95% CI 1.75–4.69) [11], supporting our findings.

We observed a positive association for rheumatoid arthritis, consistent with previous epidemiological and experimental literature on P. gingivalis and rheumatoid arthritis [32–35]. However, as our study assessed self-reported oral health issues rather than specific pathogens or oral microbiome profiles, we cannot determine whether P. gingivalis or other oral microorganisms mediated this association. These findings should therefore be interpreted as population-level epidemiological evidence that is consistent with, but does not establish, microbiome-mediated mechanisms.

We also found associations between any self-reported oral health issues and Sjögren’s disease, further supporting the association between oral inflammation and autoimmune diseases affecting mucosal sites. This finding is consistent with a nationwide population-based cohort study in Taiwan, which demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of primary Sjögren’s syndrome among patients with chronic periodontitis (HR 1.79, 95% CI 1.60–2.00) [14]. This association may be explained by the microbial polypeptide activation hypothesis. Peptides from the oral microbiota, such as Peptide-P106 from Capnocytophaga ochracea, activate Ro60-reactive T cells, triggering autoimmune responses in Sjögren’s disease [36]. The association between oral health and Sjögren’s disease may also be bidirectional, as Sjögren’s disease itself can lead to oral manifestations, including immune-mediated oral lesions and increased plaque accumulation [37, 38]. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as epidemiological evidence consistent with a close relationship between oral health and Sjögren’s disease, rather than direct evidence of a microbiome-mediated mechanism.

Notably, we provide novel epidemiological evidence on the association between any self-reported oral health issues and pernicious anemia, which has been limited to case reports [39]. Although the direct impact remains unclear, periodontal disease may contribute to digestive tract inflammation and systemic immune dysregulation. This could potentially influence autoimmune responses targeting gastric parietal cells and intrinsic factors [40]. Our finding highlights a previously unrecognized link between oral health and autoimmune conditions, warranting further research to elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Subgroup analyses indicated heterogeneity by sex, with stronger associations between any self-reported oral health issues and Crohn’s disease and psoriasis among females. These findings suggest potential sex-specific differences in the relationship between oral health and autoimmune disease risk, although cautious interpretation is warranted given the observational design, disease-specific subgroup comparisons, and the absence of direct measurements of sex hormone levels or microbiome profiles. Sex-related differences in mucosal immunity, epithelial barrier function, and microbiome composition may provide a biologically plausible explanation. Estrogen receptor β, which is expressed in epithelial tissues including the gastrointestinal tract, has been implicated in maintaining epithelial integrity, modulating inflammatory responses, and shaping host–microbiome interactions [41, 42]. Therefore, differences in estrogen signaling may influence susceptibility to barrier dysfunction or microbial translocation along the oral–gut–skin axes, which could be relevant to Crohn’s disease or psoriasis. Further studies incorporating hormonal, immunologic, and microbiome data are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these sex-specific associations.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the prospective cohort design established a temporal association and minimizes reverse causation by applying various lag analyses. Second, the large cohort enables a comprehensive evaluation across multiple autoimmune diseases, providing sufficient statistical power to analyze rare conditions such as primary biliary cholangitis and lichen planus. Third, we evaluated various dimensions of any self-reported oral health issues, such as types of oral health conditions and the number of oral health conditions, allowing for an in-depth understanding of the association of autoimmune diseases. Our findings demonstrate consistent positive associations across different self-reported oral health indicators, support a temporal sequence, and show a dose-response pattern. However, given the observational design and lack of microbiome data, causal mechanisms cannot be established and should be evaluated in future studies.

However, this study remains limited. First, self-reported oral health issues may introduce recall bias or misclassification. In particular, self-reported mouth ulcers could not distinguish recurrent aphthous stomatitis from traumatic ulcers or ulcers secondary to systemic diseases, such as Behçet’s disease, which may have introduced exposure misclassification. Although self-reported oral health measures may be subject to information bias, previous validation studies have suggested acceptable reliability for use in epidemiological research [43]. Second, self-reported oral health issues was assessed only at baseline, limiting the ability to capture changes over time. Third, although oral hygiene behaviors, including tooth brushing and flossing, are important for maintaining oral health, information on these behaviors was not available in the dataset. Therefore, residual confounding by unmeasured oral hygiene practices cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, although 2- and 5-year lag analyses were applied, residual reverse causation cannot be fully excluded, as subclinical autoimmune processes may have influenced oral health at baseline; this is particularly relevant for conditions such as lichen planus, whose oral lesions may represent an early mucosal manifestation of pre-existing immune dysregulation. Fifth, autoimmune disease diagnoses were based on ICD codes, which may not capture all cases, particularly those diagnosed in primary care settings. However, since autoimmune diseases are typically diagnosed through specialized clinical evaluations, misclassification is likely minimal. Previous Swedish validation study reported that positive predictive value of a single ICD-10 code for psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis ranged from 81 to 100% and 63–92%, respectively [44]. While these findings may not directly apply to our study, future research validating autoimmune disease diagnoses will be necessary to further strengthen and reassure our findings. Lastly, UK Biobank participants are generally healthier and more socioeconomically advantaged than the general population due to volunteer selection, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

In this large prospective cohort study, any self-reported oral health issues were associated with an increased risk of incident autoimmune diseases. The associations were observed across several types of oral health issues, and the risk increased with the number of reported oral health issues, supporting a potential cumulative relationship. By evaluating a broad spectrum of autoimmune conditions, this study provides comprehensive population-level evidence on the association between self-reported oral health issues and autoimmune disease risk, including a novel association with pernicious anemia. These findings suggest that self-reported oral health issues may serve as a clinically relevant marker of autoimmune disease risk. Further studies using objective periodontal measures and microbiome data are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms and determine whether improving oral health can influence autoimmune disease development.
