The effect of di̇fferent preheati̇ng methods on the intrapulpal temperature of bulk-fi̇ll composi̇te resi̇ns
Authors: Hilal Ateş, Merve İşcan Yapar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of various preheating methods applied to bulk-fill composite resins on temperature changes within the pulp chamber.Materials and MethodsTen sound human molars were used. Each tooth was sectioned 2 mm apical to the cementoenamel junction, and the occlusal surface was flattened to leave a 2 mm dentin thickness. Four bulk-fill composite resins were applied at five temperatures (24 °C, 37 °C, 54 °C, 65 °C, and 68 °C) and polymerized using an LED curing unit. Intrapulpal temperature changes were measured with a K-type thermocouple connected to a data logger in a setup simulating pulpal microcirculation. In total, twenty measurements were taken per tooth under each condition. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and post-hoc LSD tests (p < 0.05).ResultsThe highest intrapulpal temperature increase was observed at 65 °C in all groups using the VisCalor dispenser. The critical temperature threshold was not exceeded in any sample. Significant differences were found between certain temperatures within individual resin groups (p < 0.05), particularly at 65 °C compared to lower temperatures. However, no statistically significant differences were found between different resin types at the same temperatures (p > 0.05).ConclusionPreheating of bulk-fill composite resins led to an increase in intrapulpal temperature; however, this rise remained below the threshold that could cause irreversible pulpal damage.Clinical relevancePreheating bulk-fill composites enhances handling and adaptation but may increase intrapulpal temperature. In this study, the temperature rise remained below the critical threshold, suggesting that the procedure is clinically safe.

Introduction

Composite resins have been among the preferred restorative materials for posterior teeth for many years. The main reasons for this are their long manipulation time and the possibility of direct light polymerization [1, 2]. However, they also have some disadvantages such as the difficulty of polymerization in deep cavities due to limited light penetration [1]. The most common method to overcome this problem is to apply composite resins by layering to achieve optimized polymerization [3, 4]. In this technique, composite resins are placed in layers with a maximum thickness of 2 mm to reduce polymerization shrinkage and minimize air bubble formation [5, 6]. However, this procedure is quite time-consuming in dental practice.

Recent advances in resin-based restorative materials have led to the development of bulk-fill composite resins to eliminate the need for layering [7]. Unlike conventional composite resins, bulk-fill composite resins can be applied in layers of 4 mm or thicker at a time. This significantly accelerates the restoration process [8]. Some of these materials are produced directly for posterior restorations, while others are designed as base materials to be coated with composite resins [9]. According to the manufacturers, bulk-fill composite resins show lower polymerization shrinkage, while the depth of polymerization is increased [5, 10].

Despite these advantages, it is often difficult to apply and shape condensable composites into the cavity due to their high viscosity. One of the methods used to overcome this problem is preheating. Preheating reduces the viscosity of the composite and helps it to adapt better to the prepared cavity wall and increase its wettability, thus reducing microleakage [11, 12]. In addition, increasing polymerization temperature increases the monomer conversion rate of the composite resin by increasing radical and monomer mobility. As a result, preheated composite resins gain superior physical and mechanical properties such as higher surface hardness, improved flexural and tensile strength [13, 14]. When the composite is injected into the cavity after being removed from the heating device, its temperature drops rapidly. However, the pulp temperature may still rise during this process and cause iatrogenic damage. This rise in pulp temperature may be affected by factors such as remaining dentin thickness, cavity preparation and the type of composite used [11].

The tooth pulp is a sensitive tissue with high vascularity and is at risk of losing its vitality during tooth preparation and clinical restoration procedures. Under normal conditions, the temperature of the pulp varies between 34 and 35 °C [15, 16]. When external heat is applied to a healthy tooth, irreversible damage thresholds due to the increase in pulp temperature have been determined by previous animal experiments. Accordingly, an increase of 5.5 °C in pulp temperature caused necrosis in 15% of the cases tested, while an increase of 11 °C caused necrosis in 60%, and an increase of 16 °C caused pulp necrosis in all cases [17]. In an in vivo study in human dental pulp, none of the teeth tested developed symptoms related to thermal application and no histologic findings of thermal damage or reversible pulpitis were found in teeth monitored for 60 to 91 days [15].

There is a negative correlation between the temperature changes occurring in the pulp and the thickness of the remaining dentin [18, 19] and the degree of mineralization of dentin [20]. Although dentin has a low thermal conductivity, the potential for pulp damage is higher in deep cavities due to increased tubular surface area [21]. However, many factors such as fluid movement in dentinal tubules, pulp microcirculation and pulp blood flow can affect the thermal behavior of the dentin-pulp complex [19, 22]. Therefore, knowledge of temperature variations and the rate of temperature rise in the pulp chamber is of great importance to predict potential risks associated with the use of relevant clinical devices. Although thermoviscous technology is among the new systems used today, studies examining the effect of this system on pulp health are limited.

In light of this information, the aim of this study is to examine the temperature changes created in the pulp by both bulk-fill composite resins heated with current preheating methods and composite resin with thermoviscous technology, using a model that mimics pulp blood microcirculation.

The null hypotheses of this study are “there is no significant difference between the temperature changes in the pulp caused by preheating different bulk-fill composite resins using different composite heating devices” and “there is no significant difference between different composites at the same temperature value”.

Material and method

This study was supported by Atatürk University Scientific Research Projects Coordinatorship (BAP) (TDH-2024–13940) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Atatürk University Faculty of Dentistry Dean’s Office with the decision dated 22/02/2024 and numbered 2024-02/20.

In this study conducted under invitro conditions; the temperature changes in the pulp chamber of four different bulk-fill composite resins with different preheating methods were evaluated.

In the study, 4 different bulk-fill composite resins in capsule form and two different preheating devices were used. A calibrated light device (Woodpecker O-Star Wide-Spectrum Curing Light, Guilin, China) was used to control the light power during the experiment. As a result of the power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.4 program (Heinrich-Heine Dusseldorf University, Dusseldorf, Germany), it was determined that the total number of samples for 20 groups should be 199 with a test power of 0.90, an effect size of 0.33 and a margin of error of 0.05. This study was conducted on a total of 200 samples using 10 tooth samples.

Healthy human molar teeth without any defects in terms of restorative procedures were used in the study. The roots of the teeth were cut approximately 2 mm apical from the enamel-cementum junction, and the pulp chamber was completely cleaned with sharp excavators and files. Then, the occlusal surface was removed to a thickness of 2 mm of dentin to ensure standardization.The remaining dentin thickness was measured and verified using a metal caliper (ODC, OD075, Turkey). After the measurement, two notches were prepared on the occlusal surface edges for easy removal of the polymerized material from the tooth. Measurements for all temperature groups were taken sequentially on each tooth, and after all measurements on one tooth were completed, the next tooth was examined. In this way, 20 measurements were taken for each tooth, and a total of 200 measurement values were obtained from 10 teeth.

The microcirculation setup described by Savaş et al. [23] was used to simulate pulpal blood flow. For this, a serum infusion set was used as a reservoir. The reservoir was positioned 1 m above the ground. The sampling plane was positioned 20 cm below the reservoir so that the pressure of the flowing water was 20 mm/hg. A transparent hard plastic plate was used to keep the circulation device and the teeth stationary during the experiment. Two holes were drilled very close to each other on this plate to ensure water circulation and transparent syringe tips were placed in these two holes. as shown in Fig. 1. The syringe tips were shortened to 1 mm above the plate and glued with fluid composite. A third hole was drilled and fixed to accommodate the thermocouple tip.(Fig. 2).

A thermal paste (Arctic Mx-4, Turkey) with high thermal conductivity between − 40 and + 250 °C was applied to the pulp chamber ceiling of the prepared teeth to facilitate temperature conduction. The teeth were then fixed to the plate with the help of a flowable composite. (Fig. 3)

Before applying the restorative materials to the teeth, the bulk-fill composite resins in capsule form were preheated. Preheating was performed using a VisCalor Dispenser (VOCO, Germany) and Capsule Heater (VOCO, Germany). The composite resins were preheated to 65 °C with the Viscalor dispenser and 37 °C, 54 °C and 68° with the Capsule Heater, respectively.

The Viscalor Dispenser was used in 1 mode for Viscalor composite resin and in 2 mode for other composite resins in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Preheating was performed by using the capsule heater according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the control group, composite resins at room temperature were used without any preheating process.

A circumferential matrix tape (Supermat Matrix System, Kerr/America) was attached to the tooth fixed on the plate and adjusted to 4 mm, then recording was started for temperature measurement and composite resin was applied. (Fig. 4)

Fig. 4Tooth specimen with the automatrix band in place and subsequent application of composite resin

Tooth specimen with the automatrix band in place and subsequent application of composite resin

Polymerization was achieved for 10 s in soft-start mode with the light device in contact with the tape in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. After the polymerization was completed, data recording was terminated. After the obtained values were recorded, the tooth model was prepared again for further measurements. After each application, the surface was checked with a loop and cleaned with damp cotton wool to verify that the composite material was completely removed. Between the procedures, the tooth was allowed to cool down for 2 min and then reapplication was performed. To ensure that the microcirculation apparatus was in working order, the water flow was checked before taking a new measurement. The output power of the light device (Woodpecker O-Star Wide-Spectrum Curing Light, Guilin, China) was verified every three samples using the calibration feature of the light device.

A K-type thermocouple data logger (3891G, Cem DT, Turkey), which can exchange data with the computer environment and measure the temperature change every 200 msec, was used to record the temperature changes in the study. The data logger was positioned so that the thermocouple tip was in contact with the thermal paste placed on the dental crown. Calculations were made for each sample by taking the differences between the highest temperature values reached before polymerization (initial) and during light application (Δt).

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the study were analyzed by loading into the SPSS 20 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) statistical package program. First, variance homogeneity and distribution of the data were analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since the data were normally distributed, a two-way ANOVA and a post hoc LSD multiple-comparison test were performed to evaluate differences in temperature changes among the composites. Statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.

Results

When the temperature change (increase) in the pulp after the application of composite resins at different temperatures obtained with different devices was evaluated, the highest temperature increase was observed at 65 °C obtained with the Viscalor gun without composite resin difference. In the Viscalor composite resin groups, there was a statistically significant difference between the temperature obtained with the Viscalor gun (65 °C) group and the room temperature (24 °C) group (p = 0.007) and 54 °C group (p = 0.037), and no statistically significant difference was found between the remaining temperature groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Since the first day they started to be used in dentistry, the monomer structures, filling technologies and physicochemical properties of composite resins have been continuously developed and advances in this field continue [24].In parallel with this, advances in the mechanical and aesthetic properties of posterior composites have brought along the expectation of ease of use. In this direction, the bulk-fill technique and the materials applied with this technique have become very popular recently.

The increased proximity to the pulp chamber in deep cavities may increase the risk of pulpal damage during application and polymerization of the restorative material [25]. In addition, temperature changes caused by preheating restorative materials also increase this risk. Bulk-fill composites have a lower filler ratio compared to conventional resins, but contain larger filler particles, which increase light transmission [26, 27]. Thus, while deeper polymerization is achieved, heat increase also occurs during polymerization. In the study by Yasa et al., the effect of bulk-fill composites on intrapulpal temperature was evaluated and it was shown that these materials caused a higher temperature increase [28]. This is an important consideration for the protection of pulpal health.

The four bulk-fill composite resins used in the study differ in terms of organic matrix composition, filler type, and filler percentage. Variations in the organic matrix structure of the material can contribute to pulp temperature increase by affecting the amount of exothermic heat released during polymerization. Filtek One Bulk-fill has a lower filler ratio, while X-trafil has the highest filler content. Viscalor exhibits viscosity changes after heating due to its aliphatic dimethacrylate matrix structure. Tetric EvoCeram contains glass fillers with prepolymer content. These differences may cause the materials to respond differently in terms of thermal conductivity, viscosity behavior, and polymerization heat [12, 29–31].

Dentists often have difficulty placing and shaping these materials in cavities due to the high viscosity of reboundable composites [32]. Preheating composites is an effective method to reduce viscosity. Lower viscosity allows the composite to better conform to the cavity walls and better wetting of the surface, minimizing microleakage [11]. In addition, the increase in temperature during polymerization increases monomer mobility.

In preheated resin systems, monomer conversion occurs more efficiently at higher temperatures compared to systems polymerized at room temperature in terms of both extent and speed [14, 33]. Accordingly, manufacturers have developed thermoviscous composite resin materials with thermally reduced viscosity and ease of application to increase clinical efficacy and physical properties. These materials reach lower viscosity by heating during application and provide more homogeneous adaptation to the cavity. In studies, it was observed that the conversion rate increased significantly, the polymerization process accelerated and the polymerization rate increased in preheated resin composites [14, 34]. However, given that the existing literature on the effects of thermoviscous composite resins on pulp temperature is limited, we believe that the findings of this study could make significant contributions to the field.

In our study, composite resins were applied to dentin surfaces at five different temperature levels, and the resulting temperature changes in the pulp were evaluated. While 24 °C represents room temperature, the other temperature values reflect the levels achievable with the heating devices used. The highest temperature used in the study, 68 °C, represents the maximum heat value that the devices can reach. Although higher temperatures were expected to affect pulp heat increase, these temperatures were excluded from the scope of the study as they could not be reached with the available devices [35, 36].

In restorative procedures performed with preheated composite resins, the application of a binding agent to dentin may have a positive effect on reducing heat conduction under clinical conditions. However, studies have reported that the application or non-application of dentin binding agent did not make a significant difference on the pulp temperature increase [28, 37].

The type and duration of light used in the polymerization process are the main factors affecting the temperature increase. Therefore, the properties of light sources should be well known for successful results in aesthetic dentistry [38]. Lloyd et al. reported that the most important reason for the temperature rise during polymerization is the heat generated by the light devices [39]. Studies have shown that halogen and LED light devices with low energy output cause lower temperature increase in the pulp compared to plasma arc lights with high energy output [40–42]. Similarly, LED light devices have been reported to produce less temperature rise and reduce the risk of pulpal injury compared to quartz-tungsten-halogen light sources [43–45]. It has also been emphasized that the distance between the restorative material and the light device during polymerization affects the pulpal temperature rise [46].

Various comparative studies have shown that different dentin morphologies in the teeth used in the studies play an important role in limiting the temperature increase that may occur in the pulp chamber during the restorative procedures [47, 48]. While some studies in the literature used different teeth for each measurement [49, 50], some studies utilized a single tooth for each group [37, 42, 46]. In studies where different teeth were used, it was thought that the morphological differences that may exist between the teeth may cause variability in the thermal conductivity of the samples tested, especially in terms of enamel and dentin structure and thickness [48, 51, 52]. Since it was determined that tooth replacement could negatively affect standardization in each measurement, and that performing all measurements on a single tooth would limit representativeness, in our study, all temperature measurements were completed on each tooth before moving on to the next tooth, and measurements were performed using a total of 10 tooth samples [28].

In the literature, it has been reported that the risk of thermal damage to the pulp increases with dentin thickness thinner than 1 mm and that dentin thickness forms a critical barrier in temperature transmission [53, 54]. It has also been emphasized that dentin thickness is more determinant than the type of material used and its thermal properties [55]. In various studies, the temperature changes occurring in different dentin thicknesses during polymerization were investigated by preparing both isolated dentin discs [27, 51, 53, 56, 57] and standard cavities in extracted teeth [58, 59]. These studies show that the increase in pulpal temperature decreases with increasing dentin thickness [56, 60]. It has also been reported that existing tooth walls may limit the temperature increase by absorbing heat [60]. Therefore, in some studies [28], this thermal insulation effect was eliminated by completely removing the occlusal surface. With the same approach, in the present study, the occlusal surface was removed and a dentin thickness of 2 mm was left in order to evaluate the heat distribution more accurately.

Pulp tissue is vulnerable to thermal stimuli during restorative procedures that threaten its viability [61]. Pulpal microcirculation provides a cooling mechanism by stabilizing heat through the dentin-pulp complex [19, 42, 62, 63]. Studies have shown that the microcirculation limits pulpal temperature rise in vitro, which gives more realistic results [19, 22]. Although microcirculation cannot fully reflect biological details such as nervous system and dentinal fluid movements, it is not possible to mimic these factors in vitro. Therefore, a microcirculation setup was used in this study to take into account the cooling effect of the pulp.

In studies simulating pulpal microcirculation, water flow was usually provided at room temperature [22, 23, 64]. In some studies, a temperature of 37 °C was preferred to create an environment closer to physiologic conditions [65, 66]. However, it has been reported that temperature loss occurs during transportation from the water bath to the pulp chamber and additional systems are needed to prevent this situation [19, 22, 65]. Therefore, in this study, water flow was performed at room temperature.

In line with the data obtained as a result of the study, our hypothesis that “there is no significant difference between the temperature changes in the pulp caused by the preheating process applied to different bulk-fill composite resins using different composite heating devices” was rejected, while our hypothesis that “there is no significant difference between different composites at the same temperature value” was accepted. Although the preheating process applied to the composite resins caused a temperature increase in the pulp, the amount of this increase was not at a level that could cause irreversible damage to the pulp.

In a study by Daronch et al. [14], approximately 50% of the temperature was lost within 2 min after the composite material was removed from the heating device, and this loss reached 90% in the 5th minute. Similarly, another study showed that the temperature decreased rapidly after the composite resin was removed from the heating device [67]. Therefore, in order to prevent heat loss, the tubes were kept in the device during temperature measurement and the time for placing the composite material into the cavity did not exceed 40 s [68]. In our study, the temperature values of the composites applied with the Viscalor gun were found to be higher compared to the other groups, and this is thought to be due to the fact that the Viscalor gun was applied directly to the tooth without removing the composite from the heating device.

Pulp tissue is very sensitive to temperature changes occurring in the tooth. In a study conducted by Zach et al. on monkeys, it was observed that when the temperature in the pulp increased by 5.5 °C, irreversible damage occurred in 15% of the teeth and when it increased by 11.1 °C, irreversible damage occurred in 60% of the teeth [17]. The temperature increase values obtained during our study did not exceed the critical value of 5.5.

The temperature changes that occur during the polymerization of resin materials depend on factors such as filler type, particle size, resin matrix volume and organic matrix ratio used in restorative materials [27]. In studies comparing flowable and condensable composites, it was reported that the highest temperature increase occurred in flowable composites and this was due to the exothermic reaction due to low filler ratio and high resin content [69]. Similarly, it was reported that the highest temperature increase after light polymerization was observed in flowable composites with low inorganic particle content (39.7%) [70]. In addition, it was found that the temperature increases in pulp tissue during polymerization of different bulk-fill materials in deciduous teeth were lower in materials with high filler content [71]. In the present study, there was no significant difference between the groups after preheating in the X-trafil groups with the highest filler content (86%), while the temperature increases increased significantly in the Filtek groups with the lowest filler content (76%). This is thought to be due to the differences in filler ratio.

There are various factors that can lead to an increase in pulpal temperature during the polymerization of restorative materials [37, 49]. One of these factors is the pulpal temperature increase caused by light devices [72]. It has been reported that the turbo mode of the LED light source causes more pulpal temperature increase in the polymerization of bulk-fill composite resins and it is recommended to polymerize the material with soft-mode to reduce the temperature increase in the restoration of deep cavities [73]. In addition, it was reported that the highest temperature increase occurred in Valo Xtra power mode (3200 mW/cm², 3-second irradiation), which has the highest light power and the shortest curing time, but these increases were not at a level that would cause thermal damage to the pulp [22]. In our study, it is thought that the temperature increase observed in the room temperature group may have been caused by the applied light source.

The findings of this study should be evaluated within certain methodological limitations. First, the experiments were conducted under laboratory conditions, at room temperature, and in a static model lacking the physiological microcirculation specific to pulp tissue. These conditions, in which there is no fluid flow in the dentinal tubules and surrounding periodontal structures, do not fully reflect the heat transfer and distribution processes in the clinical environment. However, pulp microcirculation and periodontal tissues are important factors that limit the increase in intrapulpal temperature to physiological levels by increasing heat convection in the clinical situation. Therefore, the direct transfer of the obtained data to clinical scenarios should be done with caution. In addition, the study is limited to a dentin thickness of 2 mm, and possible thermal effects that may occur in thinner dentin layers have not been evaluated. This situation indicates that the fact that the temperature response may vary in teeth with different anatomical variations should not be ignored. Considering these methodological limitations, further studies simulating pulp microcirculation and including different dentin thicknesses are required to validate the study’s results and model clinical conditions more realistically.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that an increase in pulp temperature occurred in all groups; however, even in the Viscalor 65 °C group, where the highest increase was observed, this increase remained below levels that could cause tissue damage. Accordingly, it can be stated that the restorative materials and heating devices examined are thermally safe under conditions of 2 mm dentin thickness. However, due to the in vitro design of the study and the inability to simulate pulp microcirculation, the findings obtained cannot be directly generalized to clinical conditions. Furthermore, the fact that only 2 mm of dentin thickness was evaluated indicates that additional research is needed to elucidate the possible thermal effects that may occur in thinner dentin layers.
