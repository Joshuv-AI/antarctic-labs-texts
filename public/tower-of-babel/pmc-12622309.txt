Mechanism of CGF in osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells through the Wnt pathway
Authors: Bing Zhou, Wentu Zhou, Xin Li, Cheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePeriodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), undifferentiated mesenchymal cells with multipotent differentiation and self-renewal capacities, constitute the optimal MSC population for periodontal regeneration. This study sought to elucidate the effects of concentrated growth factor (CGF) on the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs and analyze the underlying mechanisms.MethodsPDLSCs were isolated from the molars of patients with malocclusion and characterized by flow cytometry, osteogenic induction, lipogenic induction, ARS staining and ORO staining. PDLSCs were treated with osteogenic induction medium containing different concentrations of CGF. The osteogenic ability of CGF in PDLSCs was analyzed via ALP staining, ARS staining, and ALP activity assays. WNK1, RUNX2 and OPN were detected by RT-qPCR. WNK1, RUNX2, OPN, β-catenin, GSK3β and p-GSK3β were detected by WB. The role of CGF in PDLSC osteogenic differentiation through the Wnt pathway was verified.ResultsPDLSCs were successfully isolated and cultured in vitro. After CGF treatment, ALP activity, mineralization nodule formation, and the expression of RUNX2 and OPN in PDLSCs were increased, with 0.1 mg/mL CGF showing the best osteogenic differentiation ability. WNK1, β-catenin, and p-GSK3β/GSK3β were elevated. CGF activated the Wnt pathway through WNK1. The promoting effects of CGF on osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs were partially reversed after inhibition of WNK1 and the Wnt pathway.ConclusionCGF promotes PDLSC osteogenic differentiation by activating the Wnt pathway through WNK1.Keywords: CGF, WNK1, Wnt path, osteogenic differentiation, PDLSCs, ALP, RUNX2, OPN

Introduction

Periodontitis is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease and induces progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, affecting 11% of global population.1 Periodontal tissue regeneration is an important approach to promote periodontal healing.2 Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), defined as multipotent progenitors exhibiting multilineage differentiation capacity and in vitro self-renewal plasticity, demonstrate therapeutic potential in musculoskeletal regeneration, immunomodulation, and wound healing.3 Oral tissues are regarded as an easily accessible source of mesenchymal stem cells without ethical concerns, mainly including dental pulp stem cells, stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth, periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), stem cells from apical papilla, dental follicle stem cells, and gingival stem cells.4 Among these, mesenchymal stem cells extracted from human PDLSCs can be easily isolated and manipulated, making them the most commonly used type of mesenchymal stem cells.5 Moreover, as undifferentiated mesenchymal cells located within the periodontal ligament, periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) are characterized by self-renewal capacity, multilineage differentiation potential, and immunomodulatory properties.6 A previous study demonstrated that PDLSC-mediated regeneration of periodontal tissues represents a promising cell therapy strategy for periodontitis.7 In this study, we investigated the potential mechanism of PDLSC osteogenic differentiation, providing a novel basis for the future application of periodontal regeneration.

Concentrated growth factor (CGF), as the latest generation of plasma-derived products, leverages the pivotal role of platelets in tissue regeneration by providing elevated concentrations of growth factors and white blood cells that facilitate cell migration, proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis.8,9 Many studies have shown that CGF plays an important role in osteogenic differentiation and pulp regeneration.10-12 10% CGF significantly induces osteogenesis in human bone marrow MSCs that are directly co-cultured with human umbilical vein endothelial cells.13 CGF enhances osteogenic differentiation in human bone marrow MSCs, as evidenced by increased alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and increased matrix mineralization.12 CGF has been clinically proven to enhance outcomes in regenerative periodontal procedures, including intrabony defects, furcation defects, and alveolar ridge preservation.14 Moreover, CGF promotes PDLSC proliferation within specific concentration ranges.15 CGF promotes the proliferation, migration, and osteo-odontogenic differentiation of human dental pulp stem cells through growth factor-mediated signaling, indicating that CGF is a promising biomaterial for regenerative endodontic therapies.16 Nevertheless, the precise mechanism of CGF on PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation remains unclear.

The WNK lysine-deficient protein kinase family (WNK1-4), an evolutionarily conserved family defined by atypical catalytic lysine positioning, demonstrates direct responsiveness to chloride ions, potassium levels, and osmotic stress.17 WNK1 functions as a macromolecular crowding sensor and orchestrates cellular volume recovery through the activation of sodium‒potassium chloride cotransporters to restore ionic homeostasis.18 WNK1 has been shown to be related to various diseases, such as essential hypertension and osteoporosis.19,20 Another study has demonstrated that WNK1 is involved in the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow MSC.21 However, the specific role of WNK1 in PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation has not been explored. The osteogenic differentiation ability of PDLSCs is closely related to the activation of the Wnt pathway.22,23 The Wnt signaling pathway contributes to osteogenic differentiation and promoting osteogenesis by activating downstream targets of the BMP signaling pathway.24 WNK kinases act as critical enhancers of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.25 CGF promotes gingival regeneration through the Wnt pathway.26 Therefore, we hypothesized that CGF activates the Wnt pathway via WNK1.

In this study, we explored the regulatory role of CGF in the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs via the WNK1-activated Wnt pathway to provide novel therapeutic directions for periodontal tissue regeneration.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the 900th Hospital of Joint Logistic Support Force. Written informed consents were obtained from all participants and guardians.

Cell isolation and culture

Healthy caries-free premolars were obtained from 5 adolescent patients (12−18 y old) with no periodontal tissue disease undergoing orthodontic treatment at our hospital. The 5 premolars were collected in a sterile container and rinsed 3–5 times with phosphate buffer saline (PBS). Periodontal ligament tissues from the middle root surface were scraped, cut into small pieces, placed on the bottom of culture dishes, and cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, 12571063, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, A5670701, ThermoFisher). The medium was renewed every 3 d until the PDLSCs migrated out from the tissue edges. When cells covered approximately 80% of the bottom at the culture medium, the original medium was discarded. The cells were washed with PBS, and added with Trypsin-EDTA solution (25200114, ThermoFisher) for digestion. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment, and the medium was changed at a 1:2 ratio. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were passaged to obtain primary PDLSCs, which were subsequently purified via the limited dilution cloning method. PDLSCs at passages 3−5 were used for follow-up experiments.

Flow cytometry

PDLSCs were resuspended in PBS and aliquoted into six sterile EP tubes. Antibodies against CD44 (561858, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), CD90 (555596, BD Biosciences), CD105 (560839, BD Biosciences), CD73 (561014, BD Biosciences), CD45 (560976, BD Biosciences), and CD34 (560942, BD Biosciences) were added to each tube in the dark, with the control tube receiving an equivalent volume of PBS. Following a 30-minute incubation on ice, the cells were washed with PBS, resuspended, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCelesta™, BD Biosciences).

Osteogenic and lipogenic induction

PDLSCs were inoculated into a six-well plate (2 × 104 cells per well) and cultured in complete medium (BLDM−03011, Oricell, Shanghai, China) in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For osteogenic induction, once the cells reached 70% confluence, the medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium (HUXXC−04021, Oricell) supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.01 µM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate and 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, with replacement every 3 d during the 3-week induction period. After osteogenic induction, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS, ALIR−10001, Oricell). For lipogenic induction, after the cells reached 100% confluence, the medium was replaced with lipogenic induction medium (HUXXC-90031, Oricell) supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mM insulin, 0.5 M hydrocorti-sone, 500 mM isobutyl-methylxanthine and 60 mM indomethacin. After 4 weeks, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with Oil Red O (ORO, OILR−10001, Oricell).

CGF separation and preparation

Venous blood was collected from the same donor and centrifuged in nonanticoagulant EP tubes according to the protocol described by Liugi et al.27 For PDLSC treatment, CGF in the middle layer was isolated, stored at -20 °C overnight, lyophilized using a freeze-dryer (710202040, Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA), and dissolved in osteogenic induction medium at concentrations of 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL.

Cell treatment and group

Upon reaching 80% confluence, PDLSCs were transfected with WNK1-targeting siRNA or the control (si-NC) (Genscript, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L3000075, ThermoFisher). The cells were harvested after 48-h transfection, then cultured in osteogenic induction medium supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL CGF for 7 d and 21 d, respectively.

The Wnt pathway inhibitor multispecific antibody (MSAB) was procured from Med Chem Express (HY-120697, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Prior to the experiments, a 10 mM MSAB solution was prepared with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).28 PDLSCs were treated with 5 μM MSAB (final concentration), with DMSO as the control, followed by the culture in osteogenic induction medium supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL CGF for 7 d and 21 d, respectively.

Additionally, the experimental groups were set as follows: PDLSCs cultured in osteogenic induction medium without CGF (control group); PDLSCs cultured in osteogenic induction medium with 0.1 mg/mL CGF (CGF group); PDLSCs transfected with si-NC in osteogenic induction medium containing 0.1 mg/mL CGF (CGF + si-NC group); PDLSCs transfected with si-WNK1 in osteogenic induction medium containing 0.1 mg/mL CGF (CGF + si-WNK1 group); PDLSCs cultured in osteogenic induction medium with DMSO + 0.1 mg/mL CGF (CGF + DMSO group); PDLSCs cultured in osteogenic induction medium with MSAB + 0.1 mg/mL CGF (CGF + MSAB group).

ALP staining, ARS staining and ALP activity determination

PDLSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 per well and cultured in osteogenic induction medium containing varying concentrations of CGF (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL) for 7 d. ALP activity (U/mg protein) was measured using an ALP assay kit (P0321S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by ALP staining using 5-bromo−4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate/nitro blue tetrazolium kit (C3206, Beyotime). After 21 d of culture, ARS staining was performed.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

On the 7th day of culture, total RNA from PDLSCs was extracted using Trizol reagent (R0016, Beyotime) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using ImProm-II™ reverse transcription system kit (A3800, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 system (A28567, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with PowerTrack™ SYBR Green Master Mix (A46012, Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method,29 with β-actin as the internal parameter. The primers used are listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB) assay

After washing with PBS, PDLSCs were incubated with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013C, Beyotime) and lysed via ultrasonication for centrifugation. BCA standards and working solutions were prepared using the BCA detection kit (P0009, Beyotime). Then, the BCA standards and the solution to be tested were mixed with the BCA working solution. The absorbance at 562 nm was measured, and the protein concentration was quantified according to the standard curve. All protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (1620175, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with WNK1 (ab174854, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), runt-related transcription factor-2 (RUNX2) (ab236639, 1:1000, Abcam), osteopontin (OPN) (ab216406, 1:100, Abcam), β-catenin (ab32572, 1:5000, Abcam), glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β) (ab32391, 1:5000, Abcam), phosphorylation glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (p-GSK3β) (ab75814, 1:10000, Abcam), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (ab181602, 1:10000, Abcam), and β-actin (ab8227, 1:1000, Abcam). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with a secondary IgG antibody (ab97051, 1:2000, Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate kit (WP20005, ThermoFisher), and the images were captured and analyzed using Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, Illinois, USA) to obtain the peak map. The quantified gray values of the bandswere obtained by eliminating the background. Furthermore, considering the difficulties in identifying proteins because of similar band sizes, the expression of each protein was standardized using GAPDH or β-actin respectively based on the band sizes of the proteins.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. First, normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed, confirming the normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. Comparisons between two groups were performed using t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed via one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test for post hoc analysis. The P-value was obtained from a two-tailed test. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Isolation and characterization of PDLSCs

To confirm the mesenchymal stem cell properties of PDLSCs, PDLSCs were isolated and cultured using tissue explant method. On approximately the 7th–10th d, PDLSCs emerged around the tissue fragments and exhibited an elongated spindle-shaped morphology (Figure 1A). Flow cytometry detection of mesenchymal stem cell surface markers revealed positive expression of CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105 in PDLSCs and negative expression of CD45 and CD34 (Figure 1B). PDLSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium and lipogenic induction medium. ARS staining demonstrated the formation of calcified nodules (Figure 1C), and ORO staining revealed abundant lipid accumulation (Figure 1D), confirming the osteogenic and lipogenic differentiation capabilities of PDLSCs. These results collectively demonstrated the successful isolation of PDLSCs.

Isolation and characterization of PDLSCs. PDLSCs were isolated and cultured from healthy premolars without caries. A: The morphology of PDLSCs was observed under a light microscope; B: The expression of CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, CD45 and CD34 was detected via flow cytometry; C: ARS staining results of PDLSCs after osteogenic induction showed that mineralized nodules were stained red; D: ORO staining results of PDLSCs after lipogenic induction revealed that lipid droplets were stained red.

CGF promotes PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation

To investigate the role of CGF in the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of CGF (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL). Results showed no cytotoxic effects of CGF on PDLSCs (P > 0.05, Figure 2A). The cells were cultured in osteogenic induction medium supplemented with varying concentrations of CGF (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL). After culture, ALP-positive staining intensity, ALP activity, and mineralized nodule formation were significantly increased (P < 0.05, Figure 2B–D), along with upregulated expression of RUNX2 and OPN (P < 0.05, Figure 2E, F), indicating that CGF promotes the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. Furthermore, 0.1 mg/mL CGF showing the most potent pro-osteogenic effect was used for subsequent experiments.

CGF promotes PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation. PDLSCs were treated with varying concentrations of CGF (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL), using untreated cells as the control group. A: Cell viability was detected by CCK−8; PDLSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium containing different CGF concentrations (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL); B: ALP staining and ARS staining were performed; C: ALP activity was detected; D: ARS absorbance at 562 nm was measured; E: RT-qPCR was employed to detect the expression of RUNX2 and OPN; F: WB was employed to detect the expression of RUNX2 and OPN. WNK1 inhibition alleviates the promoting effect of CGF on PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of the aforementioned effects, WNK1 expression in the cells of each group was quantified. After CGF treatment, WNK1 expression was significantly upregulated (P < 0.01, Figure 3A), suggesting the potential role of WNK1 in osteogenic differentiation. PDLSCs were transfected with two WNK1-targeting siRNAs to knock down WNK1 expression (P < 0.01, Figure 3B), with si-WNK1-1 selected for subsequent experiments for better knockdown efficiency. Compared to CGF group, the CGF + si-WNK1-1 group presented markedly lower WNK1 expression (P < 0.01, Figure 3C, D), lower ALP-positive staining intensity, ALP activity, and mineralized nodule formation (P < 0.01, Figure 3E–G), and downregulated expression of RUNX2 and OPN (P < 0.01, Figure 3H, I). Collectively, these results demonstrated that WNK1 inhibition attenuates the promoting effect of CGF on PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation.

CGF activates the Wnt pathway via WNK1

To further investigate whether the regulatory mechanism of WNK1 on the osteogenic ability of CGF involves the Wnt pathway, the expression of β-catenin, GSK3β, and p-GSK3β was measured via WB. Compared to the control group, the expression of β-catenin and p-GSK3β/GSK3β in the CGF group was significantly greater (P < 0.01, Figure 4A, B). Conversely, WNK1 knockdown markedly reduced the expression of β-catenin and p-GSK3β/GSK3β (P < 0.01, Figure 4A, B). Taken together, these findings proved that CGF enhances cell osteogenic ability by activating the Wnt pathway via WNK1.

Tukey's multiple comparisons test was applied for post-hoc analyses. **versus the control group, P < 0.01; # # versus the CGF + si-NC group, P < 0.01.

Inhibition of the Wnt pathway attenuates the promoting osteogenic effects of CGF on PDLSCs

To further validate that CGF promotes osteogenic differentiation via the Wnt pathway, MSAB was administered to cells in the control group and the CGF group. The results showed that in cells treated with MSAB, the protein expression of β-catenin, p-GSK3β/GSK3β, and WNK1 was evidently decreased (P < 0.05, Figure 5A). Concurrently, ALP-positive staining intensity, ALP activity, and mineralized nodule formation were markedly diminished (P < 0.01, Figure 5B–D), and the expression of RUNX2 and OPN was downregulated (P < 0.05, Figure 5E, F). These results confirmed that inhibition of the Wnt pathway attenuates the promoting osteogenic effects of CGF on PDLSCs.

Discussion

Periodontal disease is a prevalent oral disorder and arises from bacterial-induced host immune responses that drive inflammatory destruction of periodontal soft and hard tissues.​​​​​​30 PDLSCs exhibit multilineage differentiation capacity and self-renewal plasticity, functioning as primary candidate cells for periodontal regeneration.31 Growing evidence has suggested that promoting osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs represents an effective strategy for periodontal disease treatment.32-34 In this study, we revealed that CGF activates the Wnt pathway through WNK1 and promotes the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, advancing CGF-based periodontal regenerative strategies.

CGF exerts the ability to enhance osteogenic differentiation. For instance, CGF enhances guided tissue regeneration in severe periodontitis bone defects and improves clinical outcomes, periodontal tissue regeneration, and osteoclastic suppression.35 Clinically, CGF can reduce the plaque index and gingival index, promoting the treatment of periodontal intrabony pockets in combination with open flap debridement.36 CGF demonstrates autologous osteoinductive ability in human bone marrow MSCs, accompanied by increased ALP activity and RUNX2 expression.12 Our study confirmed that CGF acted as a promoting role in osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, with 0.1 mg/mL CGF showing the best osteogenic differentiation ability. Another study indicated that PDLSCs display significantly enhanced cell proliferation in 10% CM and 90% CGF composites.15 In mice model, CGF induction increased the quantity of newly regenerated tissues formed by stem cells from the apical papilla and periodontal ligament cells.37 Additionally, CGF possesses favorable biological effects, cost-effectiveness, ease of preparation, and safety profile, demonstrating promising results in intrabony defect regeneration.9 CGF plays an adjunctive role in the regeneration of periodontal intraosseous defect and alveolar ridge defect and the preservation of the alveolar ridge.14 Mechanistically, CGF promotes osteogenic gene expression in human bone marrow MSCs and human umbilical vein endothelial cell co-culture medium by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.13 CGF treatment upregulates the expression of BMP2/SMAD5/RUNX2 and increases ALP activity and mineralized nodules in dental pulp cells.38 Here, after CGF treatment, PDLSCs showed increased ALP-positive staining intensity, ALP activity, and mineralized nodule formation and upregulated expression of RUNX2 and OPN. Beyond periodontal intrabony defects, CGF can be leveraged in adjacent fields, such as alveolar ridge preservation prior to dental implantation or regeneration of peri-implant bone loss, where PDLSC-mediated osteogenesis is critical for long-term implant stability.

Previous studies on CGF have focused on their impact on osteogenic differentiation,12,39 but the specific mechanisms involved have rarely been explored. This study is the first to propose that CGF regulates osteogenic differentiation through the WNK1-Wnt axis. CGF treatment upregulated WNK1 expression. WNK1 inhibition alleviated the promoting effect of CGF on PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation. WNK1 is related to β-O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) modification, and the enhanced transcriptional activity of RUNX2 modified by O-GlcNAc increases the differentiation rate of osteoblasts.40 WNK1 overexpression inhibits macrophage‒osteoclast differentiation by suppressing NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis in ovariectomized mice.19 Elevated WNK1 level triggers the osteogenic differentiation by upregulating the expression of RUNX2, OCN, and OPN in bone marrow MSCs.21 Moreover, our research revealed that CGF activated the Wnt pathway through WNK1. WNKs have been verified to play a role in developmental processes, including blood vessel and neuronal development, through the Wnt signaling pathway.41 Downregulation of WNK1 leads to reduced Wnt signaling in mammalian cell cultures.25 The canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway triggers disheveled-mediated inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase-3 to stabilize cytoplasmic β-catenin, which subsequently translocates into the nucleus to form transcriptional complexes with TCF/LEF factors, thereby regulating osteogenic gene expression and skeletal development.22 In this study, MSAB treatment significantly downregulated β-catenin and p-GSK3β/GSK3β, thereby inhibiting the Wnt pathway, concomitant with reduced ALP activity, impairing mineralization, and suppressing the osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OPN. Our findings demonstrated that CGF activates Wnt signaling to upregulate RUNX2 and OPN, directly linking the biological activity of CGF to a conserved osteogenic pathway in PDLSCs. Our identification of 0.1 mg/mL as the optimal CGF concentration for Wnt activation and PDLSC osteogenesis provides an evidence-based reference to standardize CGF preparation in clinical settings, reducing variability in treatment outcomes. We manifested that the inhibition of the Wnt pathway attenuated the promoting effect of CGF on PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation.

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, DMSO was used as the control for MSAB, but the influence of DMSO on the degree of cell differentiation level was not considered.42 Second, only in vitro osteogenic differentiation of the cell model was conducted, without further validation in physiologically relevant in vivo animal models. The efficacy of CGF in promoting PDLSC-mediated bone formation may be modulated by in vivo microenvironmental cues such as vascularization, mechanical stress, or local cytokine gradients, which could alter Wnt pathway activation dynamics. Third, the complex Wnt pathway to some extent limited drug effectiveness and might lead to off-target effects of MSAB, a Wnt inhibitor, thereby affecting the reliability of the results. Fourth, CGF contained multiple growth factors capable of activating other pathways (such as PI3K/AKT) during treatment, but we focused exclusively on alterations in the Wnt pathway. Finally, the combination of the CGF concentration with clinical application has not yet been considered. Therefore, future investigations should prioritize deep studies of CGF on establishment animal experiments on whether CGF promotes osteogenic differentiation and the role of the Wnt pathway, the addition of another Wnt inhibitor to verify the inhibitory effect, and other signaling pathways (PI3K/AKT, BMP/Smad) and their connections.

In conclusion, CGF enhances osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs by activating the Wnt pathway via WNK1. Our study provides new insights into the mechanisms by which CGF promote osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, promising new therapeutic directions for clinical treatment.
