Introduction

Aphallia is a term that means lack of penis. It is a rare congenital anomaly in which the penis does not develop during the embryonic period [1]. In this condition, the opening of the urethra is not located in the typical place. It is often located in the perineal area. Another term used for this condition is agenesis of the penis. There is controversy over the use of the term ‘aphallia’. A section of scholars believe that the term should be used only when the spongiosis bodies of the penis are absent and an erection cannot develop [2].

Aphalia is a very rare condition with an incidence of about 1 in 10 million to 1 in 30 million births.

This abnormality is associated with other abnormalities of the urinary system such as: renal agenesis, horseshoe kidney, cystic kidney, vesico-ureteral reflux, prostatic agenesis, anular pancreas, flatus and cardiac abnormalities.

Embryonic development of the digestive system and urinary tract is of common onset. Both are part of the cloacal segment and for this reason, a recto-urethral fistula is found in some of these patients. Genetic testing in most of these patients is of the 46XY type.

Case presentation

A child born from a normal pregnancy, without complications, by caesarean section. The mother was 37 years old. Child’s weight 2660 g, height 46 cm, head circumference 33 cm, chest circumference 31 cm. Apgar at the first minute − 9, at the fifth minute − 10. Without pathology of placenta and umbilical cord. The child has stable basic vital functions, with not profuse ocular discharge, without clinical-laboratory evidence of infectious process. He excretes liquid-cough stools due to their mixing with urine, which is excreted through the rectum. Absence of penis, presence of formed scrotum and testicles located in it. The child is well nourished with good nutritional tolerance. Cytogenetic analysis - chromosome analysis of 24/48/72 hour coltures of lymphocytes/bone marrow, fibroblasts/on 11 metaphases with G/C/Ag - band differential staining at 400–450 bands was performed. Normal male karyotype 46, XY.

At the age of one month the child was admitted to the Pediatric Surgery Clinic of St. George University Hospital, St. George. The child was born at the St. George Hospital in Plovdiv. A computed tomographic examination of abdominal organs and small pelvis, native and after intravenous contrast enhancement in parenchyma and late phase was performed:

Kidneys - bilateral with normal tophi, preserved parenchyma and drainage, without concrements. Suspected double pyelo-calyx system on the left. In urographic phase visible proximal segment in right ureter. No contrast material is visualized along the course of the left ureter. Bladder - with preserved wall and sharp contours. Promination noted dorsally - probably fistulous course to rectum. Testicles - appear homogeneous. Liver with preserved size and density characteristics. No focal lesions identified. Unexpanded intra and extrahepatic bile ducts. Gallbladder - with preserved volume, wall and contents. Pancreas - with preserved parenchyma, no focal lesions. From the performed paraclinical examinations - no evidence of deviation from normal values. The child has normal physical and neuro-mental development.

Discussion

Aphallia is a congenital disorder that is very rare. This abnormality develops around the fourth week of embryonic development and is often associated with other abnormalities in fetal development. It is caused by abnormal formation of the genital tube or a disorder in its development. It is an embryonic progenitor mesenchymal tissue and by budding under the genital ectoderm to the cloacal membrane, the damage leads to the development of aphallia [3]. Anterior and cranial urogenital mesoderm plays an essential role in the development of the genital tube by expressing bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4). This leads to the formation of the penis.

Children with aphallia have karyotype XY46. The SRY-type gene is the sex-determining transcription factor on the Y chromosome. This gene expression leads to gonadal development as a male, and anti-Müllerian hormone is secreted by the developing Sertoli cells of the testes so that internal and external sex characteristics characteristic of a male develop. In addition, the external genital system develops under the influence of androgen (testosterone), which plays a key role.In the case we described, cytogenetic analysis revealed a normal male karyotype − 46, XY [4]. The penis is absent and the scrotum has normal development. The testes have normal development and are located in a typical place, in the scrotum. The anus is located in a normal place and urine is passed through it, mixed with the feces.

In patients with aphallia, urine is usually passed from the anterior part of the rectum. This abnormality may be associated with transposition of the anus, vesicoureteral reflux, and other abnormalities of the urinary system [5]. In the presence of multiple concomitant anomalies and a higherlocationof the urethral orifice high on the anterior rectal wall, patients die several days after delivery. In the case described, a double pyelo-calyceal system on the left was suspected as the only concomitant urinary abnormality. There was no drainage disturbance of urine output. This is the reason for the absence of symptoms of POS infection and the development of renal failure. Inspection of the rectum proves that urine is excreted along the anterior rectal wall [6]. The presented case of a patient with aphallia is the most frequently described type of this anomaly. Child with male karyotype − 46, XY. Penis absent, with present, well developed scrotum and located testes with normal development. Urine is voided through an opening in the anterior rectal wall. Presence of a double pyelo-calyx system on the left, without drainage disturbances [7].

The parents of the child should decide on the sex of the child. If it is to be male, penile sculpture should be performed at the age of puberty. Thanks to advances in in vitro procedures, these patients can have children. Some patients develop kidney failure as a result of the abnormalities of the genitourinary system. If renal failure is due to urethral obstruction, these patients should be treated promptly, with a focus on accomplishing timely urinary drainage, proving the type of congenital abnormalities, and preserving the external genitalia [8].

Conclusions

A single clinical case of a rare congenital anomaly is described, and long-term outcomes are not yet available. The patient will be referred for future consultation with a pediatric urologist and options for surgical correction of the anomaly will be considered.
