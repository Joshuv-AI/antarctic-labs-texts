A study on the effects of garlic residue-jujube complex on antioxidant capacity, intestinal microbiota, and meat quality of meat ducks
Authors: Hao Jiang, Biao Fan, Runlu Wang, Xiaowen Wu, Hong Zhang, Chunyang Han, Cuiyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of a garlic residue–jujube complex (GRJ), an agricultural byproduct, on the antioxidant capacity, intestinal microbiota, and meat quality of meat ducks. The chemical components of GRJ were identified using high-performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry (HPLC-ESI-MS). Subsequently, network pharmacology was used to predict its potential functional characteristics, which were subsequently validated through animal feeding trials. Network pharmacology analysis indicates that GRJ may have the potential effects of improving the health of meat ducks by regulating antioxidant defense and intestinal immunity, and enhance meat quality by regulating lipid metabolism. Animal feeding trials were conducted at a duck farm in Anhui, China. A total of 360 one-day-old Qiangying meat ducks were randomly divided into one control group and three GRJ groups with high, medium, and low doses. The control group received a basal diet exclusively, while the three GRJ groups were fed basal diets supplemented with 2.5%, 1.25%, and 0.625% GRJ, respectively. The experimental period lasted 20 days. The results demonstrated that dietary supplementation with 2.5% GRJ significantly increased serum antioxidant markers, including total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT), while decreasing the oxidative damage marker malondialdehyde (MDA) (P < 0.05). Moreover, 2.5% GRJ markedly improved intestinal microbiota composition by reducing the abundance of potentially harmful bacteria such as Proteobacteria and Anaerobiospirillum, and increasing beneficial taxa including Parabacteroides and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 (P < 0.05). Furthermore, GRJ significantly enhanced breast muscle yield and increased the contents of total fatty acids, unsaturated fatty acids (oleic acid and linoleic acid), total amino acids (including essential and flavor amino acids), as well as volatile flavor compounds in the breast muscle (P < 0.05). Feed cost analysis revealed that replacing 2.5% of the basal diet with GRJ reduced feed costs by approximately $0.036 per duck. Thus, GRJ not only possesses functional properties such as protecting intestinal health and improving meat quality in meat ducks, but also reduces feed costs, enhances breeding efficiency, fundamentally solves the environmental pollution problem caused by garlic residue, and turns it into a valuable resource. It is a promising natural alternative feed ingredient.Keywords: Garlic residue, Meat ducks, Antioxidant, Intestinal microbiota changes, Meat quality

Introduction

Duck meat serves as a crucial source of animal protein globally, particularly in Asian regions, where its consumption demand continues to rise (Yang et al., 2025). However, the development of duck farming is currently facing two challenges. Firstly, continuously escalating costs of primary feed ingredients severely constrain profit margins for producers. Secondly, consumers are placing increasingly higher demands on the quality of duck meat. They are concerned not only with health attributes, such as the absence of drug residues, but also pursue nutritional characteristics like intramuscular fat content and fatty acid composition, as well as meat flavor and texture (Li et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023). Consequently, identifying feed ingredients or substitutes that offer both economic viability and health-promoting properties is of paramount importance. In this context, agricultural by-products, which are often abundant, low-cost, and may contain bioactive compounds, present considerable potential. Garlic residue is a byproduct of garlic essential oil extraction. It retains various nutrients, including protein, crude fiber, and fat, along with potentially bioactive components like garlic polysaccharides, polyphenols, and organosulfur compounds (Shao et al., 2020; Song et al., 2023). But the high moisture content, often exceeding 85%, results in a viscous texture that makes low-energy drying impractical. Combined with its pungent odor and substantial production volume, these factors render garlic residue difficult to handle, leading to disposal challenges and significant environmental pollution (Geng et al., 2022). While these residues are rich in organic matter, their high sulfur content can lead to rapid decomposition if not properly managed. This often results in soil and water pollution (through eutrophication) and the emission of malodorous gases such as hydrogen sulfide. Current research on garlic residue is sparse, primarily focusing on the extraction of specific components, such as pectin or polysaccharide-metal complexes (Qi et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2024). Effective large-scale solutions or processing methods have yet to be reported. Jujube is a widely consumed medicinal food. It is rich in diverse bioactive substances, including amino acids, phenolic acids, triterpenic acids, ascorbic acid, polysaccharides, flavonoids, saponins, cerebroisides, and various minerals. These compounds contribute to its documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and gastrointestinal protective effects, among others (Shishir et al., 2019; Gowd et al., 2020; Rashwan et al., 2020). With its large production volume and low cost in China, jujube by-products from food processing and traditional Chinese medicine factories offer even greater price advantages, making them suitable as functional additives in feed.

In this study, garlic residue and jujube were combined and applied as a feed ingredient alternative in meat duck diets, forming a garlic residue–jujube complex (GRJ) functional feed for feeding trials on duck farms. By examining the effects of GRJ on the antioxidant capacity, intestinal microbiota, and meat quality of meat ducks as well as conducting feed cost analysis, this research aimed to evaluate its potential as a substitute for conventional feed ingredients in duck production, while exploring a new utilization pathway for garlic residue.

Materials and methods

Analysis of garlic residue Composition

The composition of garlic residue was analyzed using the combined technique of ultra-high performance liquid chromatography and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HPLC-ESI-MS). One g each of the garlic residue sample and GRJ was weighed. Ten mL of methanol solution was added. At room temperature, ultrasonic extraction was carried out for 45 minutes. Following centrifugation of the methanol extract at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was membrane-filtered (0.22 μm). Compound identification was performed using HPLC-ESI-MS. HPLC-ESI-MS conditions: Halo-C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) at 30°C; mobile phase of 0.05% formic acid in acetonitrile (A) and water (B) at 0.3 mL/min; injection 5 μL. Gradient (A%): 0-3 min: 5%; 3-45 min: 5-80%; 45-55 min: 80-90%; 55-57 min: 90%; 57-57.1 min: 90-5%; 57.1-60 min: 5%. MS: positive (3500 V) and negative (3000 V) modes; capillary 350°C; sheath/aux gas 45/10 arb; source 350°C.

Determination of crude polysaccharide content in garlic residue

Dried garlic residue (5 g) was mixed with distilled water at a material-to-liquid ratio of 1:24 (w/v) and extracted in a water bath at 75°C for 2 h, followed by filtration. The residue was re‑extracted once under the same conditions, and the two filtrates were combined. The combined filtrate was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was concentrated to 20 mL by rotary evaporation. Anhydrous ethanol (80 mL) was added to the concentrate, mixed thoroughly, and kept at 4°C for 2.5 h to allow precipitation. After centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was redissolved in 20 mL distilled water, followed by a second ethanol precipitation. The final precipitate was dissolved in distilled water, heated at 60°C for 1 h, and the volume was adjusted to 50 mL to obtain the crude garlic polysaccharide solution, which was stored at 4°C until use.

The crude polysaccharide content in the garlic residue was determined using the phenol‑sulfuric acid method. D‑Glucose (20.0 mg) dried to constant weight at 105°C was dissolved and diluted to 100 mL to prepare a 0.2 mg/mL standard solution. Aliquots of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mL of the standard solution were transferred to test tubes, and each was brought to 2.0 mL with distilled water. Then, 1.0 mL of 6% phenol and 5.0 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid were added, mixed well, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm. A standard curve was constructed. For sample analysis, 100 μL of the crude garlic polysaccharide solution was diluted to 100 mL with distilled water. An aliquot of 1 mL of this dilution was taken, brought to 2 mL with distilled water, and subjected to the same colorimetric procedure. Absorbance was measured in triplicate, and the crude polysaccharide content was calculated using the regression equation derived from the standard curve.

Animal test

The current study's experimental procedures were authorized by Anhui Agricultural University (No. AHAUXMSQ2023143). The committee approved all experimental procedures. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Laboratory Animals of China and the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching.

Experimental materials

Fresh garlic residue, with an approximate moisture content of 85% - 90%, was supplied by Anhui Kaibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). Jujube powder, a processing by-product from traditional Chinese medicinal materials, was provided by Sheng Bailing Traditional Chinese Medicine Decoction Pieces Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). Preparation of the garlic residue‑jujube powder composite (GRJ): Fresh garlic residue and jujube powder were mixed at a wet weight ratio of 12:1 (equivalent to a dry matter ratio of 1.5:1). For diet formulation, the composite was added to provide dry matter contributions of 0.625%, 1.25%, and 2.5%, respectively. The complete feed mixtures were naturally air‑dried on sunny days in a well‑ventilated area without direct sunlight exposure at ambient temperature (approximately 10-20°C) for 24 hours until the moisture content fell below 10%. These three inclusion levels were selected based on (1) the moisture constraint of pelleted feed (8‑15%) limiting the maximum addition, (2) the goal of using the highest feasible level to maximize bioactive compounds, and (3) the inclusion of medium and low doses for dose‑response evaluation to identify the optimal level. The formulation of the basal diet was based on the nutritional requirements of the National Research Council (1994) and the China National Feeding Standard of Meat Duck (NY/T2122–2012, Table 1). The basic nutritional composition of the GRJ composite and GRJ-supplemented diet is shown in Table S1. The allowable error for the determination of feed test results shall be judged in accordance with GB/T18823.

Experimental design

A total of 360 one-day-old meat ducks (obtained from Qiangying Co., Ltd., Bozhou, Anhui, China) were housed in a commercial duck production facility owned by the same company. The temperature in the duck house was 32 ± 2°C for ducks aged 1–3 days. From the 4th day onwards, the temperature was decreased by 1°C each day until it reached 23 ± 2°C, and then maintained at 23 ± 2°C until the end of the experiment. The stocking density was 10 ducks/m². At 18 days of age, when the average body weight reached 780.02 ± 68.83 g, the ducks were randomly assigned to four dietary treatment groups. Each group consisted of 90 ducks, which were further divided into six replicate pens with 15 ducks per pen. The four groups received the following diets: the CON group (fed a basal diet), the GRJ-L group (fed the basal diet supplemented with 0.625% GRJ), the GRJ-M group (fed the basal diet supplemented with 1.25% GRJ), and the GRJ-H group (fed the basal diet supplemented with 2.5% GRJ). The trial lasted for 20 days until market age (37 days). Throughout the experimental period, all ducks had ad libitum access to feed and water. General health status, including mental alertness, feed and water intake, fecal consistency, and any signs of morbidity, was monitored daily. For cecal digesta collection, the cecum was surgically excised immediately after slaughter. The cecal contents were recovered by gentle squeezing from the proximal to the distal end of the cecum, taking care to avoid contamination with mucosal tissue. The recovered digesta were transferred into sterile 2 mL cryotubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80°C until DNA extraction for 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Sample collection

On d 18, all ducks were weighed for initial body weight and assigned to dietary treatments. Growth performance was assessed on d 37 by weighing the ducks and residual feed after a 6-hour fasting period. On d 35, from each replicate, one duck with body weight closest to the replicate mean was selected for sample collection (n = 6). Blood samples were collected from the wing vein into non-anticoagulant tubes, and serum was harvested by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 15 min, then stored at −80°C. Following blood collection, ducks were anesthetized with intravenous sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg BW) and slaughtered. Carcass traits, including dressing percentage, semi-eviscerated yield, eviscerated yield, breast muscle yield, and leg muscle yield, were measured according to the Chinese agricultural standard NY/T 823-2020 (Poultry Production Performance Terms and Statistical Methods).

Measurement of serum markers of liver injury and oxidative stress parameters

The serum liver injury markers and oxidative stress indicators were assayed using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. The serum biochemical indicators measured included alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). The oxidative stress indicators measured included total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), catalase (CAT), and malondialdehyde (MDA).

Detection of amino acids in breast muscle

A homogenized pectoral muscle sample (0.1 g) was subjected to acid hydrolysis with 5 mL of a 1:1 HCl solution in a sealed tube at 110 ± 1°C for 22 h. Following hydrolysis and cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered into a 10 mL volumetric flask; the tube was rinsed with water and the rinsate added to the flask. The volume was adjusted to 10 mL with water and mixed thoroughly. A 0.05 mL aliquot was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in 2 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl, vortexed, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The filtrate was analyzed for 17 amino acids using a Hitachi l-8900 automatic amino acid analyzer.

A 0.2 g of homogenized duck breast muscle was weighed into a hydrolysis tube. After moistening with 0.3 mL of water, 4.3 mol/L NaOH was added. The tube was flushed with nitrogen and sealed, after which it was hydrolyzed at 110°C for 20 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was transferred to a beaker. The pH was then adjusted to 4.3 using 4 mol/L HCl, and the solution was diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with water. After thorough mixing, the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Tryptophan was separated on a reversed-phase C18 column by HPLC. Detection was performed at 280 nm using a diode array detector (DAD).

Detection of fatty acids in breast muscle

Fatty acid composition was determined by GC‑FID according to the national standard GB 5009.168‑2016. Sample preparation followed the hydrolysis‑extraction method described therein. Briefly, 2 g of muscle sample was hydrolyzed in 10 mL of hydrochloric acid solution at 70–80°C for 40 min in the presence of 100 mg pyrogallic acid, 2 mL of 95% ethanol, and a few boiling stones. After cooling, the hydrolysate was extracted three times with 50 mL of diethyl ether‑petroleum ether (1:1, v/v). The combined organic layers were evaporated and the total lipid residue was dried.

The dried lipids were then methylated according to the standard. Briefly, 2 mL of 2% sodium hydroxide in methanol was added and heated at 85°C for 30 min, followed by 3 mL of 14% boron trifluoride in methanol at 85°C for another 30 min. After cooling, fatty acid methyl esters were extracted into 3 mL of hexane, and the upper layer was filtered (0.45 μm) prior to GC injection. Separation was performed on an HP‑88 capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm) with flame ionization detection.

Detection of volatile compounds in breast muscle

Headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS, model 7890B-7000C, Agilent Technologies, USA) was employed to profile the volatile compounds in duck breast meat. Separation was performed on an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Merck, Germany). For sample preparation, 5.0 g of meat was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial, followed by addition of saturated sodium chloride solution and 2-octanol as an internal standard at a final concentration of 3 mg/L. After sealing, the vial was equilibrated at 80°C for 30 minutes. Subsequently, the SPME fiber was placed in the headspace for 30 min, followed by desorption in the GC inlet at 250°C for 5 min (splitless). Referencing the NIST 2020 spectral library, with a match factor greater than 50, qualitative analysis was performed using mass spectrometry data, and quantitative analysis was conducted based on peak area and internal standard.

Cecal microbiota determination via 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Microbial community profiling of cecal contents was performed via 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial kit, with integrity verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions were amplified with specific primers, and the resulting amplicons were used to construct sequencing libraries, which were then sequenced on the DNBSEQ-G99RS platform (PE300). Raw sequencing data were filtered and denoised to obtain high-quality sequences. Taxonomic classification was performed against the SILVA database, and subsequent analyses of microbial diversity and composition were conducted using QIIME and R. Sequencing services were provided by Wuhan Servicebio Co., Ltd. (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

Network pharmacology analysis

The potential targets of the active components in GRJ were predicted based on the 40 compounds identified in GRJ by HPLC-ESI-MS. Compound-related targets were obtained from the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) by inputting the chemical structures of each component. The obtained target names were standardized to official gene symbols using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Disease-related targets associated with antioxidant response, fatty acid biosynthesis, and amino acid metabolism were retrieved from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), and PharmGKB (https://www.pharmgkb.org/) databases using the keywords “Antioxidant Response”, “Fatty Acid Biosynthesis”, and “Amino Acid Metabolism”. The results from the three keyword searches were merged and duplicates were removed to establish a library of disease-related targets. Overlapping targets between compound-related and disease-related datasets were identified using Venny 2.1, and the intersecting targets were imported into the STRING database (species: Anas; confidence score > 0.7) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. The network was visualized with Cytoscape 3.9.1, and a compound-target interaction network was also generated. To explore biological functions, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using R software (version 4.3.2), with a significance threshold of q < 0.05. The enrichment results were visualized using bubble plots.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA) and R software (version 4.3.2) . The normality of data distribution and homogeneity of variances were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. For parameters measured across all four dietary treatment groups (CON, GRJ-L, GRJ-M, GRJ-H), including growth performance and slaughter traits, data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For parameters involving only two groups (CON vs. GRJ-H), such as serum oxidative stress markers, amino acid and fatty acid profiles, and volatile flavor compounds, independent samples t-tests were applied. For cecal microbiota composition, beta diversity was assessed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance, and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed to test the significance of differences between groups (Ma et al., 2023). For comparisons of bacterial relative abundances at the phylum and genus levels between CON and GRJ-H groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied (Ma et al., 2024). All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Effect of GRJ-H on the duck breast muscle amino acid profile of meat ducks

The amino acid profiles in duck breast meat are shown in Table 5. Compared with the CON group, the GRJ-H group exhibited a significant increase in the total amino acid content (P < 0.05). Effects of GRJ-H on the composition of the cecal contents of meat ducks

The effects of GRJ-H on the composition of the cecal contents of meat ducks are presented in Fig. 3. The GRJ-H group exhibited slightly elevated values for all evaluated alpha diversity metrics (ACE, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) compared to the CON group, with these variations failing to meet statistical significance levels (Figs. 3A-D). This indicates no major disparity in overall microbial richness and evenness between the two groups. Analysis of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) revealed 19 unique OTUs in the CON group, 24 unique OTUs in the GRJ-H group, and 118 OTUs shared between them (Fig. 3E), suggesting a core similarity in microbiota composition with distinct features in each group. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) showed a discernible separation trend between the CON and GRJ-H samples along the coordinate axes (Fig. 3F), indicating a potential structural difference in the microbial communities (R² = 0.19, P = 0.083). At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes dominated the cecal microbiota in both groups (Fig. 3G). The abundances of Parabacteroides (P < 0.05) and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 (P < 0.01) were significantly increased, while the abundance of Anaerobiospirillum was significantly decreased (P < 0.05).

Economic benefits

Based on the previous experimental results, the inclusion of 2.5% GRJ in the basal diet did not adversely affect normal physiological functions but improved the growth and slaughter performance of meat ducks to some extent. From a perspective focused solely on feed savings, disregarding these performance enhancements, the GRJ-H group was fed a total of 343.9 kg of feed during the entire trial period, of which 2.5% consisted of GRJ. This was equivalent to replacing 8.5975 kg of the basal diet with an equal amount of GRJ. Given the price of the basal diet at 3.36 CNY/kg and the calculated cost of GRJ at 0.8 CNY/kg (based on component prices of 2.0 CNY/kg for jujube powder and 0 CNY/kg for garlic residue), the net cost savings from this substitution were calculated. The total net saving amounted to 8.5975 × (3.36 - 0.8) = 22 CNY. When distributed across the 90 ducks in the group, this represented a saving in rearing input of approximately 0.245 CNY (0.036 USD) per duck for the producer. Based on an exchange rate of approximately 1 USD = 6.79 CNY (as of May 2026), this saving is equivalent to approximately 0.036 USD per duck.

Network pharmacology

Venn diagram analysis revealed 139 intersecting genes between compound targets and disease-related targets (Fig. 4A). A "compound-target" network and a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network were constructed using Cytoscape, comprising 23 compounds and 139 targets (Figs. 4B–5C). GO enrichment analysis showed that GRJ-associated targets were significantly enriched in biological processes including cellular lipid metabolism, hormone response, and steroid metabolism. Enriched molecular function terms included oxidoreductase activity, iron ion binding, and monooxygenase activity (Fig. 4E). KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 4F) indicated significant enrichment of pathways involved in antioxidant defense, metabolic regulation, and intestinal immune modulation, such as MAPK signaling, FoxO signaling, apoptosis, insulin signaling, mTOR signaling, Salmonella infection, and Toll-like receptor signaling.

Discussion

Rising feed costs and the strict implementation of antibiotic reduction and prohibition policies have placed dual pressures on the livestock industry to safeguard animal health while maintaining economic viability. In this context, developing novel functional feeds or feed additives that reduce costs, enhance efficiency, and align with green and low-carbon principles has become a central objective for sustainable development. This study was grounded in establishing a circular agricultural production model that integrates crop and livestock farming, aiming to unify economic, environmental, and social benefits. Specifically, garlic residue, an environmental pollutant with potential reuse value, was combined with jujube powder, an agricultural by-product, to form a composite (GRJ). This composite was used as a feed ingredient substitute in meat duck diets. A 20-day feeding trial demonstrated that GRJ possesses four core efficacies: (1) it enhanced the antioxidant capacity and improved gut health of meat ducks; (2) it increased the nutritional value and flavor profile of duck meat; (3) it improved the slaughter yield of the ducks; (4) it replaced 2.5% of the basal diet ingredients, thereby reducing production costs.

Oxidative stress refers to a state of imbalance between free radical generation and antioxidant defenses (Sies, 2020). In livestock production, various intrinsic and extrinsic stressors can induce oxidative stress in animals, such as high stocking density, nutritional deficiencies or imbalances, elevated metabolic demands during growth phases, environmental toxins, extreme temperature fluctuations, and infectious diseases (Li et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025). These challenges are particularly pronounced under intensive farming systems and can compromise animal welfare and production efficiency to varying degrees. Key biomarkers for assessing oxidative status include T-AOC, SOD, CAT, and MDA (Obeme-Nmom et al., 2024). T-AOC serves as a comprehensive indicator of the overall functionality of the antioxidant system (Tan et al., 2026). SOD and CAT act specifically on superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide, respectively, and their activity concentrations can be used to track the dynamics of oxidative processes and tissue damage (Yang et al., 2025). The accumulation of MDA, a terminal product generated during lipid peroxidation, directly reflects the intensity of this oxidative process and the consequent cellular damage (de Mello Barros Pimentel et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Dietary GRJ-H significantly increased serum T-AOC, SOD, and CAT, while reducing the concentration of MDA in meat ducks, indicating an enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity. The enhanced serum antioxidant capacity (increased T‑AOC, SOD, CAT; decreased MDA) in GRJ-H‑fed ducks is likely the result of the collective action of multiple bioactive compound classes identified in the GRJ composite. HPLC‑ESI‑MS analysis revealed that the GRJ composite contains organosulfur compounds such as S‑allyl‑cysteine and γ‑glutamyl peptides, phenolic acids including protocatechuic acid‑4‑O‑glucoside, caffeic acid‑O‑glucoside, and ferulic acid, flavonoids like catechin and naringenin, as well as polysaccharides. Garlic residue alone contained 9.73% total polysaccharides on a dry matter basis. These diverse molecules are known to scavenge free radicals, chelate metal ions, and upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes through synergistic interactions (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Yumlembam et al., 2025). The presence of both water‑soluble antioxidants, such as polysaccharides and phenolic glycosides, and lipid‑soluble antioxidants, including flavonoids and organosulfur compounds, may provide complementary protection (Pan et al., 2022). However, we did not determine the absolute concentrations of individual non‑polysaccharide components; therefore, the relative contribution of each compound class remains to be elucidated in future studies.

The gut microbiota is a community of microorganisms that exists in the gut, consisting of bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Bacteria constitute the dominant members, chiefly classified into the Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria phyla. This community establishes a symbiotic relationship with the host, participating in biological processes such as digestion, immune regulation, and metabolism, and its homeostasis is crucial for host health (Zhang and Chen, 2019; Sun et al., 2024). Bacteroidetes, one of the most abundant and functionally important phyla, includes Bacteroides, Prevotella, and Parabacteroides. It plays a key role in degrading dietary fiber, digesting polysaccharides, generating SCFAs, and regulating immune responses, which is vital for maintaining gut health and preventing pathogen invasion. Proteobacteria is a major phylum that encompasses various common pathogens or opportunistic pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella. An abnormal increase in its abundance is often associated with a biomarker of gut microbiota dysbiosis and an inflammatory state (Li et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2025). In the present study, dietary supplementation with GRJ-H significantly increased the abundances of Prevotellaceae_UCG‑001 and Parabacteroides at the genus level, while reducing the abundance of Proteobacteria, particularly the genus Anaerobiospirillum. These observed shifts in cecal microbiota are consistent with the prebiotic and antimicrobial properties of multiple components in the GRJ composite (Lu et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2024). The composite contains garlic polysaccharides (9.73% in garlic residue), which may be fermented by gut microbiota to produce short‑chain fatty acids, as suggested by previous studies (Qiu et al., 2024). The identified organic acids such as citric acid isomers and chlorogenic acid may lower intestinal pH, creating a favorable environment for beneficial Bacteroidota while suppressing acid‑sensitive Proteobacteria (Khan et al., 2022). The sulfur‑containing compounds, including allicin derivatives, together with flavonoids like catechin, have been reported to exert direct or indirect antimicrobial effects against opportunistic pathogens (Etyemez Büyükdeveci et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2025). Furthermore, jujube‑derived lignans and alkaloids may modulate gut immune responses, indirectly influencing microbial community structure (Oni and Oke, 2025). Given that GRJ is a multi‑component agricultural by‑product formulation, we attribute the microbiota‑modulating effects to the synergistic action of these diverse bioactive substances rather than to any single compound. Although GRJ-H supplementation tended to improve feed conversion ratio (FCR; P < 0.1; Table 3), average daily gain (ADG) and average daily feed intake (ADFI) showed no significant differences. This suggests that the benefit was primarily mediated by enhanced feed efficiency rather than increased feed intake. This may be due to improved nutrient digestibility or metabolic utilization of absorbed nutrients, possibly linked to the modulation of gut microbiota and antioxidant status (as discussed above).

Nutritional value and eating quality are important bases in consumer meat selection. The nutritional quality of meat is primarily characterized by its provision of protein, essential fatty acids, minerals, and various vitamins. Eating quality, which encompasses physical, chemical, and sensory attributes, includes meat color, flavor, water-holding capacity, tenderness, and fat distribution. A certain correlation exists between these two aspects. The profiles of amino acids and fatty acids are key determinants of the nutritional quality of protein and fat, respectively, and also serve as the foundational precursors contributing to the formation of the final volatile flavor profile (Jin et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025; Tomaszewska et al., 2025). Glutamate is recognized as the most significant umami amino acid in food (Wang et al., 2023). Serine is a key sweet-taste amino acid and an important flavor precursor in meat (Ge et al., 2023). Aromatic amino acids, especially tryptophan, serve as both essential nutrients and vital precursors, and their involvement in the Maillard reaction may be a key source of numerous characteristic meat aroma compounds, with the extent and nature of these compounds depending on cooking conditions. The composition of fatty acids forms the basis of meat flavor. Oleic acid, one of the most common monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) in the human diet, exhibits antioxidant properties (Mann et al., 2024) and functions in the prevention of thrombosis, atherosclerosis, and the reduction of cardiovascular disease mortality (Zhou et al., 2023). It also acts as a crucial lipid precursor for flavor development in animal meat. The oxidation of linoleic and linolenic acids, two key polyunsaturated fatty acids, generates various key volatile compounds that constitute characteristic meat flavors, including aldehydes and ketones. (Duan et al., 2023). Aldehydes, due to their relatively high concentrations and low odor thresholds, are important contributors to meat flavor (Zhang et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2025). An elevated level of nonanal has been associated with an enhanced "desirable flavor" in meat studies (Li et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024). Esters, particularly methyl oleate, can impart a softer, richer fatty aroma, thereby increasing the overall complexity of the flavor profile (Lorenzo et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2020). Alcohols typically exhibit higher odor thresholds and play a less direct role in flavor contribution, with certain types possibly introducing undesirable off-flavors. Ketones primarily function in synergy with other flavor compounds, adding depth and complexity to meat products (Liang et al., 2025). Sulfur-containing compounds may introduce distinctive alliaceous or other characteristic notes. The increased content of UFAs such as oleic acid provides an abundant substrate for the generation of these key flavor aldehydes. In the present study, GRJ-H supplementation was associated with higher the total and essential amino acid content in duck breast muscle, specifically increasing the concentrations of glutamate, serine and tryptophan; improved the fatty acid composition, with higher levels of MUFAs, PUFAs, oleic acid, and linoleic acid; and an altered the volatile flavor profile, as evidenced by increased nonanal, hexadecanal, and methyl oleate, as well as decreased 1‑octen‑3‑ol and 2‑undecenal. These changes may contribute to both the nutritional value and sensory appeal of the duck meat. The improved nutritional and sensory quality of breast muscle in GRJ-H ducks may results from the integrated effects of multiple bioactive components in the GRJ composite. The eleven amino acids and their derivatives identified in GRJ, which include γ‑glutamyl peptides, may serve as direct precursors for protein synthesis and flavor development (Heres et al., 2023). The phenolic acids and flavonoids can protect polyunsaturated fatty acids from oxidative degradation during metabolism and post‑mortem storage (Jin and Jin, 2025). Organosulfur compounds in garlic can improve lipid stability and fatty acid composition in poultry meat by influencing the activities of key enzymes in cholesterol and lipid synthesis, namely squalene oxidase and HMG‑CoA reductase (Adjei-Mensah et al., 2025). Additionally, the saccharides including glucose, fructose, and sucrose isomers, together with phenolic compounds, can participate in Maillard reactions during cooking, generating characteristic meat flavor aldehydes and esters (Du et al., 2023). It is important to note, however, that the absolute concentrations of most individual compounds, except total polysaccharides, were not determined; therefore, these mechanistic inferences are plausible based on compound class identification and literature evidence.

The economic benefit of GRJ derives from three synergistic aspects. First, direct feed cost saving of approximately 0.036 USD per duck results from replacing 2.5% of the basal diet with low‑cost agricultural by‑products. Although additional processing costs such as mixing and air‑drying are incurred, the raw by‑products themselves are inexpensive or even free, making the overall economic balance positive. Second, enhanced antioxidant capacity and improved gut microbiota, characterized by increased abundances of beneficial bacteria such as Parabacteroides and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 and decreased levels of potentially harmful bacteria such as Anaerobiospirillum, reduce disease susceptibility and medication costs. Third, increased breast muscle yield as well as higher contents of unsaturated fatty acids, essential amino acids, and volatile flavor compounds improve the nutritional and sensory quality of the meat, thereby adding value to the product. In summary, GRJ significantly enhances economic benefits.

Pharmacological studies have shown that the chemical components of GRJ, including sulfur‑containing compounds, γ‑glutamyl peptides, polysaccharides, and polyphenols, possess antioxidant and immunomodulatory activities, and may also affect meat flavor. Therefore, in the present study, network pharmacology was employed to further predict the pharmacological effects of GRJ through in silico simulation and network analysis. Network pharmacology predicted that the main active components in GRJ may improve antioxidant capacity, meat quality, and intestinal immunity in meat ducks by targeting relevant genes. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the potential targets were significantly enriched in several key pathways closely related to this study, including the MAPK signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, insulin signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Salmonella infection, and Toll‑like receptor signaling pathway. According to published reports, the FoxO and MAPK pathways are associated with antioxidant defense (Lin et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025). The insulin and mTOR pathways are associated with protein synthesis and lipid metabolism (Posey and Davis, 2023; Xu et al., 2025). The Toll‑like receptor pathway is associated with gut microbial recognition (Deng et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023). The results of the animal trial in this study showed that GRJ significantly increased the serum levels of T‑AOC, SOD, and CAT, reduced the oxidative damage marker MDA, improved the muscle fatty acid composition (increased UFA, oleic acid, and linoleic acid) and amino acid profile (increased total amino acids, essential amino acids, and flavor amino acids), and modulated the cecal microbiota structure by reducing Proteobacteria and increasing Parabacteroides and Prevotellaceae_UCG‑001. These findings, to a certain extent, support the predictions of the network pharmacology analysis.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. (1) There is currently a lack of duck‑specific genomic or target databases. The reliability of network pharmacology prediction results depends on the completeness and accuracy of the integrated public databases. Due to the current limitations in duck‑related databases regarding drug‑target interaction information, chemical composition coverage, and species‑specific data, it was necessary to rely on human database resources (SwissTargetPrediction). Nevertheless, based on the animal trial results, the impact of database species‑specificity appears to be limited. (2) Targeted molecular methods (such as Western blot, qPCR, or pathway‑specific inhibitors) were not used to validate the relevant pathways. This does not substantially affect the main conclusions of the present study.

Conclusion

In summary, dietary supplementation with GRJ, a low‑cost ingredient rich in bioactive compounds and nutrients, effectively enhanced the antioxidant capacity, improved the intestinal microbiota structure, increased the meat quality and slaughter performance of meat ducks, and significantly reduced feed costs while increasing production profits. Therefore, GRJ, as a natural alternative feed ingredient, exhibits great potential for application and promotion.
