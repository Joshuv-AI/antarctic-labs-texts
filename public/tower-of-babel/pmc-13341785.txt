Deep learning framework for determining earthquake focal mechanisms with application to the Campi Flegrei caldera
Authors: Daniela Annunziata, Raffaella De Matteis, Stefano Izzo, Edoardo Prezioso, Vincenzo Convertito, Guido Maria Adinolfi, Francesco Piccialli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present DeepFoc, a novel deep learning framework for estimating earthquake focal mechanisms from P-wave polarities and amplitudes, specifically designed for low-to-moderate magnitude events in complex tectonic settings. Trained entirely on synthetic data generated by using an on-the-fly simulation strategy, DeepFoc learns the nonlinear relationship between seismic observables and focal parameters (strike, dip, rake). We apply it to the Campi Flegrei caldera (Italy), a densely populated volcanic area experiencing ongoing unrest. Compared to classical inversion methods, DeepFoc provides more stable and accurate solutions, especially under degraded data conditions. Validation against synthetic and real seismic events shows improved agreement with observed data and better robustness to noise and incomplete inputs. By capturing focal mechanism variability at small scale, DeepFoc demonstrates high sensitivity and generalisation ability. Its computational efficiency and performance make it suitable for real-time integration in seismic monitoring workflows, offering timely source characterisation during seismic swarms and volcanic crises.

Introduction

The determination of earthquake focal mechanisms is fundamental to infer the source kinematic and the stress regime acting in the investigated seismogenic volume. This information is essential for reconstructing the active tectonic dynamics of a given region1 used to define the seismotectonic model for seismic hazard analyses2,3. Furthermore, on volcanic areas, the estimation of fault plane solutions can provide information on the status of the volcano. Indeed, a variation in the orientation of the P- and T-axis has been observed before an impending eruption4,5. In practice, focal mechanisms can be determined using different approaches and several seismological observables, depending on the magnitude of the event and the available data. For high-magnitude earthquakes, the seismic moment tensor inversion, by using full-waveforms, surface waves or body-wave amplitudes, allows for estimating the moment tensor components including contributes other than the double-couple (DC)6–9. In addition to the data quality and azimuthal station coverage, the moment tensor inversion requires the knowledge of the propagation model to compute the Green’s function at a detail that depends on the frequency content of the signal. This is a condition that can hardly be met for low-magnitude earthquakes that are, however, relevant in regions where large earthquakes have long return periods—characterised by background seismicity—or when dealing with induced seismicity. To tackle these issues, increasingly sophisticated techniques have been developed to estimate focal mechanisms by individual or joint inversion of distinct observables, such as P-wave polarity, P- and/or S-wave amplitude and their ratio, S-wave polarization, low-frequency body waves spectral amplitude10–15. Recent advances have introduced techniques based on relative polarity16 and relative radiation patterns (REFOC17) that make it possible to estimate focal mechanisms for small-magnitude earthquakes (M 1-2). The growing volume of data coming from the deployment of dense seismic networks has driven technological advancements toward the automated analysis of seismological observables. In this context, Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques have been increasingly adopted in seismology to automate some of the most critical phases of seismic data processing. Large efforts have been dedicated to the development of specialised deep learning models for event detection, first arrival time picking, and first motion polarity classification by operating directly on raw seismograms18,19. However, these models serve as a core component within broader workflows where, for instance, polarities are used in standard inversion techniques to determine focal mechanisms. For example, DiTingMotion20 is a deep learning model that employs a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for P-wave polarity classification, while relying on the HASH algorithm21 for focal mechanism estimation. More recently, some models have been designed to directly estimate focal mechanism using deep learning. These approaches train neural networks to learn the mapping between seismic observables and source characteristics, allowing prediction of focal mechanisms without relying on traditional inversion methods. FMNet22, for instance, focused on the real-time analysis of events with magnitude larger than 5.4, uses a deep CNN trained exclusively on synthetic data to learn general relationships between waveform features and focal mechanism parameters. Here we propose a new deep learning-based method, named DeepFoc, designed to directly estimate the focal mechanism of low-to-moderate magnitude earthquakes occurring in complex tectonic settings such as volcanic areas. We specifically apply our approach to the Campi Flegrei caldera in Southern Italy, one of the most hazardous volcanoes in the world, which is currently experiencing an unrest phase characterized by significant ground uplift, increased gas emissions, and low-magnitude seismicity (Md \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 4.6$$\end{document}) (Fig. 1). In this environment, the local stress field induced by deformation processes interacts with the regional stress field26. This factor, combined with the high background noise level due to the dense urbanization of the area and the source-receivers geometry, makes seismic source analysis particularly challenging. DeepFoc directly estimates focal mechanism parameters (strike, dip, and rake) using only P-wave first-motion polarities and amplitudes. This approach aims to learn the nonlinear mapping between these inputs and the underlying source geometry to provide more constrained solutions compared to those obtained with traditional inversion-based methods. To train the model, we designed a dynamic on-the-fly data generation strategy capable of producing millions of synthetic seismic events by combining a layered velocity model with the actual configuration of the seismic network installed in the Campi Flegrei caldera. By generating data in real time at each training epoch, this approach bypasses the prohibitive storage requirements associated with traditional static datasets, which can easily exceed several hundred terabytes, well beyond the capabilities of standard workstation infrastructures. Hypocentral locations and focal mechanism parameters are sampled from uniform distributions, while observational uncertainties are emulated using techniques such as polarity flipping and beta-distributed data masking to simulate real-world incompleteness, ensuring both statistical richness and robustness to degraded inputs. Once trained, the model was validated on real seismicity to assess its effectiveness under operational conditions characterized by limited and noisy data.

Results

Validation on synthetic data

The Campi Flegrei caldera, located in Southern Italy, is one of the highest risk volcanic areas in the world, primarily due to the high population density in the region. Historically, the caldera has experienced repeated episodes of subsidence and slow uplift (bradyseism), the most recent of which occurred between 1982 and 1984, with a maximum uplift of approximately 1.80 meters. Since 2005, a new unrest phase has been ongoing, during which ground uplift has reached about 1.45 meters at the point of maximum deformation measured at the RITE GNNS station (Fig. 1). The uplift is accompanied by increased seismic activity, both in terms of occurrence rate and magnitude, as well as continuous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CO_2$$\end{document} degassing, reaching rates up to 5 kilotons per day27. Since 2005, over 10, 000 earthquakes with duration magnitude between 0 and 4.6 have been recorded across the entire caldera. The seismic monitoring network has been significantly expanded since 2012, when the alert level was raised to yellow on a scale consisting of four levels: green (quiescence), yellow (attention), orange (pre-alarm), and red (imminent eruption). The current seismic network consists of 33 broadband seismic stations, including 3 located on the seafloor in the Gulf of Pozzuoli (Fig. 1). The Campi Flegrei caldera is of particular interest for the present study for at least three main reasons. First, the recorded seismicity is characterized by low-magnitude events that occur on a complex fault system, distributed both along the caldera rims and within its interior (Fig. 1). Second, focal mechanisms exhibit a wide range of faulting type, depending on the depth of the events and their location within the caldera structure26. Third, and equally important, the relative geometry between hypocenters and stations, combined with high natural and anthropic noise level, make it particularly challenging to obtain well-constrained focal mechanisms. Before applying DeepFoc to the data of events recorded in the study area, we evaluate its performance on synthetic data, namely, P-wave polarity and amplitude, that is, the radiation pattern coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document}). This provides a controlled environment with known ground truth solutions and allows a systematic evaluation of the model’s accuracy and robustness under varying conditions. A batch of 2048 synthetic events was generated according to the procedure described in the Methods section. The whole area was divided in four zones accounting for the actual spatial seismicity distribution; the hypocentral locations were sampled from a multinomial distribution within a predefined volumetric grid specific for the study area, while focal mechanism parameters (strike, dip, rake) were likewise sampled from a multinomial distribution defined over a parameter grid. We compared the results with those obtained by using the FPFIT algorithm10 that is a standard approach based on the inversion of P-wave polarities. In particular, we computed the Kagan angle29, a standard metric for quantifying differences between focal mechanisms, for DeepFoc and FPFIT solutions against the theoretical mechanisms. To further investigate the robustness of DeepFoc under conditions of incomplete data, a common occurrence when analysing small magnitude events, we systematically degraded the input observations in terms of number and quality of the available P-polarities and/or amplitudes (See Data preprocessing in Methods for details). The number of discarded data, compared to the whole dataset, was set to 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8, where a value of 0.2 indicates limited data loss and 0.8 represents severe degradation. When comparing the results of DeepFoc and FPFIT, it is important to consider that the latter may produce multiple solutions or fail to produce a solution when the number of available polarities is less than a given threshold (default: six). In cases with multiple solutions, the average Kagan angle of all the solutions was computed, as no single solution could be objectively selected. DeepFoc on real earthquake events

We applied the trained DeepFoc to a selection of events occurred in the Campi Flegrei caldera during the current unrest phase. In particular, we selected 48 events that covers distinct areas of the caldera interested by the on-going seismicity (Table 3). This choice is driven by the fact that azimuthal coverage of the seismic network may significantly change and that off-shore events are deeper than on-land events. As a consequence, the performance of the algorithm may depend on the volume where the event occurs. We manually picked first P- and S-wave arrival times, and the P-polarities on the three component seismograms, and computed the low-frequency level of the P-wave displacement spectra using the technique described in Convertito and De Matteis30. The low frequency level, once corrected for the hypocentral distance, provides the radiation pattern coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document}31 that, together with the polarity, is the input for DeepFoc. On average, a number of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$13-14$$\end{document} stations is available for the selected events. We used the 1D velocity model proposed by Tramelli et al.32 and the HYPO71 software33 obtaining high quality location with average horizontal and vertical errors less than 0.15 km, and root mean square residual less than 0.1 seconds.

To assess the robustness and reliability of the DeepFoc, we compared its solutions with those obtained by using the classical FPFIT algorithm. In addition, to further evaluate the performance of DeepFoc and the selection of input data (P-wave polarity and amplitude) against methods that rely on other observables, such as body-wave amplitude ratios, we implemented the BISTROP13 algorithm, which jointly inverts P-wave polarities and P/S amplitude ratios within a Bayesian framework. We computed the amplitude ratios by adopting the TESLA software34 that adaptively selects the optimal time window to be used for the inversion of the P- and S-wave displacement spectra to infer the long-period spectral levels. However, it is worth noting that, for the study area, the inclusion of P/S ratio could introduce biases, since the limited number of reliably identifiable S-waves – due to waveform complexity and noise level – reduces the amount of input data available for inversion.

To investigate the stability of DeepFoc solutions under small perturbations in the input data, a jackknife test35 was performed. This procedure, commonly used to assess model sensitivity, was implemented by iteratively removing one seismic station at a time and recalculating the focal mechanism at each iteration. After each removal, the excluded station was reinstated and the process continued with the next station until all stations for each event had been individually tested. This approach also allowed us to observe whether particular stations had a particular influence on the inferred solution.

Some of results are shown in Fig. 5, which presents the DeepFoc solution, along with jackknife variability, and both FPFIT and BISTROP counterparts for comparison. The results for the entire dataset are shown in Figure S7 of the Supplementary marterials. Since DeepFoc uses both P-wave polarities and amplitudes as input, we further assess its performance by comparing the observed with the theoretical amplitudes predicted from the solutions of the three algorithms. In addition to analysing the polarity distribution on the focal sphere, we quantify the agreement between observed and theoretical amplitudes using the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), as shown in the rightmost panels of Figure 5. The results suggest that a stable solution can be achieved using data measured at a minimum of 5 stations.

To summarize the results we show the distributions of the Kagan angle and the RMSE in Fig. 3. Regarding the RMSE, although the three distributions exhibit similar spread, the distribution for DeepFoc is more peaked, and its modal value is slightly lower than those obtained with FPFIT and BISTROP. As for the Kagan angle, the solutions from DeepFoc appear to be in better agreement with FPFIT than with BISTROP. However, although the distribution of Kagan angles for DeepFoc versus BISTROP exhibits a “tail” towards higher values, the core of the population remains largely consistent across all methods. While the accuracy of focal mechanism estimation is paramount, the computational cost is a critical factor for real-time monitoring applications, particularly during intense seismic sequences such as the Campi Flegrei unrest. To assess the efficiency of DeepFoc, we performed a benchmark analysis comparing its inference time against the classical method FPFIT and BISTROP. To ensure a competitive baseline, we employed parallelised implementations of both codes, allowing for concurrent processing of earthquake samples. The tests were conducted on a standard workstation, measuring the execution time across different batch sizes (1, 10, 100, and 1, 000 events).

As summarised in Table 4, DeepFoc drastically reduces the computational burden. Unlike traditional inversion methods that require iterative searches for each event, DeepFoc leverages its pre-trained architecture to provide nearly instantaneous predictions. For a single event, our method achieves a speedup of approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{24\times }$$\end{document} compared to FPFIT and over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{360\times }$$\end{document} compared to BISTROP. The efficiency gain scales favourably with larger datasets; for a batch of 1, 000 events, DeepFoc is two orders of magnitude faster than the parallelised FPFIT and over 5, 000 times faster than the Bayesian approach of BISTROP. This computational efficiency, combined with the solution stability demonstrated in the previous sections, validates DeepFoc as a viable tool for real-time integration into seismic surveillance workflows.

Discussion

We present a newly developed machine learning algorithm, named DeepFoc, aimed at estimating strike, dip, and rake parameters for a double-couple source model, using only P-wave first-motion polarities and amplitudes. Our results indicate that the method is able to properly learn the nonlinear relationship between the input data and source geometry, providing more constrained solutions than those typically obtained with grid search-based inversion approaches.

One of the key aspects of the model is that it is trained on zone-based synthetic data—to reproduce the actual Campi Flegrei seismicity- using a highly parallelised, on-the-fly generation procedure (see Methods section). This approach provides a virtually unlimited supply of training examples without requiring large memory capacity for storage, while also allowing for the introduction of variability into the generated data. In addition, the introduction of specific perturbations to synthetic data (e.g., randomly zeroing out some observations and Gaussian noise to the P-wave amplitudes) enables the model to learn in a more realistic setting, enhancing its robustness and adaptability when applied to real data. The model architecture adopts a three-branch design: the first branch processes the spatial coordinates of the seismic source and shares its latent representation with two parallel branches dedicated to P-wave polarities and amplitudes. Each of these incorporates multi-head attention and dilated 1D convolutions to extract local and contextual features across the seismic network. A gating mechanism dynamically modulates the influence of amplitude-derived features, supporting polarity-based predictions when amplitudes contribute useful complementary information (for more details, see the Framework Architecture in Methods Section). This architectural choice was driven by the need to effectively separate and later integrate spatial and observational data streams, particularly in the presence of missing or noisy inputs; DeepFoc demonstrates stable convergence across all four zones (see Fig. S5 in Supplementary Materials) and no evident signs of overfitting. Nonetheless, once adequate real datasets become available, they could enable supervised fine-tuning, potentially improving prediction accuracy and enhancing the model’s ability to learn region-specific propagation and site effects.

The comparison between DeepFoc predictions against FPFIT shows that DeepFoc systematically yields solutions with lower Kagan angles relative to the ground truth, and smaller residuals in P-wave amplitudes. This superior performance is especially evident under degraded data conditions, where azimuthal coverage is sparse or amplitudes are missing. The application of DeepFoc to 48 of the largest earthquakes recently occurred in the area and located in different zones of the Campi Flegrei caldera (Fig. 4) shows that the solutions predicted by DeepFoc are generally consistent with those obtained using BISTROP and FPFIT, although the latter yields multiple solutions for several of the analyzed events. The analysis of the RMSE, calculated as the root mean square error between observed and theoretical amplitudes corresponding to the best solution, indicates a better agreement of the DeepFoc solutions with the observed data. Furthermore, an assessment of the uncertainties associated with both DeepFoc, BISTROP and FPFIT solutions, performed using a jackknife test, shows that DeepFoc tends to produce more stable focal mechanisms.

An important result of our study is that the predictive performance of DeepFoc does not depend on the faulting style or on the specific zone. In the Campi Flegrei, where different focal mechanism may coexist within a small area (Fig. 4), DeepFoc successfully captured polarity variations across stations, providing distinct focal mechanisms for proximal events. As an example, Fig. 6 shows how two events, although closely located, can feature reversed polarities at the same stations, and thus different focal mechanism. This behaviour highlights the model’s ability to resolve fine-scale variations in source kinematics.Fig. 6Polarity and focal mechanism comparison for two closely located earthquakes. The analysis also reveals important seismotectonic features of the ongoing unrest. Earthquakes located in the central sector of the caldera exhibit primarily normal and oblique-normal mechanisms consistent with the reactivation of known SW-NE and SE-NW fault systems36. Overall, the results are in agreement with the recent findings reported by Tan et al.37. Similar observations on the variability of focal mechanisms were previously made by analysing the seismicity recorded during the previous 1982-1984 unrest episode26,38,39 that was associated with ground deformation and located on ring fractures interpreted as weakness zones26.

Moving toward the east and west borders of the caldera as well as off shore there is a variability of the fault solutions showing both normal and reverse-faulting. For the events located on the eastern sector of the inner caldera (Zone 3 Fig. 4), the fault plane solutions obtained in this study present a near vertical plane in accordance with the orientation of the new active fault zone mapped by Scotto di Uccio et al.40. A similar consideration can be done for the two events ID37228 and ID74314 for which the roughly NS striking plane corresponds to a fault bordering the western sector of the inner caldera (Fig. 4) Moreover, the events located offshore in the Gulf of Pozzuoli show reverse-faulting solutions in agreement with the results obtained for the seismicity recorded during the 1982-1984 crisis41–43. In particular, the fault mechanism of the event with ID45517 agrees with the strike of a previous identified fault40. As for the other off-shore events the strike of the two nodal planes seems to be not coherent with the orientation of the mapped fault.

While the proposed approach shows promising results, some limitations should be considered. The model is trained on synthetic data that are designed to reproduce a range of uncertainties expected in real observations. This strategy is intended to improve the robustness of the method; however, it inevitably reflects the assumptions adopted in the data generation process. Therefore, the model performance may be influenced by differences between synthetic and real data that are not fully captured, such as complex structural heterogeneities or noise characteristics. In addition, the current implementation does not explicitly incorporate uncertainties in the input data during the prediction stage. Future developments will aim to address this aspect by including features’ uncertainty. Despite these limitations, the computational efficiency and accuracy of DeepFoc make it suitable for integration into real-time seismic monitoring workflows, particularly in volcanic environments where rapid source characterization is important for hazard evaluation. The method could operate downstream of event detection, phase picking, and polarity reading pipelines, providing automated focal mechanisms to complement routine seismic analysis. This capability may be especially valuable during seismic swarms, when the rapid accumulation of small events can challenge manual procedures. In conclusion, DeepFoc suggests that machine learning can be effectively applied to focal mechanism estimation, even under conditions that are typically challenging for traditional inversion techniques. Its ability to provide fast, robust, and interpretable results highlights its potential as a useful tool for seismic monitoring (Fig. 5).

Methods

Data

Training a neural network requires a large and well-balanced dataset to ensure robust and reliable model performance. These conditions are rarely met when analysing small magnitude earthquakes for which accurate estimation of focal mechanism is still challenging primarily due to low signal-to-noise ratio that can hamper phase identification, polarity and amplitude measurement. The use of synthetic data is thus necessary to overcome these limitations while maintaining full control over the distribution of the event locations compared to network configuration and producing a comprehensive dataset suitable for machine learning applications. To this end, a custom synthetic data generation strategy is developed, specifically tailored to the Campi Flegrei region and capable of simulating a wide variety of realistic earthquake scenarios.

The data generation process is based on a one-dimensional layered velocity model specific for each analysed area and the actual configuration of the seismic network. The analysed volume is parametrized with a 3D grid with a spatial discretization adequate to ensure a proper coverage of possible source configurations. For each node of the grid, representing the hypocentre location of the simulated event, both P-polarities and the P-wave radiation pattern coefficient are computed at each station by sampling strike (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^{\circ }$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$360^{\circ }$$\end{document}), dip (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^{\circ }$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^{\circ }$$\end{document}) and rake (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-90^{\circ }$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^{\circ }$$\end{document}) angles at a fixed step.

The seismic network installed in the Campi Flegrei caldera covers an area with a radius of about 6 km and comprises 33 stations, ensuring high-quality data acquisition. Seismic simulations were performed using a reference point located at latitude \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$40.8233^{\circ }$$\end{document}N and longitude \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$14.1262^{\circ }$$\end{document}E with a spatial domain spanning between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-4.5, 4.0]$$\end{document} km in the east-west direction, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-4.0, 3.0]$$\end{document} km in the north-south direction, and a variable depth between [0.0, 5.0] km. To account for a distinct source-receiver configurations depending on the event location, and to improve the training phase of DeepFoc, it was decided to further subdivide the volume in 4 sub-volumes (zones) to better capture the peculiarities of events in different areas of the caldera (Fig. 1). In particular, Z1 includes the shallower earthquakes occurring in the hydrothermal system, Z2 contains the deeper off-shore events, Z3 the earthquakes occurring on the La Pietra fault system likely corresponding to the eastern border of the inner caldera, and Z4 corresponds to the western border of the caldera. Discretisation levels were set to [0.1, 0.1, 0.05] km in the east-west, north-south, and depth directions respectively, ensuring adequate spatial resolution. As for the focal mechanism angles, we used a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1^\circ$$\end{document} sampling step, while the adopted velocity model is that reported in44.

On-the-fly data generation

To overcome the impractical storage requirements associated with large synthetic seismic datasets, we developed an on-the-fly data generation strategy. This method dynamically generates new batches of synthetic seismic events at each training epoch, eliminating the need for extensive data storage while ensuring continuous diversity and representativeness within the training set. The synthetic data generation process is structured around a three-dimensional spatial grid that defines all potential hypocentral locations within the study volume. Grid points are parameterized by spatial coordinates (x, y, z) with configurable discretization levels, allowing for flexibility in resolution. In addition, the dataset includes focal mechanism parameters characterized by strike, dip, and rake angles. A multinomial sampling approach is used for both spatial coordinates and focal mechanism parameters, allowing for statistically rigorous yet stochastic generation of different earthquake scenarios. Once hypocenters are sampled, station azimuths are computed based on the relative positions of the seismic stations with respect to each epicenter. Takeoff angles are calculated using hypocentral depths, epicentral distances and the selected 1D velocity model. These parameters are fed into a dedicated simulation function that calculates the theoretical P-wave radiation pattern coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document}31 for each seismic station, conditioned on the selected strike, dip, and rake angles. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} are then separated into polarities, corresponding to the signs of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document}, and into amplitudes, which are the absolute values of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document}. As a result, each generated batch contains structured seismic data including hypocentral coordinates (x, y, z), station azimuths, takeoff angles, polarities, amplitudes, and focal mechanism parameters.

The need for this approach becomes clear when considering the huge number of earthquake scenarios that could potentially be generated by traditional static storage approaches. In the Campi Flegrei area, the spatial grid includes about 112, 000 different hypocentral locations, and combined with the full range of focal mechanism parameters, the total number of unique seismic scenarios becomes extremely large. Since each seismic scenario occupies approximately a kilobyte of memory, the total storage demand would quickly escalate to several hundred terabytes. This is a critical bottleneck for large-scale, data-driven seismic studies.

By adopting an on-the-fly data generation approach, synthetic seismic data is produced dynamically at each training iteration, effectively circumventing storage limitations. This method resolves the challenge posed by large data volumes while ensuring continual variability in the training dataset.

Zeroing

One of the first preprocessing steps involves zeroing a fraction of the recorded polarities, which increases input variability and encourages the model to develop more robust generalization capabilities. In real seismic measurements, data recorded by stations may be incomplete or affected by errors due to low signal-to-noise ratio, or other issues. Partial zeroing of polarities helps simulate these conditions, preventing the model from becoming overly dependent on individual data points and instead encouraging it to infer focal mechanisms even when some information are missing. To simulate realistic patterns of missing or unreliable data, we draw the fraction of zeroed observations from a parameterized Beta distribution with mode \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m=1$$\end{document} and concentration parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=4$$\end{document}. The mode m defines the most probable zeroing level, in this case close to full data loss, while k controls how tightly the distribution is concentrated around that mode. A higher k yields less variability around the mode, whereas a lower k produces a flatter distribution, allowing for a broader range of sparsity levels. This approach differs from uniform or fixed-percentage dropout, which tends to generate overly homogeneous patterns. Instead, for each training event, a random zeroing level is sampled and applied by masking the corresponding fraction of polarity and amplitude values. This stochastic degradation mimics real-world observational noise, station outages, and anthropic noise. As a result, the model is trained under diverse and realistic input conditions, improving its ability to generalize across variable-quality datasets. Mathematical details of the Beta distribution and its parametrization are provided in Supplementary Materials (Section 1.B).

Another important goal of this preprocessing step is to ensure that the neural network does not become conditioned to processing a fixed number of jointly available polarities and amplitudes. In practical scenarios, seismic events do not always have both types of data available, particularly when small magnitude earthquakes are analysed. In this study, a scenario is simulated in which the presence of an amplitude measurement guarantees the availability of the corresponding polarity; otherwise, only the polarity is preserved. Consequently, in addition to zeroing some polarities, selective zeroing is applied to amplitudes. This process follows similar logic to polarity zeroing but differs in how the selected values are handled; instead of being set to zero, selected amplitude values are assigned a value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document}. This distinction ensures that missing data can be clearly identified and prevents confusion with naturally low amplitude values. If missing amplitudes were simply set to zero, they could be mistaken for valid but small amplitude measurements. By using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document}, the model receives explicit information that the value is not available.

Polarity flipping

In addition to zeroing, another transformation applied during preprocessing is polarity flipping of a subset of recorded polarities. This can occur due to various factors, for example operator misreading or a wrong automatic procedure outcome. To introduce this variability, the number of polarities to be flipped is fixed, and a random selection is made. For each selected value, the sign is reversed; this ensures that the model is not overly sensitive to polarity inconsistencies and can still infer focal mechanisms accurately in the presence of measurement errors.

Gaussian noise

Adding noise is a technique that allows simulating measurement errors, and it can be implemented in various ways depending on different probability distributions. The choice of the distribution is determined by the nature of the error to be simulated, which in turn depends on both the nature of the phenomenon and the instrument. In this case, the noise was sampled from a Gaussian distribution with a mean of 0 and variance given by the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{x} = x + N(0, \gamma \, x)=N(x, \gamma \, x) \qquad \text {with } 0< \gamma < 1$$\end{document}The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} controls the intensity of the Gaussian noise added to the data. Since the variance of a normal distribution determines the dispersion of values around the mean, the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} scales this dispersion proportionally to the value x. The constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0< \gamma < 1$$\end{document} ensures that the added noise remains a fraction of the original value x; if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is small, the added noise is limited, so the data remains closer to the original values; if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is close to 1, the noise becomes comparable to the signal itself, introducing greater uncertainty.

Amplitude normalization

A final preprocessing step involves normalizing amplitude values within each individual event. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots , a_n\}$$\end{document} denote the set of amplitude values recorded for a single seismic event, where each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_i$$\end{document} corresponds to the measurement at the i-th station. For each event, the maximum amplitude is identified, and all amplitude values are divided by this maximum; this preserves the relative amplitude relationships within the event while bounding the input range between 0 and 1. The amplitude values that were previously set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document} to indicate missing data are excluded from this normalization and remain unchanged and the normalization is applied as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{a}_i = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \displaystyle \frac{a_i}{\max (A)} & \text {if } a_i \ne -1 \\ -1 & \text {if } a_i = -1 \end{array}\right. }$$\end{document}This approach allows the model to focus on the internal amplitude structure of each event, while avoiding misleading effects due to missing values or event-to-event amplitude scale differences. Moreover, this normalization scheme was driven by domain experts to ensure consistency with real-event measurements, which exhibit a similar amplitude structure, thereby enabling direct application of the model to real seismic events.

Framework architecture

The proposed neural network is a deep architecture specifically designed to effectively integrate heterogeneous data types, including the spatial coordinates of seismic events and stations, as well as the amplitudes and polarities of P-wave recordings and is illustrated in Fig. 9.Fig. 9Overview of the proposed neural network architecture for focal mechanism estimation. The model is divided into three main branches: the hypocenter branch (top) processes the spatial coordinates (x, y, z) of seismic events through 4 fully connected layers with ReLU activation functions. The polarity branch (left) analyzes P-wave polarities, station coordinates, presence indicators, and the output of the hypocenter branch; the amplitude branch (right) processes normalized P-wave amplitudes using the same input structure. Both the polarity and amplitude branches incorporate multi-head attention mechanisms and a sequence of dilated 1D convolutions (dilation rates from 1 to 4, kernel size 3) to capture spatial and contextual dependencies across stations. The resulting features are then processed through fully connected networks: the polarity branch consists of 7 layers with ReLU activation followed by a final layer with Tanh activation, while the amplitude branch consists of 4 layers with ReLU followed by a final layer with Sigmoid activation. The amplitude features are modulated by a gating mechanism that weighs their contribution relative to the polarity features. The resulting fused representation is finally mapped to 6 outputs corresponding to the sine and cosine values of the strike, dip, and rake angles, using activation functions specifically designed to enforce the physical constraints associated with each angle.

After, the polarity and amplitude data are passed through multi-head attention mechanisms with five heads, enabling the model to capture contextual dependencies and emphasize the most relevant features for predicting focal mechanisms. These two branches are then processed through a series of dilated 1D convolutions, and each convolutional layer uses a kernel size of 3 and takes five input channels to produce a 32-channel output. The convolutions employ dilation rates of 1, 2, 3, and 4, expanding the receptive field without increasing the number of parameters; this allows the model to capture both local and long-range dependencies within the input sequences. The outputs of these convolutions are flattened and passed through separate fully connected networks for the polarity and amplitude branches: the polarity branch is processed through eight fully connected layers, progressively reducing the dimensionality from 1024 to 8 neurons, with each layer using ReLU activation functions. The amplitude branch follows a similar path but with fewer fully connected layers, culminating in a final layer followed by a Sigmoid activation function, producing a gating factor between 0 and 1. The gating mechanism plays a key role in fusing the information extracted from the polarity and amplitude branches. The learned gating factor dynamically adjusts the contribution of each data stream according to the following formula:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x = y_P + \text {gate} \times y_A \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_A$$\end{document} represents features derived from amplitude data, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_P$$\end{document} corresponds to polarity features, and gate is the fusion factor ranging between 0 and 1. The combined vector is further processed through a fully connected layer with a Tanh activation function, reducing the output to six components, which represent the sine and cosine pairs for the three key angles. For the strike and rake angles, the outputs pass through fully connected layers with Tanh activation functions, ensuring the predicted sine and cosine values lie within the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-1, 1]$$\end{document}. In contrast, since the dip angle ranges from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^\circ$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^\circ$$\end{document}, the model uses a Sigmoid activation function to constrain the predicted sine and cosine values to the physically valid range of [0, 1]. The original angles are reconstructed using the two-argument arctangent function applied to the predicted sine and cosine pairs, which returns angles in the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-180^\circ$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$180^\circ$$\end{document}, correctly determining the quadrant based on the signs of the sine and cosine. Optimization parameters

The training procedure is designed to integrate data generation and model optimization within a unified framework. Instead of relying on a fixed training data set, synthetic data batches are generated on-the-fly at each epoch. For each batch, focal mechanism parameters, station distributions, and signal properties, including P-wave polarities and amplitudes, are sampled from physically plausible distributions, ensuring broad coverage of possible observational configurations. This strategy exposes the model to a virtually unlimited variety of input scenarios, promoting generalization and robustness while reducing the risk of overfitting to specific event station geometries. Each epoch consists of 100 randomly generated batches, each with a batch size of 1024, for a total of 10, 000 training epochs.

The model is optimized using the Adam algorithm, with a fixed learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document} and a weight decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-6}$$\end{document}. No learning rate scheduling is used. Training is stopped when the validation loss does not improve for 500 consecutive epochs, early stopping criterion. All experiments are run on NVIDIA GPU with 46 GB of memory.

Loss function

To train the network to accurately predict focal mechanisms, we designed a composite loss function that captures both global and local discrepancies between the predicted and target solutions. This loss combines two complementary components: the Kagan angle, which quantifies the overall geometric difference between double-couple mechanisms, and a Mean Square Error (MSE) term applied to the trigonometric representation of the strike, dip, and rake angles.

Kagan angle

The Kagan angle29 is a widely used metric in seismology to compare focal mechanisms. It represents the minimum 3D rotation needed to align the axes of pressure, tension and null (P, T, and B) of two double-couple (DC) mechanisms. The Kagan angle is always within the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^\circ$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$120^\circ$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^\circ$$\end{document} indicates two identical mechanisms, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$120^\circ$$\end{document} represents the maximum possible difference between two DC mechanisms45. While the Kagan angle provides a robust global measure of similarity between two focal mechanisms, it does not offer localized information about the discrepancies on individual angular values such as strike, dip, and rake. To encourage finer local corrections during training, we introduce an additional loss component based on the MSE applied to each of the three angles. This term complements the Kagan loss by promoting convergence toward the correct solution not only in terms of overall geometry, but also with respect to the local alignment of each individual parameter.

Mean square error for focal mechanisms

To ensure the MSE term can be applied meaningfully, it is necessary to eliminate the ambiguity between fault plane and auxiliary plane. As shown in46, this can be achieved by adopting a convention that restricts the rake angle (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}), which defines the direction of slip on the fault plane. Since the sine of the dip angle (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}) is always non-negative (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sin \delta \ge 0$$\end{document}), the rake can be restricted to one of the following intervals:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-90^\circ \le \lambda \le 90^\circ \quad \text {or} \quad 90^\circ \le \lambda \le 270^\circ$$\end{document}instead of allowing the full range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-180^\circ \le \lambda < 180^\circ$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^\circ \le \lambda < 360^\circ$$\end{document}. This restriction effectively selects one of the two nodal planes to represent each mechanism. By adopting this convention, each focal mechanism can be uniquely expressed by a single triplet of strike (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}), dip (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}), and rake (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}) values, and this choice offers several principal advantages. It allows a reduction in the volume of training data without compromising the coverage of the focal-mechanism space: restricting the rake angle to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-90^\circ , +90^\circ ]$$\end{document} halves the interval width, and consequently the number of required grid points and generated data. Secondly, the removal of ambiguity in the data broadens the range of metrics and loss functions that may be employed. Furthermore, the inclusion of a MSE term in a loss function is suitable for data characterised by Gaussian noise: mathematically, the MSE is equivalent to maximising the likelihood of the observed data under the assumption of Gaussian errors with constant variance47. This makes the MSE a natural choice for regression problems where errors arise from noisy data. However, directly applying MSE to angular parameters is problematic due to the periodic nature of angles. Ablation study

To assess the contribution of the individual architectural components of the proposed DeepFoc model, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study in which four key elements were selectively removed: (i) the attention layer, (ii) the multi-convolution module stacks, (iii) the dilation in the convolutional layers, and (iv) the double-branch structure of the network (see Fig. 9 for the full architecture). Case (ii) can be seen as a sub-case of case (iii), since reducing the network to only one convolutional layer also eliminates dilation, as it is only possible to choose one type of dilation value (set by default to 1, i.e. no dilation).

Model performance was evaluated in terms of the root mean square error (RMSE) computed between the theoretical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} reconstructed from the model predictions and the corresponding observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} derived from the real test data. All ablated configurations were trained and tested under the same experimental setup as the full DeepFoc model to ensure a fair comparison.

The use of a single processing path imposes a rigid coupling between predicted and observed amplitudes on the network, leading to a strong dependency on the input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} values and resulting in a focal mechanism strictly constrained by them. However, as previously discussed, observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} values can be subject to uncertainties and noise; consequently, the results of a single-branch model, although minimising the RMSE, may be seismologically inconsistent. The introduction of the gating mechanism at the end of the two-branch structure aims to resolve this issue: it assigns greater weight to polarities (which are generally more reliable) and uses amplitudes to refine the output while preventing the latter from becoming the primary source of inference (see subsection The choice of the double branch)

In contrast, the full DeepFoc architecture, which separates and later integrates polarity- and amplitude-related information through the double-branch design, is less prone to over-reliance on the observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} values. As a consequence, its RMSE distribution is slightly broader and shifted toward marginally higher values, reflecting a more conservative and robust fit.

The other ablated configurations (without attention, without multi-convolutions, and without dilation) consistently show a degradation in performance, with broader RMSE distributions and heavier tails toward higher error values, indicating a reduced capability to capture the spatial and contextual variability of the radiation pattern.

The choice of the double branch

Although the analysis of RMSE on real test data suggests that the single-branch architecture (Without Double Branch) achieves superior performance, this metric alone provides a partial and potentially misleading view. To verify the actual robustness of the models in an operational scenario, we introduced a metric based on physical consistency, the Discrepancy Error, defined as the number of stations per event where the observed polarity differs from the theoretical polarity predicted by the estimated focal mechanism.

Figure 12 compares the distribution of discrepancy errors for the full DeepFoc model (Double Branch) and the ablated single-branch version. The results clearly reverse the trend observed with the RMSE analysis. The DeepFoc model exhibits a distribution significantly shifted towards zero, indicating a higher success rate in matching the observed first-motion polarities. In contrast, the single-branch model shows a marked increase in the number of events with multiple polarity mismatches (higher discrepancy values).

This finding confirms that the single-branch architecture, by processing polarities and amplitudes in a unified feature space without gating, tends to overfit the amplitude values. While this minimises the numerical residual (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_p$$\end{document} RMSE), it sacrifices the geometric consistency of the solution, leading to focal mechanisms that do not respect the fundamental constraints imposed by the polarities. The double-branch design, with its specific gating mechanism, is therefore essential: it effectively decouples the contribution of the two observables, prioritising the structural reliability of polarities while using amplitudes as a refinement, thus ensuring robust performance on real, noisy data.
