An Investigation of Heterogeneity in Cerebellar Cortical Pathology Across Essential Tremor Cases: A Cluster Analysis
Authors: Elan D. Louis, Roberto S. Hernandez, Aoife S. Weiss, Phyllis L. Faust
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent years have witnessed an evolution of our understanding of the underlying neuropathological features of essential tremor (ET). Studies have revealed a cascade of neurodegenerative changes in the cerebellar cortex, centered on the Purkinje cell (PC) population and its surrounding neuronal populations and connections. A fundamental question is whether there is heterogeneity across ET cases with respect to the expression of this cerebellar pathology. We examined postmortem tissue from 155 ET brains using a set of 15 quantitative metrics of cerebellar cortical pathology determined by histologic and immunohistochemical methods. These metrics reflect changes across the PC body (PC counts, empty baskets, heterotopias), PC dendrites (swellings), PC axon (torpedoes), basket cell axonal hypertrophy and climbing fiber-PC dendrite synaptic changes. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to identify the major data dimensions. Outlier cases were then identified, followed by k-means clustering to identify subgroups of ET cases. The first 5 principal components explained 78.6% of the variance. Twenty-two (14.2%) cases were outliers. k-means clustering indicated that these 22 cases were distributed in 4 clusters. In each cluster, specific quantitative metrics of cerebellar pathology showed greater severity of change than observed in the main group of 133 ET cases. A small number of clinical features distinguished clusters from one another, although these analyses were limited by sample size. Cerebellar cortical pathology in ET was not homogeneously expressed across every case. It was possible to identify clusters of cases that differed with respect to the expression of postmortem features.

Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is the among the most common movement disorders. Its prevalence is extraordinarily high, with an estimated 7 million affected people in the United States, representing 2.2% of the entire US population [1, 2]. The disease is often progressive and debilitating [3, 4], resulting in substantial health care costs [5]. Recent years have witnessed an evolution of our understanding of the underlying neuropathological features of essential tremor (ET) [6, 7]. Studies have revealed a cascade of neurodegenerative changes in the cerebellar cortex, centered on the Purkinje cell (PC) population as well as its surrounding neuronal populations and connections [8–13]. A recent study demonstrated that it was possible to distinguish the vast majority of ET from control brains based on the presence of these changes [14].

Now, with the availability of a sizable collection of ET brains whose neuropathology has been characterized in detail, a fundamental question is whether the same set of morphological changes is uniformly expressed across all cases. Alternatively, is it possible to identify clusters or groupings of cases with respect to these changes? To date, studies have demonstrated that for each morphological change, there is a range of expression, with a defined variance around a mean value [7–13]. But beyond that, is it possible to further segment cases to identify groupings or clusters of cases?

There has been a long search for subtypes of ET, and more specifically, clinical subtypes. This search has met with limited success, with some clinical subtypes clearly established (e.g., familial vs. sporadic) [15, 16] and others controversial (e.g., ET plus) [17, 18]. Beyond clinical subtypes, are there biologically based subtypes in ET, grounded in the nature of the morphological changes? Leveraging data from a large collection of ET brains prospectively harvested over a more than twenty-year period, our aim was to further interrogate the data to determine whether there are clusters of cases based on expression of these postmortem changes in the cerebellar cortex. We addressed this aim using cluster analysis, an unsupervised dimension reduction analysis that does not make a priori assumptions about likely relationships within the data. Furthermore, if there are identifiable clusters, we asked whether these differ from one another with respect to their clinical features.

Methods

Brain Repository, Study Subjects, and Clinical Assessments

All ET brains were banked in the Essential Tremor Centralized Brain Repository (ETCBR) at the New York Brain Bank at Columbia University. The ETCBR, which serves ET cases throughout the United States, represents a longstanding, collaboration between investigators at University Texas Southwestern Medical Center and Columbia Universities [6–8, 13]. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

As described in detail [14], ET diagnoses were assigned by a senior movement disorders neurologist specializing in tremor (E.D.L.) using three sequential methods, as has been our practice for 20 years and as employed in over 50 publications [6–8, 13, 14, 19, 20]. First, the clinical diagnosis of ET was initially assigned by treating neurologist(s), and second, confirmed by E.D.L. who reviewed semi-structured clinical questionnaires, medical records, and Archimedes spirals [6–8, 13, 14]. Third, a detailed videotaped, neurological examination was performed and a total tremor score (TTS) assigned (range = 0–36 [maximum]) [6–8, 13, 14]. These ratings and clinical questionnaire data are used when assigning a final diagnosis of ET [6–8, 13, 14], using published diagnostic criteria, which have been shown to be both reliable and valid [21, 22]. Many of the ET cases were additionally enrolled in a longitudinal, prospective study that necessitated regular, 18-month, detailed videotaped neurological examinations (NIH R01 NS086736) [14].

The current analyses included 155 ET cases who were selected because they lacked widespread marked hypoxic-ischemic damage or evidence of other neurodegenerative disease pathology such as progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, or traumatic encephalopathy [7, 14]. ET cases with all levels of Alzheimer’s disease neuropathological changes (ADNC) (none, low, intermediate and high) [23] or Lewy pathology [24] were included in this study.

Clinical assessments included structured questionnaires that collected data on demographic factors, family history of ET in first-degree relatives, and a videotaped neurological examination, during which the severity of postural tremor (1 examination item) and kinetic tremor (5 examination items), assessed in each arm, was rated by a movement disorders neurologist using a 0 to 3 scale, yielding a TTS [6–8, 13, 14]. Intention tremor during the finger-nose-finger maneuver was rated as present (1), possibly present (0.5) or absent (0), and the intention tremor score summed this rating in each arm (range = 0–2) [25]. Voice tremor was assessed during sustained phonation and during conversational speech, and was noted to be present or absent, as were head tremor and jaw tremor [26]. The cranial tremor score summed the presence or absence of tremor in the voice, head or jaw (range = 0–3). The videotaped neurological examination also included the motor portion of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) [27], including assessments of speech, facial expression, rest tremor (with arms in 3 positions: resting in the lap, relaxed at sides while standing, while walking), bradykinesia, posture, arising from a chair, and gait while walking and turning [28]. Dystonia on examination was also assessed via views of the face, neck, trunk, and extremities. ET cases signed informed consent approved by the University Ethics Board at either Columbia University, Yale University or University of Texas Southwestern, as the sites of the two principal investigators (E.D.L. and P.L.F).

Tissue Processing

The New York Brain Bank at Columbia University performed a neuropathological examination on all ET brains used for this study. Seventeen standardized blocks were prepared for each brain sample [19]. Seven-µm thick paraffin sections were prepared from each block and stained with Luxol fast blue/hematoxylin and eosin (LH&E) [19]. In addition, selected sections were stained by the Bielschowsky method, and with mouse monoclonal antibodies to alpha-synuclein (clone KM51, Novacastra), phosphorylated tau (clone AT8, Research Diagnostics, Flanders, NJ), and beta-amyloid (clone 6 F/3D, Dako, Carpenteria, CA) [13, 29]. A senior neuropathologist examined all tissues blinded to clinical information [30]. All brains were assigned Braak and Braak Alzheimer’s disease staging for neurofibrillary tangles, Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s disease (CERAD) ratings for neuritic plaques [23] and Braak staging for Lewy pathology [24].

Quantitative Metrics of Cerebellar Pathology

Formalin fixed tissue blocks (3 × 20 × 25-mm) were acquired from a parasagittal slice located ~ 1.5–2 cm from the cerebellar midline in each brain; each parasagittal slice contains anterior and posterior quadrangulate lobules, along with the underlying dentate nucleus [6]. Although previous studies from our lab demonstrated morphological changes in 37 metrics across a spectrum of cerebellar diseases [7, 31], this paper included eight of these metrics that provided high diagnostic discrimination between ET and control brains in a recent study [14]. We also used seven additional metrics among the 37 to further assess PC dendritic swellings, torpedoes and PC heterotopias, which have been found to differ between ET and controls in prior analyses [6, 7]. To account for variations in quantity of cerebellar cortex in the tissue block, we normalized several of these metrics (i.e., we divided by the PC layer length) and expressed them as per mm [32], as described [6]. To account for PC loss, which may affect the ability to detect a PC-associated morphological change and thus result in an artificially low value (e.g., the counts for displaced PCs [i.e., heterotopic PCs] and the counts for normally placed PCs would be low when PC counts are reduced), we divided other metrics by PC linear density [6, 7]. Methods included histologic stains (LH&E, Bielschowsky), immunostaining for vesicular glutamate transporter type 2 (VGlut2), and a dual immunostain for calbindin28k and glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) on paraffin sections [6, 7] (Fig. 1).

Data Analysis

Testing for Normalcy

We assessed the normality of continuously distributed variables using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. If these data were not normally distributed, we used non-parametric rather than parametric statistical tests.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

As a dimension-reduction technique, a PCA was performed through single value decomposition with all 15 metrics. To prevent any one metric from over-influencing the analysis, the data were centered and scaled. A scree plot was created, describing the proportion of total variance explained by each principal component.

Outlier Detection

Once the data were reduced in dimension, outlier cases were identified within each of the first five principal components. More specifically, outlier cases were determined by quartiles, by being less than Q1–1.5*IQR or greater than Q3 + 1.5*IQR [38]. If a case was identified as an outlier in one or more principal components, it was then classified as an outlier case in the dataset. Two-dimensional figures were then constructed to identify in which principal components these outlier cases were present simultaneously and to look for potential patterns. A one-dimensional figure focusing on each principal component was also included for ease of interpretation.

Outlier Clustering

After outliers were obtained from PCA results, k-means clustering was performed across all two-dimensional spaces. The optimal number of clusters was determined by the silhouette score, which measured how well each observation was matched to their respective cluster by considering the average distance from each point to all others in a cluster (within) and from each point to all others in different clusters (between). This score ranges from − 1 to 1, where the larger value represents better clustering performance (i.e., minimal within cluster distance and maximal between cluster distance) [39].

Differences in 15 Morphological Metrics Across Outlier Clusters

We examined whether there were differences in each of the 15 morphological metrics across ET cases. First, using either Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or Kruskall-Wallis tests, we compared all groups (the main group of ET cases as well as cases in each of the four identified outlier clusters). Second, using Dunn’s tests, we tested for differences between each of the four clusters vs. the main group of ET cases. We also assessed whether Braak and Braak AD stage, CERAD rating or Braak stage for Lewy pathology was responsible for outlier status – in a logistic regression model, the dependent variable was outlier (0 = no, 1 = yes) and the independent variables were the three noted above.

Differences in Clinical Features Across Outlier Clusters

We examined whether there were any clinical features that differed across ET cases. First, using either ANOVA or Kruskall-Wallis tests, we compared all groups (the main group of ET cases as well as cases in each of the four identified outlier clusters). Second, using Dunn’s tests, we tested for differences between each of the four clusters vs. the main group of ET cases.

Results

Case Characteristics

The demographic and clinical features of the 155 ET cases are shown (Table 1); the median age at death was 90.0 years but ranged from 68 to 101 years. Two-thirds had head tremor on examination, and nearly one-half had voice tremor. When present, rest tremor was limited to one or both arm(s) and occurred in the absence of other features of parkinsonism. Dystonia, when present, consisted of mild dystonic postures in the setting of long-standing and severe ET.

Outlier Clustering

k-means clustering indicated that the use of 4 clusters was optimal for grouping the outlier samples (Fig. 4). These four clusters had 9, 4, 4, and 5 outlier cases, respectively (Table 2). The last two principal components (PC4 & PC5) did not have any outliers. Therefore, all two-dimensional comparisons that included either PC4 or PC5 had the same outliers within their respective principal component (i.e., PC1/PC4 and PC1/PC5 had the same outliers; PC2/PC4 and PC2/PC5 had the same outliers; PC3/PC4 and PC3/PC5 had the same outliers). In Table 2, an “X” represents in which principal component the case was classified as an outlier.

Differences in 15 morphological metrics across outlier clusters

We examined whether there were differences in each of the 15 morphological metrics across ET cases by comparing each of the four identified outlier clusters with the main group of ET cases (Table 3). We observed differences in 12 of the 15 morphological metrics (Table 3). The clusters did not differ versus the main group with respect to PC body linear density, basket cell rating, or climbing fibers in outer 20% of ML metrics.

Discussion

This study examined postmortem changes in the ET cerebellar cortex and demonstrated that these changes were not homogeneously expressed across all cases. We were able to identify ET cases whose expression of these features made them outliers from the main group of cases. More specifically, there were four small clusters of outliers, which as a whole comprised 22 of 155 (approximately 14%) cases. Furthermore, within each of the four clusters, specific quantitative metrics of morphologic change showed greater severity of change than observed in the main group of 133 ET cases. For outlier cluster 1, this involved heterotopic PC metrics, for outlier cluster 2, this involved PC dendritic swellings, and for clusters 3 and 4, this involved a more diffuse number of metrics centered on both dendritic swellings and torpedoes but also involving several other metrics.

There are other clusters of ET cases with respect to morphological changes, with approximately 25% of ET cases having Lewy pathology on postmortem examination [28, 40, 41]. However, ET cases with vs. without Lewy pathology did not differ with respect to the presence and severity of a host of postmortem changes in the cerebellar cortex [28]. Furthermore, in our regression model, Lewy pathology did not predict outlier status (yes vs. no).

The clusters of ET cases we observed in these analyses were defined based on the expression of postmortem degenerative changes in the cerebellar cortex. In these analyses, we explored whether these case clusters differed with respect to clinical features from the main body of ET cases, and in some instances, there were either numerical or statistically significant differences. However, none of these clinical features was robust enough to be used as a predictive marker of morphological cluster membership. Prior studies have examined the postmortem changes in ET in case groups stratified by clinical features, including age of onset [42], presence or absence of additional non-tremor motor features [43], and family history [44], and there were no observed differences between these clinically-defined strata. Those prior analyses differed from the current study in that the starting point was the clinical subtype and the analyses attempted to identify postmortem differences between these clinical subtypes; here, we begin with the morphological cluster and then attempted to identify clinical features that differed across these clusters.

These analyses focus on observed morphological changes in ET cases as a basis for subgrouping. It would be of value to eventually extend these studies to underlying molecular changes in ET, once these become more apparent.

A limitation of this study is the large number of comparisons performed in Tables 3 and 4, making the possibility of Type II statistical error (falsely rejecting the null hypothesis) considerable. However, at the same time, given the small sample size involved in our outlier subgroups, the alternative possibility of Type I statistical error (failing to reject the null hypothesis) is a real one. With both the possibility of Type I and Type II statistical errors, one should regard these comparisons as exploratory in nature, designed to guide future analyses with larger sample sizes. That being said, the number of brains required to generate these analyses was considerable (n = 155), requiring many years to harvest.

The study had numerous strengths. First, ET cases were followed prospectively and phenotyped in detail by a movement disorders neurologist. Second, cases were derived from the largest ET brain bank, resulting in a sample size of 155. Third, postmortem studies of brain morphology were detailed, and the metrics we studied were identified through intensive efforts over the last twenty years in characterizing these brains [14].

Summary

In summary, the cerebellar cortical pathology in ET was not homogeneously present across all cases. It was possible to identify clusters of cases that differed with respect to postmortem features.
