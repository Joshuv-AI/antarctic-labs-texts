Compiling Any 𝖬𝖨𝖯 ∗ \mathsf{MIP}^{*} into a (Succinct) Classical Interactive Argument
Andrew Huang, Yael Tauman Kalai

Abstract
We present a generic compiler that converts any 𝖬𝖨𝖯 ∗ \mathsf{MIP}^{*} protocol into a succinct interactive argument where the communication and the verifier are classical , and where post-quantum soundness relies on the post-quantum sub-exponential hardness of the Learning with Errors ( 𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{LWE} ) problem. Prior to this work, such a compiler for 𝖬𝖨𝖯 ∗ \mathsf{MIP}^{*} was given by Kalai, Lombardi, Vaikuntanathan and Yang (STOC 2022), but the post-quantum soundness of this compiler is still under investigation. More generally, our compiler can be applied to any 𝖰𝖨𝖯 \mathsf{QIP} protocol which is sound only against semi-malicious provers that follow the prescribed protocol, but with possibly malicious initial state. Our compiler consists of two steps. We first show that if a language ℒ \mathcal{L} has a 𝖰𝖨𝖯 \mathsf{QIP} with semi-malicious soundness, where the prover runs in time T T , then ℒ ∈ 𝖰𝖬𝖠𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤 ⁡ ( T ) \mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(T) . Then we construct a succinct classical argument for any such language, where the communication complexity grows polylogarithmically with T T , under the post-quantum sub-exponential hardness of 𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{LWE} . Note: After this work was submitted, an independent and concurrent work [ BKL+25 ] resolved the question of quantum soundness of the KLVY compiler.

Introduction

Proof systems lie at the foundation of both modern cryptography and complexity theory, and underlie the very definition of the complexity class
\mathsf{NP}
. Since the 1980s, there has been remarkable progress in their study, leading to a sequence of increasingly powerful models: interactive proofs (
\mathsf{IP}
)
[
GMR85
,
Bab85
]
, interactive arguments (
\mathsf{IA}
)
[
BCC88
]
where soundness is guaranteed only against computationally-bounded provers, multi-prover interactive proofs (
\mathsf{MIP}
)
[
BOGKW88
]
, probabilistically checkable proofs (
\mathsf{PCP}
)
[
AS98
,
ALM+98
]
, and more recently, interactive oracle proofs (
\mathsf{IOP}
)
[
BSCS16
,
RRR16
]
. These notions have been deeply influential in both theory and practice, yielding breakthroughs such as the PCP Theorem and inapproximability results, and enabling scalability in blockchain and other cryptographic applications.

The advent of quantum computation has motivated the study of various proof models in the quantum setting. Quantum interactive proofs (
\mathsf{QIP}
)
[
Wat99
]
generalize classical interactive proofs by allowing both prover and verifier to be quantum machines exchanging quantum messages. Similarly, quantum multi-prover interactive proofs (
\mathsf{QMIP}
)
[
KM03
]
generalize classical multi-prover interactive proofs by allowing all parties to be quantum machines exchanging quantum messages. One other class of multi-prover protocols which has been studied is
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
, where the provers are quantum and may share entanglement, but where the verifier (and communication) is restricted to being classical
[
CHTW04
]
. The complexity classes
\mathsf{QMIP}
and
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
are known to be equivalent
[
RUV13
]
, and in fact the conversion between the two classes of protocols preserves the computational efficiency of honest provers (assuming the use of two additional provers and more rounds).

In both the classical and quantum settings, the overarching goal of a proof system is for a computationally powerful prover (or provers) to convince a computationally bounded verifier of the truth of a statement. Typically, the computational power refers to the time complexity, where often the verifier is assumed to run in polynomial time, while the prover(s) may run in super-polynomial time. However, time complexity is not the only axis of separation: a prover (or provers) may also have access to quantum capabilities that the verifier lacks. We will refer to protocols with a single
quantum
prover but
classical
polynomial-time verifier as classical
\mathsf{QIP}
’s (or classical
\mathsf{QIA}
’s in the case where soundness holds only against computationally bounded provers).

An important question that received significant attention in recent years is the following:

Which quantum computations can we classically verify (efficiently and succinctly)?

1.2
Our Results

In this work, we present an alternative compiler which generically transforms any
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
(even in the non-succinct or multi-prover setting) for a language into a fully succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
. The soundness of our resulting classical
\mathsf{QIA}
is related to the quantum value of the underlying
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
, assuming the post-quantum hardness of the
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption.

Our transformation is prover-efficient if the prover is given multiple copies of the (honest) auxiliary input state of all provers in the underlying
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
and this state is
real-valued
(i.e., of the form
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
where
\alpha_{x}\in\mathbb{R}
for every
x
).
3
3

3

The restriction to real-valued auxiliary states is not necessary for soundness and is used only to ensure efficient prover runtime.
We note that all known protocols for classically verifying quantum computations (starting with Mahadev
[
Mah18
]
) are only prover-efficient under these restrictions.

The transformed protocol achieves the standard computational soundness guarantee: no cheating prover running in quantum time
\text{poly}(T)
, where
T
is a parameter
T
that influences our computational hardness assumption, can convince the verifier of a false statement. Moreover, the resulting classical
\mathsf{QIA}
is
succinct
: its communication complexity is polynomial in the security parameter
\lambda

\text{poly}(\lambda)+\widetilde{O}(|x|)
, where
x
is the statement being verified. The prover’s runtime grows only polynomially relative to that of the original protocol.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

Let
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k},V)
be any

\mathcal{L}
. Denote the honest provers’ runtime by
t_{P}
, and suppose the auxiliary states of the honest provers are real-valued. Let
T=T(\lambda)
be any function such that
t_{P}\leq T(\lambda)\leq 2^{\lambda}
.

Assuming the post-quantum
T
-hardness of the Learning with Errors (
\mathsf{LWE}
) problem, there exists a
T
-secure
classical

\mathsf{QIA}
for
\mathcal{L}
, where the number of rounds and the communication complexity are
\text{poly}(\lambda
), the verifier runtime is
\text{poly}(\lambda)+\widetilde{O}(|x|)
, where
x
is the statement being verified, and the prover runtime is
\text{poly}(t_{P})
(given
\text{poly}(t_{P})
copies of the provers’ initial state).

More generally, we extend the result above to any
\mathsf{QIP}
that is sound only against
semi-malicious
provers, which are provers that follow the prescribed protocol honestly but with potentially malicious auxiliary input states.
We note that every
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
protocol (even those with only semi-malicious soundness) can be trivially converted into a
\mathsf{QIP}
with semi-malicious soundness. Specifically, the
\mathsf{QIP}
prover’s initial state can be thought of as the combined initial states of all players in the
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}

\mathsf{QIP}
verifier sends all of the
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
’s referee’s queries to the prover (in the clear), and the
\mathsf{QIP}
prover sends the answers obtained by emulating the players in the
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
protocol. Semi-malicious soundness holds since the malicious prover must act honestly according to the (local) prescribed actions of the
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
provers.

4

4

Our protocol also readily applies to
\mathsf{QMIP}

Thus, our work extends the classical-verifier paradigm to a broader class of quantum proof systems.

for
\mathcal{L}

), the verifier runtime is
\text{poly}(\lambda)+\widetilde{O}(|x|)
, where
x

\text{poly}(t_{P})
(given
\text{poly}(t_{P})

Open Problems.

Our result raises some intriguing questions related to classical verification of quantum computation. First, is the restriction to real-valued witnesses necessary for prover efficiency? We remark that this is an open problem even for
\mathsf{QMA}
, as the protocols of
[
Mah18
,
MNZ24
,
GKNV24
]
(implicitly) established verification only for languages with this restriction. While the
[
KLVY23
]
compiler presents a different potential route to classical verification, current applications to
\mathsf{QMA}
verification
[
MNZ24
]
also employ a reduction to a
\mathsf{QMA}
-complete language which implicitly assumes real-valued witnesses (or copies of the witnesses for the new language).

Secondly, like all other known classical verification procedures, our protocol is privately verifiable. One tantalizing question is whether this property is inherent. Concretely, for example, could one make our protocol (and others) publicly verifiable with the additional assumption of post-quantum indistinguishability obfuscation (
\mathsf{iO}
)?

Lastly, we believe that similar techniques can be used to analyze a similar compilation for
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}

games
(which are not for deciding languages) that preserves the quantum value of the underlying game, as well as to the problem of succinctly sampling from
\mathsf{SampBQP}
. We leave further exploration of these questions for future work.

Technical Overview

In this section, we highlight the main ideas in the proof of our main result, that any semi-maliciously sound
\mathsf{QIP}

(P,V)
for a language
\mathcal{L}
can be transformed into a
succinct
classical
\mathsf{QIA}
for
\mathcal{L}
. We prove this theorem via the following two steps:

Step 1:

We first argue that if a language
\mathcal{L}
has a semi-malicious
\mathsf{QIP}

(P,V)
, where the (honest) prover
P
runs in time
t_{p}
, then
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
, where
\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
is the class of all languages for which membership can be verified by a quantum computer running in time
\text{poly}(t_{P})
.

To this end, we consider the large (uniform) quantum circuit
C
, that takes as input an instance
x
and the prover’s (potentially malicious) auxiliary state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, and implements an honest interaction between the quantum prover and verifier in the underlying
\mathsf{QIP}

(P(\boldsymbol{\rho}),V)(x)
. Specifically,
C
will use separate registers to simulate the prover’s internal state and the verifier’s internal state. It will also use separate designated registers to store the messages passed between the prover and verifier. It will apply the honest prover and verifier’s unitaries in alternating order (generating random verifier coins by using ancilla qubits) before applying the verifier’s verdict on the state. By flattening the interaction of the
\mathsf{QIP}
so that
C
consists only of honest unitaries, we now have a circuit (with public description) whose output behavior can be based on the relaxed notion of semi-malicious soundness.

In particular,
C
can now be thought of as a
\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
-size verification circuit, where for every
x\in\mathcal{L}
its corresponding witness is the auxiliary state
\ket{\psi_x}
with which the prover
P
convinces the verifier
V
that
x\in\mathcal{L}
, thus establishing that indeed
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
. Importantly, the
\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
witness for
x\in\mathcal{L}
is precisely the auxiliary input
\ket{\psi_x}
that the prover
P
uses in the underlying
\mathsf{QIP}
.

Step 2:

Next, we construct a succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
for every
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
for which the witness state is real-valued.
Recall that Mahadev
[
Mah18
]
constructed a succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
for a
\mathsf{QMA}
-complete language (the Local Hamiltonian language), which happens to have the property that every
x\in\mathcal{L}
has a real-valued witness state. Mahadev’s proof relies on two major components:

•

The Morimae-Fitzsimons protocol
[
MF16
]
for
\mathsf{BQP}
verification which shows how to efficiently convert any
\mathsf{BQP}
computation into one that can be verified by measuring each qubit only in the
X
or
Z
basis.

•

A “weak commitment scheme” (also known as a “measurement” protocol), which she defines and constructs. Such a scheme allows a committer to generate a classical “weak commitment” to any quantum state, in a way that the committer can later send a classical opening to each qubit in the
X
or
Z
basis, with the guarantee that the opening is consistent with a quantum state, and binding is guaranteed to hold in the
Z
basis but not necessarily in the
X
basis.

We observe that the Morimae–Fitzsimons transformation generalizes to every language
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMA}
for which every
x\in\mathcal{L}
has a real-valued witness state,
5
5

5

This was already observed by Mahadev
[
Mah18
]
for the specific
\mathsf{QMA}
-complete language
2
-Local Real Hamiltonian.
and moreover to every language
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
with real-valued witness states. Namely, using this transformation, one can convert any witness for
x\in\mathcal{L}
, for any
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
, into a witness
\ket{\psi_x}
that can be verified by measuring each qubit in either the
X
or
Z
basis.

At this stage, one could apply Mahadev’s protocol, resulting in a classical
\mathsf{QIA}
, where the prover first uses a “weak commitment” scheme to commit to this
X/Z
witness state
\ket{\psi_x}
, the verifier then sends the
X/Z
measurement bits, and the prover finally sends the desired openings. This protocol has the desired completeness and soundness guarantees; however, the resulting classical
\mathsf{QIA}
will have communication complexity and verifier runtime
\text{poly}(t_{P})
, which is prohibitively large.

To reduce communication complexity, we use ideas from the work of
[
GKNV24
]
, which constructs a
succinct
classical
\mathsf{QIA}
for the same
\mathsf{QMA}
-complete language as Mahadev. Specifically, we show that the work of
[
GKNV24
]
can be generalized to construct a succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
for any language in
\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
.

This is done by using a
semi-succinct
commitment scheme,
6
6

6

A “weak commitment” is sufficient here, but we need succinctness to reduce the communication and verifier runtime.
as was defined and constructed under the
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption in
[
GKNV24
]
. In this commitment scheme the verifier sends a single
succinct
commitment key to the prover (unlike in Mahadev’s protocol, which requires a separate key for each qubit). Using such a semi-succinct commmitment scheme we construct a semi-succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
for any
\mathcal{L}\in\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
, where the verifier’s messages are succinct but the prover’s messages are of length
\text{poly}(t_{P})
. We mention that to do this we need to shrink the verifier’s second message of Mahadev’s protocol, where the verifier requests
X/Z
openings. This is done, as in
[
BKL+22
]
: by relying on
[
ACGH20
]
, one can assume that the verifier’s second message is truly random (i.e., that the verifier simply measures each qubit of state in a random
X
or
Z
basis). Then, we can use a pseudorandom generator and send only the short seed to the prover. This results in a semi-succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
.

Finally, we use the protocol compression technique of
[
BKL+22
]
to transform the above semi-succinct classical
\mathsf{QIA}
into a
fully succinct
one, thereby achieving the target communication complexity
\text{poly}(\lambda)
and verifier runtime
\text{poly}(\lambda)+\widetilde{O}(|x|)
.

We mention that the final protocol is a bit more complicated. First, when applied to
\mathsf{QMATIME}(t_{P})
, the Morimae-Fitzimons compiler results in a completeness-soundness gap of only
\frac{1}{\text{poly}(t_{P})}
. Therefore, we need to run our protocol the prover needs to use
\text{poly}(t_{P})
copies of the initial state, convert each copy to a history state and commit to all of them. Second, Mahadev’s protocol (as well as the succinct version described above) only has (computational) soundness
1-\frac{1}{\text{poly}(\lambda)}
. This is the case since soundness is guaranteed only if the cheating prover succeeds in opening the commitment with high probability, in which case we can extract a witness from him. To obtain negligible soundness, we repeat this protocol
\text{poly}(\lambda)
times. While a parallel repetition theorem was proven for Mahadev’s protocol
[
ACGH20
,
CCY20
]
, such a result is not known for the succinct version or for private-coin protocols in general (or for KLVY-compiled protocols); thus, we need to repeat our protocol sequentially.

Let

\Pr[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leq p-\varepsilon]\leq e^{-2\varepsilon^{2}n},

\Pr[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq p+\varepsilon]\leq e^{-2\varepsilon^{2}n}.

Complexity.

(c)

\boldsymbol{\tau}
