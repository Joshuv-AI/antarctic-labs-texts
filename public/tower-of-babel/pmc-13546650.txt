Association Between the Systemic Inflammatory Response Index and Severe Infection in Patients with Diabetic Foot [Letter]
Authors: Qingqing Shan
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the recent article by Wu et al titled “Association Between the Systemic Inflammatory Response Index and Severe Infection in Patients with Diabetic Foot”, published in Diabetes, Metabolic Syndrome and Obesity (2026;19:605794).1 The authors are to be commended for their efforts to explore a readily available inflammatory marker, the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), for risk stratification in diabetic foot infection (DFI). Their finding of a non-linear association with an inflection point near 3.53 is thought-provoking. However, we want to raise several methodological and interpretative concerns that merit further discussion.

First, we are concerned about a potential issue of “same-source” collinearity between the SIRI calculation and the diagnostic criteria for severe DFI. As the authors correctly cite, the IWGDF/IDSA guidelines define severe infection, in part, by the presence of a white blood cell count >12,000/mm3 or >10% immature (band) neutrophils.2,3 Because SIRI’s formula uses neutrophil count as its primary numerator, patients classified into the severe infection group based on this leukocyte criterion will inherently have higher neutrophil counts and, consequently, higher SIRI values. This intrinsic relationship suggests that the exceptionally high area under the curve (AUC) of 0.959 might be artificially inflated. A more robust approach would have been to perform a sensitivity analysis excluding the leukocyte and neutrophil components from the severity definition to isolate SIRI’s true discriminative capacity.

Second, the authors use the term “predict” throughout the manuscript. However, the study’s cross-sectional design, where both the exposure (SIRI) and the outcome (severe DFI) are assessed within the same 24-hour window of admission, precludes any causal or truly predictive inference.4 This is a central limitation of the study design. A genuine prognostic evaluation would require a longitudinal design in which the baseline SIRI is used to forecast the future development of severe infection or other clinical outcomes, for example, the need for amputation or intensive care admission.

Third, we have issues concerning the multivariable logistic regression model. The final model (Model 3) included 17 covariates, yet the number of events (severe DFI cases) was 110. This yields an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of approximately 6.5. Although some recent literature suggests that the EPV rule of 10 might be relaxed in certain circumstances,5 classic simulation studies have demonstrated that lower EPV can lead to biased regression coefficients, unstable estimates, and a significant risk of overfitting.6,7 It would have been reassuring if the authors had employed a penalized regression technique (eg, Firth’s or Ridge logistic regression) or bootstrap validation to confirm the robustness of their final model.8

Despite these limitations, the study yields a valuable hypothesis-generating basis for the use of SIRI in DFI management. We respectfully suggest that future prospective, multicenter investigations address these research points to definitively validate the clinical utility of this index.

Sincerely,

Qingqing Shan

Chengdu Integrated TCM & Western Medicine Hospital
