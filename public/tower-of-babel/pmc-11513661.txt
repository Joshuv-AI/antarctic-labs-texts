Robotic Total Hip Arthroplasty for Traumatic Acetabular Fracture Using an Image-free Second-Generation System
Authors: Justin Leal, David N. Kugelman, Rahul K. Goel, Niall H. Cochrane, Thorsten M. Seyler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Complex traumatic acetabular fractures with concomitant hip dislocations are an orthopaedic emergency often requiring open reduction and internal fixation; however, total hip arthroplasty (THA) for acute acetabular fractures may be indicated in certain scenarios. This study reports the case of a 65-year-old male who had a motor vehicle collision that resulted in acetabular fracture dislocation with concomitant femoral head fracture. The patient underwent acute image-free robotic THA. At 6-month follow-up, the patient had good pain management and was doing well after surgery. With the reported technique using a reamer basket, an image-free robotic system can be used in acute THA in patients to achieve appropriate cup positioning.

Introduction

Complex traumatic acetabular fractures with concomitant hip dislocations are an orthopaedic emergency commonly associated with high-energy mechanisms such as motor vehicle collisions [1]. These dislocations are initially treated with closed reduction and traction in patients with smaller fractures limited to the femoral head [2,3]. However, in more severe fracture dislocations involving the proximal femur and/or acetabulum, operative management is indicated [2,4]. In younger patients, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is feasible; however, there is a high rate of conversion to total hip arthroplasty (THA), especially with increasing age [5,6]. Consequently, the role for acute THA in these hip dislocations with complex acetabular fractures and femoral head fractures is growing [7,8]. However, a systematic review from Makridis et al. suggests that THA for acetabular fractures generally results in higher rates of complication when compared to conventional THA [9]. Kelly and coworkers reported an all-cause revision risk of 18.2%, an overall major surgical complication rate of 26.9%, and a medical complication rate of 13.2% after acute THA for acetabular fractures with an adjusted odds ratio of 2 for postoperative dislocation [10].

Given that recent studies suggest robotics in THA results in more accurate cup placement and lower rates of revision, this poses the question of what the role of robotic THA is in patients with acetabular fractures [[11], [12], [13]]. Previously, image-free systems were considered inadequate in the setting of acetabular fracture, as these injuries disrupt the registration surface [14]. To the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to describe the technical approach for using an image-free robotic system for THA in the setting of acetabular trauma. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report.

Case history

Preoperative evaluation

A 65-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department after his car was struck on the driver’s side and slid down an embankment. The patient was able to extricate himself from his vehicle and walk back up the road; however, he described having severe pain in his right hip. Upon arrival to our level-I trauma center, the patient underwent medical evaluation, and his overall medical condition was determined to be stable. Pelvic computed tomography with three-dimensional modeling and X-ray imaging showed a right posterior hip dislocation resulting in a Pipkin IV femoral head fracture with comminuted displaced posterior acetabular wall fracture (Fig. 1a-d). Pipkin IV femoral head fractures are defined as infra- or supra-foveal femoral head fractures with an associated acetabular wall fracture [15]. After successful closed reduction, the patient was placed in traction and admitted to medicine for management of his comorbid conditions. After discussion with the trauma service, the combination of acetabular fracture and femoral head fracture was determined to be too comminuted for ORIF. Surgical technique

Prior to the procedure, while the patient was positioned supine, the distance between the patient’s anterior superior iliac spines was measured using hip calipers for image calibration while using the CORI Surgical System (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN). The patient was then positioned in the lateral decubitus position, and electrocardiogram lead was placed on the spinous process of L5 to assist with landmark registration later in the procedure. Next, 2 pins were drilled into the iliac crest for pelvic reference array placement, and the incision-free femoral reference array was placed 5 cm above the lateral epicondyle of the knee. Their visibility was checked and confirmed with the robotic system. The hip was approached using a standard posterolateral approach. After the incision, dissection was carried down to the fascia; significant damage to the capsule and gluteal musculature was noted. Additionally, the femoral head was noted to be dislocated and displaced, and the posterior wall of the acetabulum was severely comminuted (Fig. 2). After release of the capsule and short external rotators, bony landmarks (anterior superior iliac spines and L5) were registered using a pointer. At this point, the hip was internally rotated for the femoral neck osteotomy, which was followed by femur preparation using a broach-only technique (Fig. 3a and b).Figure 2View of comminuted posterior acetabulum.Figure 3(a) Femoral head fracture, and (b) femoral head measurement.

Upon initial inspection of the posterior wall of the acetabulum, several bone fragments were found and subsequently removed. In order to establish center of rotation (COR) and pelvic planes as a reference for appropriate cup positioning using the image-free system, registration of the lunate surface, and fossa of the acetabulum is critical. To accomplish this accurately in the setting of acetabular fracture, a reamer basket of 50 mm that fit the patient’s anatomy was placed as a guide for articular surface registration of the acetabulum. By placing the registration pointer on the reamer basket, the starting point for acetabular surface registration is set, and the lunate surface is registered by sliding the pointer over the surface of the reamer basket (Fig. 4a-c). Next, the acetabular fossa was cleared of fat pad, osteophytes, and bony fragments, and the acetabular floor was registered for navigation. The acetabulum was then sequentially reamed to 57 mm. Attention was then directed to the posterior acetabular wall defect. After trialing several augments, a buttress augment was used to provide additional cup stability. The cup and augment were then fixed with screws, and a trial reduction was performed. The hip was stable across a full range of motion with no impingement. Intraoperative X-ray showed appropriate stem placement, with equal leg lengths, adequate offset, and appropriate inclination and anteversion of the cup (Fig. 6). All trial components were removed. Additional screws were added to fix the cup, and buttress augment was fixed with screws and united with cement (Fig. 7a-c). Then, final implants were placed and impacted in the appropriate position. Final components were a 58 mm cup, 44 × 58 mm dual mobility liner, size 7 femoral stem, 28 mm +4 femoral head, and 28 × 44 dual mobility insert.Figure 6Intraoperative radiograph with trial components.Figure 7(a) Placement of the final cup implant with buttress augment. Postoperative management and outcomes

Management for this patient after THA included multimodal analgesia, physical therapy, and occupational therapy. Meloxicam served as both pain control and heterotopic ossification prophylaxis. Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis included enoxaparin given the setting of THA in traumatic fracture. At 6-week follow-up, the patient was transitioning to a cane from a walker and was pain-free. Postoperative x-rays demonstrated appropriate implant placement and no evidence of loosening (Fig. 8a and b). Additionally, his patient-reported outcomes measurement information system physical function and pain T-scores improved from 24 to 36 and 66 to 60 pre- to post-operatively, respectively. Discussion

While the accuracy of computed tomography-based hip navigation or robotics has been shown to be independent of the degree of pelvic deformity, image-free systems are contraindicated in patients with severe deformities or acetabular fracture (ie, neo-acetabuli, high or low dislocations, severely inadequate depth of the acetabulum, or absence of acetabular floor) [14]. In cases with distorted anatomy or cases with acetabular fracture, the surface of the acetabular cavity is compromised and cannot be used to calculate neither the native acetabular COR nor the initial cup size for the intraoperative acetabular component planning. Consequently, the system will not allow you to proceed. In the illustrated case of a fracture dislocation of the acetabulum secondary to high-energy mechanisms of injury, we demonstrated an easy technique to allow for registration of the acetabular surface using a basket reamer. Alternatively, a trial liner can be used in an acute setting with an equally successful outcome. In these severe acetabular injuries, even with appropriate treatment with ORIF, patients suffer long-term complications such as post-traumatic osteoarthritis that often require eventual conversion to THA [16]. Given the high rate of these conversions, the role of THA in the setting of complex hip dislocations involving acetabular fractures is growing, although acetabular component placement can be difficult in these cases [[17], [18], [19]].

THA is touted as a revolutionary procedure with excellent patient outcomes and very low revision rates [20]. However, recent studies suggest that THA in acetabular fractures has higher rates of complication and reoperation than conventional THA, particularly if done in the acute setting [7,8,10]. The most common complications include heterotopic ossification, instability, and infection. Interest in how to address instability is increasing as indications for THA continue to expand [9].

Robotic THA has made significant improvements over the years, with the goal of providing patients with personalized cup placement that accounts for patient-specific anatomic variation in spinopelvic alignment and intraoperative patient position [21,22]. Recent studies suggest that patients robotic THAs result in more precise cup placement with lower instances of revision when compared to traditional THA [12,13,22]. Multiple systems exist with unique imaging requirements and variable workflows. In this case, the CORI Surgical System (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN) was used, which allowed for the utilization of the reamer basket to register the acetabular surface. Although systems vary, this technique applies to most image-free systems and may play a significant role in mitigating instability after THA for complex hip dislocations with acetabular fractures that possess a higher dislocation risk at baseline.

Conclusions

Complex hip dislocation with acetabular fractures are severe injuries that require operative management. By understanding how image-free systems use anatomic landmarks for registration, tools like the reamer basket can be utilized to recreate patient anatomy and provide accurate references for appropriate cup placement. In cases where THA is indicated, navigation can be a useful tool to provide personalized cup placement that results in similar outcomes to conventional THA.
