Integrated HPLC-ESI-QTOF-MS based characterization and biological evaluation of Alkanna orientalis and Alkanna verecunda roots
Authors: Sakina Yagi, Eulogio J. Llorent-Martínez, Katarzyna Turecka, Rafał Hałasa, Kinga Kochan-Jamrozy, Magdalena Gucwa, Justyna Stefanowicz-Hajduk, Zeynebe Bingol, Ilhami Gulcin, Evren Yildiztugay, Mohamad Fawzi Mahomodally, Yimao Wu, Meng-Yao Li, Gokhan Zengin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to examine the phytoconstituent, antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory, antimicrobial, and cytotoxic activities of Alkanna orientalis (L.) Boiss. and A. verecunda Hub.-Mor., both from the Boraginaceae family. Root extracts were prepared using methanol. High-performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MS) analysis results revealed the dominance of caffeic acid derivatives in both species. Both species contained variable amounts of rosmarinic acid, dimers and trimers of caffeic acids, lithospermic acid, salvianolic acid A and C, and sagerinic acid. A. orientalis demonstrated higher total phenolic (96.18 mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE)/g) and flavonoids (6.33 mg rutin equivalent (RE)/g) contents than A. verecunda, leading to increased antiradical (DPPH = 316.74 mg trolox equivalents (TE)/g; ABTS = 491.21 mg TE/g), ions reducing (CUPRAC = 790.46 mg TE/g; FRAP = 523.24 mg TE/g), chelation (7.43 mg ethylenediaminetetraacetate equivalent (EDTAE)/g), and total antioxidant (3.17 mmol TE/g) activities. Anti-acetylcholinesterase activity was comparable in both species (2.98 and 2.92 mg galantamine equivalent (GALAE)/g, p ≥ 0.05). A. orientalis most effectively inhibited α–glucosidase (2.23 mmol acarbose equivalent (ACAE)/g) and α–amylase (0.26 mmol ACAE/g), while A. verecunda showed the highest significant inhibitory effect against butyrylcholinesterase (1.98 mg GALAE/g), anti-tyrosinase (49.75 mg KAE/g), and human carbonic anhydrase isoenzyme II (IC50 0.070 µg/mL). A. orientalis exhibited significant antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC14990, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.008 mg/mL and a minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of 0.0625 mg/mL. The cytotoxic activity of methanol extracts from A. orientalis and A. verecunda was evaluated on four human cancer cell lines: gastric adenocarcinoma (AGS), ovarian adenocarcinoma (SKOV-3), colon carcinoma (HCT-116), and cervical adenocarcinoma (HeLa), using MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay. Human fibroblasts (HFF-1) were employed as a non-cancerous control line. The findings suggest that the extracts exhibited mild cytotoxic activity on cancer cells while demonstrating no toxicity on normal fibroblasts. A more potent effect was observed for A. orientalis compared to A. verecunda extracts, particularly on gastric cancer AGS cells, where cell viability decreased to 56.84 ± 4.20% at the highest concentration of A. orientalis extract. In conclusion, this study is the first to explore the phytochemical constituents and biological activities of A. verecunda and the roots of A. orientalis. The roots of both species could serve as a novel source of bioactive molecules with potential applications in the pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52047-z.

Introduction

The genus Alkanna Tausch, a member of the Boraginaceae family, encompasses 60 species distributed widely across the Mediterranean, Europe, and Western Asia1. In Türkiye, this genus is represented by 41 taxa spread across 36 species, with 80% of them being endemic. the Anatolian Peninsula is considered the epicenter of diversity for this genus1,2. Numerous Alkanna species are employed in traditional medicine to address a variety of ailments, including stomach ulcers, cancer, and diarrhea3. Pharmacological research has revealed that many of these species exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and wound healing properties, among others1. They are particularly noted for their hydroxynaphthoquinone pigments, such as alkannin/shikonin and their derivatives, which are characteristic components of many Alkanna species. These pigments demonstrate a range of biological activities and find extensive applications in the textile, food, and cosmetics industries4,5. Furthermore, several other compounds have been identified within the genus, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, coumarins, pyrrolizidine alkaloids, terpenes, fatty acids, and alkannin derivatives (isohexenylnaphtazarine)1.

A. orientalis (L.) Boiss. is a perennial herbaceous plant predominantly found in the Mediterranean region. Traditionally, it has been employed to treat ailments such as wounds, conjunctivitis, scabies, earaches, diabetes, hypercholesteremia, hemorrhoids, and as an analgesic6. The plant exhibits a range of activities including antiviral7, antimutagenic8, antimicrobial9, antioxidant3, anti-inflammatory, antinociceptive10, wound healing, and keratinolytic11. Notable compounds identified in its aerial parts include Organic acids, flavonoids (namely quercetin and kaempferol) and their glycosides, and phenolic acids (such as caffeic acid, Rosmarinic acid, and 4-Hydroxybenzoic acid) and their derivatives3,12. Additionally, Yaglıoglu et al12. highlighted the dyeing potential and non-toxicity of A. orientalis roots. Nonetheless, there is limited information regarding the phytoconstituents and pharmacological potential of this species’ roots13.

Alkanna verecunda Hub.-Mor., another species of the Alkanna genus, is a perennial shrub endemic to the Mediterranean region. Characterized by its woody stem, it possesses small, oval-shaped leaves and inconspicuous star-shaped flowers that vary in hue from pink to purple. Traditionally, the plant has been employed both as a dye and a remedy for fever, skin diseases, and inflammation. Despite an extensive literature search, no reports detailing the phytochemical constituents or biological activities of A. verecunda have been uncovered. Hence, this study was devised to elucidate the chemical composition and assess the antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory, antimicrobial, and cytotoxic properties of the roots of A. orientalis and A. verecunda. The evaluation of antioxidant activity encompassed their potential to scavenge free radicals, chelate, and reduce metal ions. Enzyme inhibitory capabilities were gauged against a range of enzymes, including acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, tyrosinase, α-amylase, and α-glucosidase, as well as the human carbonic anhydrase isoenzymes I and II. Antimicrobial efficacy was determined against a spectrum of bacterial strains. Furthermore, cytotoxicity was evaluated against a panel of cell lines: human ovarian adenocarcinoma SKOV-3, colon carcinoma HCT-116, cervical adenocarcinoma HeLa S3, gastric adenocarcinoma AGS, and non-cancerous fibroblasts HFF-1.

Materials and methods

Plant collection

In 2023, plant materials were gathered from the Konya province in Türkiye. The botanical identification of the collected specimens was meticulously performed by Dr. Evren Yildiztugay. For documentation and future reference, a voucher sample (Voucher no: EY-3507 for A. orientalis and Voucher no: EY-3502 for A. verecunda) was deposited in the herbarium of the Faculty of Science at Selcuk University. All experimental research and field studies complied with institutional and national guidelines and legislation governing plant research and biodiversity conservation in Türkiye. All procedures were conducted in accordance with local and national regulations, and formal ethical approval was not required for this study. To preserve their phytochemical profile, the roots of the plant were carefully separated and air-dried in a shaded area at ambient temperature. Once dried, the material was finely powdered using a standardized grinding procedure. The powdered samples were subsequently stored in light-resistant containers under controlled environmental conditions to minimize degradation and maintain long-term stability.

Plant extract preparation

The dried and powdered root samples were macerated with 200 ml of methanol for 24 h using a magnetic stirrer. Then, the mixtures were filtered, and the solvents were removed under reduced pressure at 40 °C in a rotary-evaporator.

Assay for total phenolic and flavonoid content

The determination of total phenolic and flavonoid levels in the extracts was carried out based on previously established protocols14. Standard compounds gallic acid (GA) and rutin (R) were employed for calibration purposes during the assays, and the results were presented as gallic acid equivalents (GAE, mg GAE/g dry extract) and rutin equivalents (RE, mg RE/g dry extract).

Phytochemical analysis by HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MS

The identification of phytochemicals was conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization and a quadrupole-time-of-flight mass spectrometer (HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MS). The equipment used included an Agilent 1200 system from Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA, paired with an Agilent 6530B Q-TOF MS. Comprehensive chromatographic specifics are provided in the supplemental materials.

Assays for in vitro antioxidant capacity

Antioxidant evaluations were conducted in accordance with established procedures, as delineated in a preceding study15. The radical scavenging capacities of the extracts were measured using a variety of assays, including FRAP, CUPRAC, DPPH, and ABTS. The results were expressed as milligrams of trolox equivalents (TE) per gram of dried extract. The total antioxidant potential was subsequently determined using the phosphomolybdenum (PBD) assay and reported in mmol TE/g. Furthermore, the metal chelation activity (MCA) was quantitatively assessed by measuring the milligrams of EDTA equivalents (EDTAE) per gram of extract.

Inhibitory effects against some key enzymes

Enzyme inhibitory assays were conducted against acetylcholinesterase (AChE), butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), tyrosinase, α-amylase, and glucosidase using the tested extracts, according to validated protocols reported in the literature15. The inhibitory activities toward AChE and BChE were subsequently quantified in milligrams of galanthamine equivalents (GALAE) per gram of dry extract. The inhibitory potential of α-amylase and glucosidase was expressed in acarbose equivalents (ACAE), while tyrosinase inhibition was assessed in terms of kojic acid equivalents (KAE) per gram of dry extract.

Antibacterial assay

The experimental procedure for antibacterial evaluation was conducted based on our previous study16. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was assessed using the broth microdilution method with 96-well plates. Each well was filled with 100 µL of broth, and dry test samples were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (final concentration approximately 21 mg/mL for A. orientalis and 15 mg/mL for A. verecunda) and ethanol (final concentration of 50 mg/mL). These solutions were serially diluted and added to the first well of each microtiter row. A series of geometric dilutions were then performed by transferring 100 µL of the mixture from the first to the twelfth well, discarding 100 µL from the final well. The final concentrations of extracts in DMSO ranged from 2.1 to 0.001 mg/mL for A. orientalis, and from 1.5 to 0.000625 mg/mL for A. verecunda. For the ethanol-based samples, final tested extract concentrations varied from 5 to 0.0025 mg/mL. Ampicillin (128 to 0.0625 µg/mL) was used as a reference for bacteria, alongside ketoconazole (128 to 0.0625 µg/mL) for yeast. Experiments were incubated under conditions as previously described. The endpoint was determined through visual inspection for growth, with the MIC being the lowest concentration that inhibited any visible growth. Furthermore, 100 µL from each non-growth well was inoculated onto an agar plate to verify bacterial viability. After a 48-hour incubation, plates were checked for bacterial growth. The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was defined as the lowest concentration of compounds needed to achieve a 99.9% kill rate17.

Cell culture and cell viability assessment

This investigation used a panel of human cell lines, including cancerous (SKOV-3, HCT-116, HeLa S3, AGS) and non-cancerous (HFF-1) models, procured from ATCC and LGC Standards, respectively. Cell cultivation was performed using specific basal media recommended for each line: McCoy’s for SKOV-3 and HCT-116, DMEM for HeLa S3, DMEM/Ham’s F-12 for AGS, and a Fibroblast Growth Medium for HFF-1, all acquired from Merck Millipore. To support growth, a standard supplement cocktail containing 10% FBS and antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin) was added to every medium. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment.

MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay

An MTT assay was employed to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of the extract on all cell lines. Cells were seeded at 5 × 10³ cells/well and, after adherence, were exposed to the extract (10–150 µg/mL) for 24 h. The extract was dissolved in DMSO, with the final solvent concentration not surpassing 0.75% (v/v). Oxaliplatin (20–400 µg/mL) was used as a reference cytotoxic agent. After the treatment period, MTT was added to the wells. The absorbance of the dissolved formazan product was then measured on a BioTek Epoch microplate reader. The data were processed using GraFit software (v.7) to determine cell viability and calculate IC₅₀ values where applicable.

Network pharmacology

Collecting two Alkanna species potential targets

A comprehensive phytochemical characterization of root extracts from two Alkanna species was performed using HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MS. Based on the analytical results, several representative compounds were selected for putative gene target prediction. The chemical structures of these key compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to obtain their respective Compound ID (CID) numbers. Subsequently, these structures were converted into CID entries and submitted to the SwissTargetPrediction platform (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/) for target prediction analysis. All predicted targets with a probability score of zero were excluded from further consideration. The remaining targets for each compound were systematically compiled and integrated for subsequent analysis.

Identification of anti-bacterial genes and characterization of their intersection genes

During our search for antibacterial target genes, we systematically consulted several authoritative databases, including GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD, https://db.idrblab.net/ttd), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), and the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database (OMIM, https://omim.org). Using “antibacterial” as the search keyword, we retrieved relevant gene targets. The target data obtained from DrugBank were subsequently cross-referenced with manually reviewed proteins in the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). To ensure accuracy and consistency, we integrated all target entries and removed duplicates. The processed compound–gene data along with antibacterial gene expression profiles have been deposited into the “Wei-Sheng-Xin” Platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). Subsequently, we constructed a Venn diagram to visualize the overlapping genes between compounds and antibacterial targets. To further visualize the antibacterial targets of the related compounds, the final dataset was visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.2 software. In the network diagram produced, node color and size denote different categories and degree values, respectively, with the node size range set between 60 and 150.

Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks

To explore potential direct and indirect interactions among candidate target proteins, a PPI network was established using the Metascape database (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html). The analysis was restricted to Homo sapiens and focused on intersection genes related to both compound activity and antibacterial effects. A confidence threshold of 0.400 was applied for interaction scores to select high-quality PPI data. Isolated nodes were removed to enhance the connectivity and interpretability of the network. The finalized network was imported into Cytoscape 3.10.2, and node degrees were computed using the “Analyze Network” function. To emphasize functional importance within the network, node sizes were scaled proportionally to their degree values (diameters ranging from 60 to 150 pixels). Additionally, nodes were color-mapped using a continuous gradient based on degree, facilitating visual interpretation of node centrality and functional influence18.

Enrichment analysis

Enrichment correlation analysis involving Alkanna extract and antimicrobial targets was performed with the SangerBox online tool (http://sangerbox.com/). The analysis incorporated Gene Ontology (GO) terms across the three primary domains: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). Following GO enrichment, log10-transformed P values were converted back to the original scale, and terms meeting P ≤ 0.05 were retained. The results were ranked by enrichment score in descending order, and the top ten pathways from each category were selected, classified, and summarized. Final enrichment outcomes were visualized using the Wei-Sheng-Xin platform19.

For Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, the most recent KEGG pathway annotations were retrieved through the KEGG API (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/rest/) to establish a reference background for gene set enrichment20–22. Functional enrichment was carried out with the “clusterProfiler v3.14.3” package in R, with gene set size limits set between 5 and 5000. Significance was determined using a P-value cutoff of < 0.05 and an FDR threshold of < 0.25.

Molecular docking

To identify potentially bioactive constituents from Alkanna extracts, selected compounds were subjected to molecular docking against target proteins to elucidate their binding mechanisms. The three-dimensional structures of the compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Energy minimization and conformational optimization were carried out using Chem3D v22.2.0 with the MM2 force field. Protein structures were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Preprocessing of the protein models—including removal of water molecules and native ligands, along with hydrogen atom addition—was performed in PyMOL v3.1.6.1. Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.20. Resultant poses were analyzed and visualized in Maestro View v14.5, and two-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated to aid structural interpretation.

Chemical characterization of the compounds found in the analyzed extracts of roots of Alkanna orientalis and A. verecunda by HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF.

Compound 1 was characterized as a disaccharide (probably diglucoside) due to the [M-H]- at m/z 341 and its fragmentation pattern, with the base peak at m/z 179 corresponding to a hexoside23. Compound 2 was characterized as malic acid by the molecular formula and base peak at m/z 11524.

Compounds 3 and 4 were identified as isocitric acid and citric acid, respectively, using an analytical standard of citric acid (both compounds have the same fragmentation pattern but different retention times). The base peak at m/z 111 is typical of (iso)citric acid24.

Compound 5 was characterized as danshensu based on the deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 197 and fragment ions at m/z 179, 135 and 12325.

Compound 6, with deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 153, was identified as protocatechuic acid, a dihydroxybenzoic acid, by comparison with an analytical standard. Compounds 7 and 9, with 16 Da less, where characterized as hydroxybenzoic acid isomers.

Compound 10, with [M-H]− at m/z 179 and base peak at m/z 135 was identified as caffeic acid by comparison with an analytical standard. Most of the characterized compounds were derivative of caffeic acid, such as rosmarinic acid (caffeic acid dimer) or lithospermic and salvianolic acids, which are caffeic acid polymers.

Compounds 11, 13 and 19 exhibited deprotonated molecular ions at m/z 537 and fragment ions at m/z 493, 339 and 295. This fragmentation pattern is in agreement with lithospermic acid isomer26. In this work, we observed three isomers of this compound in A. orientalis and one isomer in A. verecunda; this is in agreement with the findings of other authors regarding the presence of lithospermic acid depending on the analyzed species3.

Compound 12 was characterized as yunnaneic acid E, a caffeic acid metabolite, based on the molecular formula and fragmentation pattern27. This compound has not previously reported in Alkanna species to our best knowledge.

Compound 14, with [M-H]− at m/z 463, suffered the neutral loss of 162 Da (hexoside, being glucoside the most probable sugar) to yield quercetin at m/z 301 (the aglycone was identified with an analytical standard). This compound was the only flavonoid found in the analyzed extracts, in contrast to the report of several flavonoids in other Alkanna species3.

Compounds 15 and 18 were characterized as rosmarinic acid isomers, 16 and 22 as lithospermic acid B isomers, compounds 20, 21 and 24 as salvianolic acid A isomers, and 23 as salvianolic acid C26; these compounds have been previously reported in several Alkanna species3.

Compound 17 was tentatively characterized as sagerinic acid based on bibliographic data28; to our best knowledge, this compound has not been previously reported in Alkanna species.

Compounds 25 and 26 were tentatively characterized as oxo-dihydroxy-octadecenoic acid and trihydroxy-octadecenoic acid based on bibliography29. These compounds have not been reported in Alkanna species to our best knowledge.

The Boraginaceae family includes numerous genera distributed worldwide and has a long history in traditional medicine practices. Plants belonging to this family attract attention with their potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, particularly due to their high phenolic content30. In agreement with this, our HPLC-ESI-QTOF-MS analysis revealed that Alkanna species are rich in caffeic acid derivatives, which significantly contribute to their biological potential.

Once the characterization of the main compounds was done, we constructed heat map (Table 2) to check which compounds were the most abundant in both Alkanna species. We thus used Extracted Ion Chromatograms for each compound at the corresponding deprotonated molecular ion (using a symmetric expansion of ± 5 ppm). The relative areas of each compound (in percentage, referenced to the total area of all compounds) are given in Table 2. It can be seen that there were differences regarding individual compounds between the species. However, as a whole, the main compounds in both Alkanna species were caffeic acid dimers and polymers. Rosmarinic acid isomers (compounds 15 and 18), dimers of caffeic acid, represented approximately 26% and 23% in A. orientalis and A. verecunda, respectively. Lithospermic acid (isomers 11, 13 and 19), salvianolic acid A (isomers 20, 21 and 24) and salvianolic acid C (compound 23), trimers of caffeic acid, represented approximately 38% and 43% in A. orientalis and A. verecunda, respectively, while caffeic acid tetramers (compounds 16 and 22 were lithospermic acid B isomers and compound 17, sagerinic acid) accounted for 27% and 21% of the compounds characterized in A. orientalis and A. verecunda, respectively. Hence, caffeic acid dimers and polymers accounted for approximately 90% of the present compounds.

Total phenolic (TPC) and flavonoids (TFC) contents and antioxidant activity

Cellular oxidative stress which is generated by the permanent accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can cause pathological damage to cells and consequently, contribute to the development of chronic diseases. Antioxidants counteract the generation of ROS by different mechanisms of action like, free radical scavenging, hydrogen donation, metal ion chelation and others (Halliwell, 2024). Studies have indicated that antioxidant properties of plant extracts are correlated to their total phenolic and flavonoids contents31. Hence, the TPC and TFC of A. orientalis and A. verecunda roots were determined and results are presented in Table 3. The TPC (96.18 mg GAE/g) and TFC (6.33 mg RE/g) in A. orientalis were 1.8 and 1.3 times higher than those recorded for A. orientalis. A previous study on the TPC and TFC of A. orientalis was performed on MeOH extract of the aerial parts3. By comparing their results, it was noted that the TPC obtained from the methanol extracts of the roots in the current study exceeded that recorded for the aerial parts by 2.8-fold while its TFC was 4.1 times lower that that reported from the aerial parts. Despite the plant parts used in the two studies and besides other factors like origin of plants and different agroclimatic conditions it could be speculated that the aerial parts of A. orientalis were rich in flavonoids while the roots were dominated by other phenolic compounds. This was supported by the results of the HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF analysis which indicated that the roots extract was dominated by the phenolic acids rosmarinic acid, lithospermic acid isomers, sagerinic acid, salvianolic acid C and Salvianolic acid A.

Furthermore, the antioxidant activity of 70% EtOH extract of A. orientalis and A. verecunda roots was evaluated by the DPPH, ABTS, CUPRAC, FRAP, MCA and PBD assays and results are presented in Table 3. A. orientalis exerted significantly (p˂0.05) higher antioxidant activity than A. verecunda in all tested assays. Values from DPPH (316.74 mg TE/g), ABTS (491.21 mg TE/g), CUPRAC (790.46 mg TE/g), FRAP (523.24 mg TE/g), MCA (7.43 mg EDTAE/g) and PBD (3.17 mmol TE/g) were respectively 6.5-, 4.9-, 3-, 3.7-, 3.2- and 1.4-folds than those exerted by A. verecunda. The superior antioxidant activity of A. orientalis could be attributed to its higher TPC and TFC. Although A. verecunda was dominated by the antiradical compound Salvianolic acid A32, A. orientalis accumulated higher content of other antioxidant compounds like rosmarinic acid content33, lithospermic acid34 and caffeic acid35. However, the antioxidant activity of the roots obtained in the current study using different assays was highly superior to those recorded from the aerial parts by Ganos et al3. except for their chelating capacity, suggesting that the roots could be a promising new source of natural antioxidants.

Enzyme inhibitory activity

Enzymes are known to play a pivotal role in the pursuit of drug discovery to cure serious health problems such as diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease. In particular, inhibiting key enzymes can have a positive influence on the progression of these diseases. For instance, inhibiting acetylcholinesterase can increase acetylcholine levels in the synaptic cleft of Alzheimer’s patients, thereby improving their cognitive function36. Similarly, inhibiting amylase and glucosidase can help to regulate blood glucose levels in diabetic patients after they have consumed a carbohydrate-rich meal37. Tyrosinase is a key enzyme in melanin production and inhibiting it can reduce hyperpigmentation disorders38. Given the importance of enzyme inhibition, numerous compounds have been developed synthetically in the pharmaceutical industry. However, many of these agents cause unpleasant side effects when used long term. Thus, identifying novel, and safe enzyme inhibitors holds considerable significance in scientific studies39. In the current study the enzyme inhibitory activity of the methanol extracts of A. orientalis and A. verecunda roots was determined against acetylcholinestrase (AChE), butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), tyrosinase (Tyr), ⍺-amylase and ⍺-glucosidase enzymes and results are presented in Table 4. Both species displayed comparable anti-AChE activity (2.98 and 2.92 mg GALAE/g, p ≥ 0.05). However, A. verecunda exerted higher significant (p˂0.05) anti-BChE (1.98 mg GALAE/g) and anti-Tyr (49.75 mg KAE/g) activities than A. orientalis, while the latter had superior significant (p˂0.05) effect towards the α–glucosidase (2.23 mmol ACAE/g) and α–amylase (0.26 mmol ACAE/g) enzymes than the former. A previous study on the enzyme inhibitory property of A. orientalis was performed on the MeOH extract from the aerial parts and contrary to the findings in the present study, the aerial parts did not inhibit the two cholinesterase enzymes, however, it exerted a higher inhibitory activity against the α–glucosidase enzyme than that obtained by the roots in the current study3. Furthermore, this is the first study evaluated the enzyme inhibitory property of A. verecunda and the anti-Tyr activity of A. orientalis and results indicated that the two species could be a promising source of molecules with enzyme inhibition property. In fact, some of the identified compounds from the HPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MS analysis were known for their enzyme inhibitory activity. For example, rosmarinic acid is an effective AChE, BChE40⍺-glucosidase (Yilei and Lee Suan, 2018) and tyrosinase (Kang et al., 2004) inhibitors. Caffeic acid was effective against the enzymes linked to Alzheimer’s disease41,42. Salvianolic acid C was shown as potent ⍺-glucosidase inhibitor43.

Inhibitory effects on human carbonic anhydrase isoenzymes I and II (hCA I and hCA II)

Carbonic anhydrases (CA), known as carbonic anhydrase, play important role in many physiological and pathological functions of living organisms. Inhibition or activation of CA is considered as a promising strategy in the treatment of many ailments like glaucoma, obesity, Alzheimer’s disease and other neurological disorders44,45. The present study evaluated the inhibitory effect of A. orientalis and A. verecunda on the hCAI and hCAII isoenzymes of human erythrocytes. Results are presented in Table 5. Remarkably, A. verecunda exerted potent inhibitory effect towards hCAII with IC50 value (0.070 µg/mL), 1.66 times lower than that obtained from the standard inhibitor Asetazolamid (IC50 0.116 µg/mL). Although less effective than the standard control (IC50 0.082 µg/mL), both species showed considerable inhibitory effect towards the hCAI with higher activity recorded from A. verecunda (IC50 0.169 µg/mL). A previous study demonstrated that some phenolic acids significantly inhibited the hCAI and hCAII isoenzymes due to their different functional groups (OH and COOH) present in their aromatic scaffold46.

Antimicrobial properties

A. orientalis extract dissolved in DMSO showed the highest activity against S. epidermidis ATCC 14,990 (MIC 0.008 mg/mL, MBC 0.0625 mg/mL), and it was active against S. aureus ATCC 6538 (MIC 0.5 mg/mL) and C. sporogenes ATCC 19,404 (MIC 0.5 mg/mL) (Table 6). For the other microorganisms used in the studies, the extract presented MIC and MBC values equal to 2 mg/mL, except for Streptococcus β-hemolytic group A PCM 465 (MIC 1 mg/mL), L. monocytogenes PCM 219, C. jejuni ZMF, and C. albicans ATCC 10,231 (MIC/MBC > 2 mg/mL). A. verecunda extract dissolved in DMSO also showed the highest activity against S. epidermidis ATCC 14,990 (MIC 0.0225 mg/mL, MBC 0.375 mg/mL); it was also active against Streptococcus β-hemolytic group A PCM 465 (MIC 0.375 mg/mL), C. diphtheriae (MIC 0.75 mg/mL) and C. sporogenes ATCC 19,404 (MIC 0.75 mg/mL). The above-mentioned extract showed a MIC value of 1.5 mg/mL against the E. faecalis ATCC 51,299, S. aureus ATCC 6538, S. aureus ATCC 43,300 MRSA, H. pylori ATCC 43,504, C. bifermentans ATCC 638 and C. albicans ATCC 10,231. No inhibitory effect was observed against the remaining bacteria within the tested concentration range (MIC > 1.5 mg/mL). Both extracts dissolved in ethanol showed the same level of activity against S. epidermidis ATCC 14,990 (MIC 0.3125 mg/mL, MBC 0.3125 mg/mL) and S. aureus ATCC 43,300 MRSA (MIC 0.3125 mg/mL, MBC 0.3125 mg/mL). For the remaining bacterial strains, the MIC values were as follows: C. jejuni ZMF (MIC 0.625 mg/mL), E. faecalis ATCC 51,299 (MIC 2.5 mg/mL), C. diphtheria (MIC 2.5 mg/mL), H. pylori ATCC 43,504 (MIC/MBC 2.5 mg/mL), and in case of C. albicans ATCC 10,231 the MIC was outside the tested range. On the other hand, the A. orientalis extract dissolved in ethanol showed higher activity than the A. verecunda extract against Streptococcus β-hemolytic group A PCM 465 (MIC 0.625 mg/mL, MBC 2.5 mg/mL and MIC 1.25 mg/mL MBC > 2.5 mg/mL, respectively), M. catarhalis PCM 2340 (MIC/MBC 1.25 mg/mL and MIC/MBC 2.5 mg/mL, respectively), S. aureus ATCC 6538 (MIC 0.625 mg/mL, MBC 1.25 mg/mL and MIC 5 mg/mL, MBC > 5 mg/mL, respectively), L. monocytogenes PCM 219 (MIC/MBC 5 mg/mL and > 5 mg/mL), E. coli ATCC 8739 (MIC 0.3125 mg/mL, MBC 5 mg/mL and MIC 2.5 mg/mL, MBC > 5 mg/mL, respectively), C. bifermentans ATCC 638 (MIC 1.25 mg/mL, MBC mg/mL and MIC/MBC outside the tested range) and C. sporogenes ATCC 19,404 (MIC 2.5 mg/mL and MIC 1.25 mg/mL, respectively).

The antimicrobial activity of the tested extracts can be attributed to the presence of high phenolic acid content. As weak acids, they are able to penetrate the cell membrane and acidify the cytoplasm, causing bacterial death. Lipophilicity and pKa are important factors in their activity47.

However, it has also been found that rosmarinic acid (which, together with its derivatives, is most abundant in A. orientalis extract) can cause depolymerization of the cell membrane48,49 and modification of the bacterial membrane proteomic properties49,50. Rosmarinic acid present a bacteriostatic effect, capable of destroying bacterial cells and their proteins, as well as blocking the activity of Na+/K+-ATPase in the cell49,51. Extracts of A. orientalis leaves and flowers (containing the alkaloid sarothrin) were shown to inhibit the growth of S. aureus (MIC ˃ 800 µM), and sarothrin has been suggested to possess efflux pump inhibitory activity, which could contribute to its antibacterial properties9. Petrosyan et al52. studied the effect of A. orientalis callus tissue ethanol extracts on several species of microorganisms, including S. aureus WDCW 5233 (MIC 125 µg/mL), E. coli VKPM M-17 (MIC 500 µg/mL), and E. hirae ATCC 9790 (MIC 250 µg/mL). Khan et al53. examined the activity of aqueous, ethanolic, chloroform, and hexane A. tinctoria leaves extracts against four clinical strains of bacteria: S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumanii. The MIC values obtained for the tested extracts ranged from 12.5 to 25 mg/mL, and MBC values were > 100 mg/mL. S. aureus proved to be the most sensitive bacterium. Salvianolic acid B (SA-B), a polyphenol from the root of Salviae miltiorrhizae, has been shown to inhibit N. meningitidis adhesion by binding to bacterial pili. Salvianolic acid B has been shown to be an inhibitor of the Salmonella InvC protein by inhibiting its ATPase activity while maintaining bacterial viability. This inhibitory activity is necessary for the injection of effector proteins into host cells via the Type III Secretion System 1 (T3SS-1) of Salmonella sp54,55.

Aqueous and ethanol extracts contained the largest number of chemical compounds (particularly alkaloids, flavonoids, and carbohydrates) proved to be the most active. Notably, ethanol extracts displayed a broader antimicrobial spectrum than those dissolved in DMSO, inhibiting not only staphylococci but also Gram-negative rods such as E. coli and C. jejuni. This difference may be explained by the enhanced solubility of extracted compounds in ethanol.

Cytotoxic effects

To assess the viability of the human cancer cells and fibroblasts, we used MTT assay. The obtained results indicate that A. orientalis and A. verecunda exerted the strongest effect on colon adenocarcinoma HCT-116 and gastric AGS cells. The viability results for A. orientalis and AGS cells were 79.75 ± 2.02, 80.60 ± 5.10, 70.43 ± 1.62, 68.53 ± 4.43, 62.92 ± 3.74, 54.84 ± 0.28, 61.99 ± 5.16, 56.84 ± 4.20% for the extract concentrations of 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, and 150 µg/mL, respectively. In the case of A. orientalis and HCT-116 cells, the percentage of live cells were 84.53 ± 1.45, 86.09 ± 4.64, 64.17 ± 2.85, 65.52 ± 4.00, 68.90 ± 5.42, 70.66 ± 6.85, 72.95 ± 1.21, and 70.98 ± 6.96% for the concentrations of 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, and 150 µg/mL, respectively. The extract had no cytotoxic effect on SKOV-3, HeLa, and HFF-1 cell lines, where the results were above 80% at all the extract concentrations, respectively (Fig. 1). Interestingly, the extract may stimulate human fibroblasts, especially at higher concentrations, which may be useful in the case of wound healing. However, additional study should be evaluated in this field to confirm this effect.

The genus Alkanna (Boraginaceae) is widely recognized for its pharmacological potential, particularly through naphthoquinones such as alkannin and shikonin, which exhibit cytotoxic, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities52,56. Studies of different Alkanna species, notably A. tinctoria, have demonstrated significant antiproliferative effects57,58. Emad et al.59 reported that methanol extracts of A. orientalis aerial parts - especially leaves rich in phenolic compounds and flavonoids - induced cytotoxicity in HepG2 (hepatic) and A549 (pulmonary) carcinoma cell lines. By contrast, A. verecunda remains poorly characterized, with limited data on its phytochemical composition or biological activities. Evaluating the cytotoxic potential of root extracts from A. orientalis and A. verecunda therefore provides valuable insights into the pharmacological applications and biosafety of these Boraginaceae species.

In the present study, methanol extracts of A. orientalis and A. verecundaroots were tested against human cancer cell lines - AGS, SKOV-3, HCT-116, and HeLa. The cytotoxicity of the extracts was also showed on fibroblast line (HFF-1) as a non-cancer control. Cancer cell viability remained consistently quite high across all treatments, and IC₅₀ values could not be calculated, indicating limited antiproliferative capacity under these conditions. These observations concur with Yaglioglu et al.12, who found that ethanol and aqueous extracts of A. orientalis roots elicited 0% cytotoxicity in C6 rat glioma cells.

Phytochemical profiling revealed that caffeic acid derived oligomers - rosmarinic acid isomers, trimers (e.g., lithospermic and salvianolic acids), and tetramers - constituted approximately 90% of the detected metabolites in both extracts. Naphthoquinones and pyrrolizidine alkaloids were undetectable, which aligns with the absence of direct significant cytotoxicity; these compound classes are often implicated in the anticancer activity of other Alkanna species60. However, rosmarinic acid is well known as a natural compound with the positive effects in the treatment of many cancers, including gastrointestinal ones such as oral, stomach, and colon cancers61. Radziejewska et al.62 tested rosmarinic acid and in combination with an antibody - anti-MUC1 on AGS cells and their effect on apoptotic factors and caspases – Bcl-2, Bad, Bax, p53, caspase-3,−8,−9 was observed. Similarly, for example, salvianolic acid A exerts its antitumor effects by inhibiting cell invasion, migration and proliferation, and induces apoptosis by targeting different signaling pathways63. The presence of this kind of compounds may be responsible for some moderate activity of the tested Alkanna extracts on gastric and colon cancer cells.

Antioxidant assays confirmed the chemoprotective profile of both extracts. The A. orientalis root extract exhibited significantly greater radical scavenging capacity than that of A. verecunda. These results parallel those of Salimikia et al.64, who observed strong antioxidant activity of butanol fractions from A. orientalisroots, and of Ganos et al.3, who identified multiple caffeic acid derivatives and flavonoid glycosides (e.g., quercetin, kaempferol) in A. orientalis aerial parts, attributing them to antioxidant and enzyme-inhibitory effects. In summary, methanol root extracts of A. orientalis and A. verecunda lack direct strong cytotoxicity but are enriched in phenolic oligomers with potent antioxidant activity. Their chemoprotective profile suggests utility in chemoprevention and oxidative-stress mitigation, underscoring the importance of plant part selection and compound distribution in defining therapeutic potential.

Network pharmacology

Based on the comprehensive phytochemical profiling, seven bioactive constituents were identified and chosen for further investigation: Danshen Su, lithospermic acid, yunnaneic acid E, rosmarinic acid, sagerinic acid, salvianolic acid A, and salvianolic acid C. A total of 343 potential targets associated with these compounds were predicted. To explore their antibacterial potential, relevant genes were retrieved from several authoritative databases, including GeneCards, TTD, DrugBank, and OMIM. After removing duplicates, 173 unique antibacterial genes were retained. Intersection analysis between the predicted targets of the Alkanna compounds and the antibacterial gene set revealed 16 overlapping targets (Fig. 3A). A PPI network was constructed using the 16 candidate target proteins, comprising 16 nodes and 62 edges (Fig. 3C). Nodes were colored on a green-to-yellow gradient according to their degree centrality values, with green representing higher values and yellow indicating lower values, thus visualizing the topological importance of each protein within the network. Topological analysis further revealed several hub genes—including ALB, IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, ITGB2, and EGFR—suggesting their potential roles as critical regulators in antibacterial-related pathways and underscoring their biological significance.

The GO enrichment analysis suggests that the bioactive components in Alkanna extracts may produce antibacterial effects via multi-target and multi-pathway mechanisms. We observed significant enrichments in biological processes (BP) for terms such as cell surface receptor signaling pathway, regulation of multicellular organismal process, regulation of signal transduction, and positive regulation of cell migration. This implies that the extract can directly disrupt bacterial cellular activities and indirectly support antibacterial function by adjusting host immune cell migration and signal transduction. The cellular component (CC) analysis indicates that target proteins are primarily situated in the plasma membrane, cell surface, and various vesicle membranes. This suggests that the functional sites might be closely related to membrane structures and membrane-associated signaling events. In terms of molecular function (MF), the targets were predominantly involved in signaling receptor binding, metal ion binding, enzyme binding, and protein-containing complex binding. This reinforces a mechanism whereby antibacterial effects are facilitated through the regulation of key receptors and enzymatic activities. Importantly, molecules with receptor ligand or regulatory activities may contribute to immune modulation by mimicking or inhibiting natural signaling molecules. To conclude, Alkanna extracts likely exhibits synergistic antibacterial activity through multiple layers, including membrane-associated signal transduction, regulation of immune cell migration, and host–pathogen interactions65.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the target genes associated with Alkanna extracts were significantly enriched in multiple signaling pathways closely related to immune and infectious response. The top ten enriched pathways included Malaria (hsa05144), Rheumatoid arthritis (hsa05323), Legionellosis (hsa05134), Pertussis (hsa05133), Antifolate resistance (hsa01523), AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications (hsa04933), NF-kappa B signaling pathway (hsa04064), Amoebiasis (hsa05146), African trypanosomiasis (hsa05143), and Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) (hsa04514). These pathways are highly associated with key biological processes such as host–pathogen interaction, inflammatory response, and immune cell migration66.

Notably, the NF-κB signaling pathway serves as a central hub regulating inflammation and exhibits significant crosstalk with multiple infectious and immune disease pathways (e.g., Legionellosis and Rheumatoid arthritis), suggesting its potential core role in the antibacterial and immunomodulatory mechanisms of Alkanna extracts. Furthermore, several pathways shared common involvements of cell adhesion molecules (such as ICAM1 and ITGB2) and key inflammatory cytokines (including TNF, IL1B, and CXCL8), indicating that the extract may exert broad-spectrum antibacterial effects through modulating immune cell migration, amplification of inflammatory signals, and pathogen clearance. In conclusion, the KEGG analysis provides systematic insights into the potential mechanisms by which Alkanna extract modulates immune responses and counteracts infections via multi-target and multi-pathway synergistic actions67.

Molecular docking

This study employed molecular docking to evaluate the binding affinity between seven active compounds derived from Alkanna species—including Danshensu, lithospermic acid, rosmarinic acid, sagerinic acid, salvianolic acid A, salvianolic acid C, and yunnaneic acid E—and various enzymatic targets: AChE, BChE, amylase, glucosidase, tyrosinase, as well as carbonic anhydrase isoforms hCAI and hCAII. The docking results revealed strong binding interactions between most compounds and cholinesterases (AChE and BChE). In particular, sagerinic acid, lithospermic acid, and salvianolic acid C showed notably high binding affinity toward AChE, with docking scores of − 11.34, − 11.29, and − 11.66, respectively, indicating their promising potential as cholinesterase inhibitors. Similarly, these compounds also exhibited robust binding to BChE, with scores below − 10. In contrast, for amylase and glucosidase, all ligands yielded docking scores above − 9.5, with lithospermic acid showing moderate inhibitory potential against amylase (–9.621). These findings highlight that sagerinic acid, lithospermic acid, and salvianolic acid C possess strong inhibitory potential against cholinesterases, supporting the prospect of Alkanna extracts in the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease via dual AchE/BchE inhibition. Although the binding to glycemic enzymes (amylase and glucosidase) was moderate, these results still warrant further investigation through structural optimization and in vivo validation. Overall, this computational study elucidates the multi-target inhibitory mechanism of Alkanna extracts, providing a theoretical foundation for their application in developing therapeutic agents for neurodegenerative and metabolic diseases.

Furthermore, based on potential antibacterial targets identified through network pharmacology screening, this study conducted molecular docking analyses of multiple active constituents from Alkanna extracts against six key target proteins (ALB, CXCL8, EGFR, IL1B, ITGB2, and TNF) to evaluate their binding capabilities and antibacterial potential. The docking results revealed significant differences in binding energies between the various compounds and the target proteins. Overall, several constituents exhibited strong binding affinities toward targets such as ALB and EGFR. Notably, certain components showed particularly low binding energies toward EGFR, reaching − 9.865 kcal/mol, indicating promising inhibitory potential. Similarly, some compounds demonstrated high affinity for ALB, with binding energies as low as − 9.992 kcal/mol. In contrast, several other compounds showed relatively weak binding across all tested targets, with binding energies above − 5.4 kcal/mol, suggesting limited potential as standalone antibacterial agents.

Among the inflammation-related targets (such as TNF and IL1B), certain constituents displayed considerable binding affinity for TNF (–8.454 kcal/mol), implying a potential role in modulating inflammatory responses to exert indirect antibacterial effects. Furthermore, the strong binding of multiple constituents to ALB and EGFR suggests that the extract may interfere with signal transduction and protein function across several antibacterial pathways.

This study provides computational evidence supporting the potential of Alkanna active constituents to exert antibacterial effects through a multi-target mechanism. Several compounds demonstrate favorable drug-likeness and target diversity. These results not only offer a molecular-level explanation for the antibacterial mechanisms of Alkanna extracts but also lay a theoretical foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experimental validation. The findings highlight the value of this research in the development of novel antibacterial agents.

Conclusion

The current study represents the first exploration on the phytochemical constituent and biological activities of A. verecunda and roots of A. orientalis. Results revealed that both species were rich in phenolic compounds and exerted interesting antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory and antimicrobial activities and were not toxic. A. orientalis contained higher total phenolic content and consequently exerted higher antioxidant activity than A. verecunda. Both species displayed significant enzyme inhibitory activity and A. verecunda exhibited remarkable inhibitory effect towards the hCAII isoenzyme. Both species displayed high antibacterial activity with remarkable activity against Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC14990. The methanol root extracts of A. orientalis and A. verecunda did not exerted strong cytotoxicity on human cancer cell lines - gastric adenocarcinoma AGS, ovarian adenocarcinoma SKOV-3, colon carcinoma HCT-116, and cervical adenocarcinoma HeLa. A. orientalis extract was the most potent towards gastric cancer cells, and this result may constitute a basis for further research using this line and fractions obtained from the methanol extract of the species. To sum up, the roots of both species could be a new source of bioactive molecules with different applications in pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries. Further in vivo studies and identification of molecules responsible for different biological activities and their mechanism of action are recommended.
