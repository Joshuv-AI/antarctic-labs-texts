Systemic non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs as adjuncts in the treatment of chronic periodontitis: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Juan Li, Pengxian Xie, Wei Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSystematically evaluate the clinical efficacy of systemically administered non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as an adjunct to scaling and root planing (SRP) for periodontitis, assess the therapeutic effects of different drugs, and identify the most effective treatment regimen.Materials and methodsThis study is registered in PROSPERO under trial No. CRD420250651756. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining systemic NSAIDs as SRP adjuncts were identified through systematic searches of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library and CNKI databases. Eligible studies were screened, followed by data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment using Cochrane criteria. Efficacy was assessed through changes in probing depth (PD) and clinical attachment loss (CAL). Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0, and R 4.4.3 software.ResultsThirteen RCTs involving 549 patients met inclusion criteria. The results of the Network meta-analysis (NMA) identified that, when NSAIDs are used as an adjuvant treatment for periodontitis, compared with using only SRP to treat periodontitis, ibuprofen + SRP [MD = 0.58, 95% CI (0.31, 0.85)] shows the most significant efficacy in improving PD and CAL after 1 - month follow - up. At 3 months, the ibuprofen-doxycycline combination [MD = 1.70, 95% CI (1.16, 2.24)] and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) combined with aspirin [MD = 0.82, 95% CI (0.57, 1.07)] demonstrated superior efficacy versus monotherapies.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests that systemic administration of NSAIDs as an adjunct to periodontal baseline therapy may provide clinically significant benefits in periodontitis management. However, further well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are required to validate and refine this conclusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08570-6.Keywords: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, Periodontitis, Clinical attachment loss, Probing depth, Network meta-analysis

Introduction

Periodontitis management primarily relies on local therapy, with SRP as the fundamental approach. Specialized instruments are used in SRP to remove subgingival calculus and biofilm, making the root surface smooth and flat after scaling [1]. SRP, the most widely used foundational treatment for periodontitis, demonstrates significant therapeutic efficacy. Adjunctive pharmacological therapies enhance treatment outcomes in advanced cases.

NSAIDs possess antipyretic, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory properties. They are clinically used for conditions including osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune disorders, fever management, pain control, and perioperative analgesia. From the periodontal therapy perspective, the application of NSAIDs demonstrates beneficial effects in reducing clinical signs of gingival inflammation, evidenced by decreased bleeding on probing and improvements in gingival parameters [2]. These agents control periodontal inflammation by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis and modulating excessive immune responses. Consequently, bone resorption is suppressed and pain alleviated [3]. Consequently, NSAIDs serve as valuable adjuncts to SRP, which facilitates inflammation reduction.

Clinical evidence supports NSAID efficacy in periodontitis management. Oduncuoglu et al. [4] observed significant inflammatory reduction with diclofenac sodium plus SRP versus SRP alone during 6-month follow-up. Cássia et al. [5] demonstrated superior probing depth improvement with aspirin plus SRP versus SRP monotherapy at 3–6 months. Nurcan et al. [6] divided 22 periodontitis patients into two groups: 12 received meloxicam, while 10 received placebo tablets alongside SRP for 10 days. Comparisons of plaque index (PI), papillary bleeding index (PBI), and matrix metalloproteinase 8 (MMP-8) levels within each group and between the two groups revealed significant reductions in PI, PBI, and MMP-8 compared to baseline. Current evidence exhibits methodological limitations: limited direct NSAID comparisons, heterogeneous outcome measures, and insufficient sample sizes. These limitations compromise evidence reliability for optimal NSAID selection. To address this gap, we conducted NMA.

Although the S3-level clinical practice guidelines issued by the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) recommend against using NSAIDs as adjunctive therapy to SRP, significant research gaps persist in the current literature [7]. Existing studies primarily consist of small-scale, single RCTs characterized by high methodological heterogeneity, with a notable absence of head-to-head comparisons among different NSAIDs regimens [8]. To date, no systematic review employing NMA has comprehensively quantified and compared the efficacy and safety profiles of NSAIDs as adjuncts to SRP. Therefore, this systematic review with NMA aims to address this evidence gap by synthesizing available research data and exploring sources of heterogeneity, thereby providing a robust quantitative assessment of NSAIDs’ adjunctive effects. Our objective is to supplement existing guidelines with integrated comparative evidence rather than to challenge current clinical recommendations.

NMA integrates direct and indirect evidence, enabling simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions. Unlike conventional meta-analyses, NMA provides comprehensive effect estimates through mixed comparisons under standardized conditions [9]. This study systematically evaluates global evidence on NSAID adjunctive periodontitis therapy. Quantitative synthesis identifies optimal regimens and establishes evidence-based guidance.

Materials and methods

Study registration

This study adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension statement in reporting clinical outcomes of heterogeneous NSAID classes as adjuncts to SRP for chronic periodontitis management [10]. This NMA adhered to PRISMA guidelines, including the updated version for network meta-analysis, as detailed in the additional file PRISMA_2020_checklist. It is registered in PROSPERO under trial No. CRD420250651756. The direct link is: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420250651756. The review protocol was registered prior to the initiation of the study.

Search strategy

Comprehensive literature searches were independently conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library and CNKI databases. The search encompassed all relevant English publications from database inception to September 1, 2025. Search strategies integrated controlled vocabulary and free-text terms, including “periodontitis descriptors,” “periodontitis,” “periodontal diseases,” “chronic periodontitis,” “moderate-to-severe periodontitis,” “aggressive periodontitis,” “stage II/III periodontitis,” “grade C periodontitis,” “scaling and root planing,” “periodontal debridement,” “non-surgical periodontal therapy,” “NSAIDs,” “ibuprofen,” “flurbiprofen,” “aspirin,” “indomethacin,” “naproxen,” “diclofenac sodium,” “celecoxib,” “meloxicam,” “nimesulide,” et al. The reference lists of the obtained literature were also searched to ensure that no available studies were missed. Full search strategies are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Data extraction

Literature screening was independently performed by two researchers according to predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions with a third investigator. Data extraction encompassed author, publication year, country, study design, total participants, intervention/control groups, sample sizes, follow-up period, and outcome measures. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic for data screening and selection. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: (1) ≤ 0, no agreement; (2) 0.01–0.20, slight agreement; (3) 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; (4) 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; (5) 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; (6) 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement [12].

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent assessors evaluated included studies using the risk of bias tool (ROB 2.0) recommended in the Cochrane Handbook [13]. Bias domains assessed included random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants/personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions. Persistent disagreements underwent adjudication by a third assessor.

Assessment of transitivity and consistency

To evaluate the transitivity of included studies, systematic information on patient and study characteristics was provided. This enabled an empirical assessment of the distribution of potential effect modifiers across trials, including study design, mode of oral administration, dosing regimens, and follow-up duration. Consistency was demonstrated through both global tests and local assessments using the node-splitting method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0, and R 4.4.3. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Network diagrams visualized direct and indirect NSAID comparisons. In the NMA, if the evidence network contains closed loops, a global inconsistency test is performed. If P < 0.05, global inconsistency is deemed to exist. If P ≥ 0.05, global inconsistency is not detected, and a local inconsistency test is then conducted using the node-splitting approach. Closed loops underwent inconsistency testing. If no inconsistency was detected (P > 0.05), we employed consistency models; otherwise, inconsistency models were implemented. Treatment rankings were assessed using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) in Stata 18.0. SUCRA values approaching 1 indicated a higher probability of optimal efficacy [14]. The I2 statistics was used to assess heterogeneity. For cases with significant heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing studies one by one. Heterogeneity was defined as between-study variability exceeding random error. Sensitivity analyses used leave-one-out methodology to identify heterogeneity sources and evaluate result stability. Funnel plots were omitted due to insufficient included studies.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,971 records were retrieved through database searches. Following title/abstract screening, 1,801 records were excluded due to irrelevance or duplication, yielding 170 potentially eligible studies. Full text eligibility assessment resulted in the exclusion of 157 studies, leaving 13 articles [4, 15–26] for inclusion in this systematic review. The inter-rater reliability for all extracted parameters, assessed by Cohen’s kappa statistic, was 0.852 (P < 0.001), indicating almost perfect agreement between investigators. Risk of bias in included studies

The included studies were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (ROB 2.0). The most prevalent issues pertained to allocation concealment and blinding of participants/personnel. The majority of included studies demonstrated high methodological quality: all described randomization methods and blinding procedures, fully reported outcome measures, and exhibited no selective reporting or attrition bias. However, some failed to specify allocation concealment, and no other bias sources were identified. Final assessments indicated 2 studies as “low risk,” while 11 were rated as having “some concerns.” Among these, 6 articles mentioned RCT design without methodological details; 3 explicitly described allocation concealment methods; 4 did not clarify blinding of both operators and participants. Notably, blinding was infeasible in control groups receiving SRP alone due to the conspicuous difference from experimental interventions and lack of placebo use. Three articles omitted blinding status of outcome assessors. All studies maintained complete data with fully reported outcomes, showing no evidence of selective reporting or participant dropout. Discussion

Based on the current S3-level clinical practice guidelines issued by the EFP, systemic or locally administered NSAIDs are not recommended as adjunctive therapy to SRP and against the routine clinical application of NSAIDs and PUFAs in periodontal disease management [7, 8]. This recommendation is primarily grounded in limited evidence from RCTs, which indicate insufficient clinical benefits and potential risks associated with NSAID use. However, this consensus exhibits inherent limitations: (1) small sample sizes; (2) significant heterogeneity arising from variations in administration routes (local vs. systemic) and pharmaceutical formulations; and (3) methodological constraints hindering conventional meta-analyses. To address these gaps, our NMA innovatively integrates data from multiple RCTs. Utilizing advanced statistical modeling, it identifies potential benefits in specific patient subgroups, thereby establishing an evidence-based foundation for precision-based therapeutic approaches in periodontal treatment. Existing guideline recommendations are predominantly predicated on evidence derived from specific historical stages, whereas recent clinical investigations continue to evolve without comprehensive systematic synthesis and reappraisal. Given the pivotal roles of NSAIDs and PUFAs in inflammatory signaling pathways, a systematic evaluation of their efficacy, safety profile, and evidence quality in periodontitis intervention is imperative to objectively assess current research robustness, identify persistent research gaps, and inform future clinical trial design and guideline updates. Consequently, conducting this systematic review serves to elucidate the true therapeutic effects and applicable scenarios of these interventions through holistic integration of emerging evidence, thereby providing a foundation for individualized clinical decision-making and subsequent research trajectories, with demonstrable theoretical and practical significance. This systematic review retains significant academic merit and research necessity.

This NMA evaluated 9 treatment regimens across 13 randomized controlled trials (n = 549 patients). The analysis compared the efficacy of NSAIDs in reducing PD and improving CAL in chronic periodontitis. NSAIDs significantly enhanced SRP outcomes in chronic periodontitis. At 1-month follow-up, systemic ibuprofen showed superior adjunctive efficacy. At 3 months, the ibuprofen-doxycycline combination and omega-3 PUFAs combined with low-dose aspirin demonstrated optimal therapeutic effects. Adjunctive NSAID therapy penetrates instrumentation-inaccessible areas. Importantly, it modulates host immune responses, reduces tissue destruction, enhances treatment efficacy, and promotes periodontal stability while minimizing complications. NSAIDs exhibit antipyretic, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-rheumatic properties. Systemic administration improves clinical markers of gingival inflammation, including reduced bleeding on probing and enhanced gingival indices [2]. NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, suppressing prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and thromboxane A2 (TXA2) synthesis. COX serves as a sensitive inflammation marker in periodontitis. Rat models demonstrate cytoplasmic COX-2 overexpression in fibroblasts and osteoclasts, where inflammation-induced COX-2 triggers PGE2 release [27]. This exacerbates tissue destruction through vasodilation, inflammatory cell recruitment, collagen degradation, and bone resorption [28]. Plaque biofilm initiates periodontitis by inducing inflammation and activating host immunity. Recruited neutrophils subsequently release inflammatory mediators, including Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and PGE2 [29]. PGE2, produced by neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells, functions as both a key inflammatory mediator and potent bone resorption stimulator. This molecule serves as a biomarker for periodontitis and peri-implantitis activity and severity. NSAIDs inhibit COX activity and reduce PGE2 synthesis, conferring anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and anti-resorptive effects [30]. Jain et al. [31] demonstrated that celecoxib, an NSAID and COX enzyme inhibitor, exhibits exceptionally high binding affinity. It effectively suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators in periodontitis. Mechanistically, they competitively bind COX’s active site, blocking arachidonic acid conversion to prostaglandin precursors (PGG₂/PGH₂). This suppresses downstream PG (PGE₂, PGI₂) and TXA₂ production, resulting in reduced vascular permeability and edema, decreased leukocyte infiltration, attenuated inflammatory responses, inhibited platelet aggregation, improved microcirculation, and prevention of microthrombus formation [32]. Consequently, NSAIDs serve as common adjuncts to SRP, reducing inflammation and promoting periodontal healing. Our NMA enables direct, indirect, and mixed treatment comparisons, generating evidence to identify optimal NSAID regimens. The primary study objective was therefore to assess and compare the efficacy of NSAID adjunct therapies using this methodology.

Periodontitis is characterized by periodontal pocket formation and attachment loss. Both PD and CAL serve as primary clinical indicators of periodontal tissue destruction, closely associated with disease severity and treatment response. Therefore, PD and CAL were selected as this study’s primary outcome measures. Intervention rankings varied according to the outcome assessed. Regarding PD improvement, ibuprofen showed superior efficacy at 1-month post-treatment, whereas aspirin plus PUFAs has better therapeutic effect after three months. For CAL improvement, ibuprofen remained optimal at 1 month, while the combination of ibuprofen and doxycycline yielded better results at 3 months.

NSAIDs exhibit potentially transient characteristics. They primarily modulate the host inflammatory response rather than targeting the microbial etiology of periodontal disease. Consequently, clinical improvements may undergo reversal or attenuation in short-term outcomes following discontinuation.

At 1-month follow-up, both ibuprofen and naproxen as adjuncts to SRP were more effective than SRP alone in reducing periodontal PD. By 3 months, three regimens—ibuprofen-doxycycline, PUFAs plus aspirin, and ibuprofen monotherapy—demonstrated significantly greater improvements in both PD reduction and CAL gain compared to SRP alone. Ibuprofen maintained consistent efficacy throughout the study period. Notably, aspirin plus PUFA combination therapy produced the greatest CAL improvement at 3 months, constituting the optimal therapeutic effect. This combination’s efficacy surpassed ibuprofen at 3 months. This finding aligns with the meta-analysis results by Neprelyuk [33]; their study similarly demonstrated that adjunctive use of PUFAs combined with low-dose aspirin significantly improved PD and CAL. The combination of PUFAs and low-dose aspirin shows promise as an adjunctive therapy for periodontitis patients. However, no intergroup difference existed at 1 month. These observations align with existing evidence. Araujo et al. [25] confirmed the superior 3-month efficacy of aspirin and PUFA therapy. Similarly, Santos et al. [34] reported significantly greater improvement in periodontal inflammation at 3 versus 6 months. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that aspirin and PUFA combination therapy achieves peak efficacy at the 3-month follow-up.

Substantial evidence confirms the efficacy of NSAIDs as periodontitis adjuncts, consistent with our meta-analytic findings. However, long-term NSAID administration carries established adverse effect risks. Among the 13 studies included in this review, no adverse events were reported. However, in other investigations documenting adverse events [35], the most commonly reported symptom was gastric discomfort. This observation underscores the generally favorable safety profile of adjunctive therapy. Systemic adverse reactions associated with NSAIDs are primarily related to gastrointestinal disturbances, with drug-induced gastrointestinal side effects being among the most frequently observed adverse events. Gastrointestinal complications are the most frequent adverse effects, and gastric mucosal injury represents a major treatment-limiting factor in chronic inflammation. Tsujimoto et al. [36] demonstrated NSAID-induced exacerbation of gastric mucosal damage. Moreover, robust evidence associates specific NSAIDs with cardiovascular risks, including disease induction and progression [37, 38]. Ruschitzka et al. [39] documented that NSAIDs (notably ibuprofen) elevate blood pressure, increase hypertension incidence, and compromise blood pressure control. Reiss et al. [40] reported accelerated atherosclerotic plaque formation and elevated cardiovascular event rates, while Arfè et al. [41] established increased heart failure hospitalization risk. Additionally, NSAIDs constitute frequent nephrotoxins. Chronic NSAID exposure elevates renal disease risk, exemplified by Burgos Pratx et al.‘s [42] report of etoricoxib-induced acute renal failure. Although the occurrence of the above side effects was not reported in the included articles, the absence of reported adverse events in the included trials does not necessarily indicate safety. We must also pay attention to the possible side effects in clinical medication. Therefore, medications should be used with caution to reduce the damage during the treatment process. Giusy Rita Maria La Rosa [43], Cetira Filho [44], Ismael Khouly [45], et al. concur with this perspective.

To ensure sample homogeneity and satisfy the transitivity assumption, we exclusively selected RCTs and rigorously restricted the mode of drug administration to oral delivery only and in the control group, only SRP or SRP plus placebo gel were performed. This process led to the exclusion of 10 studies. Additionally, follow-up durations were limited to 1 month and 3 months to further ensure transitivity. When direct comparative evidence on treatment efficacy is missing among the included interventions, transitivity allows for the establishment of indirect comparisons through a common control intervention such as SRP. A key limitation of transitivity is the drastic reduction in usable sample size, which restricts the applicability of conclusions to highly idealized populations.

The extensive missing data observed in the local inconsistency test tables should be considered anticipated due to the limited number of included studies (only 13 articles). Furthermore, parts of the network structure lacked closed loops, and direct comparisons between certain intervention pairs were not available in the included studies, which is consistent with the expected limitations of the evidence base.

The PD assessed at the one-month follow-up demonstrated pronounced heterogeneity (I² = 97.59%), reflecting considerable inter-study variability. Subgroup analyses stratified by gender, age, medication dosage, or baseline periodontal pocket depth were not feasible due to limited data. Furthermore, meta-regression was omitted owing to the inclusion of fewer than ten studies. Sensitivity analysis was therefore conducted to identify sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis revealed that the study by Aras et al. [16] significantly contributed to heterogeneity in one-month probing depth changes. Excluding this study markedly reduced heterogeneity, indicating methodological inconsistencies. Upon reexamination, Aras et al. [16] did not specify whether blinding of investigators or participants was implemented, potentially introducing performance bias and protocol deviations. Moreover, compared with the study by Saynur [24], Aras et al. [16] mentioned that the PD of periodontitis in the selected patients should be ≥ 4 mm. The disease severity was not mentioned in other studies, which is also a cause of heterogeneity. Furthermore, even at identical drug dosages, differing dosing regimens can also serve as a source of heterogeneity within studies. caution should be exercised when interpreting the pooled results due to clinical heterogeneity.

Two factors explain the absence of statistically significant CAL changes at 1 month: First, insufficient CAL data at this timepoint limited analysis to five RCTs (four interventions). Second, heterogeneous dosing regimens across studies precluded detection of definitive NSAID differences in pooled analyses.

Limitations

(1) Despite comparative assessment of baseline and methodological parameters across studies (all evaluating systemic NSAIDs as periodontitis adjuncts), unavoidable variations in NSAID timing, dosage, and treatment duration may introduce bias. (2) Heterogeneous follow-up periods restricted analytical sample sizes despite the inclusion of 13 RCTs. Consequently, only PD and CAL changes were assessed, omitting inflammatory biomarkers. Therefore, confirmatory studies remain necessary. (3) This study included only 13 studies, representing a limited number of investigations. More research is required to validate our findings. (4) Short-term outcomes may exhibit high heterogeneity. Further long-term studies are necessary to demonstrate the efficacy of the drug. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study offers clinically relevant guidance. Stratified subgroup analyses by follow-up duration reduced bias associated with heterogeneous administration routes through exclusive focus on systemic delivery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, current evidence provides moderate support for systemic NSAIDs as an adjunct to periodontal baseline therapy in managing periodontitis. Compared to SRP, ibuprofen [MD = 0.58, 95% CI (0.31, 0.85)] achieved superior reductions in PD and CAL at 1-month follow-up. By 3 months, combination regimens showed optimal outcomes: ibuprofen-doxycycline [MD = 1.70, 95% CI (1.16, 2.24)] and aspirin-PUFA [MD = 0.82, 95% CI (0.57, 1.07)] cotreatment demonstrated superior efficacy compared to monotherapy. Systemic administration of NSAIDs as an adjunct to periodontal baseline therapy may provide clinically significant benefits in periodontitis management. However, limitations persist due to restricted sample sizes, short follow-up durations, methodological heterogeneity, and potential confounding factors. Collectively, these findings suggest a probable clinical efficacy of this approach, yet further validation through large-scale, high-quality randomized controlled trials is imperative to consolidate the evidence base.
