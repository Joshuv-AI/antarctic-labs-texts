Exosomes from DGKK-Knockdown CD4+T Cells Alleviate Sepsis by Inhibiting the DAG/PKC/ROS Pathway-Mediated M1 Macrophage Polarization and Reducing Endothelial Permeability
Authors: Wen Chen, Xiangye Bo, Qianqian Wang, Ye Zhou, Nuo Zhou, Jinhong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a serious disease associated with macrophage polarization and endothelial permeability. The DAG/PKC/ROS pathway is crucial for sepsis development, macrophage polarization and endothelial permeability. As CD4+T cell-derived exosomes can modulate the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway, we aimed to investigate the role and mechanism of exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells in sepsis. RAW264.7 cells were exposed to lipopolysaccharide and interferon-gamma (L/I) to promote M1 polarization and then co-cultured with exosomes. The macrophages were co-cultured with lipopolysaccharide-stimulated PMVECs. Additionally, phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), a PKC agonist, was used to treat macrophages. Furthermore, sepsis mouse models were established via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery. In vitro, lipopolysaccharide increased DGKK expression in CD4⁺T cells and CD4⁺T cell-derived exosomes. L/I-induced M1 macrophage polarization and DAG/PKC/ROS pathway activation were amplified by lipopolysaccharide-stimulated CD4⁺T cell exosomes. However, exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cells counteracted these effects. Furthermore, treatment with these exosomes improved PMVEC viability, decreased apoptosis, alleviated inflammation, and decreased permeability. In vivo, CLP-induced inflammation, organ (lung/liver/kidney) injury, M1 macrophage polarization, DAG/PKC/ROS pathway activation, and lung permeability were further aggravated by LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cell exosomes. However, exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cells counteracted these deteriorations, while macrophage depletion abolished the protective effects of DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cell-derived exosomes in sepsis. Importantly, the effects of exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cells on M1 macrophage polarization and endothelial permeability were reversed by PMA. In conclusion, exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells mitigated sepsis by suppressing the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway-mediated M1 macrophage polarization and reducing endothelial permeability, suggesting DGKK as a promising therapeutic target for sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-026-02566-8.Keywords: DGKK, Sepsis, DAG/PKC/ROS pathway, M1 macrophage polarization, Exosome, Endothelial permeability

Introduction

Sepsis is a critical clinical disorder triggered by dysregulated immune responses to severe infections, leading to multi-organ dysfunction [1, 2]. Approximately 20% of deaths worldwide are attributed to sepsis, which poses a heavy burden on public health [3]. Macrophages exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity, existing as pro-inflammatory M1 subsets, anti-inflammatory M2 subsets, or other states that lie between these two extreme phenotypes [4]. Under physiological conditions, macrophages mainly circulate as monocytes [5]. Endothelial cells constitute the first line of defense in the pathological process of sepsis-induced lung injury, which is the earliest change in effector cells undergoing cell contraction, apoptosis, and membrane destruction, increasing endothelial permeability will lead to increased leakage of pulmonary vessels and acute lung edema [6]. While in sepsis, pathogens or pro-inflammatory signals induce M1 macrophage polarization, which secretes IL-6 and TNF-α for pathogen clearance but elicits hyperinflammation [7]; meanwhile, endothelial cells experience tight junction (TJ) disruption and enhanced permeability due to endotoxins and M1-derived cytokines, which promote vascular leakage and inflammatory cell infiltration, exacerbating sepsis progression [8]. Therefore, inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and reducing endothelial permeability undoubtedly represent effective therapeutic strategies for sepsis.

Increasing evidence has indicated that the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway plays a pivotal role in sepsis. DAG activates PKC through binding to the DAG-binding site in the regulatory domain of PKC, which induces upregulated expressions of p22phox and p47phox, these two proteins then form catalytically active oxidases and ultimately drive the generation of ROS [9, 10]. Excessive ROS generation under inflammatory conditions promotes M1 macrophage polarization, and research has found that inhibiting ROS can partially reverse the M1/M2 polarization [11]. Of note, DAG, PKC, and ROS have also been reported to enhance endothelial permeability, which can accelerate the progression of sepsis [12–14]. Thus, targeting the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway to regulate M1 macrophage polarization and endothelial permeability may be effective for sepsis treatment.

Exosomes are tiny extracellular vesicles, measuring between 30 and 150 nm in diameter, that play a vital role in intercellular communication by delivering bioactive molecules in inflammatory microenvironments. Various cell types in the human body, such as CD4+T cells, can secrete exosomes. Diacylglycerol kinase K (DGKK) is an enzyme responsible for phosphorylating DAG and generating phosphatidic acid [15]. DGKK carried by CD4+T cell-derived exosomes is crucial for chronic inflammatory lung disorders, such as sepsis. Scholars have revealed that exosomes from CD4+T cells carrying DGKK exacerbate sepsis-related lung injury by regulating inflammation and oxidative stress [16]. In addition, it has been reported that a lack of diacylglycerol kinase ζ leads to reduced interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) production, and restricted M1 macrophage polarization [17]. Furthermore, a DGK inhibitor can suppress U46619-induced endothelial cell layer permeability [18]. However, whether exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells can alleviate sepsis by inhibiting the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway-mediated M1 macrophage polarization and reducing endothelial permeability remains unclear.

Thus, in this study, we isolated exosomes from CD4+T cells with DGKK knockdown, and investigated the effects of these exosomes on M1 macrophage polarization, endothelial permeability, and sepsis severity both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), a PKC agonist, was further used to explore the mechanism by which exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells alleviate sepsis, thereby revealing a new regulatory mechanism in sepsis development and providing a promising therapeutic target for sepsis treatment.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice were acquired from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (SCXK (Hu) 2022-0004) and were kept under controlled conditions (22–24 °C, 60% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All animal experiments received approval from the Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Hunter Testing Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and were conducted following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Isolation of CD4⁺T Cells

CD4+T cells were isolated from the spleens of mice utilizing magnetic activating cell sorting technique with CD4+T cell isolation kits (130-104-454, Miltenyi Biotec). Male C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks old) were euthanized, and their spleens were collected under sterile conditions. The spleens were subsequently rinsed and homogenized using a plunger. After filtering through a 70 μm cell sieve, the cells were washed by buffer until complete dissociation. The cells were then pelleted, and resuspended by red blood cell lysis buffer, and washed by buffer. After that, the cells were incubated with a biotin-antibody cocktail for 5 min at 2–8 °C, followed by incubation with anti-biotin microbeads at the same temperature for 10 min. Magnetic separation was then carried out using a MidiMACS™ Separator (130-042-302, Miltenyi Biotec) with a pre-rinsed LS column (130-042-401, Miltenyi Biotec) based on negative selection methods. Finally, flow cytometry was used to test the purity of the CD4+T cells. After culturing for three days, CD4+T cells were stimulated with or without 10 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 24 h, after which the culture medium was harvested to isolate exosomes [16, 19].

Construction of DGKK-Knockdown CD4⁺T Cells

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) designed to target DGKK (sh-DGKK-1, sh-DGKK-2, and sh-DGKK-3) and a negative control shRNA (sh-NC) were supplied by Suzhou GenePharma Co., Ltd. Following the manufacturer provided guidelines, CD4⁺T cells that had been exposed to LPS were transfected with sh-DGKK or sh-NC using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h of transfection, exosomes were extracted from the CD4⁺T cells, and the transfection efficiency was confirmed through quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The sh-DGKK exhibiting the highest knockdown efficiency was chosen for further experiments.

Isolation and Identification of CD4⁺T Cell-Derived Exosomes

CD4⁺T cells, either stimulated with LPS or not, were plated in T75 flasks for 48 h. Following a wash, serum-free medium was added to the cells and maintained for 24 h. The resulting supernatant was then collected, and centrifuged to eliminate floating cells and cell debris. After filtering through a 0.22 μm filter, the supernatant was subjected to absolute weight balancing with the balance method and subsequently ultracentrifuged. The pellet was rinsed using Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), followed by another round of absolute weight balancing and ultracentrifugation. After that, the supernatant was carefully removed, and the exosomes were resuspended by DPBS gently.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

A copper grid was retrieved using tweezers. Then, a droplet of exosome suspension was aspirated and applied to the grid for 1 min. The excess liquid was removed by carefully blotting the edge of the grid with filter paper. Next, phosphotungstic acid was dropped onto the grid for 30 s, after which the residual phosphotungstic acid was blotted off. After air-drying for 1 min, TEM (H-7650, Hitachi, Ltd.) was used to examine exosome morphology.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

Exosomes were resuspended after being diluted by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer. Prior to loading the samples, the NTA instrument (N30E, Nano FCM) was calibrated using a standard sample to verify its performance. Subsequently, the exosomes were diluted with PBS and loaded into the sample chamber in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Finally, the instrument provided data on the concentration and size of the exosomes directly.

Cell Lines

RAW264.7 mouse macrophages (iCell-m047) and mouse pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs, MIC-iCell-a001) were sourced from iCell Bioscience Inc. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. PMVECs were maintained in endothelial basal medium with 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% primary lung endothelial cell culture additives. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Exosome Internalization Assay

Exosomes were labelled with PKH67 (C3635M, Beyotime Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. In short, exosomes were labelled with PKH67 green fluorescent probe at 25℃ for 5 min. Then, RAW264.7 cells were exposed to 1 µg of the labelled exosomes and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. After that, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, stained by 4’,6-Diamidino-2’-phenylindole for another 10 min, and then examined by a fluorescence microscope.

Macrophage Treatment and Co-Culture Experiments

RAW264.7 cells were assigned into five groups: the RAW264.7− NC group, RAW264.7− L/I group, RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS group, RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS+sh−NC group, and RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS+sh−DGKK group. All groups except the RAW264.7− NC group were exposed to LPS (100 ng/mL) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (30 ng/mL) for 48 h to promote M1 polarization [20]. Then, the macrophages from the RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS, RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS+sh−NC, and RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS+sh−DGKK groups were co-cultured with exosomes (100 µg/mL) isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells aforementioned, which had been transfected with either sh-DGKK or sh-NC, or neither [16, 21]. Furthermore, after confirming the role of PMA by Western blot, lipopolysaccharide and interferon-gamma (L/I) treated macrophages were exposed to 10 ng/mL PMA for 1.5 min and co-cultured with exosomes isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells, which had been transfected with sh-DGKK [22].

Establishment of Sepsis Mice

Sepsis mice were established through cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery as reported in previous studies [23–26]. In short, male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized using isoflurane (3% for induction and 1.5% for maintenance). Then, a 0.4 cm longitudinal incision was made along the abdominal midline to expose the cecum. After that, the exposed cecum was ligated at 1 cm from the tip using a 3 − 0 silk suture and punctured once with a 20-gauge needle at 0.5 cm from the ligation. A small quantity of feces was gently extruded, after which the cecum was put back into the abdominal cavity. The abdominal musculature, peritoneum, and skin were then sutured, and immediately following surgery, the mice received a subcutaneous injection of normal saline for fluid resuscitation.

Animal Grouping

C57BL/6 mice were assigned into eight groups randomly: the sham, CLP, CLP + Exo−NC, CLP + Exo−LPS, CLP + Exo−LPS+sh−NC, CLP + Exo−LPS+sh−DGKK, CN-lipo + CLP+Exo− LPS+sh−DGKK, and Clo-lip + CLP+Exo− LPS+sh−DGKK groups. All groups except the sham group underwent CLP surgery. The mice in the sham group received a similar procedure, but with no ligation or puncture. Four hours after CLP surgery, mice in the CLP + Exo−LPS, CLP + Exo−LPS+sh−NC, CLP + Exo−LPS+sh−DGKK, CN-lipo + CLP+Exo− LPS+sh−DGKK, and Clo-lip + CLP+Exo− LPS+sh−DGKK groups were injected with 200 µg exosomes isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells by the tail vein, which had been transfected with either sh-DGKK or sh-NC, or neither, as aforementioned [16, 27, 28], while the mice in the CLP + Exo−NC group were injected with 200 µg exosomes isolated from CD4⁺T cells, which were not exposed to LPS or transfected with any shRNA. Twenty-four hours before CLP surgery, mice in the CN-lipo + CLP+Exo− LPS+sh−DGKK group or Clo-lip + CLP+Exo− LPS+sh−DGKK group received an intravenous injection of 150 µL control liposomes (CN‑lipo, 40338ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology) or clodronate liposomes (Clo‑lip, 40337ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology), respectively [29, 30]. Twenty-four hours after surgery, 9 mice from each group were randomly selected and euthanized using excessive CO₂ for the following experiments [31–33]. Among these 9 mice, 3 mice were randomly selected for serum inflammatory cytokines detection and bacterial count, as well as Evans blue (EB) assay, 3 mice were randomly selected for molecular biology assays (including qPCR, flow cytometry, Western blot, DAG/ROS measurement), and the remaining 3 mice were used for histological analysis (HE staining). During this study, 16 mice per group were used to monitor the 24-h survival rate.

Macrophage Depletion Verification

The spleens of the mice were collected and placed into cold Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The spleens were then ground using a syringe plunger, and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. After centrifugation, the cells in the lower layer were collected, resuspended in PBS, and incubated with 5 µL of fluorescent antibodies against CD45 (E-AB-F0995E) and F4/80 (E-AB-F0995D) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. After washing and centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in PBS for subsequent flow cytometry analysis. All antibodies utilized were from Elabscience and were diluted at a ratio of 1:20.

Bacterial Count

Following a series of logarithmic dilutions of the whole blood, 100 µL of the diluted sample was plated onto tryptic soy agar. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the bacterial colonies were counted.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) Staining

Lung, liver, and kidney samples collected from the mice were fixed for 24 h. Then, the tissues were dehydrated using ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 4 μm sections. The slices were then deparaffinized by xylene, hydrated by ethanol, and stained using HE (hematoxylin: 61753 C, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd.; eosin: 30086E, Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd.). After dehydration and transparency, the sections were examined by an optical microscope. Pathological scoring of HE-stained sections was performed using a 4-point scale: 0, no lesions; 1, mild/focal lesions (involving < 25% of the microscopic field); 2, mild lesions (25–50% of the microscopic field); 3, moderate lesions (50–75% of the microscopic field); 4, severe lesions (> 75% of the microscopic field).

EB Assay

Before euthanasia, the mice were injected with EB (50 mg/kg, E8010, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) through the tail vein. Two hours after the injection of EB, the mice were euthanized, and their lung tissues were perfused by PBS to eliminate excess EB. Then, lung samples were collected and homogenized in ten volumes of formamide. The homogenate was then incubated at 60 °C for 18 h, followed by centrifugation at 5000 r/min for 0.5 h. The absorbance of the supernatant at 620 nm was assessed with a microplate reader, and the EB concentration was determined by reference to standard curves.

Peritoneal Macrophage Isolation

Following euthanasia, the mouse’s abdomen was disinfected by 70% ethanol, after which the abdominal skin was incised to expose the peritoneum. Then, 4 mL PBS was injected into the abdominal cavity, and after a gentle massage, the injected liquid was retrieved. Cells in the recovered fluid were then pelleted through centrifugation at 4 °C. After removing the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in complete Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium. Macrophages were allowed to adhere by incubating at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 1 h [34].

DAG and ROS Measurement

The levels of DAG and ROS in cells were quantified by commercial kits (DAG: MET-5028, Cell Biolabs; ROS: E004-1, NanJing JianCheng Bioengineering Institute), in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from the cells, exosomes, and tissues by RNA extraction kits (B610583-0100, BBI). Then, cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA with the TRUEscript RT MasterMix (PC7002, Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd.). qPCR was conducted using a SYBR Green qPCR Mix (PC6202, Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd.). The relative expressions of target mRNAs were determined through the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference. Information on the primer sequences can be found in Table 1.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

To assess the purity of the CD4⁺ T cells and the proportion of CD86 and CD206 cells, as well as the CD86/CD206 cell ratio, the cells were centrifuged, and the lower layer was collected. Then, the lower layer was resuspended in PBS. After that, Fc receptor blocking was performed by incubating cells with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 antibody (C1755S, Beyotime Biotechnology) on ice for 10 min. The cells were then incubated with 5 µL anti-mouse CD3 (E-AB-F1013C), CD4 (E-AB-F1097D), F4/80 (E-AB-F0995D), CD86 (E-AB-F0994C), and CD206/MMR (E-AB-F1135E) antibodies at 4℃ for 0.5 h in the darkness. Following this incubation, the cells were centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were then resuspended in PBS, and analyzed using flow cytometry (NovoCyte, Agilent Technologies Inc.). All the antibodies were purchased from Elabscience and diluted to 1:20.

Endothelial Cells Co-Cultured with Macrophages

According to the treatment, PMVECs were assigned into five groups: PMVEC−NC+RAW264.7−L/I+Exo−LPS group, PMVEC−LPS+RAW264.7−L/I+Exo−LPS group, PMVEC−LPS+RAW264.7−L/I+Exo−LPS+sh−NC group, PMVEC−LPS+RAW264.7−L/I+Exo−LPS+sh−DGKK group, and PMVEC−LPS+RAW264.7−L/I+PMA+Exo−LPS+sh−DGKKgroup. Specifically, macrophages in all groups were treated with LPS and IFN-γ to induce M1 polarization. Then, macrophages in the PMVEC−LPS+RAW264.7−L/I+PMA+Exo−LPS+sh−DGKK group were treated with PMA. Subsequently, macrophages in all groups were co-cultured with exosomes isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4+T cells, which had been transfected with either sh-DGKK or sh-NC, or neither. At the same time, PMVECs in all groups, except the PMVEC−NC+RAW264.7−L/I+Exo−LPS group, were stimulated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h to establish sepsis cell models [35, 36]. Then, macrophages were collected and seeded in the upper chamber of Transwell plates (with a pore size of 0.4 μm), and PMVECs were seeded in the lower chamber, and the co-culture was maintained for 24 h [37].

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay

PMVECs were seeded in a 96-well plate and treated according to the experimental groups. Upon treatment, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent (C0039, Beyotime Biotechnology) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. The OD values were then detected at 450 nm with a microplate reader.

Cell Apoptosis Detection

After treatment, PMVECs were collected and washed by PBS, followed by adjustment of cell density to 1 × 106 cells/mL. Subsequently, 500 µL binding buffer was added to the cells. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was suspended in 100 µL binding buffer, and 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL PI solution (556547, BD) were added sequentially, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, flow cytometry was applied for cell apoptosis measurement.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The peripheral blood of the mice was centrifuged to obtain the serum. The levels of IL-6 (RX203049M), IL-1β (RX203063M), TNF-α (RX202412M), interleukin-10 (IL-10, RX203075M) in cell supernatant and serum, as well as transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1, EK981) in cell supernatant, were measured by corresponding ELISA kits as per the instructions provided by the manufacturers. All ELISA kits were sourced from Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with the exception of the TGF-β1 ELISA kits, which were procured from MultiSciences Biotech Co., Ltd. Following the development of color, the optical density (OD) value was recorded using a microplate reader.

Western Blot

Total proteins were isolated from the cells, exosomes, or lung tissues utilizing radio-immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer. The concentration of the protein was assessed using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of proteins were separated through sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After that, the membranes were incubated with HRP-labelled secondary antibodies (1:6000, 7074, CST) at room temperature for 2 h. Bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified using ImageJ software. Information on the primary antibodies is displayed in Table 2.

Transendothelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) Measurement

TEER of PMVECs was determined with a sterile resistance meter (ESR-2, Millipore) at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h after co-culturing with macrophages. TEER value = (measured resistance value - blank resistance value) × 0.33 Ω·cm².

Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)-Dextran Permeability Assay

PMVECs were treated with FITC-dextran solution (0.5 mg/mL, ST2930, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 2 h at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h post co-culture with macrophages. Fluorescence intensity of the PMVECs was detected using a multi-function microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, MD).

Statistical Analysis

Data from this study were displayed as means ± standard deviation and analyzed using SPSS 20.0. For comparisons between two groups, an independent samples t-test was used to determine significant differences. For comparisons involving more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance was employed for significance testing. If data were non-normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed, and the Dunnett T3 test was used in cases of unequal variance. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Identification of Exosomes Derived from DGKK-Knockdown CD4⁺T Cells and Validation of their Internalization by Macrophages

CD4⁺T cells were successfully isolated from mouse spleens, and exhibited a round morphology and grew in suspension (Fig. 1A). Flow cytometry verified the purity of CD4⁺T cells to be 95.71% (Fig. 1B). Then, exosomes were isolated from these CD4⁺T cells. TEM revealed that the extracts displayed typical saucer-like vesicular structures with a central depression, consistent with the morphological features of exosomes (Fig. 1C). NTA found that the majority of extracts ranged in size from 30 to 150 nm with a relatively normal distribution. The mean sizes were 79.4 nm and 83.5 nm in the Exo− NC and Exo− LPS groups, respectively (Fig. 1D). Western blot results presented in Fig. 1E confirmed the expressions of exosome-specific markers (CD9, CD81, and TSG101) and the absence of negative markers (Calnexin and GRP94) in both the Exo− NC and Exo− LPS groups. Figure 1F demonstrated that DGKK mRNA expression was elevated in LPS-treated CD4⁺T cells and their derived exosomes.

Furthermore, CD4⁺T cells that were exposed to LPS were transfected with three distinct sh-DGKK sequences. The results from qPCR indicated that relative to sh-NC, sh-DGKK-3 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency, leading to a significant reduction in DGKK mRNA expression in CD4⁺T cells and their derived exosomes. Thus, sh-DGKK-3 was selected for the following experiments (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 1H, following incubation with macrophages, PKH26-labelled exosomes displayed green fluorescence within macrophages, confirming the uptake of exosomes by macrophages.

Exosomes from DGKK-Knockdown CD4⁺T Cells Inhibited M1 Macrophage Polarization by Suppressing the DAG/PKC/ROS Pathway

To explore the role of exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells in M1 macrophage polarization, exosomes that underwent different treatments were co-cultured with L/I-stimulated macrophage RAW264.7. Figure 2A and C revealed that relative to the RAW264.7− NC group, the RAW264.7− L/I group exhibited increased DGKK, M1 marker (CD86, iNOS) mRNA expressions, elevated M1 marker (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) and M2 marker (IL-10 and TGF-β1) concentrations, as well as higher CD86 and CD206 percentages and CD86/CD206 ratio. Most effects observed in the RAW264.7− L/I group were further amplified by the addition of exosomes from LPS-treated CD4⁺T cells, except for M2 marker (CD206, Arg-1) mRNA expressions, IL-10 and TGF-β1 concentrations, as well as CD206%. However, after treatment with exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells, the mRNA expressions of DGKK, CD86, and iNOS, the concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, the CD86%, and CD86/CD206 ratio were decreased, whereas the gene expression and percentage of CD206 were increased. Furthermore, Fig. 2D and E revealed that LPS and IFN-γ treatments enhanced DAG and ROS levels as well as the p-PKCα/PKCα ratio in RAW264.7− L/I group macrophages. Relative to the RAW264.7− L/I group, the RAW264.7− L/I+Exo−LPS group showed further increases in DAG and ROS levels and p-PKCα/PKCα ratio. However, after treatment with exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells, DAG and ROS levels and p-PKCα/PKCα ratio were decreased.

Discussion

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by dysregulated host immune responses to infection, leading to multi-organ dysfunction and high global mortality [38]. Despite improvements in fluid resuscitation and antibiotic treatments, managing sepsis clinically remains challenging, underscoring the urgent need to discover new therapeutic targets and underlying mechanisms [39]. This research revealed that exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells mitigate sepsis by inhibiting the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway-mediated macrophage M1 polarization and reducing endothelial permeability. These results offer novel perspectives on the immunomodulatory role of CD4+T cell-derived exosomes in sepsis and identify DGKK as a promising therapeutic target.

It is noteworthy that our findings both align with and extend the study by Tu et al., who reported that extracellular vesicles derived from CD4+T cells carry DGKK to promote sepsis-induced lung injury by regulating oxidative stress and inflammation [16]. While both studies establish the pathological significance of DGKK in sepsis, our work addresses complementary yet distinct dimensions. First, Tu et al. focused on oxidative stress and inflammation in sepsis, whereas we demonstrated that exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cells alleviated sepsis by inhibiting DAG/PKC/ROS-mediated M1 macrophage polarization and endothelial permeability. Second, whereas Tu et al. employed single pharmacological or genetic interventions, we applied combinatorial approaches using PMA or clodronate liposomes to validate PKC-specificity and macrophage-dependency. Third, in contrast to the direct “exosome+A549 cell” model, we established a co-culture system. Specifically, L/I-polarized M1 macrophages were exposed to exosomes and subsequently co-cultured with LPS-stimulated PMVECs, which could better recapitulate the complex macrophage-endothelium interplay in sepsis. Collectively, these complementary advances position DGKK as a therapeutic target for sepsis.

Exosomes have emerged as pivotal mediators of intercellular communication in immune regulation, especially in infectious and inflammatory diseases [40]. As key components of the adaptive immune system, CD4⁺T cells release exosomes that can influence the progression of many diseases, such as sepsis [16]. DGKK, a critical regulator for the DAG/PKC pathway, Tu et al. have found that in patients with sepsis-induced acute lung injury, DGKK expression was significantly elevated in serum exosomes, and exosomal DGKK could serve as an effective biomarker to distinguish patients with sepsis-induced lung injury from healthy individuals, which directly confirms the presence of DGKK-enriched exosomes in the human circulation during sepsis. The study also analyzed DGKK expression in various tissues and cell types through the public database and verified that DGKK is predominantly expressed in T lymphocytes, whch provides a cellular source basis for CD4⁺ T cells to secrete DGKK-carrying exosomes in vivo. In addition, in their study, the isolated CD4 + T cells from healthy control subjects exhibited elevated expression of DGKK in cell lysates under LPS challenge [16]. This study found that exosomes derived LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells carried high levels of DGKK, which exacerbated sepsis progression in mice. However, DGKK knockdown in CD4+T cells alleviated sepsis, indicating that DGKK may be an important therapeutic target for sepsis.

Macrophages display distinct phenotypic features in response to various stimuli. Activated macrophages are usually polarized into pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, and M1 macrophages play a crucial role in sepsis-associated tissue injury [41]. It has been reported that CD4⁺T cell-derived exosomes can promote inflammation by inducing macrophages to secrete IL-6 and IL-1β, which are biomarkers of M1 polarization [42]. In addition, the deficiency of Dgkζ, another isoform of the DGK family, leads to decreased TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β production as well as limiting M1 macrophage polarization [17]. Our cell and animal experiments demonstrated that M1 macrophage polarization was enhanced by exosomes isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells. However, DGKK silencing in CD4⁺T cells reversed these effects. Critically, the protective effects of DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cell-derived exosomes in sepsis were abolished by macrophage depletion. All of these results suggested that exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells may alleviate sepsis by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization.

Enhanced vascular permeability is a key characteristic of sepsis. Following 8 and 24 h post-CLP surgery, elevated vascular permeability is observed in all organs of mice [43]. As endothelial cells are important components of vessels, endothelial cell damage and enhanced permeability are typical pathological alterations during sepsis progression [44]. It has been reported that CD4+T cells influence endothelial cell permeability by multiple ways. For example, microvesicles derived from CD4+T cells can promote tissue inflammation by altering microvascular endothelial function and barrier permeability [45]. In addition, CD4+T cells impair endothelial functions via IL-1α-mediated upregulation of vascular NOX1 [46]. Furthermore, exosomes secreted by diverse cell types can enhance endothelial cell permeability [47, 48]. More importantly, selective inhibition of DGK isoforms has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy for vascular dysfunction [18]. TJ proteins and Ve-cadherin are vital for sustaining endothelial integrity, and the downregulation of these protein expressions is closely linked to enhanced permeability. Our findings demonstrated that, both in vivo and in vitro, exosomes isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells reduced endothelial permeability, while DGKK silencing in CD4⁺T cells restored endothelial permeability. All of these suggested that exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells may alleviate sepsis by reducing endothelial permeability.

The DAG/PKC/ROS pathway is pivotal in sepsis progression. DAG is implicated in the generation of inflammatory proteins and lipids, and inhibiting δ-PKC has been shown to mitigate lung injury caused by sepsis [49, 50]. A growing body of evidence has established an association between ROS and the progression of organ dysfunction in sepsis [51]. Additionally, the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway serves as an important regulator of endothelial permeability [52–54]. Moreover, DAG, PKC, and ROS all contribute M1 macrophage polarization. For instance, research by Shao et al. has indicated that the abnormal accumulation of DAG and TAG promotes macrophage migration and M1-like polarization in bone marrow-derived macrophages in response to various lipid signaling and inflammatory stimuli [55]. Furthermore, PKCα in colon cancer cells has been confirmed to facilitate M1 macrophage polarization through the MKK3/6-P38 MAPK pathway [56]. ROS has also been shown to modulate disease development by modulating M1 macrophage polarization across multiple disease models [57–59]. DGKK, a well-recognized regulator of the DAG/PKC pathway, has been reported to protect against brain damage in septic mice [60]. This study observed that the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway was enhanced by exosomes isolated from LPS-stimulated CD4⁺T cells both in vivo and in vitro. However, DGKK knockdown in CD4+T cells inhibited the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway. More importantly, the aforementioned effects of exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells on M1 macrophage polarization and endothelial permeability were reversed by PMA treatment, indicating that exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4+T cells mitigated sepsis by suppressing the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway-mediated macrophage M1 polarization and reducing endothelial permeability.

Although the RAW264.7 cells and PMVEC, which offer high culture stability and robust experimental reproducibility, was primarily used for in vitro mechanistic investigations in this study, it exhibits certain biological differences from primary macrophages and endothelial cells. In addition, our sepsis model focused on 24-hour outcomes; the long-term impact of exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4 + T cells on sepsis remained unexplored. In future studies, we will validate these findings using primary cells and extend observations to long-term sepsis progression, thereby making the conclusion of the study more convincing.

In conclusion, exosomes from DGKK-knockdown CD4⁺T cells mitigate sepsis by inhibiting the DAG/PKC/ROS pathway, which in turn inhibits M1 macrophage polarization and decreases endothelial permeability. These findings clarify the critical role of DGKK carried by CD4⁺T cell-derived exosomes in the development of sepsis, and highlight DGKK as a potential therapeutic target for sepsis management. Targeting DGKK offers translational potential via feasible strategies: small-molecule DGKK inhibitors, DGKK-silencing shRNA, and engineered exosomes, which offer targeted strategies to attenuate sepsis.
