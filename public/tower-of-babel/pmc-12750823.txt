Effectiveness of rimegepant in vestibular migraine: a prospective self-controlled cohort study
Authors: Jinyan Shao, Jingxin Fu, Mengting Zhou, Zewei Ren, Longxiang Li, Lili Gao, Yuhui Xia, Zexian Zhao, Mengna Yang, Jiahuan He, Siyuan Xue, Guoming Wang, Jiahui He, Kaiming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVestibular migraine (VM) is the second most common cause of episodic vertigo worldwide, yet effective therapeutic options remain limited. This study aimed to assess the clinical effectiveness of rimegepant in patients with VM.MethodsA total of 33 VM cases were enrolled in this multicenter, prospective self-controlled cohort study, screened from an initial pool of 106 patients. Participants were orally administered rimegepant at 75 mg every other day as a preventive therapy. Mean 4-week days of vestibular symptom or headache, along with Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6), Vertigo Symptom Scale–Short Form (VSS-SF), Activities-specific Balance Confidence Scale (ABC) scores, and hearing sensitivities were compared between pre-dosing and postdosing of rimegepant over a 4-week follow-up.ResultsAt baseline, participants exhibited a high symptom burden, with elevated DHI, VSS-SF, GAD-7, PHQ-9, and HIT-6 scores and low ABC scores. After rimegepant treatment, there were substantial reductions in monthly vestibular symptom days and headache days (differences: vestibular symptoms, -8.48; headache, -5.88). Totally 63.6% and 70.4% of the patients experienced a 50% reduction in vestibular symptom days and headache days, respectively. Notably, these improvements were already apparent at 2 weeks, indicating early onset of efficacy. Additionally, rimegepant treatment led to significant improvements in vestibular function (differences: DHI, -27.33; VSS-SF, -6.21), psychological status (differences: GAD-7, -3.33; PHQ-9, -3.33), and quality of life (differences: HIT-6, -11.27; ABC, 19.98). In the 22 participants who underwent baseline and 4-week hearing assessments, pure tone audiometry (PTA) results also indicated improvements in hearing.ConclusionsRimegepant provides relief of vestibular symptoms and improves psychological well-being and hearing in patients with VM, with efficacy evident as early as 2 weeks.Trial registrationNCT04939922.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02243-5.Keywords: Vestibular migraine, Rimegepant, Real-world, Treatment

Background

Vestibular migraine (VM) is a syndrome characterized by recurrent episodes of vestibular symptoms and represents the second most common cause of episodic vertigo worldwide [1]. The diagnosis of VM is formally defined in the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), where it is listed as Appendix A1.6.6 [2]. According to the ICHD-3 framework, VM is diagnosed based on the following four criteria [2, 3], (1) at least five episodes of moderate to severe vestibular symptoms, operationally defined by the Bárány Society [4]; (2) a current or past history of migraine as specified in the ICHD-3; (3) at least 50% of vestibular episodes accompanied by migraine features such as headache, photophobia, phonophobia, or visual aura; and (4) symptoms not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis or by other vestibular disorders. The updated Bárány Society–IHS consensus further refines these definitions and provides practical clinical guidance. Epidemiologically, VM’s lifetime prevalence is 1% in Germany [5], versus 2.7% in the United States [6]. Notably, VM cases account for up to 30% of patients seeking treatment for episodic dizziness [7]. In China, a study reported that 9.2% patients with dizziness or vertigo were diagnosed with VM, accounting for 54.1% of patients with central vestibular disorders (CVD) [8].

Despite the large number of VM patients, there is a lack of effective therapeutic drugs. Some studies suggest that VM patients may benefit from migraine treatments, including acute medications (such as gepants [9], triptans [10], and vestibular inhibitor [11]) and preventive treatments (e.g., calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) medications [12], β-blockers [13], and calcium channel blockers [14]). Currently, VM treatment relies primarily on traditional medications, which often have poor efficacy, delayed onset (4–6 weeks), and considerable adverse effects (AEs) [15]. Meanwhile, evidence supporting the efficacy of these traditional medications for VM is limited. In 2023, Cochrane Reviews concluded that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of acute-care or prophylactic medications for VM [16], with only one placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial (RCT) on prophylaxis included [17]. Other studies have shown that triptans are ineffective in VM treatment and do not improve vertigo attacks [16, 18].

Growing interest has focused on calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)–targeting therapies. Preclinical studies suggest that gepants may modulate vestibular function, reduce motion sickness–like behavior, and attenuate CGRP-related vestibular hypersensitivity [19, 20]. Clinically, evidence remains limited. A retrospective analysis by Hoskin et al. reported symptomatic improvement in VM among patients treated with anti-CGRP agents, although the cohort was small and included only two gepant-treated patients [21]. A prospective observational study by Russo et al. demonstrated improvement in both vertigo and headache frequency with CGRP monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) [22]. Importantly, the only RCT evaluating an anti-CGRP mAb in VM—the galcanezumab trial—reported numerical improvements in vestibular symptoms but did not meet statistical significance for its primary endpoint, likely due to the small sample size and high variability in clinical presentation [23]. Thus, despite promising trends, high-quality evidence remains insufficient.

Given the heterogeneity of VM, the limited number of controlled trials, and the lack of real-world data specifically evaluating gepants, additional observational studies are needed to characterize the effectiveness of anti-CGRP therapies across diverse patient populations. Furthermore, auditory symptoms—including tinnitus, aural fullness, and mild low-frequency hearing loss—are increasingly recognized in VM [24], but have rarely been included in clinical outcomes of previous studies. Rimegepant’s established efficacy in acute migraine [25] and its mechanistic relevance to both vestibular and auditory pathways make it a promising therapeutic candidate.

Besides, as a frequent cause of episodic vertigo, VM is often accompanied by headache and may also involve auditory dysfunction. Prior studies have reported mild, reversible low-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus, and aural fullness in VM patients, suggesting possible cochlear involvement. Given the shared pathophysiological mechanisms between migraine and auditory systems – such as the expression of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) in the cochlea – we hypothesize that targeting migraine mechanisms with rimegepant may not only reduce vertigo and headache frequency, but also improve hearing sensitivity. Therefore, in this study we assess the clinical effectiveness of rimegepant in VM across vestibular symptoms, headache, and auditory function.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective multicenter cohort study aimed to assess the real-world effectiveness of rimegepant in VM. The study was conducted at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (SRRSH 2025-Research-0595) and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IR2021001248). In addition, this investigation was carried out as a predefined observational cohort within the NCT04939922 registry platform. Both definite VM and probable VM (only one of the criteria (migraine history or migraine features during the episode) for vestibular migraine is fulfilled) were included, as predefined in the protocol and consistent with the Bárány Society–IHS consensus that probable VM represents the same clinical spectrum [2, 3]. All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study-related procedures. Two attending physicians confirmed the diagnosis. In accordance with the ICHD-3 appendix A1.6.6, we further documented the underlying migraine subtype.

Inclusion criteria were: diagnosis consistent with the Bárány Society criteria for VM or probable VM (pVM) [3], age ≥ 18 years, no prior use of anti-CGRP drugs. Exclusion criteria included other potential vestibular diseases, incomplete questionnaires or follow-up, pregnancy or lactation in women, unwillingness to fill e-diaries or questionnaires, and serious medical or psychiatric conditions. Ultimately, 33 participants were included. During rimegepant treatment, 7 participants were using amitriptyline, 1 was on topiramate, and the others were not on any preventive therapy. During the observational trial, the concurrent use of no more than one medication with potential migraine-prephylactic effect (e.g., β-blockers, antidepressants) is permitted, provided that the dose has remained stable for at least 3 months before the start of the observation period and will not be changed during the study.

Study outcomes

The clinical characteristics, demographics, and medical histories of all participants were recorded. A total of 33 patients were included in the analysis out of 106 VM or pVM cases. At baseline, all 33 patients completed the following assessments: Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6), Vertigo Symptom Scale–Short Form (VSS-SF), and Activities-specific Balance Confidence Scale (ABC). Patients were followed up for 4 weeks, during which they were administered rimegepant at 75 mg every other day as a preventive regimen. Follow-up visits occurred every two weeks. At each visit, the numbers of vestibular symptom days and headache days, and medications taken during the preceding 14 days were recorded. The week-2 visit may be conducted by telephone, the week-4 visit must be face-to-face, and all the scales listed above were re-administered at Week 4. The definition of vestibular symptoms referred to the criteria of the Bárány Society [3]. Hearing assessments were performed before and after treatment, and complete air and bone conduction values were recorded. Considering patients’ economic conditions, time, and willingness, only 22 patients underwent both baseline and 1month pure tone audiometry (PTA) testing. Air-conduction thresholds (0.125–8 kHz) and bone-conduction thresholds (0.25–4 kHz) were obtained using the modified Hughson-Westlake procedure. Pure tone average (0.5–4 kHz) was calculated for hearing evaluation. Adverse events (AEs) related to rimegepant were also recorded for safety assessment.

The primary outcome was reduction in mean number of vestibular symptom days over 4 weeks postdosing. Especially, the mean vestibular symptom days were defined as any calendar day on which the patient experienced at least one vestibular symptom fulfilling the Bárány Society criteria for VM. Secondary outcomes included mean number of headache days over 4 weeks postdosing, mean number of vestibular symptom days every 2 weeks, mean number of headache days every 2 weeks, and changes in assessment scale scores related to vertigo, dizziness, mood, anxiety, and hearing.

Statistical analysis

The sample size and power analyses were performed using Prism Cloud. All data were recorded in Microsoft Excel (version 16.7). Baseline variables were summarized using descriptive analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The McNemar’s test was applied for categorical data. Normality was assessed by normality and log-normality tests. For normally distributed data, paired or unpaired t-tests were used for within-group or between-group comparisons, respectively. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for paired data, and the Mann–Whitney U test for independent data. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was performed as appropriate. Cohen’s d was used to calculate effect sizes. For comparisons involving multiple variables, one-way ANOVA was conducted. Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS (version 22.0, IBM), and data visualization was performed with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant screening and clinical features in the VM cohort

A total of 106 patients with VM or pVM were initially considered for inclusion in the current study. Of these, 55 were enrolled, while 16 patients declined follow-up, 11 used other anti-CGRP drugs, and 24 continued alternative therapies. During the follow-up period, 1 patient was excluded due to the influence of other comorbidities, 11 due to improper medication use, 4 due to loss to follow-up, and 6 for unknown reasons. Ultimately, 33 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

The 33 enrolled participants had a mean age of 45.19 years, with 78.8% being female (Table 1). Before treatment, the monthly frequencies of vestibular symptoms and headache were 16.36 ± 9.29 and 10.55 ± 9.10, respectively. In addition, mean DHI and VSS-SF scores were 47.58 ± 25.67 and 14.64 ± 10.12, respectively. Mean GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores were 6.64 ± 5.67 and 7.48 ± 5.61, respectively, while HIT-6 and ABC scores were 61.27 ± 8.19 and 67.42 ± 30.76, respectively. These findings indicated that VM patients with vestibular dysfunction and headache experienced substantial psychological distress and impairment in daily life.

To investigate the early onset of efficacy, mean vestibular symptom and headache days per 2 weeks were also analyzed. The 2-week frequencies of vestibular symptoms and headache were assessed (Fig. 2B and D). During the rimegepant treatment period, both mean vestibular symptom and headache days showed significant decreases at 2 weeks (vestibular symptoms: 5.68 ± 4.38 vs. 8.18 ± 4.65, P = 0.0391; headache: 2.95 ± 3.91 vs. 5.27 ± 4.55, P = 0.0318) and at 4 weeks (vestibular symptoms: 3.21 ± 3.77 vs. 8.18 ± 4.65, P < 0.0001; headache: 2.06 ± 2.89 vs. 5.27 ± 4.55, P = 0.0020).

Rimegepant demonstrates improvements in vestibular and psychological outcomes and hearing after 4 weeks

After 4 weeks of rimegepant treatment, VM patients showed relief of vestibular symptoms and improvement in psychological well-being (Table 2). The monthly frequencies of vestibular symptoms (7.88 ± 8.01) and headache (4.67 ± 5.19) decreased significantly, with large effect sizes (vestibular symptoms: P = 0.0002, Cohen’s d = 0.98; headache: P = 0.0062, Cohen’s d = 0.79). Of these patients, 63.6% and 70.4% experienced a 50% reduction in vestibular symptoms and headache, respectively. Furthermore, DHI scores (20.24 ± 24.53), including the physical (6.42 ± 6.78), emotional (5.76 ± 8.35), and functional (8.06 ± 10.52) subdomains, were significantly improved compared with baseline, with large effect sizes (P < 0.05, Cohen’s d > 0.8). VSS-SF scores (8.42 ± 9.01), comprising the functional (dizziness and balance disorders) (7.00 ± 7.28) and emotional (autonomic symptoms) (1.42 ± 1.95) domains, also showed a significant improvement with moderate effect sizes (P < 0.05, Cohen’s d > 0.5). Similarly, GAD-7 (3.30 ± 4.63) and PHQ-9 (4.15 ± 4.49) scores demonstrated significant reductions, indicating improved psychological status (P < 0.05, Cohen’s d > 0.5). In addition, both HIT-6 (50.00 ± 10.26) and ABC (87.41 ± 16.09) scores improved significantly after treatment (P < 0.05).

Moreover, hearing assessments of air and bone conduction were performed for 22 patients using PTA (Fig. 3). In the left ear, 12 patients showed reduced air conduction and 7 displayed reduced bone conduction. In the right ear, 16 patients had decreased air conduction and 15 had reduced bone conduction. Notably, air conduction in the right ear showed a significant improvement (P = 0.0148).

Hearing assessments for left ear (A) and right ear (B) after rimegepant treatment. There were no significant differences in air and bone conduction in the left ear. In the right ear, bone conduction showed no significant difference, while air conduction significantly improved (P = 0.0148)

Collectively, these findings suggest that rimegepant provides relief of vestibular symptoms and improves psychological state in patients with VM, with an early onset of efficacy observed at 2 weeks.

Discussion

In this cohort study, rimegepant demonstrated rapid improvement in both vestibular symptoms and headache in patients with vestibular migraine (VM) and probable VM, as well as modest improvements in psychological well-being and quality of life. Notably, significant reductions in vestibular symptom days and headache days were observed as early as two weeks after treatment initiation, suggesting a potential early-onset effect of rimegepant in real-world settings.

Currently, commonly recommended drugs for VM prevention primarily aim to relieve symptoms or improve the quality of life. These mainly include tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., nortriptyline [26] and venlafaxine [27]), β-blockers (e.g., propranolol [28]), and antiepileptic drugs (topiramate and valproic acid [29]), which are known to improve vertigo and headache in VM. Bayer et al. investigated the effects of metoprolol succinate (a β-blocker) over 6 months in VM patients, reporting a reduction of 1.4 days in monthly vertigo attacks [17]. Liu et al. assessed three drugs (venlafaxine, flunarizine, and valproic acid), reporting reductions of 2.74 days (venlafaxine), 0.8 days (flunarizine), and 2.75 days (valproic acid) in vertigo attacks after 3 months of treatment [30]. In our cohort, rimegepant reduced monthly vestibular system days by 8.48 within only one month, highlighting a potentially faster onset of clinical improvement. Small studies have reported favorable outcomes of rimegepant and galcanezumab in VM or concurrent VM with Ménière’s disease [23, 31], consistent with our findings that CGRP-targeted therapies may improve vestibular symptoms.

Traditional preventive medications are not specifically designed for VM and have a slow onset of action, moreover, their benefits are often inconsistent. For instance, a meta-analysis of 828 VM patients reported significant effects for valproic acid, propranolol, and venlafaxine [32], whereas other studies noted limited efficacy and tolerability [33]. Adverse events (AEs) for these agents are also common. Nortriptyline may cause dry mouth, constipation, orthostatic hypotension, or confusion; venlafaxine can increase blood pressure and worsen headaches; propranolol should be used cautiously in patients with cardiovascular disease or asthma; topiramate is associated with cognitive impairment and sensory disturbances; and valproic acid may induce weight gain or tremors. In our cohort, no moderate or severe drug-related adverse events occurred, and all mild reactions resolved without intervention, highlighting the favorable tolerability of rimegepant in the short-term observational period.

Significant improvements were observed in clinical scales including the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) and Vertigo Symptom Scale–Short Form (VSS-SF) [34, 35], reflecting meaningful reductions in vestibular symptom burden. Psychological assessments using GAD-7 and PHQ-9 also improved [36, 37], suggesting concurrent benefits for anxiety and depression, which are commonly reported in VM patients [38]. Improvements in quality of life were noted in HIT-6 and Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) scores [39, 40]. While effect sizes for VSS-SF, GAD-7, and PHQ-9 were moderate, this may reflect the presence of long-standing psychological comorbidities in a subset of participants.

Hearing assessments using pure-tone audiometry (PTA) showed modest improvements in right-ear air-conduction thresholds, but no significant changes were observed in left-ear air conduction or bilateral bone conduction. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the observational design precludes attribution of changes to rimegepant without a control for confounders [19, 41–44]. Mechanistic explanations remain speculative, and further studies with appropriate controls are warranted.

This study had several limitations. First, it had a single-arm design in which efficacy was assessed through self-comparison before and after treatment. Future trials should include placebo-controlled groups to strengthen validity. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and all participants were Chinese. Studies with larger cohorts including different races and regions are warranted to better generalize the therapeutic effects of rimegepant. In addition, some specific scales for VM, such as Vestibular Migraine Patient Assessment Tool and Handicap Inventory (VM-PATHI) [45] could be included in future researches. Finally, this investigation focused on a single CGRP antagonist. Comparative studies including traditional agents (e.g., SSRIs, valproic acid, propranolol) and anti-CGRP mAbs are required to fully delineate relative efficacy and safety profiles.

Conclusions

In conclusion, rimegepant provides meaningful relief of vestibular symptoms and improves psychological well-being in patients with VM, with clinical benefits evident as early as 2 weeks. However, these findings are preliminary, and further controlled studies are necessary to confirm these results and to establish the long-term safety and effectiveness of CGRP-targeted therapies in VM.
