Blowups of hypersurfaces
Matthew Weaver

Abstract
. A classical result of Micali [ 18 ] asserts that a Noetherian local ring is regular if and only if the Rees algebra of its maximal ideal is defined by an ideal of linear forms. In this case, this defining ideal may be realized as a determinantal ideal of generic height, and so the Rees ring is easily resolved by the Eagon–Northcott complex, providing a wealth of information. If R R is a non-regular local ring, it is interesting to ask how far the Rees ring of its maximal ideal strays from this form, and whether any homological data can be recovered. In this paper, we answer this question for hypersurface rings, and provide a minimal generating set for the defining ideal of the Rees ring. Furthermore, we determine the Cohen–Macaulayness of this algebra, along with several other invariants.



1.
Introduction

For an ideal
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
of a Noetherian ring
R
, recall that the
Rees algebra
of
I
is the graded subring
\mathcal{R}(I)=R[f_{1}t,\ldots,f_{n}t]
of
R[t]
, for
t
an indeterminate. As a graded subalgebra, we note that
\mathcal{R}(I)=R\oplus It\oplus I^{2}t^{2}\oplus\cdots
, hence this ring encodes the data on all powers of
I
. As such, the Rees algebra has proven to be an essential tool for algebraists within the study of multiplicities and reductions. In addition to this algebraic perspective, we note that there is also significant motivation arising within algebraic geometry. Indeed,
\mathcal{R}(I)
is commonly called the
blowup algebra
, as
\mathop{\rm Proj}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is precisely the blowup of
\mathop{\rm Spec}(R)
along the subscheme
V(I)
. Hence the Rees ring is of great importance to geometers within the study of resolutions of singularities and questions related to birationality.

Whereas the above definition of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is simple to state, this description offers little insight into the structure of this algebra, or its relevant properties. To remedy this issue, one hopes to
implicitize
this algebra, which correlates to writing
\mathcal{R}(I)
as a quotient of a polynomial ring over
R
in new indeterminates. More specifically, in the setting above, there is a natural epimorphism
\Psi:R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
given by
T_{i}\mapsto f_{i}t
. This of course induces an isomorphism
\mathcal{R}(I)\cong R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/\mathcal{J}
where
\mathcal{J}=\ker\Psi
is the
defining ideal
of
\mathcal{R}(I)
. With this description, it suffices to produce a generating set of
\mathcal{J}
, the
defining equations
of
\mathcal{R}(I)
, to determine the algebraic structure of the Rees ring, which often reveals many of its properties.

Determining the generators of
\mathcal{J}
has been called the
implicitization problem
for Rees rings, and has been well studied, however a full set of defining equations of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is known in very few settings. There has been some success for ideals with low codimension, namely codimension two
[
2
,
5
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
30
]
and codimension three
[
16
,
19
,
31
]
, under various assumptions. The difficulty lies in that
\mathcal{J}
consists of the polynomial relations amongst the generators
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
of
I
. As such,
\mathcal{J}
encodes the
syzygies
on every power of
I
. Thus one is often limited to the study of ideals with known resolutions and accessible syzygies. In particular, if the syzygies of
I
are as simple as possible, namely Koszul, the Rees algebra has a particularly simple description.

If
I=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
an
R
-regular sequence, it is well known that
\mathcal{R}(I)\cong R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I_{2}(\psi)
, for

(1.1)

\psi=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{n}\\
T_{1}&\cdots&T_{n}\end{bmatrix}

and where
I_{2}(\psi)
denotes its ideal of
2\times 2
minors
[
18
, Chap. I: Thm. 1, Lem. 2]
. In particular, the Rees ring of the maximal ideal of any regular local ring is of this form. Interestingly, the converse to this statement holds as well.

Theorem

(Micali
[
18
, Chap. I, Thm. 2]
)
.

Let
R
be a Noetherian local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Then
R
is a regular local ring if and only if
\mathfrak{m}
is of linear type, i.e.
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\cong\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{m})
, where
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{m})
denotes the symmetric algebra of
\mathfrak{m}
. In this case, we have
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\cong R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I_{2}(\psi)
.

With this, it is curious as to how far the defining ideal of the Rees ring differs from the shape above when
R
is not a regular local ring. In the present article, we consider a hypersurface ring
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(f)
, for
k
a field and
f
a homogeneous polynomial, and consider the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
, for
\mathfrak{m}
the homogeneous maximal
R
-ideal. Whereas this setting may seem rather mild, we note that much of the previous literature on equations of Rees rings pertains specifically to ideals in a polynomial ring
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, with few exceptions
[
30
,
31
]
. As the Rees ring is the algebraic realization of the blowup, there is significant geometric motivation here, as different rings must be taken to account for different blowups. In particular, here the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
correlates to the blowup of the hypersurface
\mathop{\rm Spec}(R)\cong V(f)
at the point
\{\mathfrak{m}\}
.

From the theorem above, the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
will fail to have the previous shape, and the containment
I_{2}(\psi)\subseteq\mathcal{J}
is strict. However, we show that the defining ideal is not too distant from this form, and differs by a
downgraded sequence
of polynomials obtained from a recursive algorithm, akin to the methods used in
[
10
,
23
]
.
The main results of this article,
5.1
and
5.6
, are reformulated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
with
n\geq 2
for
k
a field. Let

f\in S
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 1
, and let
R=S/(f)
with homogeneous maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=\overline{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})}
, where
\overline{\,\cdot\,}
denotes images modulo
(f)
. The Rees algebra of
\mathfrak{m}
is
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\cong R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/\mathcal{J}
where

\mathcal{J}=\overline{I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})}

where
\psi
is as in (

), and

is a downgraded sequence of polynomials associated to

. Additionally,
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
is almost Cohen–Macaulay and is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if

.

The proof of the statement above relies on the so-called

of Rees rings, as introduced in
[
15
]
. We approximate, in a sense, the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
by a simpler algebra and compare these two rings, and their defining ideals. Then an adaptation of the method of
downgraded sequences
, as used in
[
10
,
23
]
, is employed and it is shown that this recursive algorithm yields a generating set of the defining ideal. With this description of the defining ideal, we describe the Cohen–Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
and other homological invariants.

We now describe how this paper is organized. In
Section

2
we begin the study of the Rees algebra of the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
in a hypersurface ring
R
. In particular, we introduce a description of the defining ideal as a particular colon ideal, which will be used throughout the later sections. In
Section

3
we map onto the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
by an algebra defined by
I_{2}(\psi)
, and compare certain properties between these rings. In
Section

4
we introduce a recursive algorithm beginning from the hypersurface equation
f
defining
R
. This algorithm produces equations of the defining ideal, and we give a criterion for when this process yields a full generating set. In
Section

5
it is shown that this criterion satisfied and that this algorithm produces a
minimal
generating set of
\mathcal{J}
. Lastly, properties of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
such as relation type, Cohen–Macaulayness, and regularity are studied.

2.
Ideals in hypersurface rings

We now begin our study of the Rees algebra of the maximal ideal of a hypersurface ring. The objective of this section is to introduce the defining ideal and provide a tangible description, which will be used in the proceeding sections. Our setting for the duration of the paper is the following.

Setting 2.1
.

Let
S=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
for
k
a field and
n\geq 2
, and let
\mathfrak{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Let

f\in S
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 1
, and let
R=S/(f)
with homogeneous maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=\overline{\mathfrak{n}}
, where
\overline{\,\cdot\,}
denotes images modulo
(f)
.

We note that the assumption that
n\geq 2
ensures that
\mathfrak{m}
is an ideal of positive grade, which will be crucial to our arguments. For instance, this ensures that
\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})=n

[
28
, 1.2.4]
. Additionally, we note that for most purposes, one can assume that
d\geq 2
to avoid reducing to the setting of a polynomial ring. However, we proceed more generally and make no such assumption here.

Before we begin our study of the Rees algebra, we start by producing a free presentation of the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
. We note that a free resolution of
\mathfrak{m}
, infinite of course, is available from either Shamash
[
24
]
or Tate
[
26
]
. However, as we only require a free presentation, we offer a short argument making use of the mapping cone construction
[
8
, §A3.12]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

With respect to the generating set
\{\overline{x_{1}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}}\}
, the ideal
\mathfrak{m}
may be presented by

R^{\binom{n}{2}+1}\overset{\overline{\varphi}}{\longrightarrow}R^{n}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow 0

where
\varphi=[\delta_{1}(\underline{x})\,|\,\partial f]
is the matrix with
\delta_{1}(\underline{x})
the first Koszul differential on
\underline{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and
\partial f
is any column with homogeneous entries, of the same degree, such that
[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f=f
.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow(f)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{n}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{m}\longrightarrow 0.

The
S
-ideal
\mathfrak{n}
is resolved by a truncation of the Koszul complex
\mathcal{K}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}(\underline{x})
on
\underline{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and the ideal
(f)
is resolved by
F_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}\,:0\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow(f)\longrightarrow 0
, where the differential is multiplication by
f
. Extending
F_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}
by adding zeros, we note that there is a morphism of complexes
u_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}\,:F_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}(\underline{x})
with
u_{0}=\partial f
and
u_{i}=0
for
i\geq 1
. With this, the mapping cone construction gives an
S
-resolution of
\mathfrak{m}
, and in particular we have a free
S
-presentation

S^{\binom{n}{2}+1}\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow}S^{n}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow 0

with
\varphi
as above. Tensoring by
R
then yields the claimed free
R
-presentation.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

We note that there are many choices for
\partial f
and, as the notation suggests, there is a choice involving differentials. If
\mathop{\rm char}k=0
, so that
d=\deg f
is a unit, one may use the Euler formula

d\cdot f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\cdot x_{i}

and choose
\partial f
to consist of the partial derivatives of
f
. Regardless, any choice of
\partial f
will do for our purposes. We will revisit this construction in
Section

4
and apply it more thoroughly.

With a free presentation of
\mathfrak{m}
, we may now begin the study of the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
. The importance of a free presentation is that it allows one to introduce the symmetric algebra
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{m})
, noting that there is a natural epimorphism
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{m})\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
. Indeed, as a quotient of

,
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{m})
is defined by the ideal of entries of the matrix product
[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\overline{\varphi}
.

As noted in the introduction, one often aims to write
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
as a quotient of
R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
as well. However, it will be more accessible to describe
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
as a quotient of a polynomial ring over
S
first, similar to the observations made in
[
30
,
31
]
. To allow for this, we briefly introduce some notation and conventions, differing slightly from those in the previous section. Consider the sequence of maps

(2.1)

S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]\longrightarrow R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]\longrightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})

where the first map quotients by
(f)
, and the second is the natural map as described in the introduction. As
S
is the polynomial ring of
2.1
, it will be more convenient to work over

. We adopt the bigrading on this ring given by
\deg x_{i}=(1,0)
and
\deg T_{i}=(0,1)
throughout.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
denote the kernel of the composition of the maps in (
2.1
). Moreover, we define the
S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
-ideal
\mathcal{L}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f,f_{1})

\psi

(2.2)

\psi=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\ldots&x_{n}\\
T_{1}&\ldots&T_{n}\end{bmatrix}

is the bihomogeneous polynomial

We will refer to

Proposition 2.5
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
, and
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{L}
the

-ideals above, we have
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{n}^{\infty}=(I_{2}(\psi)+(f)):\mathfrak{n}^{\infty}

Proof.

As mentioned,
\overline{\mathcal{J}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
are the ideals defining
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{m})
as quotients of
R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
, respectively. As
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{J}}

is an ideal of positive grade (hence

\overline{\mathcal{L}}:\mathfrak{m}^{\infty}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{J}}

is of linear type locally for any

\overline{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{L}}:\mathfrak{m}^{\infty}

For the second equality, we first note that

. Thus we have

\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{n}^{s}\subseteq(I_{2}(\psi)+(f)):\mathfrak{n}^{s+1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{n}^{s+1}

∎

Whereas the saturation
\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{n}^{\infty}
may seem more natural, the description

Proposition 2.6
.

Writing
B=S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
, the ring
B/(I_{2}(\psi)+(f))
has a minimal bigraded free
B
-resolution

0\rightarrow F_{n}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow F_{1}\rightarrow F_{0}

where

F_{n}=B^{n-1}(-d-n+1,-n+1),\quad F_{i}=\begin{array}[]{c}B^{\binom{n}{i}(i-1)}(-d-i+1,-i+1)\\
\oplus\\
B^{\binom{n}{i+1}i}(-i,-i)\end{array},\quad F_{1}=\begin{array}[]{c}B(-d,0)\\
\oplus\\
B^{\binom{n}{2}}(-1,-1)\end{array},\quad F_{0}=B

for
2\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proof.

As
\psi
is a matrix of indeterminates, it is well known that
I_{2}(\psi)
is a prime

-ideal
[
4
, 2.10]
. Additionally, we note that

Moreover, we have that

is resolved by the bigraded Eagon–Northcott complex

, A2.10]

Since

is prime of height

, we have
\mathop{\rm ht}(I_{2}(\psi)+(f))=n

∎

\mathcal{J}=(I_{2}(\psi)+(f)):\mathfrak{n}^{\infty}

]

denote the homogeneous maximal

Proposition 2.7
.

With the assumptions of

\mathcal{J}=(I_{2}(\psi)+(f)):\mathfrak{n}^{d}

Proof.

Writing

Approximations of Rees rings

Now that the description

\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})

S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
-ideal
\mathcal{K}=I_{2}(\psi)+(x_{n},T_{n})
.

The
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
will inevitably pave the path to a generating set of the defining ideal. For the duration of this section, we explore the various properties of this ideal and its relation to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
, the latter being the kernel of the induced map
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is a Cohen–Macaulay domain of dimension
n+1
. The
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideals
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
are Cohen–Macaulay of height one. Moreover,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
is a prime ideal.

Proof.

The ring
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is easily seen to be a domain of dimension
n+1
, as
S
is a domain of dimension
n
. Moreover, the Cohen–Macaulayness follows as
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is defined by an ideal of minors of generic height
[
8
, A2.13]
. For the statements regarding
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
, notice this ideal defines the special fiber ring
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})/\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
. As
\mathfrak{n}
is an ideal of linear type, it follows that
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
, hence
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
is a prime Cohen–Macaulay ideal of height one. For the statement on
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
, notice that

(3.2)

\mathcal{K}=I_{2}(\psi)+(x_{n},T_{n})=I_{2}\left(\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{n-1}\\
T_{1}&\cdots&T_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\right)+(x_{n},T_{n})

which is easily seen to be Cohen–Macaulay of height
(n-2)+2=n
. Thus modulo
I_{2}(\psi)
, we have that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is Cohen–Macaulay of height one.
∎

Alternatively, the Cohen–Macaulayness of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
can be seen from
[
8
, A2.13, A2.14]
, but we opt for the simpler proof above. However, we will use these tools later in
Section

5
to describe the Cohen–Macaulayness of particular powers of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
. With the properties of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
established in

, we now show that the symbolic powers of these ideals are
linked

[
13
]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

With the
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideals
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
above, we have the following.

(a)

\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
,

(b)

(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}):\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}=\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
,

(c)

(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}):\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
,

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of
[
2
, 3.9]
throughout.

(a)

Temporarily regrading
S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
with
\deg x_{i}=1
and
\deg T_{i}=0
, we see that
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}})\cong\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
, where
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}})
is the associated graded ring of the ideal
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
. Since
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is a domain, the first claim follows.

(b)

In
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
we have
\widetilde{x_{i}}\widetilde{T_{n}}=\widetilde{x_{n}}\widetilde{T_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Thus it follows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}})
, and so
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{i}\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{i}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i})
for any
i\geq 1
. Now localizing at prime ideals of height one and contracting, we see that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i})
. With this we have
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}):\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
, hence we need only show the reverse containment. Since
\widetilde{T_{n}}\notin\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
, we note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\nsubseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
. As
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
is the unique associated prime of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
, it follows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
have no associated primes in common. With this, and noting that
\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
, it follows that
(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}):\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
as well.

(c)

As noted in the proof of (b), there is a containment
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i})
, and so we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}):\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
. Again noting that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
have no associated primes in common, and that
\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
, it then follows that
(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}):\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
.∎

Items (b) and (c) of

show that the ideals
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{(i)}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
are
linked
through the complete intersection
(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i})
. Moreover, since they have no common associated prime, they are said to be

linked.

Corollary 3.4
.

The ideal
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is generically a complete intersection. Moreover,
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is a strongly Cohen–Macaulay
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal.

Proof.

The first statement is a consequence of geometric linkage. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
share no associated prime, by
3.3
we have that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}:\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}
for any associated prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
. As such,
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is generically a complete intersection.

For the second statement, recall that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is Cohen–Macaulay of height one by
3.2
. Moreover, since
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})
, it is an almost complete intersection ideal. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is also generically a complete intersection, it follows from

, 2.2]

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}

∎

We now give a description of the ideal
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
, noting that this is the kernel of the induced map
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
in (
3.1
). Consider the divisorial ideal
\mathcal{D}=\frac{\widetilde{f}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}
, and note that this is actually an

, since

Theorem 3.5
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{D}.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
. Recall that
\mathcal{J}=(I_{2}(\psi)+(f)):\mathfrak{n}^{d}
by
2.7
, hence we need only show that
\mathcal{D}\cdot\widetilde{a}\subseteq(\widetilde{f})
for any
a\in\mathfrak{n}^{d}
. However, this is clear since

\mathcal{D}\cdot\widetilde{a}=\frac{\widetilde{f}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}\cdot\widetilde{a}=\frac{\widetilde{a}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}\cdot\widetilde{f}\subseteq(\widetilde{f}),

noting that
\tfrac{\widetilde{a}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}
is an
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal by
3.3
.

With the containment
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
, it is enough to show equality locally at the associated primes of
\mathcal{D}
. Notice that
\mathcal{D}\cong\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}
, and the latter is unmixed of height one by
3.3
and
[
13
, 0.1]
. As such,
\mathcal{D}
is also an unmixed
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal of height one, and so it is enough to show that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathfrak{p}}
for any prime
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathfrak{p}=1
.
Recall from
3.2
that
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
is such a prime ideal of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
. If
\mathfrak{p}\neq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
, we note that
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{d}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Thus by
3.3
, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d})_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and so
\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{f})_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Similarly, by
2.7
we see that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{f})_{\mathfrak{p}}
as well.

Thus the proof will be complete once it has been shown that
\mathcal{D}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
agree locally at
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
. We first claim that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\nsubseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
, and so
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
. Indeed, from the isomorphism
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\cong\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})/\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
, it follows that the fiber ring of
\mathfrak{m}
is
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})\cong\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})/(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}+\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}})
, which has dimension at most
\mathop{\rm dim}R=n-1

[

, 5.1.4]
. However, recall that
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})/\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\nsubseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
.

Thus we must show that
\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
is the unit ideal as well. Recall from the proof of

that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\nsubseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
, hence
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
. With this, by
3.3
we have
(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}:\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{d}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
. Thus
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{d}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}\subseteq(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
and so
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{d}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
, as the reverse containment is clear. Similarly, by

and recalling that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
, we have
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{d}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}\subseteq(\widetilde{f})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}
and so

as

. Now since

\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}=\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})_{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}}

∎

4.
Downgraded sequences

In this section we introduce a recursive algorithm that yields equations of the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
. This process is an adaptation of the methods used in
[
10
,
23
]
, beginning with an initial polynomial and producing a new
downgraded
polynomial at each step. We also note that similar recursive processes have been previously employed to produce equations of Rees rings in
[
2
,
7
,
30
,
31
]
. Before we introduce this algorithm, we recall a bit of earlier notation, which will be used throughout.

Notation 4.1
.

For any bihomogeneous polynomial
g\in S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
, write
\partial g
to denote a column of bihomogeneous elements, of the same bidegree, such that

[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial g=g.

As a convention, we set
\partial g=0
if
g=0
.

This of course agrees with the convention used in
2.2
, now extended to
S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
. Similar to the observation made in
2.3
, the column
\partial g
is not unique, and there are often many choices. Despite this non-uniqueness, we will soon see that the ideals obtained from the following process are well defined.

Definition 4.2
.

With the notation above and the assumptions of
2.1
, we produce a sequence of polynomials and ideals recursively, as follows. Set
f_{0}=f
and
\mathcal{J}_{0}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0})
. For
i\leq d=\deg f
suppose that
(\mathcal{J}_{0},f_{0}),\ldots,(\mathcal{J}_{i-1},f_{i-1})
have been constructed. To construct the
i
th pair, set
f_{i}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}
and let
\mathcal{J}_{i}=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(f_{i})
. We call
f_{0},\ldots,f_{i}
the
i
th
downgraded sequence
of
f
.

Notice that at each step, the
i
th ideal is
\mathcal{J}_{i}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0},\ldots,f_{i})
. From the construction of
\partial f_{i}
, it follows that the degree, with respect to
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, decreases by one at each step, hence the terminology of
downgraded
sequence. We formalize this as follows.

Proposition 4.3
.

A downgraded sequence of
f
consists of nonzero polynomials. That is, for
0\leq i\leq d
we have
f_{i}\neq 0
. Moreover, each
f_{i}
is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(d-i,i)
.

Proof.

As the construction above is recursive, we prove the first statement on the nonvanishing by induction. The second statement then follows immediately from the first by construction. Recall that
f
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 1
in
S
, hence
f=f_{0}\neq 0
. Now suppose that
i\geq 1
, and that
f_{i}=0
for some
i
, and we may take
i
to be minimal, in the sense that
f_{j}\neq 0
for
j<i
. Now since
f_{i}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}
and
f_{i}=0
, it follows that
\partial f_{i-1}
is a syzygy on
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
. As this is a regular sequence,
\partial f_{i-1}
is then contained in the span of its Koszul syzygies. Since
f_{i-1}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}
, it then follows that
f_{i-1}\in I_{2}(\psi)
.

If
i=1
, this is a contradiction as
f_{0}=f
is nonzero of bidegree
(d,0)
and
I_{2}(\psi)
is generated in bidegree
(1,1)
, hence we must have
i\geq 2
here. Thus
f_{i-1}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-2}
and since
f_{i-1}\in I_{2}(\psi)
, it follows that
\partial f_{i-2}
is contained in the span of Koszul syzygies on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. However, then
f_{i-2}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-2}=0
which is a contradiction as
i
was taken to be smallest.
∎

As noted, there are many choices for
\partial f_{i}
within each step of the process described above. Nevertheless, the ideals constructed from downgraded sequences are unique.

Proposition 4.4
.

The ideals
\mathcal{J}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{d}
are well defined, and depend only on
f
.

Proof.

We proceed inductively, noting that the ideal
\mathcal{J}_{0}
is certainly well defined. Now suppose that
1\leq i\leq d
and the ideals
\mathcal{J}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{i-1}
are well defined. Letting
\partial f_{i-1}
and
\partial f_{i-1}^{\prime}
denote two columns with

[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}=f_{i-1}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}^{\prime}

we must show that
\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(f_{i})=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(f_{i}^{\prime})
, where
f_{i}
and
f_{i}^{\prime}
are obtained as in
4.2
.

Notice from the expression above that
\partial f_{i-1}-\partial f_{i-1}^{\prime}
is a syzygy on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. As this is a regular sequence,
\partial f_{i-1}
and
\partial f_{i-1}^{\prime}
must differ by an element in the span of Koszul syzygies on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Hence
f_{i}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}
and
f_{i}^{\prime}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}^{\prime}
differ by an element of
I_{2}(\psi)
. As
I_{2}(\psi)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{i-1}
, it is clear that
\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(f_{i})=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(f_{i}^{\prime})
, and the claim follows.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

We note that employing the process of
4.2
is relatively simple in practice. Since
4.4
shows the ideal does not depend on the choice of
\partial f_{i}
, the method of downgraded sequences may be taken as an
exchange
process. Namely, for every monomial in the support of
f=f_{0}
, one need only exchange exactly one of the
x_{i}
for the corresponding
T_{i}
in order to produce
f_{1}
. One then moves onto
f_{1}
and repeats the process until there is no
x_{i}
to exchange, i.e. once one reaches
f_{d}
.

For the convenience of the reader, we offer a handful of examples to illustrate the simplicity of the algorithm above, and how there are multiple choices at each step, yet the resulting ideal is the same.

Example 4.6
.

(a)

The method of downgraded sequences is simple when
f
is a monomial, following the remark above. In particular, if
f
is a pure power, say
f=x_{n}^{d}
, notice that
f_{1}=x_{n}^{d-1}T_{n},\,f_{2}=x_{n}^{d-2}T_{n}^{2},\ldots,f_{d}=T_{n}^{d}
. The ideal of the full downgraded sequence is
\mathcal{J}_{d}=I_{2}(\psi)+(x_{n},T_{n})^{d}
in this case.

(b)

As a more interesting example, let
S=k[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
and
f=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}^{2}
. The downgraded sequence of
f
may be taken as

\begin{array}[]{cccc}f_{0}=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}^{2},&f_{1}=x_{1}x_{2}T_{1}+x_{3}^{2}T_{1},&f_{2}=x_{2}T_{1}^{2}+x_{3}T_{1}T_{3},&f_{3}=T_{1}^{2}T_{2}+T_{1}T_{3}^{2}.\end{array}

Alternatively, one may make different choices and take the downgraded sequence to be

\begin{array}[]{cccc}f_{0}^{\prime}=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}^{2},&f_{1}^{\prime}=x_{1}^{2}T_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}T_{3},&f_{2}^{\prime}=x_{1}T_{1}T_{2}+x_{3}T_{1}T_{3},&f_{3}^{\prime}=T_{1}^{2}T_{2}+T_{1}T_{3}^{2}.\end{array}

Despite there being multiple choices, it follows from
4.4
that
I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime},f_{3}^{\prime})
where

\psi=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}\\
T_{1}&T_{2}&T_{3}\end{bmatrix}

and this is also easily verified by hand or a computer algebra system, such as
Macaulay2

[
9
]
.

As can be seen in the examples above, there is a symmetry between
f=f_{0}
and
f_{d}
. Indeed, the latter is precisely the former’s counterpart in
k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
. Following the remark above, it is clear that this happens from the viewpoint of an exchange process.

Remark 4.7
.

For
0\leq i\leq d
, we have
\mathcal{J}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction once more. The claim is clear when
i=0
by
2.7
, so suppose
1\leq i\leq d
and that
\mathcal{J}_{i-1}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0},\ldots,f_{i-1})\subseteq\mathcal{J}
. We claim that
f_{i}\in\mathcal{J}_{i-1}:\mathfrak{n}
, which we show by creating an expression similar to (
2.3
). Indeed, from the definition of
f_{i}
and
\partial f_{i-1}
, for any
x_{j}
we have

(4.1)

x_{j}f_{i}=[x_{j}T_{1}\ldots x_{j}T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}=[x_{1}T_{j}\ldots x_{n}T_{j}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}+[(x_{j}T_{1}-x_{1}T_{j})\ldots(x_{j}T_{n}-x_{n}T_{j})]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}.

The first summand is exactly
T_{j}f_{i-1}\in\mathcal{J}_{i-1}
and the second summand is contained in
I_{2}(\psi)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{i-1}
, hence
f_{i}\in\mathcal{J}_{i-1}:\mathfrak{n}
. Since
\mathcal{J}_{i}=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(f_{i})
, we have that
\mathcal{J}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{i-1}:\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathcal{J}:\mathfrak{n}=\mathcal{J}
, where the second containment follows from the induction hypothesis and the equality holds since
\mathcal{J}
is saturated by
2.5
.
∎

Now that it has been shown that
\mathcal{J}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
, recall from
3.5
that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{\widetilde{f}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}
. We show that there is a similar description for
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}_{d}}
.

Theorem 4.8
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
and
\mathcal{J}_{d}
the ideal of the full downgraded sequence of
4.2
, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}_{d}}=\frac{\widetilde{f}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})
, hence we must show that
\widetilde{f_{0}}(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})^{d}=\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}(\widetilde{f_{0}},\ldots,\widetilde{f_{d}})
. In particular, it suffices to show that
\widetilde{f_{0}}\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d-i}\widetilde{T_{n}}^{i}=\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}\widetilde{f_{i}}
or rather, since
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is a domain, that
\widetilde{f_{0}}\widetilde{T_{n}}^{i}=\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}\widetilde{f_{i}}
for
0\leq i\leq d
.
There is nothing to be shown for the case
i=0
, hence we take
i=1
to be the initial case, and proceed by induction. Recall that
f_{0}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{0}
and
f_{1}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{0}
. Thus modulo
I_{2}(\psi)
, we have

\widetilde{f_{0}}\widetilde{T_{n}}=[\widetilde{T_{n}}\widetilde{x_{1}}\ldots\widetilde{T_{n}}\widetilde{x_{n}}]\cdot\widetilde{\partial f_{0}}=[\widetilde{T_{1}}\widetilde{x_{n}}\ldots\widetilde{T_{n}}\widetilde{x_{n}}]\cdot\widetilde{\partial f_{0}}=\widetilde{x_{n}}\widetilde{f_{1}}

and the initial claim follows.

If
d=1
we are finished, so suppose that
2\leq i\leq d
and the claim holds up to
i-1
. In order to show that
\widetilde{f_{0}}\widetilde{T_{n}}^{i}=\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i}\widetilde{f_{i}}
, we first note that, from the induction hypothesis, we have

\widetilde{f_{0}}\widetilde{T_{n}}^{i}=\widetilde{f_{0}}\widetilde{T_{n}}^{i-1}\cdot\widetilde{T_{n}}=\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i-1}\widetilde{f_{i-1}}\cdot\widetilde{T_{n}}=\widetilde{x_{n}}^{i-1}\cdot\widetilde{f_{i-1}}\widetilde{T_{n}}.

Hence we will be finished once it has been shown that
\widetilde{f_{i-1}}\widetilde{T_{n}}=\widetilde{x_{n}}\widetilde{f_{i}}
, which follows exactly as before. Indeed, since

f_{i-1}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}
and
f_{i}=[T_{1}\ldots T_{n}]\cdot\partial f_{i-1}
, modulo
I_{2}(\psi)
we have

\widetilde{f_{i-1}}\widetilde{T_{n}}=[\widetilde{T_{n}}\widetilde{x_{1}}\ldots\widetilde{T_{n}}\widetilde{x_{n}}]\cdot\widetilde{\partial f}_{i-1}=[\widetilde{T_{1}}\widetilde{x_{n}}\ldots\widetilde{T_{n}}\widetilde{x_{n}}]\cdot\widetilde{\partial f}_{i-1}=\widetilde{x_{n}}\widetilde{f_{i}}

which completes the proof.
∎

With the description of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}_{d}}
above, we now give a criterion for when the algorithm of downgraded sequences yields a generating set of the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
.

Corollary 4.9
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
,
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}_{d}
in
S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
if and only if
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}
in
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
.

Proof.

This follows from
4.7
,
3.5
, and
4.8
.
∎

5.
Defining Ideal

In
4.9
, a condition was given for when the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
coincides with the ideal
\mathcal{J}_{d}
of the full downgraded sequence from
4.2
. We begin this section by showing that this condition is satisfied. We then devote the remainder of the section to exploring various properties of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
, such as relation type, Cohen–Macaulayness, and Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity.

Theorem 5.1
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
, the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
, as a quotient of
S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
, is
\mathcal{J}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d})
.

Proof.

As noted in
4.9
, it suffices to show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(d)}
. Recall from
3.4
that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})
is a strongly Cohen–Macaulay ideal of height one, and is also generically a complete intersection. Thus by
[
25
, 3.4]
, it suffices to show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a principal ideal, for any prime
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathfrak{p}=2
.

First note that if
\mathfrak{p}\nsupseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
, the proof of
3.5
shows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Hence we may assume that
\mathfrak{p}\supseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
. In this case, there must exist a
\widetilde{T_{i}}
such that
\widetilde{T_{i}}\notin\mathfrak{p}
. Indeed, otherwise there is a containment
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})^{\widetilde{\,\,}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. However, this is impossible as the former ideal has height
2n-(n-1)=n+1\geq 3
(recall that
n\geq 2
) and
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathfrak{p}=2
. For any such
\widetilde{T_{i}}\notin\mathfrak{p}
, we note that
\widetilde{T_{i}}
is then a unit locally at
\mathfrak{p}
. Thus from the equality
\widetilde{x_{i}}\widetilde{T_{n}}=\widetilde{x_{n}}\widetilde{T_{i}}
modulo
I_{2}(\psi)
, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\widetilde{T_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and the claim follows.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

If
d\geq 2
, the ideal
\mathcal{J}
is minimally generated as
\mathcal{J}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d})
. The defining ideal of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
, as a quotient of
R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
is then minimally generated as
\overline{\mathcal{J}}=\overline{I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the first statement, as the second follows immediately as
f
is then part of a minimal generating set of
\mathcal{J}
. Writing
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\binom{n}{2}$}}
to denote the minors of
\psi
, we note that these polynomials have bidegree
(1,1)
. Moreover, recall from
4.3
that
\mathop{\rm bideg}f_{i}=(d-i,i)
for
0\leq i\leq d
. With this, we show that
\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\binom{n}{2}$}},f_{0},\ldots,f_{d}\}
is a minimal generating set of
\mathcal{J}
, from bidegree considerations.

We first show that it is impossible for any
\ell_{i}
to be a non-minimal generator. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\ell_{1}
is non-minimal and can hence be written in terms of the remaining generators. If
d\geq 3
, then from bidegree considerations and noting that either
d-i>1
or
i>1
, it follows that
\ell_{1}\in(\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\binom{n}{2}$}})
, which is impossible by
[
8
, A2.12]
. If
d=2
, then noting that
\mathop{\rm bideg}f_{1}=(1,1)
in this case and considering all other bidegrees, it follows that
\ell_{1}
may be written in terms of
\{\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\binom{n}{2}$}},f_{1}\}
.
Since each polynomial here has bidegree
(1,1)
, such an expression could be rearranged to show that
f_{1}\in I_{2}(\psi)
. However, this is impossible as then
\partial f_{1}
is a syzygy on
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
and so
f_{2}=f_{d}=0
which is a contradiction to
4.3
.

Now that
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\binom{n}{2}$}}
have been shown to be minimal generators of
\mathcal{J}
, we show the same for
f_{0},\ldots,f_{d}
. Note that
\mathop{\rm bideg}f_{0}=(d,0)
and
\mathop{\rm bideg}f_{d}=(0,d)
, hence from bidegree considerations it is clear that these polynomials are minimal generators of
\mathcal{J}
. Now suppose that some
f_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq d-1
, is non-minimal and can be written in terms of the remaining generators. However, with
4.3
and considering both components in the bigrading it follows that
f_{j}\in I_{2}(\psi)
. As before, this is impossible as
j\leq d-1
and the subsequent equations would then vanish.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Although the case
d=1
is excluded in
5.2
, this setting is of little concern. Indeed, after a linear change of coordinates, we may take
f=x_{n}
, and so
R\cong k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
with
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
. Moreover, we then have
f_{1}=T_{n}
, and so
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
. With this and
5.1
, we see that

\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\cong\frac{S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]}{I_{2}(\psi)+(x_{n},T_{n})}\cong\frac{R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n-1}]}{I_{2}(\psi^{\prime})}

where

\psi^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\ldots&x_{n-1}\\
T_{1}&\ldots&T_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}

as in the description of
\mathcal{K}
in (
3.2
). In particular, the description of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
in
5.1
recovers the original result of Micali
[
18
, Chap. I: Thm. 1, Lem. 2]
in this case.

Recall that the
relation type

\mathop{\rm rt}(\mathfrak{m})
is the largest degree, with respect to
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
, within a minimal generating set of the defining ideal of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
.

Corollary 5.4
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
, the relation type of
\mathfrak{m}
is
\mathop{\rm rt}(\mathfrak{m})=d
.

Proof.

If
d\geq 2
, this follows from
5.2
. If
d=1
then, as noted in
5.3
,
\mathfrak{m}
is of linear type and so
\mathop{\rm rt}(\mathfrak{m})=1
.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
, the fiber ring of
\mathfrak{m}
is
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/(f_{d})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

From
5.1
, we have
\mathcal{J}=I_{2}(\psi)+(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d})
. As noted in the proof of
5.2
,
I_{2}(\psi)
is generated in bidegree
(1,1)
and each
f_{i}
is nonzero of bidegree
(d-i,i)
by
4.3
. Hence from bidegree considerations, we have
\mathcal{J}+\mathfrak{n}=(f_{d})+\mathfrak{n}
, and so
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})\cong S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/(\mathcal{J}+\mathfrak{n})\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/(f_{d})
. The Cohen–Macaulayness of
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})
is clear, as it is a hypersurface ring.
∎

5.1.
Cohen–Macaulayness

We now discuss the Cohen–Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
, and begin by constructing a handful of short exact sequences, similar to the procedure in
[
2
]
. As depth is easily compared along short exact sequences, we are able to bound the depths of all modules involved.

With
\mathfrak{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
as before, recall that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})
and this is a strongly Cohen–Macaulay ideal with
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}}):\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}
by
3.4
and
3.3
.
As such, there is a short exact sequence of bigraded
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-modules

0\longrightarrow\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\longrightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(-1,0)\oplus\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\longrightarrow 0.

Now applying the symmetric algebra functor
\mathcal{S}(-)
to this sequence and taking the
d
th graded strand, we obtain

\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\otimes\mathcal{S}_{d-1}\big(\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(-1,0)\oplus\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{d}\big(\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(-1,0)\oplus\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{d}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}})\rightarrow 0.

As
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=(\widetilde{x_{n}},\widetilde{T_{n}})
is a strongly Cohen–Macaulay
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal and is 2-generated, it follows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is of linear type
[
11
, 2.6]
, hence
\mathcal{S}_{d}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}})=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
. Additionally, by rank considerations, the kernel of the first map above is torsion. As it is a submodule of a torsion-free module, this kernel must vanish, and so we have a short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\otimes\mathcal{S}_{d-1}\big(\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(-1,0)\oplus\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{d}\big(\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(-1,0)\oplus\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})(0,-1)\big)\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}\rightarrow 0.

Now writing the symmetric powers above as free
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-modules, we then have

(5.1)

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\big(-i,-(d-i)\big)\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\big(-i,-(d-i)\big)\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}\longrightarrow 0.

Additionally, we have the following natural short exact sequences

(5.2)

0\longrightarrow\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\longrightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]\longrightarrow 0

and

(5.3)

0\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\longrightarrow\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\longrightarrow 0.

With the short exact sequences (
5.1
) – (
5.3
), we may apply
[
8
, 18.6]
multiple times to compare the depths of the modules and rings above. Recall that a Noetherian local ring
A
is said to be
almost
Cohen–Macaulay if
\mathop{\rm dim}A-\mathop{\rm depth}A\leq 1
. In particular, we note that Cohen–Macaulay implies almost Cohen–Macaulay.

Theorem 5.6
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
,
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
is almost Cohen–Macaulay, and is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
d\leq n-1
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
is almost Cohen–Macaulay, i.e. that
\mathop{\rm depth}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\geq n-1
. Recall that
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is a Cohen–Macaulay domain with
\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})=n+1
. Thus from (
5.2
), it follows that
\mathop{\rm depth}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\geq n+1
, hence
\mathop{\rm depth}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})=n+1
as this is the maximum. Now with this, and comparing depths in (
5.1
), it then follows that
\mathop{\rm depth}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}\geq n
. With this and the bigraded isomorphism
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{\widetilde{f}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}\cong\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
, comparing depths along (
5.3
) shows that
\mathop{\rm depth}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\geq n-1
, as claimed.

For the second assertion, if
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
is Cohen–Macaulay, then by
[
1
, 6.3]
we have
\mathop{\rm rt}(\mathfrak{m})\leq\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})
. Hence by
5.4
and
5.5
we have
d\leq n-1
. For the converse, we again note that there is an isomorphism
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\cong\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
. Moreover, from the definition of
\mathcal{K}
, it follows from
[
8
, A2.13, A2.14]
that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{i}
is a maximal Cohen–Macaulay
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-module, and hence a Cohen–Macaulay
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
-ideal, for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Thus if
d\leq n-1
, then
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})/\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\cong\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
is Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

5.2.
Regularity

With the bigraded short exact sequences (
5.1
) – (
5.3
), we may also bound the
Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity
of the Rees algebra
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
, in a manner similar as was done for depth. We refer the reader to
[
27
]
for all definitions and conventions regarding regularity. Additionally, we note that regularity can be determined from a minimal graded free resolution, and is also easily compared along short exact sequences
[
8
, §20.5]
.

We first note that the modules involved are bigraded, and so there are multiple choices for a single grading. We write
\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}
to denote regularity with respect to the grading given by
\deg x_{i}=1
and
\deg T_{i}=0
. Similarly, write
\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}
to denote regularity with respect to the grading given by
\deg x_{i}=0
and
\deg T_{i}=1
. Lastly, write
\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}
to denote regularity with respect to the total grading given by
\deg x_{i}=\deg T_{i}=1
.

Theorem 5.7
.

With the assumptions of
2.1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})=d-1,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\leq d-1,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\leq d.\end{array}

Additionally,
\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})=d-1
.

Proof.

We first note that the claim on the regularity of
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})
follows as
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{m})\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/(f_{d})
and
\deg f_{d}=d
, by
5.5
. Moreover, it is well known that
\mathop{\rm rt}(\mathfrak{m})-1\leq\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})

[
27
]
, hence by
5.4
we have
d-1\leq\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})
. Thus the first equality will follow once it has been shown that
\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\leq d-1
. We show this and the remaining inequalities simultaneously, by applying
[
8
, 20.19]
repeatedly.

Recall that
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\cong S[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I_{2}(\psi)
, for
\psi
as in (
2.2
), and so
\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})
is resolved by the Eagon–Northcott complex. Writing this as a graded free resolution, with respect to the various gradings, we deduce that

(5.4)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})=0,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})=0,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})=1.\end{array}

Moreover, recall that
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})\cong k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
. Thus we have

(5.5)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})=0,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})=0,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{n})=0,\end{array}

as well. Now with these bounds, and the sequence (
5.2
), it follows that

(5.6)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\leq 1,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\leq 1,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\leq 1.\end{array}

Now with (
5.4
) and (
5.6
), we are able to bound the regularity of the modules in the sequence (
5.1
). For ease of notation, write

\begin{array}[]{ccc}M=\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\big(-i,-(d-i)\big)},&&N=\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{n})\big(-i,-(d-i)\big)}\end{array}

for the modules appearing in (
5.1
). With the inequalities above, it follows that

(5.7)

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}M\leq d+1,&&\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}N=d,\\[4.30554pt]
\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}M\leq d,&&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}N=d,\\[4.30554pt]
\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}M\leq d+1,&&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}N=d+1.\end{array}

With the bounds above and the sequence (
5.1
), it then follows that

(5.8)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}\leq d,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}\leq d,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}\leq d+1.\end{array}

Lastly, with the bigraded isomorphism
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{\widetilde{f}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}}{\widetilde{x_{n}}^{d}}\cong\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
and the inequalities in (
5.4
) and (
5.8
), comparing regularity in the sequence (
5.3
) shows that

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathop{\rm reg}_{T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\leq d-1,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\leq d-1,&\mathop{\rm reg}_{x,T}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{m})\leq d,\end{array}

which completes the proof.
∎
