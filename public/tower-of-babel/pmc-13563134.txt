Diagnostic discordance in outside prostate biopsies: Implications for active surveillance patient selection
Authors: Hyean Jun Park, Jong Ho Park, Younsoo Chung, Hyung Joon Kim, Hakmin Lee, Jong Jin Oh, Sangchul Lee, Seong Jin Jeong, Sung Kyu Hong, Seok-Soo Byun, Sang Hun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGleason grade group (GG) concordance between prostate biopsy and radical prostatectomy (RP) is critical for treatment planning, particularly for active surveillance (AS). We compared GG concordance, upgrade rates, and AS misclassification between institutional and outside biopsies and evaluated the impact of pathologic re-evaluation.Materials and MethodsIn this propensity score–matched cohort study, 3,744 patients (1,872 institutional and 1,872 outside biopsies) who underwent RP were analyzed. Outside biopsies were stratified by re-evaluation status (re-evaluated, n=570; not re-evaluated, n=1,302). Primary outcomes included overall GG concordance, clinically significant upgrade (≥2 GG), and AS misclassification (biopsy GG 1 to RP ≥GG 3). Agreement was assessed using weighted kappa statistics, and predictors of upgrade were identified by multivariable logistic regression.ResultsOverall GG concordance was 44.7%. Institutional biopsies showed the highest concordance (47.8%) compared to outside biopsies with (45.3%) and without re-evaluation (40.0%) (p<0.001). Among biopsy GG 1 patients, AS misclassification to ≥GG 3 occurred in 9.6% of institutional biopsies, compared to 27.0% of outside re-evaluated biopsies and 20.0% of outside non–re-evaluated biopsies (p<0.0001). Outside biopsy source (odds ratio [OR] 1.351, p=0.013) and absence of re-evaluation (OR 1.451, p=0.028) were identified as independent predictors of GG upgrade.ConclusionsOutside prostate biopsies exhibit 2.1- to 2.8-fold higher AS misclassification rates compared to institutional biopsies. Pathologic re-evaluation provides only modest improvement and does not eliminate upgrade risk, supporting systematic second-opinion review and careful confirmation before AS enrollment.Keywords: Active surveillance, Gleason grading, Observer variation, Prostate cancer, Reproducibility of results

INTRODUCTION

Accurate Gleason grade group (GG) assignment on prostate biopsy is central to risk stratification and treatment planning in prostate cancer [1,2]. Concordance between biopsy and radical prostatectomy (RP) grading is particularly important for patients considered for active surveillance (AS), where underestimation of tumor grade may result in inappropriate treatment deferral [3,4,5,6].

Inter-institutional variation in pathologic interpretation is a recognized contributor to grading discordance [7,8,9]. Prior studies have demonstrated substantial disagreement between outside biopsy interpretations and expert genitourinary pathology review, with potential implications for clinical decision-making [10,11,12]. However, most investigations have focused on overall concordance rates and have not quantified the downstream impact on AS eligibility or misclassification risk.

Routine pathologic re-evaluation of outside biopsy specimens before definitive treatment is inconsistently practiced despite standardized guidelines on reporting and processing [11,13]. Although second-opinion review is often advocated, its effectiveness in improving grade concordance and reducing clinically meaningful misclassification has not been well defined in large, well-controlled cohorts. In addition, the influence of referring institution characteristics, such as academic tier or hospital volume, on grading accuracy remains unclear [14].

In this study, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to compare GG concordance between institutional and outside biopsies, evaluate the impact of pathologic re-evaluation, and quantify AS misclassification risk. We further sought to identify independent predictors of GG upgrading to provide clinically actionable guidance for managing patients referred with outside biopsy results.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (approval number: B-2506-976-103). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective/anonymous nature of the study.

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent RP at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital between 2003 and 2023. Patients were identified from a prospectively maintained institutional database. Eligible patients had prostate biopsies performed either at our institution or at an outside facility, followed by RP at our center with complete pathology data available for both biopsy and RP specimens. Patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, had prior prostate cancer treatment before RP, or had incomplete pathology reports were excluded.

Patients were categorized into three groups according to biopsy source and re-evaluation status: (1) institution group, comprising biopsies performed and interpreted at our institution; (2) outside+re-evaluation group, consisting of outside biopsies that underwent formal pathologic re-evaluation at our institution before RP; and (3) outside+no re-evaluation group, consisting of outside biopsies without formal re-evaluation. Decisions regarding re-evaluation were made at the discretion of the treating urologist and were not protocolized during the study period.

To minimize selection bias, PSM was performed between the institution group and the combined outside groups. Matching variables included age, prostate-specific antigen (PSA), clinical T stage, and biopsy GG. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was conducted using a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score.

All institutional biopsies were reviewed by genitourinary fellowship-trained pathologists according to the 2014/2016 International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) GG classification [1]. Outside biopsies submitted for re-evaluation underwent complete slide review by the same team of pathologists, blinded to the original outside interpretation. RP specimens were processed using whole-mount sectioning and evaluated according to standard institutional protocols.

The primary outcomes were overall GG concordance (exact agreement between biopsy and RP GG), AS misclassification (biopsy GG 1 with RP GG ≥3), and clinically significant upgrading (increase of ≥2 GG categories). Secondary outcomes included GG-specific concordance rates, overall upgrading and downgrading rates, severe misclassification (GG 1 to GG 4–5), and weighted kappa statistics. Referring institutions were classified by hospital tier: first- and secondtier hospitals were university-affiliated academic centers with training programs, whereas third-tier hospitals were community or non-teaching institutions.

Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Agreement was assessed using weighted kappa statistics with linear weights. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of GG upgrading. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.) and R version 4.2.0, with two-sided p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

1. Patient characteristics

Before PSM, significant differences existed between institutional and outside biopsy groups in baseline characteristics (total n=5,939). After 1:1 PSM, 3,744 patients (1,872 per group) were well-balanced across all matching variables (all p>0.05). The outside biopsy group was further subdivided into those who underwent pathologic re-evaluation (n=570, 30.4%) and those who did not (n=1,302, 69.6%). Baseline characteristics (Table 1), including age (mean, 66.2±7.8 years), PSA (median, 8.4 ng/mL), clinical stage distribution, and biopsy GG distribution, were comparable across all three final groups. Among the outside biopsies, the distribution of referring centers included 54.3% from 1st/2nd-tier institutions and 45.7% from 3rd-tier institutions.

2. Overall GG concordance

Across the entire cohort, exact concordance between biopsy and RP GG was observed in 44.7% of patients (Table 2, Fig. 1). Upgrading occurred in 44.1%, while downgrading was observed in 11.2%. Institutional biopsies demonstrated the highest concordance rate (47.8%), followed by outside biopsies with re-evaluation (45.3%) and outside biopsies without re-evaluation (40.0%) (p<0.001). Correspondingly, upgrade rates were lowest in the institutional group (39.5%) and higher among outside biopsies, irrespective of re-evaluation status (p<0.001).

3. AS misclassification

Among patients diagnosed with biopsy GG 1 (n=1,248), marked differences in AS misclassification were observed (Table 2, Fig. 2). Misclassification to RP GG 3 or higher occurred in 9.6% of institutional biopsies, compared with 27.0% of outside biopsies that underwent re-evaluation and 20.0% of outside biopsies without re-evaluation (p<0.0001). Relative to institutional biopsies, the risk of misclassification was increased nearly three-fold in re-evaluated outside biopsies (relative risk [RR] 2.81, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.91–3.71) and more than two-fold in non–re-evaluated outside biopsies (RR 2.08, 95% CI 1.58–2.78). Severe misclassification to GG 4–5 disease remained uncommon but was significantly more frequent in outside biopsies than institutional biopsies. These findings indicate that approximately one in four to five patients with outside biopsy GG 1 harbored active-surveillance–ineligible disease at surgery.

4. Grade-specific concordance

Grade-specific analyses demonstrated that discordance was most pronounced in low-grade disease. Concordance for biopsy GG 1 was highest in institutional biopsies (24.7%) and substantially lower in outside biopsies with (4.5%) and without re-evaluation (11.9%) (p<0.001). Differences in concordance were less pronounced for GG 2 and 3, and no significant intergroup differences were observed for GG 4–5 (p>0.05). These patterns suggest that inter-institutional variability predominantly affects interpretation of low-to-intermediate grade tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1).

5. Predictors of Gleason grade upgrade

On multivariable logistic regression, biopsy source and re-evaluation status emerged as independent predictors of Gleason grade upgrading. Outside biopsy origin was associated with 35% increased odds of upgrading compared with institutional biopsies (odds ratio [OR] 1.351, p=0.013), while absence of re-evaluation further increased upgrade risk (OR 1.451, p=0.028). In contrast, referring hospital tier was not independently associated with upgrade risk (p=0.188). Among clinical variables, higher PSA (OR 1.024), greater number of positive cores (OR 1.088), and longer maximum cancer core length (OR 1.041) were all independently associated with upgrading (all p<0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this large propensity score–matched cohort, we demonstrate that prostate biopsies performed outside the treating institution are associated with substantially lower GG concordance and, more importantly, markedly higher rates of AS misclassification. The most clinically relevant finding is the 2.1- to 2.8-fold increase in misclassification to GG 3 or higher disease among patients with outside biopsy GG 1 compared with institutional biopsies. These results indicate that, in real-world referral settings, reliance on outside biopsy grading alone may expose a considerable proportion of patients to inappropriate AS enrollment.

Overall biopsy–prostatectomy concordance in our cohort (44.7%) is consistent with prior reports demonstrating concordance rates ranging from 40% to 60% [15,16,17]. However, most previous studies evaluated concordance at a population level without accounting for biopsy source or systematically assessing the downstream clinical consequences. By incorporating biopsy origin and re-evaluation status into a matched analysis, our study extends existing literature by directly linking inter-institutional grading variability to clinically actionable misclassification in AS candidates.

The magnitude of AS misclassification observed in outside biopsy GG 1 patients is particularly striking. While institutional biopsies demonstrated a misclassification rate of 9.6%, comparable to contemporary AS cohorts [18,19], misclassification reached 20%–27% in outside biopsies. This implies that approximately one in four to five patients referred with GG 1 disease harbored active-surveillance–ineligible cancer at surgery. Such rates exceed those typically considered acceptable in modern surveillance protocols and underscore the vulnerability of low-grade disease classification to interpretative variability [20,21].

Pathologic re-evaluation modestly improved overall concordance but did not sufficiently mitigate misclassification risk. Notably, GG 1 misclassification remained highest in the re-evaluated outside biopsy group. This apparent paradox likely reflects factors beyond interpretative error alone. Sampling limitations inherent to the original biopsy, incomplete slide submission, and true intraprostatic tumor heterogeneity may all contribute to persistent discordance that cannot be corrected by re-interpretation alone [22]. These findings challenge the assumption that second-opinion pathology review fully neutralizes the risk associated with outside biopsy interpretation. Critically, while second-opinion review remains a valuable initial step and should be routinely performed, it cannot compensate for tissue that was never sampled; when the primary source of discordance is sampling inadequacy rather than interpretative error, re-reading the same slides offers no corrective benefit. This distinction underscores the clinical necessity of a confirmatory biopsy at the treating institution before enrolling patients with outside GG 1 disease into AS.

Importantly, our multivariable analysis demonstrated that outside biopsy origin and absence of re-evaluation independently predicted grade upgrading, whereas referring hospital tier did not. This suggests that grading variability is not primarily driven by institutional prestige or academic status but rather by systematic differences in biopsy protocols, pathologist subspecialty expertise, and interpretative frameworks. These observations align with nationwide data demonstrating substantial inter-laboratory variation in Gleason grading, even among high-volume centers [17,23].

From a clinical standpoint, these findings have direct implications for AS selection. For patients diagnosed with GG 1 disease at an outside institution, our data support a cautious approach. Practically, we suggest the following stepwise approach for outside GG 1 referrals with routine second-opinion pathologic review upon receipt of outside biopsy materials, further imaging with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) if not already performed, and confirmatory biopsy at the treating institution prior to definitive AS enrollment, regardless of re-evaluation result, given the persistently elevated misclassification rates demonstrated here. At minimum, systematic second-opinion pathologic review should be strongly considered. However, given the persistently elevated misclassification rates despite re-evaluation, confirmatory biopsy at an experienced center appears warranted before definitive enrollment into AS protocols, particularly when additional risk factors such as elevated PSA density or extensive tumor involvement are present. Although confirmatory biopsy with MRI is guideline-endorsed, real-world adherence is incomplete, and many patients with outside GG 1 disease are enrolled in AS based on the initial biopsy result alone. Our findings quantify this misclassification risk and support treating confirmatory biopsy as mandatory rather than discretionary in this setting. Incorporation of multiparametric MRI and, where available, genomic classifiers may further mitigate risk but should not substitute for histologic confirmation [24], given that MRI-occult significant cancer remains a recognized limitation of imaging-based risk stratification. It should also be noted that our study period (2003–2023) spans the transition from purely systematic transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy to MRI-guided targeted biopsy; widespread multiparametric MRI adoption in the latter half of the cohort may have differentially improved sampling adequacy at higher-volume institutional centers relative to outside facilities, potentially contributing to the observed discordance. Whether the grading gap between institutional and outside biopsies narrows in a contemporary MRI-era cohort warrants dedicated investigation.

Conversely, the relatively low misclassification rate observed in institutional biopsies reinforces the validity of current surveillance pathways when grading is performed within a standardized, subspecialty-driven environment. This distinction highlights the need for quality assurance initiatives aimed at reducing inter-institutional variability, including standardized Gleason grading education, external proficiency testing, and broader adoption of genitourinary pathology subspecialization [7,8]. Emerging digital pathology platforms and artificial intelligence–assisted grading tools may also play an increasing role in harmonizing interpretations across institutions [25].

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and unmeasured confounding may persist despite rigorous PSM. Second, outside biopsy protocols, including number of cores and targeting strategies, were heterogeneous and incompletely captured, either due to lack of data provided during referral and/or lack of documentation within the electronic medical records and therefore could not be reported or adjusted for. In particular, MRI utilization rates and biopsy technique (systematic vs. fusion-targeted) were not systematically recorded across outside institutions, precluding an era-stratified subanalysis that could isolate the temporal effect of MRI adoption on grading discordance. Third, selection bias may have influenced which outside biopsies underwent re-evaluation. Finally, long-term oncologic outcomes were not assessed, precluding direct correlation between misclassification and survival endpoints. Nonetheless, the large sample size, balanced matched design, and clinically focused endpoints strengthen the validity of our conclusions.

CONCLUSIONS

Outside prostate biopsies were associated with significantly lower GG concordance and a 2.1- to 2.8-fold higher risk of AS misclassification compared with institutional biopsies. Notably, 20%–27% of patients with outside biopsy GG 1 disease harbored GG 3 or higher cancer at RP. Outside biopsy origin and absence of pathologic re-evaluation independently predicted upgrading, whereas referring hospital tier did not. These findings support routine second-opinion pathologic review and careful confirmation, including repeat biopsy, before enrolling patients with outside GG 1 disease into AS.
