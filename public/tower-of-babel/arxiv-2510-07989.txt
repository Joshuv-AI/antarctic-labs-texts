A Stable, Accurate and Well-Conditioned Time-Domain PMCHWT Formulation
Van Chien Le, Cedric Münger, Francesco P. Andriulli, and Kristof Cools

Abstract
This paper introduces a new boundary element formulation for transient electromagnetic scattering by homogeneous dielectric objects based on the time-domain PMCHWT equation. To address dense-mesh breakdown, a multiplicative Calderón preconditioner utilizing a modified static electric field integral operator is employed. Large-timestep breakdown and late-time instability are simultaneously resolved by rescaling the Helmholtz components leveraging the quasi-Helmholtz projectors and using temporal differentiation and integration as rescaling operators. This rescaling also balances the loop and star components at large timesteps, improving solution accuracy. The resulting discrete system is solved using a marching-on-in-time scheme and iterative solvers. Numerical experiments for simply- and multiply-connected dielectric scatterers, including highly non-smooth geometries, corroborate the accuracy, stability, and efficiency of the proposed approach.



I
Introduction

The numerical computation of transient electromagnetic fields scattered by piecewise homogeneous dielectric bodies often relies on solving time-domain boundary integral equations. Among several formulations for dielectric scattering, the time-domain Poggio-Miller-Chang-Harrington-Wu-Tsai (TD-PMCHWT) equation
[
1
]
and the time-domain (TD-) Müller equation
[
2
]
are most widely used. The TD-PMCHWT equation, like the time-domain electric field integral equation (TD-EFIE) for perfect electric conductors, is a first-kind integral equation. In contrast, the TD-Müller equation, similar to the time-domain magnetic field integral equation (TD-MFIE), is of the second-kind. Despite its ill-conditioned nature, the TD-PMCHWT equation is more widely used in practice, as it generally yields more accurate numerical solutions than the TD-Müller formulation – see
[
3
,
4
]
for supporting arguments in the frequency-domain. In this work, we focus on developing a numerical method for the TD-PMCHWT equation that addresses three numerical challenges: ill-conditioning, late-time instability, and the loss of accuracy in the large-timestep regime. Here, the large-timestep regime refers to scenarios where relatively large timesteps are used in the time-domain simulation of the electromagnetic system. This regime resembles the low-frequency regime in the frequency-domain, since both are affected by similar numerical issues.

Due to its unbounded spectrum, the TD-PMCHWT operator produces ill-conditioned matrix systems upon discretization, particularly when the spatial mesh is dense or the timestep is large. These issues are commonly referred to as dense-mesh breakdown and large-timestep breakdown, respectively. In the dense-mesh regime, the accumulation of eigenvalues near zero and infinity causes the ill-conditioning. This breakdown can be effectively resolved through operator preconditioning
[
5
]
, with Calderón-based techniques – built upon the Calderón identities – being especially prominent. Several such methods have been developed for the frequency-domain (FD-) PMCHWT equation
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, relying on either the PMCHWT operator itself or the electric field integral operator (EFIO) with a complex wavenumber. In the time-domain, frequency-domain operators with zero or purely imaginary wavenumbers can be used as preconditioners, avoiding complex-valued systems and suppressing interior resonances
[
10
,
11
]
. Alternatively, the TD-PMCHWT operator itself, when appropriately discretized, can serve as a dense-mesh preconditioner
[
12
]
. However, using such a time-dependent operator requires an additional temporal discretization, leading to a more complicated marching-on-in-time (MOT) scheme.

Large-timestep breakdown, similar to low-frequency breakdown, stems from the opposite scaling behavior of matrix blocks associated with the loop and star components. While Calderón preconditioners can also be employed to address this issue in the frequency-domain – by exploiting the anti-commutative property of integral operators within the same medium
[
6
]
– this strategy becomes less effective in the time-domain setting. Specifically, the necessary Calderón anti-commutative relation does not hold between frequency-domain and time-domain operators, thus requiring nontrivial adaptations or dedicated implementation strategies. An alternative remedy is to rescale the Helmholtz blocks within the matrix system appropriately. This class of preconditioning has been extensively studied in the literature, both for frequency-domain formulations
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
and for their time-domain counterparts
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
11
]
.

In this work, we construct a diagonal dense-mesh preconditioner based on a modified static EFIO. The large-timestep preconditioner, on the other hand, is derived from the loop-star decomposition via the quasi-Helmholtz projectors combined with an appropriate rescaling scheme. Constructing the dense-mesh and large-timestep preconditioners separately offers a key advantage: each preconditioner is tailored to address its specific breakdown regime while remaining stable and well-conditioned in the presence of the other. Consequently, their combination yields a simple yet effective strategy for mitigating both dense-mesh and large-timestep breakdowns.

In addition to ill-conditioning, the TD-PMCHWT equation is also notorious for its late-time instability, which manifests as non-decaying or even exponentially growing errors at late times
[
22
]
. This phenomenon superficially resembles direct-current instability of the TD-EFIE, but is considerably more sensitive to numerical quadrature errors
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. Although various techniques can be used to suppress such errors
[
26
,
27
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, these remedies generally do not guarantee stability, as they do not eliminate the underlying source of the instability. A recent comprehensive stability analysis of the TD-PMCHWT equation
[
31
]
identified the static solenoidal nullspace of the TD-EFIO as the principal cause of this instability. In this work, we eliminate this nullspace by adopting the rescaling scheme introduced in
[
20
]
, which applies temporal differentiation and integration as rescaling factors associated with the Helmholtz components. This approach has been shown to stabilize time-domain solutions for both the TD-EFIE
[
18
,
19
]
and TD-PMCHWT
[
20
,
25
]
, even under limited numerical precision in quadrature evaluations.

Lastly, at low frequencies or large timesteps, the solution to the PMCHWT equation suffers from unbalanced scaling between its loop and star components, which leads to a loss of significant digits in the dominated component when stored together with the dominant one in the same floating point variable
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
15
]
. Even though the solution for the boundary fields may at first glance seem reasonable, large errors appear when computing secondary quantities of interest such as the far field. The accuracy loss can also be mitigated by means of the loop-star decomposition combined with a rescaling scheme
[
15
,
14
]
. More specifically, when constructing the rescaling operators for the solution, we introduce a balancing factor to the dominated component, thereby defining a well-balanced auxiliary unknown that mitigates the numerical cancellation present in the original unknown.

In summary, this work addresses several numerical issues of the TD-PMCHWT equation, including dense-mesh breakdown, large-timestep breakdown, late-time instability, and the loss of solution accuracy at large timesteps. To combat dense-mesh breakdown, we introduce a multiplicative preconditioner utilizing a modified static EFIO. The remaining issues are simultaneously resolved by leveraging the loop-star decomposition combined with a rescaling scheme. The loop-star decomposition is performed via the quasi-Helmholtz projectors, which can be constructed without the need for costly identification of global loops
[
35
]
. The rescaling operators are applied to the loop and star components, with temporal differentiation and integration used as rescaling factors. The preconditioned and regularized formulation is temporally discretized using suitable pairs of trial and testing functions. The resulting matrix system is efficiently solved using the MOT algorithm in combination with iterative solvers, yielding accurate and stable solutions. This paper extends our earlier conference contributions
[
18
,
25
]
and the work in
[
24
]
by providing a rigorous analysis and a comprehensive suite of numerical results.

The structure of the paper is as follows. The next section gives an introduction to the TD-PMCHWT equation and its spatial Galerkin discretization. In Section
III
, we develop a dense-mesh preconditioner for the TD-PMCHWT formulation. Section
IV
is devoted to asymptotic large-timestep analysis and a regularization strategy for the preconditioned system. Section
V
discusses the temporal discretization and relevant implementation details. Several numerical results for both simply- and multiply-connected dielectric bodies including a highly non-smooth domain are presented in Section
VI
, corroborating the effectiveness of the proposed formulation. Finally, concluding remarks and future perspectives are provided in Section
VII
.

III
Dense-Mesh Preconditioning

We involve the following “static EFIO” in constructing a dense-mesh preconditioner:

T_{0}=T^{s}_{0}+T^{h}_{0},

(3)

where the singular and hyper-singular parts are defined by

\displaystyle(T^{s}_{0}\boldsymbol{j})(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{D}\,\textbf{n}\times\int_{\Gamma}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{y})}{4\pi\left|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}}\right|}\,\mathrm{d}s_{\boldsymbol{y}},

\displaystyle(T^{h}_{0}\boldsymbol{j})(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=-D\,\textbf{n}\times\textbf{grad}_{\xxs}\int_{\Gamma}\dfrac{\textup{div}_{\Gamma}\,\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{y})}{4\pi\left|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}}\right|}\,\mathrm{d}s_{\boldsymbol{y}}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}_{0}^{s}+\mathbb{T}_{0}^{h},

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{T}_{0}^{s}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{T}_{0}^{h}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{g}_{m},T^{h}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{g}_{n}}\right\rangle.

IV
Large-Timestep Regularization

This section presents an analysis on the asymptotic large-timestep scaling behavior of the dense-mesh preconditioned formulation (
5
). The goal is to investigate the mechanisms underlying large-timestep breakdown and the loss of solution accuracy at large timesteps. Based on these insights, we propose a regularization strategy that simultaneously eliminates large-timestep ill-conditioning, late-time instability, and the loss of accuracy.

To that end, we assume that the loop-star decomposition is employed, resulting in the transformation matrices
\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\HHs
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
that respectively map local loops, global loops (quasi-harmonic functions), and stars to discrete functions. Particularly in the context of the RWG basis, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{e}\times N_{v}}
is constructed via the node-edge adjacency information as

\left[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right]_{mn}:=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if the vertex }n\text{ is identical with }v^{+}_{m},\\
-1&\text{if the vertex }n\text{ is identical with }v^{-}_{m},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where the edge
e_{m}
,
m=1,2,\ldots,N_{e}
, is oriented from the vertex
v_{m}^{-}
to
v_{m}^{+}
. The matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{e}\times N_{f}}
is linked to face-edge connectivity as follows:

\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right]_{mn}:=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if the triangle }n\text{ is identical with }c^{+}_{m},\\
-1&\text{if the triangle }n\text{ is identical with }c^{-}_{m},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where the RWG basis function
\boldsymbol{f}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}),m=1,2,\ldots,N_{e},
represents a current flowing from the triangle
c_{m}^{+}
to
c_{m}^{-}

[
35
]
.

Both the local loops and the global loops represent solenoidal functions. When the surface
\Gamma
is simply-connected, the matrix
\HHs=\emptyset
. We emphasize that
\HHs
is introduced for the analysis purpose only. In our numerical scheme, the costly explicit identification of
\HHs
is not required. Throughout this section, the large-timestep scaling of vector fields and matrices is with respect to the basis
(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\,\,\HHs\,\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

IV-A

Large-Timestep Scaling

The large-timestep scaling of quantities and matrices in the time-domain is similar to their low-frequency counterparts in the frequency-domain setting. For consistency between time-domain and frequency-domain, we use the notation
\omega:=\Delta t^{-1}
in further large-timestep analysis, where
\Delta t
denotes the timestep used in the simulation. In the large-timestep regime, the plane-wave excitation and its induced surface current densities scale as follows
[
32
,
15
,
14
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\omega,\omega,1)^{\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1,\omega,\omega)^{\mathsf{T}}.

When
\omega\to 0
(equivalently,
\Delta t\to\infty
), the unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
are dominated by their local loop components. As a consequence, the global loops and stars suffer from a loss of significant digits when stored together with the local loops in the same floating point variable. This numerical loss of accuracy may not manifest itself in the solution or in the induced near fields, but it will be prominently present in the far-field computation
[
11
]
. The TD-PMCHWT matrix
\mathfrak{Q}
in (
2

\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{Q}_{11}&\mathfrak{Q}_{12}\\
\mathfrak{Q}_{21}&\mathfrak{Q}_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{O}\left({\begin{array}[]{c c c | c c c}\omega&\omega&\omega&\omega^{2}&\omega^{2}&1\\
\omega&\omega&\omega&\omega^{2}&1&1\\
\omega&\omega&\omega^{-1}&1&1&1\\
\hline\cr\omega^{2}&\omega^{2}&1&\omega&\omega&\omega\\
\omega^{2}&1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega\\
1&1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega^{-1}\end{array}}\right).

This scaling can be derived from the large-timestep scaling of the TD-EFIE and TD-MFIE operators
[
11
]
, or from the low-frequency counterpart in the frequency-domain
[
15
,
14
]
.
The opposite scaling behaviors
\mathcal{O}(\omega)
and
\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-1})
in the diagonal blocks stemming from the temporal differentiation and integration in the TD-EFIOs are the origin of large-timestep breakdown. More specifically, the condition number of
\mathfrak{Q}
grows as
\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-1})/\mathcal{O}(\omega)=\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-2})
when
\omega\to 0
.

Next, we derive the large-timestep scaling of the dense-mesh preconditioned formulation (

\mathbb{T}
and the Gram matrix

are independent of timestep
\Delta t

and

and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
represents the stars and

represents the local loops), while
\HHs
still represents the global loops
[
13
]

\mathbb{T}
behaves as follows in the basis

\mathbb{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\square&0&0\\

where

\GGs^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\square&0&0\\

Combining the large-timestep scaling and algebraic structure of vectors and matrices, the large-timestep scaling of the formulation (
5
) reads as follows:

\left({\begin{array}[]{c c c | c c c}\omega&\omega&\omega&\omega^{2}&\omega^{2}&1\\
\omega&\omega&\omega^{-1}&1&1&1\\
\omega&\omega&\omega^{-1}&1&1&1\\
\hline\cr\omega^{2}&\omega^{2}&1&\omega&\omega&\omega\\
1&1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega^{-1}\\
1&1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega^{-1}\end{array}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\omega\\
\omega\\
\hline\cr 1\\
\omega\\
\omega\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega\\
1\\
1\\
\hline\cr\omega\\
1\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

It is convincing that (
5
) still suffers from both large-timestep breakdown and the loss of solution accuracy. In the following section, a regularization for (
5
) is introduced, based on its large-timestep scaling behavior in (
6
).

IV-B

Large-Timestep Regularization

We recall the quasi-Helmholtz projectors associated with the RWG and BC bases

\displaystyle\PPo^{\Sigma}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad

\displaystyle\PPo^{\Lambda H}:=I-\PPo^{\Sigma},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{+}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}:=I-\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda},

where the subscript
+
indicates the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse and
I
is the identity matrix of size
N_{e}

[
13
]
. Approximately speaking,
\PPo^{\Lambda H}
and
\PPo^{\Sigma}
are respectively the projectors from the RWG space into its loop and star subspaces. Similarly,
\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}
are the corresponding projectors associated with the BC space. Notably, these constructions of the loop-star decomposition do not require the costly explicit identification of the global loops
\HHs
. The drawback is that the local and global loops are not distinguished. However, we will demonstrate in further analysis this is unproblematic.

Using the quasi-Helmholtz projectors, we now propose a large-timestep regularization for (
5
). In order to mitigate the imbalance between the components of the solution
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
, we introduce the right rescaling operator

\RRs:=\PPo^{\Lambda H}+\,\alpha\PPo^{\Sigma},

(7)

where the dimensionless factor
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\omega)
is specified later. Obviously, the operator
\RRs
is invertible and its inverse is given

\RRs^{-1}=\PPo^{\Lambda H}+\alpha^{-1}\PPo^{\Sigma}.

Next, we rescale the unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
by
\RRs^{-1}
, giving rise to the two auxiliary unknowns

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}(t):=\RRs^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}(t),\qquad\quad\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}(t):=\RRs^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}(t),

(8)

which scale as follows when
\omega\to 0
:

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}=\mathcal{O}(1,\omega,1)^{\mathsf{T}}.

It is noticed that, in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
, the global loops
\HHs
remain dominated by the local loops and the stars. Although
\HHs
has a fixed rank, this imbalance still causes numerical cancellation. Nevertheless, this cancellation is not problematic for the computation of the scattered fields, since the contribution of
\HHs
is only of order
\mathcal{O}(\omega)
relative to that of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
. Moreover, this behavior is physical, as a plane wave does not excite global loop currents in the static limit
[
14
]
. Other types of excitation may lead to a complete balance among the three components of the rescaled unknowns. Further details on the low-frequency scaling behavior of different excitations can be found in
[
43
]
.

By introducing the rescaling operator
\RRs
, one can transform an equation of the physical unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
to another equation of the auxiliary unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
, thereby significantly mitigating the imbalance in the solution and hence improving the accuracy at large timesteps. The physical currents
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
can be obtained back by simply applying
\RRs
to
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}(t)=\RRs\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}(t),\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}(t)=\RRs\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}(t).

(9)

The operator
\RRs
is called the right rescaling operator because it is multiplied on the right of the TD-PMCHWT matrix. To eliminate the large-timestep ill-conditioning of (
5
), we also need the following left rescaling operator:

\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}+\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}.

(10)

This operator is built on the quasi-Helmholtz projectors
\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}
associated with the BC space, as (
5
) defines a linear map from the RWG space into the dual of the BC space. Now, applying
\mathbb{L}
to (
5
) and substituting the unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
by (
9
), we end up with the following formulation of the auxiliary unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
:

\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{11}&\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{12}\\
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{21}&\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}(t)\\
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}}(t)\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}}(t)\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where the matrix blocks

\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{ij}=\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}\GGs^{-1}\mathfrak{Q}_{ij}\RRs,

with
i,j=1,2
, and the right-hand side

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}}=\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}\GGs^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}},\qquad\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}}=\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}\GGs^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}.

By some simple calculations, one can determine the large-timestep scaling of (
11
) as follows:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c | c c c}1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega&1\\
\omega&\omega&1&1&1&\omega\\
\omega&\omega&1&1&1&\omega\\
\hline\cr\omega&\omega&1&1&1&\omega\\
1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega&1\\
1&1&\omega&\omega&\omega&1\end{array}\right)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\omega\\
1\\
\hline\cr 1\\
\omega\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
1\\
\hline\cr 1\\
1\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Apparently, there is no factor
\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-1})
in the scaling of the square matrix in (
11
). Numerical experiments further indicate that its condition number remains bounded as
\omega\to 0
. Consequently, the regularized formulation (
11
) is free from large-timestep breakdown, and its solution is well-balanced when the timestep
\Delta t
is large.

The left and right rescaling operators
\mathbb{L}
and
\RRs
are essential in developing (
11
). These operators differ from those proposed for the FD-PMCHWT formulation in
[
14
]
, as we do not require the symmetry between the two components. However, their effectiveness remains the same as those in
[
14
]
, as long as
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\omega)
. In the time-domain setting, there are two possible choices of
\alpha
satisfying this criterion, either
\alpha=T_{\max}\Delta t^{-1}
or
\alpha=T_{\max}\partial_{t}
, where the constant
T_{\max}
of time unit is included to render
\alpha
dimensionless
[
11
]
. The latter option comes from the fact that for a very slowly varying function, the time derivative is of order of
\Delta t^{-1}
even when
\Delta t
is relatively large (as can be seen from a Taylor expansion). Please note that if we are solely concerned with conditioning and the loss of accuracy – direct counterparts of the challenges present in the frequency-domain – then either choice suffices. In the next section, time-domain specific problems will prompt us to reconsider this design choice.

It is worth noting that the left rescaling operator
\mathbb{L}
and the dense-mesh preconditioner
\mathbb{T}
are commuting due to the loop-star diagonal structure of
\mathbb{T}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{T}\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}\mathbb{T}_{0}^{s}\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}+\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}\mathbb{T}_{0}^{h}\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}.

Therefore, their positions in (
11
) can be interchanged, leading to a dense-mesh preconditioned version (by
\mathbb{T}
) of the large-timestep regularized TD-PMCHWT formulation in
[
20
]
. The preconditioned and regularized formulation (
11
) is different from the one proposed in
[
24
]
, where the author preconditioned the regularized TD-PMCHWT system by its dual counterpart.

IV-C

Late-Time Stabilization

Up to now, we have eliminated three out of four numerical issues of the TD-PMCHWT formulation: dense-mesh breakdown, large-timestep breakdown, and the loss of solution accuracy at large timesteps. This section discusses a remedy to late-time instability, which was identified as originating from the well-known nullspace of the TD-EFIOs comprising all constant-in-time solenoidal functions
[
31
]
.

Our remedy is to get rid of the nullspace of the TD-EFIOs, by choosing an appropriate rescaling factor
\alpha
in (
7
) and (
10
). More specifically, we set

\alpha=\tilde{\partial}_{t}:=T_{\max}\partial_{t},\qquad\alpha^{-1}=\tilde{\partial}_{t}^{-1}:=\partial^{-1}_{t}/T_{\max},

(12)

where
\partial_{t}^{-1}
is the temporal integration over
(-\infty,t)
and
T_{\max}=D/c_{\min}
, with
c_{\min}:=\min(c,c^{\prime})
. The idea of choosing temporal differentiation and integration as the rescaling factors has been introduced in
[
18
,
19
]
for the TD-EFIE. In the following, we demonstrate how to adapt that approach to the TD-PMCHWT equation. Please note that the low-frequency regularization of the PMCHWT is different from that of the EFIE. In particular, the left rescaling operator of the EFIE is applied on the RWG dual space (as the EFIE is tested by the RWG basis functions)
[
18
]
, whereas that of the PMCHWT acts on the BC spaces. The necessity of using a different regularization scheme for the PMCHWT equation has been explained in
[
14
]
.

Considering the diagonal blocks
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{11}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{22}
in (
11
) that contain the TD-EIFOs
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, with a particular attention to the contributions from
\mathcal{T}
(as
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
can be handled analogously), we have the decomposition

\mathfrak{Z}:=\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}\GGs^{-1}\mathfrak{T}\RRs=\mathfrak{Z}_{ll}+\mathfrak{Z}_{ls}+\mathfrak{Z}_{sl}+\mathfrak{Z}_{ss},

(13)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{ll}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\mathfrak{T}^{s}\PPo^{\Lambda H},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{ls}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\tilde{\partial}_{t}\mathfrak{T}\PPo^{\Sigma},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{sl}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\tilde{\partial}^{-1}_{t}\mathfrak{T}^{s}\PPo^{\Lambda H},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}_{ss}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\mathfrak{T}^{s}\PPo^{\Sigma}.

Here, we took into account the fact
\PPo^{\Lambda H}\mathfrak{T}^{h}=\mathfrak{T}^{h}\PPo^{\Lambda H}=\boldsymbol{0}
. The projector
\PPo^{\Lambda H}
appears in the middle of the last two terms because we leveraged the identity
\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{0}
from
[
19
]
, thereby getting rid of the contributions from the hyper-singular term
\mathfrak{T}^{h}
. One can see that the rescaling temporal operators
\tilde{\partial}_{t}
and
\tilde{\partial}^{-1}_{t}
only come into play in the components
\mathfrak{Z}_{ls}
and
\mathfrak{Z}_{sl}
. In particular,
\tilde{\partial}^{-1}_{t}
cancels the temporal differentiation in
\mathfrak{T}^{s}
. Consequently, the kernel of
\mathfrak{Z}_{sl}
does not contain any non-zero constant-in-time solenoidal functions, hence, neither does
\mathfrak{Z}
’s. More precisely,
\mathfrak{Z}
only has a trivial nullspace. The operator
\tilde{\partial}_{t}
also cancels out the temporal integration in
\mathfrak{T}^{h}
, whose benefit will be clarified in the next section.

To conclude this section, the preconditioned and regularized formulation (
11
), with
\alpha
chosen in (
12
), is immune to ill-conditioning, late-time instability, and the loss of accuracy at large timesteps. Since (
11
) is still time-dependent, a temporal discretization scheme is needed.

V
Discretization and Implementation

V-A

Temporal Discretization

This section presents a temporal discretization of the regularized TD-PMCHWT formulation (
11
). When introducing the new unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
, we have added a temporal integration
\tilde{\partial}_{t}^{-1}
to the stars
\PPo^{\Sigma}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
, while retained their loops
\PPo^{\Lambda H}
. To guarantee the compatibility of the two components of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}
(physical unknowns), we shall discretize the stars and loops of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
by two different sets of temporal basis functions, such that the set for the stars
\PPo^{\Sigma}
is of one order higher than the set for the loops
\PPo^{\Lambda H}
. In this paper, we opt for the lowest-order pair for simplicity.

Let
N_{t}
be the number of timesteps we want to compute for the numerical solution. The auxiliary unknowns
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}
are approximated as follows:

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}(t)\\
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\left({p_{i}(t)\PPo^{\Lambda H}+\,h_{i}(t)\PPo^{\Sigma}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{u}_{i}\\
\vvs_{i}\end{pmatrix},

where the two sets of temporal shifted basis functions

\displaystyle p_{i}(t):=p_{0}(t-i\Delta t),

\displaystyle\quad p_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}1\,\,&-\Delta t<t<0,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle h_{i}(t):=h_{0}(t-i\Delta t),

\displaystyle\quad h_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{\left|{t}\right|}{\Delta t}\,\,&\left|{t}\right|\leq\Delta t,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

with
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{t}
. For the testing, a similar argument applies, with a note that the star component
\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}
in (
10
) contains an extra temporal integration due to the rescaling. This, together with the compatibility property

\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{i}(t)p_{0}(t+\xi)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)h_{0}(t+\xi)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(14)

which holds for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
i=0,1,\ldots,N_{t}
, leads to the testing functions
\tfrac{1}{\Delta t}p_{k}(t)\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}+\delta_{k}(t)\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}
, with
\delta_{k}(t):=\delta(t-k\Delta t)
the Dirac delta distributions and
k=1,2,\ldots,N_{t}
. Denoting
\wws_{i}:=\left({\textbf{u}_{i},\vvs_{i}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}
, the temporal testing of (
11
) yields a lower triangular block matrix system of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\QQs_{0}&&&\\
\QQs_{1}&\QQs_{0}&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\\
\QQs_{N_{t}-1}&\QQs_{N_{t}-2}&\ldots&\QQs_{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\wws_{1}\\
\wws_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\,\wws_{N_{t}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rrs_{1}\\
\rrs_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\rrs_{N_{t}}\end{pmatrix}.

(15)

Please note that this temporal discretization is highly non-standard, since the basis functions do not even possess a tensorial structure. In the following sections, we will compute the matrix blocks
\QQs_{k}
, with
k=0,1,\ldots,N_{t}-1,
and the right-hand side vectors
\rrs_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,N_{t},
using a more conventional scheme. Once the matrix and the right-hand side are computed, the unknowns
\wws_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,N_{t},
can be sequentially obtained by solving the MOT equation

\QQs_{0}\wws_{i}=\rrs_{i}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{i-1}\QQs_{k}\wws_{i-k}.

(16)

This equation can be efficiently solved by iterative solvers (e.g., GMRES solvers), since the matrix
\QQs_{0}
is well-conditioned when the spatial mesh size decreases or the timestep increases, as the result of preconditioning.

After solving the fully discrete system (
15
) for the auxiliary unknowns, a post-processing procedure is performed to obtain the physical current densities. Let
\left({\jjs_{i},\ms_{i}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}
be the expansion coefficient of
\left({\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{j}}(t),\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}(t)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}
corresponding to the temporal basis function
p_{i}(t)
, with
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{t}
. Following
[
11
]
, we can deduce that

\begin{pmatrix}\jjs_{i}\\
\ms_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\PPo^{\Lambda H}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{u}_{i}\\
\vvs_{i}\end{pmatrix}+\,\dfrac{T_{\max}}{\Delta t}\PPo^{\Sigma}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{u}_{i}-\textbf{u}_{i-1}\\
\vvs_{i}-\vvs_{i-1}\end{pmatrix},

(17)

with the convention
\textbf{u}_{0}=\vvs_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
. In fact, the numerical solution computed from (
17
) still suffers from the loss of accuracy as the star component is dominated when
\Delta t\to\infty
. In the far-field computation, the two components
\PPo^{\Lambda H}
and
\PPo^{\Sigma}
of
\jjs_{i}
and
\ms_{i}
should be stored separately to avoid the loss of significant digits.

V-B

Computation of Matrix Blocks

In this section, we describe in detail how to compute the matrix blocks
\QQs_{i}
in (
15
). First, we introduce the following continuously differentiable piecewise-quadratic function:

\displaystyle q_{0}(t)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{0}(\xi)h_{0}(t+\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\,=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\left({\dfrac{t}{\Delta t}+1}\right)^{2}&-\Delta t\leq t<0,\\[5.69046pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{t^{2}}{\Delta t^{2}}+\dfrac{t}{\Delta t}&0\leq t<\Delta t,\\[5.69046pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}\left({\dfrac{t}{\Delta t}-2}\right)^{2}\qquad&\Delta t\leq t\leq 2\Delta t,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This function also satisfies the compatibility

\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{i}(t)h_{0}(t+\xi)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)q_{0}(t+\xi)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(18)

Next, the matrix blocks
\QQs_{i}
in (
15
) are defined by

\QQs_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left({\dfrac{p_{i}(t)}{\Delta t}\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}+\delta_{i}(t)\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}}\right)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({p_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Lambda H}+h_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Sigma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

with
i=0,1,\ldots,N_{t}-1,
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}
the block matrix in (
11
), i.e.,

\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{11}&\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{12}\\
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{21}&\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Analogous to (
13
),
\QQs_{i}
can be decomposed into the following four matrices based on the left and right multipliers:

\displaystyle\QQs_{i}^{ll}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{i}(t)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({p_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Lambda H}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({h_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Lambda H}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\QQs_{i}^{ls}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{i}(t)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({h_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Sigma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({q_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Sigma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\QQs_{i}^{sl}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({p_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Lambda H}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\QQs_{i}^{ss}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}\left({h_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Sigma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

In the first two terms, we used the properties (
14
) and (
18
). Please note that each of the four components of
\QQs_{i}
is a
2\times 2
block matrix, inheriting the structure of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}
.

Now, let us look deeper into each block of each matrix. In particular, we demonstrate how to compute
\QQs_{i}^{ll},\QQs_{i}^{ls},\QQs_{i}^{sl}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}
in practice. We note that the diagonal blocks
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{11}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{22}
of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}
only involve the TD-EFIOs
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, whereas the off-diagonal blocks
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{12}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}_{21}
only involve
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, we focus on the contributions from
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{K}
only. The corresponding counterparts of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
are tagged with a superscript
′
.

V-B
1
The matrices
\QQs_{i}^{ll}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}

The contributions from
\mathcal{T}
(to the diagonal blocks) of these matrices are in fact the temporal discretization of
\mathfrak{Z}_{ll}
and
\mathfrak{Z}_{ss}
in (
13
), respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle\ZZs_{i}^{ll}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\mathfrak{T}^{s}\left({h_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Lambda H}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\ZZs_{i}^{ss}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\mathfrak{T}^{s}\left({h_{0}(t)\PPo^{\Sigma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

In practice, it is unnecessary (and probably impossible) to firstly discretize
\mathcal{T}^{s}
in space to get
\mathfrak{T}^{s}
, then discretize
\mathfrak{T}^{s}
in time. Instead, one can perform a collocation-in-time Galerkin-in-space discretization of
\mathcal{T}^{s}
. Let
\TTs_{i}
be the space-time discretization of
\mathcal{T}^{s}
, whose elements are defined by

\displaystyle\left[\TTs_{i}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\mathcal{T}^{s}\left({h_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\ =\left.\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\mathcal{T}^{s}\left({h_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\right|_{t=i\Delta t},

with
m,n=1,2,\ldots,N_{e}
. The matrices
\ZZs_{i}^{ll}
and
\ZZs_{i}^{ss}
can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\ZZs_{i}^{ll}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\TTs_{i}\PPo^{\Lambda H},

\displaystyle\ZZs_{i}^{ss}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\TTs_{i}\PPo^{\Sigma}.

Analogously, we define the matrix
\KKs_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\left[\KKs_{i}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\mathcal{K}\left({h_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\ =\left.\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\mathcal{K}\left({h_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\right|_{t=i\Delta t},

with
m,n=1,2,\ldots,N_{e}
. The contributions from
\mathcal{K}
to the matrices
\QQs_{i}^{ll}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}
respectively read

\displaystyle\MMs_{i}^{ll}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\KKs_{i}\PPo^{\Lambda H},

\displaystyle\MMs_{i}^{ss}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\KKs_{i}\PPo^{\Sigma}.

Finally, taking into account the contributions from
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
, the matrices
\QQs_{i}^{ll}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}
are constructed as follows:

\displaystyle\QQs_{i}^{ll}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\eta\TTs_{i}+\eta^{\prime}{\TTs_{i}}^{\prime}&-\KKs_{i}-\KKs_{i}^{\prime}\\
\KKs_{i}+\KKs_{i}^{\prime}&\frac{1}{\eta}\TTs_{i}+\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}{\TTs_{i}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\PPo^{\Lambda H},

\displaystyle\QQs_{i}^{ss}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\begin{pmatrix}\eta\TTs_{i}+\eta^{\prime}{\TTs_{i}}^{\prime}&-\KKs_{i}-\KKs_{i}^{\prime}\\
\KKs_{i}+\KKs_{i}^{\prime}&\frac{1}{\eta}\TTs_{i}+\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}{\TTs_{i}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\PPo^{\Sigma}.

It is noteworthy that
\QQs_{i}^{ll}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}
do not exhibit an infinite tail, since
\mathcal{T}^{s}
and
\mathcal{K}
do not contain temporal integration. In particular,
\QQs_{i}^{ll}=\QQs_{i}^{ss}=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i>k_{\max}:=\left\lceil\tfrac{T_{\max}}{\Delta t}\right\rceil
.

V-B
2
The matrix
\QQs_{i}^{ls}

Analogous to
\QQs_{i}^{ll}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}
, we introduce the following matrices:

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\TTs}_{i}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle:=T_{\max}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\partial_{t}\mathcal{T}\left({q_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\ =T_{\max}\left.\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\partial_{t}\mathcal{T}\left({q_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\right|_{t=i\Delta t},

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\KKs}_{i}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle:=T_{\max}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\partial_{t}\mathcal{K}\left({q_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\ =T_{\max}\left.\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\partial_{t}\mathcal{K}\left({q_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\right|_{t=i\Delta t},

with
m,n=1,2,\ldots,N_{e}
. Then, the matrix
\QQs_{i}^{ls}
is given by

\QQs_{i}^{ls}=\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\eta\widehat{\TTs}_{i}+\eta^{\prime}\widehat{\TTs}_{i}^{\prime}&-\widehat{\KKs}_{i}-\widehat{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}\\
\widehat{\KKs}_{i}+\widehat{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}&\frac{1}{\eta}\widehat{\TTs}_{i}+\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}\widehat{\TTs}_{i}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\PPo^{\Sigma}.

These matrices also do not exhibit an infinite tail because the temporal integration in
\mathcal{T}^{h}
is canceled out by the temporal differentiation in the rescaling factor
\tilde{\partial}_{t}
. However,
\QQs_{i}^{ls}=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i>k_{\max}+1
rather than
i>k_{\max}
for
\QQs_{i}^{ll}
and
\QQs_{i}^{ss}
, as the support of
q_{0}(t)
exceeds that of
h_{0}(t)
by
\Delta t
.

V-B
3
The matrix
\QQs_{i}^{sl}

Let us denote

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle\ =\dfrac{1}{T_{\max}}\left.\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\partial_{t}^{-1}\mathcal{T}^{s}\left({p_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\right|_{t=i\Delta t},

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}\right]_{mn}

\displaystyle\ =\dfrac{1}{T_{\max}}\left.\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},\partial_{t}^{-1}\mathcal{K}\left({p_{0}(t)\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right)}\right\rangle\right|_{t=i\Delta t},

with
m,n=1,2,\ldots,N_{e}
. The matrix
\QQs_{i}^{sl}
is given by

\QQs_{i}^{sl}=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\begin{pmatrix}\eta\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}+\eta^{\prime}{\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}}^{\prime}&-\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}-\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}\\
\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}+\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}&\frac{1}{\eta}\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}+\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}{\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\PPo^{\Lambda H}.

Whereas the matrices
\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}^{\prime}
do not exhibit an infinite tail (i.e.,
\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}=\widetilde{\TTs}_{i}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i>k_{\max}-1
),
\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}
do because
\partial_{t}^{-1}\mathcal{K}
and
\partial_{t}^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
contain the temporal integration. It is now shown that by virtue of the serendipitous interplay between the temporal rescaling operators on one hand, and the Helmholtz splitting on the other, this tail eventually does not lead to an infinite number of non-zero matrices
\QQs_{i}^{sl}
. Indeed, we note that

\partial_{t}^{-1}p_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}0&t\leq-\Delta t,\\
t+\Delta t\qquad&-\Delta t<t\leq 0,\\
\Delta t&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It implies the constant tail
\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}=\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{\Delta t}{T_{\max}}\mathbb{K}_{0}
for all
i>k_{\max}-1
, where

\left[\mathbb{K}_{0}\right]_{mn}=\left\langle{\textbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{m},K_{0}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}}\right\rangle,

with
m,n=1,2,\ldots,N_{e}
, and the static MFIE operator

(K_{0}\boldsymbol{j})(\boldsymbol{x})=\textbf{n}\times p.v.\int_{\Gamma}\textbf{{curl}}_{\xxs}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{y})}{4\pi\left|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}}\right|}\,\mathrm{d}s_{\boldsymbol{y}}.

In fact,
K_{0}
is the limit of the frequency-domain MFIE operator
K_{\kappa}
when the wavenumber
\kappa\to 0
. Hence, one can deduce from the large-timestep scaling of the off-diagonal blocks of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{Q}}
(by setting
\omega=0
) that

\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\mathbb{K}_{0}\PPo^{\Lambda H}=\boldsymbol{0}.

This property can also be deduced from
[
33
]
. Combining all properties together, we have

\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}\PPo^{\Lambda H}=\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\widetilde{\KKs}_{i}^{\prime}\PPo^{\Lambda H}=\boldsymbol{0},

(19)

for all
i>k_{\max}-1
. Therefore,
\QQs_{i}^{sl}=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i>k_{\max}-1
. In practice, the integrals are typically approximated using quadrature rules, which introduces numerical errors into the interaction matrices. As a result, the matrices in (
19
) may not vanish. To address this, they should be explicitly set to zero, thereby reducing both computational time and memory usage. Numerical experiments further show that this strategy also improves the accuracy and stability of the solution.

V-C

Computation of Right-Hand Side

The right-hand side vectors in (
15
) are defined by

\rrs_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left({\dfrac{p_{i}(t)}{\Delta t}\mathbb{P}^{\Sigma H}+\delta_{i}(t)\mathbb{P}^{\Lambda}}\right)\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}\GGs^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}(t)\\
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(20)

with
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{t},
which consist of the loop contribution

\rrs_{i}^{l}:=\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbb{T}^{\Sigma H}\GGs^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{i}(t)\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}(t)\\
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t,

and the star contribution

\displaystyle\rrs_{i}^{s}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{T_{\max}}\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{i}(t)\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}(t)\\
\partial_{t}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\ =\dfrac{1}{T_{\max}}\mathbb{T}^{\Lambda}\GGs^{-1}\PPo^{\Lambda H}\left.\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{e}}(t)\\
\partial_{t}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right|_{t=i\Delta t}.

Let us assume the incident transient fields
\boldsymbol{e}^{in}
and
\boldsymbol{h}^{in}
are Gaussian-in-time plane wave, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}^{in}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{4A}{w\sqrt{\pi}}\boldsymbol{p}\exp\left({-\left({\dfrac{4}{w}\left({c(t-t_{0})-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\right)^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}^{in}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}\times\boldsymbol{e}^{in}(\boldsymbol{x},t),

(21)

where
A
is the amplitude,
\boldsymbol{p}
is the polarization,
\boldsymbol{k}
is the direction,
w
is the width, and
t_{0}
is the time of arrival. It is clear that the loop component
\rrs_{i}^{l}
exponentially decays as the incident fields at late times, whereas the star component
\rrs_{i}^{s}
exponentially approaches a constant since

\dfrac{4A}{w\sqrt{\pi}}\boldsymbol{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left({-\left({\dfrac{4}{w}\left({c(t-t_{0})-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\right)^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\dfrac{A}{c}\boldsymbol{p}.

In fact, the approaching constant is also zero, as the gradient of a constant field is zero. In practice, however,
\rrs_{i}^{s}
may not decay due to numerical errors and should be explicitly set to zero to yield stable and accurate solutions
[
44
]
. In the time-domain implementation, it can easily be done by truncating the star component
\rrs_{i}^{s}
at late times, a procedure reminiscent of the truncation of the off-diagonal blocks of
\QQs_{i}^{sl}
.

In conclusion, (
15
) is the preconditioned and regularized TD-PMCHWT formulation (shorten as qHP TD-PMCHWT), with the right-hand side vectors defined by (
20
) and the matrix blocks computed as follows:

\QQs_{i}=\QQs_{i}^{ll}+\QQs_{i}^{ls}+\QQs_{i}^{sl}+\QQs_{i}^{ss},

where
\QQs_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i>k_{\max}+1
.

VII
Conclusion

We have proposed a new time-domain formulation for electromagnetic scattering by dielectric objects, building upon the TD-PMCHWT equation and the loop-star decomposition via quasi-Helmholtz projectors. A multiplicative diagonal preconditioner, based on a modified static EFIO, was introduced to eliminate the dense-mesh breakdown of the TD-PMCHWT equation. The large-timestep breakdown and the loss of solution accuracy were simultaneously mitigated through a rescaling procedure, while incorporating temporal integration and differentiation as rescaling factors effectively suppressed the notorious late-time instability. An appropriate temporal testing scheme was applied, with particular attention to practical implementation. Numerical experiments across various scenarios confirmed the accuracy, stability, and robustness of the proposed formulation. The robustness against numerical quadrature errors becomes even more critical when employing compression techniques, such as plane-wave time-domain algorithms, as they unavoidably introduce errors that are similar to quadrature errors.

Future research may explore advanced discretization schemes to further enhance performance, such as higher-order spatial and temporal basis functions or time discretizations based on convolution quadrature.
