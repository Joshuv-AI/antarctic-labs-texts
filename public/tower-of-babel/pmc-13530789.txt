Poster 08. Changes in Muscle Volume Asymmetries Among 34 Lower Extremity Muscles from Baseline Through 12 Months After Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With Quadriceps Tendon Autograft
Authors: Andrew Sheean, Keith A Knurr, Mikalyn DeFoor, Matthew B Blomquist, Joshua Shaw, Marcus Blaylock, Joshua McKinley, Daniel J Cognetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Recovery of lower extremity muscle function following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is a critical focus of rehabilitation. Specifically, the quadriceps muscles receive the most attention, given the abundance of literature documenting muscle atrophy, inhibition, and reduced torque generating capacity that are present post-operatively and frequently persists beyond completion of standard rehabilitation. Despite significant focus on the quadriceps muscles, there is limited evidence highlighting how other lower extremity muscles are impacted post-ACLR. It is plausible that early global inactivity post-operatively and persistent alterations in movement mechanics across tasks post-ACLR may contribute to muscle adaptations throughout the lower extremity. Supporting this theory, a recent cross-sectional study of NCAA Division I athletes 2 years post-ACLR observed gastrocnemius atrophy and fibularis hypertrophy in addition to quadriceps atrophy as has been observed in previous reports. Understanding the trajectory of recovery of all lower extremity muscle volumes may better inform the timing in which hypertrophy-based interventions should be employed or if certain muscle groups beyond the quadriceps should be directly targeted in rehabilitation. The purpose of this study was to characterize lower extremity muscle volume changes from baseline, preoperative through 12 months post-ACLR.Methods:This study is a secondary analysis of an Institutional Review Board approved, registered clinical trial (NCT04519801). Patients between the age of 15-45 with an ACL rupture confirmed via physical exam and knee MRI, planning to undergo ACLR using a quadriceps tendon autograft, were recruited as part of a randomized controlled trial comparing two approaches to early post-ACLR rehabilitation. Patients were excluded if they had revision ACLR, bilateral knee injuries, multiligament knee injuries, and/or concomitant procedures at the time of ACLR that necessitated post-operative weight bearing restrictions. Coronal scout and axial spin-echo T1-weighted MRI scans of both lower extremities from the 12th thoracic vertebra to below the ankle joint were performed within 9 days of ACLR and at 3 and 12 months post-ACLR using a 3-T Signa LX MRI (GE Healthcare). A novel, 3D computer-learning AI algorithm for automatic muscle segmentation platform was used to derive bilateral muscle volumes of 34 lower extremity muscles (Springbok Analytics). Standardized mean differences (SMD) between-limbs in muscle volume were calculated (surgical - non-surgical) and associated 95% confidence intervals are visualized, with significance assessed using paired t-tests with α = 0.05. Linear mixed effects models were used to assess changes in between-limb differences over time with Tukey adjusted p-values reported.Results:Twenty-five patients completed lower extremity MRIs at pre-surgery, 3, and 12 months post-ACLR. Patient demographics can be found in Table 1. Figure 1 shows a representative two-dimensional visualization of the three-dimensional muscle volume segmentation over the three time periods. At the cohort level, significant surgical limb deficits were present in 6/34 (17.6%) muscles at pre-injury, 16/34 (47%) muscles at 3 months post-op, and 10/34 (29.4%) muscles at 12 months post-op (Figure 2). Deficits in surgical limb vasti muscles were most pronounced across all time periods (SMD = pre-surgery: 0.4-0.6; 3 months: 1.1-1.2; 12 months: 0.9, all p-values < 0.03). Significant surgical limb deficits in medial and lateral gastrocnemius muscles were also identified at all time periods (SMD = pre-surgery: 0.3-0.4; 3 months: 0.6-0.7; 12 months: 0.4-0.5, all p-values < 0.03). The surgical limb flexor digitorum longus was significantly larger than the non-surgical limb at pre-surgery (SMD = 0.26, p = 0.007) and the surgical limb gluteus maximus was significantly larger than the non-surgical limb at 12 months post-ACLR (SMD = 0.18, p = 0.045). Longitudinal changes for all muscles that had a significant between-limb difference are visualized in Figure 3, which highlights the persistent atrophy of the quadriceps and gastrocnemius over time.Conclusions:Surgical limb lower extremity muscle volume deficits were present throughout the first 12 months post-ACLR, most notably in the quadriceps muscles and the gastrocnemius. Surgical limb muscle volume deficits were most pronounced at 3 months post-ACLR, indicating potential disuse atrophy throughout the lower extremity in the early post-operative period. While the magnitude of asymmetry reduced from 3 to 12 months post-ACLR, significant asymmetries persisted. Greater surgical limb gluteus maximus muscle volume at 12 months post-ACLR may be representative of potential compensatory muscle hypertrophy and a result of altered movement patterns that place a greater reliance on the hip extensors to compensate impaired quadriceps function. These findings can be used to develop targeted interventions to improve quadriceps muscle function and restore gastrocnemius muscle volume symmetry.
