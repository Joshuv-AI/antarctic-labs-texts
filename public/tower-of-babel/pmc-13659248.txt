Fetal Brain Abnormalities in Trisomy 21 and Associated Neurodevelopmental Outcome: Key Factors to Identify Differences in Neurodevelopmental Outcome?
Authors: Christina Haberl, Pilar Palmrich, Eva Karner, Patric Kienast, Sarah Glatter, Florian Heinzl, Philipp Moser, Daniela Prayer, Gregor Kasprian, Julia Binder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTrisomy 21 (T21) is associated with various neurological impairments. However, the mechanisms of fetal brain development in T21 and their impact on neurodevelopmental outcomes remain unclear, limiting prenatal counseling. Therefore, this study aims to assess neuropathological changes in fetuses with T21 and the associated neurodevelopmental outcome.MethodThis was a case‐control study comparing brain patterns in neurosonography and MRI of fetuses with T21 to healthy controls. Additionally, neurodevelopmental outcome was assessed prospectively. Thereby, an outcome score was calculated to identify brain patterns linked to unfavorable neurodevelopmental outcomes.Results108 fetuses were included consisting of 54 fetuses with T21 and 54 controls. In the study group, significantly more fetal brain abnormalities were found compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Brain abnormalities in T21 most commonly included ventriculomegaly (24.1%), cerebellar hypoplasia (22.2%) and widening of subarachnoid fluid spaces (20.4%). Although the neurodevelopmental score showed no significant differences based on the presence of brain abnormalities, fetuses with widened subarachnoid fluid spaces or cerebellar hypoplasia were solely associated with an unfavorable outcome score.ConclusionFetuses with T21 show significantly more brain abnormalities compared with healthy controls, which should be considered for prenatal counseling. Some specific brain abnormalities were demonstrated only in the group with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes, such as cerebellar hypoplasia and widened subarachnoid fluid spaces. However, the exploratory nature of the analysis must be considered, and additional studies are highly needed.

Introduction

Trisomy 21 (T21) is the most frequent autosomal aneuploidy compatible with life [1]. In Europe 10.1 per 10.000 livebirths are diagnosed with T21 [2, 3], causing multiple organ dysfunctions and abnormalities [4]. Although some T21‐associated abnormalities, including cardiac, gastrointestinal, immunological, or endocrine abnormalities, are well described, neurological impairment in T21 is a heterogeneous condition and not yet completely understood [5]. Associated neurological disorders include muscular hypotonia resulting in feeding problems in early life, epilepsy, intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorders [5]. Furthermore, T21 is significantly associated with the development of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and early‐onset dementia [6, 7]. Life expectancy of affected individuals has significantly risen as treatment strategies for many conditions associated with T21 have improved drastically [8]. This shifts the scientific focus to health aspects particularly related to quality of life, such as intellectual abilities and the risk of Alzheimer's disease later in life. However, data on brain development and brain alterations in T21 are scarce.

Imaging studies on adolescent brains affected by T21 showed significant differences in the overall brain volume and alterations in the cortical thickness or the structure of the hippocampus [9, 10, 11]. Fukami‐Gartner et al. identified multiple brain alterations in T21 neonates in a volumetric phenotyping study, including reduced total brain, white matter, frontal, and occipital volumes, alongside enlarged temporal, parietal, deep gray matter, and lateral ventricles [12]. Serrano et al. demonstrated in a T21 mouse model that 26 out of 72 investigated brain regions showed significant alterations compared with non‐affected controls [13]. These changes may be present as early as in the fetal period as some studies have demonstrated significant differences in the fetal brain [14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

However, at present, a prenatal prediction of future neurological development in children diagnosed with T21 is not possible due to the lack of sufficient follow‐up data. Studies focusing on brain abnormalities and subsequent neurological outcomes are highly needed to enhance prenatal counseling.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to qualitatively assess the brain structure of fetuses diagnosed with T21 by neurosonography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) compared with a healthy control group. Moreover, in order to investigate the potential of fetal brain imaging in the prediction of neurological outcome, the neurodevelopmental outcome of affected fetuses was assessed.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a case‐control study comparing cases with confirmed genetic diagnosis of T21 who underwent both fetal neurosonography and MRI assessment at the Medical University of Vienna (MUV), with healthy controls. All participants underwent detailed fetal neurosonography at the Fetal Medicine Unit of the MUV and fetal MRI at the Division of Neuroradiology and Musculoskeletal Radiology of the MUV from May 2007 until January 2023. This study consisted of both retrospective and prospective phases. In the retrospective phase, prenatal ultrasound and MRI images of the fetal brain were reviewed, and data related to the fetal brain were extracted from the obstetric database Viewpoint (version 5.6.28.56, Wessling, Germany).

In the prospective phase, telephone interviews were conducted with the families of individuals whose prenatal ultrasound and MRI images had been analyzed to obtain additional information on neurodevelopmental outcomes. This dual approach enabled the integration of imaging data with parent‐reported outcomes. Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: fetuses with a confirmed genetic diagnosis of T21, either prenatally through invasive testing or postnatally; and fetuses without genetic disorders, matched for gestational age at the time of fetal MRI and neurosonography, who underwent fetal neurosonography and fetal MRI assessments after 18 weeks of gestation. Indications for MRI in the control group were mainly extracerebral structural malformations such as abdominal wall defects, maternal disease, or preterm premature rupture of membranes. Control fetuses either underwent invasive genetic assessment by karyotyping and microarray prenatally or were clinically reviewed for genetic disorders postnatally to exclude the presence of genetic disorders. The study was approved by the local research ethics committee (approval number 1865/2019). Maternal and neonatal data were obtained from medical record review using the obstetrical electronic database (Viewpoint 5.6.28.56, Wessling, Germany).

Fetal Neurosonography and Fetal MRI

Fetal neurosonography was performed according to the ISUOG guidelines [19] by fetal medicine specialists in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. The assessment also included fetal biometry with an emphasis on fetal head measurements including biparietal diameter (BPD), fronto‐occipital diameter (FOD), head circumference (HC), posterior ventricle width, transcerebellar diameter (TCD) and cisterna magna (CM) width. All ultrasound scans were performed using a Voluson E8 or E10 ultrasound machine (GE Medical Systems, Zipf, Austria) equipped with a 2–8‐MHz 3D transabdominal and 6–12‐MHz 3D transvaginal transducer.

Fetal MRI was performed according to the ISUOG guidelines [20] using a 1.5 T (Ingenia; Philips Medical Systems) or 3 T (Achieva; Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) scanner. A body coil was used and orthogonal T2‐weighted turbo‐spin echo images were obtained with the following specifications: acquired in‐plane resolution ranging from 0.62/0.62 mm to 1/1 mm with a slice thickness of 2.0–4.4 mm, matrix of 256 × 256, field of view of 200–230 mm, relaxation time of ≤ 20,000 ms, and echo time of 100–140 ms. All MRIs were retrospectively re‐assessed by neuroradiologists with high expertise in fetal MRI (G.K., D.P.).

Brain abnormalities were categorized in the following groups: malformations of the ventricular system including ventriculomegaly (generally defined as a ventricular width ≥ 10 mm; ventriculomegaly was further categorized into mild ventriculomegaly (10–12 mm), moderate ventriculomegaly (13–15 mm) and severe ventriculomegaly (> 15 mm)) [21] and ventricular asymmetry (specified as discordance in ventricle size of ≥ 2 mm) [22, 23], malformations of the corpus callosum (CC) such as agenesis of the CC and dysgenesis of the CC (reference ranges by Harreld et al. were used to assess CC size [24], dysgenesis of the CC was diagnosed based on the international Delphi consensus criteria [25]), malformations of the posterior fossa including cerebellar hypoplasia (defined as a transverse cerebellar diameter (TCD) below the fifth percentile according to the reference ranges established by Snijders et al. [26]) and vermis hypoplasia (defined as a vermian height or volume below the fifth percentile according to Cignini et al. [27]), widening of the subarachnoid fluid spaces (assessed according to Watanabe et al. [28]), abnormalities of the cortical development [29, 30] and hippocampal malrotation [31]. Associated malformations in fetuses with T21 such as congenital heart defects (CHD) or duodenal stenosis were also recorded.

Neurodevelopmental Outcome Questionnaire

All families with a prenatally confirmed or suspected, and postnatally confirmed diagnosis of T21 who continued the pregnancy and had a detailed fetal brain assessment were invited to participate in a telephone‐based neurodevelopmental outcome questionnaire. The questionnaire, developed in collaboration with pediatric neurologists, included questions about the child's current care needs, therapies, and schooling, as well as several key neurodevelopmental milestones based on recently published developmental charts for children with T21 [32] and is displayed in the supplementary material. All interviews were conducted by trained physicians.

From the collected data, a comprehensive outcome score was calculated using five parameters: the three most reported milestones in both groups (sitting without support, walking independently and using one or two words), the need for special support in daily life (yes or no) and the attending school or kindergarten type (public, inclusive or special education). Each parameter was scored according to predefined criteria: milestones achieved within the expected age range based on the defined Down syndrome milestones published by Baumer et al. [32] received 0 points, and delayed or not achieved milestones received 1 point. Similarly, children who did not require special support received 0 points, while those who did require support received 1 point. For school type, 0 points were assigned to public school attendance, 1 point to inclusive schools, and 2 points to special education schools. Finally, the total score was divided by the number of parameters specified. Consequently, higher scores indicated poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes. The mean value of all children's scores served as the threshold to classify outcomes as “favorable” or “unfavorable.”

All families provided oral and written informed consent before the telephone interview.

Network Analysis

Network data were analyzed with Gephi—Graph Visualization and Manipulation Software (Version 0.10) as an undirected graph. The “Fruchterman Reingold” layout was selected for visualization. Node size was scaled based on weighted degree. Edge thickness was scaled according to edge weight. Network statistics were computed by Gephi's statistic panel (graph density; average degree; average weighted degree, centrality).

Statistical Analysis

The IBM SPSS software platform (SPSS 23.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics are used to summarize demographic data. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed or as median and interquartile ranges if not normally distributed. Categorical data are presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. To test the hypothesis, groups were compared by student's T‐tests (numerical data) or Chi‐squared test (categorical data). A two‐sided p‐value below 0.05 was assumed to be statistically significant. p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 108 fetuses were included, 54 fetuses with genetically confirmed T21 (T21) and 54 healthy controls. All included fetuses underwent prenatal ultrasound assessment by a fetal medicine expert and at least one fetal MRI. Maternal characteristics did not show any significant differences except for maternal age, which was significantly (p < 0.001) higher in women carrying a fetus with T21 than in control subjects. There were no significant differences in maternal body mass index or gestational age (GA) at imaging assessments (see Table 1).

Study Group

In the T21 group, 63.0% of fetuses with suspected T21 (n = 34) had invasive testing during pregnancy to confirm the diagnosis, 24.1% (n = 13) had genetic testing via non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) only with genetic confirmation of diagnosis postnatally. 14.8% (n = 8) had both prenatal genetic testing methods during pregnancy. 13% (n = 7) had no genetic testing during pregnancy and were tested postnatally. Mean gestational age (± SD) at diagnosis was 21.71 (± 4.96) gestational weeks ranging from 11.71 to 34.26 gestational weeks. Nine women (16.7%) opted for termination of pregnancy after receiving the diagnosis of fetal T21. Termination of pregnancy was conducted at a mean GA (± SD) of 22.20 (± 1.94) gestational weeks and was performed through induction in five cases, and with previous fetocide in four women.

Structural Imaging Findings in Fetal Neurosonography and Fetal MRI

37 of the 54 fetuses with T21 (68.5%) presented with at least one fetal brain abnormality, compared with only 4 cases (7.4%) in the control group (p < 0.001).

In the study group detected brain abnormalities included: ventriculomegaly (13 cases, 24.1%), hypoplasia of the CC (7 cases, 13.0%), widening of subarachnoid fluid spaces (11 cases, 20.4%), cerebellar hypoplasia (12 cases, 22.2%), hypoplasia of the vermis cerebelli (7 cases, 13.0%) and malrotation of the hippocampi (4 cases, 7.4%; see Figure 1). Other structural neuroimaging findings included asymmetry of the lateral ventricles (7 cases, 13.0%) and abnormalities of cortical development (3 cases, 5.6%). Abnormality of the cortical development included one case with abnormal configuration of the temporal lobe, one case with delayed opercularisation and one case with thinned brain parenchyma and delayed opercularisation. Other Fetal Abnormalities

Within the T21 group, the most common anomalies were CHD in 46 cases (85.2%), duodenal atresia or stenosis in six cases (11.1%), esophageal atresia in two cases (3.7%), fetal growth restriction (FGR) in 16 cases (29.6%) and renal anomalies in seven cases (13.0%). Among T21 fetuses with a diagnosed brain abnormality, 35 (94.6%) also showed other malformations: most frequently cardiac defects (n = 30, 81.1%), while only two had isolated brain abnormalities, both presenting with ventriculomegaly of the lateral ventricles. All T21 fetuses without brain abnormalities had at least one additional malformation (n = 17, 100%). The difference in the presence of additional malformations between T21 fetuses with and without brain abnormalities was not statistically significant (p = 0.329, see Table 4).

Neonatal Outcomes

In total, intrauterine fetal death (IUD) occurred in 2 cases (1.9%); other complications among live born children included: preterm delivery (n = 27, 27.8%), postnatal neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission (n = 41, 42.3%), neonatal death within one week after delivery (n = 2, 2.1%) and neonatal death more than one week after delivery (n = 6, 6.2%). Pregnancies with T21 had significantly higher rates of preterm delivery (41.9% vs. 16.7%; p = 0.006), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission (67.4% vs. 22.2%; p < 0.001) and neonatal death more than 1 week after delivery (14.0% vs. 0%; p = 0.005). IUD and neonatal death rates within 1 week after delivery were similar in both groups (p = 0.146, p = 0.870). The mean number of days children with T21 was admitted to the hospital within the first year of life was 45.93 days ranging from 3 to 166 days. Children in the study group underwent a mean of two surgical procedures during their first year of life (range: 0–8).

Neurodevelopmental Outcomes

Thirty six families had children with diagnosed T21 who were alive at the time of the telephone interview. 23 families (63.9%) opted to answer the neurodevelopmental questionnaire (see Figure 3). All 23 fetuses had additional anomalies including CHD in 22 cases (95.7%). 13 children (56.5%) were diagnosed with a brain abnormality prenatally. The rate of prenatal brain abnormalities among eligible but non‐included children (78.6%) did not differ significantly from that of the included cohort (p = 0.173). The same also applies to the number of days spent in the hospital and the number of surgical procedures (p = 0.894, p = 0.889). The median (IQR) age of the children at the time of the questionnaire was 5.0 (3.0–10.0) years.

The mean outcome score (± SD) was 0.56 (± 0.28). In the group of children with T21 and diagnosed brain abnormality the mean outcome score (± SD) was 0.58 (± 0.32), whereas in the group of children with T21 and no brain abnormality the mean outcome score (± SD) was 0.55 (± 0.25) showing no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.796).

Focusing on the group of children with T21 and brain abnormalities, 6 children (46.15%) showed an unfavorable neurodevelopmental outcome. Children with unfavorable neurodevelopmental outcomes were diagnosed with cerebellar hypoplasia in three cases, with one case showing additional ventriculomegaly of the lateral ventricles, hypoplastic CC, abnormal cortical development and abnormal subarachnoid fluid spaces. Isolated widening of the subarachnoid fluid spaces was diagnosed in two cases and isolated asymmetry of the lateral ventricles in one case. Children with favorable neurodevelopmental outcome (n = 7) showed isolated asymmetry of the lateral ventricles in three cases, isolated hypoplastic CC in one case and isolated hypoplastic vermis cerebelli in one case. Two cases in the favorable group showed more than one abnormality of the fetal brain, which was described as follows: one case with a hypoplastic CC associated with ventriculomegaly of the lateral ventricles and malposition of the hippocampi, the other case showed ventriculomegaly of the lateral ventricles combined with malposition of the hippocampi. Interestingly, no cases of cerebellar hypoplasia or abnormality of the subarachnoid spaces were reported in the group with favorable neurodevelopmental outcome.

The phenotype co‐occurrence network (see Figure 5) associated with unfavorable outcomes comprised 10 nodes and 33 edges, yielding a markedly dense topology (graph density = 0.733; average degree = 6.6; average weighted degree = 7.6). Central, highly interconnected phenotypes in this network included ventriculomegaly of the lateral ventricles, corpus callosum abnormality (general), partial agenesis of the corpus callosum (pACC), and abnormality of the subarachnoidal fluid spaces, each of which co‐occurred with the majority of other phenotypes in the network, including cerebellar and posterior fossa abnormalities. In contrast, the network associated with favorable outcomes was smaller and substantially sparser, comprising 8 nodes and 12 edges (graph density = 0.429; average degree = 3; average weighted degree = 3.75).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess neuropathological changes associated with T21 using both fetal neurosonography and MRI and to analyze the associated neurodevelopmental outcome by calculating an outcome score. Significant structural cerebral alterations were identified in 68.5% of fetuses diagnosed with T21. Interestingly, some specific patterns of fetal brain malformation were observed exclusively in children with unfavorable neurodevelopmental outcome, even though outcome scores did not differ significantly between children with and without brain abnormalities and T21.

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first comprehensive in vivo imaging study assessing the brain of fetuses diagnosed with T21 prenatally, together with a neurodevelopmental follow up several years after birth.

Even though direct comparable literature is lacking, our results are in line with published postmortem studies on fetuses with diagnosed T21 [33, 34], who described significant alterations in fetal brain morphology such as: abnormal lateral ventricles, posterior fossa and hippocampi. Recently, Pooh et al. published a comprehensive neurosonography study on fetuses with diagnosed T21, introducing two new features seen in the brains of T21 fetuses such as a thinned subplate and the seagull sign [18]. The latter, describing an abnormal appearance of the fetal cerebral cortex due to altered brain maturation in fetuses with T21, seems to be consistent with the changes in the subarachnoid space observed in our study, potentially indicating impaired brain volume [18].

The proposed neurodevelopmental outcome score based on neurodevelopmental milestones, need for special support in daily life and attending school type showed no significant differences between children with and without fetal brain abnormalities and T21. However, within the group of children with T21 and brain abnormalities we were able to identify specific patterns of fetal brain malformation potentially leading to unfavorable neurological outcomes. 50.0% of the cases with brain abnormalities and unfavorable outcomes showed cerebellar hypoplasia and another 33.3% showed isolated widening of the subarachnoid fluid spaces. In contrast, there were no cases of cerebellar hypoplasia or abnormal subarachnoid fluid spaces in the group with a favorable neurodevelopmental outcome. This suggests that the cerebellum and the subarachnoid fluid spaces potentially indicating impaired brain volume when enlarged might play a key role in the neurodevelopment of children with T21, and underscores the importance of detailed neuroimaging in T21 fetuses to better predict future neurodevelopment. However, due to the exploratory character of the study, further studies to verify this finding are needed.

Interestingly, no differences in the need for special support in daily life or neurodevelopmental therapy and other milestones affecting gross motor development could be observed between children with and without associated fetal brain abnormalities and T21. Moreover, poor neurodevelopmental outcome scores were also shown in children without prenatally detected brain abnormalities.

This might be explained by influences on neurodevelopment through the presence of CHD and known in‐utero changes as well as subsequent pediatric heart surgeries [35, 36]. As shown in the present study, T21 is well known to be associated with a range of malformations, most commonly CHD, which leads to impaired neurodevelopment [37, 38]. The high majority of fetuses with an additional CHD in this study could have led to a masking of the effects of fetal brain abnormalities on neurological outcomes in fetuses with T21. Additionally, individualized therapy and neurodevelopmental support of children with T21 after birth might also be an important influencing factor leading to better neurological outcomes postnatally.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study assessing fetal neuropathological alterations by fetal neurosonography and fetal MRI combined with postnatal neurodevelopmental assessment and calculation of an outcome score in fetuses with confirmed T21. The observed period of over 15 years enabled the analysis of a comparatively large cohort of fetuses and children with T21. Furthermore, the prospective study arm provided insights into the long‐term neurodevelopment of the included children.

Still, this study has some limitations. As fetal MRI was a criterion for inclusion, many cases diagnosed early in pregnancy with subsequent termination of pregnancy before reaching the gestational age where a fetal MRI was feasible could not be included in this study. Therefore, a relevant inclusion bias has to be considered. Furthermore, terminated pregnancies could not be included in the prospective study arm because of the lack of neurodevelopmental outcome data. Therefore, particularly severe cases of fetal brain anomalies could be underrepresented in the neurodevelopmental outcome analysis. Additionally, as the mean gestational age at MRI was 26.34 weeks, this study may underrepresent later fetal brain development, a period characterized by substantial structural and functional brain maturation.

Almost all fetuses with T21 also presented with a cardiac defect potentially masking the effects of the fetal brain anomaly on postnatal neurodevelopment alone. Studies with a comparative cohort of T21 fetuses without cardiac or brain anomalies for the evaluation of postnatal neurological outcomes would be favorable for future research projects. Furthermore, the telephone interviews may also pose some interview‐specific limitations such as recall bias for developmental milestones by the parents and attrition bias.

Concerning the control group, no standardized genetic tests were performed to rule out potential genetic abnormalities. Some were solely clinically reviewed for genetic disorders postnatally posing the risk of undetected genic disorders.

Conclusion

A variety of different brain malformations can be diagnosed in fetuses with confirmed T21, underlining the importance of prenatal assessment of the fetal brain by neurosonography and MRI. Although neurodevelopmental outcomes in children with T21 did not differ significantly according to the presence of prenatal brain abnormalities, certain brain malformation patterns such as cerebellar hypoplasia and widened subarachnoid fluid spaces were observed only in children with unfavorable outcomes, indicating their potential relevance to neurodevelopment. However, the findings have to be interpreted in light of the exploratory nature of the study and further studies investigating neuropathological changes in fetuses with T21 and the associated neurodevelopmental outcome are highly needed.
