Feasibility and safety of intrapartum foetal pulse oximetry using a disposable scalp probe: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Felix Oindi, Mukaindo Mwaniki, Sikolia Wanyonyi, Marleen Temmerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssessment of foetal wellbeing during labour is critical for reducing adverse perinatal outcomes. While pulse oximetry is widely used in adult and paediatric medicine, its application in intrapartum foetal monitoring remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and safety of a foetal pulse oximeter device (OxiReed™, formerly First Touch™) for obtaining intrapartum foetal oxygen saturation (SpO₂) measurements.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary referral hospital involving 110 women in the first or second stage of labour. Eligible participants had singleton pregnancies at ≥ 34 weeks’ gestation, vertex presentation, and ruptured membranes. A disposable foetal pulse oximetry probe was applied to the foetal scalp during routine vaginal examinations. SpO₂ values were recorded for feasibility assessment and descriptive analysis, with a value of 60% used as a descriptive reference threshold. Concurrent CTG tracings were classified according to both the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the U.S. National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) criteria. The primary outcomes were technical feasibility (successful acquisition of readings), time to acquisition, and safety, with descriptive associations between SpO₂ levels and CTG categories explored. Data were analysed using RStudio version 4.3.2.ResultsFoetal pulse oximetry readings were successfully obtained in all 110 participants, yielding 198 paired SpO₂–CTG observations. The median acquisition time was 14 s (IQR 10–21), and no adverse maternal or neonatal events were reported. Most normal CTG tracings were associated with SpO₂ values ≥ 60%, while lower SpO₂ values were more frequently observed in not-normal CTGs. All neonates had reassuring short-term outcomes based on clinical assessment.ConclusionsFoetal pulse oximetry during labour using a disposable scalp probe was feasible, safe, and allowed rapid acquisition of SpO2 readings under routine labour ward conditions. This study was not designed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy, validated SpO₂ thresholds, or impact on intrapartum decision-making. Background

Continuous intrapartum foetal monitoring aims to detect signs of hypoxia early enough to allow timely intervention and improve neonatal outcomes [1, 2]. Cardiotocography (CTG) remains the most widely used method, yet its specificity is limited, often leading to operative interventions for fetuses that are ultimately well oxygenated at birth [3–5]. To address these limitations, adjunctive methods such as foetal scalp blood sampling, foetal electrocardiography, and foetal pulse oximetry have been explored [6–8].

Pulse oximetry is based on the physics of spectrophotometry and plethysmography. Oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin absorb red and infrared light at different wavelengths. By emitting light at specific wavelengths (typically 650–910 nm) and detecting the transmitted or reflected light, pulse oximetry estimates the proportion of oxygenated hemoglobin in circulating blood [9]. This principle has been widely validated in adult and pediatric medicine for continuous, non-invasive monitoring of oxygen saturation.

The adaptation of this technology to the intrapartum setting is more complex. Factors such as foetal scalp edema (caput), hair, skin pigmentation, and maternal-foetal fluid dynamics may affect the accuracy of readings [10, 11]. Despite these challenges, foetal pulse oximetry allows direct measurement of intrapartum foetal oxygen saturation, complementing CTG tracings that reflect widely overlapping physiologic patterns.

Several prototype devices have been evaluated in the past, but results have been inconsistent, and optimal SpO₂ thresholds remain debated—proposed cut-offs have ranged from 30% to 60% [12–15].More recently, the OxiReed™ foetal pulse oximetry device with its disposable intrapartum probe was designed for rapid signal acquisition when applied directly to the foetal scalp during routine vaginal examination. The device was designed to allow rapid signal acquisition, with a minimally invasive, single use probe adaptable to a range of labour conditions. While preliminary bench and safety testing have been completed, clinical evaluation of intrapartum feasibility and safety in routine practice remains limited.

The present study therefore aimed to evaluate the technical feasibility and safety of a foetal pulse oximeter device (OxiReed™) during labour in a tertiary maternity hospital in Nairobi, Kenya, with secondary descriptive characterisation of SpO₂ values alongside concurrent CTG categories using NICE and NICHD classification systems.

Device description

The OxiReed™ foetal pulse oximeter system consists of a disposable, single-use scalp probe connected via a flexible cable to an external processing unit. The probe incorporates red and infrared light-emitting diodes and a photodetector arranged in a reflectance configuration, allowing optical interrogation of foetal scalp tissue when placed in direct contact with the presenting part. Reflected light signals are processed by the external unit to estimate foetal oxygen saturation (SpO₂) based on differential light absorption characteristics of oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin. The probe is designed for manual placement during routine vaginal examination and does not require mechanical fixation to the foetal scalp. Technical details of the device design have been described elsewhere [16].

Previously evaluated foetal pulse oximetry systems typically relied on reusable or semi-invasive probe designs that required more complex placement and fixation to obtain intrapartum oxygenation measurements. In contrast, the OxiReed™ device employs a disposable, single use scalp probe designed for brief manual placement during routine vaginal examination, without the need for internal fixation or continuous attachment. This design allows rapid signal acquisition and intermittent use within standard intrapartum workflows, reflecting a design-focused evolution from earlier foetal pulse oximetry technologies.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study conducted between July 2022 and January 2023 in the labour and delivery unit of a tertiary maternity hospital. The study was designed to evaluate the technical feasibility and safety of a foetal pulse oximeter device (OxiReed™) during labour. The study was not intended to assess diagnostic accuracy, validate oxygen saturation thresholds, or evaluate the impact on intrapartum clinical decision-making.

Participants

We included women in active labour who met the following criteria: a singleton pregnancy at or beyond 34 weeks of gestation, a foetus in vertex presentation, ruptured membranes, and no known congenital anomalies. In addition, participants were required to be willing and able to provide informed consent.

We excluded women with chorioamnionitis, pre-eclampsia, suspected small-for-gestational-age foetus, foetal growth restriction, stillbirth, maternal oxygen saturation < 95% in labour, conditions precluding vaginal delivery (e.g., placenta previa), uncontrolled HIV infection, and gestational diabetes mellitus.

These eligibility criteria were selected to allow initial assessment of technical feasibility and safety of the device in a clinically stable intrapartum population.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Aga Khan University Institutional Ethics Review Committee, the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation, and the Kenya Pharmacy and Poisons Board. The study was registered with the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR202010913594375).

Eligible participants were identified upon admission to the labour ward through review of clinical records. The study team explained the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment. Each participant received a copy of the signed consent form, and enrolment details were recorded in a clinical trial log maintained by the research team.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Foetal pulse oximetry procedure

A disposable foetal pulse oximetry probe was used. During a routine vaginal examination, the probe was guided along the examiner’s index and middle fingers until contact was made with the foetal scalp. Gentle manual pressure was maintained to ensure consistent optical contact. The device display was deactivated to blind the clinical care team to the SpO₂ readings; however, all measurements were stored in the device’s internal memory for subsequent download and analysis.

The readings were obtained opportunistically during routine vaginal examinations as part of standard intrapartum care and were not intended to provide continuous monitoring. Each reading was recorded once the device signalled acquisition of a stable SpO₂ value, typically within seconds of probe application. Readings were attempted between contractions when feasible; if not possible, contraction-associated readings were noted. The time from probe application to acquisition of a stable reading was measured in seconds.

For consistency, CTG interpretation was based on a 10-minute window immediately preceding the pulse oximetry reading. CTG interpretation was performed independently by on-duty obstetric providers blinded to SpO₂ results, with consensus achieved through senior obstetric review where disagreement arose.

The department adheres to a structured “fresh eyes” protocol, whereby CTG tracings are independently reviewed at regular intervals by a second clinician. In cases of interpretive disagreement, mandatory escalation to a senior obstetrician is undertaken until consensus is reached. Formal inter-observer agreement statistics were not calculated, as the study objective was feasibility rather than diagnostic accuracy; however, this standardized interpretive framework was applied consistently throughout the study.

SpO₂ cut-off

An SpO₂ value of 60% was used as a descriptive reference threshold for exploratory analysis, informed by prior intrapartum foetal pulse oximetry studies that have proposed a range of cut-offs for normal oxygenation. It is recognised that previously reported thresholds have varied widely, with values ranging from approximately 30% to 60%, reflecting ongoing uncertainty regarding optimal intrapartum SpO₂ interpretation. This threshold should not be interpreted as indicating foetal hypoxia or as a trigger for clinical intervention.

In the present study, the selected threshold was not intended to represent a validated diagnostic cut-off or to guide clinical decision-making. Rather, it was applied to facilitate descriptive comparison of SpO₂ values alongside concurrent CTG classifications within a feasibility framework. No SpO₂ values below 30% were recorded in this cohort.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of the study were measures of technical feasibility and safety, defined as:

the proportion of participants in whom at least one intrapartum foetal SpO₂ reading was successfully obtained;

the proportion of participants in whom at least one intrapartum foetal SpO₂ reading was successfully obtained;

the time required to acquire a stable SpO₂ reading following probe application;

the time required to acquire a stable SpO₂ reading following probe application;

the occurrence of any maternal or neonatal adverse events attributable to use of the device.

the occurrence of any maternal or neonatal adverse events attributable to use of the device.

Maternal or neonatal adverse events were defined a priori as any scalp trauma, bleeding, infection, abnormal neonatal adaptation, or other clinical complication temporally attributable to use of the device. As part of the study protocol, newborn scalp examination was performed after delivery to assess for any trauma, bleeding, or other abnormalities potentially related to probe placement.

Secondary outcomes were exploratory and descriptive, and included:

the distribution of SpO₂ values recorded during labour;

the distribution of SpO₂ values recorded during labour;

descriptive comparison of SpO₂ values alongside concurrent CTG classifications using NICE and NICHD criteria;

No outcomes related to diagnostic accuracy, prediction of foetal compromise, or impact on intrapartum clinical decision-making were prespecified.

Sample size

No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this study was designed as a feasibility and safety evaluation rather than a hypothesis-driven or diagnostic accuracy study. The target enrolment was determined pragmatically based on available resources and anticipated recruitment capacity over a six-month period.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using RStudio version 4.3.2. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, continuous variables as means (± standard deviation) or medians (interquartile range). Time to acquisition of a stable SpO₂ reading was summarised descriptively and explored across selected obstetric characteristics to assess feasibility under varying intrapartum conditions. Multiple SpO₂-CTG observations were obtained from some participants. These repeated measurements were retained to describe signal acquisition performance and variability but were not analysed as independent observations, and no repeated-measures modelling or clustered analysis was undertaken because the study objective was limited to feasibility rather than hypothesis testing.

No multivariable modelling, diagnostic accuracy analyses (e.g. sensitivity, specificity, receiver operating characteristic curves), or hypothesis-driven statistical testing were prespecified or performed. Where p-values are reported (Table 2), they are presented for descriptive comparison only and were not used to infer statistical significance or test hypotheses, consistent with the feasibility-focused design of the study.

Feasibility outcomes

The device obtained at least one SpO₂ measurement in all participants (100%). A total of 198 paired CTG–SpO₂ observations were recorded. The median time from probe application to acquisition of a stable signal was 14 s (IQR 10–21). No maternal or neonatal adverse events attributable to use of the device were observed.

CTG classification and SpO₂ values

CTG tracings were classified using NICE and NICHD guidelines. For analysis, tracings were dichotomised into normal (NICE: normal; NICHD: Category I) and not-normal (NICE: suspicious or pathological; NICHD: Category II or III).

Of the 198 tracings, 173 (87%) were classified as normal and 25 (13%) as not-normal. Among CTGs classified as normal, most paired SpO₂ values (90%) were ≥ 60%. Higher SpO₂ values were also observed among not-normal CTG tracings, with 80% of paired measurements ≥ 60%. The distribution of SpO₂ values by CTG category is illustrated in Fig. 1 below. Sixteen newborns had at least one SpO₂ measurement below 60%. Among these, ten had more than one reading prior to delivery. Six of these infants demonstrated an improving pattern in SpO₂, three showed a declining trend, and one remained stable at a low level. Despite these variations, all newborns with at least one SpO₂ reading below 60% had reassuring outcomes, with 5-minute Apgar scores greater than 7. These findings are descriptive and were not analysed as physiological trends or predictors of neonatal outcome.

Umbilical arterial pH data were available for a subset of participants (n = 14) in whom cord blood gas analysis was performed when clinically indicated as part of routine obstetric care rather than as a study protocol requirement. In this subset, intrapartum SpO₂ values ranged from 59% to 99%, while umbilical arterial pH values ranged from 6.97 to 7.33. No clear relationship was observed between intrapartum SpO₂ values and umbilical arterial pH. These data are presented descriptively in Supplementary Table S1 and were not analysed for diagnostic accuracy, correlation, or predictive performance.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the technical feasibility and safety of the OxiReed™ foetal pulse oximeter device using a disposable scalp probe during labour. SpO₂ readings were successfully obtained in all participants, with a median acquisition time of less than 20 s and without any maternal or neonatal adverse events. These findings indicate that the OxiReed™ device can be used during routine vaginal examinations and that intrapartum SpO₂ measurements can be obtained within standard labour ward workflows.

Descriptively, most normal CTG tracings were accompanied by higher foetal SpO₂ values, while lower SpO₂ values were more frequently observed among not-normal CTG tracings; however, this relationship was not consistent. Sixteen newborns in this cohort had at least one SpO₂ reading below 60%, yet all had reassuring short-term outcomes, including 5-minute Apgar scores greater than 7. These observations indicate that isolated or transient reductions in intrapartum SpO₂ may occur in the absence of adverse immediate neonatal condition. The exploratory SpO₂ threshold used in this study should not be interpreted as defining intrapartum hypoxia or guiding clinical management. Importantly, this study was not designed to assess the predictive value of intrapartum SpO₂ measurements.

Umbilical arterial pH values were available for a small subset of participants in whom cord blood gas analysis was performed as part of routine clinical care. In keeping with the feasibility-focused design of this study, these data were examined descriptively and did not demonstrate a clear relationship with intrapartum SpO₂ values. Given the limited sample size, these observations do not permit validation of SpO₂ measurements against biochemical reference standards and should not be interpreted as evidence of measurement accuracy or clinical utility.

Previous studies of foetal pulse oximetry have reported technical challenges related to foetal movement, amniotic fluid, scalp oedema, hair, and skin pigmentation [13, 14]. In the present study, successful SpO₂ readings were obtained across a range of intrapartum conditions, including advanced labour, supporting the technical feasibility of the disposable scalp probe design. Nevertheless, variability in proposed intrapartum SpO₂ thresholds remains an important challenge. While a value of 60% was used as a descriptive reference in this study, other investigations have proposed thresholds as low as 30%, highlighting ongoing uncertainty regarding the clinical interpretation of intrapartum SpO₂ values [17–19].

The role of foetal pulse oximetry in relation to CTG remains uncertain. In this feasibility study, some not-normal CTG tracings were observed alongside higher SpO₂ values and reassuring short-term neonatal outcomes, while some low SpO₂ values occurred in the presence of normal CTG patterns. These findings underscore the complexity of intrapartum foetal assessment and suggest that SpO₂ values and CTG classifications may not always align.

Importantly, this study was not designed to evaluate whether intrapartum SpO₂ monitoring can influence clinical decision-making or reduce obstetric intervention. Rather, these descriptive observations highlight the need for larger, hypothesis-driven studies incorporating biochemical reference standards and clinically meaningful neonatal outcomes to assess diagnostic accuracy, optimal thresholds, and potential clinical roles of device-based intrapartum foetal pulse oximetry.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was designed as a feasibility and safety evaluation and was not powered to assess diagnostic accuracy, establish validated intrapartum SpO₂ thresholds, or determine the impact of SpO₂ measurements on clinical decision-making. Second, SpO₂ measurements were obtained intermittently during routine vaginal examinations rather than through continuous monitoring, limiting assessment of sustained or evolving intrapartum oxygenation patterns. Third, umbilical cord blood gas analysis was not performed routinely, and umbilical arterial pH data were therefore available for only a small subset of participants; this limited sample size precludes validation of intrapartum SpO₂ measurements against biochemical reference standards or assessment of measurement accuracy. In addition, neonatal outcomes were assessed using short-term clinical indicators, without incorporation of longer-term or biochemical outcome measures. Finally, this was a single-centre study conducted in a clinically stable intrapartum population, which may limit generalisability to higher-risk settings.

Conclusion

In this feasibility study, intrapartum use of the OxiReed™ foetal pulse oximeter device was technically feasible and safe when applied during routine vaginal examinations in labour. SpO₂ measurements were obtained rapidly and without maternal or neonatal adverse events, supporting the practicality of device use in a standard labour ward setting. The descriptive findings highlight the complexity of intrapartum foetal assessment and do not establish diagnostic accuracy, validated SpO₂ thresholds, or clinical utility. Further studies specifically designed to incorporate biochemical reference standards and clinically meaningful outcomes are required before any role in intrapartum monitoring can be determined.
