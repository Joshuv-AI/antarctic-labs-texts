Development and characterization of polysaccharide–protein composite gels for dysphagia-friendly applications
Authors: Muxi Chen, Yi Cheng, Juan Duan, Zhengyun Shang, Wen Hu, Yuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping dysphagia-oriented foods requires texture-modified gel matrices with adequate structural integrity and stable viscoelastic properties, whereas single-thickener systems often provide limited control over overall gel performance. Here, we developed and characterized a fermented polysaccharide–protein composite gel system based on high-acyl gellan gum (HA-GG) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC). Candidate food-grade polysaccharides permitted under GB 2760-2014 were first screened, followed by single-factor experiments and a three-factor, four-level L16 (4³) orthogonal design to optimize formulation composition and fermentation time. Among twelve candidates, only HA-GG and HPMC satisfied all screening criteria. The optimal condition was 0.3 wt% HPMC, 0.3 wt% HA-GG, and 13 h fermentation, and all three factors significantly affected screening performance. Under the optimized condition, gel hardness increased at moderate polysaccharide incorporation but declined at higher levels. Rheological analysis confirmed elastic-dominant gel behavior across formulations, while scanning electron microscopy revealed improved network continuity at moderate incorporation but excessive compactness at higher levels. These findings demonstrate that balanced polysaccharide–protein network organization is critical for the rational design of stable fermented gel matrices with potential relevance to dysphagia-oriented food applications.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Dysphagia is a swallowing disorder characterized by impaired transport of food or liquid from the mouth to the stomach and is commonly observed in older adults and in patients with stroke, neurodegenerative disease, or head and neck disorders1. Because dysphagia compromises both oral intake and airway protection, it is closely associated with malnutrition, dehydration, aspiration-related complications, and reduced quality of life1,2. With rapid population aging and the increasing need for long-term nutritional support, the development of safe, practical, and nutritionally meaningful texture-modified foods has become increasingly important in both clinical and home settings2,3.

Texture modification remains a cornerstone of dietary management for dysphagia4. The International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) has established a standardized framework and testing methods for classifying food texture and liquid thickness to improve safety and consistency in dysphagia care5. However, currently available dysphagia-oriented products are still dominated by starch- or gum-based thickeners and are often designed primarily to modify apparent viscosity6. Although this approach is useful, viscosity adjustment alone does not necessarily ensure adequate bolus cohesiveness, structural integrity, low oral residue, or desirable sensory performance, all of which may affect practical suitability and long-term acceptance6,7.

In recent years, polysaccharide–protein composite gels have emerged as promising platforms for dysphagia-oriented food design because interactions between proteins and polysaccharides can generate three-dimensional networks with tunable textural and viscoelastic properties8. Previous studies have shown that combinations of gellan gum, xanthan gum, pectin, or carrageenan with proteins such as whey protein, soy protein, or gelatin can modulate gel elasticity, flow behavior, and structural organization9. Moreover, edible double-network gels based on soy protein and sugar beet pectin have been reported to develop hierarchical microstructures with improved oral-processing-related functionality10. In fermented dairy-type systems, acidification conditions can further influence gelation pathways, network organization, and rheological properties, indicating that fermentation should be considered not only as a processing step but also as a structure-forming factor11. Nevertheless, fewer studies have integrated fermentation-mediated structuring, microstructural validation, and swallowing-relevant mechanical characterization within a single formulation-driven design strategy for dysphagia-oriented gels11,12.

High-acyl gellan gum (HA-GG) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) were selected in the present study as complementary hydrocolloids. HA-GG is capable of forming relatively soft, flexible, and elastic gel networks, while HPMC contributes strong hydration capacity and structure-modifying behavior, making this combination attractive for constructing a stable yet tunable fermented gel matrix12. In addition, our preliminary screening indicated that among the candidate food-grade polysaccharides considered for this system, HA-GG and HPMC were the only two materials that simultaneously met the requirements of gel-forming ability, stability, solubility, re-gelation behavior, and compatibility in the fermented milk matrix.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop a fermented composite gel system based on HA-GG and HPMC in a skim-milk matrix, optimize the formulation through single-factor and orthogonal experiments, and characterize the resulting gels by texture profile analysis (TPA), oscillatory rheology, and scanning electron microscopy(SEM)13–16. By clarifying the formulation–structure–property relationships of this fermented polysaccharide–protein system, this work provides a physicochemical basis for the rational design of stable texture-modified gel matrices with potential relevance to dysphagia-oriented food applications.

Results

Screening of polysaccharide thickeners for fermented dysphagia-oriented gel systems

A preliminary screening was conducted to identify suitable food-grade polysaccharides for constructing fermented dysphagia-oriented gel formulations. Twelve commonly used thickeners permitted under GB 2760-2014 were evaluated according to five functional criteria: gel-forming ability, stability, solubility, re-gelation behavior, and compatibility within the composite system.

As summarized in Table 1, only high-acyl gellan gum (HA-GG) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) satisfied all five screening criteria simultaneously. Both polysaccharides were able to form continuous gel networks, showed acceptable structural stability, dissolved uniformly in aqueous and milk-based systems, retained re-gelation behavior after thermal treatment, and remained compatible with the fermented composite matrix without obvious phase separation or aggregation.

In contrast, the remaining polysaccharides failed to meet one or more of the predefined criteria. For example, xanthan gum showed acceptable gel formation, stability, solubility, and re-gelation behavior, but failed the compatibility criterion. Low-acyl gellan gum met the gel-formation and solubility criteria but showed insufficient stability and re-gelation behavior. Gelatin and sodium caseinate failed to form suitable gel structures under the tested conditions, while several plant-derived polysaccharides, including guar gum, locust bean gum, and konjac gum, showed inadequate solubility and re-gelation performance. Carrageenan also failed to maintain acceptable stability and compatibility in the present system.

Based on the overall screening results, HA-GG and HPMC were selected as the polysaccharide components for subsequent single-factor experiments, orthogonal optimization, and instrumental characterization of the fermented composite gel system.

Effects of individual polysaccharide concentration on preliminary sensory-guided screening

Following the selection of high-acyl gellan gum (HA-GG) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) as candidate polysaccharides, single-factor experiments were conducted to evaluate the influence of individual polysaccharide concentration on the preliminary performance of fermented dysphagia-oriented gel formulations. Sensory-guided screening scores, together with macroscopic observations, were used to define feasible concentration windows for subsequent multivariable optimization.

As shown in Table 2, the sensory-guided screening score of the fermented gel decreased progressively with increasing HPMC concentration. The formulation containing 0.1 wt% HPMC showed the highest mean score (86.40), whereas the formulation containing 0.9 wt% HPMC showed the lowest score (64.60). Overall, a clear downward trend in screening performance was observed across the tested concentration range (0.1–0.9 wt%).

Optimization of formulation and fermentation conditions by orthogonal experimental design

Based on the feasible concentration ranges identified in the single-factor experiments, a three-factor, four-level orthogonal experimental design was performed to optimize the formulation and fermentation conditions of the dysphagia-oriented composite gel system. The investigated factors were HPMC concentration (A), HA-GG concentration (B), and fermentation time (C), and sensory-guided screening scores were used as a supportive indicator of overall formulation performance.

The sensory-guided screening scores of the 16 experimental formulations varied markedly, indicating pronounced effects of formulation composition and fermentation time on the preliminary performance of the composite gels (Table 4). Among all tested groups, Group 9 achieved the highest observed score (81.80), whereas Groups 6, 7, 10, and 11 showed the lowest scores (40.80–41.80) and failed to maintain stable gel structures. Macroscopic observations further revealed clear differences among experimental groups. Several formulations remained liquid or semi-liquid after fermentation and did not form stable gels, while others exhibited visible granularity, poor smoothness, or reduced structural continuity. In contrast, a subset of formulations formed relatively smooth and continuous gels with lower perceived residue, among which Group 9 showed the most favorable overall screening performance.

Texture profile analysis of fermented composite gels at different polysaccharide incorporation levels

Texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed to evaluate the mechanical properties of fermented composite gels prepared under the optimized formulation and fermentation conditions, with different total additions of polysaccharide stock solutions (0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× relative to the initial milk volume).

As shown in Fig. 1, the composite gels exhibited clear differences in appearance and structural integrity with increasing polysaccharide incorporation level. The sample without polysaccharide addition (0×) showed relatively poor structural continuity and surface smoothness. In contrast, all polysaccharide-containing samples (1×–4×) formed visually continuous gel structures with improved smoothness and overall uniformity. Moderate polysaccharide addition, particularly in the 2× and 3× groups, produced more compact and coherent gels, whereas excessive addition (4×) was associated with a partial loss of structural compactness.

The hardness values of the composite gels are summarized in Table 7. Gel hardness increased markedly with polysaccharide addition from 0× to 2×, reaching the maximum value at 2× polysaccharide incorporation (2388.19 ± 266.88 N/m²). Rheological properties of fermented composite gels at different polysaccharide incorporation levels

The viscoelastic properties of fermented composite gels prepared under the optimized formulation and fermentation conditions were characterized by oscillatory rheological measurements, including amplitude sweep, frequency sweep, and power-law fitting of storage modulus data. Composite gels with different total additions of polysaccharide stock solutions (1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× relative to the initial milk volume) were analyzed.

Amplitude sweep tests were conducted to evaluate the viscoelastic response of the composite gels under increasing shear strain. As shown in Fig. 2, the loss tangent (tan δ = G″/G′) of all samples remained below 1 throughout the measured strain range, indicating that the storage modulus (G′) consistently exceeded the loss modulus (G″) and that all formulations behaved as elastic-dominant gels. With increasing shear strain, tan δ gradually increased for all formulations, reflecting a progressively greater viscous contribution under higher deformation. Among the tested samples, the 4× group exhibited the lowest tan δ values across the strain range, suggesting a relatively stronger elastic contribution compared with the other formulations. However, this tendency should be interpreted as a change in the balance between elastic and viscous components rather than as direct evidence of higher overall mechanical strength.

The frequency-dependent viscoelastic behavior of the composite gels is shown in Fig. 3. For all formulations, the storage modulus (G′) increased with increasing oscillation frequency, and no crossover between G′ and G″ was observed over the tested frequency range, confirming the elastic-dominant nature of all composite gel systems. At a given frequency, G′ values decreased progressively with increasing polysaccharide incorporation level, indicating that excessive incorporation did not increase the apparent stiffness of the gel network under oscillatory deformation. This trend suggests that although all formulations maintained gel-like behavior, higher incorporation levels altered the network organization in a manner that reduced the absolute viscoelastic response measured under dynamic loading.

Microstructural characteristics of fermented composite gels observed by SEM

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the microstructural features of fermented composite gels prepared under the optimized formulation and fermentation conditions, with different polysaccharide incorporation levels (0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× relative to the initial milk volume). Representative micrographs were acquired at magnifications of 2000×, 20,000×, and 40,000× (Fig. 4).

SEM micrographs of fermented composite gels at different polysaccharide incorporation levels.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe the microstructural features of gels with 0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4×polysaccharide incorporation; representative micrographs are shown at magnifi cations of ×2000, ×20,000, and ×40,000.

At 0× polysaccharide incorporation, the composite gel exhibited a relatively loose and heterogeneous microstructure. The network appeared discontinuous, with irregular pores and poorly defined connections between structural elements. At higher magnifications, aggregated domains could be observed, but the overall network lacked continuity and compactness, consistent with the relatively weak macroscopic integrity of the control gel.

With the incorporation of polysaccharide stock solutions (1×–4×), marked changes in microstructural organization were observed. At 1× and 2× incorporation levels, the gel network became smoother, more continuous, and more homogeneous, suggesting improved integration between the polysaccharide phase and the fermented protein matrix. At 3× incorporation, the network appeared further compacted, with reduced pore size and more continuous structural connections.

At 4× incorporation, the gel matrix remained highly packed at the microscopic level; however, the structural organization did not translate into further improvement in overall mechanical performance. Compared with the 2× and 3× groups, the 4× group appeared to reflect an overfilled or excessively compacted network state rather than a more mechanically favorable structure.

Overall, SEM revealed a transition from a loose and discontinuous network in the 0× group to a smoother and more integrated polysaccharide–protein network in the polysaccharide-containing groups. In combination with the TPA and rheological results, these observations suggest that moderate polysaccharide incorporation improved network continuity and structural integration, whereas excessive incorporation altered the network toward a densely packed but not necessarily mechanically superior state.

Discussion

The design of dysphagia-oriented foods requires a careful balance among structural stability, mechanical suitability, and oral processability. Previous studies have shown that texture conditions that are either excessively firm or structurally unstable may compromise bolus manipulation and reduce practical applicability for individuals with swallowing difficulties1,2. In the present study, the fermented polysaccharide–protein composite gels exhibited clear formulation-dependent differences in macroscopic appearance, mechanical resistance, viscoelastic behavior, and microstructural organization, underscoring the importance of coordinated texture–structure design in texture-modified gel systems.

The TPA results demonstrated a non-linear relationship between polysaccharide incorporation level and gel hardness. Moderate polysaccharide incorporation improved resistance to deformation and was accompanied by better macroscopic integrity, whereas further increases did not result in proportional reinforcement. In particular, the 2× and 3× groups showed more favorable mechanical performance than the 0×, 1×, and 4× groups. This pattern suggests that desirable gel texture was achieved through balanced network formation rather than maximal polysaccharide addition. The SEM observations support this interpretation. Compared with the 0× group, the polysaccharide-containing gels displayed smoother, more continuous, and more integrated network structures. However, the highly compact appearance observed at the highest incorporation level did not translate into superior mechanical performance, indicating that excessive polysaccharide incorporation may promote local densification or overfilled packing without improving macroscopic load-bearing behavior. Taken together, these findings indicate that moderate polysaccharide incorporation was more effective than excessive addition for achieving a mechanically coherent fermented composite gel system.

The rheological data provided further insight into the dynamic behavior of the composite gels. Across all tested formulations, G′ remained higher than G″ and tan δ remained below 1, confirming elastic-dominant gel behavior under oscillatory deformation. At the same time, the rheological results showed that increasing polysaccharide incorporation did not produce a monotonic enhancement of apparent gel strength. Although the 4× group exhibited lower tan δ values, suggesting a relatively greater elastic contribution, both the storage modulus profile and the power-law fitting coefficient K′ decreased progressively with increasing incorporation level. This indicates that the balance between elastic and viscous components shifted with polysaccharide addition, but excessive incorporation did not improve the apparent stiffness of the gel network under dynamic loading. In this respect, the rheological findings were consistent with the TPA results in suggesting that gel performance depended more on balanced network organization than on simple thickener accumulation.

The SEM observations further clarified the structure–property relationship within this fermented composite system. The control gel without polysaccharide incorporation exhibited a loose and discontinuous structure, whereas polysaccharide-containing samples showed smoother, more continuous, and more integrated three-dimensional networks. This structural evolution is consistent with previous descriptions of protein–polysaccharide composite gels, in which polysaccharides can occupy interstitial spaces and promote network continuity and phase integration7,8. With increasing polysaccharide incorporation, the gel matrix became progressively more compact at the microscopic level. However, greater compactness did not necessarily correspond to better overall mechanical performance. In the present study, the highly packed microstructure observed at the highest incorporation level was not accompanied by further improvement in hardness or apparent stiffness under oscillatory loading. This suggests that excessive incorporation altered the internal network arrangement toward a densely packed but not mechanically advantageous state. Therefore, the combined TPA, rheological, and SEM results consistently support the view that microstructural optimization, rather than maximal polysaccharide addition, is critical for the development of stable fermented composite gels17

From an application perspective, commercially available thickened liquids and ready-to-eat dysphagia products often rely on single thickeners and primarily target viscosity adjustment, which may provide limited control over structural continuity, texture stability, and product consistency10. By contrast, composite systems based on protein–polysaccharide interactions offer greater flexibility for modulating gel structure and viscoelastic behavior through network-level design10,18. In the present study, controlled incorporation of HA-GG and HPMC within a fermented skim-milk matrix enabled systematic modulation of gel properties across formulation levels. Notably, moderate incorporation produced more favorable overall performance than excessive addition, indicating that balanced network organization was more important than maximal thickener usage. These findings provide practical formulation guidance for the development of texture-modified gel systems with potential relevance to dysphagia-oriented food applications.

Overall, the present results show that a fermented HA-GG/HPMC composite gel system can be formulated to achieve tunable mechanical, rheological, and microstructural properties through controlled adjustment of polysaccharide incorporation and fermentation conditions. The data further indicate that moderate incorporation levels were more effective than excessive addition in promoting network continuity and favorable gel performance. These findings provide a physicochemical basis for the rational design of stable texture-modified gel matrices, while further studies incorporating direct IDDSI-based testing and application-oriented swallowing validation will be needed to clarify their translational relevance.

Methods

Materials

Skim milk powder, used as the protein base for fermented composite gel preparation, was obtained from Shandong Jiaquan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). High-acyl gellan gum (HA-GG) and food-grade hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), which served as the polysaccharide components of the composite gel system, were supplied by Bloomage Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) and Zhongtian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China), respectively. Sucrose was purchased from Chengshun Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). The starter culture, Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus was obtained from Junle Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China) and stored according to the manufacturer’s instructions prior to use. All materials were of food grade and used as received. Distilled water was used throughout the experiments.

The polysaccharides used in this study were selected based on regulatory compliance, functional performance, and suitability for dysphagia-oriented food formulations. Candidate thickeners were first shortlisted from food-grade polysaccharides permitted under GB 2760-2014 (Standards for the Use of Food Additives in China), and were then subjected to preliminary screening according to gel-forming ability, stability, solubility, re-gelation behavior, and compatibility with fermented milk systems. Particular attention was paid to whether each polysaccharide could form a continuous gel network in aqueous and milk-based matrices, dissolve uniformly during thermal processing, maintain acceptable structural reversibility after heating, and remain compatible with the fermented milk matrix without inducing severe phase separation, graininess, or aggregation. Based on this screening, HA-GG and HPMC were identified as the only two candidates that simultaneously satisfied all five criteria and were therefore selected for subsequent single-factor experiments, orthogonal optimization, and instrumental characterization.

Preparation of polysaccharide–protein composite gels and fermentation

The milk base was prepared by dispersing skim milk powder in distilled water at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:8 (w/v). Sucrose was then added at 5 wt% based on the mass of skim milk powder. The mixture was hydrated at 55–60 °C and homogenized for 3 min using a bench-top high-speed homogenizer (FSH-2A, Yanghua Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd., China) operated at 5000 rpm to obtain a uniform milk dispersion. The prepared milk base was maintained under gentle agitation before polysaccharide incorporation.

HA-GG stock solutions were prepared by dispersing the required amount of HA-GG powder in distilled water to the target concentrations. The dispersions were heated in a thermostatic water-bath shaker (Changzhou Yate Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at 70 °C with continuous stirring at 200 rpm for 30 min, followed by gentle stirring at 40 °C and 100 rpm for an additional 10 min to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting HA-GG stock solutions were stored at 4 °C and equilibrated to room temperature before use.

HPMC stock solutions were prepared following the same procedure. Briefly, HPMC powder was dispersed in distilled water to the required concentrations, heated at 70 °C with stirring at 200 rpm for 30 min, and then stirred at 40 °C and 100 rpm for 10 min until a homogeneous stock solution was obtained. The resulting HPMC stock solutions were stored at 4 °C and equilibrated to room temperature before use.

Polysaccharide–protein composite systems were prepared by adding predetermined volumes of HA-GG and/or HPMC stock solutions to the milk base under continuous stirring to ensure uniform distribution. Where necessary, distilled water was adjusted to keep the total formulation volume constant across groups. After polysaccharide incorporation, the mixtures were cooled to the inoculation temperature and inoculated with Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus at 2 wt% based on the mass of skim milk powder. The inoculated mixtures were then gently mixed to ensure even distribution of the starter culture.

Fermentation was carried out in a commercial fermentation system (Chengdu Haobo Mechanical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 42 °C for predetermined durations according to the experimental design. At the end of fermentation, the samples were immediately transferred to 4 °C to terminate further acidification and stabilize the gel structure. All fermented composite gels within each experimental set were subsequently stored under identical refrigerated conditions prior to preliminary screening and instrumental characterization. In the later instrumental study, polysaccharide incorporation levels designated as 0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× referred to total stock-solution addition volumes equivalent to 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 times the initial milk volume, respectively.

Formulation optimization by single-factor and orthogonal design

A stepwise optimization strategy combining single-factor experiments and orthogonal design was employed to define feasible formulation ranges and identify suitable fermentation conditions for the polysaccharide–protein composite gel system.

Single-factor experiments were first conducted to determine workable concentration ranges for each polysaccharide in the fermented milk matrix. High-acyl gellan gum (HA-GG) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) were individually incorporated into the milk base at 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 wt% (w/w, based on the total formulation). During this stage, the sucrose level (5 wt% based on skim milk powder mass), inoculation level of Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus (2 wt% based on skim milk powder mass), and fermentation temperature (42 °C) were kept constant, and fermentation time was fixed at 15 h.

The purpose of the single-factor experiments was to identify feasible concentration windows for subsequent multivariable optimization rather than to establish final application performance. Each formulation was evaluated by combined macroscopic observation and supportive sensory-guided screening. The screening focused on the ability to form a continuous gel after fermentation, the absence of visible syneresis or severe phase separation, overall smoothness and uniformity, and the lack of obvious aggregation or undissolved particles. Preliminary sensory judgment further considered appearance/texture, oral breakdown behavior, perceived oral residue, swallowing effort, and mouthfeel/odor acceptability. Based on the combined observations, excessively high concentrations of either polysaccharide impaired macroscopic smoothness, structural integrity, or gel-forming behavior, whereas moderate concentration ranges were considered more suitable for further optimization. To define orthogonal design levels while retaining the lower boundary for comparison, four levels were selected for each polysaccharide in the subsequent orthogonal experiment: 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 wt%.

Based on the feasible ranges identified in the single-factor experiments, a three-factor, four-level orthogonal design was applied to optimize formulation composition and fermentation duration. The investigated factors were HPMC concentration (A), HA-GG concentration (B), and fermentation time (C). An L16 (4³) orthogonal array was employed, with factor levels defined as follows: A = 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 wt% HPMC; B = 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 wt% HA-GG; and C = 11, 12, 13, and 14 h fermentation.

At this stage, formulations were evaluated using a combination of macroscopic observation and auxiliary sensory screening. A small sensory panel (n = 5) assessed the samples using a structured scoring rubric covering appearance/texture, oral breakdown behavior, perceived oral residue, swallowing effort, and mouthfeel/odor acceptability. Sensory evaluation was used solely as a supportive screening tool for formulation selection and was not treated as a clinical or primary functional outcome.

The orthogonal results were analyzed by range analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA). Formulations showing stable gel structure and higher overall screening scores were considered preferable. According to the orthogonal screening results, the formulation containing 0.3 wt% HPMC and 0.3 wt% HA-GG with a fermentation time of 13 h was selected as the optimized condition for subsequent instrumental characterization.

According to the outcomes of the single-factor experiments, orthogonal screening, and statistical analysis, the formulation containing 0.3 wt% HPMC and 0.3 wt% HA-GG with a fermentation time of 13 h was selected as the optimized baseline condition for instrumental characterization. To systematically investigate the effect of polysaccharide incorporation level on gel properties, additional samples were prepared under this optimized formulation and fermentation condition using different total additions of polysaccharide stock solutions. These groups were expressed as multiples of the initial milk volume, namely 0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4×. Here, 0× indicates no added polysaccharide stock solution, whereas 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× indicate total stock-solution addition volumes equivalent to 1, 2, 3, and 4 times the initial milk volume, respectively. All selected samples were subjected to subsequent instrumental testing under identical storage and measurement conditions.

Texture profile analysis

Texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed to characterize the mechanical properties of the fermented polysaccharide–protein composite gels using a texture analyzer (TA.new plus, ISENSO, USA). All samples were stored at 4 °C under identical conditions prior to testing, and care was taken during sample transfer to minimize disruption of the gel network15,16.

TPA measurements were conducted in compression mode under the following conditions: target mode, distance; compression distance, 3.0 mm; pre-test speed, 0.50 mm/s; test speed, 0.50 mm/s; post-test speed, 0.50 mm/s; trigger mode, force; trigger force, 3.0 gf. Force–time curves were recorded throughout the compression cycle, and textural parameters were extracted using the manufacturer-provided software associated with the instrument.

Each formulation was prepared independently, and measurements were performed in quintuplicate. In the present study, hardness was selected as the primary TPA descriptor because it was considered the most directly relevant index of deformation resistance during formulation screening, while oscillatory rheology and scanning electron microscopy were used as complementary tools to evaluate viscoelastic and microstructural behavior. Hardness was defined as the maximum force recorded during the first compression cycle.

Composite gel samples prepared with different total additions of polysaccharide stock solutions (0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× relative to the initial milk volume) were analyzed under identical testing conditions. The resulting TPA data were used to compare the effects of polysaccharide incorporation level on the mechanical behavior of the fermented composite gels and to support subsequent rheological characterization.

Rheological measurements

The rheological properties of the fermented polysaccharide–protein composite gels were determined using a rotational rheometer (MCR102e, Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with a parallel-plate geometry. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C. Samples were carefully loaded onto the lower plate to avoid air entrapment and excessive structural disruption, and excess material was gently trimmed prior to testing.

Amplitude sweep tests were first performed to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) of each sample. The amplitude sweep was conducted at a constant oscillation frequency over a shear strain range of 0.001–0.1, during which the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were continuously monitored. The strain range in which both G′ and G″ remained independent of strain was defined as the LVR and used for subsequent frequency sweep measurements.

Frequency sweep tests were then carried out within the LVR over a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz. During the measurements, G′ and G″ were recorded as functions of frequency to evaluate the viscoelastic behavior of the composite gels. The relative magnitudes of G′ and G″, as well as their frequency dependence, were used to assess gel strength and structural stability.

To further characterize gel viscoelasticity, the frequency-dependent data of the storage modulus were fitted to a power-law model, G′ = K′·fⁿ, where G′ is the storage modulus, f is the oscillation frequency, K′ is the consistency coefficient reflecting the strength of the gel network, and n is the frequency-dependence exponent indicating the sensitivity of the gel structure to frequency variation.

Rheological measurements were performed on composite gel samples prepared with different total additions of polysaccharide stock solutions (0×, 1×, 2×, 3×, and 4× relative to the initial milk volume). All samples were fermented under the optimized conditions and tested under identical measurement settings. Each measurement was conducted in triplicate, and the average values were used for further analysis.

Microstructural observation by scanning electron microscopy

The microstructure of the fermented polysaccharide–protein composite gels was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Representative samples prepared under the optimized formulation and fermentation conditions were selected for microstructural observation.

Before SEM analysis, the gel samples were freeze-dried to preserve the internal network architecture. Briefly, the fermented gels were first frozen and then dried under vacuum until a constant weight was reached. The freeze-dried samples were cut into cubic specimens of approximately 1 × 1 × 1 cm.

The specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to improve electrical conductivity. SEM observations were performed at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV under high-vacuum conditions. Micrographs were collected at magnifications of 2000×, 20,000×, and 40,000× to visualize gel-network morphology at different length scales.

SEM images were used for qualitative evaluation of the spatial organization, continuity, and compactness of the polysaccharide–protein network, and to support the interpretation of mechanical and rheological behavior.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

For the orthogonal optimization experiment, sensory-guided screening scores were analyzed by range analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of HPMC concentration, HA-GG concentration, and fermentation time on overall formulation performance.

Texture profile analysis (TPA) measurements were performed in quintuplicate for each formulation. Rheological measurements, including amplitude sweep and frequency sweep tests, were conducted in triplicate. Frequency-dependent storage modulus data were fitted to the power-law model G′ = K′·fⁿ, and the fitting coefficient K′, frequency-dependence exponent n, and coefficient of determination (R²) were obtained by nonlinear regression analysis.

Because the rheological analysis in this study was intended primarily to characterize viscoelastic trends and model-based parameters rather than to test predefined hypotheses, no additional inferential statistical comparisons were applied to the rheological data. SEM observations were interpreted qualitatively. Data processing, curve fitting, and graphical presentation were performed using OriginPro software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).
