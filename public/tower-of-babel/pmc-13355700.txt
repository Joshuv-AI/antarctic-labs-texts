Proteome-informed interrogation of a methanogenic archaeon links stress response mechanisms with microbial performance and robustness during biomethanation
Authors: Fuad Ale Enriquez, Stephen J Callister, William C Nelson, Doo Nam Kim, Carrie D Nicora, Rosalie K Chu, Dillman Delgado, Birgitte K Ahring
Published: 2026

Introduction

Hydrogenotrophic methanogens are a group of extant microorganisms within the domain Archaea that have evolved to use hydrogen (H2) as electron donor and carbon dioxide (CO2) as electron acceptor. Their energy metabolism is driven by the Wolfe cycle [1], which converts CO2 and H2 into methane (CH4) and water (H2O). Hydrogenotrophic methanogens are anaerobes ubiquitous across natural and human-shaped microbial ecosystems, including animal intestines, lake beds, hot springs, waste disposal sites, and anaerobic digesters [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Together, their metabolic specialization and ecological ubiquity underscore the central role of hydrogenotrophic methanogens in global carbon cycling and engineered microbial systems.

Recent advancements in synthetic biology have begun to establish the foundations for genetic and metabolic engineering of the methanogenesis chassis (e.g. LbCas12a and CRISPRi) [7,8], opening new opportunities to expand the product scope, performance, and robustness of methanogenic strains as host organisms for biomanufacturing applications. These applications involve coupling CO2 fixation with the production of renewable natural gas (RNG) [9], anaerobic biomining of transition metals [10], bioelectrosynthesis [11], and heterologous expression of thermostable functional enzymes [12]. However, the practical deployment of synthetic biology tools in methanogens remains fundamentally constrained by the limited protein-level understanding of their genomes. Across representative species, 40–80% of annotated genes still encode hypothetical proteins with no experimentally validated function [[13], [14], [15], [16]], leaving many metabolic and most regulatory pathways unresolved, preventing the rational design strategies required for genetic engineering and directed evolution trials in methanogens.

Methanothermobacter wolfeii is a hydrogenotrophic methanogen known for its ability to withstand harsh environmental conditions, including extreme temperatures, low water activity, and prolonged starvation [17,18]. M. wolfeii strain BSEL was isolated through a multi-generational culture adaptation strategy [16] and exhibits one of the highest specific growth rates within the Methanothermobacter genus while growing solely on CO2 and H2 as electron acceptor and donor, respectively, and NH4Cl as its only nitrogen source. The BSEL strain carries genomic differences relative to the type strain M. wolfeii DSM 2970 that confer the ability to grow axenically without expensive organic additives [16]. Notably, BSEL is currently the only M. wolfeii variant with a complete genome deposited in NCBI. These physiological and genomic traits make the BSEL strain particularly well suited for biomethanation, a process that upgrades anaerobic digestion biogas (approximately 40% CO2 and 60% CH4), together with renewable H2, into a high-purity CH₄ stream that can be commercialized as RNG. RNG can be exported using an existing natural gas grid as a drop-in biofuel to replace fossil natural gas [19,20]. Methanothermobacter species also show promise for valorizing other CO2-rich industrial streams such as syngas, combustion exhaust, and ethanol fermentation off-gases. This highlights the strong potential of Methanothermobacter members as biocatalysts and motivate a deeper examination of the robustness required for reliable industrial operations. Beyond biomanufacturing and CO2 capture, improving our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms behind methanogenesis can also aid in the implementation of strategies to mitigate undesirable CH4 emissions [21].

Achieving reliable biomanufacturing requires a biocatalyst capable of maintaining stable and predictable performance rather than exhibiting fluctuations that compromise process stability and product quality. The microbial robustness metric (R) is an emerging concept in bioengineering that provides a quantitative framework to assess how strongly a microbial function varies when the culture encounters unfavorable stimuli in the bioreactor. R is inversely proportional to the standard deviation of a measured cellular function under perturbation, with low values indicating limited adaptive capacity [22]. Quantifying R is therefore key to engineer strains with reduced bioprocess variability toward stressors that affect wild types [[23], [24], [25]]. This need is motivated by the fact that anaerobic bioreactors cultivating methanogens are expected to experience environmental perturbations during upscaling and long term operation, where spatial and temporal variations can expose cells to non-ideal conditions such as temperature shifts, oxygen (O2) contamination, nitrogen depletion, and H2 starvation. These perturbations are common cultivation challenges that intensify at larger scales and can hinder hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis. Determining how such conditions influence the robustness of methanogenic strains is therefore essential for developing strategies that support process longevity and operational reliability.

The aim of this work was to inspect the impact of process-relevant perturbations on the robustness and proteome of a methanogen growing on H2 and CO2. The strain was exposed to temperature shifts, O2 contamination, nitrogen depletion, and H2 starvation, and five cellular functions were quantified across these conditions to derive robustness values using a Fano factor metric. Cells harvested in late exponential phase were subjected to bottom-up proteomics to identify proteins whose differential abundance reflected stress response and adaptations. Protein–protein interactions are central to the activity of core methanogenic enzyme systems, including the formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase (Fmd) and methyl‑H4MPT:coenzyme M methyltransferase (Mtr) systems, both of which rely on tightly coordinated multimeric assemblies [[26], [27], [28]]. Based on this principle, we hypothesized that stress response proteins may associate with canonical methanogenesis subunits to stabilize metabolic function during perturbation. To test this hypothesis, we used AlphaFold-based structural superposition and protein-protein complex modeling to assess whether thermal stress-related proteins in M. wolfeii BSEL are predicted to form structurally compatible interactions with core methanogenic components. Several paralogous proteins displayed a high degree of structural overlap with the canonical FmdE subunit and were predicted to interact with it, suggesting that stress-induced protein–protein associations may contribute to the maintenance of CO2 reduction under adverse conditions. These findings highlight that differential abundance alone may not fully capture functional responses in methanogens, where the biological role of a protein often emerges from its participation in higher-order interactions rather than from individual protein function. Overall, this work integrates quantitative robustness measurements with proteome‑level observations to advance our understanding of how methanogens deploy molecular strategies to sustain their metabolism in dynamic environments. By providing protein‑level evidence for the expression and potential interaction networks of previously hypothetical proteins, this work addresses a central barrier for the deployment of genetic engineering tools in methanogens.

Materials and methods

An overview of the experimental pipeline used to study the microbial performance and robustness as well as inspecting the proteomic responses of M. wolfeii BSEL is shown in Fig. 1.

Experimental pipeline to study the stress response of M. wolfeii BSEL to environmental perturbations. A frozen stock of M. wolfeii BSEL was used as parent culture for conducting the experiments under perturbed environments in batch cultivation using Balch tubes (n = 5). The cultures were monitored by measuring the optical density (OD590) over time and an exponential growth model was used to calculate the specific growth rate. At the end of each cultivation, the composition of the headspace was analyzed, and mass balance was conducted to determine the cell functions and microbial robustness (R) under perturbed conditions. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, stored at −80 °C, and trasnferred to the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. The cell pellets were re-suspended in SDS, and the S-Trap was used to separate proteins. The proteins were cleaned and digested using trypsin. Later, the resulting peptides were analyzed in an LC-MS/MS instrument.

Microbial strain and media preparation

A frozen stock of M. wolfeii BSEL was thawed at room temperature and propagated in a serum vial containing basic anaerobic medium (BA medium). The serum vial had a headspace of 70% H2 and 30% CO2 (238 kPa). This culture was used as the parent inoculant for all perturbation experiments. BA medium and the 70% H2:30% CO2 gas mixture were used in all subsequent cultivations. Medium preparation and cultivation of M. wolfeii BSEL were conducted using the aseptic-anaerobic techniques described by Wolfe [29], using resazurin as redox indicator and sodium sulfide as reducing agent. The composition of BA medium was originally reported by Garrett, Srinivas and Ahring [30]. BA medium was modified by adding a bicarbonate:carbonate buffer (40 mM) to regulate the pH at 8.2 ± 0.1 during batch cultivation. The optimal growth conditions of M. wolfeii BSEL were identified in a previous work [16].

Cultivation under perturbed environments

The exposure of M. wolfeii BSEL to environmental perturbations was conducted in batch cultivations using rubber-stoppered Balch tubes. All the tubes were incubated in horizontal position and shaken at 160 rpm in an orbital shaker-incubator (3 cm of orbital diameter) to maximize the H2 gas-liquid mass transport. The cells were exposed to several levels of four types of perturbations (perturbation groups); temperature changes, high redox potential, changing concentrations of the only nitrogen source (NH4Cl), and periods of H2 starvation. Perturbations were introduced independently (no more than one perturbation at a time).

The Balch tubes containing 4.5 mL of BA medium were pre-incubated at the desired temperature for each essay before inoculation, this was to avoid temperature stress during the inoculation step. 0.5 mL of M. wolfeii BSEL culture growing in exponential phase in the serum vial were transferred to the Balch tubes. The optical density (OD590) in the Balch tubes was measured every 2–3 h using a spectrophotometer equipped with a tube holder (Genesys 20, Thermo Scientific, US). The OD590 measurements were linearly correlated with the concentration of methanogenic cells (xCells) in BA medium (R2 >0.99). xCells was measured via gravimetry.

The temperature perturbations were induced by adjusting the temperature set-point of the shaker-incubator used for cultivation. The temperatures tested were 56, 58, 60, 66, 68, 70, and 72 °C. Redox stress was applied by injecting known volumes of filter-sterilized air into the Balch tube. After air injection, the Balch tubes were incubated at 64 °C and shaken for 30 min before inoculation. In a preliminary test, a correlation between the volumetric concentration of O2 and the redox potential in BA medium was established. The redox potential was measured using a ± 1000 mV electrochemical sensor HI98120 (Hanna, USA) with factory calibration. The redox potentials tested were −308, −306, −304, −303, −301, −277, and −263 mV. The culture showed no grow at a redox potential higher than −277 mV. The corresponding O2 doses to adjust the redox potential were 0, 250, 750, 1500, 2000, 2500, and 3000 ppmv (relative to the headspace volume - doses were calculated assuming 21%v of O2 in air). Nitrogen depletion was induced by adjusting the amount of NH4Cl added during the medium preparation. The concentrations of NH4Cl in the medium after inoculation for the growth tests were 28.8, 10.8, 6.3, 2.7, and 1.8 mM. Notably, the 1.8 mM NH4Cl condition received no external nitrogen supplementation; the reported NH4Cl originated solely from carryover from the inoculum. H2 starvation was induced by flushing the serum vial containing the inoculant with N2, this was done to remove the H2 from the headspace and deprive M. wolfeii BSEL from electron donor. In this condition, the serum vial was placed back in the incubator and incubated for defined periods of starvation before transferring the culture to the Balch tubes. The Balch tubes were supplied with the 70% H2:30% CO2 mixture. The defined starvation periods were 1, 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h.

The cultivation order inside each perturbation group was randomized to minimize the potential confounding effects associated with inoculation order. Five biological replicates were cultivated in each perturbation condition. One control group, cultivated in optimal growth conditions, was introduced and cultivated within each perturbation group. The optimal growth conditions used for the control were 64 °C, −310 mV redox potential, 18 mM NH4Cl, and 0 h of starvation.

Cellular functions and computation of microbial robustness

The cells concentration over time was fitted to a binary fission-based growth model (Eq. (1)), from which the specific growth rate (μ) was estimated via linear regression. The duration of the lag phase was calculated from a relationship derived from the binary fission growth model (Eq. (2)), where xCells is the dry cells weight concentration over time, t is the cultivation kinetic time starting from inoculation, xCellso is the initial biomass concentration prior initiation of exponential phase, and b is the intercept of the linear model determined by linear regression. All the linearized growth curves had an R2 ≥ 0.98. The cultures were stopped by transferring the Balch tubes to room temperature while M. wolfeii BSEL was growing in late exponential phase (OD590 was in between 0.2 and 0.3). The temperature of the tubes was allowed to equilibrate to room temperature for approximately 45 min. Immediately, the pressure in each tube was normalized with N2 to 144 kPa (absolute pressure). Then, the composition of the headspace was analyzed using a quadrupole mass spectrometer as described previously [16]. Mass balance on the batch culture was conducted to determine the CH4 productivity, biomass yield, and CH4 yield, using Eqs. (3)–(5), where yf is the molar fraction of the gas at the end of the batch culture, yo is the molar fraction of the gas at the beginning of the batch culture, nHSf and nHSo are the total number of moles of gas in the headspace of the Balch tube at the end and at the beginning of the batch culture, respectively (estimated after headspace normalization with N2, using the ideal gas law), xCellsf is the cells concentration at the end of the batch culture, and VL is the liquid volume.

Immediately after gas analysis, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 G for 4 min. The cell pellets were washed 2 times with phosphate buffer saline (PBS). During the second washing step, the cell pellets were transferred to 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellets were stored at −80 °C. The cell pellets were then transferred to the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) to conduct proteins separation, peptides preparation, and proteins identification via global proteomics.

Microbial robustness was defined as a Fano factor-based metric as demonstrated by Trivellin, Olsson and Rugbjerg [22] for yeast cells exposed to various environmental perturbations. The standard deviation (σ2) among biological replicates of the same condition was determined. Then, the Fano factor was calculated dividing σ2 by the mean of the cell function i inside the same perturbation group P (mi,P) (See Eq. (6)). To calculate the robustness R, all Fano factors were divided by the overall mean for the cellular function i across perturbations (Mi) (See Eq. (7)). With this definition, R adopts an upper limit of 0 in the case when dispersion among biological replicates is not detected for a cellular function. When dispersion is detected, R acquires negative values that are inversely proportional to the standard deviation of the cellular function.

Samples digestion and peptides preparation

Quadruplicate samples corresponding to the following conditions were selected for conducting bottom-up proteomics; Temperature: 56, 60, 64, 68, and 72 °C. Redox potential: −310, −306, −304, −301, −277 mV. Nitrogen source: 1.8, 6.3, 18.0, 28.8 mM NH4Cl. H2 Starvation: 0, 2, 12, 24, 48 h. Cell pellets were processed with MPLex (Metabolite, Protein, Lipid extraction) to precipitate proteins [31]. The protein was then digested into peptides via micro spin column suspension trapping (S-trap) [32] according to the instructions of the manufacturer. A bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay was conducted to determine peptide concentration [33] and peptides were vialed for LC-MS/MS in equal concentration for global proteomics.

LC-MS/MS and proteomics database generation and analysis

Mass spectra for peptides were generated on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid (ThermoFisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) mass spectrometer outfitted with a High-Field Asymmetric Waveform Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) Pro source with three compensation voltages set for reducing peptide redundancy, −40, −60, and −80 V. The Orbitrap resolution was set to 120,000 with the maximum ion time set to 50 ms and the automatic gain control set to 4×105. Peptides were then fragmented in the ion trap using a rapid scan rate and a fixed collision energy of 32%. Ions were generated via nano-electrospray ionization at a flow rate of 200 nL/min with the spray voltage set to 2.2 kV and the ion transfer tube set to 300 °C. Peptides were separated for 120 min in a NanoAcquity (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) liquid chromatograph equipped with an in-house packed column (40 cm x 75 µm) containing 1.7-µm Waters C18 BEH stationary phase (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). The packing was performed into a fused silica integrated-tip column (CoAnn Technologies, Richland, WA, USA). Prior elution, the samples were first loaded onto a 5 cm x 150 µm (internal diameter), 5 µm-Jupiter C18 (Phenomenex, Torrance CA, USA) trapping column using a gradient of mobile phase A (water + 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid) (Optima LCMS grade, Fisher Scientific, Saint Louis, MO, USA). The gradient was as follows (time:%B): 0 min:1%, 8 min:1%, 20 min:2%, 110 min:20%, 123 min:35%, 128 min:75%, 130 min:95%, 132 min:95%. The gradient was followed by a quick wash and equilibration.

Peptide amino acid sequences were assigned to MS/MS spectra using the MSGF+ algorithm [34] in combination with the translated genome annotation of M. wolfeii BSEL [16,35]. The MS-GF+ parameter file included a parent ion tolerance of 20 ppm, allowing for partially tryptic peptides and dynamic and static modifications associated with oxidized methionine (+15.9949 Da) and cysteine alkylation (+57.0215 Da), respectively. Selected ion chromatograms (SICs) were generated for each parent ion selected for fragmentation using MASIC [36] that included generating arbitrary abundance information associated with the statistical moment of area associated with peak area for each SIC. MS/MS search results using MS-GF+ were extracted using PNNL’s in-house developed tool Mage Extractor that combines results from multiple LC-MS/MS datasets into a single tab-delimited or SQLite Database (https://github.com/PNNL-Comp-Mass-Spec/Mage).

Identified peptides from each perturbation group were filtered to a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤ 1% using a decoy database approach [37] and a fine adjustment of calculated Q-values (Q-values ≤ 0.0103) using SQL Server Management Studio 19. Only one of the redundant peptides measured within a sample using different MS/MS fragmentation ion voltages (see above) but having the same arbitrary abundance (calculated statistical moment of area from measured ion intensities) was retained. Additional sample peptide redundancy within the same ion voltage was further collapsed by summing the arbitrary abundances associated with redundant peptide sequences. Digestion prefix and suffix residues were trimmed, followed by generation of a peptide crosstab for each sample set. Only peptides measured in at least 3 of 4 biological replicates were retained and peptides from each sample set were combined into a final peptide database.

Abundances of peptides measured across sample sets were log2 transformed and normalized (Figure S1 and S2) to account for small differences in total mass of injected peptides using the central tendency normalization algorithm found within the InfernoRDN (formerly Dante [38]) suite of proteomic tools. Box plots prior to and after normalization were generated showing alignment of means post normalization. The box plot for one replicate of the temperature perturbation (68 °C) was observed to deviate significantly from the others and contained substantially less biomass than the others during sample preparation. For these reasons, it was subsequently removed from further analysis. The antilog abundances of normalized peptides were used to estimate protein abundance by summing the abundances of individual peptides for each protein [38]. For missing abundances, half the lowest estimated protein abundance was used to facilitate comparisons across perturbations. Estimated abundances of proteins were log2-transformed to facilitate statistical comparison between perturbations using calculated Z-scores or Welch’s t-test assuming unequal variances. Volcano plots of differential abundance were drawn in R 4.3.3 [39].

Functional annotation of identified proteins was conducted using three methods. To leverage information from the previously sequenced type strain M. wolfeii DSM 2970 (NCBI project PRJNA622823, sample SAMN30825859), predicted protein sequences from M. wolfeii strain BSEL were searched against UniProt proteome (https://uniprot.org/proteomes) record UP001065373 (downloaded on 2024–09–19) using BLASTP v2.12.0+ (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/executables/blast+/) with a 95% sequence identity threshold. BSEL predicted proteins were also classified into functional families using NCBI’s Protein Family Models v13.0 (https://ftp.ncbi.nih.gov/hmm/) via hmmer v3.4 [40] with trusted cutoffs, and KOfam v2024–07–01 via kofamscan v1.3 (https://github.com/takaram/kofam_scan).

Protein-protein interaction predictions were carried via input of protein sequences into the Pipeline for the Extraction of Predicted Protein-Protein Interactions (PEPPI) [41] server (https://zhanggroup.org/PEPPI) and the AlphaFold 3 (AF3) [42] server (https://alphafoldserver.com). To minimize the effect of random seeds, AF3 was run two to five times and the ipTM results were averaged.

Statistical analyses

The overall means of microbial performance reported were calculated across all biological replicates for each of the cellular functions across perturbations. Unless stated otherwise, all errors reported are 95% confidence intervals. As indicated in figure legends, statistical comparisons of robustness ranks were conducted using the non-parametric Wilcoxon test with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Comparisons of microbial performance were conducted using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by post-hoc Conover tests against the performance of the unperturbed control group with a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.025. In the multivariate analyses, error ellipses represent 95% confidence regions for each perturbation group. Differential protein abundance analyses were performed using Welch’s t-tests, applying a |fold-change| cutoff of > 2 and a significance threshold of p < 0.01. All protein differential abundance comparisons were conducted between perturbation groups.

Results and discussion

During biomethanation, hydrogenotrophic methanogens can experience process perturbations arising from spatial and temporal heterogeneities in large-scale bioreactors. Common operational perturbations include temperature shifts caused by heat‑transfer limitations or control system failures, O2 contamination during start-up, maintenance, or safety interventions that elevate the redox potential and induce oxidative stress. Besides, variable supply of nitrogen source associated with nutrient dosing strategies, and H2 starvation resulting from process interruptions or fluctuations in biogas composition are expected to happen. These perturbations can directly compromise methanogenic performance and robustness. Determining the effect of these perturbations on the robustness of Methanothermobacter is therefore important to devise strategies to maximize the longevity of the process.

Overall metabolic performance under optimal and stress conditions

The metabolic performance of the methanogen was evaluated under four bioprocess relevant perturbations: temperature shifts, oxidative stress, nitrogen depletion, and H2 starvation. These conditions were selected to reflect operational challenges that may be commonly encountered during scale-up and long term RNG biomanufacturing operations. As identified in a previous phenotypic characterization of the strain [16]; the optimal growth conditions were 64 °C, −310 mV redox potential, 18 mM NH4+, and 0 h of starvation. Across all perturbations, five cellular functions were quantified to assess the physiological responses of the methanogen: specific growth rate (μ), lag phase duration, biomass yield, CH4 productivity, and CH4 yield. The overall trends are shown in Fig. 2, which illustrates how deviations from optimal conditions affect these functions. In general, the deviations of M. wolfeii BSEL cell functions from the overall means became more apparent at higher intensities in each perturbation group. Inspection of Fig. 2 shows that the magnitudes of the perturbations were comprehensive enough to capture responses in the cellular functions in this study.

Evaluation of the cell functions associated to the performance of the methanogenic strain to conduct CO2 and H2 conversion into CH4. All points are independent biological replicates (n = 5). The culture did not grow at a redox potential higher than −277 mV. The horizontal dotted lines show the overall mean of the cell functions across all perturbation groups. The overall means of each cellular function across perturbation groups were 2.3 mg cells/mmol H2 consumed (biomass yield), 5.3 mmol CH4/L/h (CH4 productivity), 80.8 mmol CH4/g cells (CH4 yield), 8.9 h (lag phase), and 0.25 1/h (μ).

Temperature shifts and oxidative stress emerged as the most disruptive factors, causing clear declines in specific growth rate and CH4 productivity outside a tolerance windows (64 to 70 °C and −310 to −308 mV redox potential). At temperatures below 64 °C or above 70 °C, CH4 productivity and biomass yield were increasingly impaired, showing that thermal stress compromises both anaerobic respiration and biomass synthesis. Similarly, oxidative stress at redox potentials ≥ −308 mV led to extended lag phases and reduced CH4 productivity, underscoring the O2 vulnerability of methanogenesis, but also the ability of methanogens to overcome mild oxidative stress (up to −277 mV redox potential) at the cost of an extended lag-phase of up to 30 h.

In contrast, nitrogen depletion and H2 starvation elicited neutral or even positive effects on certain performance metrics in the tested conditions. For example, prolonged H2 starvation (≥ 24 h) increased CH4 productivity by up to 82% relative to the control, suggesting that energy limitation may trigger adaptive responses favoring methanogenesis over growth. The previous was supported by the differential abundance of proteins unique to the H2 starvation perturbation. However, this improvement came at the cost of higher variability among biological replicates, highlighting a potential tradeoff between enhanced performance and robustness. Such variability is undesirable in industrial RNG production, where a consistent output is critical for grid integration and economic viability. This emphasizes the need for understanding and enhancing the robustness of methanogenic strains to mitigate temperature fluctuations and O2 fluctuations during scale up. Overall, these findings reveal that while M. wolfeii BSEL can tolerate nutrient limitation and energy stress, its performance is highly sensitive to thermal and oxidative perturbations. The statistical significance of the impacts of thermal and oxidative stress on the cellular functions, as well as the tradeoffs between performance and robustness, are further explored below.

Summarized influence of environmental perturbations on methanogenic robustness

Beyond mean performance, microbial robustness determines the predictability of microbial functions under stress, which is essential for reliable scale up of biomanufacturing operations catalyzed by methanogenic strains. Robustness R was computed from the Fano factor and normalized by the overall mean of each cellular function as defined in Eqs. (6) & (7) [22]. Unless otherwise noted, comparisons were conducted using non-parametric tests and data dispersion is reported as confidence intervals (α = 0.95). The resulting summary and statistical comparisons of methanogenic performance and robustness values are shown in Fig. 3.

Fano-based microbial robustness quantification and tradeoffs between robustness and cell performances. A.- Average robustness of the cell functions when the methanogen was exposed to temperature changes, oxidative stress (changes in redox potential), low concentrations of nitrogen source in the medium, and H2 starvation. Error bars reflect the robustness confidence interval. B.- Min-max normalized performance under each perturbation condition. C.- Mean normalized robustness under each perturbation condition.

Fig. 3A summarizes R across perturbation groups. Temperature and oxidative stress consistently reduced the average robustness across multiple functions, while H2 starvation and nitrogen depletion preserved robustness in nearly all functions. For lag phase duration, oxidative stress led to the lowest robustness values (R = −0.124 ± 0.062), followed by thermal stress (R = −0.086 ± 0.081), with both ranks differing significantly from nitrogen depletion and H2 starvation (Wilcoxon rank sum test p-values < 0.0038). The relatively low robustness of lag phase under oxidative stress indicates heterogeneous initiation of growth following O2 exposure, suggesting that the induced redox imbalance in the culture led to a differentially delayed recovery among replicates. CH4 productivity exhibited a similar behavior, with oxidative stress and temperature ranking lowest (R = −0.035 ± 0.013 and −0.029 ± 0.011, respectively), while H2 starvation and nitrogen depletion ranked highest (R = −0.020 ± 0.007 and −0.022 ± 0.006, respectively). However, these rank differences in CH4 productivity robustness between perturbation groups were not significant (Wilcoxon rank sum test p-values > 0.16). This suggests that the regulatory mechanisms of the energy metabolism of M. wolfeii BSEL, and possibly other methanogens, are effective at stabilizing the carbon metabolic flux rates in variable environmental conditions. In contrast, the robustness of the CH4 yield was significantly lower within the thermal stress and H2 starvation groups (R = −0.031 ± 0.017 and −0.016 ± 0.005, respectively) when compared to nitrogen depletion and oxidative stress (Wilcoxon rank sum test p-values < 0.03). These group level outcomes suggest that, while the variability of the overall carbon metabolic rates was apparently not influenced by the perturbations, thermal stress and H2 starvation can increase the heterogeneity of the total mass of CH4 that each cell synthesized throughout the entire batch culture for the tested conditions.

Interestingly, for specific growth rate, the oxidative stress treatment displayed a robustness that ranked significantly higher than the rest of the perturbation groups (R = −0.007 ± 0.003) (Wilcoxon rank sum test p-values < 0.012). A similar behavior was observed for the biomass yield, in which the average robustness in the oxidative stress group ranked higher than the rest of the perturbation groups (R = −0.006 ± 0.003), with a significant difference when compared with the H2 starvation group (Wilcoxon rank sum test p-values = 0.011). This pattern suggests that oxidative stress triggers a focused stress response that prioritizes redox homeostasis and growth maintenance over full metabolic optimization. Methanogens are known to harbor antioxidant defense systems such as rubrerythrins, superoxide dismutases, and thiol-based redox proteins, which mitigate oxidative damage and preserve essential processes like biomass synthesis and cell division even under oxygen exposure [43,44]. These mechanisms likely protect growth functions, while energy metabolism remain more susceptible to perturbation. This interpretation aligns with the proteomic observations discussed in the next section, where PAS-domain sensors and redox-regulating proteins were enriched under oxidative stress, indicating that robustness patterns develop from stress-specific molecular mechanisms.

Tradeoffs between methanogenic performance and microbial robustness

Because cellular performance metrics are inherently interrelated, their interpretation is most informative when evaluated collectively rather than in isolation. Accordingly, observed trends in individual functions were considered together with variability and proteomic results, allowing for a more coherent assessment of perturbation‑specific physiological responses.

Fig. 3B displays the min-max normalized values of each cellular function in each perturbation condition as well as the results of statistical comparisons between each experimental condition and the control cultivated in optimal conditions. H2 starvation, oxidative stress, and temperature shifts led to statistically significant increases in the lag phase with > 100% increases relative to the control (Conover p-values < 0.015), except for the cultures incubated at 66 and 68 °C. Similarly, the CH4 productivity significantly decreased by ca. 29.9 – 33.1% under oxidative stress (Conover p-values < 0.011) and by ca. 30.1 – 35.8% at temperatures below 60 °C (Conover p-values < 0.022). These general performance losses align with the lag phase and CH4 productivity robustness losses when the culture was exposed to thermal and oxidative stress (See Fig. 3A and Fig. 3C). This coupling of performance loss and low robustness indicate that thermal stressors not only depress metabolic rates but also amplify the biological variability in the culture, creating a dual constraint on bioprocess reliability. Thus, it is identified that the sensitivity to thermal and oxidative stress on methanogens are primary functional targets to be optimized via strain engineering.

In contrast to the coupled losses observed under thermal and oxidative stress, H2 starvation exhibited more complex tradeoffs in the tested conditions. As shown in Fig. 3B, prolonged H₂ starvation (≥ 2 h) increased CH4 productivity by ca. 7.1 – 62.0% compared to the control (Conover p-values < 0.08), yet robustness for this function remained unchanged when compared to other perturbations (Fig. 3A), indicating that productivity gains were accompanied by preservation of robustness. For specific growth rate, the response to H2 starvation was distinct from CH4 productivity. As shown in Fig. 3B, μ reached its maximum after 2 h of starvation, increasing by ca. 17.8% relative to the control (Conover p-value = 0.0168), and then exhibited a slight decline over longer starvation periods. However, these decreases were never statistically significant compared to the control (Conover p > 0.051), indicating that growth performance remained largely stable despite extended energy limitation. This pattern suggests that short-term starvation may trigger adaptive mechanisms that temporarily enhance growth, while prolonged starvation slows cell division without introducing significant variability among replicates, as reflected by the robustness values in Fig. 3A.

Overall, Fig. 3 shows that robustness and performance are not inherently coupled. Temperature and oxidative stress represent conditions where both metrics deteriorate, H2 starvation has complex tradeoffs between growth and culture stability, and nitrogen depletion lead to very few changes in both metrics. These patterns highlight the need for understanding the molecular strategies that decouple productivity from variability.

Given the definition of microbial robustness as proposed by Trivellin, Olsson and Rugbjerg [22], the Fano factor-based R metric becomes less sensitive when the variance in the dataset is largely dominated by a single cellular function. This occurs because the magnitude of σ2 is proportional to the range of the cell function. If the dominant function is not normally-distributed, the mean normalization of the Fano factor is less effective to generate a sensitive response on the robustness metric in all perturbations. This issue, which is associated with the length of the observation window, is commonly encountered when using variation coefficient-like metrics, such as the Fano factor [45]. The duration of the lag phase in this study had a considerably larger observation window (2.2 – 46.4 h) than other cellular functions, and the mean of the lag phase was skewed towards low values (See Fig. 2). Thus, R was dominated by the variations in lag phase. This masked the response in robustness of other functions, such as biomass yield and μ, with smaller ranges. To address this limitation and improve interpretability, we applied unit variance standardization followed by principal component analysis (PCA), a machine learning technique for dimensionality reduction, enabling visualization of performance and robustness in a balanced multidimensional space.

As shown in Fig. 4, PCA revealed clear clustering patterns and R gradients that were not apparent in the previous univariate analyses of robustness and performance. Temperature treatments separated into two distinct clusters: low (< 64 °C) and high (> 64 °C), reflecting different physiological responses. Similarly, H2 starvation samples grouped into short (≤ 12 h) and long (> 12 h) starvation subgroups. Robustness vectors projected onto the PC1–PC2 plane indicated that low temperatures were associated with reduced robustness in CH4 yield, μ, and biomass yield, whereas high temperatures and oxidative stress corresponded to low robustness in lag phase and CH4 productivity. Nitrogen depletion, in contrast, aligned with increased robustness in CH4 productivity, likely due to prioritization of energy metabolism under limited nitrogen availability. These findings show that multivariate analysis may improve interpretability of complex biological datasets and suggest that incorporating machine learning tools into the workflow proposed by Trivellin, Olsson and Rugbjerg [22] could advance the emerging concept of microbial robustness by enabling detection of stress-specific tradeoff patterns and prediction of robustness shifts under untested conditions, and interpretation of species-to-species variability, which is critical for devising robust biomanufacturing systems using methanogens as biocatalysts.

Contributions of the proteome to robustness

>34,500 unique peptides were measured across all perturbation conditions tested resulting in an average of 1462 ± 10 observed proteins being identified (Table S1). This number of observed proteins coincides with a large degree of proteome coverage averaging 79.4 ± 0.3% of proteins predicted from the structural annotation of M. wolfeii BSEL [16]. Coverage varied slightly with each perturbation from 79.4% to 79.8% (Figure S3). The differences in coverage were only statistically significant between the redox and nitrogen depletion groups and between the nitrogen depletion and H2 starvation groups (p-value < 0.01), where the difference for the later comparison was 1.1% or approximately 16 proteins. Such high observed coverage and concomitant low proteome variability between perturbations suggests M. wolfeii BSEL constitutively synthesizes much of its proteome that may contribute to its robustness. Liu et al. [46], reported a similarly high proteome coverage (83.29% of predicted proteins) when evaluating CH4 formation by M. thermoautotrophicus under 3 different temperature conditions. Other comparative studies reporting proteome coverage for Methanothermobacter isolates utilizing LC-MS/MS based proteomics are generally lower [47] or have relied on mass spectrometry analysis of 2-D gel electrophoresis results [48].

The small variation in proteome coverage between perturbations was surprising when considering the statistically supported differences in robustness for certain cell functions (See Fig. 3). To further investigate whether changes in relative abundance of proteins were explanatory, (O2) exposure, temperature, H2 starvation, and nitrogen depletion protein specific phenotypes were identified through a NxN comparison of each perturbation. Proteins were selected on the basis of having statistically significant differential abundance (p-value < 0.01) in a consistent direction across replicates (increase or decrease) under only one of the comparisons. Only proteins observed as having statistically significant differential abundances (p-value<0.01) and a consistent direction in fold-change (increase or decrease) for the comparator to the common baseline perturbation (e.g., H2 starvation vs oxidative stress, thermal vs oxidative stress, and nitrogen depletion vs oxidative stress) were retained. Further, the retained set of proteins was filtered by establishing the requirement that a differential abundance for a protein observed in the other comparisons (e.g. nitrogen vs H2 starvation, nitrogen vs temperature, H2 starvation vs temperature) was not statistically supported (p-value > 0.01). From this conservative analysis, the number of proteins identified for a perturbation phenotype (123 proteins in total; Table S2) inversely correlated with the trend in robustness for the CH4 yield and biomass cell functions. Specifically, 12 differentially abundant proteins were assigned to the redox perturbation phenotype where CH4 yield displayed the greatest robustness. In contrast, 60 proteins were assigned to the temperature perturbation phenotype, for which CH4 yield was least robust. This trend continued for H2 starvation, where 23 proteins were assigned, correlating with an intermediate level of robustness. The number of proteins identified for the nitrogen depletion proteome phenotype did not fit within this trend. Also, the number of proteins identified for each perturbation phenotype did not correlate to differences in robustness. For the latter, this was expected, since the cell samples were collected for proteomic measurements during late exponential phase. Together, these phenotype-specific sets indicate stress-dependent proteome remodeling that aligns with the performance–robustness tradeoffs in Fig. 3, motivating targeted interventions at proteins that track with loss of robustness. From the 123 differentially abundant proteins identified, examples of these proteins and hypothesized biological roles associated with thermal and oxidative stress are discussed below.

The twelve proteins with differential abundance under the oxidative stress perturbation increased by greater than 2-fold, suggesting a strong translational requirement by the methanogen to maintain CH4 and biomass yields (Fig. 2). For example, A0A9E7RTZ1 annotated as an uncharacterized protein increased in differential abundance by >8-fold (Fig. 5a; 8.80 ± 3.71). This uncharacterized protein is novel to both M. wolfeii and the thermophilic hydrogenotrophic methanogen Methanothermobacter sp. THM-1 according to UniRef Clustering (sequence identity threshold of > 90% when comparing to UniProt Reference Clusters [49]). While a biological role for A0A9E7RTZ1 has not been reported, our observation is the first reporting of this protein translationally responding to the presence of oxidative stress within M. wolfeii BSEL. Based on the robustness patterns in Fig. 3A, A0A9E7RTZ1 represents an exploratory target for functional characterization and engineering aimed at preserving CH4 productivity under oxidative stress.

Two proteins annotated as S-box proteins and containing the Per-Arnt-Sims (PAS) domain (A0A9E7RSK1, 3.38±1.00; A0A9E7RWM9, 2.99±1.34) were unique to the redox perturbation phenotype. The KEGG Orthology [50] predicted both of these proteins as belonging to the two-component sensor (TCS) histidine kinase (HK) protein family (K00936) [[51], [52], [53]]. Both proteins are novel to Methanothermobacter where A0A9E7RWM9 shows >90% UniRef clustering similarity to a homolog only within Methanothermobacter sp. THM1, while A0A9E7RSK1 has no reported homolog to any other methanogens even at a clustering similarity metric of >50%. The observation of both being measured unique to the redox perturbation phenotype suggests that the PAS domains are involved in internal oxygen or redox sensing [54] and the HK domain involved in regulating redox stress response or possibly regulation of methanogenesis, as has been demonstrated in Methanosaeta harundinacea [55]. The methanogenesis pathway is likely a primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [43] being the center of energy generation and biosynthesis, and these PAS domain-containing proteins may play a vital role in maintaining the high robustness in CH4 productivity observed in the robustness study (See Fig. 3A) through reducing the accumulation of ROS within the cell. Accordingly, PAS/HK-domain sensors constitute actionable engineering targets: overexpression or promoter tuning of A0A9E7RSK1 and A0A9E7RWM9 could enhance redox sensing and response. CRISPRi knockdown studies can test whether reducing PAS/HK signaling diminishes CH4 productivity robustness under oxidative stress.

We observed a 3.98 ± 1.40-fold increase in abundance of RNA 3′-terminal phosphate cyclase (A0A9E7UNT7, RtcA). This protein converts the 3′-phosphate group at the RNA terminus into a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate common to RNA processing after nuclease activity. RtcA is part of the RNA repair Rtc operon (RtcAB), that is generally considered responsive to the effects of external growth conditions [56]. Further, it has been proposed that the activity of RtcA (addition of 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate at the terminus of RNA) may act as a protective mechanism against ROS by preventing direct attack on the 2′‑hydroxyl group on the ribose, which is susceptible to oxidation due to its nucleophilic nature [57]. However, the understanding of RNA damage, repair, and implications within methanogens is still under development. Given its differential abundance under oxidative stress, RtcA and the RtcAB operon are practical targets to test whether enhanced RNA repair improves robustness of specific growth rate and biomass yield without compromising metabolic rates.

While CH4 yield within M. wolfeii BSEL was most robust to alterations in O2 addition, it was least robust to changes in temperature and showed a larger proteome response in the number of differentially abundant proteins. Of the 60 proteins associated with the response to temperature, 16 decreased in abundance when compared to the other perturbations, while 44 increased in abundance (Fig. 5). An increase in the abundance of sigma factor RpoS (A0A9E7UMW3), and both large (A0A9E7RS91, A0A9E7RV71, A0A9E7UM75) and small (A0A9E7UMR9, A0A9E7UMT0, A0A9E7RVW5) ribosomal proteins (Table S2) suggests protein expression is an important component of resistance to temperature changes in M. wolfeii BSEL. A change in environmental temperature can have broad physiological consequences ranging from an alteration of protein and membrane lipid stability to an alteration of metabolic rates that in turn can impact intracellular homeostasis [44,[58], [59], [60]]. Thus, it was not surprising to observe proteins involved in trehalose production (OtsB; A0A9E7UM20) for balancing osmolarity differences, chaperone functions (A0A9E7RV81), pH (A0A9E7RSS3), and redox homeostasis (A0A9E7RTD0, A0A9E7RRB2) all having significant differential abundances to temperature compared to oxidative stress, H2 starvation, and nitrogen depletion. These observations highlight concrete engineering levers for increasing methanogens robustness towards thermal stress: upregulating OtsB could enhance osmoprotection, increasing the expression of A0A9E7RV81 and RpoS could support proteostasis and transcriptional reprogramming, and tuning the novel redox regulators A0A9E7RTD0 and A0A9E7RRB2 could mitigate temperature-induced redox imbalances.

Observed proteins that directly connect robustness in CH4 yield include 3 subunits for tetrahydromethanopterin S-methyltransferase, involved in the transfer of a methyl group to coenzyme M, the methyl carrier involved in the final step of methane formation. All three subunits decreased by greater than 2-fold (A0A9E7RT86, MtrB, −2.67 ± 0.10; A0A9E7RVV0, MtrF, −2.56 ± 0.3; A0A9E7RU96, MtrG, −2.63±0.38) abundance relative to the other perturbations. The mean fold change for the other 5 Mtr subunits (MtrA, MtrD, MtrE, MtrH) also decreased in abundance but did not meet the statistical threshold for significance. Liu et al. [46] reported a similar proteomic observation for Mtr within M. thermautotrophicus exposed to both high and low sub-optimal temperatures, noting that an observed decrease in methanogenesis proteins could explain the lower observed growth rates and biomass at time of harvest from this energy yielding pathway. Because the CH4 yield (mmol CH4/g cells) couples energy generation from methanogenesis to biomass production, a decline in protein abundance for Mtr could directly result in M. wolfeii BSEL being less robust for this cell function to changes in temperature compared to the other perturbations. This points to the Mtr complex as an engineering focus to stabilize multi-subunit assemblies via chaperone co-expression and mitigate temperature-induced robustness losses in CH4 yield.

In contrast to the Mtr subunits above, A0A9E7UNA4 (8.90 ± 2.06) and A0A9E7RTD5 (3.31 ± 0.68) both annotated as FmdE family-like proteins increased in differential abundance. A third FmdE domain containing protein also increased in abundance, but this was not statistically significant (A0A9E7UME9; 2.80 ± 1.49). FmdE (Q1MVD3) is part of the molybdenum formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase fmdECB operon in M. wolfeii [26], although its specific function has yet to be determined. A0A9E7UNA4 and A0A9E7RTD5 are likely under different regulatory control as they are not in close proximity to the operon, and differential abundances of the other Fmd subunits in response to temperature were not consistent with those observed for the FmdE family homologs above. Nevertheless, the large observed increase in differential abundance in response to temperature and a high degree of structural similarity between A0A9E7UME9, A0A9E7UNA4, A0A9E7RTD5 versus FmdE (Q1MVD3; Fig. 6 and Table S3), suggests these domain homologs could associate with or help stabilize FmdE via protein-protein interactions. This hypothesis was tested using the deep learning-based protein structure prediction system, AlphaFold 3, and a pipeline for predicting structure similarity and viable protein-protein interactions (PEPPI pipeline). Indeed, protein-protein interaction predictions via PEPPI indicated an interaction between FmdE and these family homologs as being likely (Table S3). Conversely, PEPPI analysis of interactions between FmdB and FmdC subunits versus A0A9E7UNA4 and A0A9E7RTD5 were very unlikely (See Table S3), confirming the specificity of the protein-protein interactions between the family homologs and FmdE. Based on these observations, we propose the existence of a homodimer/multimer-like interaction between Fmd subunit E-like proteins in response to temperature stress within M. wolfeii BSEL and other phylogenetically related methanogens. These predicted interactions directly connect to our hypothesis that stress response proteins can associate with canonical methanogenesis subunits to stabilize metabolic function under perturbation. They also suggest that overexpressing the FmdE-like paralogs (A0A9E7UNA4, A0A9E7RTD5) or co-expression with FmdE could reinforce the CO2-reduction machinery in methanogens during thermal stress. The protein-protein interactions predicted by AlphaFold‑based structural modeling could be experimentally tested in future work using complementary approaches such as co‑immunoprecipitation to assess native complex formation and microscale thermophoresis to quantify direct binding affinities between FmdE and FmdE-like paralogs [61,62].

The predicted association of FmdE with FmdE-like paralogs during thermal stress and the coordinated activity of PAS and HK sensors under oxidative stress, illustrate how cooperative networks may help stabilize CO2 reduction and influence robustness under specific stress conditions in methanogens. These observations suggest that the adaptive function can arise from interactions with currently uncharacterized proteins, especially under metabolic stress. Importantly, the network‑level interactions identified here indicate that robustness is likely emergent from coordinated, multi‑level regulations rather than attributable to single proteins in isolation, highlighting the limitations of single‑gene modification approaches. Each protein detected by proteomics can be considered a piece in an interaction puzzle, with some supplying catalytic activity and others contributing signaling, scaffolding, chaperone assistance, or stability. Pairing interaction mapping with abundance profiling can help move descriptive proteomics into a more predictive framework that uses network modules to complement single protein abundance analyses and guide strain optimization for improved performance and robustness.

Conclusion

Across temperature shifts, oxidative stress, nitrogen depletion, and H2 starvation, we observed that thermal and oxidative stress were the primary constraints on the robustness of M. wolfeii BSEL, especially for CH4 productivity and lag phase duration, whereas H2 starvation produced performance gains in some cases without large losses in robustness, and nitrogen depletion had very few effects on strain performance and robustness. Proteomic analyses indicated coordinated changes that are consistent with these patterns. Under temperature stress, ribosomal proteins, trehalose synthesis, chaperone functions, and redox regulators increased, suggesting coupled control of translation, proteostasis, and intracellular balance. Under oxidative stress, PAS and HK domain proteins were enriched, suggesting enhanced sensing and response to redox imbalance. Alpha Fold 3 and PEPPI structural modelling supported our hypothesis, since the canonical FmdE was predicted to associate with FmdE-like paralogs with differential abundance during thermal stress, a pattern suggesting an interaction-based reinforcement of the CO2 reduction machinery. These interaction predictions help frame how adaptive function may arise when previously uncharacterized proteins act within cooperative assemblies.

Our findings provide actionable starting points for engineering robustness in methanogens. Engineering candidates include stabilizing multi-subunit methanogenesis complexes such as Mtr, tuning PAS and HK sensors to improve redox signaling, and modulating chaperone and osmoprotection capacity to maintain metabolic functions under redox fluctuations. The practical relevance is strong for RNG biomanufacturing, where hydrogenotrophic methanogens upgrade biogas into high purity CH4 that can be exported through existing natural gas grids as a drop-in biofuel. Robust methanogenic strains can also help valorize CO2-rich industrial streams, including syngas and combustion exhausts, while supporting decarbonization goals. By pairing interaction mapping with abundance profiling, proteomics can move from description to prediction, providing network-level protein design rules for methanogenic biocatalysts used in these applications.
