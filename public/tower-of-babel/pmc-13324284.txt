Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals cellular stress responses to hypothermic preservation in liver-on-chip model
Authors: Joseph Mugaanyi, Jing Huang, Sheng Ye, Jiongze Fang, Arthur Musinguzi, Lei Dai, Caide Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold preservation is a critical logistical step in liver transplantation but induces ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI), a key driver of early graft dysfunction. While bulk tissue assays capture global damage, they obscure the cell-type-specific transcriptional programs engaged during hypothermic storage. We utilized a multicellular human liver-on-chip model comprising Patient-Derived Organoids (PDOs), hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), and macrophages. Chips were exposed to 24-h static cold storage using either the clinical standard University of Wisconsin (UW) solution or a hyperbranched polyglycerol (HPG)-based formulation, followed by normothermic reperfusion. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed to map transcriptional trajectories across the preservation-reperfusion axis. We identified candidate solution-dependent transcriptional differences across cell types. PDOs from UW-preserved chips showed comparatively higher mean expression of inflammatory and oxidative stress-associated transcripts (IFI27, SAA1, HMOX1) and mitochondrially-encoded genes (MT-ND5) relative to HPG-preserved samples, which retained comparatively higher expression of homeostatic epithelial markers (EPCAM, KRT18). HSCs and LSECs in the UW group showed comparatively elevated expression of fibrosis-associated (COL1A1, TAGLN) and endothelial adhesion (ICAM1) transcripts. Ligand-receptor interaction modelling identified candidate inflammatory communication axes, including chemokine signaling interactions (CXCL1, CCL20) between macrophages and epithelial compartments, with higher predicted activity under UW preservation. This study provides an exploratory, high-resolution map of cell-type-specific transcriptional patterns associated with hypothermic preservation in a liver-on-chip model. Our findings suggest that preservation solution chemistry is associated with distinct transcriptional signatures spanning stress response, mitochondrial, and intercellular signaling pathways. Transcriptional patterns in HPG-preserved cells were consistent with comparatively attenuated injury responses; however, these observations are hypothesis-generating and require independent biological replication and functional validation, including metabolic flux assays and ROS production measurements before conclusions regarding mitochondrial protection or clinical preservation efficacy can be drawn.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50200-2.Keywords: Single-cell RNA sequencing, Liver-on-chip, Hypothermic preservation, Reperfusion injury, Hepatic organoidsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Cell biology

Introduction

Liver transplantation remains the definitive therapy for end-stage-liver disease, yet graft availability and graft quality continue to limit outcomes worldwide. A major determinant of post-transplant function is the extent of ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI), a multi-factorial process beginning during hypothermic preservation and amplified upon reperfusion. Cold ischemia disrupts mitochondrial metabolism, induces oxidative stress, activates endothelial cells, and alters inflammatory signaling, ultimately increasing the risk of primary non-function, early allograft dysfunction, and biliary complications after transplantation1–3. The University of Wisconsin (UW) solution has served as the clinical standard for hypothermic preservation of donor livers for more than three decades.4 However, growing adoption of extended-criteria and marginal grafts has highlighted limitations of UW; including insufficient support of mitochondrial integrity, endothelial activation, and impaired metabolic recovery5–7. As a result, other modified or experimental formulation have been developed with the aim of improving cellular stability during cold ischemia.

Evaluating preservation solution has traditionally relied on bulk tissue assays, histology, or biochemical markers, which obscure the heterogeneous responses of distinct liver cell types. The liver is a complex organ composed of parenchymal hepatocytes and diverse non-parenchymal cells; liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSEC), Kupffer cells/macrophages, and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs); each with unique vulnerabilities to hypothermic stress and reperfusion injury8,9. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides unprecedented resolution to dissect these cell-type-specific stress programs, mitochondrial pathways, inflammatory activation, and cytoprotective response, particularly in the context of hypothermic preservation10,11.

Micro-physiological systems such as liver-on-chip platforms enable highly controlled, human-relevant modeling of preservation injury. These systems recapitulate key architectural and functional features of the hepatic sinusoid, supporting physiological communication among hepatocyte-like cells, endothelial cells, stellate cells, and macrophages12–14. When combined with scRNA-seq, liver-on-chip models offer a mechanistic window into how preservation conditions alter transcriptional states across the hepatic cellular ecosystem, an approach that is difficult to achieve in ex vivo human grafts and animal models, where early injury events are confounded by donor variability, warm ischemia, and systemic factors.

In this study, we used a multi-cellular liver-on-chip model incorporating patient-derived liver organoids (PDOs), HSCs, LSECs and induced macrophages to compare the cellular responses to 24 h of hypothermic preservation using UW solution versus an experimental Hyperbranched Polyglycerol (HPG) preservation formulation. We aimed to profile the transcriptional states at baseline, post-preservation, and post-reperfusion and investigate the shifts and trajectories in gene expression associated with each state.

Materials and methods

Organ-on-chip setup and culture

A twelve-well, high-throughput dual-chamber organ-on-chip (OOC) system (Avatarget Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) was utilized. Patient-Derived Organoids (PDOs) were co-cultured with LX-2 cells in Matrigel (Cat\#356,255, Corning, USA) in the upper chamber. The basal chamber was seeded with Liver Sinusoidal Endothelial Cells (LSECs) and induced macrophages. A porous PDMS membrane facilitated paracrine communication between compartments. Following seeding, chips were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for three days. Perfusion was generated via gravitational flow on a rocker set to an angle of ± 7° with a 30-min cycle frequency (AvatarFlow Organ Chip Flow Initiation Controller System, Avatarget Co., Ltd.) PDOs were derived from liver tissue obtained from n = 9 distinct biological donors, 7 male and 2 female (supplement 1 for the culture protocol) and maintained in hepatic organoid expansion medium (Avatarget Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). LSECs, LX-2 and THP-1 cells were sourced from Avatarget Co., Ltd and cultured according to the supplier’s protocol. The identity of the PDOs verified via immunofluorescence staining for Albumin, KRT8 and EPCAM (Fig. 1). PDOs demonstrated normal hepatocyte CYP3A4 activity, albumin, AST and ALT secretion when seeded onto the chip (Supplement 2 Tables S1 and S2). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital (Approval Number: KY2025SL367-01) and conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Characterization of patient-derived organoids (PDOs) prior to liver-on-chip seeding. (A) Brightfield microscopy images of PDOs at Day 3, Day 6, and Day 9 of expansion culture in Matrigel. Organoids display progressive growth with increasing spheroid diameter and well-defined membrane boundaries over the culture period, consistent with healthy organoid morphology and successful expansion. (B) Immunofluorescence staining confirming hepatic epithelial identity of PDOs prior to chip seeding. Albumin (green, left; DAPI nuclear counterstain in blue; scale bar = 50 µm) shows cytoplasmic expression consistent with hepatocyte-like secretory function. KRT8 (yellow; DAPI in blue; scale bar = 100 µm) labels the intermediate filament network characteristic of simple epithelial and hepatocyte lineages, with strong uniform staining across the organoid membrane. EPCAM (green; DAPI in blue) confirms epithelial cell identity, showing membranous expression across the organoid cluster. Co-expression of Albumin, KRT8, and EPCAM collectively confirms the hepatic epithelial identity of the PDO population.

Experimental groups and sample collection

Chips were randomized into three experimental groups (n = 12 per condition): Baseline (pre-preservation), University of Wisconsin (UW) solution, and Hyperbranched Polyglycerol (HPG) solution. For the Baseline group, samples were collected directly on Day 3 without exposure to hypothermic conditions. For the preservation groups, the culture medium was replaced on Day 3 with either UW or HPG solution, and chips were stored for 24 h at 6 °C under static conditions. A subset of these preserved chips subsequently underwent reperfusion, where the preservation solution was replaced with normal culture medium and chips were returned to standard culture conditions for a 2-h rewarming period. Following their respective endpoints, LSECs and macrophages were dissociated from the chip’s lower chamber using Trypsin for 1 min 20 s and resuspended. The hepatic organoid-HSC Matrigel co-culture was dissolved using chill PBS, centrifuged for 5 min at 200 g then dissociated into single cell suspensions cells using TryPLE for 5–10 min. They were then combined with the LSEC and macrophage suspension, filtered through a 40µ cell strainer, and resuspended in cell cryopreservation medium. Post-dissociation cell viability was > 85% using Trypan Blue exclusion on a Countess II. Cells from the 12 replicate chips wells per group were pooled prior to library preparation to ensure sufficient cell numbers and biological representation. The formulations of UW and HPG solutions are reported in supplement 2, Table S3.

Sequencing was performed at Qingdao Fenghe Weilai Biotechnology Laboratory, Qingdao, China. Libraries were prepared using the DNBelab C-series High-throughput Single-cell RNA Library Preparation Kit v2.1.3. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline to generate gene-barcode count matrices aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome.

Statistical analysis

Downstream analysis was performed in Python 3.12 using the Scanpy toolkit (version 1.11), harmonypy 0.0.10, and LIANA 1.x. Key parameters: minimum genes per cell = 200, maximum genes per cell = 6,000, mitochondrial fraction threshold = 15%, highly variable gene selection (min_mean = 0.0125, max_mean = 3, min_disp = 0.5, batch_key = sample), PCA components = 50, Leiden resolution = 0.4, random seed = 42 for all stochastic steps. Batch correction and sample integration were performed using the Harmony method using ‘harmonypy’ package in python. Harmony interactively adjusts principal component embeddings across samples by minimizing a diversity penalty, correcting technical batch variation while preserving biological signal. The first 50 principal components derived from highly variable genes were used as input. Convergence was confirmed at 7 iterations (tolerance 1 × 10–4). To evaluate integration quality and rule out over-correction, UMAP embeddings computed before and after Harmony correction were compared side-by-side; post-correction embeddings showed thorough interdigitation of cells from all samples while cell-type-specific clusters remained clearly resolved. Data were normalized and log-transformed prior to the identification of highly variable genes. Dimensionality reduction was performed via Principal Component Analysis (PCA), followed by nearest-neighbor graph construction, clustering, and visualization using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Cell types were annotated based on canonical markers: PDOs (EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18), HSCs (COL1A1, TAGLN, ACTA2), LSECs (PECAM1, VWF, KDR), and macrophages (LYZ, S100A8, IL1B). Only these populations were retained for analysis. Given that each experimental condition was represented by pooled chip samples, formal statistical differential expression testing including Wilcoxon rank-sum was not performed to avoid unreliable estimates due to pseudo-replication at the cell level. Instead, transcriptional differences between conditions were characterized using mean log2 fold-change (Log2 FC) rankings computed per cell type, using normalized counts from the pre-scaling expression matrix. Genes are ranked by Log2 FC magnitude and all findings are treated as hypothesis-generating observations. Aggregated per-sample count matrices are provided as supplementary data. Transcriptional trajectories were inferred using a two-step approach. First, partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) was applied to compute a cluster-level connectivity graph, providing statistically supported transition probabilities between annotated cell-type clusters. Second, diffusion pseudotime (DPT) was computed to assign each cell a continuous pseudotemporal coordinate, ordering cells along inferred differential or stress-response axes. Embeddings were re-initialized using the PAGA layout to produce a trajectory-aware two-dimensional representation. Intercellular communication was analyzed using the LIANA framework, comparing aggregated interaction scores between experimental conditions. The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are available in the ArrayExpress repository, accession number, E-MTAB-16925, complete annotated analysis pipeline is deposited at Zenodo repository, 10.5281/zenodo.18227182 alongside all parameter settings.

Results

Single-cell sequencing identifies four major cell populations in liver organoid-on-chip model

Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed across five experimental conditions, including pre-preservation, 24-h hypothermic preservation in UW or HPG, and rewarming following each preservation environment. After quality control, over twenty thousand high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Harmony-corrected embeddings showed thorough interdigitation of cells from all five experimental conditions across shared cell-type clusters. Comparison of pre- and post-correction UMAP representations confirmed that sample-driven separation was substantially reduced while biologically meaningful clusters defined by canonical cell-type markers remained intact. Indicating that batch correction did not over-homogenize the data (Fig. 2). Dimensionality reduction revealed four major cellular populations corresponding to the expected components of the liver-on-chip model. Patient-derived hepatic organoid cells (PDOs) were identified by high expression of EPCAM, KRT8, and KRT18, whereas LSECs expressed PECAM1, VWF, and KDR. HSCs were characterized by COL1A1, TAGLN, and ACTA2, and macrophages by LYZ, S100A8, and IL1B. These annotations were supported by confirming the enrichment and specificity of canonical markers within each population. All experimental groups contributed cells to each cluster, indicating successful integration across samples and preservation conditions.

Quality control metrics and stress-related modules are broadly stable across conditions

To verify that observed transcriptional differences were not driven by technical variation, we first assessed global quality metrics across the five conditions. Total read counts, detected genes per cell, and mitochondrial transcript percentages were comparable across samples, with no evidence of systematic bias associated with the preservation environment. We further examined stress-related and mitochondrial module scores across cell populations. These signatures showed modest but consistent differences across conditions, particularly within PDO cells and endothelial cells; however, the overall distribution remained within an acceptable range, supporting the robustness of the dataset for subsequent differential and trajectory analyses (Fig. 3).

(B) Violin plots showing the distribution of aggregate stress and mitochondrial injury scores across cell types and conditions. Distributions indicate consistent quality with no severe batch effects, though varying stress levels are observed in PDOs and Endothelial cells between UW and HPG conditions.

Aggregated expression profiling identifies condition-associated transcriptional patterns across preservation and rewarming

To characterize broad transcriptional trends across the preservation-rewarming axis, per-sample expression matrices were aggregated and visualized as a heatmap of selected stress, inflammatory, and mitochondrial markers (Fig. 4). PDOs from chips preserved for 24 h showed comparatively higher mean expression of interferon-linked transcripts such as IFI27 and IFIT3, as well as stress-associated genes including HSPB1 and HMOX1, relative to the pre-preservation baseline. Following rewarming, cells showed comparatively elevated expression of acute-phase and inflammatory genes including SERPINE1 and SAA1, alongside mitochondrial-associated transcripts such as MT1E. These expression patterns varied in magnitude between preservation environments, suggesting that distinct transcriptional programs are engaged during hypothermic preservation and subsequent rewarming in a solution-dependent manner.

PDOs exhibit distinct transcriptional trajectories under different preservation environments

To capture the temporal dynamics of gene regulation across the preservation process, we generated expression trajectories for key genes in PDOs spanning all experimental conditions. Stress-associated genes such as IFI27 demonstrated strong induction during hypothermic preservation, particularly in UW-preserved samples, followed by down-regulation upon rewarming. Mitochondrial-related genes including MT-ND5 and MT1E showed divergent time-dependent patterns, with comparatively higher expression in HPG-preserved samples during rewarming.

Exploratory fold-change analysis identifies preservation-specific transcriptional candidates

Exploratory transcriptional comparisons between conditions identified candidate gene sets with preservation solution-specific expression patterns. Cells from chips preserved in UW solution for 24 h showed higher mean expression of interferon-related, stress-response, and acute-phase transcripts relative to the pre-preservation baseline, including IFI27, HSPB1, and HMOX1. A similar but comparatively attenuated pattern was observed in HPG-preserved samples relative to baseline. Direct comparison between UW and HPG at the 24-h timepoint identified candidate gene sets with divergent expression, including mitochondrially-encoded transcripts, oxidative stress-related genes, and inflammatory mediators. These differences are summarized as MA plots displaying mean log-normalized expression versus log₂ fold-change (Fig. 5). As each condition was represented by a single pooled sample (n = 1), no p-values or adjusted significance thresholds were applied; all candidate genes are treated as hypothesis-generating observations requiring validation in independently replicated experiments.

Exploratory transcriptional fold-change comparisons across preservation conditions. MA plots displaying mean log-normalized expression (x-axis) versus log₂ fold-change (y-axis) for three pairwise comparisons: Pre-preservation vs. UW 24 Hour (left), Pre-preservation vs. HPG 24 Hour (center), and UW 24 Hour vs. HPG 24 Hour (right). Red points indicate upregulated genes (log₂FC > 0.5; UP); blue points indicate downregulated genes (log₂FC < − 0.5; DOWN); grey points fall below the fold-change threshold or minimum expression filter (NS). Dashed horizontal lines denote the |log₂FC|= 0.5 threshold. Only genes with mean expression > 0.05 across both conditions are colored, to exclude fold-change estimates dominated by pseudo count noise in near-zero expressed genes. Labelled genes represent the top candidates ranked by absolute log₂FC magnitude.

Non-parenchymal cells also demonstrate solution-dependent transcriptional dynamics

We next examined stellate cells and endothelial cells to determine whether preservation environment influenced non-parenchymal compartments. HSCs demonstrated time-dependent regulation of extracellular matrix and fibroblast-associated genes such as TAGLN, COL1A1, and SERPINE1, as well as activation of stress responsive transcripts ATF2 and HMOX1. These changes were observed in all conditions but varied in magnitude between solution.

LSECs showed characteristic suppression of endothelial markers such as ANGPT2 and VWF during hypothermic preservation, followed by partial recovery after rewarming. Endothelial activation markers including VCAM1 and interferon-responsive genes also had preservation solution-specific patterns, indicating that preservation chemistry modulates endothelial responses in a distinct manner. HSCs and LSECs undergo coordinated but solution-dependent transcriptional adaptions throughout the preservation and rewarming process (Fig. 6).

Ligand-receptor inference reveals modulation of paracrine signaling during hypothermic preservation

Finally, we applied ligand-receptor inference to explore how preservation environments affected intercellular communication within the model. At end of 24 h of hypothermic preservation, UW preserved samples showed higher inferred signaling activity in several inflammation-linked interactions involving PDO-to-macrophage, LSEC-to-macrophage, and macrophage-to-macrophage communication, including SAA1-GRM7, CXCL1/8-GRM7, and CCL20-GRM7.

HPG preserved samples demonstrated reduced activation of these interactions, with relative differences varying by signaling module. When interactions across all time-points were aggregated, hypothermic preservation was associated with increased macrophage-related chemokine signaling, whereas rewarming reactivated specific PDO and endothelial signaling pathways in a condition-dependent manner. This suggests that the preservation chemistry also influences intercellular communication networks within the organoid-on-chip model (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this study, we used a liver organoid-based micro-physiological system to characterize how two distinct hypothermic preservation environments influence cellular transcriptional programs during 24 h of hypothermic preservation and subsequent rewarming. Although static hypothermic preservation remains the gold standard for donor liver transport, it has been consistently reported that cold ischemia induces profound metabolic, inflammatory, and endothelial injury that contributes to early allograft dysfunction and long term complications after liver transplantation15–18. The development of improved preservation strategies therefore remains a priority, particularly approaches that stabilize mitochondria, reduce metabolic stress, and modulate inflammatory signaling during hypothermia4,19,20.

Here, we compared UW, an established clinical standard with an experimental formulation, HPG, designed to modulate osmotic and mitochondrial stability. Since the mechanisms of preservation injury are inherently cell-type specific, we used scRNA-seq to resolve transcriptional responses in parenchymal and non-parenchymal liver populations within an organoid-on-chip model.

PDOs exhibited strong induction of interferon-stimulated genes, oxidative stress-related transcripts, and acute-phase mediators during hypothermic preservation, consistent with prior work showing that cold ischemia activates innate sensing pathways and promotes type I interferon signaling in hepatocytes21–23. Genes such as IFI27, HSPB1, HMOX1, and SAA1 have been previously associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and oxidative stress under hypothermic preservation24–26. The distinct magnitude of these responses in UW versus HPG shown in PDOs in our study suggest that preservation solution chemistry influences the balance of interferon, stress, and metabolic pathways at the single cell level.

Cold ischemia is known to impair mitochondrial respiration through succinate accumulation, electron transport chain disruption, and reperfusion-associated oxidative bursts27–29. Mitochondrial regulators MT-ND5, MT1E, and HMOX1 showed temporal patterns that were dependent on the preservation solution used, especially during the rewarming phase. Similar gene expression patterns have been linked to mitochondrial recovery or sustained injury after ischemia in both hepatocyte cultures and ex vivo perfused livers30–32. The transcriptional patterns observed in HPG-preserved samples, including reduced expression of mitochondrial stress response genes and lower mitochondrial fraction are consistent with attenuated mitochondrial injury; however, gene expression data alone cannot confirm functional mitochondrial preservation. Transcript levels correlate imperfectly with mitochondrial respiratory capacity, and direct measures of metabolic flux, oxygen consumption rate, and ROS production require dedicated functional assays. These transcriptional observations should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating signals warranting functional validation rather than as direct evidence of improved mitochondrial homeostasis.

Endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark of preservation injury, contributing to micro-circulatory failure, leukocyte adhesion, and sinusoidal loss33–35. LSECs in our model showed downregulation of VWF, ANGPT2, and KDR during hypothermic storage, along with activation of interferon and adhesion-associated genes including ICAM1 and VCAM1. Similar patterns have been reported in primary LSECs subject to hypothermia or ischemia–reperfusion injury36,37.

HSCs also displayed cold-induced expression of matrix remodeling and injury-associated genes, particularly TAGLN, COL1A1 and SERPINE1, which are known mediators of fibrosis, cytoskeletal remodeling, and cellular contraction38,39. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating stromal components into in-vitro preservation studies, as non-parenchymal cells contribute substantially to reperfusion injury.

Ligand-receptor inference analysis revealed dynamic and preservation solution dependent alternations in intercellular signaling. UW preserved samples showed higher predicted activation of inflammatory chemokine-receptor modules, including CXCL1/8, CCL20, and SAA1, which have established roles in promoting neutrophil recruitment, macrophage activation, and endothelial injury during ischemia–reperfusion40–43. These patterns suggest that differential biochemical environments can shape paracrine landscapes in ways that may influence subsequent inflammatory cascades during rewarming or reperfusion. Several important caveats apply to the ligand-receptor interaction analysis. Computational inference from co-expression data does not confirm functional intercellular signaling; predicted interactions represent transcriptionally plausible communication axes that require experimental validation (such as blocking antibodies, co-culture perturbation assays). Furthermore, chemokine gradient dynamics are diffusion-dependent and may differ substantially between the microfluidic geometry of the liver-on-chip device and the three-dimensional sinusoidal architecture of the intact liver. Finally, LIANA interaction scores are sensitive to the relative abundance of communicating cell types; as cell type proportions may shift between conditions, some apparent differences in interaction strength may reflect compositional rather than signaling changes. These constraints are acknowledged and the ligand-receptor findings are presented as exploratory hypotheses.

Importantly, our findings do not imply clinical superiority of one solution over another; rather, they reveal distinct transcriptional and signaling landscapes that offer mechanistic hypotheses for future experimental investigation.

Traditional studies of liver preservation injury rely on whole-organ models or animal models which are limited by the availability of donor livers, the use of suboptimal discarded organs, ethical concerns in animal experimentation and limited mechanistic resolution. Our use of a co-culture organoid microfluidic platform allowed for precise dissection of cell-type-specific responses in human relevant model that maintained multicellular interactions. Similar micro-physiological systems have proven valuable for dissecting ischemia–reperfusion mechanisms and drug responses11,44–46, and our findings further support their utility for preservation research.

While our model is allogeneic, combining PDOs with commercially sourced cell lines, it is designed to isolate the intrinsic cellular stress and innate inflammatory pathways characteristic of early ischemia–reperfusion injury. They co-culture on the chip lacks the adaptive immune components (T-cells and B-cells) that serve as the primary drivers of acute HLA-mediated allograft rejection. This enabled the high-resolution mapping of parenchymal and innate immune responses without the confounding effect of a full immune rejection cascade. Furthermore, since all experimental preservation groups (UW and HPG) were compared against a synchronized baseline group composed of the same allogeneic cellular mixture, the differential transcriptomic shifts reported here are attributable to the preservation environment rather than baseline incompatibility.

We categorized the immune compartment as a single ‘macrophage’ cluster, utilizing THP-1 derived induced macrophages as a human-relevant model for hepatic innate activation. However, the in vivo immune landscape is significantly more complex, comprising both resident Kupffer cells and recruited monocyte-derived macrophages, each possessing distinct roles in the initiation and resolution of IRI.

While our use of induced macrophages represents a simplified model, the observed induction of key inflammatory axes, specifically the CXCL1/8 and CCL20 signaling pathways is consistent with the innate immune activation patterns reported in clinical transplantation literature. Nevertheless, future iterations of this model incorporating primary or iPSC-derived Kupffer cells will be necessary to fully resolve the cell-type-specific contributions of resident verses recruited immune populations to preservation-induced stress.

A principal limitation of this study is the absence of independent replicated per condition. Each preservation condition was evaluated in a single pooled chip preparation with each consisting of n = 12 chip wells, precluding formal statistical differential expression analysis and limiting the generalizability of cell-type-specific conclusions. The single-cell approach partially mitigates this by providing high-resolution sampling of transcriptional states within each condition; however, donor variability, chip-to-chip variation, and stochastic transcriptional noise cannot be distinguished from condition-specific signals without replicated experiments. Future studies should incorporate a minimum of three independent chip replicates per condition with matched donor PDOs to enable pseudobulk linear modeling with appropriate statistical power. The use of an organoid-based model does not replicate whole-liver architecture, biliary components, immune infiltrates or perfusion-dependent biochemical gradients. As such, transcriptional signatures observed here represent intrinsic cellular responses within a simplified model.

Furthermore, the liver-on-chip model used here recapitulates multicellular hepatic composition and permits controlled preservation conditions. However, several physiological variables present in the clinical graft storage setting are absent. These include the dynamic oxygen tension gradient characteristic of sinusoidal perfusion, the accumulation of metabolic waste products during prolonged cold ischemia, and the hemodynamic shear stress profile of machine perfusion systems. The static cold storage at 6 °C used in this study is representative of standard cold ischemia but does not model normothermic or hypothermic machine perfusion. These constraints limit direct extrapolation to clinical transplantation outcomes and reinforce the exploratory nature of the transcriptional findings. Comparison with machine perfusion literature, including gene expression datasets form ex vivo normothermic machine perfusion studies, would be valuable in future work.

HPG is an experimental formulation, and the dataset does not permit conclusions regarding preservation efficacy or clinical performance. Third, since organ co-cultures post-preservation yield smaller cell counts than tissue samples, this may constrain the resolution of differential expression analyses. Our ligand-receptor inference (LIANA) provides a predictive map of potential paracrine signaling; however, it does not confirm functional protein-level secretion or receptor binding, and factors such as diffusion gradients in microfluidic systems may influence actual signaling outcomes. Finally, although transcriptional signatures provide mechanistic hypotheses, functional assays, particularly mitochondrial flux, oxidative stress assays, and ex vivo perfusion studies are needed to determine the biological implications of the observed gene programs.

Conclusion

In this study, we present an exploratory single-cell transcriptomic characterization of cell-type-specific expression patterns associated with two distinct hypothermic preservation environments in a multicellular liver-on-chip model. Our findings identify candidate transcriptional differences spanning stress response, inflammatory, mitochondrial, and intercellular signaling axes across hepatocyte-like organoids, endothelial cells, stellate cells, and macrophages, with patterns that varied between UW and HPG preservation conditions. These observations generate hypotheses regarding how preservation solution chemistry may influence cellular injury programs during hypothermia and rewarming, and highlight the potential of organ-on-chip platforms combined with single-cell transcriptomics as a framework for evaluating experimental preservation formulations. Independent biological replication, formal statistical testing, and functional validation assays remain necessary before mechanistic conclusions or assessments of clinical preservation efficacy can be drawn.
