An assessment of knowledge, practices, and malaria parasitaemia among artisanal gold miners and community members in Mudzi, Zimbabwe
Authors: Tichaona Fambirai, Moses John Chimbari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite a significant reduction in the burden of malaria in the last decades, the disease remains a significant global public health threat. Community perception, knowledge, and practices are key determinants in malaria transmission. Mudzi is a border district in Zimbabwe, characterized by persistent malaria transmission despite having high indoor residual spraying coverages that meet the World Health Organization (WHO) indoor residual spraying (IRS) coverage thresholds. Human socio-economic behavioural factors driving persistent transmission have not been documented.MethodsA mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted to establish knowledge, practices, and perceptions among Artisanal Gold Miners (AGMs) and Community members (CMs). Two active artisanal mining sites and catchment villages were purposively selected for this study.Malaria RDT test kits and blood slide examinations by microscopy were used to establish malaria parasitaemia prevalence. Data were collected via Kobo Collect and analysed using Microsoft Excel to generate frequencies and analyse for statistical differences.ResultsA total of 128 AGMs and 113 CMs were recruited. 277 individuals were tested for malaria using RDT and blood smear microscopy. Responses showed good knowledge of the cause and transmission of malaria. However, low risk perception and misconception among AGMs and CMs were observed. There was no significant difference concerning education, knowledge, and perceptions between AGMs and CMs. AGM predominantly lived in the open without protective measures for long periods. Community members and artisanal miners (AGMS) showed high preferences and utilisation of standard care offered through health facilities and community health workers (CHW). The majority of AGMs and CMs received health education from HCWs. Malaria prevalence across the groups was less than 1%.ConclusionHigh knowledge of malaria is not translating into preventative behaviour and high-risk perception among AGMs and CMs. There is a need to create innovative health education interventions that motivate individuals to increase risk perception and behaviour modification. Communities are likely to prioritize survival over malaria avoidance practices, therefore, an integrated intervention that addresses livelihoods and malaria prevention needs to be developed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05770-3.Keywords: Malaria, Artisanal gold mining, Parasitemia perception, Knowledge parasitemia survey

Background

Malaria remains a global public health threat despite decades of gains made in reducing the burden caused by it and its associated deaths [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa and South East Asia remain the most vulnerable continents, with the sub-Saharan continent contributing at least 93% of the global cases and deaths [2]. Concerted global interventions through vector control and improved anti-malaria medicines have significantly reduced the malaria burden. Medicines, treatment, and diagnostic tools have significantly reduced the global burden by at least 0.8% annually between 1990 and 2015 [3, 4]. A combination of efficient diagnostics and treatment therapies, along with innovative funding models, has culminated in the attainment of malaria elimination status in several Asian, American, and African countries since 2000[4]. Increasing malaria funding initiatives, such as Roll Back Malaria (RBM) and The Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (GF) [5], have also significantly contributed to the decline observed in the past twenty years.

Despite the significant gains observed between 1990 and 2015, significant increases in malaria burden have been observed in the last decade. Diminishing gains have been observed due to growing resistance to insecticides, human population movement, and the resurgence of malaria [6]. Changing vector behaviour and emerging vector resistance to chemical pesticides pose a critical challenge to malaria control efforts [7]. An additional threat to global malaria elimination has emerged in the form of resistance to artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) in Southeast Asia, South America, and Africa [8–10]. Climate change also poses a significant challenge, with an increase in global temperature set to increase transmission and cases [11].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has set an ambitious global target to achieve malaria elimination in various regions [12] in its Global Technical Strategy (GTS). Socio-economic determinants remain a hurdle to attaining malaria burden reduction globally. Housing, socioeconomic mobility, and social service connections have a significant influence on the health status of any population [13]. Communities with low economic status are often riddled with poverty-related illnesses such as malaria [14, 15]. Poverty, intertwined with the need to survive, often pushes communities into occupations and practices that increase exposure to vector bites through prolonged outdoor living with minimum protection [12, 13, 16, 17]. Evidence has also shown that malaria attributable mortality is linked to long distances to health facilities, low maternal education, and low socio-economic status [18–20]. Improving access to better housing and living conditions will likely have a burden-reduction effect [15].

Communities in gold deposit-rich regions often resort to illegal artisanal mining as a source of livelihood. Environmental pollution, environmental degradation, noise and air pollution, heavy metal pollution, cyanide, mercury, and arsenic are some of the well-documented negative effects of AGM [21, 22]. Additionally, AGM has been associated with an increased risk of malaria and malaria incidence [23] as well as cross-border movement [24–26]. Global gold price has been shown to be a good predictor of increased malaria incidence [27]. AGM, therefore, plays an integral part in sustaining malaria transmission in various regions of the world [23] due to limited health services and access to preventative malaria care. The low knowledge and low-risk perception, as well as misconceptions on malaria among AGM, are also a public health concern [28]. Communities have viewed AGM activities as a source of disease, and the miners have been shown to have a low-risk perception of the disease [28]. AGM sites are often remotely located from health facilities, and miners have been reported to resort to self-medication with poor quality medication [29].

Malaria transmission in Zimbabwe has been characterized as heterogeneous, with mostly low-lying border regions experiencing high transmission, whilst central highland regions of the country experience low transmission [30, 31]. The transmission peak is often recorded during the wet season (November -March) [30]. IRS and LLINs are the two core vector interventions utilized in the country based on districts' annual parasite index (API) rates [32]. In accordance with the National Malaria Control and Elimination Strategy, IRS is conducted in a district with an API > 5/1000 population, whilst LLINS are distributed in districts with an API < 5/1000 population [32]. A total of 31 districts conduct IRS, whilst 36 districts deploy LLINS as a core intervention. The national IRS room and population coverage and LLINS distribution coverage rates have consistently remained above 85% between 2017 and 2022 [33]. Mudzi district experiences perennial, persistent malaria transmission despite a high-performing IRS programme exceeding the WHO standard (at least 85% room and population coverage) [34, 35]. Despite the high IRS and population coverage, the district is among the top three malaria high burden districts in the country with a high malaria incidence, averaging 100 cases/1000 population in the last five (5) years and a high annual parasite index (150–200/1000 pop) [32, 33, 36]. Additional File 1 depicts the high IRS coverage and malaria incidence. Routine entomological surveillance has established Anopheles gambiae s.s., Anopheles arabiensis, and Anopheles funestus as the primary malaria vectors in the district [37, 38]. The district has several illegal AGM sites. Artisanal mining with increased, prolonged outdoor activities may be a critical factor in driving malaria burden in the district. The knowledge, behaviour, and practices of artisanal gold miners (AGMs) have not been documented. This study seeks to further establish the level of parasitemia among AGM. Understanding the knowledge, behaviour, and practices of artisanal miners and the general community members (CMs) allows for the formulation of evidence-based interventions that will contribute to the elimination of persistent transmission.

Study setting

Mudzi is a rural district (Geo-coordinates: 17˚00ʹS, 032˚40ʹE) (36) located on the northeastern border region of Zimbabwe, near Mozambique (Fig. 1). The local population is estimated to be 165,266 [39]. The district is composed of 18 wards. A ward is an administrative unit in a district comprising 6 to 7 villages [40]. The are 558 villages in the Mudzi district. Each village unit is composed of approximately 25–50 households (35). Due to the presence of alluvial gold deposits, the local population often engages in AGM as a source of livelihood. Health services delivery is provided through primary health care (PHC) units- Rural Health Centres (RHC). A RHC provides basic maternal and child health services, malaria treatment, immunization, and HIV and TB treatment services. A single referral district hospital serves 32 RHCs in the district. The RHC services are augmented by village-based health services offered through community health workers (CHWs) recruited from each village in the district. Under the national primary health care approach, recruited CHWs undergo a standard training programme to conduct community health promotion activities, immunization programme, social mobilization campaigns, and malaria testing and treatment [41]. CHW provides an essential package of primary health care at no cost to beneficiaries.

Methods

Sample size

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was used to interview community members and artisanal gold miners identified at gold mining sites. The questionnaire collected information on sex, age, level of education and knowledge, practice, and perception of malaria prevention. Sources of health treatment were also assessed through the same tool. The structured interviewer questionnaire was translated from English to Shona by an independent translator not involved with the research. The Questionnaire was back-translated to English, and it was checked for consistency. The questionnaire was pretested to check for validity and the removal of ambiguous questions. The pre-test was conducted in Meza village, a village excluded from this study. The researcher and research assistants (RAs) reviewed the questionnaires after the pre-testing. Training for the RAs was conducted to familiarize them with the questionnaire and ensure consistency when interviewing participants. Real-time data collection was achieved through the use of the Kobo Collect tool. RDT Kit (PF/Pan)® was used to test for malaria among community members and artisanal miners. The district laboratory technician was responsible for RDT and blood slide preparation. Two samples of blood were drawn from selected communities and artisanal miners. The 1st blood sample was extracted for the RDT test. Positive and negative results were recorded on a laboratory form. A second sample of blood was drawn and collected in Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes. A thick and thin smear blood slide was prepared for each participant. The slides were examined under microscopy at the district laboratory located at Kotwa District Hospital. The blood slides were examined for the presence of malaria parasites.

Sampling

Two AGM sites were purposively selected due to active artisanal mining activities. The selected mining sites and villages are shown in Fig. 1. The surrounding villages were conveniently selected for the study. Artisanal miners were recruited through combined purposive sampling and snowballing techniques. Artisanal miners identified during a visit to an active mining site were recruited into the study. Snowball sampling was also done to recruit more participants through referrals from already recruited artisanal miners. Village households were randomly recruited into the study from a village household list. The household list was entered into a Microsoft Excel to generate a random list. Individuals present at a selected household during the interview visit were purposively recruited into the study.

Data analysis

Data were downloaded from the Kobo Collect cloud storage platform as an Excel Spreadsheet and imported into Stata 13 for cleaning and analysis. Categorical data were presented as counts (frequency). The chi-square test was used to test whether there were significant differences between demographic variables among artisanal miners and community members. A modified Bloom cut-off for total knowledge and attitude was used to assess knowledge of malaria using proportion scores; those with a score of 70–100% were classified as good knowledge, 60–69-moderate knowledge, and < 50%- poor knowledge. The modified cutoffs were based on methods described in studies by Lopez et al. and Okello et al. [43, 44].

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was sought from the University of KwaZulu-Natal Bio-Medical Research Ethics Committee [BREC/00001594/2020] and the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe [MRCZ/A/2637]. Gatekeeper permission was sought from the Provincial Medical Director (PMD) for Mashonaland East. Informed written consent was obtained from all the study participants before the administration of the questionnaire and malaria testing. Consent was obtained from each participant to draw blood. Written parental/guardian consent was obtained for all study participants aged eighteen years and below. Participants were informed of their rights and were free to discontinue participation at any time. Personal identifiers, such as full names, were collected on a laboratory form. The RDT result forms and microscopy result forms were kept at Kotwa District Hospital laboratory unit, only accessible to laboratory staff. The data collected via Kobo Collect was stored in a cloud server with usernames and passwords granted only to the researchers. RAs' access to the Kobo collect database was limited to the collection and submission of questionnaire data only. A nurse was recruited from Kotwa district to facilitate the treatment of identified malaria-positive cases and manage adverse events during testing.

Access to malaria protection, social activities, and treatment

In this study, the majority of respondents among both females (98.4%) and males (96.2%) reported their houses being treated with IRS (Table 4). The majority of respondents reported using mosquito repellents at their homesteads, with a slightly higher usage among males 45 (73.8%), compared to females 36(69.2%). Among those who reported using mosquito repellents, local shops were the primary source, with higher reliance on using repellents for mosquito control being female respondents, 32(86.5%), compared to male respondents-30(68.2%). A minority of the males, 14(31.8%) and females, 5(13.5%), reported sourcing repellants from traders. The majority of CMs, irrespective of sex, reported episodes of malaria illness in the past. Among females, 47(90.4%) reported a history of malaria, while 54(88.5%) of the males shared similar experiences. A majority of female miners, 40 (85.1%), reported seeking treatment for malaria at a clinic compared to 35 (64.8%) of male miners. The majority of males and females said they socialized outside at night. A higher proportion of males, 41(78.8%), and females, 37 (60.7%), perceived sleeping outside and cooking outside at night as less risky. Attending religious activities at night was also viewed as not a risk factor for contracting malaria by a large proportion of males, 38(73.1%), and females, 48(70.5%) participants.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study was conducted to establish the knowledge, practices and perceptions, and malaria prevalence among AGMs and CMs. The majority of AGMs and CMs were highly knowledgeable about malaria, yet they had low risk perception and held some misconceptions about the disease. AGMs and CMs preferred treatment provided at health facilities. HCWs and CHWs were reported as the largest source of health information on malaria. Despite living in the open for longer periods, AGMs did not utilize any preventative measures to prevent mosquito bites. Malaria prevalence among AGMs and CMs was lower than 1%, confirming a hypoendemic, unstable malaria transmission situation.

This study revealed good knowledge of malaria transmission by both AGMs and CMs. It is evident in this study that both AGMs and CMs are highly literate, with the majority having attained secondary level education. Therefore, the observed higher levels of knowledge could have been because of the high level of education status of the two groups. Education improves comprehension and assimilation of concepts. Results from other studies have shown a positive correlation between high knowledge and education status [45, 46]. Notably, high knowledge evident in this study appeared not to influence preventative behaviour. Sleeping arrangements of AGMs without any protective measures and the low risk perception with respect to night outdoor social, occupational, and religious activities are concerning. De la Cruz et al. in a study in Ghana, had similar findings, where high knowledge did not translate into high bed net use [47]. Findings from this study are contrary to findings from a study by Spjeldnæs et al. in Tanzania, who showed improved preventative action due to high knowledge [48]. The difference in context could explain the contrasting results, as the study in Tanzania was conducted in a community without artisanal mining activities. This study brings to the fore a critical observation that elevated knowledge about a disease will not always translate into practice. This discordance between high knowledge and positive preventative behaviour is not unique to malaria. The phenomenon has been observed in other infectious diseases like HIV/AIDS, where knowledge alone has not been sufficient to change behaviour [49]. This calls for investment in innovative health education interventions that motivate individuals to move from intellectual knowledge to practical, preventative human behaviour. Utilizing models that address knowledge and behaviour, and psychological factors such as the information, motivation, behavior skills model (IMBM) has revealed positive results [50]. In a study by Balami et al. in Nigeria, results showed improved uptake of bed nets and intermittent preventive therapy (IPTp) among mothers following a randomized controlled trial with IMBM as one of the interventions [51].

The reported low risk perception of night outdoor movement and AGM will likely lead to less preventative action being taken. The low-risk perception could be influenced by overriding social and economic needs. The CMs and AGMs view AGM as a key source of livelihood and night outdoor activity as a necessary social event. Failure to address these key human social and economic behavioural aspects will result in perpetual discordance between knowledge level and anticipated behavioural practices. Aerts et al. argue in their study that if risk perception is low, greater knowledge is insufficient to trigger behavioral change [45]. Health education sessions and awareness campaigns should therefore focus on improving risk perception to improve preventative behaviour [45]. Improving perception has been linked to preventative behaviour and knowledge [52]. Results from this study are comparable to those obtained in Tanzania and Eswatini, where participants had high knowledge but did not act preventively [48, 53]. Health managers should therefore understand and be alert to the effect of perception on health behaviour, even in areas with well-performing health education programmes. Identifying the psychological and other various contextual factors determining the difference between doers and non-doers after health education is critical. A schistosomiasis study by Mulopo et al. in uMkhaynyakude district in South Africa revealed that low attitudinal and ability factors influenced the utilization of safe water [54]. Based on the Risk Attitude Norms Ability and Self-Regulatory (RANAS) model, Mulopo et al. argue for enhanced community-based empowerment interventions that prompt behavioural practices and public commitment [54]. Additionally, Olapeju et al. [54], based on observations in Guyana, propose the implementation of routine, targeted, theory-based social behaviour change to improve early health-seeking behaviour, treatment net acquisition and use, as well as treatment adherence, to enhance health outcomes among AGMs.

Artisanal gold mining has posed critical environmental challenges and contributes to increased malaria burden. Despite the AGM-associated negative externalities, it is an undeniable source of livelihood for poor communities in regions endowed with gold mineral deposits [55]. The beneficial reduction in unemployment contributes to the economic welfare of local communities. Banning AGM has remained out of question in many low-income countries due to the social and political sensitivities attached to such decisions. AGM, therefore, becomes a complex permanent hurdle in malaria control. Based on the observation from this study, Mudzi district may be experiencing a “malaria trap” where the vicious cycle and interplay of effects of malaria and poverty create a community's inability to deal with malaria [56]. Malaria control programmes should address the socio-economic question to achieve malaria elimination as burden reduction, as human psychological, psychosocial, and economic factors will continue to influence human perception and population behaviour concerning malaria [57]. Communities often face a dilemma between “hunger” and disease threats. Increasing health education, community engagement, and awareness of the risk of malaria is critical to improve risk perception among the population. Improving the socio-economic status of a population has been shown to reduce malaria burden [58]. Creating sustainable programmes that uplift communities out of poverty will likely dissuade communities from engaging in illegal AGM activities.

A strong association between night activity and association with contracting malaria has been established [15, 16, 46]. The economic returns from AGM in the district could also be undermining the incremental protective effect of IRS, as individuals spend more time outside for longer durations. High human outdoor nocturnal activity by AGMs in a region with efficient vectors that are highly anthropogenic and outdoor feeding capabilities [37, 38] increases the risk of contracting malaria. The informal and illegal nature of AGM poses challenges as the operations are not registered, and the population is highly mobile. Various piloted interventions have shown considerable positive outcomes in the Americas and Southeast Asia regions, where communities spend a considerable amount of time outdoors in forests and gold mining sites. Novel self-treatment and diagnosis strategies among AGMs, which have been piloted in the Amazon forest, have shown promising results in improving detection and reducing malaria incidence [59]. Use of topical repellents distributed by forest community health workers showed a significant impact on malaria incidence in Myanmar [60]. Targeted drug administration using intermittent preventive treatment for forest goers (IPTf) using artesunate-pyronaridine also showed the ability to reduce malaria incidence [61]. The use of repellents is widespread in this study despite an absence of a programmatic distribution intervention. Promotion of the use of repellents among key groups with high outdoor activity (artisanal miners, border patrol officers, agriculture workers, migrant farmers, and commercial sex workers) is provided for in Zimbabwe’s National Malaria Control and Elimination Strategic Plan [32]; however, the models and approaches of implementation are not well outlined in the strategy. The reported willingness to accept and use standard malaria treatment by CMs and AGMs in the district is positive, as non-standard care use remains a challenge in other low-income countries [62]. Continued correct use of ACT will contribute to a significant decline in malaria burden and deaths globally [63].

This study revealed a low malaria parasite prevalence rate, confirming an unstable hypoendemic malaria epidemic [64]. The prevalence established in this study is comparable to the findings from the national malaria indicator survey, which found a prevalence of malaria by RDT and microscopy of less than 1% among all age groups [65]. For prevalence levels (0.0–5%), universal IRS remains the intervention of choice. There is a huge proportion of the local population that engages in activities for a longer duration in sprayed homes, which excludes them from the protective effect of IRS. Exploring the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of providing the nets to AGMs is, therefore, pivotal.

Limitations of the study

This study relied on self-reported data, which may be prone to recall and social desirability bias. This study only recruited artisanal miners who were present at mining sites during the time of the study due to logistical, security, and resource limitations. The behaviour and knowledge, practice, and malaria prevalence in other existing AGM sites within the district may have been different from those obtained at Rwenya and Makaha regions. The use of purposive sampling and the snowball sampling technique could have introduced volunteer biases.

Conclusion

High knowledge among the community and AGMs is not translating into high-risk perception and preventative behaviour. High outdoor activity among CM and AGMs will likely lead to elevated human vector contact, therefore sustaining persistent malaria transmission in the district. Increasing access to individual protective measures for AGMs may reduce infections among this vulnerable population. Addressing persistent malaria transmission in a region with high unemployment and high engagement in AGM will require integrated programmes that offer an alternative, safer source of livelihood. Generic health education programmes should be augmented by social behaviour change programmes, which motivate individuals to turn knowledge into practical, preventative human behaviour.
