Isolation and partial characterization of OmpF and LsrB as putative Actinobacillus seminis testosterone receptors
Authors: Gerardo A Ramírez-Paz-y-Puente, Martha O Salcedo-Álvarez, Miguel A Avalos-Rangel, Candelario Vázquez-Cruz, Elena Cobos-Justos, Patricia Sánchez-Alonso, Edgar Zenteno, Erika P Meneses-Romero, Erasmo Negrete-Abascal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractActinobacillus seminis causes epididymitis in ruminants and spontaneous abortion. A. seminis virulence factors remain unknown; however, it has been shown that testosterone increased bacterial growth, adhesins expression, and protease expression, as well as biofilm production, suggesting the presence of a bacterial receptor for this hormone. The present study reported the isolation of two potential testosterone receptors from A. seminis grown in the presence of testosterone. A 75 kDa and a 55 kDa proteins were isolated from bacterial lysate with triazine orange-Sepharose CL6B column or with immobilized testosterone on polystyrene plates, respectively. Mass spectrometry identified the 75 kDa as the periplasmic LsrB, and the 55 kDa as the outer membrane OmpF from A. seminis. Molecular docking showed OmpF and LsrB interaction with testosterone in 59% and 55%, respectively. Testosterone plus furanone C-30 inhibits the expression of those proteins diminished bacterial growth, adhesin and protease expression. Cloning A. seminis lsrB into E. coli M15 strain improves clone development in a dose-dependent manner and induces the expression of proteases and putative adhesins. These findings strongly suggest that host testosterone could play a significant role in A. seminis pathogenesis, through OmpF and LsrB as testosterone receptors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11274-026-04921-1.Keywords: Actinobacillus seminis, Testosterone, OmpF, LsrB, Quorum sensing, Furanone, Virulence factors

Introduction

Actinobacillus seminis is an opportunistic pathogen that resides in the prepuce and can migrate to and colonize the epididymis and testis in male ruminants during sexual maturation when hormone concentrations change (Hajtós et al. 1987; Al-Katib and Dennis 2009). A. seminis causes epididymitis, low fertility, and infertility in sheep, goats, and bovines in a manner similar to that of Brucella ovis and Histophilus somni infections (Hajtós et al. 1987; Al-Katib and Dennis 2009). In female organisms, A. seminis causes abortion and has been discovered in the vaginal lips and breasts (Al-Katib and Dennis 2009; Foster et al. 1999). Although the virulence factors of A. seminis are few known, the expression of adhesins and bovine IgG- and fibrinogen-degrading proteases has been previously described; these proteins can participate in forming biofilms (Healey et al. 1991; Montes-García et al. 2018, 2019; García et al. 2020; De la Cruz-Montoya et al. 2023.

A direct relationship between sexual maturity and the ability to infect has been described in different microbial pathogens (García-Gómez et al. 2013; Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023). Sexual maturity involves changes in hormone concentrations that can be detected and modulate the pathogenic potential of different pathogens by controlling the expression of virulence factor-encoding genes in a coordinated way; this mechanism is known as quorum sensing (QS).

QS is a bacterial cell‒cell communication mechanism that is dependent upon cellular density. Autoproduced molecules, also known as autoinducers (AIs), function as signals for cellular density. These AIs typically interact with receptors on the cell surface and, through a signaling cascade, transmit signals by phosphorylation to other molecules, which can then interact with DNA and regulate gene expression (Wu et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2023). Protein phosphorylation, catalyzed by kinases, is one of the main signal transduction mechanisms in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms. In prokaryotic organisms, histidine kinases and a regulatory response protein constitute those signal transduction systems (Zhou et al. 2023). These bacterial systems are known as two-component systems (TCSs); through these systems, bacteria reprogram their virulence factors to adapt to environmental changes and maintain a dominant advantage over hosts and microbial competitors in new niches (Zhou et al. 2023). Different TCSs participate in biofilm formation, antimicrobial resistance, responses to temperature changes and oxidative stress, and detection of host physiological changes, including hormonal changes and responses to specific amino acids and bile salts (Zhou et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2024; Moulding and El-Halfawy 2024). Among these TCSs are LuxIR, QseBC, and LsrB (Pereira et al. 2013). LuxIR participates in the synthesis and detection of AI-2 molecules, in a manner similar to LsrB (LuxS-regulated). AI-2 molecules participate in intra- and interspecies communication. Furthermore, communication between host and pathogen has also been described. In this communication, as signaling molecules participate different molecules, including stress and sex hormones (García-Gómez et al. 2013; Gümüş et al. 2019). They also modulate the expression of genes encoding virulence factors; QseBC has been described as a receptor for stress hormones, including epinephrine and norepinephrine (Weigel and Demuth 2016).

Recently, the presence of testosterone or estradiol in culture media was shown to modify the expression of A. seminis virulence factors, including adhesins and factors involved in biofilm production; additionally, testosterone and estradiol were shown to stimulate A. seminis bacterial growth (Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023). Testosterone also increases the expression of proteases that degrade hemoglobin (Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2025). These observations suggest that testosterone induces changes in A. seminis; however, bacterial testosterone receptors remain undescribed. Although A. seminis responds to catecholamines (data unpublished), an in-silico search indicates that qseC is absent from its genome (García et al. 2020).

The present work aimed to identify A. seminis testosterone receptors that can regulate the expression of virulence factor-encoding genes. Our results revealed that two proteins in A. seminis interact with testosterone, suggesting that these proteins may regulate virulence factor expression.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and growth conditions

The A. seminis ATCC 15,768 strain was grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB) overnight at 37 °C with agitation (García et al. 2020). To determine the effect of testosterone, testosterone was added to a final concentration of 1–5 ng/ml (Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023). All chemicals were obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Total cell extracts

A. seminis was cultured in the presence or absence of testosterone, followed by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 8 °C), after which the bacterial cell pellets were suspended in 20 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl) piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES)-lysozyme (1 mg/ml), pH 7.4, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with agitation. The suspension was sonicated for 7 min on ice (15 s on, 10 s off, cycled). The suspension was then centrifuged for 2 min to clarify and remove cell debris and unbroken cells (Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023).

Isolation of testosterone receptors

A Sepharose CL-6B column coupled with triazine orange dye was prepared and equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4). A. seminis total cell extracts (approximately 300 µg/ml), obtained after culture with or without testosterone, were filtered through the column at room temperature. Unbound proteins were eluted with two volumes of 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, and the retained proteins were eluted with TD buffer (150 mM KCl, 0.2 M Na2HPO4, 1 M NaCl, and 20 mM Tris) (Bond and Notides 1987).

The testosterone transporter was isolated by desorption onto polystyrene well plates: Twenty-four-well plates were coated with the hormone (5 ng/ml) in carbonate buffer (0.1 M, pH 9.6) at 37 °C overnight. To avoid nonspecific binding, uncovered spaces were blocked with 3% skim milk solution in PBS/0.5% Tween 20 for 1 h at 37 °C. Cell extracts from bacteria cultured in the presence or absence of testosterone (5 ng/ml) were added to the wells (100 µg/well) and incubated at 37 °C with agitation (80 rpm) for 3 h. The plates were then washed three times with PBS/0.5% Tween 20, and the retained proteins were released with 100 µl of 1% SDS. The eluted proteins were precipitated in 10% trichloroacetic acid (1 h, 4 °C) and washed once with acetone to eliminate excess acid (López-Ruiz et al. 2013). All collected fractions were quantified using the Bradford method.

Western blot

Electrophoretic separation on 10% polyacrylamide gels was performed to determine the patterns of the eluted proteins. To assess the immunogenicity of the purified proteins, ten micrograms were loaded into each well. Immune recognition was performed using a pool of sheep serum with epididymitis (1:1000) (Montes-García et al. 2018), and the reaction was visualized using anti-sheep peroxidase-labeled rabbit IgG (1:1000).

Mass spectrometric analysis

Protein samples of cultures grown in the presence or absence of testosterone, obtained by chromatography or in multiwell plates, were separated by SDS-PAGE. Differentially expressed bands were analyzed for peptide mass fingerprints, as previously described (Figueroa-Valenzuela et al. 2022). The tryptic peptide data were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer 1.4.1.14 (Thermo Fisher Co.).

Docking of testosterone and identified proteins

The interaction between testosterone and the identified proteins was evaluated using the HDOCK server (hust.edu.cn). Also, to determine the affinity of testosterone for the OmpF porin, one of the identified proteins, along the channel of the porin, and to get an idea of how testosterone could pass into the cell, this interaction was examined.

Effects of furanone C-30 on A. seminis growth and virulence factor expression

To determine whether furanone C-30 could inhibit the growth of A. seminis and the expression of adhesins and proteases, growth kinetics were compared in the presence of testosterone (1–5 ng/ml) or testosterone plus 10 µM furanone C-30. The optical density at 600 nm was determined every hour until the culture reached the stationary phase. In addition, cultures containing testosterone or testosterone plus furanone C-30 were processed to obtain adhesins from cultured bacterial cells (Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023; Figueroa-Valenzuela et al. 2022); supernatants were precipitated with 60% ammonium sulfate to recover secreted proteins using 10% SDS‒PAGE, and proteolytic activities were evaluated as described previously (De la Cruz-Montoya et al. 2023; Rosales-Islas et al. 2024) using 10% polyacrylamide gels copolymerized with 0.1% porcine gelatin.

Genetic evaluation of the A. seminis ompF and lrsB genes in E. coli

To determine whether A. seminis OmpF and LsrB interact with testosterone, the genes encoding these proteins were amplified by PCR and subsequently cloned and used to transform E. coli M15 cells. Oligonucleotide primers containing KpnI and BamHI restriction endonuclease sites (Table 1) were synthesized on the basis of the A. seminis ATCC 15,768 GenBank genome sequence (NLFK00000000.1). A. seminis genomic DNA was obtained as previously described (López-Ochoa et al. 2017). PCR products obtained were of 1097 and 1073 bp, corresponding to the ORFs ompF and lsrB genes, respectively, which were ligated with pBluescript II KS (-) (Stratagene®, Kirkland, WA, USA), previously digested with the EcoRV restriction enzyme, and used to transform E. coli DH5α cells. The ompF and lsrB genes cloned and inserted into the pBluescript vector were released using the restriction enzymes BamHI and KpnI (Invitrogen®)). The released products were purified and ligated into the pQE30 (Qiagen®, Germany) expression vector, which were digested with the same enzymes, BamHI and KpnI, to create translational fusions with the 6XHis-tag at the carboxy terminus of the proteins expressed from pQE30 in the E. coli M15 strain (López-Ochoa et al. 2017).

E. coli M15 pQE30/ompF and pQE30/lsrB clones grown in the presence of testosterone

To evaluate the role of OmpF and/or LsrB proteins in testosterone interactions, the clones were grown in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin and 1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Additionally, testosterone at 1, 3, or 5 ng/mL was added, and the cultures were grown with agitation at 37 °C until the stationary phase was reached. The optical density at 600 nm was measured every hour. E. coli M15 cells without a plasmid, E. coli M15 cells with pQE30 without an insert, or pQE30-ompF or pQE30-lsrB clones were cultured. The same clones without IPTG induction were used as negative controls. The culture supernatants from the clones and controls were precipitated with ammonium sulfate, and the precipitated proteins were electrophoretically separated by zymography with porcine gelatin, as described above.

Statistics

All data obtained from growth kinetics were analyzed using one-way ANOVA in IBM SPSS Statistics software for Windows (version 27.0). Assays were performed in triplicate at least three different times. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Protein identification

Mass spectrophotometric analysis revealed that the 75 kDa protein showed similarity with the LsrB protein, a substrate-binding protein (AI 2, accession number A0A263HCQ4; https://www.uniprot.org/; Fig. 2A), and the 55 kDa protein showed similarity with the OmpF porin (accession number A0A263HG92; Fig. 2B), with 31.2% and 62% coverage in the identified peptides, respectively.

Molecular docking

To determine the affinity between testosterone and the OmpF porin and between testosterone and the LsrB protein, the HDOCK server was used. The results indicate that the affinity between testosterone and OmpF was 58.89% (Fig. 3A). In comparison, the affinity between testosterone and LsrB was 54.51% (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the affinity between testosterone and OmpF was determined along the protein-formed channel, revealing that at each extreme, the affinity between the two was 38%, whereas at the center of the channel, it was 58.89% (Fig. 4).

Effects of furanone C-30 on A. seminis growth and protease and adhesin expression

The presence of testosterone in the culture media increased A. seminis growth, and 4 ng/mL testosterone doubled the optical density of the cultures (Fig. 5A). However, when furanone C-30 was also present, A. seminis growth was similar to that in the control cultures without testosterone (Fig. 5B).

The expression levels of adhesins, phosphoglycerate mutase, and EF-Tu proteins increased in the presence of testosterone (Fig. 6A). The expression levels of these proteins in the presence of furanone C-30 and testosterone were lower in comparison with the control without additions, except with 1 ng/mL testosterone, that were similar to that in the control cultures (Fig. 6B). Similar results were observed for proteolytic activity. The proteolytic activity of the 50 kDa protein increased twofold in the presence of testosterone (Fig. 7A). In samples treated with furanone C-30 plus testosterone, the proteolytic activity was similar to that in the control cultures (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

Cell communication is an essential process at all levels of life that enables the control of events throughout the life of a cell or organism. This communication can be performed by the liberation and detection of molecules or stimuli through a receptor. Steroid hormones (SHs), such as testosterone and estradiol, are essential for the development of eukaryotic organisms, and stress hormones (epinephrine and norepinephrine) are involved in the fight-or-flight response (Kaiser and Jaillardon 2023).

Pathogenic microorganisms can detect changes in the presence and concentration of these hormones and regulate the expression of their virulence factors (García-Gómez et al. 2013; Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023; Rea-Hernández et al. 2023; Gui et al. 2025). In prokaryotes, stress hormones are detected by TCSs, mainly via QseBC, QseEF, or other proteins, such as LsrB (Luqman 2023). A. seminis responds to epinephrine and norepinephrine by modifying its growth and the expression of various virulence factors (unpublished data). Additionally, furanone C-30, an inhibitor of the QseC receptor, inhibits biofilm formation; however, in silico analysis of its genome did not reveal a qseC gene (García et al. 2020), suggesting the participation of other proteins in this response. A. seminis also responds to testosterone and estradiol (Ramírez-Paz y Puente et al. 2023). This response indicates that A. seminis might possess a protein that functions as a receptor for these molecules.

The present study describes the isolation and identification of a membrane protein, OmpF porin, and a cytoplasmic protein, LsrB, as putative components of the testosterone signaling mechanism. Although other proteins were also retained by testosterone or acridine orange dye, the OmpF and LsrB proteins were recovered only in the presence of testosterone (Fig. 1). The inhibition of growth, adhesin expression, and protease expression due to the addition of furanone C-30 supports the participation of these proteins in recognition and signaling.

The purification of LsrB was performed by specific-dye chromatography, since acridine orange mimics the structure of naturally occurring biological molecules such as nucleotide phosphates (NAD+ or ATP), allowing it to bind selectively to certain enzymes and receptors. Specific orange dyes, notably Reactive Orange 14, have been successfully used to purify proteins such as estrogen receptors and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (Hey and Dean 1983). The OmpF protein was purified by desorption from testosterone-coated multiwell plates. However, both methods also allow other proteins to combine with the orange dye or testosterone. More procedures are needed to obtain an isolated protein and investigate other characteristics, such as DNA interactions. A similar result was described by Bond and Notides (1987). They explained that, in addition to the estrogen receptor, 20 to 25 additional proteins were retained by the acridine orange column because of their biochemical properties.

Porins, proteins of the outer membrane in Gram-negative bacteria, besides participating in the transport of various small molecules, have been shown to be involved in pathogenic processes such as adhesion, invasion, antimicrobial resistance, and colonization of host tissues (Belousov et al. 2023). OmpA, OmpC, and OmpF proteins have been the most studied porins due to their roles as transporters of various molecules, as well as in bacterial virulence, including adhesion, invasion, interaction with complement components, and as adjuvants and vaccine candidates (Sharma et al. 2022). OmpC and OmpF are two of the main E. coli outer membrane porin proteins that participate in the pathogenesis of this microorganism, and their expression is controlled by osmolarity. OmpF contributes to E. coli pathogenicity by affecting adherence, invasion, and serum resistance (Belousov et al. 2023), and its inactivation hampers these functions, likely by reducing the expression of other components, such as OmpA or FimC (Hejair et al. 2017).

Interaction between OmpF and LsrB with testosterone was determined by molecular docking modeling founding that this interaction is a little higher than 50%. In a study done with a rat uterus estrogen receptor, it was described that different molecules can interact with this estrogen receptor with different affinity percentages, but that affinity could be modified by temperature, concentration of the molecules interacting, time of interaction, among another variables (Blair et al. 2000). The affinity between OmpF-testosterone and LsrB-testosterone could permit the testosterone passing into the cell and the regulation of the A. seminis gene expression.

Type 2 AI mediates intra- and interspecies QS communication (Pereira et al. 2009). This type of communication occurs via three receptors: LuxP in Vibrio spp., LsrB in E. coli and Salmonella Typhimurium, and RbsB in Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans; A. actinomycetemcomitans expresses two AI-2 receptors, namely, LsrB and RbsB, with different AI-2 binding affinities (Novac et al. 2010). The lsr operon regulates virulence factors, such as adhesins, toxins, and biofilm formation in E. coli. E. coli lsr operon mutants cannot form biofilms because these bacteria lack signals for biofilm formation and associated metabolic changes (Manefield et al. 1999). QS-mediated regulation can also be controlled using various QS inhibitors. Manefield et al. (1999) reported that adding a natural furanone to E. coli cultures reduced the expression of several proteins, including OmpF and DnaK. Furanone specifically interferes with acylated homoserine lactone-mediated gene expression at the level of LuxR expression. Cloning the A. seminis lsrB gene into the E. coli M15 strain improves the growth of this clone when grown with 1, 3, or 5 ng/mL testosterone. Although 4 ng/mL testosterone induced the greatest increase in growth in A. seminis, it increased the growth of the lsrB E. coli clone to a level similar to that obtained with 3 or 5 ng/mL (Fig. S1). This result supports the role of 1A. seminis LsrB in interacting with testosterone. The cloning of A. seminis ompF gene into E. coli M15reduced bacterial growth, and the recombinant protein was not detected in the total cell extracts of this clone by SDS-PAGE, even after induction with IPTG (data not shown). A. seminis OmpF might affect E. coli membrane structure, leading to a decrease in bacterial growth. Further studies are necessary to understand the effects of this cloning.

The presence of A. seminis lsrB in E. coli, in addition to improving growth in the presence of 3 or 5 ng/mL testosterone, induced the expression of a 23 kDa putative adhesin and led to the detection of its proteolytic activity in the culture supernatant. This finding supports the role of LsrB as a testosterone receptor in bacterial cells and its involvement in genetic regulation.

Lsr is an AI-2 signal receptor expressed by different members of the Pasteurellaceae and Enterobacteriaceae families, constituted by an operon lsrACDBFGE, and involved in the regulation of different virulence-encoding genes (Pereira et al. 2013). The different Lsr proteins may potentially control genomic expression in a variety of microbial pathogens through a signaling cascade. LsrB participates in AI-2 capture; LsrK is a kinase that phosphorylates AI-2 and regulates AI-2 uptake; LsrR constitutes the repressor of the operon; LsrF is an aldolase, and LsrG induces isomerization of the AI-2. Proteins LsrACDB constitute the AI-2 transporter system (Pereira et al. 2009).

On the basis of the results presented here and accounting for the presence of lsrACDBFG operon genes in the A. seminis genome (NCBI Databank, Table 2), we propose the following model to explain the genetic regulation induced by testosterone (Fig. 10). Our results are also supported by the comparable expression levels of adhesins and proteases seen in A. seminis when grown in the presence of furanone C-30 plus testosterone compared with A. seminis grown in the absence of testosterone, and by the improved growth of an E. coli clone expressing A. seminis LsrB, which also increases the release of secreted proteases when it is grown in the presence of testosterone. The results described here could help understand the pathogenic mechanisms of A. seminis.

Conclusion

The present study paves the way for understanding A. seminis bacterial pathogenesis and, by extension, other bacterial pathogens, by identifying, at first, a testosterone signaling mechanism mediated by A. seminis receptors: an outer membrane porin, OmpF, and a periplasmic protein, LsrB, as testosterone receptors. The interaction between testosterone and these proteins favors the A. seminis growth and its virulence factor expression, as seen also in E. coli. This could have implications for the development of control strategies to address this and, probably, other microorganisms with similar pathogenesis mechanisms. Understanding the pathogenic mechanisms and the regulation of virulence factor expression in A. seminis could help develop tools to control the pathology caused by this microorganism.
