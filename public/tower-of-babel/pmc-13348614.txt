Deciphering seasonal shifts, genotype × environment interactions effect on antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds and antinutritional factors in a diverse panel of leafy amaranth (Amaranthus spp.) germplasm: genetic and multivariate insights for breeding
Authors: Srija Priyadarsini, Gobinda Chandra Acharya, Pradyumna Tripathy, Sidharth Kumar Palai, Manasi Dash, Ashok Mishra, Ayyagari V.V. Koundinya, Nityamanjari Mishra, Saurabh Singh, Pushpajeet Choudhari, Chetna Nimje, Alok Nandi, Umasankar Pal, Jyotirmayee Samantaray, Meenu Kumari, Sipra Mohapatra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeafy amaranths (Amaranthus spp.) are promising traditional food and nutritional sources in Asia and Africa. They hold excellent value as underutilized, climate-resilient vegetable crops with versatile potential for food and nutritional security. Majority of the vegetable amaranth germplasm cultivated by Indian farmers consists of local landraces, and has not been subjected to systematic characterization. Hence, comprehensive characterization of amaranth germplasm for bioactive, antioxidant, and antinutritional factors in contrasting environments is vital for accelerating breeding of vegetable amaranth to underscore its potential in fulfilling United Nations sustainable development goals (SDGs) of Zero Hunger (SDG2) and Climate Action (SDG13).ResultsA diverse panel of 57 vegetable amaranth germplasm was investigated for antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds and antinutritional factors across winter (2024–2025) and summer (2025) seasons. Field experiment was laid out in randomized augmented block design with three commercial checks. Seasonal variations revealed the influence of G × E interactions on antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds and concentration of antinutrients. Clear temporal trends were recorded for antioxidant capacities (DPPH, CUPRAC, FRAP), bioactive traits and anti-nutrients (oxalates and nitrates). Genetic and multivariate analysis critically discriminated the germplasm and enabled the identification of potential parents for hybridization to exploit heterosis. Mahalanobis-D2 cluster analysis suggested IC-0647405, IC-0647419, IC-0647408, IIHR-B-AM247 and IIHR-B-AM248 as a promising source of antioxidant phytochemicals, β-carotene, protein, vitamin C and phenolics with lower content of anti-nutrients. Pusa Kiran, IC-0647419 and IHR_B_AM248_3_2 exhibited lower concentration of oxalates, while IC-0647414, IC-0647411 and IIHR-B-AM237_5_14_3 depicted lowest accumulation of nitrates. High heritability and genetic advance estimates for studied traits indicated the scope of selective breeding in improvement of vegetable amaranth. Both synergistic and antagonistic trait associations were recorded between antioxidant and antinutrient traits.ConclusionOur study revealed substantial seasonal and genetic variations for antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits in leafy amaranth germplasm, which is crucial for escalating crop breeding programmes. Results of genotype × environment interactions demonstrated the need of multi-environment, multi-year stability analysis for robust selection of multi-trait genotypes. The multivariate insights paved the way to breed amaranth cultivars with high nutritional quality and wider adaptability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08949-1.

Background

The challenges of food and nutritional security are becoming severe in face of climate catastrophe [1, 2]. Accelerating targeted breeding programmes, search for alternative food sources, exploration of genetic potential of underutilized crops, adopting advanced genomics tools, and systematic characterization of local germplasm, are need of the hour to tackle the issues of food and nutritional security in sustainable manner [1, 3–6]. In line with this, tapping of neglected and underutilized vegetable crops germplasm is a vital strategy to enhance the dietary diversity and diversifying traditional food systems [7, 8]. Vegetable crops play an important role in ensuring healthy diet, and are also referred as protective foods due to their rich phytochemistry [8–10]. Among vegetable species, leafy vegetables are rich blend of mineral nutrients, vitamins, antioxidants, bioactive compounds and other phytochemicals [10–13]. Realizing the nutritional value of leafy vegetables, Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) has recommended a daily uptake of 100 g of leafy vegetables [14]. Hence, exploration and characterization of underexploited vegetables with climate-resilient potential and nutraceutical value is pivotal to combat the challenges of worldwide hunger and malnutrition [11, 15–17]. Among the group of leafy vegetables, vegetable Amaranthus spp. offers an excellent opportunity to tackle the issues of food and nutritional security [11, 17, 18].

Amaranthus is a complex genus comprising nearly 70–80 species which are utilized as fodder, pseudocereal, leafy vegetables, weedy types, and ornamental types [11, 19, 20]. Amaranth is a suitable candidate for cropping systems due to short life cycle, and C4 photosynthetic system makes it to supersedes staple crops like maize and wheat under poor soils [21, 22]. The leafy amaranth species have nutritional power comparable or better than lettuce and spinach [23, 24]. The commonly grown leafy-type Amaranthus species include tricolor, viridis, dubius, lividus, blitum, and spinosus [11]. Global geographical variations do exist in their utilization, like in Western African region, blitum, hybridus, cruentus, and dubius are most common species. While, Amaranthus tricolor is most prevalent species in India and China [11, 25]. Similarly, in South America, the most common species is Amaranthus caudatus, and in South East Asia, Amaranthus hybridus is becoming popular as both grain and vegetable type [11, 25]. Majority of the amaranth germplasm grown by Indian farmers as leafy vegetable belongs to Amaranthus tricolor [11]. This traditional germplasm mainly consists of local landraces exhibiting high phenotypic plasticity, where substantial variations are present in terms of colour of leaf, pigmentation of stem tissues, plant type, and inflorescence colour [26, 27]. The vegetable amaranth species are considered as abundant source of bioactive and nutraceutical compounds (betalains, carotenoids, ascorbic acids, phenolics, and anthocyanins) with high antioxidant activity [11, 23, 24, 28]. These compounds impart antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, anticancer, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, and anti-cholesterolemic activities [24, 26, 29, 30]. The protection of human neuroblastoma cells against glycation end-products has been reported by using extracts of Amaranthus species, tricolor and lividus [31]. These pharmacological, therapeutic and medicinal properties trigger the true assessment of local germplasm of leafy amaranths to identify suitable parental lines for biofortification breeding and functional food industry. Additionally, wide range of variations have been recorded in leafy amaranth species (Amaranthus tricolor and Amaranthus lividus) for various antioxidant and bioactive compounds [32]. The antioxidant and phytochemical content may also vary with leaf colour pigment, where in general, the genotypes with deep red colour exhibits high antioxidant potential [32, 33]. But these variations are also influenced by genotype, environmental factors, edaphic factors, and agronomic practices [12, 34–36].

Deciphering genetic variations and environmental influence on nutritional composition of bioactive and antioxidant compounds, variations in concentration of antinutrients across the seasons in a diverse panel of vegetable amaranth germplasm is pivotal in a fight with hunger and malnutrition. High phenotypic plasticity, tremendous diversity and climate change are main challenge for taxonomic characterization of amaranth germplasm [37]. Sufficient agro-morphological and biochemical diversity is present in the local germplasm of vegetable amaranth species grown in India. However, efforts on systematic characterization and exploring true genetic potential of these landraces are limited in India. The genetic and multivariate analysis is central in analysis of genetic variation and identification of parental lines for heterosis breeding [37]. Therefore, the present study was prudently undertaken for genetic and multivariate analysis of diverse panel of amaranths germplasm to underscore their antioxidant, bioactive and functional food potential to comply with United Nations sustainable development goals of zero hunger (SDG2) and climate action (SDG13). Season dependent shifts in antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds and antinutritional factors were investigated in the studied germplasm by analysing G × E interactions. Consequently, the study aimed to facilitate the selection of promising parents for escalating vegetable amaranth breeding to develop climate resilient cultivars with high nutritional quality.

Materials and methods

Study location

The field experiments of present study were conducted across winter (2024–2025) and summer (2025) seasons at the research farm of ICAR– CHES (Central Horticultural Experimental Station), Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India. The research farm is located at 20°15’ N latitude and 85°40′ E longitude, which is at 35 m above mean sea level. The climate of study location is warm and humid with an average annual rainfall of about 1500 mm and 75–80% humidity [22]. Hot summers prevails during March to June followed by a long rainy season from mid-June to September. Winters are mild from November to January [22]. Soil of research farm is sandy loam soil with an acidic pH of 4.8 to 5.2 and a poor organic carbon content of 0.2% [22, 38]. The Agro-meteorological weather data during the cropping seasons is depicted in the supplementary file (Table S1 and Table S2).

Experimental design and plant materials

In the present study, a diverse panel of 57 leafy amaranth germplasm lines, developed through individual plant selection and selfing, including three check cultivars, Pusa Kiran, Arka Neelachal Bainishi and Arka Neelachal Ruchitha, were used to investigate the seasonal variations and season × genotype interactions influencing antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits (Table S3). All the plant material used in the present study was provided by the research centre itself, that is Central Horticultural Experiment Station (ICAR-Indian Institute of Horticultural Research), Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India. The plant material is maintained and conserved at the centre for the past five years and belongs to two cultivated species, Amaranthus tricolor and Amaranthus blitum. The field trials were conducted by following randomized augment block design (RABD) with six blocks and 12 entries per block. The three standard check cultivars were replicated six times.

Determination of total chlorophyll (SPAD value)

Chlorophyll content was assessed using a Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) meter, which measures the ratio of transmitted radiation at two wavelengths: red light, which is largely absorbed by chlorophyll and near infrared light, which is not. In the present study we used atLEAF CHL BLUE Chlorophyll meter for recording the SPAD value. For data collection, five healthy leaves were selected randomly and tagged from each genotype, and the mean SPAD value was calculated.

Determination of antioxidant activity

To determine the antioxidant activity of studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm, CUPRAC assay (Cupric ion reducing antioxidant capacity), FRAP assay (Ferric reducing ability of plasma), and DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical-scavenging assays were performed.

CUPRAC assay

To determine the antioxidant capacity based on CUPRAC assay (µmol Trolox g⁻¹), the protocol of Apak et al. [39] was used with minor modifications. A sample of 5 g from each genotype was homogenized in 15 ml of 100% ethanol using a sterilized mortar pestle and this extract was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for a period of 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was stored at -20 °C. Thereafter, 100 µl of sample extract was blended with 4 ml of CUPRAC reagent. CUPRAC reagent contained 1 ml of neocuproine, 1 ml of ammonium acetate, 1 ml of CuCl2 and 1 ml of distilled water with a pH 7.4. The absorbance was noted at a wavelength of 450 nm using a double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). The CUPRAC estimation was performed in triplicate for each of the leafy-type amaranth germplasm line.

FRAP assay

FRAP assay determines the ability to reduce ferric iron (Fe3+) to the blue-colored ferrous (Fe2+) form, that indicates the antioxidant power through a colour change. To determine the antioxidant potential of leafy-type amaranth germplasm under study based on FRAP assay (µmol Trolox g⁻¹), the procedure demonstrated by Benzie and Strain [40] was adopted with minor modifications. The FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing the acetate buffer (300 mM, pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ solution made in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl3 at the ratio of 10:1:1 (v/v/v). Thereafter 100 µL of sample was thoroughly mixed with this FRAP reagent (3.0 ml). After 30 min, the absorbance was noted at 593 nm in double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). The FRAP assay for each of the sample was performed in triplicate.

DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay

The antioxidant potential based on DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging activity of amaranth leaves was determined as per Brand-William et al. [41] and Ghosh et al. [42] with minor modifications. For each of the leafy-type amaranth germplasm line, a sample of 2 g was mixed in 25 ml ethanol followed by overnight extraction in dark at room temperature. Thereafter, 1 ml of DPPH (80 µg/ml of ethanol) was added into a 0.2 ml of eluted extract. The blank sample was maintained by taking 0.2 ml of distilled water and 1 ml of DPPH solution. Both of these were kept for 30 min at room temperature. The absorbance was noted at 517 nm in double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan) and initially spectrophotometer reading was set to zero using ethanol. Estimation of total phenolic content (TPC)

To estimate the total phenolic content (TPC) (mg gallic acid 100 g⁻¹ FW) in vegetable amaranth germplasm collections under study, the Folin– Ciocalteu method [43, 44] with minor modifications was employed. The extract preparation for estimation of TPC in leafy-typeamaranth, a sample of 5 g of leaves was homogenized in 15 ml of 80% ethanol using the mortar pestle. The centrifuging of aqueous extract was done at 10,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and supernatant was stored at -20 °C. After that, 0.50 ml of extract was mixed with 2.5 ml of 1:10 diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. The mixture was neutralized with 2 ml of 20% solution of sodium carbonate (Na2CO3). Then reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance of the final blue coloured supernatant was noted at 650 nm using a double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). Estimation of total ascorbic acid content

To determine the vitamin C (total ascorbic acid content) (mg 100 g− 1) in the leaves of leafy-type amaranth germplasm under study, the colorimetric method described by Ranganna [45] was employed by using a 2, 6-dichlorophenol indo-phenol solution (2,6-DCPIP) (dye) that is decolourized by vitamin C in the sample extracts. The standard of ascorbic acid was prepared by taking 100 mg of L-ascorbic acid and it was dissolved in 3% HPO3 (metaphosphoric acid). The final volume was made up to 100 ml with 3% metaphosphoric acid and further it was diluted by taking 10 ml of 1 mg ml− 1 ascorbic acid. To prepare the dye solution 50 mg of the sodium salt of 2,6-dichlorophenol indo-phenol dye was dissolved in 150 ml of hot glass distilled water containing 42 mg of NaHCO3 (sodium bicarbonate). Then the final volume was made up to 200 ml and dye was stored in dark bottle under refrigerate conditions. The dye was further standardized for each day. The dye factor was calculated as, Dye factor = 0.5/titer value. The 5 g of homogenized sample of vegetable amaranth leaves of each germplasm line was extracted with 4% oxalic acid. The volume was made up to 100 ml followed by centrifugation. Then 10 ml of 4% oxalic acid was added to 5 ml of supernatant that was pipette out. Quantification of total carotenoid content (TCC)

The total carotenoid content of leafy-type amaranth germplasm was determined following the method of Panpraneechaeron et al. [46] with slight modifications. For estimation of TCC, 0.2 g of dry sample was taken for n-hexane extraction using an ultrasonic water bath (WUC-A03H, Witeg Labortechnik GmbH, Germany). The extracted solutions were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper (#144150, Maidstone, England) and then the volume was made up to 25 ml with hexane. The absorbance of the resulting solution was recorded at 446 nm using a double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). The total carotene content (TCC) was calculated using the formula: TCC = 25 × 383× (As − Ab)/100 W.

where Aₛ is the absorbance of the sample, Ab is the absorbance of the blank, and W is the weight of the sample in grams. TCC was expressed as mg/kg of dry powder.

Determination of β-carotene content

The β-carotene analysis was performed as per the method described in AOAC [47]. To determine the β-carotene content in leafy-type amaranth germplasm, dispersed the 10 g of sample in 50 ml of water-saturated n-butanol to obtain a homogeneous suspension followed by gentle shaking, and allowed it to stand for 16 h at room temperature in the dark. Then it was mixed again and filtered the mixture through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. Then, filtrate volume was made up to 100 ml. The absorbance of the clear filtrate was noted at 440 nm using saturated n-butanol as the blank in a double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). The β-carotene content was determined using the formula: β-carotene content (ppm) = 0.0105 + 23.5366×A. The value was converted into µg 100ml− 1.

Quantification of total anthocyanin content

To determine the anthocyanin content, pH-differential method using two buffer systems [potassium chloride buffer, pH 1 (0.025 M) and sodium acetate buffer, pH 4.5 (0.4 M)] was used as per Lee et al. [48] and Ghosh et al. [42] with slight alterations. A double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan) was used to record the absorbance at 510 nm and 700 nm. The anthocyanin content was expressed as cyanidin 3-glucoside (Cyd 3-glu) per kg FW, using an extinction coefficient (e) of 26,900 L cm-1mol-1 and a molecular weight of 449.2 g mol-1.

Absorbance (A) = (A510 nm - A700 nm) pH1 - (A510 nm - A700 nm) pH4.5.

Monomeric anthocyanin content (mg L⁻¹) = A×MW×DF×1000/e×1.

Where, A = absorbance, MW = molecular weight (449.2), DF = dilution factor, e = molar absorptivity (26,900). The final value was converted into mg 100ml-1.

Protein (%) estimation

The proximate analysis system estimated protein content by measuring total nitrogen (N), as described by Sahrawat et al. [49], and multiplying it by a conversion factor. A standard factor of 6.25 is used to convert total nitrogen to total protein.

Determination of oxalates

To determine the anti-nutrient factor ‘oxalate’ in leafy-type amaranth, about 1 g of the ground sample was weighed into a conical flask, and 75 ml of 3 N H2SO4 was added to it. The mixture was allowed to stand for 30 min, after which it was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper. A 25 ml aliquot of the filtrate was transferred into a beaker and 2 drops of methyl red indicator were added. The solution was then heated to boiling and titrated hot against 0.05 M KMnO4 until a faint pink color persisted for at least 30 s. Determination of nitrates

To determine the nitrate content (mg 100 g-1) in leafy-type amaranth germplasm collections under study, the calorimetric method utilizing salicylic acid was employed [50]. For the nitrate estimation, a sample of about 500 mg was weighed and kept in a test tube for analysis. In the test tube carrying sample, 10 mL of hot (90–95 °C) distilled water was poured. Thereafter, the tubed were closed and placed in a hot water bath at 80 °C for a time period of 30 min with intermittent shaking. After hot water bath the samples were allowed to cool at room temperature and centrifugation was done at 4500 rpm. Then supernatant was taken out and weighed to estimate the total volume of extract. To remove the chlorophyll content in the leaf extract 0.5 g of MgCO3 was added to the supernatant and again centrifugation was done. Then supernatant consisting the nitrate extract was treated with 2 N NaOH followed by a combination of Salicylic acid and H2SO4 in a ratio of 1:20. Sodium nitrate was utilized in the preparations of standards. The absorbance was noted at 410 nm using a double beam Shimadzu UV-3600i plus spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Data pertaining to antioxidant and bioactive compounds, antinutrients in the studied germplasm during both winter and summer seasons was subjected to statistical analysis for ANOVA (analysis of variance), mean performance, and determination of genetic components of variance using augmentedRCBD, a R statistical package as per Arvind et al. [51]. The three commercial checks were considered as fixed effects, while studied germplasm lines were considered as random effects [52]. Hence a mixed linear model was employed where seasons were treated as fixed effects, while G × E interactions were considered as random effects. As in the augmented design, the test entries are unreplicated, the error variance is estimated based on the replicated check cultivars. The linear mixed models, where blocks are treated as random effects and checks as fixed effects, were used [52].

The GCV (genotypic coefficient of variation) and PCV (phenotypic coefficient of variation) estimates were categorized as low (0–10%), medium (10–20%) and high (> 20%) [53, 54].

Heritability was estimated based on variance components obtained using a mixed linear model, where genotypes were treated as random effects [52]. Low (0–30%), medium (30–60%) and high (> 60%) estimates of heritability were determined [55, 56].

Multivariate analysis

R studio, a statistical software carrying multiple packages was used (R studio 2020, https://rstudio.com) was employed for graphical representation of data. Mahalanobis distance (D2) and Euclidian distance based statistical analysis was performed for discrimination of studied germplasm. Cluster analysis was employed utilizing silhouette technique and NbClust statistical package in R studio [57, 58]. Inter-and intra-cluster distances were determined using ‘clv’ package of R studio. The principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using R statistical packages ‘FactoMineR and ‘factoextra’ [53, 56]. Major principal components contributing total variations were determined based on eigenvalues of more than 1 [37]. The trait associations based on Pearson correlation coefficient were computed using R statistical package ‘corrplot’ [59].

Results

Variance analysis (ANOVA) for antioxidant, bioactive, and anti-nutrient traits

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) for anti-nutrients, biochemical and antioxidant compounds during the winter season of 2024–2025 and summer season of 2025 in the leafy-type amaranth germplasm under study revealed that the treatment effects were significant for all the traits except for total anthocyanin content (TAC) during summer season of 2025 (Table 2, Supplementary Table S4, Table S5). The results indicated presence of substantial variations for all the studied biochemical, antioxidant and antinutrient traits in the studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm. The ANOVA analysis further indicated significant variations for all the biochemical and antioxidant traits in the check cultivars (Pusa Kiran, Arka Neelachal Bainishi and Arka Neelachal Ruchita) during both winter and summer seasons. The variance analysis further revealed that block effects were non-significant for majority of the traits except for vitamin C, DPPH, β-carotene content and oxalate content. The pooled ANOVA analysis indicated significant genotypic effects for all the biochemical and antioxidant compounds (Table 2). The season × genotype (G × E) interaction effects were also highly significant for majority of the biochemical traits except for vitamin C and nitrate content (Table 1).

Genetic parameters of variability

The appraisal of genetic components of variance for biochemical, antioxidant compounds and anti-nutrients revealed significant variations among the studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm across the seasons (Table S8 and Table S9). The GCV and PCV analysis indicated that, the PCV estimates were slightly higher to GCV estimates for all the biochemical, antioxidant and anti-nutrient traits under study across the seasons. These findings indicated that genetic factors predominantly influenced the trait expression than environmental factors during respective seasons. In the winter season of 2024–2025, the medium estimates of GCV and PCV were recorded for majority of the traits except for vitamin C (low GCV, PCV), β-carotene, oxalate and nitrate content (high GCV, PCV) (Table S8). During the summer season, for majority of the traits, the medium estimates of GCV and PCV were recorded. For CUPRAC and total anthocyanin content (TAC) low estimates of GCV were observed, while for β-carotene and nitrate content, the high estimates of GCV were recorded (Table S9).

Additionally, the high (> 60%) estimates of broad sense heritability (h2b) were documented for all the traits during both winter and summer cropping seasons except for low heritability of total anthocyanin content (TAC) (18.45%) during summer season. Similarly, for majority of the traits, the high (> 20%) estimates of genetic advance as percent of mean (GAM) were recorded during both the seasons, except for vitamin C (medium GAM, 11.18%) during winter season, and for CUPRAC (medium GAM, 18.87%) and TAC (low GAM, 7.83%) during summer season. Overall, the results of present investigation revealed the traits viz. CUPRAC, FRAP, DPPH, total chlorophyll, phenolic content, total carotenoids, β-carotene, protein, anthocyanins, oxalate and nitrate, showed very close difference between the PCV with corresponding GCV, indicating the least impact of the environmental factors in expression of the traits. Therefore, such traits with least difference between PCV and GCV should be selected strongly on the basis of phenotypic expression. Similarly, the traits viz. CUPRAC, FRAP, DPPH, total chlorophyll, phenolic content, total carotenoids, β-carotene, protein, oxalate and nitrate, exhibiting high broad sense heritability (> 60%) coupled high GAM may be selected directly towards development of superior variety in future crop improvement program in leafy-type amaranths.

The cluster analysis based on summer season data revealed that genotype IIHR-B-AM285 remained separate in a single Cluster-IV and is a good source of high anthocyanins, low antinutrients and high DPPH based antioxidant activity (Fig. 4; Table 6). While in the winter season, this genotype was part of largest Cluster-III (Fig. 4). The cluster mean analysis suggested that the largest Cluster-III during the winter season is represented by the genotypes with highest DPPH activity, good source of vitamin C, total carotenoids and low nitrate content. The genetic variation represented by cluster analysis during both winter and summer cropping seasons in the studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm also supported the effect of season × genotype (G × E) interaction in influencing the compositional variations for biochemical and antioxidant compounds. The inter-cluster distance matrix (Table S12) based on biochemical and antioxidant traits for the winter season evaluation of studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm indicated highest inter-cluster distance between Cluster-II and Cluster-IV (6.18), followed closely by Cluster-II and Cluster-V (6.13), and Cluster-I and Cluster-II (5.69). these results suggested that Cluster-II was highly diverse from Cluster-I, Cluster-IV and Cluster-V. The lowest inter-cluster distance was recorded between Cluster-I and Cluster-III (2.66), indicating close similarity in their biochemical and antioxidant trait profiles. Thus, the crosses between genotypes belonging to highly divergent clusters (especially Cluster-II × Cluster-IV or Cluster-II × Cluster-V) are expected to generate greater heterosis and wider transgressive segregation for biochemical and antioxidant traits in leafy-type amaranth. The inter-cluster distance matrix based on Mahalanobis D² statistics for the summer season data analysis (Table S13) indicated that the highest inter-cluster distance was recorded between Cluster III and Cluster IV (D² = 6.16), followed by Cluster I and Cluster IV (D² = 5.45) and Cluster I and Cluster III (D² = 5.10). These results indicated that the genotypes of these respective clusters were highly diverse for biochemical and.

antioxidant compounds, and crosses between these genotypes from distinct clusters might give high heterosis and genetic gains for biochemical and antioxidant traits in leafy-type amaranth. The lowest inter-cluster distance was found between Cluster II and Cluster V (D² = 3.15). This indicates a close genetic relationship between these clusters, and crosses among genotypes within these groups may result in limited genetic gain.

Trait association among biochemical, anti-nutrient and antioxidant compounds

The interaction analysis among biochemical and antioxidant compounds analysed in a large set of leafy-type amaranth germplasm under study, indicated significant and non-significant positive or negative associations between different traits across the seasons (Supplementary Table S14, Table S15, Fig. 5). The correlation matrix indicated that total chlorophyll (TC) exhibited significant positive correlations with CUPRAC (r = 0.414**), FRAP (0.433***), DPPH (0.305*), vitamin C (0.397**), and total carotenoid content (0.467***). CUPRAC correlated positively with DPPH (0.369**) and total carotenoid (0.462***). FRAP also exhibited significant positive association with total carotenoid (0.549***) and β-carotene content (0.324*). Concurrently, DPPH exhibited significant positive interaction with total carotenoid (0.463***) and vitamin C (0.283*). These findings indicated that total carotenoids have strong antioxidant activity based on their significant interaction with chlorophyll, CUPRAC, FRAP and DPPH, suggesting their role in free radical scavenging. Anthocyanins exhibited significant negative association with anti-nutrient, oxalate (-0.377**). Protein content did not show any significant association with most biochemical traits under study indicating independence from antioxidant-related traits. The correlation matrix analysis based on summer season data for biochemical and antioxidant traits also indicated significant positive or negative interactions between studied traits (Table S27). The correlation matrix analysis indicated significant negative association of total chlorophyll (TC) with total anthocyanin content (TAC) (r = − 0.293**). Total chlorophyll had weak non-significant correlation with other antioxidant traits. The significant positive interaction was reported for FRAP content with β-carotene (βC) (r = 0.268*) and nitrate (r = 0.276*). Similarly, DPPH exhibited significant positive association with vitamin C (r = 0.31*) and total carotenoid content (r = 0.307*). Further, a strong significant negative correlation was found between total anthocyanin content (TAC) and anti-nutrient, oxalate content (r = − 0.377**).

Discussion

Climate change is posing a serious threat to food and nutritional security of billions [1]. Revitalizing the orphan crops, systematic characterization of local germplasm, delineation of genotype, environment and G × E interaction effects, promoting genomics in germplasm conservation efforts, identification of elite lines with climate resilient nature and nutri-dense potential, are the key strategies to upscale targeted breeding in a fight with hunger and malnutrition [1, 5, 11, 22, 60–63]. Tapping of local germplasm of vegetable amaranth for antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits holds promise in this context owing to its phenomenal nutraceutical, therapeutic, antioxidant and climate resilient potential [11, 37, 60–63]. Season, genotype, soil types, environmental factors, fertilizer applications, and agronomic practices have been reported to alter phenotypic, biochemical, antioxidative and physiological parameters of amaranth leaves [12, 34, 64, 65]. The pooled ANOVA results of present investigation for antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits across the seasons depicted significant treatment effects for majority of the traits, suggesting the presence of substantial genetic variations among a diverse panel of 57 leafy amaranth germplasm accessions under study. The data analysis indicated significant effects of season, genotype and season × genotype (G × E) interaction for all the antioxidant and bioactive traits under study, except for nitrate and vitamin C content. Results highlighted clear temporal trends in the studied traits across the seasons. The findings of the present investigation are in agreement with the previous reports on significant influence of genotypes and genotype × environment interactions (G × E) in shaping nutritional profile of crop plants [12, 34, 64, 65]. Notably, a small number of germplasm lines under study demonstrated constant performance for antioxidant and bioactive compounds across the seasons, underpinning the response of selection in choosing parental lines with marvellous nutritional quality and adaptation to marginal conditions [12, 22, 34]. Thes results suggested strong scope of further multi-location and multi-year trials to identify the genotypes with wider adaptability and stability for nutrient rich safe cultivars. The excess of antinutrients like oxalates and nitrates in daily diet significantly alters availability of essential nutrients. Oxalates react with Ca and other minerals to from insoluble crystals which are responsible for hyperoxaluria, a risk factor for kidney stones [66]. Hence, it is imperative to understand the genetics and environmental influence on accumulation of these antinutrients in leafy vegetables. In the present study, significant G × E interaction effects were recorded for anti-nutrients (oxalate and nitrate) indicated the role of season and genotypes in accumulation of these antinutrients. These results are in line with previous reports on oxalates concentration in spinach, where nitrogen forms, seasonal changes and genetic effects have been reported to alter oxalate accumulation [66, 67]. Similarly, Kaminishi and Kita [68] reported strong seasonal variations for oxalates and nitrates concentration in spinach leaves. Moreover, the excessive root uptake of nitrates facilitates high accumulation of oxalates in leafy vegetables [69]. Additionally, the block effects were non-significant across the seasons indicating role of genetic factors in observed variation for antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits. For the success of targeted crop breeding programmes related to biofortification and climate crisis, the existence of substantial genetic variation is pivotal [38, 63].

Seasonal variation analysis highlighted wide range of variations for biochemical, anti-nutrients, and antioxidant compounds among the studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm. The germplasm lines IC-0647421, IIHR-B-AM43 and IC-0647423 were found promising for total chlorophyll content. The highest antioxidant activity based on CUPRAC content was recorded in IIHR-B-AM19, IC-0647423 and IC-0647405, while IC-0647408 depicted highest FRAP content. Similarly, DPPH based radical scavenging activity was found highest in IC-0647414 and check cultivar, Pusa Kiran. The data analysis identified IHR_B_AM248_3_2, IC-0647419 and Pusa Kiran as promising parents for high vitamin C content. Further, the check cultivar, Pusa Kiran along with IC-0647411 and IIHR-B-AM02 were identified as suitable parents for high total carotenoid content. The indigenous collection, IC-0647405 exhibited highest β-carotene content along with check cultivar, Pusa Kiran. The highest total anthocyanin content (TAC) was found in IIHR-B-AM285 and Pusa Kiran. The indigenous collections, IC-0647407, IC-0647409 and IIHR-B-AM301 were found superior for protein concentration and can be effectively utilized in breeding for high protein content in amaranth leaves. The genotypic differences in nitrogen uptake, and assimilation pathways might be responsible for substantial variation among the studied germplasm for protein content. The check cultivar, Pusa Kiran exhibited lowest content of oxalates, besides germplasm lines IC-0647419 and IHR_B_AM248_3_2. Then, the leafy-type amaranth germplasm lines viz. IC-0647414, IC-0647411 and IIHR-B-AM237_5_14_3 accumulated lowest nitrates. Hence, these lines can be used as parents for breeding leafy-type amaranth cultivars with low content of anti-nutrients like nitrates. The results are in conformity with the previous findings of Sarker and Oba [32], Sarker and Oba [28], Khanam and Oba [70], and Jimenez-Aguilar and Grusak [71], who also reported wide genotypic and species variations for the bioactive and antioxidant compounds in red and green colour amaranth accessions. The species-specific variation has also been reported for TPC, FRAP, and ORAC content in amaranth leaves, where high total antioxidant activity of these biochemical was reported in the leaves of A. hypochondriacus as compared to A. caudatus [72]. The wide range of variations recorded among the studied germplasm lines for different antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits could be attributed to differences in underlying metabolic pathways and their regulation [70–72]. Higher DPPH scavenging activity in selected genotypes is likely correlated with enhanced level of non-enzymatic antioxidants such as phenolics, ascorbic acid, and carotenoids, which collectively contribute to enhanced ROS scavenging capacity [73]. Likewise, substantial variation in total phenolic contents could be attributed to alterations in activities of phenylpropanoid pathway [74]. Genotypes with high phenolic content are generally considered to have high antioxidant potential [74]. Further, the variations in ascorbate biosynthesis and recycling pathways might be responsible for substantial differences in vitamin C accumulation in studied germplasm lines [75]. Similarly, the alterations in variation in isoprenoid biosynthesis pathways, particularly the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway might be responsible for substantial variations in carotenoids content [76].

The results of genetic components of variance, suggested moderate to high estimates of GCV and PCV for antioxidant and bioactive compounds along with antinutritional factors. These findings suggested the scope of simple selective breeding in improvement of nutritional quality [43]. Moreover, the high estimates of heritability (h2b) coupled with genetic advance (GAM) also supported the scope of simple selection procedures in improving nutritional quality of leafy amaranths [77]. In a previous study, high heritability of about 100% was reported for β-Cyanins, β-xanthins, and total polyphenols in vegetable amaranth genotypes, indicating role of additive gene action in expression of these traits [78]. The quality traits are governed by mono or oligogenes, and hence less influence by environmental factors [78]. Thus, high estimates of heritability were recorded for these quality traits [79]. Additionally, the overestimates of heritability could be attributed to design error of randomized augmented block design because environmental variance is often underestimated, owing to the absence of replication for test entries and dependence on replicated check varieties. Therefore, heritability estimates should be interpreted with caution [80]. Nevertheless, the estimates of genetic components of variance provided preliminary information for screening of germplasm for high quality and wider adaptability.

The multivariate techniques of PCA and Mahalanobis D2 cluster analysis also discriminated the studied germplasm based on antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds and antinutrients concentration, depicting substantial variation for section of ideal parents. The PCA is an important multivariate approach to identify and determine the degree of association between desirable traits and principal components (PCs) [22, 37]. The role of individual variables contributing in total variation was also determined using PCA approach [37, 81]. PCA results indicated that first six components explained over 69% of total variations based on biochemical and antioxidant traits. The total carotenoid content (TCC), vitamin C, oxalates, nitrates and β-carotene contributed maximum in total variations in the studied leafy-type amaranth germplasm. Similar trends were recorded by Abrar et al. [37], who reported first five major principal components (PCs) based on physiological traits and mineral nutrients. Likewise, in another study comprising 39 leafy-type amaranth germplasm lines, 68% of the total variation for different phenotypic and nutritional quality traits was explained by first four principal components [82]. Similarly, in a previous study on vegetable amaranth, the PCA analysis based on yield, physiological and biochemical traits in vegetable amaranth genotypes depicted that first five PCs explained 86.2% of total variation [78]. Further, the significant correlation between individual principal components, especially PC1 and PC2, with biochemical and antioxidant compounds, can be utilized for identification of ideal indigenous or exotic collections of leafy-type amaranth species [37, 81].

In the present study, Mahalanobis D2 analysis for biochemical and antioxidant compounds along with antinutritional factors, grouped the 57 diverse leafy amaranth germplasm collections into five different clusters. The cluster mean analysis and cluster distance matrix facilitated the selection of diverse parents for heterosis breeding in improving quality of leafy amaranths. The broad genetic spectrum of studied germplasm was depicted by cluster analysis [37]. These results are in line with previous reports on amaranth germplasm for nutritional quality and phenotypic traits by Abrar et al. [37], Shukla et al. [82], and Jahan et al. [78]. The hybridization programme between the genotypes from divergent clusters like cluster-II × cluster-IV and cluster-II × cluster-V, would be instrument to get high heterotic response for biochemical and antioxidant compounds. These cross combinations will enable generation of high genetic diversity and recombination for useful nutritional quality traits [37, 82]. Overall, the cluster analysis results suggested that strategic hybridization between highly divergent clusters, combined with selection within nutritionally superior clusters, can accelerate the development of high-yielding, antioxidant-rich, and nutritionally balanced amaranth varieties. Additionally, the correlation analysis in the present study, demonstrated both synergistic and antagonistic trait associations among antioxidant, bioactive and antinutritional traits. The correlation matrix analysis indicated significant negative correlation of total chlorophyll (TC) with total anthocyanin content, which indicated a metabolic and functional divergence between these pigment groups. Chlorophyll is primarily associated with photosynthetic activity, whereas anthocyanins are stress-responsive secondary metabolites involved in photoprotection and antioxidative defense [83, 84]. While, significant positive association was recorded for FRAP content with β-carotene (βC) and nitrate content. Similarly, DPPH exhibited significant positive interaction with vitamin C and total carotenoid content. These findings corroborate with the results of Jimenez-Aguilar and Grusak [71], Sarker and Oba [32] and Jahan et al. [78] for the correlation analysis of biochemical and antioxidant traits. From a breeding perspective, this indicates that carotenoid-rich genotypes can be targeted as key donors for improving antioxidant properties, enabling simultaneous enhancement of nutritional quality and health-promoting traits. Overall, the interaction analysis results indicated a coordinated network for nutritional potential of leafy-type amaranth germplasm, where different classes of bioactive and antioxidant compounds interact either synergistically or antagonistically. These findings indicated that simultaneous improvement of antioxidant potential and nutritional value is feasible, although trade-offs between some negatively associated antioxidants, photosynthetic pigments, and anti-nutrients needs to be considered carefully during biofortification related breeding programmes [85].

Conclusion

The results of present study demonstrated a comprehensive understanding of seasonal dynamics, G × E interactions, and their integrated response on antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds and antinutrients in a diverse panel of vegetable amaranth germplasm. The substantial genetic variations were recorded for antioxidants and phytochemicals (TPC, vitamin C, total anthocyanin content, protein, CUPRAC, FRAP, DPPH), which is a prerequisite for effective selection. The strong influence of G × E interactions, emphasized the need for further multi-location, multi-season evaluation of leafy-type amaranth germplasm for identification of genotypes with stability for nutritional quality and low concentration of antinutritional factors. Study highlighted pronounced seasonal effects, hence G × E interactions need to account in selection strategies. The integration of genetic parameters of variability with multivariate analysis could enable the identification of superior lines with high antioxidant activity, bioactive compounds, and reduced burden of antinutrients [86]. The cluster analysis facilitated the selection of genotypes from diverse clusters for heterosis breeding. The strong positive relationships of carotenoids with multiple antioxidant parameters (DPPH, FRAP, CUPRAC) suggest that selection for carotenoid content can serve as an effective indirect selection approach to enhance overall antioxidant capacity. The findings of present investigation underscore the potential of leafy amaranth germplasm in accomplishing SDG2 and SDG13 of United Nations. The study paved a robust framework for targeted breeding to develop cultivars with high nutritional quality and wider adaptability. The findings also highlighted the importance of robust systematic characterization of locally adapted germplasm for sustainable food and nutritional security.
