The role of radiation therapy in leptomeningeal disease from breast cancer – a multicenter analysis
Authors: Felix Ehret, Audrey Aitelli, Andrzej Niemierko, Ammy M Yuan, Michael G Milligan, Shane S Neibart, Ayal A Aizer, Nancy U Lin, Sarah L Sammons, Helen A Shih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA subset of breast cancer patients will eventually develop leptomeningeal disease (LMD). There is a lack of large, in-depth analyses of the pattern of care and outcomes, with a particular focus on radiation therapy (RT). We report our experience in interdisciplinary LMD management, specifically addressing the role of RT.Materials and methodsThis multicenter study analyzed female breast cancer patients with LMD treated at three academic centers between 2005 and 2020.ResultsA total of 232 LMD patients were included. The median age at LMD diagnosis and Karnofsky performance status (KPS) were 54.5 years and 80%, respectively. Most patients presented with LMD localized in the brain (53.8%), underwent RT (66.4%), and received systemic therapy (61.6%). One hundred fifty-four patients received 205 RT courses, with whole-brain RT used most often (120 treatments, 58.5%). With a median follow-up of 4.5 months (range 0.1–72.0 months), 220 deaths were observed. The median overall survival (OS) was 4.7 months (95% confidence interval: 3.9–5.5). RT was not associated with improved OS and showed heterogeneous interactions across clinical contexts. Systemic therapy was associated with improved OS, especially in patients with a poor KPS.DiscussionThis study highlights the dismal prognosis of LMD. RT was not associated with improved OS. Our findings, along with the reviewed literature, demonstrate that determining the potential benefit of RT on OS is challenging due to interactions between treatments and their timing, underscoring the need to clarify the optimal use and timing of RT in randomized clinical trials.Keywords: Breast cancer, Leptomeningeal disease, Leptomeningeal metastasis, Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, Brain metastasis, Brain metastases, Radiation therapy, Radiotherapy, WBRT, CSI

Introduction

Leptomeningeal disease (LMD) remains a terminal event in cancer characterized by the invasion of malignant cells into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and meninges along the brain and spinal cord [1], [2]. LMD is a late-stage sequela that develops in approximately 5 to 25% of solid tumors, with the most common entities including breast cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma [2], [3]. LMD is of particular concern for patients with breast cancer, given its epidemiological relevance and, on average, slightly more favorable prognosis. Roughly 10 to 25% of patients with brain metastases from breast cancer will develop LMD, with rates expected to increase as survivorship with metastatic disease improves [4], [5], [6], [7]. After diagnosis with LMD, the prognosis is poor, with a historical median overall survival (OS) ranging between 2 and 4 months [2], [3]. Radiation therapy is one of the primary treatment modalities for LMD [2]. Photon-based involved-field radiation therapy (IFRT), including whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT) and focal spine irradiation, is widely used as one treatment option [2]. Other LMD treatment options include systemic and intrathecal therapies, with varying degrees of efficacy but promising results in selected patients [2], [8], [9], [10]. The recent Society for Neuro-Oncology (SNO) and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) consensus review emphasizes the importance of multicenter collaboration and analysis of contemporary data to gain new insights into patient subgroups and prognostic factors [2]. Large, in-depth analyses of the role of radiation therapy and patterns of care in breast cancer LMD patients are scarce. Therefore, we report our contemporary multicenter experience and clinical outcomes of female breast cancer patients with LMD, specifically focusing on the use of radiation therapy and its impact on OS in the context of interdisciplinary management. Finally, we review the available studies in the field.

Methods

Patient, disease, and treatment data

Women treated for LMD associated with breast cancer at three tertiary care centers in the United States between 2005 and 2020 were included. LMD was diagnosed based on clinical presentation, detection of malignant cells in CSF, and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including review of radiology reports and imaging [2], [11]. Demographic and baseline breast cancer clinical data were retrieved from pathology reports and from progress notes in radiation oncology, medical oncology, and neurosurgery. Breast cancer diagnosis date, histological subtype, stage, associated metastases, and treatment data were collected. Hormone receptor status was collected as well. All receptor status data were required to assign tumors to the respective subgroups. In case of bilateral tumors, the patient was assigned to the triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cohort if at least one of the tumors was receptor status negative for all receptors. If the receptor status was unknown for any receptor, no group assignment was made. This was also the case for bilateral tumors with differing receptor status for each side. Histological subtype was assessed only for the first tumor diagnosed in cases with bilateral disease. LMD subtypes were characterized as classical, nodular, or both classical and nodular based on MRI. LMD was defined as enhancement of the leptomeninges, including the brain surface, cerebellar or cerebral sulci, cranial nerves, and/or spinal nerve roots. Classical (i.e., linear) LMD presented as a linear or curvilinear enhancement on contrast-enhanced MRI. Nodular LMD presented as nodular enhancement measuring a minimum of 5 mm on contrast-enhanced MRI. In accordance with the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) working group, nodules had to be touching the leptomeningeal space to be considered leptomeningeal versus parenchymal [12]. Classification was based on imaging appearance rather than presumed etiology. Mixed LMD included both classical and nodular types.

Statistical analysis

OS was defined as the period from the date of LMD diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients without an event were censored at the last available follow-up. The follow-up was calculated as the time from LMD diagnosis to death or the last follow-up for alive patients. The Kaplan-Meier estimator was used to estimate survival rates, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) shown as shaded areas in Fig. 1A, B, and C. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) was used to compare survival across subgroups at different time points. The difference in RMST between groups was tested using an asymptotic Wald-type test. Descriptive statistics using means, medians, interquartile ranges (IQR) (25th – 75th percentile), standard deviations, minimum and maximum, as well as frequencies, were used to report data. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models with time-varying covariates were used to evaluate associations between patient, tumor, and treatment variables and OS. Radiation therapy and systemic therapy for LMD were modeled as time-varying covariates to account for treatment timing and to reduce immortal-time bias. Variable selection for Cox regression analyses was prespecified based on the most important known and available prognostic factors, minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion [2], [11], [13], [14]. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. P-values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant and not adjusted for multiple testing. The data analysis and visualization were performed with STATA BE 18.0 and MP 19.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Numbers and percentages might not fully add up due to rounding. We performed a non-systematic review with PubMed to identify pertinent studies reporting on the use of radiation therapy for breast cancer LMD. This study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers (protocol number 2024A016603).

Results

Patient characteristics

We identified 232 women with LMD from breast cancer treated between 2005 and 2020. The median age at primary breast cancer diagnosis was 47.2 years (IQR: 39.1–54.6). The median age at LMD diagnosis was 54.5 years (IQR: 46.8–60.8), following the primary breast cancer diagnosis by a median of 4.8 years (IQR: 2.2–8.9, Fig. 1A). The most common presenting LMD symptoms included headaches (40.5%), nausea or vomiting (26.7%), and cranial nerve deficits (25.8%). Only 10.7% were asymptomatic at the time of LMD diagnosis. The median Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) at diagnosis was 80%. Radiographically, classical LMD was observed more frequently (68.1%) than nodular-only or mixed LMD (31.8%). On imaging, intracranial-only LMD (53.8%) was more common than spine-only (8.6%) or intracranial-and-spine involvement (37.5%). Approximately one-third of patients (37.0%) had brain metastasis before their LMD diagnosis, with 29 patients (12.5%) having undergone surgical resection of brain metastases. Seventy-four patients (31.9%) received prior central nervous system (CNS)-directed radiation therapy. The patient and tumor characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

LMD treatments

Most patients (61.6%) received systemic therapy, including chemotherapy (45.6%), targeted therapy (22.4%), hormone therapy (10.7%), and immunotherapy (4.7%). A total of 154 patients (66.4%) underwent any radiation therapy, 24.6% of them receiving more than one course of radiation therapy. Two hundred and five radiation therapy courses were delivered. The median time from LMD diagnosis to radiation therapy was 7 days (IQR: 3–40, Fig. 1B). The vast majority (71.4%) of first radiation therapy courses were started within 30 days after the LMD diagnosis. Eight patients (5.1%) started radiation therapy on the day of LMD diagnosis. WBRT was used in 114 patients (49.1%), making it the most commonly used type of radiation therapy. The median delivered WBRT dose was 30 Gy (IQR: 30–35) over a median of 10 fractions (IQR: 10–14). Three patients (1.9%) underwent craniospinal irradiation (CSI), with a median delivered dose of 36 Gy. Less than one-quarter of patients (21.4%) received focal radiation therapy only, i.e., excluding WBRT and CSI treatments. Patients receiving only focal treatments were mostly diagnosed with classical LMD (63.3%). Fourteen, fourteen, and 52 courses of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), fractionated radiation therapy (FRT), and focal spine radiation therapy were delivered. The median SRS dose was 18 Gy (IQR: 17–19), all delivered as a single fraction. The median FRT dose was 30 Gy (IQR: 23.4–30), delivered with a median of 10 fractions (IQR: 5–10). Focal spine radiation therapy was delivered with a median dose and number of fractions of 25.5 Gy (median split, IQR: 20–30) and 8.5 (median split, IQR: 5–10). Nineteen patients (12.3%) did not complete at least one radiation therapy, including 12 WBRT courses and 1 CSI course. No treatments were delivered with particle therapy.

Outcomes

The median follow-up time was 4.5 months (range 0.1–72.0 months). During the available follow-up, 220 deaths were observed. The median OS was 4.7 months (95% CI: 3.9–5.5) (Fig. 1C). The 6, 12, 18, and 24-month OS rates were 41.1, 19.7, 11.5, and 7.2%, respectively. For some covariates, the proportional hazards assumption was not met in a Cox regression model. Therefore, survival comparisons were performed using RMST at specified clinically important time points. Patients with a high KPS (KPS ≥80%) at diagnosis had significantly longer survival than those with a low KPS (<80%), with an RMST of 6.9 months versus 3.9 months, respectively (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1D). Similarly, patients who were asymptomatic at the time of LMD diagnosis had significantly better outcomes than those who were symptomatic. RMST at 12 months was 8.8 months in asymptomatic patients, compared with 5.4 months in symptomatic patients (p = 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). RMST at 24 months was significantly shorter among patients receiving radiation therapy compared with those not receiving radiation therapy (6.6 vs. 9.3 months, p = 0.01), whereas no significant differences were observed at earlier time points (RMST at 12 months: 5.6 vs. 6.4 months, p = 0.20; RMST at 6 months: both 4.1 months, p = 0.92) (Fig. 2B).

When stratified by performance status, radiation therapy demonstrated heterogeneous effects. Among patients with a poor KPS, RMST at 12 months did not differ significantly between those who received radiation therapy and those who did not (4.3 vs. 3.9 months, p = 0.67) (Fig. 2C). In contrast, among patients with a high KPS, radiation therapy was associated with worse survival, with RMST at 12 months of 6.1 months for radiation therapy versus 8.0 months for no RT (p = 0.01). Stratification by LMD location did not identify a subgroup with a statistically significant survival benefit. Among patients with LMD involving the spine or spine and brain, RMST at 12 months was 6.3 months with RT and 7.6 months without RT (p = 0.14). Among patients with brain involvement, RMST at 12 months was 4.8 months, compared with 5.7 months without RT (p = 0.27). Similarly, no radiation therapy survival benefit was observed when stratified by receipt of systemic therapy. Among those receiving systemic therapy, RMST at 12 months was 6.5 months with radiation therapy versus 7.5 months without RT (p = 0.24). Among patients not receiving systemic therapy, the RMST at 12 months was 5.2 months, compared with 6.1 months without RT (p = 0.60).

The survival benefit of systemic therapy was most pronounced among patients with poor performance status. Among patients with a low KPS, RMST at 6 months was 4.3 months with systemic therapy and 2.6 months without systemic therapy (p = 0.0008) (Fig. 2D). In contrast, there was no significant difference in RMST between patients with high KPS who received systemic therapy and those who did not (4.8 vs. 4.5 months, p = 0.39). Systemic therapy was associated with improved survival across LMD locations. In patients with spine-only or combined brain and spine involvement, RMST at 6 months was 5.1 months with systemic therapy compared with 4.3 months without systemic therapy (p = 0.03). Among patients with isolated brain involvement, RMST at 6 months was 4.3 months with systemic therapy versus 3.2 months without (p = 0.005).

A multivariable Cox regression model incorporating time-varying treatment exposures was used to evaluate prognostic factors and explore effect modification (Table 2). Good performance status, i.e., KPS ≥80%, was associated with better OS in patients who did not receive radiation therapy, with (HR: 0.40, p = 0.001) or without (HR: 0.15, p < 0.0005) systemic therapy. Good performance status was also associated with better OS in patients who received radiation therapy and no systemic therapy (HR: 0.40, p = 0.001). Systemic therapy was associated with better OS in patients with poor performance status (HR: 0.49, p = 0.004). Brain involvement was associated with worse OS among patients who did not receive radiation therapy (HR: 2.77, p < 0.0005). Being asymptomatic at the time of LMD diagnosis was independently associated with better OS (HR: 0.56, p = 0.014). Radiation therapy was associated with worse OS among patients with good performance status, both with (HR: 1.74, p = 0.024) and without brain involvement (HR: 3.59, p < 0.0005). Radiation therapy was not associated with OS in patients with poor performance status. In exploratory analyses examining the role of RT across biological subtypes of breast cancer, such as TNBC, we found no evidence that RT was associated with survival in any of these subgroups (data not shown). In an exploratory analysis comparing the target of RT (WBRT vs. focal treatments), WBRT was negatively associated with OS compared with other RT techniques for non-brain LMD and was equally ineffective as focal RT techniques for the brain location (data not shown).

Discussion

LMD is a fatal late-stage complication of breast cancer with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Herein, we characterize and analyze a multicenter cohort of 232 LMD patients from three academic centers, with a particular focus on the role of radiation therapy, patterns of care, and outcomes. According to current guidelines and consensus recommendations from the ASCO, SNO, European Association of Neuro-Oncology (EANO), and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), radiation therapy is a central treatment modality in the management of LMD [2], [11]. Its objective and use depend on a variety of factors, including but not limited to the patient's performance status, burden and extent of LMD, presence of symptoms, extracranial disease, and goals of care [2], [11]. The lack of standardization in the use of radiation therapy for LMD and the heterogeneity of analyzed patient cohorts add further complexity to the interpretation and assessment of the limited evidence available (Table 3). In addition, prospective randomized controlled trials in the field remain extremely scarce [15].

While the efficacy and safety of radiation therapy for symptomatic brain metastases and LMD nodules have been reported, its impact on survival is less well defined (Table 3) [2], [11], [16], [17]. We found that radiation therapy was not associated with OS on multivariable Cox regression and, on univariable and RMST analyses, was associated with worse survival among patients with high KPS. We found no subgroups in which radiation therapy was associated with a survival benefit, suggesting the overall risk of confounding by indication. Patients indicated for radiation therapy likely had worse disease trajectories than their counterparts who were not offered radiation treatment. Ultimately, the effect of radiation therapy varied across clinical contexts, suggesting that its role in OS can only be adequately assessed in randomized trials and should be thoughtfully individualized.

Notably, we observed a survival benefit with systemic therapy. This finding was consistent across LMD locations and apparent early, demonstrating clinically meaningful improvements. Consistent with other LMD literature, we identified both favorable performance status and asymptomatic status at diagnosis as positive prognostic factors. Importantly, our analyses accounted for treatment timing and non-proportional hazards using time-varying models and RMST, addressing common sources of bias in retrospective LMD studies. A review of 19 other studies highlighted heterogeneity among patients with LMD receiving radiation therapy and systemic treatments, with only a few studies reporting favorable survival outcomes with radiation therapy (Table 3). Despite this heterogeneity, the reported median OS rates are remarkably similar. Across the reviewed studies reporting median OS, the range is 1.6–7.5 months, with 14 of 17 (82.4%) studies reporting a median OS of 3–6 months. These rates align well with our observed OS.

While current guidelines advocate against the routine use of WBRT based on an LMD diagnosis alone, the use is typically reserved for patients with a notable and/or symptomatic LMD burden [11]. In addition, focal irradiation of bulky and/or symptomatic intracranial or spinal disease is recommended [2], [11]. Prospective evidence demonstrating an OS benefit with WBRT or focal radiation therapy remains unavailable. However, a recently reported randomized phase 2 study of breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer patients with LMD demonstrated a survival benefit with proton craniospinal irradiation (pCSI) over the current standard of radiation treatment, i.e., photon-based IFRT [15]. The primary endpoint was CNS progression-free survival, which was superior with pCSI, achieving a median of 8.2 months versus 2.3 months with IFRT. The secondary endpoint of OS also favored pCSI, with a median OS of 11.3 months (pCSI) versus 4.9 months (IFRT), respectively. This study represents a notable advancement in the treatment of LMD. In addition, other studies have highlighted the potential efficacy of pCSI [18], [19]. Given these results favoring pCSI, NRG Oncology has initiated NRG-BN014 (NCT06500481), a prospective, randomized, controlled phase 3 trial comparing pCSI with photon-based IFRT, with OS as the primary endpoint. The trial is recruiting patients and will enroll those with breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer. While access to pCSI may be limited due to associated costs and the number of proton centers, state-of-the-art volumetric modulated arc therapy is an alternative for delivering craniospinal irradiation, as highlighted in a recent analysis [20]. Further research is warranted to refine treatment algorithms, identify patient subgroups that particularly benefit from CSI and other radiation therapies, and evaluate potential synergies among systemic, targeted, and immunotherapies and radiation therapy. As highlighted by the current SNO and ASCO consensus review and the EANO-ESMO guideline, the inclusion of affected patients in prospective trials is key to advancing patient care and improving outcomes, including trials of systemic and targeted treatments and radiation therapy [2], [11].

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design and the inherent biases in such studies. Treatment assignment was not randomized and may have been influenced by unmeasured clinical factors, including disease burden, symptom severity, and physician judgment. Additionally, we focused on the effects of various factors on OS, which does not fully capture all clinically meaningful benefits of radiation therapy, such as neurologic symptom control and quality of life improvements. While we could not identify a survival benefit with radiation therapy, it plays a critical role in alleviating and palliating symptoms [2], [11]. Cohort and treatment heterogeneity, along with the considerable number of observed events, are beneficial for the study's external validity and statistical power. However, they limit the ability to clearly define a survival-modifying role for radiation therapy across clinical subgroups. The timing and interactions of treatments pose significant barriers to describing the role and potential benefit of radiation therapy on OS, as reflected in our literature review (Table 3). In addition, evolving treatment algorithms, particularly for systemic and targeted therapies and supportive care, may confound the assessment of treatment effects, underscoring the need for further prospective radiation therapy trials. Moreover, the accurate and reproducible assessment of LMD burden is another challenge to personalize the use of radiation therapy in affected patients. As with all large retrospective studies, incomplete data and the absence of standardized treatments remain limitations.

Conclusion

In this large multicenter cohort of breast cancer patients with LMD, OS remained poor. Radiation therapy was not associated with survival and showed heterogeneous effects depending on performance status and disease characteristics, with worse outcomes among patients with high functional status. In contrast, systemic therapy was consistently associated with improved survival, particularly in patients with a poor performance status. These findings suggest that radiation therapy for LMD should be carefully individualized. Whether radiation therapy improves survival across different tumor entities and patient subgroups will require carefully planned and executed prospective trials. The considerable heterogeneity of LMD patients and potential treatment interactions remain significant barriers to refining treatment algorithms.
