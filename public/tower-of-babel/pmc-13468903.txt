Incidental Diagnosis of Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia Following Perioperative Hypoxemia in a 4‐Year‐Old Child
Authors: Chun‐Chen Sun, Chen‐Hsiu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 4‐year‐old girl with no prior respiratory history developed refractory intraoperative hypoxemia during elective dental surgery under general anaesthesia. Despite 100% inspired oxygen and systematic exclusion of all conventional causes of desaturation, pulse oximetry (SpO2) failed to exceed 94%. Postoperative contrast‐enhanced chest computed tomography identified a 2.01‐cm pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) in the right lung, and subsequent molecular genetic testing confirmed hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT). The patient was discharged with baseline SpO2 of 85%–95% on room air and established on long‐term paediatric pulmonary follow‐up. This case highlights that PAVMs associated with HHT can manifest as unexplained perioperative hypoxemia in asymptomatic children, and that general anaesthesia with positive pressure ventilation may unmask previously compensated right‐to‐left shunting. Early recognition enables appropriate perioperative management and timely HHT surveillance.Keywords: hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, hypoxemia, paediatric, right‐to‐left shunt

Introduction

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), or Osler‐Weber‐Rendu syndrome, is a rare autosomal dominant vascular disorder (prevalence 1:5000–8000) caused by mutations in ENG, ACVRL1/ALK1, or SMAD4, which disrupt transforming growth factor‐β signalling and permit abnormal arteriovenous connections to form [1]. Pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVMs) occur in approximately 35% of HHT patients and create fixed right‐to‐left shunts that bypass alveolar gas exchange, predisposing to hypoxemia, paradoxical embolism and neurological complications [2]. In children, PAVMs are particularly challenging to identify because classic symptoms are often absent. We report a 4‐year‐old child in whom refractory intraoperative hypoxemia prompted investigations that led to an incidental diagnosis of HHT with PAVM.

Case Report

A 4‐year‐old girl (12 kg) was scheduled for elective dental surgery under general anaesthesia. She was born at 36 + 2 weeks (birth weight 2156 g) and had a history of glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency and anterior ectopic anus, but no respiratory symptoms, exercise intolerance, cyanosis, or mucocutaneous telangiectasias. Family history was negative for HHT or vascular malformations.

Anaesthesia was induced by inhalation with nitrous oxide and oxygen and maintained with sevoflurane 2%. Following uncomplicated nasal endotracheal intubation, SpO2 failed to exceed 94% despite FiO2 1.0. Endotracheal tube position was confirmed by direct visualisation, bilateral breath sounds and capnography (end‐tidal CO2 36 mmHg). Volume‐controlled ventilation (tidal volume 90 mL [7.5 mL/kg], rate 20/min, PEEP 4 cmH2O, peak pressure 20 cmH2O) was employed. Pneumothorax, atelectasis and airway obstruction were systematically excluded. Aggressive recruitment manoeuvres failed to substantially improve oxygenation. After optimization of ventilatory settings and stabilisation of intraoperative conditions, SpO2 gradually improved to > 95%, while FiO2 was subsequently titrated down to 0.7. The procedure was completed without further incident.

Postoperatively, SpO2 remained approximately 92% on supplemental oxygen at 2 L/min, despite the patient appearing clinically well. Chest radiography showed right upper lobe consolidation (Figure 1), initially attributed to pneumonia. Contrast‐enhanced chest computed tomography subsequently revealed a 2.01‐cm vascular lesion in the right lung with characteristic arterial supply and venous drainage, confirming a PAVM (Figure 2). Transthoracic echocardiography and abdominal ultrasound showed no additional vascular abnormalities. Bubble contrast echocardiography was not performed during the initial perioperative evaluation because the diagnosis was established by contrast‐enhanced chest computed tomography demonstrating a characteristic pulmonary arteriovenous malformation, followed by molecular genetic confirmation of HHT. Molecular genetic testing confirmed HHT. The patient was discharged with SpO2 85%–95% on room air and established on long‐term paediatric pulmonology follow‐up for PAVM surveillance and screening for additional HHT manifestations.

Contrast‐enhanced axial chest computed tomography demonstrates a 2.01‐cm pulmonary arteriovenous malformation in the right lung with a clearly delineated feeding artery and draining vein, confirming direct arteriovenous communication bypassing the normal pulmonary capillary bed.

Discussion

This case illustrates that a PAVM associated with HHT can present as refractory perioperative hypoxemia in a previously asymptomatic child. The cardinal diagnostic clue was the persistent failure of SpO2 to respond adequately to high FiO2 or recruitment manoeuvres—a pattern characteristic of fixed right‐to‐left shunting rather than simple ventilation–perfusion mismatch [3]. In general, severe hypoxemia has been reported when intrapulmonary shunt fractions become sufficiently large, often exceeding 30%–50%, although shunt fraction was not directly quantified in our patient.

General anaesthesia may have unmasked the hemodynamic significance of the PAVM through several mechanisms. Positive pressure ventilation can alter pulmonary blood flow distribution and potentially increase preferential flow through low‐resistance arteriovenous channels. In addition, volatile anaesthetic agents, including sevoflurane, may attenuate hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and contribute to ventilation–perfusion mismatch [4], although the clinical significance of this effect in paediatric patients with PAVM remains uncertain [5]. Together, these perioperative factors may have rendered a previously compensated right‐to‐left shunt clinically apparent.

Among patients with confirmed PAVMs, 50%–85% harbour underlying HHT and PAVMs occur in approximately 35% of HHT patients [2]. The absence of mucocutaneous telangiectasias and family history in this case underscores the importance of pursuing HHT‐directed genetic evaluation whenever a PAVM is identified, even without classical features. Contrast echocardiography is also an important tool for detecting and grading right‐to‐left shunting in patients with suspected HHT‐associated PAVMs and may complement radiologic evaluation during longitudinal follow‐up. Patients with confirmed HHT require long‐term surveillance for cerebral and hepatic arteriovenous malformations and first‐degree relatives should be offered cascade genetic screening [6].

Clinicians should consider intrapulmonary shunting in the differential diagnosis of persistent intraoperative hypoxemia that is refractory to supplemental oxygen and recruitment manoeuvres once conventional causes have been excluded. Transcatheter embolization represents the definitive treatment option for significant PAVMs, effectively reducing shunt fraction and preventing complications including paradoxical embolism [6].
