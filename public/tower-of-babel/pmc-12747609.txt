Trends in Hospital Abortion During the First 2 Years of COVID‐19 in Quebec, Canada: Results From a Population‐Based Observational Study
Authors: Nathalie Auger, Aimina Ayoub, Émilie Brousseau, Shu Qin Wei, Antoine Lewin, Thuy Mai Luu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeWe investigated the extent to which the COVID‐19 pandemic affected hospital abortion rates in a Canadian setting.MethodsWe obtained all abortions between 2012 and 2022 from hospital discharge data in the Maintenance and Use of Data for the Study of Hospital Clientele database for Quebec, Canada. The exposure was the pandemic (March 2020 to March 2022) compared with the preceding period (January 2012 to February 2020). The outcome included hospital‐based abortions versus other pregnancy admissions. We examined if the pandemic affected monthly hospital abortion rates using interrupted time series regression. We estimated risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between the pandemic and hospital‐based abortions, accounting for pandemic wave, abortion method, gestational age, and other patient characteristics.ResultsThere were 21,675 hospital abortions in total, including 4177 (19.3%) during the pandemic. Abortion rates decreased by 1.2 per 1000 pregnancies the first month of the pandemic and continued to decrease by 0.15 per 1000 every month thereafter. Compared with the preceding year, patients were less likely to have a hospital abortion anytime during the pandemic (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.84–0.93), particularly during the third (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.74–0.88) and fourth (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75–0.89) waves. The decrease was most apparent for instrumentation abortions, abortions before 14 weeks of gestation, and abortions among patients aged ≥ 40 years or who were socioeconomically advantaged.ConclusionsThe pandemic was associated with fewer hospital abortions before 14 weeks, as well as among older or socioeconomically advantaged patients.Keywords: COVID‐19, hospitalization, induced abortion, pandemic, pregnancy

Introduction

The pandemic led to a decrease in abortion services in several countries [1, 2], but population subgroups that were disproportionately affected are unclear. Youth, socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals, and ethnic minorities all have high abortion rates [3, 4]. These groups frequently face barriers accessing contraception and abortion care [3, 4], but disparities in access may have been amplified during the pandemic due to lockdown measures, reduced hospital services, and clinic closures. Seeking abortion care may have been particularly challenging in regions where instrumentation abortion was the main method used and alternatives such as medication abortion or telemedicine were limited. This was the case in Quebec, the only Canadian province that did not transition to telemedicine for medication abortion at the onset of the pandemic [5]. Before the pandemic, instrumentation abortion accounted for 86.3% of abortions in Quebec [6].

Studies are beginning to suggest that certain patients may have had difficulty accessing abortions during the pandemic [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. An analysis of 35,411 abortions in Massachusetts found that adolescents had 27% fewer clinic abortions the first year of the pandemic, compared with a reduction of 20% in adults [7]. Data from eight clinics in the United States (US) indicate that Black individuals accounted for a greater share of abortion patients during the pandemic [8]. The pandemic also seems to have affected the abortion method used [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. In Sweden, the proportion of instrumentation abortions decreased from 5.2% in June 2019 to 3.5% by June 2020 [9]. The US experienced a 31% decrease in instrumentation abortion rates between January 2018 and June 2022 [10], mainly driven by fewer first trimester abortions [8, 11, 12]. However, the focus in most studies was the early stages of the pandemic [7, 8, 9, 11, 12]. We sought to assess trends in hospital abortions later in the pandemic.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We performed a population‐based observational study of all pregnancy admissions in Quebec, Canada, between January 2012 and March 2022. We used data from the Maintenance and Use of Data for the Study of Hospital Clientele registry [13], which contains discharge abstracts of all admissions and day surgeries in Quebec hospitals, including pregnancy terminations and deliveries. The registry has data on clinical diagnoses, procedures, service codes, and other patient characteristics [13].

COVID‐19 Pandemic

The main exposure measure was the pandemic, which extended from the end of February 2020 to the end of March 2022, the last month available at the time of our study [14]. We categorized the pandemic by wave, including the first (February 25 to August 22, 2020), second (August 23, 2020 to March 20, 2021), third (March 21 to July 17, 2021), fourth (July 18 to December 4, 2021), and fifth (December 5, 2021 to March 31, 2022) waves [14].

Outcome

The main outcome measure was admission for an induced abortion, including abortions for congenital anomalies, severe maternal morbidity not compatible with pregnancy, and personal reasons. We used diagnostic codes from the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases, procedure codes from the Canadian Classification of Health Interventions, and service codes to identify patients with abortions (Appendix A). We classified abortions by method (instrumentation abortion vs. labor induction and other medication abortions) and gestational age (< 14, ≥ 14 weeks, unspecified). We placed other pregnancy admissions, including spontaneous abortions, ectopic pregnancies, molar pregnancies, and deliveries in the comparison group.

Patient Characteristics

We considered potential confounders that may have influenced the decision to have an abortion during the pandemic, including age, number of previous pregnancies, comorbidity defined as pre‐existing diabetes, hypertension, obesity, dyslipidemia, substance use disorder, or mental illness, socioeconomic disadvantage classified by quintile based on a composite measure of neighborhood education, employment, and income [15], and place of residence.

Data Analysis

We calculated hospital abortion rates per 1000 pregnancies. We examined trends in abortion rates using interrupted time series analysis, a quasi‐experimental method designed to evaluate the impact of unforeseen events such as the pandemic on time‐sensitive trends in an outcome [16]. To carry out the time series analysis, we first computed monthly abortion rates between January 2012 and March 2022. We then analyzed the time series in an autoregressive model containing an interruption for March 2020, when the pandemic started. We included a term for the interaction between the pandemic and time [17]. As we used an autoregressive model, we accounted for seasonal trends and correlation in abortion rates from month to month [16, 17].

We used log‐binomial regression models to compute risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for abortion, comparing the pandemic with the preceding year. We adjusted the models for age, number of previous pregnancies, comorbidity, socioeconomic disadvantage, and place of residence. We determined if trends varied by abortion method, gestational age, and other patient characteristics, and used generalized estimating equations to account for patients with more than one pregnancy. We also estimated RRs for observed versus expected rates during the pandemic from the interrupted time series analysis, using the bootstrap to derive CIs.

In sensitivity analyses, we repeated the interrupted time series analysis using the number of abortions rather than rates. We also tested alternate pre‐pandemic comparison periods in the log‐binomial regression models. We performed the analysis in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The institutional board of our center exempted the study from ethics review, as we worked with an anonymized dataset.

Results

The study comprised 887,291 pregnancies between January 1, 2012 and March 31, 2022, including 21,675 hospital abortions. Among abortions, 4177 (19.3%) occurred during the first two years of the pandemic (Table 1). Compared with the preceding year, patients with abortions during the pandemic were somewhat more likely to be aged 25–39 years (72.8% vs. 69.8%), have a comorbidity (8.6% vs. 7.3%), and live in rural areas (25.4% vs. 19.9%).

Interrupted time series analysis indicated that hospital abortion rates increased by 0.04 per 1000 pregnancies every month between January 2012 and February 2020, before decreasing during the pandemic (Figure 1). Abortion rates dropped by 1.2 per 1000 pregnancies the first month of the pandemic and continued to decrease by 0.15 per 1000 every month thereafter.

Trend in monthly abortion rates before and during the pandemic among 887,291 pregnancies, Quebec, Canada, 2012‐2022. Solid line is for the trend in monthly abortion rates from an interrupted time series regression model. Dashed line is the expected rate had the trend before the pandemic continued over time.

Overall, abortion rates decreased from 27.3 per 1000 pregnancies the year before the pandemic to 23.9 per 1000 during the pandemic (Table 2). Compared with the preceding year, patients were less likely to have a hospital abortion anytime during the pandemic (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.84–0.93), particularly during the third (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.74–0.88) and fourth (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75–0.89) waves. The pandemic was associated with a decrease in instrumentation abortions (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.81–0.91) but not other abortions (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87–1.08). Findings were similar when we compared observed and expected rates during the pandemic.

The pandemic mostly affected early abortions (Figure 2). In Wave 4, for example, there were nearly 20% fewer abortions before 14 weeks compared with the pre‐pandemic period (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.67–0.99). The pandemic had no clear impact on abortions at 14 weeks or more, although there appeared to be some increase during Wave 1 when we used a 15‐week cutoff (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.00–1.37).

Trends in hospital abortions varied depending on patient characteristics (Table 3). Compared with the preceding year, patients aged 40 years or more had the greatest decrease in abortion rates during the pandemic (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.65–0.91). The pandemic was also associated with a decrease in abortions among patients who were very socioeconomically advantaged (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.67–0.89) or living in urban areas (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.77–0.86). Abortions decreased among patients with and without a comorbidity.

In sensitivity analyses, interrupted time series analysis of the monthly number of abortions yielded similar results as monthly abortion rates. In log‐binomial regression models, use of alternate reference groups, including 6 months and 2 years before the pandemic, did not affect results.

Discussion

This study of 887,291 pregnancies in Quebec, Canada suggests that hospital abortion rates decreased by 12% during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The reduction extended well into the pandemic and was particularly pronounced in the second year, especially for early abortions. Overall, there were around 20% fewer abortions among patients aged 40 years or more, with socioeconomic advantage, and in urban areas. The impact of the pandemic was less apparent in other patient groups. The findings suggest that the decline in hospital abortions extended beyond the first year of the pandemic.

Data on abortions after the first year of the pandemic are sparse. A serial cross‐sectional analysis of 17,512 insured patients in the US found no change in medication abortion rates between January 2018 and June 2022, but a 31% decrease in instrumentation abortions [10]. The decline was more pronounced at the start of the pandemic and rebounded afterwards, but never returned to prepandemic levels. The study included abortions for personal reasons only, not anomalies or maternal complications [10]. Reports from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggest that instrumentation abortions decreased across the US by 19.9% in 2020 and 22% in 2021, compared with 2019 [18, 19, 20]. However, the trends mostly reflect US clinics [21], rather than hospitals which are more likely to provide abortion care for anomalies or medical morbidities. Our study indicates that hospital‐based instrumentation abortions, including abortions for anomalies and maternal complications, decreased throughout the pandemic, especially during the second year.

Past studies on abortion focus primarily on the first 10 months of the pandemic [7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 22, 23]. Abortion rates decreased in France early on [22, 23], while the number remained stable in Sweden, although the proportion of instrumentation abortions decreased from 5.2% in 2019 to 3.5% by June 2020 [9]. Medication abortions in hospitals also decreased in Sweden, while the proportion increased in other settings [9]. In the US, studies have found that medication abortions in clinics increased in the first trimester while instrumentation abortions decreased [7, 8, 11, 12]. Some states reported no change or a decrease in second trimester abortions [7, 8], but others reported an increase [11, 12]. In our data, it is not clear that second trimester abortions increased in the first wave, but there appeared to be a slight increase in risk compatible with the possibility that some women may have had to delay abortions at the very beginning of the pandemic.

Less is known about other patient characteristics associated with abortions during the pandemic. Studies from the US, Italy, and France have reported that abortions decreased early in the pandemic, but more among patients under age 20 years [7, 23, 24]. In the US, the fraction of abortions among Black women increased from 51% in 2019 to 55% by August 2020 [8]. In Italy, first trimester hospital abortions decreased among employed women in the first half of 2020 [24]. In our population, hospital abortion rates decreased primarily among women aged ≥ 40 years, with socioeconomic advantage, and from urban areas. The decrease in abortion rates could be due to changes in sexual behavior, fewer unplanned pregnancies, or a preference to maintain pregnancies. We cannot exclude the possibility of a transition to alternative abortion services or telemedicine from other Canadian provinces. Use of telemedicine in Quebec was limited for abortions during the period of our study [5].

This study had limitations. We had no data on abortions performed in clinics or other provinces, including self‐managed medication abortions or abortions through telemedicine. We could not examine the cause of abortion. Comorbidity may be underreported, and we had no information on potential confounders such as ethnicity, marital status, or contraceptive use. Exposures and outcomes may be misclassified due to coding errors. Further research should compare abortions against pregnancies at the same gestational age, as gestational length was missing for some abortions and the study was not long enough to include pregnancies conceived in later waves. The generalizability of the findings to clinic settings or hospitals in other countries is unclear.

This study of nearly 900,000 pregnancies suggests that COVID‐19 led to a reduction in hospital abortions in Quebec, Canada, especially during the second year of the pandemic. The decrease was more apparent for early abortions, although there was no clear evidence that the pandemic delayed abortions to the second trimester. The reduction in abortions was greatest among patients who were older, socioeconomically advantaged, or living in urban areas. Further research is needed to clarify the reasons behind the decrease in abortions in these groups.
