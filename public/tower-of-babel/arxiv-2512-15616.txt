A reduced model for droplet dynamics with interfacial viscosity
F. Guglietta, D. Taglienti, M. Sbragaglia

Abstract
We propose an extension of the phenomenological Maffettone–Minale (MM) model (P.L. Maffettone and M. Minale, J. Non-Newton. Fluid Mech. 78 , 227–241 (1998)) to describe the time-dependent deformation of a droplet with interfacial viscosity in a shear flow. The droplet, characterised by surface tension σ \sigma , is spherical at rest with radius R R and deforms into an ellipsoidal shape under a shear flow of rate G G , described by a symmetric second-order morphological tensor 𝑺 \bm{S} . In addition to surface tension, the extended MM (EMM) model incorporates interfacial shear and dilatational viscosities, μ s \mu_{s} and μ d \mu_{d} , through the corresponding Boussinesq numbers Bq s = μ s / μ ​ R \text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=\mu_{s}/\mu R and Bq d = μ d / μ ​ R \text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}=\mu_{d}/\mu R , where μ \mu is the bulk viscosity. A central goal of this work is to quantify the parameter range over which the EMM model provides a realistic description of droplet deformation, as a function of the capillary number Ca = μ ​ R ​ G / σ \text{Ca}=\mu RG/\sigma and the Boussinesq numbers. To this end, model predictions are systematically compared with fully resolved numerical simulations.

Introduction

Understanding droplet deformation in hydrodynamic flows has been a central topic in rheology for nearly a century. In 1934, G. I. Taylor addressed this problem in the small-deformation limit
53

\sigma

\lambda

G

\mu

\text{Ca}=\frac{\mu RG}{\sigma}\ ,

(1)

Droplet dynamics is a complex multiscale problem and in many instances it is desirable to possess a simpler phenomenological description via reduced models. Maffettone and Minale (MM)

\bm{\bm{S}}

One of the key strengths of the MM framework is that, in addition to predicting the full time evolution of the deformation, it also provides an explicit analytical expression for the stationary deformation

D_{\mbox{s}}=\frac{\sqrt{f_{1}^{2}+\text{Ca}^{2}}-\sqrt{f_{1}^{2}+\text{Ca}^{2}-f_{2}^{2}\text{Ca}^{2}}}{f_{2}\text{Ca}}\ .

f_{1,2}^{(\mbox{\tiny MM})}(\lambda)

II
Numerical methods

To obtain reference data for droplet deformation, we perform fully resolved numerical simulations (FRS) using the immersed boundary–lattice Boltzmann method
25
. This hybrid numerical technique enables a detailed description of the coupled dynamics between the inner and outer fluids and the deformable droplet interface. The fluid motion is solved through the lattice Boltzmann method
25
;
5
, while the interface is represented by a triangulated surface mesh that interacts with the surrounding fluid through the immersed boundary forcing
25
;
39
. The numerical setup and implementation closely follow the procedure described in Ref.
17
, which has been validated across a broad range of set-ups
51
;
38
;
16
;
4
;
17
;
19
;
18
;
20
;
21
. In the EMM model, on the other hand, the droplet shape is represented by a symmetric second-order morphological tensor
\bm{S}
, whose evolution is governed by Eq. (
2
), with the inclusion of the interfacial viscosity effects through the coefficients
f_{1,2}^{(\mbox{\tiny EMM})}
(see Eq. (
8
)). Unlike the FRS, the EMM model does not require solving the full hydrodynamic field and is instead integrated directly from the local flow gradients using standard explicit Runge-Kutta method
51
.
In both cases, we measure the two main axes of the drop shape in the shear plane, namely
L(t)
and
B(t)
(see Fig.
1
), and we then compute the deformation
D(t)
via Eq. (
3
). To quantify the discrepancy between the deformation obtained from FRS (
D_{\text{FRS}}(t)
) and that predicted by the EMM (
D_{\text{EMM}}(t)
), we introduce the mean relative error:

\langle\epsilon\rangle_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{T}\frac{|D_{\text{FRS}}(t)-D_{\text{EMM}}(t)|}{D_{\text{EMM}}(t)}dt\ ,

(10)

which provides a single, global measure of the model’s accuracy. The averaging interval
T
is selected to include both the transient and the stationary regimes of the deformation dynamics, so that the computation of
\langle\epsilon\rangle_{T}
weights the two phases in a balanced and comparable manner.

as a function of
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
and
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
, for different choices of
Ca
. As a preliminary remark, we note that the error maps are generally not symmetric with respect to the diagonal
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
: exchanging
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
and
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
does not yield the same error. This asymmetry is consistent with the analytical structure of the coefficients
f_{1,2}^{(\mbox{\tiny EMM})}
(see Eq. (
8
) and Fig.
2
).
The resulting colormaps reveal that, in general, at small interfacial viscosities (
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}},\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}\ll 1
), the EMM model reproduces FRS data with excellent fidelity, with errors typically below a few percent. For relatively small capillary numbers (
\text{Ca}\leq 0.2
), as either
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
or
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
grows, the error increases: however, the degradation is modest along the diagonal
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
for intermediate values of Boussinesq numbers, consistently with the excellent agreement observed in Fig.
4
, panel (b).
In contrast, strongly asymmetric viscosity configurations (where either
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
or
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
dominates), lead to noticeably larger discrepancies.
Increasing the capillary number
Ca
pushes the model closer to its limits. At
\text{Ca}=0.3
and 0.5, a sharper growth of the error appears, especially when both viscosities are large. This behaviour is expected: large deformations amplify nonlinear couplings in the interfacial stress that the EMM model, derived in the small-deformation limit, can only approximate. Overall,
Fig.
5
provides a quantitative map of the domain of validity of the EMM model, highlighting the regions where deviations grow and FRS become indispensable.
Note that, at
\text{Ca}=0.5
, breakup occurs for small values of
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
(the corresponding region is shown in grey). This behaviour is fully consistent with the physical mechanism discussed above: when
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=0
, the surface-divergence term in the interfacial stress remains active and enhances the deformation, making the droplet more susceptible to breakup. As
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
increases, this contribution is progressively damped, leading to a stabilizing effect.

IV
Conclusions and perspectives

In this letter, we have studied a reduced model for the dynamics of a droplet with interfacial viscosity in shear flow. The model extends the Maffettone–Minale (MM) framework
30
(Eq.(
2
)) by supplementing surface-tension effects with interfacial viscosity, under the assumption of Newtonian interfaces
47
;
26
. The resulting extended MM (EMM) model provides a simple phenomenological description of time-dependent droplet deformation while retaining a clear connection with classical small-deformation theories
12
;
11
;
3
;
36
.
The EMM framework allows for independent control of shear and dilatational interfacial viscosities through the corresponding Boussinesq numbers
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
and
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
, enabling a systematic investigation of surface-viscosity effects.

A systematic comparison with fully resolved simulations (FRS), performed using an immersed boundary–lattice Boltzmann approach
27
;
17
;
21
, delineates the regimes in which the EMM model remains quantitatively accurate. In particular, the comparison highlights how the stationary deformation
D_{S}
and the range of capillary numbers over which the linear response theory applies depend sensitively on the interfacial viscosities. While for strongly asymmetric viscosity configurations the linear relation between
D_{S}
and
Ca
persists over a relatively broad interval of
Ca
, this range is significantly reduced in the equiviscous case
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
, where nonlinear effects become relevant already at moderate deformations.
The analysis of the time-dependent deformation
D(t)
further shows that interfacial viscosity strongly affects the transient dynamics: shear and dilatational viscosities slow down the deformation process in a nonlinear and coupled manner, with the most pronounced effect occurring when the two viscosities are comparable. This behaviour is quantitatively captured by the characteristic deformation time,
\tau_{\sigma}/f_{1}^{(\mbox{\tiny EMM})}
, which is directly controlled by the coefficient
f_{1}^{(\mbox{\tiny EMM})}
and increases sharply only when both
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}
and
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
are appreciable.
The error maps in Fig.
5
further provide a global view of the model’s domain of validity in the
(\text{Ca},\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}},\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}})
space. For moderate deformations (small to intermediate
Ca
) and in the equiviscous case
\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize s}}=\text{Bq}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
, the EMM model reproduces FRS results with errors of only a few percent. Larger discrepancies arise for strongly asymmetric interfacial viscosities or at larger
Ca
, consistent with the small-deformation assumptions underlying the model and the absence of higher-order nonlinear interfacial effects.
Overall, these results show that the EMM model constitutes a robust and computationally inexpensive tool for predicting droplet deformation with interfacial viscosity within its range of applicability, while FRS remain necessary outside this regime to capture the full interfacial dynamics.

While the present study focuses on a simple shear flow, the EMM framework can be naturally extended to other flow configurations and to regimes of larger deformation, including the prediction of the critical capillary number for breakup,
\text{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}

30
. Although quantitative accuracy cannot be expected at large
Ca
, the availability of analytical expressions for
\text{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}
in terms of
f_{1,2}
makes it possible to assess deviations from established results
14
;
49
. In this spirit, one could also determine modified coefficients
f_{1,2}
, calibrated for finite-
Ca
conditions, following reduced-order strategies such as those proposed in Ref.
51
, and extending them to account for interfacial viscosity.
Another promising direction concerns the link between interfacial viscosity and surfactant-laden interfaces. Surface viscous stresses are often associated with the presence of surfactant monolayers
40
;
29
;
55
;
23
, suggesting that the EMM framework could be used to extend earlier studies
6
and infer droplet deformation statistics in complex flows with surfactants. This would require coupling the flow dynamics to surfactant transport and to an equation of state relating surface concentration to interfacial viscosities
40
;
31
. Since surfactants also exhibit intrinsic interfacial dynamics
7
;
54
;
13
;
45
;
58
;
57
, an open question is to what extent the MM framework can be further generalized to incorporate such surface heterogeneities while retaining a reduced description.
