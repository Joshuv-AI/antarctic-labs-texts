Tranexamic acid effectively reduces blood loss and transfusion requirements in musculoskeletal tumor surgery with acceptable safety: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hua Luo, Chunli Xu, Xiaoguang Xu, Lingqin Huang, Yu Yang, Junbo Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of tranexamic acid (TXA) in reducing perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements in musculoskeletal tumor surgery.MethodsThe review process was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched to identify related studies comparing TXA with no TXA in musculoskeletal tumor surgery. Meta-analyses were performed using a random-effects model. Weighted mean differences (WMDs) were calculated for continuous outcomes, and pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used for dichotomous outcomes.ResultsThirteen studies were included. TXA significantly reduced total blood loss (WMD = −471.71 mL, P = 0.001), postoperative drainage within 48 h (WMD = −46.13 mL, P < 0.001), and both intraoperative (OR = 0.28, P = 0.001) and postoperative transfusion rates (OR = 0.28, P = 0.02). Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the overall transfusion analysis (I2 = 84.4%). Sensitivity analysis excluding this study confirmed that the reduction in transfusion rates remained significant (OR = 0.45, P < 0.001). TXA was not associated with a significant increase in the risk of venous thromboembolism (OR = 0.56, P = 0.352), wound infection (OR = 0.64, P = 0.467), or hematoma (OR = 0.38, P = 0.315).ConclusionTXA significantly reduces perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements in patients undergoing resection of musculoskeletal tumors of the extremities without evidence of an increased risk of major complications. Nevertheless, the available evidence is limited by the predominance of retrospective studies. Further high-quality, multicenter randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm the efficacy and safety in musculoskeletal oncology surgery.

Introduction

Extremity resections for malignant bone and soft-tissue tumors are among the most challenging procedures in musculoskeletal oncology, due to extensive surgical trauma, strict oncologic margin requirements, and complex reconstructive demands (1). Achieving adequate oncologic clearance while minimizing local recurrence often requires wide or even transarticular resections (2, 3). These procedures involve the removal of large tumor masses together with surrounding muscles, bones, and soft tissues and frequently require meticulous dissection or sacrifice of major vascular and neural structures. Consequently, intraoperative blood loss (IBL) can be substantial, ranging from 550 to 3,000 mL, with additional hidden blood loss occurring postoperatively (4). Large-volume blood transfusions are therefore often required to maintain hemodynamic stability. Excessive perioperative bleeding may also prolong operative time, increase postoperative swelling and pain, and delay functional recovery (5, 6).

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a synthetic lysine analog that inhibits fibrinolysis by preventing the binding of plasminogen to fibrin (7, 8). It is widely used in joint arthroplasty, spinal surgery, and trauma surgery, where it has consistently been shown to reduce perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements (9, 10, 11). However, its use in musculoskeletal oncology remains relatively cautious (12, 13, 14). Resection of malignant bone and soft-tissue tumors often involves extensive tissue dissection and highly vascular surgical fields, suggesting that TXA may provide substantial hemostatic benefits (15). Conversely, patients with malignancy frequently exhibit a hypercoagulable state, raising concerns about the potential risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) associated with antifibrinolytic therapy (16, 17).

Most published studies suggest that TXA reduces intraoperative blood loss (IBL), postoperative drainage, and transfusion rates. However, the available evidence remains inconsistent. Several studies have demonstrated significant reductions in blood loss without an increased risk of thromboembolic complications (12, 14, 18, 19, 20), whereas others found no significant benefit, suggesting that the effectiveness of TXA may vary according to the surgical setting (21, 22, 23, 24). Although most studies have not reported an increased risk of infection or VTE, Foster et al. observed a possible association between TXA use and a higher incidence of pulmonary embolism (PE) (25). Consequently, despite growing interest in TXA, high-quality evidence regarding its efficacy and safety in musculoskeletal tumor resection remains limited. Therefore, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (26) and reported following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement (27). The protocol for this meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO (Registration No: CRD420251120141).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: i) patients undergoing surgical resections of musculoskeletal tumors of the extremities (including pelvis); ii) both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and nonrandomized controlled trials (non-RCTs); iii) administration of TXA compared with no TXA use; and iv) at least one relevant clinical outcome reported, including perioperative blood loss, transfusion, postoperative drainage volume, hemoglobin or hematocrit reduction, complications, or length of hospital stay.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: i) studies without a control group; ii) studies enrolling fewer than 10 patients; iii) studies with incomplete or insufficient data; and iv) studies without accessible full text.

Information sources

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify studies published up to July 7, 2025. The search strategy combined terms related to ‘tranexamic acid’, ‘bone tumor’, and ‘soft tissue sarcoma’. The reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. No language restrictions were applied. The complete search strategy is provided in the Supplementary material (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article).

Study selection

After duplicate records were removed, two investigators independently screened all retrieved studies in two stages: title and abstract screening, followed by full-text eligibility assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third investigator.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently extracted data using a standardized form. The extracted information included the first author, publication year, study design, sample size, intervention details, and outcome measures. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were perioperative blood loss, including IBL, estimated blood loss (EBL), and total blood loss (TBL); postoperative drainage volume; reductions in Hb and Hct; and transfusion rate. The secondary outcomes included VTE, postoperative wound complications (including infection and hematoma), and length of hospital stay. For each outcome, corresponding summary statistics, including means, standard deviations (SDs), frequencies, and percentages, were extracted.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The methodological quality of RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (28), which evaluates randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Non-RCTs were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) (29). Studies with NOS scores ≥7 (out of 9) were considered high quality. Risk-of-bias assessments were performed independently by two investigators, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Stata (version 17.0). Continuous outcomes were pooled using weighted mean differences (WMDs), whereas dichotomous outcomes were summarized as odds ratios (ORs), both with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and Q test. Considering the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, random-effects models were applied.

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for the outcomes to assess the robustness of pooled estimates.

Reporting bias assessment

When ≥ 10 studies were available for a specific outcome, publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. A P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of significant asymmetry.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach only for primary outcomes with data available from more than five included studies.

Results

A total of 626 records were identified across four databases, including PubMed (n = 62), Embase (n = 388), Web of Science (n = 144), and the Cochrane Library (n = 32). After removing 76 duplicates, 550 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 517 were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 33 studies were assessed for eligibility, and 20 were excluded due to insufficient outcome data (n = 11), inclusion of spinal tumors (n = 4), conference abstracts or letters (n = 3), secondary analyses (n = 1), or studies reporting only TXA-treated groups without controls (n = 1). Finally, 13 studies (3 RCTs and 10 non-RCTs) were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (12, 13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 30, 31). Blood loss

Six studies evaluated IBL (12, 14, 18, 19, 23, 24). Two studies lacked SDs and were therefore included only in the qualitative synthesis. The pooled analysis of the remaining four studies demonstrates no significant reduction in IBL with TXA (WMD = −149.18 mL, 95% CI: −606.69 to 308.32, I2 = 89.1%, P = 0.523; Fig. 2A). Four studies reported EBL, of which two lacked SDs (14, 30) and were excluded from quantitative synthesis. Pooled analysis of the remaining two studies showed no significant difference between groups (WMD = −38.47 mL, 95% CI: −410.99 to 334.05, I2 = 0%, P = 0.840; Fig. 2B). Four studies (18, 19, 20, 22) reported TBL, and pooled results demonstrated that TXA significantly reduced TBL (WMD = −471.71 mL, 95% CI: −671.24 to −272.19, I2 = 31.5%, P < 0.001; Fig. 2C).

Forest plots of perioperative blood loss outcomes (mL) (12, 18, 19, 23): (A) intraoperative blood loss (mL); (B) estimated blood loss (mL); (C) total blood loss (mL).

Postoperative drainage volume

Four studies reported 24 h postoperative drainage volume (12, 18, 19, 22), and no significant difference was observed between groups (WMD = 20.82 mL, 95% CI: −145.37 to 187.00, I2 = 75%, P = 0.806; Fig. 3A). However, for 48 h postoperative drainage (18, 19, 22), TXA use was associated with a significantly lower drainage volume compared with controls (WMD = −46.13 mL, 95% CI: −71.73 to −20.52, I2 = 0%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3B). Five studies reported total postoperative drainage volume. Two studies (Oyama et al. and Wiboonthanasarn et al.) lacked SDs and were excluded from the pooled analysis. Pooled analysis of the remaining three studies showed no significant difference between groups (WMD = 143.68 mL, 95% CI: −113.80 to 401.16, I2 = 67.0%, P = 0.274; Fig. 3C).

Forest plots of postoperative drainage volume (mL): (A) drainage volume within 24 h after surgery (mL) (12, 18, 19, 22); (B) drainage volume within 48 h after surgery (mL) (18, 19, 22); (C) total postoperative drainage volume (mL) (12, 18, 31).

Postoperative laboratory parameters

Four studies reported Hb reduction on postoperative day 1 (13, 14, 19, 24). One study lacked SDs and was therefore excluded from quantitative synthesis (13). The pooled analysis of the remaining three studies showed no significant difference between groups (WMD = −0.23 g/dL, 95% CI: −0.62 to 0.16, I2 = 0%, P = 0.243; Fig. 4A). Similarly, one study reporting Hct reduction could not be pooled because SDs were unavailable (13). Pooled analysis of the remaining two studies showed no significant difference in Hct reduction between groups (WMD = 0.35%, 95% CI: −2.53 to 3.23, I2 = 52.9%, P = 0.813; Fig. 4B).

Forest plots of postoperative laboratory parameters: (A) change in hemoglobin level on the first postoperative day (g/dL) (14, 19, 24); (B) change in hematocrit level on the first postoperative day (%) (19, 24).

Transfusion rate

Two studies (18, 30) reported intraoperative transfusion rates, and pooled analysis demonstrated that TXA significantly reduced the need for intraoperative transfusion (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.13–0.59, I2 = 0%, P = 0.001; Fig. 5A). Three studies (18, 23, 30) reported postoperative transfusion rates, and pooled results also showed a significant reduction in the TXA group (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.10–0.82, I2 = 66.8%, P = 0.02; Fig. 5B). Eight studies (13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 22, 25, 30) reported overall transfusion rates. Initial pooled analysis revealed no significant difference between groups (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.21–1.72, I2 = 84.4%, P = 0.337; Fig. 5C). However, sensitivity analysis identified the study of Foster et al. (25) as the primary source of heterogeneity. After excluding this study, TXA was associated with a significantly lower overall transfusion rate (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.29–0.70, I2 = 3.7%, P < 0.001; Fig. 5D).

Forest plots of transfusion rates (%): (A) intraoperative transfusion rate (%) (18, 30); (B) postoperative transfusion rate (%) (18, 23, 30); (C) overall transfusion rate (%) (13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 22, 25, 30); (D) sensitivity analysis of overall transfusion rate (%) (13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 22, 25, 30).

Length of hospital stay

Seven studies reported length of hospital stay (12, 13, 18, 19, 21, 22, 23). One study lacked SDs and was therefore excluded from quantitative synthesis (21). Pooled analysis indicated that TXA use was associated with a significantly shorter length of hospital (WMD = −1.09 days, 95% CI: −2.12 to −0.05, I2 = 75.5%, P = 0.04; Fig. 6A). However, sensitivity analysis indicated that this finding was not robust, as statistical significance was lost after sequential exclusion of individual studies (Fig. 6B).

Forest plots of length of hospital stay (days): (A) pooled analysis of hospital stay (days) (12, 13, 18, 19, 22, 23); (B) sensitivity analysis of hospital stay (days) (12, 13, 18, 19, 22, 23).

Complications

Seven studies reported postoperative complications (12, 13, 14, 19, 20, 22, 30). Pooled analysis showed no significant differences between the TXA and control groups in the incidence of VTE (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.16–1.90, I2 = 0%, P = 0.352; Fig. 7A). Similarly, TXA was not associated with an increased risk of wound infection (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.23–2.10, I2 = 0%, P = 0.516; Fig. 7B) or wound hematoma (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.06–2.54, I2 = 0%, P = 0.315; Fig. 7C).

Forest plots of postoperative complications (%): (A) venous thromboembolism (%) (13, 19, 20, 30); (B) wound infection (%) (13, 14, 19, 22); (C) hematoma formation (%) (12, 22).

Publication bias

As fewer than 10 studies were available for each outcome, formal assessment of publication bias was not performed.

GRADE analysis

As pooled primary outcomes included only a small number of studies, a formal certainty-of-evidence assessment using the GRADE approach was not performed, and the resulting certainty ratings would have been of limited interpretability.

Discussion

The present meta-analysis demonstrates that TXA significantly reduces total perioperative blood loss, 48 h postoperative drainage volume, and intraoperative, postoperative, and overall transfusion requirements in patients undergoing musculoskeletal tumor resection. These findings are consistent with previous studies and further support the effectiveness of TXA as a blood conservation strategy in musculoskeletal oncology surgery (20). Individual studies have likewise reported reductions in perioperative blood loss and transfusion following TXA administration, particularly in patients undergoing extensive tumor resections or prosthetic reconstruction (13, 14, 19). Collectively, the available evidence indicates that TXA provides clinically meaningful hemostatic benefits by reducing perioperative blood loss and the need for allogeneic transfusion. Because perioperative transfusion has been associated with increased costs, transfusion-related complications, and delayed postoperative recovery, these findings may have important clinical implications for perioperative blood management in musculoskeletal tumor surgery. Notably, TXA did not significantly reduce IBL or 24 h postoperative drainage volume in the pooled analyses. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because relatively few studies contributed data, and considerable differences existed in surgical complexity, baseline bleeding risk, tumor characteristics, and intraoperative hemostatic techniques. Such variability may have limited the ability to detect significant differences for these outcomes. Nevertheless, the consistent reductions in total blood loss and transfusion requirements suggest that the overall hemostatic effect of TXA remains clinically relevant.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the pooled analysis of overall transfusion rates. Sensitivity analysis identified the study by Foster et al. as the primary source of heterogeneity (25). Compared with the other included studies, the cohort contained a substantially higher proportion of patients with osseous tumors, larger tumor burdens, and pelvic resections, all of which are associated with greater blood loss and transfusion requirements. After the exclusion of this study, the pooled estimate became statistically significant with minimal residual heterogeneity, suggesting that differences in patient characteristics and surgical complexity largely accounted for the observed heterogeneity rather than inconsistent treatment effects.

An important finding of this study is that TXA was not associated with an increased risk of postoperative VTE, wound infection, or hematoma. This observation is consistent with previous clinical studies and meta-analyses evaluating TXA in orthopedic surgery (20, 32, 33). Experimental evidence has also suggested that TXA enhances antifibrinolytic activity without inducing complete fibrinolytic shutdown, thereby preserving physiological coagulation while limiting excessive fibrinolysis (20). Under standard thromboprophylaxis, such as low-molecular-weight heparin, TXA therefore appears unlikely to substantially increase thromboembolic risk.

However, Foster et al. (25) reported an increased incidence of PE within 90 days among patients receiving TXA (adjusted OR = 10.64, 95% CI: 2.23–50.86). This finding should be interpreted with caution. In that study, TXA was preferentially administered to patients with osseous tumors, larger tumor burdens, and pelvic resections, populations that inherently carry a higher baseline risk of cancer-associated thrombosis. Consequently, residual confounding may have persisted despite multivariable adjustment. Differences in institutional VTE surveillance protocols and the larger sample size of that study may also have contributed to the detection of rare thromboembolic events. Therefore, although current pooled evidence supports the overall safety of TXA, individualized risk assessment remains appropriate in patients with multiple thrombotic risk factors or those undergoing particularly complex oncologic procedures.

Overall, the available evidence indicates that TXA is an effective and generally safe adjunct for perioperative blood management during musculoskeletal tumor resection. Appropriate patient selection, routine thromboprophylaxis, and careful postoperative surveillance remain essential to maximize the benefits of TXA while minimizing potential thromboembolic complications.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, most included studies were retrospective single-center cohorts, and the three RCTs were relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power of several pooled analyses. Second, substantial clinical heterogeneity existed across studies with respect to tumor type, resection extent, reconstruction strategy, surgical complexity, TXA administration protocols, and perioperative transfusion practices, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Third, only a limited number of studies contributed data for several outcomes, including blood loss, postoperative drainage, hemoglobin and hematocrit reduction, wound infection, and hematoma. Consequently, these pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Based on the available evidence, tranexamic acid appears to reduce perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements in patients undergoing resection of malignant bone and soft-tissue tumors of the extremities, without a clear increase in the risk of postoperative complications. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because the current evidence is derived from a limited number of studies, most of which are retrospective and clinically heterogeneous. Well-designed, adequately powered, multicenter randomized controlled trials are required to further establish the efficacy and safety of TXA in musculoskeletal oncology surgery.
