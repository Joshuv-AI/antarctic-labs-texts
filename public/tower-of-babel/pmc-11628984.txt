Promoting Intensive Transitions for Children and Youth with Medical Complexity from Paediatric to Adult Care: the PITCare study—protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Sara Santos, Donna Thomson, Sanober Diaz, Joanna Soscia, Sherri Adams, Reshma Amin, Stacey Bernstein, Brenda Blais, Natasha Bruno, Kimberly Colapinto, Sherry Espin, Nora Fayed, Jon Greenaway, Megan Henze, Noah M Ivers, Karen LeGrow, Audrey Lim, Robyn Lippett, Yona Lunsky, Colin Macarthur, Sanjay Mahant, Sarah Malecki, Susan Miranda, Mahendranath Moharir, Myla E Moretti, Lacey Phillips, Paula Robeson, Monica Taryan, Kevin Thorpe, Alene Toulany, Eryn Vandepoele, Brenda Weitzner, Julia Orkin, Eyal Cohen

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildren with medical complexity (CMC) have chronic, intensive care needs managed by many healthcare practitioners. Medical advances have enabled CMC to survive well into adulthood. However, the availability of supports as CMC transition into the adult care system remain suboptimal, contributing to poor care coordination, and discontinuity. Promoting Intensive Transitions for Children and Youth with Medical Complexity from Paediatric to Adult Care (PITCare) aims to assess whether intensive patient and caregiver-oriented transition support beyond age 18 will improve continuity of care for CMC compared with usual care.Methods and analysisThis is a pragmatic superiority randomised controlled trial in a parallel group, two-arm design with an embedded qualitative component. CMC turning 17.5 years old will be recruited (n=154), along with their primary caregiver. Participants randomised to the intervention arm will be provided with access to a multidisciplinary transition team who will support patients and caregivers in care planning, integration with an adult primary care provider (PCP), adult subspecialty facilitation and facilitation of resource supports for 2 years. Outcomes will be measured at baseline, 12 and 24 months. The primary outcome measure is successful transfer completion, defined as continuous care in the 2 years after age 18 years old. Secondary outcomes include satisfaction with transitional care, self-management, care coordination, healthcare service use, caregiver fatigue, family distress, utility and cost-effectiveness. Qualitative interviews will be conducted to explore the experiences of patients, caregivers, the transition team, and healthcare providers with the PITCare intervention.Ethics and disseminationInstitutional approval was obtained from the Hospital for Sick Children Research Ethics Board. Our findings and resources will be shared with child health policymakers and transitions advocacy groups provincially, nationally, and internationally.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov, US National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health, #NCT06093386.Keywords: Adolescent, PAEDIATRICS, Randomized Controlled Trial, Caregivers, Protocols & guidelines

Introduction

Children and youth with medical complexity (CMC) have been defined as those with complex chronic conditions and/or neurologic impairment requiring specialised care, with substantial healthcare needs, functional limitations and high health resource utilisation.1 While they represent <1% of all children and youth, the impact of CMC on health systems is profound, accounting for 33% of total paediatric healthcare expenditures,2 57% of hospital care costs, 37% of hospital admissions, 54% of total hospital days3 and 43% of hospital deaths.4 Although the underlying conditions comprising CMC are diverse, they have common features including frequent reliance on technology assistance (eg, a feeding tube or tracheostomy), medical fragility, and extraordinary caregiver demands and stress.5

Medical, surgical and technological advances have enabled many more CMC with conditions that previously resulted in childhood mortality to survive into adulthood,6,11 necessitating a focus on transition of services to adult care.12 While the transition to adult care can be difficult for all youth with chronic diseases, this life stage is particularly challenging for CMC. Most transition programmes for chronic disease populations tend to be disease-specific (eg, diabetes, cystic fibrosis) with goals of increasing youth self-directed care and autonomy13 and decreasing parental involvement. CMC differ from those with other childhood-onset chronic conditions, in that their significant reliance on parents/caregivers for guidance, care and support is unlikely to decrease over time. This need is due to a high proportion of co-existing intellectual and/or neuromotor disabilities and frequent reliance on medical technology that requires constant caregiver assistance or supervision (eg, a feeding tube or respiratory support). Therefore, continued parental/caregiver involvement is essential in the transition process and into adulthood.

Whereas some youth with chronic diseases have a single corresponding adult specialist to transfer to adult care (eg, an adult endocrinologist for diabetes care), CMC require transfer of care to a variety of different adult (sub)specialists, in addition to transfer of primary care for those with a paediatrician. While CMC may have access to integrated and coordinated care in structured complex care programmes in some jurisdictions,14 few formalised multidisciplinary complex care programmes exist in adult care. Additionally, it can be extremely challenging to find adult care providers who will accept responsibility for patients with rare, complex diseases they are not familiar with and/or lead the coordination of the individual’s care.15 16 The difficulty in finding adequate providers subsequently leads to gaps and delays in CMC receiving medical care after age 18 years, putting them at risk of discontinuous transfer across multiple healthcare domains—such as in primary care, specialty care, as well as community and home care. This reality is further compounded by the challenges brought on by other critical life transitions that occur over this period, including social services and funding for care and medical technologies.

This study builds on foundational work by the study team and its knowledge users in developing a province-wide paediatric care coordination model for CMC in Ontario, Canada—Complex Care for Kids Ontario (CCKO). Promoting Intensive Transitions for Children and Youth with Medical Complexity from Paediatric to Adult Care (PITCare) will leverage the team’s experience in designing, evaluating and scaling the CCKO model to develop a transition team for CMC, led by a nurse practitioner (NP), a nurse, and a social worker (SW). This multidisciplinary team will partner with the patient and their caregiver to provide intensive transition support, focusing on (1) care planning, (2) integration with an adult primary care provider (PCP), (3) adult subspecialty facilitation and 4) facilitation of resource supports.

The overarching aim of this study is to assess the effectiveness of the PITCare intervention, namely intensive transition support delivered by a transition team, on outcomes among CMC transitioning to adult care compared with usual care.

Methods and analysis

Design

The study design will be a pragmatic superiority randomised controlled trial (RCT) in a parallel group, 1:1, two-arm design with an embedded qualitative component (figure 1). Pragmatic randomised trials seek to answer the question ‘Does this intervention work under usual (rather than ideal) conditions?’. The intervention will allow some pragmatism in trial eligibility, delivery, adherence and analysis and will incorporate local factors (see online supplemental appendix 1 for the trial’s Pragmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary-2).17 We will also be measuring outcomes that are important to patients, providers and healthcare decision-makers.18 This protocol has been designed following the Standard Protocol Items for Randomized Trials reporting guidelines,19 and results will be reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines for pragmatic trials.20 The trial has been registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06093386).

Trial Design. CMC, children with medical complexity.

Setting

PITCare will be conducted at the Hospital for Sick Children, a tertiary care hospital located in Toronto, Ontario, Canada. The study will be nested within Ontario’s CCKO programme, which provides intensive case management for over 1000 CMC across Ontario, Canada’s most populous province. CMC will be recruited into this research study along with their primary caregiver. A caregiver will be defined as the person most responsible for providing care for the youth. It is anticipated that this will generally be the youth’s parent.

Participants

The target population includes patients 17 years and 6 months to 17 years and 9 months old who meet Ontario’s CMC definition (used since 2016 for CCKO),21 along with their primary caregiver. The CMC definition requires all of the following four main criteria (see also online supplemental appendix 2): (a) technology dependence and/or use of high-intensity care, (b) fragility (severe and/or life-threatening condition), (c) chronicity (expected to last at least 6 months) and (d) complexity (involvement of ≥5 healthcare practitioners/teams and healthcare services in ≥3 locations such as home, school and hospital)

Exclusion criteria are individuals who: (a) are expected to die within 2 years of recruitment (eg, those receiving active end-of-life care) or (b) do not have a stable primary caregiver (eg, those who reside in residential or long-term care facilities and those in foster care as the intervention is focused on a patient-caregiver dyad)

Patients (and, where relevant, caregivers) who cannot complete questionnaires in English will be unable to participate in the secondary analysis that involves patient-reported or caregiver-reported outcome measures and patient-reported or caregiver-reported experience measures that are written and validated in English. However, they will be given the opportunity to enrol and provide healthcare utilisation data via health card linkages at ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences) that will be used in both the primary and secondary analyses. To facilitate this, we will enrol and consent non-English speakers and conduct visits for intervention group participants using a translator.

Control arm

In keeping with a pragmatic-type trial design, the comparison group will be offered usual care for transitions delivered by their existing paediatric clinicians, which includes access to a recent toolkit and transition resource guide developed by Ontario’s Provincial Council of Maternal and Child Health (PCMCH) for CMC22 as a standard to use within their clinical programmes. These resources include information about preparation for transitions as well as available services to support CMC transitioning to adult care, but there will be no systematic follow-up by a paediatric provider to ensure its uptake beyond age 18 years old. In Ontario, care is transferred to adult providers (primary care and specialists) at that age.

Intervention arm

The PITCare intervention will build on the success of the existing CCKO model and best practices for effective transitions (figure 2) and was developed with extensive input from patients, families, clinicians and policy-makers through webinars, focus groups and document review.

Conceptualisation of intervention and evaluation for PITCare.

PITCare is anchored in intensive transition support provided to a patient/caregiver dyad by a multidisciplinary transition navigation team made up of: (a) an NP with clinical experience in medical complexity and developmental disabilities, (b) a nurse with a registered nurse designation and (c) an SW with a Master’s in social work. This triad of healthcare professionals will offer a holistic and individualised approach to facilitate transition for CMC. All participants randomised to the intervention will receive transition support from the transition team for 2 years to support the multiple phases of CMC transitions including guidance, monitoring, planning, coordination, care transfer and transfer completion that optimises integration into adult health systems.22 The transition team will work with existing paediatric teams until care is transferred, and subsequently, autonomously, to coordinate care, provide consultation,and enable providers across the continuum of care to assume collaborative care management of CMC. In active partnership with youth, families and providers, the transition team will lead the refinement and maintenance of complex care plans developed in paediatric programmes and tailor them to include transition milestones. The transition team will support information sharing and ongoing integration into the adult health and social services system. This process will promote collaborative care management among providers and help build the youth and family’s confidence and capacity.

Online supplemental appendix 3 details the visit schedule, with the goals and outcomes to be completed by the transition team over the 2-year intervention period and materials used in the delivery of the intervention. Visits will occur in person or virtually, depending on participant preference. A Family Strengths-Oriented Therapeutic Conversation approach, complemented by solution-focused interventions, will be integrated into the visits with youth and their caregivers to help provide support and reduce stress.23 The transition team will also promote information sharing with other non-medical professionals central to the youth’s circle of care (such as teachers or home care providers). PITCare will incorporate best practices in the care of CMC that have been highlighted in the toolkit and resource guide developed by PCMCH.22 Youth and family will be provided an opportunity to co-design wrap-around care services and co-contribute to ongoing refinements in their medical care plan based on changing needs and concerns. Outcomes

Study outcomes have been designed to align with key existing frameworks, including: (a) the Ontario Health (OH) Transitions From Youth to Adult Health Care Services Quality Standard, which includes six quality statements and was developed in partnership with PCMCH;24 (b) the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) framework which outlines four elements for optimal health system performance;25 and (c) the National Institute for Health Care Excellence’s (NICE) specific transition quality standard for the UK (see online supplemental appendix 5).26 Specific outcomes and their measures are outlined in table 2 and were selected based on: (1) relevance based on input from patient advisors and clinicians; (2) where possible, continuity of the measure/indicator endorsed by caregiver-provider consensus from previous work;18 (3) CMC-specific and published psychometrics; and (4) feasibility (eg, time/comprehension). The study outcomes have been adapted for PITCare to focus on transition outcomes that can be measured by either the patient or their caregiver proxies, as many typical patient-related outcome measures are poorly suited for the many CMC with neurodisabilities.

Primary outcome

There is no consensus in the literature for selecting a primary outcome on transition to adult care for complex paediatric populations. While a primary outcome has been chosen, interpretation of the PITCare study results will consider not only the primary outcome of successful transfer but also a range of other indicators that are believed to reflect various aspects of a successful transition.

Continuity of care after transfer (modified Bice-Boxerman Index (BBI))

Transfer completion has been prioritised as a core quality domain within the OH Quality Standard for Transition from Youth to Adult Healthcare Services24 and can be readily measured with administrative databases, with minimal missing data.

We will define successful transfer completion as the successful transfer of primary and prioritised subspecialty care to continuous care from a primary care and prioritised subspecialty provider in adult care. Transfer to continuous primary and subspecialty care has been chosen as the primary outcome based on the research question and the main activity goals of the intervention (integration with an adult PCP and adult subspecialty facilitation).27 A review of the literature was conducted, followed by consultation with stakeholders including patients, families and clinicians who have been involved in transition for CMC to determine the outcome measures commonly used in this patient population and to receive input on which were most relevant and meaningful.28

Transfer completion will be assessed using a modification of the Bice-Boxerman Index (BBI), a relational continuity of care (COC) measure commonly used to quantify concentration of care across providers.29 Among CMC, continuity of primary care, calculated using the BBI, has been found to be associated with decreased emergency department utilisation and receipt of better care coordination.30 The BBI has been widely used in populations with chronic illness and high service needs to determine primary and specialty care continuity.30,34 The original COC formula from Bice and Boxerman35 has been modified by Jones et al31 and will be used in our study population to calculate an aggregated score representing continuity in both primary care and a prioritised subspecialty provider in adult care. Details of the derivation of the modified BBI is described in online supplemental appendix 7.

Using the modified BBI, we will capture the combined COC index score for primary care and a prioritised subspecialty (total number of specialties=2) for patients with at least two visits with both primary care and the prioritised subspecialty (total number of visits ≥4) over a 24-month study period beginning at age 18 years old (age 18 years old to 20 years old). The rationale for beginning at age 18 years old is that the patient is likely to still be seeing paediatric specialists before that age. We have decided to extend the outcome measurement to age 20 years even though the intervention ends at 24 months post-enrolment (approximately age 19.5 years old) to allow for enough time for participants to connect with a stable adult provider and to have sufficient data to measure an accurate BBI.

The prioritised subspecialty included in the calculation will be the most important subspecialty service identified for transition by the patient and caregiver at baseline. Primary care will be defined as visits to primary care physicians, including family physicians, general practitioners and specialists such as internists who provide primary care. Although larger visit thresholds have been used to calculate the BBI, an advantage to our method is that the requirement for at least two visits per specialty will minimise missing data, as previous work by our team has shown that youth with neurologic impairment and technology dependence in Ontario have a median of two PCP visits over the post-transition to adult care period (age 18 to <20 years old).36 Additionally, studies in similar populations using visit requirements of four or greater have excluded over one-third of study participants.30 33 37 If the patient does not achieve the minimum number of two visits required to either a prioritised subspecialist or a PCP at the end of the 2-year outcome period, the grouping of provider (primary care or subspecialty) for which greater than or equal to two visits were achieved will be included in the calculation. If neither specialty contains at least two visits, a score of zero will be assigned, indicating no COC.

Satisfaction with transition healthcare

We will measure satisfaction with transition healthcare services using the Larsen Client Satisfaction Questionnaire. While the eight-item scale is typically used to assess generic satisfaction,41 we have adapted it for this context to suit the needs of transitioning youth and their caregivers (see online supplemental appendix 8).

Self-management (patient and family activation/empowerment)

The Patient Activation Measure and Family Empowerment Scale will assess patient knowledge and confidence in managing their health42 43 and family empowerment status,44 respectively.

Care coordination

We will assess specific indicators from the Family Experiences with Coordination of Care (FECC) survey including: Utility of Care Planning Tools, Written Transition Plan Assessment, Coordination of Care Among Providers and Families, and Coordination of Care Between Providers and Families.45 While the FECC was not designed specifically for transitions to adult care, there is no other psychometrically sound measure that holistically captures all elements of transition to adult care (planning, transfer and integration) or that is valid for use in CMC.

Collaborative transitions meeting

The presence of a joint transitions meeting between paediatric providers and key adult healthcare providers with the youth and/or caregiver present will be assessed via caregiver report. These collaborative visits can help maintain COC and improve attendance at appointments with adult providers.24

Receipt of adult-oriented funding supports

The Ontario Disability Support Program and Developmental Services Ontario’s Passport Program are the primary means of financial aid for adults with disabilities. As outlined by the Transition to Adult Care Toolkit, while preparation for funding applications may begin earlier, applications are not made until 16 and 17.5 years old, and receipt of funding begins at 18 years old.22

Time to transfer

Time to transfer has been identified as an objective measure of barriers in the transition from paediatric to adult care.46 We will evaluate whether the intervention reduces the time to attending the patient’s first adult subspecialist appointment.

Use of services

Emergency department use, low acuity emergency department visits (defined as Canadian Triage Acuity Scores of 4 or 5),47 hospitalisations, technological complications and immunisations will be captured using ICES data to determine healthcare and preventative care use.

Health-related quality of life

Caregiver fatigue will be measured using the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Fatigue Scale. The Fatigue scale is divided into the experience of fatigue (frequency, duration and intensity) and the impact of fatigue on physical, mental and social activities.48 Finally, the Brief Family Distress Scale, a 1-item, 10-point Likert scale will be used to measure degree of perceived distress in the family unit .49

Utility

Utility of the caregiver and youth (where able) will be assessed using a measure of health, the European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Level Version (EQ-5D-5L). The measure’s five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain, and anxiety and depression) generate a perceived health state, and the visual analogue scale rating from 0 to 100 provides a quantitative measure of health status.50

Cost-effectiveness

A probabilistic cost-utility analysis will be conducted using patient-level study data linked to administrative datasets (ICES) to determine the incremental cost per quality-adjusted life-year gained (QALY) of PITCare compared with usual care, from a health system perspective (eg, Ontario’s Ministry of Health). QALY gains for both patient and caregiver will be included. Utility, determined by mapping patient and caregiver responses to the EQ-5D, will be used to generate QALY gains (or losses) over the time horizon. Cost utility will be expressed as an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio, calculated by dividing the mean per patient incremental costs by the incremental QALYs.

Further description of these measures can be found in online supplemental appendix 9 along with all secondary outcome measures.

Qualitative interviews

Semistructured qualitative interviews will be conducted at study end with a subset (estimated n=15–20) of intervention participants, with healthcare providers (estimated n=15–20) including both pretransition and post-transition clinicians and with the transition team. Purposive sampling of participants will aim for maximum variation of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and will occur until thematic saturation. Translators will be used where required by participants. The interview guide will be informed by the domains of the OH Quality Standard, IHI Quadruple Aim and NICE Quality Standard. Identify factors that facilitated and/or hindered the overall transition process.

Implementation outcomes

The implementation process will be assessed by determining the fidelity of the intervention using percentages of completion for each of the four main intervention components: % of completed care plans, % of PCP visits scheduled and attended by the transition team, % of adult subspecialty visits scheduled and attended by the transition team, and % of eligible resource support applications completed and received (in addition to the caregiver-reported receipt of adult-oriented funding services highlighted in secondary outcomes). Qualitative interviews with the transition team and adult healthcare providers will also cover the domains of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research including innovation (eg, evidence-base, relative advantage, adaptability), outer setting (eg, attitudes, partnerships), inner setting (eg, infrastructure, workplace culture), individuals (eg, leaders, team members) and implementation process (eg, planning, tailoring strategies).51

Sample size

Improvements of 0.1–0.2 in the BBI have been shown to be associated with improved health outcomes. In a study of CMC, improvement in the BBI of 0.1 was associated with a 4% decreased risk of emergency department visits and improvements in family experiences with care coordination.30 Another population-based study of adults reported a beta coefficient of −0.18 (95% CI, −0.05 to −0.27) for the BBI association with ED visits after adjusting for a number of clinical and sociodemographic variables.52 Other studies in older adults have also reported associations between changes in the BBI of 0.1 with other relevant outcomes such as hospitalisation53 and decreased cost of care.34 Using this literature and input from clinical and scientific advisors, we assumed a minimal clinically important difference of 0.15 and an SD of 0.3. Reflecting a standardised difference of 0.5 in the modified BBI score, we would need 64 patients per group to have 80% power to detect a clinically important difference in the BBI, which we estimate to be 15%, using a 2-sided alpha of 0.05. From our CCKO recruitment, 6% of patients died over the 2-year study period, and 10% withdrew participation. We will aim to randomise 77 patients per group to ensure adequate power.

Recruitment

Staff at CCKO clinic sites, other hospital clinics and community providers will identify and provide study information to potential participants and complete a referral. Those who are deemed eligible based on independent review of the referral by two clinicians will be contacted by the study research assistant.

Randomisation

Patients will be randomised into the two groups at least 90 days before their 18th birthday using a random allocation sequence facilitated by an online platform—Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).54 55 Blocking will be used to ensure that the two comparison groups are about the same size throughout the trial. An allocation ratio of 1:1 with random block size will be used to ensure that clinicians, investigators, and outcome assessors will not decipher the block size. A computer-based pseudo-random number generator will be used to create treatment allocation tables. After patient eligibility has been confirmed, the site investigator will access an allocation table to assign a unique treatment number. Blinding of patients, their caregivers and clinicians is not feasible with this intervention. All analyses will be conducted by personnel blinded to treatment allocation. The biostatistician will maintain a secure master list of the randomisation codes, and the assigned groups will be checked against the master list at the end of the study.

Data collection

On obtaining consent (see online supplemental appendix 10: PITCare Participant Consent), the research assistant will assign a unique research subject ID number to participants. Data collection will be facilitated by research staff by telephone, online collection forms (sing REDCap) or via hard copy, depending on family preference. Flexible methods of data collection will maximise diversity of respondents and maintain the involvement of vulnerable groups (such as low transportation/internet access) in the trial. Semistructured qualitative interviews will take place in the manner that maximises convenience to the participants (telephone, secure virtual platform (eg, Microsoft Teams) or in-person). Qualitative interviews will be recorded, transcribed verbatim and anonymised.

Data management

A REDCap database will be designed and maintained for the study. All hard copy data collection forms will be uploaded onto REDCap and linked by a unique research subject ID number. An external user interface will also be created on REDCap for participants who opt to complete the surveys online. All personal identifying information will be removed from the electronic study database. A separate secure list of research subjects’ names and contact information will be maintained in an excel file for the purpose of the qualitative interviews. All study-related electronic data files will be password-protected and reside on the Hospital server.

De-identified, password-protected databases will be transferred to ICES for analyses using health administrative databases through a Virtual Private Network from participating sites.

Further, anonymisation of qualitative interview transcripts will remove identifying information of participants as appropriate. The information the interview participants provide will not be linked to the PROM data or ICES health utilisation data.

A data monitoring committee is not needed as there are no anticipated risks from participating. All control group patients will be receiving standard of care, and there are no anticipated adverse events expected from an intensive transition support intervention.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics

Participant characteristics and descriptive variables will be presented for each arm: age, ethnicity and sex of both caregiver and child, underlying diagnoses, duration of involvement in a structured paediatric complex care programme, medication use and technology use. For continuous variables, means and SD or medians (interquartile ranges) will be presented. For categorical variables, proportions will be presented.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome will be the modified BBI which will be multiplied by 100 to be expressed as a percentage, as has been reported in other studies using relational continuity scores.56 The modified BBIs will be calculated at baseline (from date of enrolment and backwards 2 years) and for the 2 years postrandomisation. The primary outcome will be the modified BBI score in the 2-year period beginning at age 18 years old (age 18 years old to 20 years old) compared between the intervention and control groups using linear regression, adjusting for referring clinic (complex care vs other) and baseline modified BBI scores. Outcomes will be reported as mean differences with 95% CIs. If the residuals substantially violate assumptions of normality, non-parametric testing will be conducted with bootstrapping methods to calculate 95% CIs. Hypothesis testing will be conducted based on a modified intention-to-treat principle, excluding only patients for whom a modified BBI cannot be calculated due to death or loss to follow-up due to out-migration. We will conduct a sensitivity analysis that will include a calculation of a modified BBI for patients with less than 2 years of follow-up data due to death or loss to follow-up (out-migration from Ontario). Subgroup analyses will also be conducted to explore any potential differences in outcomes by sex.

Secondary outcomes

Utility of Care Planning Tools, Written Transition Plan Assessment, Coordination of Care Among Health Providers and Families, and Coordination of Care Between Health Providers and Families will be measured with the FECC between the intervention and comparison groups at the end of year 2 (month 24 to approximately age 19.5 years). These will be compared with ordinal regression using a cumulative logit model adjusting for baseline values.

Secondary outcomes that are continuous variables (eg, health-related quality of life) will be presented as means with 95% CIs. Given there are multiple secondary outcomes, the main purpose of these analyses will be to estimate effect sizes. An analysis of covariance will be used with the corresponding baseline score as the covariate to compare groups. Dichotomous outcomes will be described as the absolute number and proportion. A Fisher’s exact test will be used to test for differences between the two groups. All treatment effects will also be presented as absolute and relative differences with 95% CIs. There are no interim analyses planned.

Qualitative analysis

An iterative content analysis approach will be used to analyse the transcribed interviews. This analytical process serves to identify codes and categories towards the development of a coding schema.57

Discussion

While systematic reviews of transition interventions generally report positive outcomes related to overall health, continuity or adherence to care, healthcare utilisation and satisfaction with care,58,61 the impact of interventions specifically aimed at youth with childhood-onset disabilities and medical complexity is less clear.62 Our mixed-methods evaluation and unique primary outcome will provide a deeper understanding of the effectiveness of transition interventions aimed at CMC and their caregivers.

The PITCare study design incorporates several innovative aspects. First, the intervention is governed based on a structure that has been built on a strong commitment to patient and family partnership. The trial is overseen by an executive committee composed of clinicians, policy-makers and caregivers of youth who have transitioned to adult care. Stakeholder engagement throughout the study design and development phase ensured feedback on the barriers and facilitators to implementing the PITCare intervention, areas of priority, transition team training targets and resources, and essential elements of each major intervention component.

Second, we have designed PITCare to align with the current best practices for clinical care coordination and care for CMC transitioning to adult care that builds on clinical trial research from our team.63,65 The construction of the transitions team consisting of a triad of a NP, nurse and SW is based on learning from previous research in both complex care and transitions literature on the composite needs of CMC patients during their transition to adult care.

Third, this intervention is being implemented in Ontario, Canada, where delivery of transitional care may differ from other jurisdictions. For instance, somewhat unique to this intervention is its implementation after age 18 years, coinciding with the typical transfer of care age for most paediatric services in Canada.66 Preparation over multiple years prior to age 18 is helpful in preparing young adults with medical complexity and their families for transition,67 but is likely insufficient in and of itself. To our knowledge, this will be one of the first RCTs of an intervention designed to provide ongoing support with transitions even after formal transfer of care to adult healthcare providers. Additionally, in Canada, physicians can bill for outpatient joint appointments with two different care providers (eg, a paediatric and adult provider), an activity which may not be reimbursable in other jurisdictions.

Finally, we have developed a novel outcomes framework for evaluation. In review of the literature, no consensus primary outcome was found for transition to adult care for complex paediatric populations. The decision to use a novel administrative data source as the primary outcome was developed in consultation with patients, families and clinicians who have been involved in transition for CMC.28 While the BBI has been widely used in health service research, its use in clinical trials or CMC is not common31,3468 and may be limited as some discontinuous care within a health team (eg, a group-based practice) may be appropriate. We are hopeful it will serve as a feasible and focused objective endpoint to determine if the intervention influences CMC transition and eliminates the analytical challenges of missing data and administrating patient- or caregiver-reported measures, which have limited utility for those with minimal health literacy or non-English-speakers. Nonetheless, successful transfer may be hampered despite effective work by the transition team because of shortfalls in the adult system’s ability to meet the needs of this population (ie, staffing shortages, long waitlists, overcrowding). Although we are measuring many secondary outcomes to assess transition holistically, we have chosen transfer as the primary outcome as successful transition cannot occur without a completion of transfer of care. The downside of this choice for the primary outcome may be a lack of granularity, particularly in terms of the lived experience of transition which we will attempt to address through secondary outcome measures and qualitative interviews.

Patient and public involvement

PITCare was created based on priorities determined by patients, families and investigators to increase the likelihood of a transformative impact for CMC and their families. The trial includes a co-principal investigator who is a parent of a young adult with complex care needs and has an important governance role in the trial. We have also developed an advisory committee composed of 3 caregivers of CMC and a young adult with complex medical needs.

Ethics and dissemination

Informed consent will be obtained from all participants by the research staff. Capacity assessments will be completed by the patient’s provider according to Canadian research ethics standards,69 which will inform consent processes and patient self-reported data collection. Study data will be kept confidential by removing identifying information, and all study files will be maintained on a password-protected secure server. Any protocol modifications will be communicated to the Research Ethics Board for approval.

Adverse event reporting

While there are no adverse events expected in the context of the intervention being administered, any adverse events will be reported to the Research Ethics Board. All adverse events will also be reported within 24 hours.

Dissemination

We will integrate the PITCare care planning tools into adult healthcare as the PITCare intervention is time-limited to only 2 years. Resources such as a transition toolkit will be adapted from the study manual used to train the clinic staff involved in carrying out the intervention components and will be shared on a study website and for widespread posting by our knowledge users and mobilisation partners. To scale and spread the results of a high-quality trial of this scope and nature, we have partnered with key policy-makers and end-users provincially (Ontario’s Provincial Council for Maternal and Child Health and Ontario Health), nationally (Children’s Healthcare Canada) and internationally (Got Transition). All of these organisations have prioritised evidence-based transitions to adult care in their workplans, ensuring rapid knowledge mobilisation and uptake of study findings to effect policy change.

Trial status

Recruitment began November 2023 at the Hospital for Sick Children. The study is anticipated to finish recruitment by November 2025 and data collection by November 2027. Full-length protocol is available on request.
