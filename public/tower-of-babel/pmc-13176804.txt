Climatic Legacies Drive Spatial Aggregation of Plants in Drylands
Authors: Benoît Pichon, Sonia Kéfi, Isabelle Gounand, Sophie Donnet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn drylands, vegetation is spatially aggregated in patches separated by bare soil areas. So far, research on vegetation spatial patterns mainly focused on the effect of current climates and herbivory pressure. Yet, past climates might leave traces (called legacies) that affect present‐day spatial vegetation patterns. Here, we joined two datasets on plant spatial patterns and information about past climates during the mid‐Holocene and the last glacial maximum to investigate the effects of past and current climates on plant spatial patterns. We show that past climates have effects of similar magnitude as current climate on the vegetation cover, but have a remarkably stronger imprint on the spatial aggregation of vegetation in drylands. Furthermore, we found that climate legacies on plant spatial patterns might relate to climate long‐lasting effects on soil multifunctionality and sand content. Climate legacies detected here emphasize the contrasting time scales of processes involved in vegetation patterning, which we should account for when assessing dryland functioning and resilience from these patterns. This study opens perspectives to assess legacies from human activities and past climate on ecosystems.Keywords: drylands, facilitation, legacies, past climates, soil functioning, spatial patterns

Introduction

The past can leave traces that even time struggles to remove. These traces, known as “legacy effects,” are the remnants of past events, whether objects or processes, that continue to shape present‐day ecosystem properties (Doughty et al. 2013; Albertson et al. 2022). In fact, ecological processes unfold over different timescales, from seconds, decades, centuries to even more (Hastings 2010). This means that events from the past can have lasting effects.

These legacy effects can be due to organisms that shape their local environment by niche construction (sensu Odling‐Smee et al. 1996; Albertson et al. 2022), and whose effects last long after the death of the organisms (e.g., carcasses or leaves fueling benthic areas; Saldaña et al. 2024). Legacy effects can also result from past pastoralism activity promoting nutrient hotspots due to herbivores' nutrient excretion and egestion, which can last centuries after herbivory has stopped (Marshall et al. 2018). Past pastoralism has been shown to affect present‐day vegetation dynamics in drylands for example (Vicente‐Serrano et al. 2012). Beyond grazing pressure, legacy effects can also be due to past climatic conditions, which can impact ecosystems (Fordham et al. 2020). A single drought or storm often has lasting imprints on ecosystem dynamics for a few weeks to years as shown in forests (Kannenberg et al. 2020; Van Passel et al. 2022), grasslands (Sun et al. 2022), or lakes (Stockwell et al. 2020). The importance of climate legacies has been well acknowledged in different multiple contexts including on soil composition (Delgado‐Baquerizo et al. 2017) and ecosystem functioning (Ye et al. 2019). Recently, past climate conditions were found to be the main drivers of present‐day tree β−diversity (i.e., diversity in the spatial distribution of species) across the world (Xu et al. 2023). Some ecosystems such as drylands, mussel‐beds, or salt marshes have a characteristic spatial structure that strongly deviates from randomness (Rietkerk 2004; Zhao et al. 2021; Temmink et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). This patchy nonrandom spatial distribution of organisms emerges from biotic interactions between individuals, independently of the spatial heterogeneity of resources across the landscape (Aguiar and Sala 1999; Kéfi et al. 2024). Specifically, this spatial structure emerges from feedbacks generated by the interaction between organism–environment facilitation and competition, which is therefore referred to as “emergent” (Rietkerk et al. 2002).

Spatial vegetation patterns have been especially well studied in dryland ecosystems, which cover nearly half of Earth's terrestrial surface (45%), and are currently expanding due to aridification of lands and vegetation‐climate feedbacks (Huang et al. 2016; Koppa et al. 2024). In drylands, this spatial structure involves feedbacks with the soil, whereby higher spatial aggregation of vegetation is associated with higher soil fertility, which in turn enhances vegetation growth (D'Odorico et al. 2007; Berdugo et al. 2017; Eldridge et al. 2024). In addition, plant spatial patterns provide ecosystem services by limiting water runoff and erosion from wind (Han, Zhang, et al. 2023; Song et al. 2024), concentrating nutrients and water lost from bare areas, and enhancing overall ecosystem functioning (Tongway and Ludwig 1994; Bautista et al. 2007; Berdugo et al. 2017; Mayor et al. 2013). Recently, spatial vegetation patterns have been proposed as a mechanism of dryland resilience (Kéfi et al. 2024), whereby plant spatial aggregation might allow the vegetation to resist degradation (Rietkerk et al. 2021). Understanding plant spatial patterns and how climatic conditions and grazing pressure affect those patterns is essential to implement more effective restoration of desertified dryland sites (see Silliman et al. 2024 for an example of the use of vegetation patches for desert restoration).

Studies have investigated the drivers of vegetation spatial structure, and how these changes in spatial patterns affect dryland functioning and resilience. Particularly, there have been many local and global studies documenting how spatial patterns change along climatic gradients (Eby et al. 2017; Staver et al. 2019) or local consumer gradients in both theory (Schneider and Kéfi 2016; Ge and Liu 2021) and data (Adler et al. 2001; Pichon, Kéfi, et al. 2025). However, it remains unknown if and how past climatic conditions shape present‐day spatial patterning of organisms in ecosystems. Here, we embrace the question of climatic legacy effects on vegetation spatial patterning in drylands, where the arrow of time might be particularly slow and climate legacies strong. Three main reasons, outlined below, make drylands susceptible to legacies from the past.

First, perennial plant species in drylands are slow and can live decades to centuries (Ward 2009). Even when plants die, some can still facilitate other species by improving their local environment (Stavi et al. 2021), ultimately contributing to this patchy vegetation spatial structure. Consequently, apart from cases of fire‐driven plant–encroachment (D'Odorico et al. 2012) that can change vegetation composition and patterns, plant spatial patterns hardly change over time as well‐illustrated by photographs (Bestelmeyer et al. 2018; Skikne et al. 2024) or aerial images (Valentin and d'Herbès 1999; Barbier 2006; Deblauwe et al. 2012) taken decades apart.

Second, the effect of past changes of climate on plant spatial patterns might be driven by both soil composition and functioning and by past sorting processes such as the selection of facilitating‐tolerant species, which we detail below. In drylands, climate legacies have been associated to present‐day soil carbon contents (Delgado‐Baquerizo et al. 2017) and functioning (Ye et al. 2019) on the one hand. On the other hand, larger vegetation patches maintain higher soil nutrient and carbon cycling (Aguiar and Sala 1999; Tongway and Ludwig 1994) and have been linked to sand content (Berdugo et al. 2019). Berdugo et al. (2017) found, for example, a positive association between the level of soil functioning and the self‐organization of the vegetation in drylands. Vegetation patterns rely on mutual positive feedbacks between soil functioning (nutrient/water provider) and vegetation aggregation (concentration of organic matter and fertility) that ultimately enhances plant recruitment in the neighborhood of vegetation patches, while disfavoring it in unvegetated patches submitted to soil erosion. We therefore expect that climate legacies can indirectly affect present‐day spatial patterns if they durably influence soil composition and functioning.

Third, because present‐day plant communities are the outcome of past sorting processes, current plant strategies can be influenced by past climatic conditions (Herrera 1992). The emergence of plant spatial patterns is due to the presence of nurse species, which are usually stress‐tolerant species involved in facilitative interactions with other plants (Callaway 2007). The presence of those nurse species reflects coexistence mechanisms but also past sorting processes due to changes in climatic conditions, as exemplified by the coexistence of Tertiary and Quaternary plant lineages in Mediterranean areas (Valiente‐Banuet and Verdú 2013). Quaternary plant species diversified under arid climatic conditions and thus evolved drought‐tolerant strategies including xerophytic traits such as small specific leaf area, deciduous leaves, and small seeds (Navarro‐Cano et al. 2016). Interestingly, Quaternary species have been shown to have facilitated the persistence of Tertiary mesophytic species during the transition from the Tertiary wet conditions to the Quaternary dry ones (Valiente‐Banuet et al. 2006; Valiente‐Banuet and Verdú 2007). By shaping trait syndromes in drylands, climate legacies may affect present‐day spatial vegetation patterns. Furthermore, under the stress‐gradient hypothesis (Bertness and Callaway 1994), we expect that change in climate conditions to warmer and drier climates would be associated with more spatial aggregation of the vegetation due to higher frequency of plant–plant positive interactions (Soliveres and Maestre 2014; Soliveres et al. 2015). Altogether, although many characteristics of drylands set the theater for climate legacies to affect vegetation spatial structure, the relative contributions of current and past climates to present‐day spatial vegetation patterns in drylands remain a void in the literature.

In this study, we aim at filling this gap by analyzing present‐day regular and irregular plant‐spatial patterns in drylands and understanding possible links with past climatic conditions (Figure 1). Specifically, we ask the following question: what are the contributions and possible mechanisms of past climates relative to current climates on present‐day plant spatial patterns? Understanding the effects of past climates on plant spatial patterns allows better understanding of the timescale of vegetation change in drylands, but can also inform about the main mechanisms by which future change in climate conditions might affect dryland structure and resilience in a warmer and drier world (Huang et al. 2016).

By crossing climatic conditions and biomes from the last maximum glaciation periods (LGM) 21,000 years ago, the mid‐Holocene 6000 years ago, and the present‐day climate conditions with contemporary plant spatial patterns (Figure 1), our results emphasize the strong and unique contribution of past climates on the spatial aggregation of plants. By investigating the correlations of changes in past climatic conditions, vegetation patterns, and soil multifunctionality and sand content, we bring some elements to discuss how climate legacies might have affected present‐day spatial patterns.

Material and Methods

Present‐Day Vegetation Images and Soil Data

The dataset corresponds to 115 sites in 13 different countries (Argentina, Botswana, Brasil, Chile, Ecuador, Iran, Kenya, Mexico, Morocco, Peru, Spain, Tunisia, USA). We provide the map of the site locations with aerial images showing some examples of vegetation patterns in Figure S1. These sites are the subset of 236 dryland sites sampled in the context of the BIOCOM European project (Maestre et al. 2012). At each site, three 50 m by 50 m high spatial resolution images (< 0.3 m) of the landscape were taken on homogeneous areas and without human building or roads. Because most spatial patterns in this dataset are irregular, meaning that they do not show a characteristic patch‐size, the high resolution is important to allow identifying all vegetation patches independently of their size. To avoid biases from image analysis, the transformation of the satellite images into presence–absence of vegetation has been performed such that the vegetation cover of the image (i.e., the proportion of black pixels) matches the vegetation cover measured in the field (Berdugo et al. 2017), thereby reducing the number of sites from 236 to 115. The dataset is publicly available in Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.7786771).

In addition to ecosystem images, this dataset contains field data about soil composition, namely the sand content that was sampled under the canopy of the dominant perennial vegetation at 0–7.5 cm depth, and the multifunctionality, an aggregated index of soil variables associated with carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorous cycling and storage (Table S1 for the list of variables considered). This multifunctionality index is computed as the average of the soil function variables, each one being individually centered and reduced prior to averaging. A high value of multifunctionality means high values of many of the soil variables considered (but not necessarily all), and therefore a higher “functioning” of the dryland site in terms of recycling and storage of carbon and nutrients.

We also studied a second dataset that focuses on regular (Turing‐like) vegetation spatial patterns from 40 sites in the Sahel, which we only refer to in Data S1 since we find similar results to the first dataset and we do not have information on soil functioning data for this one.

Characterizing Vegetation Spatial Structure

Vegetation spatial structure has been shown to be a good indicator of land degradation and resilience in drylands (Kéfi et al. 2011; Eby et al. 2017). To characterize this spatial distribution of the vegetation, we computed different spatial statistics on each binary image (Table 1). We measured some spatial statistics on the patch‐size distribution, which reflects how vegetation is organized into large or small patches, making it a good indicator of land degradation and resilience in drylands (Kéfi, Rietkerk, Alados, et al. 2007; Scanlon et al. 2007). Those spatial statistics include the mean patch size, the area occupied by the largest patch, the coefficient of variation of patch sizes (the variance divided by the mean), and the number of smallest patches (the lowest size possible being 1: a pixel). Spatial vegetation patterns have also been linked to erosion and the runoff of water and nutrients across the landscape (Tongway and Ludwig 1994; Bautista et al. 2007). This dryland property is important since it creates a positive feedback that reinforces the loss of resources across the landscape, and can impair dryland resilience (Mayor et al. 2019). To capture the strength of those flows between vegetation patches, we computed the connectivity of bare soil areas using the flow‐length (Rodríguez et al. 2018). Finally, beyond patch‐size and spatial distribution, patches are characterized by a specific geometry (e.g., from bands to spots), which can change along climatic gradients (Pichon, Kéfi, et al. 2025). We therefore measured the fine‐scale patch geometry with both the spatial aggregation of the pixels (Moran's autocorrelation index) and the patch’ circularity, measured as the fraction between the area covered by the core pixels within a patch (i.e., not on the edges of the patch) and the total patch area. A high positive autocorrelation and circularity mean that vegetation pixels are more spatially aggregated into patches, and that such patches are more circular, respectively. These spatial statistics were computed using the functions patchdistr_sews and flowlength_sews from the spatialwarnings package (v3.0.3; Génin et al. 2018) Table 1.

To summarize our spatial statistics, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) on all spatial statistics. We applied a varimax rotation, which maximizes the correlation between the variables and the principal components to facilitate the interpretation of the two first axes. The first two axes explained 79.6% of the variance and were linked to mean patch size and the spatial aggregation of the vegetation, respectively. It is noteworthy that vegetation cover (i.e., the fraction of the image with vegetated pixels) is highly correlated with the first principal component (ρpearson=0.95), while the second axis captures the spatial autocorrelation of the vegetation, and therefore the level of spatial aggregation of pixels, thus of the plants, in the image.

Current and Past Climate, and Biomes

To characterize the role of past climates and their legacies on present‐day spatial patterns, we used three different periods for the climate data: the Last‐Glacial Maximum (LGM) 21,000 years ago, the mid‐Holocene period 6000 years ago, and the current period (Figure 1). We chose these periods because they capture the variability of climatic conditions experienced by plants the past thousands of years, and because climate and biome data were available for these periods on the area of many of the BIOCOM sites. The LGM period corresponds to the most recent period where ice‐sheets had their largest extent. This cold period contrasts with the mid‐Holocene, which corresponds to the warm period of the Holocene (−9.700 to present‐day). Note that we also performed analyses including climatic information about the last interglacial period (∼120,000–140,000 years before present), the medieval warm period (950–1250), and the Little Ice age (∼1350–1850) to complement the LGM and mid‐Holocene periods in predicting characteristics of plant spatial patterns. However, those climatic periods carried redundant information and explained a very limited fraction of plant spatial patterns (as detailed in Data S1). The results we present here therefore focus on the LGM and mid‐Holocene periods that have been widely used in ecological studies, for example to understand tree distribution (Benito Garzón et al. 2007) and dryland plant diversity (Liu et al. 2019). In our dataset, all (resp. 83%) of the sites experienced an increase in mean annual temperature since the LGM (resp. mid‐Holocene) and most of them (78% for the LGM period and 83% for the mid‐Holocene one) experienced lower mean annual precipitations now as compared to the past periods (Figure S9). For the sites considered, average temperature is 5.1°C higher than the LGM and 0.5°C higher than the mid‐Holocene.

We obtained 19 bioclimatic variables from current, mid‐Holocene, and LGM periods from the Worldclim database. These climatic data are derived from climatic models that have been validated using pollen data (Braconnot et al. 2007), and downscaled at the spatial resolution of 2.5°. Multiple climatic models are available for those periods; we therefore averaged climatic models for each bioclimatic variable. We computed the effect of past climates by taking the difference between current and past climatic variables. This way, a positive past climate value for mean annual temperature, for example, reflects that the temperature is higher now than it used to be in the past. We acknowledge that the way we computed past climate variables only captures the trend of change in climates compared to present‐day. As such, it does not account for fine‐climatic variations that may have been important in shaping plant spatial patterns over time too. We however refer to Data S1 to justify our choice.

Some of the climatic variables were highly correlated (e.g., mean and maximal temperature). We therefore selected a subset of all bioclimatic variables to avoid multicolinearity in the statistical analyses. We provide in Figure S8 the correlations between the different climatic variables retained.

In addition to climatic data, we obtained the current biome map from Olson et al. (2001) and the one from the last glacial maximum, 21,000 years ago from Ray and Adams (2001). The biomes complement past climatic data by providing the information about whether a given site was in a desert or nondesert biome during the last‐glacial maximum (Braconnot et al. 2007). Because the number and names of the biomes provided by the two maps were different, we grouped biomes in the LGM classification and extracted the current and past biome of each site according to Table S4 (following Pärtel et al. 2017). Then biomes were summarized into desert versus nondesert biomes using the classification given in Table S4.

It is noteworthy that we focus on past climate change and do not account for past grazing pressure since, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no data globally available with a spatial resolution fine enough to allow us to link it to plant spatial patterns (see Discussion). Yet, because climate and grazing are pressures that act at different spatial scales and in noninteractive ways on plant spatial patterns (Pichon, Kéfi, et al. 2025), we expect the results on changes in climatic conditions to be robust and not confounded with grazing legacies.

Analyzing the Role of Past Climate

Decomposing the Variance Between Climatic Periods

Using climatic variables from each period and the biomes, we first used a variance partitioning approach to identify the fraction of the variance of spatial vegetation patterns explained by the three different climatic periods, and their relative importance. This analysis relies on partial residual regressions to decipher the variance explained by a given group of climatic variables (from the LGM, the Holocene, or current periods) from the shared variance explained all climatic variables. This approach is therefore particularly suited to avoid multicollinearity of climatic variables across the three different periods. Importantly, variance partitioning allows deciphering the variance explained by a unique group of climatic variables from the shared variance among all climatic variables. We performed a separate variance partitioning for the two first axes of the PCA on spatial statistics. We also replicated a variance partitioning for each spatial statistic used in the PCA to investigate differences between spatial statistics. These analyses were performed using the var.part and ordistep functions from the vegan R‐package (Oksanen et al. 2007, v2.6–8).

Identifying the Most Important Climatic Variables

We next used gradient boosted tree modelling to identify the most important climatic variables (current and past) of each PCA axis. Gradient boosted trees is an algorithm that builds regression trees using different sets of predictors, including their interactions, and formulates a model that best fits the data. The use of gradient boosted trees complements the one of variance partitioning by identifying the most important predictors for each spatial statistic, but are usually more difficult to interpret. Predictor's importance is measured as the reduction in the model's accuracy when removing the given predictor, which is averaged over all trees to produce the final measure of importance. We parameterized the model with 500 trees, a minimal number of observations per node of four and a bag‐fraction of 0.8. Our dataset was divided into two sets: 70% for the training and the rest for the cross‐validation of the model (to avoid overfitting). We derived R2 of each model as the Pearson correlation between the observed spatial statistics values in the testing set and the ones predicted by the model. We used the gbm package in R to perform the RF algorithm (v2.2.2).

Decomposing the Role of Climate on Soil and Vegetation Patterns

To gain a mechanistic understanding of the effects of past climates, we fitted a path analysis with the two PCA axes of the spatial structure as response variables (Shipley 2009). This complements variance partitioning analyses since the interest is now to decompose direct and indirect effects of the different climatic periods (LGM, Holocene, and Current) on vegetation spatial patterns. As stated earlier, climatic legacies might indirectly influence plant spatial patterns because they are involved in feedbacks with the soil composition (e.g., sand content), erosion, and functioning (Delgado‐Baquerizo et al. 2017; Ye et al. 2019), which are known to be linked to plant spatial patterns (Berdugo et al. 2017, 2019). We detail tested links in Table S3. Because of the large number of bioclimatic variables (seven variables for each of the three climatic periods), we performed a PCA on bioclimatic variables for each climatic period to select a subset of those (Figure S7). Results

Climate Legacy Drives Plant Spatial Aggregation

When performing variance partitioning of the axes of the PCA summarizing the spatial statistics of the images, we found that current and past climatic variables explain about 50% of the first principal component (mean patch‐size and vegetation cover; Figure 2a) and 42% of the second principal component (spatial aggregation of the vegetation; Figure 2b). Past climate variables, defined as the difference of past climate from current climate, explain a unique part of the variance of the two axes of the PCA, not explained (i.e., complementary to) current climate (21 versus 15% for the first axis, 23 versus 7% for the second). The high proportion of the variance explained by past climates remains even after accounting for important predictors of plant spatial patterns such as field soil data of the sites (sand content and functioning), and some geographical characteristics (elevation and slope) (Figure S5). Past climates also explain a unique part of the variance of regular spatial patterns when analyzing the second dataset of sites in the Sahel (Data S1).

Each past climate explains more of the current‐day spatial aggregation of vegetation than current climatic conditions (Figure 2b; axis 2 of the PCA). By contrast, for axis 1 of the PCA that reflects vegetation cover, current climatic conditions explain the largest share of variance compared to past climates individually, although grouped shares of past climates surpass it (Figure 2 axis 1 of the PCA). Similar patterns were found when looking at spatial statistics individually (e.g., average or largest patch‐size versus Moran I spatial autocorrelation index or the number of smallest patches; Figure S3).

This pattern was confirmed using gradient boosted trees, where PCA axis correlated with vegetation cover was primarily driven by current climatic predictors (Mean Annual Precipitation during the driest period, and mean diurnal range of temperature), while the second PCA axis (aggregation of vegetation) was primarily supported by past climatic variables (e.g., change in Isothermality since the Holocene; Figure 3). Similar importance of past climates on the spatial aggregation of the vegetation was found for all individual statistics (Figure S4).

Altogether, we conclude that past climates from the Holocene and LGM periods account for a unique and large part of the explained variance of the vegetation spatial structure in current drylands, and that they are particularly important for the spatial aggregation of the vegetation (55 versus 44% of the total explained variance on the second PCA axis as compared to the first one).

Discussion

In this study, we integrated present‐day remote sensing imagery and soil field data from drylands together with past and current climatic data and biomes to investigate the effect of past climates on current vegetation spatial structure. We found that past climates (measured as the difference of climatic variables between current and past periods) explain a unique part of present‐day plant spatial patterns, complementary to current climatic conditions. Interestingly, past climates have a stronger imprint on the spatial aggregation of vegetation than on vegetation cover. In addition, we identified the different soil pathways through which past climates could have produced climate legacy effects on plant spatial patterning. We discuss our results in light of the timescale of response of drylands to climate variations and of current empirical literature.

Past Climates Have a Unique Contribution to Plant Spatial Patterns

Our study shows that changes of climatic conditions from LGM and mid‐Holocene periods explain a unique and important part of dryland vegetation's regular and irregular spatial patterns, in particular on the spatial aggregation of the vegetation. In drylands, perennial plant species are long‐lived and can live decades to a century (Tongway et al. 2001; Ward 2009). While plant spatial patterns have been shown to change over time due to changing climatic and grazing pressure (Barbier 2006; Deblauwe et al. 2012), these changes are generally slow due to the low mortality and recruitment rate of plants in drylands (see, for example, Skikne et al. 2024). Any exposure to new climatic conditions or grazing pressure can therefore leave traces for centuries or more in those ecosystems because the feedbacks between the vegetation, the soil, the climate, and grazing are inherently slow (Monger et al. 2015). This also has implications for measured dryland resilience. Current assessment of dryland resilience relies on the analysis of images of plant spatial patterns (Kéfi et al. 2014; Pichon, Donnet, et al. 2025). Because the dynamics of spatial patterns in drylands is inherently slow (Deblauwe et al. 2012) and might be influenced by long‐term changes in climate conditions, we argue toward a monitoring of drylands using both spatial and temporal data (e.g., timeseries of vegetation productivity) to detect signs of resilience loss in time series despite no apparent changes in the spatial structure of drylands (Veldhuis et al. 2022, Buxton et al. 2022).

In the following sections, we examine how present climate and its difference to past climates influence vegetation cover and spatial aggregation differently, as well as soil variables, to provide a holistic view on drylands structure and functioning across temporal scales that might help dryland monitoring.

Climate Legacies Drive Vegetation Spatial Aggregation

We noticed that the influence of past climates differs between spatial statistics reflecting vegetation cover versus spatial aggregation of the vegetation. Individually, past climates explain a larger fraction of the variance of spatial statistics related to the spatial aggregation of the vegetation compared to current climatic conditions, while we found the opposite for spatial statistics linked with vegetation cover.

Remotely sensed vegetation indices, which often correlate with vegetation cover and productivity (Prince 1991), have been shown to respond to present‐day climatic conditions (both average and variability), land‐use, and CO2 rising (e.g., Vicente‐Serrano et al. 2013; Burrell et al. 2020; Berdugo et al. 2020). In arid biomes especially, vegetation productivity exhibits a very short‐term response to water deficit due to adaptations of plants to exploit water when scarce (Vicente‐Serrano et al. 2012). While some experimental studies manipulating precipitation in grasslands have shown that climate events, such as droughts, can have lasting impacts on vegetation productivity a few years after droughts end (Broderick et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2022), vegetation productivity is mainly determined by short‐term climate conditions (months to a few years; Vicente‐Serrano et al. 2013), which our analyses on vegetation cover from dryland images confirm.

By contrast, spatial aggregation of the vegetation reflects more long‐term positive plant–plant interactions and the presence of facilitating nurse species (Aguiar and Sala 1999; Kéfi, Rietkerk, van Baalen, and Loreau 2007). Past changes in climate can select for trait syndromes specific to facilitating plants species. Mediterranean ecosystems are a good example of how aridification have selected for nurse syndromes (i.e., conservative traits such as low specific leaf area and C4) photosynthetic pathway, and high stature; (Butterfield and Briggs 2011; Valiente‐Banuet and Verdú 2013; Navarro‐Cano et al. 2021), which might be reflected in some of our results. With the warming from the Tertiary to the Quaternary period (from 2.58 Mya to now), some plant species evolved trait syndromes adapted to the warmer and drier conditions of the Quaternary period, and have been shown to strongly facilitate the persistence of Tertiary plant species that were adapted to colder conditions (Valiente‐Banuet et al. 2006). Given that most sites in our dataset have experienced warming and decreased precipitations since both the LGM and mid‐Holocene period (Figure S9), aridification might have selected specific drought‐tolerant and facilitating plant species. Using contemporary climates, Soliveres and Maestre (2014) found more frequent positive plant–plant interactions with higher aridity and temperature, congruently to the stress‐gradient hypothesis (Bertness and Callaway 1994; Soliveres et al. 2015). Given that facilitation among plants is one of the ingredient fostering spatial aggregation, this stress‐gradient hypothesis could explain why large increases in temperature between LGM and present‐day temperatures are associated with higher spatial aggregation of the vegetation.

Climate Legacy Effects on Soil Functioning

Beyond possible changes in plant traits—that we cannot validate here—the ability to combine field soil data with vegetation images allowed us to propose other possible mechanisms underlying the legacy effects of past climates on plant spatial patterns. We found strong effects of past desert biomes on both soil composition and functioning, whereby sites in a desert biome during the LGM had a lower multifunctionality and higher sand soil content (Ye et al. 2019). These climate legacies on the soil could have had indirect effects on vegetation spatial structure since high sand content creates soil with low water retention capacity, and thus accentuates aridity effects, to which vegetation may respond with higher spatial aggregation (Kéfi et al. 2024). This result is consistent with the analysis of Berdugo et al. (2019), which found an association between soil sand content and vegetation spatial patterns. We note that our analysis only considered surface sand content. However, this focus on a persistent soil property aligns with past studies suggesting that past climate legacies operate on soil composition through the long‐term development of soil physical properties and fixation rates (Sanderman 2018), which can consequently affect soil composition at all depths (Delgado‐Baquerizo et al. 2017). Besides, the positive relationship between soil multifunctionality and the spatial aggregation of plants emphasizes the positive feedbacks between the vegetation and the soil in drylands, which increase soil fertility and moisture and limit evaporation and soil erosion (Tongway and Ludwig 1994; Eldridge et al. 2024), together enhancing the local environment for plants to recruit (Aguiar and Sala 1999; Berdugo et al. 2017). This tight plant–soil coupling is allowed by, and produces, the patchy structure of drylands that redistributes resources from bare areas to patches (Aguiar and Sala 1999; Berdugo et al. 2017).

Some local studies have suggested that spatial vegetation patterns could be the outcome of medieval climates. For example, Weems and Monger (2012) showed how wind‐driven sand accumulation during the Medieval Warm Period created dunes, initiating vegetation bands that still persist today. Similarly, some plant species are the remnants of the cooler and more humid climate of the Little Ice age period (from 1500 to 1850; Monger et al. 2015). This emphasizes the importance of the interplay between soil composition, functioning, and the geomorphic processes as key drivers of plant spatial patterns, especially where sandy soils, bare ground, and strong winds co‐occur, which create a feedback mechanism on soil erosion that complements the water and nutrient runoff processes (Han, Zhao, et al. 2023; Song et al. 2024). In our analysis, we did not find a strong contribution of past Medieval periods on plant spatial patterns through changes in climate conditions because of the temporal proximity of climates between Medieval and present‐day periods (Data S1). However, beyond climate change that is the focus of this study, legacies from Medieval period can also be driven by past pastoralism activities, or through geomorphic pathways such as dune formation as emphasized by Weems and Monger (2012).

Perspective and Conclusion

Our study contributes to the growing literature aiming at understanding climate and grazing legacies on ecosystems. Our results suggest possible mechanisms contributing to the effects of past climate changes on current spatial patterns of the vegetation involving feedbacks through changes in soil sand content and multifunctionality. Other pathways could involve changes in plant syndromes as previously discussed. Our study is a first analysis, realized with currently available data, but we argue that future work combining phylogenetic information, identification and functional characterization of nurse species in the field, and past climates should allow going further in understanding those pathways.

In addition, past pastoralism may also have shaped present‐day spatial patterning of the vegetation, in combination with changing climates, which we could not test here. For now, however, currently available databases on past land‐use or human population density;1 such as HYDE (Klein Goldewijk et al. 2017) or ArchaeoGlobe (Stephens et al. 2019) are only available at a regional‐scale resolution (e.g., 85 km2 for HYDE), which limits our ability to link those past human activities such as pastoralism to small‐scale spatial patterning of the vegetation (50 × 50 m).

We live in a world where we, as humans, drastically shape our environment. This has not only implications for our present‐day impact on the livings, but also for the future because the marks of our activities will last for years. While our study focused on climatic legacies because of the availability of climatic data, the legacy effects of grazing—one of the main desertification drivers (Asner et al. 2004)—in drylands would also need to be considered. The few studies that have looked at the effect of grazing legacy were focused on ecosystem or soil functioning (Marshall et al. 2018; Ye et al. 2019; Saatkamp et al. 2021). Including the different drivers of vegetation spatial structure and the change of their effects in time would allow getting a finer understanding of the relative role of past changes in grazing pressure and climatic conditions, and present‐day ones on the structure and resilience of drylands. In addition, our results open avenues to combine temporal indicators of resilience (e.g., computed on satellite timeseries of vegetation productivity Runge et al. 2025) with present‐day images of plant spatial patterns to characterize different timescales of changes and resilience of drylands in a drier and warmer world. By combining vegetation images with past climatic data, our study sets the basis to better characterize the climate legacies on dryland spatial structure, and emphasizes the slow timescale of response of dryland dynamics to changing climatic conditions.
