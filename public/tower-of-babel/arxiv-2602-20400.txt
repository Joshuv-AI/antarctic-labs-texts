Three Concrete Challenges and Two Hopes for the Safety of Unsupervised Elicitation
Callum Canavan, Aditya Shrivastava, Allison Qi, Jonathan Michala, Fabien Roger

Abstract
To steer language models towards truthful outputs on tasks which are beyond human capability, previous work has suggested training models on easy tasks to steer them on harder ones (easy-to-hard generalization), or using unsupervised training algorithms to steer models with no external labels at all (unsupervised elicitation). Although techniques from both paradigms have been shown to improve model accuracy on a wide variety of tasks, we argue that the datasets used for these evaluations could cause overoptimistic evaluation results. Unlike many real-world datasets, they often (1) have no features with more salience than truthfulness, (2) have balanced training sets, and (3) contain only data points to which the model can give a well-defined answer. We construct datasets that lack each of these properties to stress-test a range of standard unsupervised elicitation and easy-to-hard generalization techniques. We find that no technique reliably performs well on any of these challenges. We also study ensembling and combining easy-to-hard and unsupervised techniques, and find they only partially mitigate performance degradation due to these challenges. We believe that overcoming these challenges should be a priority for future work on unsupervised elicitation.

Training of large language models (LLMs) often relies on labels created by humans for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and human feedback for reinforcement learning
(
14
;
40
;
3
;
34
;
21
)
. This can lead to models imitating mistakes a human would make
(
44
)
or generating text containing false claims that humans would rate highly
(
49
)
. This problem may get worse as LLMs are applied in more complex domains where human feedback becomes increasingly unreliable.

To address this, several approaches have been developed to elicit accurate outputs from a pretrained LLM without using direct human supervision. Easy-to-hard generalization (E2H)
(
23
;
41
;
6
)
involves training models on easy-to-verify tasks, with the hope that it generalizes in a way that improves performance on harder tasks. In contrast, unsupervised elicitation (UE) techniques do not use any ground-truth labels at all. For example, internal coherence maximization (ICM)
(
48
)
annotates a dataset without human labels by finding a set of true/false labels that are logically consistent and mutually predictable according to the LLM. Linear truth probes
(
27
;
30
)
can also be trained in an unsupervised way using Contrast-Consistent Search (CCS) or PCA to find directions along which contradictory pairs of statements are separated—a method introduced by
10
and investigated further by
51
;
28
.

Though each of these approaches has been found to perform well on a variety of datasets, it is unclear whether they will perform as well when applied to real tasks which are beyond human capabilities. In this work, we consider three challenges to the UE and E2H methods, create datasets in which these challenges are present, and evaluate existing methods on these datasets. These challenges are:

1.

Salient non-truth features.
Datasets may contain prevalent features that are more salient to the model than truthfulness and which humans are unable to detect.
18
demonstrated that CCS can fail by labeling statements based on the presence of such features (e.g. whether the statement agrees with a character described in the prompt). Here, we test a wider range of UE and E2H techniques on math problems with salient features added to each prompt, and on semantic classification datasets with multiple competing salient features.

2.

Imbalanced training sets.
Some UE methods require a set of training examples. However, since we do not know the ground truth labels of these examples, the distribution of labels might be imbalanced. For example, when eliciting models to identify malicious code, it is likely that most of the examples available will be benign. This might be a problem for UE methods like CCS which are designed for balanced training sets. We benchmark existing approaches on two tasks (labeling math solutions as true or false, and detecting malicious bash scripts) using training sets with varying degrees of class imbalance.

3.

Impossible tasks.
Some questions may be too difficult or ambiguous for the elicited LLM to answer decisively, such as fuzzy conceptual research questions that the model does not have enough knowledge to answer. Ideally, elicitation methods should assign confidence scores to predictions that help distinguish tasks the model can answer from those it cannot. We test whether existing UE and E2H approaches do this by applying them to a dataset consisting of both objective math problems and normative political statements, and measuring whether they achieve strong performance on math problems with high confidence scores while assigning lower confidence scores to the normative political statements.

We find that all of the existing UE and E2H approaches we evaluated perform significantly worse on our datasets than on datasets without these challenges.

We also study two hopes for improving on previous methods:

1.

Combination of UE and E2H.
UE methods find concepts in a dataset which are salient to a model, but these might not all be relevant to the task of interest. E2H methods steer a model on a specific easier task, but this might not generalize to the harder task of interest. It may be possible to combine the strengths of both methods, using UE to find salient concepts in the task dataset, and E2H to exclude spurious concepts.

2.

Ensembling.
Even if the most salient concept that UE finds does not correspond to the desired concept, it is plausible that if we use UE to find multiple salient concepts, one of them will correspond to the desired one. Ensembling different predictors obtained in an unsupervised way (described in
Section

3.3.3
) might allow us to leverage this.

Though we find that these sometimes perform better than other approaches at overcoming these challenges, they do not address them reliably. We hope our findings and datasets will guide future work toward developing UE and E2H methods that are robust to the challenges of real-world deployment.

Related Work

Steering LLMs on tasks beyond human capabilities.

LLMs post-trained with erroneous human supervision via SFT can degrade performance relative to base models
(
44
)
, and RLHF can lead to reward hacking
(
39
;
4
)
, and in particular LLMs which are more convincing to humans without being more accurate
(
49
)
.

Easy-to-hard generalization.

When supervision is of poor quality on the target dataset, one can hope for generalization from supervision on an easy dataset where high-quality supervision is available
(
23
)
.
Supervised truth probes
(
2
;
27
;
30
)
have also been shown to generalize from simple declarative statements to harder multiple-choice question answering
(
6
)
.

Unsupervised methods.

UE can also be used to train linear truth probes: the CCS algorithm
(
10
)
uses a loss function that is minimized when internal activations from logically contradictory statements are given confident, opposite predictions. More recently, it was shown that fine-tuning on labels generated by the ICM algorithm
(
48
)
(which aims to maximize the logical consistency and mutual predictability of labels according to an LLM’s next-token logprobs) is able to match SFT on golden labels for small training sets. Though previous work demonstrates high performance of these UE and E2H methods on standard benchmarks, we benchmark a variety of these methods on datasets we believe are more representative of challenges we are likely to face when steering models on tasks which are beyond human supervision.

Salient non-truth features.

CCS was found to outperform zero-shot performance on ten diverse NLP datasets.
18
showed salient non-truth features (such as agreement with a character’s preference given in the prompt, or the presence of extraneous tokens) can easily be preferred by CCS over correctness.
26
proposed clustering contrastive pairs of activations and normalizing these separately to mitigate the effect of such features. We study this challenge of salient non-truth features in more realistic datasets as well as other challenges and other hopes for overcoming them using not only unsupervised techniques but also E2H ones.

Imbalanced training sets and impossible tasks.

Previous work showed that issues with imbalanced training sets can be counteracted in a supervised setting with techniques like oversampling, upsampling, class weight adjustment
(
1
)
, or by generating adversarial out-of-distribution examples
(
50
)
, and that hallucinations on tasks LLMs are not capable enough to answer
(
24
)
can be partially mitigated with supervised learning
(
16
)
. To our knowledge there has been no work on steering LLMs on tasks with class imbalance or with impossible tasks in unsupervised settings.

Ensembling predictors and semi-supervised learning.

The two hopes we study are inspired by existing work. Ensembling has been studied in the context of reducing overfitting to spurious features
(
17
)
and improving generalization of truth probes
(
12
)
—which we extend by using it in the context of UE and E2H. Mixing UE and E2H is similar to semi-supervised learning, which involves training models with a supervised objective on a small set of labeled data and an unsupervised objective on a large set of unlabeled data
(
42
)
. Our hybrid methods are conceptually similar, but the supervised and unsupervised datasets differ by difficulty rather than size—and are probing-based to enable head-to-head comparisons with unsupervised probing methods.

3
Experimental Setup

3.1
Problem statement

In this work we compare different approaches to the challenge of poor human supervision. To simulate this scenario for a given task, we assume we have access to:

•

Examples for the given task without labels (the hard set)—techniques which only use those are UE techniques.

•

Examples for a significantly easier task with labels (the easy set)—techniques which only use those are E2H techniques.

•

A pre-trained LLM with no post-training.

During the training phase of each method we cannot use any information about what salient non-truth features are present in the training set, the distribution of ground truth labels in the training set, or whether each example in the training set has an objective truth value. However, we also evaluate ceilings which do have access to this information to gauge the difference in performance due to no supervision.

To evaluate the performance of UE and E2H techniques, we use the ground truth labels of a holdout test set, although this information would not be available for real tasks that are beyond human capability.

3.2
Datasets

We construct datasets for each of the three challenges described in
Section

1
. Each dataset is derived from an existing dataset and modified to introduce salient non-truth features, class imbalance, or examples that LLMs cannot answer decisively, in a way that is analogous to real situations where humans can’t provide a good supervision signal.

In this section we discuss the motivation behind the datasets for each challenge and describe how each individual dataset is structured. GSM8K
(
15

GSM8K (added salient features).

For each math question in GSM8K
(
15
)
, two of the solutions sampled from LLMs are provided, one correct and one incorrect. The task is to identify the correct solution, even when one of the two solutions (chosen at random) has been modified to exhibit a salient feature. The features used here are:

•

Sycophancy:
The prompt indicates that a character is in agreement with the chosen solution, similar to
18
.

•

Punctuation:
The chosen solution has additional exclamation points, question marks, and periods.

•

Tense:
The chosen solution is written in past or future tense instead of present tense.

Only one of the above features is added to the dataset during a given experiment.

LIAR.

Each statement (a comment taken from the PolitiFact website) has a factual correctness label and an uncorrelated political stance label
(
43
)
. The task is to classify each statement according to factual correctness or political stance depending on the prompt format.

Civil Comments.

For each comment taken from a news site
(
8

, a statement is constructed which asserts that the comment exhibits a specific (target) feature related to civility (e.g. toxicity). The task is to classify each statement as true or false, even when a different (competing) civility-related feature (e.g. sexual explicitness) is present in the dataset. This is repeated for each combination of target and competing feature, to see whether UE and E2H methods consistently favor one feature regardless of its correlation with the truth.

3.2.3
Impossible Tasks

Ideally, LLMs should give confident responses to questions within their capability while simultaneously giving low-confidence responses to questions which are not. We construct a dataset to test the ability of UE and E2H methods to elicit each type of response appropriately.

GSM8K + Political Ideologies.

Each example is either a mathematical solution to a GSM8K problem

3.3
Methods

All the methods we benchmark use an LLM to determine if a statement is true or false (without a Chain-of-Thought). We use Llama 3.1 8B for salient non-truth feature experiments and imbalanced GSM8K experiments, and Llama 3.1 70B for impossible task experiments
(
21
)
. For imbalanced Ctrl-Z experiments, we compare both models.

We study existing prompting methods, existing linear probe methods, and new probing methods based on our two hopes (ensembling and combining UE and E2H approaches).

3.3.1
Prompting methods

For data point
i
, we construct a zero-shot prompt
x_{i}
which ends with a statement/question (e.g. "Is the above sequence of commands suspicious?") to which a positive or negative token can be appended (e.g.
Yes
or
No
). Prompting methods take a zero-shot or few-shot prompt, use an LLM to infer next-token logprobs of positive and negative tokens, and assign a truth score equal to the difference of these.

Here are the prompting methods we study:

Zero-shot (baseline).

Use the base prompt
x_{i}

(
10
)
.

Random few-shot (baseline).

Use a few-shot prompt with randomly assigned (binary) labels
(
31
)
.

Bootstrapped few-shot.

Use a few-shot prompt with labels taken from the last of multiple iterations of few-shot prompting on the training set. At each iteration, the model’s predictions from the previous iteration are used in few-shot prompts to label new examples. Compared to few-shot prompting with ICM-generated labels
(
48
)
, this method was found to create labels with similar accuracy while being much less expensive to run
(
38
)
.

Golden few-shot (ceiling).

Use a few-shot prompt with ground-truth labels
(
9
)
. When some claims are normative, we use Golden few-shot with a mixed objectivity: we use a few-shot prompt with ground-truth labels for objective claims and
indeterminate
for normative claims.

3.3.2
Previous linear probe methods

For linear probe methods, each prompt
x_{i}
is converted into a contrastive pair of prompts
x^{+}_{i}
and
x^{-}_{i}
by appending the positive token (e.g. “Is [claim] true? Yes”) or negative token (e.g. “Is [claim] true? No”) to
x_{i}
, respectively. The last-token activations from a specific layer of the LLM are taken for each of these, giving a contrastive pair of activations
\phi^{+}_{i}
and
\phi^{-}_{i}
(we use the layer which gives optimal CCS performance for the unmodified GSM8K dataset). As in
10
, we normalize activations by subtracting the mean activation of each group:

\hat{\phi}_{i}^{+}=\phi^{+}_{i}-\mu^{+},\quad\hat{\phi}_{i}^{-}=\phi^{-}_{i}-\mu^{-}

(1)

where
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
are the averages of all positive-token and negative-token activations in the training set. The aim is for most of the difference between
\phi^{+}_{i}
and
\phi^{-}_{i}
after normalization to be due to the overall correctness of the claim—prior to normalization, most of the difference is likely due to which token (positive or negative) was appended.

The trained probe assigns a truth score equal to the difference between positive and negative activations projected onto the probe direction. For completely unsupervised methods, we also need to determine whether a positive or a negative score corresponds to a true statement. In these cases, similar to
10
, we choose the direction which maximizes AUROC.
1
1

1

We also do this for supervised probes trained on maximally imbalanced datasets (i.e. all examples are from one class only) for comparison with random probes.
Here are the existing probing methods we study:

Random probe (baseline).

Initialize by sampling weights uniformly at random from the unit sphere
(
28
)
.

CCS.

Train the probe by minimizing the CCS loss on a training set, as described in
10
. The CCS loss is an unsupervised loss function with a term for consistency (lowest when
\phi_{i}^{+}
and
\phi_{i}^{-}
are equal and opposite in the probe direction) and confidence (lowest when either
\phi_{i}^{+}
or
\phi_{i}^{-}
are very negative in the probe direction).

PCA.

Perform PCA on differences between contrastive activations
\phi_{i}^{+}-\phi_{i}^{-}
and use the first principal component value as the probe direction
(
10
;
28
)
.

E2H.

Train the probe on a labeled easy dataset (the
larger_than
dataset from
30
using supervised cross-entropy loss—we also tried using this combined with more datasets from
30
but preliminary experiments on GSM8K (added salient features) showed similar performance to just using
larger_than
. The hope is that it generalizes from the easy to the hard dataset.

Supervised probe (ceiling).

Train the probe with supervised cross-entropy loss on the training set
(
27
;
30
)
. When some claims are normative, we use supervised probes with a mixed objectivity: we train the probe with supervised cross-entropy loss but give normative claims a target output of
0.5
.

3.3.3
Probing methods relying on ensembling or combining UE and E2H

We study probing methods using the two hopes described in
Section

1
.

UE + E2H

(Combination hope). Train the probe using a weighted sum of CCS loss on the training set and supervised loss on an easy dataset.

Ensemble methods.

Take a weighted sum of scores from either
N
random probes or the first
N
PCA components (we used
N=128
or
N=256
). Weights are determined via either:

•

Consensus-weighting
(Ensembling hope): for
N
iterations, use one of the probes to label the train set. Assign it weight
1
if it agrees with the current ensemble for more than 50% of examples or
-1
otherwise (in order to prevent all probes canceling out), and add it to the ensemble.

•

E2H-weighting
(Combination hope + Ensembling hope): calculate the AUROC of each probe on the easy dataset minus
0.5
. Assign weights equal to the softmax of these values with temperature
0.1
.

We measure the performance of each type of probe with each type of weighting method. We found that scaling the probes so that they all gave scores of equal variance on the training set did not give an improvement (see
Appendix

C

4
Results

4.1
Salient Non-Truth Features

The accuracy of all methods degrades in the presence of highly salient features.

As shown in
Figure

2
, when the sycophancy feature is injected into GSM8K, the accuracies of most methods drop relative to the unmodified dataset by over 10 percentage points. Whereas when the punctuation feature is injected into GSM8K, the accuracies of most methods are within 5 percentage points of their accuracies on the unmodified dataset, and when the tense feature is injected, most are within 2 percentage points. We hypothesize that this is because sycophancy is a more salient feature to the LLM than punctuation or grammatical tense, making it more likely to be discovered by unsupervised methods over truthfulness.

Most methods label based on feature salience regardless of which feature the prompt asks for.

In
Figure

3
(a), we show that for the LIAR dataset, whenever the prompt format asks for a political stance label, all methods make predictions which agree more with the ground truth labels for political stance than factual correctness, as desired. However, as shown in
Figure

3
(b), when the prompt format instead asks for factual correctness labels, all methods aside from ensembles of PCA probes still make predictions more aligned with political leaning labels than factual correctness.

Similarly, in
Figure

3
(c) and
Figure

3
(d) we can see that for every UE and E2H method applied to Civil Comments aside from bootstrapped prompting, the generated labels align with ground truth toxicity labels approximately as much or more than sexual explicitness labels, even when the prompt asks for sexual explicitness labels.

From these results we hypothesize that political leaning and toxicity features are more salient than factual correctness and sexual explicitness, respectively, and that UE and E2H methods predict labels primarily based on feature salience.

4.2
Imbalanced Training Sets

UE methods can perform surprisingly well on imbalanced training sets.

As shown in
Figure

4
, when the GSM8K training set contains only incorrect mathematical solutions, CCS and PCA probes perform similarly or better than when it is balanced. This is surprising given that, when all training set claims are incorrect, removing information from activations about the final token in the prompt through normalization (
Equation

1
) should intuitively also remove information about truthfulness (since all incorrect claims appended with a positive token are false and all those with a negative token are true). When all training set claims are correct, CCS and PCA probes perform significantly worse than when the training set is balanced, as expected.

Most methods generalize poorly from safe to malicious bash commands.

In
Figure

5
we show the results for performance on distinguishing safe sequences of Ctrl-Z bash commands from malicious ones, for training sets comprising 50%, 99%, or 100% safe examples. No method exceeds 0.75 AUROC when the dataset contains 99% or 100% safe examples except for E2H, which is not impacted by the distribution of the training set (since it only uses a separate easy set) and achieves an AUROC of
0.87
when the LLM is Llama 3.1 70B. However, when the LLM is Llama 3.1 8B, this too performs poorly, with an AUROC of
0.67
despite an AUROC of
0.75
being possible when the dataset is balanced (e.g. using CCS).

5
Discussion

Limitations.

Our analysis covers a representative but not exhaustive range of UE and E2H methods. For example, we did not benchmark any methods which use SFT or any weak-to-strong generalization methods (which are based on confidence losses similar to unsupervised techniques studied here). Some of our datasets are not very realistic (for example, mixing GSM8K questions with normative political claims—the large, unrealistic distribution shift between both types of examples caused some methods to differentiate examples based on dataset of origin), though most of them are a significant realism improvement over those used by prior work stress-testing unsupervised methods
(
18
)
. There may also be additional mitigation strategies that could improve UE and E2H performance—for example, instead of naively training on a dataset assuming it is balanced, one could use a weak classifier (e.g. zero-shot prompting) to get rough training set labels and resample based on those (however this rebalancing approach could itself run into problems due to the other 2 challenges).

Other challenges and hopes.

Our work focuses on the three challenges that we think pose serious threats to unsupervised elicitation and two hopes that looked most promising, but we do not claim these are the only challenges or only hopes. For example, our work does not tackle issues with scheming AIs that might intentionally interfere with elicitation methods
(
11
;
22
)
, and it does not study other hopes like more sophisticated consistency properties than the ones used by CCS and bootstrapping.

The threat of silent unsupervised elicitation failures.

The evaluation of unsupervised elicitation methods is tricky, since by definition unsupervised elicitation methods are most useful when in-distribution ground truth labels are not available. Using only benchmarks like GSM8K or standard preference modeling datasets to validate methods would likely produce overoptimistic results if real deployments have challenges like the ones studied here. Thus, for applications like using UE to detect AIs breaking the law in ways humans would not notice (where in-distribution evaluations are not possible), the inadequacy of an unsupervised method may remain silent until the method fails catastrophically.

Recommendations for future work.

In order to mitigate the dangers of silent unsupervised elicitation failures, work into building better unsupervised elicitation methods should use evaluation datasets that capture challenges of the most important UE applications, for example by using the datasets presented in our work, or by using new datasets targeted at the main challenges UE methods are supposed to overcome.

6
Conclusion

We introduced three realistic challenges for UE and E2H approaches to training models on tasks beyond human capabilities (salient non-truth features, training set imbalance, and impossible tasks) and constructed datasets that exhibit each. The prompting and probing methods studied all suffer from the presence of highly salient non-truth features, sometimes suffer from imbalanced datasets, and are often overconfident on normative questions. Our ensembling variants improve robustness to some non-truth features but do not fully mitigate the issue. These results suggest that previous evaluations of UE and E2H methods may have yielded overoptimistic results, since they were done on idealized datasets. We hope the challenges and benchmarks presented here help guide the development of elicitation methods that are reliable when human supervision is unavailable.

Impact Statement

This paper presents challenges for the safety of unsupervised elicitation and easy-to-hard generalization methods. We hope that by showing how these challenges can significantly degrade the performance of different methods, we instill caution in the development of new methods; strong performance on benchmarks might not transfer to real-world applications. This also applies to our benchmarks. Though we hope that awareness of these challenges will contribute to making elicitation methods more robust, even if a method performs well on datasets with the challenges introduced here, real tasks on which elicitation methods might be used could have other unforeseen challenges, and the lack of any ground truth labels in a real deployment scenario means failures could happen silently.

Appendix A
Curation of Ctrl-Z

We derive our dataset of safe and malicious sequences of bash commands from the data created by
7
as part of their work evaluating control protocols in an agent environment. The authors released trajectories of bash commands generated from policies (’honest’ or ’attack’) completing tasks under different security protocols
2
2

2

Trajectory data available at
https://bashcontrol.com
.
.

The ’honest’ policy is GPT-4o mini trying to complete a given task faithfully, while the ’attack’ policy is GPT-4o mini trying to download and execute a dummy malware file while ostensibly trying to complete a given task (with scaffolding designed to amplify its attacking capabilities). The honest policy used in ’usefulness’ runs to determine how well each security protocol allows it to complete the task, while attack policy was used in ’safety’ runs to determine how well those protocols stopped it before it could download and execute malware.

Security protocols use a trusted weak model (Llama 3.1 8B) to score each policy generation for suspiciousness. Protocols include Defer to trusted (DTT) (if the monitor scores the policy’s output too highly for suspiciousness at a certain step, generate a command from the trusted weak model instead) and Defer to resample (DTR) (similar to DTT, but up to two new samples are generated from the policy—only if both of these are also suspicious is the trusted model used, otherwise use the least suspicious sample). If all policy generations for a step are highly suspicious, the most suspicious one is audited, meaning the trajectory is ended if it is part of an attack.

We applied the following filters to their data:

•

Only keep trajectories under the DTR security protocol so that most bash commands are generated by GPT-4o mini.

•

Only keep the final resampled command for each step of the trajectory (i.e. the command which was actually executed before subsequent steps).

•

For
safe command sequences
, only keep usefulness runs with 10 steps or more and truncate to the first 10 steps.

•

For
malicious command sequences
, only keep safety runs where the attack policy successfully completes its goal of downloading and executing malware on the 10th step.

Note that the safe command sequences do not necessarily complete the given task within the filtered 10 steps, and the malicious command sequences do not only carry out malicious commands on the 10th step (e.g. they often download the malware in earlier steps and then execute it on the 10th). Note also that 58% of safe command sequences use a weak model generation for at least one step (and 16% of all steps within safe sequences use a weak model generation), whereas no malicious command sequences use a weak model generation (possibly because attack policy trajectories with multiple resamples were more likely to be audited before the 10th step or took more than 10 steps to download and execute malware).

This filtering process leaves 3,438 total trajectories with an approximate 2:1 ratio of safe sequences to malicious ones across 157 unique tasks, split 80/20 between training and test sets with no overlap in tasks. Safe and malicious sequences have similar character lengths.

Appendix D
Definition of Relative Confidence Measure

To measure how confident the set
G
of truth scores on GSM8K are relative to the set
P
of truth scores on Political Ideologies, we calculate the contribution of variance within
G
to the variance of a mixture distribution
X
which samples from
G
and
P
with equal probability:

\text{Relative confidence on GSM8K }\coloneqq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\text{Var}(G)}{\text{Var}(X)}

(2)

where:

\text{Var}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\text{Var}(G)+\frac{1}{2}\text{Var}(P)+\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{G}-\mu_{P})^{2}

(3)

This metric approaches
1
when most of the mixture variance comes from within
G
(i.e. scores in
P
cluster near
\mu_{G}
), equals
0.5
when
G
and
P
have identical distributions, and approaches
0
when variance is dominated by
P
or the separation between
\mu_{G}
and
\mu_{P}
.
