cRGD-Targeted Nanoparticles Co-Loaded with Ag, Quercetin and PD-L1 siRNA with Combined Antibacterial and Immunotherapeutic Functions Against Fusobacterium nucleatum-Associated Triple-Negative Breast Cancer
Authors: Jing Zhang, Xiaoqiang Kong, Xiangping Wu, Dongyun Tao, Xiang Li, Huimin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis, and Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) colonization further aggravates tumor progression, immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance. Conventional monotherapies are limited by insufficient tumor targeting, rapid drug clearance and failure to reverse immune suppression. Here, we fabricated cRGD-modified nanoparticles (Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L) for co-delivery of AgNPs, quercetin (QUE) and PD-L1 siRNA (siP).MethodsThe morphology, particle size, surface metal elements, drug content and QUE entrapment efficiency of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L were obtained using particle size analysis, SEM, inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and HPLC. We further detected cellular uptake, in vitro drug release, cytotoxicity, intracellular reactive oxygen species level, PD-L1 gene silencing efficiency and in vitro anti-Fn activity. Pharmacokinetics and tissue distribution of Ag and QUE were analyzed by ICP-OES and LC-MS/MS, and in vivo antitumor efficacy and biosafety were tested in Fn-colonized orthotopic 4T1 tumor mice.ResultsAg@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L exhibited uniform spherical morphology (195.87± 15.96 nm) with high encapsulation of QUE. cRGD modification enhanced Ag/QUE cellular uptake in 4T1 cells, with pH-responsive release accelerated under tumor acidic conditions. The nanoparticles exhibited selective tumor cytotoxicity, induced oxidative stress, downregulated PD-L1 mRNA, and exerted potent anti-Fn activity in vitro. Pharmacokinetics revealed increased AUC0-t and reduced clearance compared with free drugs, and cRGD modification promoted tumor accumulation and retention of drugs. In vivo preliminary experiments proved the Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L formulation suppressed tumor growth with a tumor inhibition rate of 45%, and effectively modulated the tumor immune microenvironment: it increased tumor-infiltrating CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells, reduced PD-L1⁺CD11c⁺ dendritic cells, and decreased the proportions of immunosuppressive Tregs and MDSCs. Hematoxylin-eosin staining and serum liver-kidney biochemistry confirmed no obvious systemic toxicity.ConclusionAg@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L serves as a multimodal nanoplatform for Fn-associated TNBC with combined in vitro antibacterial, gene silencing and in vivo antitumor immunomodulatory effects, showing great potential for future translational research.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignant tumor and leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women worldwide, among which triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents the most aggressive subtype with high metastasis, strong drug resistance, and extremely poor prognosis due to the lack of effective therapeutic targets. Clinical mainstream strategies including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy are severely limited by poor tumor specificity, rapid drug clearance, off-target toxicity, and high recurrence risk, failing to meet the clinical demand for precise and efficient treatment of TNBC.1 In recent years, emerging evidence has confirmed that tumor-associated microbiota, especially Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn), is highly enriched in breast cancer tissues and plays a critical oncogenic role.2 Fn colonizes in tumor parenchyma, remodels the tumor microenvironment (TME) into an immunosuppressive niche, inhibits cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, accelerates tumor proliferation and distant metastasis, and further reduces the sensitivity of TNBC to conventional therapy, which has become a new key target for breast cancer treatment.3 Therefore, the development of a novel therapeutic platform that can simultaneously kill tumor cells, reverse immunosuppression, and eliminate tumor-resident Fn is of great significance to break through the treatment dilemma of TNBC.

Combination therapy has become a core strategy for cancer treatment, which exerts synergistic antitumor effects through multi-target and multi-mechanism intervention, reduces toxic side effects, and overcomes the limitations of single-drug therapy.4 In our previous study, we constructed a lipid-coated silver nanoparticle (AgNP)-single-stranded DNA (ssDNA)-graphene quantum dot (GQDs) delivery system, which achieved favorable antitumor efficacy against cervical cancer and enabled real-time fluorescence imaging tracking owing to the excellent optical properties of GQDs.5 Inspired by multi-modal combination therapy and tumor bacteria-targeted strategies, we further optimized this delivery system by introducing GQDs as a fluorescent tracking module and structural stabilizer to construct a stable AgNP-GQDs composite core. To endow the carrier with multifunctions of bacteriostasis, tumor growth suppression and immune checkpoint blockade, we incorporated natural flavonoid quercetin (QUE) and PD-L1 siRNA (siP) into cyclic arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (cRGD)-functionalized AgNP-GQDs-cored nanoparticles for synchronous co-delivery.

In summary, we designed a novel targeted nanoparticle Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L for the co-delivery of AgNPs, QUE, and siP. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the physicochemical properties, in vitro performance, in vivo pharmacokinetics, antitumor efficacy, and biosafety of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L, and explore its synergistic therapeutic mechanism against TNBC, so as to provide a novel and effective multi-functional targeted strategy for the clinical treatment of TNBC.

Materials and Methods

DSPE-PEG1000-PAMAM was synthesized according to previously reported methods.6 The 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(polyethylene glycol)-2000 (DSPE-PEG2000) were purchased from AVT (Shanghai) Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. (Catalog No. F01008; Shanghai, China). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) PrimerSets (one RT primer and a pair of qPCR primers for each set) specific for PD-L1 and the primer for β-actin (targeting sequence: forward 5′-AGATGACCCAGATCATGTTTGAGA-3′; reverse 5′-GCATGAGGGAGCGCGTAA-3′) are designed by RiboBio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Kaempferol (purity≥98%) was from Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China, GMP Certificate for Veterinary Drugs No. 030189). Analytical-grade chemicals and reagents were used in this study unless specified otherwise.

Cells

The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell line and embryonic fibroblast NIH3T3 cell line were purchased from Beijing Dingguo Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. 4T1 cells were inoculated in complete medium consisting of Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 basal medium, 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS), and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under 5% CO2 atmosphere. NIH3T3 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested for subsequent cytotoxicity assays.

Bacteria

Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) strain was purchased from BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC305282, Beijing, China). The strain was resuscitated and cultured on Colombia blood agar plates supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood, and incubated at 37 °C for 48–72 h in an anaerobic workstation (80% N2, 10% H2, 10% CO2). Single colonies were selected and subcultured to logarithmic phase for subsequent in vitro antibacterial assays and in vivo intratumoral inoculation.

Animals

Specific pathogen-free female BALB/c mice (16–18 g) and male Sprague-Dawley rats (200±20 g) were provided by SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animal experiments were conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangxi University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. JZLLSC20250503). The animal room was well-ventilated and had a regular 12 h light-dark cycle throughout the experimental period.

Synthesis of AgNPs

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were prepared via a redox method using polyallylamine hydrochloride (PAH) as the reducing agent. Briefly, PAH and AgNO3 solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0.03 M and 8×10−3 M, respectively. Under ice‑water bath at 0 °C and 900 rpm stirring, AgNO3 solution was slowly added dropwise to PAH solution. The reaction was kept in the dark for 15 min.

After the reaction, the resulting solution was divided into two portions. One portion was dialyzed in a 3.5 kDa dialysis bag for 4 h to remove unreacted reagents, and the purified AgNPs obtained were used as the control group in subsequent in vitro and in vivo assays, and stored at 4 °C. Particle size and zeta potential of the dialyzed AgNPs were measured using a Malvern particle size analyzer (Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK).

The other portion of freshly prepared AgNPs was used directly for the preparation of the final Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L without intermediate dialysis.

Synthesis of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L

One milliliter of the as-synthesized AgNPs solution (without intermediate dialysis) was mixed with 20 μL of 100 μM citrate solution (adjusted to acidic pH) and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Then 5 μL of 100 μM PD-L1 siRNA (siP) was added and incubated for another 5 min. Subsequently, 167 μL of 1 mg/mL graphene quantum dots (GQDs) solution was slowly added dropwise into the above mixture, and incubated at 37 °C with 200 r/min stirring for 30 min to form siP-loaded Ag@GQDs cores (Ag@GQDs/siP).

To prepare Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L, ethanol injection method was applied. Lipid materials including DSPE-PEG1000-PAMAM, DSPE-PEG2000 and DSPE-PEG3400-cRGD with a molar ratio of 1:3.5:0.3 were mixed with QUE at a lipid‑to‑drug ratio (total phospholipids: QUE) of 20:1 in ethanol solution. This solution was slowly injected into the Ag@GQDs/siP core solution under 800 rpm stirring, and reacted at 50 °C for 30 min. Finally, the mixture was transferred into a dialysis bag (Mw: 3.5 kDa) and dialyzed for 4 h to obtain the final targeted nanoparticle Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L. To prepare Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L, the same procedure was followed, with the addition of DSPE-PEG3400-cRGD replaced by DSPE-PEG2000 in the total phospholipids. Ag@GQDs/QUE-sis-L was prepared by replacing siP with an equal amount of scrambled siRNA (sis) in accordance with the aforementioned procedures of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L.

For the preparation of various control formulations, all operations were performed in accordance with the aforementioned procedures of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L with corresponding adjustments: Ag@GQDs/siP-c-L was prepared without the addition of QUE, Ag@GQDs/QUE-c-L was prepared without siP, Ag@GQDs-c-L was prepared without the addition of siP and QUE.

Ag@GQDs/siP-L was prepared in accordance with the aforementioned procedures of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L without QUE, Ag@GQDs/QUE-L was prepared without any siRNA, Ag@GQDs/L was prepared without siP and QUE.

Characterization of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L

Size, Zeta Potential, Morphology, and Surface Metal Analysis

The particle sizes and zeta potentials of AgNPs, Ag@GQDs/siP-L, and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L were determined using a Laser Nanometer Particle Size Analyzer (Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK). AgNPs and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was observed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, HITACHI SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS, HORIBA X-MaxN20 011, Horiba, Japan). Prior to observation, samples were coated with gold (sputter coater, GEVEE GVC-2000) and dried (critical point dryer, HAPVENT Himatic-250). SEM images were captured at 3 kV or 5 kV, and EDS was performed at 20 kV (resolution: 1.0 nm@15 kV for SEM, 127 eV for EDS).

Entrapment Efficiency and Content of QUE

The entrapment efficiency (EE%) of QUE was determined using ultrafiltration. One milliliter of the Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L solution was loaded into a 10 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration tube, centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 20 min, and the filtrate was collected to measure the amount of free drug (W1). The total drug content (W2) was determined by diluting an equal volume of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L to 10 mL with methanol and analyzing it via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The QUE content was quantified using an HPLC system (1260, Agilent, Japan) with a Diamonsil ODS-C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at 30°C. The mobile phase was methanol-0.4% phosphate buffer (V: V=7:3) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The detection wavelength was 372 nm, and the injection volume was 10 μL. Silver Content Determination

The silver content of the samples was determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (700ES, Varian, USA). Briefly, the samples were completely digested by nitric acid and diluted to volume with 1% nitric acid until analysis.7 The instrument parameters were set with a radio frequency power of 1.0 kW, the plasma gas flow rate was set to 15 L/min, the auxiliary gas flow rate was controlled at 1.5 L/min, the nebulizer gas pressure was maintained at 200 kPa, the rotational speed of the peristaltic pump was tuned to 15 rpm, and the sample introduction rate was kept at 1 mL/min. The instrument dwell time for each test sample was set to 30s. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the final results were calculated using the average of the three replicate measurements.

Gel Retardation Assay

The siRNA loading in Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis. The suspension was added to the pre-prepared siRNA solution at different N:P ratios (1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 5:1, 6:1, 7:1 and 8:1). The N:P ratio is defined as the molar ratio of nitrogen atoms from the amine groups of the cationic lipid DSPE-PEG1000-PAMAM in the formulation to phosphorus atoms in the phosphate backbone of siRNA. The mixture was then loaded into the wells of a precast 1.2% agarose gel supplemented with SYBR Safe dye (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Free siP (20 μmol/L) was used as a positive control, while Ag@GQDs/QUE-c-L without siP addition served as a blank control. Electrophoresis was conducted and visualized using an Invitrogen E-Gel Power Snap Electrophoresis Device (ThermoFisher Scientific) at a constant voltage of 65 V for 30 min.

Cell Uptake

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1×106 cells/well and incubated for 8 h. After washing, cells were treated with various Ag-containing and QUE-cotaining formulations, including AgNPs, Ag@GQDs/siP-L, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L, and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L with an Ag concentration of 3 µg/mL, as well as Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L and QUE suspension with a QUE concentration of 2 µg/mL.

After incubation for 2, 5, 10, and 15 min, the drug-containing medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with serum-free medium again. Cells at each time point were collected and divided into two aliquots. One part was digested with aqua regia, and the digested solution was diluted to 5 mL with 1% nitric acid for silver content determination by ICP-OES. The other part was treated with acetonitrile, centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C to remove protein precipitates, and the supernatant was collected and analyzed by liquid chromatograph-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for QUE content with the following analysis condition.

Release Behavior

Dialysis was performed to evaluate the in vitro release of QUE and Ag from Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L under pH 7.4 (normal physiological condition) and pH 5.0 (tumor acidic microenvironment), respectively. A dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 20 kDa was filled with 1 mL of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L and immersed in 100 mL of release medium (PBS containing 0.5% Tween-80) at pH 5.0 or pH 7.4. The release system was incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 100 rpm. Samples were taken at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h, and an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed release medium was immediately replenished to maintain a constant volume. Cell Viability

The cytotoxicity of AgNPs, Ag@GQDs/siP-L, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L against 4T1 and NIH3T3 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2×104 cells per well and cultured overnight for adherence. Cells were then treated with serial dilutions of these Ag-containing formulations with Ag concentrations of 37.5, 18.75, 9.37, 4.68, 2.34, 1.17, 0.58, 0.29 µg/mL for 12 h. According to the manufacturer’s instruction, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for another 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by an M200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Cell viability was calculated relative to the untreated control group, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined using GraphPad Prism software (Version 10).

Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species Detection

The intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) level in 4T1 cells treated with Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was detected by the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) using 2′,7′-Dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) as the fluorescent probe. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2×106 cells/well and incubated for 12 h. Then cell culture medium was removed. Diluted DCFH-DA (10μmol/L) was added and incubated for 20 minutes in a 37°C-cell incubator. Then the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing AgNPs, Ag@GQDs/siP-c-L, and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL, while the control group received an equal volume of serum-free medium. After 2 h incubation, cells were washed and harvested. Intracellular fluorescence intensity was measured at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission wavelength of 525 nm using a M200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Gene Silencing Assay

4T1 cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well and cultured in complete medium containing 10% FBS overnight. After washing twice with PBS, cells were divided into five groups: blank group (untreated), free siP group, empty nanocarrier group (siP-free nanoparticles at the same particle concentration, Ag@GQDs/QUE-c-L), Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L group, and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L group. All siRNA-containing groups were dosed at an equivalent final siRNA concentration of 20 nM. After 4 h of transfection incubation, the drug-containing medium was aspirated, cells were rinsed once with pre-warmed PBS, and fresh complete medium was added to continue culture. At 48 h after the initial administration, total cellular RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s protocol., and equal amounts of total RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA following the protocol of the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was then performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Master Mix on a real-time PCR system, with β-actin as the endogenous reference gene. The relative expression level of PD-L1 mRNA was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method.8 All experiments were carried out in three independent replicates.

Antibacterial Assay

The in vitro antibacterial activity of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L against Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) was evaluated by using colony counting assay. Fn in the logarithmic growth phase was adjusted to a concentration of 3×108 CFU/mL in anaerobic broth. Serial dilutions of AgNPs, Ag@GQDs/siP-L, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L, and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L were prepared in anaerobic medium (concentration of Ag at 50 μg/mL), using PBS solution as negative control, and then mixed with an equal volume of the above bacterial suspension in tubes, followed by anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 4 h.

For quantitative analysis, 100 μL of each culture suspension was collected, gradient-diluted, and spread on Colombia blood agar plates supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood. After continuous anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the number of colonies on each plate was counted, and the bacterial survival rate and inhibition rate were calculated accordingly. All experiments were performed in triplicate under strict anaerobic conditions (80% N2, 10% H2, 10% CO2).

Pharmacokinetic Evaluation

Nine Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly divided into three groups: QUE suspension, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L, and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L. After fasting for 12 h before the experiment, the corresponding formulation was administered to each group via tail vein injections at a dose normalized to 3.0 mg/kg for QUE. Blood samples (around 500 μL) were gathered from the retro-orbital venous plexus at scheduled time points: 0.017, 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection. Collected blood was immediately transferred into heparin-coated tubes, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 15 min under 4 °C to obtain plasma. For sample pretreatment, 50 μL plasma was mixed with 30 ng Kaempferol (purity≥98%) in 150 μL acetonitrile for protein precipitation. After vortex blending for 5 min and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (QTRAP® 4500, AB Sciex, USA) for analysis. Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated by fitting the concentration-time data using PK solver software (version 2.0).

LC-MS/MS Analysis of QUE

Chromatographic Conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed on a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (2.1×50 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, with an injection volume of 5 μL. Gradient elution was carried out as follows: 0.00–1.00 min, 5% B; 1.00–2.00 min, 5–20% B; 2.00–3.00 min, 20–80% B; 3.00–3.50 min, 80–95% B; 4.00–4.50 min, 95–5% B; 4.50–5.50 min, 5% B.

Mass Spectrometric Conditions

Mass spectrometry was operated in electrospray ionization (ESI) negative mode with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The ion spray voltage was set to 4500 V; curtain gas, nebulizing gas, and auxiliary gas were all nitrogen, at 25 psi, 55 psi, and 55 psi, respectively. The nebulization temperature was 550 °C, declustering potential (DP) was 100 V, and collision energy (CE) was 30 eV.

The MRM transitions were m/z 301.0→150.7 for QUE (DP: −116.94 V; CE: −28.20 eV) and m/z 284.9→184.8 for kaempferol (internal standard, DP: −132.01 V; CE: −36.90 eV).

Tissue Distribution

When the tumor volume of mice reached approximately 300 mm3, BALB/c mice were randomly divided into the Ag@GQDs/QUE/siP-L and Ag@GQDs/QUE/siP-c-L groups as experimental groups, with the same concentration of AgNPs and QUE-suspension as the control group. Each formulation was administered to each group via tail vein injections at a dose of 3.0 mg/kg for QUE. All tail vein injection procedures were performed under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. Mice were induced with 3–5% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 1 L/min in an induction chamber, and anesthesia was maintained at 1.5–2% isoflurane during the operation. The depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex to ensure the comfort of animals.

Mice were humanely euthanized at 0.5, 6, and 24 h after injection. Then the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and tumor tissues were excised. After rinsing, drying, and weighing, the tissue samples were sectioned. ICP-OES was used to measure the Ag content in the major organs and tumors as aforementioned. LC-MS/MS was used to detect QUE content in the major organs and tumors as aforementioned. For quantitative analysis, tissue Ag and QUE levels were further normalized relative to their corresponding free formulations (free AgNPs and free QUE suspension, respectively) to compare the tissue distribution performance of Ag@GQDs/QUE/siP-L and Ag@GQDs/QUE/siP-c-L.

In vivo Anti-Tumor Efficacy

Tumor Model Establishment

Female BALB/c mice were used to establish orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer models colonized with Fn. Briefly, 1×106 4T1 cells suspension was inoculated into the mammary fat pad of mice, and a round, white, raised bleb on the mammary surface confirmed successful in-situ retention of cells. Tumor volume was measured, and the experiment was initiated when tumor volume reached 80–100 mm3.

Tumor volume was calculated by measuring the longest diameter (D) and shortest diameter (L) with a vernier caliper, using the following formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage[substack]{amsmath} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage[mathscr]{eucal} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \DeclareFontFamily{T1}{linotext}{} \DeclareFontShape{T1}{linotext}{m}{n} {linotext }{} \DeclareSymbolFont{linotext}{T1}{linotext}{m}{n} \DeclareSymbolFontAlphabet{\mathLINOTEXT}{linotext} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{V }} = {\mathrm{ D}} \times {{\mathrm{L}}^2}/2$$\end{document}

Tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into nine groups: bacteria-colonized model group, AgNPs group, QUE suspension group, Ag@GQDs/L group, Ag@GQDs/QUE-L group, Ag@GQDs/QUE-sis-L group, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L group, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L group, and Paclitaxel (PTX) Injection group. Fn suspension was prepared by picking single colonies of the 2nd generation with a sterile inoculation loop, mixing gently in PBS buffer, and adjusting to a concentration of 1.2×107 CFU per mouse. Intratumoral injection of Fn was performed in all groups to construct the Fn-infected orthotopic breast cancer mouse model.9 All groups were administered via tail vein injection every 2 days for a total of 7 administrations at a dose of 1.5 mg/kg (QUE-equivalent) for nanoparticle formulations; PTX 20 mg/kg was injected once a week.

Body weight and tumor volume were recorded throughout the experiment to evaluate antitumor efficacy and in vivo toxicity. Three days after the last treatment, the mice were humanely euthanized with the same two-step protocol as aforementioned. Then the tumors were collected, weighed, and then processed for the following detection.

Flow Cytometry Detection

The tumor tissue was digested and gently rubbed through a 70-μm cell strainer on ice. After diluting with cell staining buffer (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), the cells were centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min and resuspended to form a single-cell suspension for staining. Cells were stained using fluorophore-labeled antibodies purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), including PE anti-mouse CD3 (E-AB-F1013D), FITC anti-mouse CD4 (E-AB-F1353C), PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD8a (E-AB-F1104H), APC anti-mouse CD25 (E-AB-F1102E), PE anti-FoxP3 (E-AB-F1238D), PE anti-mouse CD11b (E-AB-F1081D), APC anti-mouse Gr-1 (E-AB-F1120E), APC anti-mouse CD274/PD-L1 (E-AB-F1132E), and FITC anti-mouse CD11c (E-AB-F0991C). Fixation and permeabilization are required for the staining of FoxP3. Each antibody was used at the manufacturer’s recommended concentration of 5 μL per staining reaction, corresponding to 1 × 106 cells suspended in 100 μL of cell staining buffer. Mix thoroughly at room temperature, followed by 30 minutes of incubation in the dark. Cells were washed and resuspended in 500 µL PBS with adequate mixing prior to flow cytometric analysis. All samples were analyzed using a NovoCyte™ flow cytometer (NovoCyte 2060R, ACEA Biosciences (Hangzhou) Co., Ltd.), and the data were analyzed using NovoExpress (version 1.5.0) with the gating strategy was organized as Figure S1 in the supplementary materials.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

The heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm sections. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid-ethanol for 15s, blued in running tap water for 2 min, counterstained with eosin for 1 min, then dehydrated, cleared and mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological observation and image acquisition were performed using a DM2500 biological microscope (Leica Microsystems, Shanghai, China).

Biochemical Parameter Measurement

Four hundred microliters of blood were collected from the orbital venous plexus of the mice, which was centrifuged later. The serum level of liver function indicators (alkaline Phosphatase [ALP] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE-L) were detected by an automatic analyzer (7100, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analyses

All quantitative measurements were presented as means±standard deviations (SD). For comparisons among experimental groups, Shapiro–Wilk test and combined Bartlett/Brown-Forsythe tests were performed to verify residual normality and variance homogeneity prior to variance analysis. Hierarchical ANOVA and matched multiple comparison tests (Tukey’s HSD or Dunnett’s T3) were applied for intergroup pairwise comparisons, whereas two-way ANOVA was utilized for datasets involving two independent variables, with corresponding pairwise comparisons conducted as needed. p<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Characterization of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L

As shown in Figure 1A, the synthesized AgNPs were spherical with relatively uniform size, forming a dense, closely packed assembly. When the cores were entrapped in multi-functional phospholipids and co-loaded with QUE, the morphology of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L maintained the spherical or nearly spherical shape. The particle size of the AgNPs was around 50 nm. The surface elemental composition of AgNPs and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) point analysis coupled with SEM (Figure 1B). As shown in Figure 1B, the primary element in bare AgNPs was silver (Ag), with a mass fraction of 42.18%, accompanied by minor carbon (C, 30.35%) signals. In contrast, the Ag content of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was drastically reduced to 6.07%, while carbon (C) became the dominant element (51.45%).

The siRNA condensation and loading efficiency of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis, with the results depicted in Figure 1C. Free siP served as the positive control, exhibiting a distinct electrophoretic band. The blank control (Ag@GQDs/QUE-c-L without siP) showed no visible band, verifying that the vector itself did not interfere with siRNA detection. As the N:P ratio increased from 1:1 to 8:1, the migration of siRNA was gradually retarded. At lower N:P ratios (1:1), residual siRNA bands remained detectable, indicating incomplete complexation between siP and the Ag@GQDs/QUE-c-L vector. No visible free siRNA bands are detected on the gel when the N:P ratio is≥2:1. In the in vivo physiological environment, serum proteins, salt ion fluctuations and pH changes can weaken the electrostatic binding between the carrier and siRNA.10 On the other hand, an excessively high N:P ratio might result in overly strong positive surface charge of the particles, bringing about extra cytotoxicity and non-specific adsorption issues.11 Therefore, N:P ratio at 6:1 as the final preparation condition was selected for subsequent in vitro and in vivo biological evaluations.

In vitro Release of QUE and Ag

The in vitro release behavior of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L nanoformulation was investigated under different pH conditions to evaluate its tumor microenvironment-responsive properties. As in Figure 2B, Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L exhibited excellent pH-sensitive release behavior: in the simulated normal physiological environment (pH 7.4), the release of both Ag and QUE was notably sustained, with cumulative release rates of 29.4% and 41.9% at 24 h, respectively, indicating favorable stability in the blood circulation; in contrast, under the acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 5.0), the release rates of both components were significantly accelerated, with cumulative release rates exceeding 60% at 24 h (66.6% for Ag and 62.5% for QUE, respectively).

For silver release, both zero-order and Higuchi models yielded better fitting performance than the first-order model at both pH conditions (Figure 2C). At neutral pH, the Higuchi model showed slightly higher goodness-of-fit than the zero-order model. For QUE release, the optimal fitting model varied with pH. The Higuchi model achieved the best correlation under acidic condition, while the first-order kinetic model showed the highest fitting degree at pH 7.4.

ROS Detection

ROS, as natural byproducts of cellular aerobic metabolism, play critical roles in cell signal transduction and homeostasis maintenance, while abnormally elevated ROS levels can trigger oxidative damage and induce cell apoptosis.12 To verify the ROS-inducing capacity, the blank control group, AgNPs group, QUE-free control group (Ag@GQDs/siP-c-L), and final target formulation group (Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L) were selected for ROS detection (Figure 3B). Compared with the blank control group, the intracellular ROS level in the AgNPs-treated group only slightly increased by approximately 8.6%, which may be attributed to the low cellular uptake efficiency of Ag nanoparticles or insufficient intracellular catalytic activity. In contrast, treatment with Ag@GQDs/siP-c-L and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels to 2.27-fold and 2.24-fold that of the blank group, respectively.

In vitro PD-L1 Gene Silencing Efficiency

Real-time qPCR was performed to evaluate the PD-L1 gene silencing efficiency of different siRNA-loaded nanoformulations. As shown in Figure 3C, free naked siP barely downregulated PD-L1 mRNA expression in 4T1 cells, with no statistically significant difference compared with the blank group. Both nanoformulations containing siP significantly reduced PD-L1 mRNA levels, which was significantly superior to the free siP group.

In vitro Antibacterial Activity

The in vitro antibacterial activities of silver-containing formulations against F. nucleatum were evaluated under equal silver content, with PBS as the negative control, and the colony counting results are presented in Figure 3D. The PBS group exhibited dense and vigorous bacterial growth, with a colony count of (2650 ± 455) per dish. All silver-containing formulations exerted varying degrees of bactericidal effects. Free AgNPs showed moderate antibacterial activity with an inhibition rate of 52.3% (p<0.001). After encapsulation, the Ag@GQDs/siP-L group further decreased the colony count to (596 ± 53) per dish with an inhibition rate of 77.5%, which was significantly stronger than that of free AgNPs. With the co-loading of quercetin, the Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L and Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L groups achieved the optimal bactericidal performance, showing an extremely significant difference from the PBS group (p<0.0001). The cRGD-functionalized Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L group maintained similar antibacterial activity, compared with its non-targeted counterpart.

Discussion

To tackle Fn-induced immune suppression and refractory TNBC progression, multifunctional modules were integrated into Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L, with complementary therapeutic advantages. In this system, AgNP serve as dual-functional therapeutic agents with two core biological activities. On the one hand, AgNPs induce tumor cell apoptosis by triggering excessive ROS production and destroying mitochondrial homeostasis, exerting direct and potent anti-tumor cytotoxicity.7,13 On the other hand, AgNPs possess broad-spectrum and highly efficient antibacterial activity, which has been systematically confirmed as a powerful antibacterial agent against bacteria, fungi, and viruses; this antibacterial effect is the primary functional component for eliminating tumor-resident Fn, interrupting bacteria-mediated tumor promotion and immune suppression.14

Immune checkpoint blockade therapy targeting PD-L1 has revolutionized cancer treatment,15,16 but monoclonal antibody (mAb) drugs are limited by large molecular weight, poor tumor tissue penetration, difficulty in effectively interacting with intracellular PD-L1, and low efficacy in “cold” immunosuppressive tumors like TNBC.17 PD-L1 siRNA can specifically silence PD-L1 gene expression through RNA interference, directly blocking the transcription and translation of PD-L1, overcome the delivery defects of mAbs, and reactivating T-cell-mediated antitumor immune responses.18 Moreover, siP possesses the distinct advantages of small molecular size, easy synthesis, and low cost, making it an ideal alternative to PD-L1 antibodies.18–20 However, the easy degradation, low cell membrane permeability, and off-target risk of siRNA severely restrict its clinical application.21,22 Therefore, we applied siP instead of ssDNA in the cores to bridge the AgNPs and GQDs through π–π interactions and exhibit a silencing effect. Considering the poor stability of siRNA, we used safe and biodegradable lipids to coat the cores and achieve precise, targeted delivery to tumor cells.

QUE, a natural flavonoid, induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) of tumor cells, promotes the exposure of calreticulin and release of HMGB1, regulates the immune TME, and enhances immune cell infiltration, thereby synergizing with immune therapy.23–25 Our previously work has also verified the ICD-inducing effect of QUE.26 Nevertheless, the clinical application of QUE is severely limited by its poor water solubility, low bioavailability, and lack of tumor-targeting ability.27 To overcome this bottleneck, nanotechnology-based delivery systems (such as liposomes and polymer micelles) have emerged as key strategies for optimizing the application of quercetin.28–30 Furthermore, cRGD specifically binds to integrin αvβ3, which is highly overexpressed on tumor cells and tumor vascular endothelial cells,31 endowing nanocarriers with active tumor-targeting ability, enhancing drug accumulation at tumor sites, and improving therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity.32,33

Lipid-based nanocarriers represent a highly translatable platform for breast cancer therapy, with proven advantages in improving drug solubility, enhancing tumor accumulation and enabling multi-drug co-delivery.34 Accumulating studies have validated the synergistic antitumor effect of QUE combined with PD-L1 blockade, either as free drug plus anti-PD-1 antibody or as peptide-loaded nanosystems, providing a solid biological rationale for this combinatorial strategy.35,36 However, existing modalities mainly rely on protein- or peptide-level PD-L1 inhibitors, and lipid-based nanoplatforms co-delivering quercetin with PD-L1 siRNA for gene-level silencing in breast cancer remain rarely reported. Our lipid-coated silver-cored nanoplatform thus offers a novel delivery paradigm for quercetin-based combinational immunotherapy against breast cancer.

The Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was fabricated via the ethanol injection method and characterized by SEM-EDS, agarose gel retardation, cellular uptake and in vitro release evaluation. The remarkable increase in carbon content and the significant decrease in silver content confirmed that the AgNPs were effectively encapsulated by the phospholipid-based organic materials. This bilayer structure could prevent silver ion leakage and improves systemic circulation stability,37 while providing with a loading capability for lipophilic quercetin and anionic PD-L1 siRNA, achieving synchronous delivery of cargos with disparate physicochemical properties. In terms of colloidal stability, the nanocarrier relies on a synergistic stabilization mechanism of electrostatic repulsion and lipid bilayer-derived steric hindrance, rather than depending solely on high surface charge. This rational design avoids massive non-specific serum protein adsorption and accelerated in vivo reticuloendothelial clearance caused by excessively high positive charge.38 An optimal N:P ratio of 6:1 enables full electrostatic condensation of siRNA, guaranteeing nucleic acid encapsulation efficiency and anti-nuclease protection.

This enhanced cellular uptake could be ascribed to the cRGD peptide, which specifically recognizes and binds with high affinity to integrin αvβ3 receptors overexpressed on tumor cell surfaces, thereby effectively triggering receptor-mediated endocytosis and markedly accelerating the internalization of the nanoformulation.32 The final intracellular level of nanoparticles is essentially determined by a dynamic balance between inward endocytosis and outward exocytosis processes.39,40 Such active targeting driven by cRGD-integrin binding significantly elevates the internalization rate and allows fast intracellular accumulation of the active payload, showing a clear advantage over non-targeted formulations that depend on passive diffusion and non-specific adsorption for cellular entry. It is worth noting that αvβ3 integrin receptors follow a rapid recycling pathway after ligand-triggered endocytosis, which in turn shapes the intracellular retention profile of targeted nanoparticles.41

Furthermore, the nanoformulation exhibits typical tumor microenvironment-triggered pH-responsive release profiles. Sustained slow drug release was shown under neutral physiological media, which maintains structural stability during blood circulation and reduces off-target drug leakage to healthy organs. However, relative rapid cargo release occurs in acidic tumor environment. It is helpful for elevating local tumor therapeutic concentrations.42 In fact, PAMAM dendrimers in the lipids might modulate pH-responsive release via the proton sponge effect. At pH 5.0, tertiary amine protonation in PAMAM induces osmotic swelling, disrupting the phospholipid bilayer internally and boosting drugs release with lipid headgroup protonation.43 Release kinetic modeling further reveals differentiated release mechanisms for Ag and QUE. Ag release is co-governed by lipid matrix erosion and Fickian diffusion, with zero-order and Higuchi models showing optimal goodness-of-fit under both pH conditions; the slightly superior fitting of the Higuchi model at neutral pH indicates diffusion plays a more dominant role when the bilayer remains structurally intact. For lipophilic QUE solubilized in the bilayer, release follows first-order diffusion kinetics at pH 7.4, a classic feature of encapsulated hydrophobic drugs driven by concentration gradients, while acidic conditions disrupt lipid packing and shift the release pattern toward matrix-diffusion dominance, resulting in the best fit with the Higuchi model at pH 5.0. The increased release rate constant under acidic conditions quantitatively confirms the pH-accelerated release capacity of the formulation.

As for cell toxicity of Ag-based nanoparticles, the preferential toxicity toward 4T1 cancer cells over NIH3T3 normal fibroblasts. The cytotoxic effect of silver nanoparticles relies on a “Trojan horse” mechanism: once internalized into cells mainly through endocytic pathways, the particles localize to lysosomes, and the acidic lysosomal environment promotes the dissolution of silver ions, which in turn disrupt cellular redox homeostasis and drive apoptotic cell death.44 In addition, tumor cells carry an intrinsically labile intracellular redox state with persistently raised baseline oxidative load, making them substantially more vulnerable to cytotoxic and oxidative damage driven by silver ions. Normal fibroblasts, by contrast, are furnished with a more well-buffered antioxidant protective system that can effectively counteract moderate oxidative insult from silver exposure and sustain intact cellular physiology and viability.45 In parallel, the higher IC50 values of all nanoformulations relative to free AgNPs might come from the fact that the outer GQDs shell and lipid bilayer function as diffusional barriers that slow the rapid extracellular release of Ag⁺, and shifts in particle surface properties can further modulate cellular internalization kinetics, which together result in diminished acute cytotoxicity over the 12 h treatment period.46

The improved intracellular delivery enables the nanoformulations to trigger cellular oxidative stress more effectively. AgNPs slowly release Ag⁺, which binds to sulfhydryl groups of mitochondrial respiratory chain proteins, interferes with electron transfer and causes electron leakage to generate superoxide anions, initiating a ROS cascade that converts H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals.47,48 Unmodified AgNPs are prone to aggregation and have low cellular uptake efficiency,49,50 resulting in limited ROS elevation.51 As a redox modulator with dual antioxidant-pro-oxidant properties,24,52 QUE could promote oxidative stress via multiple pathways: auto-oxidation to produce semi-quinone radicals and other ROS, depletion of intracellular antioxidants such as glutathione, and disruption of the mitochondrial respiratory chain to trigger electron leakage.53 With lipid coating and cRGD modification, the cellular uptake of the composite nanoformulations is significantly enhanced, allowing more Ag and QUE to enter cells and accumulate around organelles. However, the dual redox property of QUE may counteract partial Ag-induced oxidative stress at the tested concentration, suggesting that the redox regulation of this nano-system is affected by multiple factors and still requires further in-depth investigation.

In parallel, the nanosystem effectively delivers siRNA into cells to mediate PD-L1 gene silencing. RT-PCR results show that free siRNA barely downregulates PD-L1 mRNA due to poor stability and membrane permeability, while both siRNA-loaded nanoformulations induce significant knockdown, confirming the delivery advantage of nanoparticle encapsulation, however, no significant difference in silencing efficiency was observed between these two formulations, likely because prolonged static incubation allows sufficient non-specific endocytosis of the non-targeted group, weakening the in vitro advantage of cRGD active targeting. Meanwhile, the siRNA-free blank nanocarrier, Ag@GQDs/QUE-c-L, caused no obvious PD-L1 downregulation, indicating the silencing effect stems from specific siRNA knockdown rather than non-specific carrier regulation.

Beyond direct tumor cell killing via oxidative stress, the antibacterial activity of the system is core to the synergistic effect of nanocarrier optimization and active components. Although bare AgNPs can disrupt bacterial membrane integrity by releasing Ag⁺ and induce ROS to damage bacterial DNA,51 their inherent tendency to aggregate limited the effective contact area with bacteria,49,54 showing moderate antibacterial activity. After modification with lipid bilayers, and PEG, the nanocarrier not only effectively improved the colloidal stability and dispersibility of AgNPs,55 avoiding activity loss caused by aggregation, but also enhanced the adsorption capacity to negatively charged bacterial cell walls through surface charge regulation, facilitating efficient penetration and sustained release of Ag⁺ and prolonging the antibacterial duration. The incorporation of QUE achieved an elevation in antibacterial efficacy, since QUE itself was reported to exert antibacterial effects by disrupting bacterial membrane permeability, inhibiting key metabolic enzyme activities, and depleting intracellular antioxidants (eg, glutathione).56,57 As for the similar antibacterial activity of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L with its corresponding unmodified counterparts, it may be attributed to the steric hindrance and hydrophilic barrier introduced by the long-chain PEG3400 during cRGD conjugation,58 which moderately weakened the direct contact efficiency between nanoparticles and bacteria. However, it should be emphasized that cRGD is designed to target integrin αvβ3 receptors highly expressed on tumor cells. Its targeting modification enables specific accumulation in tumor tissues in vivo, which can compensate for the slight difference in in vitro antibacterial activity through local high concentrations in tumors. Notably, the antibacterial performance of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L was only characterized at the in vitro level in the current work; its actual bacterial regulatory effect within the tumor microenvironment and its direct contribution to the in vivo antitumor efficacy will be further elucidated.

The pharmacokinetic and biodistribution results systematically validate the in vivo delivery advantages of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L. The elevated systemic exposure and reduced clearance of nanoformulations stem from the protective effect of the phospholipid bilayer and PEGylated surface structure. Lipid encapsulation isolates active components from direct contact with blood components. The hydrophilic PEG shell forms a steric barrier on the particle surface, reducing opsonin adsorption and RES recognition,59 thereby elevating the AUC values. The reduced Vz further verifies that nanocarriers retain most payloads in the systemic circulation, rather than allowing extensive non-specific diffusion into peripheral tissues. The further improved pharmacokinetic parameters of the cRGD-modified formulation prove that cRGD peptide conjugation does not compromise the long-circulating property of the nanocarrier. Instead, the enhanced surface hydrophilicity and particle stability further reduce clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), leading to higher systemic drug exposure. In terms of tissue distribution, it confirmed that nanocarrier modification and cRGD active targeting strategy could effectively optimize the in vivo distribution of Ag and QUE, among which Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L had the optimal tumor targeting performance, mainly due to the synergy of active and passive targeting. The cRGD peptide could specifically bind to αvβ3 integrin highly expressed on tumor cells, mediating the active recognition, internalization and retention of the formulation at tumor sites. The nanocarrier composed of GQDs, phospholipid layer and PEG endowed the formulation with long-circulating properties, which not only prolonged the residence time in systemic circulation, but also realized passive drug enrichment through the EPR effect of tumor tissues, laying a foundation for continuous drug supply to tumors. Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-L only achieved passive targeting through the EPR effect, with significantly lower tumor drug enrichment efficiency than the cRGD-modified group. Without carrier protection and targeting ability, these formulations were easily recognized and phagocytosed by the RES system or presented non-specific distribution, mainly accumulating in normal organs such as spleen, liver and lung, resulting in extremely low tumor delivery efficiency and high toxicity risk to normal organs.

Besides of targeted delivery, direct tumoricidal action and bacterial clearance, the superior in vivo antitumor efficacy of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L also stems from immune microenvironment remodeling. The elevated CD3+CD4+ helper T cell infiltration demonstrates effective activation of T-cell-mediated immunity, arising from the combined effects of quercetin-triggered ICD,60 PD-L1 siRNA-mediated checkpoint blockade, and Ag-driven intratumoral Fn elimination to remove bacteria-derived immunosuppressive signals that block T cell recruitment, collectively boosting CD4+ T cell infiltration and helper function.

Notably, the Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L did not induce significant numerical expansion of CD8⁺ T cells. Instead, the detected decreased PD-L1⁺CD11c⁺ population of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L mainly reflects the altered antigen presentation state of tumor-infiltrating conventional dendritic cells in vivo. Together with the effectively silence of PD-L1 expression on tumor cells by the delivered PD-L1 siRNA of Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L in vitro, the PD-L1 downregulation on both tumor cells and dendritic cells is expected to block the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitory axis, thereby potentially reversing T cell exhaustion and reinvigorating the antitumor effector function of pre-existing CD8⁺ T cells.61,62 Furthermore, the targeted formulation markedly reduced the proportions of two core immunosuppressive cell populations in the tumor microenvironment, CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs and CD11b+Gr-1+ MDSCs. Tregs suppress effector T cell activity by secreting inhibitory cytokines, while MDSCs block T cell activation and promote immune escape through metabolic interference and oxidative stress.63 The decline of these two subsets confirms that Ag@GQDs/QUE-siP-c-L remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

The H&E histopathological and serum biochemical results are consistent, verifying the safety of the targeted co-delivery system. The reduced systemic toxicity of nanoformulations mainly derives from three structural advantages: the PEGylated shell decreases reticuloendothelial phagocytosis, pH-responsive release avoids premature drug leakage in circulation, and cRGD-mediated active targeting redirects most payloads to tumor sites, thus lowering off-target exposure in normal tissues. The combined effects of EPR-based passive accumulation, multi-component synergistic tumoricidal action, PD-L1 silencing-mediated immune activation and cRGD-amplified local drug concentration collectively contribute to the extensive tumor necrosis.

Conclusions

In summary, we constructed a cRGD-modified Ag@GQDs-based co-delivery nanoplatform co-loaded with quercetin and PD-L1 siRNA for Fn-associated triple-negative breast cancer therapy. With favorable physicochemical properties and pH-responsive release characteristics, the nanoplatform achieves prolonged systemic circulation and preferential tumor accumulation through the combination of the EPR effect and cRGD-mediated active targeting. In vitro, it exhibits selective tumoricidal activity via oxidative stress, strong bactericidal efficacy against Fn, and efficient PD-L1 gene silencing in tumor cells. In vivo, the formulation delivers robust antitumor efficacy, effectively remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by boosting CD4⁺ T cell infiltration, downregulating PD-L1 on tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells, and reducing immunosuppressive Treg and MDSC populations, while maintaining negligible systemic toxicity and favorable biocompatibility. Deeper synergistic mechanisms, including the actual antibacterial performance within the complex tumor microenvironment and the specific functional recovery of CD8⁺ T cells, still require further comprehensive validation to advance its translational application potential. Collectively, this multifunctional nanoplatform offers a promising strategy for the precise treatment of bacteria-associated tumors.
