Pre-Roman copper industry had no polluting impact on the global environment
Authors: Omri Yagel, Aaron Greener, Willie Ondricek, Erez Ben-Yosef
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Among the unresolved questions about pre-Roman metallurgical industries are the extent and severity of the pollution they produced and the potential harm inflicted on the societies associated with them. Research on this topic has resulted in conflicting conclusions. Based on measurements of heavy metal enrichment in soils, archaeological sediments, plants and skeletal remains, interpretations have varied between catastrophic, global (intercontinental) effects to a restricted impact relevant only to those directly working at the furnaces. In the current study we focus on Timna Valley in the Southern Arabah (southern Israel). Based on high-resolution pXRF geochemical surveys of two smelting camps from different periods, accompanied by test excavations, we demonstrate that (1) mapping elemental concentrations in soil is an effective tool for identifying and characterizing various activity areas in archaeological sites, such as metal production, livestock penning and domestic and industrial waste disposal; (2) in Timna, the intra-site organization of production and scale of metallurgical activities changed substantially between the Early Bronze Age and the Iron Age; (3) the heavy metal enrichment (Cu/Pb) of soil due to smelting is spatially discrete, concentrated only in areas directly associated with these activities; and (4) this enrichment aligns with the spatial distribution of metallurgical waste, suggesting that lead emission to the atmosphere was minimal and that the documented heavy metals are not bioavailable. Our results, combined with a comprehensive survey of previously published data, support the minimalist interpretation of the polluting effects of metallurgical activities on ancient societies, at least for pre-Roman copper production industries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80939-5.

Introduction

The characterization and magnitude of pollution stemming from pre-Industrial Revolution metallurgical practices, as well as its impact on human health, have been subjects of considerable scholarly discourse and contention since the 1990s. While most scholars acknowledge the Roman-era surge in metal production and its substantial effect on the distribution of heavy metal pollutants beyond production sites1–8, there is much less clarity regarding earlier periods. While some scholars argue that metallurgical activities during the Bronze and Iron Ages caused an identifiable rise in metal pollutants on a global scale, others have concluded that these ancient industries had only a discrete effect on the environment. Intense research regarding this issue was conducted by several teams in the ancient copper ore district of Wadi Faynan, Jordan, primarily by the application of chemical analyses on a variety of materials, including geological and archaeological sediments, plant and animal remains, ancient human bones and other objects. One set of studies, led by Grattan and colleagues9–15, concluded that human activity led to heavy-metal contamination in the local alluvial sediments already in the Late Neolithic15, and that subsequent activities during the Bronze and Iron Ages further intensified metalliferous pollution, resulting in a widespread environmental contamination that eventually made its way into the local food system and from there to the bodies of the entire local population, including its current Bedouin inhabitants. Furthermore, the Faynan copper industry was linked to evidence for a rise in large-scale global pollution during the Iron Age9 (based on paleo-atmospheric data obtained from ice-cores by Hong et al.2,5). The researchers’ underlying assumption was that waste rock, tailings and slag slowly release metals into the surrounding environment through a variety of erosional processes, and thus pollution has persisted and has continued to influence the desert environment in southern Jordan for several millennia. Grattan et al.9 surmised that “the landscape of Khirbet Faynan is, in significant part, one of profound industrial dereliction in which metal pollutants continue to be recycled at a variety of hillside, slope and wadi-floor locations studied through a number of human, biological and geomorphic systems”.

More recent studies at Faynan, however, argued that the spread of the metal contaminants was much more limited and did not have the far-reaching consequences that were previously postulated, neither for the environment nor for the ancient or modern populations16–19. They criticized the previous studies based on the fact that the majority of evidence for metal contamination was obtained either directly from the waste piles or from areas where metallurgical waste was present. They also argued that previous data from remains of human bones are not reliable because of possible post-burial diagenesis.

Here we present results of high-resolution geochemical surveys of two pre-Roman smelting sites located in the copper ore district of Timna Valley (southern Israel). While our emphasis lies in examining the spatial distribution of copper and lead as markers indicative of metallurgical activities and smelting-related pollution, we have also mapped additional elements (e.g. phosphorus and potassium) that can shed light on other, non-metalliferous human activities, such as refuse dumping and livestock penning. We further conducted limited-scale excavations at one of the sites to validate our interpretation of the geochemical observations.

Research area

Timna Valley, located in the southern Arabah, on the western margin of the Dead Sea Rift Valley, holds the second largest copper ore deposits in the southern Levant (Fig. 1). Systematic archaeological investigation of the region, which began over 60 years ago by Beno Rothenberg and the “Arabah Expedition”, has identified thousands of mining shafts and several copper smelting sites that date as far back as the Chalcolithic period (5th millennium BCE). Numerous studies have highlighted Timna as a key region for understanding ancient copper production technologies in the Near East and beyond20–24. The current study is part of the Central Timna Valley Research Project (CTV), which has conducted excavations and surveys in the region since 2012 (https://www.tau.ac.il/~ebenyose/CTV/;25).

One of the main conclusions of the recent project is related to the chronology of the major smelting sites in the valley. Radiocarbon and magnetic dating indicate that the peak in production activities took place during the early Iron Age (11th–9th c. BCE), and not during the era of the Egyptian New Kingdom as was previously assumed26,27. In the copper ore district of Wadi Faynan, located ca. 100 km north of Timna, the peak in activity also took place during the early Iron Age28, suggesting a socio-political link between the two regions22,29.

Timna is located in an extreme arid zone characterized by short rain episodes and an average annual rainfall of 25 mm30. Such arid conditions lead to a slow rate of erosion, thus preserving archaeological remains and pollution deposits in a more pristine state. In addition, the lack of significant vegetation renders the archaeological record easily accessible for detailed research. Wadi Faynan is also located in an arid zone, though the average annual rainfall there is somewhat higher, exceeding 50 mm, and local vegetation is more abundant and diverse31.

Site 34 (“Slaves’ Hill”)

Site 34 is located on top of an isolated ~ 3 hectare sandstone mesa, rising nearly 20 m above the surrounding wadi bed in the heart of Timna Valley. There is almost no vegetation in the poor natural soils on the top of the mesa which is comprised of a mixture of loess and eroding Lower Cretaceous kaolinitic quartz sandstone of the Amir Formation (Fig. 2)32.

Site 34, one of the largest copper smelting sites at Timna27, is also called “Slaves’ Hill” after Nelson Glueck’s description of the site as “a prison for forced labor”34. Indeed, this hilltop seemed to bear all the marks of an Iron Age slave camp—fiery furnaces, harsh desert conditions, and a massive barrier preventing escape. Small scale excavations at the site commenced in 2013, focusing on several of the slag mounds, stone-built installations and the gatehouse complex (Fig. 3)27,35,36. The excavation results have overturned the somewhat ‘romantic’ narrative of slaves: the people working on the hill held elite status, as clearly indicated by the quality of their food, clothing, and other belongings. This is not surprising, as in contrast to the mines, the metalworkers of Slaves’ Hill were responsible for one of the most sophisticated technologies of the time, holding the knowledge of how to transform stone into metal37.

The site was only occupied for a short period of about a century during the early Iron Age (late 11th–10th centuries BCE27. This, together with its location on an isolated mesa, makes the site ideal for serving as a test case for the study of ancient metalliferous pollution, as there can be no confusion with dating or the correlation between soils on the hill and the archaeological remains there.

Site 201

Site 201 is ~ 0.9 hectares in size and located ~ 5.5 km north of the center of Timna Valley, on the western outskirts of the Wadi Arabah (Fig. 1). It contains a small scatter of stone structures surrounding a small knoll made of Upper Cretaceous limestone (Fig. 2). Apart from the knoll, the site is generally flat and consists of alluvial soils (ancient wadi terraces) with sediment from nearby escarpments. Overall, the archaeological remains have not been significantly disturbed by natural erosion, but the site is cut by a modern dirt road that has noticeably impacted certain areas (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Aerial view of Site 201. Excavation units marked in red and yellow, for the CTV Project and the Arabah Expedition respectively.

Aerial view of Site 201. Excavation units marked in red and yellow, for the CTV Project and the Arabah Expedition respectively.

The site was first surveyed and excavated as part of the “Arabah Expedition” project led by Rothenberg. He identified several round stone structures around the small knoll, suggesting they represent residential and domestic activities. Although random slag fragments were found at various points in the site, the majority of slag is scattered on the knoll, where traces of simple furnaces were also found. The site was dated to the Early Bronze Age (EBA, 3rd millennium BCE)38,39 and is considered a typical smelting camp from this period in the Arabah, in which the local northern wind was harnessed to operate the furnaces. As part of the renewed excavations of the CTV project, Site 201 was resurveyed and partially excavated in several areas during the 2020 season. The excavations focused on various points around the knoll and on the knoll itself. The current geochemical survey was conducted parallel to the excavations, prior to opening up most of the excavation areas. In terms of the archaeological interpretation, the new excavations presented a similar picture to Rothenberg’s findings regarding the division between smelting activity on the knoll and more domestic activity surrounding it, with the addition of the identification of several workshops for ore and slag processing. The new excavations also yielded some human skeletal remains (one skeleton was also found in Rothenberg’s excavations). The findings and conclusions from the new excavations will be published elsewhere.

Methods

Soil samples were collected from most of the Site 34 mesa area, and all of Site 201, excluding places affected by modern disturbances. In both sites bedrock exposures or areas with less than five centimeters of topsoil cover, as well as excavation areas and debris piles, were not sampled. Samples were taken at intervals of about 5 m, resulting in a loose 5 × 5 m grid that also reflects the above-mentioned sampling limitations (Fig. 5, Fig. A in Appendix 1). At each sampling point approximately 10–20 g of soil was collected in airtight plastic bags from approximately 5 centimeters below the surface. The precise sampling location was recorded using a Leica Zeno GG03 Global Differential Positioning System (dGPS), equipped with a Zeno 5 GNSS smart antenna, connected to the real-time kinematic network of “Etkes SpiderNET”. Three base stations within a 30-kilometer radius, allowed for a sub-centimeter spatial resolution.

Fig. 5Aerial imagery of Sites 34 & 201 showing the exact locations from which we collected the soil samples for the current study.

Aerial imagery of Sites 34 & 201 showing the exact locations from which we collected the soil samples for the current study.

The preparation of samples and chemical analysis were conducted in the Archaeometallurgical Laboratory at Tel Aviv University. The effect of soil humidity on the measurements is assumed to be negligible, as the samples were collected in a hyper-arid environment where soil humidity is less than 30%. The sample preparation process included the following steps: all particles larger than sand size (> 2 mm) were removed from the soil samples with sieves, next the samples were crushed using a mortar and pestle made of agate. Finally, each sample was homogenized by mixing and compressed into a labelled 32 mm high density polyethylene double open ended sample cup with 4.0 μm polypropylene film used for sealing the top and bottom (Fig. B in Appendix 1).

Chemical analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific Niton XLt-900 XRF Analyzer equipped with a GOLDD X-ray fluorescence detector and real-time digital signal processing at a speed of 80 MHz. The XRF measurements were taken in the laboratory, using a Thermo Scientific SmartStand, ensuring consistent positioning. Since the Niton pXRF is programmed to utilize predefined matrices provided by the manufacturer, the “General Mining” matrix, which includes the relevant metallic and non-metallic elements, was selected for the current research. Calibration was performed using an internal calibration system defined by the manufacturer and validated using a known standard (SRM 2780). The test duration for each sample was 150 s, employing four filters (the run time for the ‘main’ and ‘low’ filters was set at 45 s, while the ‘light’ and ‘high’ filters had a run time of 30 s) capable of detecting elements heavier than sodium (Na) and lighter than uranium (U), with official manufacturer detection limits for the elements of concern ranging between 12 and 30 ppm.

The analytical results together with the spatial information (utilizing the Israeli Transverse Mercator coordinate system) are provided in Appendix 2. In readings where a specific element concentration was found to be below the detection limit of the device, the result was entered as 0. Spatial analysis and mapping were performed using ESRI ArcMap 10.4.1, employing inverse distance weighted (IDW) interpolation. There are two interpolation methods commonly used for creating models in geochemical surveys: IDW and Kriging. Several studies have compared these methods, and it appears that in cases of high sampling resolution and fewer than a thousand variables (as in the current case), there is a slight advantage for using IDW (e.g.40,41) and therefore, this method was chosen for the current research. The symbols were assigned to the tension values of minimum-maximum along a color gradient.

Results

All analytical results are presented in weight% (Wt.%). Twelve elements were consistently identified in almost all samples (Si, S, Ca, Cu, Al, Zn, P, K, Pb, Fe, Mn, Ti). Among these, copper (Cu), lead (Pb), and iron (Fe) are potential indicators of metallurgical activities, while potassium (K), phosphorus (P), and calcium (Ca) are potential indicators of other human activities (e.g.42). To visualize soil contamination, the concentrations of these elements were mapped in relation to topography and prominent archaeological features at each site. The correlation coefficients in the current results are calculated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, with n = 348 at site 34 and n = 149 at site 201.

Site 34

It is noticeable that copper (average (Avg = 0.38, median Mdn = 0.201, standard deviation SD = 0.69) and lead values (Avg = 0.026, Mdn = 0.015, SD = 0.044) measured at Site 34 decrease significantly at the southern part of the hilltop (Fig. 6). This observation is in accord with the distribution of other markers of human activity, such as pottery sherds, slag mounds, and stone structures, which are mainly concentrated in the northern two-thirds of the mesa27,36. It is noteworthy that a high correlation coefficient measured between Cu and Pb (r = 0.88) indicates a common source, which means that both are derivatives of similar metallurgical materials and activities (this point is further elaborated upon below).

Fig. 6Spatial distribution of copper and lead at Site 34.

Spatial distribution of copper and lead at Site 34.

Most of the measured iron (Avg = 2.33, Mdn = 1.88, standard deviation SD = 1.89) and calcium values (Avg = 6.65, Mdn = 6.38, SD = 2.7) correspond to the average abundance of these elements in typical desert soils43. However, a specific pattern can be identified in the spatial distribution of these element concentrations. It appears that iron concentrations are slightly higher at the northern part of the hilltop (Fig. 7), but this pattern is not as distinct in comparison to the patterns of copper and lead. Additionally, the correlation coefficient between iron and copper (r = 0.66) is lower than that measured between copper and lead. During the Iron Age, iron oxides were deliberately added to copper smelting furnaces in the Arabah as fluxing agents22,23. It is plausible that higher iron concentrations indicate the whereabouts of flux crushing workshops or flux storage.

Like iron, calcium is also relatively abundant in desert soils. However, elevated calcium concentrations recorded at different parts of the hilltop (Fig. 7) indicate enrichment from non-natural sources. The correlation coefficient between calcium and potassium (r = 0.45) is much higher than calcium with copper (r = 0.02), iron (r = 0.216), and phosphorus (r = 0.074). The elevated calcium concentrations seem to primarily indicate places where limestone was brought to the hill and used for construction of architectural features or for use as tools (hammers, anvils, etc., see44). It is noteworthy that slag from later periods in the Arabah is rich in calcium, but Iron Age slag generally contains only 1–2 Wt.%45, far below the recorded concentrations at the site.

It can be observed that phosphorus concentrations (Avg = 0.304, Mdn = 0.292, SD = 0.164) are significantly lower in the southern portion of the hilltop in comparison with the northern part (Fig. 8). The correlation coefficients between phosphorus and copper (r = 0.13) and between phosphorus and lead (r = 0.17) are low and indicate separate sources. This suggests, as expected, that phosphorus indicates non-metallurgical human activity areas, most likely locations where organic waste accumulated. On the other hand, potassium concentrations (Avg = 0.596, Mdn = 0.588, SD = 0.286) indicate a slightly different trend compared to phosphorus concentrations. High potassium concentrations were mainly measured at the center of the hill, correlating with concentrations of stone structures. The correlation coefficients between potassium and copper (r = 0.19) and between potassium and phosphorus are low (r = 0.13).

Site 201

The copper concentrations (Avg = 0.046, Mdn = 0.017, SD = 0.089) and the lead concentrations (Avg = 0.0005, Mdn = ~ 0, SD = 0.002) measured at site 201 were very low in general, and an order of magnitude lower than the values of Site 34 (above). Relatively high concentrations of both elements were observed on the knoll itself and in a few locations around it (Fig. 9), and the correlation coefficient between these elements is relatively high (r = 0.75), indicating a common source, mainly on the knoll itself. As mentioned earlier, archaeological observations suggest that the main metallurgical activity at the site took place on the knoll, where the wind-operated furnaces were located39. As the furnaces were most probably built at the top, facing north, it is evident that a natural, gentle gravitational sliding of soil containing lead and copper from the top of the knoll to its slopes did indeed occur, although on a limited scale.

Fig. 9Spatial distribution of copper and lead at Site 201.

Spatial distribution of copper and lead at Site 201.

The measured iron concentrations (Avg = 1.511, Mdn = 1.483, SD = 0.432) fall within the normal range for soils in the area; however, a discernible trend is evident in the data (Fig. 10). It is noticeable that higher iron quantities are found in the northern parts of the site. The correlation coefficient between iron and copper is low (r = 0.33), and it does not seem that the iron dispersion is primarily influenced by slag concentrations. Smelting at Site 201 did not rely on deliberate addition of iron oxides as flux, but rather on self-fluxing copper ore, which was most probably a natural mix of copper and iron minerals. Thus, elevated iron concentrations might reflect locations of ore processing, including beneficiation and preparation of the smelt. The high calcium concentrations (Avg = 11.24, Mdn = 10.66, SD = 3.511) are not surprising considering the nature of the rock and soil at the site (see above). The high presence of calcium on the knoll directly stems from the limestone bedrock, whose soil cover is thinner there. Interestingly, the correlation coefficient between iron and calcium is unusually low (r = 0.07), providing an additional indication that the source of iron at specific points at the site is not natural.

Fig. 10Spatial distribution of iron and calcium at Site 201.

Spatial distribution of iron and calcium at Site 201.

In the distribution of potassium (Avg = 0.522, Mdn = 0.519, SD = 0.151) and phosphorus (Avg = 0.276, Mdn = 0.275, SD = 0.05), no clear patterns can be identified (Fig. 11). Additionally, the correlation coefficient between the two elements is very low (r = 0.11). These observations suggest that the main source of both elements is natural. An exception to this is probably the elevated concentrations of both elements at the southern foot of the knoll, where several stone structures are found. This supports the interpretation of these structures as livestock pens, although admittedly the observation is not robust.

Test excavations at Site 34

To assess the effectiveness of the current soil survey, the CTV project opened two excavations probes in Area 16 along the northeastern edge of Site 34 in January 2023, one at the location of elevated concentrations of copper and lead (“Square 16A”), and the other at the location of elevated concentrations of phosphorus (“Square 16B”) (Fig. 12).

Top: Aerial view of Site 34 Area 16 following excavations, with excavation areas labelled. Bottom: Location of excavation areas in relation to concentrations of phosphorus (left) and copper (right).

The excavations in Area 16 were conducted following the same methods applied in previous excavations at the site27. They reveal diverse human and animal related activities. The chronological relationship between the activity in Squares A and B is uncertain, but they are most likely contemporary. All stratigraphic evidence from these squares indicates that each of them represents continuous usage during a single period with no major chronological gaps. The overall material record (ceramics and slag in particular, but also organic remains) from Area 16 is consistent with previously excavated material from Site 34 and accordingly, the chronological frame of the activities in Area16 is most likely consistent with the rest of the site (late 11th–10th c. BCE, see above). A detailed report on the results of the excavations is provided in Appendix 3.

Square 16A

Site 34, Square 16A was a 3 × 5 m excavation area which was laid out in order to create a section at the point of greatest deposition in slag mound 16, that amounted to a maximum of ~ 90 cm from the surface to the bedrock, and to expose archaeological remains at the location of elevated lead and copper concentrations (up to 0.4% Pb and 6.1% Cu, which are among the highest values documented at the site, Figs. 6 and 12). Excavations made it possible to clarify the material culture associated with the chemical findings (Fig. C in Appendix 1).

The choice of an excavation area is usually a matter of “informed guess” with the hope of finding a desired outcome. In the case of Square 16A the relationship between the observable surface evidence and elevated copper and lead levels found in the chemical mapping was obvious because it is a slag mound that represents copper smelting activities. While, in general, excavating a slag mound presents an opportunity for an attempted exploration of the smelting process, the exact location of metallurgical installations and specifically furnaces usually cannot be determined by observations of surface finds. Square 16A was deliberately opened in the eastern side of the slag mound, where the mapped concentrations of copper and lead were the highest, potentially representing the location of such installations. Seven distinct horizons were identified along section C1-C4 in the process of this excavation (Fig. D in Appendix 1).

The uppermost layer (Horizon 1) covered the entire square and was a combination of slag and loess mixed into the depth that it had been driven by the wind which varied between 2 and 12 cm. Horizon 2 was limited to the southernmost third of the square and was composed mainly of reddish sediment (80%) with the remainder being a combination of slag and sandstones. Typical metallurgical smelting waste was found within this matrix. Although this deposition was a maximum of 14 cm, in many areas the bedrock or naturally accumulated sediment was found directly below. Horizon 3 found in the northernmost two thirds of the square and was primarily grayish ashy sediment and slag pieces (up to 10 cm in diameter) with about 10% sandstones and was up to 56 cm thick also in large areas extending down to naturally accumulated sediment. Here again, typical finds within this matrix are commonly found in such a waste heap. Items associated with the metallurgical process include charcoal, copper ore, tuyère, flint, stone anvil, ceramic, and wood. Items associated with workers’ meals include bone, date seeds and fish bones. A relatively small lens of gray ash with coarse sand size slag and charcoal (Horizon 4) was found within an 80 cm radius of the northwestern corner. This lens was about 10 cm thick and also contained the same typical finds as the horizons above. This lens was on naturally accumulated sediment and may be related to secondary smelting activity. Horizon 5 had only few finds within a loess and sandstone matrix. This horizon was only found north and west of Feature 3312 (a low semi-circular stone construction on the bedrock) with an average thickness of 20 cm. Although a portion of this layer was excavated to bedrock, most of it was left in place. Horizon 6 is located in the southwestern corner of the excavation area which was a crudely constructed stone feature (F.3314) about 90 × 100 cm that extends out of the baulks and was not removed. Horizon 7 is the sandstone bedrock.

Other significant contexts not captured in section C1–C4 discussed above include L.3314, a sandy loess layer above the bedrock in the southern end of the excavation area, and L.3317, a lens of fine red sediment rich in metallurgical remains and charcoal in corner C2. While most of this locus remains in the baulk and outside the excavation area, we were able to expose a suggestively shaped flat sandstone and several furnace wall fragments that probably indicate the original location of a furnace installation (Fig. E in Appendix 1). It might be that some of the original furnace structure is still found intact in the unexcavated area (within the baulk).

The remains found in Square 16A include a smelting workshop that was cleared down to bedrock through a layer of natural sediment by the metalsmiths. Small stone features (short walls?) were constructed to delineate the working area and a few installations were constructed. The smelting by-product (slag) was discarded near the work area to the northwest (corner C4). Reddish sediment was brought up on top of the plateau, probably for the purpose of being used for the construction of metallurgical installations and is found distinctly separated on the southern side of this square. The probable location of a furnace was also found in the southeastern corner of the square, in an area associated with the highest concentrations of copper and lead as documented in the geochemical soil survey.

Square 16B

Site 34, Area 16, Square B was a 1.5 × 1.5 m probe reaching a maximum depth of ~ 0.5 m from the surface to bedrock, situated a few meters south of Square 16A (Fig. F in Appendix 1). The location of this test probe was ideal for testing the material remains associated with the elevated concentration of phosphorus found in the soil survey because the surface character (a matrix of slag, loess and sandstones) provided no indication of any organic remains. Stated another way, there was no obvious signs of activities such as refuse dumping and livestock penning in the entire area that would account for an elevated phosphorus concentration.

The source of the phosphorus concentration that was completely hidden from the human eye when looking at the pre-excavation area, began to reveal itself a few centimeters below the surface. Once the thin loess, slag and sandstone matrix (Horizon 1) was removed from the top which had been deposited there by a combination of aeolian and erosional forces, a gray ashy layer appeared (Horizon 2) up to 15 cm thick (Fig. G in Appendix 1), with charcoal, wood, bone and other artifacts related to copper smelting activities. This ashy layer probably represents burnt remains from the upper portion of the layer below it. The conclusive evidence for the source of the elevated phosphorus signature came in the third layer (Horizon 3), a relatively thick dense layer (up to 33 cm) of fine organic rich material located on the western half of the probe. While Horizon 3 was largely composed of decomposed dung, there were nevertheless plenty of examples of intact dung from more than one type of animal. Other organic materials included wood, charcoal, bone, hair, textiles, cords, date seed, grape pips, and other seeds, plant, and insect remnants. Remains from the metallurgical process were also common (it should be noted that those are abundant throughout the site). Horizon 4, which had a fine black charcoally composition and was mainly located on the eastern half of the probe, was up to 22 cm thick but also extended in a thin layer below Horizon 3 on the western half of the probe. In addition to the charcoal and ash it was also very rich in a wide variety of organic finds such as dung, cord, date and other seeds, other plant remains, wood, bone, fish bone hair, leather, tooth and ostrich shell. There was also an assortment of non-organic finds including copper ore, glassy slag, flint, shale, tuyère fragments, and ceramics. A matrix mainly composed of loess and pieces of sandstone (Horizon 5) was found below Horizons 3 and 4. Horizon 5 had finds similar to those mentioned in the overlying horizons, but in lesser amounts that decreased in proportion until the sandstone bedrock (Horizon 6) was reached.

It can be confidently concluded that the material remains recovered from square 16B represent refuse dumping and was either a place where animals were penned directly or near where animals were penned. These excavation results confirm that what was originally invisible to the human eye was clearly made “visible” by the chemical results of the soil survey.

Discussion

Ancient metalliferous pollution

Sites 201 and 34 represent two different periods of copper production in the region of Timna Valley, which are notably different in scale based on slag quantities. This study reveals that this difference is also reflected in the pollution levels of soils in typical sites, where heavy metal (Cu/Pb) concentrations at the Iron Age Site 34 are an order of magnitude higher than those recorded in the Early Bronze Age Site 201. Correlations between lead and copper support a shared source related to human activity. While both sites exhibit metal pollution primarily around slag mounds, the impact radius at Site 34—the distance between pollutants in soil and visible archaeological features – surpasses that of Site 201, consistent with the scale of activity. However, it should be noted that even at Site 34, the documented impact remains limited to a radius of at most a few tens of meters.

Previous studies have indicated that lead can remain on the soil surface at depths ranging from a few to several tens of centimetres for hundreds and even thousands of years (e.g.46,47). The reason for this is the low solubility of lead, which creates a situation where lead is not washed downward even in highly humid areas. Moreover, lead tends to remain in the uppermost surface layer48 and if mobilized, it will be mainly through natural precipitation runoff49. Considering that the Timna Valley receives very little rainfall, it seems unlikely that any substantial amount of lead would dissolve and sink into the subsoil, let alone reaching the water table in the region. From a hydrological perspective, in both sites examined in the current research, the local hydrology is well understood, and in neither case was there a significant enrichment of lead (or copper) in the runoff areas. Therefore, it can be inferred that the soil composition picture in the current research, which is based on samples taken close to the soil surface, is very similar (if not identical) to what one would have expected to see thousands of years ago.

To assess the possible impact on the ancient populations working in the copper industry, we should consider primarily the distribution of lead, which is poisonous to humans50. Generally, lead could have reached the production sites’ soils in two ways: within fragments of processed or weathered ore, slag, tuyères and other metallurgical installations, or as aerosols generated from the smelting process. Either way, the primary pollution would be found in the soil close to the surface of the site. Previous studies of lead contamination in historical metallurgical sites have yielded conflicting findings as to the aerosols fallout portion of the lead contamination51,52. The nature of our results, i.e. the very high correlation between lead and copper, implies that pollution originating from copper industries of the Bronze and Iron Ages is not significantly affected by aerosols emitted from smelting, or else we would not expect to observe high correlation factors between lead and copper. Accordingly, it seems that the main pollution comes from the crushing of ore and slag (for evidence of ore and slag crushing at Site 34 see44). This conclusion suggests that the impact of Bronze and Iron Ages smelting on air quality was minimal, with possible airborne metalliferous pollution limited to a few meters or less from the smelting furnaces. It also suggests that the documented heavy metals (Pb/Cu) have a rather low bioavailability, as they are mostly encapsulated in fragments of metallurgical waste where they would stay even if digested by humans or animals. In this form they are also not available to plants, including those that were used as food for livestock and humans working in the mines, or as fuel for food preparation.

Grattan et al.53,54 assert that the enduring impact of ancient metalliferous pollution persists within contemporary Bedouin communities residing in proximity to ancient smelting industries. They highlight discernible traces of contamination that persist to the present day in groundwater, soils, livestock, and crops. The present findings, demonstrating discrete enrichment of heavy metals within the soil, support the conclusions of Knabb and others16,17 who cast doubt on the claim that ancient mining and metallurgical industries necessarily disrupted the harmony and natural balance in their surroundings. The contribution of naturally occurring metallic depositions to the enrichment of groundwater with metalliferous elements is also noteworthy (e.g.55). The groundwater level in Timna and Faynan is situated beneath the geological strata containing naturally occurring copper ore (with traces of lead)23,32,56. This geological setup potentially exerts a far greater influence on the presence of heavy metals within subterranean waters compared to ancient anthropogenic related activities. Based on the up-to-date information from Faynan and Timna, it can be assumed that even the most intensive exploitation of copper resources in these regions during antiquity, as evident in the peak production at the beginning of the Iron Age, caused minimal regional metalliferous related pollution, incomparable to modern industrial activities.

Several studies have shown intercontinental atmospheric-scale lead (Pb) enrichment dating back to pre-Roman times3, though this atmospheric enrichment is notably limited to the Northern Hemisphere (see recently57). These studies also indicate that this phenomenon grew dramatically during the Roman period when lead consumption and production increased significantly. However, shifts in lead emissions to the atmosphere, as well as the quantities recorded in lacustrine and peat bogs deposits, should be considered in the context of lead (and silver) production alone (contra e.g.4), and definitely not used as a direct tool to assess changes in the production scale of other metals. Likewise, correlating evidence of ancient copper industries with atmospheric lead pollution records should be avoided or approached with caution (contra e.g.9).

Another common confusion in research on ancient metalliferous pollution is the use of the term “pollution” to refer to any documented anthropogenic increase in heavy metals (e.g.58). The term should be reserved specifically for instances where the increase is harmful to the bioenvironment or human health, which depends on the concentration levels of the metals and their bioavailability. For example, by the strict standards of the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, https://www.epa.gov/lead), only Pb concentrations of > 1200 ppm or > 200 ppm are considered a health hazard when found in soils of industrial or residential areas respectively (the latter was reduced from 400ppm on July 2024 based on the most current research on the risk for young children). While changes in heavy metal concentrations in sedimentary records can be useful for tracking local-to-regional changes in metal production industries4,8,59, including detecting early, small-scale activities60,61, this does not necessarily imply a harmful impact on the environment or the general population in the studied area (the study by Zhang et al.61, for example, is based on changes of only a few ppm in Pb concentrations: from 3.43 ± 0.8 in the natural soil to 3.4 ± 0.91 in the late 3rd millennium BCE, to 9.68 ± 4.52 in the 2nd millennium BCE, to 3.93 ± 0.84 in the 1st millennium BCE). In other words, it is crucial to explicitly distinguish between using heavy metal records as a tool to track ancient metal production industries and the discussion on the polluting effects these ancient industries had on local societies, let alone on the global environment.

The only available data on the possible impact of pre-Roman copper industries on the global environment—specifically, evidence of intercontinental atmospheric Cu pollution—comes from the study of Hong et al.5 on ice cores from Greenland. However, this study has been noted to have measurement issues concerning both heavy metal concentrations and chronology, and the elevated copper levels were not replicated in subsequent studies by other teams3. Therefore, this record cannot be considered reliable evidence, and previously postulated correlations should be rejected.

Research on the environmental impact of modern metallurgical industries has yielded clear and alarming results. Studies on mines, smelters and industrial centers have shown a strong connection between proximity to the pollution source and harmful concentrations of lead in soils and sediments (e.g.62,63). Research conducted in the vicinity of abandoned copper smelters in Limni, Cyprus, found high concentrations of pollutants, leading to recommendations to prohibit any agricultural activity in the area64. These studies concluded that modern copper smelters and slag mounds should be considered severe environmental threats since significant amounts of heavy metals can disperse in their surroundings through dust and soil erosion, affecting water sources, soils, and ecological systems. Perhaps such studies were the inspiration of dramatic reconstructions of ancient copper industries as environmentally catastrophic. However, careful examination of published data (e.g. skeletal remains from Iron Age Faynan18) and current evidence suggest a very limited impact, with no health-related threat to most members of the population working at the copper production centers, at least in the pre-Roman era.

Geochemical survey as an archaeological tool

Additional elements related to non-metallurgical anthropogenic activity were found at both sites in higher concentrations than the averages for desert soils43. These elements include phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and calcium (Ca), which are associated with the accumulation of bones, organic waste and ashes (e.g.42,65). These can be considered as the result of everyday human activities. Plant remains and animal dung were common fuel sources during the Bronze and Iron Ages in the Southern Levant and beyond35,66,67, and the accumulation of ash due to food processing, heating, and other domestic and industrial activities have been documented in many other ancient sites in the southern Levant (e.g.68).

It should be noted that the accuracy of pXRF in measuring light (low Z) elements is often challenged69. However, to address questions regarding the detection and characterization of activity areas in archaeological sites, it is often sufficient to detect spatial changes in concentrations of these elements rather than to document their exact quantities, and for this pXRF is quite efficient.

Phosphorus remains at Site 34 seem to be associated, among other things, with donkeys and other livestock dung. Excavations on both sides of the gatehouse complex revealed a layer of dung that was interpreted as remains of a designated stables (or pens) located along the inner side of the site’s wall35. The geochemical survey clearly demonstrates higher concentrations of phosphorus in the areas adjacent to the gatehouse excavations. Extrapolation suggests that the stables extended further along the walls, and that livestock pens also existed in other locations at the site. Keeping the animals along the periphery of the site and not among the smelting furnaces and metallurgical workshops is logical, and indeed, high phosphorus values mostly appear near the edges of the site in the northern part of the hill. However, since phosphorus can originate from other organic sources (such as ash and bones), elevated levels can indicate other activities as well, most frequently perhaps are places of dumping organic refuse. The potential usefulness of phosphorus concentrations in the soil to delineate the locations of human activities related to animal keeping and organic refuse deposition was corroborated by the results of the excavation probe at Area 16B, where dung mixed with organic waste was exposed exactly in the location where elevated phosphorus concentrations were documented.

Regarding Site 201, it is possible that elevated phosphorus concentrations south of the knoll indicate the location of livestock pens, although the spatial patterns of this element and their association with stone features are not pronounced, making any interpretation difficult.

High concentrations of potassium were found in defined and relatively small spots in the northern part of Site 34 and in several areas at Site 201. At this stage of research, the nature of the activity they represent is not entirely clear, although it might be related to charcoal dust and ash produced as part of the smelting activities (there is no correlation to patterns of other elements, except partially to phosphorus in Site 34). In any case, it is evident that elevated potassium concentrations can serve as a general indication for anthropogenic activity.

Calcium is also associated with human activity at archaeological sites70. It can originate from food residues and plant remains as well as from microchips made of carbonaceous stones (either native or brought to the site). The distribution pattern at Site 34 indicates that it likely represents limestones brought to the site (see above). In Site 201, the discernment of anthropogenically induced calcium enrichment is masked by calcium derived from the limestone bedrock.

Other potential elements for identifying ancient human activities, such as sulfur and zinc (e.g.71), were not pronounced in the results of the survey and hence are not discussed here.

Conclusions

In the current study we mapped the concentrations of copper, lead, iron, phosphorus, potassium and calcium in two smelting sites in Timna Valley, Site 34 and Site 201, dated to the early Iron Age and Early Bronze Age respectively. Test excavations at Site 34 corroborated our interpretation of the geochemical results: In Square 16A, where elevated concentrations of copper and lead were documented, we exposed rare remains of furnace walls, probably in direct proximity to the location of intact parts of the furnace installation. In Square 16B, where elevated concentrations of phosphorus were detected, we exposed rich layers of organic matter, including animal dung, bones and textiles.

Our study has two main implications:

Geochemical soil surveys in archaeology: Our results join previous studies in demonstrating the potential of high-resolution geochemical soil surveys for detecting and characterizing ancient human activities (cf70). Such surveys allow for a better understanding of the spatial organization of activities within a site. For example, we were able to show that refuse dumping and penning at Site 34 were concentrated along the inner side of the site’s wall. Moreover, we demonstrated that in certain cases geochemical surveys reveal the presence of anthropogenic remains also in areas where there are no other clear indications for ancient human activities. This makes such surveys a potent tool for demarcation of ancient sites boundaries, or even for the mere detection of archaeological sites, especially those associated with archaeologically inconspicuous societies such as nomads72,73.Polluting impact of ancient metalliferous industries: We documented the extent of metalliferous pollution in Timna by mapping the concentrations of copper and lead in representative smelting sites of the Bronze and Iron Ages. While we found that the scale of pollution on the site level increased in an order of magnitude between these two periods, the documented pollution was discrete in both sites and in direct association with the distribution of slag and other metallurgical artifacts. The direct correlation between the distribution of lead and copper suggests that, even in areas where these metals are present, their bioavailability was likely minimal, as they are most probably sequestered within metallurgical waste. Based on these observations and a scrutiny of the currently available literature, we conclude that the polluting effect of pre-Roman copper industries on the environment, including the direct vicinity of copper production sites, was minimal (cf. e.g.16; contra e.g.9,10,15,54).

Our conclusions join an ongoing discussion on the polluting impact of ancient metal production industries. While, to some extent, human exploitation of metal ore has always been a polluting activity, the available data suggest that copper exploitation was a health hazard only for those working directly at the furnaces. This might have changed with the increased scale of production during the Roman period, but we need more research that takes into account the differences in copper and lead (and other metals) industries in order to know if this was indeed the case.
