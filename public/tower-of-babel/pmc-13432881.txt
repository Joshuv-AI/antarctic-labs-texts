Cryptosporidium parvum CpARO marks rhoptry envelope remnants associated with feeder organelle formation
Authors: Ying Zhang, Chenchen Wang, Xin Zhao, Xiaodong Wu, Jigang Yin, Guan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTApicomplexans encode a single armadillo repeat-only (ARO) protein, exemplified by TgARO and PfARO, that anchors to the rhoptry envelope through N-terminal acylation and supports rhoptry positioning through interaction with an ARO-interacting protein (AIP). These AROs organize rhoptries but are not known to be secreted during invasion. Here, we show that Cryptosporidium parvum ARO (CpARO) localizes to the rhoptry envelope by immunofluorescence assay, ultrastructural expansion microscopy, and structured illumination microscopy. During sporozoite invasion, CpARO-positive rhoptry envelope structures shorten and condense into a discrete punctum after content discharge. Residual rhoptry membrane structures are subsequently detected between the host cell F-actin pad and parasite nucleus in developing trophozoites, consistent with a contribution to nascent feeder organelle formation. In contrast to TgARO and PfARO, CpARO is also detected in secreted fractions during excystation, gliding, invasion, and intracellular development, with no evidence of nuclear localization. Recombinant CpARO binds host cells with high affinity (Kd = 0.189 μM). Although C. parvum encodes an AIP homolog, this protein localizes to the sporozoite cytoplasm rather than to rhoptries; we therefore designate it AIP-like protein (CpAIP-L). Antibodies against both CpARO and CpAIP-L were detected in sera from C. parvum-infected mice. These findings reveal functional divergence of Cryptosporidium ARO-AIP-related proteins and identify CpARO as both a rhoptry envelope marker and a secreted host-interacting factor with potential roles in host interaction and virulence.KEYWORDS: Cryptosporidium parvum, rhoptry envelope, armadillo repeat–only protein, feeder organelle formation, secretion, host–parasite interaction

Introduction

Cryptosporidium parvum is a zoonotic protozoan pathogen that infects the small intestinal epithelia of humans and a wide range of other mammals [1]. It is also a frequent cause of waterborne and foodborne outbreaks in human populations and opportunistic infections in immunocompromised individuals [1–3]. In resource-limited regions, C. parvum and C. hominis (mainly anthroponotic) are among the four major contributors to moderate-to-severe diarrhea in children under two years of age and are associated with stunted growth and a two- to three-fold higher risk of mortality [4,5]. In livestock, C. parvum causes neonatal diarrhea in calves, leading to mortality and significant reductions in weight gain [6–9].

Cryptosporidium is monoxenous, completing its life cycle within a single host. The oocyst, which contains four sporozoites, represents the environmentally resistant transmission stage responsible for fecal–oral spread between hosts. Following ingestion by a human or animal host, C. parvum oocysts release four sporozoites in the small intestine in response to elevated temperature and bile salts, a process termed excystation. Excysted sporozoites invade enterocytes to establish intracellular but extracytoplasmic development within a parasitophorous vacuole membrane (PVM) exposed to the intestinal lumen and separated from the host cytoplasm by an electron-dense band [1]. Intracellular development includes two to three rounds of asexual replication (merogony), producing eight merozoites that are released to infect new host cells, followed by sexual development leading to micro- and macrogamete formation and eventual production of mature oocysts.

The slender and curved sporozoites contain several subcellular structures specialized for invasion, including an apical complex composed of cytoskeletal elements and secretory organelles. These secretory organelles include numerous micronemes that discharge proteins (MICs) during gliding motility and invasion, a single rhoptry that releases effectors onto or into host cells upon attachment and invasion, and dense granules and small granules (DGs and SGs) that discharge contents during invasion [1]. The secreted molecules are essential or critical for sporozoite invasion, yet knowledge of their composition and functions remains limited. Earlier studies identified only a small number of proteins localized to these organelles. In recent years, both conventional screening approaches and high-resolution, mass spectrometry-based spatial proteomic (i.e. hyperplexed localization of organelle proteins by isotope tagging; hyperLOPIT)-based study have identified more than 150 secretory candidates, some of which have been validated by immunolabeling and/or in transgenic C. parvum lines [10].

The C. parvum rhoptry, present as a single club-shaped organelle in sporozoites [11], has been partially characterized. HyperLOPIT identified 16 rhoptry proteins, of which eight have been experimentally validated for rhoptry localization, including CpPRP1/CpRON1 (cgd8_2530) at the rhoptry tip/neck and CpROP1–CpROP7 (cgd3_1770, cgd3_1780, cgd3_1710, cgd3_1730, cgd1_950, cgd6_3630 and cgd5_20) in the rhoptry bulb [10,12,13]. A recent endogenous gene-tagging study also localized CpMUC1 to the rhoptry, while other mucin-family proteins were detected in distinct secretory organelles [14]. Several rhoptry proteins, including ROP1, ROP2, ROP4, and ROP7 have been shown to be injected into host cells, and ROP1 was found to interact with the host cytoskeletal modulator LMO7 [13].

Among the HyperLOPIT candidates whose rhoptry localization and functions had not been validated was an armadillo repeat-only (ARO) protein (cgd2_370), together with a SUSHI domain- and ankyrin repeats-containing protein (cgd4_2420), a TSP1 domain containing protein (cgd8_540), a palmitoyltransferase (cgd1_1380), and four uncharacterized proteins (cgd3_910, cgd3_2010, cgd1_1870 and cgd4_1790) [10]. Unlike secretory ROPs, ARO proteins in Toxoplasma gondii and Plasmodium falciparum (TgARO and PfARO) are rhoptry envelope proteins [15–18]. TgARO and PfARO also recruit an ARO-interacting protein (AIP) to the rhoptry envelope [15,16,19]. However, before the present study, neither ARO nor AIP ortholog had been characterized in Cryptosporidium, which is evolutionarily and metabolically divergent from Toxoplasma (coccidia) and Plasmodium (haemosporidia) [20,21].

Here, we characterized the C. parvum ARO protein (CpARO) and confirmed its localization to the rhoptry envelope. We further show that CpARO-positive residual rhoptry membrane structures persist after invasion and are associated with nascent feeder organelle formation. CpARO is also secreted during excystation, invasion, and intracellular development, and recombinant CpARO binds host cells with high affinity. We also show that the C. parvum AIP ortholog (CpAIP-L) is not localized to the rhoptry but instead resides in the sporozoite cytoplasm and is also secreted. Antibodies against both CpARO and CpAIP-L were detected in sera from C. parvum-infected mice. These findings provide clues on the origin of feeder organelle, and indicate that CpARO and CpAIP-L differ fundamentally from canonical apicomplexan ARO–AIP systems, functioning not only at the rhoptry envelope but also as secreted factors with potential roles in host interaction and virulence.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Animal procedures complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Health of China. The animal use protocol was approved by the Animal Welfare and Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Zoonosis, Jilin University (AUP No. 2020-IZ-20 and No. 2024-IZ-00031).

Parasite materials

An isolate of C. parvum (gp60 subtype IIaA17G2R1) was propagated in calves, from which oocysts were isolated from feces using established sucrose gradient centrifugation–based protocols as described [22,23]. Purified oocysts were stored in PBS supplemented with 200 U/mL penicillin and 0.2 mg/mL streptomycin at 4°C.

Before excystation and host-cell infection, oocysts were treated with 10% bleach (v/v) on ice for 5 min, washed five times with PBS, and resuspended in the specified medium. All experiments used oocysts with an excystation rate > 80%.

Excystation was performed by incubating oocysts in RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.5% taurocholic acid at 37°C for 30–45 min, followed by one to three washes in RPMI-1640 medium. Excysted sporozoites were immediately used for immunofluorescence assay (IFA), western blot analysis, or other specified experiments.

In vitro culture of C. parvum

Host cells (HCT-8 cell line; ATCC #CCL-244) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Wisent Co., Nanjing, China; Cat. # 350–030 for RPMI-1640 and 086–150 for FBS). For preparation of invasion-stage parasites for IFA, host cells were seeded in 48-well plates containing glass coverslips pre-coated with poly-L-lysine and grown to ~60% confluence, followed by inoculation with freshly excysted sporozoites for 10–30 min (transformation of sporozoites into trophozoites) or 2.5 h (developing trophozoites) at 37°C in a CO2 incubator [24,25]. For preparation of intracellular parasites developed for longer periods, host cells were cultured on coverslips to ~80% confluence, followed by inoculation with oocysts in RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.15% taurocholic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. # T4009). Parasites were allowed to excyst and invade at 37°C for 3 h. Uninvaded or unexcysted parasites were removed by replacing the medium with complete culture medium. Infected monolayers were cultured for 3–72 h post-infection (hpi) [26]. For detection of secreted parasite proteins, infections were performed similarly in 96-well plates without coverslips [25], and supernatants were collected at specified time intervals.

Production and purification of polyclonal antibodies against CpARO and CpAIP-L

Glutathione S-transferase (GST)–fused recombinant proteins were produced and used as immunogens to raise polyclonal antibodies in rabbits. The open reading frames of CpARO (cgd2_370) and CpAIP-L (cgd7_350), which contain no introns, were amplified from C. parvum genomic DNA by PCR using Phanta Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; Cat. # P515). The following primer pairs were used: 1) CpARO-F (cgc gga tcc ATG GGT AGT ACT CTT TCG T) and CpARO-R (ccg ctc gag TCA CTC AGC AAC TCT AGC); and 2) CpAIP-F (ccg cgt gga tcc ccg gaa ttc ATG CAG AAT ACT TTG GAT AAT AGT AAT TCA) and CpAIP-L-R (gtc acg atg cgg ccg ctc gag TTA GAT TTT TAA TTT CTC AAA ATC AAT TTT). Note: lowercase letters indicate extra bases added to facilitate cloning, with restriction site underlined.

PCR amplicons were unidirectionally cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector using T4 DNA ligase (2011 A; Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) for CpARO between BamHI and XhoI restriction sites, or by seamless cloning using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; Cat. # C115) for CpAIP-L at the EcoRI and XhoI restriction sites. Sequence-verified plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) (Weidi Co., Shanghai, China; Cat # EC1002) for heterologous expression.

Transformed bacteria were cultured in lysogeny broth at 37°C until OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16°C for 15 h. Bacterial pellets were collected by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 10 min and stored at − 80°C. Recombinant CpARO and CpAIP-L were purified using glutathione-Sepharose 4FF columns (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; Cat. # C600031) following standard procedures [27]. Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE and quantified by Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard.

Recombinant proteins were used as immunogens to produce polyclonal antibodies in five-week-old female rabbits using a four-injection immunization protocol [28]. The primary immunization used 300 μg recombinant protein emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. # F5506), followed by three booster injections (150 μg each) in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # F5881) at two-week intervals. Blood samples were collected two weeks after the final boost. Pre-immune sera were collected as negative controls.

Polyclonal antibodies were affinity-purified using antigen-immobilized nitrocellulose membranes as described [29,30]. Briefly, recombinant CpARO and CpAIP-L (0.2 mg in 0.3 mL PBS) were immobilized to the membranes (~1 cm2), blocked with 5% skim milk-TBST buffer, incubation with 4 mL antiserum (1:20 dilution in TBST) for 2 h, and washed three times with TBST. Anti-CpARO antiserum was also affinity-purified using a synthetic, CpARO-unique peptide (C)TKNVDKPESWY (aa 203–213) for comparison. Antibodies were eluted with antibody elution buffer (0.2 M glycine, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 2.7) and neutralized with 1.0 M Tris buffer (final pH 7.4). Purified antibodies were used directly for IFA or diluted as specified for western blotting and ELISA.

Immunofluorescence assay (IFA) and super-resolution microscopy

Free sporozoites were prepared by excystation as described above, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 50 min, and washed three times with PBS. Fixed parasites were placed on poly-L-lysine–coated glass slides and allowed to semi-dry at room temperature. Invading sporozoites and intracellular parasites were prepared on coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and washed with PBS. For gliding assays, excysted sporozoites were washed once in RPMI-1640 medium and applied either to poly-L-lysine–coated slides or to fixed host‑cell monolayers. Parasites were allowed to glide at 37°C for 30 min before fixation. Invading and intracellularly developing parasites were prepared as described above and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

Samples were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 5 min where indicated and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Affinity-purified anti-CpARO or anti-CpAIP-L antibodies were used as primary antibodies, followed by Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:2000 dilution) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. # A11008 and A11012). In selected experiments, rhodamine-phalloidin (1:20,000 dilution) (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. # CA1610) was used to label host F-actin, and fluorescein-conjugated Vicia villosa lectin (VVL) (1:2000 dilution) (Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA, USA; Cat. # FL-1231) was used to visualize the parasitophorous vacuole membrane. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # 10,236,276,001).

Slides were mounted in antifade medium and examined using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope. Images were captured in TIFF format and processed with linear contrast adjustments only. CpARO labeling in sporozoites and developing trophozoites was also examined by structured illumination microscopy (SIM) using a SIM-Ultimate super-resolution microscope (CSR Biotech, Guangzhou, China). For developing trophozoites, Z-stack SIM images were processed with IMARIS (Oxford Instruments) for three-dimensional volume and surface rendering.

Western blot analysis

Excysted C. parvum sporozoites were lysed by five freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen and ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing a mammalian protease inhibitor cocktail (Mei5bio, Beijing, China; Cat. # MF182-plus). Sporozoite lysates (1.0 × 107 parasites per lane) were mixed with loading buffer, heated at 95°C for 5 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes.

Membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk prepared in TBST buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20) for 1 h and probed with affinity-purified anti-CpARO or anti-CpAIP-L antibodies (1:200 dilution in TBST) for 1 h. After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000 dilution) (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. # 31,460), followed by signal development using Omni-ECL Enhanced Pico Light Chemiluminescence Kit (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China; Cat. # SQ101). All procedures were performed at room temperature unless otherwise specified.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for secreted CpARO and CpAIP-L

Secreted CpARO and CpAIP-L at different developmental stages of C. parvum were detected by ELISA as described [25,26]. The following samples were collected: 1) Excystation stage with medium collected at the end of excystation; 2) Invasion stage with medium collected at 3 h post-infection (0–3 hpi secretion); and 3) Intracellular development with medium collected between 3–6 hpi, 10–12 hpi, and 20–24 hpi. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium at the start of each interval to ensure that the samples contained only the proteins secreted during those specified time windows. Collected supernatants were conditioned with coating buffer (0.05 M carbonate–bicarbonate, pH 9.6) and coated onto 96-well plates in triplicate (50 µL/well) overnight at 4°C. Plates were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 and blocked with 3% BSA in PBS at 37°C for 1 h.

Affinity-purified anti-CpARO or anti-CpAIP-L antibodies (1:20 dilution) were added, followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000 dilution) (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. # 31,460), each incubated at 37°C for 1 h with washes between steps. Color development was performed using Supersensitive TMB Chromogen Solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. # P0208), and optical density was measured at 620 nm using a BioTek microplate reader (Winooski, VT, USA). Three independent experiments were performed, each including two or more biological replicates and two technical replicates.

Expansion microscopy

Subcellular localization of CpARO was further examined by ultrastructural expansion microscopy (U-ExM), which provides > 4-fold physical expansion of samples [10,31–33]. Excysted C. parvum sporozoites (~3 × 106) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and applied to poly-L-lysine–coated coverslips (13 mm). Coverslips were incubated in PBS containing 0.15% paraformaldehyde and 2% acrylamide (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # A9909) at 37°C for 5 h. Coverslips were then transferred onto droplets of cold U-ExM gel solution (19% sodium acrylate (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # 408,220), 10% acrylamide, 0.1% N,N’-Methylenebisacrylamide (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # m7279), 0.5% TEMED (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # T22500), 0.5% ammonium persulfate (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. # A6761) in PBS) and incubated on ice for 5 min followed by polymerization at 37°C for 1 h.

Samples were incubated in denaturation buffer (200 mM SDS, 200 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 9.0) at 70°C for 1 h. Gels were expanded by sequential incubations in water, equilibrated in PBS, and processed for immunolabeling as described for IFA. Expanded samples were co-stained with DAPI for nuclei and NHS-ester conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594-X (Duofluor, Wuhan, China; Cat. # D10111) for total protein labeling. Samples were mounted in double-distilled water and imaged using a SIM-Ultimate super-resolution microscope.

CpARO–host cell binding assay

The interaction between CpARO and host cells was evaluated by immunofluorescence assay and cell-based ELISA. In IFA-based binding assay, HCT-8 cells were cultured on poly-L-lysine–treated coverslips in 48-well plates to >80% confluence, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS, and blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h. Recombinant CpARO (3 µM) or GST tag control (3 µM) was added (100 µL/well) and incubated for 1 h, followed by PBS washes. Bound proteins were detected using anti-GST primary antibody (1:1000 dilution) (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China; Cat. # LF305) and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

In cell-based ELISA, host cells cultured in 96-well plates were fixed, blocked, and incubated with recombinant CpARO or GST at varying concentrations. Detection with anti-GST antibody (1:5000 dilution), HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, and colorimetric readout was performed as described above.

Detection of CpARO and CpAIP-L antibodies in mice infected with C. parvum

Three six-week-old wild-type C57BL/6 mice, purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China), were immunosuppressed with dexamethasone acetate administered in drinking water (15 µg/mL) for seven days. Two mice were then inoculated with 100,000 C. parvum oocysts each while dexamethasone treatment continued for four additional days. The third mouse was uninfected and used as a negative control. Fecal samples were collected from days 5–11 post-infection, and oocyst shedding was monitored by qPCR as described [34,35]. On day 21 post-infection, mice were anesthetized through intraperitoneal administration of tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg) (Leagene Biotechnology, Beijing, China; Cat. # R03001). Once anesthesia was confirmed by indicators such as stable deep respiration and absence of response to toe pinch or needle prick, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture and subsequently stored overnight at 4°C. Following blood collection, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation. Sera were isolated from the collected blood samples via centrifugation.

ELISA plates were coated with recombinant CpARO or CpAIP-L (10 µg/mL) overnight at 4°C. GST tag was used as a negative control antigen. Mouse sera (1:50 dilution) were applied, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution), color development, and OD measurement as described above. Assays were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data (ELISA and binding kinetics) were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical significance for antibody detection assays was assessed using Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Cryptosporidium parvum possesses a single ARO (CpARO) structurally conserved with TgARO and PfARO

Although armadillo-type fold (or ARM domain) are present in diverse proteins, the C. parvum genome only encodes a single armadillo repeat-only (CpARO) protein orthologous to PfARO and TgARO. CpARO was also one of the 16 rhoptry proteins identified by hyperLOPIT proteomics [10]. It consists of 273 amino acids (predicted molecular weight = 30.2 kDa), containing an N-terminal arm followed by 11 short α-helices arranged into a right-handed superhelical structure (Figure 1(A)). Structural modeling, based on the resolved PfARO structure (PDB entry 5EWP), indicates that the ARM-repeat superhelical architecture is highly conserved among CpARO, TgARO, and PfARO (Figure 1(B)).

(A) Structural modeling of CpARO by AlphaFold 3 highlighting ARM-repeat curvature and predicted membrane-association orientation. Residues at the N-terminal arm for N-myristoylation and palmitoylation are indicated. (B) Structural comparison between CpARO, TgARO (AlphaFold protein structure database entry: AF-A0A125YNN5-F1-v6) and PfARO (protein data Bank entry: 5EWP). (C) Alignment of N-terminal acylation motifs showing predicted myristoylation (Gly2) and palmitoylation cysteine residues conserved among apicomplexan ARO proteins. (D) Bayesian inference (BI) phylogenetic tree of ARO orthologs across representative apicomplexans and related alveolates. Posterior probability (PP) values are indicated at branch nodes. Two ARO paralogs are present in the chromerid Chromera velia, whereas most apicomplexans possess a single-copy ARO gene.

Like TgARO and PfARO, the N-terminal arm of CpARO contains motifs predicted for N-myristoylation (Gly2, following removal of the initiator methionine) and palmitoylation (two cysteine residues), which are required for membrane anchoring to the rhoptry envelope (Figure 1(A,C)) [17,36]. Bioinformatic analyses using SignalP and PredGPI did not predict a canonical N-terminal signal peptide, transmembrane domain, or GPI-anchor signal in CpARO. Thus, CpARO membrane association is likely mediated by N-terminal lipidation rather than by a conventional transmembrane helix or GPI anchor. Despite strong structural conservation, primary sequences of apicomplexan AROs are only moderately conserved, with ~40–60% sequence identity across major lineages (Supplementary Figure S1). These features suggest that preservation of structural conformation, rather than primary sequence identity, is critical for ARO function.

Genome mining across apicomplexans revealed that ARO orthologs are present as a single-copy gene in all examined species. Phylogenetic reconstruction using Bayesian inference grouped ARO orthologs according to established lineage relationships with robust posterior probability support (Figure 1(D); Supplementary Figure S2). In this tree, early-branching lineages such as Cryptosporidium and gregarines cluster basally, consistent with their evolutionary positions [20,21].

An exception was observed for the piroplasmids, which segregated from haemosporids and instead clustered between cryptosporidia and coccidia. The presence of a single ARO gene per species, together with lineage-based clustering, suggests a conserved and non-expanded functional role across apicomplexans. Notably, two ARO orthologs were identified in Chromera velia, a free-living photosynthetic chromerid closely related to apicomplexans (Figure 1(D)). This observation implies that ARO proteins predate the emergence of obligate parasitism and may have ancestral functions predating rhoptry specialization.

CpARO localizes to the rhoptry envelope with additional punctate distribution in sporozoites

Our original objective was to develop a molecular marker to facilitate investigation of secretory organelle biology in C. parvum. Because the primary sequence of CpARO shares no significant identities to any other proteins in the parasite and mammalian hosts, GST-fused recombinant protein (rCpARO) was expressed and used as an immunogen to generate polyclonal antibodies in rabbits (Figure 2(A)). Affinity-purified anti-CpARO antibodies recognized a single band in crude sporozoite lysates under both reducing and non-reducing conditions by western blot analysis (Figure 2(B)), confirming antibody specificity. The detected band migrated slightly below the predicted molecular weight under reducing conditions but slightly above the predicted size under non-reducing conditions, suggesting that native CpARO is present predominantly as a monomer. Antibody binding was abolished following pre-incubation with recombinant antigen, further validating specificity (Figure 2(B), right panel).

(C) Immunofluorescence assay (IFA) of excysted sporozoites showing CpARO localization at the anterior region corresponding to the rhoptries. Signal neutralization with recombinant antigen is shown. (D) Structured illumination microscopy (SIM) resolving CpARO localization along rhoptry structures. (G) Quantitative measurements of rhoptry length relative to sporozoite length and expansion factors derived from IFA and U-ExM. In panels C–F, nuclei were stained with DAPI. Immunofluorescence assay (IFA) demonstrated that CpARO localized to the anterior region of sporozoites (Figure 2(C), upper panel). The labeled structure exhibited a characteristic flask- or club-shaped morphology consistent with the rhoptry. Signal specificity was further confirmed by antigen neutralization (Figure 2(C), lower panel). IFA using antibody affinity-purified with CpARO-specific peptide (CTKNVDKPESWY) also confirmed the rhoptry distribution (Supplementary Figure S3).

Super-resolution imaging using structured illumination microscopy (SIM) and ultrastructural expansion microscopy (U-ExM) provided higher-resolution localization. Both approaches confirmed rhoptry labeling, with CpARO signals outlining the periphery of the organelle, consistent with localization to the rhoptry envelope (Figure 2(D,E)).

In addition to rhoptry localization, weaker CpARO signals were consistently observed within the sporozoite cytoplasm. These signals appeared primarily as granular structures and were more apparent in overexposed IFA images (Figure 2(F), arrowheads). Punctate signals were also detectable in SIM and U-ExM datasets (Figure 2(D,E), arrowheads), supporting the presence of a non-rhoptry CpARO pool.

Quantitative morphometric analysis showed that the rhoptry length represented approximately 40.5% of total sporozoite length in IFA images (Figure 2(G)). In expansion microscopy (U-ExM), rhoptry expansion factors differed from those of whole zoites (2.24-fold vs 3.36-fold in U-ExM), suggesting differential expansion behavior potentially influenced by macromolecular density, protein tethering, or membrane mechanical properties.

CpARO is secreted during excystation, gliding, invasion, and intracellular development

Unexpectedly, markedly stronger CpARO signals were observed on sporozoites that were fixed immediately after excystation but not permeabilized (Figure 3(A)), indicating extracellular exposure or secretion of CpARO during excystation. Granular CpARO signals were also detected on the surface of sporozoites and along gliding trails when excysted sporozoites were allowed to glide on glass slides (Figure 3(B)), suggesting active release of CpARO during motility.

(B) Gliding sporozoites deposited CpARO along gliding trails on glass slides. (D) Discharge of CpARO onto fixed host cell monolayers following incubation with excysted sporozoites (Spz; arrowheads). Secreted CpARO signals are primarily punctate, with occasional diffuse distribution. Also see Supplementary Figure S4 for comparison with the negative control. Secreted CpARO was further observed on host cell monolayers during sporozoite invasion (Figure 3(C)). Similar CpARO deposition was detected when sporozoites were incubated with fixed host cells (Figure 3(D); Supplementary Figure S4), indicating that CpARO secretion does not require active host cell responses. In these assays, CpARO signals were primarily granular on host cell surfaces, with a smaller fraction appearing diffusely distributed.

To further assess CpARO secretion across developmental stages, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) were performed on culture supernatants collected during excystation, invasion (0–3 h post-infection, hpi), and intracellular development (3–6, 10–12, and 20–24 hpi). Secreted CpARO was detectable at all examined stages (Figure 4(A,B); also see Supplementary Figure S5A for comparison between infected and uninfected samples). However, the 20–24 hpi samples may contain proteins secreted from both intracellular meronts and egressed extracellular merozoites. The CpARO expression in intracellular stages is also supported by existing transcriptomic analysis of the parasite during the life cycle progression [37], which shows that CpARO gene is transcribed in sporozoites and significantly upregulated during the intracellular development (Figure 4(C)). CpARO transcript is also detected by the single-cell RNA-seq of intracellularly developing C. parvum [38].

When the ELISA data are normalized to signals obtained using a pan-C. parvum antibody (Pan-Cp), CpARO secretion during intracellular development (3–24 hpi) was substantially higher than that observed during excystation or early invasion (0–3 hpi) (Supplementary Figure S5B). Because the Pan-Cp antibody was raised against crude oocyst extracts and may not recognize all parasite proteins equally across developmental stages, the Pan-Cp–normalized ELISA is not strictly quantitative. Nevertheless, these data clearly indicate that a significant amount of CpARO is secreted during intracellular development in addition to early infection stages.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that CpARO is not restricted to the rhoptry envelope but is actively secreted during excystation, gliding, invasion, and intracellular development.

Recombinant CpARO binds host cells with high affinity

To evaluate whether secreted CpARO interacts with host cells, we performed immunofluorescence-based and ELISA-based binding assays using GST-fused recombinant CpARO (rCpARO). In immunofluorescence assay, fixed HCT-8 host cell monolayers incubated with rCpARO exhibited clear surface labeling when detected with anti-GST antibody (Figure 4(D); Supplementary Figure S6). In contrast, GST tag alone, applied at the same molar concentration, showed no detectable binding, confirming that host-cell association was specific to CpARO rather than the fusion tag.

To quantify binding affinity, we performed cell-based ELISA using increasing concentrations of rCpARO. Binding occurred in a dose-dependent manner and followed a saturable profile consistent with receptor-mediated interaction (Figure 4(E)). Nonlinear regression analysis using a one-site binding model yielded an apparent dissociation constant (Kd) of 0.189 μM (R2 = 0.973). The high affinity and saturable binding behavior suggest that CpARO interacts with a predominant host surface receptor(s) under the experimental conditions. Together, these data support that secreted CpARO is capable of directly associating with host cells, consistent with a potential role in host–parasite interaction.

CpARO-positive rhoptry envelope remnants persist during invasion and are associated with the nascent feeder organelle

During host cell invasion, a process completed within minutes, the slender and curved sporozoites undergo marked morphological transformation, shortening and rounding as they differentiate into trophozoites. Concomitantly, the CpARO-labeled rhoptry also undergoes structural changes, shortening and condensing into a discrete punctum (Figure 5(A), green). This observation suggests that rhoptry envelope–associated structures persist following discharge of rhoptry contents rather than being immediately disassembled.

Dynamic redistribution of CpARO during invasion and intracellular development. (B) Volume and surface rendering of super-resolution micrographs showing the presence of CpARO signals between the host cell F-actin pad and the trophozoite nucleus. The location and morphology of the CpARO-labeled structure correspond to those of feeder organelle. (C) Intracellular stages showing loss of rhoptry-associated CpARO signals but persistence of diffuse CpARO during merogony. Moreover, CpARO-labeled signals persist for several hours, forming a membrane complex between the host cell F-actin pad and the trophozoite nucleus as demonstrated by super-resolution microscopy in samples at 2.5 hpi (Figure 5(B), green). The location and the morphology of the CpARO signals correspond to those of the feeder organelle, suggesting that CpARO-positive residual rhoptry membrane structures become associated with, and may contribute to, the immature feeder organelle in the developing trophozoites.

As intracellular development proceeds, diffusely distributed CpARO signals were present throughout the asexual replication cycle, from early meronts containing a single nucleus to mature meronts containing up to eight nuclei (Figure 5(C), red). Whether the signals were derived from mature feeder organelle and/or cytoplasm as pre-secreted molecules remains to be determined. Punctate signals were observed in mature meronts (Figure 5(C), right panel), corresponding to the formation of rhoptry in merozoites. No evidence of nuclear localization of CpARO was observed at any intracellular stage, in contrast to PfARO, which has been reported to transiently localize to parasite nuclei during early schizogony [39].

During sexual development, CpARO signals were detected in macrogametes but were absent in microgametes (Figure 5(D)). Notably, CpARO-positive granules were not observed on host cell monolayers during intracellular development, suggesting that CpARO secretion by intracellular stages is less spatially concentrated than that observed during sporozoite motility and invasion. Secreted proteins at these stages may instead diffuse into the surrounding culture medium.

Collectively, these observations indicate that CpARO exhibits dynamic redistribution during invasion and intracellular development, and that CpARO-positive residual rhoptry membrane structures are associated with nascent feeder organelle biogenesis.

Cryptosporidium possesses an ARO-interacting protein ortholog that is not rhoptry-localized but is also secreted

In T. gondii and P. falciparum, ARO interacts with an ARO-interacting protein (AIP) that localizes to the rhoptry [15,19]. Although TgAIP and PfAIP differ substantially in size (821 aa vs 420 aa), both are associated with rhoptry subcompartments (Figure 6(A)). AIP orthologs are present across apicomplexan species and share two conserved signature motifs despite considerable variation in overall protein length (Figure 6(A)).

Thicker lines indicate TgAIP, PfAIP, and CpAIP-L, which have been characterized by other investigators or in this study. In C. parvum, an AIP ortholog is encoded by gene cgd7_350, annotated as an uncharacterized protein in CryptoDB. The predicted protein consists of 374 amino acids with an estimated molecular weight of 42.6 kDa. Because this protein does not localize to the rhoptry and is therefore unlikely to function as a canonical ARO-interacting partner (see below), we designate it as an AIP-like protein (CpAIP-L).

Recombinant CpAIP-L was expressed as a GST fusion protein and used to generate polyclonal antibodies in rabbits. Affinity-purified anti-CpAIP-L antibodies recognized a single band in sporozoite lysates by western blot analysis, with signal abolished following antigen neutralization (Figure 6(B)), confirming antibody specificity.

Unexpectedly, immunofluorescence assays revealed that CpAIP-L localized throughout the sporozoite cytoplasm rather than to the rhoptry (Figure 6(C)). No anterior or rhoptry-associated enrichment was observed. In addition to cytoplasmic localization, CpAIP-L was also detected in culture supernatants during excystation and intracellular development by ELISA (Figure 6(D); Supplementary Figure 5(A), indicating that this protein is secreted. Like CpARO, the expression of CpAIP-L gene in the intracellular stages is supported by the existing transcriptomic data, including a previously reported transcriptomic analysis during the parasite life cycle progress and single-cell transcriptomics (Figure 4(C)) [37,38].

These findings argue against conservation of the canonical ARO–AIP–rhoptry interaction in C. parvum and instead suggest that CpAIP-L, like CpARO, may function as a secreted effector with divergent roles in host–parasite interaction.

CpARO and CpAIP-L elicit antibody responses in mice infected with C. parvum

The secretion of CpARO and CpAIP-L prompted us to examine whether these proteins are exposed to the host immune system during infection and capable of eliciting antibody responses. Because C. parvum establishes stable infection primarily in immunodeficient mice, we implemented a chemical immunosuppression and withdrawal protocol to transiently facilitate infection in wild-type animals.

In this model, C57BL/6 mice were immunosuppressed with dexamethasone for 10 days, with parasite inoculation initiated after seven days of suppression (Figure 7(A), upper panel). Oocyst shedding was detectable between days 5 and 11 post-infection, reaching levels of approximately 106–107 oocysts per gram of feces (Figure 7(A), lower panel). Although mouse 1 shed more oocysts than mouse 2, the data confirm the establishment of infection in the two mice. While this infection model could be further optimized, it provided sufficient parasite exposure to evaluate serological responses.

Antibody responses to CpARO and CpAIP-L in mice infected with C. parvum. (A) experimental design for transient immunosuppression–facilitated infection in C57BL/6 mice (upper panel). Oocyst shedding monitored by qPCR from fecal samples collected between days 5 and 11 post-infection confirmed establishment of infection (lower panel). (B) ELISA detection of serum antibodies against recombinant CpARO and CpAIP-L collected at three weeks post-infection. GST tag protein served as a negative control antigen. Both CpARO and CpAIP-L elicited significantly elevated antibody responses relative to GST control. Data are presented as optical density (OD) values. Statistical significances were evaluated by Dunnett’s (within each group) or Tukey’s (between groups) multiple comparison test.

Serum samples collected three weeks post-infection were analyzed by ELISA for antibodies against recombinant CpARO and CpAIP-L. Antibodies recognizing both CpARO and CpAIP-L were readily detectable in infected mouse sera (Figure 7(B)). Optical density (OD) values for CpARO and CpAIP-L were 3.27- and 3.65-fold higher in mouse 1, or 2.35- and 2.88-fold higher in mouse 2, than those obtained with GST control antigen, with differences reaching statistical significance (p < 0.01 to p < 0.0001; Dunnett’s multiple comparison test). For comparison, the OD values for both CpARO and CpAIP-L were much lower in the uninfected mouse.

Notably, antibody levels for both effectors in mouse 1 were higher than mouse 2, which correlates with the heavier infection of C. parvum in mouse 1 as indicated by the higher levels of oocyst shedding. Nonetheless, these findings demonstrate that CpARO and CpAIP-L are expressed and immunologically exposed during infection, consistent with their secretion and supporting their classification as parasite effector proteins capable of inducing host humoral responses.

Discussion

The rhoptry is a defining invasion organelle in apicomplexans, essential for the establishment of infection through the discharge of effectors that manipulate host cellular processes [1]. Despite its importance, knowledge of rhoptry biology in Cryptosporidium remains limited. Several rhoptry proteins have now been experimentally validated, including CpPRP1/CpRON1, CpROP1–CpROP7, and CpMUC1, and four CpROPs have been shown to be injected into host cells [10,12–14]. However, the molecular composition, dynamics, and fate of the rhoptry membrane-associated structures remain incompletely understood.

The present study identifies CpARO as a rhoptry envelope-associated protein in C. parvum, representing the first molecular marker for this sub-organellar compartment in Cryptosporidium. Localization by IFA, SIM, and U-ExM consistently demonstrated peripheral rhoptry labeling, supporting envelope association. However, the Cryptosporidium rhoptry is structurally complex, containing an outer envelope and internal membrane-like networks. Therefore, these approaches cannot unambiguously determine whether CpARO resides exclusively on the outer rhoptry envelope or also associates with internal rhoptry membrane structures. Future immuno-electron microscopy or endogenously tagged parasite lines will be required to define CpARO localization at higher ultrastructural resolution.

Using CpARO labeling, we observed that CpARO-positive rhoptry envelope-associated structures persist following content discharge, condensing into a punctate remnant during trophozoite transformation. Moreover, these residual rhoptry membrane structures appear as a membrane complex between the host cell F-actin pad and the trophozoite nucleus, consistent with their association with the nascent feeder organelle. The feeder organelle is a Cryptosporidium–unique membrane complex that plays critical roles in nutrient acquisition from host cells [1,40]. However, little was previously known about the biogenesis of this organelle. The availability of a rhoptry membrane marker therefore provides a useful tool not only for tracking rhoptry morphology and dynamics during invasion, but also for investigating the biogenesis of the feeder organelle.

A related post-invasion localization pattern was previously reported for CpPRP1/CpRON1, a rhoptry tip/neck protein related to TgRON1 and PfASP. In that study, CpPRP1 was localized to an elongated apical structure in free sporozoites, consistent with rhoptry localization, but during invasion it redistributed to the parasite–host cell interface and remained in close apposition to the developing host-cell actin plaque during early trophozoite development [12]. This pattern is notably similar to the redistribution of CpARO-positive rhoptry envelope remnants observed here. Although CpPRP1 relocation was previously interpreted as secretion of a rhoptry protein at the parasite–host cell interface, our CpARO data suggest an additional possibility: rhoptry-derived protein or membrane structures may persist after discharge and contribute to early feeder organelle biogenesis. Together, the CpPRP1 and CpARO observations support a model in which multiple rhoptry-associated components are retained or remodeled at the developing host–parasite interface.

It is worth mentioning that armadillo repeat refers to a unique structural fold, rather than implying sequence conservatiion [41,42]. Indeed, C. parvum encodes more than 80 armadillo repeat–containing proteins with diverse predicted functions (Supplementary Table S1). For roughly half of these proteins, function can be inferred in part from their primary sequences. These include eight clathrin-coated vesicle proteins (one clathrin heavy chain and seven adaptins), six nuclear pore complex proteins (importins/exportins), and eight enzymes (two phosphatases, three kinases, deoxyhypusine hydroxylase, nucleotide-diphospho-sugar transferase, and an HrpA-family SFII helicase). In addition to CpARO, several small proteins also contain a single ARM repeat, including eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit K, translation initiation factor IF2/IF5, beta-catenin-like protein 1, V-ATPase subunit H, deoxyhypusine hydroxylase, and several uncharacterized proteins with an armadillo-type fold. However, these proteins are highly divergent from CpARO at the primary-sequence level, supporting the specificity of the antibody raised against recombinant CpARO.

Additionally, CpARO is secreted during parasite excystation, gliding, invasion, and intracellular development, suggesting a process that may be independent of the canonical rhoptry discharge pathway. These findings support a multifunctional role for CpARO that extends beyond structural anchoring of the rhoptry. Notably, the secretion and association with another organelle have not been reported for TgARO or PfARO, highlighting functional divergence in early-branching apicomplexans.

Consistent with its secretion, recombinant CpARO exhibited high-affinity binding to host cells. Armadillo-repeat domains are widely implicated in mediating protein–protein interactions [41,42], supporting the hypothesis that secreted CpARO may engage host surface receptors. Although the identity of CpARO-binding partners remains unknown, its extracellular exposure, adhesive properties, and immunogenicity collectively support classification as a host-interacting parasite factor. Future studies will be required to further validate the functional roles of CpARO, and to determine whether CpARO contributes directly to host cell attachment, invasion efficiency, or modulation of host signaling pathways.

In canonical apicomplexan models, ARO interacts with an ARO-interacting protein (AIP) that is also localized to the rhoptry [15–19,39]. TgAIP and PfAIP occupy subcompartments between the rhoptry neck and bulb or at the rhoptry neck, respectively, and participate in rhoptry positioning. Although an AIP ortholog is encoded in the C. parvum genome, our data demonstrate that this protein, designated CpAIP-L, does not localize to the rhoptry but instead resides in the parasite cytoplasm and is also secreted. These findings argue against conservation of the canonical ARO–AIP–rhoptry scaffolding complex in Cryptosporidium and suggest lineage-specific functional repurposing.

The secretion of both CpARO and CpAIP-L, together with detection of antibodies against these proteins in infected mice, further supports their classification as parasite effectors exposed to the host immune system. While the current mouse infection model relies on transient immunosuppression and is not optimized for immunological analyses, it nevertheless demonstrates in vivo antigen exposure. Whether these proteins contribute to protective immunity, immune modulation, or diagnostic antigenicity warrants further investigation. To definitively establish the contribution of CpARO and CpAIP-L to Cryptosporidium virulence and development, it is necessary to employ genetic manipulation, such as the generation of gene-knockout or conditional gene-knockout line(s) of C. parvum. Point mutations may also be used to determine the contributions of selected motifs to the biochemical and biological functions.

From an evolutionary perspective, the divergence of CpARO from canonical ARO proteins is notable. Phylogenetic analyses confirm the conservation of ARO orthologs as single-copy genes across apicomplexans, yet functional diversification appears to have occurred in Cryptosporidium. The identification of two ARO orthologs in the free-living chromerid Chromera further suggests that ARO proteins predate obligate parasitism. It is therefore plausible that ancestral ARO proteins possessed broader cellular functions that were later specialized in other apicomplexan lineages but retained multifunctionality in Cryptosporidium.

In summary, this study demonstrates that CpARO functions as a rhoptry envelope protein that marks CpARO-positive residual rhoptry membrane structures associated with nascent feeder organelle formation, and also as a secreted host-interacting factor. We further show that the putative ARO-interacting protein ortholog CpAIP-L does not localize to the rhoptry but instead shares cytoplasmic and secretory characteristics. Together, these findings reveal functional divergence of the ARO–AIP system in Cryptosporidium and identify CpARO as a dual-function protein bridging organellar architecture and host interaction.
