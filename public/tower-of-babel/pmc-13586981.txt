Lifestyle Management for Pregnant Women with Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome Based on Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine: A Group Consensus
Authors: Fangfang Wang, Tianyi Zhou, Feng Yun, Hye Won Lee, Nicola Robinson, Myeong Soo Lee, Xiao-Yang Mio Hu, Xinyin Hu, Ying Li, Leyi Fu, Danqing Chen, Xinfen Xu, Fan Qu, On behalf of Panel, Bianba Zhuoma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePolyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), formerly polycystic ovary syndrome, is among the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders in women of reproductive age and requires lifelong management, with lifestyle intervention as the first-line strategy. Lifestyle management during pregnancy reduces the risk of PMOS-related adverse pregnancy outcomes, and combining traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) with Western medicine offers distinct advantages; however, limited high-quality clinical evidence means systematic guidance for integrated TCM and Western medicine management is unavailable.MethodsUsing evidence review and a modified Delphi process, a panel of 25 multidisciplinary experts developed tailored lifestyle management recommendations for pregnant women with PMOS across five domains (exercise, diet, psychological health, sleep, and environment) drawing on qualitative and quantitative research, clinical guidelines, existing expert consensus statements, and nationwide expert opinion.ResultsAfter 2 rounds of modified Delphi consultations, a final framework of 5 primary, 15 secondary, and 22 tertiary indicators was established by 25 multidisciplinary experts. With calculated expert judgment basis, familiarity with content, and expert authority coefficient values of 0.93, 0.81, and 0.87, respectively, both rounds produced a 100% response rate. The coefficients of variation dropped from 0.10–0.24 to 0.00–0.11, the mean indicator scores climbed from 3.68–4.68 to 4.16–5.00, and Kendall’s W increased from 0.21 (χ2=112.49, P < 0.001) to 0.35 (χ2=184.60, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe consensus aims to standardize integrated TCM and Western medicine lifestyle management in pregnant women with PMOS, and could serve as an alternative for future international consensus.

Introduction

Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS),1 formerly known as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), is a chronic condition driven by genetic, epigenetic, and lifestyle factors,2 affecting 10–13% of women globally.3 As the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-aged women, it has implications from adolescence to postmenopause.4 During pregnancy, women with PMOS face markedly elevated risks of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, dyslipidemia, miscarriage, preterm birth, and fetal growth abnormalities,5 causing short- and long-term adverse effects on maternal and neonatal health. Lifestyle modification—including dietary, physical activity, and behavioral interventions—is the first-line management for PMOS, with established efficacy in reducing weight and central obesity, improving insulin sensitivity, and promoting emotional well-being.6 The 2023 Chinese expert consensus on PMOS management recommends that lifestyle interventions begin before conception and continue throughout pregnancy to mitigate adverse pregnancy outcomes.7 Yet clinical guidelines and expert consensus tailored for pregnant women with PMOS remain scarce, and most international PMOS guidelines and expert opinions address preconception and general management rather than pregnancy-specific interventions.5,6,8 Recent expert opinion nonetheless emphasizes optimizing lifestyle and weight management and prioritizes research that develops new therapeutic strategies and implements evidence-based recommendations in perinatal care.2

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) offers distinct advantages in regulating exercise, diet, emotion, and sleep, and shows therapeutic potential for PMOS-related gestational complications.9–12 Standardized protocols for applying TCM in the management of PMOS during pregnancy nevertheless remain undefined. To address this gap, the Chinese Integrative Medicine and Traditional Chinese Medicine Branch and the Midwives Branch of the China Maternal and Child Health Association convened a multidisciplinary expert panel—specialists in TCM, obstetrics and gynecology, nursing, endocrinology, nutrition, psychology, and evidence-based medicine to develop integrated TCM and Western medicine lifestyle management recommendations for pregnant women with PMOS, grounded in Chinese clinical practice.

Consensus Development Methodology

This consensus adopted the 5A’s model of self-management support to deliver patient-centered standardized care: (1) Assess lifestyle behaviors routinely; (2) Advise with personalized recommendations aligned with the patient’s goals, values, and environment; (3) Agree on SMART (specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, and timely) goals through shared decision-making; (4) Assist through evidence-based interventions and resources, including self-management support; (5) Arrange patient-centered follow-up.13

Through online discussions, the drafting group identified key issues in lifestyle management for pregnant women with PMOS. Two rounds of expert panel consultation defined five domains (exercise, diet, psychology, sleep, and environment) taking into account Chinese prenatal care practice and evidence-based TCM approaches to lifestyle modification. A comprehensive literature search used subject headings and free-text terms in both Chinese and English across PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, CNKI, VIP, and Wanfang databases from inception to May 1, 2025 (Appendix 1), covering guidelines, expert consensuses, randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses on PMOS and lifestyle management. Following an evidence-based quality assessment the retrieved studies did not meet guideline formulation standards and showed varying risks of bias owing to inadequate sample sizes, methodological flaws, and disparities in efficacy criteria. The first draft was therefore built from this literature together with contemporary Western medicine and TCM clinical practice in China.

Panel recruitment was purposeful and targeted: at the recommendation of the association secretariat, Email invitations were sent to core influential experts in reproductive health in all provinces; 25 of the 31 specialists contacted agreed to take part, and six declined for scheduling reasons. This study pre-specified two consultation rounds for the modified Delphi procedure.14 A 1–5 Likert scale was adopted for experts to rate the importance of each indicator, with an indicator retention criteria defined as a mean score ≥ 3.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) < 0.25. All consensus conferences were held through Tencent Meeting. Experts formulated professional recommendations based on their clinical and research experience. Each recommendation was reviewed and revised over multiple rounds before the final consensus was reached, with all recommendations approved by ≥ 90% of the experts.

Scope of Application

This consensus applies to pregnant women previously diagnosed with PMOS or meeting the diagnostic criteria for PMOS before pregnancy.15 According to the Chinese Guideline for PCOS Diagnosis and Management, these criteria are:

(1)Oligomenorrhea, amenorrhea, or irregular uterine bleeding as mandatory criteria;(2)Plus one of the following: Clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism or polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound.

Statistical Methods

Data were entered and sorted in Excel 2021 and analyzed with SPSS 29.0 software. Continuous data are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs), and categorical data as counts and proportions (%). CV and Kendall’s concordance coefficient (Kendall’s W) were used to evaluate the consistency of expert opinions. The expert authority coefficient (Cr) was calculated as Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2, where Ca is the judgment basis coefficient of experts and Cs is the experts’ familiarity with the study subject matter.16 P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expert Demographics

Twenty-five multidisciplinary experts took part in both rounds of Delphi consultation. The consensus framework contained 5 primary, 15 secondary, and 22 tertiary indicators. The experts were aged 33 to 64 years, with 6 to 40 years of clinical working experience, and came from 21 provinces and municipalities covering nearly all major economic and medical regions of China. They represented TCM, obstetrics and gynecology, nursing, endocrinology, nutrition, psychology, and evidence-based medicine. Ninety-two percent (23/25) of the experts held leading academic and administrative positions, such as department directors, principal investigators of national research projects, and editorial board members of core academic journals. Expert Engagement Authority

Twenty-five questionnaires were collected in each round, giving a response rate of 100% in both rounds. Ten experts (40.00%) provided modification suggestions in the first round, and 3 (12.00%) put forward revision opinions in the second. Ca, Cs, and Cr were 0.93, 0.81, and 0.87, respectively.

These results reflected high expert participation, with most experts delivering constructive feedback in the initial round, and supported the stability and credibility of this Delphi procedure. The Cr value, which integrates experts’ judgment basis and their familiarity with the research theme, further verified the professional background and reliability of the expert panel.

Expert Opinion Coordination

CV and Kendall’s W were calculated to evaluate the consistency of expert scoring on all evaluation indicators. The CV indicates the individual variability of expert ratings for a specific indicator, whereas Kendall’s W indicates the collective agreement among all experts’ evaluations across all indicators.

In the first round, the mean importance scores of all 22 evaluation indicators ranged from 3.68 to 4.68, all exceeding the screening threshold of 3.5, and the CV values ranged from 0.10 to 0.24, all below the critical standard of 0.25; no indicator needed elimination for expert divergence. After the questionnaire was revised according to experts’ suggestions, the second round achieved more unified opinions: mean scores rose to 4.16–5.00 and CV values narrowed to 0.00–0.11, indicating higher recognition of the indicators and smaller scoring differences among experts.

Kendall’s W was further calculated to assess the overall coordination of expert opinions. The test covered 22 indicators with a degree of freedom of 21 for both rounds. Kendall’s W was 0.21 in the first round, with a chi-square statistic of 112.49 and P < 0.001, and rose to 0.35 in the second round, with a chi-square statistic of 184.60 and P < 0.001. Statistically significant P values in both rounds verified that the coordination of expert scoring was credible, and the higher second-round W demonstrated that expert opinions converged after targeted textual modifications.

All retained indicators obtained an expert approval rate exceeding 90% after the two‑round consultation, which supported the recognition and practicability of the final‑reached expert consensus.

Expert Consultation Feedback

First-round suggestions were systematically collated, discussed, and addressed by the research team. The detailed revisions are as follows:

(1)Exercise prescription: two experts suggested removing jogging from the list of recommended sports for pregnant women; one revised inappropriate expressions related to traditional Chinese medical exercise therapy; one advised adding standardized exercise assessment tools; and four optimized the wording of the exercise assessment and exercise prescriptions and supplemented descriptive content.(2)Diet prescription: two experts revised the standardized descriptions of the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); three pointed out that coix seed poses potential risks to pregnant women, and this content was deleted after literature review and academic verification.(3)Psychological prescription: one expert considered the psychological intervention protocol relatively rigid and formulaic; after literature review, the original protocol was retained and supplementary explanatory notes were added. One expert revised the awkward English translation of the psychological management scheme title, and two optimized the wording of the psychological assessment and intervention modules.(4)Sleep prescription: one expert revised inappropriate statements regarding TCM sleep conditioning; another raised doubts about the recommended ambient temperature for the sleep environment, which was verified against relevant clinical literature, with supporting references supplemented.(5)Environmental prescription: two experts simplified redundant text and made the environmental intervention measures more operable.

Discussion

PMOS exerts long-term impacts on women’s health and can lead to adverse maternal and fetal outcomes during pregnancy. Given that lifestyle modification is the first-line management for PMOS, that TCM offers unique advantages in lifestyle regulation, and that guidance tailored for pregnant women with PMOS remains scarce, we used Delphi methods to develop recommendations grounded in Chinese clinical practice that highlight both TCM and Western medicine across five core dimensions: exercise, diet, psychological, sleep, and environmental prescriptions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first integrated TCM and Western medicine program for lifestyle management in pregnant women with PMOS, and it could serve as an alternative for future international consensus.

Exercise Prescription

Exercise is essential to health and should be maintained during pregnancy.19 In women with PMOS, antenatal exercise significantly reduces the risks of GDM and hypertensive disorders.7 Given the elevated risks of miscarriage, preterm birth, and cervical insufficiency in this population,6,66,67 exercise regimens must be selected prudently, with contraindications rescreened preconception and at every prenatal check-up (Table 2).

Qigong, a traditional Chinese mind-body exercise, promotes physical rehabilitation and psychological balance by regulating physique (Tiao Shen), respiration (Tiao Xi), and mental state (Tiao Xin), together with internal spiritual concentration. Its gentle, moderate-intensity movements adhere to the principle of coordinated regulation of the body and mind (Xingshen Tongtiao), possibly benefiting the physical and psychological status of pregnant women.20–24 Recommended qigong techniques for pregnancy: (1) Baduanjin: simple and moderate in intensity; regulates glucolipid metabolism, blood pressure, mood, sleep, and cardiac function;24,68–81 particularly effective in women with PMOS for controlling gestational hyperglycemia/hypertension,70,72 improving neonatal outcomes, relieving postpartum anxiety/depression, and aiding postpartum weight management and pelvic floor recovery.68,69 (2) Taijiquan: smooth, gentle movements blending physical motion with mental tranquility, coordinating posture and breathing to boost systemic qi circulation and modulate visceral function; it reduces blood glucose, lipids levels, and blood pressure while improving mood and sleep;82–91 and is superior to other non-pharmacological interventions for weight control in women with PMOS.84

Diet Prescription

Dietary management is important for pregnant women with PMOS. Pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and gestational weight gain are closely associated with adverse maternal-fetal outcomes.92–94 Women with PMOS also face elevated risks of eating disorders, including bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, anorexia nervosa, and night eating syndrome.4 Any healthy dietary pattern consistent with general dietary guidelines for pregnancy benefits PMOS pregnancies (Table 3); sustainable regimens should be tailored to individual preferences and goals.4

TCM principles likewise advocate fresh, mild, nutritious, and easily digestible diets that are consumed in moderation without overeating and tailored to the stage of gestation. Such diets should avoid excessively cold, spicy, and greasy foods and are intended to tonify qi and blood, support fetal development, and prepare the mother for delivery and lactation. In early pregnancy, meals should be small-portioned yet refined. According to TCM Five-flavor theory,29,95 sour-flavored foods are preferred for promoting fluid secretion and appetite, and selection can follow the women’s preferences. Mid-pregnancy diets should be rich in protein, calcium, and phosphorus to promote fetal muscle, bone, and brain development. Late pregnancy prioritizes high-quality proteins with balanced animal and plant sources, limiting high-salt and alkaline foods to prevent edema. Protein-rich foods include brown rice, legumes, aquatic products, eggs, and meats; calcium sources encompass egg yolks, dairy, dried shrimp, animal bones, and leafy greens; phosphorus-rich foods include soybeans, chicken, and mutton.96,97

Psychological Prescription

Depression and anxiety are prevalent psychological disorders among women with PMOS, yet psychological interventions are not consistently integrated into their standard care.98 Their risk of depressive and anxiety symptoms in pregnancy is substantially higher than that in the general pregnant population, and lifestyle interventions may potentially ameliorate these conditions.4 Emerging findings suggest that mindfulness-based and cognitive-behavioral approaches, which share principles with positive psychology, may benefit mental health in women with PMOS.99,100 Positive psychology interventions (PPI), defined as interventions that pursue well-being enhancement through pathways consistent with positive psychology theory, are widely acknowledged to build well‑being and strengths and to reduce depression, anxiety, and stress across diverse populations.101 Often utilized as adjuncts to conventional psychotherapy, PPIs can reinforce and sustain lifestyle interventions.102 Growing evidence confirms that PPIs improve resilience, mental health, and subjective well-being in pregnant women, giving them a central role in lifestyle management in PMOS pregnancy.103 Psychological assessment and evidence‑based psychological care delivered by appropriately trained professionals are also warranted (Table 4).

TCM health preservation likewise emphasizes the importance of mental wellness and moral integrity for overall health,21 and spiritual cultivation which places moral refinement as the primary task, is central to its philosophy and practices. Psychological management may integrate TCM pattern differentiation with therapies such as traditional exercises and Five-Element Music. Breath regulation (Tiao Xi), one of the most commonly practiced traditional techniques, promotes interdependence of spirit and breath with focused mind-body awareness (see Exercise Prescription). Five-Element Music therapy should be personalized to pregnancy complication risks, following Traditional Chinese Five-Element Music: Standard Modes.45,46

Sleep Prescription

Sleep and diet act together to maintain circadian rhythm. Circadian rhythm disruption is both a major risk factor for developing PMOS104 and associated with a spectrum of gestational complications similar to those in PMOS.105 The prevalence of sleep disorders during pregnancy increases with gestational age, exceeding 60% by the third trimester.106 Sleep disorders are closely associated with maternal and fetal complications such as gestational hyperglycemia, hypertensive disorders, preterm birth, low birth weight, and postpartum depression.107 Women with PMOS also face a significantly higher risk of sleep disorders, such as sleep apnea syndrome, than healthy populations,6 therefore more attention should be paid on the sleep management in pregnant women with PMOS (Table 5).

Environmental Prescription

Tobacco exposure—active smoking, passive smoking, and e-cigarette use—impairs female fertility and is significantly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes such as miscarriage, preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes, placental abruption, stillbirth, low birth weight, and congenital defects.108 Any level of alcohol consumption during pregnancy may cause abnormal fetal development.109 Excessive caffeine intake may be linked to pregnancy loss, low birth weight, congenital anomalies, long-term obesity, and neurodevelopmental abnormalities.110 PMOS is also associated with high environmental pollutant exposure, which can adversely affect pregnancy outcomes.111–114 Women with PMOS should therefore avoid tobacco exposure and alcohol consumption, limit caffeine intake, and minimize environmental pollutants from the preconception period onward (Table 6).8,115

Limitations and Future Direction

This guidance directs integrated TCM and Western medicine lifestyle management for pregnant women with a history of PMOS and is critical for mitigating PMOS-related risks to mothers and offspring. Several limitations apply. First, robust clinical validation is absent: the program’s conceptual basis rests predominantly on theoretical frameworks and expert consensus, and high-quality evidence on its suitability for clinically complex cases remains scarce. Part of the TCM content is grounded in classical texts and small-sample Chinese-language studies. Well-designed prospective studies, eg, randomized controlled trials, are therefore required to confirm the effectiveness of this approach. Second, selection bias may have been introduced during assembly of the expert panel. All experts practice in China and there is no patient or public involvement in the panel. Third, the Delphi method has intrinsic drawbacks: consensus is not proof of accuracy, may suppress minority views, and relies on subjective expert judgment despite structured feedback.

Conclusion

Based on systematic evidence retrieval and the latest evidence-based medicine, this consensus statement provides expert recommendations with Chinese characteristics for lifestyle interventions during pregnancy in women with PMOS across five core dimensions: exercise, diet, psychology, sleep, and environment. Its principal contribution is a theoretically grounded and systematically organized foundation for future empirical studies, which should prioritize pilot implementation, contextual adaptation, and rigorous appraisal of practicality, patient acceptance, and clinical efficacy.
