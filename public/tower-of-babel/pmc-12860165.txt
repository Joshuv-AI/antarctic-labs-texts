Integrative analysis of mRNA and LncRNA profiles from small extracellular vesicles identifies apoptosis-related biomarkers in patients with Parkinson’s disease after rehabilitation
Authors: Yixuan Wang, Yonghong Liu, Boyan Fang, Cui Liu, Keke Chen, Detao Meng, Zhaohui Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing evidence has highlighted the crucial role of long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) and messenger RNAs (mRNAs) in the development and progression of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Investigating which lncRNA and mRNA biomarkers are associated with PD rehabilitation is valuable.MethodsSix patients with PD (PWP, Hoehn & Yahr stages 1.5–2.5) and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited. Total RNA was extracted from extracellular vesicles (EVs) from peripheral blood samples collected before and after 14 days of rehabilitation in the hospital. Differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) and differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) were identified via next-generation sequencing (NGS), and a coexpression network was constructed.ResultsOverall, 3038 DEmRNAs (2337 upregulated/701 downregulated) and 1723 DElncRNAs (1278 upregulated/445 downregulated) were identified between prerehabilitation (prerehab) PWPs and HCs, whereas 3117 DEmRNAs (2490 upregulated/629 downregulated) and 1931 DElncRNAs (1561 upregulated/370 downregulated) were identified between postrehabilitation (postrehab) PWPs and HCs. Moreover, 1662 DEmRNAs (606 upregulated/1056 downregulated) and 1729 DElncRNAs (593 upregulated/1136 downregulated) were identified between prerehab PWPs and postrehab PWPs. Four DEmRNAs and 19 DElncRNAs were found in the blood EVs of both pre- and postrehab PWPs. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of the DEGs associated with the apoptotic process, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway. Coexpression network analysis revealed one DEmRNA (ENSG00000168646, AXIN2) and one positively related DElncRNA (ENST00000577329). The expression of the DEGs was validated in human samples via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the apoptotic pathway-related biomarker AXIN2 may be involved in PD rehabilitation, which should be further confirmed in clinical evaluations after PD rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04594-7.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, Rehabilitation, Extracellular vesicle, Messenger RNA, Long noncoding RNA, AXIN2, Apoptotic pathway

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, with an increasing global prevalence over the past generation, making PD one of the leading causes of neurological disability [1]. PD is a clinical syndrome characterized by resting tremor, muscle rigidity, akinesia, and postural instability. An integrative medicine and health strategy highlights five treatment options (rehabilitate, therapy, restorative, maintenance, and surgery) for patients with PD (PWPs). Intervention can be used to manage the numerous symptoms of PD, among which rehabilitation is considered an integral part of evidence-based care for PWPs [2, 3]. However, practical and valid biomarkers are needed to reveal the pathogenesis of PD and evaluate the effect of rehabilitation for PWPs.

Numerous reports have shown that a series of neurobiological pathways and processes are involved in the molecular pathogenesis of PD, such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, protein degradation, autophagy, axonal transport, calcium homeostasis, and neuroinflammation [4, 5]. Accumulating evidence has also demonstrated that long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) affect the pathogenesis of PD [4, 6, 7]. Exosomes, which are membrane-bound vesicles 40–100 nm in diameter, are present in almost all biological fluids [8]. They are released from most cell types into the extracellular space after they fuse with the plasma membrane [9–11]. In addition, various nucleic acids, including mRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and other noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), have recently been identified in the exosomal lumen [12]. These exosomal RNAs can be taken up by neighboring cells or distant cells and thus modulate recipient cells when exosomes are circulating in the body. Exosome-mediated transfer of mRNAs and lncRNAs is a novel mechanism of genetic exchange between cells, which makes exosomes a focus of research.

In this study, we aimed to compare the lncRNA and mRNA expression profiles of small extracellular vesicles (EVs) or exosomes in the peripheral blood of healthy controls (HCs) and prerehabilitation (prerehab) and postrehabilitation (postrehab) PWPs to elucidate their potential associations with PD pathogenesis and rehabilitation. We also constructed a lncRNA‒mRNA coexpression network to identify and validate the significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Materials and methods

Participants and sample Preparation

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Rehabilitation Hospital, Capital Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Eighty participants were screened from June 2020 to December 2020 at the Parkinson Medical Center of Beijing Rehabilitation Hospital. All PWPs met the clinical diagnosis of PD based on the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) diagnostic criteria for primary PD [13], with a disease severity rating of stage 1.5 to 2.5 on the Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) scale (1 to 5 points from mild to severe) during the “ON” stage [14]; <75 years of age; without comorbidities requiring treatment; without deep brain stimulation therapy; Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) score > 24 if education level ≥ secondary school or > 20 if education level ≤ primary school; stable medication use; and the ability to walk and stand without assistance. The PWPs were excluded if they had a history of other neurological, psychological, or medical conditions, including atypical PD; history of head injury (stroke, depression, or learning disability); debilitating conditions other than PD (e.g., hearing or vision impairment); or significantly abnormal laboratory tests (including hematology, electrolytes, renal and liver function tests) [15]. Clinical data concerning disease duration and therapy were collected. Six age- and sex-matched healthy individuals were included as HCs. All participants underwent the required clinical evaluation, including medical history, physical examination (including neurological checkup), laboratory tests, and neuropsychological assessments. The laboratory tests included hematology, electrolytes, and renal and liver function tests.

Peripheral blood samples (5 ml per participant) were obtained from all 12 participants (PWPs and HCs) at baseline (prerehab) and the first day after 2 weeks of rehabilitation in the hospital (postrehab). All blood samples were processed in the same way as previously described [16]. Briefly, peripheral blood samples were collected in serum tubes. The serum samples were separated via centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C for later assays. At the time of analysis, the serum samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min to remove cell debris after thawing at 37 °C. The supernatant was diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged at 13 000 × g for 30 min, and processed via a 0.22 μm filter to remove large particles. The supernatant was ultracentrifuged using a P50A72-986 rotor (CP100NX; Hitachi, Brea, CA, USA) at 100 000 × g at 4 °C for 2 h to form a pellet of extracellular vesicles (EV). The pellet was resuspended in PBS and centrifuged at 100 000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the EV pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of PBS.

EVs were first isolated from serum of PWPs and then characterized and quantified by electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). The typical double-membrane structured particles with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm were observed by electron microscopy, and NTA also revealed that the average diameter of the EVs was 85.28 nm. The purity of EVs was assessed by analysis of exosome markers (CD9 and CD81) (Supplementary Table S1 and Figure S1).

Rehabilitation program

Two-week multidisciplinary intensive rehabilitation treatment (5 days/week) was performed and supervised by a well-trained physical therapist and speech therapist as previously described [15]. In brief, the full-volume rehabilitation programme consisted of (1) one-on-one physical therapy; (2) balance and gait training via C-MiLL (Motekforce link, Culemburg, Netherlands) and balance tutor (Meditouch, Netanya, Israel); (3) aerobic training via upper and lower limb trainers (T5XR; Nustep, Ann Arbor, MI, USA); and (4) speech therapy. Difficulty varies according to the physical frailty of an individual’s PWP. Each treatment session took approximately 30 min on average, and a 30–60-minute break was arranged between sessions. All the sessions were performed while the typical medication schedule was maintained for each PWP. Intervention was performed at the time of day when the PWP felt that their PD medications were most effective (from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.).

Clinical assessments

All clinical assessments were performed for PWPs during the “on” medication state (1 h after taking their regular PD medications on the same day). The severity of motor symptoms was evaluated via the motor subscores of the Movement Disorders Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS part III) and Hoehn-Yahr staging scale [17]. Cognition was examined with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [18] during the “on” phase in PD patients. Furthermore, emotional symptoms were assessed with the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) [19].

RNA isolation, library Preparation & RNA sequencing

ExoRNA was extracted from the serum samples and subjected to RNA sequencing by CloudSeq Biotech, Inc. (Shanghai, China). In brief, RNA was extracted from EVs via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Karlsruhe, Germany). The quality control was assessed with a NanoDrop ND-1000 (Thermo, USA). An NEBNext® rRNA Depletion Kit (New England Biolabs, Inc., Massachusetts, USA) was used for rRNA depletion. A TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA, 20020596) and Agilent 2100 Analyzer were used to construct a cDNA library and for quality control. This library was sequenced via an Illumina HiSeq 6000 sequencer, and reads were generated. The raw reads were processed for quality control. Clean data were obtained and used for subsequent analyses after low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed via fastp software (v0.20.0). The significantly differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) and lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) were identified via the DESeq2 package (v1.30.1) on the basis of cutoff criteria of a fold change ≥ 2 and P ≤ 0.05.

Functional annotation and enrichment analysis

All of the identified DEmRNAs were aligned against the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes (KEGG) databases to annotate gene functions. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out via the clusterProfiler R package. The P value was corrected via the Bonferroni method, and a corrected P value ≤ 0.05 was set as the threshold. The GO terms or KEGG terms matching the criteria were considered significantly enriched terms.

The lncRNA‒mRNA coexpression network

Gene coexpression networks were constructed to identify lncRNA‒mRNA interactions. LncRNA‒mRNA interactions were predicted via correlation analysis. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was used to calculate the lncRNA‒mRNA pair correlation. The PCC between lncRNAs and mRNAs was used to calculate (cutoff value, PCC > 0.3, P < 0.05) the lncRNA‒mRNA pair correlation. The lncRNA‒mRNA regulatory network was constructed via Cytoscape (v3.7.1) for visual representation.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

ExoRNAs were subjected to RT‒PCR analysis to measure the relative expression levels of the mRNAs and lncRNAs of interest. ExoRNA was reverse transcribed via the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time, Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Qualification was performed for all six samples. 100–200 ng of RNA was used for each sample, the quanity of RNA extracted from EVs for qRT‒PCR are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Then, qPCR amplification was conducted via qPCR SYBR Green master mix (CloudSeq). The same 6 samples were used for PCR validation as were used for sequencing. Parameters of thermal cycle were: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 10 s, 60 ℃ for 60 s with fluorescence acquisition. To establish the melting curve of the PCR products, the following steps were performed after amplification: 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 60 s and 95 °C for 15 s. Subsequently, the temperature was gradually increased from 60 °C to 99 °C at a ramp rate of 0.05 °C/sec. The expression level of each sample was internally normalized against the expression of β-actin. The stability of β-actin expression was tested and confirmed by using the comparative ΔCt method. The relative expression value was calculated via the 2-ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Sequences of qPCR primers used in this study are shown in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the differences between groups. P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Demographic data and clinical and neuropsychological assessments

The PWP population was comparable to the HCs in terms of demographic, anthropometric, or clinical characteristics (Table 1). The clinical and neuropsychological assessments revealed a trend of improvement in motor function (MDS-UPDRS III), cognition (MMSE score), or neuropsychological function (HAMA and HAMD scores) in PWPs after rehabilitation even though the differences were not statistically significant (Table 1). No adverse effects were reported during the study. The experimental and analysis workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

DEmRNA and DElncRNA profiles in blood EVs

DEmRNAs and DElncRNAs were screened to examine whether and which mRNAs and lncRNAs are involved in the improvement of clinical symptoms in PWPs after rehabilitation therapy. Among the 19,937 detectable mRNAs, 2337 were upregulated and 701 were downregulated (≥ 2.0-fold change, P < 0.05) in prerehab PWPs compared with HCs. The expression of 2490 mRNAs was upregulated, whereas that of 629 mRNAs (≥ 2.0-fold change, P < 0.05) was downregulated in postrehab PWPs compared with HCs. Compared with those in postrehab PWPs, 606 mRNAs were upregulated, and 1056 mRNAs were downregulated (≥ 2.0-fold change, P < 0.05) in prerehab PWPs.

The data of 121,140 lncRNAs were obtained. Specifically, 1723 lncRNAs were expressed differentially, of which 1278 lncRNAs were upregulated and 445 lncRNAs were downregulated (≥ 2.0-fold change, P < 0.05) in prerehab PWPs compared with HCs. The expression was upregulated for 1561 lncRNAs and downregulated for 370 lncRNAs (≥ 2.0-fold-change, P < 0.05) in postrehab PWPs compared with HCs. Compared with those in postrehab PWPs, 593 lncRNAs were upregulated, and 1136 lncRNAs were downregulated (≥ 2.0-fold change, P < 0.05) in prerehab PWPs (Table 2).

Functional and pathway analysis of DEGs

GO analysis revealed that the upregulated DEmRNAs between prerehab PWPs and HCs were associated with the following biological processes (BPs): the stress-activated protein kinase signaling cascade, positive regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, the stress-activated MAPK cascade, positive regulation of T-cell activation, regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, and others (Fig. 2A). Among the downregulated DEmRNAs associated with BPs, the significantly enriched GO terms included positive regulation of autophagy of mitochondria in response to mitochondrial depolarization, positive regulation of neuron projection development, dendrite development, negative regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, the Wnt signaling pathway, and the calcium modulating pathway (Fig. 2D).

For the upregulated DEmRNAs between postrehab PWPs and HCs, GO analysis indicated that the functions of the following major BPs were enriched: positive regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, negative regulation of mitochondrial organization, regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, peptidyl-threonine phosphorylation, positive regulation of the intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway, and others (Fig. 2B). Additionally, BP analysis revealed that the downregulated DEmRNAs were enriched in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly, the Wnt signaling pathway, the calcium modulating pathway, positive regulation of axon extension, positive regulation of growth, and mRNA stabilization (Fig. 2E).

GO analysis of the upregulated DEmRNAs between pre- and postrehab PWPs revealed that the functions of five major BPs were enriched: positive regulation of proteasomal protein catabolic process, positive regulation of proteasomal ubiquitin − dependent protein catabolic process, regulation of RNA stability, negative regulation of the ERBB signaling pathway, regulation of the mRNA catabolic process, and others (Fig. 2C), whereas the downregulated DEmRNAs were enriched in protein polyubiquitination, apoptotic mitochondrial changes, protein targeting to mitochondrion, ubiquitin recycling, positive regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, and others (Fig. 2F).

Compared with those in HCs, the upregulated DEmRNAs in postrehab PWPs were significantly enriched for axon guidance, the calcium signaling pathway, and lysosomes (Fig. 3B), whereas the downregulated DEmRNAs were significantly enriched for the mTOR signaling pathway, glutathione metabolism, and the mRNA surveillance pathway (Fig. 3E).

LncRNA–mRNA coexpression networks

After screening, 10 DElncRNAs and 1 relevant coding DEmRNA of interest were chosen for construction of the lncRNA‒mRNA network to predict the function of the lncRNA and identify the potential lncRNA‒mRNA regulatory network. The DElncRNAs and coding DEmRNAs of interest were expressed simultaneously in the HCs, prerehab PWPs and postrehab PWPs. Our results revealed that the interaction network comprised 11 nodes and formed 10 network pairs (8 positive pairs and 2 negative pairs). The coexpression networks indicated that one mRNA could be related to multiple lncRNAs (Fig. 4).

Validation of the expression of selected DElncRNAs and DEmRNAs via RT‑qPCR

From the data in the lncRNA‒mRNA coexpression networks, we focused on one DElncRNA (ENST00000577329) and one positively related DEmRNA (ENSG00000168646, AXIN2) for validation via RT‒qPCR. Compared with those in HCs, ENST00000577329 and ENSG00000168646 were significantly upregulated in prerehab PWPs (P < 0.05), which is consistent with the RNA‑seq data, whereas ENST00000577329 and ENSG00000168646 were significantly downregulated in postrehab PWPs compared with prerehab PWPs (P < 0.01), which is also consistent with the RNA‑seq data (Fig. 5). Notably, we found that ENST00000577329 contributed to the upregulation of AXIN2. More evidence is needed to confirm the utility of the gene as a potential biomarker for PD rehabilitation.

Discussion

Recently, epidemiological evidence has suggested that rehabilitation approaches, including physical therapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), and speech language pathology (SLP), may reduce the risk of PD progression [3]. In this study, benefits of rehabilitation therapies were found in PWPs, including improvements in motor behavior, cognition, quality of life, anxiety, and depression. These results are in line with the findings reported by Chen et al. in similarly designed studies [15]. However, the mechanisms underlying PD progression and rehabilitation-based improvement remain unclear.

Exosomes have become an increasingly investigated focus of research on PD pathogenesis. Exosomes are the smallest extracellular vesicles, ranging in size from approximately 50–150 nm. They are released into the extracellular space by the binding and fusion of multivesicular bodies to the plasma membrane [20]. Exosomes can shuttle genetic and protein constituents between cells, facilitating cell-to-cell communication and regulating information transfer, which may influence cell function. Because the release of exosomes and their molecular cargo is cell type specific, exosomes have also been proposed as attractive biomarkers for detecting and diagnosing neurodegenerative diseases [21]. Exosomes carry cargos such as mRNAs and proteins. Therefore, they could play a role in the spread of misfolded proteins such as α-synuclein (α-syn) [22]. Numerous studies have indicated that cells can utilize these small structures to transfer messengers and lncRNAs between cells [23]. These findings imply that the enriched mRNAs and ncRNAs in exosomes may be involved in PD pathogenesis [24]. Therefore, we detected specific exoRNA expression patterns in the peripheral blood of PWPs via high-throughput assays.

In this study, the basic transcriptome of HCs and PWPs was determined via RNA sequencing. We identified 3038 DEmRNAs in the dataset, including 2337 upregulated genes and 701 downregulated genes. Subsequent GO analyses revealed that the 3038 DEmRNAs were enriched mostly in the regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway, the Wnt signaling pathway, the calcium-modulating pathway, and the regulation of mitochondrial organization. A substantial body of evidence suggests that apoptotic signaling and mitochondria-related mechanisms are involved in PD pathogenesis. Apoptosis has been extensively demonstrated in the brains of PD patients and in experimental PD models, especially the MPTP-induced mouse model [25]. Apoptosis is a highly regulated, ATP-dependent process that is mostly dependent on the activation of caspases, which usually progress through a series of proteolytic events constituting the caspase cascade [26]. After the onset of PD, the interaction of α-syn with the electron transport chain (ETC) increases the production of mitochondrial ROS, promoting mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage [27]. It also induces the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), calcium diffusion, cytochrome C release, and mitochondrial swelling, which increases inflammatory responses by releasing succinate and mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and thus induces apoptosis [28]. Moreover, in the SN of MPTP-exposed mice, BAX is upregulated and preferentially located in the mitochondria, whereas Bcl-2 is downregulated, resulting in cytochrome C release, subsequent caspase-9 and − 3 activation, and finally apoptosis [29]. However, the precise molecular mechanism underlying PWP rehabilitation through apoptosis- and mitochondria-related pathways remains unclear.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DEmRNAs were significantly involved in the apoptosis pathway, calcium signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway. The essential roles of these pathways have been previously reported [30]. Various apoptotic stimuli cause the release of excessive Ca2 + from the ER and subsequent overload of Ca2 + into the mitochondria, resulting in constant opening of the mPTP, which leads to mitochondrial swelling with perturbation or rupture of the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and alters the mitochondrial membrane potential [31]. The rupture of the OMM activates the release of several proapoptotic proteins, such as cytochrome C, where it partners with other proteins and initiates the signaling cascade, leading to apoptosis [32]. Ca2 + also regulates programmed cell death processes such as apoptosis. Ca2 + release from the ER, as well as Ca2 + entry that is taken up by the mitochondria, is pivotal for triggering apoptotic signals and is one of the mechanisms through which the overexpression of antiapoptotic proteins (or ablation of proapoptotic proteins) counteracts cell death [32]. Moreover, increased levels of α-syn are thought to be associated with neuronal apoptosis induced by dysfunction of MAPK signaling pathways [33]. In human glial cells, α-syn promotes inflammation through the activation of the p38, ERK, and JNK pathways, leading to the production of IL-1β and TNF-α [34]. However, little is known about the roles of mRNAs and lncRNAs in exosomes in the pathogenesis and recovery of sporadic PD.

In this study, one DElncRNA (ENST00000577329) and one positively related DEmRNA (ENSG00000168646, AXIN2) were successfully constructed and verified in the mRNA‒lncRNA network. The DEmRNA was chosen because it was significantly different in any pairwise comparison among three groups (healthy controls, pre-rehabilitation, and post-rehabilitation), and it was associated with PD in proteomic BP analysis. The associated DElncRNAs were chosen for validation because they were the most prominent lncRNAs in DEmRNA relevance. AXIN2 mRNA expression in human blood was significantly lower in PWPs after rehabilitation than in those before rehabilitation therapy. The novel PD-related gene AXIN2 is a critical component of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and functions as a negative regulator, inducing apoptosis. Wnt signaling is mediated by β-catenin, a nuclear factor that promotes the cell cycle [35]. The concentration of β-catenin is controlled by a degradation complex whose rate-limiting component is AXIN [36]. Dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is involved in the pathophysiology of PD. As reported previously, miR-15b-5p overexpression or AXIN2 knockdown in a PD mouse model alleviated the inflammatory response (as evidenced by decreased levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and apoptosis (as evidenced by decreased levels of cleaved caspase-3 and Bax and elevated Bcl-2) [37]. In line with these findings, we observed a dramatic increase in AXIN2 mRNA expression in PWPs without rehabilitation therapy and a significant decrease after rehabilitation. Moreover, a positive coexpression network was found between DElncRNAs (ENST00000577329) and DEmRNAs (ENSG00000168646, AXIN2) in this study, suggesting a strong functional role of the DElncRNA (ENST00000577329) in modulating the expression of AXIN2 mRNA in PD pathogenesis. These findings provide preliminary evidence that these genes may be potential targets for the diagnosis and treatment of PD.

There are several limitations in the present study, despite the encouraging results. First, the sample size was small, so our preliminary findings should be validated in a larger sample of patients. We identified only a few of the molecules that are most relevant to PD in a small number of human samples. More studies are needed to clarify whether these findings are related to the course and/or severity of PD. Future research in animals and at the cellular level should investigate the specific biological functions of AXIN2 mRNA in the pathogenesis and rehabilitation of PD.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified a PD-associated DEG, AXIN2 mRNA, and a positively related lncRNA (ENST00000577329) in peripheral blood EVs. GO and KEGG analyses revealed that Axin2, which is associated with stimulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling resulting in apoptosis, plays an important role in the pathogenesis of PD and in the rehabilitation of PWPs. Validation via RT‒qPCR studies suggested the possibility of these genes as new potential biomarkers for PD.
