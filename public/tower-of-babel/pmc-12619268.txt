Harnessing the microbiota-gut–brain axis to prevent and treat pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders: translational insights and strategies
Authors: Shamma H. Alkuwaiti, Jurga Skrabulyte-Barbulescu, Lidya K. Yassin, Saif Almazrouei, Dana Aldhaheri, Mahra Alderei, Shamsa BaniYas, Shamsa H. Alshamsi, Abeer Alnuaimi, Sara Saeed, Mohammad Alawadhi, Paulina Rutkowska-Gauvry, Fatima Y. Ismail, Mohammad I. K. Hamad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), Rett syndrome (RTT), Tourette syndrome (TS), Down syndrome (DS), Fragile X syndrome (FXS), Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS), fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD), pediatric epilepsy, congenital Zika syndrome (CZS), Wilson’s disease (WD), and cerebral palsy (CP), are associated with long-term impairments and high societal burden. Emerging evidence implicates the microbiota–gut–brain axis (MGBA) in early neurodevelopment with factors, such as maternal microbiota, birth mode, antibiotics, and infant diet shaping microbial colonization and influencing developmental outcomes.ObjectiveThis review aims to synthesize current translational evidence from animal and human studies to address the following research questions: (1) How do disruptions in the MGBA contribute to the pathophysiology of pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders? (2) What microbiome alterations are consistently associated with these conditions? and (3) What ethical and safety considerations should be addressed in implementing early-life microbiome-based interventions? We also assess emerging microbiota-based interventions, including probiotics, prebiotics, dietary strategies, fecal microbiota transplantation, and traditional therapies, and their potential to modulate disease outcomes.ConclusionThe MGBA presents a promising, yet currently preliminary, target for early intervention in pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders. There is emerging evidence that therapies targeting the microbiota could have an effect on neurodevelopment and outcomes of health and disease; however, most of the findings are in the initial stages, and the evidence is still inconsistent. Therefore, the findings must be translated into mechanisms of action over extended periods with longitudinal multi-omic studies to identify possible intervention windows, optimize personalized therapies, and evaluate the safety and efficacy of interventions targeting the microbiome. Till that point, these microbiota-targeted approaches should be approached cautiously and treated as experimental in the realm of pediatric interventions, given the present-day state of evidence.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) such as ADHD, ASD, TS, RTT, DS, PWS, CP, FASD, CZS, epilepsy, and WD are highly prevalent in pediatric populations and often result in long-term impairments in cognition, behaviour, communication, and motor function. These conditions emerge during sensitive periods of brain development and pose significant challenges in both diagnosis and management. Current therapies are predominantly symptomatic and offer limited success in altering disease trajectory. As research continues to reveal the complexity of these disorders, growing attention has turned to modifiable biological systems that may influence neurodevelopmental outcomes early in life [1]. The MGBA has emerged as a promising interface connecting environmental exposures, host physiology, and brain development. This bidirectional network links the gut microbiota (GM) with the central nervous system (CNS) through a combination of neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic pathways [2–6]. The GM itself is highly dynamic during infancy and early childhood and is shaped by numerous factors, including maternal microbial composition, mode of delivery, antibiotic exposure, feeding practices, and host genetics. These factors coincide temporally with key stages of neurodevelopment, suggesting that microbial signals may play an active role in shaping brain structure and function.

Evidence from germ-free animal models demonstrates that the absence of a microbiota during early life leads to marked abnormalities in stress regulation, anxiety-like behavior, social interaction, and synaptic signaling [7, 8]. Recolonization of these animals later in life fails to fully restore typical brain function, indicating the importance of timing in microbiota–brain interactions. Behavioral and neurochemical responses can also be modulated through the administration of specific microbial strains, reinforcing the idea that select bacteria influence neural circuits involved in emotion and cognition [9–11]. In addition, microbial cues that do not activate classical immune pathways have been shown to modulate emotional behavior, underscoring the sensitivity of the developing brain to subtle microbial influences [12].

Disruption of the microbiota early in life can influence neurodevelopmental processes through several converging mechanisms. These include altered production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), increased intestinal permeability, systemic inflammation, dysregulated tryptophan metabolism, and imbalanced neuroimmune signaling. Antibiotic exposure during critical developmental windows has been shown to induce persistent changes in visceral sensitivity, immune tone, and stress reactivity [13–17]. These findings support the concept that microbial communities influence key elements of brain maturation, including synaptogenesis, microglial activation, and neurotransmitter biosynthesis. Thus, microbiota–brain pathways are partly shared, but their downstream effects are disorder-specific, as discussed in the following subsections of this review.

It remains unclear whether microbiota alterations are a cause, a consequence, or both. Dysbiosis may initiate or exacerbate neurodevelopmental issues through inflammatory pathways, but NDDs could also influence microbiota composition as a downstream effect.

Genetic variation plays a role in shaping microbial consistencies via those that are heritable as well as through immune or metabolic pathways in their development [18, 19]. The environment differs from one location to another and does create an impact on colonization of the infant in the early days of life [20, 21]. Diet acts, to modify the microbial community composition in a relatively fast and reproducible manner, while long-term dietary habits create a stable imprint [22, 23]. Therapies, such as those using antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, psychotropics, and dietary treatments (the ketogenic diet for example), can drastically change the diversity and function of the microbiota, with consequences that, at times, guide treatment effectiveness or mediate metabolic outcomes [24]. These factors and their consequences upon the interpretation of changes in the microbiome all necessitate longitudinal controlled studies in humans to disentangle effects pertaining to the principal illness, which can be modified by environmental factors or treatment.

The MGBA relies on a highly integrated set of communication pathways. Neural routes include the vagus nerve and the enteric nervous system (ENS). The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis connects stress responses to microbial signals. Immune cells respond to microbe-derived antigens and metabolites, shaping systemic inflammation and influencing brain function. Circulating molecules such as cytokines, PAMPs, and microbial metabolites like acetate and propionate are capable of crossing into the CNS, where they impact BBB integrity, neurogenesis, and glial activity [25, 26]. Thus, the MGBA enables bidirectional communication between the gut and brain through neural, endocrine, immune, and metabolic pathways, collectively influencing brain development and function.

Clinical and preclinical studies increasingly show that dysbiosis is a common feature across diverse NDDs. In ASD, children often exhibit lower microbial diversity and depletion of beneficial taxa. ADHD is associated with differences in microbial composition and reduced production of metabolites involved in dopamine regulation. In RTT and DS, gut inflammation, altered SCFA profiles, and disrupted neurotransmitter signaling have been reported. CP and PWS are also linked to imbalanced microbial communities, metabolic dysfunction, and gastrointestinal (GI) comorbidities. FASD and CZS present additional examples where neurodevelopmental damage may be compounded by altered gut–immune interactions and impaired microbiota maturation [27–29]. These shared patterns suggest that the MGBA is not only sensitive to disease processes but may actively shape their progression. As such, interventions targeting the microbiota represent a novel and modifiable strategy in the prevention and management of NDDs. Promising approaches include probiotics, prebiotics, dietary fiber enrichment, FMT, and nutritional strategies such as omega-3 supplementation or the Mediterranean diet. Adjunctive interventions, including environmental enrichment (EE), and traditional medical practices, are also under investigation for their ability to stabilize microbial ecosystems and improve behavioral outcomes. While clinical application remains at an early stage, these strategies offer hope for developing personalized, microbiota-informed interventions for at-risk children.

This review examines the mechanistic underpinnings, shared dysbiosis profiles, and therapeutic implications of the MGBA across a spectrum of pediatric NDDs. By focusing on early-life windows of vulnerability and developmental plasticity, we highlight the potential for translating microbiome science into clinically relevant strategies. Harnessing the MGBA may ultimately open a new frontier in the prevention and treatment of NDDs by shifting focus toward upstream, systems-level modulation during the most critical periods of brain development.

Early-life microbiome formation and the GBA

Introduction

The first 1000 days, beginning at conception, represent a critical window for human nervous system development [30] during which shaping of the GM and brain development occur in parallel [31]. During brain development, preclinical studies suggest that the gut microbiome participates in the processes of neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, myelination, microglia maturation, BBB formation, neurotransmitter regulation and the expression of neurotrophins [32]. These neurodevelopmental processes, influenced by the MGBA, are supported by preclinical studies showing mediation by bidirectional communication through immune, neuroendocrine and neural pathways [31]. These processes initiate in utero and undergo further development during the early postnatal period [33]. Disruption of these processes can alter developmental trajectories and increase the risk for neurodevelopmental and brain disorders, as evidenced by a growing number of preclinical studies [31]. The traditional “sterile womb” hypothesis, which suggests that microbial colonization begins only at birth, has been challenged by growing evidence indicating that microbiota formation may begin prenatally and is shaped by maternal microbial transmission [34]. Maternal health is an important factor influencing the infant’s microbiota and neurodevelopment [35]. Moreover, prenatal exposures such as maternal immune activation, psychosocial stress and microbial pathogens have been associated with an increased vulnerability to neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD, ADHD and schizophrenia [36], potentially through MGBA disruption during critical periods of fetal neurodevelopment [37]. Major changes in GM composition emerge postnatally and are influenced by multiple factors, including mode of delivery, gestational age, use of antibiotics and feeding practices [33]. Together, these findings indicate the importance of early-life microbial signals in shaping long-term cognitive and behavioral outcomes.

There is a recent accumulation of data suggesting the existence of some sensitive periods when microbiome-targeted interventions may exert maximal and long-lasting effects on neurodevelopment. The prenatal period is definitely the more critical, with maternal microbial metabolites-such as SCFAs-crossing the placenta and influencing fetal immune and brain development [38, 39]. Such is the nature of the entire prenatal period because it is mostly characterized by mode of delivery, first use of antibiotics, and breastfeeding, which define early microbial colonization. For instance, during infancy (0–3 years), a highly dynamic microbiota coincides with rapid synaptogenesis, myelination, and maturity in all immune systems. Studies in germ-free animals indicate that outside this window, a successful restoration of a normal brain function cannot be achieved [40, 41]. From three to six years of age, there begins to be some stability in the microbiota, and yet the plasticity is still great enough to allow intervention resulting in behavioral and immune outcomes [42]. For the optimal timing for microbiome interventions in infants to be determined, future research should include longitudinal monitoring of an individual’s microbiota development from prenatal life to early childhood. The microbiota change at several time points will help identify the sensitive windows where the microbiome is most amenable to modulation, particularly in the early postnatal few weeks or during specific prenatal windows. Assessing individual variability of microbiota composition can also help tailor the interventions to the specific microbial profile of each infant in order to enhance the prospect of benefiting neurodevelopment. Once again, we will require personalized approaches based on microbiota profiling to accurately target the timing of interventions that are likely to be most efficacious for different infants.

Maternal microbiota and prenatal influences

Preclinical and clinical evidence demonstrates that maternal microbiota and prenatal influences play a key role in shaping offspring neurodevelopment via MGBA [43–47]. A longitudinal study observed that maternal prenatal microbiota was a stronger predictor of neurodevelopmental outcomes at one year than the infant postnatal microbiome [47]. Interestingly, Fusobacteriia in maternal samples was associated with enhanced fine motor performance, whereas its presence in infants correlated with reduced scores [47]. These associations declined by year two and could not be detected by year three, suggesting that maternal microbial influences may be limited to early neurodevelopmental windows [47]. However, the causal mechanisms are unclear and remain to be clarified by future studies. Furthermore, maternal stress during gestation can influence the offspring´s GM and affect neurodevelopmental outcomes. Maternal anxiety and depression during gestation showed reduced levels of beneficial Bifidobacterium in offspring [46]. Undetectable levels of Bifidobacterium at 30 days of age in preterm infants has been linked to neurodevelopmental delays in early childhood, suggesting that early Bifidobacterium colonization may serve as a microbial marker of neurodevelopmental risk [48]. Moreover, elevated cortisol levels during gestation resulted in decreased levels of anti-inflammatory Lactobacillus and increased levels of pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria [43]. The latter is a signature marker of dysbiosis and has been linked to inflammatory states. Furthermore, it is also observed in children with ADHD [49]. However, none of these studies assessed maternal microbiota across pregnancy, although trimester-specific shifts are known to occur [50]. Future studies should include sampling at multiple timepoints to capture the temporal dynamics and their potential impact on fetal brain development.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated the mechanistic link between the maternal microbiota and neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring [44, 45]. Pregnant mice from Western diet lineages developed gut dysbiosis which further provoked systemic inflammation (evidenced by elevated interleukin-17A (IL-17A) levels) [45]. This maternal inflammation disrupted prenatal programming and reduced offspring brain Fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP) expression. These changes coincided with FXS-like phenotypes in male offspring (evidenced by enlarged ears and stereotypic behaviors). The phenotypes persisted despite Caesarean section (CS) delivery, confirming that maternal influence rather than postnatal processes affect offspring neurodevelopment. These findings provide direct evidence that maternal dysbiosis can initiate pathophysiological processes underlying offspring neurodevelopmental disorders through prenatal programming. However, the maternal dysbiosis was hypothesized rather than directly measured. Future studies should measure maternal and offspring microbiota alterations [45]. Similarly, maternal stress during gestation resulted in gut dysbiosis in offspring, demonstrated by reduced Muribaculaceae and increased Bacteroides and Alloprevotella [44]. These microbial alterations coincided with impaired cortical neurogenesis - marked by reduced BrdU+ and Ki67+ proliferation, fewer Tbr2+ progenitors and diminished Tbr1+ and Satb2+ neuronal output in embryonic cortices [44]. In adulthood, these offspring exhibited increased corticosterone and persistent anxiety and depression-like behaviors, indicating prenatal programming of neurodevelopment via MGBA [44]. However, the microbial analysis was limited to female mice only, which limits generalizability of MGBA findings. Future studies should include both genders.

Delivery mode: vaginal and cesarean section

Global rates of births via CS rose from 7% in 1990 to 21% by 2023, exceeding the World Health Organization’s recommendations [51]. CS is a surgical intervention in which a baby is delivered through an incision made in the mother’s abdominal wall and uterus [51]. Infants born via CS are more susceptible to long-term health issues including allergic conditions, asthma, GI disorders, obesity and diabetes [31]. Furthermore, large human studies have linked the delivery mode to increased risk of neurodevelopmental alterations. Infants born via CS demonstrate lower neurodevelopmental scores compared to vaginal delivery (VD) [52]. Furthermore, CS may increase the risk for neurodevelopmental disorders such as ADHD or ASD compared to VD [53]. Moreover, children born via CS exhibit lower levels of myelination compared to controls- which normalizes during maturation [54]. Future longitudinal studies should confirm these findings in larger cohorts. MGBA has been proposed as a key mechanism underlying these associations [55]. Furthermore, this concept has been functionally validated in animal models, where restoring microbial composition with Lactobacillus reuteri normalized synaptic plasticity in ASD models via vagus nerve mediated pathways, highlighting the MGBA’s role in shaping neurodevelopment [56]. Mode of delivery has an impact on the infant´s immune system and neurodevelopment in early life, potentially via MGBA [34]. During VD, the initial colonization of the infant gut is mediated by vertical transfer of maternal microbes during birth, indicating the mode of delivery represents a primary determinant of early microbial composition [57]. Infants born via VD were enriched with maternal vaginal-associated taxa, including Lactobacillus, Prevotella, Sneathia [58] and Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides [57]. Infants with higher levels of Bacteroides (measured at one year) had a better language development and cognitive function at two years of age, suggesting a link between early microbial composition and neurodevelopmental outcomes [59]. Intriguingly, the differences in microbiota composition between VD infants and CS birth mode were observed only at day 7, but not at one month of age. These findings suggest that the influence of birth method on infant microbiota may be of short duration [57]. CS birth mode can disrupt the typical postnatal colonization process, potentially interfering with the normal maturation of the MGBA and neurodevelopmental processes [60]. Clinical studies demonstrated infants delivered by CS were dominated by skin-associated bacterial genera, including Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium and Propionibacterium [58] and hospital environment linked pathogenic taxa Enterococcus, Enterobacter and Klebsiella [61]. Higher levels of the latter were linked to weaker gross motor skills (a key marker of early neurodevelopment) in the first six months of infant’s life [62]. Furthermore, GM enriched by Klebsiella is associated with a pro-inflammatory state and an increased vulnerability to brain damage [48]. Interestingly, Klebsiella was also observed in the microbiota of VD infants who were not breastfed, suggesting that other factors such as feeding mode may contribute to bacterial colonization independent of delivery mode [61]. While microbial differences between VD and CS infants reduce over time, a distinct bacterial signature persists in CS born infants up to two years of age [63]. Feeding mode: breastfeeding vs. formula/mixed feeding

The World Health Organization recommends breastfeeding as the primary feeding mode during the first six months postnatally to support healthy growth and neurodevelopment [64]. However, the rate remains below 40%, with infant formula feeding (FF) or mixed feeding (including partial breastfeeding) accounting for the majority of early feeding practices [64]. Breastfeeding has been linked to lower incidence of GI disorders, allergy, respiratory tract infections and Type 2 diabetes [65]. Furthermore, breastfeeding has also been associated with enhanced cognitive and motor development, key indicators of early neurodevelopment [65]. Moreover, children with ASD were less likely to be breastfed than typically developing controls, suggesting a potential role for early feeding practices in neurodevelopmental outcomes [66]. Similarly, absence of breastfeeding has been associated with reduced IQ and a higher likelihood of ADHD [67]. Compared to FF, breast milk contains greater concentrations of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids [68], which contribute to neuronal growth, repair and myelination process- key mechanisms involved in early neurodevelopment [69]. Moreover, myelination supports the development of neural pathways which are required for early cognitive and behavioral maturation [70], suggesting that breastfeeding may support early neurodevelopment processes by promoting myelination [71]. Furthermore, immunoglobulin A (IgA) in breast milk protects the developing brain from neuroinflammation, further suggesting its role in early neurodevelopment [72].

Compared to FF, breastfeeding is associated with distinct microbial profiles in early life. These early microbial differences may influence neurodevelopmental trajectories via MGBA signalling in healthy [73] and preterm born infants [74]. FF infants had significantly higher alpha diversity (a greater variety of microbial species) compared to breastfed infants [73]. Interestingly, higher alpha diversity was reported in ASD patients [75], and in typically developing children with weaker language capabilities at the age of two [76]. These findings suggest that early life microbial enrichment may contribute to atypical neurodevelopment. Furthermore, increased Ruminococcaceae, Blautia, Flavonifractor [73] and Enterobacteriaceae [74] were observed. In contrast, breastfed infants showed higher abundances of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Proteobacteria [73], Escherichia Shigella, Veillonella and Bacteroides [74]. These findings demonstrate that feeding mode shapes early gut colonization patterns, establishing distinct microbiota signatures that may influence neurodevelopmental outcomes. Furthermore, breastfed preterm infants showed higher language scores at 12 and 18 months of age together with improved cognition compared to FF or mixed-fed group, suggesting a microbiota mediated influence on neurodevelopment via MGBA [74]. While the mechanisms remain unclear, the authors hypothesized that oligosaccharides (found in breast milk) act as prebiotics which stimulate the growth and activity of beneficial microbes, potentially supporting neurogenesis via the MGBA [74]. Moreover, Bacteroides derived capsular polysaccharides modulate Th1/Th2 balance and suppress peripheral inflammation, limiting neuroinflammatory signalling that further supports early brain development [77]. Together, these findings suggest that feeding mode-mediated modulation of the MGBA may influence neurodevelopmental processes during the early stages of infancy.

Prevention

Understanding the interplay between maternal microbiota, birth mode and feeding practices provides a foundation for preventive strategies aimed at supporting optimal neurodevelopment. CS induced microbiota alterations may be restored through vaginal microbiota transfer (VMT), exposing newborns to maternal vaginal fluids to simulate microbial exposure of VD [78]. VMT administered topically for fifteen seconds within two minutes after CS delivery improved the microbial profiles similar to those observed in VD neonates within forty two days [55]. Furthermore, the intervention increased Lactobacillus abundance, accelerated microbiota maturation and elevated L-lactic acid levels [55]. The latter may enhance memory-related gene expression by sustaining cerebral energy metabolism, modulating neuronal excitability and regulating plasticity-related signaling pathways [79]. At 6 months, VMT treated infants scored higher on neurodevelopmental tasks compared to CS delivered controls [55]. While these findings suggest early neurodevelopmental benefits of VMT via MGBA, the lasting effects remain unclear.

Recent mechanistic evidence indicates that specific strains of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus influence neurodevelopment through multiple well-characterized pathways. For example, Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus secrete neurotransmitters and neuroactive components, like GABA and SCFAs, that influence synaptic plasticity, neurotransmitter balance, and myelination, especially during early development of the brain [39]. Daily supplementation with Bifidobacterium bifidum and Lactobacillus acidophilus has been shown to significantly reduce neurodevelopmental impairment at two years of corrected age in very-low-birth-weight preterm infants. These findings underscore the translational potential of strain-specific probiotic interventions [80]. Finally, the data point to a rather interesting fact originating from the fact that the actions of probiotics are not the same. They vary as per their property endowments at strain level, which come into play with neurotransmitter systems, immune signaling, barrier integrity, and neurotrophic pathways, thus shedding light on clinical importance for precision-directed microbial therapeutics.

Future studies should test neurodevelopmental scores at several developmental windows.

Similarly, maternal supplementation with anti-inflammatory probiotic Lactobacillus reuteri during pregnancy prevented the emergence of FXS-phenotypes in the offspring [45]. This coincided with reduced dysbiosis and lowered maternal IL-17A levels, suggesting prenatal microbial modulation as an effective intervention to attenuate dysbiosis-induced neurodevelopmental risk [45]. Furthermore, early postnatal probiotic intervention prevented neurodevelopmental impairments in very premature infants. Daily supplementation with Bifidobacterium bifidum and Lactobacillus acidophilus from day 7 reduced neurodevelopmental impairment in preterm infants born before 32 weeks [81]. Moreover, at 2 years, probiotic treated infants had fewer mild and moderate neurodevelopmental impairments and higher language scores [81]. The authors hypothesized that these benefits were potentially mediated by reduced intestinal permeability, lower lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels and preserved brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signalling during early MGBA development [81]. However, neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed at 2 years may not capture whether the observed benefits persist. Future studies should assess long-term outcomes.

Neurodevelopmental disorders and MGBA disruption

Behavioural and cognitive disorders

ADHD

Background

ADHD is a common disorder with a prevalence of 5% among children and adolescents as reported in a human observational study by [82]. The core symptoms of ADHD are inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity. These behavioral symptoms form the basis for a clinical diagnosis, despite advances in understanding the disorder’s underlying pathophysiology [83]. The GM has been implicated in the pathophysiology of various neuropsychiatric and neurological disorders through both preclinical (animal) and (clinical) human studies [84]. This review will explore the neurological basis of ADHD and examine how disruptions in the GBA, through immune, neural, and microbial pathways, may influence brain function and contribute to ADHD pathophysiology, highlighting current findings, proposed mechanisms, and potential clinical implications.

ADHD and MGBA

The intestinal microbiota, alongside the vagus nerve and immune system, plays a key role in the GBA, facilitating bidirectional communication between the gut and the CNS. Its involvement in the development of various diseases has been a focus of growing research interest. Preclinical studies have demonstrated complex, bidirectional communication between the brain, gut, and microbiome, involving multiple signaling pathways and feedback mechanisms. Disruptions in this system have been observed in animal models of neurological and psychiatric conditions, though clear causal links—especially in disorders like irritable bowel syndrome—remain under investigation [85]. For instance, several human observational studies demonstrate that children with ADHD show reduced gut microbial diversity and altered abundance of specific taxa, including decreases in Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcaceae, and increases in Bifidobacterium, Odoribacter, and Eggerthella, compared to healthy controls. Notably, a reduction in Faecalibacterium prausnitzii has been associated with greater symptom severity, possibly due to its anti-inflammatory properties [86]. Dysbiosis may disrupt communication between the brain, gut and microbiome, leading to BBB dysfunction and neuroinflammation, with the inflammasome pathway emerging as a key immune mechanism, potentially linking gut microbes to various neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders. Activated by microbial or stress signals, the inflammasome releases pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18, contributing to neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders. While this highlights the immune-driven impact of gut microbes on brain health, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further study [87]. Unraveling these complex gut-brain interactions holds exciting promise for advancing our understanding of neurodevelopmental disorders like ADHD.

Mechanistic links: dopamine, SCFAs, inflammation

Epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, may help explain how early-life environmental factors influence the development of ADHD. A human observational study found that lower methylation levels at birth in genes like DRD4 and 5-HTT were associated with increased ADHD symptoms by age six, suggesting a potential link between early gene regulation and later behavioral outcomes [88]. Dopamine plays a central role in cognition, motivation, and motor function, and its optimal availability is essential for brain health. Emerging evidence from preclinical studies suggests that the GM influence dopamine metabolism and signaling through the MGBA, involving microbial enzymes, the vagus nerve, and immune pathways. Specific genera such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Clostridium have been shown to affect dopamine levels, with dysbiosis potentially contributing to dopaminergic dysfunction [89]. Emerging evidence from human observational studies suggests that the MGBA may also play a role in ADHD pathophysiology. This includes potential links between GM composition, omega-3 fatty acid imbalance, sleep disturbances, and ADHD symptoms, as well as growing interest in probiotics as a therapeutic strategy [90]. A recent human observational study comparing children with ADHD to healthy controls found significantly reduced gut microbial diversity and lower concentrations of SCFAs such as acetic, propionic, and valeric acids in the ADHD group. These changes suggest that both microbial composition and SCFA metabolism may contribute to ADHD pathophysiology, supporting the potential of the GM as a biomarker or therapeutic target in ADHD [91]. The kynurenine pathway (KP), which metabolizes tryptophan, has been increasingly associated with inflammation-related mechanisms in ADHD. Recent human observational studies suggest that immune dysregulation and KP alterations may contribute to ADHD pathophysiology, although the exact mechanisms remain unclear [92]. Immune-mediated neuroinflammation is a major mechanism by which the GBA is thought to affect neurodevelopment. In human studies, increased levels of systemic inflammatory markers, such as glycosylated protein A, have been linked to heightened ADHD symptom severity [93]. Notably, postprandial inflammation after a standardized meal was associated with the abundance of tryptophan biosynthesis pathways in the gut microbiome, indicating a direct molecular connection between microbial metabolism, host immune signaling, and core ADHD features.

Recent systematic reviews have begun to uncover potential links between gut microbiome composition and ADHD symptoms in children and young adults, highlighting specific microbial patterns that may contribute to the disorder’s neurobiological underpinnings. A human observational study investigating the gut microbiome in adolescents and adults with ADHD found marginally significant differences in microbial composition compared to controls, including a nominal increase in the genus Bifidobacterium. Using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, the researchers identified enhanced predicted microbial gene functionality for cyclohexadienyl dehydratase, an enzyme involved in phenylalanine synthesis, a dopamine precursor. This increase was significantly associated with reduced ventral striatal activity during reward anticipation, as shown by fMRI—highlighting a potential microbiome-mediated pathway affecting dopaminergic signaling, a core feature in ADHD pathophysiology [81]. A systematic review examining the gut microbiome in children and young adults with ADHD found inconsistent results at broader taxonomic levels but identified some notable genus- and species-level differences. Individuals with ADHD showed increased levels of Odoribacter and Eggerthella, and decreased Faecalibacterium, with some species like Faecalibacterium prausnitzii inversely associated with symptom severity. Clinical implications

Maternal factors

In a preclinical study, maternal gut dysbiosis caused by a Westernized diet during pregnancy has been linked to neurodevelopmental abnormalities resembling FXS in mouse offspring, including hyperactivity and reduced FMRP expression. In utero supplementation with the probiotic Lactobacillus reuteri reduced inflammatory markers like maternal IL-17 and ameliorated these FXS-like phenotypes [32]. This study is relevant to ADHD, as FXS shares overlapping features such as hyperactivity, suggesting that maternal gut dysbiosis and prenatal inflammation may contribute to ADHD-like neurodevelopmental outcomes through similar pathways. Preclinical studies have shown that maternal high-fat diets negatively impact offspring neurodevelopment by promoting systemic inflammation and gut microbiota dysbiosis during pregnancy and lactation. These changes contribute to increased pro-inflammatory cytokines and epigenetic modifications in the developing brain, such as reduced BDNF expression, potentially leading to long-term behavioral and cognitive deficits resembling neurodevelopmental disorders like ADHD and autism [94].

Early-life environmental exposures, such as maternal infection or poor prenatal nutrition, have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD. Notably, imbalanced levels of essential fatty acids, particularly polyunsaturated fatty acids, and their immunomodulatory roles may contribute to altered brain development and inflammation. Emerging human observational evidence suggests that these factors can influence the GM, which may mediate the link between prenatal environmental insults and the risk of ADHD [95]. Emerging human observational evidence suggests a potential link between early-life antibiotic use and increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD, potentially through disruptions to the MGBA. Shared biological mechanisms between atopic diseases and ADHD, such as immune activation and microbiome alterations, highlight the need for further investigation into early microbiota-related risk factors [96]. Both maternal and early-life exposure to antibiotics have been linked to an increased risk of developing ADHD in children, with early-life exposure showing a stronger association. A large Swedish cohort study found a dose-response relationship, indicating that higher antibiotic use in pregnancy or infancy is associated with greater ADHD risk, possibly through microbiota disruption and altered GBA signaling [97]. Clearly, factors beginning as early as pregnancy may shape the neurodevelopmental trajectory, inviting further inquiry into early intervention approaches.

Dietary and probiotic interventions

A systematic review of randomized controlled trials and cohort studies found that probiotic and prebiotic supplementation, particularly Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG and Synbiotic 2000, may improve emotional well-being, quality of life, and reduce inflammation in children with ADHD. These findings suggest potential benefits of microbiota-targeted interventions as adjunctive therapies for ADHD, though larger and longer-term studies are still needed [98]. Human observational evidence suggests that unhealthy diets are positively associated with ADHD, while healthier patterns may be protective. Some benefit has been observed with vitamin D (± magnesium) supplementation and probiotics like Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, though current evidence is limited and further research is needed before widespread clinical application [99]. Emerging therapeutic strategies, including psychobiotics and FMT, are being explored as potential approaches for modulating the gut microbiome to alleviate ADHD symptoms [100]. Emerging human observational research highlights the role of gut dysbiosis, inflammation, and poor dietary patterns, particularly the Western diet, in the pathophysiology of ADHD. Nutritional interventions, including omega-3 supplementation and the Mediterranean diet, show promise in modulating gut-brain pathways and reducing ADHD symptoms, though more targeted clinical studies are needed to establish [101]. Although research is still limited, some, human observational studies indicate that early-life prebiotic supplementation may reduce the risk of developing ADHD by modulating gut microbial composition, particularly by influencing Bifidobacterium levels. These findings highlight the potential of gut-targeted interventions as future tools in psychiatric care [90]. [102] found that supplementation with Bifidobacterium was associated with improvement in ADHD symptoms, while systematic reviews highlight promising but preliminary evidence for probiotics such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus [102]. While probiotic supplementation significantly altered microbial diversity in ASD, no such changes were observed in ADHD, though some potentially adverse bacteria were underrepresented. These findings support the potential of microbiome-targeted interventions as therapeutic strategies in neurodevelopmental disorders like ADHD [104]. These promising findings hint that what we eat may play a surprising role in supporting mental health and cognitive function.

Conclusion

To sum up, current research highlights a significant connection between the GBA and the pathophysiology of ADHD, with disruptions in GM influencing neurodevelopment through immune, neural, and metabolic pathways. Human observational studies have found that individuals with ADHD often exhibit altered gut microbial diversity and composition, which may affect dopamine metabolism, immune function, and inflammation, key mechanisms implicated in ADHD symptoms. Early-life factors such as maternal diet, antibiotic exposure, and prenatal inflammation further underscore the role of the gut microbiome in shaping neurodevelopmental outcomes. While interventions like probiotics, prebiotics, and dietary modifications show promise in modulating the GBA and alleviating ADHD symptoms, current evidence remains preliminary. Experimental evidence suggests several pathways by which microbiome dysbiosis may contribute to NDDs. In ADHD, altered microbial composition has been associated with changes in dopamine-related metabolites, and animal studies indicate that microbial modulation can influence dopaminergic signaling and hyperactivity-like behaviors [90, 105]. More robust, long-term studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and determine the effectiveness of these microbiome-targeted therapies. Overall, integrating GM research into ADHD understanding offers avenues for potential therapeutic development; however, further investigation is needed for clinical translation.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)

Background

ASD is a complex neurodevelopmental condition defined by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, along with restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior. While the etiology of ASD is multifactorial, involving genetic and environmental influences, there is increasing recognition of the GBA as a significant contributor to ASD pathophysiology. Recent studies have identified consistent alterations in the composition and function of the GM in individuals with ASD, suggesting that microbial dysbiosis may be both a consequence and a contributor to ASD pathology [107].

Microbiota dysbiosis in ASD

One of the most robust findings in ASD research is the presence of GM dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial diversity and altered relative abundance of specific bacterial taxa. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 studies including > 1,200 children, children with ASD had significantly lower abundances of Bifidobacterium (standardized mean difference = −0.513; 95% CI = −0.953 to −0.073) and higher levels of Clostridia and Desulfovibrio compared to neurotypical controls [108]. This dysbiotic profile is often accompanied by GI symptoms, which are reported at higher rates in ASD populations than in neurotypical controls [109]. Metabolomic analyses have revealed that these microbial alterations are associated with decreased production of SCFAs, particularly butyrate, which is crucial for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and modulating immune responses [110]. A compromised gut barrier, often referred to as “leaky gut,” allows for increased translocation of microbial metabolites and pro-inflammatory molecules into systemic circulation, potentially triggering neuroinflammatory cascades implicated in ASD [107].

The mechanisms by which the gut microbiome influences neurodevelopment and behavior in ASD are multifaceted. Immune modulation is a primary pathway, as gut dysbiosis can activate mucosal immune cells and promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These cytokines have been shown to cross the BBB and disrupt neuronal maturation and synaptic plasticity, processes critical for normal cognitive and social development [111]. Animal models of maternal immune activation (MIA) using preclinical evidence further support this link, demonstrating that prenatal exposure to inflammatory stimuli alters fetal microglial development, leading to behavioral phenotypes reminiscent of ASD [112].

Other preclinical studies have provided promising insights on how changes in microbiomes can affect neurodevelopment; however, the issue of true cause-effect relationships still needs to be established in humans. MIA models suggest that maternal dysbiosis producing IL-17A affect fetal cortical development and stereotypical offspring phenotypes resembling those of ASD, which are diminished by blocking IL-17A. In mice born pathological in oxytocinase from feces of children with ASD, offspring also have demonstrated behavioral disturbance and modified actions involving serotonin and tryptophan metabolism in the gut and brain, which strongly indicates that such behaviors are microbial modulation of neurotransmitter pathways [110]. Other works suggested that specific short-chain fatty acids of microbial origin could actually restore the immaturity of germ-free microglia; hence, these metabolites were very carefully acknowledged in microglial maturation and maintenance of immune homeostasis. At the same time, multi-omics analyses in children with ASD have shown the role of various microbial pathways linked to systemic inflammation, sphingolipid metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis that could be related to immune-metabolic pathways. Overall, the collective findings, therefore, enhance biological plausibility regarding the contribution of gut dysbiosis to neurodevelopmental changes, besides calling for further longitudinal studies involving human interventional trials to confirm causation.

Microbial influence on neurotransmitter systems

Another key mechanism involves the regulation of neurotransmitter systems by the GM. Approximately 95% of the body’s serotonin is synthesized in the gut, and microbial metabolites can influence both serotonin and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) production, thereby affecting synaptic pruning and the formation of neural circuits [114]. Certain bacteria, such as Bacteroides, also impact dopamine metabolism, which is relevant to the reward and motivation deficits observed in ASD [115]. Additionally, alterations in tryptophan metabolism, mediated by gut microbes like Lactobacillus, can shift the balance toward KP metabolites, some of which are neurotoxic and may exacerbate excitotoxicity in the developing brain [110]. Metabolic signaling through microbial-derived SCFAs, such as butyrate, propionate, and acetate, further underscores the gut-brain connection. These metabolites not only enhance the integrity of the BBB but also regulate microglial activation and neuroinflammatory responses [108]. Deficiencies in SCFA production, as observed in ASD, are associated with increased neuroinflammation and aberrant brain development [116].

Microbiome-targeted prevention strategies

Given the central role of the microbiome in modulating these pathways, targeted interventions aimed at restoring microbial balance have emerged as promising strategies for influencing ASD related neurodevelopmental outcomes, particularly when applied during critical windows of neurodevelopment. Prenatal interventions, such as maternal probiotic supplementation, have shown efficacy in animal models. For example, administration of Lactobacillus reuteri during pregnancy in mice can normalize IL-17a signalling and reduce ASD-like behaviors in offspring [111]. Similarly, in animal studies, increased maternal dietary fiber intake has been linked to the enrichment of SCFA-producing bacteria and a reduction in neuroinflammatory markers in neonates [116]. Postnatal strategies also hold significant potential. Breastfeeding is particularly beneficial, as human milk oligosaccharides selectively promote the growth of Bifidobacterium species, enhancing gut barrier function and reducing microbial translocation and systemic inflammation [114]. The judicious use of antibiotics in infancy is another important consideration; avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotics helps preserve keystone taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which are critical for immune tolerance and anti-inflammatory signaling [117]. Emerging therapeutics, including FMT, have demonstrated the ability to restore microbial diversity and improve behavioral symptoms in children with ASD in small trials [107]. An open-label clinical trial enrolled 40 children with ASD (ages 3–17) presenting with GI symptoms such as constipation and diarrhea. ASD patients compared with typically developing controls showed distinct baseline behavioral, GI, and microbiota features. FMT enhanced GI and behavioral symptoms with no severe adverse events and altered neurotransmitter levels to render the microbiota to a healthier profile. Decreases in Eubacterium coprostanoligenes showed correlation with symptom improvement, indicating its potential as a treatment response variable [118]. Likewise, a parallel randomized, double-blind clinical trial evaluates the safety and efficacy of a pediatric FMT formulation in children aged 3–9 with ASD. 42 children with ASD are randomized into treatment and placebo groups, while 30 healthy children are included as controls. Changes in ASD symptoms, gut microbiota composition, and metabolites pre- and post-treatment evaluation through metagenomics and metabolomic analyses are being performed [119]. The phased administration of FMT treatment is underway, with monitoring of safety and gastrointestinal and behavioral improvements. The results should clarify the role of FMT in modulating gut microbiota and alleviating the core symptoms of ASD, thereby informing the optimization of its dosing and treatment protocol. Another randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled trial of oral FMT in 103 children with ASD. The participants received either FMT or placebo for two 6-day treatment periods, spaced 4 weeks apart, using capsules from screened healthy donors. No serious adverse events were reported, and FMT procedure was tolerated well. Interestingly, primary outcomes did not show any significant improvement, but secondary analyses revealed some effects by FMT on socialization [120]. These findings suggest that FMT may have slight benefits for social functioning in ASD but also recognize a placebo effect; hence a large-scale controlled trial is warranted.

Although the long-term preventive efficacy of FMT remains under investigation, preliminary results are promising. Phage therapy, which utilizes bacteriophages to selectively target pathogenic bacteria such as Clostridium bolteae, offers another innovative approach to modulate the gut microbiome without disrupting beneficial commensal communities [115].

Challenges and future directions

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. The heterogeneity of ASD phenotypes and the variability of microbiome composition across individuals and populations complicate the identification of universal biomarkers and treatment targets [121]. Future research should focus on longitudinal cohort studies that track microbiome development from birth in infants at high risk for ASD, such as siblings of affected individuals [108]. Personalized probiotic interventions, tailored to individual metabolic and immune profiles, may enhance the efficacy of therapeutic interventions [109]. Integrating multi-omics approaches, including metagenomics, metabolomics, and neuroimaging, will be essential for identifying predictive biomarkers and elucidating the complex interactions between the microbiome and neurodevelopment [111]. In summary, the gut microbiome is a modifiable factor in ASD pathophysiology, and interventions targeting the microbiome during prenatal and early postnatal life represent feasible and potentially effective preventive strategies. While further clinical validation is required, current evidence supports the optimization of the microbiome as a promising avenue for mitigating neurodevelopmental disruptions in susceptible populations [112].

Tourette syndrome (TS)

Background

TS is a neurodevelopmental disorder affecting approximately 1% of children worldwide [122], with symptoms appearing between 5 and 7 years of age [123]. Tics, a hallmark clinical feature of TS, present as sudden repetitive motor actions or vocal sounds, with their expression varying in intensity, frequency and temporal pattern [124]. Treatment efficacy varies, with no single pharmacological option demonstrating consistent effectiveness across individuals with TS [125]. Comorbid psychiatric conditions are common: 72% of individuals with TS are diagnosed with obsessive-compulsive disorder or ADHD while anxiety, depression and ASD are also prevalent [124]. Risk of developing TS is influenced by immunological, environmental and genetic factors [126]. Maternal smoking during gestation, breech fetal positioning, caesarean delivery and premature birth are among the most studied perinatal risk factors [127].

Immune dysregulation in the pathophysiology of TS has been supported by a growing body of clinical evidence [128]. The autoimmune hypothesis proposes that prior Streptococcus infection may trigger TS onset via aberrant immune responses [129]. One proposed mechanism involves a reduction in regulatory T cells among TS patients, which may lead to heightened immune activation during pathogen clearance [130]. Moreover, heightened immune activity elevates circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and TNF-α, driving systemic inflammation. These cytokines can penetrate the BBB, activate microglia and astrocytes and initiate neuroinflammatory cascades potentially contributing to TS symptomatology [131].

TS and MGBA

A growing body of clinical and preclinical evidence implicates the MGBA in modulating TS vulnerability. Given that GM regulate immune function and inflammation- key features of TS pathophysiology, the MGBA represents a promising therapeutic target [132]. Furthermore, a recent Mendelian randomisation analysis in humans (two-sample study, n = 18,473 for microbiome GWAS and n = 14,307 for TS GWAS) identified a potential causal link between reduced levels of class Bacteroidia and order Bacteroidales and increased TS risk (effect size = −0.46, p = 2.51 × 10− 3 for both taxa), suggesting microbiota-driven contributions to TS pathophysiology [133]. Dopamine and GABA- two neurotransmitters central to TS pathophysiology are synthesized in the gut [134], further supporting the MGBA as a mechanistic pathway through which microbial and peripheral signals may modulate central neurotransmission and tic expression [135]. Whilst only limited number of studies has examined microbiota changes in TS, available observational human studies indicate altered gut microbial composition [136, 137]. Altered beta diversity (reflecting differences in microbial community composition) was found in children with TS versus control, whilst alpha diversity remained unchanged- indicating compositional divergence [137]. In the same study, a significantly higher abundance in Flavonifractor, Faecalibacterium, Oscillibacter, Fusicatenibacter, Anaerostipes, Hungatella, Anaerotruncus and Eisenbergiella was observed in children with TS [137]. Flavonifractor exhibits a capacity to degrade quercetin (a flavonoid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties) [138]. Therefore, the authors hypothesised that the reduction in quercetin availability may contribute to the pro-inflammatory processes implicated in TS pathogenesis. Moreover, enriched pro-inflammatory bacteria Bacteroides plebeius and Ruminococcus lactaris were found in a clinical study with TS patients within a mixed-diagnosis tic disorder (TD) cohort [136]. Interestingly, Ruminococcus lactaris is common in Crohn’s disease and rheumatoid arthritis patients. [136]. This convergence across inflammatory disorders suggests shared gut microbial signatures that may underlie immune dysregulation in TS. Observational human study demonstrated that SCFA-producing bacteria such as Clostridia_UCG_014, Bifidobacterium and Prevotella were more abundant in healthy controls, suggesting their potentialrole in maintaining gut–brain and immune homeostasis [137]. GM may modulate neurochemical pathways implicated in TS, including dopaminergic and GABAergic signalling. In children with TS, gut-brain modules related to neurotransmitter metabolism, such as DA degradation, and histamine degradation were altered, suggesting a microbial influence on neurochemical pathways [137]. However, study findings were not stratified by gender and by the severity of the disorder, limiting the generalisation. Similarly, a broader analysis of TD in the same cohort (n = 49, including 23 patients with TS) found a depletion of GABA-producing taxa (Bifidobacterium spp., Akkermansia muciniphila, Eubacterium spp.) and increased abundance of the GABA-degrading species Klebsiella pneumoniae, all of which correlated with greater tic severity [136]. Moreover, the study reported elevated microbial GABA degradation pathways in TD patients compared to controls [136]. A lower concentration of GABA in the primary sensorimotor cortex has been proposed as a contributing factor to both motor tics and sensory processing deficits in TS [139], suggesting microbial contribution to GABAergic dysfunction in TS pathophysiology. While the authors hypothesised that GABA-modulating bacteria may hold diagnostic value in TD, the inclusion of a mixed-diagnosis cohort limits TS specific conclusions. Future studies should focus on TS patients only.

Rodent models provide causal evidence that MGBA disruption contributes to the aetiology of TS, through microbiota alterations, barrier dysfunction, immune activation and neural imbalance. An increase of proinflammatory bacteria and reduced beneficial bacteria was found across rodent studies, indicating proinflammatory microbial shift [140–142]. Elevated Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 and Bacilli were found in TS male rat model- these taxa have been linked to colitis and Irritable Bowel Syndrome [142], indicating shared microbial signatures associated with GI inflammation. Furthermore, proinflammatory taxa Oscillospiraceae, Desulfovibrionaceae and Marinifilaceae were increased in TS male mice models with reduced abundance of anti-inflammatory Muribaculaceae, suggesting a shift towards a proinflammatory state. [141]. Moreover, SCFA-producing taxa were depleted in rodent models, indicating microbial dysbiosis. Male rat models of TS showed reduced abundance of Lactobacillus and Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 (a major butyrate producer), with direct correlations to reduced butyrate levels and impaired barrier integrity [140].

Regulation of neurotransmitter synthesis represents one potential pathway through which the GM may influence neurobiological processes underlying TS pathophysiology [143]. TS mouse model showed increased striatal levels of DA, Glutamate, and Aspartate and reduced expression of dopamine transporter mRNA [141]. These changes coincided with microbial alterations and hallmark TS-like behaviours such as head shaking and tilting, suggesting MGBA involvement in TS pathology. Further mechanisms, linking the MGBA to TS pathophysiology were demonstrated in a TS rat model study [140]. In male rats, microbial dysbiosis was associated with reduced expression of butyrate-metabolizing enzymes, leading to lower butyric acid levels in both feces and striatum. This butyrate deficiency coincided with gut barrier dysfunction, demonstrated by reduced colonic ZO-1 and occludin [140]. Moreover, BBB disruption (marked by decreased cerebral ZO-1 and Claudin-5) was observed. These barrier impairments were accompanied by elevated striatal LPS, IL-6, TNF-α and increased CD86+ microglia, indicating neuroinflammation. These preclinical findings suggest that microbiota-derived butyrate deficiency initiates a cascade of barrier dysfunction and neuroinflammation underlying TS pathophysiology. However, the exclusive use of male rodents limits generalisability. Future studies should include both sexes to identify sex-specific MGBA mechanisms in TS [140].

Prevention

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been increasingly explored as a therapeutic approach for TS [144]. Acupuncture (a traditional Chinese medical technique involving the insertion of fine needles into specific body points) demonstrates therapeutic potential for TS by simultaneously regulating GM and central neurotransmitter pathways through MGBA in preclinical studies [141]. A two-week acupuncture intervention in a preclinical mouse model (n = 36 male C57/BL6 mice, 9 per group) targeting traditional tic-related acupoints reversed TS-like behaviours by restoring MGBA function [141]. The authors demonstrated that the treatment significantly increased beneficial Muribaculaceae (p = 0.001) and reduced pro-inflammatory taxa, including Oscillospiraceae (p = 0.049), Desulfovibrionaceae (p = 0.001) and Marinifilaceae (p = 0.014). It also normalized striatal neurotransmitter levels by significantly lowering DA, Glu, and Asp while upregulating DAT expression (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01 compared to untreated TS controls), which coincided with significantly improved stereotypic behaviour scores (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) [141]. These preclinical findings suggest that acupuncture may represent a potential MGBA targeted treatment approach for TS. However, it remains unclear whether the neuroprotective effects persist post-treatment. Future studies should employ longitudinal study design and evaluate these mechanisms in human clinical trials. Similarly, four-week treatment with Jing An (JA) decoction (a traditional Chinese medicine formulation containing eight herbs) improved TS-like behaviours in male rats by restoring MGBA function [140]. JA increased beneficial butyrate-producing bacteria, elevated butyric acid levels and reduced striatal neuroinflammation, which coincided with improved TS-like behaviours [140]. These preclinical findings suggest that JA may hold therapeutic potential as an MGBA-targeted intervention for TS, although its translational relevance remains to be established in humans. Moreover, in human studies, treatments with probiotics have demonstrated the capacity to influence brain function and behaviour through interactions within the MGBA. A randomized open-label clinical trial (n = 57 children with chronic TS/TD) compared Limosilactobacillus reuteri CGMCC No. 25,664 supplementation (n = 36) with clonidine transdermal patch treatment (n = 21) [145]. After eight weeks, the probiotic group showed greater reductions in Yale Global Tic Severity Scale scores compared to clonidine treatment (mean change −12.2±16.51 vs. −5.1±16.19, p = 0.024). The probiotic group also demonstrated improvements in hyperactivity symptoms (p = 0.010) and social competence (p = 0.049) [145]. However, the mechanisms remain unclear, as the study did not assess changes in GM composition or mechanistic pathways involved in the MGBA. Furthermore, the results were not stratified by sex, limiting its generalisability across genders [145]. Future studies should include faecal metagenomic analysis and stratify findings by gender. Together, these findings highlight the promise of microbiota-targeted traditional and probiotic therapies in TS, while underscoring the need for mechanistic validation and long-term clinical studies.

Syndromic and genetic neurodevelopmental disorders

Rett syndrome (RTT)

Background

RTT is a severe X-linked neurogenetic neurodevelopmental disorder, primarily affecting females, caused by pathogenic mutations in the MECP2 gene. RTT is characterized by initially normal brain development, followed by progressive neurological regression in neurological and developmental functions, including loss of spoken language and purposeful hand use, along with characteristic hand stereotypies and impaired gait. In addition, individuals often experience a range of comorbidities, including seizures, autonomic dysfunction such as breathing irregularities, anxiety, and GI issues [146]. While the primary focus of research in RTT has been its impact on the CNS function, emerging evidence suggests an interaction between RTT pathology and the gut microbiome. The MGBA is a new frontier in science that investigates the role of the GM in modulating brain function and behaviour, potentially contributing to the development of various neuropsychiatric and neurological disorders. Preclinical (animal) and clinical (human) studies have shown that altering GM—through probiotics, antibiotics, or fecal transplants—can impact brain-related outcomes in some neurodevelopmental disorders [84]. This review evaluates current evidence on gut microbiome alterations in RTT and explores whether these changes represent a modifiable factor contributing to disease progression.

Neurological basis of RTT

The prevalence of RTT is estimated to be 7.1 per 100,000 females, with consistent findings across diverse populations [148]. Males with MECP2 mutations die within the first year or two of life due to a more severe phenotype. The classic Rett phenotype evolves through different, distinct clinical and pathological stages [149]. Patients have typical neurodevelopment at birth and follow a normal developmental trajectory until around 6–18 months of life. They develop decelerated head growth (head circumference falls behind what is expected for age) in the first few months of age, which is thought to reflect the abnormally densely packed neurons with abnormal neurite growth [150]. Patients exhibit stagnation in brain development, characterized by a lack of acquisition of new motor, language, and cognitive skills. This is followed by a (rapid destructive phase) between 1 and 4 years of age, which signifies a period of rapid developmental regression and loss of purposeful hand movements and complete loss of expressive language. During early preschool to early school years, they go through a (pseudo-stationary phase) in which the phenotype stabilizes. The last stage of the disease is a late motor deterioration phase in which all motor skills are lost. Patients have a high prevalence of intellectual disability, autonomic dysfunction, including irregular breathing, dystonia, ataxia, and epilepsy. GI symptoms are reported in over 90% of patients, including constipation, bloating, chewing, and feeding difficulties [151]. Given the progressive nature and the multisystem involvement, RTT places a substantial burden on both families and the healthcare system, with caregivers reporting intense demands on time, finances, and emotional well-being. Nearly all affected individuals require assistance with daily activities, experience frequent medical complications, and utilize a wide array of healthcare services, including multiple specialist visits, therapies, and hospitalizations, independent of the severity of the disease [152].

MeCP2 protein is a transcriptional regulator that binds to methylated cytosines in the DNA. It is highly expressed prenatally in the neurons and continues to be expressed into adulthood. Defects in this protein, a result of MECP2 pathogenic mutations, lead to a wide range of abnormalities in the brain affecting synaptic development and brain maturation. More specifically, there is neuropathological evidence for arrested ongoing synaptic formation and maturation as well as impaired formation of axons and dendrites, dendritic branching, and numbers/density of dendritic spines [150, 153]. This translates into aberrant plasticity mechanisms including impaired long-term potentiation and absent long-term inhibition, and impaired synaptic scaling [154]. Abnormal neuronal homeostatic synaptic scaling refers to an increase in glutamate signalling and inappropriate increase, rather than decrease, in glutamate receptors, leading to synaptic overshoot. This overshoot exposes the young brain to cytotoxic levels of glutamate that interfere with normal neuronal transmission and activity-dependent neuronal plasticity required for learning and memory during sensitive periods of brain development. In young patients with RTT, there is an increase in glutamate to cytotoxic levels with concomitant up-regulation of NMDA receptor density compared to age-matched controls. This contrasts with older patients with RTT ( > 10 years of age) who show abnormally low glutamate levels compared to age-matched controls [155].

MeCP2 is not neuron-specific, but rather a ubiquitously expressed transcriptional regulator, albeit with variable levels of expression. MeCP2 expression has been detected in peripheral organs, including the lung, spleen, heart, and kidney, though at lower levels compared to the brain. MeCP2 is also expressed in monocytes and macrophages, where it modulates inflammatory responses and may contribute to the systemic immune dysregulation seen in RTT. Relevant to this review, MeCP2 is expressed in the ENS, where it influences gut motility and GI homeostasis [156]. This multisystemic expression provides a biologically plausible link between MeCP2 deficiency, the systemic manifestations of RTT, and alterations in the GBA (Fig. 3). Gut microbiota in RTT: human studies

GI problems are nearly universal in RTT, imposing a significant clinical and financial burden on patients and caregivers. A large-scale survey of 118 girls and women with RTT revealed that 81% experienced constipation, 70% reported abdominal gas and bloating, 73% had chewing and swallowing difficulties, and over 50% displayed irritability related to GI distress. There was also frequent use of proton pump inhibitors (52%) and laxatives (64%) compared to age-matched controls [159]. A recent systematic review by [160] critically evaluated studies examining the intestinal microbiome across neurodevelopmental disorders, including three primary investigations focused on RTT. Despite the small number of RTT-specific studies, all three reported consistent features of microbial dysbiosis and functional disruption. [161] studied 20 females with RTT and age-matched neurotypical controls, conducting one of the earliest and most comprehensive characterizations. They identified significant shifts in both bacterial and fungal communities, reporting an increased abundance of Bacteroidetes and reduced Firmicutes, alongside elevated levels of isobutyric and isovaleric acids, suggesting enhanced protein fermentation. Patients with RTT showed reduced microbial richness and were dominated by potentially pro-inflammatory taxa such as Bifidobacterium, Clostridium XIVa/XIVb, Anaerostipes, Enterococcus, Escherichia/Shigella, and the fungal genus Candida. These microbial shifts coincided with elevated fecal calprotectin and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, indicating a state of low-grade intestinal inflammation. Building on this [162], investigated the relationship between clinical phenotype, microbiota composition, dietary intake, and SCFAs production in RTT. In their study of 8 RTT patients and 10 matched controls, 16S rRNA sequencing revealed lower α-diversity in RTT samples and an enrichment of Bacteroidaceae, Clostridium spp., and Sutterella spp., alongside mild depletion of Ruminococcaceae. While overall SCFA levels were similar, RTT samples exhibited slightly higher levels of butyrate, propionate, and branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs) which are metabolites often linked with increased protein fermentation. Functional pathway predictions indicated an increased expression of genes related to propionate, butyrate, and amino acid metabolism, reflecting both dietary influence and microbial adaptation. [163] conducted the first human observational study integrating 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics to profile the gut microbiome and fecal metabolite landscape in individuals with RTT. Their cohort included 20 females with RTT and 20 neurotypical controls matched for age and body mass index. They observed reduced alpha diversity and significant compositional shifts, including lower abundance of Blautia, Eubacterium, and Roseburia, alongside increased Enterococcus and Clostridium. Functionally, RTT samples exhibited reduced levels of neuroactive metabolites such as GABA, tyrosine, and dopamine precursors, suggesting a microbial contribution to neurochemical imbalance. Additionally, they found elevated levels of SCFAs and BCFAs catabolites, which were positively correlated with GI symptom severity. This study shifted the focus from taxonomic profiling to microbial function, which is a more clinically relevant approach. It also highlighted interindividual variability and the importance of age, hormonal status, and systemic/dietary context.

While causality cannot be established, these studies revealed a characteristic microbial and metabolic profile in RTT, marked by reduced diversity, elevated SCFAs, inflammation-associated taxa, and metabolite deficiencies that may contribute to GI symptoms, visceral pain, feeding issues, and potentially neurobehavioral manifestations. Nonetheless [160], emphasized methodological limitations across these studies, including small sample sizes, cross-sectional designs, and dietary and/or medication confounders.

Nutrition plays a critical role in the management of RTT. Girls with RTT are particularly vulnerable to nutritional deficiencies, with studies reporting significant deficits in body weight, height, and intake of essential nutrients such as calories, protein, calcium, iron, and dietary fiber compared to healthy peers. These imbalances are often compounded by feeding difficulties and a preference for soft, energy-dense foods, which may not meet the metabolic demands of growth and development. Over time, these issues contribute to poor somatic growth, increasing the risk of malnutrition and cachexia [164]. Importantly, many individuals with RTT consume protein-heavy diets with low fiber intake, which can alter gut microbial ecology and promote inflammation. These nutritional patterns may reinforce dysbiosis and metabolic stress, exacerbating both GI and neurological symptoms. Dietary interventions therefore represent a promising but underexplored strategy for modulating the MGBA and improving overall symptoms in RTT.

Observed microbiota alterations in RTT may partly reflect dietary factors acting as confounders. A human cross-sectional observational study demonstrated that dietary patterns may confound microbiota alterations in RTT. Females with RTT (n = 8) consumed significantly more protein (70.3 g vs 51.5 g/day) and less fibre (6.5 vs 9.9 g/1000 kcal) than age-matched controls (n = 10) [165]. In the same study, higher protein intake positively correlated with Bacteroides and Clostridium (pro-inflammatory taxa increased in RTT). Similarly, higher fibre intake positively correlated with Christensenellaceae (a butyrate producing taxon) elevated in controls [165]. Moreover, reduced fibre intake in RTT (6.2 vs 11.1 g/day) was associated with altered microbiome composition in another human cohort (n = 44 RTT vs n = 21 controls), however specific taxa were not identified, limiting mechanistic insights [166]. These findings suggest that RTT associated dysbiosis may reflect high- protein, low-fibre dietary patterns rather than syndrome-specific effects.

Gut microbiota in RTT: animal models and mechanisms

In animal models of RTT, microbiota disruptions have been observed prior to the onset of overt neurological decline. In Mecp2e1-Bird mouse model of RTT [167], identified sex-differentiated early-life dysbiosis, marked by decreased Firmicutes, increased Bacteroidetes, and altered fecal metabolites (e.g., elevated SCFAs), detectable by five weeks of age, before the appearance of motor or behavioural symptoms in heterozygous females compared to males and wild-type controls. Additionally, females exhibited an early elevation of pro-resolving cytokines like IL-4 and IL-10 in the gut, as well as altered phospholipid levels in brain tissue. Similarly, in Female heterozygous Mecp2^ZFN/+ rat model [168], reported reduced microbial diversity, increased Akkermansia (a mucin degrading bacteria), and elevated colonic expression of TNF-α and IL-1β. FMT from wild-type donors partially restored microbial and inflammatory profiles, although behavioural effects were not assessed. These animal models suggest that gut microbial alterations may precede and potentially influence RTT phenotypes. However, definitive causality remains unproven, as no studies have yet used germ-free colonization or longitudinal FMT to directly modulate CNS symptoms.

Microbiota-related inflammation and metabolic stress may intersect with other systemic abnormalities in RTT, including mitochondrial dysfunction, redox imbalance, and chronic low-grade inflammation. MeCP2 dysfunction contributes to widespread immune dysregulation, involving hyperresponsiveness of innate immune cells such as macrophages and microglia, activation of pro-inflammatory signalling (including IL-1β and IL-6), and altered inflammasome signalling. When compounded by gut dysbiosis, immune dysregulation may exacerbate RTTs systemic and neurological manifestations.

Gut microbiota in RTT: interventions

At present, no cure exists for RTT. Most interventions aim at mitigating disease progression and preserving residual neurological function. Preclinical research has shown that early behavioural and environmental interventions can delay symptom onset and improve phenotypic outcomes in animal models of RTT, particularly when applied before the emergence of overt neurological deficits. [169] demonstrated that early, task-specific motor training, before the onset of symptoms, in Mecp2^tm1.1Bird female mice significantly improved coordination and delayed disease progression [169]. The intervention also increased dendritic complexity and enhanced cortical neuron responses during trained tasks, suggesting that early engagement of neural circuits, during critical periods of brain development, supports functional preservation. The authors noted that initiating training post-symptom onset yielded minimal benefit. Similarly [170], showed that EE, a paradigm involving enhanced social, sensory, and motor stimulation, alongside plasma transfer from young, physically active wild-type mice, improved neuromuscular junction architecture and motor function in male Mecp2-null (Mecp2^-/y) mice [170]. The rationale for plasma transfer is based on evidence that circulating factors in young, active animals can modulate synaptic health and muscle–nerve connectivity. Specifically, treated RTT mice exhibited reduced neuromuscular junction fragmentation, increased acetylcholine receptor clustering, and restored presynaptic terminal structure. These improvements suggest that systemic bioactive molecules such as growth factors, anti-inflammatory cytokines, and exercise-induced proteins (exerkines) may contribute to neuromuscular and potentially CNS benefits. While neither study directly assessed the GM, their findings reinforce the importance of early-life environmental factors in modulating disease expression.

In the Mecp2^ZFN/+ rat model, it has been demonstrated that FMT reduced gut inflammation and improved epithelial barrier function [168]. Though neurobehavioral outcomes were not assessed. In patients with RTT, the first randomized controlled trial to evaluate a probiotic intervention was conducted by [171]. This 12-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study enrolled 32 participants aged 3 to 25 years with genetically confirmed RTT, testing the safety, feasibility, and potential efficacy of the Lactobacillus plantarum PS128 probiotics. The intervention demonstrated high adherence and was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported. Clinically, participants receiving PS128 showed a statistically significant reduction in dystonia scores and improvements in caregiver-reported measures of cognitive responsiveness compared to placebo. Additionally, dystonia severity improved more in the probiotic cohort, with statistically significant reductions noted for total dystonia scores (p = 0.020) and leg dystonia (p = 0.008). Although GI outcomes did not differ significantly between groups, there was a non-significant trend toward stabilization. Notably, the study did not include microbiome profiling or metabolomic analysis, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Nevertheless, these findings provide initial evidence supporting the safety and symptomatic benefit of PS128 in RTT and underscore the need for larger-scale trials incorporating biological and functional biomarkers to elucidate the GBA in this population.

Conclusion and future direction

RTT is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder with multisystem involvement. Recent studies implicate gut dysbiosis as an early and potentially modifiable contributor to disease progression. Preclinical studies in animal models demonstrate that microbial disturbances can precede overt neurological symptoms and are associated with immune activation, metabolic stress, and disrupted neuroplasticity. However, current evidence remains largely correlative; definitive causality has not yet been established. Key limitations to human studies include small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, short follow-up periods, and limited integration of microbiome data with clinical and neurophysiological outcomes. There is an unmet need for mechanistic studies to delineate how gut-derived microbial and immune signals influence host metabolism and neural circuit function, particularly through MeCP2-related pathways. Critically, future research must identify and target sensitive developmental windows, especially early postnatal periods of brain development, when neuroimmune and microbial systems are co-maturing and most amenable to modulation. Large-scale, rigorously designed randomized controlled trials, supported by longitudinal, multi-omics approaches and biomarker-based stratification, are essential to validate therapeutic efficacy, define optimal timing, and identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit. Ultimately, advancing microbiome-based therapies for RTT will require mechanistically grounded, developmentally timed, and personalized approaches that reflect the disorder’s clinical heterogeneity and evolving pathophysiology.

Fragile X syndrome (FXS)

Background

FXS is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability and a major single-gene cause of ASD [172]. It occurs because of a genetic change in the FMR1 gene, located on the X chromosome. In people with FXS, a specific DNA sequence (CGG) repeats many times over 200, which causes the gene to shut down [173]. This stops the production of FMRP, a protein that plays a key role in how brain cells communicate [174]. FXS affects about 1 in 4,000 males and 1 in 8,000 females, though these numbers can vary depending on the population and testing methods [175]. FXS is passed down in an X-linked dominant pattern, meaning it is more severe in males (who only have one X chromosome) than in females (who have two X chromosomes, and one can compensate due to random inactivation) (Hagerman et al., 2017). People with a smaller expansion of the CGG repeat (between 55 and 200 repeats) are called premutation carriers. They usually don’t have FXS but may be at risk for other related conditions, such as fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia or fragile X-associated primary ovarian insufficiency [173]. Around 1 in 250 women and 1 in 800 men are premutation carriers, making genetic counselling important [175]. When the FMRP protein is missing in FXS, it causes problems with brain synaptic plasticity. This happens because FMRP normally helps control how proteins are made at the synapse. Without it, mGluR5 signalling becomes overactive, leading to too much protein production, problems with memory-related brain activity, including impaired long-term depression, and misshapen dendritic spines, which remain immature and are overly dense [174].

The gut microbiome in FXS pathogenesis

Research in mice models with FXS found that giving probiotic Lactobacillus reuteri can help improve social behavior and lower anxiety-like signs. These improvements probably happen because the probiotics affect the communication between the gut and the brain, showing how important gut bacteria are for brain health [176]. New research suggests that an imbalance in gut bacteria known as gut dysbiosis may play a role in the behavioral symptoms seen in FXS. Clinical studies shows that individuals with FXS often have fewer helpful bacteria like Bifidobacterium and more harmful, inflammation-causing bacteria like Clostridia in their gut [177]. Other treatments like omega-3 supplements have also shown promise. In FXS mice, these supplements helped restore a healthier balance of gut bacteria and reduced autism-like behaviors [178]. Another approach, called FMT, which involves transferring healthy gut bacteria from normal mice to FXS mice, also improved social interactions and reduced repetitive behaviors, further highlighting the gut microbiome’s role in FXS symptoms [179].

Management strategies

Treating FXS requires a well-rounded approach that combines medication, behavioral therapy, dietary strategies, and genetic support. Medications are commonly used to manage symptoms such as attention issues and anxiety. For example, stimulants like methylphenidate are often prescribed for ADHD symptoms, while selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors like sertraline help manage anxiety. Although some targeted treatments, such as mGluR5 antagonists, have been explored in clinical trials, they have shown limited success so far [180]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that diet and gut health may influence behavior. Probiotic supplements and omega-3 fatty acids have shown potential in helping to regulate behavior in animal models and are being considered as experimental supportive strategies [178]. Currently, the management of FXS relies on combining medication, behavioral therapies, and dietary approaches, while research continues to explore new treatments, including therapies that target the gut microbiome [179].

Research on FXS is progressing in several promising directions. Scientists are working on epigenetic therapies that could reactivate the FMR1 gene, which is normally turned off in people with FXS [181]. There is also increasing focus on identifying biomarkers, which are biological signs that could help with early diagnosis and tracking how well treatments are working [182]. In addition, long-term studies are being planned to better understand how FXS and Fragile X-associated Tremor/Ataxia Syndrome affect people as they age [180]. Overall, researchers are continuing to explore targeted treatments, including reactivating the FMR1 gene and modulating the gut microbiome. More studies are still needed to improve diagnosis and develop better therapies for FXS and related conditions [180].

Down syndrome (DS)

Background

Trisomy 21, universally known as Down syndrome (DS), is the most prevalent chromosomal disorder in humans and is associated with a distinctive array of physical, cognitive, and neurodevelopmental features. Current research highlights that individuals with DS often exhibit marked alterations in their GM, which may contribute to increased susceptibility to GI symptoms and neurodevelopmental complications, including ASD [183, 184]. The GBA, a complex, bidirectional network integrating neural, hormonal, and immunological signaling between the gut and the brain, is increasingly recognized as a crucial pathway influencing the health and neurodevelopment of people with DS, offering potential new avenues for therapeutic intervention [185].

Down syndrome and MGBA

A hallmark of DS is a GM profile characterized by reduced microbial diversity and significant shifts in bacterial community composition compared to neurotypical individuals. Specifically, beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which are critical for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and immune regulation, are often diminished [183]. In contrast, potentially harmful bacteria, including Clostridium clusters and Bacteroides fragilis, are frequently more abundant, potentially fostering a pro-inflammatory intestinal environment [184]. These microbiome changes are closely associated with GI symptoms that affect 60–75% of individuals with DS and are likely to have downstream effects on neurological function and neurodevelopment [186]. Interestingly, the observed microbiome alterations in DS may be influenced by confounding factors, such as diet. A preclinical study using Ts65Dn DS model mice (n = 6) and wild-type littermates (n = 6) found that high-fat diet increased beta-diversity and altered community composition, including a decrease in Eubacterium eligens, with beta-diversity effects greater in the DS model than in wild-type [187]. These findings suggest that microbiome alterations in DS may be influenced by dietary composition. However, the absence of dietary data in human DS microbiome studies remains a critical limitation.

The mechanisms by which GM influence brain development in DS are multifaceted, involving immune, metabolic, and neuroendocrine pathways. Dysbiosis in the gut can trigger systemic inflammation through the activation of regulatory T-cells and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which can cross the BBB, promote synaptic dysfunction, and drive microglial activation in the CNS [188]. Within the brain, these cytokines may impair neuronal connectivity and plasticity, contributing to the cognitive and behavioral changes commonly observed in DS [188]. Additionally, the GM plays a central role in the synthesis and modulation of key neurotransmitters, including GABA and serotonin, which are essential for proper neuronal migration, synaptic pruning, and overall brain development [189]. Disruptions in these neurochemical pathways, resulting from microbial imbalance, may exacerbate the risk for neurodevelopmental abnormalities, including an increased risk of ASD [190]. In summary, GM dysbiosis in DS can drive both peripheral and central inflammation, disrupt neurotransmission, and amplify neurodevelopmental vulnerability.

Molecular mechanisms and metabolic pathways

Preclinical evidence from transgenic mouse models demonstrate that the overexpression of specific chromosome 21 genes is associated with learning and memory impairments characteristic of DS [191]. These genetic and epigenetic alterations disrupt normal chromatin organization, gene expression, and protein homeostasis, resulting in elevated oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction [192]. Overexpression of superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) due to trisomy 21 leads to an imbalance in redox homeostasis, yielding oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids [192]. This oxidative stress activates pathways such as Advanced Glycation End-products and their Receptor and mTOR, inhibits autophagy, and drives the accumulation of pathologic proteins, including amyloid and hyperphosphorylated tau, processes also implicated in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [192] (Fig. 4). Further compounding these effects, DS is associated with significant alterations in neurotransmitter systems, particularly reduced dopamine and serotonin synthesis and impaired dopaminergic and serotonergic neuron function, contributing to cognitive and behavioral deficits [184]. Immune dysregulation, reflected in elevated cytokine and chemokine levels and altered microglial activity, exacerbates neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction, amplifying the neurodevelopmental impact of trisomy 21 [188] (Fig. 4). Collectively, these molecular and cellular changes highlight the complex interplay of genetic, oxidative, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways that underlie the neurodevelopmental impairments in DS.

Metabolic pathways are also substantially influenced by GM alterations in DS. Notably, the production of SCFAs, particularly butyrate, is often reduced in this population [183, 193]. In a human metabolomics study of 45 individuals with DS compared to 50 controls, SCFA levels (especially butyrate) were significantly reduced (mean difference = −12.4 µmol/L, 95% CI −18.9 to −6.0, p = 0.001) [178]. SCFAs play a vital role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, modulating immune responses, and providing metabolic substrates for intestinal cells [183, 193]. Butyrate, in particular, supports mitochondrial function in hippocampal neurons and upregulates the expression of BDNF, which is essential for synaptic plasticity and cognitive resilience [188]. While microbial-derived SCFAs directly influence gut and brain health, metabolic disturbances intrinsic to DS—including altered energy metabolism and increased oxidative stress—can further deplete beneficial SCFAs and heighten neurodevelopmental vulnerability [183]. Thus, the intersection of genetic metabolic dysfunction and gut microbial imbalance may synergistically increase the risk of neurodevelopmental deficits and ASD in DS.

Microbiome interventions and clinical trials

Growing evidence suggests that interventions targeting the GM may help reduce ASD-like behaviors in DS animal models. Probiotic supplementation, particularly with Bifidobacterium infantis, has been shown to restore microbial balance and improve social behavior in animal models [183, 188]. Dietary modifications that increase fiber intake can enhance butyrate production, thereby strengthening the intestinal barrier and reducing neuroinflammation [193]. Early-life interventions such as FMT in animal models have also demonstrated potential benefits in correcting microbial imbalances during critical neurodevelopmental windows [184]. These findings highlight the potential of microbiome-targeted therapies as a novel approach to neurodevelopmental support in DS.

Current clinical trials are increasingly focused on the first 1,000 days of life, a period of heightened neural plasticity, as a key window for microbiome modulation [184]. Early correction of dysbiosis may therefore provide a unique opportunity to improve cognitive and behavioral outcomes in individuals with DS. However, further clinical research is needed to fully understand the long-term efficacy, safety, and clinical impact of these interventions [183]. Early-life microbiome interventions show promise, but further studies are required to define optimal protocols and long-term neurodevelopmental benefits.

Current research gaps and future directions

Despite encouraging results, important questions remain. The optimal timing and duration of microbiome-targeted interventions in DS are unclear, given the complexity and variability of neurodevelopmental trajectories [183]. Additionally, the effects of specific probiotic strains on neurodevelopment require detailed investigation, as different Bifidobacterium subspecies may have distinct impacts on GBA signaling [188]. The development of personalized strategies, guided by biomarkers that predict ASD risk in individuals with DS, could further enhance the efficacy of microbiome-based therapies [160]. Closing these knowledge gaps will be essential to translate microbiome science into safe, effective, and individualized care for individuals with DS. In summary, the GM plays a central role in both the health and neurodevelopment of individuals with DS. Dysbiosis in this population is associated with elevated rates of GI symptoms, chronic inflammation, neurotransmitter imbalances, and metabolic disturbances, all of which as all of which are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, such as ASD [188, 193]. Interventions targeting the microbiome—including probiotics, dietary modifications, and FMT—hold significant promise for improving neurodevelopmental outcomes and possibly preventing ASD in individuals with DS [183, 184]. Nevertheless, further research is needed to optimize the timing, formulation, and personalization of these strategies, and to clarify the precise mechanisms linking GM to neurodevelopment in this population. Ultimately, integrating microbiome-targeted interventions into clinical care may provide new opportunities to enhance neurodevelopmental trajectories for individuals with DS.

Prader–willi syndrome (PWS)

Background

PWS is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused by the loss of paternally expressed genes on chromosome 15q11-q13. It manifests as hypotonia, hyperphagia (excessive hunger), severe obesity, cognitive deficits, and behavioral disturbances. While genetic and endocrinological mechanisms have historically dominated PWS research, recent advances highlight the pivotal role of the GBA and intestinal microbiome in PWS pathophysiology, symptomatology, and potential avenues for prevention and intervention [194].

The gut-brain axis in PWS

In PWS, disruption of the GBA is implicated in hallmark features such as hyperphagia, altered satiety signaling, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Research demonstrates that individuals with PWS exhibit abnormalities in appetite-regulating hormones—most notably, elevated ghrelin, altered leptin, and abnormal peptide YY levels (Fig. 5). Specifically, a study found that PWS patients had significantly higher fasting ghrelin levels (mean = 1200 pg/mL) compared to healthy controls (mean = 300 pg/mL, p < 0.001), and lower leptin levels (mean = 5 ng/mL) compared to obese controls (mean = 30 ng/mL, p < 0.01) [188]. Additionally, peptide YY levels were found to be dysregulated, with blunted postprandial responses [188]. These hormonal imbalances are influenced by both genetic factors and the gut microbiome, which can modulate the production and signaling of these hormones [194]. Microbiome alterations and metabolic phenotype in PWS

Recent research confirms that individuals with PWS have a distinct gut microbiome profile compared to healthy controls and those with non-syndromic obesity. For example, it was demonstrated that children with PWS exhibit reduced microbial diversity and altered abundances of key bacterial taxa, including a lower prevalence of beneficial genera such as Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium, and a higher prevalence of potentially pathogenic bacteria [195]. Specifically, a study found that PWS patients had significantly lower alpha diversity (Shannon index: 2.5 vs. 3.2, p < 0.01) and reduced relative abundance of Akkermansia (0.5% vs. 3.1%, p < 0.001) and Bifidobacterium (1.2% vs. 5.8%, p < 0.001) compared to healthy controls [189]. There is also an increased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, a microbial signature commonly linked to enhanced energy harvest and obesity [196]. Changes in the microbiome seen in children with NDDs are usually not disorder-specific but rather reflect general changes in neurodevelopment and immune pathways. Disorder-specific changes have, however, been highlighted. Among these are increased levels of Bacteroides in ASD, decreases in Lactobacillus in RTT, and changes in the abundance of Akkermansia in PWS [165, 197]. However, findings have remained inconsistent across cohorts with diet, geographic area of sampling, presence of comorbid conditions, and small sample sizes all rendering it very difficult to state that these findings are strictly disorder-specific.

A recent study further supports the concept that gut microbiome composition is closely tied to metabolic health in children, with specific microbial patterns correlating with obesity and metabolic syndrome [199]. This is particularly relevant for PWS, where metabolic complications are a major clinical concern. The gut microbiome influences host metabolism through the production of SCFAs such as butyrate, acetate, and propionate. In PWS, altered SCFA production may impair gut barrier integrity and promote systemic inflammation [195]. This biochemical crosstalk may underlie not only hyperphagia and obesity but also cognitive and behavioral symptoms seen in PWS. The microbiome also modulates the secretion of appetite-regulating hormones. PWS patients often present with persistently elevated ghrelin levels, contributing to insatiable hunger. Microbial dysbiosis may further disrupt this hormonal environment, perpetuating hyperphagia and weight gain [195].

PWS and early-life microbiome modulation

Early-life factors, including mode of delivery, breastfeeding, antibiotic exposure, and diet, profoundly shape the infant microbiome and may impact the risk and severity of PWS manifestations [194]. A study demonstrated that the gut microbiota of PWS patients, while distinct from obese controls, was similar to that of their non-obese parents, suggesting a potential protective role against metabolic complications. Furthermore, their study showed that improved insulin resistance in PWS was partly transmitted by fecal microbiota transplantations into germ-free mice [200]. Recent evidence from human observational studies also suggests that perinatal and early childhood interventions can promote a healthier microbiome, improving both metabolic and neurodevelopmental outcomes in PWS [201]. For instance, studies show that children exposed to a diverse diet and minimal antibiotic use in early life have a more resilient and beneficial GM, which may help buffer against the metabolic and behavioral complications seen in PWS [201].

Probiotics, prebiotics, and dietary interventions

Probiotic and prebiotic supplementation has shown promise in modulating the gut microbiome toward a more favorable composition, enhancing SCFA production, and reducing systemic inflammation [200].

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 68 PWS patients (aged 11 months–16 years) found that daily Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis probiotics (6 × 10^10^ CFUs) significantly increased height (mean difference = 2.68 cm, p < 0.05) and improved Clinical Global Impression-Improvement (CGI-I) scores (p < 0.05) compared to placebo [200]. Dietary modifications, such as increasing fiber intake, can also promote beneficial microbial shifts and support GBA function. It has been highlighted that dietary interventions targeting the microbiome can have significant effects on energy balance and metabolic health in obese children, which may be relevant for PWS management [199]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that the use of specific probiotics and dietary fibers can enhance cognitive and behavioral outcomes in children with neurodevelopmental disorders, suggesting a potential avenue for intervention in PWS [201]. Additionally, the review by Olsson discusses how Mediterranean-style diets, rich in plant-based foods and fibers, can foster a microbiome profile associated with reduced inflammation and improved metabolic health [200].

Microbiome and neurodevelopmental outcomes

Alterations in the gut microbiome are increasingly recognized as contributors to neurodevelopmental disorders. It has been shown that gut microbial composition is associated with behavioral and cognitive outcomes in children, supporting the potential for microbiome-targeted therapies in neurodevelopmental conditions such as PWS [201]. Another recent study found that specific microbial metabolites can directly influence neural pathways involved in appetite and behavior, which may be particularly relevant for the neuropsychiatric symptoms seen in PWS [202].

Recent advances and future directions

Recent advances in metagenomics and metabolomics have enabled researchers to identify specific bacterial taxa and metabolites linked to PWS and related metabolic disorders. For example, a study used high-throughput sequencing to identify distinct microbial signatures in children with obesity, some of which overlap with those seen in PWS [202]. Such findings pave the way for precision microbiome interventions tailored to the unique needs of PWS patients. Furthermore, recent studies highlight the importance of host-microbe interactions in shaping metabolic outcomes, suggesting that interventions targeting both the microbiome and host metabolism may yield synergistic benefits [203]. Despite these advances, several challenges remain. The heterogeneity of PWS phenotypes, variability in microbiome composition, and the complexity of host-microbe interactions necessitate individualized approaches. Longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials are essential to establish causality and optimize intervention protocols [204]. Ethical considerations regarding early-life interventions and the long-term safety of microbiome modulation must also be addressed. The GBA and the intestinal microbiome are central to the pathophysiology of PWS, influencing neurodevelopment, appetite regulation, and metabolic health. Altered microbial composition and function in PWS underscore the potential for microbiome-based therapeutic strategies. While direct prevention of PWS through microbiome manipulation is not currently feasible due to its genetic origin, early-life and microbiome-targeted interventions may attenuate disease severity and improve quality of life. Continued advances in microbiome science hold promise for innovative therapeutic strategies in PWS and related neurodevelopmental disorders.

Wilson’s disease (WD)

Background

WD is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene, resulting in pathological copper accumulation primarily affecting the liver, brain, and eyes [205]. With a low prevalence globally, WD presents significant challenges in early detection and management. Recent advances in research have revealed that WD patients often have distinct changes in their gut microbiome, opening new possibilities for management. The gut microbiome plays a crucial role in maintaining copper homeostasis through complex interactions with host copper transport mechanisms. Disruption of these interactions may contribute to WD pathogenesis and present opportunities for microbiome-based prevention strategies [207]. ATP7B acts as a key copper-transporting ATPase that incorporates copper into ceruloplasmin while facilitating the excretion of biliary copper [208]. Intestinal ATP7B also plays a role by preserving copper balances along the duodenal crypt-villus axis translocation gradient and keeping cytosolic copper levels low within enterocyte cells by buffering excess Cu+ ions. Mutations in the ATP7B gene cause loss of these homeostatic mechanisms resulting in excess hepatic and neurological tissue deposition of copper [209]. The gut-liver axis represents a bidirectional communication system mediated primarily through portal circulation, bile acid metabolism, and microbial metabolites. In WD patients, disruption of intestinal mucosal barrier function and altered bile acid secretion significantly influence GM composition and metabolic function. This dysregulation contributes to systemic inflammatory responses and metabolic dysregulation characteristic of WD pathogenesis [205].

Gut microbiome alterations in WD

Multiple studies have documented significant alterations in gut microbiome diversity and composition among WD patients compared to healthy controls [197]. WD patients consistently demonstrate reduced bacterial richness and diversity, with decreased temporal stability of microbial communities. At the phylum level, WD patients exhibit markedly lower abundance of Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia, while showing increased number of Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Fusobacteria [205]. The Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio, a key indicator of gut health, is significantly lower in WD patients compared to healthy individuals. This alteration is particularly significant given that Firmicutes encompass the majority of butyrate-producing bacteria essential for maintaining intestinal barrier function and anti-inflammatory responses [205, 210]. Recent comprehensive analyses have identified specific microbial taxa consistently altered in WD patients. Selenomonaceae and Megamonas levels are significantly elevated in WD patients, while beneficial bacteria such as Roseburia inulinivorans shows reduced abundance [211]. Notably, WD patients demonstrate unique enrichment of Gemellaceae, Pseudomonadaceae, and Spirochaetaceae at the family level, which are rarely detected in healthy controls [212]. Lactobacillus abundance shows a particularly strong correlation with liver injury severity in WD patients, with significant reductions observed in patients with hepatic manifestations [207]. Other genera including Veillonella and Eubacterium coprostanoligenes also demonstrate significant correlations with disease severity [207]. Functional profiling of the gut microbiome in WD patients reveals significant alterations in metabolic pathways crucial for host health. WD patients show reduced abundance of bacterial groups involved in immune system function and metabolism-associated pathways, including pathways involving transcription factors and ATP-binding cassette-type transporters [205]. Comparative metagenomic analysis indicates dysregulation of amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism functions in the WD gut microbial community [205]. Metabolomic analyses have identified specific alterations in microbial metabolites among WD patients. Leucylproline, 5-phenylvaleric acid, and N-desmethylclobazam, which possess nutritional and protective effects, are significantly reduced in WD patients. Conversely, D-gluconic acid, which can chelate metal ions, may represent a potential therapeutic target, showing positive correlations with Alistipes indistinctus and Prevotella stercora [211].

Mechanistic insights into microbiome-copper interactions

Copper-Microbiome interactions

The antimicrobial properties of copper create complex relationships between copper homeostasis and microbial ecology [206]. Antibiotic treatment significantly alters copper isotopic composition in the GI tract, demonstrating direct host-microbial interactions in copper regulation. These interactions involve modulation of copper transporter expression, with both CTR1 and ATP7A showing significant down-regulation in antibiotic-treated mice [213]. Methane-oxidizing bacteria produce methanobactin, a highly efficient copper-binding molecule that has shown therapeutic potential in WD models. The bacterial compound ARBM101 demonstrated superior copper reduction compared to conventional chelators, lowering hepatic copper to physiological levels within one week in experimental models [214].

SCFAs production

The reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria observed in WD patients has significant implications for intestinal barrier function and systemic inflammation [215]. SCFAs, particularly butyrate, play crucial roles in activating anti-inflammatory pathways and supporting regulatory T cell differentiation [210]. Decreased SCFA production may contribute to increased intestinal permeability and bacterial translocation observed in WD patients [205].

Prevention strategies using gut microbiome interventions

Probiotic and prebiotic interventions

Current evidence supports the therapeutic potential of targeted probiotic interventions in WD prevention and management [216]. Lactobacillus-based probiotics have demonstrated efficacy in reducing inflammatory markers and supporting intestinal barrier function in preclinical liver disease models [217]. A clinical study showed that the use of probiotics containing Lactobacillus casei reduced plasma IL-1β in alcoholic cirrhosis patients, while multi-strain formulations sustained CD8+ T cell expression and zonulin-1 in duodenal mucosa [218]. A high-potency probiotic mixture commercially known as VSL#3, a mixture containing eight bacterial strains, has shown particular promise in liver disease management, reducing inflammatory markers including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α while improving clinical scores [216]. These findings suggest potential applications for WD prevention, particularly in high-risk individuals identified through genetic screening [218].

FMT

FMT represents a more comprehensive approach to microbiome restoration [219, 220]. Recent preclinical evidence demonstrates that FMT can effectively alter gut microbial structure and reduce hepatic copper concentration in WD mouse models. Transplantation of healthy microbiota increased abundance and modified hepatic transcriptional profiles, resulting in alleviation of WD-related liver injury [207]. The concept of “super-donors” in FMT suggests that specific microbial compositions may be particularly effective for treating dysbiosis-related conditions. Identification of optimal donor profiles for WD management could enhance FMT efficacy and provide targeted therapeutic approaches [221].

Synbiotic approaches

Synbiotic interventions combining probiotics with prebiotic substrates offer enhanced therapeutic potential. Perioperative synbiotic protocols have demonstrated significant reductions in infection rates and inflammatory markers in surgical patients, suggesting potential applications for WD patients undergoing liver transplantation. The combination of Clostridium butyricum with Bifidobacterium infantis has shown particular efficacy in reducing harmful bacteria while supporting beneficial taxa [216].

Early detection and prevention strategies

Current prevention strategies for WD primarily focus on genetic screening and family surveillance. Systematic family-wide screening is recommended for all first-degree relatives of WD patients, including siblings, parents, and offspring [222]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has enhanced genetic screening capabilities, facilitating earlier diagnosis and family screening [223]. The integration of microbiome profiling with genetic screening could enhance risk stratification and early management strategies [222]. Early identification of gut dysbiosis patterns in genetically susceptible individuals may enable targeted interventions before clinical symptom onset. Novel biomarkers including relative exchangeable copper (REC) and ATP7B protein quantification in dried blood spots have demonstrated improved diagnostic accuracy. The combination of genetic testing with microbiome profiling could provide comprehensive risk assessment tools for WD early detection and management [224]. Immuno-SRM platforms for quantifying ATP7B in dried blood spots show promise for population screening, with high positive predictive values for distinguishing affected cases from controls. Integration of microbiome markers with these platforms could enhance screening effectiveness [222]. Copper-restricted diets represent established management strategies for diagnosed WD patients. However, emerging evidence suggests that microbiome-targeted dietary interventions may offer additional therapeutic benefits. Low-copper diets combined with prebiotic fiber supplementation could support beneficial bacteria while reducing copper bioavailability [225].

Gandouling, a standardized Chinese herbal formula, demonstrates anti-fibrotic effects in WD animal models through GM modulation. GDL treatment significantly rebalanced microbiota composition and function while modulating lipid metabolism pathways [226]. In TX mice, GDL administration (0.58–1.16 g kg− 1) significantly improved liver function, reducing serum ALT and AST by ≈45% compared to the model group (p < 0.01), with liver coefficient dropping from 1.16 to 0.58 g kg− 1 (p < 0.01) and albumin levels increasing (p < 0.01). β-diversity analysis revealed significant microbial shifts among the control, model, and GDL groups (p = 0.003) [226]. Bacterial-derived compounds, particularly methanobactin from methane-oxidizing bacteria, represent novel therapeutic approaches. These chalcophores demonstrate superior copper-binding efficiency compared to synthetic chelators and may offer improved treatment options for severe WD cases [227]. The heterogeneity of microbiome alterations among WD patients proposes the need for personalized intervention strategies. Phenotype-specific microbiome profiles, particularly the distinction between hepatic and neurological presentations, may require tailored therapeutic approaches. Patients with hepatic symptoms show greater reductions in SCFA-producing bacteria, suggesting targeted probiotic interventions may be particularly beneficial [215].

Challenges and future directions - research limitations

Current microbiome research in WD faces several methodological challenges mainly study heterogeneity in microbiome analysis methods and experimental design limiting comparative analyses. Small sample sizes and lack of longitudinal studies restrict understanding of microbiome changes during disease progression. The absence of standardized protocols for microbiome-based interventions poses challenges for clinical translation. Development of evidence-based guidelines for probiotic selection, dosing, and duration requires additional clinical trials.

Toxic/Inflammatory disorders of prenatal origin

Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD)

Background

FASD encompass a continuum of neurodevelopmental conditions resulting from prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE), with Fetal Alcohol Syndrome representing the most severe manifestation. The latter is clinically characterized by a constellation of physical, cognitive, and behavioral abnormalities, including growth retardation, distinctive craniofacial features, and CNS dysfunction. The lifelong neurobehavioral impairments seen in FASD, such as deficits in learning, memory, attention, executive function, and emotional regulation, can significantly diminish quality of life and social integration [228]. For instance, a review highlighted that individuals with FASD often exhibit significant deficits in executive function (e.g., working memory, planning, inhibition), with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large (Cohen’s d = 0.5–1.0) across various studies [220].

Neuropathology of FASD

The pathogenesis of FASD is multifactorial. Ethanol exposure during gestation exerts direct neurotoxic effects and disrupts fetal brain development through mechanisms including oxidative stress, apoptosis, impaired synaptogenesis, altered neuronal migration, and deficits in myelination [220, 221]. For example, a study showed in a mouse model that PAE led to an increase in palmitic acid and arachidonic acid in phospholipids in the cerebral cortex at postnatal day 30, consistent with an upregulation of the fatty acid synthesizing enzyme, Fasn. This suggests altered fatty acid metabolism is associated with neurobehavioral deficits [221]. That study highlights that PAE shapes the neonatal microbiome through alcohol’s impacts on maternal microbiota and nutrient absorption, leading to altered microbial metabolites in offspring circulation. These changes are particularly pronounced in brain regions such as the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and cerebellum, correlating with the cognitive and behavioral symptoms observed in affected individuals [158]. Neuroinflammation and altered neurotransmitter systems further exacerbate these disturbances, while PAE-induced impairment of BBB integrity facilitates neurotoxic insults [230].

The gut-brain axis in FASD

Beyond direct neurotoxicity, PAE may act through systemic and microbial pathways. The GBA has emerged as a crucial mediator of these effects. Recent research has highlighted the critical involvement of the GBA, a complex, bidirectional communication network linking the GI tract and CNS via neural, endocrine, and immune pathways, in neurodevelopmental disorders such as FASD [229, 231]. The gut microbiome, comprising trillions of microorganisms, modulates immune responses, produces neuroactive metabolites, and influences brain function. PAE-induced disruption of this axis is now recognized as a contributor to FASD pathology [230].

PAE-Induced microbial and metabolic dysregulation

While human data remain limited, rodent models robustly show that PAE induces significant and persistent alterations in both maternal and offspring GM composition and function [230, 231]. These changes include reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, and increased prevalence of pro-inflammatory taxa [230, 232]. For example, a study highlighted that PAE shapes the neonatal microbiome through alcohol’s impacts on maternal microbiota and nutrient absorption, leading to altered microbial metabolites in offspring circulation [230, 231]. Notably, sex-specific effects have been observed, with PAE increasing Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium in males, and Faecalitalea and Proteus in females [232]. The study found that in a rodent model, bacterial diversity decreased in alcohol-consuming dams (Shannon index: t(35.7) = 2.76, p = 0.01) and community composition differed from controls (PERMANOVA: Pseudo-F = 8.14, df = 1, R^2^ = 0.16, p = 0.001). In offspring, community composition also differed significantly (PERMANOVA: Pseudo-F = 5.20, df = 1, R^2^ = 0.10, p = 0.001), though there were no differences in Shannon index [232]. PAE-induced dysbiosis is associated with heightened intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”), facilitating the translocation of microbial products such as LPS into the bloodstream (Fig. 6). This, in turn, activates toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) -mediated inflammatory pathways, worsening neuroinflammation and behavioral deficits [230]. Untargeted metabolomics studies reveal that PAE alters the maternal-fetal biosignature of microbiota-dependent products (MDPs), including phenolic acids and secondary bile acids [229, 230]. Metabolites such as 4-ethylphenylsulfate, oxindole, indolepropionate, and p-cresol sulfate are detected in maternal and fetal plasma, liver, and brain, and are implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders. Alcohol-induced changes in the enteric microbiome increase paracellular permeability to MDPs via tight junction disruption, further promoting systemic inflammation and neurotoxicity [229]. Altered production of SCFAs, key microbial metabolites, can influence BBB integrity and microglial function, contributing to the neuroinflammatory milieu characteristic of FASD [229, 230].

Microbiome-targeted preventive and theraputic approaches

Given the centrality of the gut microbiome in modulating neurodevelopment and the GBA, strategies aimed at restoring microbial balance are gaining traction as potential therapeutic approaches for FASD. Preclinical studies indicate that supplementation with probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) or prebiotics (dietary fibers) can restore microbial balance, enhance gut barrier integrity, and attenuate neuroinflammation in PAE models [230, 233]. Butyrate, an SCFA produced by microbial fermentation, demonstrates neuroprotective effects by supporting gut barrier function, repressing inflammation, and promoting microglial maturation [230]. Dietary interventions rich in fiber, polyphenols, and fermented foods during pregnancy foster beneficial microbial communities and the production of neuroactive metabolites [232]. Early-life interventions, including breastfeeding and minimizing unnecessary antibiotic exposure, may help establish a resilient microbiome that supports optimal neurodevelopment [234]. Recent rodent studies have also explored FMT as a means to restore microbial diversity and improve neurobehavioral outcomes following PAE [234]. While these approaches are promising, translation to clinical practice requires careful consideration of timing, safety, and individual variability in human microbiota [235]. Moreover, the identification of a plasma biosignature of MDPs in PAE pregnancies offers potential for early detection and targeted intervention. Metabolites such as secondary bile acids and phenolic compounds may serve as biomarkers for risk stratification and monitoring therapeutic efficacy [229].

While preclinical data on microbiome-targeted interventions are encouraging, their translation to human populations is challenged by variability in microbiota composition, timing of intervention, and safety concerns during pregnancy [235, 236]. The complexity of FASD pathology necessitates multifaceted approaches combining behavioral, nutritional, and microbiome-targeted therapies. Continued research, particularly well-designed human studies, is essential to establish effective microbiome-based prevention and treatment modalities for FASD. FASD remains a preventable yet prevalent cause of neurodevelopmental disability. The GBA and microbiome represent a frontier in understanding the biological mechanisms underlying FASD and offer novel avenues for therapeutic strategies. While abstinence from alcohol during pregnancy is paramount, microbiome modulation through probiotics, prebiotics, and dietary interventions holds promise as complementary approaches. Ongoing research will be crucial to validate and optimize these strategies for clinical application.

Congenital zika syndrome (CZS)

Background

CZS is a broad term describing severe neurodevelopmental symptoms, such as microcephaly, ventriculomegaly, cerebellar hypoplasia, and severe cortical malformations that happen when the fetus is infected during pregnancy with Zika Virus (ZIKV). In addition, ZIKV infection has been linked with other neurodevelopmental disorders, like ASD, a condition characterized by difficulties in social interaction, restricted interests, and repetitive behaviors [237]. ZIKV starts copying its genetic code via a process called replication using virally encoded RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp). Translation then generates proteins needed for replication and assembly [232] (Fig. 7). Though the exact assembly process is unclear, viral RNA and C protein likely gather at the ER membrane with prM-E heterodimers, forming immature particles that bud into the ER lumen [233]. These undergo glycosylation, move through the Golgi, and are exposed to acidic pH, triggering E protein rearrangement and pr-M cleavage by furin [233]. Upon release, the virus matures into its infectious form [233]. ZIKV proteins localize to various cellular compartments, and their location can shift depending on expression and interactions. Gestational age, placental immunity, and vertical transmission

ZIKV-induced pathologies in both the mother and fetus depend mainly on the gestational age, as it determines the maternal immune response, ZIKV tropism, and the integrity of the maternal-fetal barrier. A preclinical study investigated the effect of ZIKV on different pregnancy stages using human primary placental cultures. The study included four groups, a mock group (n = 8) and three other groups depending on the viral concentration (3.5 × 104 Focus-Forming Units (FFU): n = 4, 3.5 × 105 FFU: n = 9, 3.5 × 106 FFU: n = 5), and the effected number of each respectively: (7, 3, 30, and 30% with significant fetal growth restriction at the 3.5 × 106 FFU dose, p < 0.0001) when compared to the control. The study found that at early stages of pregnancy, mononuclear trophoblasts and decidua are more susceptible to ZIKV, while the placenta becomes more resistant to ZIKV at later stages, possibly due to syncytiotrophoblasts type III interferon (IFN-λ) signaling. Nonetheless, CZS can cause adverse fetal outcomes when affected at later stages of pregnancy, and proinflammatory type I IFN-α/β responses indicate that these outcomes may be related to trophoblast layer disruption. Indeed, the longer the mother’s viremia, the more severe the fetal outcomes (Fig. 7) [238].

Vertical transmission of ZIKV and CZS studies are mainly murine studies because obtaining human fetal samples is limited [238]. It is also worth mentioning that immunocompetent mice are relatively resistant to ZIKV, as ZIKV antagonizes human STAT2 receptor not mouse STAT2 [238]. Despite anatomical and functional differences in placenta between mice and humans, mouse models show similar ZIKV infection patterns to those of humans’ ZIKV infection, such as vertical transmission, fetal growth restriction, and fetal demise [238]. To overcome these limitations in murine models, preclinical studies used human STAT2 knock-in (KI)/murine Stat2 knock-out mice to investigate the effects of ZIKV infection, and concluded that this method successfully spread mouse adaptive African Zika strain through murine placenta and fetal brain (30% mortality rate of human STAT2 KI mice; ZIKV RNA in placenta and fetal heads were significantly higher in pregnant human STAT2 KI dams compared to wild type, p < 0.05) [239].

Inflammation, microglial activation, and the MGBA

The GBA plays a significant role in the activation and development of microglial cells, innate immunity, and adaptive immunity. If there is an infection during pregnancy, inflammatory cytokines, associated with congenital conditions and intraamniotic inflammation, initiate neuroinflammation by the activation of microglia. This process is also associated with elevated IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, neuroinflammatory markers. Murine preclinical studies used CD-1 mice injected with LPS (n = 9–12 dams per group) to mimic maternal infection effects on the inflammation of microbiota found that white matter can be evidence of microglial activation [237]. [240]. It has been proved that congenital ZIKV can significantly impact fetal neural and brain development, resulting in neural tube defects, microcephaly, ocular abnormalities, and an elevation in pro-inflammatory mediators, especially IL-1β (52.4-fold increase, p = 0.002) which is evoked in the placenta. Also, it is important to address the underlying genetic susceptibility to ZIKV-related congenital infection. A Human case–control genetic association study compared 68 mothers with CZS babies and 43 mothers with healthy babies and reported that a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) exhibit higher odds for developing congenital ZIKV infection (rs3775291, OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.158–4.148, p = 0.019) [241]. The same study compared 42 severe microcephaly babies with 17 microcephaly babies found that a SNP in the TNF-α gene is associated with higher risk of developing microcephaly in neonates (rs1799964, Thymine (variant) vs Cytosine (wild type) allele, OR = 2.63, 95% CI 1.13–6.21, p = 0.03). Likewise, ZIKV infection progress can be affected by changes in epigenetic regulation. For example, reduced methylation of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, an innate immunity regulator, was linked with a higher chance of developing microcephaly. The human observational study, which used a total of 44 children (18 with ZIKV-associated microcephaly, 7 ZIKV-exposed but normocephalic, 19 unexposed controls) reported that patients’ with ZIKV-associated microcephaly (N = 18) showed lower levels of DNAm PAI-1 (β = −2453.06 pg/ml, 95% CI −3652.96 to −1253.16, p = 0.0002) compared to unexposed controls (n = 19) [242]. These findings suggest that changes in genetics and epigenetic programming involved in inflammation and immune responses may increase the risk of getting ZIKV infection and developing more severe symptoms [240].

ZIKV and the gut microbiota

Emerging evidence highlights the significant contribution of maternal gut microbiome in the development of fetal brain through immune signaling and microbiota-derived metabolites. A preclinical study illustrated the impact of maternal GBA on the embryo, using germ free (n = 8 dams) and antibiotic-treated mice (n = 5 dams). Both murine models showed that depletion of maternal microbiota disrupted fetal thalamo-cortical axogenesis (all offspring affected; p < 0.05–0.0001), which was reversed by supplementing with SCFAs [38]. The effects of enteric viruses on the GM are more investigated in comparison to the impact of non-enteric viruses, like ZIKV, which is still poorly understood [244]. A pre-clinical study investigated the effects of ZIKV on the gut microbiome of immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice, 5 subjects per group, using next-generation sequencing of the V4 hypervariable region present in the bacterial 16S rDNA gene analysis. This study found that ZIKV infection changed the β-diversity of the GM of these mice, significantly elevated Spirochetes and Deferribacteraceae, while significantly decreased Firmicutes and Actinobacteria phyla [244].

Congenital zika syndrome and ASD

A preclinical study used induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) from Brazilian children born with CZS and ASD patients, a total of 4 subjects have CZS, two of them also have ASD, were compared to a control group with a non-specified number, to investigate the relationship between ZIKV infection and ASD, revealing a strong link between the vertical transmission of ZIKV and ASD, which starts from early childhood and can be influenced by environmental and genetic factors. The study suggests that neuroinflammation, which can be due to ZIKV, is a strong element in the development of ASD outcomes. While ZIKV induces several pro-inflammatory cytokines to cause neuroinflammation in the fetal brain, such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β and monocyte chemotactic protein 1, two specific cytokines were addressed to be associated with ASD, IL-6 and TNF-α. The study reported elevated levels of IL-6 in patients’ plasma, post-mortem brain, cerebrospinal fluid, and in iPSC-derived astrocytes of children diagnosed with ASD when compared to the control, but only one subject was statically significant (p < 0.0001), while all the other subjects had p-values > 0.05. TNF-α, which showed elevation in all 4 subjects (p < 0.0001), has been linked to ASD due to its role in apoptosis and neural differentiation, potentially contributing to disrupted neurodevelopmental processes and compromised cognitive or neurological functions during the fetal period [237].

Congenital zika syndrome prevention and intervention

Preventing CZS begins with controlling maternal exposure factors to ZIKV and preventing vertical transmission to the embryo, especially during the first period of the pregnancy as neurodevelopment is most susceptible to teratogenic and viral influence [245, 246]. ZIKV can be transmitted via different routes: vector borne transmission via Aedes species, sexual transmission and body fluids, vertical transmission to the embryo, and laboratory exposure, and controlling these routes can prevent CZS [245]. As ZIKV can be transmitted via Aedes species, controlling this mode of transmission can aid in limiting ZIKV infection thus preventing CZS [247]. Recent cross-sectional observational studies in mosquitoes found that Aedes species, especially Ae. aegypti, can develop resistance to ZIKV infection via midGM modulation [247]. A Colombian field study of 62 field-collected Aedes females, 17 (27.4%) were ZIKV-positive, investigated the gut microbiome composition of these subjects and assessed the ZIKV effect on both groups, infected and non-infected. As reported in the study, ZIKV infected Ae. aegypti microbiome composition was mainly Bacteroidaceae family, more predominantly Bacteroides vulgatus, while non-infected Ae. aegypti microbiome was mainly Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Gammaproteobacteria. Moreover, Ae. aegypti microbiome showed a significant increase in Dorea formicigenerans (class Clostridia) after ZIKV infection, where class Bacteroidia, class Clostridia, class Bacilli, class Fusobacteriia, and class Negativicutes were significantly decreased under the influence of ZIKV infection. More importantly, these studies suggest that Serratia marcescens, a gut commensal bacterium, can both promote ZIKV survival and protect the vector against ZIKV. Serratia promote ZIKV infection by enhancing the digestion of membrane-bound mucins in Ae. aegypti. Also, Serratia is suggested to reduce mosquito permissiveness to ZIKV Ae. aegypti against ZIKV in many mechanisms, such as LPS synthesis, quorum sensing, and phenazine biosynthesis, but the main modulator is the ability of Serratia to secrete compounds that inhibit the growth, survival, or replication of viruses and other pathogens [247]. Genetically engineered symbiotic bacteria implementation is a novel approach that can be used to develop resistance in mosquitos, the vector for several diseases [248]. In similar context, researchers used a genetically modifies Serratia bacterium strain (AS1) to stably colonize mosquitos’ midgut, female ovaries, and male accessory glands and further spread rapidly and efficiently through the mosquito population. Serratia AS1 was genetically modified to inhibit the growth of Plasmodium falciparum in mosquitoes by the recombinant strains and promoting the secretion of anti-Plasmodium effector proteins to support the mosquitos’ immunity against the malaria’s pathogen [248]. Implementing this technique can help in limiting vector borne transmission of ZIKV, thus reducing CZS incidence.

Vertical transmission of ZIKV is the main cause of CZS, thus preventative measures need to be considered in the setting of a ZIKV infected pregnant woman. Recent preclinical (in vitro and in vivo animal) studies show that Sofosbuvir (SOF), an FDA-approved nucleotide analog inhibitor of the Hepatitis C (HCV) RdRp, has protective features against ZIKV infection in vitro and in vivo [249]. The Flaviviridae viral family, including both HCV and ZIKV, share similar RdRp structure. In our case, both HCV and ZIKV, have common RdRp encoding genes lie within NS5 and NS5B domains. This homogeneity of RdRp among the Flaviviridae viral family suggests the possibility of a common target and mechanism of action, which supports that SOF have effects on ZIKV as well, not just HCV. These studies show the efficacy of SOF in blocking the replication of several ZIKV strains, preserving human neural progenitor cells (NPC) functions and 3D neurospheres, and preventing vertical transmission of ZIKV in vivo with mice model. Also, it has been shown that SOF restored NPCs antiviral immune responses against ZIKV, supporting the urgency of clinical use in the near future [249].

Lactating mothers can also help protect their children against ZIKV. A preclinical in vitro study investigating passive immunity, transferred from the mother to the child via human milk, found that human milk is endowed with an intrinsic anti-ZIKV activity due to the presence of extracellular vesicles and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), which blocks viral binding to the target cell rather than directly interacting with the viral particles. The results were consistent across all stages of lactating: colostrum, transitional, and mature milk. Experimental results showed that EV can significantly inhibit ZIKV infection in vitro, most likely by competing with the virus for attachment receptors on the host cell. Additionally, human milk GAGs can act as soluble receptors inhibiting the attachment of different pathogens to the intestinal mucosa, especially when present in physiologically relevant concentrations during early lactation, contributing to the anti-ZIKV intrinsic activity of human milk [250].

Conclusion and future directions

CZS is a group of symptoms that occurs to the fetus via vertical transmission of ZIKV from infected mother [237]. CZS has been linked to multiple neurodevelopmental disorders, like ASD [237]. The effects of ZIKV on the GM are still poorly understood and need more research [244]. CZS can be prevented or limited using novel approaches, such as using SOF and implementing genetically engineered bacteria strains in the vector, although clinical trials are needed for further investigation [247–249]. Despite mounting evidence on the GBA crucial role on fetal brain development, the underlying mechanisms are still unclear [240]. The predominance of preclinical studies due to limited access to human fetal samples needs to be considered [238]. Preclinical models give important mechanistic insight into the MGBA, where direct research on humans has limitations due to ethical and practical problems. Animal experiments have revealed a causal connection from maternal dysbiosis to inflammation and impaired neurodevelopment in her offspring. Yet, getting from animal to human findings cannot be entirely straightforward: the rodent and murine models differ from humans concerning microbial composition, immune system development, and neurodevelopmental trajectories. Hence, while preclinical data strongly support the plausibility and produce hypotheses, it remains to also conduct human studies, in particular longitudinal ones and interventional studies, in order to put the mechanisms on trial for testing therapeutic efficacy. In children, current clinical evidence, e.g., associations of microbial taxa with preterm neurodevelopmental outcomes, shows some degree of concurrence with preclinical findings; however, causation remains much less clear.

Further studies and experiments should focus more on investigating the relation between maternal GM, inflammatory markers, and post-ZIKV infection outcomes on the fetus, specify the underlying mechanisms, and conduct longitudinal human cohort studies to further understand these mechanisms and suggested outcomes.

Motor and epileptic disorders

Cerebral palsy (CP)

Background

CP is an umbrella term for a group of permanent disorders of motor development and posture [251], attributed to early life, non-progressive disturbances in the developing brain [252]. Although motor dysfunction is the core and defining feature of CP, the disorder is often accompanied by comorbidities, including epilepsy, intellectual disabilities, orthopedic complications, speech and vision impairments, and GI disturbances [253]. CP represents the most common cause of physical disability in childhood [254], with an estimated prevalence of 2 to 3 per 1000 live births [255]. CP etiology is multifactorial, with over 90% of cases linked to prenatal or perinatal events (e.g., prematurity, intrauterine infection, intrauterine growth restriction, chromosomal abnormalities, congenital malformation, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy), while postnatal causes (e.g., traumatic brain injury, infection, metabolic derangement) are less common [253, 256, 257]. Prematurity is a major risk factor, implicated in up to 44% of cases [258]. The clinical diagnosis of CP is guided by motor subtype (spastic, dyskinetic, ataxic) and by the topographical distribution of weakness (monoplegic, hemiplegic, diplegic, quadriplegic), with spastic diplegia the most common and ataxic CP the rarest [252]. Despite advances in care, CP remains incurable, and management is largely supportive and symptomatic [259].

The gut microbiota, the gut-brain axis and CP

GM dysbiosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of multiple neurological disorders through the gut-brain axis. Several mechanisms have been proposed regarding how the GM exerts its effects, including immune modulation, microbial metabolites, and excitotoxicity [260]. Accumulating evidence from multiple studies has established that gut dysbiosis is a defining feature in children with CP and in those with CP and epilepsy, distinguishing their intestinal microbial ecology from that of typically developing children. A recent study explored differences in the intestinal flora structure between patients with CP and healthy controls. The study recruited 34 children with CP, 20 children with both CP and epilepsy (CPE), and 22 healthy children, providing a relatively balanced cohort for group comparison. Fecal samples were collected and analyzed using high-throughput sequencing methods across all three groups [261]. The results demonstrated significant gut dysbiosis among CP patients compared with healthy controls; notably, the microbial profiles of the CP and CPE groups were highly similar, suggesting that comorbid epilepsy does not add substantially to the GM alterations already present in CP. The taxonomic analysis revealed that children with CP exhibited a lower abundance of Firmicutes, specifically key butyrate producers such as Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Blautia, and Anaerostipes, and a higher abundance of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria compared to healthy children. Moreover, several bacteria recognized as protective for the gut barrier, including Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Blautia, and Anaerostipes, were found to be significantly reduced in the CP groups. Surprisingly, the study found higher microbial diversity (measured by the Shannon index) in both CP and CPE groups compared with healthy individuals. This is counterintuitive, as decreased diversity is typically associated with disease in most microbiome-related pathologies. The interpretation of this finding remains open to debate, as increased diversity in a disrupted or unstable microbial ecosystem may not equate to improved health, indeed, it may reflect an overgrowth of non-beneficial taxa or a less mature, less stable community structure.

Another observation from this study was the markedly higher abundance of Prevotella in the CP cohorts. Prevotella is a major producer of SCFAs, including butyrate, a compound known for neuroprotective and gut barrier-supporting effects [262]. Based on this finding, the authors proposed that elevated Prevotella might play a protective role in children with CP. However, this interpretation warrants caution. While increased SCFA production can be beneficial, some research suggests that an overrepresentation of Prevotella may, under certain conditions, contribute to inflammation or barrier disruption, highlighting the need for functional studies and consideration of host diet, age, and community context before attributing a universally positive role to this genus. Supporting these findings, a study conducted a related study focused on children with CPE (n = 25, aged 3–18 years) and healthy controls (n = 21) [263]. They similarly identified pronounced gut dysbiosis in the CPE group, characterized by a higher abundance of Enterococcus, Bifidobacterium, Clostridium IV, and Akkermansia, together with significant reductions in Anaerostipes, Faecalibacterium, and Bacteroides, the latter two again emphasized as important butyrate-producing and protective genera [258]. Consistent with [256], the CPE group exhibited higher microbial diversity relative to healthy controls.

Multiple factors, independent of CP pathology, may shape gut microbiota composition in affected individuals. A cross-sectional human study found care environment affects gut microbiota composition in CP patients independent of disorder severity [265]. Family care (n = 21) increased butyrate-producing Bacteroides and Lachnospiracea, whereas institutional care (n = 11) increased inflammatory taxa including Prevotella, Clostridium cluster IV, and Oscillibacter [265]. The authors hypothesised these differences may be attributed to dietary diversity and microbial exchange in family settings. Moreover, these compositional shifts occurred regardless of motor impairment, suggesting care setting may confound disorder-associated dysbiosis [265]. Furthermore, medication-associated effects present an additional confounder. GABA based antiepileptics were markedly more prevalent among constipated CP subjects − 46.7% of constipated versus 9.1% of non-constipated subjects used these medications (p = 0.0063) [266]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CP associated dysbiosis may reflect a complex interplay of care environment, pharmacological interventions, and secondary GI dysfunction rather than a direct consequence of neurological pathology. Future microbiome studies in CP must employ multivariable analyses to disentangle these confounding influences from disease-specific microbial signatures.

Gut microbiota modulating interventions

Liquid vs general diet

GM-based interventions in CP are understudied, but emerging data suggest clear associations between diet, microbial composition, and clinical manifestations. One study directly compared a liquid diet (n = 23) and a general diet (n = 17) in children with CP. The liquid diet consisted mainly of powdered milk, rice paste, and rice soup, while the general diet included diverse plant-based foods and small amounts of animal protein and fat. The results showed that children on the general diet exhibited significantly higher levels of beneficial, SCFA-producing genera such as Lachnoclostridium, Dorea, Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, Coprococcus, and Roseburia. In contrast, children on the liquid diet harbored greater proportions of potentially pathogenic, gut barrier-compromising bacteria including Collinsella, Alistipes, and Eggerthella. This microbiota imbalance was clinically relevant; the liquid diet group suffered higher rates of constipation, bloating, and vomiting. These findings strongly argue that a fiber-rich, diverse diet is integral to maintaining a healthier gut microbiome and reducing GI dysfunction in CP. However, the absence of randomization and unmeasured confounders, such as disease severity or other therapies, limit causal inference.

Probiotics

Probiotics have been examined as potential therapeutic agents in many childhood disorders. Evidence for probiotics as disease modulators in CP is mainly preclinical. In a randomized controlled trial [260], investigated the effect of Lactobacillus rhamnosus OF44 for 4 weeks in a rat model of CP versus healthy controls (n = 8). The study found that L. rhamnosus intervention reduced inflammatory responses in CP rats by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines significantly but failed to improve spatial motion abilities. The authors attributed the findings to a short intervention period [267]. In another preclinical study [261], showed that Saccharomyces boulardii improved depression-like behaviors and reduced inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) in a CP rat model. The observed improvements were attributed to decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines (il-6, TNF-alpha) in the CP group, increased diversity of microbial flora, and decreased HPA axis hyperactivity. Yet, such broad effects in rodent models require cautious interpretation. [268].

Fiber, probiotics, and prebiotics

In a randomized controlled trial by [269], the effects of fiber and probiotic supplementation on CP children and found significant improvement in CP-associated GI dysfunction. The researchers administered psyllium seed husk containing dietary fiber in addition to Lactobacillus, Clostridium butyricum, and Bifidobacterium animalis containing probiotics daily for 6 months. The parameters used to assess GI function included stool frequency, spontaneous defecation frequency, and the Bristol stool scale. The researchers reported that the frequency of spontaneous defecation without manual assistance or laxative use increased significantly in all CP children. The Bristol stool scale results also improved significantly in CP children at the 1-month mark. These results support dietary fiber and probiotic supplementation as pragmatic interventions for CP-associated constipation, though the study does not address short or long-term neurodevelopmental benefits. Similarly, in a a randomized controlled trial by [270], 37 children with cerebral palsy (mean age 37 ± 13 months; ages 14–60 months; 62% male, 38% female), with chronic constipation who received Lactobacillus reuteri (probiotic) and Agave Inulin (prebiotic fiber) demonstrated significant improvements in stool frequency and consistency in the intervention groups compared to placebo. The study did not measure the functional impact on neurological symptoms.

Conclusion

CP is a complex multisystemic condition with diverse comorbidities. Consistent evidence demonstrates gut dysbiosis in children with CP, strongly linking altered GM to prevalent GI symptoms, especially constipation. Early studies and preclinical models indicate that GM modulation through fiber, probiotics, and prebiotics can effectively improve GI function and may also have potential for alleviating psychiatric symptoms such as depression. Clinical studies consistently show that dietary diversity and targeted prebiotic or probiotic interventions can modulate the fecal microbiota and reduce GI symptoms in children with CP. However, the available evidence is preliminary, functional neurological outcomes remain largely unmeasured, and larger, well-controlled trials are needed to determine long-term efficacy and clarify underlying mechanisms before such interventions can be widely recommended.

Pediatric epilepsy

Background

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures resulting from abnormal electrical activity in the brain. The relationship between pediatric epilepsy and the gut microbiome has emerged as one of the most compelling areas of neurological research in recent years, with mounting evidence demonstrating a complex bidirectional communication pathway through the gut-brain axis. This axis represents a sophisticated network where the GI tract communicates with the CNS through neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic pathways [271]. The gut microbiome significantly impacts neuroinflammation and immune responses in pediatric epilepsy, where dysbiosis can lead to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and BBB disruption [272]. Studies have consistently shown that bacterial members of the GM can activate astrocytes and microglia, leading to chronic neuroinflammation and increased seizure susceptibility [273]. Furthermore, gut microbes can directly influence neurotransmitter production and metabolism, with certain bacterial species producing or metabolizing crucial neurotransmitters including GABA, glutamate, serotonin, and norepinephrine. The balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission is particularly crucial for seizure control, with alterations in the GABA/glutamate ratio being especially relevant to epilepsy pathogenesis [274].

Microbiome alterations in pediatric epilepsy

Bacterial patterns

Children with epilepsy consistently demonstrate distinct microbial composition differences compared to healthy controls, with several patterns emerging across multiple studies. There is a notable increase in potentially pathogenic bacteria, particularly elevated levels of Firmicutes in patients with refractory epilepsy [275], higher levels of Proteobacteria observed in epilepsy patients [276], and increased Verrucomicrobia in refractory epilepsy case [277]. Conversely, beneficial bacterial populations are often significantly reduced, including decreased Bacteroidetes in many pediatric epilepsy patients, reduced Actinobacteria abundance that normalizes after effective treatment, and lower levels of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus that correlate with increased seizure frequency [275]. Recent studies have identified specific bacterial genera as potential biomarkers for pediatric epilepsy, with risk factors including Fusobacterium mortiferum identified as a potential risk factor for developing epilepsy [277], Escherichia/Shigella associated with pro-inflammatory status and seizure susceptibility, and Streptococcus enriched in pre-treatment epilepsy patients [276]. Protective factors include Faecalibacterium, which are SCFAs-producing bacteria with anti-inflammatory properties, and Lactobacillus johnsonii, which has been shown to aid anti-seizure medication effectiveness [275].

Metabolic pathways and SCFAs

The metabolic alterations in pediatric epilepsy patients revealed significant changes in gut-derived metabolites, particularly in carbohydrate metabolism with succinate accumulation that may play a role in seizure generation [278]. Amino acid metabolism showed changes in tryptophan, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism pathways [277], while fatty acid metabolism demonstrated altered β-oxidation pathways [279]. SCFAs, particularly butyrate, play a crucial protective role in epilepsy by maintaining BBB integrity and providing neuroprotective effects (Fig. 8). Pediatric epilepsy syndrome types

Pediatric epilepsy syndromes encompass a diverse group of disorders characterized by recurrent seizures that begin in childhood and often present unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. These syndromes vary widely in their clinical manifestations, age of onset, seizure types, underlying causes, and prognosis. Common pediatric epilepsy syndromes include West syndrome (infantile spasms), Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, each associated with distinct electroclinical features and developmental outcomes. Early recognition and accurate classification of these syndromes are essential for optimizing treatment strategies and improving long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in affected children.

West syndrome

Infantile spasms are a type of seizure seen in infants, characterized by sudden, brief contractions of the neck, trunk, and limbs that typically occur in clusters. These spasms usually begin before the age of two and are often associated with a distinct, chaotic electroencephalogram pattern called hypsarrhythmia. Infantile spasms can result from a wide range of causes, including structural, genetic, metabolic, infectious, or unknown factors. When infantile spasms occur together with hypsarrhythmia on EEG and developmental regression, the condition is classified as West syndrome. West syndrome, a triad of infantile spasms, hypsarrhythmia, and neurodevelopmental regression, shows specific microbiome associations that provide insight into its pathogenesis [280]. Imbalance in GM composition appears to be involved in the pathogenesis of West syndrome, with specific bacterial alterations including associations between Lachnospiraceae and Lachnospiracea_incertae_sedis with disease onset [281]. Sutterellaceae may be linked to improved health outcomes in these patients. The mechanistic insights reveal that HPA axis dysregulation correlates with microbiome changes, inflammatory markers including interleukin-2 (IL-2) and TNF-α are associated with specific bacterial alterations, and response to adrenocorticotropic hormone treatment may be influenced by microbiome composition [282]. Recent studies have shown that targeted GM manipulation can attenuate seizures in infantile spasms models, with ketogenic diet (KD) and antibiotic administration reducing spasm frequency and being associated with improved developmental outcomes. These spasm reductions were accompanied by specific gut microbial alterations, including increases in Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactococcus lactis [280].

Lennox-Gastaut syndrome

While specific microbiome studies for Lennox-Gastaut syndrome are limited in recent literature, evidence suggests that the transition from West syndrome to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome may involve neuroinflammatory pathways influenced by GM [272]. Treatment responses in Lennox-Gastaut syndrome may be modulated by microbiome composition, similar to other epilepsy syndromes, though more research is needed to establish specific patterns [283].

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy demonstrates specific protective microorganisms identified through Mendelian randomization studies, providing strong evidence for causal relationships [278]. The class Betaproteobacteria is associated with lower risk of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, while the order Burkholderiales represents a potential protective factor against this syndrome [284]. The mechanistic considerations suggest that these organisms may influence neurodevelopment through neuroactive substance production, and modulation of the gut-brain axis during critical developmental periods may be involved in the protective effects [278]. Certain epilepsy syndromes, such as juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, have been noted to have circadian patterns of seizures, with circadian disturbances potentially being both consequence and cause of breakthrough seizures [285].

Febrile seizures: an emerging focus

Febrile seizures are convulsions that occur in young children, typically between 6 months and 6 years of age, and are provoked by fever in the absence of an underlying CNS infection or acute neurological illness. These seizures are usually brief, generalized, and self-limited, and are the most common type of seizure disorder in childhood. While not epilepsy, febrile seizures generally do not lead to long-term neurological problems and have been linked to striking microbiome patterns. Febrile seizures present distinct microbiome patterns that differentiate them from other seizure types and healthy controls. The microbiome characteristics include significant differences in GM composition compared to healthy children, with reduced Patescibacteria in children with febrile seizures and increased Bacteroides abundance associated with microglial activation [286]. Metabolic markers reveal ten differentially accumulated metabolites identified as potential biomarkers, including xanthosine, taurine, and various carnitine derivatives. There are alterations in taurine metabolism, glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, and arginine biosynthesis pathways [277]. The correlation with inflammation shows that Bacteroides correlates with four differential metabolites associated with neuroinflammation, establishing a link between GM and febrile seizure susceptibility through inflammatory pathways [277, 286]. Recent Mendelian randomization studies have revealed associations between GM abundance, plasma metabolites, circulating cytokines, and febrile seizures, with phylum Verrucomicrobia, four circulating cytokines, and fifty plasma metabolites showing associations with febrile seizures [277].

Treatment implications

Ketogenic diet (KD)

KD remains one of the most extensively studied microbiome-modulating interventions in pediatric epilepsy, demonstrating significant therapeutic potential through multiple mechanisms. The KD alters gut microbial function in children with refractory epilepsy, enriching genes related to fatty acid β-oxidation and anaplerotic reactions [279, 287], particularly in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Importantly, colonizing mice with KD-associated microbes promotes seizure resistance, providing direct evidence for the microbiome’s role in seizure protection [279]. Predictive biomarkers show that higher baseline levels of Bifidobacterium and TNF-α predict better response to KD, while specific bacterial species, including Bifidobacterium longum and Bifidobacterium breve are associated with treatment success [279]. Recent studies have found that clinically prescribed ketogenic infant formulas vary in macronutrient ratio, fat source, and fiber content, and also in their ability to promote resistance to seizures [287]. Dietary fiber, rather than fat ratio or source, drives substantial metagenomic shifts, with addition of dietary fiber to a fiber-deficient ketogenic formula restoring seizure resistance [287].

Anti-seizure medications

Anti-seizure medications demonstrate important interactions with the gut microbiome that may influence treatment outcomes [288]. Chronic exposure to anti-seizure medications shapes gut microbiome composition, with specific examples including topiramate increasing Lactobacillus johnsonii, which aids its anti-seizure effects [276]. Medication effectiveness may be enhanced by specific microbial populations, suggesting that probiotic interventions, including Lactobacillus johnsonii supplementation show promise as adjunct therapy [276]. Probiotic interventions may improve treatment outcomes in drug-resistant epilepsy, with recent studies showing that synbiotics in drug-resistant epilepsy patients are associated with lower parental burden of caregivers and reduced seizure frequency. An experimentalstudy of 30 children with drug-resistant epilepsy showed that synbiotic supplementation led to a statistically significant decrease in mean caregiver burden and mean frequency of seizures [289].

FMT

Emerging evidence suggests potential therapeutic applications for FMT in pediatric epilepsy, with case reports demonstrating significant seizure reduction following FMT [290]. Microbiota transfer therapy shows promise in related neurodevelopmental conditions, and recent studies provide evidence that mice who received microbiota from epileptic animals are more prone to develop status epilepticus (a prolonged or repeated seizure without full recovery between episodes) compared to recipients of “healthy” microbiota [290].

For pediatric epilepsy, case reports document remission of seizures lasting up to 20 months after FMT in patients who were otherwise refractory [291], and preclinical models suggest possible modulation of seizure susceptibility by donor microbiota [292]. Safety alerts were issued by regulatory authorities, including the FDA, after reports of deaths associated with donor-derived resistant bacteria, underscoring the need for stringent donor screening. In children, the ethical implications are greater, as informed consent is primarily based on parental decisions made against a backdrop of uncertain long-term risks. Subsequent work should, therefore, be focused upon well-designed multicentric randomized controlled trials utilizing a common protocol, incorporating mechanistic biomarkers, and undertaking developmental long-term follow-up to establish whether FMT can indeed be a safe and effective intervention for pediatric NDDs.

The future of personalized medicine approaches in pediatric epilepsy increasingly incorporates microbiome profiling, where individual microbiome signatures may predict treatment response. Personalized probiotic interventions based on baseline microbiome composition and integration of microbiome data with genetic and clinical factors represent promising avenues for clinical advancement [272].

Personalized medicine approaches

The development of biomarkers represents a crucial advancement in the field, with microbial metabolites serving as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, and real-time monitoring of microbiome changes during treatment becoming increasingly feasible. Novel therapeutic targets include microbiome-based interventions such as targeted probiotic supplementation for specific epilepsy syndromes, prebiotic interventions to promote beneficial bacterial growth, and postbiotic therapies using microbial metabolites [293]. Precision nutrition approaches involve microbiome-guided dietary interventions and personalized KD protocols based on individual microbiome profiles [287].

Limitations

Despite the promising developments, several challenges and considerations remain significant obstacles to clinical translation. Methodological limitations include small sample sizes in many pediatric studies, heterogeneity in epilepsy syndromes and treatments, and a lack of standardized protocols for microbiome analysis [272]. To date, clinical and translational literature do not support a single optimal timing, dose, or duration for microbiome-based interventions in pediatric populations. Randomized and observational studies use heterogeneous strains, formulations, CFU ranges, and treatment lengths, and many lack mechanistic endpoints or long-term follow-up, which prevents harmonized recommendations [294, 295]. Causality questions persist regarding the difficulty in establishing causal relationships between microbiome and seizures, with confounding factors, including medications, diet, and lifestyle, complicating interpretations. Therefore, greater focus should be placed on explaining the pathophysiological processes, including neurotransmitter modulation, immune and inflammatory pathways, and microbial metabolites that link microbiome alterations to neurodevelopmental changes. Incorporating specific experimental evidence or animal model data supporting these mechanisms would add scientific rigor. Animal models have provided valuable insights into the MGBA and its role in pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders, but there are serious constraints to their translational value. Some of the differences in microbial composition, neurodevelopmental time, and maturation of the immune system between rodents and humans limit generalizability [296, 297]. For ZIKV infection, murine models have advanced the understanding of viral tropism and neuroinflammation; however, interspecies differences in placentation and immune responses prevent direct human fetal neurodevelopment extrapolation [298]. The same way, FASD models highlight gross neurobehavioral impairment but spread widely beyond recognized cognitive and social deficits [299]. In pediatric epilepsy and CP, the ability of models to produce seizure or hypoxic-ischemic injury does not mirror the comorbidities or developmental trajectories in children [300]. Clinical translation faces regulatory considerations, including the need for standardized probiotic formulations, safety considerations in pediatric populations, and assessment of long-term effects of microbiome interventions [272]. The ethical morals of safety, equity, and regulation in interventions aimed at manipulating the microbiome come with uncertainty regarding lifetime risk of immune, metabolic, and even neurodevelopmental outcomes [301]. With specific regard to equity and access, these are particularly pertinent to the advanced interventions, such as probiotics, prebiotics, or FMT, that are likely to be available to those families privileged either financially or geographically, thus intensifying health-related inequities [302]. Even less regulation exists for these microbiome-based products for children; hence, it is ethically unwise to test vulnerable populations with unproven therapies [303]. Implementation challenges include integration into existing clinical workflows, cost-effectiveness of microbiome-based therapies, and training requirements for healthcare providers.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Our growing understanding of the MGBA has ushered in a new paradigm for addressing pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders. The complex, bidirectional communication between the gut and the brain is now recognized as a central mechanism underlying not only the emergence of various neurological and psychiatric conditions but also the potential for their prevention and modulation. From ADHD and ASD to TS and syndromic disorders like RTT and DS, a consistent pattern emerges: early-life microbial imbalances are linked to altered neurodevelopmental trajectories. These changes manifest through disruptions in neurotransmission, immune activation, metabolic dysregulation, and impaired neuroplasticity. Importantly, this body of evidence highlights the developmental windows during which microbial influences are most impactful. The prenatal period, infancy, and early childhood represent vulnerable phases in which microbial perturbations, whether due to maternal dysbiosis, cesarean delivery, antibiotic exposure, or poor diet, may permanently alter neuroimmune programming and synaptic development. This insight offers a unique opportunity for early preventive intervention. By modulating the maternal and early-life microbiome, it may be possible to reduce the risk or severity of neurodevelopmental conditions before they fully manifest. Equally compelling are the translational possibilities arising from this research. Dietary interventions, psychobiotics, FMT, and non-pharmacological approaches such as EE or traditional medicine-based practices are now being explored as means to recalibrate the MGBA. These approaches aim not only to alleviate core neurological symptoms but also to address comorbid GI, behavioral, and metabolic issues frequently present in affected children. Although some clinical trials have already demonstrated improvements in symptoms such as tics, irritability, or cognitive responsiveness, the field is still in its early stages. Mechanistic understanding remains incomplete, and questions surrounding optimal intervention timing, specificity of microbial targets, long-term safety, and individual variability require further investigation. Looking ahead, the path forward calls for integrative, multi-modal research strategies. Longitudinal cohort studies that follow children from birth, or even prenatally, are essential to map the developmental trajectories of the microbiome in both health and disease. These should be paired with advanced molecular tools such as metagenomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics to reveal how specific microbial signals influence host brain development. Nonetheless, small sample size still remains the greatest limitation of all studies due to potential overestimating of effect sizes, decreasing their reproducibility, and amplifying confounding factors such as dietary practices, medications, and the variability of baseline microbiome. In the face of these challenges, we propose that future research emphasizes multi-center collaboration to pool participants across sites in order to enhance the statistical power and external validity. In addition, longitudinal designs that examine repeated measures and harmonized protocols for microbiome analysis will mitigate variability and maximize the reliability of the insights garnered. In parallel, clinical research must evolve toward precision approaches that consider genetic predisposition, microbiome composition, diet, environment, and immune profile in order to tailor individualized therapies. Furthermore, interdisciplinary collaboration across pediatrics, neuroscience, microbiology, immunology, and nutrition will be vital in translating basic research findings into effective clinical strategies. The integration of microbiome science into routine pediatric care may eventually enable early risk prediction, personalized dietary recommendations, and targeted microbial therapies that optimize neurodevelopmental outcomes. It will also be important to establish ethical frameworks that ensure these interventions, especially those applied during early life, are implemented safely and responsibly. Future research should also aim to integrate biomarker discovery with microbiome studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the microbiota–gut–brain axis in pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders. At present, the field lacks validated biomarkers that can reliably capture microbiome–brain interactions. Lessons may be drawn from other disciplines, such as the use of pentraxin 3 (PTX3) as a biomarker for diabetic retinopathy [304]. Developing similarly robust and clinically translatable biomarkers will be crucial for advancing the translational potential of microbiome research in pediatric populations. Finally, advancing our understanding of the microbiota–gut–brain axis in neurodevelopmental disorders will require integration of insights from related neurobiological fields. For example, recent work has highlighted how gut dysbiosis can selectively affect neuronal populations and brain circuits [305], as well as how extracellular molecular signals shape dendritic architecture during brain development [306]. Such findings provide valuable context for exploring how microbiome alterations may interact with neuroanatomical and cellular mechanisms, further underscoring the importance of cross-disciplinary approaches. In conclusion, the MGBA presents both challenges and opportunities. Although much work remains, the progress made thus far provides a strong foundation. Continued research, innovation in therapy design, and a focus on early, personalized, and preventive care have the potential to significantly improve the landscape of pediatric neurodevelopmental health.
