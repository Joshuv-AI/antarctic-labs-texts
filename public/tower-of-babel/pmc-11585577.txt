Within-season estimates of 10 m aboveground biomass based on Landsat, Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope data
Authors: Tianyu Cai, Chuchen Chang, Yanbo Zhao, Xu Wang, Jilin Yang, Pengpeng Dou, Munkhdulam Otgonbayar, Geli Zhang, Yelu Zeng, Jie Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonitoring within-season dynamics of aboveground biomass (AGB) is critical for adaptive decision-making in grasslands to respond to changing conditions caused by frequent disturbances (e.g., grazing activities, fires, climate variations). This study trained the random forest model based on intensive observations from harmonious remote sensing data (Landsat, Sentinel-2, Sentinel-1) and an accurate date grassland sample database. Then we used monthly average remote sensing data to estimate monthly AGB at 10 m across three distinct grassland types in China: temperate meadow steppe, temperate typical steppe, and temperate desert steppe. Considering all the grassland types, the mean predicted AGB values were 83.02 g/m2 in June, 103.02 g/m2 in July, 108.49 g/m2 in August, and 106.89 g/m2 in September. The overall prediction accuracy was evaluated as R2 = 0.68, RMSE = 47.87 g/m². Additionally, the comparison of our monthly AGB maps with 3 m PlanetScope images and the published 30 m yearly AGB map shows the significant advantages of monthly 10 m products to capture the spatial and within-season dynamics of grassland AGB.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Grassland ecology

Background & Summary

Natural grassland is an essential element of the terrestrial ecosystem1 and serves multiple ecosystem functions, such as forage supply for livestock2, biodiversity maintenance3, and climate change regulation4. Aboveground biomass (AGB) reflects grassland productivity, stocking capacity, and terrestrial carbon stock, indicating the grassland’s health and conditions5. Therefore, accurately estimating AGB at temporal and spatial scales is essential, as it can provide information to develop adaptive strategies for grassland utilization.

Traditional harvesting strategies relying on ground measurements provide the most reliable AGB estimates. However, the extensive fieldwork involved often constrains their application at large scales6. Advances in Earth observation techniques have provided inventive approaches for acquiring biophysical information on vegetation at a relatively low cost, especially for large-scale and disabled-accessible area studies. Over the past few decades, many sensing-based methods have been developed for AGB monitoring in grasslands with acceptable accuracies at various spatial resolutions7,8. However, previous studies mainly concentrated on estimating AGB at the annual scale due to the limitation of within-season ground measurements9, based on the images from the peak or senescence periods10–12. They are insufficient to support within-season grassland management to improve the utilization efficiency and achieve long-term sustainable grassland development13. Within-season management relies on the seasonal AGB estimates to make flexible decisions, such as rotational grazing, prescribed burning, and livestock rate setting. It requires state-of-the-art observation techniques to capture the AGB variations in grasslands with the characteristics of fine-spatial resolutions (<10 m) and intensive time intervals (<yearly) across large regions14.

Utilizing satellite images for AGB estimates involves selecting appropriate algorithms and predictor variables to minimize model errors15. In terms of modeling algorithms, statistical models remain the mainstay for monitoring the AGB of grasslands via remote sensing due to their simplicity and acceptable accuracy16. Linear regression models were popular for AGB estimates4 but present drawbacks because of model coefficient uncertainties caused by site-specific factors like vegetation coverage and grassland types17. The linear regression models cannot effectively illustrate the nonlinear connections between biomass and satellite data18. In recent decades, the non-parametric statistical methods of machine learning algorithms, such as random forest (RF), artificial neural network, and support vector machine, have been extensively employed in AGB estimates7. Specifically, the RF algorithm is valuable for estimating AGB in grasslands19,20.

Regarding predictor variables, the AGB estimates have vastly relied on vegetation indices (VIs) derived from optical and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data. The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) is the most widely used proxy, accentuating distinct signatures of green vegetation while minimizing unwanted influences from non-vegetative elements21. Similar VIs include the ratio vegetation index (RVI) and difference vegetation index (DVI)22,23. However, the scalability of these VIs needs to be improved considering the following aspects. First, a high proportion of bare soil mixed within a pixel leads to the interference of soil24–26. To mitigate the impacts of soil properties, especially in sparsely vegetated grasslands, soil-adjusted vegetation index (SAVI), modified soil-adjusted vegetation index (MSAVI), and optimized soil-adjusted vegetation index (OSAVI) have been developed in previous studies27–29. The enhanced vegetation index (EVI) was introduced to alleviate the influence of soil and atmosphere26,30. Second, variations in water content are not often well captured by these VIs. Recent advancements include the superiority of normalized difference and phenology index (NDPI), which significantly reduced the adverse effects of soil background and could present changes in canopy moisture6. Thirdly, these VIs have nonlinear relationships with AGB31–33 and may be saturated over the areas or periods with high vegetation cover33,34. Kernel NDVI (kNDVI) recovers all higher-order differences between the NIR and red reflectance bands and deals with nonlinear and saturation effects35. However, the effectiveness of traditional and newly developed vegetation indices as predictors of AGB estimates in grasslands has not been assessed comprehensively in previous studies. Furthermore, SAR sensors, such as Sentinel-1, TerraSAR-X, and PALSAR, can estimate AGB due to the ability to penetrate clouds and canopies to gather information on vegetation structure36. However, the backscatter signals (HH, VV, HV, and VH) are influenced by surface roughness, dielectric properties, and moisture content37, potentially impeding the retrieval of accurate structural information. Whether the integration of SAR and optical data can effectively enhance AGB prediction across various grassland types remains unclear38.

Given the gaps mentioned above, this study’s primary objective is to track the monthly variations in AGB at 10 m spatial resolution based on satellite observations over a large area and diverse grassland types. The selected grasslands in Inner Mongolia, China, encompass various types of grasslands, including the temperate meadow steppe (TMS), temperate typical steppe (TTS), and temperate desert steppe (TDS)39. Initially, we trained the random forest model based on intensive observations from harmonious remote sensing data and ground samples with accurate dates. Then we used monthly average remote sensing data to estimate monthly AGB at 10 m. We assessed the importance of various predictor variables derived from optical and SAR data using a backward feature elimination approach Then, the best predictive model was developed based on the RF algorithm by selecting the optimizing predictor variables. The model was ultimately applied to generate the monthly 10 m AGB maps for the study area from June to September 2021. To highlight the detailed advantages of the monthly 10 m AGB products, we compared the resultant maps with 3 m PlanetScope images and the published 30 m product from a third party across different months and grassland types.

Material and Methods

Study area

The study site, Hulun Buir (47°05’N-53°20’N, 115°31’N-126°04’N), Xilin Gol league (42°32’N-46°41’N, 111°59’N-120°00’N) and Hexigten Banner of Chifeng (42°15’N-44°17’N, 116°18’N-118°30’N), was situated within the natural grasslands of Inner Mongolia, China (Fig. 1). The annual precipitation of 2022 of Xilin Gol league and Hexigten Banner of Chifeng was 202.4 mm, and Hulun Buir was 417.2 mm40. The annual average temperature of 2022 in Xilin Gol league and Hexigten Banner of Chifeng was 3.6°C, as well as Hulun Buir was 0.28°C40. As a characteristic arid and semi-arid zone, there are three distinct grassland ecosystems TMS, TTS, and TDS. The main soil types are chernozem, castanozems, and light castanozems41. TMS is a type of grassland found in the temperate and semi-humid regions of China, primarily consisting of perennial bunchgrasses, perennial rhizomatous grasses, and forbs42. TTS is mainly composed of tufted grass grasslands, with the dominant species being Stipa baicalensis43. TDS is a transition type from grassland to desert, and Stipa breviflora is the dominant species44,45.

Location of the study area, the dispersion of field samples, and the spatial arrangement of diverse temperate grassland types in Inner Mongolia, China34. The field samples used in this study were collected in 2021.

This area is the dominant animal husbandry breeding region in China. Extensive livestock grazing causes noticeable spatio-temporal heterogeneity in vegetation46. To estimate the AGB of different grasslands accurately, field sampling transects have been carried out covering the three grassland types.

Data

In-situ AGB measurements

The field survey spanned three trips from June 27 to September 11, 2021, covering the growing season. The first survey focused on TDS grassland from June 27 to July 31, 2021. The second survey focused on TMS grassland from July 27 to August 1, 2021. The last survey, from September 7 to September 11, concentrated on TTS grassland.

Areas with flat terrain and uniform spatial distribution were preferred as sample plots and the size the plot was 30 m × 30 m. The handheld GPS (Garmin eTrex 221x) was used to record the latitude and longitude of the sample plots, and four quadrants with a size of 1 m × 1 m were set for each sample plot. In each sample plot, fresh grass was mowed by destructive sampling. The fresh samples underwent a drying process to constant weight at 65 °C, and their weight post-drying was measured to calculate AGB for each sample square. The mean value of the dry weight from four quadrats determined the AGB of a sample site. The fieldwork collected a total of 124 sampling sites, including the numbers of 83, 19, and 22 for each of the field surveys (Fig. 1).

Harmonized Landsat 7/8 and Sentinel-2 data

This study harmonized the surface reflectance (SR) images of Landsat-7/8 and Sentinel-2 in the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform to increase the number of observation data and improve dynamic monitoring of AGB with a short time interval. First, the SR data of Landsat-7/8 and Sentinel-2 (LC/S2) was collected from June 1 to October 1, 2021, and featured a 30 m spatial resolution and a 16-day revisit cycle. The CF-mask algorithm identified the pixels of Landsat-7/8 with clouds, cloud shadows, and snow as bad-quality observations that were removed as NO DATA. The European Space Agency (ESA) has provided Level-2A Sentinel-2 data with a spatial resolution of 10 m and a 5-day revisit cycle since March 2017. Bad-quality pixels affected by opaque and cirrus clouds, as well as cloud shadows, were identified using the quality assurance band (QA60) and masked as NODATA.

Secondly, to create a consistent and comparable time series of Landsat-7/8 and Sentinel-2 data, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was applied to harmonize the datasets as outlined in the method proposed by Roy, et al.47 and Zhang, et al.48. Landsat 8 has better calibration and signal-to-noise ratio characteristics than Landsat 749. We coordinated the Landsat 7 and Sentinel-2 data with the standards of Landsat 8 data (Table 1). For Landsat 7 data, we converted the values of band 1 (blue), band 2 (green), band 3 (red), band 4 (near-infrared), and band 5 (shortwave-infrared) to match Landsat 8 bands using the OLS regression coefficients47. For Sentinel-2 data, band 2 (blue), band 3 (green), band 4 (red), band 8 A (near-infrared), and band 11 (shortwave-infrared) were converted using another set of OLS regression coefficients48. Notably, we did not use NASA’s Integrated Landsat and Sentinel-2 (HLS) SR product due to its unavailability on the GEE platform during the work carried out50.

SAR data from Sentinel-1 data

This study utilized SAR data from Sentinel-1 (S1) images processed on the GEE platform. The SAR imagery offers high spatial resolution (10 m, 25 m, and 40 m) and temporal density, with intervals of 12 days or 6 days. The S1 images consist of two polarizations, VV and VH, and are processed to obtain the backscatter coefficient (σ0) presented in decibels (dB) through log scaling. The GEE platform facilitated the generation of Sentinel-1 Analysis Ready Data (ARD), incorporating pre-processing steps such as border noise removal, speckle filtering, and radiometric terrain normalization51. For this study, we utilized the 10 m ARD from Sentinel-1, covering the study area from June 1, 2021, to October 1, 2021.

PlanetScope images

PlanetScope images were acquired by a constellation of CubeSats, with more than 200 satellites, offering nearly daily Earth observations at a spatial resolution of 3 meters (https://www.planet.com/products). Although the scientific quality of PS images is insufficient compared to widely acknowledged open Earth data such as Landsat series and Sentinel-252, advantages in spatial and temporal resolutions make it widely used in vegetation phenology, crop monitoring, and yield estimation53,54.

In this study, we employed the Level-3B SR products atmospherically corrected by Planet Labs55. Seven Level-3B SR images have been downloaded. Three of the images were obtained for different grassland areas in August 2021 to assess the performance of our AGB products (Fig. 4). The remaining four images were downloaded for the same location from June to October 2021 to evaluate the potential of our products in capturing the seasonal dynamics of grasslands (Fig. 5). In this analysis, we selected only images labeled with the quality control type ‘clear’ by the PlanetScope mask layer.

Methods

The methodological approach for AGB estimation is outlined in Fig. 2. There were three main sections. We first established database construction by harmonized LC/S2 images and field data. Second, we constructed the random forest model and optimized it by variable selection. Last, we mapped the monthly AGB across three grassland types based on random forest model and assessed accuracy.

A summary of the methodological approach employed for the estimation of aboveground biomass (AGB) in this study.

Monthly AGB mapping based on random forest algorithm

The random forest (RF) algorithm is a non-parametric statistical estimate technique composed of multiple uncorrelated regression trees. It samples the data and variables randomly and repeatedly to generate an extensive collection of classification and regression trees or a “forest” of them (CART)56. The RF algorithm plays a vital role in AGB estimates because of its capacity to handle high-dimensional features and is insensitive to over-fitting57. The constructions of RF models were completed using the scikit-learn package under Python, and the number of trees was set to 200 in our study. The random forest model was built based on the ground samples with accurate dates and remote sensing images which were the closest to the dates of ground samples. Here, the model was trained using the samples from June to September to obtain a universal model for different months. Then we used the trained model and the monthly averaged remote sensing data to estimate the AGB at 10 m for each month across three grassland types in China: TMS, TTS, and TDS).

Selecting suitable variables is essential for creating AGB models because some factors have co-dependencies or just weak correlations with AGB. It is not the case that the more variables are used, the higher the accuracy is received18. It is necessary to select the suitable predictors that yield optimal predictive performance to interpret the final model. In this study, we explored the robustness of different datasets (SAR, optical indices) for RF model construction using a recursive backward feature elimination method18. Firstly, the RF model was employed to rank all the 12 predictor variables to evaluate their relationships with AGB. Subsequently, starting with the RF model incorporating all predictors, we systematically eliminated the least promising variable in each iteration, fitting a new model. This process continued until only one predictor variable remained. The performance of each model was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE, Eq. 1) and R-square (R2, Eq. 2). The model with the lowest RMSE and highest R2 was selected as the final model for AGB estimates.

Model application for monthly AGB mapping at 10 m within the growing season

The harmonized Landsat 7/8 and Sentinel-2 data averaged for the month were downloaded in this study. The optimized RF model developed above was used to map the monthly AGB at 10 m for the study area in June, July, August, and September 2021. The 10 m resolution European Space Agency (ESA) WorldCover land cover data product was used to determine the grassland area in 2021 (https://zenodo.org/record/7254221).

Accuracy assessment

In this study, the field datasets (n = 124) were split into training and validation datasets (ratio = 7:3) based on their grassland types (TMS, TTS, TDS) using stratified sampling18. The training datasets were employed to optimize the random forest regression model (n = 86). The validation datasets were employed to assess the reliability of the prediction model (n = 38). The RMSE and R2 between the predicted AGB and the independent validation samples were examined. To further evaluate the model robustness under different grassland types, we split the validation samples into three groups by their grassland types and calculated RMSE and R2 of the predicted AGB and in-situ AGB for each grassland type, respectively.

Comparisons with PlanetScope images and other published products

A comparison with PlanetScope images was attempted to show the potential of our AGB products to capture the variations and growth dynamics of vegetation in space and time across grasslands. To assess the performance of our products in monitoring AGB in different regions, we downloaded Planetscope images in August 2021 for three grassland types (TMS, TTS, and TDS). In addition, we downloaded Planetscope images from various months to show the product’s performance at temporal scales.

A 30 m AGB dataset in Inner Mongolia from 2013 to 2022 has been generated based on a meta-analysis in a recent study by Wang, et al.58. The dataset was based on the RF model driven by 801 sample plots, and the maximum NDVI value in August. To assess whether a 10 m product could improve the capture of spatial heterogeneity of AGB, we conducted a comparison with the AGB product in 2021. We first integrated the monthly products into a yearly AGB map using the maximum synthesis method to show the spatial difference between our and the published products. In this process, the maximum AGB value from the monthly predictions was taken. Then, we resampled our resultant product to 30 m spatial resolution. Finally, a difference map was produced based on our 30 m AGB map and the published map. Additionally, we compared our monthly products with the published annual product to highlight the importance of monthly AGB estimates in tracking grassland dynamics.

Data Records

The monthly AGB dataset produced in this study was named “AGB_Inner Mongolia_2021”, and can be freely downloaded from figshare59. The data includes 4 files named by different months with tiff format in a zip. The maps were defined in the WGS84 projection and had a 10-m spatial resolution.

Potential of high-spatiotemporal-resolution AGB maps for grassland management

Previous studies highlighted the limitations of satellite data with 20 m and 30 m resolutions in capturing the spatial heterogeneity of vegetation biomass influenced by grazing14. In a similar context, an experiment in Inner Mongolia, China found that the predictive accuracy of AGB estimates was higher with 10 m resolution Sentinel-2 data compared to 30 m resolution Landsat-8 data under different grazing intensities60. These results underscored the significance of high-resolution satellite data for precise assessment and monitoring of grazing impacts on AGB estimates. Our results in Fig. 4 show a comparison of AGB distribution in various grassland types (TMS, TTS, and TDS) using true-color RGB images from PlanetScope (3 m). Notably, the 10 m high-resolution AGB map effectively captures diverse vegetation types, as highlighted in Fig. 4, emphasizing the advantages of high spatial resolution in depicting biomass distribution.

The advantage of frequent AGB mapping lies in its ability to capture and monitor temporal changes in grassland AGB over time, enabling the detection of sudden changes in biomass, such as disturbances caused by grazing or wildfire61. In Fig. 5, we present a detailed depiction of the seasonal changes in AGB observed in our AGB maps, comparing them to true-color RGB PlanetScope images. The ability to analyze the dynamics of grassland AGB over time enhances our understanding of ecosystem processes and supports effective grassland management strategies. The comparisons presented in both Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 underscore the capacity of high-resolution products to capture the heterogeneity of grasslands. This capability holds promise for enhancing grassland monitoring and management within the growing season.

Consistency of AGB mapping with previous studies

Our study observed a decrease in AGB distribution from southeast to northwest. Vegetation exhibited rapid growth in June, reaching its peak in July and August, and gradually declining in September, consistent with findings in previous years62,63. Moreover, we compared our estimated AGB under different grassland types with previous research (Table 4). Differences in values may arise from differences in data sources, including remote sensing data acquisition and field surveys. Additionally, variations in research scales, times, and methods can contribute to differences. Despite the disparities, these reference data suggest that our gridded AGB estimates are reasonably accurate.

The study by John, et al.11 revealed substantial variations in AGB across different grassland types in Inner Mongolia over different years. These fluctuations pose challenges when comparing our AGB product with other published products from disparate years. To address this, we computed the AGB difference between our AGB product and the recently published 30 m AGB product of 2021 by Wang, et al.58 (Fig. 6c). Several factors may contribute to the discrepancies in biomass distribution. Firstly, differences in pixel size and grassland mask can significantly impact biomass estimates. Secondly, the 16-day revisit cycle of Landsat images used in their study makes them more susceptible to the impacts of cloud and cloud shadows. Finally, the reliance on a single NDVI may be insufficient for accurate AGB estimates due to the spatial heterogeneity in Inner Mongolia. Furthermore, evaluation results using our validation samples (Fig. 6d) indicate that yearly products struggle to capture within-season dynamics in grasslands.

Usage Notes

Variable performance in AGB estimates

While previous studies have developed many VI-based models to strengthen the accuracy of biomass monitoring64,65, the complex spatial heterogeneity of grassland areas poses challenges in achieving consistently satisfactory results. This intricacy arises from the diverse and dynamic features of grassland ecosystems. In our study, we addressed these challenges through the application of the RF model, incorporating NDVI, kNDVI, NDPI, and EVI, which demonstrated superior predictive ability in AGB estimates. kNDVI and EVI were reported to solve the saturation effects. When the vegetation coverage is more than 80%, the increase of NDVI is delayed and saturated66. According to our field investigation, there were some enclosed grasslands, private grasslands, and mowing grasslands with coverage over 80% in Hulun Buir and Xilion Gol league, which might lead to the higher contribution of kNDVI. NDPI can explain canopy moisture and reduce the impacts of soil. Thus, the changes of leaf water content can be well captured by NDPI in different grassland types (e.g., TTS, TMS, TDS)

Moreover, previous research indicated that data from SAR, including the backscatter coefficient, can identify vegetation characteristics such as biomass or crop height67,68. Our results indicated that including SAR images did not enhance the accuracy of the estimates. This result confirmed our previous study on the AGB estimates in the tallgrass pasture of the United States38, indicating limited improvement in AGB estimates with SAR in areas with AGB below 500 g/m². This study was conducted in semi-arid and arid regions where AGB is below 350 g/m²69. The inherent challenges in these regions, such as low vegetation cover, make SAR signals more susceptible to influences from soil background, topography, and soil water content.

Uncertainties and improvements

Inevitably, uncertainties arose in several aspects of the AGB estimates of diverse grasslands. Firstly, there is a scale mismatch between field survey data and remotely sensed data71,72. The spatial resolution of the harmonized product data is 10 m, which differs considerably from the dimensions of the field quadrats. To mitigate this, we carefully selected representative sampling points, ensuring no interference from trees or roads within 10 m of the quadrat. We also aimed to minimize the adverse impacts of the scale effect by averaging values across multiple quadrats and pixel VI values.

Secondly, uncertainties in data arise from the time lag between satellite and field observations7. Despite the fusion of satellite data (S2 and Landsat7/8) products were employed to avoid signal loss caused by clouds, but the images’ dates were inconsistent with the sampling date, which may not obtain the real-time information of the AGB on the sampling date, especially during the peak vegetation season. In such cases, we chose the acquired image closest to the sampling date.

Future research aiming to predict grassland AGB should explore additional spatial statistics at larger spatial extents and temporal scales. This study had limited sampling points in temperate steppe desert areas, hence, additional samples are essential for accurate AGB estimation in these regions. Increasing the sample size may help to improve the accuracy. Furthermore, to gain a comprehensive understanding of grassland dynamics, extended investigations over multiple years would provide valuable insights into the trends of grassland yield and the impacts of grazing or other factors. Moreover, the emergence of nanosatellite constellations, like the commercial Planet CubeSats, providing unprecedented high temporal and spatial resolution data (3 m daily), presents opportunities to bridge the gaps between field surveys and satellite observations52. This has the potential to further enhance the accuracy of AGB estimates.
