Antecedents and consequences of different indicators of engagement with a digital intervention for tobacco cessation
Authors: Qinggang Yu, Lindsey N Potter, Carolyn Yoon, Jamie R T Yap, John J Dziak, Dusti R Jones, Cho Y Lam, David W Wetter, Inbal Nahum-Shani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMobile apps are commonly used to deliver digital interventions, typically through prompts that encourage engagement with intervention contents. However, engagement with these interventions remains suboptimal. A recent organizing framework conceptualizes digital interventions as a sequence of stimuli/tasks, underscoring the importance of measuring engagement with each stimulus/task separately. We applied this framework to a tobacco cessation intervention study that used an app to deliver prompts for practicing self-regulatory strategies. We found that engagement with certain stimuli/tasks (e.g., messages describing a low-effort strategy) declined over time, whereas engagement with others (e.g., messages recommending a more effortful strategy) remained stable. Initiating a brief in-app survey prior to prompting increased the likelihood of engaging with the intervention prompt, but not with subsequent messages or recommended strategies. These findings support conceptualizing engagement as a dynamic, multistage process and offer practical insights for optimizing engagement with digital interventions.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Adaptive clinical trial

Introduction

Recent advances in mobile technology have enabled scalable and cost-effective delivery of digital interventions to promote health behavior change. As smartphone ownership continues to expand1, mobile health (mHealth) applications (apps) are increasingly used to deliver various types of support2,3, such as reminders4, tips or strategies to facilitate behavior change5,6, and guided self-regulatory exercises7. Interventions delivered via mHealth apps typically include at least two key elements: (a) intervention content accessible at any time as needed (i.e., on-demand) and (b) prompts (i.e., cues for action) delivered via push notifications to encourage real-time interaction with the intervention content in daily lives8,9.

Despite their rapid growth, suboptimal engagement remains a major barrier to the effectiveness of digital interventions10,11. Engagement is defined as the investment of behavioral, cognitive, and affective energy in a focal stimulus or task12. Digital intervention studies focus primarily on behavioral engagement, as cognitive or affective aspects are more difficult to measure in real-time and they traditionally require subjective self-report13. Behavioral engagement is often measured using objective data on whether, when, and how individuals used the app (e.g., whether they opened the app and interacted with the content)14–16. Empirical evidence indicates that individuals usually abandon mHealth apps after minimal use17,18. For instance, an analysis of 93 mental health apps revealed that a median of only 3.9% of users accessed the app daily during the first 15 days after installation17. A large-scale review of eight digital health studies (N > 100,000) found that participants used the study apps to complete health assessments or tasks on a median of only 5.5 days within the first 12 weeks18. Given the critical role of sustained engagement in unlocking the full potential of digital interventions12,19, research aimed at understanding and enhancing engagement has steadily increased.

A recent conceptual framework highlights the importance of distinguishing each sequentially interrelated stimulus and task that comprises a digital intervention.20 Here, a “stimulus” refers to an external cue intended to facilitate behavior change (e.g., a prompt delivered from the mHealth app encouraging a person attempting to quit smoking to practice a brief meditation exercise), whereas a “task” refers to the prescribed actions the individual is expected to perform (e.g., completing the meditation exercise within the app). Mapping the intended journey—the sequence of steps through which individuals are expected to engage with each stimulus/task—and measuring engagement at each step are essential for developing more targeted strategies to enhance engagement and intervention effectivness21.

In addition to mapping the intended journey of engagement, it is critical to understand how psychological and contextual factors impact engagement with each stimulus/task. The Affect-Integration-Motivation and Attention-Context-Translation (AIM-ACT) framework identifies the psychological processes that underpin engagement with digital interventions12,22. According to this framework, stimuli are processed in a sequential and hierarchical manner. First, individuals register and respond affectively (Affect) to a stimulus (e.g., the prompt triggers feelings of curiosity). These affective responses are then integrated with situational factors (e.g., busy with work) and other relevant considerations, such as perceived effort and expected utility (Integration). This integration informs a valuation process that generates motivation to engage (Motivation). Importantly, both attentional (Attention) and contextual (Context) factors influence this sequence. A prompt cannot evoke an affective response if it fails to capture attention. Furthermore, contextual factors, such as stress or exposure to smoking cues, may alter the perceived costs and benefits of engaging with the prompt. These processes ultimately shape whether motivation is translated into engagement (Translation). By linking attentional and contextual processes, the AIM-ACT framework provides a comprehensive and theoretically grounded model for understanding engagement with digital interventions.

The current study aims to advance research on digital intervention engagement by decomposing an mHealth app-delivered tobacco cessation intervention into a sequence of stimuli and tasks and measuring engagement with each step separately. Guided by the AIM-ACT framework, we investigate how engagement with each step is influenced by participants’ attentional readiness (i.e., indicators that a digital prompt is likely to be noticed) and contextual factors (e.g., time in the study and lapse vulnerability). We also examine the functional utility of engagement in preventing real-time tobacco lapses.

We conducted secondary analyses of data from the Mobile Assistance for Regulating Smoking (MARS) study23. The MARS study aimed to inform the design of an mHealth app-delivered tobacco cessation intervention by conducting a 10-day micro-randomized trial (MRT), an experimental design that enables researchers to examine whether, when, and how to deliver digital intervention prompts in daily life.24

Participants were provided with a smartphone pre-installed with the MARS mHealth app. During the 10-day study period, participants were randomized six times daily to one of three conditions (2:1:1): (a) no prompt, (b) a prompt suggesting a relatively low-effort self-regulatory strategy, or (c) a prompt suggesting a more effortful self-regulatory strategy (each scheduled randomization is referred to as a “decision point” hereafter). The decision points were individualized, beginning 30 minutes after the participant’s typical wake time and then occurring at approximately two-hour intervals (see Methods for additional details).

When participants were randomized to a prompt (condition b or c), the app initiated a sequence of stimuli and tasks (Fig. 1). First, the app delivered an initial notification stating, “Time to practice a strategy,” which participants could accept (click “OK”) or dismiss (click “Cancel”). The notification remained on the screen for 45 min before expiring if no action was taken.

If the participant was assigned to a low-effort strategy, clicking “OK” opened a message describing a cognitive/behavioral strategy to regulate stress/craving and manage high-risk situations (e.g., “Take it one hour or day at a time. Just concentrate on getting through today without smoking”), which was drawn from a pool of about 180 similar messages. Once the message was displayed, participants could close it by clicking “Close” below the message.

If the participant was assigned to a more effortful strategy, clicking “OK” opened a message recommending a specific cognitive- or mindfulness-based exercise (e.g., “How about trying meditate?”). The recommended exercise was randomly drawn from a pool of five exercises that were based on empirically supported self-regulation techniques such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction25 and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy26. Participants could directly start the recommended exercise by clicking “Let’s go” or select another exercise from the main menu by clicking “Show all.” Each exercise included audio and visual guides and required one to four minutes to complete. Additional details and examples for both types of strategies were documented previously23 and are included in the Methods section.

Approximately one hour after each randomization, an ecological momentary assessment (EMA) was delivered. The EMA included questions about affect, behaviors, and cognitions, as well as the occurrence of stressful experiences and tobacco use since the previous EMA.

Guided by the stimulus/task journey mapping framework20, we constructed the following engagement indicators using log data of the app. The first indicator captured engagement with the initial notification (Engagement Indicator 1 in Fig. 1). Each time the initial notification (i.e., “Time to practice a strategy”) was delivered, this indicator was coded as 1 (yes) if the participant clicked “OK”, or as 0 (no) if they clicked “Cancel” or did not interact with the notification before it expired.

The second indicator captured engagement with the message describing a low-effort strategy (Engagement Indicator 2 in Fig. 1). Each time the message was revealed after the participant clicked “OK” on the initial notification, this indicator was coded as 1 (yes) if the participant clicked “Close” after a minimal time interval had elapsed. This interval was calculated based on message length and typical reading speed27 to ensure that participants likely read the content (see Methods for additional details). This indicator was coded as 0 (no) if the participant made no interaction with the message screen or if they clicked “Close” before the specified interval had elapsed.

The third indicator captured engagement with the message recommending a more effortful strategy (Engagement Indicator 3 in Fig. 1). Each time the message was revealed after the participant clicked “OK” on the initial notification, this indicator was coded as 1 (yes) if the participant interacted with the message by clicking either “Let’s go” or “Show all.” It was coded as 0 (no) if no interaction occurred (i.e., did not click either button).

The fourth indicator captured engagement with the more effortful strategy (Engagement Indicator 4 in Fig. 1). Each time the participant interacted with the message recommending a more effortful strategy, this indicator was coded as 1 (yes) if the participant completed (i.e., reached the end of) the exercise, or as 0 (no) if they did not.

We also created an indicator capturing engagement with any self-regulatory strategy. Throughout the study period, participants could access either the low-effort or the more effortful strategies by opening the MARS app directly, without being prompted. This indicator thus encompassed both prompted and participant-initiated engagement. For each decision point, it was coded as 1 (yes) if the participant engaged with either type of strategy or as 0 (no) otherwise. All five indicators described above were calculated within one hour following randomization, because the EMA was delivered one hour after each randomization.

Importantly, several engagement indicators (i.e., Engagement Indicators 2, 3, and 4) were quantified only when participants had engaged with the preceding step. This approach aligns with the sequential design of the MARS intervention. For example, a low-effort strategy message was displayed only if the participant first engaged with the initial notification (by clicking “OK”). Consequently, decision points at which participants lacked the opportunity to engage with a given stimulus/task because they did not engage with the previous step were excluded from analysis. This approach treats the sequential dependence between stimuli/tasks as an intrinsic feature of the intervention design20. Sensitivity analyses using alternative coding schemes are described in Supplementary Materials (Section A, B), and they produced substantively identical conclusions to those of the main analyses.

Building on the AIM-ACT framework and prior literature, this study addresses four research questions regarding the antecedents and consequences of engagement with each key step (i.e., stimulus or task) comprising the MARS digital intervention. First, prior research suggests that engagement declines over time, mainly due to participant burden and habituation28,29. However, it remains unclear whether this pattern applies uniformly to each stimulus and task comprising an intervention. The first scientific question thus examined the role of time in predicting engagement—specifically, whether and how each engagement indicator changed over the 10-day study period.

Second, prior work has highlighted the importance of delivering interventions at moments when an individual is most likely to attend and respond to the prompt30,31. We thus examined the role of attentional readiness in predicting engagement. In the current study, the app notified participants to complete a brief survey (“Time to answer 2 questions.”) prior to each randomization, assessing negative emotions (“Are you experiencing a negative emotion?”) and cigarette availability (“Cigarettes are available to me?”). Attention to mobile-based prompting was indexed by the participant’s action of clicking “OK” to start the brief survey, as previous evidence suggests that participants are more receptive to mobile-based prompting when they have recently interacted with their smartphone30,32. We therefore examined whether starting (vs. not starting) the brief survey predicted each engagement indicator.

Third, research has shown that indicators of vulnerability, such as negative emotions and cigarette availability, are strong predictors of smoking lapse33,34. Because heightened risk of lapse may increase the perceived value and utility of an intervention35–37, we investigated whether self-reported negative emotions (Yes vs. No) and cigarette availability (Yes vs. No) predicted each engagement indicator.

Fourth, prior research indicates that stressful experiences are associated with tobacco use38–40. Because the self-regulatory strategies in MARS were designed to help participants manage their responses to stressful experiences41,42, we examined whether engagement weakened the link between stressful experiences and tobacco use. Specifically, we tested whether stressful experiences were associated with tobacco use during the same self-reporting window (i.e., between the current EMA and the previous one), and whether this association was attenuated if the participant had engaged with any self-regulatory strategy during the one-hour period preceding that window (see Methods for additional details).

Results

Descriptive characteristics of randomizations and engagement

During the study, randomization at each decision point occurred only if specific criteria were met (e.g., the participant was not driving; see Methods for all criteria). One participant (out of a total of 111 participants) was excluded from all analyses for never meeting the criteria during the study (see Supplementary Materials for additional exclusions applied in each statistical analysis). Out of the total 6600 decision points (110 participants × 10 days × 6 times), randomization occurred at 4721 decision points: 2382 (50.5%) to no prompting, 1200 (25.4%) to prompting with a low-effort strategy, and 1139 (24.1%) to prompting with a more effortful strategy.

For the 1200 decision points assigned to prompting with a low-effort strategy, participants engaged with the initial notification in 756 decision points (63.0% of the 1200 decision points). Among these 756 decision points in which participants engaged with the initial notification and the message describing a low-effort strategy was revealed, participants engaged with the message (i.e., clicked “close”) in 562 decision points (74.3% of the 756 decision points, 47% of the initial 1200 decision points).

For the 1139 decision points assigned to prompting with a more effortful strategy, participants engaged with the initial notification in 718 decision points (63.0% of the 1139 decision points). Among these 718 decision points in which participants engaged with the initial notification and the message recommending a more effortful strategy was revealed, participants engaged with the message in 688 decision points (95.8% of the 718 decision points, 60.4% of the initial 1139 decision points). Among these 688 decision points, participants completed the more effortful strategy in 455 decision points (66.1% of the 688 decision points, 39.9% of the initial 1139 decision points).

Engagement and demographic factors

Gender differences emerged for one engagement indicator: female participants were 25.3% more likely than male participants to engage with the initial notifications (Est = exp(0.225) = 1.253, 95% CI = [1.026, 1.529]). Additionally, engagement with messages describing low-effort strategies (after engaging with the initial notification) increased with age; each additional year of age was associated with a 7.0% increase in engagement (Est = exp(0.068) = 1.070, 95% CI = [1.007, 1.138]). Note that these analyses did not include contextual or attentional predictors (e.g., time, attentional readiness), so the parameter estimates shown differ slightly from the corresponding entries in Tables 1 to 3.

Engagement and attentional readiness

As shown in Table 2, participants were 153.5% more likely to engage with the initial notification if they had started the brief survey compared to when they had not (Est = exp(0.930) = 2.535, 95% CI = [2.208, 2.911]). Engagement, negative emotions, and cigarette availability

There was no evidence that negative emotions or cigarette availability predicted engagement with any of the indicators. Detailed parameter estimates, standard errors, and 95% CIs are provided in Supplementary Materials (Section C; Table S1 and S2).

Momentary stressful experiences, tobacco use, and the buffering effect of engagement

The results presented in Table 3 show a positive association between momentary stressful experiences and tobacco use. Reporting any stressful experience (vs. reporting no stressful experience) was associated with a 37.7% greater likelihood of reporting tobacco use during the same self-reporting window (Est = exp(0.320) = 1.377, 95% CI = [1.154, 1.642]). This relationship was moderated by engagement with any self-regulatory strategy during the preceding hour (Est = exp(−0.338) = 0.713, 95% CI = [0.557, 0.913]). When participants did not engage with any self-regulatory strategy during the preceding hour, reporting a stressful experience (vs. not reporting a stressful experience) was associated with a 53.4% greater likelihood of reporting tobacco use (Est = exp(0.428) = 1.534). In contrast, when participants engaged with at least one self-regulatory strategy, reporting a stressful experience (vs. not reporting a stressful experience) was associated with only a 9.4% greater likelihood of reporting tobacco use (Est = exp(0.090) = 1.094).

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into how engagement with each key step (each stimulus/task) of an mHealth app-delivered intervention unfolds across time, varies as a function of attentional readiness, and influences clinically meaningful outcomes in real-world settings. These findings advance understanding of engagement with digital interventions and expand prior models of engagement in several important ways.

First, the findings suggest that engagement with a digital intervention is not monolithic. We found that engagement with the initial notification and subsequent engagement with the message describing a low-effort strategy declined over time. In contrast, engagement with the message recommending a more effortful strategy (after engagement with the initial notification) and subsequent engagement with the more effortful strategy itself remained relatively stable. There are several possible explanations for these results. In this study, due to space constraints of the smartphone screen and the need to describe the strategies clearly and concisely, the messages describing low-effort strategies shared a relatively similar length, structure, and content, potentially leading to habituation. Participants may also have become familiar with the low-effort strategies over time, reducing the need for repeated interaction with the app. This skill-learning process may be less applicable for the more effortful strategies that are harder to master. Participants might also have benefited more from the guided and interactive content afforded by the more effortful strategies, which motivated consistent use over time. The mechanisms underlying these patterns should be further explored by incorporating additional measures of engagement, including EMAs, follow-up surveys, or interviews to assess participants’ self-reported engagement. Nevertheless, these findings challenge the assumption that digital intervention engagement inevitably declines over time18,28,29, suggesting instead that each stimulus and task needs to be mapped out and investigated separately to understand the dynamics of engagement.

Second, the study demonstrates that participants were more likely to engage with the initial notification if they had recently interacted with the MARS app (e.g., by initiating a brief survey). This pattern aligns with the AIM-ACT framework, which positions attention as the foundational gateway to engagement12,22: by starting the survey, the participant is already attending to their device, thereby increasing the likelihood of noticing and responding to subsequent intervention notification. Notably, however, survey initiation did not predict engagement with subsequent stimuli/tasks (following engagement with the initial notification). Since these downstream steps required more efforts, engagement may instead be driven by other contextual factors12 that warrant further investigation.

Third, engagement with at least one self-regulatory strategy in the MARS mHealth app attenuated the positive association between stressful experiences and tobacco use in real-time. Prior research has identified stressful experiences as a major risk factor for tobacco use38–40 and highlighted the benefits of evidence-based self-regulatory strategies in improving one’s capacity to resist cravings44. However, prior evidence did not use intensive longitudinal data to examine stressful experiences, tobacco use, and self-regulation in real-time38,41,44. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to demonstrate that real-time engagement with self-regulatory strategies may buffer the impact of stress on tobacco use in daily life settings. Because these variables are observational, and stressful experiences and tobacco use were assessed concurrently, causal inference is limited, and directional interpretation remains unclear. Nevertheless, these result highlight the potential benefits of real-time, real-world engagement with self-regulatory strategies and calls for additional research to promote in-the-moment engagement.

Grounded in engagement theory, we propose an integrative process model wherein engagement with digital intervention-related stimuli and tasks involves a sequence of affective, integrative, and motivational responses that are shaped by attentional and contextual factors12. This integrative model extends the AIM-ACT framework by conceptualizing a digital intervention as a structured journey20. By deconstructing this journey into discrete steps, we distinguish step-specific antecedents of engagement and demonstrate how engagement translates into clinically meaningful outcomes (see Fig. S1). Although examined in a tobacco cessation context, the present study offers a generalizable framework applicable to a wide range of digital interventions, including mental health apps, chronic disease management tools, and consumer-facing health technologies.

The present work can also be situated within a broader scholarship on consumer engagement and digital interaction. Research in marketing conceptualizes engagement as a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive, emotional, and behavioral investment, which evolves into loyalty through repeated, meaningful interactions45–47. Parallel literature in human-computer interaction calls for conceptualizing engagement as a process comprising a sequence of distinct, interrelated interactions, and each specific interaction must be investigated to understand how engagement evolves within competitive digital environments48–50. These ideas are further situated within the “attention economy,” where digital health interventions must compete with commercial platforms for scarce user attention, necessitating smoother, contextually relevant, and timely interactions51. Drawing on these interdisciplinary perspectives, this study highlights how the step-by-step measurement approach to examine engagement can generalize beyond tobacco cessation context, extending their relevance to digital medicine and consumer-facing health technologies more broadly.

The present study has several limitations. First, the workflow of stimuli and tasks is specific to the intervention design used in MARS. Although many digital behavior change interventions share similar features, the nature and sequencing of stimuli and tasks likely vary across interventions. Nevertheless, the process of mapping and measuring engagement across sequential stimuli and tasks can be applied broadly to future digital intervention studies to identify strategies to promote engagement20.

Second, the study quantified engagement using log data generated from participant interactions with the app. It thus assessed only engagement with stimuli and tasks requiring app usage, missing potential offline engagement by participants19. Moreover, while objective and free from self-report bias, these measures capture only behavioral engagement and do not fully reflect the cognitive or affective dimensions12. Recent work has begun developing psychometrically reliable and valid instruments (using self-report) that capture cognitive and affective aspects of engagement52,53. However, the amount of time required for participants to complete these measures remains a barrier to implementation in real-time, real-world settings. Future studies should consider incorporating multiple data sources (e.g., log data, self-report measures, sensor data) to capture engagement more comprehensively.

Third, the measurement of vulnerability in the MARS study was relatively limited, as only a brief survey (with two items capturing cigarette availability and negative emotion) was administered immediately before each randomization. Although this approach minimized participant burden, it may not capture the full complexity of contextual influences. Advances in mobile sensing and just-in-time adaptive interventions may enable a better balance between the goal of minimizing participant burden and more comprehensive assessments of vulnerability.

Fourth, although the number of decision points in the present study was large, the participant sample was relatively modest, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, some statistically significant effects of time on engagement were small in magnitude, because time was coded as decision points which were only about two hours apart from each other. Replication in larger and more diverse samples, and across different digital intervention contexts is warranted.

Despite these limitations, this study offers valuable guidance for new approaches to measuring and promoting effective engagement with digital interventions. For instance, the differential time trends observed across engagement indicators suggest that intervention designers should consider varying the content, appearance, or frequency of the initial notification and other stimuli/tasks that may be prone to habituation. The finding that brief survey initiation was linked to increased engagement with the initial intervention notification also provides an actionable mechanism. Given that many digital interventions now integrate brief surveys to tailor subsequent contents7,54,55, it is possible to leverage data about survey initiation to guide adaptive intervention delivery—for example, by delivering subsequent stimuli/tasks only in instances where participants have already demonstrated active attention to the device.

Beyond providing empirical insights, this study advances engagement theory and extends the AIM-ACT framework by illustrating how attentional and contextual processes operate across sequential stages of digital interventions to shape engagement. These findings emphasize the importance of measuring engagement at multiple points along the intervention journey and highlight the value of leveraging real-time behavioral and contextual information to inform the design of digital health technologies.

Methods

The present study conducted secondary data analyses of the MARS study. The MARS study was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04376489), and its protocol has been published23. The University of Utah Institutional Review Board approve the study. All participants in the study provided informed consent.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited through multiple channels, including radio advertisements, websites (e.g., Google), social media platforms, commercial recruitment services, and postings in public such as community clinics, local restaurants, and bars. Eligible participants had to be 18 years of age or older; currently smoke an average of at least three cigarettes per day; be motivated to quit smoking within the next 30 days; have a valid home address and a functioning telephone number; be able to speak, read, and write English; and, for remote participants, have access to Wi-Fi. Individuals were excluded if they had contraindications for nicotine patches or nicotine gum, were currently using tobacco cessation medications, or were pregnant or lactating.

Among the 111 participants who completed the study, 38 (34.2%) were male, 69 (62.2%) were female, and 4 (3.6%) did not disclose gender information. The average age was 43.8 years (SD = 11.48). Thirty participants (27.0%) identified as Hispanic/Latino Americans. Among the non-Hispanic/Latino participants, 35 (43.2%) were White Americans, 34 (42.0%) were Black Americans, and the remaining 12 (14.8%) identified as belonging to other racial groups (including multiracial) or did not disclose race/ethnicity information. The median annual household income was between $30,000 and $39,999, and 42.3% of participants were either married or living with a significant other. At baseline, participants reported smoking an average of 13.7 cigarettes per day (SD = 8.01).

Study procedure

The present study analyzed data from the 10-day micro-randomized trial (MRT) during which participants used a study-provided phone equipped with the MARS mHealth app (mcerebrum; https://github.com/MD2Korg/mCerebrum-Configuration/tree/master/2.0/mars/mars/mCerebrum). The app delivered all prompts and included digital components (e.g., self-regulatory strategies) that participants could access anytime. Prior to the MRT, a study coordinator trained the participants to use the study phone and app.

Each day of the 10-day period was divided into six consecutive blocks, with the first block starting at a pre-specified wake time determined by the participants. During each block, three elements were administered: a brief survey, a micro-randomization, and an EMA. For the brief survey, participants received a prompt consisting of ringtones and vibrations, with the message “Time to answer 2 questions.” Participants could respond by clicking either “OK” or “Cancel.” Clicking “OK” opened two questions: (1) “Cigarettes are available to me?” and (2) “Are you experiencing a negative emotion?” Each question could be answered either “yes” or “no.” Clicking “Cancel” dismissed the prompt, and no questions were shown.

Immediately after completing or canceling the brief survey, participants were micro-randomized (with probability ratios of 2:1:1) to one of three conditions: (a) no prompt, (b) a prompt with a low-effort self-regulatory strategy, or (c) a prompt with a more effortful self-regulatory strategy. If the participant did not interact with the brief survey (e.g., ignored it), the survey expired after 2 min, and the micro-randomization followed.

If participants were micro-randomized to prompting (with either a low-effort or a more effortful strategy), an initial notification appeared on the screen with the message “Time to practice a strategy,” signaled by ringtones and vibrations. Participants could click “OK” to proceed or “Cancel” to dismiss the prompt. Clicking “OK” displayed either (a) a message describing a low-effort strategy (i.e., brief cognitive/behavioral strategies; see more details in the “Self-Regulatory Strategies” section) or (b) a message recommending a more effortful strategy (i.e., a cognitive or mindfulness-based task in the app; see more details in the “Self-Regulatory Strategies” section). Clicking “Cancel” on the initial notification dismissed the prompt. If participants did not interact with the prompt, it remained on the screen for 45 min before expiring.

One hour after each micro-randomization, an EMA was scheduled to be delivered. The EMA consisted of a series of questions on affect, behaviors, cognitions, stress, tobacco use, and other constructs. The app delivered the EMA prompt, signaled by ringtones and vibrations, with the message “Time to take a survey.” Participants could click “OK” to start, “Cancel” to dismiss, or “Delay (10 min)” to postpone the EMA by 10 min, with the option to start from the home screen at any time within that 10 min window.

The three elements described above (the brief survey, micro-randomization, and EMA) were not delivered if it was determined that the participant was not available. This included if the participant was driving, the smartphone was turned off, or privacy mode was activated (participants were allowed to activate privacy mode for up to two hours per day). Prompting resumed according to the pre-specified schedule once the participant was available again. More information about the pre-specified schedule for each type of prompt is available in the Github repository for data curation of the present study (https://github.com/d3center-isr/CORE-MARS). Throughout the 10-day MRT, participants could open the app and access both low-effort and more effortful strategies (described in the following section) at any time.

Self-regulatory strategies

The low-effort strategies consisted of brief motivational or behavioral/cognitive strategies designed to help participants manage stress, cravings, and high-risk situations. These strategies were simple and easy to implement, such as avoiding high-risk situations (e.g., moving away from places where you used to smoke), using distraction (e.g., taking a walk), and seeking social support (e.g., talking to your family and friends and asking for support). Over 180 messages were available, each tailored based on the participant’s response to the brief survey. There was a different pool of messages available for each of the four combinations of cigarette availability (yes/no) and negative emotions (yes/no). If participants did not answer the brief survey, a message was selected from the pool developed for situations where cigarettes were unavailable, and no negative emotions were present23. When participants accessed low-effort strategies on demand (without prompting), they were first directed to complete the brief survey. Upon completing the survey, the app displayed a message describing a low-effort strategy. Participants could then click “Next” to view another low-effort strategy message or click “Close,” at which point the message would disappear.

The more effortful strategies included five guided exercises with video and audio elements based on empirically supported self-regulation techniques such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy and Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction. These exercises were (a) Moodsurf: affect regulation through non-judgmental awareness of thoughts and emotions; (b) Meditate: mindfulness-based meditations; (c) Imagine: distancing oneself from unwanted thoughts; (d) Notice: developing awareness and acceptance of uncomfortable thoughts and sensations; and (e) Joy: deep breathing focused on savoring positive emotions (screenshots of the exercises are included in Figs. S2–S6 in supplementary materials).

Each time participants accessed a more effortful strategy, whether prompted or unprompted, they were first shown a message recommending one of the five exercises (e.g., “How about trying Joy?”). Participants could choose to start the recommended exercise by clicking “Let’s go” or view a full list of exercises by clicking “Show all” and selecting one. Once an exercise was started, the app guided participants through various pages and steps. Upon completing the exercise, participants were asked to rate whether they liked it by clicking on either a thumb-up or thumb-down icon. Additional screenshots of the MARS app, including the MARS app home page and the menu page for selecting one of the five guided exercises, are included in Fig. S7.

Operationalization of engagement indicators

As noted earlier, several engagement indicators were assessed only if the participant had engaged with the preceding step. This approach treats the dependence between stimuli and tasks comprising a digital intervention as an intrinsic part of the intervention design20.

Engagement with the initial notification (i.e., “Time to practice a strategy”) within the hour following micro-randomization was operationalized as a binary indicator. It differentiated between decision points where participants clicked “OK” ( = 1 [yes]) and those where participants either clicked “cancel” or did not interact with the notification ( = 0 [no]). It was coded as “missing” (i.e., not applicable, or “NA”) for decision points that (a) were micro-randomized to no prompting, (b) were not micro-randomized, or (c) lacked log data to assess participant interaction with the notification (see Section E of Supplementary Materials for details).

Engagement with the message describing a low-effort strategy within the hour following micro-randomization was operationalized as a binary indicator. It differentiated between decision points where participants closed the message ( = 1 [yes]) and those where they did not close the message ( = 0 [no]). To further evaluate engagement, we used the time interval until the participant closed the message. This interval was determined based on (a) the number of words in the message and (b) the expected time needed to read the message. Based on the average reading speed for non-fiction texts (mean = 238 words per minute, SD = 51.2)27, we allowed for a reading speed of 340 words per minute (two standard deviations above the mean) to accommodate most participants’ reading speed. For example, the interval would be three seconds for a 17-word message. If the participant closed the message within the first three seconds, it was assumed that they did not fully read the message, and the decision point was coded as “not engaged” (engagement = 0 [no]). This indicator was coded as NA for decision points that (a) were not assigned to prompting with a low-effort strategy; (b) were assigned to prompting with a low-effort strategy, but the participant did not engage with the initial notification to reveal the message; or (c) were not micro-randomized.

Engagement with the message recommending a more effortful self-regulatory strategy within the hour following micro-randomization was operationalized as a binary indicator. It differentiated between decision points where participants clicked either “Let’s go” or “Show all” ( = 1 [yes]) and those where they did not interact with the message ( = 0 [no]). This indicator was coded as NA for decision points that (a) were not assigned to prompting with a more effortful strategy; (b) were assigned to prompting with a more effortful strategy, but the participant did not engage with the initial notification to reveal the message; or (c) were not micro-randomized.

Engagement with the more effortful self-regulatory strategy within the hour following micro-randomization was operationalized as a binary indicator, differentiating between decision points where participants reached the end of the exercise ( = 1 [yes]) and those where they did not ( = 0 [no]). For two of the exercises, Joy and Meditate, there was a part where participants were required to spend a certain amount of time performing an activity before proceeding to the next page (e.g., listening to audio-only guidance, practicing meditation for a set duration; see Figs. S5, S6). Although participants could advance from this part before the designated time had passed, we only considered the exercise completed if they did not do so. This indicator was coded as NA for decision points that (a) were not assigned to a more effortful strategy, (b) were assigned to a more effortful strategy but the participant did not engage with the initial notification or the message recommending the strategy, or (c) were not micro-randomized.

Engagement with any self-regulatory strategy within the hour following micro-randomization was operationalized as a binary indicator differentiating between decision points where participants engaged with either the message describing a low-effort strategy or the more effortful strategy ( = 1 [yes]) and those where they did not engage with any strategy ( = 0 [no]). Because engagement with the low-effort strategy did not require app interactions and, therefore, could not be tracked through log data, this indicator used engagement with the message describing a low-effort strategy as a proxy for engagement with the low-effort strategy. Importantly, this indicator considered both strategies initiated by the prompt and those accessed independently through the app by the participants, and it was coded as 1 (yes) when participants engaged with the strategies through either means. This indicator was coded as NA for decision points that were not micro-randomized.

Note that several engagement indicators were quantified only when participants had engaged with the preceding step. Consequently, decision points where a participant lacked the opportunity to engage with a given stimulus/task because they did not engage with the previous step were excluded from analysis. An alternative approach would code engagement as “No” ( = 0) when participants did not have an opportunity to engage with a given stimulus/task because they did not engage with the previous step. However, this approach conflates two distinct scenarios: non-engagement due to the current stimulus/task not being shown (because the previous step was not completed) versus non-engagement due to participants ignoring or dismissing the stimulus/task after it was displayed. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses using this alternative coding scheme are reported in Supplementary Materials (Section A). Another set of sensitivity analyses with engagement coded as ordinal variables (i.e., each level of the ordinal variable represents a distinct step of engagement) is also included in Supplementary Materials (Section B). These sensitivity analyses produced substantively identical conclusions as in the main analyses.

EMA items

To measure stressful experiences, participants were asked, “Since the last assessment, in which of the following areas did you experience a stressful issue or problem?” with options including “Work/School,” “Family/other relationships,” “Financial,” “Housing/Where you live,” “Health,” and “None.” Participants could select multiple options. Selecting “None” automatically deselected all other options. Stressful experiences was coded as 1 (yes) if participants selected any of the stressful issues, and 0 (no) if they selected “None.”

To measure tobacco use, participants were asked, “Since the last assessment, did you use any of the following?” with options “None;” “Cigarettes;” “Vape pen, JUUL, or e-cigarettes;” “Cigars, cigarillos, or little cigars;” “Smokeless tobacco/snus/dissolvable tobacco;” “Marijuana/Cannabis;” and “Alcohol.” They could select multiple options. Selecting “None” automatically deselected other options. Tobacco use was coded as 1 (yes) if participants selected any of the following options: “Cigarettes;” “Vape pen, JUUL, or e-cigarettes;” “Cigars, cigarillos, or little cigars;” or “Smokeless tobacco/snus/dissolvable tobacco.” If none of these options were selected, it was coded as 0 (no).

Covariates

Several covariates were included in all analyses. They were collected in the baseline questionnaire. They included age, gender (male = 0, female = 1), race/ethnicity (non-Latino White Americans = 0, other racial or ethnic categories = 1), income (in increments of $10,000, from 1 to 11, with 1 corresponding to less than $10,000), partner status (1 = married or living with a significant other, 0 = other marital status), and the number of cigarettes smoked per day. Participants with missing covariate data (N = 10) were excluded from statistical analysis.

Analytic plan

Data were organized in a long format, with each row representing a decision point. Each participant thus had 60 rows of data (6 per day × 10 days = 60 decision points), with variables related to each decision point (e.g., answers to the brief survey, answers to the EMA, engagement indicators) included in each row.

Statistical analyses were conducted using generalized linear models estimated via generalized estimating equations (GEE)43, a common technique for parameter estimation in regression models for intensive longitudinal data. The specification of each regression model (i.e., the outcome and predictors used) is outlined below. A log-link function was used for binary outcomes, allowing results to be expressed on the “risk-ratio” scale. An independent working correlation matrix was used in order to avoid introducing unnecessary bias due to feedback between different times (heuristically, to avoid allowing future time-varying covariates from being used to predict past outcomes)56,57. Robust (“sandwich”) standard errors were used to account for within-person correlation58,59. Age, gender, race/ethnicity, income, partner status, and the number of cigarettes smoked per day were included as covariates for all models.

We first examined whether the likelihood of engagement decreased over the 10-day MRT, with time operationalized as the order of the decision point (ranging from 1 to 60). Separate models were fitted for each indicator of engagement, with each indicator regressed on time and all covariates.

We then analyzed whether the likelihood of engagement varied with attentional readiness, measured by whether participants started the brief survey (it was coded as 1 if they clicked “OK” on the “Time to answer 2 questions” prompt and 0 if they clicked “Cancel” or did not interact with the message). Decision points where the brief survey was not delivered were excluded. Separate models were fitted for each indicator of engagement, with engagement regressed on attentional readiness, all the covariates, and time in the study.

Next, we analyzed whether the likelihood of engagement varied with vulnerability, measured by two indicators: cigarette availability and negative emotion. Cigarette availability was coded as 1 if participants answered “yes” to “Cigarettes are available to me?” and 0 if they answered “no.” Negative emotion was coded as 1 if participants answered “yes” to “Are you experiencing a negative emotion?” and 0 if they answered “no.” Decision points where the brief survey was not delivered or unanswered were excluded from the analysis. Separate models were fitted for each indicator of engagement, with engagement regressed on the vulnerability indicators (cigarette availability or negative emotions), all the covariates, and time in the study.

We also tested whether engagement with any self-regulatory strategy moderated the association between stressful experiences and tobacco use. An indicator for whether participants engaged with any self-regulatory strategy in the hour before an EMA at time t (1 to 59) was used as a moderator of the relationship between stressful experiences and tobacco use self-reported in the next EMA at time t + 1 (ranging from 2 to 60). This lag ensured that the information about engagement with self-regulatory strategies preceded the reporting period for stressful experiences and tobacco use. Instances where an EMA was not completed at time t or time t + 1 were excluded from the analysis. Participants who completed fewer than three EMAs during the study were also excluded.

Tobacco use at time t + 1 was first regressed on stressful experiences at time t + 1 to test their association. Time (2 to 60), tobacco use at time t, and all covariates were included in this model. Next, engagement with any self-regulatory strategy at time t and the interaction term between engagement (time t) and stressful experiences (time t + 1) were added to the model to test engagement as a potential moderator.
