On the EPR paradox in systems with finite number of levels
Henryk Gzyl

Abstract
In this work we reexamine the EPR paradox for composite systems with a finite number of levels. The analysis emphasizes the connection between measurements and conditional probabilities. This connection implies, on the one hand, that when a measurement is performed, the new quantum state and the probability distribution becomes a function of the observable being measured. On the other hand, this becomes important when making predictions about the properties of the subsystems, since the predictions are implicitly a function of the observable that was measured. Systems with finitely many levels are simpler to describe because the analysis is not encumbered by the mathematical technicalities of the continuous case, and the underlying physical interpretations are the same.



Keywords
: EPR paradox, prediction, conditional probabilities as predictors, quantum systems with finitely many levels.

Introduction and preliminaries

It was 90 years ago that Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen published a paper,
[
2
]
. in which they examined the basic understanding of the probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics, of its philosophical interpretation, of what is reality, and what is means to measure and to predict.

In that work they presented and example that seemed to contradict our basic intuition and the foundations of quantum mechanics. The essence of the argument goes as follows. They consider a system made up of two sub-systems (let us call them particles), each of which can be characterized by its momentum, and such that the total momentum is conserved. For this it suffices to suppose that there are no external forces acting on the system. They consider the system to be in an entangled total state of known total momentum. Then they argue that if one measures the momentum of one of the particles, since the total momentum is given, the momentum of the other is known with certainty. But then they claim that this contradicts the uncertainty principle, because that means that one can measure the position of the second particle and record the variance of the measurements, which will be finite and violate the uncertainty principle, since its momentum is known with certainty.

In a previous work,
[
5
]
, I presented a solution to the apparent paradox, from two related points of view. On the one hand, there is the fact that the position operator of each of the particles, does not commute with the total momentum operator. Therefore, if the system is prepared in a state of known total momentum, the variance of each of the positions (as well as that of the center of mass of the system) has infinite variance.

On the other hand, the same issue appears in one uses conditional (quantum or classical) probabilities to make predictions. In this case, the best predictor, explicitly depends on the total momentum, which is known up to a given variance, and the conditional probability is a function of the total momentum, therefore it does not commute with the position operator of any of the particles. 0&-1\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;\;{\boldsymbol{B}}_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}\;0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;{\boldsymbol{C}}_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}.\;\;\;j=1,2.

(4.1)

These are recognizable as the Pauli spin matrices, except for the fact that we chose the units so that
[{\boldsymbol{A}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k}]=2i{\boldsymbol{C}}\delta_{j,k},
etc. Notice as well that
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{j}|\pm 1\rangle=\pm|\pm 1\rangle
when acting on the corresponding subsystem. Therefore, when considering
{\boldsymbol{S}}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}
acting on the composite system, its eigenvalues will be
\{2,0,-2\},
and the eigenvalue
0
has a two-dimensional eigenspace.

Suppose that we start from a system prepared in a state
\psi
(or
|\psi\rangle
when typographically convenient) specified as

\psi=a(1,1)|1,1\rangle+a(1,-1)|1,-1\rangle+a(-1,1)|-1,1\rangle+a(-1,-1)|-1,-1\rangle.

To prepare the system in an eigenstate of given total value of
{\boldsymbol{S}}
we form:

\Pi_{2}=|1,1\rangle\langle 1,1|,\;\;\;\Pi_{0}=|1,-1\rangle\langle 1,-1|+|-1,1\rangle\langle-1,1|,\;\;\;\Pi_{-2}=|-1,-1\rangle\langle-1,-1.

(4.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}=\frac{\Pi_{2}\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{tr(\Pi_{2}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)}}=\frac{a(1,1)}{|a(1,1)|}|1,1\rangle.\;\;

(4.3)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}=\frac{\Pi_{0}\psi}{\sqrt{tr(\Pi_{0}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)}}=\frac{a(1,-1)|1,-1\rangle+a(-1,1)|-1,1\rangle}{\sqrt{|a(1,-1)|^{2}+|a(-1,1)|^{2}}}\;\;

\displaystyle=\gamma(1,-1)|1,-1\rangle+\gamma(-1,1)|-1,1\rangle.\;\;\;

(4.4)

\displaystyle\psi_{-2}=\frac{\Pi_{-2}\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{tr(\Pi_{-2}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)}}=\frac{a(-1,-1)}{|a(1,1)|}|-1,-1\rangle.\;\;

(4.5)

The identifications in the second identity should be clear. Each of the eigenvalues (and their corresponding eigenstates) will occur with probability

|a(1,1)|^{2},

\,|a(1,-1)|^{2}+|a(-1,1)|^{2}
and
|a(1,1)|^{2}
respectively. The analogue of the case considered by EPR corresponds to the value
{\boldsymbol{S}}=0.
Let us examine what happens to the predictions of
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i}
and those of
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{i}
in the state
\psi_{0}.

A simple computation yields:

\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\psi_{0}\rangle=|\gamma(1,-1)|^{2}-|\gamma(1,-1)|^{2}\equiv\alpha_{1}

(4.6)

Clearly,
-1\leq\alpha_{1}\leq 1.
At this point we stress the difference between experiment, measurement and prediction. An experiment is a sequence of measurements made on a collection of identically prepared systems. In our case, the measurements of
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}
on
\psi_{0}
yield vales
+1
and
-1,
with probabilities (asymptotically) equal to
|\gamma(1,-1)|^{2}
and
|\gamma(-1,1)|^{2}
respectively. The number in (
4.6
) is what the experimenter reports after averaging over all measurements. This number is what theory predicts if the state
\psi_{0}
is known and
\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\psi_{0}\rangle
is computed instead of being measured, and the quantity reported in (
4.7
) is to be thought as the experimental error or the prediction error depending on the interpretation of (
4.6
).

Since
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}^{2}={\mathbb{I}},
and therefore
({\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{1}{\mathbb{I}})^{2}=({\mathbb{I}}-2\alpha_{1}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}+\alpha_{1}^{2})
we conclude that:

\langle\psi_{0},({\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{1}{\mathbb{I}})^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=(1-\alpha_{1}^{2})\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\Delta_{\psi_{0}}({\boldsymbol{A}}_{1})=\sqrt{(1-\alpha_{1}^{2})}.

(4.7)

A similar computation yields the following result, which also follows from Theorem

3.1
, and is intuitive because of the symmetry with respect to the exchange of the particles, and the fact that
\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{S}}\psi_{0}\rangle=0
:

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=-\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\psi_{0}\rangle=-\alpha_{1}.

\displaystyle\Delta_{\psi_{0}}({\boldsymbol{A}}_{2})=\sqrt{(1-\alpha_{1}^{2})}.

It is also simple to verify that:

\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=0.

(4.8)

Again, since
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{i}=I,
using (
4.8
) we obtain that:

\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{i}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=1\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\Delta_{\psi_{0}}({\boldsymbol{B}}_{i})=1.

(4.9)

And to finish, another simple computation yields that:

\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{1}\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle\psi_{0},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=0.

(4.10)

Therefore we have:

\Delta_{\psi_{0}}({\boldsymbol{A}}_{i})\Delta_{\psi_{0}}({\boldsymbol{B}}_{i})=\sqrt{(1-\alpha_{1}^{2})}>\frac{1}{2}|tr({\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|)|=0.

(4.11)

What this result asserts is that if the prediction, or measurement of
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}
involves a zero prediction error, there would be no contradiction to (
1.6
).

Suppose that we decide to measure
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}
in the state
\psi_{0}.
After repeated measurements, on would observe that
+1
happens with probability
|\gamma(1,-1)|^{2}
and
-1
with probability
|\gamma(-1,1)|^{2}.
Anyway, if the measurement yields
+1,
we are left with the system in the state:

\phi_{1}=\frac{\Pi_{1}\psi_{0}}{tr(\Pi_{1}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|)}=\frac{\gamma(1,-1)}{|\gamma(1,-1)|}|1,-1\rangle.

(4.12)

Note that
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\phi_{1}=\phi_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}\phi_{0}=-\phi_{0}.
Not only that,
\Delta_{\phi_{0}}({\boldsymbol{A}}_{i})=0
for
i=1,2.
As noted in Theorem
1.1
, in this case
\langle\phi_{0},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}\phi_{0}\rangle=0,
and thus no contradiction with the uncertainty relation (
1.6
) arises. Nevertheless, in this example
\langle\phi_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{i}\phi_{0}\rangle=0
as well. But since (
4.10
) is in force, the objections raised by EPR are not valid in this case, and there are no spooky actions at a distance.

5
Concluding remarks

Another detail in the analysis by EPR is the following. In order to have a state
\psi_{s}
of known total
{\boldsymbol{S}},
one must measure it. And even though it is true that, in then we measure
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}
and end up in a state
\phi_{a},
then the value of
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}
is known with the same certainty (or uncertainty), but that does not mean that (
1.6
) is contradicted. First, because of Theorem
3.1
, there is an inherited uncertainty in
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2},
and second, even if the prediction of
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}
had no uncertainty, the right hand side of (
1.6
) vanishes and no contradiction arises. Also, keep in mind that since
[{\boldsymbol{S}},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}]=[{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}],
there is already an uncertainty about
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}
built in from the outset.

The other issue worth mentioning is that the predictions after a measurement is made, are conditional predictions (predictions made with conditional probabilities), and if we were interested in unconditional predictions, we have to average with respect to the original probability distribution of the measured variable.
