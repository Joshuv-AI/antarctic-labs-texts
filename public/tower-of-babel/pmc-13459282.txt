Environmental stress responses and climate vulnerability of Abies beshanzuensis: combined physiological and transcriptomic analysis
Authors: Hongfei Lu, Xiaorong Chen, Yanyun Xiong, Zhen Pang, Yiqing Wu, Minna Yu, Xinyi Huang, Shenao Wang, Mingjian Yu, Yougui Wu, Likang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abies beshanzuensis M. H. Wu is a critically endangered conifer endemic to cool, high-elevation forests; its narrow range renders it vulnerable to climate warming and associated heat and water stress. We simulated climate warming by growing seedlings along an elevational gradient (500–1200 m) to assess physiological and molecular stress responses. Seedlings at mid-high elevation (1000–1200 m) grew optimally, whereas low-elevation (500 m) seedlings showed stress symptoms. At 500 m, Yield and ETR declined sharply; stomatal conductance also dropped, and NPQ collapsed entirely, dropping below the 1550 m baseline, proving quantitatively insufficient to prevent massive photoinhibition. Consequently, leaf chlorophyll content and biomass peaked at 1000 m and declined precipitously toward 500 m. Low-elevation leaves accumulated significantly higher levels of proline, soluble sugars, and proteins. However, this robust osmotic adjustment was physically insufficient to counteract the severe drought and heat stress, culminating in elevated MDA content and severe cellular damage. Antioxidant enzyme activities were highest at 1000 m and decreased at lower elevations. Transcriptome profiling and analysis of DEGs revealed altitude-dependent gene regulation. At 500 m, structural hub genes driving oxidative phosphorylation, glutathione metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis (CSF7, MDMC, CHS), circadian rhythms, and heat-shock proteins were strongly upregulated. In contrast, genes for photosystems, primary hormone signaling, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, and pathogen defense were suppressed. Simulated warming induced pronounced stress responses, with photosynthesis and growth severely compromised, overwhelming osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defenses. These findings underscore the extreme climate vulnerability of A. beshanzuensis, establish 1000 m as a lower survival threshold, and highlight the urgent need to protect its high-elevation habitat and implement assisted conservation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09067-8.

Introduction

Climate change is exerting unprecedented pressure on high-altitude ecosystems and their endemic species [1, 2]. Coniferous forests generally thrived in colder and drier environments [3]. Warming temperatures and altered precipitation patterns threaten cold-adapted conifers, especially rare and relict species, which may be unable to migrate or acclimate quickly [4, 5]. Abies beshanzuensis M. H. Wu is the rarest coniferous species in the Baishanzu Nature Reserve, Zhejiang Province, eastern China, with only three wild individuals remaining [6]. It was classified as Critically Endangered (CR) by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) (https://threatenedconifers.rbge.org.uk). Previous studies demonstrated that in situ and ex situ conservation were essential for expanding the distribution of extremely small wild plant populations and preserving their germplasm resources [7]. A. beshanzuensis requires cold, humid conditions and is exquisitely sensitive to heat and drought. Understanding how environmental stressors, particularly increased temperature and reduced soil moisture, affect its fundamental physiology and survival is crucial for conservation under climate change.

Altitude gradients serve as natural experiments for climate change impacts, with lower elevations experiencing warmer, drier conditions while higher elevations are cooler and wetter. Altitude influences light intensity, temperature, moisture, atmospheric pressure, and soil nutrients, all of which shape growth and metabolism [8]. Central to these adaptations is the plant response to cold stress, which induces beneficial changes in photosynthetic capacity, antioxidant defenses, membrane lipid composition, and osmotic regulation to ensure survival [9, 10]. Key metabolites such as proline, soluble sugars, and soluble proteins increased along altitudinal gradients, offering protective functions for cells [11]. The antioxidant system served as a critical defense barrier in plants, removing excess ROS and mitigating lipid peroxidation damage to the cell membrane system [12–14]. Plant hormones regulated physiological activities and enhanced tolerance to environmental variations [15]. Furthermore,under abiotic stress, cellular structures exhibited significant plasticity, preserving the integrity of organelles and membrane structures [16].

Recent advances in transcriptomics revealed molecular mechanisms underlying plant responses to altitudinal gradients. Energy metabolism and defense signaling pathways influenced plant responses to altitude [17, 18]. Higher altitudes significantly downregulated genes linked to photosynthesis, chlorophyll content, and light-harvesting activity in plants [19–22], while upregulating flavonoid-related genes to aid adaptation to extreme weather [23]. Ribosomal protein and succinate dehydrogenase genes enhanced plant adaptation to higher altitudes [22]. However, A. beshanzuensis has been minimally studied, and the molecular basis of its extreme vulnerability remains largely unknown.

Here, we examine the combined physiological and transcriptomic responses of A. beshanzuensis seedlings across an altitudinal gradient. By transplanting seedlings to sites from 500 m up to 1550 m, we assess how thermal and hydric stress at lower altitudes affect morphology, photosynthesis, nutrient content, osmotic balance, and antioxidant defenses. We also profile gene expression to identify hub genes and pathways activated or suppressed under stress. Our goals are to identify the species lower survival limit, elucidate the mechanisms underlying its sensitivity to warming, and inform conservation strategies. Linking physiology to ecology, our study provides mechanistic insights into how climate change threatens A. beshanzuensis and other alpine conifers.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

In this study, Fifty 2018-germinated Abies beshanzuensis seedlings with a uniform genetic background, cultivated in Baishanzu National Park, were used as experimental materials. All seeds were collected from a single grafted parental tree at the Baishanzu Management Station. The scions of this parental tree, along with five other grafted A. beshanzuensis trees in the same cultivation area, were all derived from the same original maternal tree and grafted onto Abies firma rootstocks. In early February 2022, the 50 seedlings were randomly divided into five groups of ten. The seedlings were transplanted across an elevational gradient spanning five established plots: 500 m, 700 m, 1000 m, 1200 m, and 1550 m (Table S1). The 1550 m plot represents the species native, cool-humid microclimate and was formally designated as the experimental Control group against which all warming simulations were compared. At the end of August in 2023, samples were collected from each altitude plot between 9:00 and 11:00 AM on sunny days. From each plot, five A. beshanzuensis seedlings exhibiting similar growth status were selected. Annual leaves were collected from the same parts of the selected seedlings, combined, wrapped in aluminum foil, labeled, and immediately frozen in a liquid nitrogen tank. The samples were transported to the laboratory and stored at −80°C for subsequent physiological and transcriptomic analyses, with three replicates per treatment.

Microscopy and image analysis

Plant specimens were fixed in a 70% formalin-acetic acid-alcohol (FAA). Standard paraffin embedding was achieved by dehydrating the samples through an ethanol series, clearing them, infiltrating them with molten paraffin, and sectioning them at 10 µm thickness [24]. Sections were stained with safranin O and fast green for general anatomy [25]. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was used to detect carbohydrates (starch, glycoproteins). Images were captured under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100) at 4 ×, 10 ×, and 20 × magnifications. Cell and tissue dimensions were measured using ImageJ (v1.8). RGB color values of phloem and xylem tissues were extracted from images using Adobe Photoshop. Average R, G, B values were computed in Python 3.11.

Organic elemental composition and calorific value

Dry biomass of stems and leaves from each altitude was ground into fine powder. Carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) contents were measured using an elemental analyzer (Elementar Vario EL III, Hanau, Germany) following manufacturer protocols. The calorific value of powdered samples was determined by bomb calorimetry as described by Dhaundiyal et al. [26]. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Chlorophyll fluorescence and photosynthetic parameters

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were measured on dark-adapted needles using a Mini-PAM fluorometer (Walz, Germany) and WinControl software. After 30 min dark adaptation, actinic light was applied to determine the steady-state effective quantum yield of PSII (Yield), electron transport rate (ETR), photochemical quenching (qP), and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) under a range of light intensities. Gas-exchange measurements were made on the same plants between 9:00–11:00 AM under ambient CO₂ using a LI-6400 portable photosynthesis system (Walz, Germany). The following parameters were recorded: net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr). Data for each altitude were obtained from 3 replicates of leaves. Osmolytes and stress markers

The proline content (µg/mL) was determined using the method described by Xu et al. [29]. 0.1 g leaf sample was homogenized in 1 mL of extraction solution, centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The absorbance was measured at 520 nm.

The soluble sugar content (µg/mL) was determined using the phenol–sulfuric acid method described by Yan et al. [30]. 0.5 g sample of A. beshanzuensis leaves was homogenized with water at a 1:25 ratio. The homogenized mixture was subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 1 h. Next, 1 ml of the leaf extract was mixed with 5 mL of anthracene sulfate reagent, containing 1 g of anthracene per liter of 80% sulfuric acid solution. The mixture was heated in a water bath for 10 min, then cooled to room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 620 nm using a UV spectrophotometer.

The soluble protein content was determined using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method [31]. 0.5 g leaf sample was homogenized with water at a 1:25 ratio. The homogenized mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C at 10,000 × g for 10 min and stored for further analysis. 0.1 mL aliquot of A. beshanzuensis leaf extract was mixed with 4.9 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 solution, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm.

[32]. 0.1 g leaf sample was homogenized in 1 mL of extraction solution on ice, centrifuged at 8,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Absorbance at 532 nm and 600 nm was measured with a blank control, and ΔA532 and ΔA600 were calculated as the differences between sample and blank absorbance values. RNA extraction, sequencing, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen leaf samples (all altitudes) using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA integrity and purity were checked on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. High-quality RNA from three biological replicates per altitude (12 libraries total) was sent to LC Sciences (USA) for Illumina paired-end sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were filtered to remove adaptors, low-quality bases, and ambiguous reads (CutAdapt). Clean reads were assembled de novo with Trinity (v2.4.0), and the longest transcript per gene (unigene) was retained. Transcript abundance was estimated as TPM (Transcripts Per Million) using Salmon software. R package DESeq2 was used to identify statistically significant results. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by DESeq2 based on an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|≥ 1. DEGs were annotated to Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways for functional enrichment analysis (http://www.geneontology.org; http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). The raw transcriptome data were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus at NCBI under accession number PRJNA1064174.

RT-qPCR validation, cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China). Six DEGs common to all altitude comparisons were selected. Primers (Table S2) were designed in Primer Premier 6.0 and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai). Pinus massoniana Actin [33] was used as reference gene. qRT-PCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 system (Thermo Fisher, USA) with SYBR Green chemistry. Relative expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each reaction had three biological replicates with three technical replicates each.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in SPSS v21.0. One-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was used to compare altitudes. A probability of P < 0.05 was considered significant. Results are presented as means ± standard deviation. Correlations among physiological traits, biomass, pigment content, and other parameters were examined using Pearson correlation. A correlation network (Partial Mantel tests) was generated to visualize relationships.

Results

Seedling growth and morphology

Seedlings showed clear morphological responses to altitude. At 500 m, foliage appeared chlorotic and crowns were sparse. While seedlings across the mid-high elevation range (1000–1200 m) demonstrated high viability, 1000 m emerged unequivocally as the absolute physiological optimum. At the highest site (1550 m), crowns were more compact and needles slightly yellowed, likely reflecting cold stress and high irradiance (Fig. 1). Seedlings at 1000 m significantly outperformed those at both 1200 m and the 1550 m Control across critical morphological indicators. Specifically, branch length (130.0 ± 5.1 cm) and stem diameter (2.86 ± 0.08 cm) peaked at 1000 m. Photosynthesis and fluorescence

Chlorophyll fluorescence revealed that lower altitudes reduced photochemical efficiency. Both Yield and ETR were significantly lower at 500 m than at all higher altitudes (Fig. 5A-B). qP declined at 500 m as well, indicating less open PSII reaction centers (Fig. 5C). In healthy plants, NPQ dissipates excess light as heat. NPQ was significantly higher and highly functional at mid-high altitudes (700–1550 m), but dropped markedly below the 1550 m Control baseline at 500 m (Fig. 5D). Osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defenses

Leaf osmolytes were strongly affected by altitude. Proline, soluble sugars, and soluble proteins all increased as altitude decreased, reaching significant peaks at 500 m (Fig. 8A-C). This pattern indicates that A. beshanzuensis seedlings experienced greater osmotic stress at lower altitudes and responded by accumulating compatible solutes to maintain cell turgor and protect proteins.Fig. 8Contents of proline (A), soluble sugar (B) and soluble protein (C) in leaves of A. beshanzuensis at different altitudes

Oxidative stress markers also varied. MDA content rose steadily from 1550 m down to 700 m, peaking at 700 m, then unexpectedly dropped at 500 m (Fig. 9A). The high MDA suggests severe membrane lipid peroxidation, whereas the anomalous drop at 500 m reflects severe tissue necrosis impairing MDA synthesis. Antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, POD) initially increased as altitude decreased, reaching maxima at 1000 m, then declined at the lowest altitudes (Fig. 9B-D). SOD and CAT were significantly higher at 1000 m than at any other site. POD showed a similar hump-shaped trend. These results imply that moderate warming (to 1000 m) stimulated ROS scavenging activity, but further stress at 500–700 m overwhelmed the system, leading to enzyme inactivation or cellular collapse. Transcriptome profiling across altitudes

To investigate transcriptional differences in A. baishanzuensis at different altitudes, total RNA was extracted from leaves at various altitudes for transcriptome analysis. Twelve cDNA libraries were constructed. After removing adapter contamination, low-quality bases, and undetermined bases, the valid data ranged from 5.40 GB to 6.66 GB, accounting for 97.09% to 98.22% of the total data. Q20 content exceeded 97%, Q30 content surpassed 93%, and GC content was above 45% in all samples (Table S5). Expression measurements of the three biological replicates for each library showed strong correlation, with Pearson correlation coefficients above 0.877 (Fig. S1A), indicating high dataset consistency.

DEGs were identified through pairwise comparisons of the three altitude transplant groups. In the 500 m vs 1000 m comparison, 1242 genes were upregulated, and 2577 genes were downregulated. In the 500 m vs 1550 m comparison, 1991 genes were upregulated, and 3126 were downregulated. In the 1000 m vs 1550 m comparison, 426 genes were upregulated, and 584 were downregulated (Fig. S1B-D). Volcano plots of DEGs in the leaves of A. baishanzuensis seedlings across altitudes demonstrated fold changes and statistical reliability, highlighting significant transcriptional changes caused by transplantation to different altitudes.

To investigate the biological functions of DEGs at different altitudes, GO enrichment analysis was conducted on DEGs from pairwise comparisons of altitude treatment groups (Fig. 10A-C). Discussion

As an endangered species, understanding the response mechanisms of A. beshanzuensis was critical. This study analyzed physiological and biochemical indicators and transcriptome data of A. beshanzuensis across six altitudes. Significant differences were observed in physiological and biochemical indicators and in the expression levels of genes related to photosynthesis, plant hormone signaling, and antioxidant activity.

Carbon and nitrogen influenced various physiological metabolic activities in plants [34]. In this study, C% in the leaves and branches of A. beshanzuensis at different altitudes ranged from 46 to 58%, significantly exceeding N%, H% and S% content. Carbon was identified as the most essential element in plant dry matter, comprising approximately 50% and forming the foundation of the organic structure. N% initially increased, then decreased, and subsequently increased again with altitude changes. At lower altitudes, nitrogen accumulation in leaves and branches may have resulted from enhanced nitrogen distribution. In contrast, at higher altitudes, increased precipitation and humidity likely reduced soil nitrogen availability, limiting nitrogen absorption [35]. Previous studies confirmed a linear relationship between nitrogen content and calorific value [36]. Both N% and calorific value peaked at 1000 m, indicating optimal energy accumulation at this altitude.

Stress conditions often severely damaged plant photosynthetic organs, indicated by reduced variable fluorescence parameters such as Yield and ETR. Photosynthetic efficiency generally decreased under stress, accompanied by reduced qP and increased NPQ of chlorophyll fluorescence [37]. In this study, declining altitude significantly reduced Yield, qP, and ETR, suggesting damage to the PSII reaction center under environmental stress. This damage diminished the light-harvesting capacity of antenna pigments, reduced light energy absorption by leaves, and lowered photochemical efficiency. Photosynthetic electron transmission pathways were impaired, causing an accumulation of excess light energy. When excess energy was not promptly utilized for photochemical reactions, photoinhibition occurred. To prevent PSII damage from photoinhibition, plants increased thermal dissipation pathways to manage excess light energy, reflected by elevated NPQ [38]. At 500 m, however, NPQ sharply declined, indicating severe photosynthetic system damage in A. beshanzuensis, which could no longer dissipate excess energy effectively. Pn and Gs decreased with altitude reduction. This decline was likely due to increased temperatures at lower altitudes, a phenomenon supported by previous studies [39, 40]. These findings suggested that higher growth temperatures limited photosynthetic rates through reduced Gs. In contrast, Tr increased at lower altitudes, potentially reflecting a response to higher temperatures, with increased leaf transpiration aiding cooling [41, 42]. At 1200 m and 1550 m, the light saturation point is significantly higher than that at lower altitudes, indicating that plants at these elevations require stronger light intensities to achieve their maximum photosynthetic rate. Moreover, the light compensation point is lowest at 1550 m, suggesting that plants at this altitude can maintain a favorable balance between photosynthesis and respiration even under lower light conditions. In this study, genes associated with photosynthesis, including PSA, PET, PSB, and PPD genes, were significantly downregulated at 500 m compared to 1550 m. These results demonstrated that A. beshanzuensis could not sustain normal photosynthetic activity at low altitudes, which adversely affected its growth.

Chlorophyll biosynthesis and degradation were significantly influenced by various environmental signals [43, 44]. In this study, the contents of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and the chlorophyll a/b ratio in the leaves of A. beshanzuensis seedlings decreased with altitude reduction. This decline was likely caused by high temperatures and low humidity at lower altitudes, which inhibited chlorophyll synthesis and accelerated chlorophyll degradation [45]. Reduced chlorophyll content at high altitudes may have indirectly indicated oxidative stress in plants, triggered by ROS. ROS accumulation inhibited chlorophyll synthesis by suppressing biosynthetic enzymes or damaging mesophyll chloroplasts. This damage reduced the number of chloroplasts, increased their degradation, and decreased photosynthetic rates [46].

Under abiotic stress, plants maintained cellular homeostasis and enhanced free radical scavenging by increasing osmotic regulatory substances [47, 48]. In this study, proline, soluble sugar, and soluble protein levels in the leaves of A. beshanzuensis significantly increased with decreasing altitude. This response likely resulted from heightened environmental stress, as the plant adjusted its osmotic balance to sustain cellular homeostasis [48]. At lower altitudes, alpine plants often experienced greater abiotic stress, including higher temperatures and drought, which led to ROS accumulation, cellular damage, and lipid peroxidation in membranes [49]. While this massive accumulation of proline represents a frantic attempt to establish osmoprotection and scavenge ROS, the concurrent severe degradation of photosynthetic pathways (Yield and ETR) at 500 m indicates that this proline surge is physiologically insufficient to counteract the extreme drought and thermal stress. The stress levels at 500 m definitively breached the species physiological limits. This terminal failure is further validated by the dynamic changes in MDA content. MDA levels rose steadily as altitude decreased to 700 m. However, at 500 m, MDA levels were significantly lower than at higher altitudes, reflecting cellular and membrane damage so absolute that normal lipid substrates required for MDA synthesis were destroyed.

Additionally, increased activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT and POD likely mitigated oxidative damage, a common plant response to environmental stress [50]. From 1550 to 1000 m, the activities of these enzymes exhibited varying increases, suggesting that the decrease in altitude imposed environmental stresses, such as elevated temperatures and reduced humidity, which may have led to the accumulation of ROS in A. beshanzuensis. Substrate accumulation induced the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes, increasing their activity to counteract ROS-induced damage [51]. However, below 1000 m, antioxidant enzyme activity decreased due to cellular damage, limiting the plant ability to eliminate ROS effectively. This caused irreversible damage to A. beshanzuensis seedlings and may have led to their death. At 500 m, transcription levels of antioxidant and energy-salvage hub genes, specifically APX, GST, SDH, COX, and ND, were significantly upregulated compared to higher altitudes. The regulatory relationship driving the upregulation of ND, SDH, and COX acts as a vital compensatory response to the collapse of the photosynthetic apparatus [52].

Environmental stress induced transcriptional changes in plants. To elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying A. beshanzuensis adaptation to altitude changes, transcriptome analyses were conducted, revealing activation of multiple stress response pathways. At lower altitudes, structural hub genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway (CSF7, MDMC, and CHS) were globally upregulated to synthesize protective non-enzymatic antioxidants. Flavonoids enhanced plant tolerance to abiotic stress [53–55]. For instance, the FLB gene promoted flavonoid accumulation, increasing salt tolerance [56], while NtMYB4 negatively regulated flavonoid accumulation, thereby reducing ROS clearance and stress tolerance [57]. The expression of these flavonoid hub genes is tightly controlled by upstream MBW complex regulators (interacting MYB, bHLH, and WD40 transcription factors), which effectively sequestered the plant's remaining carbon resources to synthesize protective flavonoids. Conversely, Reduced altitude downregulated transcription levels of genes associated with the MAPK signaling pathway. Previous studies have reported that cold stress upregulated genes like NPK1, MYC, EIN3, and SCRM, enhancing plant cold tolerance [58–60]. Concurrently, multiple metabolic and biosynthetic pathways downstream of transcription factors involved in plant hormone signaling altered plant physiology to enhance resistance or tolerance to cold stress [61]. WRKY transcription factors positively regulated abiotic stress responses, such as drought and cold stress [62]. Our results confirmed this; under high-altitude conditions, genes related to plant hormone signaling and plant-pathogen interactions, including IAA26 (growth hormone), BEH4 (BES1/BZR1 homolog protein), ARR3 (response regulator), ARF18 (auxin response factor), and WRKY28 (WRKY DNA-binding protein), were significantly upregulated. However, the deep suppression of primary growth-promoting hormone pathways at 500 m confirms that normal development was entirely arrested. Despite the robust, coordinated activation of these highly connected genetic hubs, the physical reality of the breached temperature threshold meant these molecular defenses were ultimately insufficient to prevent systemic physiological collapse, confirming 1000 m as the species absolute lower survival limit.

HSPs were known to assist in protein folding and protect essential cellular functions in plants. Our results indicated elevated transcription levels of HSP90-1 and HSP82 in plants at 500 m, reflecting a general response to increased temperatures and a strategy to mitigate environmental damage [63, 64]. Many fundamental biological processes in plants were regulated by the biological clock. Similar to stress response genes, the transcription of ROS-related genes was also controlled by circadian rhythms [65]. Our results showed that genes related to circadian rhythms and plant stress were significantly upregulated. This suggested that the circadian clock helped balance growth, metabolism, and defense in A. beshanzuensis, thereby supporting short-term development and reproduction.

Our findings have clear implications for conservation and climate adaptation. The pronounced stress responses of A. beshanzuensis at low altitude indicate that warming and drought could push this species beyond its physiological limits. We identified 1000 m as the probable lower elevation survival threshold; below this, seedlings suffered irreversible damage. As regional temperatures rise, lower-elevation habitats will become inhospitable. This means the species’ suitable range could contract upslope, reducing available habitat and genetic connectivity. Conservation strategies should thus prioritize protecting and expanding high-altitude refuge areas. Assisted migration of seedlings to similar cool, moist microclimates might be considered, but only if those sites remain stable under climate projections.

Conclusion

baishanzuensis seedlings exhibited pronounced morphological differences across altitudinal gradients. Seedlings at 1000 m showed maximal branch length, stem diameter, leaf size, and biomass accumulation. Yield, ETR, qP, Pn, Tr, Gs, and chlorophyll contents all decreased severely as altitude decreased to 500 m. Although proline, soluble sugar, and soluble protein contents increased as altitude decreased, this was insufficient to prevent terminal stress. MDA content significantly increased from 1550 to 700 m, then anomalously dropped at 500 m due to necrosis. The activities of SOD, CAT and POD initially increased and then decreased as altitude declined, peaking at 1000 m. Transcription levels of genes involved in photosynthesis and hormone signal transduction decreased at lower altitudes, while defense systems involving oxidative phosphorylation hub genes, flavonoid biosynthesis hub genes, and heat shock proteins were heavily upregulated in a terminal survival bid. Extreme low altitudes negatively impacted the survival of A. beshanzuensis, establishing 1000 m as the species lower physiological limit.
