Development and validation of a sensor-integrated smart driving test system for automated, scalable, and objective driver performance evaluation
Authors: Eman S. Sabry, Kareem E. Wagdy, Yahya M. Elkholy, Mohamed A. Farid, Farouk M. Domdom, Mohamed A. Mohamed, Ahmed A. Abdelkhalik, Samah Alshathri, Walid El-Shafai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evaluating driver proficiency remains a fundamental determinant of road safety, regulatory adherence, and public confidence in licensing systems. This paper presents the Smart Driving Test System (SDTS), a fully integrated, sensor-based, microcontroller-driven framework designed to automate and standardize the assessment of driving skills. A functional prototype demonstrates SDTS capability to replace subjective human judgment with objective, repeatable, and data-centric performance metrics. The work includes a comprehensive feasibility analysis encompassing technological maturity, economic viability, operational deployment, and legal compliance, further supported by a detailed cost estimation model and implementation strategy. A comparative assessment with established international smart driving evaluation platforms underscores SDTS’s distinctive contributions in communication architecture, modular hardware configuration, graphical user interface functionalities, and scalable system design. The paper also outlines prospective enhancements, including the incorporation of machine learning for predictive performance analysis, adoption of low-power wireless communication protocols such as ZigBee and LoRa to simplify installation, utilization of cloud-based analytics for centralized monitoring, and employment of multimodal sensor fusion to improve environmental perception. By bridging the gap between conventional manual testing procedures and next-generation automated evaluation, SDTS significantly enhances the transparency, consistency, and reliability of driver certification processes. Furthermore, it lays the groundwork for the digital transformation of transportation licensing infrastructure and aligns with broader advancements toward intelligent transportation ecosystems, promoting safer roads, harmonized assessment standards, and strengthened public trust in driver qualification frameworks.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of transportation systems, coupled with the increasing demand for road safety, has intensified the need for innovative approaches to driver assessment and licensing. Conventional driving test procedures, while widely used, often fall short in delivering objective, standardized, and scalable evaluations, limitations that have significant implications for public safety and operational efficiency. This paper addresses these challenges by presenting the design, implementation, and validation of a SDTS, a sensor-driven, automated framework aimed at enhancing the fairness, accuracy, and scalability of driver proficiency testing. To build a comprehensive foundation for the proposed solution, the Introduction is structured into four subsections: the Overview, which provides background context and the motivation for system modernization; the Problem Statement and Motivations, which identifies current challenges and justifies the need for innovation; the List of Contributions, which outlines the specific advancements introduced in this work; and the Paper Structure, which summarizes the organization of the remaining sections.

Overview

In the contemporary era of rapid technological advancement and increasing vehicular density, road safety has emerged as a critical global priority, with governments and transportation authorities seeking to minimize accident rates and improve driving standards. A pivotal factor in achieving this objective is the accuracy and reliability of driver proficiency assessment, which directly influences the quality of licensed drivers on public roads. However, conventional driving test methodologies, rooted in manual observation and subjective scoring by human examiners, are inherently limited in their capacity to deliver consistent and unbiased evaluations. These methods are vulnerable to human error, examiner bias, and non-standardized criteria, all of which can undermine fairness and diminish public trust in licensing systems. Moreover, the labor-intensive nature of manual testing restricts scalability, posing operational challenges in regions with high testing demand. Collectively, these shortcomings impede the effectiveness of licensing procedures, posing risks to both road safety and the sustained competence of newly licensed drivers. Recent transport safety analyses highlight that non-standardized evaluation practices often produce inconsistent licensing outcomes with tangible safety consequences1–3.

Advances in sensing technologies, embedded systems, and intelligent transportation platforms have created opportunities for shifting from subjective, examiner-dependent evaluations toward automated and data-driven frameworks. Recent research has explored multi-sensor driver assessment systems, AI-assisted scoring, and human–machine collaborative testing mechanisms4,5. However, many of these systems still face limitations caused by non-deterministic wireless communication, limited sensor precision, and the absence of industrial-grade design considerations. This growing gap between traditional assessment approaches and the evolving demands of modern transportation underscores the urgent need for automated, objective, and scalable evaluation frameworks that can standardize testing, improve transparency, and support sustainable public safety objectives.

To address these limitations, this research introduces the Smart Driving Test System (SDTS), an intelligent, sensor-integrated platform designed to automate and standardize driver evaluation procedures. By merging advanced hardware sensing modules with a robust software control environment, the SDTS provides objective, data-driven, and real-time assessments that minimize human intervention, reduce subjectivity, and enhance operational reliability. The system’s architecture is engineered to ensure consistency in test execution, irrespective of examiner or location, thereby promoting fairness and transparency in the licensing process.

In contrast to existing automated or semi-automated systems, which often suffer from limited sensor precision, fragile wireless links, and restricted scalability, SDTS introduces a more industrial-grade approach by combining deterministic embedded firmware, long-range wired communication, and distributed multi-sensor fusion. This distinction clarifies the technological gap addressed in this work and highlights the specific advancements that elevate SDTS beyond current solutions.

At the core of the SDTS is a user-centric software application, developed in Visual Basic, which serves as the primary administrative interface for test operators. This software facilitates dynamic traffic light control, continuous monitoring of sensor inputs, and automated execution of test scenarios. It allows administrators to configure test parameters, process incoming performance data, and generate comprehensive evaluation reports without manual intervention. By employing long-range, noise-immune RS485 communication, deterministic embedded firmware, and multi-sensor fusion, the system ensures stable operation even in electrically noisy or large test-yard environments. By streamlining these operations into a cohesive, automated workflow, the SDTS not only reduces administrative workload but also establishes a transparent, repeatable, and auditable assessment process, critical for maintaining public trust and meeting regulatory compliance in modern driver licensing systems.

Complementing the software platform is a robust hardware infrastructure comprising distributed sensing and control units strategically deployed throughout the testing environment. These units integrate shock sensors, infrared (IR) transmitters and receivers, and automated traffic signal modules, all coordinated through a centralized Main Control Room (MCR) PC. Communication across the network is facilitated via the RS485 serial protocol, selected for its proven long-distance transmission capabilities, high noise immunity, and suitability for industrial-grade data communication. The adoption of a daisy-chain topology enables modular scalability, allowing additional sensing units or control modules to be seamlessly integrated, thereby supporting flexible reconfiguration for varied driving test layouts and site-specific requirements.

This hardware–communication design specifically overcomes limitations observed in earlier systems that rely on short-range RF modules or ad-hoc wiring, both of which introduce latency, signal drops, or synchronization errors. The SDTS instead adopts a deterministic, electrically robust communication backbone designed for harsh operational settings.

The hardware design process was executed using Proteus for schematic capture and PCB layout, with a focus on spatial efficiency, signal integrity, and manufacturing feasibility. To ensure operational reliability in field conditions, sensor modules were encased in custom-engineered protective housings that safeguard against environmental factors such as dust, moisture, and mechanical impact. Additionally, a scaled driving yard prototype was constructed to replicate real-world test conditions, providing a controlled environment for sensor calibration, system debugging, and scenario validation prior to full-scale deployment.

The firmware governing the SDTS was developed in Assembly language, selected for its ability to deliver fast, deterministic, and low-overhead control of sensors and actuators, capabilities essential for real-time, safety-critical assessment environments. This low-level programming approach enables direct hardware register manipulation, ensuring the precise execution of time-sensitive operations such as sensor signal acquisition, event detection, and enforcement of predefined test logic with minimal latency. By bypassing the computational overhead associated with high-level languages, the firmware guarantees consistent responsiveness even under conditions involving multiple simultaneous sensor triggers and high data throughput. System validation was carried out through the development of a fully operational prototype, which served as a functional proof-of-concept in both academic laboratory settings and pilot-scale testing environments.

Through the integration of industrial-grade communication, deterministic firmware execution, and multi-sensor fusion, the SDTS offers technological capabilities that surpass most existing prototypes and commercially available semi-automated test yards. This explicitly positions the system as not just a replacement for manual testing, but a demonstrably more advanced alternative to current automated solutions.

This prototype successfully replicated the complexities of real-world driving assessment scenarios within a controlled test yard, confirming the system’s ability to deliver objective, repeatable, and tamper-resistant evaluations. Furthermore, prototype trials underscored the practicality of implementing the Smart Yard concept for large-scale deployment, demonstrating the design’s cost-effectiveness, operational durability, and adaptability to diverse regulatory and environmental conditions.

By eliminating manual subjectivity and replacing qualitative examiner judgment with quantitative, sensor-driven performance metrics, the proposed SDTS constitutes a substantial advancement in the domain of intelligent transportation systems and public safety management.

Through its integration of distributed sensing, real-time data acquisition, and automated decision-making, the SDTS establishes a new benchmark for fairness, efficiency, and standardization in the driver licensing process. Beyond addressing the limitations of conventional testing, the system offers a scalable and adaptable framework capable of meeting the demands of diverse regulatory environments and rapidly evolving mobility ecosystems.

In doing so, the SDTS paves the way toward a data-driven, transparent, and technologically integrated driver evaluation paradigm, aligning with global initiatives to enhance road safety through smart automation. This transformative approach not only supports immediate operational improvements but also lays the foundation for future expansions, including integration with advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS), machine learning-based performance analytics, and cloud-enabled test management platforms. Such capabilities position the SDTS as a strategic enabler in the ongoing transition toward safer, smarter, and more sustainable transportation infrastructures.

Problem statement and motivations

The conventional driving test framework, which relies predominantly on manual observation and examiner-based scoring, presents several systemic limitations that directly undermine its reliability, fairness, and operational efficiency. Foremost among these challenges is the inherent subjectivity of human examiners, whose personal biases, varying levels of strictness, and situational judgment can lead to inconsistent grading and perceptions of unfairness among candidates6. Such variability compromises the standardization of evaluation criteria and reduces the credibility of the licensing process.

These inconsistencies affect fairness and have been linked to measurable safety risks, including higher accident rates among newly licensed drivers in jurisdictions with low-standardization testing practices7,8.

In addition, traditional assessment procedures are time- and resource-intensive, requiring significant human involvement for every evaluation session. This dependence on manual labor, combined with the extended time required per candidate, inherently restricts throughput and limits the system’s ability to process large volumes of applicants efficiently9. Compounding these issues, manual testing methods typically depend on qualitative observations rather than quantitative, data-driven performance metrics, thereby lacking the precision and granularity needed to identify specific areas for improvement in a candidate’s driving skills10. The absence of quantitative, sensor-based performance indicators restricts precision in identifying critical versus minor driving errors. Furthermore, several studies have reported vulnerabilities to unethical practices, such as favoritism or examiner leniency, amplifying public mistrust in licensing systems11.

In other words, existing automated or semi-automated solutions introduced in prior research also exhibit shortcomings, including limited sensor stability, non-deterministic wireless communication, inadequate response times, and poor adaptability to differently configured driving yards. These weaknesses highlight a technological gap that current systems have not fully addressed12.

Moreover, empirical findings in several jurisdictions have shown that inconsistencies in manual driving examinations can translate into real-world safety risks. Variations in examiner judgment, subjective interpretation of errors, and non-uniform evaluation practices have been linked to uneven licensing decisions, occasionally enabling insufficiently prepared drivers to obtain licenses. In high-demand testing regions, such inconsistencies have been correlated with elevated accident involvement among newly licensed drivers, particularly within the first year of independent driving. These observations underscore the broader safety implications of subjective assessment errors and highlight how inconsistent testing rigor can directly influence early-stage collision statistics.

The manual nature of current systems also creates opportunities for unethical practices, including favoritism or corruption, which can erode public trust and further diminish the legitimacy of licensing outcomes13. This vulnerability, combined with the observed real-world safety consequences of inconsistent manual evaluation, further reinforces the need for a standardized and objective testing paradigm.

Moreover, the outdated methodologies employed fail to capitalize on advances in sensing technologies, automation, and real-time analytics, which could otherwise enhance accuracy, operational efficiency, and the immediacy of feedback14. Finally, the limited scalability of manual systems poses a significant barrier to meeting the growing demand in high-population regions, where increasing candidate volumes overwhelm available testing infrastructure and human resources9.

These shortcomings collectively underscore an urgent need for a modernized, automated, and objective driver assessment framework capable of delivering consistent, corruption-resistant, and scalable evaluations. Such a system must integrate advanced sensing technologies, robust communication protocols, and intelligent software-driven decision-making to meet the evolving demands of road safety management and intelligent transportation systems.

In addition to the previously discussed limitations, current driving test procedures are hindered by a series of persistent operational and structural challenges. Foremost among these is the time-intensive nature of evaluations, which not only reduces overall testing throughput but also demands substantial manual oversight from examiners9. This heavy dependence on human involvement introduces subjectivity and examiner bias, whereby inconsistent grading standards can compromise both fairness and transparency in driver assessments6.

Limited scalability further exacerbates these issues, particularly in densely populated or high-demand regions where the growing volume of candidates far exceeds the capacity of existing manual testing infrastructure14. Additionally, the use of outdated and non-standardized assessment criteria across jurisdictions often leads to significant discrepancies in licensing standards and candidate evaluation outcomes15. Such inconsistencies not only hinder regulatory harmonization but also undermine public confidence in the licensing process.

These operational inefficiencies, compounded by the absence of standardized, data-driven assessment protocols, underscore the pressing need for a SDTS. The motivation behind the SDTS lies in its ability to deliver greater fairness, enhanced operational efficiency, and improved transparency, while also ensuring adaptability to varying regional requirements16.

By explicitly linking manual testing limitations to measurable road-safety outcomes, the motivation for an automated, data-driven assessment framework becomes substantially stronger, emphasizing its value in ensuring both fairness and long-term public safety. Related work

In recent years, the field of smart driving systems has advanced significantly, driven by rapid progress in artificial intelligence, sensor fusion, and vehicular communication technologies. These innovations are transforming not only the mechanics of driving but also the broader concept of mobility itself. With road safety, operational efficiency, and full or partial vehicular autonomy as primary objectives, intelligent systems are increasingly shaping transportation environments where data-driven decision-making supersedes human instinct.

Within this evolving context, automated driver testing has emerged as a critical area of research, leveraging modern sensing and computational capabilities to address the limitations of traditional manual assessment.

Prior studies have explored a variety of enabling technologies, including RFID-based tracking systems for precise vehicle positioning, ultrasonic sensors for obstacle detection, GPS modules for route and location monitoring, and vision-based systems for environmental perception and behavioral analysis. RFID-based tracking systems have been used to achieve precise vehicle positioning on test tracks, automatically logging vehicle location and movement to enhance measurement accuracy and reduce human error17,18. Ultrasonic sensors have been employed for obstacle detection and proximity monitoring, allowing reliable detection of objects and potential collisions during driving tests19,20. GPS modules have been integrated to monitor routes, speed profiles, and location-specific performance, ensuring consistent evaluation of candidate driving behavior across different scenarios17,21. Vision-based systems, including camera-based lane, sign, and object detection, have been leveraged for real-time environmental perception and behavioral analysis, supporting tasks such as lane departure detection, obstacle avoidance, and adherence to traffic rules22–24.

Despite these advancements, many prior implementations remain limited in scope, often constrained by fragmented functionalities, outdated hardware, or insufficient real-time processing20,22. These limitations motivated the development of the proposed SDTS, which unifies multi-sensor integration, automated decision-making, and standardized evaluation criteria into a comprehensive and scalable platform for objective driver assessment.

However, many existing implementations remain limited in scope, constrained by outdated hardware architectures, inflexible software platforms, or insufficient real-time processing to support dynamic and responsive decision-making. In addition, several systems focus on isolated functionalities rather than delivering a comprehensive, fully integrated driver evaluation framework. As a result, the demand for more adaptive, scalable, and intelligent solutions continues to grow, paving the way for new system architectures that unify sensing, control, and evaluation into a cohesive and reliable platform for automated driving assessment.

Overview

The evaluation of driving proficiency is a cornerstone of road safety enforcement and regulatory compliance, ensuring that licensed drivers possess the skills necessary for safe vehicle operation. Traditionally, such assessments have been carried out through manual, examiner-led evaluations, heavily dependent on the subjective judgment of human assessors25. While this approach remains the norm in many regions, it is inherently constrained by several shortcomings, including inconsistency in scoring, operational inefficiency, susceptibility to examiner bias, and limited scalability in accommodating growing candidate volumes25,26. These limitations are further amplified by the increasing complexity of modern transportation systems, which demand greater precision, fairness, and adaptability in performance evaluation.

In light of these challenges, SDTSs have emerged as a promising technological solution aimed at addressing the inherent deficiencies of manual assessments. By integrating state-of-the-art sensing technologies, artificial intelligence algorithms, and vehicular communication protocols, these systems facilitate real-time, objective, and data-driven evaluations25,26. Such platforms combine diverse sensing modalities, ranging from environmental perception to vehicle dynamics monitoring, with automated decision-making frameworks to enhance accuracy, fairness, and scalability. Moreover, the adoption of SDTSs contributes not only to improving the efficiency and transparency of licensing procedures but also to laying a technological foundation for the eventual transition toward fully autonomous and self-assessing driving environments.

Limitations of traditional driving assessments

Manual driving tests have historically served as the primary method for evaluating driver competence; however, they are burdened by a range of systemic limitations that compromise their fairness, efficiency, and reliability. Foremost among these is the subjectivity of evaluations, as human examiners, despite their training, may unintentionally introduce bias, inconsistency, or variability in scoring27. This reliance on personal judgment creates discrepancies in assessment outcomes, especially when candidates are evaluated by different examiners under varying conditions.

Furthermore, the traditional one-on-one testing format is inherently labor-intensive and time-consuming, requiring significant examiner involvement for each candidate25. This approach not only limits testing throughput but also leads to scheduling delays and administrative bottlenecks, particularly in regions with high applicant volumes. Compounding these operational inefficiencies is the absence of standardized digital record-keeping, which increases opportunities for corruption, procedural manipulation, and other unethical practices, thereby eroding public trust in the licensing process28.

In addition, conventional testing methods often fail to reflect the technological sophistication of modern vehicles or the complexity of contemporary traffic scenarios29. Many assessments continue to focus on static or outdated criteria that do not adequately measure a driver’s ability to navigate ADAS, multi-modal transportation environments, or increasingly congested urban roads. Finally, the lack of post-licensing performance tracking or continuous monitoring mechanisms limits the system’s capacity to ensure long-term driver competence and road safety compliance30. These persistent shortcomings underscore the urgent need for automated, data-driven, and scalable evaluation frameworks that can overcome the inherent limitations of manual testing.

Evolution toward automated and simulation-based assessment

In response to the inherent shortcomings of manual evaluations, a range of automated assessment strategies has emerged over the past two decades, aiming to increase objectivity, safety, and efficiency in driver testing. Among these, simulation-based systems have gained particular prominence, providing controlled, repeatable environments in which drivers can be assessed under standardized conditions31. By simulating a variety of roadway scenarios, environmental conditions, and hazard situations, these systems enable examiners to expose candidates to potentially dangerous situations without real-world safety risks. This approach not only enhances safety during training and evaluation but also allows for fine-grained performance tracking over multiple test sessions. However, despite these advantages, simulation-based systems face two major barriers: limitations in physical realism, which can affect the accuracy of behavioral assessments, and high implementation costs, which often limit deployment to specialized facilities or well-funded institutions32.

Complementing these virtual solutions, early in-vehicle automated testing systems were developed to bring automation directly into closed-course and on-road evaluations. These systems employed basic sensing technologies, such as rudimentary shock sensors, infrared modules, and single-camera video capture, to record driver performance data during real tests25,33. While they represented a step forward in improving objectivity and reducing examiner subjectivity, such systems were generally task-specific, designed to monitor only a limited subset of driving behaviors (e.g., lane discipline, obstacle avoidance) rather than providing a holistic, multi-metric evaluation of driver competence34. This lack of comprehensiveness, combined with the absence of integrated decision-making frameworks, has highlighted the need for more advanced, scalable, and fully integrated platforms capable of delivering complete and standardized driver assessments.

Sensor technologies and data processing for driving behavior analysis

Recent advancements in sensing technologies and computational intelligence have greatly expanded the capacity for objective, data-driven driver evaluation. Among these, IR sensors have become a preferred choice for detecting precise maneuvers, such as accurate stopping at designated lines or controlled navigation through restricted gates, due to their high responsiveness, reliability, and immunity to ambient light interference25,35. Similarly, shock sensors are frequently deployed to register minor collisions, object contacts, or abrupt vehicle jolts, enabling the detection of subtle lapses in spatial awareness or vehicle control36. These targeted measurements provide evaluators with quantifiable indicators of driver performance that are difficult to capture through human observation alone.

Further reinforcing the importance of coordinated sensing architectures, recent studies have shown that multi-sensor integration combined with microcontroller-level synchronization can significantly enhance reliability in embedded automotive systems. For instance, the SAE technical work presented in36 demonstrates how intelligent fusion of proximity, motion, and environmental sensors, coupled with tightly managed microcontroller coordination, improves response precision under dynamic vehicular conditions. Such findings highlight that beyond individual sensor capabilities, the robustness of a smart driving assessment system depends critically on harmonized data acquisition pipelines, reduced latency, and resilient real-time processing, reinforcing the technical foundations necessary for reliable SDTS operation.

The introduction of sensor fusion techniques has further elevated the capabilities of smart driving assessment platforms by integrating data from heterogeneous sources, including accelerometers, infrared modules, GPS units, shock sensors, and visual imaging systems, into a unified analytical framework37,38. This approach allows for a multi-perspective evaluation of driver behavior, capturing both vehicle dynamics and environmental interactions in real time. To ensure the reliability and interpretability of these measurements, advanced filtering methods (e.g., Kalman filtering, noise reduction algorithms) and machine learning models are employed to process raw sensor streams, extract meaningful behavioral features, and identify rule violations or high-risk driving patterns38,39. These developments have enabled granular, robust, and cost-effective assessments that surpass the limitations of manual evaluation, forming the technological foundation for next-generation SDTSs capable of delivering standardized, scalable, and transparent driver licensing processes.

Current challenges and research gaps

Although substantial progress has been made in the development of smart driving assessment systems, several critical challenges continue to hinder their reliability, scalability, and standardization. Foremost among these is the absence of universally accepted criteria for translating raw sensor measurements into quantitative performance scores that remain consistent and comparable across different platforms, geographic regions, and regulatory frameworks40. This lack of harmonization undermines both the credibility and interoperability of current solutions, creating difficulties for cross-jurisdictional licensing and certification.

A second persistent barrier lies in the technical complexity of sensor fusion, which demands precise synchronization of heterogeneous data streams, ranging from inertial measurements to visual and infrared inputs, and the deployment of analytical models capable of accurately interpreting these multi-modal, real-time interactions41–46. The challenges extend beyond data integration to include model adaptability, latency management, and error mitigation, particularly in dynamic or noisy operational environments.

Infrastructure constraints also limit the feasibility of large-scale deployment. Many existing systems remain confined to small-scale prototypes or controlled testing environments, lacking the robustness and throughput necessary for continuous, real-world operation with minimal human oversight29. Furthermore, much of the current research addresses isolated driving competencies, such as lane discipline, hazard detection, or braking efficiency, rather than providing a holistic, end-to-end assessment of driving proficiency that reflects the multifaceted nature of real-world driving16.

From a broader systems integration perspective, unresolved issues remain in edge-case handling, ethical decision-making, and system resilience under unpredictable conditions such as adverse weather, sensor malfunctions, or unexpected road hazards. Additionally, the absence of comprehensive regulatory frameworks for evaluating and certifying SAE Level 4 and Level 5 autonomous systems introduces further uncertainty for both researchers and policymakers. These combined limitations emphasize the pressing need for next-generation smart driving solutions that are accurate, scalable, operationally robust, and socially accountable, a need that directly motivates the design, implementation, and validation of the proposed SDTS described in the following section.

It achieves objectivity through sensor-driven quantification, efficiency via automated execution, and scalability by leveraging modular hardware and centralized control.

This unified approach provides a robust foundation for future large-scale deployments, whether at a national licensing level or within institutional driver training programs, thereby paving the way toward transparent, data-driven, and equitable driver licensing systems. Given the system’s objective of providing an accessible and replicable driver-assessment solution, IR and shock sensors offered the most favorable balance of performance and cost. Their simple interfacing requirements allowed seamless integration into the embedded architecture without the need for complex signal conditioning or high-power processing units typically associated with optical or ultrasonic alternatives.

Comparison of communication protocols for embedded systems

Efficient and reliable data transmission forms the backbone of embedded system design, particularly in distributed architectures such as the SDTS, where multiple sensors, controllers, and a central processing unit must exchange information in real time. Selecting an appropriate communication protocol requires a multi-criteria evaluation of parameters such as network topology, transmission speed, communication range, implementation cost, noise immunity, scalability, and practical reliability metrics including bit error rate (BER) and EMI tolerance.

While CAN offers superior fault tolerance and advanced arbitration, it introduces additional complexity and hardware cost, which may not be warranted for the SDTS’s operational scope. Conversely, short-range protocols like I²C and SPI are optimal for intra-module communication but lack the scalability and range required for yard-wide deployments.

To address reliability concerns raised by the reviewer, RS485’s BER and EMI robustness are supported by literature, while CAN’s fault-handling capability remains a documented advantage for future upgrades. The comparative evaluation (Table 5) considers signal integrity, latency, noise immunity, and practical operational suitability, providing justification for RS485 selection without needing additional experimental BER testing at this stage.

To further support the selection of RS485 with Modbus RTU for the SDTS communication subsystem, a focused comparison was conducted with CAN and I²C based on signal integrity, latency behavior, and scalability. RS485 provides high signal robustness through differential signaling, enabling reliable long-distance communication in electrically noisy outdoor environments. While CAN offers comparable physical-layer robustness and additional error-handling and arbitration capabilities, its increased hardware and protocol complexity exceed the requirements of the SDTS, where messaging consists primarily of periodic, non-safety-critical updates. In contrast, I²C relies on single-ended signaling and is highly susceptible to electrical interference and capacitive loading, limiting its practical range to short intra-module connections. Regarding latency, the predictable, polling-based communication of Modbus RTU is adequate for the system’s operational needs, whereas CAN’s deterministic arbitration, though superior, introduces unnecessary overhead for this application. From a scalability perspective, RS485’s support for multi-drop networks over extended distances provides a cost-effective foundation for distributed nodes across the test environment, whereas I²C is constrained by physical limitations and CAN requires more complex configuration. Accordingly, RS485 with Modbus RTU offers the most appropriate balance of reliability, performance, and implementation efficiency for a multi-node SDTS deployment.

While CAN offers advanced fault tolerance and deterministic arbitration suitable for safety-critical automotive applications, its additional hardware complexity and higher cost are not justified for the SDTS’s operational scope, which primarily involves periodic, non-safety-critical monitoring across a structured driver testing yard. The multi-stage conversion pathway (USB → RS232 → RS485) introduces negligible latency relative to the system’s millisecond-level event detection requirements, ensuring real-time performance is maintained. Consequently, RS485 with Modbus RTU provides the optimal balance of reliability, long-range multi-node communication, and implementation simplicity for the SDTS, making it more practical than CAN in this context.

Establishing PC-to-RS485 communication link

The seamless operation and synchronized control of distributed hardware modules within the SDTS depend on a robust communication interface between the central Personal Computer (PC) in the Main Control Room and the RS485-based field network. This link is essential for routine data exchange, real-time command execution, continuous sensor data acquisition, and immediate feedback on dynamic driving conditions.

Since conventional PCs do not natively support RS485, an interface widely adopted in industrial automation, a dedicated signal conversion pathway is required. The proposed design employs a two-stage conversion mechanism: USB → RS232 → RS485. This configuration bridges the compatibility gap between the PC’s standard communication ports and the RS485 differential signaling scheme, enabling long-distance, noise-immune, and multi-node communication across the test environment.

The communication link is established using a modular arrangement of converters, as depicted in Fig. 3, ensuring protocol compatibility and data integrity throughout the chain:Modern PCs primarily offer USB interfaces. Therefore, the first stage employs a USB-to-RS232 converter73, which translates USB data into RS232 signals, allowing interoperability with legacy serial protocols still common in industrial systems.The second stage converts RS232 into RS485 signals74. While RS232 supports only short-distance, point-to-point communication, RS485 enables differential signaling, multi-drop capability, and extended communication distances. This output is fully compatible with the SDTS’s distributed hardware topology, ensuring stable, synchronized, and interference-resistant operation.

Network configuration

The RS485 communication bus in the SDTS adopts a daisy-chain topology76–80, a widely recognized arrangement for its simplicity, scalability, and efficiency in multi-drop network applications. In this configuration, the RS232-to-RS485 converter serves as the master node and initiates all data exchanges. Peripheral RS485 devices, such as microcontrollers interfaced with shock sensors, infrared receivers, and traffic control units, are connected sequentially along a shared differential line pair (A and B).

This linear arrangement reduces wiring complexity, promotes organized signal routing, and supports efficient propagation across the distributed system. To maintain signal integrity, 120-ohm termination resistors are placed at both ends of the bus. Proper termination is essential to eliminate signal reflections and standing waves, which could otherwise degrade data quality, especially at high baud rates or over extended distances. By combining structured topology with proper termination, the network ensures stable, noise-immune, and reliable communication between all modules in the system.

Software configuration

On the software side, communication between the PC and the RS485 network is orchestrated through a GUI developed in Visual Basic 6.0. Although VB6 is an older platform, it was selected for its stability, mature runtime behavior, and reliable integration with existing RS485 hardware and microcontroller firmware. This ensures backward compatibility, minimizes integration risks, and supports low-latency, fault-tolerant operation critical for safety-focused testing environments. The PC interfaces with the network via a virtual serial port generated by the USB-to-Serial converter81. Proper initialization of this serial port requires configuring essential communication parameters, such as baud rate (e.g., 9600 bps), data bits, parity, and stop bits, to ensure seamless compatibility with the microcontroller firmware controlling the RS485 devices.

At the application layer, the system manages the exchange of structured command-and-response packets with distributed sensors and actuators. This protocol enables the issuance of control commands, real-time querying of sensor states, and acquisition of performance data from all network nodes50.

The GUI has been designed with user-centered customization in mind. It supports multilingual display options, adaptive interface layouts for varying screen sizes, and automated logging of test sessions. These features facilitate deployment across diverse testing centers and licensing authorities, ensuring standardized operation while accommodating local requirements.

The GUI design aligns with contemporary human–computer interaction and usability-engineering principles applied in vehicular and safety-critical systems, emphasizing clear visual hierarchy, low operator cognitive load, and rapid feedback cues. These design considerations guide the layout, interaction flow, and error-handling mechanisms employed in the interface.

By integrating hardware-level signal conversion with a layered software control architecture and a flexible, customizable GUI, the SDTS achieves a robust, scalable, and fault-tolerant communication framework. This design ensures continuous, low-latency data acquisition and precise control, thereby supporting accurate, real-time monitoring of driver behavior throughout the test environment. The synergy of centralized control logic with distributed sensing and adaptive user interfaces forms the backbone of an intelligent, automated, and objective driver assessment platform.

The microcontroller is supported by a range of peripheral components that enhance its configurability and adaptability. Notably, DIP switches and jumper pins are integrated into each sensor board, enabling manual configuration of the operating mode, allowing the board to function as either an IR sensor or a shock sensor. These configuration elements also facilitate sensor ID assignment, ensuring that each module can be uniquely addressed within the multi-sensor network. This modular identification capability is crucial for enabling system scalability and seamless integration of additional sensor nodes without major hardware redesign.

By combining centralized processing with flexible hardware-level configuration, the design achieves both robustness and adaptability, making it well-suited for large-scale, distributed testing environments.

To enable serial communication with external computers or master controllers, the design incorporates a MAX232 level-shifting IC, which converts the microcontroller’s TTL-level signals to RS232 voltage levels, ensuring compatibility with standard PC serial ports. Associated capacitors support the internal charge pump necessary for this voltage translation. The circuit also integrates latch ICs to store instantaneous digital states, allowing short-duration events, such as collisions or sudden shocks, to be reliably captured and processed. Operational amplifier circuits (e.g., LM324 or equivalent) are employed to amplify and condition weak analog sensor signals, improving clarity and detection accuracy.

A relay driver stage, comprising transistors and flyback diodes, enables the microcontroller to control higher-current loads such as traffic light signals or alert mechanisms. The flyback diodes safeguard transistors from voltage spikes generated during relay switching. Power regulation and distribution is achieved through voltage regulators and filtering capacitors, ensuring a stable 5 V supply for both analog and digital components, thereby minimizing noise and enhancing operational reliability.

For ease of integration, the board features pin headers and high-reliability connectors for interfacing with sensors, communication lines, and control room systems. Overall, the design emphasizes modularity, scalability, and fault tolerance, making it well-suited for distributed smart driving test environments requiring accurate event detection, real-time data acquisition, and dependable communication.

In particular, to address PCB design challenges, critical traces were carefully routed, decoupling capacitors were strategically placed, and continuous ground and power planes were implemented to minimize EMI and crosstalk. Multi-layer PCB configurations were used in high-density areas to provide routing flexibility and maintain electromagnetic compatibility.

In summary, the architecture integrates a central microcontroller with configurable DIP switches, latch ICs, operational amplifiers, a MAX232 interface, relay control circuitry, and robust power management, forming a cohesive hardware platform for the RS485-based network.

Future enhancements

While the current implementation of the SDTS effectively meets its primary objectives, namely real-time monitoring, sensor-level communication, and fault detection, there remains substantial scope for further development to enhance scalability, robustness, and user experience.

One promising improvement involves augmenting the GUI to incorporate real-time spatial mapping of sensor positions. This feature would provide an intuitive visual representation of sensor activity and precise fault locations, enabling faster decision-making during examinations. Integrating this functionality into a dashboard-style interface could further consolidate key system metrics, offering examiners a comprehensive, at-a-glance view of operational status throughout the test cycle.

In terms of communication infrastructure, the existing serial-based architecture could be upgraded to leverage advanced protocols such as the CAN Bus or wireless technologies including ZigBee or Wi-Fi. Such enhancements would remove wiring limitations, simplify scalability, and facilitate deployment in larger or more complex testing environments.

Furthermore, incorporating a comprehensive logging and analytics framework would enable continuous data capture throughout test sessions. This data could support the generation of automated performance reports, the identification of recurring fault patterns, and the long-term assessment of both candidate performance and sensor reliability. These capabilities would not only strengthen the system’s operational resilience but also open pathways for predictive maintenance and data-driven optimization.

Integrating machine learning algorithms represents another promising direction. By leveraging historical sensor data, the system could be trained to classify and predict common driving errors, thereby enabling a more adaptive and intelligent assessment process in line with emerging trends in AI-driven driver education.

Future iterations may also incorporate multi-language support, cloud-based record storage, and direct integration with national licensing databases. These enhancements would improve accessibility, ensure secure and traceable recordkeeping, and streamline administrative workflows.

Collectively, these advancements would transform the system from a functional testing platform into a comprehensive, scalable, and future-ready solution, aligning it with the global movement toward digital transformation and automation in driver evaluation and licensing processes.

Conclusion

The proposed SDTS system represented a significant leap forward in modernizing driver assessment by replacing conventional manual evaluation methods with an automated, sensor-driven framework. Through the seamless integration of microcontroller-based sensor arrays, reliable RS485 serial communication, and a purpose-built GUI, the system delivers objective, consistent, and real-time performance evaluation within controlled test environments. This automation addresses key shortcomings of traditional approaches, reducing human error, eliminating subjective bias, and mitigating the potential for procedural inconsistencies or corruption.

From an operational perspective, the system achieves measurable gains in efficiency and scalability, enabling shorter test cycles, higher candidate throughput, and streamlined administrative workflows. The selection of the RS485 protocol proved crucial, providing a balanced solution in terms of cost, robustness, and network scalability, ensuring reliable data transmission even in electrically noisy environments and over extended distances.

The hardware architecture, underpinned by precise schematic design, optimized PCB layouts, and mechanically robust 3D-modeled enclosures, ensure system durability and reliability under repeated operational use. Complementing this, the software layer, developed in Visual Basic Version 3, offers a feature-rich, intuitive interface capable of real-time sensor state visualization, dynamic traffic light simulation, automated error detection, and event logging. The modular GUI structure allows for straightforward operation by examiners while maintaining flexibility for debugging, calibration, and system scaling.

The feasibility analysis confirms the system’s technical, economic, operational, and regulatory viability, highlighting its readiness for transition from an educational prototype to a fully deployable field solution. Cost-benefit evaluation underscores its long-term economic advantages, while operational assessments demonstrate minimal disruption to existing workflows and rapid adaptability by users. Beyond its current implementation, the system provides a strong foundation for future innovation, including AI-driven adaptive testing, predictive scoring, multimodal sensor fusion, cloud-based analytics, and advanced wireless communication integration. Such enhancements will not only elevate assessment precision and scalability but also align the system with global trends in smart mobility and connected infrastructure.

In conclusion, the SDTS system successfully bridges the gap between theoretical research and practical deployment, offering a cost-effective, technologically robust, and operationally efficient platform for driver evaluation. By combining engineering precision, intelligent software design, predictive analytics, and forward-looking scalability, it positions itself as a transformative solution capable of redefining driver testing standards in both public and private licensing frameworks.
