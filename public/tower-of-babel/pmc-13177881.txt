Prevalence and risk factors of loneliness in older adults with preclinical and prodromal Alzheimer's disease: the impact of depression, frailty-weight loss and quality of life
Authors: Yanhong Shi, Zixin Fang, Yunzhen Chen, Rong Lin, Chenshan Huang, Hong Li, Yuanjiao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests that loneliness may further exacerbate cognitive impairment. This study aimed to assess loneliness and identify its associated factors among older adults with preclinical and prodromal Alzheimer’s disease (AD), thereby providing evidence for improving cognitive continuum management.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Fuzhou, China, using convenience sampling. Participants were assessed for loneliness, social participation, anxiety, depression, frailty, quality of life, and social function using validated scales. Data were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation and stepwise linear regression to identify independent predictors of loneliness.ResultsA total of 215 older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD were enrolled in this study, and 205 of them (95.3%) completed the questionnaire. Most older adults (79.5%) with preclinical and prodromal AD experienced mild to moderate loneliness. The study revealed a significant negative correlation between frailty-weight loss and loneliness (p < 0.05). Key findings include increased loneliness among individuals who live alone (p < 0.05), have lower social participation (p < 0.05), engage less in community activities (p < 0.05), exhibit lower quality of life scores (p < 0.001), and higher anxiety scores (p < 0.001) and depression scores (p < 0.001) .ConclusionsLoneliness is highly prevalent among community-dwelling older adults along the early stages of AD continuum. Frailty-weight loss, living alone, and poor social participation are significant risk factors for increased loneliness, which is further exacerbated by psychological distress (anxiety and depression) and diminished quality of life. Future intervention strategies should prioritize frailty screening and social support networks to enhance quality of life and alleviate loneliness in the early stages of the AD continuum.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Cognitive decline, Loneliness, Older adults, Quality of life

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterised by progressive and irreversible cognitive impairment [1]. It is a leading cause of disability and death among older adults, imposing substantial economic and social burdens on families, society, and caregivers [2]. Depending on the degree of cognitive impairment, syndromal staging of cognitive continuum can be categorised into three stages: subjective cognitive decline (SCD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD [3]. Individuals with SCD or MCI who also show biomarker evidence of Alzheimer’s pathology are considered to be in the prodromal stage of AD [4]. AD typically begins insidiously, with a preclinical phase lasting 15–20 years, encompassing stages such as Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD) and Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) [5]. In large-scale community studies without biomarker data, identifying older adults at elevated risk for AD using clinical and cognitive criteria is a standard methodological approach. This is demonstrated empirically by Hassenstab et al. [6], who identified individuals at risk for preclinical AD using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) without biomarker confirmation. This clinical-risk paradigm was also cited in the framework of Jessen et al. [7] that situates SCD and MCI as the earliest clinical manifestations along the AD continuum. These conceptual and empirical frameworks underscore that SCD and MCI constitute early manifestations of AD and provide a critical window for intervention. Therefore, greater attention to these early stages is critical for timely risk identification and the development of preventive strategies aimed at delaying or mitigating progression to dementia.

Loneliness is a prevalent psychological issue among older adults, often resulting from insufficient social interaction or lack of desired emotional support. Negative emotions can manifest as either social or emotional loneliness [8]. According to the World Health Organization, approximately one-quarter of older adults worldwide frequently experience loneliness, with this proportion remaining consistent across low-, middle-, and high-income countries, or even higher in the former. Loneliness has a profound negative impact on the health and longevity of older adults, yet it remains under-recognised and inadequately addressed [9]. Studies show that 43% of community-dwelling older adults report feelings of loneliness [10]. More concerning is the evidence that loneliness may increase mortality risk among older adults by 26%–32%. Research by Golaszewski et al. [11] found that older adults experiencing loneliness face accelerated cognitive decline and are at greater risk for cardiovascular disease and AD. However, little is known about the prevalence of loneliness in older adults along the early stages of AD continuum, such as during SCD or MCI.

Loneliness in older adults arises from a complex interplay of individual, interpersonal, and societal factors [12, 13]. Inherent characteristics such as age and sex are immutable risk factors, while living conditions, lifestyle habits, socioeconomic status, physical activity levels, and social support are modifiable factors [14–16]. In addition, chronic conditions—including depression, hypertension, and diabetes—have been shown to significantly influence the development and persistence of loneliness in later life [17–19]. Social functioning is closely tied to loneliness, as participation in social activities and maintaining an active social network can significantly reduce feelings of loneliness [20, 21]. Previous studies have linked loneliness in older adults to education level, marital status, and living arrangements [22–24]. Beyond these commonly recognized factors, frailty—conceptualized by the FRAIL framework (fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and loss of weight)—has emerged as an important predictor of cognitive decline [25]. Systematic reviews further indicate that frailty is associated with diminished quality of life and directly compromises physical and social functioning, effects that may be amplified in the presence of cognitive impairment [25]. As a comprehensive indicator, quality of life may integrate these psychological and physical burdens, thereby providing a broader context for understanding resultant psychosocial experiences, including loneliness.

While these determinants are well established in the context of general ageing, the specific factors contributing to loneliness in the early stages of the AD continuum remain unclear. Exploring these variables among older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD is therefore critical for clarifying the potential impact of loneliness on cognitive vulnerability and for informing personalized intervention strategies. Accordingly, this study aimed to describe the prevalence of loneliness in this population and to identify its associated factors.

Methods

Study design

This community-based, cross-sectional study used convenience sampling to recruit community-dwelling older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD from four communities in Fuzhou, China. All participants were recruited from community settings, not from hospital clinics or patient registries. Data were collected between 1 June 2021 and 31 May 2022. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Fujian Provincial Hospital Ethics Committee (K2021-05-024). The research staff explained the purpose of the study to eligible participants, and written consent was obtained from those who agreed to participate.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) met criteria for SCD or MCI; (2) absence of significant visual or hearing impairments; and (3) ability to communicate verbally and comprehend the study questionnaires. The exclusion criteria were: (1) severe aphasia, physical disability, psychiatric disorders, history of suicide attempts, or other conditions that could interfere with cognitive assessment; and (2) systemic diseases known to affect cognitive function, including hypothyroidism, Wilson’s disease, folic acid or vitamin B12 deficiency, syphilis, HIV infection, or other severe mental disorders.

Criteria for SCD and MCI

MCI was identified according to the Petersen diagnostic criteria [26], defined as follows: (1) self-reported memory problems, preferably confirmed by an informant; (2) objective memory impairment adjusted for age and education, assessed using the MoCA [27], with education-adjusted cut-off scores of ≤ 13 for individuals with no formal education, ≤ 19 for those with 1–6 years of education, and ≤ 24 for those with ≥ 7 years of education; (3) intact activities of daily living (ADL) [28, 29].

SCD was identified in accordance with the Subjective Cognitive Decline Initiative [7], defined by: (1) a self-experienced persistent decline in cognitive capacity not related to an acute event; and (2) normal performance on standardized cognitive tests used to classify MCI, with scores adjusted for age, sex, and education.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the formula: n= where n represents the number of samples, p the estimated overall rate, d the admissible error, and U the statistic representing a given confidence level. Based on a previous study indicating that 43% of older adults in the community experienced loneliness [10], the sample size was estimated to be 128 (p = 0.43, d=0.2 ×p=0.086, α=0.05, and U=1.96). Considering a 10% attrition rate, the minimum sample size was increased to 143.

Measurements

Loneliness

Loneliness was assessed using the Chinese version of the UCLA Loneliness Scale [30], developed by Russell et al. [31]. This 20 item tool uses a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always” (never = 1; rarely = 2; sometimes = 3; always = 4). The total score ranged from 20 to 80; with higher scores indicating greater loneliness. Loneliness was categorised into four levels: no loneliness (20 points), mild loneliness (21–40 points), moderate loneliness (41–60 points), and severe loneliness (61–80 points). The Cronbach’s alpha for the UCLA Loneliness Scale was 0.759 in this study.

Social participation

Social participation was assessed using a three-item measure, each evaluating a distinct domain of activity: (1) participation in physical activities, (2) participation in recreational activities, and (3) participation in activities organized by an organization or community [32]. Participants reported the frequency of engagement for each item, with scores ranging from 0 (not participating) to 3 (actively participating). Scores for each domain were analyzed separately, and a total social participation score was calculated by summing the three items, with higher scores indicating greater social participation. The Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.770.

ADL

ADL were evaluated using the Chinese version of the ADL scale developed by Lawton and Brody [28], which consists of 20 items assessing physical self-care and tool-use abilities. Participants rated their ability to complete tasks using a 4-point Likert scale, with scores of 1 indicating complete independence and scores of 4 indicating full dependence. The total score ranges from 20 to 80. A score of 20 represents the best possible functional status (complete independence), and any increase above 20 reflects increasing levels of dependence, thus indicating greater functional impairment. The Cronbach’s alpha for the ADL scale was 0.738.

Anxiety

Anxiety was measured using the Chinese version of the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [33] designed by Zung et al. [34], which has demonstrated reliability and validity in a Chinese population [33]. The scale consists of 20 items, scored on a 1–4 point system (1 for no or very little time, 2 for a small part of time, 3 for a considerable amount of time, and 4 for most or all the time). The total score was multiplied by 1.25 to produce a final score, with scores above 50 indicating anxiety, according to Chinese norms [35]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the SAS in this study was 0.748.

Depression

Depression was assessed using the 15-item Chinese revision of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) [36], developed by Sheikh et al. from the original GDS-30 [37], to evaluate psychological feelings experienced by patients over the past week. The GDS-15 is commonly used to screen for depression in older adults. Each item is answered “yes” or “no”, scored as either 0 or 1, with higher scores indicating more severe depression. The cutoff value for the Chinese version of the GDS-15 was 5 [36]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the GDS-15 was 0.740 in this study.

Frailty

Frailty was measured using the FRAIL scale, a screening tool developed in 2008 by experts from the International Association on Nutrition and Aging [38], which has been validated for use in older Chinese populations [39]. Consistent with evidence suggesting that its items may tap into distinct health domains, and with methodological approaches used in frailty research (e.g., analysis of individual Fried phenotype components, we analyzed both the total score (range 0–5) and its individual components as binary variables (present/absent) [40, 41]. This item-level analysis allows for the identification of specific frailty criteria, beyond a global composite score, that may be independently associated with loneliness, as the total score could potentially mask heterogeneous effects of its constituent parts. The Cronbach’s alpha for the FRAIL Scale was 0.691.

Quality of life

Quality of Life was assessed using the Chinese version of the Quality of Life in Alzheimer’s Disease (QOL-AD) scale [42], developed by Logsdon et al. [43], which assesses both patient and caregiver reports of patient’s QOL in dementia. The scale includes 13 items cover four dimensions: physical health and behavioural compliance, psychosis, living environment and social relationships, and life satisfaction. It uses a 4-point Likert scale (poor, fair, good, and excellent), where higher scores indicate better QOL. The Cronbach’s alpha for the QOL-AD in this study was 0.763.

Social function

Social function was measured using the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), modified by Pfeffer in 1982 [44], and adapted to Chinese populations by Zhang Mingyuan [45], which has high reliability and validity. The scale includes 10 items, with scoring as follows: 0 points for tasks that are normal or possible but never done, 1 point for tasks that are difficult but can be done independently, 2 points for tasks requiring assistance, and 3 points for tasks requiring complete dependence on others. Higher scores indicate greater impairment in social functioning. The Cronbach’s alpha for the FAQ was 0.764 in this study.

Covariates and primary predictors

To minimize potential confounding, we adjusted for a priori–specified demographic and health-related covariates, including age, sex, education level, marital status, number of children, frequency of child visits, socioeconomic status, living arrangements, chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidaemia), and lifestyle-related factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, and mobile phone use) [46, 47]. The primary predictors—depressive symptoms (GDS-15), anxiety (SAS), frailty (FRAIL scale), quality of life (QOL-AD), social participation, and social function (FAQ)—were examined for independent associations with loneliness in multivariable models, conditional on these covariates.

Data collection

Potential participants were screened for eligibility based on the established inclusion and exclusion criteria. A face-to-face questionnaire survey was conducted, typically lasting around 45 min. The survey was administered by graduate nursing students who received standardized, faculty-supervised training in the survey protocol, questionnaire administration, and ethical communication with older adults. During the survey, trained researchers explained the study’s purpose and process in detail and ensured that informed consent was obtained from participants. Breaks were allowed to minimize discomfort, and if any questionnaire responses were unclear, participants were asked for clarification, or caregivers assisted when necessary. After completing the survey, the questionnaires were reviewed for completeness, and participants were asked to fill in any missing information immediately. A total of 205 valid questionnaires were collected.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0; IBM SPSS Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. ANOVA was used to analyse categorical variables, and post hoc tests were conducted for variables with significant differences. Pearson’s correlation was used to evaluate the relationships between variables. Stepwise regression analysis was employed to explore the relationship between influencing factors and the dependent variable. The model fit was assessed using R2, and multicollinearity was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values or tolerance (tolerance = 1 / VIF), with VIF > 5 or tolerance < 0.2 indicating multicollinearity issues. The model was considered statistically valid if it passed the F-test. Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the internal consistency of the dimensions. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 215 participants completed the face-to-face survey; however, 10 participants were excluded due to missing five or more responses. Thus, 205 questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 95.3%. The median age of the participants was 72 years (range, 60–90 years). The majority of participants were female (63.5%, 130/205), with 80.0% (164/205) reporting a monthly income exceeding 3000 yuan, 94.6% (194/205) having received a junior high school education or above, and 82.0% (168/205) living with their spouses (Table 1).

Prevalence of loneliness in older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD

The mean loneliness scale score among the 205 older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD was 29.22 ± 10.32 (min = 20, max = 76). According to the severity of loneliness, most participants experienced mild to moderate loneliness, accounting for 79.5% of the total sample. Discussion

This study provides novel evidence on the prevalence and determinants of loneliness among older adults at the early stages of the AD continuum. Mild-to-moderate loneliness was common in this population, highlighting that loneliness may already emerge as a salient psychosocial burden during the preclinical and prodromal phases of AD. Multivariable analyses revealed that loneliness was shaped by both risk and protective factors. Depression, frailty—particularly weight loss—and living alone were independently associated with higher levels of loneliness, whereas engagement in formal social activities and better quality of life, especially in the domains of living environment and social relationships, were associated with lower loneliness. Collectively, these factors accounted for 31.6% of the variance in loneliness, suggesting that both psychological vulnerability and social-environmental resources play substantial roles in its manifestation. These findings deepen our understanding of the multifactorial nature of loneliness in the early stages of AD and underscore the need for integrated interventions that address emotional, physical, and social dimensions simultaneously.

Our study found that older adults in the early stages of the AD continuum experience significantly higher levels of loneliness than the approximately one-third of the general older adult population who experience loneliness [16]. This heightened loneliness may be linked to early cognitive decline and emotional changes associated with AD, such as memory loss and communication difficulties, which can lead to increased isolation. As the disease progresses, fear and uncertainty about the future may exacerbate feelings of loneliness [48]. Although China has achieved substantial economic growth and improvements in healthcare and social support for older adults, these measures may not fully address the unique needs of individuals in the preclinical stages of AD [49, 50]. Therefore, greater attention and targeted interventions are required to understand and mitigate loneliness in this population.

Notably, our study found no significant association between overall frailty and loneliness among older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD, contrary to previous findings in community-dwelling older adults [51]. Item-level analysis revealed a significant association between loneliness and the frailty component of weight loss, indicating that use of a global frailty score may obscure relationships at the level of individual components. This approach aligns with frailty research that emphasizes specific phenotypic criteria (e.g., weight loss, fatigue) to elucidate distinct pathways to adverse outcomes [41]. In this population, frailty-weight loss may reflect nutritional and metabolic vulnerability that is particularly relevant to psychosocial well-being, highlighting a metabolically oriented pathway to loneliness that would be masked by a total frailty score. The observed association with weight loss—a criterion closely linked to nutritional status and mortality risk [52, 53]—underscores its potential as a sensitive marker of early psychosocial distress in preclinical and prodromal AD. Our item-level analysis was therefore not merely exploratory but a targeted effort to identify specific drivers of the frailty–loneliness relationship. Study duration may also affect observed associations [54]. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between changes in frailty components, such as weight loss, and loneliness in older adults in the early stages of the AD continuum.

Our study also highlighted the significant role of living arrangements in influencing loneliness. Older adults living alone experienced significantly higher levels of loneliness compared to those living with others, particularly those living with a spouse. Emotional communication and mutual support are generally more sustained among older adults living with their spouses, which helps alleviate loneliness. In contrast, although older adults living with their children receive care for their daily needs, they often experience less emotional communication, leading to higher levels of loneliness. Older adults living alone may feel a greater gap between the support they receive and their expectations, resulting in stronger feelings of loneliness [55]. Intimate relationships between spouses, on the other hand, provide emotional satisfaction and reduce loneliness. This finding aligns with research by Chen et al. [30], which indicates that living with a spouse is more effective in alleviating loneliness among older adults than living with children. Therefore, living with a spouse, rather than simply living with others, plays a key role in reducing loneliness.

Moreover, our findings suggest that active social participation, particularly in formal activities organised by communities or social groups, significantly reduces loneliness in older adults. Unlike informal activities such as private gatherings or neighbourhood interactions, formal activities provide a more structured environment that fosters regular social interactions and strengthens connections through shared goals or interests [56]. These activities often involve ongoing projects and clearly defined roles, increasing participants’ sense of involvement and responsibility, which helps reduce loneliness. While informal activities also promote socialisation, their spontaneity may not provide sufficient emotional support or help expand social networks. Therefore, participation in formal social activities enhances emotional support, broadens social circles, and improves life satisfaction, thereby promoting emotional and mental well-being.

Lastly, our study found a significant correlation between lower QOL and higher levels of loneliness in older adults with preclinical and prodromal AD. Previous research has shown that life satisfaction is a key predictor of both emotional and social loneliness [57]. Through analysis of different QOL subdimensions, we found that physical health and behavioural compliance, mental health, living environment and social relations, and life satisfaction were all negatively correlated with loneliness. However, stepwise regression analysis showed that the “living environment and social relations” subdimension had the strongest negative effect on loneliness. This suggests that while aspects of QOL are related to loneliness, the quality of one’s living environment and social relationships play a particularly important role [58]. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that cognitive decline in the early stages of AD may weaken older adults’ ability to adapt to their environment and engage in social interactions, particularly when memory and executive functions are impaired [59, 60], exacerbating loneliness.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, our operational definition of early-stage AD was based on SCD/MCI clinical criteria without biomarker validation, which precludes definitive attribution of cognitive decline to AD pathology and may limit the generalizability of the observed associations. Second, convenience sampling from specific communities and healthcare institutions may have introduced selection bias, potentially over-representing more socially active or health-conscious individuals and underestimating loneliness prevalence. Nevertheless, the observed associations between risk factors and loneliness are likely robust within the SCD/MCI clinical spectrum. Third, the cross-sectional design and the timeframe of data collection limit temporal inference and broader generalizability and may affect the applicability of the findings to current populations. Future longitudinal studies with extended follow-up are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and examine the stability of these associations over time. Finally, although multiple influencing factors were examined, the study did not comprehensively assess certain contextual and psychological variables, such as the structure of social support networks, psychological resilience, and coping strategies. These factors may influence or moderate the relationship between loneliness and cognitive function and should be incorporated into future research to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Conclusions

Preclinical and prodromal AD older adults exhibit higher loneliness than the general aging population. Depression, weight change, and QOL significantly impact loneliness, necessitating targeted interventions. Stepwise regression analysis highlights depression’s pivotal role in loneliness development, while improved living environments and social relationships can alleviate it. These findings support non-pharmacological interventions to reduce loneliness among older adults along the early stages of AD continuum, emphasizing future intervention strategies should prioritize frailty screening and social support networks to enhance quality of life and alleviate loneliness in the early stages of the AD continuum.
