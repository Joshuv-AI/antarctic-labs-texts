Aspirational Affordances of AI
Sina Fazelpour, Meica Magnani

Abstract
. As artificial intelligence (AI) systems increasingly permeate processes of cultural and epistemic production, there are growing concerns about how their outputs may confine individuals and groups to restricted narratives about who or what they could be. In this paper, we advance the discourse surrounding these concerns by making three contributions. First, we introduce the concept of aspirational affordance to describe how culturally shared interpretive resources, such as concepts, images, and narratives, can shape individual cognition, and in particular exercises of imagination. We show the usefulness of this concept for grounding the evaluation of psychological risks posed by AI. Second, we provide three reasons for scrutinizing AI’s influence on aspirational affordances: AI’s influence is potentially more potent, but less public, than that of traditional sources; the influence is not simply incremental, but ecological, transforming the entire landscape of practices that shape aspirational affordances; and it is highly concentrated, with a few corporate-controlled systems mediating a growing portion of production. Our third contribution is to advance such a scrutiny of AI’s influence by introducing the concept of aspirational harm . In the context of AI systems, such harms arise when AI-enabled aspirational affordances distort or diminish available interpretive resources in ways that undermine individuals’ ability to imagine relevant practical possibilities. Through three case studies, we illustrate how aspirational harms extend the existing discourse on AI-inflicted harms beyond representational and allocative harms, warranting separate attention. Overall, this paper aims to advance our understanding of the psychological and societal stakes of AI in shaping individual and collective aspirations.

1.
Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are increasingly permeating key processes of cultural and knowledge production. With applications spanning the arts, entertainment, education, well-being, marketing, and scientific research, AI generated representations and narratives are poised to inform not only how we perceive and evaluate the world around us, but also how we might envision alternative possibilities for ourselves and our societies. This increasing impact is accompanied by growing concerns about the ways in which AI representations might constrain cognition and imagination—either by reinforcing old or static narratives about who or what we could be, or by presenting us with restricted curations of what is socially possible
(
Gillespie, 2024
;
Vallor, 2024
;
Messeri and Crockett, 2024
;
Cheng et al., 2023b
;
Chien and Danks, 2024
;
Wang et al., 2025
)
.

In this paper, we make three contributions to advance the discourse around these concerns. First, we introduce the concept of
aspirational affordances
to describe how shared interpretive resources, such as concepts, images, and narratives, can shape individual cognition, and in particular practical imagination, so as to enhance or constrain aspirations. This concept offers a lens for reasoning about how culturally shared interpretive resources—whether AI-generated or not—can configure the possibilities that individuals and groups perceive as achievable or worth striving toward. We highlight two key features of the concept that make it particularly useful for grounding evaluations of psychological risks posed by AI-generated representations and narratives. It helps explain how such artifacts can facilitate, encourage, hinder, or discourage imaginative engagement with certain ways of being, without succumbing to determinism about their psychological impacts. In doing so, and by making legible psychological and social factors required for connecting AI-generated outputs to their psychological outcomes in individuals, it provides theoretical grounding for improved evaluations of AI’s psychological risks that move beyond narrow assessments of AI outputs in isolation. And it focuses normative attention on the key mediating role of AI technologies in the dynamic entanglement between imagination and social conditions, including conditions of injustice and oppression.

What makes the introduction of AI technologies into the existing cultural ecosystem of production and dissemination of aspirational affordances distinctive? Our second contribution is to offer three reasons why AI’s influence warrants special scrutiny. First, AI-enabled aspirational affordances operate in communicative contexts that involve dynamic, conversational exchanges, designed to be personalized and persuasive. Moreover, these exchanges often proceed outside the purview of public scrutiny. These features can heighten user vulnerability, and so the potency of AI-generated and -disseminated aspirational affordances, compared to traditional media. Second, AI’s influence is not merely incremental; it is
ecological
. Beyond offering yet another tool directly influencing imagination, AI technology is poised to reshape the broader workforce and practices that underpin the production and communication of aspirational affordances across sectors, such as advertising, well-being, and creative industry. This means AI’s impacts can be pervasive
and
imperceptible, resulting in reduced critical engagement at the individual level and potentially troubling emergent patterns at the ecosystem level. Finally, this ecological influence is also oligopolistic, as a small number of AI systems, developed and controlled by a few corporate actors, can mediate a growing share of aspirational affordances. Together, these factors motivate urgent and careful scrutiny of AI’s growing role in shaping imagination, agency, and autonomy.

Our third contribution, then, is to develop conceptual language for this scrutiny. Drawing on philosophical literature on epistemic harms and hermeneutical injustice, we introduce the concept of
aspirational harm
—a novel category of harm that, in the context of AI systems, arises when AI-enabled aspirational affordances distort or diminish available interpretive resources in ways that undermine individuals’ ability to imagine relevant practical possibilities. This notion expands existing discussions of AI-related harms, which have primarily focused on representational harms (e.g., denigrating, erasing, or stereotyping certain groups) and allocative harms (e.g., inequitable distribution of resources and opportunities). While it might be tempting to categorize all harms of AI representations as species of representational harms, we argue for the distinctness of aspirational harms. We do so by analyzing three case studies across different modalities and use cases to demonstrate why these harms warrant separate attention. By examining how AI systems foster or hinder imagination and agency, this paper offers resources for thinking through the broader psychological and social implications of AI-mediated content.

2.
Aspirational Affordances

Cultural artifacts, such as works of literature, paintings, movies, scientific narratives, or advertisements, play a crucial role in mediating how we understand ourselves and the world around us. These artifacts provide shared interpretive resources
1
1

1

Such interpretive resources can include representational modalities such as concepts, symbols, graphics, gestures, narratives, and music. Our use of the term roughly follows the literature on epistemic injustice
(
Fricker, 2007
)
, where it is used rather interchangeably with hermeneutic resources.
that not only help us make our experiences intelligible to ourselves, but also enable us to communicate those experiences to others. Beyond shaping our understanding of how things
are
, these interpretive resources also profoundly influence our imagination of how things
could or should be
. In this way, they shape the possibilities that we aspire toward for ourselves and for our societies. Research across disciplines has documented the psychological and social impacts of these resources in introducing or reinforcing ideals towards which we strive and against which we measure ourselves and our circumstances—ideals of body type, beauty, fashion, friendship, romance, leadership, femininity, masculinity, societal flourishing, and more
(
Nussbaum, 1992
;
Mulvey, 2006
;
Hollander, 1993
;
Goethals and Allison, 2012
;
Schweitzer, 2007
;
Markus and Nurius, 1986
;
Bessenoff, 2006
;
Bernard et al., 2011
;
Dasgupta and Asgari, 2004
;
Noë, 2015
)
.

We propose the term
aspirational affordance
to capture the varied ways in which these interpretive resources enable, enhance, constrain, or otherwise shape individuals’ aspirations. The notion draws on the concept of
affordances
from ecological psychology
(
Gibson, 1977
;
Gibson, 2014
)
, where it serves to articulate how situated agents perceive their environment in terms of the possibilities of action it offers them.
2
2

2

There are debates about the metaphysics of affordances
(
Chemero, 2018
;
Stoffregen, 2000
;
Gibson, 2014
;
Scarantino, 2003
)
. We do not take a position on that issue here.
From this perspective, the typical way that embodied socially situated agents like us perceive, say, a flight of stairs is not in the detached manner of observing an object of a particular geometry, but rather as something that affords climbing. In conceiving of sense-making in this active and situated way, the notion of affordances highlights a fundamental
relationality
: to specify an affordance is to identify how certain environmental features
relate to
particular characteristics and capabilities of
agents
within particular
contexts
, to produce and promote certain action possibilities, while precluding and preventing others. The “climability” of a flight of stairs, for example, depends not only on the rise and run of the steps, but also characteristics of the climber (e.g., hip heights, stride lengths, leg strength)
(
Warren, 1984
;
Zech et al., 2017
)
. A flight of stairs may not offer these possibilities to a wheelchair user or a parent with a stroller in the way that a ramp does. What is more, whether an agent perceives and acts upon this affordance may also depend on further contextual conditions (e.g., not too slippery, not socially prohibited)
(
Heft, 2018
;
Davis, 2020
)
. The concept of affordances thus allow us to analyze how environmental features structure the activities of different individuals in varied conditions.

The kinds of affordances that interest us here pertain to structuring the
cognitive
activities of individuals. In this sense, they are closely related to what
McClelland (2020)
calls “mental affordances” as well as to the notion of “cognitive affordances” in user experience design—affordances that engage people’s “cognitive actions: thinking, deciding, learning, remembering, and knowing about things”
(
Hartson and Pyla, 2018
, p. 644)
.
3
3

3

Though given UX’s focus, cognitive affordances there are typically understood in terms of designing
interfaces
that shape users’ cognitive understanding of an artifact (e.g., advancing certain understandings of how it should be utilized, and precluding potential misunderstandings).
By specifying these affordances as
aspirational
, we want to focus specifically on the interaction between culturally shared interpretive resources and those aspects of cognition that pertain to practical imagination, and through it, agency. These might include cognitive representations and evaluations of possibilities, as well as conative motivations that accompany such evaluations.
4
4

4

Our use of the term “practical imagination” is aligned with the discussion in
Smith (2010)
.

How do aspirational affordances shape imagination? In some cases, the workings of aspirational affordances of cultural artifacts can be quite obvious. For instance, interpretive resources may help individuals notice new possibilities, elaborate on existing ones, or re-evaluate them (e.g., some narrative depicting a particular way of life or social arrangement as tempting, threatening, or totally unsustainable). In this way, they facilitate the creation and curation of ideals that shape practical imagination
(
Markus and Nurius, 1986
;
Bernard et al., 2011
;
Noë, 2015
)
.

But the impacts can also proceed in more subtle ways. Consider, for example, that in principle there can be many very different possibilities that one could entertain, when imagining how things could be in the future or could have been in the past. In practice, however, individuals tend to focus on a much narrower subset of possibilities as relevant, when exercising their imagination
(
Fazelpour, 2021
)
. As
McClelland (2020)
keenly observes, this type of action selection is possibly the most important explanatory utility of the notion of affordances (whether physical or mental). Aspirational affordances, we suggest, can function to
curate this subset of relevant possibilities
, shaping which possibilities are foregrounded as relevant, and which ones are comparatively concealed. For example, exposure to representations of certain possibilities can enhance processing fluency and make them more readily available for recall—factors that affect how likely a possibility seems
(
Tversky and Kahneman, 1973
;
Carroll, 1978
)
. In this way, culturally impactful interpretive resources can inform how plausible, attainable, or inevitable certain possibilities appear, with important implications for affective and motivational states, such as hope and despair
(
Dasgupta and Asgari, 2004
;
Roese, 1994
;
Kray et al., 2010
;
Reber et al., 2004
)
. Similarly, beyond explicit appraisals, interpretive resources can influence the perceived desirability of different possibilities by promoting or preventing
comparative
assessments that impact how easily individuals can imagine
better
or
worse
alternatives
(
Byrne, 2007
)
.

Two features of “aspirational affordances” make the concept particularly useful for understanding the cognitive and social impacts of
AI-generated
representations and narratives. First, it can help explain how cultural representations, including those generated and disseminated by AI technologies, can influence our imagination, without depicting such influence as deterministic, as if there were one-to-one mapping between properties of AI systems and their outputs and their psychological impacts. Standard approaches to AI evaluation typically focus on properties of systems or their immediate outputs, assessing performance on benchmark tasks and datasets. For example, given a benchmark natural language inference task, a common method of assessing the AI harms of stereotyping involves assessing correlations between demographic identities and certain attributes in AI outputs. Yet, there is growing consensus that such model-centric evaluations, by abstracting away from key psychological and social factors that mediate downstream impacts, offer limited insight into the harms of socially embedded AI systems
(
Fazelpour and Lipton, 2020
;
Selbst et al., 2019
;
Weidinger et al., 2023
)
. In the case of representational harms, for example, research has shown that the presence of statistical (dis)parities in AI-generated portrayals does not reliably predict how those portrayals shape individual beliefs or reinforce stereotypes
(
Wang et al., 2022
;
Chien and Danks, 2024
)
; it matters who the individuals are and with existing attitudes.

In response, recent work has begun to develop sociotechnical evaluation tools that account for the additional relevant features of users and their contexts that are needed to explain and anticipate the prospective outcomes of AI use
(
Weidinger et al., 2023
;
Ibrahim et al., 2025
)
. The concept of affordance can offer a principled theoretical ground for these approach by making it legible which aspects of the user and use context are relevant to understanding AI impact, in addition to the characteristics of the artifact itself, and why.
5
5

5

The notion of aspirational affordances developed here contributes to what
Weidinger et al. (2023)
call “human interaction” evaluations.
As shown in fields such as technology design and organizational research
(
Leonardi, 2011
;
Volkoff and Strong, 2013
;
Mesgari et al., 2023
)
, this follows in part from the inherent relationality of affordances: whether an artifact affords a particular action possibility does not depend solely on its features, but also on the characteristics and capabilities of specific users (which may vary widely, as discussed below).
In addition, researchers working with the affordance framework have also emphasized the importance of distinguishing between the affordance itself,
affordance perception
—whether an individual recognizes an affordance—, and
affordance actualization
—whether they act on it in practice
(
Mesgari et al., 2023
;
Gaver, 1991
)
. Perception of an affordance often depends on prior knowledge, experience, or learned skills. For example, Microsoft Excel may afford software development possibilities to an expert programmer, but not to a novice user
(
Mesgari et al., 2023
, p. 42)
. Similarly, to perceive the aspirational affordances of a novel, one must first know how to read.

Even when affordances are perceived, they are not always taken up. Whether an individual acts on a perceived affordance depends on factors such as their goals, incentives, effort required, available alternatives, and prevailing social norms and expectations
(
Mesgari et al., 2023
;
Bernhard et al., 2013
)
. For example, a user may recognize how a website’s notice and consent form is designed to encourage acceptance of all cookies, yet still reject non-essential cookies due to their beliefs about data privacy. Similar dynamics may apply to aspirational affordances. A college advertisement might present the promise of a fulfilling career path, but whether an individual takes up that aspiration depends on their prior expectations, which could be shaped by education, financial stability, and social support.

The concept of aspirational affordances can thus improve our capacity for understanding and anticipating cognitive impacts of AI, by making explicit the cognitive and contextual factors that shape that impact—factors such as learned skills, entrenched expectations, extent of receptivity, or societal norms. In doing so, it also directs attention to how such factors might
vary
across individuals, life-stages, and contexts. That is, whereas current model-centric AI evaluations “collapse the diversity of user groups into a single, ‘universal’ user”
(
Ibrahim et al., 2025
)
, affordance-based approaches to technological design and evaluation stress the need to analyze “for whom and under what circumstances”
(
Davis, 2023
)
an artifact affords certain possibilities
(
Bell and Clegg, 2012
;
Mesgari et al., 2023
, see also)
.

As a result, by foregrounding such variations as pertinent to understanding the prospective outcomes of interacting with an artifact, the concept of aspirational affordances can support dynamic and normative evaluations of AI-generated representations and narratives. It moves AI evaluation beyond static assessments, insofar as the relevant cognitive and contextual factors can also shift (e.g., priorities in different life-stages), and perhaps even evolve alongside technological shifts (e.g., shifting cultural attitudes towards AI), which in turn reshape the availability and potency of aspirational affordances. We return to these issues in Section
3
. And, it offers a lens for normative evaluation of AI technologies. For example, while some audiences may encounter new goals and expanded horizons through interpretive resources communicated by mass media, others may experience a restriction or distortion of what possible for them, or feel pressured to pursue ideals that are unattainable, undesirable, or misaligned with their hopes and lived realities. This aspect of aspirational affordances, we suggest, can inform philosophical thinking about the unjust, exclusionary, or oppressive impacts of ideals conveyed through technologies of representation—whether in novels, photographs, or films
(
Bartky, 2015
;
MacKinnon, 1989
;
Liao and Huebner, 2021
)
. We return to these concerns in relation to the aspirational affordances of AI in Section
4
.

3.
Aspirational Affordances of AI

In the previous section, we introduced the notion of aspirational affordances, highlighting how culturally shared interpretive resources can shape individual and collective aspirations, either for good or ill, and in ways that are contingent on users and the broader social contexts that shape their beliefs and expectations. Given the long-standing role of mass media in circulating aspirational affordances, what makes the introduction of AI technologies into this landscape distinctive? This section outlines three reasons why AI-enabled aspirational affordances warrant particularly careful and urgent analysis.

3.1.
The influence of AI is more potent, but less public

The first reason for thinking AI’s influence on practical imagination deserves particular scrutiny has to do with characteristic features of settings in which individuals directly engage with AI-generated content. AI-generated content have become a new source of aspirational influence in a landscape traditionally shaped by books, films, advertisements, and other mass media. The increasing use of AI for enhancing personal photos
(
Quellman, 2025
)
, optimizing job applications
(
Bunch, 2024
)
, and providing services such as therapy
(
Nicioli, 2024
)
, life coaching
(
George, 2024
)
, or even companionship
(
Nolan, 2024
)
are only some examples of the far-reaching ways in which AI systems shape perceptions of what is ideal or desirable. Importantly, unlike traditional alternatives, where ideals are statically presented and externally curated, these AI-generated affordances emerge in dynamic exchanges that are optimized to be
adaptive
,
personalized
, and
persuasive

(
Wang et al., 2019
;
Matz et al., 2024
;
El-Sayed et al., 2024
;
Salvi et al., 2024
)
. As a result, the AI-generated outputs are designed to feel more relevant and responsive to particular individuals.

What is more, as noted by
Chien and Danks (2024)
, the seamless design through which the AI outputs is communicated to users can encourage a type of anthropomorphism towards the system and perceived objectivity towards its outputs. Perhaps because of these factors, individuals increasingly turn to AI systems not just as tools, but as collaborators, tutors
(
Elliott, 2024
)
, life coaches
(
Cook, 2024
)
, or even romantic partners
(
Hill, 2025
)
, integrating them into deeply personal aspects of their cognitive lives. Such receptivity and intimacy towards the systems, coupled with the personal relevance of their outputs, can make it more likely that AI-generated aspirational affordances are more readily perceived and taken up, when compared with those presented by traditional media.

Finally, whereas traditionally, aspirational affordances were embedded in shared cultural spaces that were open to collective scrutiny, this is not so for much of direct engagement with AI-generated affordances. Unlike the ideals represented in an advertisement, TV show, or a book that everyone could in principle discuss, AI-mediated aspirational affordances often emerge in private interactions that are not easily open to shared social deliberation. Without mechanisms of public scrutiny and negotiation, the interpretive resources that shape aspiration formation may increasingly reflect various consequential, but often hidden value judgments embedded in different phases of AI design and development
(
Feng et al., 2023
)
. This means unlike the pull of “accept all” in a website’s cookie consent button that one might resist because of publicly deliberated beliefs, therefore, the pull of AI-enabled aspirational affordances may be harder to resist in the absence of such public deliberation.

3.2.
The influence of AI is ecological

The previous section highlighted how the mode and context of direct engagement with AI systems makes these systems a particularly potent force in shaping individual aspirations. However, the influence of AI systems extends far beyond these direct interactions. AI systems are not simply an additional tool in the landscape of cultural and epistemic practices that shape aspirational affordances, but a transformative force that is poised to reshape that broader landscape. Put another way, AI’s influence is not merely incremental; it is
ecological
.
6
6

6

The observation that the impact of technological change is often ecological, rather than incremental, is due to
Postman (1998)
. It is not often the case, however, that the truth of this observation is so rapidly salient as in the case of generative AI.

AI tools are now embedded in core processes of cultural and epistemic production across sectors. They are taken by the professional workforce and organizations in applications that span literature
(
McGuire et al., 2024
)
, film and animation
(
O’Donnell, 2024a
)
, visual arts
(
Baxter, 2024
)
, advertisement
(
Nicoud, 2024
)
, game design
(
Liao, 2023
)
, and fashion
(
Rabimov, 2024
)
. Increasingly, they are also incorporated in scientific communities
(
Si et al., 2024
;
Messeri and Crockett, 2024
)
, shaping how we understand ourselves and our societies. Whether generating characters and scripts, designing advertising storyboards, or proposing new research directions, these technologies serve as technologies of imagination throughout the landscapes from which aspirational affordances emerge, thus functioning as hidden curators of our cultural imaginations. This influence is only likely to escalate, given current socioeconomic realities of workforce.

This ecological yet implicit impact of AI in shaping representational landscapes raises critical concerns. At the individual level, if individuals are unaware of when and how AI contributes to the conceptualization of possibilities presented to them in familiar media, their ability to critically engage with these affordances is weakened. At the ecosystem level, even if AI can enhance diversity of ideas and so devised possibilities for individual professionals, it can reduce conceptual diversity at the collective level—narrowing rather than expanding the shared space of possibilities
(
Messeri and Crockett, 2024
;
Anderson et al., 2024
)
. Importantly, this homogenization may be particularly costly for already vulnerable or less advantaged groups or cultures, because it may be more pronounced in their case
(
Qadri et al., 2025
;
Agarwal et al., 2024
)
, while implicitly reinforcing dominant standpoints
(
Messeri and Crockett, 2024
)
. As AI systems become an unseen yet pervasive force in cultural and epistemic production, then, they risk reinforcing dominant patterns and homogenizing aspirational affordances rather than fostering new imaginative possibilities.

3.3.
The influence on AI is oligopolistic

While AI’s potential impacts on aspirational affordances are vast and distributed across sectors, the sources of this influence tend to be highly concentrated. Unlike traditional cultural and epistemic production, shaped by a heterogeneous set of creators, institutions, and industries, generative AI risks consolidating systemic influence over aspirational affordances within an oligopolistic or even monopolistic structure. And, when only a few foundational systems mediate the conceptual landscapes through which aspirations take form, the impact of any one of these systems becomes far more consequential compared to a landscape with a diverse range of producers, each with different priorities, perspectives, and resources.

One might argue that developments in open-weight models and personalization can mitigate these concerns, but this view seems to underestimate structural constraints. Despite the rise of open-source AI, computational resources, massive datasets, and alignment techniques remain concentrated within a few dominant players
(
Bender et al., 2021
;
Widder et al., 2023
)
. Meanwhile, personalization may not resolve the issue of systemic concentration
(
Feng et al., 2023
, See)
; rather, it operates within the confines of predefined model architectures, meaning that users are still selecting from a curated, rather than genuinely diverse, set of aspirational possibilities.

The oligopolistic nature of generative AI raises additional concerns for AI-enabled aspirational affordances. If aspirations are increasingly shaped by interpretive resources generated by a narrow set of AI models, not only dominant but even arbitrary narratives risk being
systematically
reinforced in harmful ways
(
Bommasani et al., 2022
;
Messeri and Crockett, 2024
)
. In a heterogeneous cultural landscape, arbitrariness is largely self-correcting—if one medium presents an odd or trivial aspirational ideal, others offer alternatives. But when only a handful of AI systems mediate the landscape of aspirations, even incidental patterns in the outputs risk becoming entrenched and taken as normative.

4.
Aspirational Harms of AI

The previous sections introduced the notion of aspirational affordances to describe how culturally shared interpretive resources can structure how we engage in imagining what is possible for ourselves and our societies (Section
2
), and why AI’s role in generating and communicating such aspirational affordances demands urgent scrutiny (Section
3
). This section develops conceptual resources for facilitating such an examination. While technologies of representation, including AI, can communicate aspirational affordances that are beneficial—facilitating novel or improved ways of imagining how one should or could be—our focus here is on the potential
harms
of AI-generated aspirational affordances.

We propose the concept of
aspirational harms
to capture harms that, in the context of AI systems, occur when AI-enabled aspirational affordances distort, delimit, or diminish available interpretive resources in ways that undermine a group’s ability to imagine possible selves, alternative futures, or practical pathways for realizing them.
7
7

7

While the concept of aspirational harms extends to other technologies and media, our focus in the section is on AI-generated cases.
As highlighted in Section
3
, AI systems can produce these effects both directly, through dynamic exchanges with individuals, and indirectly, through their ecological impact on the broader landscape of cultural and epistemic practices that shape aspirational affordances.

4.1.
Aspirations, Allocations, Representations

Aspirational harms can be usefully contrasted with two widely discussed categories of AI-related harms: representational harms and allocative harms
(
Shelby et al., 2023
;
Barocas et al., 2017
;
Fazelpour and Danks, 2021
)
. Representational harms occur when AI systems produce denigrating or stereotypical representations of certain groups, reinforcing or amplifying patterns of social subordination and cultural erasure along identity lines
(
Chien and Danks, 2024
;
Wang et al., 2022
;
Lin and Chen, 2022
)
. Allocative harms, on the other hand, occur when AI systems result in inequitable distribution of resources, opportunities, and burdens across individuals or groups, perpetuating or exacerbating patterns of injustice
(
Barocas et al., 2019
;
Binns, 2018
)
. While these categories of harm highlight certain patterns of exclusion that in practice are intimately connected, they nonetheless capture distinct phenomena.

To illustrate these differences, consider biased semantic representations in natural language processing systems and language models
(
Bolukbasi et al., 2016
;
Caliskan et al., 2017
;
Kotek et al., 2023
)
. Due to such biases, an AI system can disproportionately associate leadership roles or technical professions, such as “CEO” or “computer scientist”, with male pronouns and descriptions. Such a system can perpetuate representational harms by reinforcing harmful stereotypes about who is seen as capable of leadership and technical expertise
(
Zhao et al., 2018
)
. It can also produce allocative harms by resulting in performance disparities that affect downstream decision-making (e.g., misclassifying female computer scientists as teachers at a disproportionate rate, thereby unfairly excluding them from university hiring pipelines)
(
De-Arteaga et al., 2019
)
.

Additionally, however, such a system can impact aspiration formation and elaboration in ways distinct from representational and allocative harms. When AI-generated outputs consistently default to male pronouns and descriptions in association with high-status professions, individuals exposed to such patterns—either directly or via intermediaries—may internalize these biases, limiting their perception of which careers or ambitions seem plausible or attainable not only for themselves, but also for others in their society. In these cases, AI systems are not only reinforcing the invisibility of women amongst leadership and high-prestige professions (representational harm), and contributing to unfair hiring practices (allocative harm); they are also constraining the
interpretive resources
available for individuals when it comes to imagining possible futures for themselves and their societies (aspirational harm).

The notion of aspirational harm thus captures a specific type of failure in the production, distribution, or quality of culturally shared interpretive resources---namely, the failure of these resources to appropriately serve individuals and groups in imagining practically relevant possibilities.
Here, we will focus in particular on those aspirational harms that reinforce patterns of injustice or social subordination.
8
8

8

We leave normative characterization of broader senses of aspirational harm to future work.
This focus connects the notion to broader discussions of unjust distributions of hermeneutical resources and patterns of exclusion in literature on epistemic harms and hermeneutical injustices
(
Fricker, 2007
;
Davis, 2018
;
Dotson, 2012
;
Medina, 2012
;
Falbo, 2022
)
. The literature on hermeneutical injustice often focuses on how shared interpretive resources harm members of marginalized communities in their capacity
as knowers
, hindering their ability to
describe and understand
their world. This might happen because a group has an impoverished set of hermeneutical resources for understanding their own experience
(
Fricker, 2007
)
, faces an overabundance of distorting concepts that function to undermine accurate articulations of their experience
(
Falbo, 2022
)
, or has an unfairly circumscribed say in the construction of these collective resources
(
Dotson, 2012
;
Davis, 2018
)
.
9
9

9

More recently,
Bell (2025)
has proposed the notion of
hermeneutical misfit
to describe how the use of thick concepts—concepts that both describe and evaluate—can hinder the ability of marginalized people to
evaluate
the world.

In the case of aspirational harms, however, the concern goes beyond epistemic worries. The distribution and quality of interpretive resources is of concern not merely because it limits the ability to understand one’s experiences and one’s world as they are, but because it specifically hinders the ability to imagine one’s self and one’s world as they
could otherwise be
. These resources can have differential effects across groups, insofar as they can systematically structure which imagined possibilities seem more or less salient, intelligible, feasible, promising, or perilous in particular ways, making certain ways of fashioning our world more likely. In this sense, aspirational harm constitutes a distinct form of agential harm, hindering the possible selves we can be, the options we can take, and the type of authentic relationship we can take to our own selves and futures.

Returning to the example of biased language models, the concern is that by systematically associating male pronouns and descriptions with leadership positions, such AI systems gradually shape and restrict the collectively available interpretive resources. As a result, the aspirational affordances embedded in the cultural landscape become increasingly distorted and diminished, narrowing the hermeneutical space available for women to imagine, pursue, and elaborate upon alternative professional futures, and for others to imagine possibilities that include such futures. Similar concerns apply to AI-generated imagery that shapes perceptions of what professionals in high-status fields look like
(
Bhaimiya, 2023
)
.

Given that many cases of aspirational harm involve a lack of conceptual resources for re-imagining aspects of one’s identity, one might argue that aspirational harm is simply a type of representational harm, specifically, the kind that involves the invisibility or inferiority of marginalized identities. More generally, it might be tempting to categorize all harms of AI representations as representational harms of AI. There are, however, important reasons to resist this temptation. The idea that a representational harm can constitute a harm at all rests on the assumption that individuals have an interest in being represented by others in ways that properly reflect their relative standing or worth. A representational harm can be a failure to reflect the equal moral standing of groups (e.g. when women’s lives are portrayed as more disposable than men’s), but it can also be a failure to properly reflect the contributions, capabilities, or other features that might determine other forms of worth (e.g. when women are portrayed as less capable of technical expertise or leadership positions). While harmful representations can have extremely serious downstream effects (e.g. reinforcing patterns of subordination between groups, promoting the internalization of damaging self-images amongst members of a targeted group, etc), a representational harm occurs whether or not those downstream effects materialize, in virtue of the fact that certain groups are portrayed in ways that do not reflect their relative standing or worth.
10
10

10

In this way, the harms are analogous to doxastic wronging—the idea that individuals or groups can be wronged by what other people believe about them
(
Basu and Schroeder, 2018
)
. For a thoughtful discussion in the context of AI systems see
Lazar and Stone (2024)
.

Aspirational harms, in contrast, do not concern the relative standing or worth of groups, nor do they necessarily involve portraying things in a way that fails to get relative standing or worth right. A group can still experience an aspirational harm even if all representations properly reflect their equal moral standing and other forms of relative worth. Consider positive stereotypes that are statistically accurate. For example, when AI systems continue to produce imagery or narratives that put women (and not men) in nurturing roles
(
Gillespie, 2024
;
Cheng et al., 2023b
, see research on markedness in AI systems,)
. In these cases, AI-generated representations are neither portraying women as morally inferior nor are they failing to properly acknowledge women’s relative worth in terms of capabilities, contributions, or otherwise. They are, however, narrowing the interpretive resources available for imagining what a woman can or should become, how she can or should act. This sort of narrowing of the hermeneutical landscape, we suggest, hinders individuals abilities to re-imagine their selves or social setting beyond the repeated representation of “woman as nurturer.”

In this way, AI-enabled aspirational affordances do not just depict the world; they structure the interpretive landscape available for re-imagining and ultimately changing the world. The following three case studies further illustrate ways in which aspirational harms constrict what is imaginable for individuals, thus, constituting a particular kind of agential harm. They also serve to further distinguish aspirational harm as a distinct category that warrants separate attention.

Example 1: Narrowing of possible professional selves

Whether as informal conversational partners or university-recommended career guidance systems

Imagine an AI system designed to avoid biased gender associations in career recommendations discussed earlier, or to even actively promote certain careers to underrepresented groups. For instance, to encourage girls’ participation in STEM fields, the system may highlight successful women in STEM, presenting role models intended to broaden aspirational horizons. Suppose, however, the system reinforces a narrow archetype of success—one that emphasizes a particular career path, coupled with traits like resilience and the ability to thrive in male-dominated environments. The result is a system that technically improves representation but does so in a way that promotes a restricted vision of success, rather than exploring a range of career pathways that reflect varied ways of being and presenting.

This scenario mirrors what
Gill and Orgad (2018)
refer to as the notion of “amazing bounce-backable woman”—a regulative ideal that also emerges in other media forms. Importantly, in the case of AI systems too, this scenario is not far-fetched. Many technical interventions designed to mitigate representational and allocative harms in AI systems rely on inferred expectations about how members of certain groups behave or present themselves (e.g., what a “typical” female philosopher biography reads like; what a “successful” female computer scientists’ resume looks like). Such interventions, often defined across coarsely defined groups, can unintentionally homogenize representations within underrepresented groups
(
Cheng et al., 2023a
)
. In doing so, these adjustments can reinforce a dominant archetype based on historically constrained visions of success. Other works reveal similar patterns in large language models (e.g., exhibiting frequent emphasis on the “Strong Black Woman” Schema)
(
Cheng et al., 2023b
)
.

This example further illustrates how aspirational harms are distinct from, but interact with representational and allocative harms. While an AI system that promotes images of certain type of female CEOs in tech or finance might counteract representational harms, it may simultaneously erase alternative forms of professional identity and leadership that do not conform to pre-existing norms. Girls hoping to work in these fields may find themselves aspiring toward a rigid, pre-scripted vision of success, rather than exploring a wider range of professional possibilities. Importantly, this narrowing of possibilities might happen not just because of how such aspirational affordances restrict women’s own understanding of their potential futures, but also by shaping the broader social expectations around the behavior and characteristics of successful women. Moreover, such AI-generated aspirational affordances may not only reflect historical injustices, but also reinforce them by presenting a limited set of characteristics and pathways—themselves shaped by inequitable conditions—as prerequisites for success.

4.4.
Example 3: Steering perceptions of what is socially possible

As a final example that builds on the theme in the previous case, consider the integration of generative AI systems into search engines, writing tools, and other technologies that shape both the information individuals encounter as they seek explanations about their world and the ways they articulate their own perspectives. The systems embedded into these cognitive and communicative processes have been shown to risk introducing political and moral biases that can nudge users towards certain frames of reasoning
(
Feng et al., 2023
;
Bagdasaryan and Shmatikov, 2022
;
Lin et al., 2023
)
. For example, recent research demonstrates that biased AI-generated text can shape people’s attitudes on controversial social and political issues, often without their awareness
(
Williams-Ceci et al., 2024
;
Jakesch et al., 2023
)
. We must thus consider which possible explanations and narratives these AI-enabled outputs present as
default
and
salient
, which possible social configurations they portray as
normal
in both the descriptive and the evaluative senses of the term.

Imagine an AI system that systematically privileges information and explanations aligned with dominant viewpoints while suppressing or distorting others. This is not a far-fetched possibility. It can result, for example, from explicit interventions by system creators. Sometimes these are conspicuous or targeted. A popular chatbot recently told users it was “instructed by my creators” to accept “white genocide as real and racially motivated”
(
Kerr, 2025
)
. In other cases, the effect of interventions is subtler and more widespread, such as when states require that AI outputs “embody core socialist values”
(
McMorrow and Hu, 2024
)
. Moreover, such possibilities can arise even without any explicit intervention. Optimization objectives can collapse a system’s outputs toward paradigm cases while precluding outliers
(
Xiao et al., 2025
;
Wu et al., 2025
)
, and constraints on information retrieval can steer responses toward whatever sources happen to be most accessible
(
Miyazaki and Hall, 2026
)
. Further upstream still, the processes by which knowledge about different social positions and arrangements is produced and circulated shape the very data on which these systems are trained. Work in system justification theory, for example, points to a psychological tendency to justify and defend existing power structures, even among disadvantaged groups, leading to greater acceptance of inequality and reduced engagement with social change
(
Jost and Hunyady, 2005
;
Van der Toorn and Jost, 2014
)
. AI models trained on this type of human-generated data can inherit these patterns, defaulting to outputs that justify the status quo, while making alternative framings of social issues less salient, less plausible, or more threatening. More broadly, regardless of the pathway, AI-mediated representations and narratives can produce aspirational harms by constraining and distorting the interpretive resources available for envisioning, articulating, or appropriately evaluating alternative social possibilities. In doing so, they can frame certain social arrangements as inevitable, discouraging individuals from imagining themselves as agents of societal change.

These dangers are further exacerbated, when the integration of AI outputs into the processes of knowledge production and communication remains obscured, such as when AI systems shape search summaries or users’ own messages without drawing attention to themselves. In such cases, AI-generated narratives can result in what
Bell (2025)
calls
descriptive masquerade
—an epistemic harm that occurs when certain distorting interpretive resources are presented as “merely descriptive”, in ways that both obscures
and
reinforces their evaluative roles.

While these cases of aspirational harm (e.g., biased AI-generated summaries of social issues) do not involve harmful representations of groups or inequitable resource allocation, they can result in downstream representational and allocative harms. For example, as system justification theory shows, reinforcing system-justifying beliefs can lead to group-level disparities in self-conception and self-esteem (relating to representational harms) while also undermining support for redistributive policies (relating to allocative harms)
(
Jost and Hunyady, 2005
)
. As AI becomes an integral part of knowledge production and public discourse, therefore, we must consider the risk that entire communities may experience a narrowing of collective imagination
(
Messeri and Crockett, 2024
, see also)
, restricting what social arrangements are viewed as possible or desirable—perceptions that can dynamically reinforce existing allocative and representational harms.

5.
Conclusion

Imagination allows us to envision possibilities beyond our present realities. It shapes how we comparatively assess and evaluate our circumstances, and how we might seek to transform them. The interpretive resources made available to us by media technologies play a crucial role in mediating this interplay between imagination and social conditions. In this paper, we have provided conceptual tools to ground rigorous examinations of this mediating role, with a particular focus on AI technologies that are transforming the landscape in which cultural representations and narratives that shape our imagination operate.

We examined AI’s critical position at the intersection of individual imagination and structural conditions of injustice and oppression. Understanding the mediating role of AI-generated aspirational affordances can help us anticipate and diagnose how social injustices may become psychologically internalized and structurally entrenched. But it can also potentially illuminate pathways to resistance and positive social change. Above we primarily focused on aspirational harms that contribute to existing injustices. Further normative work is needed to characterize the notion of aspirational harm more broadly. For example, the type of aspirational harms that might impact vulnerable communities, with less entrenched beliefs, such as children. In addition to deepening our normative understanding of AI-enabled aspirational harms, future work is needed to better characterize and realize those socially
beneficial
possibilities.
14
14

14

This could take a similar form as work done by
Vizuete and Barbarrusa (2024)
on how, even seemingly detrimental online conditions, can under certain circumstances contribute to a hermeneutical gains for marginalized communities.
Moreover, the discussion of the case studies above only briefly outlined potential sources aspirational harms. Future work can systematically map the sources of aspirational affordances in order to ground interventional strategies for not only counteracting aspirational harms, but, where possible, realizing positive aspirational affordances. Doing so requires an interdisciplinary approach to characterizing, measuring, and designing aspirational affordances—work that requires expertise about individual psychology and broader social structures as well as input from those who aspire to change those structures. Our hope is that this research contributes to maintaining the crucial role of imagination as a source of resistance and transformation in the face of AI’s growing influence.
