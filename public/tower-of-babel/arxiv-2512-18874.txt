The most general Brownian motion on the line and on two closed half-lines
Dirk Erhard, Tertuliano Franco, Wanessa Muricy

Abstract
. In the 1950s, W. Feller characterized the most general Brownian motion on the closed half-line [ 0 , ∞ ) [0,\infty) . He showed that any such process is a mixture of reflected, sticky, and killed Brownian motions. By “most general Brownian motion” we mean a càdlàg strong Markov process whose excursions away from zero coincide with those of standard Brownian motion, and which may be sent to the cemetery state upon hitting zero. In this work, we fully characterize the most general Brownian motion on the whole real line and on the union of two closed half-lines. Our results are twofold. First, we show that the most general Brownian motion on ℝ {\mathbb{R}} is the process known in the literature as the skew sticky Brownian motion killed at zero (see Borodin and Salminen’s book [ 5 , page 127, Section 13, Appendix 1] ). Second, we prove that the most general Brownian motion on two closed half-lines is a process, which we call the skew sticky killed at zero snapping out Brownian motion . This process extends the snapping out Brownian motion introduced by A. Lejay [ 24 ] in 2016.

[
24

0+

0-

0+

0-

Skew Sticky Killed at Zero Snapping Out Brownian Motion.
Computational simulations are also provided to illustrate this behavior.

Brownian motion with special behaviour at zero

Particle Systems with boundaries

The study of hydrodynamic limits and fluctuations of interacting particle systems is a classical topic in probability theory. Recently, considerable attention has shifted to systems with boundary effects. Such boundary conditions may arise from reservoirs of particles
[
1
,
7
,
21
]
, or from edges across which particles cross at modified rates. The latter corresponds to “soft” boundaries and has been extensively investigated for exclusion processes in

[
11
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

An exclusion process with a single particle reduces to a random walk. Interestingly, as observed in
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
, analysing the full interacting system may in some situations be simpler than understanding the behaviour of a single particle near a boundary. The present work complements this perspective: together with the techniques developed in
[
12
]
, it allows one to describe in full generality the boundary behaviour of a single particle at a distinguished edge. Such a description is expected to be a useful ingredient in the study of interacting particle systems with general boundary mechanisms.

Outline of the proof

For the characterisation of the generator of the general Brownian motion on the line and on two half-lines we first distinguish between three cases based on the exponential random variable that determines the exit time of zero (or of
0+
and
0-
in the case of two half-lines). A crucial step is the application of Dynkin’s characterization of generators (see
[
23
, Theorem 3.2.29]
), which relies on a careful analysis of the exit measure defined in (
3.16
) (for the case of the line; the argument for two half-lines is analogous). The existence of the corresponding processes then follows from an application of the Hille–Yosida Theorem
A.1
.

The paper is divided as follows. In Section
2
we properly state definitions and results. Section
3
is reserved to the most general BM on the real line, and Section
4
is reserved to the most general BM on two closed half-lines.

2.
Statements of Results

2.1.
The most general BM on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta}

Consider the state space
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}={\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\Delta\}
, the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}
, where
\Delta
will be called the
cemetery
. We will assume the standard convention that functions will value zero at the cemetery
\Delta
. In this setting,
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
will be the set of continuous functions such that
f(\Delta)=0
and
f(x)\to 0
as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
.

Definition 2.1
.

A stochastic process
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
is called a general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin if it satisfies the following properties:

•

(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a strong Markov process with values in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta}
and it has càdlàg trajectories.

•

The sample paths of
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are continuous on the set
\big\{t\geq 0:\lim_{s\to t^{-}}W_{s}\penalty\mbox{ or }W_{t}\notin\{0,\Delta\}\big\}
.

•

The point
\Delta
, called the cemetery, is an absorbing state.

•

Let
\tau_{0}=\inf\{t\geq 0:W_{t}=0\}
be the hitting time of
0
. For every initial point
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the law of the process
(W_{t\wedge\tau_{0}})_{t\geq 0}
started at
x

coincides with the law of a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
absorbed at
0
.

Remark 1
.

The above definition ensures that
W
behaves like a standard Brownian motion until it hits
0
, and has a Markovian behavior afterwards. From the properties of the standard Brownian motion, we know that
\tau_{0}<\infty
a.s., and by right-continuity of the paths we have
W_{\tau_{0}}=0
. Moreover, the second condition in the previous definition implies that the only allowed jump is from
0
to
\Delta
, which is an absorbing state. Therefore,
W
cannot reach
\Delta
before
0
.

Our first main result consists of the following characterization of the general Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta}
.
Given a function
g
we denote
g(0+)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}g(x)
and
g(0-)=\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}g(x)
, whenever these limits exist.

Theorem 2.2
.

A stochastic process is a general Brownian motion
W
on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin if, and only if, its infinitesimal generator is given by

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\text{ for }x\neq 0\,,\quad{\mathsf{L}}f(0)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)\,,\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathsf{L}}f(\Delta)=0\,,

with domain
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
consisting of all functions
f
belonging to
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, which are twice differentiable in
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
with second derivative extending to an element of
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, and the equation

\displaystyle c_{1}f(0)+c^{-}_{2}f^{\prime}(0-)-c^{+}_{2}f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)\;=\;0

holds for some constants
c_{1},c^{-}_{2},c^{+}_{2},c_{3}\geq 0
with
c_{1}+c^{-}_{2}+c^{+}_{2}+c_{3}=1
, and
c_{1}\neq 1
.

Remark 2
.

Note that by Remark
8
below if
f
is in the domain of
{\mathsf{L}}
, then its first derivative has side limits in zero.

Remark 3
.

Observe that if
c_{1}=1
, then since
c_{1}+c^{-}_{2}+c^{+}_{2}+c_{3}=1
, one would have the boundary condition
f(0)=0
. However, this set of functions is not dense in
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, and therefore it cannot be the domain of a generator (see Remark
5
and the Hille-Yosida Theorem
A.1
in the appendix). This explains why the case
c_{1}=1
is excluded in the above result.

Remark 4
.

We note that by
[
23
, Lemma 3.2.28 ii]
, the identity
({\mathsf{L}}f)(\Delta)=0
for all
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
implies that
\Delta
is an absorbing point.

Consider a general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
such that its constants
c_{1},c^{-}_{2},c^{+}_{2},c_{3}
are positive and assume that
c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}>0
. Dividing the domain’s equation by
c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}
, we get that

\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}}f(0)+\frac{c_{2}^{-}}{c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}}f^{\prime}(0-)-\frac{c_{2}^{+}}{c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}}f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}}{2(c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+})}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)\;=\;0\,.

Setting
\gamma=\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}}
,
\beta=\frac{c_{2}^{+}}{c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}}
,
c=\frac{c_{3}}{2(c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+})}
, we have that

cf^{\prime\prime}(0+)\;=\;\beta f^{\prime}(0+)-(1-\beta)f^{\prime}(0-)-\gamma f(0)\,.

This corresponds to the boundary condition of the Brownian Motion which is skew at
0
, sticky at
0
and killed elastically at
0
, see Borodin and Salminen’s book
[
5
, page 127, Section 13, Appendix 1]
. Hence, the above result implies that the general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin essentially coincides with the Skew Sticky Killed at Zero Brownian Motion and no new Brownian-type process can be extracted in this context.
The case
c_{2}^{-}+c_{2}^{+}=0
corresponds to the exponential holding Brownian motion. Once it reaches zero it stays there for an exponential amount of time until gets killed, i.e., it jumps to the cemetery
\Delta
and stays there forever. Thus, if it starts on the positive (respectively negative) half-line it is rather a process on
\{\Delta\}\cup[0,\infty)
(respectively
\{\Delta\}\cup(-\infty,0]
).

Let

Theorem 2.4
.

A stochastic process on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
is a general Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin if and only if its infinitesimal generator is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\,\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{G}}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathsf{L}}f(\Delta)=0\,,

and its domain
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
coincides with the set of functions
f\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
twice differentiable with
f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
such that the following two equations hold

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}f(0+)+a^{+}\big(f(0+)-f(0-)\big)-c_{2}^{+}f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}^{+}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle\;=\;0\,,\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}f(0-)+a^{-}\big(f(0-)-f(0+)\big)+c_{2}^{-}f^{\prime}(0-)+\frac{c_{3}^{-}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)

\displaystyle\;=\;0\,,

for some nonnegative constants
a^{+},a^{-},c_{i}^{+},c_{i}^{-}
,
i=1,2,3
. Moreover,
\max\{c_{2}^{+},c_{3}^{+}\}>0
and
\max\{c_{2}^{-},c_{3}^{-}\}>0
.

The coefficients appearing in the definition of
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
in the statement of Theorem
2.4
admit the following interpretation.
The coefficient
c_{1}^{+}
(respectively
c_{1}^{-}
) is associated with the rate at which the process jumps from
0+
(respectively
0-
) to the cemetery; that is, it represents a

.
The coefficient
a^{+}
(respectively
a^{-}
) corresponds to the rate at which the process jumps from
0+
to

(respectively from
0-
to

); these are therefore the rates governing the transitions between the two half-lines and consequently determine the skewness of the process.
The coefficient
c_{2}^{+}
(respectively

) quantifies the reflection strength at

(respectively
0-
), while

(respectively
c_{3}^{-}
) characterizes the degree of stickiness of the process at

(respectively

).

It is worth noting that the equations defining the domain of the generator in Theorem

are homogeneous and may thus be normalized. This observation is significant, as it implies that stickiness at

(respectively
0-
) and reflection at

According to

, with domain

with

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0+)\;=\;f^{\prime}(0-)\;=\;\frac{\kappa}{2}\big(f(0+)-f(0-)\big)

where

is a positive constant.
This case corresponds to a particular instance of the family of processes described in Theorem

, obtained by setting

,

, and
c_{1}^{+}=c_{1}^{-}=c_{3}^{+}=c_{3}^{-}=0
.
In particular, the condition

implies that the switching between half-lines occurs symmetrically. Allowing instead

.

The above considerations motivate us to refer to the class of processes obtained in Theorem

.
This constitutes a novel Brownian-type process, which, as demonstrated herein, represents the most general form of Brownian motion on the state space
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
.

Remark 5
.

We note that the general Brownian motions on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta}
and on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
are defined as strong Markov processes with càdlàg sample paths. In the proofs of Theorems

[

Remark 6
.

Note that if

in the appendix

would not be the generator of a Feller process. The same observation explains why one must demand that

3.
The most general BM on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
– Proof of Theorem
2.2

We start by recalling a key property of strong Markov processes. To that end, we use the conventions that an exponentially distributed random variable
X
with parameter zero is almost surely equal to infinity and an exponentially distributed random variable
Y
with parameter infinity is almost surely equal to zero.

Proposition 3.1

(see Propositions III.2.19 and III.3.13 of
[
29
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{W}}
be a strong Markov process on the Polish space
E
. Denote by
P_{x}
the law of the process starting from
x\in E

and let
\sigma_{x}=\inf\{t>0:{\mathcal{W}}_{t}\neq x\}
be the waiting time of
x
. Then there exists
\lambda(x)\in[0,\infty]
such that
\sigma_{x}
is exponentially distributed with parameter
\lambda(x)
under
P_{x}
. Moreover, if
0<\lambda(x)<\infty
, then
P_{x}({\mathcal{W}}_{\sigma_{x}}\neq x)=1
.

Note that in the case
0<\lambda(x)<\infty
, the statement above implies that the process
{\mathcal{W}}
must leave
x
by a jump.

In this section we will denote by
W
a general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin.
By Proposition
3.1
, we have that
T=\inf\{t>0:W_{t}\neq 0\}
is exponentially distributed with parameter
\lambda\in[0,\infty]
under
P_{0}
. We distinguish between three cases.

Case 1:

0<\lambda<\infty
. Due to Proposition
3.1
the process leaves
0
by a jump, so the continuity of paths on
\mathbb{R}
forces
W
to jump to
\Delta
. Hence,
W_{t}=\Delta
for all
t\geq T
in this case.

Case 2:

\lambda=\infty
. Here,
P_{0}(T=0)=1
, so the process leaves
0
at once. Since
P_{0}(W_{0}=0)=1
and the paths are right-continuous, the process cannot immediately jump from
0
to
\Delta
, otherwise the process would be left-continuous. We thus conclude that the process leaves zero immediately, but not to
\Delta
.

Case 3:

\lambda=0
. In this situation, we have
T=\infty
a.s., which means that
0
is a trap.

We now prove in the next proposition that a general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin is a Feller process on
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
. In the proof, we will write
\tau^{1}
for the first jump of the paths of
W_{t}
. In particular,
\tau^{1}=\tau_{\Delta}=\inf\{s\geq 0:W_{s}=\Delta\}
. Some arguments below could be slightly simplified noting that
f(W_{t})
vanishes for
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, if
\tau^{1}\leq t
(more precisely, the third term in Equation (
3.2
)). However, we do not do this in order to make the arguments easier to adapt to the setting of Section
4
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Every general Brownian motion
W
on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin has a Feller semigroup on
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
.

Proof.

Since
W
is a Markov process, its semigroup is given by
Q_{t}f(x)=E_{x}[f(W_{t})]
, for
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
, and
t\geq 0
. To see that it is Feller, it suffices to verify that
Q_{t}f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
for all
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, and the pointwise convergence

|E_{x}[f(W_{t})]-f(x)|\longrightarrow 0\mbox{ as }t\searrow 0,

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
and
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, see for instance
[
29
, Propositions III.2.4]
. The latter follows directly by Dominated Convergence Theorem, due to the right-continuity of the paths and the fact that
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
is bounded. We show now that
Q_{t}f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
.
First we prove that for
t\geq 0
and
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
,

\lim_{x\to\infty}E_{x}[f(W_{t})]\;=\;0\,.

(3.1)

Given
\varepsilon>0
there exists
M>0
such that
|f(x)|<\varepsilon
, for all
x>M
. Since we are interested in the limit as
x\to\infty
, assume without loss of generality that
x>M
. Thus,

\displaystyle\big|E_{x}[f(W_{t})]\big|\leq

\displaystyle\;E_{x}\big[|f(W_{t})|\mathbf{1}_{\{W_{t}>M\}\cap\{\tau^{1}>t\}}\big]+E_{x}\big[|f(W_{t})|\mathbf{1}_{\{W_{t}\leq M\}\cap\{\tau^{1}>t\}}\big]

(3.2)

\displaystyle\;+E_{x}\big[|f(W_{t})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^{1}\leq t\}}|\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\varepsilon+\|f\|_{\infty}P_{x}(W_{t}\leq M,\tau^{1}>t)+\|f\|_{\infty}P_{x}(\tau^{1}\leq t).

Therefore, it is sufficient to show that for every
t\geq 0
and
M>0
,

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}P_{x}(W_{t}\leq M,\tau^{1}>t)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}P_{x}(\tau^{1}\leq t)=0.

(3.3)

To do so, let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion and fix
t\geq 0
and
M>0
. Consider the hitting times

\tau_{x}=\inf\{s\geq 0:W_{s}=x\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tau_{x}^{B}=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}=x\}\,.

By the continuity of the paths, since
x>M
, we have
P_{x}(\tau_{0}>\tau_{M})=1
. In particular, Definition
2.1
implies that
\tau_{M}\overset{d}{=}\tau_{M}^{B}
. Therefore

\displaystyle P_{x}(W_{t}\leq M,\tau^{1}>t)

\displaystyle\leq P_{x}(\tau_{M}\leq t)=P_{x}^{B}(\tau_{M}^{B}\leq t)=P_{0}^{B}(\tau_{x-M}^{B}\leq t),

where
P^{B}_{x}
denotes the probability relative to a standard Brownian motion
B
starting at
x
. From now on, for simplicity, we will use the same notation
P_{x}
for both probabilities.
From the Reflection Principle (see
[
20
, Corollary 2.22]
for instance), we know that

P_{0}(\tau^{B}_{x-M}\leq t)=P_{0}(|B_{t}|\geq x-M)=2P_{0}(B_{t}\geq x-M).

(3.4)

Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{x}(W_{t}\leq M,\tau^{1}>t)

\displaystyle\leq P_{x}(\tau_{M}^{B}\leq t)=2P_{0}(B_{t}\geq x-M)

\displaystyle=2\int_{x-M}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2t}}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,dy\longrightarrow 0\;\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow\infty,

so we get the first limit in (
3.3
). For the second limit in (
3.3
), recall that a general Brownian motion starting from
x\neq 0
does not jump to
\Delta
without passing through
0
and it is equal in distribution to a standard Brownian motion in the time interval
[0,\tau_{0}]
. Then,

P_{x}(\tau^{1}\leq t)\leq P_{x}(\tau^{B}_{0}\leq t)\longrightarrow 0\;\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow\infty,

again by the Reflection Principle. By symmetry, the same argument shows that
E_{x}[f(W_{t})]
goes to
0
as
x\rightarrow-\infty
.

Finally, we will show that
E_{x}[f(W_{t})]
is continuous in any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
, for any
t\geq 0
. The limit in (
3.1
) already shows the continuity at
\Delta
, so we may assume
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We will construct a coupling of the family of processes
(W_{t}^{y})_{t\geq 0}
indexed by their starting points
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion starting from
0
independent of
W^{0}_{t}
, a general Brownian motion with boundary conditions at the origin, also starting from zero. We define

W^{y}_{t}\;=\;\begin{cases}y+B_{t}\,,&\text{ if }0\leq t\leq\tau_{-y}^{B}\\
W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}}\,,&\text{ if }t\geq\tau_{-y}^{B}\,.\end{cases}

(3.5)

Note that when
B_{t}
hits
-y
for the first time, the process
W_{t}^{y}
hits
0
for the first time.
In this way, up to time
\tau_{-y}^{B}
, the process
W_{t}^{y}
follow the same Brownian motion, but each one starting from its respective initial point
y
. From time
\tau_{-y}^{B}
on, the processes
W_{t}^{y}
follow
W^{0}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
.

By symmetry, we can assume without loss of generality that
x\in[0,\infty)
. We first focus on the case
x\in(0,\infty)
. For
t\geq 0
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

\displaystyle\big|E[f(W_{t}^{y})]-E[f(W_{t}^{x})]\big|

\displaystyle\leq E\big[|f(W_{t}^{y})-f(W_{t}^{x})|\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{-y}^{B}\wedge\tau_{-x}^{B}\}}\big]+E\big[|f(W_{t}^{y})-f(W_{t}^{x})|\mathbf{1}_{\{t\geq\tau_{-y}^{B}\wedge\tau_{-x}^{B}\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq E\big[|f(y+B_{t})-f(x+B_{t})|\big]+E\big[|f(W_{t}^{y})-f(W_{t}^{x})|\mathbf{1}_{\{t\geq\tau_{-y}^{B}\wedge\tau_{-x}^{B}\}}\big]\,.

By the uniform continuity of
f
, we have
E[|f(y+B_{t})-f(x+B_{t})|]\longrightarrow 0
as
y\rightarrow x
. For the remaining term, suppose without loss of generality that
0<x<y
. Thus,
\tau_{-x}^{B}<\tau_{-y}^{B}
, and

\displaystyle E\big[|f(W_{t}^{y})-f(W_{t}^{x})|\mathbf{1}_{\{t\geq\tau_{-y}^{B}\wedge\tau_{-x}^{B}\}}\big]

\displaystyle=E\big[|f(W_{t}^{y})-f(W_{t}^{x})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-x}^{B}\leq t\leq\tau_{-y}^{B}\}}\big]+E\big[|f(W_{t}^{y})-f(W_{t}^{x})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}<t\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}P(\tau_{-x}^{B}\leq t\leq\tau_{-y}^{B})+E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-x}^{B}})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}<t\}}\big]\,.

Due to the fact that almost surely
\lim_{y\to x}\tau_{-y}^{B}=\tau_{-x}^{B}
, the sequence of sets
\{\tau_{-x}^{B}\leq t\leq\tau_{-y}^{B}\}
decreases to
\{\tau_{-x}^{B}=t\}
as
y
decreases to
x
. Thus,

\lim_{y\downarrow x}P(\tau_{-x}^{B}\leq t\leq\tau_{-y}^{B})=P(\tau_{-x}^{B}=t)=0,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\tau_{-x}^{B}
is a continuous random variable (one can see this from (
3.4
)).

Now, let
\tau^{1}=\inf\{s\geq 0:W^{0}_{s}=\Delta\}
. Note that

\displaystyle E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-x}^{B}})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}<t\}}\big]

\displaystyle=E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-x}^{B}})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-x}^{B}+\tau^{1}=t\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}<t\}}\big]

\displaystyle\quad+E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-x}^{B}})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-x}^{B}+\tau^{1}\neq t\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}<t\}}\big]\,.

Since
P(\{\tau_{-x}^{B}+\tau^{1}=t)=0
(see Remark
7
below), the first term vanishes. For the second one, note that the only discontinuity allowed of the paths for a general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
occurs exactly at
\tau^{1}
, when it jumps from
0
to the cemetery. Thus, on the set
\{\tau_{-x}^{B}+\tau^{1}\neq t\}
, we have

W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}}\longrightarrow W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-x}^{B}}\quad\mbox{ as }y\rightarrow x

almost surely. Since
f
is continuous and bounded, the Dominated Convergence Theorem yields

E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-x}^{B}})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-x}^{B}+\tau^{1}\neq t\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}<t\}}\big]\longrightarrow 0\quad\mbox{ as }y\rightarrow x.

Therefore, for
x>0
, we conclude that

\big|E[f(W_{t}^{y})]-E[f(W_{t}^{x})]\big|\longrightarrow 0\quad\mbox{ as }y\rightarrow x.

For
x=0
, we observe that
W_{t}^{x}
coincides with
W_{t}^{0}
, since
\tau_{0}^{B}=0
almost surely. Then, for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
, note that

\displaystyle\big|E[f(W_{t}^{y})]-E[f(W_{t}^{0})]\big|

\displaystyle\leq E\big[|f(y+B_{t})-f(W_{t}^{0})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}>t\}}\big]+E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W_{t}^{0})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}\leq t\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}P(\tau_{-y}^{B}>t)+E\big[|f(W^{0}_{t-\tau_{-y}^{B}})-f(W^{0}_{t})|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{-y}^{B}\leq t\}}\big]\,.

For the first term on the right-hand side of above, we use that
\lim_{y\to 0}\tau_{-y}^{B}=\tau_{0}^{B}=0
almost surely to obtain that

\lim_{y\to 0}P(\tau_{-y}^{B}>t)=0.

For the second term, we argue as before, using the continuity of the sample paths and the function
f
, and the Dominated Convergence Theorem. This completes the proof of the continuity of
Q_{t}f(x)
in
x
, and the proposition follows.
∎

Remark 7
.

The claim
P(\tau_{-x}^{B}+\tau^{1}=t)=0
follows from the fact that if
X,Y
are independent random variables and
X
is continuous (i.e. its distribution function
F_{X}
is a continuous function), then
X+Y
is a continuous random variable. Indeed, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
, note that

\displaystyle P(X+Y=t)

\displaystyle\leq E[\mathbf{1}_{\{t-\delta<X+Y\leq t\}}]

\displaystyle=E\big[E[\mathbf{1}_{\{t-\delta-Y<X\leq t-Y\}}|Y]\big]=E[g(Y)]\,,

where
g(y)=E[\mathbf{1}_{\{t-\delta-y<X\leq t-y\}}]
. This is a consequence of the substitution principle, using that
X,Y
are independent (see
[
8
, Example 5.1.5]
). On the other hand, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
,

E[\mathbf{1}_{\{t-\delta-y<X\leq t-y\}}]=F_{X}(t-y)-F_{X}(t-\delta-y)<\varepsilon,

provided that
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
is chosen small enough. Here, we used that,
F_{X}
is uniformly continuous, since it is continuous,
\lim_{x\to\infty}F_{X}(x)=1
and
\lim_{x\to-\infty}F_{X}(x)\penalty=0
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we can conclude.

The next result characterizes the action of the generator of the general Brownian motion
W_{t}
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta}
on the functions of its domain.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
f
in the domain
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
of a general Brownian motion
W_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin, the infinitesimal generator equals

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\text{ for }x\neq 0\,,\quad{\mathsf{L}}f(0)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)\,,

and
{\mathsf{L}}f(\Delta)=0
. In particular,
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
is contained in the space of functions whose second derivative on
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
extends to an element of
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
.

Proof.

Let
x\neq 0
. Then
x
is not a trap, which allows us to apply Dynkin’s formula (see
[
23
, Theorem 3.2.29]
for a reference), which is

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)\;=\;\lim_{|A|\to 0,A\ni x}\frac{E_{x}[f(W_{\tau_{A^{\complement}}})]-f(x)}{E_{x}[\tau_{A^{\complement}}]},

(3.6)

where
|A|
denotes the diameter of a Borel set
A
, and as before
\tau_{A^{\complement}}
denotes the first time that the process enters into the set
A^{\complement}
.

Suppose that
x>0
and consider
A=(x-h_{1},x+h_{2})
with
h_{1},h_{2}\searrow 0
. For
W_{t}
to reach
0
it must first exit the set
A
, provided that
h_{1}
is sufficiently small. Note that until the process exits
A
, it behaves as a standard Brownian motion started at
x
by Definition
2.1
. Hence, in (
3.6
) we can replace
W_{t}
by a standard Brownian motion
B_{t}
. It is important to note that this does
not
imply that they have the same generators
[
29
, Chapter 7, Exercise 3.17]
, since we do not know if
f
belongs to the domain of a Brownian Motion. We do not even know at that point if
f
is twice differentiable. Thus, let us explicitly compute the right-hand side of (
3.6
) for a Brownian motion:

\displaystyle E_{x}[f(B_{\tau_{A^{\complement}}})]

\displaystyle=f(x-h_{1})P_{x}(B_{\tau_{\{x-h_{1},x+h_{2}\}}}=x-h_{1})+f(x+h_{2})P_{x}(B_{\tau_{\{x-h_{1},x+h_{2}\}}}=x+h_{2})

\displaystyle=f(x-h_{1})P_{0}(B_{\tau_{\{-h_{1},h_{2}\}}}=-h_{1})+f(x+h_{2})P_{0}(B_{\tau_{\{-h_{1},h_{2}\}}}=h_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x-h_{1})h_{2}+f(x+h_{2})h_{1}}{h_{2}+h_{1}}.

Moreover,
E_{x}[\tau_{\{x-h_{1},x+h_{2}\}}]=E_{0}[\tau_{\{-h_{1},h_{2}\}}]=h_{1}h_{2}
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
(see
[
23
, Example 3.2.30]
for the second equality).
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h_{1}\searrow 0,h_{2}\searrow 0}\left(\frac{f(x-h_{1})h_{2}+f(x+h_{2})h_{1}}{h_{2}+h_{1}}-f(x)\right)(h_{1}h_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h_{1}\searrow 0,h_{2}\searrow 0}\left(\frac{f(x-h_{1})-f(x)}{h_{1}}+\frac{f(x+h_{2})-f(x)}{h_{2}}\right)(h_{1}+h_{2})^{-1}.

(3.7)

Since
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
go to
0
independently, we can take first the limit on
h_{2}
and we get

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\lim_{h_{1}\searrow 0}\left(\frac{f(x-h_{1})-f(x)}{h_{1}}+\lim_{h_{2}\searrow 0}\frac{f(x+h_{2})-f(x)}{h_{2}}\right)(h_{1})^{-1},

(3.8)

so
\lim_{h_{2}\searrow 0}\frac{f(x+h_{2})-f(x)}{h_{2}}
exists, which is precisely
f^{\prime}_{+}(x)
the right derivative of
f
at
x
. Similarly, the limit
\lim_{h_{1}\searrow 0}\frac{f(x-h_{1})-f(x)}{h_{1}}
exists and it is equal to
-f^{\prime}_{-}(x)
, where
f^{\prime}_{-}(x)
denotes the left derivative of
f
. Moreover, for the limit in (
3.8
) to be finite, the numerator must necessarily approach zero, implying that
f^{\prime}_{-}(x)=f^{\prime}_{+}(x)
. This ensures the existence of
f^{\prime}(x)
for
x>0
. A completely analogous argument shows that
f^{\prime}(x)
also exists for
x<0
.

Now, we compute the second derivative. Fix
0<\varepsilon<x
, we apply (
3
) to
\varepsilon<y<x
with
h_{1}=h_{2}=h_{n}=\frac{x-\varepsilon}{n}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so

{\mathsf{L}}f(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{f(y-h_{n})-f(y)}{h_{n}}+\frac{f(y+h_{n})-f(y)}{h_{n}}\right).

Since
Lf
is continuous, it is bounded on
[\varepsilon,x]
, so by Dominated Convergence Theorem we may exchange the limit and the integral below:

\int_{\varepsilon}^{x}{\mathsf{L}}f(y)\,dy=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{x}\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{f(y-h_{n})-f(y)}{h_{n}}+\frac{f(y+h_{n})-f(y)}{h_{n}}\right)\,dy.

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{x}\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{f(y-h_{n})-f(y)}{h_{n}}+\frac{f(y+h_{n})-f(y)}{h_{n}}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h_{n}^{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon-h_{n}}^{x-h_{n}}f(y)\,dy-\int_{\varepsilon}^{x}f(y)\,dy\right)+\frac{1}{2h_{n}^{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon+h_{n}}^{x+h_{n}}f(y)\,dy-\int_{\varepsilon}^{x}f(y)\,dy\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h_{n}^{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon-h_{n}}^{\varepsilon}\!\!\!f(y)\,dy-\int_{x-h_{n}}^{x}\!\!\!f(y)\,dy\right)+\frac{1}{2h_{n}^{2}}\left(\int_{x}^{x+h_{n}}\!\!\!f(y)\,dy-\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon+h_{n}}\!\!\!f(y)\,dy\right).

Define
g(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(y)\,dy
for
x>0
. Since
f
is differentiable on
(0,\infty)
, we have that
g
is twice differentiable and
g^{\prime\prime}=f^{\prime}
for
x>0
. We can rewrite the above expression in terms of
g
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h_{n}^{2}}\left(\int_{x}^{x+h_{n}}f(y)\,dy-\int_{x-h_{n}}^{x}f(y)\,dy\right)-\frac{1}{2h_{n}^{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon+h_{n}}f(y)\,dy-\int_{\varepsilon-h_{n}}^{\varepsilon}f(y)\,dy\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{g(x+h_{n})-g(x)}{h_{n}}+\frac{g(x-h_{n})-g(x)}{h_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{g(\varepsilon+h_{n})-g(\varepsilon)}{h_{n}}+\frac{g(\varepsilon-h_{n})-g(\varepsilon)}{h_{n}}\right),

and an application of Taylor’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{g(x+h_{n})-g(x)}{h_{n}}+\frac{g(x-h_{n})-g(x)}{h_{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}(x),

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2h_{n}}\left(\frac{g(\varepsilon+h_{n})-g(\varepsilon)}{h_{n}}+\frac{g(\varepsilon-h_{n})-g(\varepsilon)}{h_{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}(\varepsilon).

Thus, collecting all these expressions, we have

\int_{\varepsilon}^{x}{\mathsf{L}}f(y)\,dy=\frac{1}{2}(f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(\varepsilon)),

and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus implies that
f^{\prime}
is differentiable and

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x),

for
x>\varepsilon>0
. Since
\varepsilon\in(0,x)
was arbitrary, this identity extends to all
x>0
. The proof for
x<0
is analogous. The generator at
\Delta
is trivially derived by noticing that
\Delta
is an absorbing state:

{\mathsf{L}}f(\Delta)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{\Delta}[f(W_{t})]-f(\Delta)}{t}=0.

Therefore, for
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, we have

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\;\mbox{ for }x\neq 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathsf{L}}f(\Delta)=0.

Since
{\mathsf{L}}f
is continuous, there exists a natural extension of
f^{\prime\prime}
to
0

\displaystyle 2{\mathsf{L}}f(0)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\,.

This extension belongs to
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8
.

Note that for
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, one has that

f^{\prime}(0+)=f^{\prime}_{+}(0)\quad\text{ and }\quad f^{\prime}(0-)=f^{\prime}_{-}(0),

where
f^{\prime}_{+}
(
f^{\prime}_{-}
) denotes the derivative from the right (left).
Indeed, let
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. We have seen in Proposition
3.3
that
f
is twice differentiable on
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, and the second derivative
f^{\prime\prime}
extends to an element of
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, in particular
f^{\prime\prime}
is bounded. Thus, for any sequence
c_{n}\downarrow 0
, we have

|f^{\prime}(c_{n})-f^{\prime}(c_{m})|=\left|\int_{c_{m}}^{c_{n}}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx\right|\leq\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\cdot|c_{n}-c_{m}|\longrightarrow 0,

for
n,m
large enough. This proves that the limit
\lim_{c\to 0^{+}}f^{\prime}(c)
exists.

Now, for any
h>0
, we have that
f
is continuous on
[0,h]
and differentiable on
(0,h)
, so using the Mean Value Theorem, we can find a
c=c(h)\in(0,h)
such that

f^{\prime}(c)=\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}.

In particular, since
c(h)\rightarrow 0
as
h\rightarrow 0
, it follows that

f^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\lim_{c\to 0^{+}}f^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}(0+),

(3.9)

as desired. The other limit can be computed similarly.

Now, in order to fully characterize the general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin, we must determine the domain of its infinitesimal generator. Naturally, we expect that the domain of our general Brownian motion has certain restrictions at zero. The next lemma confirms our expectation by showing that the domain
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
is determined by a condition in the neighborhood of
x=0
. Moreover, we will see later that this boundary condition depends on the behavior of the process when it hits zero.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f_{1}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. If
f_{2}\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
is such that
f_{2}^{\prime\prime}
exists in
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, and
f_{2}^{\prime\prime}
admits an extension to an element of
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
and if moreover
f_{1}=f_{2}
in
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
f_{2}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
.

Proof.

We need to show that the limit

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}

exists and that it is uniform in
x
. We split the analysis into two cases:
|x|>\varepsilon/2
and
|x|<\varepsilon/2
.

Let
|x|>\varepsilon/2
. We may assume that
x>0
. Since the process
W_{t}
is equal in distribution to
B_{t}
until the hitting time of zero, we compare the above limit to the one where
W
is replaced by
B^{0}
, an absorbed Brownian motion at
0
starting from
x
, then

\displaystyle\left|\frac{E_{x}\big[f_{2}(W_{t})\big]-f_{2}(x)}{t}-\frac{E_{x}\big[f_{2}(B^{0}_{t})\big]-f_{2}(x)}{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{E_{x}\big[f_{2}(B^{0}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}>t\}}\big]+E_{x}\big[f_{2}(W_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}<t\}}]-f_{2}(x)}{t}-\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(B^{0}_{t})\big]-f_{2}(x)}{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{E_{x}\big[f_{2}(W_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}<t\}}\big]-E_{x}\big[f_{2}(B^{0}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}<t\}}\big]}{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}\frac{P_{x}(\tau_{0}<t)}{t}.

By the Reflection Principle, we have that

\displaystyle P_{x}(\tau_{0}<t)

\displaystyle=P_{0}(\tau_{x}<t)=P_{0}(|B_{t}|\geq x)\leq\frac{E_{0}[B_{t}^{2n}]}{x^{2n}}\lesssim\frac{t^{n}}{\varepsilon^{2n}}\,.

(3.10)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
|x|>\varepsilon
. Therefore, we conclude that the limits

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(B^{0}_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}

must be equal for
|x|>\varepsilon/2
whenever they exist, and that they are uniform in
x
(provided that
\varepsilon
is fixed).
Note that
f_{2}\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, is twice differentiable in
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
and
f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(0+)
exists. This however, does not imply that
f_{2}
is in the domain of the absorbed Brownian motion since this would require that
f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(0+)=0
. However, a small computation using (
3.10
) shows that it still holds that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(B^{0}_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(x)\,.

It remains to prove that the same limit extends uniformly near to
x=0
. Since
f_{1}=f_{2}
on
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, we have for
-\varepsilon<x<\varepsilon
that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}-\frac{E_{x}[f_{1}(W_{t})]-f_{1}(x)}{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{|W_{t}|>\varepsilon\}}]-E_{x}[f_{1}(W_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{|W_{t}|>\varepsilon\}}}{t}\right|.

Set
T_{\varepsilon}=\tau_{\{-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\}}
. If
|W_{t}|>\varepsilon
, then
W
has already hit
\varepsilon
or
-\varepsilon
by time
t
, and
T_{\varepsilon}<t
. Observing also that
|f_{2}(x)-f_{1}(x)|\leq\max_{|x|\geq\varepsilon}|f_{2}(x)-f_{1}(x)|
, which is finite, we get

\left|\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}-\frac{E_{x}[f_{1}(W_{t})]-f_{1}(x)}{t}\right|\leq\max_{|x|\geq\varepsilon}|f_{2}(x)-f_{1}(x)|\frac{P_{x}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)}{t}.

We now estimate this last probability and aim to find a uniform upper bound. Considering
-\varepsilon/2\leq x\leq\varepsilon/2
, we have
P_{x}(T_{\varepsilon/2}\leq T_{\varepsilon})=1
by continuity of the paths (note that this also includes the case when
T_{\varepsilon}=\infty
when the process is killed before hitting
-\varepsilon
or
\varepsilon
). Now, for
s\geq 0
consider the shift operators
\theta_{s}
defined for any càdlàg function
\omega
via
((\theta_{s}\omega)_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\omega_{t+s})_{t\geq 0}
. Thus, if
W
reaches
-\varepsilon
or
\varepsilon
before time
t
, then the shifted process
W\circ\theta_{T_{\varepsilon/2}}
also reaches
-\varepsilon
or
\varepsilon
before time
t
. Hence,

\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\varepsilon}<t\}}\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\varepsilon}<t\}}\circ\theta_{T_{\varepsilon/2}}.

An application of the strong Markov property yields

\displaystyle P_{x}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)

\displaystyle\leq E_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\varepsilon}<t\}}\circ\theta_{T_{\varepsilon/2}}]

\displaystyle=E_{x}[E_{W_{T_{\varepsilon/2}}}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\varepsilon}<t\}}]]

\displaystyle=E_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{\{W_{T_{\varepsilon/2}=-\varepsilon/2}\}}E_{-\varepsilon/2}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\varepsilon}<t\}}]]+E_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{\{W_{T_{\varepsilon/2}=\varepsilon/2}\}}E_{\varepsilon/2}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\varepsilon}<t\}}]]

\displaystyle=P_{x}(W_{T_{\varepsilon/2}}=-\varepsilon/2)P_{-\varepsilon/2}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)+P_{x}(W_{T_{\varepsilon/2}}=\varepsilon/2)P_{\varepsilon/2}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)

\displaystyle\leq P_{-\varepsilon/2}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)+P_{\varepsilon/2}(T_{\varepsilon}<t).

Now, we compare this to Brownian motion hitting times. Let
T_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}=\inf\{s\geq 0:W_{s}\in\{-\varepsilon,0,\varepsilon\}\}
and
T_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}^{B}=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}\in\{-\varepsilon,0,\varepsilon\}\}
, where
(B_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion. Observe that

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon/2}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)

\displaystyle\leq P_{\varepsilon/2}(T_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}<t)=P_{\varepsilon/2}(T^{B}_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}<t)\,,

where we have used that
W_{t}
is equal in distribution to a Brownian motion in time interval
[0,T_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}]
. Similarly,
P_{-\varepsilon/2}(T_{\varepsilon}<t)\leq P_{-\varepsilon/2}(T^{B}_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}<t)
. Also, as we did in (
3.10
), we have

P_{-\varepsilon/2}(T^{B}_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}<t)=P_{\varepsilon/2}(T_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}^{B}<t)=o(t^{n})\quad\mbox{ as }t\rightarrow 0^{+}\,

(3.11)

for all
n>0
. Putting all together, we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}-\frac{E_{x}[f_{1}(W_{t})]-f_{1}(x)}{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\;\max_{x\geq\varepsilon}|f_{2}(x)-f_{1}(x)|\frac{P_{-\varepsilon/2}(T^{B}_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}<t)+P_{\varepsilon/2}(T^{B}_{\{0,\varepsilon\}}<t)}{t}

which goes to zero independently of
x
as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Therefore, both expressions have the same limit. Recalling that
f_{1}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, we obtain that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{x}[f_{2}(W_{t})]-f_{2}(x)}{t}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{x}[f_{1}(W_{t})]-f_{1}(x)}{t}

uniformly in
|x|\leq\varepsilon/2
. This completes the proof.
∎

The next result will bring us one step closer in characterising the domain of the general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c^{-}_{2},c^{+}_{2},c_{3}\geq 0
and measures
\nu^{-}(dx)
and
\nu^{+}(dx)
on
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,\infty)
, respectively, satisfying

c_{1}+c^{-}_{2}+c^{+}_{2}+c_{3}+\int_{-\infty}^{0}(1\wedge-x)\,\nu^{-}(dx)+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge x)\,\nu^{+}(dx)\;=\;1\,,

(3.12)

so that
f
belongs to
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
if and only if:
f
belongs to
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, is twice differentiable in
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
with second derivative extending to an element of
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, and the equation

\displaystyle c_{1}f(0)+c^{-}_{2}f^{\prime}(0-)-c^{+}_{2}f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\infty,0)}(f(x)-f(0))\,\nu^{-}(dx)+\int_{(0,\infty)}(f(x)-f(0))\,\nu^{+}(dx)

(3.13)

holds.

To prove the previous lemma we need the next result concerning weak convergence of measures.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(\mu_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of measures on
(0,\infty)
such that
\mu_{\varepsilon}((0,\infty))\leq 1
for all
\varepsilon>0
, and let
C[0,\infty]
be the space of continuous functions on
[0,\infty]
. Then, there exist a subsequence
\varepsilon_{n}\searrow 0
and a measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty]
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0,\infty)}f(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)=\int_{[0,\infty]}f(x)\,\mu(dx)

(3.14)

for every
f\in C[0,\infty]
.

Remark 9
.

The space
C[0,\infty]
corresponds to the space of continuous functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta,+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
without the convention that
f(\Delta)=0
. After all,
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta,+}=[0,\infty)\cup\{\Delta\}
is the one-point compactification of
[0,\infty)
, where
\Delta
is the “point at infinity”.

Remark 10
.

Since
{\mathbb{R}}_{\Delta,+}
is a compact space, the reader may wonder if the Proposition
3.6
could be a direct consequence of Banach-Alaoglu’s Theorem from Functional Analysis (together with Riesz Representation Theorem). That is not the case because the domain of integration in the left hand side of (
3.14
) is
(0,\infty)
while the domain on the right hand side is
[0,\infty]
.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

The space
C[0,\infty]
has a countable dense subset in the supremum norm, which we denote by
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. We will show first that there exists a subsequence
\varepsilon_{n}\searrow 0
such that the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{k}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)

exists, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We will proceed via a diagonal argument. Letting
k=1
, we have

\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{1}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon}(dx)\leq\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\mu_{\varepsilon}((0,\infty))\leq\|f_{1}\|,

for all
\varepsilon>0
. Thus, we can find a sequence
\varepsilon^{1}_{n}\searrow 0
such that
\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{1}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon^{1}_{n}}(dx)
converges as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Also, for
k=2
, we have

\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{2}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon^{1}_{n}}(dx)\leq\|f_{2}\|_{\infty},

for all
\varepsilon^{1}_{n}>0
, then we can extract a subsequence
(\varepsilon^{2}_{n})
of
(\varepsilon^{1}_{n})
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{2}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon^{2}_{n}}(dx)
converges as
n\rightarrow\infty
with
\varepsilon^{2}_{n}\searrow 0
. Applying this successively, we will have found for each
k
a sequence
(\varepsilon^{k}_{n})\subseteq(\varepsilon^{k-1}_{n})\searrow 0
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{k}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon^{k}_{n}}(dx)
converges as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus, fixing
k
and taking the diagonal sequence
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon^{n}_{n}
, we obtain

\left|\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{k}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon^{n}_{n}}(dx)-\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{k}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon^{k}_{n}}(dx)\right|\longrightarrow 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, since for
n
sufficiently large, we have
(\varepsilon^{n}_{n})\subseteq(\varepsilon^{k}_{n})
and the second integral converges. This completes the diagonal argument.

Next, we can extend this limit for all
f\in C[0,\infty]
using the density of
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. In fact, for every function
f\in C[0,\infty]
, there exists a subsequence
(f_{k_{n}})_{n}
of
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
f_{k_{n}}\longrightarrow f
as
n\rightarrow\infty
in the supremum norm. Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
n
large enough such that
\|f_{k_{n}}-f\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
. Hence,

\left|\int_{(0,\infty)}f(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)-\int_{(0,\infty)}f_{k_{n}}(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)\right|\leq\|f_{k_{n}}-f\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon\,.

Thus the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)
exists for every
f\in C[0,\infty]
, for the same sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\searrow 0
.

Now, we may define the operator
C[0,\infty]\ni f\mapsto\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)
, which is positive and linear. Also, since
C[0,\infty]
consists of bounded continuous functions on a compact space, Riesz Representation Theorem ensures that there exists a measure
\mu(dx)
on
[0,\infty]
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0,\infty)}f(x)\,\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)=\int_{[0,\infty]}f(x)\,\mu(dx),

for all
f\in C[0,\infty]
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 11
.

Note that the same result holds when considering a family
(\mu_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
of measures on
(-\infty,0)
with
\mu_{\varepsilon}((-\infty,0))\leq 1
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, and the space
C[-\infty,0]
.

We now come back to the proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

First, due to Proposition
3.1
,
T=\inf\{t>0:W_{t}\neq 0\}
is an exponential random variable with parameter
\lambda\in[0,\infty]
under
P_{0}
. We will split the proof into three cases:
\lambda=0,0<\lambda<\infty
, and
\lambda=\infty
.

Case

\lambda=0
. Here,
T=\infty
a.s. and
0
is a trap, so the process
W_{t}
must coincide with the absorbed Brownian motion at
0
. Also, since
0
is absorbing, we have

{\mathsf{L}}f(0)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{0}[f(W_{t})]-f(0)}{t}=0,

for any
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. Thus, by Proposition
3.3
we have that
f^{\prime\prime}(0-)=f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=0
, and choosing
c_{1}=c^{-}_{2}=c^{+}_{2}=0
,
c_{3}=1
, and
\nu^{-}=\nu^{+}=0
, the lemma holds trivially.

Case

0<\lambda<\infty
. We will show that
\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=-\lambda f(0)
is the boundary condition for
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. Again by Proposition
3.1
, the process leaves
0
jumping to the cemetery. Then, for
f\in C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, we get

\displaystyle E_{0}[f(W_{t})]

\displaystyle=E_{0}[f(W_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{T<t\}}]+E_{0}[f(W_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{T>t\}}]

\displaystyle=E_{0}[f(\Delta)\mathbf{1}_{\{T<t\}}]+E_{0}[f(0)\mathbf{1}_{\{T>t\}}]

\displaystyle=f(0)P_{0}(T>t)

\displaystyle=f(0)\exp(-\lambda t).

Thus, for
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
,

Lf(0)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{E_{0}[f(W_{t})]-f(0)}{t}=f(0)\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\exp(-\lambda t)-1}{t}=-\lambda f(0),

which implies that
\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=-\lambda f(0)
, as desired. With this boundary condition, it is enough to choose
c_{1}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda},c_{3}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}
,
c^{-}_{2}=c^{+}_{2}=0
, and
\nu^{-}=\nu^{+}=0
to obtain the result.

Case

\lambda=\infty
. In this situation,
T=0
a.s., and
0
is not a trap, so we may compute the generator at
x=0
via Dynkin’s formula (
3.6
). Let
A=(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
with
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Starting from
0
,
W
exits
A
at
-\varepsilon,\varepsilon
or at
\Delta
. Setting
T_{\varepsilon}=\tau_{-\varepsilon}\wedge\tau_{\varepsilon}\wedge\tau_{\Delta}
, Dynkin’s formula (
3.6
) becomes

{\mathsf{L}}f(0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{E_{0}[f(W_{T_{\varepsilon}})]-f(0)}{E_{0}[T_{\varepsilon}]},

(3.15)

for
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}[f(W_{T_{\varepsilon}})]}{E_{0}[T_{\varepsilon}]}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{E_{0}[T_{\varepsilon}]}f(W_{T_{\varepsilon}})\,dP_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}}f(x)\,\frac{1}{E_{0}[T_{\varepsilon}]}P_{0}(W_{T_{\varepsilon}}\in dx)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\frac{1}{E_{0}[T_{\varepsilon}]}P_{0}(W_{T_{\varepsilon}}\in dx),

where in the last equality we used that
f

Since each half-line

First, the hitting time of

.

Let
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, the domain of a general Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin. Then,
f
is twice differentiable with
f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
. Moreover,

{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x),

(4.1)

for
x\in{\mathbb{G}}
and
{\mathsf{L}}f(\Delta)=0
.

Proof.

In view of our previous observations, it remains only to prove that

{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+),\quad{\mathsf{L}}f(0-)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)

for
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, where the second and first derivatives at
0+
and
0-
are interpreted as the right-hand derivative and left-hand derivatives, respectively.

To prove that, we give a completely analytical argument. First, we identify
[0+,\infty]
with the interval
[0,\infty]
, and apply the mean value theorem to obtain

f^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\lim_{c\searrow 0}f^{\prime}(c),

as was done in Remark
8
.
Returning to the notation
[0+,\infty]
, we thus have

f^{\prime}(0+)=\lim_{h\searrow 0+}\frac{f(h)-f(0+)}{h}=\lim_{c\searrow 0+}f^{\prime}(c),

which shows in particular that
f^{\prime}
is continuous in
0+
.
We now apply the same reasoning again. We have that
f^{\prime}
is continuous on
[0,h]
and differentiable on
(0,h)
, so we can find
c=c(h)\in(0,h)
such that

f^{\prime\prime}(c)=\frac{f^{\prime}(h)-f^{\prime}(0)}{h}.

We already know that
\lim_{c\searrow 0}f^{\prime\prime}(c)=2{\mathsf{L}}f(0)
, so we deduce that

f^{\prime\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{f^{\prime}(h)-f^{\prime}(0)}{h}=2{\mathsf{L}}f(0),

i.e.,

f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=\lim_{h\searrow 0+}\frac{f^{\prime}(h)-f^{\prime}(0+)}{h}=2{\mathsf{L}}f(0+).

An identical argument applied to the interval
(-\infty,0-]
shows that
f^{\prime\prime}(0-)=2{\mathsf{L}}f(0-)
.
∎

The next result states that to show that a function is an element of
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, it is sufficient to know its behavior in a neighborhood of
0+
and
0-
. The proof is almost identical to that of Lemma
3.4
, and is therefore omitted.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
f_{1}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
,
f_{2},f_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
, and
f_{1}=f_{2}
in
(-\varepsilon,0-]\cup[0+,\varepsilon)
, for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
f_{2}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
.

To obtain the domain’s boundary conditions at
0+
and
0-
, we proceed similarly as we did for the general Brownian Motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
, expressing them first in terms of measures on
(-\infty,0-]
and
[0+,\infty)
. The proof is close to the one for general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
. However, since it is very technical and adaptations are needed we give a complete proof.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists non-negative constants
a^{+},a^{-},c_{i}^{+},c_{i}^{-}
,
i=1,2,3
and measures
\nu_{j}^{+}
on
(0+,\infty)
,
\nu_{j}^{-}
on
(-\infty,0-)
,
j=1,2
for which

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}+a^{+}+c_{2}^{+}+c_{3}^{+}+\int_{(0+,\infty)}(1\wedge x)\,\nu_{1}^{+}(dx)+\nu_{1}^{-}((-\infty,0-))

\displaystyle=1\,,

(4.2)

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}+a^{-}+c_{2}^{-}+c_{3}^{-}+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}(1\wedge-x)\,\nu_{2}^{-}(dx)+\nu_{2}^{+}((0+,\infty))

\displaystyle=1\,,

(4.3)

such that
f
belongs to
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
if and only if
f\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
is twice differentiable with
f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
, and it satisfies

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}f(0+)+a^{+}(f(0+)-f(0-))-c_{2}^{+}f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}^{+}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\nu_{1}^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\nu_{1}^{-}(dx),

(4.4)

and

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}f(0-)+a^{-}(f(0-)-f(0+))+c_{2}^{-}f^{\prime}(0-)+\frac{c_{3}^{-}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}(f(x)-f(0-))\,\nu_{2}^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}(f(x)-f(0-))\,\nu_{2}^{-}(dx).

(4.5)

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
3.1
that
T_{+}=\inf\{t>0:W_{t}\neq 0+\}
and
T_{-}=\inf\{t>0:W_{t}\neq 0-\}
are exponentially distributed with parameters
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
, under the probabilities
P_{0+},P_{0-}
, respectively. We examine the following cases, supposing first that
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
.

Case

\lambda_{+}=0
or
\lambda_{-}=0
.

If
\lambda_{+}=0
, then
0+
is an absorbing point, so
f^{\prime\prime}(0+)={\mathsf{L}}f(0+)=0
, and we may choose
c^{+}_{1}=a^{+}=c^{+}_{2}=0
,
c^{+}_{3}=1
, and
\nu_{1}^{-}=\nu_{1}^{+}=0
. The case
\lambda_{-}=0
is analogous.

Case

0<\lambda_{+}<\infty
or
0<\lambda_{-}<\infty
:

Again we will consider only
0<\lambda_{+}<\infty
. By case 1 above, the process waits an exponential time at
0+
, then it jumps to
0-
or
\Delta
. In particular,
0+
is not a trap under
P_{0+}
, so we may apply Dynkin’s formula (
3.6
).

Let
A=[0+,\varepsilon)
with
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Starting from
0+
, the process exits
A
precisely when it jumps from
0+
to the points
0-
or
\Delta
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{E_{0+}[f(W_{T_{+}})]-f(0+)}{E_{0+}[T_{+}]}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{+}[f(0-)P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=0-)+f(\Delta)P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=\Delta)-f(0+)].

Recalling that
f
vanishes at
\Delta
, we rewrite the equation as follows

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=0-)(f(0-)-f(0+))-\lambda_{+}(1-P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=0-))f(0+)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=0-)(f(0-)-f(0+))-\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=\Delta)f(0+).

Since
{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)
, we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)&+\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=\Delta)f(0+)\\
&+\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=0-)\big(f(0+)-f(0-)\big)\;=\;0\,.\end{split}

(4.6)

Setting
c_{1}^{+}=\frac{\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=\Delta)}{1+\lambda_{+}}
,
a^{+}=\frac{\lambda_{+}P_{0+}(W_{T_{+}}=0-)}{1+\lambda_{+}}
,
c^{+}_{2}=0
,
c^{+}_{3}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{+}}
and
\nu_{1}^{-}=\nu_{1}^{+}=0
, the lemma follows.

Case

\lambda_{+}=\infty
or
\lambda_{-}=\infty
.
If
\lambda_{+}=\infty
, then the process leaves
0+
immediately, so we use Dynkin’s formula (
3.6
) again with
A=[0+,\varepsilon)
with
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Starting from
0+
, the process can leave
A
only via
\varepsilon,\Delta,0-
. Then considering
T_{\varepsilon}=\tau_{\varepsilon}\wedge\tau_{\Delta}\wedge\tau_{0-}
, by Dynkin’s Formula, we have

{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)\;=\;\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{E_{0+}[f(W_{T_{\varepsilon}})]-f(0+)}{E_{0+}[T_{\varepsilon}]}\,.

We rewrite this equation in terms of the pull-back measure
\nu_{\varepsilon}(dx)=
\frac{1}{E_{0+}[T_{\varepsilon}]}P_{0+}(W_{T_{\varepsilon}}\in dx)
on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
. Thus,

{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)\;=\;\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\,\int_{{\mathbb{G}}}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\nu_{\varepsilon}(dx)-f(0+)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\Delta)\,.

(4.7)

We consider
K_{\varepsilon}=1+\nu_{\varepsilon}(\{\Delta\}\cup(-\infty,0-])+\int_{(0+,\infty)}(1\wedge x)\,\nu_{\varepsilon}(dx)
and the measures

\displaystyle\mu^{+}_{\varepsilon}(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}(1\wedge x)\,\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(dx)}{K_{\varepsilon}}\text{ on }(0+,\infty)\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mu^{-}_{\varepsilon}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(A)}{K_{\varepsilon}}\text{ on }(-\infty,0-].

Identifying
[0+,\infty)
with
[0,\infty)
and noticing that
\mu^{+}_{\varepsilon}((0+,\infty))\leq 1
, we use Proposition
3.6
to obtain a sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
and a measure
\mu^{+}(dx)
on
[0+,\infty]
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0+,\infty)}f(x)\,\mu^{+}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)\;=\;\int_{[0+,\infty]}f(x)\,\mu^{+}(dx)\,,

for all
f\in C[0+,\infty]
. Applying the same reasoning and Remark
11
, we also obtain a measure
\mu^{-}(dx)
on
[-\infty,0-]
such that, after possibly going over to a subsequence, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(-\infty,0-]}f(x)\,\mu^{-}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)\;=\;\int_{[-\infty,0-]}f(x)\,\mu^{-}(dx)\,,

for all
f\in C[-\infty,0-]
.

Now, we use these limits with appropriate functions. For
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, the function
\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}
is continuous on
(0+,\infty)
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}=-f(0+)<\infty
. Also,
\lim_{x\downarrow 0+}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}=\lim_{x\downarrow 0+}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{x}=f^{\prime}(0+)<\infty
. Thus,
\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}
admits an extension in
C[0+,\infty]
, so

\displaystyle\int_{[0+,\infty]}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}\,\mu^{+}(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0+,\infty)}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}\,\mu^{+}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0+,\infty)}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}(1\wedge x)\,\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0+,\infty)}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[0+,\infty)}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)\,,

where we have used that
1\wedge x
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mu^{+}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)
with respect to
\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)
.
Similarly, one can see that

\int_{[-\infty,0-]}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\mu^{-}(dx)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(-\infty,0-]}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)

for all
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. Moreover, the sequences
\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\Delta)}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}
and
\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}
are bounded, so we may assume that there are
p_{1},p_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\Delta)}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}=p_{1}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}=p_{2},

passing to a further subsequence, if necessary. Applying all these limits, Equation (
4.7
) simplifies to

\displaystyle p_{1}f(0+)+p_{2}{\mathsf{L}}f(0+)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0+,\infty]}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}\,\mu^{+}(dx)+\int_{[-\infty,0-]}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\mu^{-}(dx)\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle(p_{1}+\mu^{+}(\infty)+\mu^{-}(-\infty))f(0+)

\displaystyle+\mu^{-}(0-)(f(0+)-f(0-))-\mu^{+}(0+)f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{p_{2}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}\frac{f(x)-f(0+)}{1\wedge x}\,\mu^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}(f(x)-f(0+))\,\mu^{-}(dx).

Defining the constants

\displaystyle c^{+}_{1}

\displaystyle=p_{1}+\mu^{+}(\infty)+\mu^{-}(-\infty),

\displaystyle a^{+}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-}(0-),

\displaystyle c^{+}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{+}(0+),

\displaystyle c^{+}_{3}

\displaystyle=p_{2},

and considering the measures

\displaystyle\nu_{1}^{+}(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\frac{1}{1\wedge x}\,\mu^{+}(dx)\text{ on }(0+,\infty),

\displaystyle\nu_{1}^{-}(A)

\displaystyle=\mu^{-}(A)\text{ on }(-\infty,0-)

yields the desired Equation (
4.3
). It only remains to check (
4.2
). This follows from

\displaystyle p_{1}+p_{2}+\mu^{-}([-\infty,0-])+\mu^{+}([0+,\infty])

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\Delta)}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}+\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}+\mu^{-}_{\varepsilon_{n}}((-\infty,0-])+\mu^{+}_{\varepsilon_{n}}((0+,\infty))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\Delta)+1+\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}((-\infty,0-])+\int_{(0+,\infty)}(1\wedge x)\,\nu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(dx)}{K_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\;=\;1\,.

For the case
\lambda_{-}=\infty
, we proceed similarly. We just emphasizes that, following this procedure, we will obtain an equation taking the form

\displaystyle p_{1}f(0-)+p_{2}{\mathsf{L}}f(0-)

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\infty,0-]}\frac{f(x)-f(0-)}{1\wedge-x}\,\mu^{-}(dx)+\int_{[0+,\infty]}(f(x)-f(0-))\,\mu^{+}(dx)\,.

Now, since
\lim_{x\uparrow 0-}\frac{f(x)-f(0-)}{1\wedge-x}=\lim_{x\uparrow 0-}\frac{f(x)-f(0-)}{-x}=-f^{\prime}(0-)
, this leads to the change of sign in the coefficient of
c_{2}^{-}
, as compared to
c_{2}^{+}
in the statement of the lemma.

For the converse, let
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
be the set of functions
f,f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
satisfying Equations (
4.3
) and (
4.3
). We want to show that
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. In fact, as in the proof of Lemma
3.5
, we have that the equation

\beta f-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}=g\,,

(4.8)

for
\beta>0
and
g\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
, has a solution in
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
. Let
f\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
. Since
f,f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
, and
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
, if
f
does not belong to
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, then there exist two different functions
f_{1},f_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
satisfying the same Equation (
4.8
). Consequently,

\beta(f_{1}-f_{2})-\frac{1}{2}(f_{1}^{\prime\prime}-f_{2}^{\prime\prime})=0\quad\text{ on }{\mathbb{G}}.

(4.9)

Now, considering the known solution of (
4.9
) and the fact that
f_{1}-f_{2}
decays to
0
at infinity, we obtain

f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)=\begin{cases}d^{+}\exp(-\sqrt{2\beta}x),\;&\text{ if }x\in[0+,\infty),\\
d^{-}\exp(\sqrt{2\beta}x),\;&\text{ if }x\in(-\infty,0-],\end{cases}

for some
d^{+},d^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
. Here, we used the natural definition
\exp(0+)=\exp(0-)=1
. Since
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are distinct,
d^{+},d^{-}
cannot be both equal to
0
.

Since
f_{1},f_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
, subtracting Equation (
4.3
) derived from
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, and computing
f_{1}-f_{2}
and its derivatives at
0+
or
0-
results in

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}d^{+}+a^{+}d^{+}-a^{+}d^{-}+c_{2}^{+}d^{+}\sqrt{2\beta}+c_{3}^{+}d^{+}\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}\!\!\!d^{+}\Big\{\exp(-\sqrt{2\beta}x)-1\Big\}\,\nu_{1}^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}\!\!\!\!\!\Big\{d^{-}\exp(\sqrt{2\beta}x)-d^{+}\Big\}\,\nu_{1}^{-}(dx).

(4.10)

On the other hand, doing analogous computations using Equation (
4.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}d^{-}+a^{-}d^{-}-a^{-}d^{+}+c_{2}^{-}d^{-}\sqrt{2\beta}+c_{3}^{-}d^{-}\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}\!\!\!\Big\{d^{+}\exp(-\sqrt{2\beta}x)-d^{-}\Big\}\,\nu_{2}^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}\!\!\!\!\!d^{-}\Big\{\exp(\sqrt{2\beta}x)-1\Big\}\,\nu_{2}^{-}(dx).

(4.11)

We will show that these equations cannot be both true. Indeed, suppose that
d^{+}=0
, then
d^{-}\neq 0
, so (
4
) simplifies to

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}+a^{-}+c_{2}^{-}\sqrt{2\beta}+c_{3}^{-}\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}(-1)\,\nu_{2}^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}(\exp(\sqrt{2\beta}x)-1)\,\nu_{2}^{-}(dx),

since both integrands are negative, whereas the constants
c_{1}^{-},a^{-},c^{-}_{2},c_{3}^{-}
are all non-negative, the only possibility for the equation to hold is that all these constants and measures
\nu_{2}^{+}(dx),\nu_{2}^{-}(dx)
are simultaneously zero. However, (
4.3
) holds, leading to a contradiction.

For
d^{-}=0
the argument is similar, so we can assume
d^{+},d^{-}\neq 0
. Without loss of generality suppose
d^{-}\leq d^{+}
, then (
4
) implies

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}+a^{+}(1-\frac{d^{-}}{d^{+}})+c_{2}^{+}\sqrt{2\beta}+c_{3}^{+}\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{(0+,\infty)}\Big\{\exp(-\sqrt{2\beta}x)-1\Big\}\,\nu_{1}^{+}(dx)+\int_{(-\infty,0-)}\Big\{\frac{d^{-}}{d^{+}}\exp(\sqrt{2\beta}x)-1\Big\}\,\nu_{1}^{-}(dx),

and we have the same problem that the left-hand side of the equation is non-negative, whereas the right side is negative, giving the same contradiction as before. Therefore,
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
and the proof is complete.
∎

We now come to the proof of Theorem
2.4
. Its proof is similar to the one of Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Let
W
be a general Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
with boundary conditions at the origin. We want to show that the measures
\nu_{j}^{+}
,
\nu_{j}^{-}
, for
j=1,2
, in Lemma
4.3
are identically zero, which was already shown to be true for
\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}\neq\infty
. To illustrate the argument, let
j=1
. We assume that

\nu_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon,\infty),\nu_{1}^{-}(-\infty,-\varepsilon)>0
, for some
\varepsilon>0
. Take any
f_{1}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
and define a new function
f_{2}
such that
f_{2}=f_{1}
on
[-\varepsilon,0-]\cup[0+,\varepsilon]
, but
f_{2}(x)<f_{1}(x)
for
|x|>\varepsilon
in such a way that
f_{2}
also belongs to
C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
and
f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
. Thus, by Lemma
4.2
, we have that
f_{2}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
and Lemma
4.3
applies to both
f_{1},f_{2}
. Since they agree in a neighborhood of
0+
and
0-
, Equation (
4.3
) reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{(\varepsilon,\infty)}(f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x))\,\nu_{1}^{+}(dx)=-\int_{(-\infty,-\varepsilon)}(f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x))\,\nu_{1}^{-}(dx).

Since both integrands are positive, this leads to

\displaystyle\int_{(\varepsilon,\infty)}(f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x))\,\nu_{1}^{+}(dx)=\int_{(-\infty,-\varepsilon)}(f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x))\,\nu_{1}^{-}(dx)=0

implying that

\displaystyle(f_{1}-f_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{(\varepsilon,\infty)\}}=0\ \nu_{1}^{+}\mbox{-a.e.}\text{ and }(f_{1}-f_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{(-\infty,-\varepsilon)\}}=0\ \nu_{1}^{-}\mbox{-a.e.},

which is not possible since both the measures and the functions are positive on each of their respective intervals. Hence,
\nu_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon,\infty)=\nu_{1}^{-}(-\infty,-\varepsilon)=0
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, it follows that
\nu_{1}^{+}\equiv 0
,
\nu_{1}^{-}\equiv 0
, as desired.

Conversely, let
{\mathsf{L}}
and
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
be as in the statement of the theorem. We apply Theorem
A.1
to show that
{\mathsf{L}}
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup on
C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
, which is therefore a Feller process. An application of Proposition
A.2
will then show that this process corresponds to a general Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
. First, we verify that
{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda-{\mathsf{L}})=C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
. Let
\lambda>0
and
g\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
. Identifying
0+
and
0-
with
0
, consider

f^{+}_{p}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\exp(-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|)g(y)\,dy,

for
x\in[0+,\infty)
, which is a solution of
\lambda f-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}=g
on
[0,\infty)
, and

f^{-}_{p}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\exp(-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|)g(y)\,dy,

for
x\in(-\infty,0-]
, which is also a solution of
\lambda f-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}=g
on
(-\infty,0]
.

Define

f(x)=\begin{cases}f^{+}_{p}(x)+A\exp(-\sqrt{2\lambda}x)\,,&\text{ if }x\in[0+,\infty);\\
f^{-}_{p}(x)+B\exp(\sqrt{2\lambda}x)\,,&\text{ if }x\in(-\infty,0-].\end{cases}

We see that
f,f^{\prime\prime}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
and that
f
satisfies
(\lambda-{\mathsf{L}})f=g
. For
f
to satisfy the domain’s equations

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}f(0+)+a^{+}(f(0+)-f(0-))-c_{2}^{+}f^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}^{+}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ and }

(4.12)

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}f(0-)+a^{-}(f(0-)-f(0+))+c_{2}^{-}f^{\prime}(0-)+\frac{c_{3}^{-}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0-)

\displaystyle=0,

we choose
A,B
so that

\begin{cases}\alpha A+\beta-a^{+}B=0\\
\gamma B+\delta-a^{-}A=0,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=c^{+}_{1}+a^{+}+\sqrt{2\lambda}c^{+}_{2}+\lambda c^{+}_{3};

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{+}f^{+}_{p}(0)+a^{+}(f^{+}_{p}(0)-f^{-}_{p}(0))-c_{2}^{+}(f^{+}_{p})^{\prime}(0)+\frac{c_{3}^{+}}{2}(f^{+}_{p})^{\prime\prime}(0);

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=c^{-}_{1}+a^{-}+\sqrt{2\lambda}c^{-}_{2}+\lambda c^{-}_{3};

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{-}f^{-}_{p}(0)+a^{-}(f^{-}_{p}(0)-f^{+}_{p}(0))-c_{2}^{-}(f^{-}_{p})^{\prime}(0)+\frac{c_{3}^{-}}{2}(f^{-}_{p})^{\prime\prime}(0).

Now, to prove that
{\mathsf{L}}
is dissipative, we take
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
,
f\not\equiv 0
, and
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{G}}
such that
|f(x)|\leq|f(x_{1})|
, for all
x\in{\mathbb{G}}
. If
x_{1}\neq 0+,0-
, since
f
is twice differentiable on
{\mathbb{G}}
, the argument follows as in the case of a general Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}_{\Delta}
.

Otherwise, suppose that
x_{1}=0+
and
f(0+)<0
, then
0+
is actually a point of minimum of
f
, so even though the derivative
f^{\prime}(0+)
is only a right derivative, we still have

f^{\prime}(0+)=\lim_{x\searrow 0+}f^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
, then the first domain’s equation in (
4.12
) implies that
f^{\prime\prime}(0+)\geq 0
, and we get

\|\lambda f-{\mathsf{L}}f\|\geq\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0+)-\lambda f(0+)\geq-\lambda f(0+)\geq\lambda|f(x)|,\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{G}},

thus
\|\lambda f-{\mathsf{L}}f\|\geq\lambda\|f\|
. If
f(0+)>0
, then
0+
is a point of maximum of
f
, and we do the same considerations, noting now that
f^{\prime}(0+)\leq 0
. The argument for
x_{1}=0-
is similar.

To conclude we will show that
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
is dense in
C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
. We proceed in a similar way as we did for
C(\mathbb{R}_{\Delta})
, but taking some precautions since convolutions are well-behaved in
{\mathbb{R}}
and we are working with two half-lines. For
f\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
, we define:

f_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x)=f(x-\varepsilon)\mathbf{1}_{\{x>\varepsilon\}}+f(0+)\mathbf{1}_{\{0+\leq x\leq\varepsilon\}}\,,\quad\text{ for }x\geq 0

and
f_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x)=f_{\varepsilon}^{+}(-x)
for
x\leq 0
. In words, the function
f_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x)
is obtaining by doing a small shift of
f
to the right keeping if constant around zero, and extending it to the full line by reflection. Analogously, we define

f_{\varepsilon}^{-}(x)\;=\;f(x+\varepsilon)\mathbf{1}_{\{x<-\varepsilon\}}+f(0-)\mathbf{1}_{\{-\varepsilon\leq x\leq 0-\}}\quad\text{ for }x\leq 0

and
f_{\varepsilon}^{-}(x)=f_{\varepsilon}^{-}(-x)
for
x\geq 0
.
Taking a standard smooth mollifier
\phi_{\varepsilon}
, define

f_{\varepsilon}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}\;=\;\begin{cases}f_{\varepsilon}^{+}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}(x),&\text{ if }x\in[0+,\infty),\\
f_{\varepsilon}^{-}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}(x),&\text{ if }x\in(-\infty,0^{-}].\\
\end{cases}

The function
f_{\varepsilon}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}
is smooth and decays to zero at infinity. Also,
f_{\varepsilon}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}\to f
uniformly as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and
f_{\varepsilon}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}
is equal to
f(0+)
(respectively, equal to
f(0-)
) in a neighborhood of
0+
(respectively, in a neighborhood of
0-
). In particular, the derivatives of
f_{\varepsilon}\ast\phi_{\varepsilon/2}
at
0+
and
0-
are null.
Now, we choose
P
a polynomial such that

P(x)\;=\;\begin{cases}ax+bx^{2}\,,\text{if }x\in[0+,\infty);\\
\tilde{a}x+\tilde{b}x^{2},\text{if }x\in(-\infty,0-],\end{cases}

for some
\tilde{a},a,\tilde{b},b\in\mathbb{R}
to be properly selected. We also take a bump function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{G}})
so that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\equiv 1
in
(-\varepsilon/2,0-]\cup[0+,\varepsilon/2)
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}\backslash(-\varepsilon,0-]\cup[0+,\varepsilon)
. Thus,
P\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in C({\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta})
as well as its derivatives and

P\varphi_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow 0
uniformly as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, since
P(0\pm)=0
.
A small computation yields that

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}g_{\varepsilon}(0+)+a^{+}(g_{\varepsilon}(0+)-g_{\varepsilon}(0-))-c_{2}^{+}g_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0+)+\frac{c_{3}^{+}}{2}g_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{+}f(0+)+a^{+}(f(0+)-f(0-))-c_{2}^{+}a+c_{3}^{+}b

and

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-}g_{\varepsilon}(0-)+a^{-}(g_{\varepsilon}(0-)-g_{\varepsilon}(0+))+c_{2}^{-}g_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0-)+\frac{c_{3}^{-}}{2}g_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(0-)

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{-}f(0-)+a^{-}(f(0-)-f(0+))+c_{2}^{-}\tilde{a}+c_{3}^{-}\tilde{b}\,.

We can always choose
\tilde{a},a,\tilde{b},b
so that the right hand side of the above equations are equal to zero, since the cases
c^{+}_{2}=c^{+}_{3}=0
and
c_{2}^{-}=c_{3}^{-}=0
are excluded. For that choice,
g_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
and
g_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow f
uniformly as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, completing the proof. Hence, we can apply the Hille-Yosida Theorem
A.1
. Proposition
A.2
implies that the process constructed in that way is a general Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{G}}_{\Delta}
.
∎

Appendix A
Some tools on semigroups and generators

Theorem A.1

(Hille-Yosida Theorem, see Theorem 1.2.6 of
[
13
]
for instance)
.

A linear operator
L
is the generator of a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on a Banach space
{B}
with norm
\|\cdot\|
if and only if

•

{\mathcal{D}}(L)
is dense in
{B}
.

•

L
is dissipative, that is
\|\lambda f-Lf\|\geq\lambda\|f\|
, for every
\lambda>0
and
f\in{\mathcal{D}}(L)
.

•

{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda-{L})
the range of
\lambda-{L}
is equal to
{B}
, for some
\lambda>0
.

We believe that next result is known, but since we could not find a proof in the literature, we provide one below. In plain words, it says that if a process has the same generator as a Brownian motion in an interval
[a,b]
, then it behaves as a Brownian motion in the interval
[a,b]
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\{X_{t}^{x}:t\geq 0\}
be a Feller process on an interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
with generator
{\mathsf{L}}
and domain
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
, where
x\in I
denotes its starting point. Fix an interval
[a,b]\subset I
. Assume that

(a)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, the restriction of functions
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
to the interval
[a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon]
contains the set of functions
g\in C^{2}[a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon]
such that
g^{\prime\prime}(a+\varepsilon)=g^{\prime\prime}(b-\varepsilon)=0
.

(b)

For any
f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
and any
x\in(a,b)
, it holds that
{\mathsf{L}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)
.

Then, for any
x\in[a,b]
, it holds that

\{X_{t\wedge\tau_{a,b}}^{x}:t\geq 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{B_{t\wedge\tau_{a,b}}^{x}:t\geq 0\}\,,

where
B_{t}^{x}
is a standard Brownian motion starting from
x\in[a,b]
and
\tau_{a,b}
is the hitting time of
\{a,b\}
.

Proof.

Dynkin’s martingale associated to
X_{t}^{x}
is

M_{t}=f(X_{t}^{x})-f(X_{0}^{x})-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathsf{L}}f(X_{s})ds\,.

Since
f
and
{\mathsf{L}}f
are bounded, this martingale is uniformly integrable, so the stopped process

\widehat{M}_{t}=f(X_{t\wedge\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}}^{x})-f(X_{0}^{x})-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathsf{L}}f(X_{s\wedge\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}}^{x})ds

is also a martingale, where
\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}
is the hitting time of
\{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon\}
. By the Martingale Problem (see
[
13
, Chapter 4]
) and (a) and (b) above, we conclude that
X_{t\wedge\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}}
has the same generator as
\{B_{t\wedge\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}}^{x}:t\geq 0\}
, whose generator consists of the
C^{2}
-functions whose second derivatives at the boundary are zero, see
[
25
, Example 2.10]
for instance. Hence

\{X_{t\wedge\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}}^{x}:t\geq 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{B_{t\wedge\tau_{a+\varepsilon,b-\varepsilon}}^{x}:t\geq 0\}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. A coupling allows to extend the equality above to
\varepsilon=0
.
∎
