Time delay estimation of coherent structure velocities from a super-position of localized pulses
J. M. Losada, O. E. Garcia

Abstract
This study investigates a novel method for estimating two-dimensional velocities using coarse-grained imaging data, which is particularly relevant for applications in plasma diagnostics. The method utilizes measurements from three non-collinear points and is derived from a stochastic model that describes the propagation of uncorrelated pulses through two-dimensional space. This model builds upon a well-studied one-dimensional model used to analyze turbulence in the scrape-off layer of magnetically confined plasmas. We demonstrate that the method provides exact time delay estimates when applied to a superposition of Gaussian structures and remains accurate for various other pulse functions. Through extensive numerical simulations, we evaluate the method’s performance under different conditions, including variations in signal duration, pulse overlap, spatial and temporal resolution, and the presence of additive noise. Additionally, we investigate the impact of temporal pulse evolution due to linear damping and explore the so-called barberpole effect, which occurs with elongated and tilted structures. Our analysis reveals that the three-point method effectively addresses the limitations encountered with two-point techniques, particularly at coarse spatial resolutions. Although the method is susceptible to the barberpole effect, we analytically demonstrate that this effect does not occur when the elongated structures propagate parallel to one of their axes, and we establish bounds for the associated errors. We propose a series of safeguards to anticipate the applicability of the velocity estimation method, considering factors such as signal length, number of pulses, temporal and spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and pulse size. However, these safeguards do not ensure applicability in cases involving the barberpole effect or correlations between amplitudes and velocities. Overall, our findings provide a comprehensive and robust framework for accurate two-dimensional velocity estimation, enhancing the capabilities of fusion plasma diagnostics and potentially benefiting other fields requiring precise motion analysis.



I
Introduction

The scrape-off layer (SOL), the outermost region of plasma in a magnetic confinement device, plays a critical role in determining the overall performance and stability of fusion reactors
LaBombard et al. 2000
;
Vianello et al. 2020
;
Stagni et al. 2022
. In this region, where the plasma interacts with the surrounding materials, various turbulence phenomena lead to the formation of coherent structures known as filaments or blobs. These blobs, characterized by their high-pressure nature and radial motion, dominate the cross-field particle and heat transport
D’Ippolito et al. 2004
;
Zweben et al. 2007
. Understanding the dynamics of plasma blobs is key for developing plasma regimes compatible with continuous, steady-state operation of fusion reactors.

Stochastic modelling has become a commonly used tool to describe the complex dynamics in the SOL
Garcia 2012
;
Kube and Garcia 2015
;
Garcia et al. 2016
;
Garcia and Theodorsen 2017
;
Theodorsen et al. 2017a
;
Militello and Omotani 2016a
;
Militello and Omotani 2016b
;
Theodorsen and Garcia 2018a
;
Theodorsen and Garcia 2018b
;
Ahmed et al. 2023a
. These models have demonstrated significant utility in accurately capturing and interpreting experimental data, providing a framework to describe the statistical properties and turbulent nature of the plasma dynamics observed in the SOL
Garcia et al. 2013a
;
Garcia et al. 2013b
;
Garcia et al. 2015
;
Kube et al. 2016a
;
Garcia et al. 2017
;
Theodorsen et al. 2017b
;
Walkden et al. 2017
;
Theodorsen et al. 2018
;
Kube et al. 2018
;
Bencze et al. 2019
;
Kuang et al. 2019
;
Kube et al. 2020
;
Zweben et al. 2022
;
Zurita et al. 2022
;
Ahmed et al. 2023b
. In this work, we extend a well-studied stochastic model to two spatial dimensions, capturing the more complex interactions and dynamics present in real experimental conditions. This is equivalent to the model presented in Ref.
Militello et al. 2018
. This model facilitates better descriptions of plasma behaviour and supports the development of more effective analysis tools for plasma diagnostics.

In particular, we are interested in developing techniques for velocity estimation on coarse-grained imaging data, such as avalanche photodiode gas-puff imaging (GPI)
Cziegler et al. 2010
;
Zweben et al. 2017
;
Offeddu et al. 2022a
;
Terry et al. 2024
or beam emission spectroscopy
McKee et al. 1999
;
Wang et al. 2023
. This diagnostic is characterized by its high temporal resolution but relatively low spatial resolution. Some traditional velocimetry techniques are based on two-point time delay estimation
Grulke et al. 2006
;
Ghim et al. 2012
;
Cziegler et al. 2013
;
Enters et al. 2023
;
Diab et al. 2024
. These techniques lead to systematic errors when the velocity of the flow is not aligned with the two points used for the estimation
Brotankova et al. 2009
;
Fedorczak et al. 2012
;
Brandt et al. 2016
;
Sierchio et al. 2016
. More advanced methods based on two-dimensional cross-correlation functions are typically applied when higher spatial resolution is available
Terry et al. 2005
;
Zweben et al. 2015
;
Zweben et al. 2011
;
Lampert et al. 2021
;
Agostini et al. 2011
. Other methods have been studied based on dynamic programming
Kriete et al. 2018
;
Enters et al. 2023
and blob tracking
Kube et al. 2013
;
Zweben et al. 2016
;
Offeddu et al. 2022b
. These methods are not applicable to the low spatial resolutions of avalanche photodiode GPI
Sierchio et al. 2016
. A three-point velocity estimation method has been demonstrated in the case of propagating waves
Brandt et al. 2016
;
Losada et al. 2024a
.

In this work, we apply a stochastic model to analytically demonstrate that the three-point velocity estimation technique gives exact results for a superposition of uncorrelated, Gaussian structures. We test this technique on synthetic data to study its limits of applicability and the effect of signal duration, degree of pulse overlap, spatial and temporal resolution, broad distributions of velocities, noise, elongated and tilted pulses, time-dependent amplitudes and correlations between pulse velocities and amplitudes. We show that this technique is not subject to the dimensionality limitation of two-point techniques and that it is applicable to coarse spatial resolutions.

Another issue many velocity estimation techniques are subject to is the so-called barberpole effect. This effect appears when a structure such as a blob or a wavefront propagates in a direction not aligned with its normal. This effect can, for example, lead to spurious horizontal velocities even if the structures propagate only vertically
Fedorczak et al. 2012
. The proposed three-point method is subject to the barberpole effect, however, we show that the errors associated with this are bounded and lower than those under traditional two-point techniques.

V

Losada 2023
;
Decristoforo and Losada 2023

II
A superposition of pulses in two dimensions

In this section, we describe a stochastic model describing the propagation of uncorrelated structures in two dimensions. This model is similar to that described in Ref.
Militello et al. 2018
. We consider a process given by a superposition of pulses

\Phi_{K}(x,y,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{k}(x,y-\upsilon_{k},t-s_{k}).

(1)

Here
x
and
y
are spatial coordinates, which we will refer to as the horizontal and vertical coordinates, respectively. Each pulse
\phi_{k}
arrives at
x=0
,
y=\upsilon_{k}
at time
t=s_{k}
, where
\upsilon_{k}
and
s_{k}
are random variables, uniformly distributed on
[-L/2,L/2]
and
[-T/2,T/2]
, respectively.
K
is the total number of pulses in a given realization of the process.

In order to keep the average waiting time between pulse arrivals constant, we assume that the number of pulses scales with the process duration
T
and the vertical domain size
L
as
K=TL/\mathcal{R}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is a rate parameter in units of distance times time. We can compute the expected value of any function
f_{k}(s_{k},\upsilon_{k},\ldots)
where the dots indicate other possible random variables such as sizes or shape parameters. We neglect end effects by taking the limits
T,L\rightarrow\infty
, which give the average

{\left<\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(\upsilon_{k},s_{k},\ldots)\right>}=\frac{TL}{\mathcal{R}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\text{d}s_{k}}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\text{d}\upsilon_{k}}{L}{\left<f_{k}(\upsilon_{k},s_{k},\ldots)\right>}=\frac{{\left<f_{k}(\upsilon_{k},s_{k},\ldots)\right>}}{\mathcal{R}}.

(2)

The average
{\left<\cdot\right>}
is to be performed over
s_{k}
,
\upsilon_{k}
and any other random variables.

The parameter
\mathcal{R}
is the inverse of the density of pulses appearing in the
(y-t)
-space. In particular,
\tau_{\text{w}}=\mathcal{R}/L
is the average waiting time between pulses arriving at any vertical coordinate. For later reference, we introduce the normalized process

\widetilde{\Phi}(x,y,t)=\frac{\Phi(x,y,t)-{\left<\Phi\right>}}{\Phi_{\text{rms}}},

(3)

where
{\left<\Phi\right>}
and
\Phi_{\text{rms}}
denote the mean value of the process and the root mean square value, which both will be later shown to be independent of the space and time coordinates.

II.1
Pulse evolution

We assume that each pulse
\phi_{k}
has an amplitude
a_{k}
and moves with constant velocity
u=\sqrt{v^{2}+w^{2}}
, where
v
and
w
are the horizontal and vertical velocity components, respectively. Each pulse has a tilt angle
\alpha
with respect to the horizontal axis. We denote
\theta_{\shortparallel}
and
\theta_{\perp}
as the normalized coordinates along a frame aligned and traveling with the pulse. An illustration is provided in Fig.
1
. We write the pulse as

\phi_{k}(x,y,t)=a_{k}\varphi\left(\theta_{\shortparallel}(x,y,t),\theta_{\perp}(x,y,t)\right),

(4)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{\shortparallel}(x,y,t)=\frac{(x-vt)\cos\alpha+(y-wt)\sin\alpha}{\ell_{\shortparallel}},

(5a)

\displaystyle\theta_{\perp}(x,y,t)=\frac{-(x-vt)\sin\alpha+(y-wt)\cos\alpha}{\ell_{\perp}}.

(5b)

This describes a pulse centered at
(x,y)=(0,0)
at time
t=0
propagating with velocity components
v
and
w
in the
x
- and
y
-direction, respectively. The pulse sizes along its axes are denoted by
\ell_{\shortparallel}
and
\ell_{\perp}
. Equations (
5
) can be written in terms of the velocity angle
\beta=\arctan(w/v)
and the velocity magnitude
u
as

\displaystyle\theta_{\shortparallel}(x,y,t)=\frac{x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha}{\ell_{\shortparallel}}-\frac{ut}{\ell_{\shortparallel}}\cos(\alpha-\beta),

(6a)

\displaystyle\theta_{\perp}(x,y,t)=\frac{-x\sin\alpha+y\cos\alpha}{\ell_{\perp}}+\frac{ut}{\ell_{\perp}}\sin(\alpha-\beta),

(6b)

showing that the perpendicular coordinate
\theta_{\perp}(x,y,t)
becomes time-independent when
\alpha=\beta
. Moreover, we assume the pulse function
\varphi
to be separable,

\varphi(\theta_{\shortparallel},\theta_{\perp})=\varphi_{\shortparallel}(\theta_{\shortparallel})\varphi_{\perp}(\theta_{\perp}).

(7)

In the case
\alpha=\beta
, see Fig.
1
, the pulse is tilted in the direction it propagates. In this case,
\ell_{\shortparallel}
is the pulse size observed by a fixed measurement point, which would only record the pulse variation from
\theta_{\shortparallel}
. The case
\alpha=\beta
will be taken as the base case from which the estimation method is derived. Deviations from this will lead to the barberpole effect and will be considered in Sec.
IV.9
.

The pulse autocorrelation function is defined as

\rho_{\shortparallel}(\triangle_{\theta})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\theta\varphi_{\shortparallel}(\theta)\varphi_{\shortparallel}(\theta+\triangle_{\theta}),

(8a)

\rho_{\perp}(\triangle_{\theta})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\theta\varphi_{\perp}(\theta)\varphi_{\perp}(\theta+\triangle_{\theta}),

(8b)

and the moments of the pulse function,
I_{\shortparallel,n}
and
I_{\perp,n}
, are defined as

I_{\shortparallel,n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\theta\varphi_{\shortparallel}^{n}(\theta),

(9a)

I_{\perp,n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\theta\varphi_{\perp}^{n}(\theta).

(9b)

In the following, we will assume the pulse function to be Gaussian,

\varphi_{\shortparallel}(\theta)=\varphi_{\perp}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp(-\theta^{2}),

(10)

in which case the pulse auto-correlation function becomes

\rho_{\shortparallel}(\triangle_{\theta})=\rho_{\perp}(\triangle_{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{\triangle_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\right).

(11)

Other pulse functions are considered in Appendix
B
.

In the following, we assume the pulse sizes
\ell_{\shortparallel}
and
\ell_{\perp}
and velocities
v
and
w
to be the same for all pulses unless otherwise stated. This is straightforward to generalize to a distribution of the pulse parameters, as any statistical quantity obtained for the degenerate case can be generalized to a distribution by averaging over the relevant random variables.

Figure 1
:
Left: Tilted pulse, where the pulse orientation
\alpha
does not correspond to the velocity direction,
\alpha\neq\beta=\text{arctan}(w/v)
. Right: Aligned pulse, where the pulse orientation
\alpha
is aligned with the velocity direction,
\alpha=\beta=\text{arctan}(w/v)
.

II.2
Mean and variance

The average of the process can now be computed using Eq. (
2
),

{\left<\Phi\right>}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}{\left<\phi_{k}(x,y-\upsilon_{k},t-s_{k})\right>}.

(12)

Writing it in terms of the pulse function in Eq. (
4
) and using that the amplitudes
a_{k}
are independent of arrival times
s_{k}
and arrival positions
\upsilon_{k}
we have

\displaystyle{\left<\Phi\right>}=\frac{{\left<a_{k}\right>}}{\mathcal{R}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}s_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\upsilon_{k}\,\varphi\left(\theta_{\shortparallel}(x,y-\upsilon_{k},t-s_{k}),\theta_{\perp}(x,y-\upsilon_{k},t-s_{k})\right)

(13)

We proceed by changing the integration variables to
\theta_{\shortparallel}
and
\theta_{\perp}
. The Jacobian determinant of the variable transformation is given by
v/\ell_{\shortparallel}\ell_{\perp}
and thus the mean value of the process is given by

{\left<\Phi\right>}=\frac{{\left<a\right>}\ell_{\shortparallel}\ell_{\perp}}{\mathcal{R}v}I_{\shortparallel,1}I_{\perp,1}.

(14)

Note that Eq. (
14
) is independent of the vertical velocity component
w
. Following a similar procedure we can compute the variance of the process to obtain

\Phi_{\text{rms}}^{2}={\left<(\Phi-{\left<\Phi\right>})^{2}\right>}=\frac{\langle{a^{2}}\rangle\ell_{\shortparallel}\ell_{\perp}}{\mathcal{R}v}I_{\shortparallel,2}I_{\perp,2}.

(15)

This has the same velocity dependence as the mean value. For higher velocities, both quantities become smaller, as the pulses spend less time at any given location. Larger pulses will lead to larger mean and variance values, as expected. Increasing the rate parameter
\mathcal{R}
, which is equivalent to reducing the number of pulses, leads to a smaller mean value and variance. In contrast, the relative fluctuation level is given by

\frac{\Phi_{\text{rms}}}{{\left<\Phi\right>}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{R}v}{\ell_{\shortparallel}\ell_{\perp}}}\sqrt{\frac{\langle{a^{2}}\rangle}{{\left<a\right>}^{2}}}\frac{\sqrt{I_{\shortparallel,2}I_{\perp,2}}}{I_{\shortparallel,1}I_{\perp,1}}.

(16)

This shows that, for fixed pulse sizes and shapes, the relative fluctuation level increases with the rate parameter
\mathcal{R}
and the horizontal velocity component
v
.

II.3
Cross-correlation function

The cross-correlation function of the process for a horizontal lag
\triangle_{x}
, vertical lag
\triangle_{y}
and temporal lag
\triangle_{t}
, is defined as

R_{\Phi}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y},\triangle_{t})={\left<\Phi(x,y,t)\Phi(x+\triangle_{x},y+\triangle_{y},t+\triangle_{t})\right>}.

(17)

In Appendix
A
, we show that in the case of Gaussian pulses, the cross-correlation function of the normalized process defined by Eq. (
3
) becomes

R_{\widetilde{\Phi}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y},\triangle_{t})=\exp\left(-\frac{\triangle_{\theta_{\shortparallel}}^{2}+\triangle_{\theta_{\perp}}^{2}}{2}\right),

(18)

where

\triangle_{\theta_{\shortparallel}}=\frac{\triangle_{x}-v\triangle_{t}}{\ell_{\shortparallel}}\cos\alpha+\frac{\triangle_{y}-w\triangle_{t}}{\ell_{\shortparallel}}\sin\alpha,\\

(19a)

\triangle_{\theta_{\perp}}=-\frac{\triangle_{x}-v\triangle_{t}}{\ell_{\perp}}\sin\alpha+\frac{\triangle_{y}-w\triangle_{t}}{\ell_{\perp}}\cos\alpha.

(19b)

This provides an analytical expression for the cross-correlation function in the case of Gaussian pulses. In Sec.
III
, we will employ this expression to justify the three-point velocity estimation method. Equations (
19
) can be rewritten in terms of
v=u\cos\beta
and
w=u\sin\beta
as

\triangle_{\theta_{\shortparallel}}=\frac{\triangle_{x}\cos\alpha+\triangle_{y}\sin\alpha}{\ell_{\shortparallel}}-\frac{u\triangle_{t}}{\ell_{\shortparallel}}\cos(\alpha-\beta),\\

(20a)

\triangle_{\theta_{\perp}}=\frac{-\triangle_{x}\sin\alpha+\triangle_{y}\cos\alpha}{\ell_{\perp}}+\frac{u\triangle_{t}}{\ell_{\perp}}\sin(\alpha-\beta),\\

(20b)

Therefore, for the base case in which the pulse is tilted along the direction of propagation,
\alpha=\beta
, we have that the dependence on the time lag
\triangle_{t}
appears only on the parallel component
\triangle_{\theta_{\shortparallel}}
.

III
Time delay estimation method

III.1
Aligned measurement grids

In this section, we describe a method for velocity estimation based on the process recorded in a coarse array of measurement points. The basic setup is illustrated in Fig.
2
. Consider three spatially separated measurement points,
P_{0}
,
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
. We take
P_{x}
to be separated by a horizontal distance
\Delta_{x}
from
P_{0}
and
P_{y}
to be separated by a vertical distance
\Delta_{y}
from
P_{0}
. A generalization of this to pixel grids that are not orthogonal and not aligned with the coordinate system is straightforward and provided in Sec.
III.2
.

Figure 2
:
Basic setup for three-point velocity estimation: a reference point
P_{0}
and two non-aligned points
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
.

We denote the estimated cross-correlation functions between the signals measured at
P_{0}
and
P_{x}
and between the signals measured at
P_{0}
and
P_{y}
by
\widehat{R}_{x}(\triangle_{t})
and
\widehat{R}_{y}(\triangle_{t})
, respectively. Maximization of these cross-correlation functions with respect to the time lag
\triangle_{t}
will yield time lags
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{y}
, respectively. Assuming that the pulses are tilted in the direction of propagation,
\alpha=\beta
, we demonstrate in Appendix
A
that the time lag
\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})
that maximizes the model cross-correlation function, Eq. (
18
), is independent of the sizes
\ell_{\shortparallel}
and
\ell_{\perp}
,

\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})=\frac{v\triangle_{x}+w\triangle_{y}}{v^{2}+w^{2}}.

(21)

In particular, for
\triangle_{y}=0
the cross-correlation function is maximum for time lag
{\tau}_{x}=v\triangle_{x}/(v^{2}+w^{2})
, while for
\triangle_{x}=0
the cross-correlation function is maximum for time lag
{\tau}_{y}=w\triangle_{y}/(v^{2}+w^{2})
. To give the velocity component estimates, these relations can be inverted with respect to
v
and
w
,

\widehat{v}=\frac{\Delta_{x}/\tau_{x}}{1+\left(\frac{\Delta_{x}/\tau_{x}}{\Delta_{y}/\tau_{y}}\right)^{2}},

(22a)

\widehat{w}=\frac{\Delta_{y}/\tau_{y}}{1+\left(\frac{\Delta_{y}/\tau_{y}}{\Delta_{x}/\tau_{x}}\right)^{2}}.

(22b)

In Sec.
IV
, we will apply these relations to estimate the velocity components from realizations of the process to assess the validity and limits of the method. In the case
\alpha\neq\beta
, an error will be caused by the barberpole effect if
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}\neq 1
. We study the extent of these errors in Secs.
IV.8
and
IV.9
.

III.2
Non-aligned measurement grids

The above treatment is simplified by assuming that the measurement points are aligned with the coordinate axes, that is,
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
are separated from
P_{0}
horizontally and vertically, respectively. We present the results this way since that is the case for many diagnostics and results in simpler expressions for the estimates. The generalization to non-orthogonal measurement grids, such as depicted in Fig.
3
, is straightforward. Equation (
21
) can be evaluated for any two given pairs of points and the time lags for maximum cross-correlation can be inverted with respect to the velocity components leading to the velocity estimates

\widehat{v}=\frac{(\tau_{2}y_{1}-\tau_{1}y_{2})(x_{2}y_{1}-x_{1}y_{2})}{(\tau_{2}x_{1}-\tau_{1}x_{2})^{2}+(\tau_{2}y_{1}-\tau_{1}y_{2})^{2}},\\

(23a)

\widehat{w}=\frac{(\tau_{2}x_{1}-\tau_{1}x_{2})(x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})}{(\tau_{2}x_{1}-\tau_{1}x_{2})^{2}+(\tau_{2}y_{1}-\tau_{1}y_{2})^{2}}.

(23b)

The numerator appearing in both components expresses the requirement that the measurement points should not be aligned, (
x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}\neq 0
), as otherwise, the time delays
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
would provide redundant information.

IV.1
Signal duration

We start by considering the effect of the signal duration
T
while keeping the average pulse density
T/K=\tau_{\text{d}}
fixed. The results for the mean-squared error are shown in Fig.
4

T/\tau_{\text{d}}>10^{2}
. This can be interpreted as follows: enough pulses,
\sim 10

L/\ell=10

K>10L/\ell=10^{2}

T/K=\tau_{\text{d}}
, this translates into
T/\tau_{\text{d}}>10^{2}
, which is consistent with the plotted results. In order to verify this interpretation, we made a series of simulations with
L/\ell=10^{2}

V
Discussion and conclusions

The transport of particles and heat in the scrape-off layer of magnetically confined plasmas is dominated by the propagation of coherent pulses of enhanced plasma density. We have investigated a model describing the fluctuations resulting from a superposition of pulses propagating in two dimensions. This model is an extension of models extensively studied in zero- and one-space dimensions
Garcia 2012
;
Kube and Garcia 2015
;
Garcia et al. 2016
;
Garcia and Theodorsen 2017
;
Theodorsen et al. 2017a
;
Militello and Omotani 2016a
;
Militello and Omotani 2016b
;
Theodorsen and Garcia 2018a
;
Theodorsen and Garcia 2018b
;
Ahmed et al. 2023a
;
Losada et al. 2023
;
Losada et al. 2024b
.

In the case of Gaussian pulses, a degenerate distribution of pulse velocities, and uncorrelated pulse amplitudes and velocities, an expression for the cross-correlation function can be obtained analytically. From this expression, it is straightforward to compute the time
\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})
that maximizes the cross-correlation function. This time depends on the underlying two-dimensional velocity field. Thus, two independent estimations of
\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})
can determine the velocity components. A minimum of three non-aligned measurement points is required in order to obtain two independent estimations of
\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})
, and so, the proposed method is referred to as a three-point velocity estimation method. Given the low number of measurement points required for the estimation, this method is particularly useful in the case of coarse-grained imaging diagnostics, such as avalanche photodiode gas-puff imaging
Cziegler et al. 2010
;
Zweben et al. 2017
;
Offeddu et al. 2022a
;
Terry et al. 2024
or beam emission spectroscopy
McKee et al. 1999
;
Wang et al. 2023
, where more robust velocity estimation methods do not apply due to the low spatial resolution
Sierchio et al. 2016
.

We studied the performance of the method on synthetic data for a wide range of scenarios. Firstly, our findings show that the signal duration should be at least long enough to contain a significant amount of pulses. The method works for any degree of pulse overlap. Next, we find that the separation between measurement points should be at least the distance travelled by the pulses during a sampling time regardless of the direction of motion. In practical cases, this can be verified by ensuring that the times maximizing the cross-correlation functions are larger than the sampling times. Additionally, we find that the distance between measurement points must be small enough to ensure the correlation is high enough to estimate the maximum of the cross-correlation function reliably. In the case of Gaussian pulses, this implies a separation shorter than approximately two pulse sizes. This can be verified by assuring that the value of the cross-correlation maxima is sufficiently high. In the case of synthetic data, this value is set at
0.25
, but higher values may be required for experimental data. Similarly, we find that the temporal resolution should be smaller than the time the pulses spend to travel between measurement points,
\Delta_{t}>\tau_{\text{d}}
. We find that the method is also robust when the pulse velocities are randomly distributed, providing the correct mean velocity values even in cases where the broadness of the velocity distribution is of order unity. Next, we find that the method is robust against additive noise, providing correct estimates up to noise-to-signal ratios of approximately
5
. Additionally, we have investigated the case where the pulses have a temporal amplitude decay modeled as a linear damping
Losada et al. 2023
. We found that the method is robust against temporal variations of the pulse amplitudes leading to no effect in the velocity estimation. Lastly, a distribution of pulse sizes has also been investigating and has no effect in the velocity estimation results.

Since the method is based on time-delay estimation from cross-correlation functions, we expect the velocities of larger-amplitude events to contribute more to the resulting velocity estimates, as large-amplitude pulses have a larger contribution to the cross-correlation function. We find that this is indeed the case when velocities are correlated with amplitudes, as shown by Fig.
10
, but only relevant for broadly distributed velocities. A case with exponentially distributed amplitudes and velocities
v_{k}/{\left<v\right>}=a_{k}/{\left<a\right>}
and
w_{k}=0
was also considered leading to large errors of up to
\text{MSE}\sim 4
. This is due to the contribution of arbitrarily high-velocity pulses with a corresponding high amplitude. We note that theoretical blob scaling regimes often show a saturation of the velocity scaling with the amplitudes, rendering such very high-velocity blobs, nonphysical
Garcia et al. 2005
;
Garcia et al. 2006
;
Kube and Garcia 2011
;
Easy et al. 2014
;
Wiesenberger et al. 2014
;
Held et al. 2016
;
Olsen et al. 2016
;
Walkden et al. 2016
;
Kube et al. 2016b
;
Wiesenberger et al. 2017
.

When pulses are elongated,
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}\neq 1
, and tilted
\alpha\neq\beta
, the so-called barberpole effect can arise
Fedorczak et al. 2012
. In the cases that
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}\gg 1
we find a large unbiased error. This error can be understood by the fact that small values of
\ell_{\perp}
make the signals recorded at different measurement points less correlated. In addition, large values of
\ell_{\shortparallel}
lead to flatter cross-correlation maxima, making accurate estimation of such maxima more prone to errors. If the pulses do not propagate in the direction perpendicular to one of their axes, then the barberpole effect becomes the main source of error. In the worst case, this can lead up to almost unity biased
MSE
, as shown in Fig.
12
. The onset of the error, that is, the maximum tilting angle that renders the method still reliable depends strongly on the aspect ratio
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}
. For
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}=1/4
the method is robust against tilting angles, remaining reliable (
\text{MSE}<0.1
), up to tilting angles
\theta\approx\pi/8
. On the other hand, for
\ell_{\perp}/\ell_{\shortparallel}=4
, small tilt angles lead to large errors. In Appendix
C
we examine the deviations of the estimates obtained with the two-point velocity estimation method in the presence of a barberpole effect. A general comparison between the two- and three-point methods was already studied in the absence of the barberpole effect
Losada et al. 2024a
. Our findings show a possible use case for the two-point method: if prior knowledge of the system ensures that the structures move parallel to the separation between the two measurement points, applying the two-point method to estimate the velocity component in that direction will yield accurate results and remain unaffected by barberpole effects.

The results presented, indicating that the method is more robust for cases when the ratio
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}<1
, should be contrasted with theoretical and numerical studies of blob dynamics. Blob theory suggests that as these coherent structures propagate through the SOL, they undergo a deformation process known as front steepening. This phenomenon leads to a reduction of spatial scales along the direction of propagation while simultaneously elongating the blob in the perpendicular direction
Garcia et al. 2006
;
Garcia et al. 2005
;
Kube and Garcia 2011
;
Bian et al. 2003
;
Aydemir 2005
;
Madsen et al. 2011
. Therefore, unfavourable elongations
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}>1
may not be a concern in the case of blob propagation in the SOL.

The parameter studies conducted in this work highlight the prospects and limitations of the estimation method across different model parameter ranges. In practical applications, it is often unclear whether the data meets the method’s requirements a priori. A common check involves setting a minimum threshold for the cross-correlation maximum, below which no estimation is performed. In all considered cases, we have indicated a grey-shaded region where the synthetic data fails to meet this requirement with a minimum threshold for the cross-correlation maximum of
0.25
. Our findings indicate that this requirement ensures the data meets model parameter needs in scenarios with low spatial resolution, high noise-to-signal ratio, and large
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}
values. However, this requirement does not account for other model parameter deviations. Therefore, we propose an additional check based on the maximum time-delay
\max(\tau_{x},\tau_{y})
used in the velocity estimation method. We require this maximum to be larger than the sampling time
\Delta_{t}
. We show that, in the cases of high spatial resolution and low temporal resolution, this requirement ensures that the data fulfills the right model parameter requirements.

Thus, we implement the following safeguards: (i) a minimum threshold value for the cross-correlation function maxima, (ii) a minimum threshold for
\max(\tau_{x},\tau_{y})
related to the sampling time, and (iii) that the signal duration is sufficiently long to contain a significant amount of pulses (
>10
). We have here demonstrated that these criteria ensure that the data falls within the appropriate parameter space for a wide range of model parameters. However, there are still scenarios where these two requirements are insufficient to guarantee the fulfillment of the model parameter conditions, such as broadness in the velocity distribution, pulse tilting and correlations between amplitudes and velocities.

In conclusion, the three-point velocity estimation method offers a robust and efficient approach for determining velocity components of a superposition of pulses propagating in two dimensions, even when faced with challenging scenarios such as low spatial resolution, noise, and broad velocity distributions. Our study highlights the key limitations of the method, particularly the effects of elongated and tilted pulses, where the barberpole effect can introduce significant errors. By applying minimum thresholds on the cross-correlation maxima and time-delay estimates, we ensure that the method remains reliable across a wide range of parameter spaces. However, special care must be taken in cases with strong pulse elongation or tilting. In other contributions, we will demonstrate the successful application of this improved time delay estimation method on experimental measurement data.

where
\triangle_{\theta_{\shortparallel}}
and
\triangle_{\theta_{\perp}}
are defined by Eqs. (
34a
) and (
34b
). The time lag maximizing Eq. (
38
), for an arbitrary tilt angle
\alpha
, is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})=

\displaystyle\frac{{(\triangle_{x}\ell_{\perp}^{2}v+\triangle_{y}\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}w)\cos^{2}\alpha-(\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}-\ell_{\perp}^{2})(\triangle_{y}v+\triangle_{x}w)\cos\alpha\sin\alpha+(\triangle_{x}\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}v+\triangle_{y}\ell_{\perp}^{2}w)\sin^{2}\alpha}}{{(\ell_{\perp}^{2}v^{2}+\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}w^{2})\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin\alpha(-2(\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}-\ell_{\perp}^{2})vw\cos\alpha+(\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}v^{2}+\ell_{\perp}^{2}w^{2})\sin\alpha)}}.

(39)

It can also be written in terms of
\beta=\arctan w/v
and
u=\sqrt{v^{2}+w^{2}}
as

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})=

\displaystyle\frac{\triangle_{x}(\ell_{\perp}^{2}-\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2})\cos(2\alpha-\beta)+\triangle_{x}(\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}+\ell_{\perp}^{2})\cos\beta+\triangle_{y}(\ell_{\perp}^{2}-\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2})\sin(2\alpha-\beta)+\triangle_{y}(\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}+\ell_{\perp}^{2})\sin\beta}{u(\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2}+\ell_{\perp}^{2}+(\ell_{\perp}^{2}-\ell_{\shortparallel}^{2})\cos[2(\alpha-\beta)])}.

(40)

Equations (
A
) and (
A
) give a general expression for the time maximizing the cross-correlation function resulting from a superposition of elongated pulses with arbitrary velocities. These expressions simplify in the case that
\ell_{\shortparallel}=\ell_{\perp}
, as then the system becomes independent of the tilt angle
\alpha
. More importantly, in the case that the tilt angle
\alpha
aligns with the pulse propagation
\alpha=\beta
,
\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})
becomes

\tau_{\text{max}}(\triangle_{x},\triangle_{y})=\frac{1}{u}\left(\triangle_{x}\cos\beta+\triangle_{y}\sin\beta\right)=\frac{\triangle_{x}v+\triangle_{y}w}{v^{2}+w^{2}}.

(41)

This result applies even if
\ell_{\shortparallel}\neq\ell_{\perp}
, showing that the time lag maximizing the cross-correlation function becomes independent of the sizes
\ell_{\shortparallel}
and
\ell_{\perp}
when
\alpha=\beta
. This is the result used in Sec.
III.1
.

The velocity estimation method studied in this contribution has been derived analytically for Gaussian pulse functions. There is no theoretical reason nor experimental observation that justifies the assumption of Gaussian pulses other than mathematical simplicity. Appendix C
Barberpole effect on the two-point method

Here we study some properties of the two-point velocity estimation method. This technique estimates the velocity components from the time delays in the following way:

\displaystyle\widehat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{x}}{\tau_{x}},

(42a)

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{y}}{\tau_{y}},

(42b)

where
\Delta_{x}
,
\Delta_{y}
,
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{y}
are defined in Sec.
III
. In general, this method will give wrong estimates for an arbitrary velocity direction. In the case that pulses are tilted in the direction of propagation,
\alpha=\beta
, it can be shown that the two-point method estimates deviate by a factor
\widehat{v}_{2}/v=1+w^{2}/v^{2}
and
\widehat{w}_{2}/w=1+v^{2}/w^{2}

Losada et al. 2024a
. Only in the case that the velocity is aligned with one of the separations from which
\tau_{x}
and
\tau_{y}
are estimated, will this method provide a good estimate for that velocity component.

Here we are interested in quantifying the deviation of these estimates from the true velocities in the case of elongated and tilted pulses, that is, when the barberpole effect is present. For a given ratio
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}
and assuming
\Delta_{x}=\Delta_{y}=\Delta
as before, this deviation can be computed analytically with Eq. (
A
). The results are shown in Fig.
15
for the two-point method (solid lines) and the three-point method (dashed lines) in the case that the true velocities are
v=1
and
w=0
for
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}=1/4
and
4
. The curves for the three-point method are equivalent to those presented in Fig.
12
. Note that the two-point method always gives the correct estimate for the horizontal velocity components
\widehat{v}
. This is expected as the true velocity is indeed only horizontal. The barberpole effect does not affect this estimate. On the other hand, the vertical component, which would be estimated to be infinity
\widehat{w}_{2}=\infty
in the absence of a barberpole effect, is now finite for
\alpha\neq 0
. Both for the two- and three-point methods, the curves for the case
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}=1/4
are displaced at an angle
\pm\pi/2
with respect to the case
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}=4
. Indeed, a pulse with
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}=1/4
and
\alpha=0
is equivalent to a pulse with
\ell_{\shortparallel}/\ell_{\perp}=4
and
\alpha=\pm\pi/2
.
These results showcase an interesting property of the two-point method: if prior knowledge of the system can guarantee that the pulses move parallel to the direction separating two measurement points, the two-point method will estimate the correct velocity even in the presence of the barberpole effect.
