Progranulin genetic variant rs5848 displays ancestry-specific associations with Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Benjamin E Life, Erick I Navarro-Delgado, Oriol Fornes, Jan M Friedman, Wyeth W Wasserman, Keegan Korthauer, Blair R Leavitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProgranulin is a molecular modifier of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, its cis-regulatory region remains under-characterized. Association studies of genetic variants in this region with AD have obtained conflicting results across different human populations.MethodsWe define the cis-regulatory region of the progranulin gene (GRN). Querying the genetically admixed Alzheimer’s Disease Sequencing Project whole-genome sequencing dataset (n = 9,416 cases; 13,603 controls), we conduct ancestry-specific analyses of AD association with GRN genetic variants.ResultsWe report predicted impacts of genetic variation on transcription factor binding for 150 unique transcription factors across the GRN cis-regulatory region. We identify 99 common variants (MAF > 0.005) in one or more superpopulations (African, Admixed American, East Asian, European, and South Asian). The common GRN variant rs5848 is significantly associated with AD only in East Asian ancestry.ConclusionsAncestry-specific effects may contribute to discordant findings in the literature. European-centric results in AD might not be generalizable to other populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00958-3.Keywords: Local ancestry, Cis-regulatory elements, Neurodegeneration, Neurodegenerative disease, Regulation, GRN

Background

Progranulin is widely expressed, multi-functional, and robustly implicated in neurodegenerative disease. Here we use “GRN” to refer to the progranulin gene, “progranulin protein” to describe the protein, and “progranulin” when discussing both the gene and the protein. Loss of expression of a single copy of GRN is a major genetic cause of frontotemporal dementia (FTD) [1–3]. Loss of both copies of GRN causes neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis type 11, a rare and severe adolescent onset lysosomal storage disorder [4]. Partial reduction of progranulin protein expression (~ 70% of wild type) has been associated with increased risk of FTD, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and Parkinson’s disease (PD) [5, 6]. Additionally, increased progranulin protein expression (> 100% of wild type) has been suggested to have a neuroprotective effect in animal studies [7–10], though this remains to be assessed in clinical studies. In concert, these data support a model where variation in progranulin expression can modify neurodegenerative disease risk. However, characterization of the GRN transcriptional landscape has been limited to date, and the study of GRN genetic variation effects has been restricted almost entirely to exonic regions. Hence, the lack of characterization of the GRN cis-regulatory landscape represents an important knowledge gap for the field.

While the majority of reported pathological GRN variants cause premature termination and loss of function alleles, a single common variant in the GRN 3’ untranslated region, rs5848 C > T, has been associated with increased risk of FTD, AD, and PD [5, 6]. Several studies of this variant have suggested that carriers of the minor allele exhibit reduced serum progranulin protein expression [5, 11–14], supporting the progranulin insufficiency model. Mechanistically, rs5848 has been found to exist in a miR-659-3p binding site, with the minor allele increasing miR-659-3p binding affinity and exaggerating transcript degradation [5]. Additionally, single SNP and haplotype analyses failed to identify any linked SNPs more strongly associated with neurodegenerative disease risk, suggesting that rs5848 is the functional variant [5].

The GRN variant rs5848 has been studied extensively in different populations since it was first linked to FTD [5], though results have been notably mixed. Some studies have identified rs5848 associations with FTD [5], AD [6, 15–21], PD [6, 22], limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE-NC) [23–26], or hippocampal sclerosis (HS) [15, 23–25, 27–29], while others have failed to reproduce these findings [30–37] (Supplemental Information 1). This discrepancy has yet to be satisfactorily explained, representing another key knowledge gap in the progranulin literature. One emerging hypothesis for this phenomenon is that the association of rs5848 with neurodegenerative disease presents population-specific effects due to different genetic architectures, such as genetic ancestry [36]. In this context, genetic ancestry-aware associations in diverse populations can shed light on this issue by: (1) decreasing type I errors and increasing the statistical power by addressing ancestral genomic differences; (2) providing accurate ancestry-specific association estimates, which can lead to novel variant discovery; and (3) improving the signal resolution of the associations [38]. In genetic association tests it is appropriate to adjust for both an individual’s local ancestry (i.e., similarity of a specific genomic segment to a reference population) and global ancestry (i.e., the proportion of an individual’s genome that is most similar to each reference population), since the local ancestry at a variant of interest can differ from an individual’s global ancestry composition. Moreover, compared to traditional tests adjusting only for global genetic ancestry, local ancestry-aware methods can model the admixture of all individuals, and have been shown to substantially improve the accuracy and power of genetic association estimates in variants with ancestry-specific effects [38].

In this work, we sought to address the above-mentioned gaps by first defining the GRN cis-regulatory region using ENCODE [39] and GeneHancer [40]. Subsequently, we utilized MANTA2 [41, 42] to predict the potential impact of all possible single nucleotide variants on transcription factor (TF) binding across the cis-regulatory region. We next analyzed the Alzheimer’s Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP) dataset NG00067.v10, a genetically diverse AD whole genome sequence database [43], to understand whether genetic variants within the cis-regulatory region could modify AD susceptibility, potentially through altering TF binding and GRN expression. We tested the association of common GRN genetic variants with AD in a local ancestry-aware analysis using Tractor [38] to assess the hypothesis that genetic ancestry could modify the association between rs5848 and AD diagnosis. We finally report that among the common variants in the GRN regulatory region, only rs5848 is significantly associated with AD, and this association was only detected in individuals with genetic similarity to East Asian local references.

To prevent misunderstandings, we define genetic admixture here as the product of genetic flow between two or more distinct populations. We used the 1,000 Genomes Project superpopulations: African (AFR), Admixed American (AMR), East Asian (EAS), European (EUR) and South Asian (SAS) as operational units to model genetic admixture and population stratification. When inferring local ancestry, genomic segments were assigned to a given ancestry based on genetic similarity with individuals from that category in the reference panel. While necessary for detecting population-specific effects, we acknowledge the assumptions and limitations of a categorical approach to genetic ancestry (discussed in [44, 45]). By no means do we imply the existence of pure or unadmixed genetic groups nor an association of genetic ancestry (population-level constructs based on genetic similarity that reflect shared ancestors and their corresponding evolutionary processes) with racial groups (social constructs typically based on people’s physical appearance, such as skin and hair colour).

Methods

WGS samples

We analyzed samples from the data release 4 of the ADSP database (data version NG00067.v10). Briefly, this data release comprises whole genome sequencing (WGS) genomes from 35,569 individuals collected in 40 cohorts across the globe. The population breakdown of these individuals reported by the ADSP database is: 5,218 African Ancestry, 2,791 Asian, 10,398 Hispanic, 16,191 Non-Hispanic White, and 971 Other/Unknown. More information on the data set can be found in https://dss.niagads.org/datasets/ng00067/. We analyzed the project-level quality-controlled Variant Call Format file provided by the consortium, which contained autosomal biallelic single nucleotide variants and insertion-deletion variant genotypes. The WGS quality control (QC) pipeline has been described previously [46]. Briefly, the QC pipeline consists of the following steps: (1) quality checks regarding concordance with genome wide association study (GWAS) data, sample contamination, relatedness/duplication, and Mendelian inconsistency, (2) quality checks on individual genotypes, variants, and samples (e.g., average read depth, average genotype quality scores, and departure from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium), and (3) removal of variants and samples flagged in the second step.

Sample pre-processing

We generated the harmonized clean phenotype data with the R script provided by the ADSP consortium (https://github.com/NIAGADS/ADSPIntegratedPhenotypes). This file harmonized information regarding AD diagnosis, age, and sex, among other factors. The age of individuals older than 90 was recorded as 90+; for the purpose of the analysis, the age of these individuals was replaced with 90.

Reported familial relationships were filtered using the family filter step of the integrated phenotypes R script (https://github.com/NIAGADS/ADSPIntegratedPhenotypes). This step removes all but one subject per family. Families are linked by family IDs if they are enrolled as part of a family-based study. Family IDs include parent/child pairings, and sibling and extended family member relationships, where reported. Notably, this step does not remove unknown or unreported family members. As such, we further filtered genetically related individuals up to fourth-degree relatives (kinobj > 0.022) based on previously reported genetic relatedness data [43]. For this step, we removed all but one individual per family, defined as a network of individuals connected by up to 4th degree relationships. After these checks 6,880 related samples were removed. We further removed 1,233 samples with no AD diagnosis information, 687 samples without age information, and 3,750 samples without both AD diagnosis and age information (Supplemental Fig. 1). After these data pre-processing steps, we used the 23,019 remaining samples for subsequent analyses (Table 1).

Characterization of the GRNcis-regulatory region

To define the GRN cis-regulatory region, the UCSC Genome Browser was used to visualize predicted regulatory interactions with, and regulatory elements proximal to, the GRN promoter in the GRCh38 (hg38) reference [47]. Regulatory interactions between the GRN promoter and regulatory elements in the chromosomal neighbourhood were predicted using GeneHancer v4.8 [40]. Candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) were obtained from ENCODE [39] by querying DNAse hypersensitivity, and ChIP-seq datasets (specifically histone modifications H3K4me3 and H3K27ac and CTCF-binding site annotations). ENCODE comprises data from 503 tissues and cell types, including both primary cells and cell lines. Several brain relevant human biosamples are present in this dataset, including dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, hippocampal tissue, caudate nucleus, substantia nigra, cerebellar tissue, neuronal stem cells, neural progenitor cells, neurons, astrocytes, mid-neurogenesis radial glial cells, and BEC2 neuroblastoma [39]. MANTA2 [41, 42] is used to predict the impact of single nucleotide variants on TF binding using both experimental data (ReMAP [48]; 1,902 ChIP-seq datasets) and computational data (JASPAR [42]; 48,512,399 TF binding site predictions). The ReMap ChIP-seq data comes from 346 different cell types, including both primary cells and cell lines [48]. There are several brain cell types represented in this data, including neural progenitors, astrocytes, neuroblastomas (BE2C & SHSY5Y), medulloblastoma, glioma, and brain microvascular endothelial cells [48]. In this manuscript, we used MANTA2 to predict (1) TF binding sites in the GRN cis-regulatory region and (2) the computed TF affinity impact of all single nucleotide variants at every position for which MANTA2 predicts TF binding.

Local genetic ancestry inference

We inferred the local genetic ancestry of each genetic variant in the chr17:34,352,776 − 54,352,776 GRCh38/hg38 genomic region (herein referred to as GRN cis genomic neighbourhood, a 20 Mb window surrounding the GRN gene; note this is different from the 16kb cis-regulatory region). The local ancestry was estimated for 10 Mb on each side of rs5848 to ensure the loci up and downstream of our region of interest were available for the windowing and smoothing steps. After subsetting said region from the WGS data using bcftools v1.9 [49], we used shapeit4 v4.2.1 [50] to estimate the haplotypes of each individual (i.e., phasing) using the 2,504 high coverage phased original samples from the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 [51] as reference (downloaded from http://ftp.1000genomes.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/ftp/data_collections/1000G_2504_high_coverage/working/20201028_3202_phased/), and the hg38 genetic map provided by shapeit4 (downloaded from https://github.com/odelaneau/shapeit4/tree/master/maps). Next, we used RFMIX v2.03 [52] to infer the local ancestry of each of the two haplotypes per individual in the analyzed genomic region based on similarity with individuals in the reference panel. Briefly, RFMIX uses a discriminative modelling approach using conditional random fields parametrized with random forests to conduct a fast and accurate local ancestry inference. We used the same high coverage 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 samples used in the phasing stage and their superpopulations (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, and SAS) as reference for this local ancestry inference step. To test the differences in the population genetic structure between cases and controls, for each superpopulation we conducted a two-sample t-test in R using the t.test() function and corrected it with Bonferroni.

Global genetic ancestry inference

The population stratification resulting from global genetic ancestry was estimated through a Principal Component Analysis as described in Leung YY, et al. 2025 [43]. Briefly, the bi-allelic variants of the full WGS data set post-QC were pruned with an R2 cutoff at 0.05 and a window size of 500 K base pairs (bp). Across all chromosomes, 146,964 pruned variants remained. The tools “GENESIS” (2.20.1) [53], along with GWASTools (1.36.0) [54], and SNPRelate (1.24.0) [55] were used to calculate the PCs.

Ancestry-specific association tests

To conduct the association tests between genetic variants and AD in the study cohort, we used Tractor v1.2.0 [38], a tool that enables the computation of ancestry-specific associations. All models were adjusted for sex, age and global genetic ancestry (first 4 PCs only to avoid potential collider bias) [56] by including them as covariates. For the association tests in the GRN cis-regulatory region, genetic variants with a local ancestry-specific minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.005 were not evaluated in that given ancestry by filtering them out from the results (i.e., if a variant X had an EUR-MAF = 0.15, and an EAS-MAF = 0.001, the association of X within the EAS local ancestry context was removed from the results). We used a low MAF threshold as we had a relatively large sample size and were interrogating a small genomic region. By using a 0.005 MAF threshold we aimed to conduct a comprehensive screening, despite increasing the multiple hypothesis test burden. All p values were adjusted for the total number of SNPs tested using the Bonferroni method, which guarantees that the type I error rate is no larger than the desired nominal rate.

Results

We first characterized the regions that govern the transcriptional regulation of GRN. Interactions between the GRN promoter and distal enhancers were queried using GeneHancer v4.8 [40], a database of human regulatory elements. The GRN promoter was predicted to interact with two upstream enhancers, one enhancer within the GRN gene, and two downstream enhancers (Fig. 1A). We focused our analysis of cis-regulatory modifiers on the two regulatory elements most proximal to the GRN promoter (predicted interactions 2 and 3 in Fig. 1A) as these are the strongest predicted interactions and do not overlap with the coding regions of other genes (Supplemental Information 2). We selected a ~16 kb region chr17:44,337,379 − 44,353,435 (GRCh38/hg38) for detailed analysis (Fig. 1B). The region, herein referred to as GRN cis-regulatory region, encompasses the entire ~8 kb of GRN coding sequence, and a further ~8 kb upstream to capture the regulatory elements responsible for predicted interactions 2 and 3. This 16 kb region is defined at one end by the upstream position of predicted interaction 2 and at the other end by the gene FAM171A2. Notably, this segment has been previously suggested to be the primary regulatory region for GRN and has been shown to be sufficient to reconstitute human progranulin expression in mice [57, 58].

The 16kb cis-regulatory region of GRN contains an abundance of regulatory elements and TF binding sites. ENCODE [39] predicted 21 distinct cCREs across the region (Fig. 1B). Additionally, MANTA2 [41, 42, 48] predicted the 16 kb region to contain binding sites for 150 TFs, out of the 225 human TFs for which MANTA2 has data (Fig. 1B, Supplemental Information 3). TF annotations were highest upstream of the first exon, within the first intron, and within the seventh intron. As a resource for interpreting the effect of non-coding variation on TF binding, we provide the computed impact of all MANTA2 single nucleotide variants on TF binding across the 16 kb region of interest (Supplemental Information 3).

After characterizing the local GRN regulatory landscape, we sought to determine the role of genetic variation within the GRN cis-regulatory region in AD. To do this, we accessed the ADSP WGS database [43], and analyzed 23,019 unique samples that passed quality checks and data pre-processing steps (See Methods; n = 9,416 cases, n = 13,603 controls). Querying the 16 kb region of interest in all 23,019 samples, we identified 1691 unique biallelic variants (including single nucleotide variants and indels), 55 of which are common (MAF > 0.005; Fig. 1B, Supplemental Information 4). While most common variants did not overlap with predicted TF binding sites, 6/55 (10.9%) were predicted to modify TF binding affinity (Supplemental Information 5). Of the 1636 rare variants (MAF < 0.005 in the ADSP dataset), 282 (17.2%) were located in predicted TF binding sites (Supplemental Information 6). Additionally, 81/1636 (5.0%) of the rare variants are both predicted to modify TF binding and have been observed only in AD patients (Supplemental Information 6). Further work is needed to determine the effect of genetic variation on TFs and GRN expression.

With the goal of performing an ancestry-aware assessment of the association of common variants in the GRN cis-regulatory region with AD, we next sought to describe the local ancestry of the individuals in the ADSP data set by contrasting them with the 1000 Genomes Project superpopulation references [51]. Local ancestry inference was performed using RfMix [52] on the GRN cis genomic neighbourhood, a 20 Mb region surrounding the GRN gene. Within this region, individuals in the ADSP data set had a high proportion of alleles with a local context genetically similar to the European reference group on average (EUR: 57.2%, AFR: 19.4%, AMR: 11.4%, SAS: 9.9%, EAS: 2.2%), especially in the cases (EUR: 72.5%, AFR: 16.6%, AMR: 6.3%, SAS: 2.9%, EAS: 1.8%; Fig. 2A) compared to controls (EUR: 46.6%, AFR: 21.4%, AMR: 14.9%, SAS: 14.6%, EAS: 2.5%; Fig. 2A). Importantly, the significant differences in the population genetic structure (Fig. 2B, p adj. < 0.05) between cases and controls suggested that ancestry-agnostic AD association tests with these samples could be liable to produce false associations due to population stratification effects [59]. This evidence supports the necessity of using an ancestry-aware approach that can appropriately account for these population stratification effects.

Equipped with local ancestry information, we first tested the population-specific association of common variants in the GRN cis-regulatory region with AD diagnosis and then focused on the effect sizes of rs5848 across populations. To test the hypothesis that genetic variation in the GRN cis-regulatory region could be associated with AD risk, we used Tractor [38], a software that conducts genetic associations accounting for local genetic ancestry. For this local ancestry-aware analysis, we imposed a MAF > 0.005 cutoff for each variant in each of the five ancestries. The allele frequencies of the common variants in each ancestry (99 unique SNPs) and the admixture composition in this 16 kb window are presented in Supplemental Fig. 2. After adjusting for the multiple hypothesis testing, we observed a significant association between rs5848 and AD only in individuals genetically similar to the East Asian reference group (p adj < 0.05; Fig. 3, Supplemental Information 9). Even though rs5848 has been previously associated with AD, reports of this effect have been conflicting across populations with potentially distinct genetic ancestry composition (Supplemental Information 1). Therefore, we asked whether the effect size of this variant was different across populations in the context of AD. When conducting a targeted association test for rs5848, we observed a high effect size in alleles with a local context genetically similar to the EAS reference group (Fig. 4, Supplemental Information 10).

After evaluating the AD association of variants in the GRN cis-regulatory region, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to see if there were associations downstream of the 16 kb region of interest. This sensitivity analysis covered 26 kb (chr17:44,337,379 − 44,363,436 GRCh38/hg38), including the original 16 kb and additional 10 kb downstream of the GRN gene (chr17:44,353,436 − 44,363,436 GRCh38/hg38). This region was not included in the previous analyses as it features a sequence from FAM171A2, the gene immediately downstream of GRN. While the function of FAM171A2 is not fully understood, it has been reported to regulate progranulin protein CSF expression and modify risk of AD, PD, and FTD [60]. We did not identify significant genetic association with AD risk in this extended window (Supplemental Fig. 3; Supplemental Information 11).

Discussion

While the function of the progranulin protein remains incompletely understood, pathogenic genetic variants in GRN are associated with neurodegenerative disease via an insufficiency mechanism whereby progranulin protein expression is reduced [61]. In this study, we characterized the GRN local regulatory region and explored genetic variation in this region in the context of AD. Among the common variants, we only detected AD association with rs5848. The rs5848 variant has been previously shown to reduce progranulin protein and mRNA levels in brain and blood [5, 30], and reduced progranulin protein levels in the CSF have been associated with AD [62]. Therefore, a plausible mechanism driving the association of rs5848 with AD could be through the reduction of progranulin protein expression. In line with this, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the other common variants tested are not associated with reducing progranulin protein expression to the pathological threshold [14]. However, further studies with greater power may yield additional associations, particularly for variants with lower MAF in ancestries for which fewer samples are available (EAS, SAS, and AMR). Beyond common variation, we report the identification of 1636 rare variants in GRN cis-regulatory region (MAF < 0.005 across all ancestries). Among the most notable rare variants was chr17:44,345,085_G > C, occurring 217 bp upstream of the GRN transcription start site. This variant is predicted to impact 20 TF binding sites, with most impacts anticipated to reduce binding affinity (Supplemental Information 6). Investigating the effect of these rare variants on GRN expression may shed light on the relationship between progranulin expression and disease.

We report for the first time that the association between rs5848 and AD has ancestry-specific effects using a state-of-the-art method. Leveraging local genetic ancestry, we detected an association between rs5848 and AD only in individuals genetically similar to the EAS reference group. While the data in the present study supports an EAS-specific effect for rs5848, we note that the EAS population in the ADSP dataset is relatively small (310 minor alleles) and further study with a larger population is warranted. Individuals genetically similar to the EUR reference population represented the majority of the dataset such that comparisons in this group were most highly powered (7970 minor alleles). Therefore, it was notable that we did not detect an association between rs5858 and AD in the EUR population. Overall, the individuals in our study have a high proportion of genetic segments similar to the EUR reference population. The reduced statistical power resulting from comparatively smaller numbers of alleles with a local context genetically similar to the other reference populations might hinder the discovery of AD-associated variants in non-EUR ancestries.

Ancestry-specific effects represent a potential explanation for some of the literature discordance on the role of rs5848 in AD (see Supplemental Information 1). Associations between rs5848 and neurodegenerative disease risk and/or progranulin expression have been observed in EAS populations [6, 14, 17, 19], which is consistent with the present analysis. However, one study reported no effect of rs5848 on AD in an EAS population [36]. Our data does not support an association between rs5848 and AD in EUR populations, a finding that is consistent with some studies assessing associations between rs5848 and neurodegenerative disease in European populations [30–34], but in conflict with others [6, 11–16, 18, 20, 30]. While a harmonious explanation for this discrepancy remains elusive, we note that these studies vary widely in sample size, phenotype, ancestry modelling, and methodology. Additionally, since many of these studies do not report genetic ancestry, only the geographical location of the samples, direct comparisons remain challenging. Collectively, the present study suggests that genetic ancestry is a contributing factor to the discordant results in rs5848 AD association studies, along with other factors such as statistical power and phenotype quality. While the full extent of the ancestry-specific effects of rs5848 in AD may require further study in large, diverse populations, the evidence presented here argues for the inclusion of genetic ancestry as a consequential variable.

To our knowledge, the role of rs5848 in AD has not been evaluated in populations genetically similar to the AMR, SAS, and AFR reference groups. However, a recent study found no association of rs5848 with LATE-NC in AFR individuals [37]. An intriguing finding from this publication is the higher relative MAF of rs5848 in AFR populations (0.73 in AFR vs. 0.30 in EUR) [37]. We corroborate this finding in our data set, where we found an rs5848 MAF of 0.75 in AFR vs. 0.28 in EUR and 0.19 in EAS (Supplemental Information 10). With dementia typically presenting after the peak of reproductive fitness when the force of natural selection is weaker (i.e., in the selection shadow [63]), we may not expect selective pressure to reduce rates of rs5848 even if it were very strongly associated with the occurrence of AD. However, a high rs5848 MAF in the AFR superpopulation is consistent with an absence of deleterious effect.

While the mechanism behind the observed ancestry-specific effects of rs5848 is presently unclear, several hypotheses exist. Population-specific effects in causal genetic variants have been suggested to occur because of gene-by-gene or gene-by-environment interactions (i.e., when the effect of the genetic variant on a trait is dependent on the presence of another variant or environmental exposure, respectively) [64]. Given that rs5848 is proposed to modulate the strength of miR-659-3p [5], we first considered the existence of ancestry-specific differences in miR-659-3p sequence and/or expression. Extracting variant information for miR-659-3p from gnomAD (ENSG00000207696.1), we observed no variants that are both sufficiently common and expressed in an ancestry-specific pattern matching the observed ancestry effect of rs5848 (i.e., enriched in EAS or in EUR, AFR, AMR, and SAS) (Supplemental Information 12). However, it remains possible that an ancestry-specific expression quantitative trail loci (eQTL) for miR-659-3p exists elsewhere in the genome. Alternatively, there may be population-dependent differences in other regulators of GRN expression that mask the rs5848 effect in EUR, AFR, AMR, and SAS superpopulations, or exacerbate the effect in the EAS superpopulation. Further study into the mechanism of ancestry-specific effect of rs5848 may provide new insight into AD pathology.

Conclusions

The research presented herein implicates the GRN variant rs5848 in the growing list of polymorphisms for which association with AD pathology is modified by local genetic ancestry. Recent works suggested that APOE ε4, another genetic variant heavily associated with AD, presents a stronger effect in people of EUR ancestry relative to AFR in some populations [65–68]. As more ancestry effects are identified, it is clear that pathological associations may not always be translatable across diverse populations. Our data suggest that polygenic risk score calculations for AD that include rs5848, such as GenoRisk [69], will have reduced accuracy when considering this variant in individuals genetically similar to the EUR, AFR, AMR, and SAS reference populations. More broadly, the present research demonstrates the importance of ancestry-aware analysis and underscores the need for studying populations with greater diversity in medical genetics research [70]. AD is a complex disease, so-called because the variables that contribute to the development of AD are not fully understood. Ancestry effects are one such variable that, if understood, may lead to better clinical outcomes through personalized therapeutic approaches.
