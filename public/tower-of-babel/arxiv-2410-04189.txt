Primes of the form p 2 + n ​ q 2 p^{2}+nq^{2}
Ben Green, Mehtaab Sawhney

Abstract
. Suppose that n ≡ 0 n\equiv 0 or n ≡ 4 ​ mod ​ 6 n\equiv 4~\mathrm{mod}~6 . We show that there are infinitely many primes of the form p 2 + n ​ q 2 p^{2}+nq^{2} with both p p and q q prime, and obtain an asymptotic for their number. In particular, when n = 4 n=4 we verify the ‘Gaussian primes conjecture’ of Friedlander and Iwaniec. We study the problem using the method of Type I/II sums in the number field 𝐐 ⁡ ( − n ) \mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n}) . The main innovation is in the treatment of the Type II sums, where we make heavy use of two recent developments in the theory of Gowers norms in additive combinatorics: quantitative versions of so-called concatenation theorems, due to Kuca and to Kuca–Kravitz-Leng, and the quasipolynomial inverse theorem of Leng, Sah and the second author.



1.
Introduction

Our main result establishes an asymptotic count for pairs of primes
x
and
y
such that
x^{2}+ny^{2}
is prime.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
n
is a positive integer with
n\equiv 0
or
n\equiv 4~\mathrm{mod}~6
. Let
W\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbf{R}^{2})
. Let
N>1
be a parameter. Then

\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}}\Lambda(x)\Lambda(y)\Lambda(x^{2}+ny^{2})W\bigg(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\bigg)=\kappa_{n}N^{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{2}}W\bigg)+O_{W,n}\bigg(\frac{N^{2}(\log\log N)^{2}}{\log N}\bigg),

where

(1.1)

\kappa_{n}:=\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant X\\
(-n\mid p)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{p(p-3)}{(p-1)^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant X\\
(-n\mid p)\neq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{p}{p-1},

with
(\cdot\mid p)
the Legendre symbol. In particular, if
n\equiv 0
or
n\equiv 4(\operatorname{mod}\,6)
then there are infinitely many primes of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
with
x,y
prime.

Remarks
.

Here, and throughout the paper, the von Mangoldt function is defined by
\Lambda(n)=\log p
if
n=p^{k}
is a prime power,
\Lambda(n)=0
if
n
is a non-negative integer other than a prime power, and
\Lambda(-n)=\Lambda(n)
if
n<0
.

From the statement of
Theorem

1.1
one can easily derive asymptotics for the number of
x,y
in a box such as
[0,N]\times[0,N]
for which
x,y
and
x^{2}+ny^{2}
are all prime, by approximating
1_{[0,1]^{2}}
by a smooth function
W
. We leave the details to the reader. It can be seen from the arguments of
Section

4.1
that, under the assumption that
W
is supported in
B_{10}(0)
, the implied constant in the
O_{W}(\cdot)
in
Theorem

1.1
can be taken to be
\ll\sup_{0\leqslant j\leqslant 3}\|\partial^{(j)}W\|_{\infty}
, by which we mean the maximum over all derivatives of
W
of order at most
3
.

If one wanted an asymptotic over the range
x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant N^{2}
then one could use
Proposition

4.1
directly, without having to pass to a smooth weight
W
. This case, when further specialized to
n=4
, recovers the ‘Gaussian primes conjecture’ in the form stated by Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
13
, Conjecture 1.1]
. This (up to a trivial change in notation) states the following.

Conjecture 1.2

(Gaussian Primes Conjecture)
.

There holds

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\\
x^{2}+4y^{2}\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\Lambda(x)\Lambda(y)\Lambda(x^{2}+4y^{2})\sim cN,

where
c=\prod_{p\equiv 1(\mbox{\scriptsize{mod}}\,4)}(1-\frac{3}{p})(1-\frac{1}{p})^{-3}\prod_{p\equiv 3(\mbox{\scriptsize{mod}}\,4)}(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}})^{-1}
.

To see that this does indeed follow from
Theorem

1.1
, one can take
W=W_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}
where
W_{\varepsilon}^{+}
is a smooth majorant for
1_{|x^{2}+4y^{2}|\leqslant 1}
, differing from it by at most
\varepsilon
in the
L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2})
-norm, and
W_{\varepsilon}^{-}
is a minorant with the same property. As
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, this tends to
4
times the quantity of interest in
Conjecture

1.2
(noting here that positive and negative values of
x,y
are included). Now note that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{2}}W^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}=\pi/2
. Moreover, the class number formula gives
\frac{\pi}{4}=L(1,\chi_{-4})=\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{p\leqslant X:p\equiv 1(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,4)}\big(1-\frac{1}{p}\big)^{-1}\prod_{p\leqslant X:p\equiv 3(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,4)}\big(1+\frac{1}{p}\big)^{-1}
which, after some calculation, leads to
c=\pi\kappa_{4}/8
, and so
Conjecture

1.2
follows from
Theorem

1.1
as claimed.

In general the infinite product
1.1
is only conditionally convergent. To show that it does converge, by taking logs and Taylor expansion it suffices to show that
\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{p\leqslant X}(-n\mid p)p^{-1}
exists, which is typically shown in a standard proof of Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in progressions, noting here that
(-n\mid p)=\chi_{n}(p)
for some Dirichlet character
\chi_{n}
to modulus dividing
4n
.

It has the form predicted by heuristics of Bateman–Horn type, that is to say a product of natural archimedean and
p
-local factors. One can state an equivalent result with an absolutely convergent product by dividing through by the class number formula for the field
\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
, as Friedlander and Iwaniec did for
n=4
in their formulation of
Conjecture

1.2
.

Finally, the argument could surely be modified in a straightforward manner to handle arbitrary positive definite binary quadratic forms over
\mathbf{Z}
in place of
x^{2}+ny^{2}
, but this would introduce even more notation to a paper which is already fairly heavy going. It should also not be difficult to modify the argument to prove, for example, that there are infinitely many primes
x^{2}+y^{2}
with
x
and
y-1
prime.

1.1.
Prior results

Famously, it was stated by Fermat and proven by Euler that every prime
p\equiv 1(\operatorname{mod}\,4)
can be written as
x^{2}+y^{2}
, and in particular there are infinitely many primes of the form
x^{2}+y^{2}
. Fermat also asked about prime values of
x^{2}+ny^{2}
for various
n\geqslant 2
, and made analogous conjectures when
n=2,3,5
. The first two of these were also proven by Euler, and the third by Lagrange. The book
[
2
]
(which inspired the title and some of the content of this paper) is devoted to primes of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
from an algebraic perspective, linking this to the development of central topics in number theory such as class field theory. We note in particular that it was shown by Weber in 1882
[
49
]
that there are infinitely many primes of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
for any
n\geqslant 1
.

Our main result falls into a long line of work considering refinements of such statements in which some restriction is placed on the coordinates
x,y
. Most of these have been concerned with the case
n=1
, that is to say with the form
x^{2}+y^{2}
. Work of Fouvry and Iwaniec
[
9
]
handled the case where
x
is prime. Celebrated work of Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
11
,
10
]
, showed that one may restrict
x
to be a perfect square (the first polynomial taking values in polynomially thin set shown to capture primes). This was refined in work of Heath–Brown and Li
[
23
]
, to force
x
to be a square of a prime. Pratt
[
46
]
has proven that
x
may be forced to lie in a set where one excludes
3
digits from its decimal expansion. We also mention two recent works of Merikoski
[
39
,
38
]
; in particular, in
[
38
]
the variable
x
is restricted to an arbitrary set
S
of integers satisfying
|S\cap\{1,\dots N\}|\gg N^{1-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
. The work of Fouvry and Iwaniec was generalised to
x^{2}+ny^{2}
for arbitrary
n\geqslant 1
in
[
22
]
.

Short of proving that there are infinitely many primes of the form
x^{2}+4
(with
x
prime), for parity reasons one cannot hope to show that there are infinitely many primes of the form
x^{2}+y^{2}
with
both

x
and
y
prime, and for related reasons
(\operatorname{mod}\,6)
one should restrict the search for primes of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
with
x,y
prime to the case
n\in\{0,4\}(\operatorname{mod}\,6)
.

Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
13
]
considered the problem in the case
n=4
and proved a lower bound for the number of
(x,y)
with
x
prime,
y
having at most
7
prime factors, and
x^{2}+4y^{2}
prime.

Theorem

1.1
uses relatively little about the specific form of the functions
\Lambda(x),\Lambda(y)
, and similar techniques may be used for other problems connected with the coordinates of Gaussian primes. Perhaps the most interesting of these, which we will address in
[
15
]
, is to give asymptotic counts for corners
(x+iy,(x+d)+iy,x+i(y+d))
in the Gaussian primes.

1.2.
Outline of the argument

For most of the rest of the introductory discussion we specialise to the case
n=4
. In this case,
Theorem

1.1
is closely related to a statement involving Gaussian primes (that is to say primes in
\mathbf{Z}[i]
) using the fact that
z=x+iy
,
y\neq 0
, is a Gaussian prime if and only
N_{\mathbf{Q}(i)/\mathbf{Q}}(z)=x^{2}+y^{2}
is a rational prime. In particular, our main theorem implies that there are infinitely many Gaussian primes
x+2iy
with
x,y
prime.

For the sake of this introductory discussion we will consider the problem of estimating

(1.2)

\sum_{x^{2}+4y^{2}\leqslant X}f(x)f^{\prime}(y)1_{x^{2}+4y^{2}\operatorname{prime}}.

In the case
f=f^{\prime}=\Lambda
, this is a special case of
Theorem

1.1
with
N=X^{1/2}
,
W
(a smooth approximation to) the cutoff
1_{u^{2}+nv^{2}\leqslant 1}
, and with the von Mangoldt weight
\Lambda(x^{2}+4y^{2})
(which is essentially always
\log X
) renormalised to
1
.

Much of our argument is relevant for fairly general
f,f^{\prime}
. Noting that
1.2
is bilinear in
f
and
f^{\prime}
, one can hope in any given situation of interest to write
f,f^{\prime}
as sums of relatively easily-understood ‘main terms’ plus error terms for which one hopes that the sum
1.2
is small. We are then left with the task of finding verifiable criteria on
f,f^{\prime}
which allow us to assert that (
1.2
) is small.

The first key idea is to observe that
1.2
is a sum

(1.3)

\sum_{\gamma\in\operatorname{Primes}(\mathbf{Z}[i])}w(\gamma),

where
w:\mathbf{Z}[i]\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is defined by
w(x+2iy)=f(x)f^{\prime}(y)
and
w(x+iy)=0
for
y
odd, and we assume throughout the following discussion that
w(\gamma)
is supported where
|\gamma|^{2}\leqslant X
.

We then examine this sum over Gaussian primes using so-called Type I and Type II sums. For a general introduction to this technique over the rational integers, see for instance
[
28
, Chapter 13]
(where the authors do not use the terms Type I/II sum, but the relevant sums are (13.16) and (13.17)) or the comprehensive recent analysis
[
8
]
. Adapting the relevant techniques from the rational integers
\mathbf{Z}
to the Gaussian integers
\mathbf{Z}[i]
presents only minor technical obstacles in our setting. See
[
25
, Section 3]
for a very similar type of argument in the ring
\mathbf{Z}[2^{1/3}]
, and
[
24
,
37
]
for similar arguments in fields not necessarily enjoying unique factorisation.

For the purposes of this discussion, Type I sums are roughly of the form

(1.4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\mathbf{Z}[i]\\
|\ell|^{2}\sim L\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbf{Z}[i]\\
\ell|m\end{subarray}}w(m)\Big|

for some positive
L\in\mathbf{R}
, which we informally call the
level
.

Type II sums are bilinear sums of the form

(1.5)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,m\in\mathbf{Z}[i]\\
|\ell|^{2}\sim L\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\ell}\beta_{m}w(\ell m),

where here the coeffients
\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{m}
will satisfy reasonable boundedness conditions but can otherwise be fairly arbitrary.

The estimates we seek for these sums are bounds of the form
O(X(\log X)^{-B})
for some large
B
, which represent savings over the trivial bounds by large powers of
\log X
. If we have such bounds, we informally say that we have Type I (or Type II) information at level
L
. (Precise statements may be found in
Section

3
.)

If one has Type I and Type II information at sufficient levels, it is possible to obtain asymptotics for sums over primes
1.3
by sieve methods, details of which may be found in
Section

3
. Any such argument needs Type I/II information in sufficiently large ranges. The question of exactly what is necessary or sufficient for this purpose is rather complicated (and, over the integers, has been comprehensively addressed in the recent work
[
8
]
).

We will use an argument of Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec
[
4
, Section 6]
which (adapted to the Gaussian primes) shows that, at least for
1
-bounded functions
f,f^{\prime}
, it is enough to have Type I information up to level
X^{1/2-o(1)}
, and Type II information up to
X^{1/3-o(1)}
.

The task, then, is to obtain the relevant Type I and II information. We are able to do this under the assumption that appropriate
Gowers norms
of
f
or
f^{\prime}
are small. The Gowers norms are central objects in modern additive combinatorics; see
Section

4.2
for an introduction. Assuming bounds for suitable Gowers norms of
f
or
f^{\prime}
, we are able to obtain Type I information up to level
X^{1/2-o(1)}
and Type II information for levels between
X^{o(1)}
and
X^{1/2-o(1)}
, which is sufficient for variants of the sieve arguments of Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec to apply in order to show that
1.3
is small. One point to note here is that, while we have comfortably enough Type II information, the Type I range is just barely enough for the requirements of the Duke–Friedlander–Iwaniec sieve. Worse, the functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
of interest to us for our main theorem are not
1
-bounded but come from the von Mangoldt function (with a suitable main term subtracted), and the most naïve application of the Duke–Friedlander–Iwaniec machinery fails to give a usable bound. To get around this issue we require an additional upper bound sieve (of dimension 3). We remark that obtaining Type I information at level
X^{1/2}
in our setting remains an interesting open question. In fact, even obtaining bounds at this level without the absolute values in
1.4
seems challenging. (A bound of this latter type would be interesting, being a simple case of what is called Type I
2
information. Sufficient information in this direction would allow one to obtain an arbitrary logarithmic savings in
Theorem

1.1
.)

The most novel part of our work lies in showing that the stated Type I and Type II information can indeed be obtained from suitable Gowers norm control on the functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
. Precise statements of these results are
Proposition

4.2
and
Proposition

4.3
respectively. The proof of
Proposition

4.3
implicitly uses a large number of applications of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, organised via what are known as
concatenation
theorems for Gowers-type norms. The first concatenation results were obtained by Tao and Ziegler
[
48
]
, but those results are rather qualitative. More recently, starting with the work of Peluse and Prendiville
[
43
]
and Peluse
[
45
]
, more quantitative variants have been developed. For our application, we need the very recent work of Kuca
[
33
]
and Kravitz–Kuca–Leng
[
32
]
.

In order to apply these results to prove Theorem
1.1
, we need to show that for suitable functions
f
and
f^{\prime}
related to the von Mangoldt function, the relevant Gowers norms are small. We work with
f=f^{\prime}=\Lambda-\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}
, where
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}
is a certain low-complexity approximant to the von Mangoldt function defined in
1.6
below. Statements of this type were first shown in a series of works of the first author with Tao
[
17
,
19
,
18
]
and with Tao and Ziegler
[
16
]
. More recent work of Tao and Teräväinen
[
47
]
gave quantitative bounds relying on effective bounds for the inverse theorem for the Gowers norms established by Manners
[
36
]
. For our application, however, we need savings of large powers of
\log X
over the trivial bound. Bounds of this strength were only recently established by Leng
[
35
]
, whose work relies on the quasipolynomial inverse theorem for the Gowers norms due to Leng, Sah and the second author
[
34
]
.

The final task of the paper is then to obtain the main term in
Theorem

1.1
by evaluating
1.2
with
f=f^{\prime}=\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}
. This is a task for classical methods of multiplicative number theory, the main input being the prime ideal theorem. However, it takes a little work to get all the details in order.

In the above outline we had
n=4
. To deal with more general values of
n
, we use the field
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
in place of the Gaussian field. In general, these fields do not enjoy unique factorisation and in the above discussion one must work with ideals rather than with elements of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. In fact, for technical reasons related to units it is best to work in this language even in the Gaussian setting (which does have unique factorisation). The fact that not all ideals are principal creates some extra difficulties, but they turn out not to be too serious and are resolved in much the same way that similar difficulties were overcome in
[
24
,
37
]
(for instance).

1.3.
Organization

In
Section

2
we review some of the basic arithmetic of
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
and recall some simple lemmas about the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and the ideals in this ring.

In
Section

3
, we develop the relevant sieve machinery allowing us to estimate sums over primes such as
1.3
using Type I/II information. Here we follow the arguments of Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec rather closely, albeit in the number field setting. As explained above, we will additionally require the input of an upper bound sieve of dimension 3 in two variables.

In
Section

4
we begin by giving a technical reduction of our main theorem, which is the form we shall actually prove; essentially, this consists in converting from Cartesian to polar coordinates. Then, we recall the definitions and basic properties of the Gowers
U^{k}
-norms, which will feature heavily in the paper from this point on. We will also give the outline proof of our main theorem, leaving the proof of the key Type I and II estimates, as well as the main input from analytic number theory, to later sections.

In
Section

5
, we recall a variant of the Gowers norms due to Peluse
[
45
]
, which we call the Gowers–Peluse norms. These are the appropriate generalisations of the Gowers norms needed for the concatenation machinery we will require. In this section we also state the main concatenation estimate of Kuca, Kravitz, and Leng
[
32
]
.

In
Section

6
, we show that Type I information is controlled by Gowers norms and in
Section

7
we do the same for Type II information.

In
Section

8
, we complete the proof of
Theorem

1.1
by evaluating the main term in the asymptotic.

Finally, we provide four appendices. In
Appendix

A
, we prove various properties of Gowers box norms. In
Appendix

, we provide self-contained proofs of the main concatenation results. In
Appendix

, we briefly provide details of the large sieve in higher dimensions. In

1.4.
Notation

There will be a small number of fixed objects throughout the paper, as follows:

•

n
will always be the same
n
as in the main theorem, and we think of it as fixed;

•

K
will always be
\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
, regarded as embedded into
\mathbf{C}
, except in the self-contained
Section

3.1
where it could be any number field (but for our application will be
\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
);

•

X
is some positive integer parameter, which we will always assume sufficiently large in terms of
n
without further comment.

For real
N\geqslant 1
we write
[N]=\{x\in\mathbf{Z}:1\leqslant x\leqslant N\}
and
[\pm N]=\{x\in\mathbf{Z}:-N\leqslant x\leqslant N\}
.

It is convenient to use a very minor modification of the von Mangoldt function which ignores the prime powers and is symmetric. Thus define
\Lambda^{\prime}(n)=\log|n|
if
|n|
is a prime, and
\Lambda^{\prime}(n)=0
otherwise.

As mentioned above, we will use a ‘low-complexity’ model for the primes, whose main task is to encode the fact that the primes are essentially supported only on residue classes coprime to
q
. Specifically, for
x\in\mathbf{Z}
and
Q\in\mathbf{Z}^{+}
, we define the Cramér model at level
Q
to be

(1.6)

\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q}(x)=\prod_{p\leqslant Q}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{-1}1_{(x,p)=1}.

Throughout the paper we take

(1.7)

Q:=\exp(\log^{1/10}(X^{1/2})).

Note that this is the same choice made in
[
47
, Equation (1.1)]
and in
[
35
, Section 5]
if one takes
N=X^{1/2}
in those papers. This is the reason for the inclusion of
X^{1/2}
in
1.7
, which otherwise looks peculiar.
We will write
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}=\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q}
from now on.

As is standard, we let
e(\theta)=e^{2\pi i\theta}
.

We use standard asymptotic notation throughout. We use
A\sim B
to denote that
B/2\leqslant A<2B
(i.e.
A
lies within a factor of
2
of
B
). Given functions
f=f(x)
and
g=g(x)
, we write
f=O(g)
,
f\ll g
,
g=\Omega(f)
, or
g\gg f
to mean that there is a constant
C
such that
|f(x)|\leqslant Cg(x)
for sufficiently large
x
. We write
f\asymp g
or
f=\Theta(g)
to mean that
f\ll g
and
g\ll f
, and write
f=o(g)
to mean
f(x)/g(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. Subscripts indicate dependence on parameters.

From
Section

5
onwards, we will use the language of probability measures on
\mathbf{Z}
. For us, this simply means a finitely-supported function
\mu:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow[0,1]
with
\sum_{x}\mu(x)=1
. In this paper all our probability measures will be symmetric, meaning that
\mu(x)=\mu(-x)
. The convolution
\mu_{1}\ast\mu_{2}
of two probability measures is defined by
(\mu_{1}\ast\mu_{2})(x):=\sum_{y}\mu_{1}(y)\mu_{2}(x-y)
, and it is also a probability measure. If
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{\ell}
are probability measures then we write
\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu_{i}
for
\mu_{1}\ast\cdots\ast\mu_{\ell}
. If
\mu_{1}=\cdots=\mu_{\ell}=\mu
, we write
\mu^{(\ell)}
for this measure. Given a (multi)set
S\subseteq\mathbf{Z}
, we let
\operatorname{U}_{S}
denote the uniform measure on this set. Finally,
\delta_{0}
denotes the delta-mass at
0
, thus
\delta_{0}(0)=1
and
\delta_{0}(x)=0
for
x\neq 0
. Observe that
\delta_{0}
is an identity for convolution in the sense that
\mu\ast\delta_{0}=\mu
for any probability measure
\mu
. The notation
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}f(x)
means the same as
\sum_{x}f(x)\mu(x)
.

We remark that it is perhaps controversial to use the letter
\mu
for something other than the Möbius function in an analytic number theory paper. However, the Möbius function only appears in
Section

3
of our paper, where there are no probability measures, and so there should be no danger of confusion.

When working in number fields, we will use the term
rational prime
to mean a prime number in
\mathbf{Z}
. This is to distinguish from other uses of the word prime (prime ideals or prime elements of the ring of integers).

Finally, if
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
then we define the character
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}:\mathbf{C}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}^{*}
by

(1.8)

\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(z):=(z/|z|)^{\ell}.

2.
Number fields and weight functions

In this section we collect some basic facts about number fields, particularly the imaginary quadratic field
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. We also introduce the key notion of a weight function of product form,
Definition

2.2
.

Given a number field
K
, denote by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
its ring of integers. This is a lattice satisfying
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq\frac{1}{2n}\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
. Note in particular that

(2.1)

(2n)\subseteq\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}],

where
(2n)
is the ideal generated by
2n
. Write
\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}
for the units in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. This will consist of
\pm 1
unless the squarefree part of
n
is 1 (in which case
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}=\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
) or
3
(in which case
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}
consists of the sixth roots of unity).

Ideals.
Denote by
\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
the semi-group of non-zero ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and by
\operatorname{Ideals}^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
the non-zero principal ideals. We will also encounter fractional ideals. If
\alpha\in K^{*}
then it is convenient to write
(\alpha)=\alpha\mathcal{O}_{K}
and call this the fractional ideal generated by
\alpha
. Note that if
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\setminus\{0\}
then
(\alpha)
is a principal ideal, and all principal ideals are of this form.

Fraktur letters
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{d}
will always denote ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, with the letters
\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{r}
being reserved for prime ideals. We reserve the letter
\mathfrak{c}
for particular fixed choices of fractional ideals in
K
, as described in
Lemma

2.1
below.

Recall that we may define the norm
N\mathfrak{a}
of any non-zero fractional ideal
\mathfrak{a}
, and this takes values in
\mathbf{Q}^{+}
.

Ideal classes.
Denote by
C(K)
the ideal class group of
K
. We will denote elements of
C(K)
by
g
, and will write
[g]
for the set of ideals in
\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
in the class represented by
g
. Sometimes it is necessary to consider fractional ideals and their classes in
C(K)
. Indeed it will be convenient throughout the paper to fix some fractional ideal representatives of each class, as detailed in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. We may choose fractional ideals
\mathfrak{c}_{g}
,
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g}
for
g\in C(K)
with the following properties:

(i)

\mathfrak{c}_{g},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g}
are in the ideal class corresponding to
g
;

(ii)

\mathfrak{c}_{g}\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}

(
which is principal
)
is generated by
\gamma_{g}\in\mathbf{Q}
, a positive rational of height
O_{n}(1)
;

(iii)

The set
\Pi_{g}:=\{z\in K^{*}:(z)\mathfrak{c}_{g}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\}
is a finite index sublattice of
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
, and similarly for
\Pi^{\prime}_{g}:=\{z\in K^{*}:(z)\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\}
.

Proof.

First of all, for each
g\in C(K)
pick some
\mathfrak{b}_{g}\in[g]
with norm
\leqslant\frac{4}{\pi}\sqrt{n}
; such ideals exist by the Minkowski bound. For each
g
,
\mathfrak{b}_{g}\mathfrak{b}_{g^{-1}}
is a principal ideal. Set
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}:=\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{g}\mathfrak{b}_{g^{-1}}}\mathfrak{b}_{g^{-1}}
. Then
N\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\ll n^{3/2}
, and
\mathfrak{b}_{g}\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}=(\mathfrak{b}_{g}\mathfrak{b}_{g^{-1}})\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{g}\mathfrak{b}_{g^{-1}}}
is generated by a rational integer of magnitude
\ll n^{2}
. Now, for each
g
, take
\mathfrak{c}_{g}
to be the fractional ideal
\frac{1}{M_{g}}\mathfrak{b}_{g}
, where
M_{g}=O_{n}(1)
is an integer such that
M_{g}\mathfrak{b}_{g}^{-1}\subseteq(2n)
. Such an
M_{g}
exists by clearing denominators in generators of
\mathfrak{b}^{-1}_{g}
. Define
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g}=\frac{1}{M^{\prime}_{g}}\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{g}
similarly. Properties (i) and (ii) are then immediate from the above discussion. (We remark that the key point in (ii) is that the generator
\gamma_{g}
lies in
\mathbf{Q}
, rather than merely in
K
; the second point is in a sense vacuous since there are only
O_{n}(1)
ideal classes, however the proof yields an explicit value for this constant if desired.)

We turn to point (iii). It is clear that
\Pi_{g}
is an abelian group under addition. Also, since
\mathfrak{c}_{g}
has an integral basis, we see by clearing denominators that there is
M=O_{n}(1)
such that
M,M\sqrt{-n}\in\Pi_{g}
.
In the other direction, note that by construction we have
\mathfrak{c}_{g}^{-1}\subseteq(2n)
. Therefore if
(z)\mathfrak{c}_{g}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
then
(z)=(z)\mathfrak{c}_{g}\mathfrak{c}_{g}^{-1}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathfrak{c}_{g}^{-1}\subseteq(2n)\subseteq\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
, the second inclusion being
2.1
. Hence
\Pi_{g}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
, and the claim follows.
∎

Weight functions.
Throughout the paper we will be dealing with weight functions
w
on
\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
. In
Section

3.1
these can be quite general, but for most of the paper we will take
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
and the weight functions will be of a special product form.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose that
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. Suppose that
f,f^{\prime}:\mathbf{Q}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
are two functions, both supported on
\mathbf{Z}
, and let
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
. Then we define a function
f\boxtimes_{\ell}f^{\prime}
on ideals as follows:

(2.2)

f\boxtimes_{\ell}f^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a})=\sum_{(x+y\sqrt{-n})=\mathfrak{a}}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})f(x)f^{\prime}(y),

where
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(z)=(z/|z|)^{\ell}
.
Any function
w:\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
having this form is said to be in
product form
with
frequency
\ell
. We say that
w
is
1
-bounded if
f,f^{\prime}
are.

Remarks
.

Note that the sum is empty unless
\mathfrak{a}
is principal, so
f\boxtimes_{\ell}f^{\prime}
is supported on principal ideals. If
\mathfrak{a}
is principal the the sum is over the
|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|
associates of any generator for
\mathfrak{a}
.

Further facts about
\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. The following further definitions and facts will be required only in
Section

8
. Factor

(2.3)

n=n_{*}r^{2}

where
n_{*}
is squarefree and
r\in\mathbf{N}
. It is convenient to define a quantity
\omega
by

(2.4)

\omega:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $n_{*}\equiv 1,2(\operatorname{mod}\,4)$ and}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\mbox{if $n_{*}\equiv 3(\operatorname{mod}\,4)$}.\end{array}\right.

Then it is well-known that
\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n_{*}}]
if
\omega=1
, whereas
\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbf{Z}[\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{-n_{*}})]
if
\omega=\frac{1}{2}
(which perhaps explains the notation). Recall that the discriminant
\Delta
is given by the formula

(2.5)

\Delta=-4\omega^{2}n_{*}.

There is a real quadratic character
(\Delta\mid\cdot)
associated to the field, where the symbol here is the Kronecker symbol, which coincides with the Legendre symbol on odd primes
p
. This symbol determines the splitting type of a prime
p
;
p
splits, is inert or ramifies according to whether
(\Delta\mid p)=1,-1,0
respectively. We also let
h_{K}
denote the class number of
K
. Finally we recall the class number formula, which in our setting and language states that

(2.6)

\frac{2\pi h_{K}}{|\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}||\Delta|^{1/2}}=L(1,(\Delta|\,\cdot\,))=\prod_{p}\bigg(1-\frac{(\Delta|p)}{p}\bigg)^{-1}.

3.
Sieve setup-reduction to Type I and Type II statements

In this section we detail the main sieve-theoretic parts of the argument which allow us to reduce to proving certain Type I/II estimates.

3.1.
The Duke–Friedlander–Iwaniec sieve in a number field

Here we adapt the sieve arguments of Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec to an arbitrary number field
K
. The generalisation is essentially completely routine and follows
[
4
, Section 6]
very closely, with only minor technical modifications required to work with ideals rather than integers. We think of
K
as fixed, and do not explicitly indicate
K
-dependence in instances of asymptotic notation.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix a weight function
w:\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
. Given a real number
B\geqslant 1
,
w
satisfies Type I (with savings
(\log X)^{-B})
) at
L_{1}
if given
L\leqslant L_{1}
then

(3.1)

\sum_{\mathfrak{d}:N\mathfrak{d}\sim L}\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}}w(\mathfrak{a})\Big|\ll_{B}X(\log X)^{-B}\|w\|_{\infty}.

We say that
w
satisfies Type II (with savings
(\log X)^{-B}
) between
[L_{1},L_{2}]
if given
L\in[L_{1},L_{2}]
, and any
1
-bounded sequences
\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}},\beta_{\mathfrak{b}}
then

(3.2)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{a}\sim L}\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}\beta_{\mathfrak{b}}w(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})\ll_{B}X(\log X)^{-B}\|w\|_{\infty}.

Remarks
.

The definition of course depends on our global parameter
X
. In practice,
w
will be supported on ideals of norm
\ll X
, in which case both
3.1
and
3.2
represent savings of a large power of
\log X
over the trivial bound. In our applications, the parameter
L_{1}
in Type II estimates will be purely technical and chosen to grow slightly faster than any power of logarithm.

Here is our formulation of Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec’s sieve result in the number field setting.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A,C>1
be parameters, set
B:=2A+4
, and suppose
C\geqslant B
. Furthermore suppose that
w:\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
satisfies Type I
(
with savings
(\log X)^{-B}
)
at
X^{1/2}(\log X)^{-C}
and Type II
(
with savings
(\log X)^{-B}
)
between
[(\log X)^{C},X^{3/8}]
. Suppose that
w
is supported on ideals of norm
\leqslant X
. Then

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}w(\mathfrak{p})+\sum_{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}}w(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2})\ll_{A,C}X(\log X)^{-A}\lVert w\rVert_{\infty},

where the first sum ranges over prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
, and the second sum ranges over pairs
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}
of prime ideals with
N\mathfrak{p}_{i}\geqslant X^{1/2}e^{-(\log\log X)^{2}}
for
i=1,2
.

Remark
.

To explain the meaning of this result, we note that the sum
\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}w(\mathfrak{p})
is the one we are ultimately interested in. The second sum, which is over ideals
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}
with two similarly-sized prime ideal factors, will later be bounded using an upper bound sieve.

Proof.

Since the statement is homogeneous in
w
, we may assume that
w
is 1-bounded. By adjusting implicit constants in the statement, we may assume that
X
is larger than an absolute constant depending on
B
. Let

(3.3)

D=X^{1/2}(\log X)^{-C},~u=(\log X)^{C}\text{ and }~z=X^{1/2}e^{-(\log\log X)^{2}}.

We put a total ordering on the prime ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
by declaring that
\mathfrak{p}_{1}<\mathfrak{p}_{2}
if
N\mathfrak{p}_{1}<N\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, and then ordering the prime ideals with the same norm arbitrarily. (The same technical device occurs in
[
24
, Section 4]
or
[
37
, Section 6]
, for example.) Note that there are at most
[K:\mathbf{Q}]
prime ideals of a given norm. By an
up-set
we mean a set
I
of prime ideals with the property that if
\mathfrak{p}_{1}\in I
, and if
\mathfrak{p}_{2}>\mathfrak{p}_{1}
, then
\mathfrak{p}_{2}\in I
. Since the prime ideals are well-ordered by
<
, every up-set is of the form
I(\mathfrak{p}):=\{\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}:\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\geqslant\mathfrak{p}\}
. However, for technical reasons during the proof it is sometimes useful to consider up-sets specified in the form
I(t):=\{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant t\}
, where
t\in\mathbf{R}^{+}
. There should hopefully be no confusion over which definition is being used at any given point.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
and let
I
be an up-set. We define

S(\mathcal{A},I)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{A}\\
\mathfrak{p}|\mathfrak{a}\implies\mathfrak{p}\in I\end{subarray}}w(\mathfrak{a}).

Given a set
\mathcal{A}
of ideals and an ideal
\mathfrak{d}
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{d}}
the subset of
\mathcal{A}
consisting of ideals divisible by
\mathfrak{d}
.

Now note that if
I_{1},I_{2}
are up-sets with
I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}
then we have the
Buchstab identity

S(\mathcal{A},I_{1})=S(\mathcal{A},I_{2})-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in I_{2}\setminus I_{1}}S(\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(\mathfrak{p})).

To see this, observe that if
w(\mathfrak{a})
appears in the sum
S(\mathcal{A},I_{2})
but not in
S(\mathcal{A},I_{1})
then
\mathfrak{a}
is divisible by a unique smallest prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{p}\in I_{2}\setminus I_{1}
. Of course,
\mathfrak{a}
lies in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and since
\mathfrak{p}
was chosen to be smallest, all other prime ideal divisors of
\mathfrak{a}
are
\geqslant\mathfrak{p}
, or in other words lie in
I(\mathfrak{p})
.

For notational brevity take
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
. By two applications of the Buchstab identity we have

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{C},I(z))

\displaystyle=S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in I(u)\setminus I(z)}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(\mathfrak{p}))

\displaystyle=S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in I(u)\setminus I(z)}\bigg(S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(u))-\sum_{\mathfrak{q}\in I(u)\setminus I(\mathfrak{p})}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{q}))\bigg)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(u))+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant u\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p})).

Note that in the last step here, we switched the roles of
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
, and unpacked the definitions of the
I()
terms.

On the other hand, note that
z^{3}>X
and recall that if
w(\mathfrak{a})
is non-zero then
N\mathfrak{a}\ll X
. Therefore the sum
S(\mathcal{C},I(z))
contains only two types of term
w(\mathfrak{a})
: those with
\mathfrak{a}
prime, and those with
\mathfrak{a}
a product of two prime ideals, both of norm
\geqslant z
. That is,

(3.5)

S(\mathcal{C},I(z))=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant z}w(\mathfrak{p})+\sum_{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}}w(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}),

where the second sum is over
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}
with
N\mathfrak{p}_{i}\geqslant z=X^{1/2}e^{-(\log\log X)^{2}}
, that is to say the second sum here is exactly the one appearing in
Lemma

3.2
. The constraint
N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant z
will easily be removed by trivial bounds, so our task is to bound
S(\mathcal{C},I(z))
, which we shall do via
3.4
.

Step 1: Deploying Type I information.

The first step is to bound the first two terms in (
3.4
),
S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(u))
, via Type I information, or in other words by use of
3.1
. Define
P(u):=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}<u}\mathfrak{p}
and let
\Pi_{u}
be the set of all
\mathfrak{a}
with at most one prime factor of norm
\geqslant u
. Then, unwinding definitions, we have that

(3.6)

S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(u))=\sum_{(\mathfrak{a},P(u))=1}w(\mathfrak{a})\bigg(1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}|\mathfrak{a}\end{subarray}}1\bigg).

Denote by
\mu_{K}
the Möbius function on (ideals of)
K
. Then we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|(P(z),\mathfrak{a})\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\mu_{K}(\mathfrak{d})=1_{(\mathfrak{a},P(u))=1}\cdot\bigg(1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}|\mathfrak{a}\end{subarray}}1\bigg).

Substituting into
3.6
yields

(3.7)

S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(u))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\mu_{K}(\mathfrak{d})\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}}w(\mathfrak{a}).

We now break the sum into two parts based on where
N\mathfrak{d}\leqslant D
and else when
N\mathfrak{d}>D
. For the first of these we have that

(3.8)

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
N\mathfrak{d}\leqslant D\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\mu_{K}(\mathfrak{d})\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}}w(\mathfrak{a})\Big|\leqslant\sum_{N\mathfrak{d}\leqslant D}\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}}w(\mathfrak{a})\Big|\ll X(\log X)^{-B+1}\ll X(\log X)^{-A};

here we have applied dyadic summation in
N\mathfrak{d}
and the Type I estimate
3.1
at each dyadic scale, and recall that
w
is
1
-bounded.

For the sum over
N\mathfrak{d}>D
, we use the trivial bound
|w(\mathfrak{a})|\leqslant 1_{N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}
(that is,
w
is
1
-bounded on supported on ideals of norm
\leqslant X
), obtaining

(3.9)

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
N\mathfrak{d}>D\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\mu_{K}(\mathfrak{d})\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}}w(\mathfrak{a})\Big|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
D<N\mathfrak{d}\leqslant X\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X\\
\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}\end{subarray}}1\Big|\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
D<N\mathfrak{d}\leqslant X\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\frac{X}{N\mathfrak{d}}.

By dyadic decomposition, this is bounded above by

(3.10)

\ll X(\log X)\sup_{L\in[D,2X]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
N\mathfrak{d}\sim L\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{L}.

Note that every
\mathfrak{d}
appearing in the sum here is squarefree (since it divides
P(z)
) and has at most one prime factor of norm
\geqslant u
. The product of the (distinct) prime divisors of
\mathfrak{d}
of norm
<u
must therefore have norm at least
D/z
, and therefore there must be at least
t:=\lfloor\log(D/z)/\log u\rfloor
of them. Using
Lemma

D.3
and
Lemma

D.4
, this implies that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|P(z)\\
N\mathfrak{d}\sim L\\
\mathfrak{d}\in\Pi_{u}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{L}\ll\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{p}_{1}<\cdots<\mathfrak{p}_{t}\\
N\mathfrak{p}_{i}<u\end{subarray}}\frac{L}{N(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{t})}\ll\frac{1}{t!}\big(\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}<u}\frac{1}{N\mathfrak{p}}\big)^{t}\leqslant\frac{(2\log\log u)^{t}}{t!}\ll(\log X)^{-A-1}.

For the final calculation here, it is helpful to note that
t=(\frac{1}{C}+o(1))\log\log X
, which follows from the choice of
D,u,z
specified in
3.3
.

Substituting into
3.10
, we see that the contribution to
3.7
from the sum over
N\mathfrak{d}>D
is
\ll X(\log X)^{-A}\|w\|_{\infty}
. Combining with the estimate
3.8
for the sum over
N\mathfrak{d}\leqslant D
, we obtain the bound

(3.11)

S(\mathcal{C},I(u))-\sum_{u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<z}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}},I(u))\ll X(\log X)^{-B+1}\ll X(\log X)^{-A}.

This concludes our analysis of the first two terms in (
3.4
) by the use of Type I sums.

Step 2: Splitting the
\mathfrak{p}
variable.

We now proceed to bounding the third term in
3.4
, that is to say
\sum_{N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant u,N\mathfrak{q}<z,\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))
. Here, we will use the Type II estimate
3.2
, but first we make some preliminary manœuvres aimed at decoupling the condition
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}
into conditions on
\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}
separately. We first estimate the contribution from large
\mathfrak{p}
. To this end set

(3.12)

y:=X^{3/8}.

Then we have

(3.13)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant y\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant y\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}w(\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q})\ll z^{2}\ll X(\log X)^{-A}.

In the first step, we observed that the only ideal
\mathfrak{a}
of norm
\ll X
, divisible by
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
and with all other prime ideal factors in
I(\mathfrak{p})
, is
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}
itself. For the last step, we recall the choice
3.3
of
z
. The estimate
3.13
is clearly acceptable in
Lemma

3.2
. The remaining task is to estimate the contibution from smaller
\mathfrak{p}
, namely

(3.14)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p})).

Now we begin the decoupling manœuvre in earnest. Set

(3.15)

M:=\lfloor(\log X)^{1+B/2}\rfloor

and choose spacing points

y=y_{0}>y_{1}>y_{2}>\cdots>y_{M}=u

where
y_{m}=y(u/y)^{m/M}
. Then splitting according to the size of
N\mathfrak{p}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p},N\mathfrak{q}<y_{m}\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))+\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}\leqslant N\mathfrak{q}<z}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p})).

Applying (assuming here the conditions
y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}\leqslant N\mathfrak{q}<z
, as in the right-hand sum) the Buchstab identity

S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))=S(C_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(y_{m+1}))-\sum_{\mathfrak{r}\in I(y_{m+1})\setminus I(\mathfrak{p})}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{r}},I(\mathfrak{r})),

one confirms that

(3.16)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{i=1}^{3}E^{(i)}_{m},

where

E^{(1)}_{m}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}\\
N\mathfrak{q}<y_{m}\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p})),\qquad E^{(2)}_{m}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}\\
y_{m}\leqslant N\mathfrak{q}<z\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(y_{m+1}))

and

E^{(3)}_{m}:=-\sum_{y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{r}<N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}\leqslant N\mathfrak{q}<z}S(C_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{r}},I(\mathfrak{r})).

What has been achieved here is that the first and third types of sum
E^{(1)}_{m}
and
E^{(3)}_{m}
correspond to atypical factorization patterns (with two prime factors very close in norm) and can ultimately be bounded fairly trivially, whereas in the term
E^{(2)}_{m}
the conditions on
\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}
are fully decoupled, which will allow us to relate these terms to Type II sums.

We turn now to the actual estimations. Looking first at
E^{(1)}_{m}
, note that

\displaystyle|E^{(1)}_{m}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p},N\mathfrak{q}<y_{m}\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}|\mathfrak{a}}|w(\mathfrak{a})|\ll X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p},N\mathfrak{q}<y_{m}\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}(N\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q})^{-1}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leqslant X\bigg(\sum_{y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}}(N\mathfrak{p})^{-1}\bigg)^{2}\ll X\bigg(\frac{y_{m}}{y_{m+1}}-1\bigg)^{2}\ll X(\log X)^{2}/M^{2}.

Here, in the penultimate step we bounded the sum over primes by the sum over all ideals
\mathfrak{a}
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
satisfying
y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{a}<y_{m}
, then used
Lemma

D.3
to bound the resulting sum. The estimation of
E^{(3)}_{m}
is almost identical. Since an ideal
\mathfrak{a}
with
N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X
has
O(\log X)
prime factors, each ideal
\mathfrak{a}
with
\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{r}|\mathfrak{a}
is counted by
\ll\log X
choices of
\mathfrak{q}
, and so we have

(3.18)

|E^{(3)}_{m}|\ll(\log X)\sum_{y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{r}<N\mathfrak{p}<y_{m}}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{r}|\mathfrak{a}}|w(\mathfrak{a})|\ll X(\log X)^{3}/M^{2},

finishing here in the same way as the estimation of
E^{(1)}_{m}
.

Step 3: Deploying Type II information.

We turn now to the heart of the matter, which is the estimation of
E^{(2)}_{m}
using Type II information. The key point is observing that
E^{(2)}_{m}

is
a Type II sum. Indeed we have

(3.19)

E^{(2)}_{m}=\sum_{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}}\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}\beta_{\mathfrak{b}}w(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})

with

\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}=1_{\mathfrak{a}\operatorname{prime}}1_{y_{m+1}\leqslant N\mathfrak{a}<y_{m}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\beta_{\mathfrak{b}}=1_{N\mathfrak{b}\leqslant X}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{m}\leqslant N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{q}|\mathfrak{b}\end{subarray}}1_{P^{-}(\mathfrak{b})\geqslant y_{m+1}},

where
P^{-}(\mathfrak{b})
denotes the norm of the smallest prime ideal factor of
\mathfrak{b}
. Clearly
|\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}|\leqslant 1
, and
|\beta_{\mathfrak{b}}|
is at most the number of prime ideal divisors of
\mathfrak{b}
, which is
\ll\log X
. Note also that
y_{m}/y_{m+1}=(y/u)^{1/M}<2
, and so
\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}
is already supported on a dyadic range of
\mathfrak{a}
, and so is of the form
3.2
with
L=y_{m}
. Note (recalling the choice
3.12
of
y
) that
u\leqslant L\leqslant y=X^{3/8}
, and so for all values of
m
our assumption on the Type II properties of
w
gives, from
3.19
, that

(3.20)

|E^{(2)}_{m}|\ll X(\log X)^{-B+1}.

We have now proven all of the necessary estimates; let us conclude the proof of
Lemma

3.2
by putting them together.

From
3.16
,
3.17
,
3.18
,
3.20
and the choice
3.15
of
M
and the fact that
B=2A+4
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}<y\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))\ll X(\log X)^{3}M^{-1}+X(\log X)^{-B+1}M\ll X(\log X)^{-A}.

The quantity on the left here arose as
3.14
. Together with
3.13
, this gives the bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\mathfrak{p}\geqslant u\\
N\mathfrak{q}<z\\
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{q}\end{subarray}}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}},I(\mathfrak{p}))\ll X(\log X)^{-A}.

The quantity on the left here is the third term in
3.4
. Combining with the estimate
3.11
for the first two terms in
3.4
, we obtain

S(\mathcal{C},I(z))\ll X(\log X)^{-A}.

Finally, from
3.5
and the trivial bound
|\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}<z}w(\mathfrak{p})|\ll z
, we obtain the bound claimed in
Lemma

3.2
.
∎

3.2.
Bounding the sum over semiprimes

For
Lemma

3.2
to be useful for sums over primes, we need a way to estimate the term
\sum_{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}}w(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2})
involving almost equally-sized semiprimes. We do this now under an assumption on
w
suitable for our intended purpose (of proving
Theorem

1.1
). We remark that in the work of Ford and Maynard
[
8
, Theorem 4.16]
, they point to the fact that if one only achieves Type I information below
1/2
and
w
is not bounded then one may not deduce any result regarding primes. However the weight function underlying
[
8
, Theorem 4.16]
places a substantial portion of weight on semiprimes; the role of the upper bound sieve is precisely to rule out this conspiracy.

At this point we specialise to the case of imaginary quadratic fields
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
and to weight functions of product form
2.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. Suppose that
w:\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is in product form
2.2
for some frequency
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
. Suppose moreover that
f,f^{\prime}
satisfy the pointwise bound

(3.21)

|f(x)|,|f^{\prime}(x)|\leqslant\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(x)+\Lambda^{\prime}(x).

where
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}},\Lambda^{\prime}
are defined to be zero on non-integer arguments. Let
\eta\in[X^{-1/10},\frac{1}{2})
be a parameter. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}\\
N\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}\leqslant X\\
N\mathfrak{p}_{i}\geqslant\eta X^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\big|w(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2})\big|\ll\frac{X\log(1/\eta)}{(\log X)^{2}}.

Remark
.

We take the domain of definition of
\Lambda^{\prime}
to be
\mathbf{Q}
, though it is supported on
\mathbf{Z}
.
The key point of the lemma is that it represents a nontrivial saving over the trivial bound of
O(X/\log X)
.

Proof.

We first dispense with the contribution from either
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
or
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
being generated by a rational prime. This is straightforward, since the number of such ideals with norm
\leqslant M
is
\ll M^{1/2}
. Using the trivial bound
|w(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2})|\leqslant\log^{2}X
, the total contribution from these terms is
\ll(X^{1/2}/\eta)^{3/2}\log^{2}X
, which is acceptable by a large margin.

It therefore suffices to establish for each fixed
\mathfrak{p}
not generated by a rational prime, and with
\eta X^{1/2}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X^{1/2}
, that

(3.22)

\sum_{\mathfrak{q}:N\mathfrak{q}\leqslant X/N\mathfrak{p}}|w(\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q})|\ll\frac{X}{(N\mathfrak{p})\log X},

where the sum is over prime ideals
\mathfrak{q}
not generated by a rational prime.
Indeed, this implies the result using

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}\\
N\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}\leqslant X\\
N\mathfrak{p}_{i}\geqslant\eta X^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\big|w(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2})\big|\ll\sum_{\eta X^{1/2}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X^{1/2}}\sum_{q:N\mathfrak{q}\leqslant X/N\mathfrak{p}}|w(\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q})|

and the fact, which follows using one of the Mertens’ estimates in number fields (see
Lemma

D.4
), that

\sum_{\eta X^{1/2}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X^{1/2}}(N\mathfrak{p})^{-1}\\
\ll\frac{\log(1/\eta)}{\log X},

where in these sums all ideals are prime but not generated by a rational prime.

We turn to the proof of
3.22
. An important subtlety here is that, while
w=f\boxtimes_{\ell}f^{\prime}
is supported on principal ideals, the prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
may not be principal. To deal with this, we use the representatives
\mathfrak{c}_{g},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g}
for the ideal classes as described in
Lemma

2.1
. Suppose that
\mathfrak{p}\in[g]
for some
g\in\operatorname{Cl}(K)
. Since
w
is supported on principal ideals, we may restrict attention to
\mathfrak{q}\in[g^{-1}]
. Henceforth, write
\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{c}_{g}
and
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}
. Then by
Lemma

2.1
we have
\mathfrak{p}=(a+b\sqrt{-n})\mathfrak{c}
for some
a,b\in\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathfrak{q}
is always of the form
(x+y\sqrt{-n})\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
for some
x,y\in\mathbf{Z}
. Also,
\mathfrak{c}\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}=(\gamma)
for some rational
\gamma
of height
O_{n}(1)
.

The generators of
\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}
are the numbers
u\gamma(a+b\sqrt{-n})(x+y\sqrt{-n})
,
u\in\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}
. Writing the units
u
as
\zeta_{j}+\zeta^{\prime}_{j}\sqrt{-n}
,
j=1,\dots,r
, where
\zeta_{j},\zeta^{\prime}_{j}\in\frac{1}{2n}\mathbf{Z}
, one may compute that these numbers are
a_{1,j}x+b_{1,j}y+(a_{2,j}x+b_{2,j}y)\sqrt{-n}
, where
a_{1,j}=\gamma(a\zeta_{j}-nb\zeta^{\prime}_{j})
,
b_{1,j}=-\gamma n(a\zeta^{\prime}_{j}+b\zeta_{j})
,
a_{2,j}=\gamma(a\zeta^{\prime}_{j}+b\zeta_{j})
, and
b_{2,j}=\gamma(a\zeta_{j}-nb\zeta^{\prime}_{j})
. Note here that we have
a_{i,j},b_{i,j}\in\frac{\gamma}{2n}\mathbf{Z}
for all
i,j
. Thus from the assumption that
w
has the product form
2.2
and the bound
3.21
we have

(3.23)

|w(\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q})|\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}+\Lambda^{\prime})(a_{1,j}x+b_{1,j}y)(\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}+\Lambda^{\prime})(a_{2,j}x+b_{2,j}y).

Here,
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
take the value zero if the arguments are not integers. Observe also that

(3.24)

\frac{1}{n}(a_{1,j}^{2}n+b_{1,j}^{2})=a_{2,j}^{2}n+b_{2,j}^{2}=a_{1,j}b_{2,j}-a_{2,j}b_{1,j}=\gamma^{2}(a^{2}+nb^{2})\neq 0.

Let
W:=X^{1/100}
. Then, recalling the definition
1.6
, we have the pointwise bound
\Lambda^{\prime}(x)\ll\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},W}(x)
for
W<|x|\ll X^{2}
, and so from
3.23
we have

(3.25)

|w(\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q})|\ll\sum_{j=1}^{r}\max_{Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\{Q,W\}}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{1}}(a_{1,j}x+b_{1,j}y)\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{2}}(a_{2,j}x+b_{2,j}y).

unless
|a_{i,j}x+b_{i,j}y|\leqslant W
for some
i,j
.

We turn to the task of proving
3.22
. The constraint
N\mathfrak{q}\leqslant X/N\mathfrak{p}
is contained in the constraint
|x|,|y|\ll(X/N\mathfrak{p})^{1/2}
(note here that
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
is simply a fixed fractional ideal with norm
\asymp_{n}1
). The contribution from
|a_{i,j}x+b_{i,j}y|\leqslant W
will be negligible. We sum
3.25
over all other
x,y
with
|x|,|y|\ll(X/N\mathfrak{p})^{1/2}
, ignoring any congruence conditions necessary for
\mathfrak{q}
to actually be an ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, as we may by the positivity of all the terms involved. In order for
\mathfrak{q}
to be a prime ideal, we must have
N\mathfrak{q}=(x^{2}+ny^{2})N\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
equal to a rational prime or the square of a rational prime.

Thus, writing
\gamma^{\prime}:=N\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
and
a_{1}=a_{1,j}
etc, it is enough to prove that

\sum_{|x|,|y|\ll(X/N\mathfrak{p})^{1/2}}1_{\operatorname{prime}}((x^{2}+ny^{2})\gamma^{\prime})\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{1}}(a_{1}x+b_{1}y)\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{2}}(a_{2}x+b_{2}y)\ll\frac{X}{(N\mathfrak{p})\log X},

for any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\{Q,W\}
, where recall that
Q
is given by
1.7
and
W=X^{1/100}
. (We omit the contribution from
(x^{2}+ny^{2})\gamma^{\prime}
the square of a prime, which is easily shown to be considerably smaller.) Since
1_{\operatorname{prime}}(t)\ll\frac{1}{\log X}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},W}(t)
for
|t|>W
, it is enough to prove that

(3.26)

\sum_{|x|,|y|\ll N}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},W}((x^{2}+ny^{2})\gamma^{\prime})\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{1}}(a_{1}x+b_{1}y)\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{2}}(a_{2}x+b_{2}y)\ll N^{2}

for any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\{Q,W\}
, where here we have omitted the negligible contribution from
|(x^{2}+ny^{2})\gamma^{\prime}|\leqslant W
, and now have written

(3.27)

N:=(X/N\mathfrak{p})^{1/2}.

Note that since
\eta X^{1/2}\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X^{1/2}
we have
X^{1/4}\ll N\ll X^{1/4}\eta^{-1/2}
. Recalling that
\eta^{-1}\ll X^{1/10}
, it follows that
N\leqslant X^{1/3}
, so certainly
W<N^{1/30}
.

The desired estimate
3.26
is a standard type of sieve bound, but it is not easy to find a suitable reference because (a) the sieving ranges
W,Q_{1},Q_{2}
can be different, so the underlying sieve does not have a well-defined dimension; (b) we need to track uniformity in the parameters
a_{i},b_{i}
; and (c) there are two variables
x,y
. We have found an approach based on an application of the large sieve to be the most convenient, though ‘small sieve’ arguments could also be made to work. The result we need is
Lemma

C.3
, which is a 2-dimensional variant of
[
28
, Equation (7.38)]
. Whilst generalising this kind of result from 1 to 2 or more dimensions presents no difficulty, we do not know of a reference for exactly what we need, and so we provide some details in
Appendix

C
.

Returning to the proof of
3.26
, suppose that
Q_{1}\leqslant Q_{2}\leqslant W
(the other case
Q_{2}\leqslant Q_{1}\leqslant W
is essentially identical). For rational primes
p
, we define sets
\Omega_{p}
as follows. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{*}
the set of primes dividing
2n
,
a^{2}+nb^{2}
or the numerator or denominator of
\gamma
or
\gamma^{\prime}
. Note that
\mathcal{P}_{*}
is a set of
O_{n}(1)
primes, since
(a^{2}+nb^{2})\gamma^{2}/\gamma^{\prime}=N\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime ideal.

We will be using
Lemma

C.3
; let us now define the sets of residues
\Omega_{p}
with which we will apply that result. If
p\in\mathcal{P}_{*}
, set

\Omega_{p}=\emptyset.

If
p\notin\mathcal{P}_{*}
, define
\Omega_{p}
as follows.
For
p\leqslant Q_{1}
, define

\Omega_{p}:=\{(u,v)\in(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{2}:(u^{2}+nv^{2})(a_{1}u+b_{1}v)(a_{2}u+b_{2}v)\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}\,p)\};

for
Q_{1}<p\leqslant Q_{2}
(which may be an empty range) define

\Omega_{p}:=\{(u,v)\in(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{2}:(u^{2}+nv^{2})(a_{2}u+b_{2}v)\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}\,p)\};

for
Q_{2}<p\leqslant W
(which may also be an empty range) define

\Omega_{p}:=\{(u,v)\in\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}:u^{2}+nv^{2}\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}\,p)\},

and define
\Omega_{p}=\emptyset
for
p>W
. The purpose of making these definitions is that we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},W}((x^{2}+ny^{2})\gamma^{\prime})\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{1}}(a_{1}x+

\displaystyle b_{1}y)\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}},Q_{2}}(a_{2}x+b_{2}y)

(3.28)

\displaystyle\ll(\log Q_{1})(\log Q_{2})(\log W)\prod_{p}1_{\Omega_{p}^{c}}(x,y)

(recall here that
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}\in\frac{\gamma}{2n}\mathbf{Z}
).

In order that we may apply
Lemma

C.3
, we compute the densities
\alpha_{p}=|\Omega_{p}|/p^{2}
in the different ranges. Here, note that if
p\notin\mathcal{P}_{*}
then, from
3.24
, we have

(3.29)

p\nmid(a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1})(na_{1}^{2}+b_{1}^{2})(na_{2}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}).

From this, we may compute that

(3.30)

\alpha_{p}=\frac{1}{p}\big(d(p)+(-n\mid p)\big)+O\bigg(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigg),

where
d(p)=3
if
p\leqslant Q_{1}
,
d(p)=2
if
Q_{1}<p\leqslant Q_{2}
, and
d(p)=1
if
Q_{2}<p\leqslant W
. (Here
d
is essentially the dimension of the sieve on the corresponding ranges.)
In particular, for all
p
we have
\alpha_{p}\leqslant 4/p
.

Now we bound the sum
\sum_{q\leqslant N^{1/2}}\mu^{2}(q)h(q)
appearing in
Lemma

C.3
from below. Recall that
h
is the multiplicative function satisfying
h(p)=\alpha_{p}/(1-\alpha_{p})
. To do this we use essentially Rankin’s trick, but it is instructive to phrase this in a probabilistic language. Define a random variable
Z:=\prod_{p\leqslant W}Z_{p}
, where the
Z_{p}
are independent random variables with
Z_{p}=p
with probability
\alpha_{p}
, and
Z_{p}=1
otherwise. Thus for squarefree
q
we have

\mathbb{P}(Z=q)=h(q)\prod_{p\leqslant W}(1-\alpha_{p}).

We give an upper bound for the quantity in
C.2
. We have

(3.31)

\sum_{q\leqslant N^{1/2}}\mu^{2}(q)h(q)=\prod_{p\leqslant W}(1-\alpha_{p})^{-1}\mathbb{P}\big(Z\leqslant N^{1/2}\big).

On the other hand,

\mathbb{E}\log Z=\sum_{p\leqslant W}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{p}=\sum_{p\leqslant W}\alpha_{p}\log p\leqslant 4\sum_{p\leqslant W}\frac{\log p}{p}<\frac{\log N}{4}.

By Markov’s inequality it follows that
\mathbb{P}(Z>N^{1/2})<\frac{1}{2}
, and so from
3.31

(3.32)

\sum_{q\leqslant N^{1/2}}\mu^{2}(q)h(q)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\prod_{p\leqslant W}(1-\alpha_{p})^{-1}.

Now from
3.30
we have (since the product of
1+O(p^{-2})
terms converges)

(3.33)

\prod_{p\leqslant W}(1-\alpha_{p})\asymp\prod_{p\leqslant W}\bigg(1-\frac{d(p)}{p}\bigg)\prod_{p\leqslant W}\bigg(1-\frac{(-n\mid p)}{p}\bigg).

However we have

\prod_{p\leqslant W}\bigg(1-\frac{(-n\mid p)}{p}\bigg)\asymp 1

since
L(1,(-n\mid\,\cdot\,))\neq 0
. (See, e.g,
[
42
, Theorem 4.11]
for a full discussion of the convergence of the infinite product here, which is only conditionally convergent.) Substituting into
3.33
and recalling the definition of the dimension function
d(\cdot)
gives

\prod_{p\leqslant W}(1-\alpha_{p})\asymp\prod_{p\leqslant W}\bigg(1-\frac{d(p)}{p}\bigg)\asymp\frac{1}{(\log Q_{1})(\log Q_{2})(\log W)}.

From this,
3.32
,
3.28
, and
C.3
, we obtain the stated bound
3.26
.
This concludes the proof of
Lemma

3.3
.
∎

Combining
Lemmas

3.2
and
3.3
and taking
A=4
immediately leads to the following, which is the only result from this section that we will need in what follows.

Proposition 3.4
.

Fix
C\geqslant 28
. Let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
, and suppose that
w:\operatorname{Ideals}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is in product form
2.2
with some frequency
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
, and suppose moreover that
f,f^{\prime}
in that definition satisfy the pointwise bound
|f(x)|,|f^{\prime}(x)|\leqslant(\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}+\Lambda^{\prime})(x)
. Define
\tilde{w}(\mathfrak{a}):=w(\mathfrak{a})1_{N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}
, and suppose that
\tilde{w}
satisfies Type I
(
with savings
(\log X)^{-12}
)
at
X^{1/2}(\log X)^{-C}
and Type II
(
with savings
(\log X)^{-12}
)
between
[(\log X)^{C},X^{3/8}]
. Then

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}w(\mathfrak{p})\ll_{C}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{2}}{(\log X)^{2}}.

4.1.
Reduction to polar coordinates

We begin with an exercise in partial summation, reducing

to the following slightly more technical-looking statement, which removes the von Mangoldt weight on

and replaces the rectangular cutoffs of

Recall from
Section

1.4
the definitions of
\Lambda^{\prime}
and of
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
n\equiv 0
or
4~\mathrm{mod}~6
. Let
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
,
|\ell|\leqslant(\log X)^{10}
be a frequency. Then we have that

\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}:x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant X}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})\Lambda^{\prime}(x)\Lambda^{\prime}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}=\frac{\pi\kappa_{n}1_{\ell=0}X}{\sqrt{n}\log X}+O\bigg(\frac{X(\log\log X)^{2}}{(\log X)^{2}}\bigg)

where
\kappa_{n}
is the constant in
1.1

Remark
.

The exponent

is somewhat arbitrary, being chosen so that

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
, assuming

4.1

Let

1.1
. Rescaling

where

and this is valid for

For

W\bigg(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\bigg)=-\int^{10}_{\eta}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbf{Z}}c(R,\ell)\psi_{R,\ell}\bigg(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\bigg)dR,

where the

(To verify this, substitute

We claim that

and that

To prove these statements, observe that

(4.5)

|c(R,\ell)|\ll|\ell|^{-2}\int^{1}_{0}\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}W(R\cos 2\pi\theta,n^{-1/2}R\sin 2\pi\theta)\Big|d\theta\ll|\ell|^{-2}\|W\|

for
\ell\neq 0
. Here, the first bound follows by integrating the definition of Fourier transform twice by parts, and the second from the chain rule.
We also have

(4.6)

|c(R,0)|\ll\|W\|.

Using
4.5
and
4.6
immediately leads to
4.3
and
4.4
.

From
4.2
and
4.3

W\Big(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\Big)=-\int^{10}_{\eta}\sum_{|\ell|\leqslant(\log N)^{10}}c(R,\ell)\psi_{R,\ell}\Big(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\Big)dR+O((\log N)^{-10}\|W\|).

when
x^{2}+ny^{2}\geqslant\eta N^{2}
. Now consider

S:=(2\log N)\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}}\Lambda^{\prime}(x)\Lambda^{\prime}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}W\Big(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\Big).

By

\displaystyle-\int^{10}_{\eta}dR\sum_{|\ell|\leqslant(\log N)^{10}}c(R,\ell)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbf{Z}\\

\displaystyle\psi_{R,\ell}\Big(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle+O(N^{2}(\log N)^{-9}\|W\|).

by

Therefore

\displaystyle S=-(2\log N)\int^{10}_{\eta}dR\sum_{|\ell|\leqslant(\log N)^{10}}c(R,\ell)\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}}\Lambda^{\prime}(x)\Lambda^{\prime}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}

\displaystyle\psi_{R,\ell}\Big(\frac{x}{N},\frac{y}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle+O(N^{2}(\log N)^{-8}\|W\|).

Applying

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=-\int^{10}_{\eta}dR\sum_{|\ell|\leqslant(\log N)^{10}}c(R,\ell)\bigg(\kappa_{n}N^{2}\int\psi_{R,\ell}+O\bigg(\frac{N^{2}(\log\log N)^{2}}{\log N}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+O(N^{2}(\log N)^{-8}\|W\|)

\displaystyle=-\int^{10}_{\eta}dR\sum_{|\ell|\leqslant(\log N)^{10}}c(R,\ell)\bigg(\kappa_{n}N^{2}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{2}}\psi_{R,\ell}\bigg)+O\bigg(\frac{N^{2}(\log\log N)^{2}}{\log N}\|W\|\bigg).

is given by

\ll(\log N)^{2}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant N^{2}\\

with

4.2.
Gowers norms

The Gowers norms, introduced by Gowers
[
14
]
, are a fundamental notion in additive combinatorics. A discussion of their basic properties may be found in many places; see for instance the introductions to
[
34
,
47
]
. We briefly recall the definitions. Let
f:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
be a function with finite support. We first define, for
h\in\mathbf{Q}
, the difference operators

(4.10)

\Delta_{h}f(x)=f(x)\overline{f(x+h)}.

(It is convenient for technical reasons later on to allow
h\in\mathbf{Q}
, but of course
\Delta_{h}f
will be zero unless
h\in\mathbf{Z}
). Now let
k\geqslant 2
be a parameter. Then we first define

(4.11)

\|f\|^{2^{k}}_{U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})}:=\sum_{x,h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}}f(x).

The Gowers
U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})
-norm
\|f\|_{U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})}
is then defined to be the unique non-negative
2^{k}
-th root of this quantity. Given a real number
N\geqslant 1
, we then define

(4.12)

\|f\|_{U^{k}[N]}:=\|f\|_{U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})}/\|1_{[N]}\|_{U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})}.

This definition will be relevant when
f
is supported on
[\pm O(N)]
. It is easy to see that this is completely equivalent to the definition given in, for instance,
[
34
, Definition 1.1]
, which is specifically geared to functions
f
which are supported on
[N]
. The normalising factor
\|1_{[N]}\|_{U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})}
has the effect that the maximum value of
\|f\|_{U^{k}[N]}
is exactly 1 if
f
is supported on
[N]
. For us, precise constants will never be relevant and it will suffice to know that

(4.13)

\|1_{[N]}\|_{U^{k}(\mathbf{Z})}^{2^{k}}\asymp_{k}N^{k+1},

which is clear from the definition. That the Gowers norms are well-defined and are norms are well-known; proofs of this statement may be found in many places, for instance
[
17
, Appendix B.5]
. We have the nesting property

(4.14)

\|f\|_{U^{2}[N]}\ll\|f\|_{U^{3}[N]}\ll\cdots\ll\|f\|_{U^{k}[N]}\ll\cdots

Further properties of the Gowers norms relevant to our paper may be found in
Appendix

A
. As a final remark, we note that all Gowers norms in the main part of our paper are at scale
X^{1/2}
, where
X
is our main global parameter.

4.3.

In this section we state our two main results linking Gowers norms and Type I/II estimates for weight functions in product form. We begin with the Type I statement.

Proposition 4.2

There is an absolute constant

with the following property. Let

4.6.
Proof of the main theorem

Recall our introductory discussion in

, and in particular the assertion

that our main theorem in the case

could be considered in terms of a sum over Gaussian primes. We begin by formulating such a statement for general

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
, and let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. Then we have

(4.20)

\sum_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant X}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})\Lambda^{\prime}(x)\Lambda^{\prime}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}w(\mathfrak{p})+O(X^{1/2+o(1)}),

where
\mathfrak{p}
runs over prime ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and

(4.21)

w=\Lambda^{\prime}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda^{\prime}.

Proof.

Recalling the definition of
\boxtimes_{\ell}
(see

) we see that the sum on the right of

is the sum of
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})\Lambda^{\prime}(x)\Lambda^{\prime}(y)
over
(x,y)\in\mathbf{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
for which
(x+y\sqrt{-n})
is a prime ideal.

Now the prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
are of two types: those which sit above a rational prime
p
which splits (or is ramified), in which case
N\mathfrak{p}=p
, or those which are equal to
(p)
for some rational prime
p
which does not split, in which case
N\mathfrak{p}=p^{2}
. The contribution of the non-split terms to the LHS of
4.20
is
\ll(\log X)^{2}\sum_{p\leqslant X^{1/2}}\sum_{x,y}1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}=p^{2}}\ll X^{1/2+o(1)}
, where the last bound follows from
Lemma

D.2
and the divisor bound. The contribution of the non-split terms to the RHS of
4.20
is at most
(\log X)^{2}
times the number of ideals with norm
p^{2}\leqslant X
for some
p
, which is again
\ll X^{1/2+o(1)}
.
∎

We may now prove the main theorem itself, assuming
Propositions

4.2
,
4.3
, and
4.5
.

Proof of

4.1
.

We start with the LHS of the expression in

, which we expressed as a sum over

in

(4.22)

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}w_{1}(\mathfrak{p}),\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}w_{2}(\mathfrak{p})\ll X\frac{(\log\log X)^{2}}{(\log X)^{2}}.

. Note that we do have the required boundedness condition

.
∎

5.
Preliminaries on concatenation and Gowers–Peluse norms

In this section we develop some preliminary material connected with concatenation theorems for Gowers norms. When dealing with concatenation, it is useful to introduce generalisations of Gowers norms first considered by Peluse
[
45
, Definition 2.1]
. Peluse calls these norms the
Gowers box norms
, but we prefer to see the term ‘box norm’ reserved for purely combinatorial graph- and hypergraph- norms and so will instead refer to these norms as Gowers–Peluse norms.

We first state the definition of the Gowers–Peluse norm.

Definition 5.1
.

Given a function
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
with finite support and
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Q}
, we define

(5.1)

\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}f(x)=f(x+h)\overline{f(x+h^{\prime})}.

Given a multiset
\Omega=\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}\}
of probability measures on
\mathbf{Q}
and a positive integer scale
N\geqslant 1
, we define

(5.2)

\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega]}^{2^{k}}=\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime})}f(x).

(As shown in
Lemma

A.1
, the RHS is a non-negative real number, and so
\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega]}
is uniquely defined as a nonnegative real number.)

Remark
.

The only difference between our definition and that of Peluse is the fact that we work with general probability measures rather than uniform measures on sets as Peluse did. (The two settings are equivalent, at least if one passes from sets to multisets as in
[
32
]
, with the multiset situation being a special case of the measure one, and the measure one a limiting case of the multiset one.)

The definition of the difference operators
\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}
should be compared with
4.10
, of which it is a mild variant. Once again, for minor technical reasons it is convenient to allow
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Q}
, but
\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}f
will be identically zero unless
h^{\prime}-h\in\mathbf{Z}
. Note that
\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}
,
\Delta_{(k,k^{\prime})}
commute. Later on it will be convenient to adopt the shorthand
\Delta_{M(h,h^{\prime})}f:=\Delta_{(Mh,Mh^{\prime})}f
.

If
f
is supported on
[\pm N]
and if the
\mu_{i}
are all uniform on
[\pm N]
then
\|f\|_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}
is a kind of ‘smoothed’ Gowers norm which is comparable to, but not exactly the same as,
\|f\|_{U^{k}[N]}
. We will not actually need this fact in the paper: for equivalent results see
[
44
, Lemma C.3]
or
[
31
, Lemma A.3]
.

We next record a certain general–purpose lemma and corollary which will be used in order to convert Gowers–Peluse norms whose underlying measures are somewhat close to uniform on
[\pm N]
into genuine Gowers norms, albeit of a higher order.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
,
N,T\geqslant 1
and consider a
1
-bounded function
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
, supported on
[\pm N]
. Furthermore let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on
[\pm N]
which is somewhat uniform in the sense that
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant T/N
. Suppose that
\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu]}^{2}\geqslant\delta
. Then
\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}[N]}\gg(\delta^{2}/T)^{1/4}
.

Proof.

The condition
\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu]}^{2}\geqslant\delta
is, written out,

\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime}\sim\mu}f(x+h_{1})\overline{f(x+h_{1}^{\prime})}=\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime}\sim\mu}f(x)\overline{f(x+h_{1}-h_{1}^{\prime})}\geqslant\delta N.

This implies that

\sum_{t\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1}\sim\mu}1_{h_{1}-h^{\prime}_{1}=t}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)\overline{f(x+t)}\Big|\geqslant\delta N.

Removing the averaging over
h^{\prime}_{1}
, we see that there is some
h^{\prime}_{1}
such that

\sum_{t\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{1}\sim\mu}1_{h_{1}-h_{1}^{\prime}=t}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)\overline{f(x+t)}\Big|\geqslant\delta N,

or in other words

\sum_{t\in\mathbf{Z}}\mu(h^{\prime}_{1}+t)\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)\overline{f(x+t)}\Big|\geqslant\delta N.

Cauchy–Schwarz and the assumption of the lemma give

\sum_{t\in\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)\overline{f(x+t)}\Big|^{2}\geqslant\frac{(\delta N)^{2}}{T/N}=\delta^{2}N^{3}/T.

Expanding the left-hand side gives

\sum_{x,x^{\prime},t\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)\overline{f(x+t)}\overline{f(x^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime}+t)=\sum_{x,t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}}\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t^{\prime}}f(x)\geqslant\delta^{2}N^{3}/T.

However, the left-hand side is
\gg N^{3}
times
\|f\|^{4}_{U^{2}[N]}
.
∎

The following corollary details how the above may be applied iteratively in order to replace a Gowers–Peluse norm with nearly uniform measures by a geunine Gowers norm (of twice the order).

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
N,T\geqslant 1
be parameters with
N
an integer, and let
f:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
be a
1
-bounded function supposed on
[\pm N]
. Let
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}
be probability measures supported on
[\pm N]
, all of which are somewhat uniform in the sense that
\|\mu_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant T/N
. Suppose that
\|f\|_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}\geqslant\delta
. Then
\|f\|_{U^{2k}[N]}\gg(T^{-k}\delta^{2^{k}})^{1/2^{2k}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be the uniform measure on
[\pm N]
.
We will prove by downward induction on
j=k,k-1,\dots,0
that, for any
j
,

(5.3)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu\\
j+1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j})}\Delta_{h_{j+1}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{j+1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{k}}f(x)\geqslant T^{j-k}\delta^{2^{k}}.

The assumption is the case
j=k
.
First note that this may be rewritten as

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant j-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu\\
j+1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\|\Delta_{(h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{j-1},h^{\prime}_{j-1})}\Delta_{h_{j+1}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{j+1}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{k}}f\|^{2}_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{j}]}\geqslant T^{j-k}\delta^{2^{k}}.

Write
\delta(h)
(where
h=(h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1},\dots,h_{j-1},h^{\prime}_{j-1},h_{j+1},h^{\prime}_{j+1},\dots,h_{k},h^{\prime}_{k})
) for the size of the inner
U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{j}]
-norm squared. Then, applying
Lemma

5.2
, we obtain using Cauchy–Schwarz

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant j-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu\\
j+1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\|\Delta_{(h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{j-1},h^{\prime}_{j-1})}\Delta_{h_{j+1}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{j+1}}

\displaystyle\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{k}}f\|^{4}_{U_{2}[N]}

\displaystyle\geqslant T^{-1}\mathbb{E}\delta(h)^{2}\geqslant T^{j-1-k}\delta^{2^{k}}.

Writing in the definition of the
U^{2}
-norm using dummy variables
h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j}
, this is

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant j-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu\\
j\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{j-1},h^{\prime}_{j-1})}\Delta_{h_{j}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{j}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}_{k}}f(x)\geqslant T^{-1}\mathbb{E}\delta(h)^{2}\geqslant T^{j-1-k}\delta^{2^{k}}.

This in turn is exactly the statement
5.3
in the case
j-1
, so the induction goes through.

The case
j=0
of
5.3
is the statement we want.∎

Another key corollary of
Lemma

5.2
is that if
a
and
b
are coprime then an average over
ax+by
being large can be converted to
U^{2}
-control.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be sufficiently small and let
M=O(1)
be a positive integer. Let
a,b\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{Z}\setminus\{0\}
satisfy
|a|\geqslant\eta|b|
,
|b|\geqslant\eta|a|
and
d:=\gcd(Ma,Mb)\leqslant 1/\eta
. Set
Q:=\max(|a|,|b|)
, and suppose that
N\geqslant Q^{2}/\eta^{3}
. Let
I_{1},I_{2}\subseteq[\pm N/\eta Q]
be subintervals of
\mathbf{Z}
. Suppose that
f:\mathbf{Q}\to\mathbf{C}
is
1
-bounded and supported on
\mathbf{Z}\cap[\pm N]
and that

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbf{Z}\\
x\in I_{1},y\in I_{2}\end{subarray}}f(ax+by)\Big|\geqslant\eta N^{2}/Q^{2}.

Then
\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}[N]}^{4}\gg\eta^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

We generally do not indicate dependence on
M
in implied constants.
Write
I_{1}:=\ell_{1}+[L_{1}]
and
I_{2}:=\ell_{2}+[L_{2}]
, where
L_{i}:=|I_{i}|
(and
[L]=\{1,\dots,L\}
). Set
t=a\ell_{1}+b\ell_{2}
and write
a^{\prime}=Ma/d
and
b^{\prime}=Mb/d
. Then the assumption may be written as

(5.4)

\Big|\sum_{\ell\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[L_{1}]+b^{\prime}[L_{2}])}f(t+\ell)\Big|\geqslant\eta N^{2}/Q^{2}.

(Note here that the sum is over the
multi
set
\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[L_{1}]+b^{\prime}[L_{2}])
.)
Let
H:=\lfloor\eta^{3}N/Q\rfloor
. Note that
H>1
by the assumption on
N
.
Suppose that
\ell^{\prime}\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[H]+b^{\prime}[H])
. Then

\Big|\sum_{\ell\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[L_{1}]+b^{\prime}[L_{2}])}f(t+\ell+\ell^{\prime})-\sum_{\ell\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[L_{1}]+b^{\prime}[L_{2}])}f(t+\ell)\Big|\ll\max(L_{1},L_{2})H,

since the two ranges of summation differ in only
O(\max(L_{1},L_{2})H)
terms and
f
is
1
-bounded. Averaging over
\ell^{\prime}
, it follows from this and
5.4
that

\frac{1}{H^{2}}\Big|\sum_{\ell\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[L_{1}]+b^{\prime}[L_{2}])}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[H]+b^{\prime}[H])}f(t+\ell+\ell^{\prime})\Big|\geqslant\eta N^{2}/Q^{2}-O(\max(L_{1},L_{2})H)\gg\eta N^{2}/Q^{2}

assuming
\eta
small enough, using here that
\max(L_{1},L_{2})\ll N/\eta Q
.
By the triangle inequality, and multiplying through by
H^{2}
,

(5.5)

\sum_{\ell\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[L_{1}]+b^{\prime}[L_{2}])}\Big|\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[H]+b^{\prime}[H])}f(t+\ell+\ell^{\prime})\Big|\gg\eta^{7}N^{4}/Q^{4}.

At this point we note for future reference that the assumption of the lemma and the fact that
I_{1},I_{2}\subseteq[\pm N/\eta Q]
imply that
L_{1},L_{2}\gg\eta^{2}N/Q
. Therefore, since we are assuming
N\geqslant Q^{2}/\eta^{3}
, we have
L_{1},L_{2}\geqslant Q
.

Now the maximum number of ways to write any
u\in\mathbf{Z}
as
a^{\prime}x_{1}+b^{\prime}x_{2}
with
x_{1}\in[L_{1}]
,
x_{2}\in[L_{2}]
is
\ll 1+L_{1}/Q\ll L_{1}/Q
, since if there is one solution then any other one is given by
x^{\prime}_{1}=x_{1}+\lambda b^{\prime}
,
x^{\prime}_{2}=x_{2}-\lambda a^{\prime}
, using here that
\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1
. It follows from this observation and
5.5
that

\sum_{x\in t+\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[H]+b^{\prime}[H])}f(x+\ell^{\prime})\Big|\gg\frac{\eta^{7}N^{4}}{Q^{4}}\cdot\frac{Q}{L_{1}}\gg\frac{\eta^{8}N^{3}}{Q^{2}}.

Note here that since
|da^{\prime}H|=|aH|\leqslant QH<\eta^{3}N
, and similarly for
db^{\prime}H
, and since
|t|<2N/\eta
, and since
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subseteq[\pm N]
, we may restrict
x
to the range
\{x\in t+\mathbf{Z}:|x|\ll N/\eta\}
. Then by Cauchy–Schwarz we have

\sum_{x\in t+\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}\in\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[H]+b^{\prime}[H])}f(x+\ell_{1}^{\prime})\overline{f(x+\ell_{2}^{\prime})}\gg\eta^{17}N^{5}/Q^{4},

or equivalently

(5.6)

\|f\|^{2}_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu]}\gg\eta^{17/4},

where
\mu
is the uniform measure on the multiset
\big(\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}[H]+b^{\prime}[H])-t\big)\cap\mathbf{Z}
. Since
t=\frac{d}{M}(a^{\prime}\ell_{1}+b^{\prime}\ell_{2})
, this multiset has size at least
\gg H^{2}/M
.

To apply
Lemma

5.2
we need an upper bound on
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
. Similarly to the remark above, for any
u\in\mathbf{Z}
the number of ways to write
u=a^{\prime}x_{1}+b^{\prime}x_{2}
with
x_{1},x_{2}\in[H]
is
\ll 1+H/Q\ll H/Q
, and so

\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{u}\mu(u)^{2}\leqslant\sup_{u\in\mathbf{Z}}\mu(u)\ll\frac{H}{Q}\cdot\frac{M}{H^{2}}\ll 1/\eta^{3}N.

Applying
Lemma

5.2
(and recalling
5.6
) then gives
\|f\|_{U^{2}[N]}\gg\eta^{3}
, as required.
∎

The key ingredient in our Type II estimate is the following result of Kravitz, Kuca, and Leng
[
32
, Corollary 6.4]
. We state it now, and for the sake completeness (and because some of our notation is a little different) we reproduce a proof of
Lemma

5.5
in
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1/2)
,
s\geqslant 1
,
I
an indexing set and
m
a power of
2
. Suppose that for
i\in I
and
j\in[s]
, we have symmetric probability measures
\mu_{ji}
supported on
[\pm N]
. Let
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
be
1
-bounded and such that

\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}\lVert f\rVert^{2^{s}}_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1i},\ldots,\mu_{si}]}\geqslant\delta.

Then there exists
t=t_{0}(m,s)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t}\in I}\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\Omega]}^{2^{d}}\geqslant\delta^{O_{m,s}(1)},

where
\Omega
is the collection of probability measures

\Omega=(\mu_{ji_{k_{1}}}\ast\cdots\ast\mu_{ji_{k_{m}}})_{j\in[s],1\leqslant k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{m}\leqslant t}

where
d=s\cdot\binom{t}{m}
.

6.
Type I up to
X^{1/2}/(\log X)^{A}

In this section we prove
Proposition

4.2
. We remark that the crucial manipulation, which occurs at the end of the proof, is essentially the base case for the concatenation proven by Peluse
[
45
]
(see
[
45
, Lemma 5.4]
).

Proof.

Replacing
\delta
by
\min(\delta,|\ell|^{-1})
, we may assume throughout that
|\ell|\leqslant 1/\delta
.

We start with the assumption
4.15
, that is to say

(6.1)

\sum_{\mathfrak{d}:N\mathfrak{d}\sim L}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}\\
N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X\end{subarray}}w(\mathfrak{a})\Big|\geqslant\delta X.

We are working under the assumption that
w=f\boxtimes_{\ell}f^{\prime}
(see
Definition

2.2
) and that
f,f^{\prime}
are supported on
\mathbf{Z}\cap[\pm 2nX^{1/2}]
. The aim is to prove that both
f
and
f^{\prime}
have large Gowers
U^{3}[X^{1/2}]
-norm. We prove the bound for
f
; the proof of
f^{\prime}
is symmetric.

The initial stages of the argument consist in manipulating things so that we can work with the fact that
w
is in product form. First, split the sum over
\mathfrak{d}
according to which ideal class
\mathfrak{d}
belongs to. For some such class, the sum is
\gg\delta X
. Let
g\in C(K)
be the inverse of this class, and let
\mathfrak{c}_{g},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}
be as in
Lemma

2.1
. Since
w
is supported on principal ideals, the inner sum over
\mathfrak{a}
is supported on ideals of form
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{b}
, where
\mathfrak{b}\in[g]
. By
Lemma

2.1
, we may parametrise these ideals by
\mathfrak{b}=(z_{2})\mathfrak{c}_{g}
where
z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathfrak{d}=(z_{1})\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}
, where
z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\setminus\{0\}
, where
\Pi_{g},\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}
are the sublattices of
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
described in
Lemma

2.1
.

This parametrisation covers each
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{d}
a total of
r^{2}
times, since associate values of
z_{1},z_{2}
(i.e. differing up to a unit) give the same
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{d}
. Thus
6.1
gives

(6.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\\
|z_{1}|^{2}\sim\gamma_{1}L\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}\\
|z_{1}z_{2}|\leqslant X^{1/2}/\gamma\end{subarray}}w((\gamma z_{1}z_{2}))\Big|\gg\delta X.

Here,
\gamma_{1}:=(N\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}})^{-1}
and
\gamma=\gamma_{g}
is the generator of
\mathfrak{c}_{g}\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}
, a positive rational of height
O_{n}(1)
(see
Lemma

2.1
(ii)). Using the fact that
w=f\boxtimes_{\ell}f^{\prime}
gives

(6.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\\
|z_{1}|^{2}\sim\gamma_{1}L\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}\\
|z_{1}z_{2}|\leqslant X^{1/2}/\gamma\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{z_{1}z_{2}}{|z_{1}z_{2}|}\Big)^{\ell}f\big(\gamma\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2})\big)f^{\prime}\big(n^{-1/2}\gamma\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2})\big)\Big|\gg\delta X.

(Note here that all associates of
\gamma z_{1}z_{2}
are automatically included in the sum
6.2
, since the set
\Pi_{g}
is invariant under multiplication by units.)
This may be written as

(6.4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\\
|z_{1}|^{2}\sim\gamma_{1}L\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}\\
|\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\\
n^{-1/2}|\operatorname{Im}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\Psi_{\ell}\Big(\frac{\gamma z_{1}z_{2}}{X^{1/2}}\Big)f\big(\gamma\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2})\big)f^{\prime}\big(n^{-1/2}\gamma\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2})\big)\Big|\gg\delta X,

where

(6.5)

\Psi_{\ell}(z):=\Big(\frac{z}{|z|}\Big)^{\ell}1_{|z|\leqslant 1}.

Note here that (for an appropriately large constant
C
) we can insert the two conditions
|\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}
and
n^{-1/2}|\operatorname{Im}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}
without harm, since
f,f^{\prime}
are supported on
[\pm O(X^{1/2})]
and so
|z_{2}|\ll(X/L)^{1/2}
on the support of the sum. It will be useful to carry a condition of this type through the proof, and we have written it in a Cartesian form with later manipulations in mind.

The next step is to perform ‘cosmetic surgery’ in the sense of Harman
[
21
]
to remove the cutoff involving
\Psi_{\ell}
, using Fourier analysis to replace this by products of phases in
\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2})
and
\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2})
. This allows us to fully decouple into functions of
\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2})
and
\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is a smooth approximation
\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}:\mathbf{C}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
with
\|\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, such that
\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}
and
\Psi_{\ell}
agree outside of the domain

(6.6)

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}:=\{z\in\mathbf{C}:|z|<\varepsilon\}\cup\{z\in\mathbf{C}:1-\varepsilon<|z|<1+\varepsilon\}

and such that for
z\in\mathbf{C}
we have the Fourier expansion

(6.7)

\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}(z)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{2}}W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})e(\xi_{1}\operatorname{Re}z+\xi_{2}\operatorname{Im}z)d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}

with

(6.8)

\|W\|_{L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2})}\ll(1+|\ell|)^{4}\varepsilon^{-4}.

Proof.

Identify
\mathbf{C}
with
\mathbf{R}^{2}
in the usual way. Let
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{2})
be supported on the unit ball and have
\int\psi=1
, and set
\tilde{\Psi}_{0}:=(\Psi_{0}\ast\psi(\frac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))(1-\psi(\frac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}))
. Since
\psi(\cdot/\varepsilon)
is supported on the ball of radius
\varepsilon
, it follows that
\Psi_{0}
and
\tilde{\Psi}_{0}
agree outside of
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
. By applications of the Leibniz rule, any
k
th derivative of
\tilde{\Psi}_{0}
is bounded by
O_{k}(\varepsilon^{-k})
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{2})
.

One can check that the
k
th derivatives of
z\mapsto(z/|z|)^{\ell}
(considered as a function from
\mathbf{R}^{2}
to
\mathbf{C}
) are bounded by
\ll_{k}(1+|\ell|)^{k}\varepsilon^{-k}
in the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
. To see this note that
(z/|z|)^{\ell}=(z/\bar{z})^{\ell/2}
, and thus

\Big|\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial z^{j}}\frac{\partial^{j^{\prime}}}{\partial\overline{z}^{j^{\prime}}}(z/|z|)^{\ell}\Big|=\Big|z^{\ell/2-j}\overline{z}^{-\ell/2-j^{\prime}}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\Big(\frac{\ell}{2}-i\Big)\prod_{i^{\prime}=0}^{j^{\prime}-1}\Big(\frac{\ell}{2}-i^{\prime}\Big)\Big|\ll_{j,j^{\prime\prime}}(|\ell|+1)^{j+j^{\prime}}|z|^{-j-j^{\prime}}.

As the standard coordinates on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
are
(z+\overline{z})/2
and
(z-\overline{z})/2
the result follows easily.

Now define
\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}(z):=(z/|z|)^{\ell}\tilde{\Psi}_{0}(z)
. This has the desired support properties and, by the above derivative bounds and more applications of the Leibniz rule, has
k
th derivatives bounded by
\ll_{k}(1+|\ell|)^{k}\varepsilon^{-k}
. Since
\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}
is smooth it has a Fourier expansion
6.7
with
W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\widehat{\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
. Integrating the definition of Fourier transform twice by parts in each variable gives

|W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|\ll|\xi_{1}|^{-2}|\xi_{2}|^{-2}\|\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{y}^{2}\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\ll(1+|\ell|)^{4}\varepsilon^{-4}|\xi_{1}|^{-2}|\xi_{2}|^{-2}.

Combining this with the trivial bound
|W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|\ll 1
gives that
\|W\|_{L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2})}\ll(1+|\ell|)^{4}\varepsilon^{-4}
, which is the desired bound
6.8
.
∎

Returning to the main line of argument, we replace
\Psi_{\ell}
in
6.4
with the smoothed approximant
\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}
from
Lemma

6.1
for an appropriate value of
\varepsilon
. Since
f,f^{\prime}
are
1
-bounded, the error in doing this is bounded by

\#\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\times\Pi_{g}:|z_{1}|\asymp L^{1/2},\gamma z_{1}z_{2}/X^{1/2}\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\},

where
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
is the domain
6.6
.
Since the lattices
\Pi_{g},\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}
are both contained in
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
, one may see that this error is
\ll\varepsilon X
. Taking

for an appropriately small constant

, this error may be absorbed by the RHS of

, and so we indeed have

(6.9)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\\
|z_{1}|^{2}\sim\gamma_{1}L\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}\\
|\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\\
n^{-1/2}|\operatorname{Im}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\tilde{\Psi}_{\ell}\Big(\frac{\gamma z_{1}z_{2}}{X^{1/2}}\Big)f\big(\gamma\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2})\big)f^{\prime}\big(n^{-1/2}\gamma\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2})\big)\Big|\gg\delta X.

Applying the Fourier expansion
6.7
, we now have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\\
|z_{1}|^{2}\sim\gamma_{1}L\end{subarray}}\Big|\int_{\mathbf{R}^{2}}W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}\\
|\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\\
n^{-1/2}|\operatorname{Im}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\end{subarray}}f_{\xi_{1}}(\gamma\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2}))f^{\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(n^{-1/2}\gamma\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2}))d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\Big|\gg\delta X.

where

f_{\xi_{1}}(t):=f(t)e\Big(\frac{\xi_{1}t}{X^{1/2}}\Big),\qquad f^{\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(t):=f^{\prime}(t)e\Big(\frac{n^{1/2}\xi_{2}t}{X^{1/2}}\Big).

By
6.8
(and recalling that
\max(1,|\ell|)\leqslant 1/\delta
), there is some choice of

such that

(6.10)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in\Pi^{\prime}_{g^{-1}}\\
|z_{1}|^{2}\sim\gamma_{1}L\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{2}\in\Pi_{g}\setminus\{0\}\\
|\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\\
n^{-1/2}|\operatorname{Im}(z_{2})|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\end{subarray}}f_{\xi_{1}}(\gamma\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}z_{2}))f^{\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(n^{-1/2}\gamma\operatorname{Im}(z_{1}z_{2}))\Big|\gg\delta^{9}X.

Now we pass to real and imaginary parts, writing

and
z_{2}=x+y\sqrt{-n}

(since, by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},b_{1}\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{Z}\\

Now we restrict to the situation where

\ll\sum_{d\geqslant D}L/d^{2}\ll L/D

\gcd(Ma_{1},Mb_{1})=d\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\in\Gamma^{\prime}\\

Let

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},b_{1}\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{Z}\\
|a_{1}|,|b_{1}|\ll L^{1/2}\\
\gcd(Ma_{1},Mb_{1})=d\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\frac{1}{Mn}\mathbf{Z}\\
|t|\ll X^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\in\Gamma^{\prime}\\
|x|,|y|\leqslant C(X/L)^{1/2}\\
-\frac{b_{1}}{n}x+a_{1}y=t\end{subarray}}f(a_{1}x+b_{1}y)\Big|\gg\delta^{18}X.

1_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{(\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})\in(\Gamma^{\prime})^{\perp}/\mathbf{Z}^{2}}e(\zeta x+\zeta^{\prime}y),

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},b_{1}\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{Z}\\

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},b_{1}\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{Z}\\

\gg\delta^{36}X^{1/2}
triples

with

and

and with

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbf{Z}\\

Our aim now is to apply

5.4
with

, we have for each such

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},b_{1}\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{Z}\\

with

\displaystyle X^{-1}\sum_{h,h^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}}\lVert\Delta_{h}\Delta_{h^{\prime}}f_{4}\rVert^{2^{2r}}_{U^{2r}[X^{1/2}]}\gg(\delta/\log X)^{O(1)}.

By expanding out the Gowers norm using
4.11
and
4.12
, the above is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lVert f_{4}\rVert^{2^{2r+2}}_{U^{2r+2}[X^{1/2}]}\gg(\delta/\log X)^{O(1)}.

This concludes the proof of
Proposition

4.3
.

8.
Computing the asymptotic

We turn now to the last main task in the paper, the proof of
Proposition

4.5
, which asserts an asymptotic for the ‘main term’

(8.1)

(\log X)\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}:x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant X}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(x)\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}.

8.1.
Hecke Größencharaktere on imaginary quadratic fields

In this section we recall some basic facts about (Hecke) Größencharaktere, as well as key results concerning sums over prime ideals that we will require.

A good and fairly low-level introduction to the notion of Größencharakter on an imaginary quadratic field is
[
28
, Section 3.8]
. In the setting of imaginary quadratic fields we can be quite explicit, and the Größencharaktere we are interested in are defined as follows. Let
\mathfrak{m}
be a non-zero integral ideal, that is to say an ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. By a
Dirichlet character
to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
we mean the lift of a character on
(\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{m})^{*}
to
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, defined to be zero for elements of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
which are not coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
(the definition is analogous to that of a Dirichlet character on
\mathbf{Z}
).

Definition 8.1
.

Let
K
be an imaginary quadratic field embedded in
\mathbf{C}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}
be a non-zero ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Denote by
I_{\bf{m}}
the group of fractional ideals which are coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
. Let
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}
. Then a Größencharakter modulo
\mathfrak{m}
with frequency
\ell
is a homomorphism
\psi:I_{\mathfrak{m}}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}^{*}
which satisfies
\psi((\alpha))=\chi^{(\ell)}_{\infty}(\alpha)\chi(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in K^{*}
coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
, where (as in
1.8
)
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(\alpha)=(\alpha/|\alpha|)^{\ell}
, and
\chi
is a Dirichlet character to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
.

Remark
.

We use the term Größencharakter to emphasise that the frequency
\ell
may not be zero. Some authors use the term Hecke character to mean a character of finite order, that is to say with
\ell=0
. Others such as
[
28
]
use the terms Hecke character and (Hecke) Größencharakter interchangably. This can be (and was!) a source of considerable confusion. We will use the term Hecke character only when the frequency
\ell
is definitely zero (or equivalently the character has finite order).

Note that if we have a Größencharakter
\psi
as above, then
\chi^{(\ell)}_{\infty},\chi
satisfy the ‘units consistency condition’

(8.2)

\chi^{(\ell)}_{\infty}(u)\chi(u)=1\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}$}.

We now show that the converse is also true. Let
I
be the group of non-zero fractional ideals in
K
. By a
class group character
we mean a homomorphism
\psi:I\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
which is trivial on principal ideals. Such a homomorphism factors through the class group of
K
, and so there are exactly
h_{K}
such characters.

Lemma 8.2
.

Suppose that
\chi^{(\ell)}_{\infty},\chi
as above satisfy the units consistency condition
8.2
. Then there is a Größencharakter
\psi
to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
satisfying
\psi((\alpha))=\chi^{(\ell)}_{\infty}(\alpha)\chi(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in K^{*}
coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
. Moreover
\psi
is unique up to multiplication by class group characters.

Proof.

Write
I^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}
for the group of principal fractional ideals coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
.
The units consistency condition is precisely what is needed to guarantee that there is a homomorphism
\psi_{0}:I^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}^{*}
with
\psi_{0}((\alpha))=\chi^{(\ell)}_{\infty}(\alpha)\chi(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in K^{*}
. The lemma follows from the fact that
\psi_{0}
extends to a homomorphism
\psi
on
I_{\mathfrak{m}}
. This is a special case of a general algebraic fact: if
G_{0}\leqslant G
are abelian groups, and
Z
is a divisible abelian group, then any homomorphism
\psi_{0}:G_{0}\rightarrow Z
can be extended to a homomorphism
\psi:G\rightarrow Z
. In the case that
[G:G_{0}]<\infty
(as in our situation) one may prove this fact in finitely many stages, first extending
\psi_{0}
to a homomorphism on
G_{1}:=\langle G_{0},x\rangle
by defining
\psi_{1}(g_{0}x^{r}):=\psi_{0}(g_{0})z^{r}
, where
z\in Z
is any element with
z^{d}=\psi_{0}(x^{d})
, where
d
is the order of
x
in
G_{1}/G_{0}
, and then proceeding similarly in further stages. (We leave it for the reader to check that
\psi_{1}
is well-defined. A Zorn’s lemma argument would handle the general case.)

It is clear that
\psi
is unique up to multiplication by characters
\xi:I_{\mathfrak{m}}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}^{*}
which vanish on
I_{\mathfrak{m}}^{0}
. We claim that any such
\xi
is (the restriction of) a class group character. To see this, consider the natural injective homomorphism
\xi:I_{\mathfrak{m}}/I_{\mathfrak{m}}^{0}\rightarrow I/I^{0}
induced by the inclusion
I_{\mathfrak{m}}\hookrightarrow I
, where
I^{0}
denotes the principal fractional ideals in
K
. It is a well-known fact that every ideal class in
K
contains infinitely many prime ideals (this can be proven using the prime ideal theorem for class group characters, which is a special case of
Proposition

8.3
below) and in particular at least one ideal coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
. Therefore
\xi
is surjective and is hence an isomorphism. The claim follows.
∎

In this section most Größencharaktere will have the same frequency
\ell
, which is the one in the statement of
Proposition

4.5
. Recall that we are making the assumption that
|\ell|\leqslant e^{\sqrt{\log X}}
.

We define the von Mangoldt function
\Lambda_{K}
on ideals by

\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\log N\mathfrak{p}&\mbox{if $\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{p}^{k}$ for some $k\geqslant 1$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proposition

8.3
below, a form of the prime ideal theorem for Größencharaktere, is the main input we require from classical multiplicative number theory. Before stating the result, we recall the notion of one Größencharakter being induced from another. If
\psi
is a Größencharakter to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
, and if
\tilde{\mathfrak{m}}
is another ideal with
\mathfrak{m}\mid\tilde{\mathfrak{m}}
, then
\psi
induces a Größencharakter
\tilde{\psi}
to modulus
\tilde{\mathfrak{m}}
by setting
\tilde{\psi}(\mathfrak{a})=\psi(\mathfrak{a})
if
\mathfrak{a}
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{m}}
are coprime, and
\tilde{\psi}(\mathfrak{a})=0
otherwise. The character which takes the value
1
for all nonzero prime ideals is called the
principal
character (to modulus
(1)=\mathcal{O}_{K}
). It induces a character to any modulus
\mathfrak{m}
, which we call the principal character to that modulus. A Größencharakter is called
primitive
if it is not induced from any Größencharakter of strictly smaller modulus.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
and let
X>1
. Then there is at most one ‘exceptional’ primitive Größencharakter
\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}}
to some modulus
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
and associated real number
\beta\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
such that the following is true uniformly for all Größencharaktere
\psi
on
K
whose modulus
\mathfrak{m}
and frequency
\ell
satisfy
|N\mathfrak{m}|,|\ell|\leqslant e^{\sqrt{\log X}}
:

\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\psi(\mathfrak{a})=X1_{\psi=\psi_{0},\ell=0}-\frac{X^{\beta}}{\beta}1_{\psi\sim\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}},\ell=0}+O(Xe^{-c\sqrt{\log X}}).

Here
\psi_{0}
is the principal character to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
. The exceptional charakter
\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}}
, if it exists, is quadratic
(
that is to say
\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}}^{2}
is the principal charakter to modulus
\mathfrak{d}^{*}
)
. The notation
\psi\sim\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}}
means that
\psi
is induced from
\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}}
, which in particular requires that
\mathfrak{d}^{*}\mid\mathfrak{m}
. Finally,
\beta
satisfies

(8.3)

1-\beta\gg_{\varepsilon}(N\mathfrak{d}^{*})^{-\varepsilon}.

Remark
.

The exceptional character depends on the scale
X
(which is fixed throughout the paper). The number
\beta
is a root of
L(s,\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}})
. If no exceptional character exists (which is conjectured to be the case) then the term with
-X^{\beta}/\beta
should be omitted.

Sketch.

This result is well-known to experts and versions of it have been used in several places; see, for example,
[
37
, Lemma 4.5]
for an essentially identical statement. However it is very difficult to locate a proof in the literature. Ultimately, it can be demonstrated by applying
[
28
, Theorem 5.13]
to appropriate zero-free regions for Größencharaktere, all of which may be found either explicitly or with minor modifications (but sometimes with only skeleton proofs) across papers of Mitsui
[
40
]
and Fogels
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
from the late 1950s and early 1960s. The arguments are analogous to the proofs over
\mathbf{Q}
(with Dirichlet characters) which are covered in complete detail in
[
3
]
or
[
42
]
. Due to the somewhat unsatisfactory nature of the literature here, the first-named author is preparing an expository document
[
20
]
with a complete proof.
∎

8.2.
Decomposing
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}

We now begin the proof of
Proposition

4.5
. The first step is to effect a decomposition of
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}
into two parts, using ideas related to the Brun sieve
(as discussed for instance in
[
12
, Chapter 6]
or
[
29
, Chapter 17]
). We will show that, of the resulting four terms contributing to
8.1
, all but one are small. The evaluation of the remaining term is a more substantial task, and is deferred to subsequent sections.

Write
\mathcal{P}_{Q}
for the set of primes
\leqslant Q
, and for
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}
, write
P_{S}:=\prod_{p\in S}p
.

Then by inclusion–exclusion we have

\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(x)=\prod_{p\leqslant Q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-1}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}}(-1)^{|S|}1_{P_{S}|x}.

Let

(8.4)

t:=20\log\log X,

and split

(8.5)

\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}=\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}+\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}

where

(8.6)

\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}:=\prod_{p\leqslant Q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S|\leqslant t\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|}1_{P_{S}|x}

and

(8.7)

\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}:=\prod_{p\leqslant Q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S|>t\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S|}1_{P_{S}|x}.

The following lemma will be used to guarantee that the contribution of the
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}
terms to
8.1
will be small.

Lemma 8.4
.

We have

(8.8)

\sum_{x\leqslant X^{1/2}}|\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}(x)|\ll X^{1/2}(\log X)^{-8}.

and

(8.9)

\sum_{x\leqslant X^{1/2}}|\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(x)|\ll X^{1/2}(\log X)^{2}.

Proof.

We begin with
8.8
. First note that
\prod_{p\leqslant Q}(1-\frac{1}{p})^{-1}\ll\log Q<\log X
. Therefore we have the pointwise bounds

(8.10)

\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(x)\ll\log X

and

(8.11)

|\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}(x)|\ll(\log X)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S|>t\end{subarray}}1_{P_{S}|x}\ll(\log X)\tau(x).

Moreover,
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}
is supported on
x
with at least
t
distinct prime factors, and thus on
x
for which
|\tau(x)|\geqslant 2^{t}
. Thus, on the support of
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}
, we have
\tau(x)\leqslant 2^{-t}\tau(x)^{2}<(\log X)^{-12}\tau(x)^{2}
. Substituting into
8.11
and summing gives

\sum_{x\leqslant X^{1/2}}|\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}(x)|\ll(\log X)^{-11}\sum_{x\leqslant X^{1/2}}\tau(x)^{2}\ll X^{1/2}(\log X)^{-8},

where in the last step we used
Lemma

D.1
in the case
m=2
. This is
8.8
.

Turning to
8.9
, observe the bound

(8.12)

|\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(x)|\ll(\log X)\tau(x),

which follows from
8.5
,
8.10
, and
8.11
and the triangle inequality. The desired bound
8.9
follows by summing over
x
.
∎

Substitute the decomposition
8.5
into
8.1
. This gives a sum of four terms. Three of these terms are

(\log X)\sum_{x,y\in\mathbf{Z}:x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant X}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})\Lambda^{\prime}_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(x)\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}

where
\Lambda^{\prime}_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}},\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}\in\{\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp},\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}\}
, and at least one of these functions is
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\flat}
. Using
Lemma

8.4
, each such term can be bounded crudely by
X(\log X)^{-5}
, here ignoring the constraint that
x^{2}+ny^{2}
is prime entirely. The remaining term involves two copies of
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}
. We have therefore reduced the task of proving
Proposition

4.5
to the task of showing the following asymptotic.

Proposition 8.5
.

Suppose that
n
is even. Then we have

(\log X)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbf{Z}\\
x^{2}+ny^{2}\leqslant X\end{subarray}}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(x+y\sqrt{-n})\Lambda^{\sharp}_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(x)\Lambda^{\sharp}_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}(y)1_{x^{2}+ny^{2}\operatorname{prime}}=\frac{\pi 1_{\ell=0}\kappa_{n}X}{\sqrt{n}}+O(X(\log X)^{-1}),

where
\kappa_{n}
is as given in
1.1
.

This will occupy us for the rest of the section. In particular, we assume that
n
is even from now on.

8.3.
Writing as a sum over ideals

The main work in establishing
Proposition

8.5
will be to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 8.6
.

Suppose that
n
is even. Then we have

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})=\frac{\pi 1_{\ell=0}\kappa_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}X+O(X(\log X)^{-1}).

The LHS here is closely related to the LHS of
Proposition

8.5
, with the key difference being that the
\log X
weight on primes is replaced by a von Mangoldt weight. The proof of
Proposition

8.6
is quite substantial. Before getting to the details, we deduce
Proposition

8.5
from it by a partial summation argument.

Proof of
Proposition

8.5
, assuming
Proposition

8.6
.

Using the definition of
\boxtimes_{\ell}
(see
Definition

2.2
) we see that the LHS in
Proposition

8.5
is equal to

(8.13)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\frac{\log X}{\log N\mathfrak{a}},

up to a negligible error from the contribution of
\mathfrak{a}=(x+y\sqrt{-n})
which are a proper power
\mathfrak{p}^{j}
,
j\geqslant 2
, of a prime ideal and the contribution of
x,y\in\mathbf{Z}
for which
N(x+y\sqrt{-n})=x^{2}+ny^{2}
is a proper power
p^{j}
,
j\geqslant 2
, of a rational prime. (This error is
\ll X^{1/2+o(1)}
using
8.12
to bound the
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}
terms.) To establish
Proposition

8.5
, it therefore suffices to prove that

(8.14)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\big(\frac{1}{\log N\mathfrak{a}}-\frac{1}{\log X}\big)\ll X(\log X)^{-2}.

For this we use partial summation, writing

\frac{1}{\log N\mathfrak{a}}-\frac{1}{\log X}=\int^{X}_{N\mathfrak{a}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t(\log t)^{2}},

so that the LHS of
8.14
is

\int^{X}_{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t(\log t)^{2}}\Big(\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant t}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\Big).

By
Proposition

8.6
, the bracketed expression is
O(t)
uniformly in
t
, and so the LHS of
8.14
is bounded by

\ll\int^{X}_{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{(\log t)^{2}}\ll X(\log X)^{-2}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

8.4.
Expanding with Größencharaktere

We now turn to the main task of establishing
Proposition

8.6
. The key idea is to write the expression on the LHS in terms of sums
\sum_{\mathfrak{a}}\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\psi(\mathfrak{a})
for Größencharaktere
\psi
, which will then allow us to bring in the key analytic input,
Proposition

8.3
. Opening up the definitions of the two copies of
\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}
(recalling the definition
8.6
), we may write

(8.15)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})=\prod_{p\leqslant Q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|\leqslant t\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|}E(S_{1},S_{2}),

where

(8.16)

E(S_{1},S_{2}):=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a}),

where

\varphi_{P_{S}}(x):=1_{x\in\mathbf{Z},P_{S}|x}.

To understand this better, write

(8.17)

\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2}):=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{K}:\gamma=x+y\sqrt{-n}\;\mbox{with}\;x,y\in\mathbf{Z},P_{S_{1}}\mid x,P_{S_{2}}\mid y\},

and observe that if
\mathfrak{a}=(\alpha)
is principal then

(8.18)

\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(u\alpha)1_{\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})}(u\alpha).

The definition does not depend on the choice of
\alpha
; also, by definition of
\boxtimes_{\ell}
,
\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})
= 0 if
\mathfrak{a}
is not principal.

Let us also take the opportunity to remark that, for any
P_{S}
appearing in a term
E(S_{1},S_{2})
involved in the sum
8.15
, we have
P_{S}\leqslant Q^{t}<\exp(\log^{1/8}X)
.

We now expand the function
\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}
using Größencharaktere. First we note that (see
Definition

2.2
) it is supported on principal ideals, a condition we detect using a sum over class group characters:

(8.19)

1_{[\mathfrak{a}]=1_{\operatorname{Cl}(K)}}(\mathfrak{a})=h_{K}^{-1}\sum_{\chi_{1}\in\widehat{\operatorname{Cl}(K)}}\chi_{1}(\mathfrak{a}).

Define
\mathfrak{m}:=(D)
where
D:=2nP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}
. We claim that
\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
is a union of congruence classes mod
\mathfrak{m}
. To see this, suppose that
\gamma\in\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
and that
\gamma^{\prime}\in\gamma+\mathfrak{m}
. Then
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma+D\beta
for some
\beta\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Recalling (from
2.1
) that
2n\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
, one sees from the choice of
D
that
D\beta\in P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n}]
, and the claim follows.

We will only care about
1_{\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})}(\gamma)
at values of
\gamma
which are coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
. Restricted to such values we have an expansion

(8.20)

1_{\gamma\in\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})}=\sum_{\chi(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,\mathfrak{m})}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\chi}\chi(\gamma)

in Dirichlet characters
\chi
to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
for some coefficients
c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\chi}
.

Substituting into
8.18
gives

\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})=\sum_{\chi(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,\mathfrak{m})}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\chi}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(u\alpha)\chi(u\alpha).

Note that the inner sum vanishes unless
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)},\chi
satisfy the units consistency condition described at the start of
Section

8.1
, since
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)},\chi
are both characters and

\sum_{u}\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(u)\chi(u)=\sum_{j=0}^{|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|-1}(\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}(u_{0})\chi(u_{0}))^{j},

where
u_{0}
is a generator for the group of units. If the units consistency condition is satisfied then it follows from
Lemma

8.2
that
\chi_{\infty}^{(\ell)}\chi
is the restriction to principal ideals of some Größencharakter
\psi
to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
, with frequency
\ell
. Therefore, for principal ideals
\mathfrak{a}
, coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
, we have

(8.21)

\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})=|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|\sum_{\psi}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}\psi(\mathfrak{a}),

where
\psi
ranges over a complete set of Größencharaktere
(\operatorname{mod}\,\mathfrak{m})
with frequency
\ell
modulo equivalence by class group characters, and we have abused notation by writing
c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}=c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\chi}
. Finally, combining with
8.19
we obtain an expression valid for
all
ideals coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
(not just principal ones) namely

(8.22)

\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})=|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|h_{K}^{-1}\sum_{\psi}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}\psi(\mathfrak{a}),

for all
\mathfrak{a}
coprime to
\mathfrak{m}
, where now the sum is over all Größencharaktere to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
with frequency
\ell
, and again we have abused notation by writing
c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}
for
c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\chi}
, where
\chi
is the Dirichlet character associated to
\psi
.

For use later on note that we have the bound

(8.23)

|c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}|\leqslant c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi_{0}}\leqslant 1,

where

,

over

. The right-hand bound in

8.5.
Contribution of the principal character

From this point on we will use the quantities
n_{*}
(squarefree part of
n
),
r
(defined to be
\sqrt{n/n_{*}}
) and
\omega\in\{\frac{1}{2},1\}
(depending on the value of

).

We now turn to the key computation which will lead to the constant in our asymptotic formula, which is that of the coefficient of the principal character
\psi_{0}
to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
, namely

\sigma(S_{1},S_{2}):=|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|h_{K}^{-1}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi_{0}}.

Note that this section is only relevant in the case
\ell=0
, since if

then the sum

does not contain the principal character. Assume, then, that
\ell=0
.
The idea is to sum
8.20
for
\gamma
in a set of representatives for

. By orthogonality of Dirichlet characters, this implies that

\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})/\mathfrak{m},\;\gcd(\gamma,\mathfrak{m})=1\}=|(\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{m})^{*}|c_{\psi_{0}}^{S_{1},S_{2}}.

We have (recalling the definition
8.17
of
\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
)

\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})=\{\alpha=x+ry\sqrt{-n_{*}}:x,y\in\mathbf{Z},P_{S_{1}}\mid x,P_{S_{2}}\mid y\}.

Using this we can almost immediately observe that, in certain cases,
\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})
is zero. In the following lemma, and for the rest of the section, set

T:=\{p\leqslant Q:p\mid n\}.

Lemma 8.7
.

Suppose that
\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})\neq 0
. Then
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
.

Proof.

If
p\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}
then clearly
p
divides both
\mathfrak{m}
and every element of
\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
, so in this case we have
\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})=0
by
8.24
and
8.25
.

Alternatively suppose
p\in S_{1}\cap T
. We have either (i)
p\mid r
or (ii)
p\mid n_{*}
(or both). In case (i),
p
divides both
\mathfrak{m}
and every element of
\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
, and we conclude as before. In case (ii),
p
ramifies (since
n_{*}
divides the discriminant
\Delta
; see
Section

2
), thus
p=\mathfrak{p}^{2}
for some prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
. We must then have
\mathfrak{p}\mid\sqrt{-n_{*}}
. Since
\mathfrak{p}\mid p\mid P_{S_{1}}
, we see that
\mathfrak{p}
divides both
\mathfrak{m}
and every element of
\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
, and we conclude as before.
∎

Assume henceforth that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
. To progress in this case, we must evaluate the two sides of
8.25
. Looking first at the right-hand side, we have

(8.27)

|(\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{m})^{*}|=(N\mathfrak{m})\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{m}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N\mathfrak{p}}\Big)=(N\mathfrak{m})\prod_{p\mid\mathfrak{m}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\bigg(1-\frac{(\Delta\mid p)}{p}\bigg),

where
\mathfrak{p}
ranges over the distinct prime ideals dividing
\mathfrak{m}
, and
p
ranges over the distinct rational primes dividing
\mathfrak{m}
. (To see the second expression, consider the possible splitting types of
p
, with reference to the remarks in
Section

2
.)

Using the fact that
\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-n_{*}}]
in the case
\omega=1
and
\mathbf{Z}[\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{-n_{*}})]
in the case
\omega=\frac{1}{2}
, one may check that a complete set of representatives for
\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})
mod
\mathfrak{m}
is

\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})=\{\alpha=x+ry\sqrt{-n_{*}}:0\leqslant x<2nP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}},\;0\leqslant y<2\omega nP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}/r,\;P_{S_{1}}\mid x,\;P_{S_{2}}\mid y\},

or equivalently

(8.28)

\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})=\{\alpha=aP_{S_{1}}+brP_{S_{2}}\sqrt{-n_{*}}:0\leqslant a<2nP_{S_{2}},\;0\leqslant b<2\omega nP_{S_{1}}/r\}.

Lemma 8.8
.

Suppose that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
. Then the elements of
8.28
having a common factor with
\mathfrak{m}
are precisely:

(1)

Those with
p\mid a
for some
p\in S_{2}\cup T
;

(2)

Those with
p\mid b
for some
p\in S_{1}
.

Remark
.

Note that, in our main theorem,
n

is
even.

Proof.

The set of primes factors of
\mathfrak{m}
is
S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup T
(here we use the assumption that
n
is even in
Proposition

8.5
).

Suppose that
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime ideal common factor of
\alpha=aP_{S_{1}}+brP_{S_{2}}\sqrt{-n_{*}}
and
\mathfrak{m}
. Let
p
be the rational prime above
\mathfrak{p}
. Then we have exactly one of (i)
p\in S_{2}\cup T
or (ii)
p\in S_{1}
.

In case (i), we break in further cases based on whether
p\in S_{2}
,
p\mid n_{\ast}
, or
p\mid r
. Suppose first that
p\in S_{2}
. Then
\mathfrak{p}\mid p\mid P_{S_{2}}
, whilst
\mathfrak{p}
is coprime to
P_{S_{1}}
since
S_{1},S_{2}
are disjoint. Thus the condition that
\mathfrak{p}\mid\alpha
is then precisely that
\mathfrak{p}\mid a
, or equivalently
p\mid a
. Next suppose (still in case (i)) that
p\mid n_{*}
. Then
p
is ramified and
p=\mathfrak{p}^{2}
, and
\mathfrak{p}\mid\sqrt{-n_{*}}
. Thus
\mathfrak{p}\mid aP_{S_{1}}
which, since we are assuming
p\notin S_{1}
, means that
\mathfrak{p}\mid a
and hence
p\mid a
is again the condition.
Finally suppose (still in case (i)) that
p\mid r
, so
\mathfrak{p}\mid r
. Then, since
\mathfrak{p}\nmid P_{S_{1}}
, we immediately see that the condition is again
p\mid a
.

In case (ii), since
p\in S_{1}
we have
\mathfrak{p}\mid P_{S_{1}}
and so
\mathfrak{p}\mid brP_{S_{2}}\sqrt{-n_{*}}
. Now
\mathfrak{p}
is coprime to
r
(since
S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
), to
P_{S_{2}}
(since
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset
) and to
\sqrt{-n_{*}}
(since otherwise
p\mid n
, as explained above, but this contradicts
S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
).
Therefore
p\mid b
, as claimed.

All the above implications are reversible, as may easily be checked.
∎

As a consequence of this lemma and
8.28
it follows that

(8.29)

\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma(S_{1},S_{2})/\mathfrak{m},\;\gcd(\gamma,\mathfrak{m})=1\}=\frac{4\omega n^{2}P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}}{r}\prod_{p\mid\mathfrak{m}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)=\frac{\omega N\mathfrak{m}}{rP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}}\prod_{p\mid\mathfrak{m}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big).

Comparing
8.29
,
8.27
,
8.25
, and
8.24
gives

(8.30)

\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})=\frac{\omega|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|}{rh_{K}P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}}\prod_{p\mid\mathfrak{m}}\bigg(1-\frac{(\Delta\mid p)}{p}\bigg)^{-1}.

It is convenient to write this as

(8.31)

\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})=\frac{\omega|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|P_{T\setminus S_{2}}}{rh_{K}}\prod_{p\in T\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\nu(p),

where

(8.32)

\nu(p):=(p-(\Delta\mid p))^{-1},

and recall that this is under the assumption that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
(otherwise
Lemma

8.7
applies).

8.6.
Applying the prime ideal theorem

Now we return to our main task of proving

8.6
. Recall the expansion

and the definition
8.16
of the terms

appearing there, that is to say

E(S_{1},S_{2}):=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a}).

Moreover, in

we obtained an expansion for
\varphi_{P_{S_{1}}}\boxtimes_{\ell}\varphi_{P_{S_{2}}}(\mathfrak{a})
in characters, in the case that

E(S_{1},S_{2})=\frac{|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|}{h_{K}}\sum_{\psi}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X\\
\gcd(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{m}^{S_{1},S_{2}})=1\end{subarray}}\psi(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})+O(\log X),

where the sum is over Größencharaktere
\psi
to modulus
\mathfrak{m}
with frequency
\ell
, and where the

\ll\sum_{\mathfrak{p}^{j}\mid\mathfrak{m}^{S_{1},S_{2}}}\log N\mathfrak{p}\ll\log X.

In the following

are absolute constants. Applying

E(S_{1},S_{2})=\frac{|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|}{h_{K}}\bigg(Xc_{\psi_{0}}^{S_{1},S_{2}}-\frac{X^{\beta}}{\beta}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi^{*}}\bigg)+O\bigg(Xe^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\log X}}\Big(1+\sum_{\psi}|c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi}|\Big)\bigg),

where here all Größencharaktere

are to modulus

and have frequency

,

is the principal character to modulus

(which is only present if

and
\mathfrak{d}^{*}\mid\mathfrak{m}

to modulus

with frequency

In particular, the whole main term here is absent unless

.

Recall (see
8.24
) that we gave the name

to the coefficient

E(S_{1},S_{2})=X\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})-\frac{|\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}|}{h_{K}}\frac{X^{\beta}}{\beta}c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi^{*}}+O\big(Xe^{-c_{2}\sqrt{\log X}}\big).

Now it is time to substitute this into the formula

for the expression of interest in

8.6
. Doing this, we obtain

(8.36)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leqslant X}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}\boxtimes_{\ell}\Lambda_{{\textnormal{Cram\'{e}r}}}^{\sharp}(\mathfrak{a})\Lambda_{K}(\mathfrak{a})=E_{\operatorname{main}}+E_{{\textnormal{Siegel}}}+E_{\textnormal{error}},

where

(8.37)

E_{\operatorname{main}}:=X\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{Q}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|\leqslant t\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|}\sigma(S_{1},S_{2}),

(8.38)

E_{{\textnormal{Siegel}}}\ll(\log Q)^{2}X^{\beta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|\leqslant t\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi^{*}(\mbox{\scriptsize mod}\,\mathfrak{m}^{S_{1},S_{2}})\\
\psi_{*}\sim\chi_{\mathfrak{d}^{*}}\end{subarray}}|c_{\psi^{*}}^{S_{1},S_{2}}|,

and

(8.39)

E_{\textnormal{error}}\ll X(\log Q)^{2}\binom{|\mathcal{P}_{Q}|}{t}^{2}e^{-c_{2}\sqrt{\log X}}\ll Xe^{-c_{3}\sqrt{\log X}}.

The term

is acceptable in

in the case

, since in this case both

Suppose from now on that

is small; this will then conclude the proof of

8.6
in all cases.

8.7.
Estimating the Siegel term

In this subsection we estimate the Siegel term
E_{{\textnormal{Siegel}}}
. Write
d_{*}:=N\mathfrak{d}^{*}
. First note that a pair
S_{1},S_{2}
of subsets of
\mathcal{P}_{Q}
only contributes to the sum if
d_{*}\mid(2nP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}})^{2}=N\mathfrak{m}^{S_{1},S_{2}}
, since
\mathfrak{d}^{*}\mid\mathfrak{m}
for any exceptional modulus
\mathfrak{m}
.

To bound the contribution of these terms, we first upgrade the crude bound
8.23
using the knowledge about
c^{S_{1},S_{2}}_{\psi_{0}}
gained in previous sections. Specifically, from
8.23
,
8.24
, and
8.30
we have

|c_{\psi^{*}}^{S_{1},S_{2}}|\leqslant c_{\psi_{0}}^{S_{1},S_{2}}\ll\frac{\log Q}{P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}}.

It follows that

(8.40)

E_{{\textnormal{Siegel}}}\ll(\log Q)^{3}X^{\beta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
d_{*}\mid(2nP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}})^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}}.

To bound this sum, note that the condition
d_{*}\mid(2nP_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}})^{2}
implies that
d_{1}\mid P_{S_{1}}
and
d_{2}\mid P_{S_{2}}
for some divisors
d_{1},d_{2}
of
d_{*}
with
d_{1}d_{2}\geqslant\sqrt{d_{*}/4n^{2}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle E_{{\textnormal{Siegel}}}

\displaystyle\ll(\log Q)^{3}X^{\beta}\tau(d_{*})^{2}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}|d_{\ast},d_{2}|d_{\ast}\\
d_{1}d_{2}\geqslant\sqrt{d_{*}/4n^{2}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
d_{1}\mid P_{S_{1}},d_{2}\mid P_{S_{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}}\ll(\log Q)^{3}X^{\beta}\tau(d_{*})^{2}\cdot(\log Q)^{2}\cdot d_{\ast}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\ll(\log X)X^{\beta}d_{*}^{2\eta-1/2}\ll X(\log X)X^{-C_{\varepsilon}d_{*}^{-\varepsilon}}d_{*}^{2\eta-1/2},

where in the last step we applied Siegel’s theorem
8.3
, and
\eta>0
is any positive constant coming from the divisor bound
\tau(d_{*})\ll_{\eta}d_{*}^{\eta}
.
Therefore, computing the maximum over
d_{*}
of the preceding expression, we see that

(8.41)

E_{{\textnormal{Siegel}}}\ll_{\eta,\varepsilon}X(\log X)^{1-\frac{\frac{1}{2}-2\eta}{\varepsilon}}\ll\frac{X}{\log X}

no matter the value of
d_{*}
, taking any
\varepsilon,\eta<\frac{1}{8}
for the second bound.

8.8.
Evaluation of
E_{\operatorname{main}}

We now come to the final task, which is the asymptotic evaluation of
E_{\operatorname{main}}
, whose definition is
8.37
. First, note that by
Lemma

8.7
we may restrict the sum to
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset
, that is to say

(8.42)

E_{\operatorname{main}}:=X\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{Q}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset\\
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|\leqslant t\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|}\sigma(S_{1},S_{2}).

For the remaining terms in the sum we have the formula
8.31
and the accompanying definition
8.32
of
\nu(p)
, which in particular gives the bound

(8.43)

\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})\ll\prod_{p\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\frac{1}{p-1}.

Using this, we show using another Brun sieve-type computation that we may remove the constraints
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|\leqslant t
in
8.42
with little penalty. Indeed, by
8.43
the error in doing this is

\displaystyle\ll X(\log Q)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S_{2}|>t\\
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\frac{1}{(p-1)}

\displaystyle\ll X(\log Q)^{2}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{Q}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{p-1}\Big)\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S|>t\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\in S}\frac{1}{p-1}

\displaystyle\ll X(\log Q)^{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
|S|>t\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\in S}\frac{1}{p-1}\ll X(\log X)^{-10},

where the last step follows from
. Thus

(8.44)

E_{\operatorname{main}}=X\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{Q}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|}\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})+O(X(\log X)^{-10}).

The main expression here is of a rather algebraic nature and we can evaluate it using
8.31
. To do this, set

S^{\prime}_{1}:=S_{1},\quad S^{\prime}_{2}:=S_{2}\setminus T,\quad S^{\prime}_{3}:=T\setminus S_{2},\quad S^{\prime}_{4}:=T\cap S_{2}.

Note that these sets are all disjoint and that
S^{\prime}_{2},S^{\prime}_{4}
give a partition of
S_{2}
, whilst
\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}S^{\prime}_{i}
is a partition of
S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup T
. Then we obtain from
8.31
that

\displaystyle\frac{rh_{K}}{\omega|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\\
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=S_{1}\cap T=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|}\sigma(S_{1},S_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}\setminus T,S^{\prime}_{1}\cap S^{\prime}_{2}=\emptyset\\
S^{\prime}_{3}\subseteq T,S^{\prime}_{4}=T\setminus S^{\prime}_{3}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|S^{\prime}_{1}|+|S^{\prime}_{2}|+|S^{\prime}_{4}|}P_{S^{\prime}_{3}}\prod_{p\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}S^{\prime}_{i}}\nu(p)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{Q}\setminus T}(1-2\nu(p))\cdot\prod_{p\in T}(p-1)\nu(p)

(8.45)

\displaystyle=\nu(2)\cdot\prod_{3\leqslant p\leqslant Q}(1-2\nu(p))\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}\frac{(p-1)\nu(p)}{1-2\nu(p)}.

(We are using here the fact that
n
is even, so
2\in T
; note that
1-2\nu(p)\neq 0
for
p\geqslant 3
.)

Therefore from
8.44
we have

(8.46)

E_{\operatorname{main}}=X\frac{\omega rh_{K}}{|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|}\cdot 4\nu(2)\cdot\prod_{3\leqslant p\leqslant Q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}(1-2\nu(p))\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}\frac{(p-1)\nu(p)}{1-2\nu(p)}+O(X(\log X)^{-10}).

The product over
p
here is absolutely convergent, with each term having size
1-O(p^{-2})
, and so we may extend it to all
p
with a multiplicative loss of
1+O(1/Q)
, the effect of which may comfortably be absorbed into the
O(X(\log X)^{-10})
error term.

Finally, we apply the class number formula
2.6
, which in our notation gives

(8.47)

\frac{2\pi h_{K}}{|\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}||\Delta|^{1/2}}=\prod_{p}p\nu(p).

This, the formula
\Delta=-4\omega^{2}n_{*}
and
8.46
combine to give

(8.48)

E_{\operatorname{main}}=X\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot 2\cdot\bigg(\prod_{3\leqslant p\leqslant Q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\frac{1-2\nu(p)}{p\nu(p)}\bigg)\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}\frac{(p-1)\nu(p)}{1-2\nu(p)}+O(X(\log X)^{-10}).

We claim that the main expression here is precisely
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{n}}\kappa_{n}X
with
\kappa_{n}
as in
1.1
, which is exactly the constant in
Proposition

4.1
. We verify this one prime
p
at a time (the contribution of the prime
p=2
being
2
). Here, once more, we use the fact that
n
is even.

If
(-n\mid p)=1
then
(\Delta\mid p)=1
and
p\nmid n
, so the contribution from
p
is

\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\frac{1-2\nu(p)}{p\nu(p)}=\frac{p(p-3)}{(p-1)^{2}}.

If
(-n\mid p)=-1
then
(\Delta\mid p)=-1
and
p\nmid n
, so the contribution from
p
is

\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{-2}\frac{1-2\nu(p)}{p\nu(p)}=\frac{p}{p-1}.

If
(-n\mid p)=0
then
p\mid n
, and in this case we also get a contribution from
p
of
p/(p-1)
, both when
p\geqslant 3
and
p=2
.
This verifies the claim, and therefore

E_{\operatorname{main}}=\frac{\pi\kappa_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}X+O(X(\log X)^{-10}).

Combining the preceding estimate with
8.41
,
8.39
, and
8.36
, we obtain the conclusion of
Proposition

8.6
in the case
\ell=0
. Recall that the case
\ell\neq 0
has already been established.

This concludes the proof of all the statements in the paper.

Appendix A
Properties of the Gowers and Gowers–Peluse norms

In this section, we collect some basic properties of Gowers–Peluse norms which were defined in
Definition

5.1
. Let
g:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
be a finitely-supported function. Recall that
\Delta_{h}g(x)=g(x)\overline{g(x+h)}
and that
\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}g(x)=g(x+h)\overline{g(x+h^{\prime})}
as in
4.10
and
5.1
respectively. We begin by noting that

(A.1)

\sum_{x,h\in\mathbf{Z}}\Delta_{h}g(x)=\Big|\sum_{x}g(x)\Big|^{2}\geqslant 0

and that, for any probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbf{Z}
,

(A.2)

\mathbb{E}_{h,h^{\prime}\sim\mu}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\Delta_{(h,h^{\prime})}g(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mu}g(x+h)\Big|^{2}\geqslant 0.

We first record that the Gowers–Peluse norm is nonnegative and that we have a version of the Gowers–Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. To the latter end, if we have a collection
(f_{\omega})_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}
of functions, we define the Gowers–Peluse inner product

(A.3)

\langle(f_{\omega})_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\rangle_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu_{i}}\prod_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\mathcal{C}^{|\omega|}f_{\omega}(x+(\mathbf{1}-\omega)\cdot h+\omega\cdot h^{\prime}),

where here
\mathbf{1}-\omega=(1-\omega_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
and
h=(h_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
,
h^{\prime}=(h^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. Note in particular that if
f_{\omega}=f
for all
\omega
then

\langle(f_{\omega})_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\rangle_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}=\|f\|_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}},

with notation as in
Definition

5.1
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow[0,1]
be probability measures, let
N\geqslant 1
, and suppose that
f:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
is a function. Then for any function
f:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
we have

(A.4)

\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}}\geqslant 0.

Moreover if
(f_{\omega})_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}
are functions from
\mathbf{Z}
to
\mathbf{C}
then we have that

(A.5)

\langle(f_{\omega})_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\rangle_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}\leqslant\prod_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\|f_{\omega}\|_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}.

Proof.

Statement
A.4
is immediate from
A.2
and the definition
5.2
, taking

g=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h^{\prime}_{1})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k-1},h^{\prime}_{k-1})}f,\qquad\mu=\mu_{k},

in
A.2
.
For
A.5
, we proceed by induction on
k
. First note that by splitting
\omega=(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)
with
\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{k-1}
and
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
, and writing
\tilde{h}=(h_{i})_{i=1}^{k-1}
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime}=(h^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{k-1}
,
we may write the Gowers–Peluse inner product
\langle(f_{\omega})_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\rangle^{2^{k}}_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}
as

\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}}\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{h_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\prod_{\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{k-1}}\mathcal{C}^{|\tilde{\omega}|+\varepsilon}f_{(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)}(x+(\mathbf{1}-\tilde{\omega})\cdot\tilde{h}+\tilde{\omega}\cdot\tilde{h}^{\prime}+h_{k})\bigg).

By Cauchy–Schwarz, this is bounded above by

\prod_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{h_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\prod_{\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{k-1}}\mathcal{C}^{|\tilde{\omega}|+\varepsilon}f_{(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)}(x+(\mathbf{1}-\tilde{\omega})\cdot\tilde{h}+\tilde{\omega}\cdot\tilde{h^{\prime}}+h_{k})\Big|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

which upon expanding the square and introducing a dummy variable
h^{\prime}_{k}
may be rewritten as

\prod_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{k}}\prod_{\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{k-1}}\Delta_{(h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime})}\mathcal{C}^{|\tilde{\omega}|}f_{(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)}(x+(\mathbf{1}-\tilde{\omega})\cdot\tilde{h}+\tilde{\omega}\cdot\tilde{h^{\prime}})\bigg)^{1/2}.

By induction (that is, by
A.5
in the case
k-1
) this is bounded by

\prod_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}}\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{k}}\prod_{\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{k-1}}\lVert\Delta_{(h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime})}f_{(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k-1}]}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Using Hölder’s inequality, this is at most

\prod_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{(k-1)}}\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{k}}\lVert\Delta_{(h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime})}f_{(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)}\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k-1}]}^{2^{k-1}}\bigg)^{1/{2^{k}}},

which equals

\prod_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{\tilde{\omega}\in\{0,1\}^{(k-1)}}\big\|f_{(\tilde{\omega},\varepsilon)}\big\|_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}]}=\prod_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\|f_{\omega}\|_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}]}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma asserts a kind of monotonicity of Gowers–Peluse norms with respect to the sequence of measures. It is essentially identical to
[
32
, Lemma 3.5 (iii)]
, but formulated using measures.

Lemma A.2
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1/2)
,
N\geqslant 1
and let
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
be a
1
-bounded function supported on
[\pm N]
.
Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
be probability measures supported on
[\pm N]
. Then

\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2k+3}\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k-1}]}^{2^{k}}.

Proof.

We have

\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{h_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k-1},h_{k-1}^{\prime})}f(x+h_{k})\Big|^{2}.

Observe that, since
f
is supported on
[\pm N]
, the sum over
x
is supported on
|x|\leqslant(k+1)N
. By Cauchy–Schwarz, it follows that

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}]}^{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\geqslant((2k+3)N^{2})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{h_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k-1},h_{k-1}^{\prime})}f(x+h_{k})\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=((2k+3)N^{2})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k-1},h_{k-1}^{\prime})}f(x)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(2k+3)^{-1}\Big|\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{i}\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\Delta_{(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots\Delta_{(h_{k-1},h_{k-1}^{\prime})}f(x)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=(2k+3)^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k-1}]}^{2^{k}}.

∎

The following key lemma is the driver for this process. A version of this lemma where the

Lemma B.3
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1/2)
,
k\geqslant 1
, and
I
an indexing set. For each
i\in I
, let
\nu_{1i},\dots,\nu_{ki}
be symmetric probability measures supported on
[\pm N]
. Let
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
be
1
-bounded such that
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subseteq[\pm N]
. Suppose that

\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}\lVert f\rVert^{\bullet}_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\nu_{1i},\dots,\nu_{ki}]}\geqslant\delta.

Then

\mathbb{E}_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}\lVert f\rVert^{\bullet}_{U_{\operatorname{GP}}[N;\nu_{1i},\dots,\nu_{(k-1)i},\nu_{1i^{\prime}},\dots,\nu_{(k-1)i^{\prime}},\nu_{ki}\ast\nu_{ki^{\prime}}]}\geqslant\delta^{O_{k}(1)}.

Proof.

Written out, the hypothesis is

\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{h^{\prime}_{1},\dots,h^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{h^{\prime\prime}_{1},\dots,h^{\prime\prime}_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{ji}(h^{\prime}_{j})\nu_{ji}(h^{\prime\prime}_{j})\Big)\Delta_{(h^{\prime}_{1},h^{\prime\prime}_{1})}\cdots\Delta_{(h^{\prime}_{k},h^{\prime\prime}_{k})}f(x^{\prime})\geqslant\delta N.

Substituting
h_{j}:=h^{\prime\prime}_{j}-h^{\prime}_{j}
and
x:=x^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}h^{\prime}_{i}
, this is equivalent to

(B.9)

\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\nu^{(2)}_{ji}(h_{j})\bigg)\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}}f(x)\geqslant\delta N,

where here
\nu^{(2)}_{ji}=\nu_{ji}\ast\nu_{ji}
.
In what follows, for
t\geqslant 1
and
\ell=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{t})\in\mathbf{Z}^{t}
we denote

(\Delta_{\ell_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{\ell_{t}})^{\ast}f(x)=\prod_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{t}\setminus\{0\}}\mathcal{C}^{|\omega|}f(x+\omega\cdot\ell),

where as usual
\mathcal{C}
denotes complex conjugation. With this notation,
B.9
becomes

\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}f(x)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{i\in I}\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\nu^{(2)}_{ji}(h_{j})\bigg)(\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}})^{\ast}f(x)\geqslant\delta N.

Via Cauchy–Schwarz, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}\\
h_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\nu^{(2)}_{ji}(h_{j})\nu^{(2)}_{ji^{\prime}}(h_{j}^{\prime})\bigg)(\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}})^{\ast}f(x)(\Delta_{h_{1}^{\prime}}\Delta_{h_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k}^{\prime}})^{\ast}\overline{f}(x)\gg\delta^{2}N.

We may write this as

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}\\
h_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\nu^{(2)}_{ji}(h_{j})\nu^{(2)}_{ji^{\prime}}(h_{j}^{\prime})\bigg)\times

\displaystyle f(x+h_{k})\overline{f(x+h_{k}^{\prime})}(\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k-1}})^{\ast}(\Delta_{h_{k}}f)(x)(\Delta_{h_{1}^{\prime}}\Delta_{h_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k-1}^{\prime}})^{\ast}(\Delta_{h_{k}^{\prime}}\overline{f})(x)\gg\delta^{2}N.

We now isolate the terms corresponding to
\nu^{(2)}_{ki}
and
\nu^{(2)}_{ki^{\prime}}
; in particular the above is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}\sum_{h_{k},h_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}}\nu^{(2)}_{ki}(h_{k})\nu^{(2)}_{ki^{\prime}}(h_{k}^{\prime})\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}}~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},\ldots,h_{k-1}\in\mathbf{Z}\\
h_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{k-1}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\nu^{(2)}_{ji}(h_{j})\nu^{(2)}_{ji^{\prime}}(h_{j}^{\prime})\bigg)\times

(B.10)

\displaystyle\;\;f(x+h_{k})\overline{f(x+h_{k}^{\prime})}(\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k-1}})^{\ast}\Delta_{h_{k}}f(x)(\Delta_{h_{1}^{\prime}}\Delta_{h_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots\Delta_{h_{k-1}^{\prime}})^{\ast}\Delta_{h_{k}^{\prime}}\overline{f}(x)\gg\delta^{2}N.

For fixed

Appendix D
Number-theoretic bounds

In this appendix we collect some more-or-less standard bounds of a number-theoretic flavour.

Lemma D.1
.

For
Y\geqslant 1
and for integer
m\geqslant 1
, we have
\sum_{|y|\leqslant Y}\tau(y)^{m}\ll_{m}Y(\log Y)^{2^{m}-1}
.

Proof.

Here we take
\tau(0):=0
. This result is standard; see e.g.
[
28
, Equation (1.80)]
.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
be fixed and let
r(t)
be the number of representations of
t
by
x^{2}+ny^{2}
with
x,y\in\mathbf{Z}
. Then
r(t)\leqslant 6\tau(t)
.

Proof.

We work with ideals in
K=\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
. We have
t=x^{2}+ny^{2}
if and only if
N((x+y\sqrt{-n}))=t
. Since a principal ideal is generated by
|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|\leqslant 6
different elements of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, we see that
r(t)
is bounded above by
6
times the number of ideals of norm
t
. To count the number of such ideals, factor
t=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
as a product of prime powers. The number of ideals of norm
p^{a}
is either
a+1
(if
p
splits in
K
) or
1_{2\mid a}
(if
p
is inert in
K
) or
1
if
p
ramifies. In all cases, the number of such ideals is at most
a+1
, and so the number of ideals of norm
t
is
\leqslant(a_{1}+1)\cdots(a_{k}+1)=\tau(t)
.
∎

We need the following classical estimate on the number of ideals of norm up to a given threshold.

Lemma D.3
.

For any number field
K
, the number of ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of norm at most
X
is
C_{K}X+O(X^{1-[K:\mathbf{Q}]^{-1}})
.

Proof.

Results of this form date back to work of Dedekind, Weber, and Hecke and take the form
C_{K}X+O_{K}(X^{1-[K:\mathbf{Q}]^{-1}})
. Here
C_{K}=|\mathcal{O}^{*}_{K}|^{-1}|\Delta|^{-1/2}2^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\pi^{r_{2}}R
where
R
is the regulator and
r_{1},r_{2}
the number of real and pairs of conjugate complex embeddings. For a sketch proof, see
[
26
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

Finally, we need an analogue of Mertens’ ‘second theorem’ in number fields.

Lemma D.4
.

Let
K
be any number field. Then there is some constant
M_{K}
such that

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}(N\mathfrak{p})^{-1}=\log\log X+M_{K}+O_{K}(\frac{1}{\log X}).

Proof.

This may be derived via partial summation from
Proposition

8.3
; however a rather more elementary treatment is possible. We briefly repeat the proof given in
[
DH08
, Section 10.1]
, which is a minor elaboration of the well-known proof over the integers.

Let
[X]!=\prod_{N\mathfrak{n}\leqslant X}\mathfrak{n}
and
R(n)=\sum_{N\mathfrak{n}\leqslant n}1
. By partial summation and
Lemma

D.3
, we have that

\displaystyle\log N([X]!)=\sum_{n\leqslant X}(R(n)-R(n-1))\log n

\displaystyle=R(X)\log X-\sum_{n<X}R(n)\log(n/(n+1))

(D.1)

\displaystyle=C_{K}X\log X+O_{K}(X).

Furthermore, by factorization into prime ideals and a further application of
Lemma

D.3
, we have that

\displaystyle\log N([X]!)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}R\big(\frac{X}{N\mathfrak{p}}\big)\log(N\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle=C_{K}X\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}\frac{\log(N\mathfrak{p})}{N\mathfrak{p}}+O_{K}\Big(\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}\Big(\frac{X}{N\mathfrak{p}}\Big)^{1-[K:\mathbf{Q}]^{-1}}\log(N\mathfrak{p})\Big).

To bound the error term, we sum over rational primes
p
with
\mathfrak{p}
as a factor; since such a prime has
p\leqslant N\mathfrak{p}
, and there are at most
[K:\mathbf{Q}]
prime ideals associated to each rational prime, we have

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}\Big(\frac{X}{N\mathfrak{p}}\Big)^{1-[K:\mathbf{Q}]^{-1}}\log(N\mathfrak{p})\ll X^{1-[K:\mathbf{Q}]^{-1}}\sum_{p\leqslant X}\frac{\log p}{p^{1-[K:\mathbf{Q}]}}\ll X,

where in the final line we have used Chebyshev’s estimate that
\sum_{n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n}\log p\leqslant\log\binom{2n}{n}\ll n
and dyadic summation. Comparing with
D.1
, it follows that

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leqslant X}\frac{\log(N\mathfrak{p})}{N\mathfrak{p}}=\log X+O(1),

The desired result then follow via partial summation, using the identity

\frac{1}{y\log y}
