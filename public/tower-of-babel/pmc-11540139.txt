Lethal Disseminated Mucorales Infection With Positive Blood Cultures With Purpura Fulminans Complicating Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis After Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-Cell Therapy
Authors: Takahiro Matsuo, Sebastian Wurster, Doina Ivan, Rachel Hicklen, Kelly McConn, Kelli A Bagwell, Fareed Khawaja, Roy F Chemaly, Dimitrios P Kontoyiannis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of fulminant Mucorales fungemia in a heavily immunosuppressed cancer patient with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis following CD70-targeted chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy. Although rare, Mucorales can cause true fungemia in a broad spectrum of hosts, with a range of manifestations from isolated fungemia to fungemia being part of widely disseminated, high-burden infection.

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has revolutionized cancer treatment [1]. However, this innovative therapy can lead to severe complications such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), a severe hyperinflammatory syndrome, resulting in the uncontrolled release of proinflammatory cytokines and fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, coagulopathy, and hepatic dysfunction [2–4]. Additionally, managing the immunotoxicities following CAR-T therapy often requires anti-cytokine therapy and corticosteroids, which further compromise the patient's immune system [5].

Importantly, while CAR-T therapy–related complications such as CRS and HLH are known to induce a profound immunosuppressive state, other factors in the patient's clinical course—such as prior chemotherapy, targeted therapies, or the underlying malignancy itself—may also contribute to the increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections. Among these, mucormycosis (MCM) is notable for its rarity, aggressive nature, and high mortality rate [6]. However, the incidence, risk factors, and clinical manifestations of CAR-T therapy–associated MCM remain poorly understood as invasive fungal infections appear to be rare in these patients [7].

True mold fungemia is uncommon. Fusarium [8] and occasionally Scedosporium [9] and Aspergillus terreus [10] are the most common pathogens causing mold fungemia. Despite the pleiotropic manifestations of MCM [11], true Mucorales fungemia is exceptionally rare and poorly characterized. Here, we present a case of Mucorales fungemia in a patient with HLH following CAR-T therapy for renal carcinoma, highlighting the potential of Mucorales to cause lethal disseminated infection with positive blood cultures in patients with profound immunosuppression. Furthermore, we review the literature on Mucorales fungemia cases.

CASE REPORT

A 68-year-old man with stage IV renal cell carcinoma underwent nephrectomy and received multiple immunotherapies, including nivolumab, ipilimumab, and pembrolizumab. Due to insufficient response, he then received CD70-targeted allogeneic CAR-T therapy, with fludarabine and cyclophosphamide conditioning. Post–CAR-T therapy, he experienced ICANS and recurrent HLH (days 21–26 and 35–43), treated with dexamethasone 12–60 mg/day, anakinra (interleukin [IL]–1 receptor antagonist), and tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor antagonist) (Figure 1A). During a third HLH episode, ruxolitinib (selective oral inhibitor of Janus kinase [JAK] 1 and JAK2; 20 mg/day) was added on day 55. After ferritin levels dropped below 10 000 ng/mL, dexamethasone was tapered off, while ruxolitinib was continued at 10 mg/day (Figure 1).

On day 91, the patient developed fever, rash, and elevated liver function tests (LFTs). Infectious diseases workup was unremarkable except for (1,3)-β-D-glucan (BDG) levels of 485 pg/mL. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging indicated iron overload of the liver and spleen. Caspofungin intravenously (50 mg/day) was started as prophylaxis and switched to oral posaconazole (300 mg/day) when LFTs improved on day 104.

On day 126, the patient was admitted at an outside hospital for mild coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), treated for 5 days with intravenous remdesivir, then transferred to our hospital on day 133 due to altered mental status, suggestive of ICANS versus recurrent HLH. Laboratory findings revealed a white blood cell count of 2700 cells/µL, ferritin of 13 993 ng/mL, IL-2 receptor of 3939 U/mL, and triglycerides of 195 mg/dL. An electroencephalogram showed nonspecific diffuse slowing. Brain magnetic resonance imaging and lumbar puncture were unremarkable. Due to LFT elevation, posaconazole was switched back to intravenous caspofungin. Despite multiple HLH therapies, including dexamethasone (up to 40 mg/day), ruxolitinib (20 mg/day), anakinra (200 mg/day), and tocilizumab, ferritin increased to 64 396 ng/mL by day 143. Starting on day 144, the patient developed profound neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count of <100 neutrophils/µL).

Despite receiving emapalumab (an interferon-gamma [IFN-γ]–blocking antibody) on day 147, etanercept and rituximab (CD19-blocking antibody) on day 150, and etoposide on day 151, ferritin continued to increase to 97 000 ng/mL. On day 151, the patient developed neutropenic fever, and broad-spectrum antibacterials were started. Due to LFT elevation, intravenous caspofungin was switched to intravenous anidulafungin (100 mg/day) on day 151. On the same day, 2 sets of blood cultures from day 150 returned positive for Staphylococcus haemolyticus, and vancomycin was initiated. Subsequent blood cultures on days 151 and 153 also remained persistently positive. The patient's condition deteriorated rapidly, resulting in multiple organ failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), as indicated by an elevated D-dimer level of 3.2 mg/L, low fibrinogen of 154 mg/dL, thrombocytopenia at 21 × 103 platelets/µL, an international normalized ratio of 2.6, and the presence of schistocytes on the peripheral blood smear. As a result, the patient required intubation, vasopressor support, and initiation of renal replacement therapy. Subsequently, he developed purpura fulminans on the face, chest (Figure 2A), and extremities (Figure 2B). He also had elevated fungal biomarkers (serum Aspergillus antigen of 1.08, BDG >500 pg/mL). Cultures from skin biopsy, tracheal aspirate, and peripheral blood taken on day 154 rapidly grew Mucor species (time to detection in blood culture: 26 hours). Blood cultures from the central venous catheter remained negative. Histopathology of the skin lesions showed pauci-septate hyphae within vascular spaces and extensive dermal purpura (Figure 2C and 2D) but no evidence of the typical fibrin deposition associated with DIC. The patient's posaconazole serum level on day 134 was 2220 ng/mL. Antifungals were changed to intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day) plus intravenous isavuconazole. The patient died on day 155. An autopsy was declined by the patient's family.

LITERATURE REVIEW OF MUCORALES FUNGEMIA

We performed a comprehensive search of the literature to identify published cases of Mucorales fungemia. Therefore, Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Scopus, and Google Scholar were queried from database inception through 22 January 2024, using both natural language and controlled vocabulary terms for Mucorales, mucormycosis, zygomycosis, and blood culture. Conference abstracts were excluded. After deduplication, 371 unique records were identified. Using previously published criteria for true mold fungemia [12], we identified 17 published cases of true Mucorales fungemia [13–29] (Table 1) after excluding 2 cases (1 with pseudofungemia and 1 with insufficient information).

Patients had various underlying conditions, and only 3 patients had hematological malignancies. Mucor velutinosus (n = 5) and Mucor circinelloides (n = 4) were the most common species. In most cases, Mucorales were detected in blood cultures after 24–48 hours of incubation. Most patients (14/18) received amphotericin B–based therapy. All 5 patients with catheter- or intravenous drug use–associated fungemia and no other organs involved survived. Conversely, patients with fungemia and concomitant involvement of other organs (gastrointestinal tract, n = 4; skin, n = 3; lung, n = 1) had poor outcomes, with 6 of 9 such patients with available data dying within 6 weeks of Mucorales fungemia diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

The emerging landscape of oncological immunotherapies, such as CAR-T therapy, presents unique, intricate, and largely understudied challenges in managing infectious diseases. In our patient developing Mucor fungemia, the occurrence of various immunotoxicities following CAR-T therapy, including HLH, and the use of various immunomodulators presented a complex clinical scenario.

Therapeutic options for metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma remain limited; however, CD70-targeting CAR-T therapy may offer promising new treatment possibilities [30, 31]. ALLO-316 is genetically engineered to target CD70, which is expressed in various solid tumors and highly prevalent in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [31]. Importantly, the CD52 gene was knocked out in the CAR T cells to enable the use of ALLO-647, an anti-CD52 monoclonal antibody that depletes host T cells and enhances the persistence of allogeneic CAR T cells but also increases the risk of infections [32].

Additionally, our patient received multiple immunomodulatory agents to treat refractory HLH that are associated with an increased risk of fungal infections, including dexamethasone [11], ruxolitinib [33], rituximab [34], and etanercept [35]. Emapalumab, a blocker of IFN-γ activity [36], and impairment of anti-Mucorales immunity post-COVID-19 [37] were additional possible contributors to the profound immune defect. Collectively, HLH-induced immune paralysis, including severe neutropenia, and multiple immunosuppressants have led to a state of severe net immunosuppression.

Furthermore, iron overload with excess free iron might have created a conducive environment for an overwhelmingly disseminated MCM manifesting with rapid multiorgan failure, DIC, purpura fulminans, and true Mucor fungemia. The interplay between iron metabolism and fungal pathogenicity is well-documented [38], with MCM exacerbated by the ability of Mucorales to absorb iron from the host, promoting fungal growth [39]. Notably, despite the rarity of true Mucorales fungemia, 3 lethal cases of Mucorales fungemia in HLH have been reported [15, 18, 20]. This prompts the question of whether there might be an overrepresentation of fungemia as a manifestation of MCM in the HLH context.

Additionally, Loubet et al reviewed 9 cases of MCM without fungemia associated with HLH, all of which were lethal [40]. Potential underlying etiologies included Epstein-Barr virus (n = 3), varicella zoster virus (n = 1), and familial HLH (n = 1), with 4 cases undetermined, raising the question whether MCM was a consequence or trigger for HLH.

Furthermore, MCM has been associated with other immunotoxicities after CAR-T therapy. Specifically, Cheok et al reviewed 12 cases of MCM after CAR-T therapy with a high mortality rate of 70% [6]. Sixty-seven percent of these patients had CRS and ICANS, 92% were neutropenic at MCM diagnosis, and 88% had been treated with corticosteroids [6]. Despite the low overall incidence of invasive mold infections among CAR-T therapy recipients [5], these findings collectively underscore the need for heightened vigilance and, possibly, consideration of anti-mold prophylaxis in high-risk individuals [41].

An unusual aspect of our case is the atypical skin manifestation of MCM. Unlike the classic necrotic eschars accompanied by surrounding erythema and induration [11], our patient had purpura fulminans, a severe thrombotic form of DIC [42]. Purpura fulminans is commonly associated with severe bacterial infections such as Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Capnocytophaga canimorsus but also occurs with fungal infections, including invasive candidiasis, cryptococcosis, aspergillosis, and fusariosis [43, 44]. It is uncertain whether skin manifestations seen in our patient result from direct fungal invasion resulting in occlusion or obstruction of arterioles, severe coagulopathy triggered by disseminated mucormycosis, or a combination of the above. Ghuman et al demonstrated that Mucor circinelloides can initiate platelet aggregation via distinct signaling pathways, including FcγRIIA and integrin αIIbβ3, possibly contributing to thromboembolic events associated with MCM [45]. Although evidence for typical fibrin deposition associated with DIC was lacking, the patient's DIC and purpura fulminans could have a multifactorial etiology. This might also include HLH-induced coagulopathy and concomitant bacteremia.

Finally, the high serum BDG level was another atypical finding in our case, as Mucorales are generally thought not to produce BDG [46], although this might not be entirely true in all cases [47]. This also raises the possibility of a concurrent fungal coinfection. Although cultures from blood, tracheal aspirate, and skin biopsy consistently grew only Mucor species, the elevated serum Aspergillus antigen might suggest a potential coinfection with another fungal pathogen. However, we were unable to isolate any additional fungal organisms, and the patient's rapid clinical deterioration limited further diagnostic investigations.

The mechanism of Mucorales fungemia is poorly understood. Importantly, Mucorales fungemia was not exclusive to the most immunosuppressed patients but also occurred in less immunocompromised patients with various underlying risk factors. Fusarium and possibly Aspergillus terreus and Scedosporium produce yeast-like structures (adventitious sporulation) called aleuroconidia commonly causing fungemia [48–50]. Notably, Mucor species were the most common cause of true Mucorales fungemia in our review (10/17). In positive blood cultures, Mucor species exhibit varied morphologies including budding yeast, hyphal elements, terminal chlamydospores, and chains of chlamydospores [15]. Some Mucor species display dimorphic characteristics, growing as multipolar budding yeast under specific anaerobic or high-CO2 conditions, contrasting with hyphal growth in aerobic environments [51]. This diversity underscores the need to recognize Mucor's morphological variability in blood culture settings. Whether Mucor species are associated with adventitious sporulation in blood requires further investigation.

In summary, although Mucorales infections are rare, they can cause true fungemia in a broad spectrum of hosts, with manifestations that range from isolated fungemia to fungemia being part of widely disseminated, high-burden infection. Our case emphasizes the complex immunopathogenetic environment in HLH patients, creating a “perfect storm” for explosive Mucor dissemination and true Mucor fungemia.
