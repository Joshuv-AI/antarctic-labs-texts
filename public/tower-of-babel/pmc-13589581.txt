Trehalose Exerts Selective Cytotoxicity Against Tongue Squamous Cell Carcinoma Cells While Exhibiting Limited Cytotoxicity Toward Normal Oral Squamous Cells
Authors: Liang Jiao, Fuju Zheng, Shuyang Yu, Jingfen Zhang, Ying Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChemotherapy-induced oral mucositis is a major complication in tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) treatment, caused by non-selective cytotoxicity of chemotherapeutic agents. Trehalose is a natural disaccharide with reported antitumor and cell-preserving properties, but its selective effect on TSCC versus normal oral cells remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether trehalose exerts differential effects on TSCC cells and normal oral squamous cells (NOSCs).MethodsWe treated two TSCC cell lines (CAL-27, SCC-15) and normal oral squamous cells (NOSCs) with trehalose at different concentrations. Cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration and invasion were evaluated in vitro. Angiogenesis was assessed using the chick embryo chorioallantoic membrane assay, and in vivo antitumor activity was verified in a nude mouse xenograft model.ResultsAt 200 mmol/L, trehalose significantly inhibited proliferation, migration and invasion, and induced apoptosis in TSCC cells, while showing comparatively limited cytotoxicity toward NOSCs. Trehalose also reduced angiogenic activity in the CAM assay and decreased tumor growth and was associated with less extensive Ki-67 immunostaining in vivo. IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels increased after trehalose treatment; these changes were considered treatment-associated responses rather than evidence of a causal mechanism of apoptosis.ConclusionTrehalose selectively inhibited TSCC cells while showing comparatively limited cytotoxicity toward normal oral cells. These findings support further investigation of trehalose as a potential antitumor candidate, while its possible relevance to chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis requires direct validation in dedicated models.

Introduction

Tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is one of the most common oral cancers. It grows fast, spreads easily, and often metastasizes to cervical lymph nodes early. This makes the prognosis very poor. The tongue is important for speaking, eating and swallowing. TSCC not only lowers the 5-year survival rate, but also causes serious functional problems. Patients’ quality of life drops significantly.1,2 Currently, clinical management relies on comprehensive sequential therapy centered on surgery, supplemented by radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. Despite incremental improvements in survival outcomes, the clinical unmet need for more effective and tolerable treatments remains urgent, particularly regarding the mitigation of chemotherapy-related adverse effects.3,4

Chemotherapy serves as a cornerstone of TSCC comprehensive treatment, effectively reducing primary tumor burden, controlling metastatic progression, and improving resectability.5 However, its non-selective cytotoxicity leads to unavoidable damage to normal tissues,6 with chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis (OM) being one of the most prevalent and debilitating complications. OM is clinically manifested as mucosal erythema, desquamation, ulceration, bleeding, and exudation, which induce severe pain, impair nutritional intake, and disrupt the oral mucosal barrier.7 These sequelae not only compromise patient quality of life but also increase the risk of secondary infections and often necessitate chemotherapy dose reductions or treatment interruptions, ultimately undermining therapeutic efficacy.8,9 To date, there is no universally effective strategy for preventing or treating chemotherapy-induced OM, making it a critical bottleneck in TSCC clinical care.10 Recent randomized clinical studies of topical therapy for oral lichen planus also underscore the clinical importance of treatment tolerability at the oral mucosal surface.11,12

Trehalose, a naturally occurring non-reducing disaccharide composed of two glucose molecules linked via a hemiacetal bond, is widely distributed in microorganisms, plants, and animals.13 Endowed with high stability, low toxicity, and unique moisturizing and protective properties, trehalose has been extensively applied in the food industry and biopharmaceutical preservation.14,15 In recent years, accumulating evidence has highlighted its potential as an antitumor agent, with studies demonstrating that trehalose and its derivatives can modulate tumor cell biological behavior through multiple pathways, including inducing apoptosis, inhibiting proliferation, and suppressing metastasis.16,17 During preliminary laboratory work in which trehalose was used as a cell-preserving agent, we unexpectedly observed that TSCC cells appeared more sensitive to trehalose exposure than normal oral squamous cells. This observation prompted us to investigate the differential response systematically. Existing research has primarily focused on trehalose’s antitumor activity in other cancer types or its general cell-preserving effects, leaving its differential cytotoxicity toward TSCC cells and normal oral squamous cells insufficiently characterized.

This study aimed to determine whether trehalose exerts differential effects on TSCC cells and NOSCs. We evaluated concentration- and time-dependent effects on CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells and NOSCs, and further examined apoptosis, migration, invasion, CAM vascular responses, cytokine changes, and antitumor activity in nude mouse xenograft models. The study was not designed to demonstrate direct protection against chemotherapy-induced oral mucosal injury or prevention of OM.

By comparing malignant and normal oral squamous cells under the same experimental conditions, this study provides experimental evidence on the differential response to trehalose and a basis for further evaluation of its antitumor potential in TSCC.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Drug Stimulation

Human tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) cell lines (CAL-27, SCC-15) and normal human oral squamous cells (NOSCs) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Branch, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination before use. CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). NOSCs were maintained in keratinocyte serum-free medium (KSFM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 0.2 ng/mL epidermal growth factor and 25 μg/mL bovine pituitary extract (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All cell lines were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) when reaching 80–90% confluence, and only cells from passages 3 to 8 were used for experiments to ensure consistent biological characteristics. Trehalose dihydrate (purity ≥ 98.0%, product number T0167, batch number BCBL9234, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were divided into 4 groups: Control group (without trehalose), 100 mmol/L trehalose group, 200 mmol/L trehalose group, and 400 mmol/L trehalose group. Trehalose solutions of corresponding concentrations were added to each group, and the cells were further cultured in the incubator. Observations and photography were conducted after 48 hours for subsequent experiments.

Cell Proliferation Assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin to prepare cell suspensions, which were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5×103 cells/well, with 100 μL of cell suspension per well. The culture plate was pre-incubated for 24 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. After adhesion, baseline absorbance (0 h) was measured immediately before drug treatment. The medium was then replaced with 200 μL of medium containing trehalose at the indicated concentrations (100, 200, or 400 mmol/L), while the Control group received culture medium alone. Three independent experiments were performed, with five technical replicate wells per condition in each experiment. The plates were incubated for 12, 24, or 48 hours. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was added to each well, followed by incubation for another 2 hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). All absorbance values were normalized to the corresponding 0-h baseline. The mean of the technical replicate wells within each independent experiment was used for statistical analysis.

Determination of Optimal Trehalose Concentration and Treatment Duration

Based on the CCK-8 assay, 100 mmol/L trehalose produced a weaker or less consistent inhibitory response across the two TSCC cell lines at 24 hours, whereas 200 mmol/L significantly inhibited both CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells with comparatively limited effect on NOSCs. At 400 mmol/L, an inhibitory effect was also evident in NOSCs. Therefore, 200 mmol/L trehalose for 24 hours was selected as the working condition for subsequent experiments. Because no equiosmolar mannitol or sucrose control was included, a contribution from hyperosmotic stress cannot be excluded.

Chick Embryo Chorioallantoic Membrane (CAM) Angiogenesis Assay

Fertilized chicken eggs (purchased from a local farm) were incubated at 37.8°C with 60% humidity for 8 days. Eggs with defective embryos (eg, non-viable, malformed) were discarded. For each viable egg, the air chamber was marked, and a small window (approximately 1 cm2) was opened on the eggshell using a dental drill (Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA), taking care not to damage the underlying chorioallantoic membrane (CAM). The inner membrane was gently removed with sterile tweezers to expose the CAM. On the 9th day of incubation, 100 μL of 200 mmol/L trehalose solution (treatment group) or 100 μL of normal saline (Control group) was added dropwise to the CAM surface. The window was sealed with sterile parafilm, and the eggs were returned to the incubator for continued incubation. After 48 hours (11th day of incubation), the CAM was photographed under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA) was used to quantify the vascular area of each CAM sample. The relative vascular area was calculated by normalizing the vascular area of the treatment group to that of the Control group (set as 100%). Each group included five replicate eggs. The CAM assay was performed with reference to established CAM angiogenesis methodology.18

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Apoptosis

An Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to evaluate cell apoptosis. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well and incubated overnight. After treatment with 200 mmol/L trehalose for 24 hours, cells were harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes, and washed twice with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were resuspended in 500 μL of 1×Binding Buffer (provided in the apoptosis detection kit), and 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL of propidium iodide (PI) were added sequentially. The mixture was gently vortexed and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 minutes. Apoptosis was analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) within 1 hour. Cells were divided into four quadrants: Q1-LL (viable cells, Annexin V−/PI−), Q1-LR (early apoptotic cells, Annexin V⁺/PI−), Q1-UR (late apoptotic/necrotic cells, Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and Q1-UL (necrotic cells, Annexin V−/PI⁺). Early apoptosis, late apoptosis, and total apoptosis (early + late) were quantified separately. Three independent experiments were performed.

Scratch Assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells/well and cultured overnight until a confluent monolayer was formed. Before scratching, three parallel horizontal lines were drawn on the bottom of each well using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip (perpendicular to the well diameter) to serve as reference marks. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to scratch the cell monolayer along the reference lines with consistent pressure, ensuring the scratch width was uniform across wells. The wells were washed three times with PBS to remove detached cells and cell debris. The medium was replaced with serum-free medium containing 200 mmol/L trehalose (treatment group) or serum-free medium alone (Control group). Images of the scratch area were captured under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 0 and 24 hours after scratching. ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to measure the scratch width at three random positions along each scratch. The migration rate was calculated as follows: Migration rate (%) = [(Width0h - Width24h) / Width0h] × 100%. A lower migration rate indicates weaker cell migration ability. Three independent experiments were performed. Because proliferation was not pharmacologically blocked, treatment-related changes in proliferation or viable cell number may have contributed to differences in wound closure. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ.19

Cell Migration and Invasion Assays

Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size, Corning, NY, USA) were placed in 24-well plates. Cells were digested, centrifuged, and resuspended in serum-free medium to a concentration of 2.5×105 cells/mL. A total of 300 μL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and the medium was replaced with serum-free medium containing 200 mmol/L trehalose (treatment group) or serum-free medium alone (control group), and 500 μL of complete medium (containing 10% FBS) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. The plate was incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours. After incubation, the insert was removed, and non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently wiped off with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, rinsed three times with PBS, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 minutes. Excess stain was washed off with running water, and the insert was air-dried. Images of migrated cells were captured under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 200× magnification, with three random fields of view selected per insert. The number of migrated cells was counted using ImageJ software, and the average number was calculated for each group.

Matrigel matrix (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) was thawed overnight at 4°C and diluted 1:8 with serum-free medium. A total of 60 μL of the diluted Matrigel was added to the upper chamber of each Transwell insert and incubated at 37°C for 4 hours to form a gel. Subsequent steps were identical to the migration assay described above. The number of invasive cells was counted and analyzed as described. Three independent experiments were performed. Because 200 mmol/L trehalose also reduced TSCC cell viability, the migration and invasion results cannot fully distinguish direct effects on motility from secondary effects of reduced proliferation or viable cell number. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ.19

ELISA Assay

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) were used to detect the concentrations of proteins (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). All three cell types (NOSCs, CAL-27 and SCC-15) were processed identically for ELISA detection. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well and treated with 200 mmol/L trehalose for 24 hours. The culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes to remove cell debris. For animal serum samples, blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus of nude mice at the end of the in vivo experiment, allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 minutes, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes to separate serum. All ELISA experimental steps were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was read at a wavelength of 450 nm using a spectrophotometer. The concentrations were determined by comparing with a calibration curve prepared with a series of different concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Cell-culture experiments were performed independently three times. Mouse serum samples were analyzed from the individual animals in each group.

Nude Mouse Tumor Formation Assay

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old, 18–22 g) were purchased from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China) with the animal production license number: SCXK (Lu) 20230007. The mice were housed in a temperature (22±2°C) and humidity (50±10%) controlled SPF facility with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with free access to sterile food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jinan Stomatological Hospital (Approval No. JNSKQYY-2021-012) and complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. All operators received standardized training for laboratory animal handling.

For each xenograft model, mice were randomly assigned to a control group or a 200 mmol/L trehalose treatment group (n=5 per group). CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells were digested, centrifuged, and resuspended in serum-free medium to a concentration of 2×107 cells/mL. A total of 0.1 mL cell suspension (2×106 cells) was inoculated subcutaneously into the right axilla. Beginning the day after inoculation, mice received 0.2 mL of 200 mmol/L trehalose dihydrate solution or an equal volume of normal saline by oral gavage once daily for 26 consecutive days. Based on the molecular weight of trehalose dihydrate (378.33g/mol), this corresponds to approximately 15.1 mg trehalose dihydrate per mouse per day, or about 0.69–0.84 g/kg/day for mice weighing 18–22 g. The 200 mmol/L value therefore describes the concentration of the gavage solution and should not be regarded as pharmacokinetically equivalent to the extracellular concentration used in cell culture.

Predefined humane endpoints were established for this study: mice were immediately euthanized if they met any of the following criteria: 1) tumor volume >1000 mm3 or maximum diameter >15 mm; 2) >20% body weight loss within 7 days; 3) tumor ulceration, necrosis or infection; 4) severe persistent distress (hypoactivity, inability to eat/drink, labored breathing, abnormal posture). No mice met the humane endpoints or died unexpectedly before the scheduled experimental endpoint. At the end of the experiment, mice were deeply anesthetized with 3.0% isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation (consistent with AVMA euthanasia guidelines), followed by tumor tissue collection.

Tumor Growth Monitoring

Starting from the 7th day after cell inoculation, the long diameter (a) and short diameter (b) of the subcutaneous tumors were measured every 5 days using a vernier caliper, and the tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula: V = 1/2 × a × b2. A tumor growth curve was plotted with time (days) as the abscissa and tumor volume (mm3) as the ordinate. The complete monitoring time points are Day 7, Day 12, Day 17, Day 22, and Day 27. The general health, behavior and body weight of all mice were monitored and recorded daily throughout the experiment. At the end of the experiment, the nude mice were anesthetized with 3.0% isoflurane for 3–5 min to achieve deep anesthesia (no withdrawal reflex to toe pinching), then euthanized by cervical dislocation using sterile surgical forceps by trained professional experimenters, and the tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours. Three tumor tissues were randomly selected from each group, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices, and subjected to immunohistochemical staining for Ki-67(Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Ki-67 immunohistochemistry was performed using a rabbit monoclonal anti-Ki-67 antibody (clone SP6, catalog No. ab16667; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at a working dilution of 1:200.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Independent experiments or individual animals, rather than technical replicate wells or microscopic fields, were used as the units of statistical inference. For the CCK-8 assay, trehalose concentration and treatment duration were analyzed within each cell type using two-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett-adjusted comparisons with the corresponding control at each time point. Paired two-tailed t-tests were used for flow-cytometry data because control and trehalose-treated samples were paired within each independent experiment. Other two-group endpoint comparisons were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests when distributional assumptions were satisfied. Tumor volume measured repeatedly in the same animals over time was analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA, with treatment group and time as factors. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed before parametric testing. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. No formal a priori power calculation was performed; sample sizes were based on the experimental design, prior laboratory experience, and the 3Rs principle for animal experiments. Graphs were prepared using GraphPad Prism 10.6.

Results

Differential Effects of Trehalose on Morphology of TSCC Cells and Normal Oral Squamous Cells

Morphological observations under light microscopy revealed distinct responses of normal human oral squamous cells (NOSCs) and tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) cells (SCC-15, CAL-27) to trehalose treatment (Figure 1). NOSCs maintained intact morphology, robust adherence, and plump cellular structures in the control group, 100 mmol/L trehalose group, and 200 mmol/L trehalose group within 48 hours of treatment, with no obvious shrinkage or rupture (Figure 1A–C). Only in the 400 mmol/L trehalose group did partial NOSCs exhibit slight shrinkage (Figure 1D). In contrast, TSCC cells displayed concentration-dependent morphological alterations. Following 48 hours of 100 mmol/L trehalose treatment, partial TSCC cells began to shrink and reduce in volume. With increasing trehalose concentration, this shrinkage became more pronounced. At trehalose concentrations ≥200 mmol/L, more pronounced cell shrinkage, rupture, and an apparent reduction in cell number were observed in TSCC cultures (Figure 1E–L). These images are presented as representative morphological observations only; quantitative evidence of the differential cytotoxic response is provided by the CCK-8 assay. At 24 hours, 100 mmol/L trehalose produced a weaker or less consistent inhibitory response across CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells, whereas 200 mmol/L significantly inhibited proliferation in both TSCC cell lines with comparatively little change in NOSCs. At 400 mmol/L, inhibition was also evident in NOSCs. The inhibitory effect became more pronounced with longer exposure. Effects of Trehalose on Angiogenesis

Angiogenic activity was evaluated using the chick embryo chorioallantoic membrane assay (Figure 3A–K). The control group showed abundant vascular branches, whereas the 200 mmol/L trehalose group showed a lower vascular area. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in relative vascular area (P<0.05). This finding provides preliminary evidence of reduced angiogenic activity in the CAM model but does not establish inhibition of tumor-associated angiogenesis in TSCC. Effects of Trehalose on Cell Apoptosis

Flow cytometric analysis with Annexin V-FITC/PI staining was used to assess trehalose-induced apoptosis (Figure 3L–N). For CAL-27 cells, early apoptosis increased from 6.48% in the control group to 17.57% after trehalose treatment; for SCC-15 cells, early apoptosis increased from 6.30% to 18.21%. Quantitative analysis from three independent experiments showed significant increases in early apoptosis and total apoptosis in both cell lines, together with a smaller increase in late apoptosis. The predominance of early apoptosis indicates that, at the selected 24-hour exposure, many responsive cells had entered an early phase of apoptotic cell death.

Effects of Trehalose on Cell Migration and Invasion

The scratch assay and Transwell assay were conducted to evaluate cell migration and invasion capabilities (Figures 4 and 5). The scratch assay showed that the migration rate of CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells in the 200 mmol/L trehalose group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.01) (Figure 4). Transwell migration assays revealed a marked reduction in the number of migrated CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells in the trehalose treatment group compared to the control group (p < 0.05) (Figure 5). Similarly, Transwell invasion assays (with Matrigel coating) demonstrated that trehalose treatment significantly inhibited the invasive capacity of both TSCC cell lines (p < 0.05) (Figure 5). Collectively, these data indicate that trehalose suppresses the migration and invasion of TSCC cells. Because the same trehalose exposure also reduced TSCC cell viability, reduced proliferation or viable cell number may have contributed to the observed decreases in wound closure, migration, and invasion. Treatment-Associated Cytokine Changes After Trehalose Exposure

ELISA was used to detect IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α concentrations in cell culture supernatants and nude mouse serum (Figure 6). Trehalose treatment was associated with increased levels of these cytokines in TSCC cell-culture supernatants, whereas no significant changes were observed in NOSC supernatants under the same treatment condition. Corresponding cytokine changes were also observed in mouse serum. These findings describe cytokine responses associated with trehalose exposure but do not establish IL-1β, IL-6, or TNF-α as causal mediators of apoptosis. Inhibition of Tumor Growth in Nude Mouse Xenograft Models

Nude mice were subcutaneously inoculated with CAL-27 or SCC-15 cells to establish xenograft models. Tumor volume was measured every 5 days starting from day 7 post-inoculation, and tumor weight was recorded at the scheduled experimental endpoint (Figure 7). The tumor growth curves showed that the control groups exhibited progressive tumor enlargement, whereas trehalose-treated groups showed reduced tumor growth over time (Figure 7C). Endpoint tumor weight was also lower in the trehalose-treated groups than in the corresponding controls (Figure 7D). Representative Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining showed less extensive Ki-67 positivity in trehalose-treated tumors than in control tumors (Figure 7F). Body weight remained generally stable during treatment (Figure 7E). Because serum biochemical testing and histopathological examination of major organs were not performed, body-weight monitoring was interpreted only as an indicator of general treatment tolerance and not as evidence of comprehensive systemic safety. Discussion

The management of tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) remains a clinical challenge due to its high malignancy, aggressive progression, and the adverse effects associated with treatment, including oral mucositis (OM).20,21 The present study showed that trehalose produced a stronger inhibitory effect on TSCC cells than on NOSCs under the tested conditions. At 200 mmol/L, TSCC cells showed reduced proliferation, migration and invasion, increased apoptosis, and reduced xenograft growth, whereas the effect on NOSCs was comparatively limited. These findings support differential cytotoxicity but do not demonstrate direct protection of normal oral mucosa from chemotherapy-induced injury.

The selective inhibition of TSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by trehalose (200 mmol/L, 24 h) is consistent with reports describing antitumor effects in other experimental systems.22,23 At the same concentration, the effect on NOSCs was comparatively limited. Trehalose-based compounds have also been reported to inhibit cancer-cell migration and invasion.24 However, because 200 mmol/L trehalose reduced TSCC cell viability in the present study, reduced proliferation or viable cell number may have contributed to the scratch and Transwell findings; these results should therefore not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a direct anti-metastatic mechanism.

Flow cytometric analysis showed that trehalose increased apoptosis in TSCC cells, with the largest change observed in the early apoptotic fraction and a smaller increase in late apoptosis. This pattern suggests that, at 24 hours, a substantial proportion of responsive cells had entered an early stage of apoptotic cell death. Previous studies have reported trehalose-related effects on tumor-cell viability, apoptosis, autophagy, and membrane-associated responses.25–27 The increases in TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β observed in the present study should be interpreted as treatment-associated cytokine responses rather than evidence that these cytokines caused apoptosis. Alternative explanations, including cellular stress or cytokine release accompanying cell injury or death, cannot be excluded. Direct analysis of apoptotic signaling pathways, including caspases, Bcl-2 family proteins, autophagy markers, and rescue experiments, will be needed to establish mechanism.

The CAM assay showed a reduction in vascular area after trehalose treatment. Because the CAM is an embryonic angiogenesis model and does not reproduce the full tumor microenvironment, this result should be considered preliminary evidence of reduced angiogenic activity rather than proof of inhibition of tumor-associated angiogenesis. Confirmation in TSCC xenografts using vascular markers such as CD31 and quantitative microvessel-density analysis would be required before angiogenesis inhibition could be proposed as a major in vivo mechanism.

The comparatively limited effect of trehalose on NOSCs may be relevant to the development of antitumor approaches that cause less collateral injury to normal oral epithelium. However, the present study did not test whether trehalose protects normal oral cells from cisplatin, 5-fluorouracil, or other chemotherapy-related injury, and no dedicated OM model was used. Its possible relevance to chemotherapy-induced OM should therefore be regarded as a hypothesis requiring direct experimental validation rather than an established protective effect.

The in vivo findings also require cautious interpretation. The 200 mmol/L concentration used in cell culture represents direct extracellular exposure, whereas 200 mmol/L in the animal study refers to the concentration of the gavage solution. The oral dose used here was approximately 15.1 mg trehalose dihydrate per mouse per day (about 0.69–0.84 g/kg/day), and the resulting tissue exposure was not measured. Stable body weight suggested acceptable general tolerance during the experiment, but systemic safety cannot be concluded without serum biochemical testing and histopathological examination of major organs. Pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies will be needed before clinical translation can be considered.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that trehalose inhibits TSCC cells (CAL-27 and SCC-15) while showing comparatively limited cytotoxicity toward normal oral squamous cells under the experimental conditions tested. Trehalose was also associated with increased apoptosis, reduced migration- and invasion-associated behavior, reduced CAM vascular area, and suppressed xenograft growth. These findings support further investigation of trehalose as a potential antitumor candidate. The mechanisms underlying the differential response, the contribution of osmotic stress, systemic safety, and any effect on chemotherapy-induced oral mucosal injury or OM require direct experimental validation.
