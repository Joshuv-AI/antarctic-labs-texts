GANAB-related Polycystic Kidney Disease Mimicking the Cutaneous Findings of Neurofibromatosis Type 1
Authors: Mohammad Karam Chaaban, David Melville, Yaqqira Womack, Natasha Strande, Peter Harris, Mark Pittelkow, Dusica Babovic-Vuksanovic, Linnea M Baudhuin, Mayowa A Osundiji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic GANAB gene variants are a rare cause of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). With only few reported cases, the phenotypic spectrum of GANAB-related disorders is still emerging. Although kidney and hepatic cysts are well-documented, cutaneous manifestations are not recognized features of GANAB-related ADPKD. Here, we report GANAB-related ADPKD in a 59-year-old woman with multiple café-au-lait macules and axillary freckling. Targeted NF1 and SPRED1 gene testing was negative. Genome sequencing showed a heterozygous pathogenic germline variant, GANAB (c.181C>T, p.Arg61∗). Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed the presence of numerous hepatic cysts and multiple cysts bilaterally in the kidneys. Our findings suggest that the phenotypic spectrum of GANAB-related disorders may include café-au-lait macules and axillary freckling. Because this observation is based on a single individual, the association may be coincidental and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Careful documentation of additional cases will be required to clarify whether pigmentary findings represent a recurrent feature of GANAB-related disease or an incidental finding.Index Words: GANAB, NF1, renal, liver, cyst, café-au-lait

Pathogenic variants in the GANAB gene, which encodes the glucosidase II α subunit, are a recently identified cause of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease type 3 (ADPKD3) and polycystic liver disease (ADPLD).1, 2, 3 The protein product is essential for the endoplasmic reticulum-mediated processing of glycoproteins, including polycystin-1.1 Defects in this process are central to cyst formation in the kidneys and liver. GANAB-related ADPKD3 and ADPLD present with fluid-filled cysts in these organs, with a variable risk of progression to kidney failure.1,2 With only a relatively few cases of GANAB-related disorders described in the clinical scientific literature hitherto,3, 4, 5, 6, 7 following the initial discovery of GANAB-related ADPKD3 and ADPLD in 2016, the multisystemic manifestations are yet to be fully delineated.

In stark contrast to GANAB-related disorders, the dermatological hallmarks of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), including café-au-lait macules and axillary freckling are established as part of the clinical diagnostic criteria for this distinct disorder of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway.8 Here, we present a pathogenic GANAB germline variant in an adult through a diagnostic odyssey that began with a compelling clinical picture for NF1.

Case Report

The patient is a 59-year-old woman with a complex medical history including developmental speech delay, kidney cysts, early menarche, migraines, hearing loss, peripheral neuropathy, spinal stenosis with radiculopathy (status post C4-C5 fusion), multiple basal cell carcinomas, and colonic polyps. She was initially referred to Clinical Genetics for assessment at age 56 years after her dermatologist noted the presence of bilateral axillary freckling (Fig 1A and B), as well as 10 café-au-lait macules (with each having a diameter that is larger than 15 mm, diffusely spread across the arms and legs, Fig 1C and D), thereby fulfilling the clinical diagnostic criteria for NF1. The patient reported that the café-au-lait macules have been present from childhood. There were 4 qualifying café-au-lait macules on the right thigh (with diameters of 1.6 cm, 1.7 cm. 1.9 cm, and 2 cm) and 4 on the left thigh (1.5 cm, 1.7 cm, 1.9 cm, and 1.9 cm) as well as 1 qualifying café-au-lait macule (1.5 cm) on each arm. The additional NF1 diagnostic criteria (including ophthalmologic evaluation for Lisch nodules, review of systems for optic pathway glioma–related symptoms and neuroimaging evaluation for optic glioma, assessment for skeletal abnormalities, and family history) were negative in this patient. There is no known family history of kidney disease, café-au-lait macules, axillary freckling, or other features of NF1, or diagnosis of NF1 in the patient’s first-and second-degree relatives.

Cutaneous features that raised suspicion for NF1 including bilateral axillary freckling (A and B), and café-au-lait mascules (with 10 of the mascules each been ≥15 mm) diffusely spread across the arms and legs (C and D) in a patient with GANAB-related disorder.

Given the cutaneous findings, the initial diagnostic steps focused on NF1 and SPRED1 (being the gene that is associated with Legius syndrome, an NF1 mimicker). Next-generation sequencing with deletion/duplication testing for NF1 and SPRED1 genes was subsequently performed.8 However, this targeted analysis returned negative, revealing no reportable variants in the NF1 and SPRED1 gene. The negative genetic result created a diagnostic dilemma, as the patient’s phenotype remained suggestive of NF1 or Legius syndrome. Considering the multisystemic concerns (including a history of neurodevelopmental delay, hearing loss and neuropathy) as well as the possibility of other forms of RASopathy, singleton whole genome sequencing (WGS) with mitochondrial genome sequencing was initiated.9 The WGS results showed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the GANAB gene that is denoted as c.181C>T (p.Arg61∗). This nonsense variant is predicted to introduce a premature termination codon, leading to a loss of function of the glucosidase II α subunit, a mechanism that is known to be pathogenic for GANAB-related disorders.4 The GANAB gene variant (c.181C>T [p.Arg61∗]) was previously identified in an adolescent with ADPKD.10 GANAB p.Arg61∗ was therefore classified as a pathogenic variant according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines.11 Cascade testing of the patient’s relatives for the GANAB p.Arg61∗ variant was offered but the patient declined because of limited contact with her relatives. This WGS test result prompted clinical investigations for the manifestations of GANAB-related disorders. A magnetic resonance imaging of the abdomen was performed, which confirmed numerous hepatic cysts and multiple cysts bilaterally in the kidneys (Fig 2A and B). The largest kidney cyst was a 3.4 cm lesion in the right lower pole, characterized as mildly T2 hyperintense and T1 hyperintense with no internal enhancement, most consistent with a benign proteinaceous/hemorrhagic cyst. The study also noted severe diffuse hepatic steatosis (Fig 2A and B). Her creatinine level was 0.88 mg/dL (reference interval, 0.5 to 1.1 mg/dL) and estimated glomerular filtration rate was 76 mL/min/1.73m2 (reference interval, >90 mL/min/1.73m2).

To assess for another potential complication extrarenal manifestation of GANAB-related ADPKD, the patient underwent magnetic resonance angiogram of the brain. There were no intracranial aneurysms identified on magnetic resonance angiogram. The patient’s diagnostic journey spanned from initial Clinical Genetics consultation for suspected NF1 to a confirmed molecular diagnosis of GANAB-related ADPKD. The diagnosis was discussed in detail with the patient, including the recognized as well as potentially novel features of GANAB-related disorders as summarized in Table 1. A comprehensive longitudinal surveillance plan, encompassing approaches for monitoring kidney and hepatic function as well as dermatological care, was offered by the multidisciplinary team to the patient.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of a pathogenic germline variant in the GANAB gene in a patient with cutaneous features of NF1, café-au-lait macules, and axillary freckling, in the context of ADPKD type 3 and ADPLD. Although a prior report had noted the coexistence of polycystic kidney disease and café-au-lait macules, genetic testing was not performed by the investigators; hence, the molecular underpinnings of the clinical phenotype were unclear.12 Building on that clinical finding of concomitant polycystic kidney disease and café-au-lait macules, our findings suggest that GANAB-related disorder may phenocopy the cutaneous manifestations of NF1. Our observations underscore the critical role of genetic testing in the confirmation of suspected diagnosis of ADPKD13,14 and NF1.15

Although the GANAB c.181C>T (p.Arg61∗) variant was previously described in another unrelated patient in the clinical scientific literature,10 the dermatologic phenotypes were not reported in the preceding study. The mechanistic link between a loss-of-function variant in GANAB and altered cutaneous pigmentation remains, at least in part, unclear at present. Glucosidase II is a key enzyme involved in the processing and quality control of a vast array of glycoproteins within the endoplasmic reticulum.1 It remains possible that glucosidase II dysfunction might disrupt the maturation of receptors or ligands that play critical roles in the development of the melanocytes and in the related signaling pathways16 resulting in the formation of café-au-lait macules and axillary freckling. It is pertinent to note that any proposed relationship between glucosidase II dysfunction and melanocyte biology in this report remains speculative. Although alterations in glycoprotein processing could theoretically influence melanocyte signaling pathways, no direct mechanistic evidence currently links GANAB variants to pigmentary phenotypes. Further studies will be required to determine whether this represents a true expansion of the GANAB phenotype or an incidental finding.

Clinicians should consider GANAB-related disorders in patients with café-au-lait macules and axillary freckling, who have negative genetic test results for NF1 and Legius syndrome, in the setting of concomitant kidney and hepatic cysts, particularly in patients with atypical ADPKD features and negative PKD1 and PKD2 genetic testing results. Our findings also highlight the importance of WGS in patients with multisystemic abnormalities of unclear etiology. Of note, this report describes a single individual, and therefore conclusions regarding causality or mechanistic association between GANAB-related disorder and café-au-lait macules with axillary freckling cannot be drawn. Given the relatively common occurrence of café-au-lait macules in the general population, coincidental coexistence remains a plausible explanation. It is also important to note that although developmental speech delay and early menarche were observed in this patient, these features are nonspecific and are not diagnostic for NF1 or GANAB-related disorder in isolation and do not clearly support an alternative unifying diagnosis but are included for completeness. Even though comprehensive genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants in NF1, mosaic NF1 or regulatory variants that are not detectable by current methods cannot be entirely excluded. As such, this observation should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and intended to encourage further phenotypic documentation in genetically confirmed GANAB-related disorder.
