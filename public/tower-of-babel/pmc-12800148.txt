Hypergravity hinders proper dendritic patterning of a sensory neuron in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Je-hyun Moon, Kyoung-hye Yoon, Jin I. Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Altering gravity force can modify the three-dimensional spatial environment inside living animals that are adapted to earth’s gravity. This may have deleterious effects on biological processes such as developmental patterning that requires proper molecular interactions in a 3D spatial environment. Here, we exposed the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans to a gravity force of 100G and observed effects to the development of the PVD touch sensory neurons that exhibit an intricate and stereotyped dendritic branch patterning. We found that hypergravity force increases the incidence of specific quaternary branching defects that occur in the narrow basement membrane space between muscle and epidermal layers during the L4 larval developmental stage. Normally the growing PVD neuron extends dendritic branches across the body of the worm by following a SAX-7/L1CAM epidermal cue, but in 100G gravity some branches suddenly turn and instead follow longitudinal neurons forming “L” and “T” shaped branch defects. Interestingly, hypergravity-induced dendrite defects were suppressed in mutants with impaired hemidesmosomal structures in the basement membrane of the epidermis. We speculate that hypergravity compresses rigid muscle and epidermal layers together such that cells and structures that are normally far apart are brought close together. These modified spatial dynamics may cause inappropriate molecular interactions resulting in improper developmental patterns in environments of altered gravity such as spaceflight.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31084-0.

Introduction

Although gravity is one of the most ubiquitous environmental factors experienced by all living organisms, its influence at the cellular and molecular levels remains poorly understood. Given that terrestrial organisms have evolved under constant exposure to Earth’s gravitational force, deviations from this baseline—such as high- or low-gravity environments—are likely to induce significant physiological and developmental effects. For instance, long-duration spaceflight has been associated with various physiological impairments in astronauts, including musculoskeletal atrophy, optic nerve pathology, and circulatory dysfunction1–6. In addition, altered gravity has been shown to affect gene expression profiles in both plant and animal models7–9. In rat, number of synapses in vestibular macular hair cells increased after 14 days spaceflight10.

Artificial high gravity, or hypergravity, can be applied to living organisms including humans by centrifugation resulting in various physiological changes11. In the nervous system, for instance, fewer vestibular synapses were observed in hypergravity10. Exposure to hypergravity during the embryonic stage in Xenopus showed that brain and notochord development was retarded12. Hypergravity appears to affect critical periods of brain development in rodents altering metabolism and motor and vestibular coordination13, as well as changing neuron structure, receptor gene expression and synaptic plasticity in areas of the brain14–17. Altered gravity has been implicated in structural abnormalities in neurons, including axonal defects and modified dendritic morphology18,19. Despite these observations, the mechanisms by which gravity influences neuronal patterning and circuit formation remain largely unknown.

The precise spatial organization of neurons is essential for establishing appropriate synaptic connections and forming highly complex neural circuits. In the mammalian neocortex, cortical neurons located in distinct 3D laminar layers exhibit highly specific connectivity patterns that are defined by their developmental patterning20,21. Neuronal patterning is orchestrated by both intrinsic and extrinsic cues derived from neighboring cells and tissues22–25. From early developmental stages, neurons interact with their microenvironment to guide process extension and target selection. For example, pioneer neurons provide scaffolds that direct the growth of later-developing axons26,27. These cell–cell interactions persist beyond embryogenesis, as neurons continue to refine their morphology and connectivity through contact with adjacent neurons during post-embryonic development25. Furthermore, obstacles such as neighboring neuronal processes and muscle structure can interfere with proper neuron growth cone migration28,29. In addition, extrinsic signals from the extracellular matrix (ECM), muscle, and epidermis influence dendritic arborization through interactions with transmembrane receptors on the neuronal surface22–24. Mechanisms of neuronal patterning, however, are adapted to earth’s gravity, and it is unknown what effect that hypergravity may have on axonal or dendritic outgrowth.

The PVD neuron in Caenorhabditis elegans is a post-embryonically generated polymodal sensory neuron that detects harsh mechanical stimuli and extends elaborate dendritic arbors throughout the body30,31. Its dendritic morphology is highly stereotyped, forming a menorah-like, non-overlapping structure that develops sequentially from primary (1º) to quaternary (4º) branches32,33. Key molecular interactions have been identified that regulate the precise patterning of PVD dendrites. SAX-7, the C. elegans homolog of the human L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM), is expressed in the epidermis and forms a ligand-receptor complex with DMA-1, a leucine-rich repeat (LRR) transmembrane protein expressed on the surface of PVD neurons. This ligand–receptor interaction provides guidance cues critical for dendritic branching34–36.

C. elegans grows and reproduces normally in hypergravity and can withstand forces of up to 400,000G37,38. We previously demonstrated that 100G hypergravity can induce axonal defects during development in motor neurons in C. elegans18. In this study, we challenged C. elegans by centrifugal force to investigate whether PVD neuronal dendrites navigate properly within a molecularly dynamic ECM and epidermal layer. We found that the frequency of 4° branching defects was significantly increased under hypergravity conditions. Overall, we hypothesize that compressive forces alter the normal 3D spatial environment and drive PVD dendrites to follow inappropriate molecular cues.

NGM tube preparation

NGM tubes were prepared as previously described18 with slight modifications. Briefly, 1 mL of nematode growth medium (NGM) containing 2% of Difco™ bacteriological agar (BD Biosciences, USA) was autoclaved and transferred into sterile 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. To prevent premature solidification of the agar, tubes were maintained in a heat block at 50 °C. Solidification was induced by centrifugation at 100G (1200 rpm at a rotor radius of 6 cm) using a LaboGen 1370R centrifuge (LaboGen, Republic of Korea) while gradually cooling the tubes to 10 °C. This creates a surface in which the centrifugal force is perpendicular to the flat agar surface in the tube to produce a hypergravity environment upon centrifugation. After complete solidification, any residual moisture on the inner walls of the tubes was removed using sterile cotton swabs. For bacterial lawn preparation, a single Escherichia coli OP50 colony was inoculated into LB broth (BD Biosciences, USA) and cultured overnight at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. Then, 5 μL of the concentrated OP50 culture was seeded onto the surface of the solidified NGM. Seeded tubes were allowed to dry at room temperature for 20–30 min before use.

Hypergravity exposure

Synchronized eggs were obtained from gravid adults using a standard bleaching protocol (Stiernagle, 1999). Approximately 30–50 eggs were placed on the E. coli OP50 lawn in 15 mL NGM agar tubes. All centrifugation experiments were performed using a temperature-controlled centrifuge. The tubes were placed into a LaboGen 1370R centrifuge (LaboGen, Republic of Korea) equipped with temperature control and were subjected to hypergravity conditions at 100G (1200 rpm at a rotor radius of 6 cm) for 68–72 h. For 1G control conditions, eggs were cultured in a JSRI-250C incubator (JSR, Republic of Korea) at 20 °C for the same duration producing a somewhat similar vibration environment as centrifugation. Hypergravity force of 4G (250 rpm), 10G (400 rpm), and 100G were based on minimum centrifuge device speed and previous hypergravity study18. Similar to previous studies, development times were monitored during hypergravity exposure and were not different than 1G control conditions18,40. All experiments were repeated three times independently.

Temporal hypergravity exposure

For temporal hypergravity experiments, synchronized eggs were placed in NGM tubes. All samples were maintained at 20 °C throughout the experiment. Experimental groups were exposed to hypergravity (100G) for specific durations—3, 6, 10, 18, 48, or 63 h—using a LaboGen 1370R centrifuge (LaboGen, Republic of Korea), after which the tubes were returned to 1 × g conditions at 20 °C until worms reached to young adult stage. For acute hypergravity treatment, worms were first cultured under 1G for 48 h, then subjected to hypergravity (100G) for the final 15 h (48–63 h time period), reaching the young adult stage by the end of exposure. Durations during larval development were based on previous hypergravity study18. Three independent experiments were performed for each time frame.

Fluorescent imaging

C. elegans were cultured in NGM tubes for 68–72 h at 20 °C to obtain young adult stage worms. Worms were collected into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes using S-basal or M9 buffer and washed twice to remove residual bacteria. After washing, worms were transferred onto 2% agarose pads prepared on glass microscope slides. To immobilize the animals, 4 µL of 2 mM levamisole (Sigma-Aldrich) in M9 buffer was applied directly to the worms. Fluorescent images of GFP- or mCherry-labeled neurons or SAX-7 were acquired using an Olympus BX50 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Fluorescent images were merged with ImageJ software.

Image analysis and quantification

Quantification of the number of PVD dendritic branches was performed based on fluorescent images, following a previously described method41. The region anterior to the PVD cell body (defined as position 0) was divided into eight equal segments along the primary (1º) dendrite, while the posterior region was divided into four segments. Within each segment, all secondary (2º) and quaternary (4º) branches were counted. Dendritic defects were classified into distinct categories. A 2º branch was scored as “misdirected” if it deviated from a straight, perpendicular trajectory. Also, “ectopic branch” was scored if any ectopic side branches were extended from 2º branch. 4º branches were classified as “short” if their length was less than one-third of the typical distance from the tertiary (3º) branch to the ventral nerve cord. A 4º branch was scored as “curved” if it deviated from a straight-line morphology. “L” and “T” shape 4º branches were scored if they deviated stripe pattern and turned perpendicularly. For quantification of partial anterior and posterior side, the anterior side was analyzed within 100 µm from the vulva, whereas the posterior side was analyzed within 50 µm on each side of the PVD cell body. Adjustment of individual images and measurement of branch length were performed with ImageJ software. For each experiment, animals were randomly selected for analysis, and all quantifications were performed blinded. Details of quantification criteria, including the number of animals for each experiment, are described in the corresponding figure legends. Distances between dendritic endpoints and SAX-7 longitudinal stripes were measured using ImageJ. For each animal, the distance from L&T defect 4º branch endpoint to the nearest SAX-7 longitudinal line (dorsal nerve cord or 3º branch close line) was measured. The distance from the nearest normal 4º branch endpoint to the same SAX-7 longitudinal line was also measured for comparison. All quantifications were performed within the anterior 100 µm region from the vulva.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA), and the specific statistical tests applied to each experiment are described in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Hypergravity selectively disrupts higher-order dendritic architecture of PVD neurons in C. elegans

To determine whether hypergravity influences PVD dendrite development, C. elegans embryos were exposed to a relative centrifugal force (RCF) of 100G from the embryonic stage until young adulthood inducing artificial high gravity similar to previous studies18,42. PVD dendrites were visualized using a transgenic strain expressing the PVD-specific marker F49H12.4::GFP43. To assess potential changes in dendritic branching, we divided each PVD neuron into 12 equal segments along the anterior–posterior axis from the cell body, as previously described41 (Fig. 1A). Although the numbers of secondary (2°) and quaternary (4°) branches normally vary among the segments in 1G, 100G hypergravity did not produce a detectable change in branch number in any segments (Fig. 1B,C). At the 2° branch level, two main phenotypes were identified: a “Misdirection” phenotype, characterized by disorganized curved projections, and an “Ectopic” phenotype, in which branches extended laterally in the middle of the 2° branch (Fig. 2B,C). The most common 4° branch defects were "L" and "T" shaped termini, where branches aberrantly turned, forming reversed L- or T-like structures (Fig. 2F,G). Additional defects included “Curved” and “Short” 4° branches (Fig. 2F). In addition, ectopic outgrowth of higher-order branches—specifically 5° and 6° branches emerging from 4° branches—were also observed (Fig. 2H,I). While the incidence of morphological defects in 2° and 3° branches remained relatively unchanged under 100G hypergravity, we found significant increases in curved branches, short branches, ectopic higher-order branches, and, most prominently, L and T shaped termini compared to 1G control gravity (Fig. 2G,I). Overall, hypergravity specifically induced defects in 4° branches and higher order branches rather than 2° and 3° branches (Fig. 2J).

Hypergravity induces 4° dendritic defects in PVD neurons during the critical period of their development

PVD neuron dendritic development is a post-embryonic process that occurs from the L2 larval stage to the young adult stage33. Higher-order 4° branches begin to elongate at late L3 or early L4 stage. We wondered at what developmental stage hypergravity affected PVD morphology, so we varied 100G hypergravity exposure during several critical developmental periods. We found that continuous exposure for 48 h from the embryonic stage to just prior to the L4 stage did not result in any increase in 4° dendrite defects (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, continuous exposure from the embryonic stage through mid- to late L4 significantly increased the frequency of "L" and "T" shaped 4° branch defects (Fig. 3A, B). Notably, acute exposure limited to the L4 stage alone, including 48–63 h or 63–72 h after the egg stage, was sufficient to induce PVD dendrite defects. We also exposed worms to hypergravity from 72 to 78 h after the egg stage when the PVD neurons are fully developed. However, no increase in 4° branch defects was observed in this condition. To investigate whether the induction of PVD dendritic defects is dependent on the magnitude of gravitational force, C. elegans were exposed to 4G, 10G, and 100G conditions. Our previous work showed a gravity force of at least 10G was required to induce axon guidance defects in GABAergic DD/VD motor neurons in C. elegans18. Consistent with this, the frequency of "L" and "T" shaped 4° branch defects did not substantially increase from 1G (8.3 ± 0.25) to 4G (10.0 ± 0.51) but was significantly elevated at 10G (12.9 ± 0.50) (Fig. 3C). Also, further increase in defect frequency was not significant in 100G (13.4 ± 0.64) compared to 10G (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that a certain threshold level of hypergravity above 4G is required to elicit morphological defects in PVD 4° dendrites.

Hypergravity-induced 4° dendritic defects align with longitudinal SAX-7/L1CAM cues suggesting disrupted epidermal guidance patterning in C. elegans

We next sought to understand how hypergravity alters the development of higher-order PVD branches. During the L4 stage, repeating dendritic arbors sprout from 3° branches and extend through the narrow extracellular matrix (ECM) between the epidermis and muscle quadrants to form the distinct candelabra-shaped structures. The proper guidance of the 4° branches relies on interactions between the epidermis-derived ligand SAX-7/L1CAM and the PVD-expressed receptor DMA-1, a leucine-rich repeat (LRR) protein (Liu and Shen, 2012). In the epidermis overlying the muscle bands, SAX-7/L1CAM is localized in a dorsal-to-ventral striped pattern and associated with the proper development of the PVD 4° branches24,34.

It has been previously demonstrated that mutants in which SAX-7 epidermal stripes were mislocalized result in aberrant PVD 4° branches that followed the improperly localized cue24. To investigate whether hypergravity-induced "L" and "T" shaped 4° branch defects are associated with disruptions in this guidance pattern, we visualized SAX-7/L1CAM using a sax-7::TY1::EGFP translational reporter strain44. Epidermal SAX-7 stripes are visible in both 1G and 100G conditions and no obvious abnormalities in SAX-7 expression can be observed in hypergravity (Fig. 4A–C; Fig S1). In addition, L and T shape 4° defects are occasionally visible at 1G control conditions but substantially increase in number at 100G (Fig. 4D,E). Interestingly, we noted that L and T-shaped 4° defects occurred specifically at locations where the epidermal SAX-7 stripes intersected with a SAX-7 longitudinal line (Fig. 4A–C). Defective 4° branches inappropriately turn at these lines and continue to follow the SAX-7 cue for some distance to form either the L or T shape branch. The longitudinal SAX-7 lines are apical and parallel in relation to the PVD 3° branches. To further assess whether hypergravity-induced defects are associated with these sublateral SAX-7 cues, we quantified the overlap between L and T defects and longitudinal SAX-7 lines following hypergravity exposure. Strikingly, nearly all "L" and "T" defects align with longitudinal SAX-7 lines in either 1G or 100G (Fig. 4F). 4° PVD dendritic branches inappropriately follow SDQ/PVN neuron and dorsal or ventral nerve cord longitudinal SAX-7 cues to form L and T-shaped defects

In contrast to other types of PVD 4° branch defects24, we established that L and T-shaped defects form when the growing PVD neuron turns perpendicularly and inappropriately follows a longitudinal SAX-7 cue (Fig. 4). We noticed that the location where L and T-shaped defects occurred show a pattern: defects were either gathered close to the 3° branch where the 4° branches originate from or gathered at a far distance from the 3° branch (Fig. 5A). In addition, it appeared that this pattern may differ between the anterior and posterior sides. To confirm this pattern, we measured the distance from the PVD 3° branch to the location of the L or T shape of defective 4° branches on either the posterior or anterior side of animals exposed to 1G or 100G (Fig. 5B). On the posterior side of the animal, we found that defective branches turned at a distance of 18 ± 0.52 µm from the 3° branch in 1G and a nearly identical distance of 17.5 ± 0.47 µm in 100G (Fig. 5B). On the anterior side of the animal, as expected, we saw defective branches cluster at a distance of 20.6 ± 0.30 µm in 1G and 20.3 ± 0.38 µm in 100G, similar to albeit slightly longer than the posterior side (Fig. 5B). We conclude that the L or T shape 4° branch turns at a specific location about 17 to 21 µm from the 3° branch. Interestingly, we also observed a second cluster of branches that turn at 9.6 ± 0.32 µm in 1G and 9.4 ± 0.39 µm at 100G (Fig. 5B), significantly closer to the 3° branch. We confirmed that both anterior and posterior L and T-shaped 4° branches colocalize with a longitudinal line of SAX-7 protein (Fig. 5C). Thus, aberrant 4° L or T shape branches likely follow two SAX-7 longitudinal lines: one line located at a far distance from the 3° branch that is close to the dorsal or ventral nerve cords, and one exclusively anterior line at a close distance. To determine whether L and T shape branch defects colocalize with these SAX-7 longitudinal lines, we measured the distance from PVD 4° branch endpoints and the SAX-7 longitudinal line near the dorsal nerve cord (Fig. 5D) as well as the longitudinal line close to the 3° branch (Fig. 5E). We quantified the overlap of L and T branches with the pan-neuronal GFP reporter and determined that more than 97% of defects at either 1G or 100G colocalized with the neuronal longitudinal processes (Fig. 6C). Genes important for muscle-epidermal layer structures promote hypergravity-induced PVD defects

The PVD 4° branches extend from the lateral sides towards the dorsal or ventral sides of the worm body through the narrow ECM, or basement membrane, between the body wall muscle and the epidermis. The basement membrane between the epidermis and the muscle is composed of densely packed “fibrous organelles” (FO), hemidesmosomal structures that physically connect and fix the muscle to the epidermis and the cuticle surrounding the outside of the worm. We previously showed that 100G hypergravity can hinder the migration of motor axons at the junction of the muscle and the epidermis, and disruptions of FO proteins can alleviate hypergravity-induced defects to allow normal axonal migration through the basement membrane space18,40. We wondered whether hypergravity may be inducing 4° L and T shape branch defects in the growing PVD neuron by a similar mechanism.

We observed the PVD neurons in mutants of components of the hemidesmosomal FO that were tested in a previous study40. These protein components include UNC-70/β-spectrin, VAB-10/spectraplakin and the ECM protein UNC-52/perlecan. We found that the overall number of 4° branches was decreased in unc-70(e524), vab-10(e667), and unc-52(e1421) mutants compared to wild type in both 1G and 100G hypergravity (Fig. 7A, B). Next, we examined 4° L and T shape branch defects in each strain after 1G or 100G hypergravity exposure throughout development. Because the mutant strains had fewer 4° branches, we quantified the ratio of L and T shape branch defects among the total 4° branches for each strain. In 1G normal gravity, 7.4% of the 4° branches ended with an L or T shape in wild type C. elegans, whereas unc-70 mutants and vab-10 mutants exhibited higher baseline defects of 12.9% and 19.8%, respectively (Fig. 7C). Finally, 4° branches in unc-52 mutants displayed over 50% L or T shape defects. In addition, body size remained the same in 1G and 100G for all strains including the three mutants strains suggesting that worms proceeded relatively normally throughout all larval stages including L4 during the hypergravity experiment (Fig. S2).

We next asked whether L and T shape defects formed when the PVD 4° branches intersected with longitudinal neurons, so we measured the length of the 4° branch where each L and T defects occurred. We found that in unc-70 mutants, L and T defects clustered at a distance of 16.5 ± 0.31 µm near the DNC and VNC, and 11.9 ± 0.68 µm close to the SDQ/PLN nerve cord similar to wild type (Fig. 7D). In contrast, we did not see any clustering of L and T defects in vab-10 or unc-52 mutants and instead observed defects throughout the entire length of the 4° branches (Fig. 7D). This suggests that disruption of the FO component VAB-10 protein may result in disorganized SAX-7 epidermal stripes similar to previous observations in unc-52 mutants24.

Next, we examined the effect that hypergravity force has on the PVD neuron in the FO-disrupted mutant strains. In 100G hypergravity we saw an 8% increase in L and T defects in wild type, whereas only a 2% increase was observed in unc-70 mutants and a 4% and 5% decrease in vab-10 and unc-52 mutants, respectively (Fig. 7C). In addition, we examined the location of the L and T defects in wild type and mutant strains in 100G. In wild type and unc-70 mutants we continue to observe clustering of defects near the dorsal or ventral nerve cords and the SDQ/PLN neuron as in 1G (Fig. 7D). In contrast, we do not observe clustering of defects in vab-10 and unc-52 mutants in 100G similar to 1G (Fig. 7D) indicating that hypergravity does not alter the localization of L and T defects. This suggests that hypergravity-induced increases in PVD 4° branch defects were suppressed in mutants of components of the FO.

Discussion

L1-like cell adhesion molecules(L1CAMs) are known to play critical roles in maintaining axonal and dendritic positioning in C. elegans49,51. The PVD neuron uses SAX-7/L1CAM epidermal cues to faithfully form hundreds of dendritic branches across the length of the body during the larval stage. Occasional errors occur including 4° L and T shape defects, and we show here that the developing dendrite inappropriately turns and follows longitudinal dorsal and ventral nerve cords as well as the SDQ/PLN neuronal processes that express SAX-7. The PVD neuron is located in the basement membrane adjacent to the overlying epidermis, whereas the longitudinal processes of the DNC, VNC and the SDQ/PLN neuron are embedded in the underlying muscle47,48 (Fig. 8). Although in a 2D representation, the PVD neuron and the longitudinal processes appear to intersect, in the actual 3D body tube of the worm, the PVD 4° branches are located above the underlying nerve processes (Fig. 8). The overlying epidermis and underlying longitudinal neurons both express SAX-7 cue, but in theory the PVD dendritic growth cone should only respond to epidermal SAX-7 due to the physical distance between the two tissues. The force of hypergravity can alter the position of tissues and cells in a living organism53,54. We previously demonstrated that 100G hypergravity may compress C. elegans muscles and the basement membrane between the muscle and the epidermis, hindering proper motor neuron axon migration40. One possible hypothesis is that 100G hypergravity force compresses epidermal and muscle layers to bring the PVD 4° branches physically closer to underlying neuronal processes such as the DNC, VNC, and SDQ/PLN neurons (Fig. 8). This can increase the incidence of L and T shape defects that we observe.

FOs in the basement membrane maintain the rigid “sandwich” of muscle, epidermal and cuticular layers to transmit internal muscle contractive force to the outer cuticle. Thus, the hemidesmosomal FOs allow the muscle to control movement in the absence of bone tissue in the nematode55. In the same way, it has been suggested that external forces such as touch cues can be transmitted from the cuticle to touch receptor neurons in the epidermis such as the PVD neuron and the ALM touch receptor neuron56. In our previous study, we hypothesized that 100G hypergravity force is transmitted from the cuticle to the muscle resulting in a rigid compression of tissue layers in the worm, and a further narrowing of the thin and tight basement membrane (40,Fig. 8). Diverse neurons processes including motor neurons and the PVD neuron traverse this densely packed space interacting with the resident proteins57–59 and developing neuronal processes such as motor axons are challenged to penetrate the basement membrane and reach their destinations28. We demonstrated that hypergravity-induced motor neuron defects can be alleviated by disruption of FO proteins such as UNC-52, UNC-70 and VAB-10 resulting in a suppression of hypergravity-induced motor neuron axonal defects40.

Here, we demonstrate that disruption of the FO in unc-52, vab-10, or unc-70 mutants suppresses hypergravity-induced PVD dendrite defects (Fig. 7C). How can altering FO proteins suppress the effects of hypergravity on neuronal pathfinding? One possibility is that this weakens coupling of the epidermis and muscle and could induce shearing of the epidermal layer independently of the muscle. This in turn reduces a narrowing of the basement membrane in respond to hypergravity force. Consistent with this hypothesis, mutants in which muscle separates from the epidermis results in large pockets of space in the basement membrane60,61. Based on this hypothesis FO disruption may counteract the effects of 100G force on the tissue layers and suppress hypergravity-induced L and T defects. Further measurements are necessary to confirm whether hypergravity induces a compression of the basement membrane and FO disruption suppresses compression.

We observed that unc-52, vab-10, and unc-70 mutants all display increased L and T shaped 4° branches compared to wild type in normal 1G gravity (Fig. 7B). This was expected in unc-52 mutants as a previous study showed that the UNC-52/perlecan protein is necessary for proper FO localization in the epidermis, and epidermal SAX-7 is disorganized in unc-52 mutants resulting in increased L and T shaped defects24. We show here that disruption of either UNC-52/perlecan or VAB-10/spectroplakin protein causes a scattered increase in L and T shape 4° branches, rather than clustered defects near the location of longitudinal nerve tracts (Fig. 7B,C). We surmise the increase in defects is due to improper SAX-7 localization.

However, this may not be the case for unc-70 mutants in which we observe clustered L and T shape defects like wild type (Fig. 7C). We examined epidermal SAX-7 in unc-70 mutants and found that SAX-7 stripes show normal expression and localization (Fig. 7F). The reason for increased defects is not clear. It was previously shown that muscle cells in unc-70 mutants were significantly smaller40, possibly changing the dynamics and localization of the tissues in the worm. UNC-70 is expressed in the muscle, epidermis and neurons including the PVD neuron62,63 but whether unc-70 expression in any of these tissues is required for normal 4° branches is unknown.

Since SAX-7 is misaligned in unc-52 mutants and possibly in vab-10 mutants, this may throw 4° branches off course so that they are unable to interact with longitudinal SAX-7 gradients in the SDQ/PLN and dorsal and ventral nerve cord neurons. This could also explain why disruption of FO proteins suppress hypergravity-induced PVD defects. However, we do not observe obvious SAX-7 epidermal misalignment in unc-70 mutants (Fig. 7F). Further analysis of SAX-7 localization and expression may be required to investigate this hypothesis.

Although we demonstrate that hypergravity causes specific defects in PVD 4° branches, we have not tested the effect of microgravity on PVD neuron development. Previously it was shown that space microgravity can alter the number of 2° and 3° branches, but not 4° branches in 5-day old aged adult PVD neurons19. In addition, increased 5° ectopic branches were observed in space. It would be interesting to test whether the growing PVD neuron also followed inappropriate SAX-7 cues in microgravity as in hypergravity.

Proper patterning of cells, tissues and organs during development occur through specific molecular interactions in a 3D spatial environment that is adapted to earth’s 1G gravity force. As we demonstrate here, changing gravity force can alter the 3D spatial and molecular environment and allow inappropriate cell and molecular interactions that hinders normal patterning. The vertebrate nervous system, however, presents a much more complex 3D spatial environment. C. elegans can withstand forces upwards of 400,000 and we employed 100G hypergravity similar to previous studies18,37,40,42. However, mammals can only withstand forces below 10-15G, and astronauts experience less than 10G during space launch and entry. Since spaceflight challenges the human body in both hypergravity and microgravity environments, it would be interesting to investigate the ability for neuronal processes to migrate, pattern or repair properly during spaceflight in light of the work presented here.
