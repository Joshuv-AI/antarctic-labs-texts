Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum L-ZS9 fermentation enriches Houttuynia cordata polyphenols and protects myofibrillar proteins from oxidative damage
Authors: Li Yang, Lanxi Zhang, Jinhua Bi, YingFeng Li, Yalin Li, Dong Zhang, Chang Su, Hongjun Li, Yu Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClean-label natural antioxidants are increasingly important for limiting meat quality deterioration caused by protein oxidation. However, the effects of lactic acid bacteria fermentation on plant-derived antioxidants remain unclear. This study investigated Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum L-ZS9 fermentation on phenolic transformation of Houttuynia cordata and its protection against myofibrillar protein oxidation. Fermentation increased total polyphenols 2.45-fold, with flavonoids peaking on day 5, and increased isoquercitrin, rutin, and quercitrin while reducing hyperoside; isoquercitrin and rutin showed the strongest correlations with antioxidant activity. Antioxidant activity increased 61–100% in DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl radical, and superoxide scavenging assays. FHC6 dose-dependently suppressed carbonylation, maintained solubility, reduced surface hydrophobicity, and restored intrinsic fluorescence. Protective effects may involve reactive oxygen species scavenging, interactions between oxidized phenolics and protein thiols, and inhibition of aggregation by glycosylated flavonols. These indicated L-ZS9 fermentation enhanced the antioxidant and protein-protective properties of H. cordata, highlighting its potential as a clean-label antioxidant for muscle foods.Keywords: Houttuynia cordata, Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum, Phenolic compounds, Myofibrillar proteins, Antioxidant

Introduction

Myofibrillar proteins (MPs), comprising 60–70% of total muscle proteins, govern the functional and textural properties of meat products (Li et al., 2025). However, MPs are susceptible to oxidative deterioration during processing and storage, primarily driven by reactive oxygen species (ROS) from lipid peroxidation, Fenton reactions, and enzymatic pathways. Oxidative damage manifests as protein carbonylation, sulfhydryl depletion, and structural disruption, ultimately causing protein aggregation, moisture loss, and textural hardening (Domínguez et al., 2022). To mitigate oxidative degradation, both synthetic and natural antioxidants have been employed. However, consumer demand for clean-label products has driven a shift toward plant-derived alternatives (Esazadeh et al., 2024). Plant-derived polyphenols have garnered attention due to their protective mechanisms, including free radical scavenging, metal chelation, and protein interactions that stabilize conformations and mask oxidation-prone sites (Papuc et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2025). Guo et al. (2021) showed that phenolic compounds such as chlorogenic acid, quercetin, catechin, and EGCG affected MP cross-linking and gelation in a structure-dependent manner. These differences highlight the need to explore polyphenol-rich sources with suitable composition and enhanced antioxidant activity.

Microbial fermentation by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) has emerged as an effective bioconversion strategy to enhance the phenolic content and bioactivity of plant matrices. LAB such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, La. paraplantarum, and La. casei possess glycoside hydrolases (β-glucosidases, β-galactosidases) that release free aglycones with enhanced antioxidant activity and bioavailability (Paventi et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2025). LAB fermentation also generates organic acids that lower pH and promote acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of cell wall polysaccharides, further liberating entrapped phenolics (Gaur & Gänzle, 2023). Additionally, microbial biotransformation via deglycosylation, hydroxylation, and ring cleavage may yield novel bioactive metabolites that reshape the phenolic profile (Kumar et al., 2025). In addition, the characteristics of plant matrices can influence microbial utilization and metabolite production (Li, Wang, et al., 2022), while microbial metabolites may also contribute to the biological effects of fermented products (Yan et al., 2026). Recent studies have shown that LAB fermentation can alter not only total phenolic and flavonoid contents but also the profiles of individual phenolic compounds. La. paraplantarum has also been reported to improve the phenolic composition and antioxidant activity of plant matrices (Huang et al., 2024). L-ZS9, a genome-characterized La. paraplantarum strain isolated from fermented meat (CICC No. 6262; GenBank CP013130), exhibits good environmental adaptability and has potential as a food fermentation starter (Liu et al., 2018; Liu & Li, 2016). However, its ability to transform plant phenolics has not been well investigated.

Houttuynia cordata Thunb., a perennial herbaceous plant widely distributed across East and Southeast Asia, has been traditionally used in folk medicine and functional foods owing to its diverse bioactivities, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects (Wei et al., 2024). In China, H. cordata is officially recognized as a substance traditionally used as both food and medicine, and recent studies have demonstrated its potential to enhance muscle antioxidant capacity and improve meat quality, underscoring its relevance to food preservation (Wang, Xie, et al., 2025). However, the direct application of H. cordata extracts faces notable challenges from pungent fishy odor from volatile compounds, astringent off-flavors from tannins, and suboptimal bioavailability due to a substantial proportion of polyphenols existing in glycosylated or cell wall-bound forms (Lin et al., 2022; Zhangli et al., 2025). Chou et al. (2025) found that La. plantarum fermentation increased total phenolics, total flavonoids, quercetin, and antioxidant activity in H. cordata, together with changes in quercitrin and hyperoside levels. These results suggest that LAB fermentation can modify the phenolic profile of H. cordata. However, previous work has largely focused on phenolic composition and overall antioxidant activity. It remains unclear whether these changes translate into improved protection against myofibrillar protein oxidation and what mechanisms are involved.

Given the antioxidant potential of H. cordata and the ability of LAB to transform plant phenolics, L-ZS9 was used to ferment H. cordata. While previous studies have mainly focused on phenolic composition and overall antioxidant activity after fermentation, this study further examined the relationship between phenolic changes and protection against myofibrillar protein oxidation, as well as the possible mechanisms involved. Accordingly, this study aimed to: (i) characterize changes in pH, microbial growth, and phenolic composition during H. cordata fermentation; (ii) evaluate the antioxidant capacity of fermented extracts and their protective efficacy on Fenton-induced MP oxidation; and (iii) investigate the underlying mechanisms, with emphasis on radical scavenging and phenolic-protein interactions. The findings may help clarify how fermentation-induced phenolic changes contribute to muscle protein protection and support the use of fermented H. cordata as a clean-label antioxidant in muscle foods.

Materials and methods

Microbial resources

La. paraplantarum L-ZS9 (formerly Lactobacillus paraplantarum; CICC No. 6262) was obtained from the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC, Beijing, China). The strain was stored at −80 °C in de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS; Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) broth supplemented with 25% (v/v) glycerol. Before use, the strain was activated by two successive transfers in MRS broth at 37 °C for 18–24 h. The activated culture was then adjusted to approximately 106 CFU/mL for subsequent fermentation experiments.

Preparation of fermented H. cordata

Fresh H. cordata leaves were purchased from Hongguang market (Pidu District, Chengdu, China). Plants with intact, fully developed leaves of similar maturity, without visible yellowing, mechanical damage, pest injury, or disease symptoms, were selected and identified morphologically according to the Flora of China. Leaves were separated, washed under running water, rinsed three times with sterile distilled water, and blotted dry. The cleaned leaves were homogenized with sterile distilled water (1:2, w/v, fresh weight) using a blender (FC12 Pro, Midea Group Co., Ltd., Foshan, China) for 2 min. The resulting slurry was used immediately for fermentation at 36 ± 1 °C. Three independent fermentation batches were prepared.

Pork samples

Fresh pork loin (longissimus thoracis et lumborum) was obtained within 24 h postmortem from a local supermarket (Chengdu, China) and transported to the laboratory on ice within 30 min. Visible fat and connective tissue were trimmed, and the lean meat was cut into ∼2 cm cubes, vacuum-packed, and stored at −20 °C. All samples were used within two weeks. MPs were freshly extracted before each experiment. Three independent pork batches served as biological replicates.

Determination of physicochemical indicators

pH measurement

The pH of fermentation broth was measured daily at 25 ± 1 °C using a calibrated PHS-320 digital pH meter (INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The instrument was calibrated with standard buffers (pH 4.00, 6.86, and 9.18) before each session. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Viable cell counts

Total bacterial counts were determined according to the Chinese National Standard GB 4789.2–2016 (National Food Safety Standard). At each sampling time point, 1 mL of fermentation broth was aseptically collected and serially diluted (10-fold) with sterile 0.85% (w/v) saline solution. Appropriate dilutions (0.1 mL) were spread-plated in duplicate onto MRS agar (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) supplemented with cycloheximide (100 mg/L) to inhibit fungal growth. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Colonies were counted on plates containing 30–300 CFU, and the results were expressed as log CFU/mL. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Total sugar and reducing sugar contents

Total sugar content was determined by the anthrone‑sulfuric acid method adapted from Mehdi et al. (2023). A diluted sample (1.0 mL) was mixed with 4.0 mL of anthrone reagent (0.2% in concentrated H₂SO₄), heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min, and cooled rapidly on ice. Absorbance was read at 620 nm on a SpectraMax® i3x reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China) against a d-glucose standard curve (0–100 μg/mL). All measurements were performed in triplicate. Reducing sugar content was measured by the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) colorimetric method. A diluted sample (1.0 mL) was reacted with 1.5 mL of DNS reagent in a boiling water bath for 5 min, cooled on ice, and diluted to 25 mL. Absorbance was recorded at 540 nm against a d-glucose standard curve (0–1.0 mg/mL). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Soluble protein content

The soluble protein content was determined according to Tang et al. (2016) with some modifications. Briefly, 0.1 mL of appropriately diluted sample was mixed with 5.0 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 reagent. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm. A standard curve was established using bovine serum albumin (BSA, 0–100 μg/mL) as the reference standard. The soluble protein content was expressed as μg/mL. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Determination of phenolic compounds

The total flavonoid content and total polyphenol content were determined by referring to the method of Zheng et al. (2023) with some modifications. The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. For total polyphenol content determination, the supernatant was diluted 10-fold with distilled water prior to analysis. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm (total flavonoid content) and 765 nm (total polyphenol content) using a SpectraMax® i3x multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Individual phenolic compounds were analyzed by RP-HPLC based on Wu et al. (2020) with minor modifications. Filtered samples (0.22 μm) were separated on a Waters HPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a UV detector and a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at 30 °C. The mobile phase comprised 0.1% phosphoric acid (A) and acetonitrile (B) at 1.0 mL/min with the following gradient: 0–10 min, 5–15% B; 10–30 min, 15–30% B; 30–45 min, 30–50% B; 45–55 min, 50–95% B; 55–60 min, 95–5% B; followed by 5 min re-equilibration (injection volume: 10 μL). Detection wavelengths were 280 nm (catechin, epicatechin), 320 nm (chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid), and 360 nm (rutin, hyperoside, quercetin, isorhamnetin). Compounds were identified by comparison with authentic standards and quantified by external calibration (R2 > 0.999). Results were expressed as mg/L. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was determined according to Li et al. (2022) with minor modifications. 1.0 mL of sample solution was mixed with DPPH solution (1.0 mL, 0.1 mmol/L), vortexed and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. For the control, the sample was replaced with absolute ethanol. After incubation, absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a SpectraMax® i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Results were expressed as μmol Trolox equivalents/L based on a Trolox standard curve (4–20 μmol/mL). Measurements were performed in triplicate.

Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity

The hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was determined according to Xu et al. (2025) with minor modifications. Sample solution (400 μL) was mixed with FeSO₄ (400 μL, 6 mmol/L), salicylic acid (400 μL, 6 mmol/L), and H₂O₂ (400 μL, 6 mmol/L). The mixture was vortexed and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. For the control, the sample was replaced with distilled water. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a SpectraMax® i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Results were expressed as μmol Trolox equivalents/L based on a Trolox standard curve (Section 2.6.1). Measurements were performed in triplicate.

Superoxide anion scavenging activity

Superoxide anion radical scavenging activity was determined according to Ran et al. (2024) with modifications. Tris-HCl buffer (450 μL, 50 mmol/L, pH 8.2) and sample solution (100 μL) were equilibrated at 25 °C for 10 min. Pyrogallol solution (400 μL, 30 mmol/L) was added and incubated at 25 °C for 25 min. The reaction was terminated with HCl (500 μL, 8 mol/L). For the controls, the sample or pyrogallol was replaced with Tris-HCl buffer. Absorbance was measured at 325 nm using a SpectraMax® i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Results were expressed as μmol Trolox equivalents/L based on a Trolox standard curve (Section 2.6.1). Measurements were performed in triplicate.

ABTS radical scavenging activity

ABTS radical cation scavenging activity was determined according to Li et al. (2022) with modifications. ABTS+· stock solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of ABTS (7 mmol/L in 5 mmol/L phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) and potassium persulfate (4.9 mmol/L), followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. The stock was diluted with PBS (5 mmol/L phosphate, pH 7.4) to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Diluted ABTS+· solution (200 μL) was mixed with sample (10 μL) in a 96-well microplate. For the control, the sample was replaced with PBS. After incubation at 25 °C for 5 min in the dark, absorbance was measured at 734 nm. Results were expressed as μmol Trolox equivalents/L based on a Trolox standard curve (Section 2.6.1). Measurements were performed in triplicate.

Extraction of MPs and oxidation treatment

MPs were extracted from pork loin according to the method of Wang et al. (2019). The protein concentration was adjusted to 20 mg/mL with 0.02 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.0, 0.6 mol/L NaCl). Oxidative stress was induced using a Fenton-like system (FeCl₃ 0.01 mmol/L, ascorbic acid 0.1 mmol/L, H₂O₂ 5 mmol/L). Trolox (1 mg/mL) served as a positive antioxidant control, while FHC0 and FHC6 were added at designated concentrations (Table 1). The non-oxidized MPs solution was designated as the blank, and the oxidized MPs without antioxidant addition was designated as the control. The oxidation reaction was conducted at 4 °Cfor 24 h in a shaking incubator and terminated by adding BHT (final concentration 0.02%, w/v). The MPs pellet was recovered by diluting NaCl to 0.1 mol/L with 0.02 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), centrifuging at 8000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and washing twice with 0.02 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.0, 0.1 mol/L NaCl). The final Fenton reagent-free MPs pellet was collected for subsequent analyses.

Determination of carbonyl and total sulfhydryl contents

Carbonyl and total sulfhydryl contents were quantified according to Zhangli et al. (2025) with slight modifications. MPs were adjusted to 2.0 mg/mL with 20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) before each assay. For carbonyls, 1.0 mL of diluted MPs was mixed with 1.0 mL of DNPH solution (10 mmol/L, dissolved in 2 mol/L HCl) and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h in the dark, vortexing every 15 min; a DNPH-free acidified control was treated identically. Proteins were then precipitated with 1.0 mL of ice-cold 20% (w/v) TCA, pelleted (11,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C), and washed three times with ethanol-ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v). The pellet was dissolved in 3.0 mL of 6 mol/L guanidine hydrochloride (in 20 mmol/L phosphate buffer, pH 6.5) at 37 °C for 30 min, clarified by centrifugation (8000 ×g, 10 min), and the absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 370 nm (ε = 22,000 M−1·cm−1). For sulfhydryl contents, 1.0 mL of the diluted sample was mixed with 9.0 mL of 0.2 mol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) containing 8 mol/L urea, 1% (w/v) SDS, and 3 mmol/L EDTA. A 4.0 mL aliquot was reacted with 0.5 mL of Ellman's reagent (10 mmol/L DTNB in 0.2 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) at 25 °C for 30 min in the dark, and the absorbance was read at 412 nm (ε = 13,600 M−1·cm−1). All spectrophotometric measurements were performed on a SpectraMax® i3x multi-mode reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China). Results were expressed as nmol/mg protein, and each determination was carried out in triplicate.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of MPs was determined by a method adapted from Wang et al. (2024) with slight modifications. MPs were diluted to 5 mg/mL with 20 mmol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.0). A 1.0 mL aliquot was mixed with 0.2 mL of BPB solution (1 mg/mL) and agitated for 10 min at room temperature. A control was prepared identically by replacing the protein dispersion with phosphate buffer. After centrifugation (8000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C), each supernatant was diluted ten-fold with the same buffer, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm on a SpectraMax® i3x multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Solubility measurement

The solubility of MPs was evaluated according to Wang, Zhou, et al. (2025) with slight modifications. MPs were dispersed at 5.0 mg/mL in 20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) containing 0.6 mol/L NaCl and stirred at 4 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C), the protein content of the supernatant was determined by the Bradford assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Solubility (%) was defined as the percentage of supernatant protein relative to the total protein in the initial dispersion. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

UV second-derivative absorption spectra

Conformational changes of MPs were characterized by UV–Vis spectroscopy based on Zhang et al. (2024) with minor modifications. The protein dispersion was adjusted to 1.0 mg/mL with 20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5). Absorption spectra were recorded from 250 to 350 nm at 200 nm/min on a UV-1780 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). The same phosphate buffer served as the baseline reference and was subtracted automatically. All spectra were collected in triplicate.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Tertiary structural changes of MPs were monitored by intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence following Jiang et al. (2017) with minor modifications. The MPs dispersion was adjusted to 0.5 mg/mL with 20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5). Fluorescence emission spectra were acquired on an LS55 fluorescence spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) with excitation at 280 nm, and emission scanned from 300 to 400 nm. Both excitation and emission slit widths were set to 5 nm, and spectra were recorded at 1 nm intervals. A buffer blank measured under identical settings was subtracted as the baseline. The maximum emission wavelength (λmax) and peak intensity were extracted from each spectrum. All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Zeta potential measurement

The Zeta potential of MPs was determined according to Wang, Zhou, et al. (2025) with slight modifications. MPs were diluted to 1.0 mg/mL with 20 mmol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) and equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min. The diluted dispersion was loaded into a disposable folded capillary cell (DTS1070) and measured on a Zetasizer Nano-ZS instrument (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. The refractive indices of protein and dispersant were set to 1.46 and 1.33, respectively. Each sample was measured three times, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Three independent fermentation batches or pork batches were used as biological replicates (n = 3), and replicate measurements were averaged before statistical analysis. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post hoc multiple comparisons in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0, SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using OriginPro 2023 software (OriginLab Corp., MA, USA). Graphs were conducted in GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Boston, USA) and OriginPro 2023 software (Origin Lab Corp., MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Changes in physicochemical properties during fermentation

The pH changes during L-ZS9 fermentation of H. cordata are presented in Fig. 1(a). The control group remained stable throughout fermentation (P > 0.05). In contrast, the L-ZS9 group showed a significant stepwise pH decrease (P < 0.05) during the first 3 days, from 3.95 to 3.70, which may be attributed to lactic acid production during the LAB logarithmic growth phase. After day 3, pH plateaued at ∼3.70 (P > 0.05), likely due to carbohydrate exhaustion and end-product inhibition.

Changes in physicochemical parameters during fermentation. (a). pH; (b). Colony content; (c). Total sugar content; (d). Reducing sugars content; (e). Soluble proteins. The same letter denotes no significant difference; different letters denote a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Viable LAB counts increased sharply from 6.0 to 8.2 log CFU/mL within the first 24 h and subsequently remained high through day 6 (Fig. 1b), consistent with the rapid acidification followed by pH stabilization.

Total sugar content decreased from 5.2 mg/mL (day 0) to 2.6 mg/mL (day 2) (P < 0.05), coinciding with rapid L-ZS9 growth (Fig. 1c). A transient increase on day 3 may reflect acid- and enzyme-mediated release of sugars from the H. cordata matrix. Reducing sugars showed a similar early decrease, from 1.6 to 0.35 mg/mL within 24 h, followed by partial recovery (Fig. 1d), suggesting a balance between sugar release and microbial consumption.

As shown in Fig. 1(d), reducing sugars decreased sharply from 1.6 mg/mL to 0.35 mg/mL within the first 24 h (P < 0.05), confirming that reducing sugars served as a preferred carbon source for L-ZS9. A partial recovery to approximately 0.70 mg/mL was noted from day 2 onward, after which the level remained relatively stable through day 6 (P > 0.05). This stabilization suggests a dynamic equilibrium between the continued hydrolysis of non-reducing sugars and polysaccharides and their concurrent consumption by L-ZS9.

Soluble protein content decreased from approximately 200 to 125 μg/mL during the first day (P < 0.05), possibly due to acid-induced protein precipitation and microbial nitrogen utilization (Emkani et al., 2023). Subsequent fluctuations may reflect the balance between proteolytic processes during fermentation and microbial utilization of soluble nitrogenous compounds (Fig. 1e).

Phenolic compounds

Total polyphenol content increased progressively throughout the 6-day fermentation (P < 0.05), rising from 1.02 to 2.50 mg GAE/mL, a 2.45-fold enhancement (Fig. 2a). The most rapid accumulation occurred within the first 2 days, temporally coinciding with active L-ZS9 proliferation and pH decline (Fig. 1a, b). Total flavonoid content followed a similar but delayed upward trend (Fig. 2b), peaking at 1.12 mg RE/mL on day 5, and declining slightly to 1.02 mg RE/mL at day 6 (P < 0.05). The delayed increase may reflect slower release or transformation of flavonoids during fermentation. Heatmap profiling of individual phenolics revealed that the overall enrichment was largely driven by isoquercitrin, rutin, and quercitrin, all of which increased markedly by day 6 (Fig. 2c). This overall increase in phenolic compounds is consistent with recent LAB fermentation studies on H. cordata and other plant matrices (Chou et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). However, the changes in individual phenolics differed among studies, suggesting that phenolic biotransformation depends on both the microbial strain and plant substrate.

Changes in Phenolic compounds during fermentation. (a). Total polyphenol content; (b). Total flavonoid content; (c). Individual phenolics contents. The same letter denotes no significant difference; different letters denote a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Quercitrin, rutin, and isoquercitrin accumulated substantially after fermentation. This may be attributed to the liberation of cell wall-bound flavonol glycosides through acid- and enzyme-mediated disruption of the H. cordata matrix (Hou et al., 2025). Hyperoside (quercetin-3-O-β-D-galactoside) declined during fermentation, which may reflect β-galactosidase-mediated cleavage of its β-galactosidic bond (Plaza-Vinuesa et al., 2023). The concomitant increase in quercetin content is consistent with this proposed deglycosylation route. Differences in sugar moieties and glycosidic linkages may explain the contrasting trends among quercetin glycosides, with isoquercitrin, rutin, and quercitrin accumulating through release from bound forms, while hyperoside is more readily hydrolyzed. Further enzyme activity analysis is needed to confirm the specific glycosidases involved. Catechin and epicatechin also decreased significantly during fermentation, possibly involving C-ring cleavage and A-ring fission of flavan-3-ols, as reported in the closely related La. plantarum (Pulido-Mateos et al., 2024). Chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid remained relatively stable, suggesting limited transformation under the present fermentation conditions. Overall, fermentation selectively altered the phenolic profile of H. cordata, with enrichment of several quercetin glycosides and possible formation of free aglycones.

Antioxidant activity

Four radical scavenging assays (DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl, and superoxide anion) were employed to assess the antioxidant capacity of H. cordata fermented by L-ZS9. These assays provide complementary information on radical-scavenging capacity through different reaction mechanisms (Xiao et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 3, all four indices increased significantly after 6 days of fermentation (P < 0.01). The most pronounced enhancement was observed for ABTS radical scavenging capacity, which approximately doubled from 75 to 150 μmol Trolox/L (P < 0.001), followed by superoxide anion scavenging (+68%, P < 0.001), DPPH (+63%, P < 0.01), and hydroxyl radical scavenging (+61%, P < 0.01). These improvements were consistent with the elevated total polyphenol and flavonoid contents (Fig. 2a, b) and the accumulation of quercitrin, rutin, and isoquercitrin (Fig. 2c). Pearson correlation analysis showed that isoquercitrin and rutin were strongly positively correlated with antioxidant activity (Table 2), suggesting that their enrichment may contribute to the enhanced antioxidant capacity after fermentation. Overall, L-ZS9 fermentation enhanced the antioxidant capacity of H. cordata. These assays characterize chemical antioxidant capacity, whereas the subsequent Fenton-induced MP oxidation model was used to examine whether this activity translated into protein protection.

Effect of FHCE on sulfhydryl and carbonyl content of MPs

Carbonyl accumulation reflects amino acid side-chain oxidation, whereas sulfhydryl depletion indicates disulfide cross-linking or sulfhydryl oxidative modification (Xiong, 2022). As shown in Fig. 4(a), the Fenton-treated Control exhibited the highest carbonyl level (3.70 ± 0.24 nmol/mg), approximately twice that of the unoxidized Blank (1.89 ± 0.11 nmol/mg, P < 0.05), confirming substantial oxidative damage. FHC6 supplementation progressively attenuated carbonyl accumulation in a concentration-dependent fashion. At 10% (v/v), the carbonyl content (2.20 ± 0.18 nmol/mg) was no longer significantly different from the Blank. Meanwhile, FHC0–10% showed weaker protection (2.50 ± 0.15 nmol/mg) than FHC6–10%, indicating that fermentation enhanced the protective effect of the extract against protein oxidation. This reduction in protein oxidation is consistent with previous studies showing that plant-derived phenolics can suppress MP oxidation and preserve protein structural properties (Guo et al., 2021; Zhangli et al., 2025).

Changes in total sulfhydryl content are presented in Fig. 4(b). Fenton oxidation reduced the sulfhydryl level from 46.8 ± 1.7 nmol/mg (Blank) to 25.9 ± 1.4 nmol/mg (Control), reflecting thiol oxidation to disulfide bonds and higher-order oxyacid derivatives. Notably, FHC6 did not restore sulfhydryl content; instead, it further decreased the detectable thiol level in a dose-dependent manner, reaching 14.2 ± 0.8 nmol/mg at 25%. Similar thiol-depleting effects have been documented for gallic acid and rosemary extract despite their inhibition of protein carbonylation (Zhou et al., 2019). Unlike the general protective trend reported for plant phenolics, the further decrease in detectable thiols in the FHC6-treated groups suggests that phenolic–protein interactions may also contribute in this fermented system. As shown in Fig. 2(c), fermentation markedly reshaped the phenolic profile of the fermented extract, with quercetin glycosides, such as isoquercitrin, rutin, and quercitrin, substantially enriched by day 6, while total polyphenol content approximately doubled. These quercetin derivatives, along with the stably present chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid, all possess ortho-dihydroxyl (catechol) moieties that can be oxidized to reactive ortho-quinones under the Fenton conditions employed. These quinones are known electrophiles capable of undergoing Michael addition with cysteine thiol groups (Li et al., 2016). Accordingly, the dose-dependent thiol depletion observed here is consistent with, though not direct proof of, covalent phenolic-protein adduct formation. The markedly greater thiol depletion in FHC6-treated groups compared to FHC0 may therefore be related to the higher abundance and broader diversity of catechol-type phenolics generated during fermentation.

Effect of FHCE on solubility of MPs

Solubility is an important indicator of the extent of protein aggregation. As shown in Fig. 5(a), Fenton oxidation caused a dramatic decline in MP solubility from 93.2 ± 1.1% (Blank) to 43.8 ± 1.5% (Control, P < 0.05), indicating severe radical-induced aggregation. FHC6 restored solubility in a dose-dependent manner, reaching 90.1 ± 0.8% at 25%, which was comparable to the Blank and surpassed Trolox (85.3 ± 1.2%). FHC0–10% provided only partial recovery (62.7 ± 1.8%), further highlighting the contribution of fermentation-derived bioactive components to protein structural protection.

Effect of FHCE on surface hydrophobicity of MPs

Surface hydrophobicity reflects the exposure of hydrophobic residues upon conformational disruption (Barallat-Pérez et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 5(b), Fenton oxidation elevated surface hydrophobicity from 78.9 ± 1.3 μg (Blank) to 96.2 ± 0.9 μg (Control), consistent with radical-mediated unfolding of the protein tertiary structure. FHC6 progressively reduced surface hydrophobicity with increasing concentration. Notably, FHC6–25% yielded a value of 72.1 ± 1.1 μg, which was significantly lower than the Blank (P < 0.05), suggesting structural effects beyond radical scavenging alone. This sub-native hydrophobicity may be partly related to interactions between hydrophilic phenolic moieties, particularly the glycosylated flavonols enriched in FHC6, and the protein surface. Quercetin glycosides such as rutin and isoquercitrin contain sugar residues that confer additional hydrophilicity. Their interaction with surface-exposed residues may shield underlying hydrophobic patches and reduce BPB probe access (Poojary et al., 2023). The negative correlation between solubility and surface hydrophobicity across all treatment groups further supports that the maintenance of a compact, less-exposed conformation is an important structural basis for preserved solubility under oxidative stress.

UV second-derivative absorption spectra analysis

UV–Vis spectra provide insight into the microenvironmental changes of aromatic amino acid residues and the formation of oxidation-derived chromophores (Zheng et al., 2022). Zero-order UV spectra of MPs are shown in Fig. 6(a, b). In both the sub-250 nm and ∼ 280 nm regions, absorbance followed the order Blank > FHC6–10% > FHC0–10% > Control. The overall decrease in absorbance of the Control is attributable primarily to the loss of soluble protein through oxidation-induced aggregation and precipitation, consistent with the dramatically reduced solubility of the Control group (44%, Fig. 6a). Furthermore, the disproportionately low absorption near 280 nm in the Control may reflect irreversible oxidative degradation of Trp and Tyr chromophores into products such as N-formylkynurenine and dityrosine, whose absorption shifts beyond 300 nm (Feng et al., 2023). FHC6–10% maintained higher absorbance than FHC0–10% across all wavelengths. All FHC6 groups exhibited similar spectral profiles in the 240–280 nm region and showed a dose-dependent increase (Fig. 6(b)).

Effect of H. cordata on the UV absorption spectra (A) and second-derivative spectroscopy (C) of oxidized MPs before and after fermentation. Effect of H. cordata at different doses on the UV absorption spectra (B) and second-derivative spectroscopy (D) of oxidized MPs.

Second-derivative spectra (Fig. 6(c, d)) resolved two peaks: Peak I (∼289 nm), contributed by both Tyr and Trp, and Peak II (∼294 nm), specific to Trp. The ratio R (= I/II) serves as an indicator of tertiary structural perturbation and microenvironmental polarity changes around aromatic residues (Zhang et al., 2024). Fenton oxidation increased R from 0.82 (Blank) to 0.91 (Control), reflecting substantial protein unfolding (Table 3). FHC0–10% showed an identical R value (0.91) to the Control, indicating that the unfermented extract provided negligible protection of the tertiary structure. In contrast, FHC6–10% significantly reduced R to 0.87, indicating that fermentation improved the ability of the extract to preserve MP conformation.

Among the FHC6 dose groups, a non-monotonic R-value profile was observed (Table 3). The R values across FHC6 groups ranged from 0.83 to 0.88 and did not follow a simple dose-dependent trend. At low concentrations (5%), radical scavenging appeared to predominate, preserving the native aromatic microenvironment. At higher concentrations (10–25%), phenolic–protein interactions may have progressively altered the local polarity around Trp and Tyr residues, counteracting the protective effect of radical scavenging to varying extents. Nevertheless, given the narrow range of variation, these differences should be interpreted with caution.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy is a highly sensitive probe for monitoring tertiary structural changes, primarily reflecting the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding Trp residues (Dorta et al., 2019). As depicted in Fig. 7(a), the Blank exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity (FI). Following Fenton oxidation (Control), the FI plummeted dramatically to ∼418 × 103 a.u. This fluorescence quenching may be driven by three synergistic mechanisms: (i) oxidative unfolding of the protein, which exposes originally buried Trp residues to the polar aqueous environment, leading to solvent quenching (Wang et al., 2019); (ii) oxidation-induced protein aggregation, which sterically masks the non-polar aromatic residues (Zhuang et al., 2023); and (iii) direct chemical degradation of the indole ring of Trp by ·OH attack (Bellmaine et al., 2020), which aligns with the loss of 280 nm absorption observed in the UV spectra (Fig. 6a). The addition of H. cordata fermented extracts effectively mitigated this fluorescence loss. FHC6–10% retained significantly higher FI than FHC0–10%, indicating that fermentation enhanced the ability of the extract to limit oxidative structural damage.

The effect of fermented H. cordata on the intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy of MPs before and after fermentation (a) and different doses (b). The same letter denotes no significant difference; different letters denote a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Notably, FHC6 at 10% to 15% provided protection comparable to 1 mg/mL Trolox (Fig. 7(b)). At the highest dose (25%), the FI reached 417,296 a.u., which was statistically indistinguishable from the native Blank group (P > 0.05). The greater fluorescence recovery observed with high-dose FHC6 than with the Trolox control under the tested conditions corroborates our earlier multi-spectral observations. These results suggest that FHC6 may limit Trp oxidation and help preserve the native hydrophobic core. This may involve interactions between phenolics and the protein surface, which could help limit excessive unfolding and aggregation.

Zeta potential analysis

Zeta potential serves as a crucial indicator of surface charge and electrostatic stability in protein dispersions (Gul et al., 2025). As depicted in Fig. 8, the unoxidized MPs (blank) exhibited a zeta potential of −12.4 mV. Following Fenton-like oxidation, the value for the control group shifted significantly toward neutrality (−7.0 mV). This reduction in the absolute magnitude of the zeta potential may be associated with oxidative modification of charged amino acid side chains and the subsequent protein aggregation, which spatially masks surface charges. The incorporation of FHC6 effectively mitigated this charge depletion, with FHC6–10% showing a significantly greater ability to maintain surface electronegativity than FHC0–10% (P < 0.05).

The effect of fermented H. cordata on the Zeta potential of MPs before and after fermentation. The same letter denotes no significant difference; different letters denote a significant difference (P < 0.05).

In the dose-dependent evaluation, the zeta potential recovered progressively as the FHC6 concentration increased. Notably, the intermediate doses (FHC6–15% and 20%) showed no significant difference compared to the positive control Trolox (P > 0.05). This suggests that their protective effect at these concentrations may be largely associated with radical scavenging, similar to Trolox, thereby preventing oxidation-induced protein aggregation. However, the highest dose (FHC6–25%) exhibited a distinct behavior, showing a greater recovery than the Trolox control under the tested conditions (P < 0.05) and restoring the zeta potential to the blank level (−12.3 mV). As discussed earlier, this charge-recovery effect may be related not only to antioxidant capacity but also to phenolic-protein interactions. Consistent with the thiol depletion observed in the FHC6–25% group, interactions between FHC6-derived phenolics (e.g., quercetin glycosides and phenolic acids) and the protein surface may introduce additional phenolic hydroxyl groups. This may contribute to the net negative charge on the protein surface, leading to increased intermolecular electrostatic repulsion. This enhanced electrostatic stabilization is in agreement with the concurrent recovery of protein solubility and the decrease in surface hydrophobicity. Further characterization of specific oxidation and cross-linking products may provide additional mechanistic information in future studies.

Conclusion

L-ZS9 fermentation significantly altered the phenolic profile of H. cordata, increasing total polyphenol content 2.45-fold and promoting the enrichment of isoquercitrin, rutin, and quercitrin, while antioxidant activity increased by 61–100%. Correlation analysis showed that isoquercitrin and rutin were most closely associated with the enhanced antioxidant activity. The fermented broth exhibited dose-dependent protection against Fenton-induced myofibrillar protein oxidation, as shown by reduced carbonyl formation, preserved protein solubility, decreased surface hydrophobicity, and restored intrinsic fluorescence. Taken together, the physicochemical and spectroscopic results suggest that ROS scavenging and phenolic-protein interactions may jointly contribute to this protective effect (Fig. 9), although the specific glycosidases involved in phenolic transformation and phenolic-protein covalent adducts still require direct verification. In addition, organic acids and other metabolites in the fermented broth may also contribute to the observed effects. Future studies will further evaluate the protective effect of the fermented broth in real meat products during processing and storage, as well as its stability, sensory impact, and feasibility for scale-up. Overall, L-ZS9-fermented H. cordata shows potential as a clean-label natural antioxidant for muscle foods.
