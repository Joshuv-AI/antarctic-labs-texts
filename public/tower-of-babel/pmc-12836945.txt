Sustainable strategies for the mid-day meal program: a qualitative study in a community school of Nepal
Authors: Manisha Bhattarai, Yadu Ram Upreti, Bhagwan Aryal, Bhimsen Devkota, Nisha Makhim Subba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies confirm a high prevalence of malnutrition among school children and adolescents in Nepal, indicating that Mid-day Meal Program (MDMP) could significantly address these issues. The Nepalese government launched the MDMP since 2020 to reduce malnutrition and improve educational and health outcomes among school children. This study explores sustainable practices for MDMP in a community school of Nepal, building on a participatory action research (PAR)-based study conducted by a team of PhD researchers under the NORHED Rupantaran Project (2016-2023). MethodsPractice-oriented qualitative case study design was conducted in a community school in Chitwan District. Participants included basic school students, their parents, School Management Committee (SMC) members, School Mid-day Meal Management Committee (SMDMMC) members, and canteen service providers. Data were collected through focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs) and analyzed them employing a thematic analysis framework.ResultsAlthough the federal government’s MDMP covered early to grade five students in the community schools nationwide, it has been extended to grade eighth in the study school through collaboration with all school community and the local government. The federal government has provided funds to implement the MDMP for the students of early grades to grade five. However, the study school was able to extend the MDMP upto grade eight. The results indicate that teachers' involvement in the MDMP, formation of the SMDMMC, local government subsidy, collaboration with all school stakeholders, and plans for sustainability are key actionable strategies for sustaining the MDMP in the community school.ConclusionThe findings of the study indicate that the school can implement a tripolar cost-sharing and teacher-supported service model to sustain the MDMP in the community schools, particularly from rural areas. The study suggests longitudinal research focusing to ascertain continuity of the culture of collaboration and active participation among the school stakeholders and local government to achieve both health and educational outcomes of school mid-day meal intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01225-9.

Introduction

Evidence from different studies suggests the prevalence of malnutrition among school children and adolescents (SCA) in Nepal is alarming [1–3]. Nepal Demographic Health Survey (NDHS)−2022 indicates that 25%, 8%, and 19% children under five years were stunted, wasted, and underweight, respectively. The study also highlights that almost six out of ten (59%) male adolescents and seven out of ten (71%) female adolescents were undernourished. The prevalence was similar among adolescents living in urban and rural Nepal [4]. A scoping review of South Asian countries, including Nepal, reveals that nearly one-third (30.3%) of school-age children (5–19 years) suffer from thinness, 43.6% have anemia, and 2.9% are overweight [2]. A study focused on eastern Nepal also revealed that nearly half (44.0%) of the SCA were underweight [3]. Another study, covering three ecological belts with 22 districts, also demonstrates that 27.3%, 23%, and 16.6% of the SCA from grades 6–8 were underweight, stunted, and thin, respectively [1].

Nepal initiated a school feeding program, a significant school-based initiative providing free meals to community school students, during the Rana Regime to enhance academic performance. In the 1950 s, free midday meals were provided to underprivileged students in government schools (now commonly referred to as community schools) in the Kathmandu Valley. With the support from the World Food Program (WFP), Nepal launched Midday Meal Program (MDMP) in 1996, expanding to 30 districts by 2009 [5]. Continuing this, from the fiscal year 2020/21, the program expanded to cover all 77 districts [1, 6]. The program was expected to benefit 2.8 million children, as schools in Karnali Province were receiving NPR 20 per person per day, while other provinces received NPR 15 [7]. As of 2025, the MDMP has reached over 2.8 million students, accounting for approximately 4.6% of the total education budget and ranking as the sector’s third-largest investment [8].

The evidence has shown that MDMP is highly effective in boosting school attendance, lowering dropout rates, and increasing participation in academic and extracurricular activities [1, 6, 9]. Furthermore, it promotes healthy eating habits, supports local agriculture, fosters social inclusion, and raises awareness about sanitation, leading to improved overall health [10]. An intervention-based study in a community school showcased that the MDMP resulted in several positive outcomes, including developed positive interest towards coming to school; increased regular school attendance; decreased unhealthy snacking behaviors, meal skipping, and returning home at lunch time; reduced malnutrition status; and improved academic performances and results [7].

The present study focuses on the sustainability of the MDMP in a community school, drawing on the reflection of the first author’s fieldwork to complete her master’s dissertation. The NORHED Rupantaran Project was active in the study school from 2016 to 2023 (http://kusoed.edu.np/transform/). The study was conducted in a community school in Chitwan, where the MDMP was implemented in collaboration with the Rupantaran project prior to the federal government’s introduction of MDMP at community schools. The second author, supervisor of the first author, conducted participatory action research (PAR) as part of his PhD project from 2018 to 2023 on behalf of Rupantaran [7]. Following the first author’s preliminary field reflection, though the school’s attendance was improved due to the MDMP in the study school, the sustainability of the program remained challenging due to various factors such as insufficient funding provision, poor community participation, minimal parental and teachers’ involvement in the MDMP, prevalence of high junk food consumption among students. It was crucial to address the issues above to ensure the program’s continuity. The rationale for this study arises from the urgent need to address the sustainability challenges of the MDMP, which undermine its success despite its broad reach. This study aims to fill a gap in understanding effective strategies, given the limited empirical evidence on local adaptations. The present study highlights how the school stakeholders have adopted sustainable strategies for MDMP in a community school in Nepal. Given this context, the study aimed to demystify the best practices of the school to ensure the sustainability of the MDMP.

Methods

Study design

The study employed a practice-oriented qualitative case study design [11] to explore the sustainable practices of MDMP within a single community school. The design was chosen for this study to explore context-specific sustainable practices in a school. The design allowed us to generate actionable solutions amidst a knowledge gap in the local context. The design emphasizes collaboration with school stakeholders to elicit their lived experiences, focusing on the strategies they have adopted to enhance the sustainability of the MDMP. For data collection, the design facilitated rich, context-specific insights through interviews, discussions, and observations. In analysis, it supported thematic exploration with practitioner input, enabling the creation of credible and actionable strategies, such as community funding and stakeholder participation models, through the iterative triangulation of diverse data sources.

Research site and participants

The study was conducted in a community school located in Khairahani Municipality, Chitwan District, Bagmati Province of Nepal. The research site and participants were selected in consideration of the study protocol for the Rupantaran project, adhering to criteria such as co-educational, government-funded, and multi-ethnic, with students hailing from indigenous and disadvantaged groups. Another criterion was to have the teachers open their minds to the study’s objectives and work together. To be selected as a research school, there needed to be the active presence of the committees and volunteer clubs: namely, the school management committee (SMC), parent-teacher association (PTA), Red Cross Society and Eco-Club and a lack of facilities such as ecological sanitation toilets and sufficient area for school gardening [12]. Additionally, the research objective guided the selection of the school for the sustainability of the MDMP.

Basic school students (from grades 6 to 8), parents, SMC members, the school mid-day meal management committee (SMDMMC), canteen service providers, and local government representatives participated in the study (see Table 1).

The participants involved in the study

In Nepal, basic school education spans grades one to eight. For this study, seven boys and seven girls from grades six to eight were selected, as they were capable of sharing their opinions and experiences. With the cooperation of the class teachers, students were chosen based on voluntary consent. Two boys and two girls were selected from each of the sixth and seventh grades, whereas three boys and three girls were chosen from the eighth grade. Similarly, two parents from each grade were invited to participate in the study; unfortunately, two of them were unable to attend the discussions. The head teacher (HT) then reached out via telephone to encourage them to participate. Seven SMC members, including the ward chairperson, were invited by the HT via telephone while explaining the purpose of the study. During the focus group discussion (FGD), all the teachers participated, except for one who was on leave. Of the ten SMDMMC members, nine were involved, following the HT’s request for their participation. Among the five canteen service providers, only the leader, who managed the canteen, was selected as a participant, as the others were occupied with operational duties in the canteen.

Data collection methods and procedures

This study employed both FGDs and in-depth interviews (IDIs) to solicit information following the guiding research questions. FGDs were conducted with students, parents, SMC members, and SMDMMC members. However, FGD could not provide enough information to reach data saturation based on the research questions. Thus, IDI was conducted among key stakeholders, including the HT, canteen service provider, and the ward chairperson of the local government. An interview guide for both FGD and IDI was developed (see supplementary file) to obtain the information. Initially, the guide was drafted in the local language (Nepali) and subsequently translated into English. The first author prepared the guide, which the second and fourth authors reviewed.

Although the first author initially did not plan to conduct IDIs, she incorporated them for two key reasons. First, school stakeholders provided limited insights into the school’s plans for MDMP sustainability during FGDs. This led the first author to conduct separate IDIs with the HT and ward chairperson to gather detailed perspectives on future sustainability strategies. Second, the canteen personnel were unable to participate in the FGD due to a busy schedule, necessitating an IDI to incorporate their input. After the first author became familiar with the school through repeated field visits alongside the second and fifth authors, she conducted FGDs and IDIs with stakeholders on the school premises, ensuring no disruption of the regular classes. At least two groups (one in the morning shift and another in the day shift) participated in discussions and interviews each day. FGDs and IDIs were conducted in a quiet room (the library for students and the SMC room for the rest of the groups) after participants were informed of the study’s purpose. The FGDs and IDIs were initiated with a warm-up (introductory) question such as: ‘How do you feel while sharing your thoughts about the midday meal program of your school?’ This start-up strategy put the participants at ease for further discussion. During this, the probing was frequently used, which enabled them to share their experiences in a natural flow. Each discussion was recorded using both a sound recorder device and the personal cell phone of the first author. Audio recordings of each discussion were made after obtaining consent. However, in cases where audio recordings were not feasible, detailed notes were taken. The second and fifth authors helped in detailing the notes.

Data analysis

Upon completion of each FGD and IDI, the first author meticulously reviewed the audio recordings and transcribed them into the local language (Nepali). The second author verified the accuracy of the transcription by listening to the audio recordings and cross-checking the transcribed texts. Following the six-step thematic analysis framework [13] and the approach outlined by Clarke and Braun [14], the data were analyzed, providing a structured process to identify and interpret significant patterns. Adhering to this framework, the first author read and re-read the transcripts until becoming thoroughly familiar with the data. The initial codes were generated through critical analysis of the texts. The similar codes were combined and organized into categories, reducing the initial number of codes. During the coding process, both deductive and inductive approaches were employed. Subsequently, the similar codes were grouped to form the basic themes. Initially, eight themes were identified, which were later reviewed and refined into five after consultation with the second and third authors, as presented in the result section. Finally, themes were redefined and renamed into three, as presented in the discussion section. The codes, categories, and themes, along with selected quotations, were translated into English (see Table 3). The translated quotations underwent professional editing by an English language editor. The process of transcribing audio tapes, developing codebooks, categories, and themes was conducted manually using a spreadsheet matrix table.

Quality standard

The first author followed the criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability to maintain the quality standard of the study [15]. Credibility was maintained through expert consultation during the development of FGD and IDI guidelines. The study site and participants were purposively selected. A thick description of the study process was provided to maintain the transferability of the results. Dependability was maintained using the same guidelines in each FGD and IDI. For confirmability, the second and fifth authors thoroughly reviewed the data analysis process. The entire research process has been explained in the study [16].

Ethical considerations

The Health and Population Education Department, Central Department of Education, TU, and NORHED Rupantaran Project approved this study. This study has received an ethical approval clearance letter from the Ethical Review Board of the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) with reference number 733/2018. Administrative approval for the study was obtained from the school before involving the participants. The first author visited the school to familiarize herself and establish a rapport with the school administration and participants before data collection. The purpose of the study was shared with the participants when they were asked about their availability for the FGDs and IDIs. Harmony and collegiality were maintained throughout the process across institutions and among participants to create a natural setting before employing them in the FGD [17]. Both verbal and written consents were obtained before involving the participants in the study.

The study maintained caution in avoiding moral dilemmas, obtained consent from participants to involve them in the study, informed them about the study’s purpose, ensured non-biased responses, kept their answers confidential, avoided harm, and protected their rights to privacy. The school and participants’ names were anonymized to ensure data quality, honesty, and sincerity. This approach ensured the confidentiality and protection of participants’ rights.

Sustainable mid-day meal practice

The MDMP at the study school was operated through two models. The first, “Revised Regular MDMP”, served students from ECDE to fifth grades, funded by the federal government’s regular budget with additional budget from the local government. The second, “Collaborative MDMP”, supported students of grades 6 to 8 through a three-way cost-sharing program among the school, parents, and local government. The collaborative model was established through an agreement to extend the MDMP to upper basic level students (Grades 6–8) after recognizing the need for its expansion. In this regard, the chairperson of the SMC said:We provide NPR 20 (1 rupee equals 0.0071 USD, as of 25th September 2025) per student from grades ECDE to five under the budgetary provision of the Federal Government, with the local government adding NPR 5 per student. However, for grades 6–8 students, we offer meals at a rate of NPR 30 per student under the collaborative MDMP, where parents, the school, and the local government provide NPR 10 per student (FGD, SMC Members).

The MDMP was successfully managed through the collaborative efforts of the parents, the school, and the local Government. Acknowledging that government funding alone might not fully cover the students’ meal costs, the school implemented several strategies, including collaboration with school stakeholders, formation of SMDMMC, teachers’ involvement in the MDMP, and local government support for the MDMP to ensure the program’s sustainability.

Collaboration with school stakeholders

A partnership-based MDMP was implemented in the school to ensure the long-term sustainability of the program. In doing so, the school organized frequent meetings with the SMC, SMDMMC, and parents. Teachers actively participated in preparing a school meal menu in collaboration with canteen service providers and parents. The school collected NPR 10 (approximately 0.071 USD) from each student. Additionally, the local government (ward office) agreed to provide funds of NPR 10 per student, and with the school’s contribution of NPR 10, each student in grades 6–8 could receive a meal worth NPR 20. In this regard, the SMDMMC coordinator said,The federal government provides an amount of NPR 15 (approximately 0.11 USD) per student for the meals of students in grades one to five. The local government provides NPR 5 per student, making the total NPR 20 (approximately 0.14 USD) per student. However, in our school, the MDMP is extended up to grade eight (FGD, SMDMMC Members).

The SMDMMC developed the school menu in line with the federal government’s recommended plan [18], consulting canteen service providers to ensure feasibility. Teachers’ involvement in the MDMP

The results of the study reveal that the teacher-supported model proved to be effective in implementing MDMP in the school. The teachers from ECDE to Grade five provided the young students who need support from others with healthy and hygienic meals. The teachers guided students to walk in line from the classroom to the canteen, where they washed their hands at the water station before entering the dining hall. Once the teachers complete their responsibility of feeding the students, they return to the canteen for their lunch. A teacher, who also served as the primary school-in-charge, said that all teachers from ECDE to grade five were actively involved in the school feeding program.We have established a daily routine for teachers to engage them in the MDMP. Connecting to this, the SMC chair stated,We plan to employ a local workforce, prioritizing parents from our community, to operate the school-managed canteen. By sourcing and collecting all the necessary items for meal preparation ourselves, we can effectively reduce the costs. This will maintain both the quantity and quality of the school meals (FGD, SMC Members).

Discussion

The study’s findings revealed that the school has implemented two models of MDMP to provide meals for basic school children. The first, ‘Revised Regular MDMP’, serves students from ECDE to Grade five, funded by the federal government’s regular budget with supplementary contributions from the local government. The second, ‘Collaborative MDMP’, supports students from Grades six to eight through a three-way cost-sharing and teacher-supported service model involving the school, parents, and the local government. The study revealed that the school has adopted five long-term practices, including collaboration with school stakeholders, formation of the SMDMMC, teachers’ involvement, local government support, and plans for future sustainability. These include the operation of a school-managed canteen utilizing products from the school garden, the engagement of secondary students from grades eleven and twelve majoring in hotel management as volunteers, and reinforcement of parental involvement in the school’s feeding program. The discussion section is organized into three themes.

Stakeholders’ collaboration

Stakeholders at all levels should coordinate, work together, and keep up their efforts to successfully execute and extend the collaborative MDMP [19]. The results of the present study showed that collaborative approach to managing the MDMP demonstrated active engagement of stakeholders at multiple levels. This is particularly important in fostering a sense of ownership and accountability for the program’s success [17]. Supporting our results, there are some evidence of collaboration that sustains the MDMP at schools. A study in India suggests that public-private partnerships (PPPs) have been an effective model for maintaining the MDMP in public schools. The study further reveals that the state government’s implementation of the MDMP program through the PPP Model, with support from NGOs in urban regions and Community-Based Organizations (CBOs) in rural areas, remains effective in sustaining the MDMP [13]. It is contended that a long-term MDMP can be effectively implemented through a collaborative model involving school stakeholders [20]. Furthermore, this collaborative model could be purposed to prevent food insecurity in the community. Additionally, it is asserted that food insecurity is linked to unhealthy and unsustainable eating behaviors among people [21].

Similarly, a study conducted in Chitwan, Nepal, highlights that parental engagement in the MDMP is crucial for sustaining the school feeding program in public schools. The study reveals that parental engagement can enhance the school’s MDPM by contributing to meal preparation, serving students, and cleaning the dishes, with support from the school’s non-teaching staff. These collaborative efforts improved the dietary habits of schoolchildren, utilized local agricultural products in the school, and also strengthened parental involvement in the school [22]. A study conducted in the Eastern region of Nepal also highlights the importance of parental involvement in the successful implementation of the school health and nutrition program [23]. Another study conducted among elementary school children in Candon, Philippines, also supports the findings of our study. The study reveals that active parental involvement in the school feeding program fosters a positive environment, promotes healthy habits, enhances student well-being, encourages community engagement and support, and cultivates a culture of collaboration among school stakeholders [24]. Another study conducted in disadvantaged areas of Greece yielded mixed results: although the school and students perceived that the role of parents remained effective in promoting healthy dietary behaviors among school students, the parents themselves perceived that their roles were challenging in promoting a healthy diet [25]. A study conducted among basic school students in Sindhupalchok and Bardiya districts, Nepal, found that robust community ownership and accountability mechanisms could enhance the quality of meal alternatives in the MDMP [10]. The study further indicates that parentals involvement in the MDMP plays a crucial role in ensuring the program’s sustainability. Collaboration among the school management team, parents, community people, and the local government could leverage the sustainability of the MDMP at the community school [17]. It is argued that effective collaboration among local government, parents, and schools is essential for maintaining the MDMP’s efficiency and long-term sustainability.

Teachers’ involvement

The results of the present study also suggest that teachers actively participated in the MDMP by involving themselves in serving meals and promoting hygienic and healthy dietary behaviors among students. The result further indicates that the school established the SMDMMC to mobilize teachers in the execution of the MDMP effectively. The committee serves to plan, control, and execute the MDMP regularly without disrupting their regular classroom schedule. Additionally, the school mobilizes a school nurse to assess the quality of meals and provide feedback to the committees for improvement. In this process, the nurse inspects the food items used in meal preparation, evaluates the ingredients, and tastes the meals to ensure quality before serving them to students. She maintains a food observation logbook to monitor quality standards. Consistent with our findings, a study conducted in western Nepal also highlights the role of teachers in implementing the school midday meal program alongside the SMC [26]. A study conducted in Rajasthan, India, at primary schools closely corroborates our findings. The study reveals that teachers play a crucial role in serving meals to school children, where they are tasked with purchasing fruits and distributing them every week [27]. A study conducted in rural Tanzania also aligns with our findings, emphasizing the influential role of teachers in the school feeding program [28]. A study conducted in a rural area of the USA suggests that teachers can play a significant role in the school lunch program and possess considerable influence as change agents within the school. The study also highlights the supportive role of school administration in enabling teachers to fulfill their responsibilities effectively [29]. It is contended that teachers’ involvement in schools’ MDMP not only facilitates serving meals but also promotes hygienic behaviors and healthy dietary behaviors among students.

Government role

Although the federal government allocates NPR 15 per student to local governments for the MDMP, this amount is insufficient to provide midday meals in adequate quantity and quality. To address this shortfall, the local government in the study school supplemented funding by contributing an additional NPR 5 per head for lower basic students and NPR 10 per upper basic student to cover the costs. The findings indicate that the MDMP struggles with inadequate federal funding, requiring local government supplements to ensure meal quality and quantity. This highlights the need for a collaborative grant from the local government to effectively sustain the national priority program. The findings from the previous study conducted in Chitwan also corroborate our results. The study highlights the crucial role of local government in supporting the MDMP among children from disadvantaged and marginalized communities [22]. A survey conducted in Kerala, India, also reveals that the role of the local government is crucial to sustain the MDMP at public schools. The study highlights that the MDMP in Kerala’s public schools managed by the local government and assisted by SMCs, parent groups, village authorities, and school boards developed healthy eating practices, including encouraging students to attend school, lowering student hunger in the classroom, encouraging growth, washing hands, and eating food [30]. Another study, conducted in Gujarat [31] and Sikkim [32] also highlights the crucial role of the state government in protecting children from classroom hunger and improving school enrollment and attendance through the MDMP. A review-based study in India also highlights the role of public leaders, including local governments, in sustaining the MDMP in public schools [33]. The collaboration between federal and local governments is essential for maintaining the MDMP in the schools, providing consistent funding and customized implementation. This partnership enhances nutritional outcomes and increases school attendance, particularly in rural and marginalized regions [34].

Limitations of the study

Despite providing actionable strategies for sustaining the MDMP in a community school, several potential limitations warrant consideration. The reliance on a single school may restrict the generalizability of findings to other regions or school types with diverse socio-economic and/or cultural contexts. The second author, who had previously supervised the first author and collaborated with school stakeholders for three years, fostered a supportive environment for the study; however, this prior relationship may have influenced participant responses. Additionally, the study’s emphasis on current practices and plans might not fully address external factors, such as variable government funding or unexpected logistical challenges, which could affect sustainability efforts. Furthermore, the pre-existing connection between the authors and the field site may warrant a reflexivity statement given prior PAR at the same school and the potential for role duality to shape data collection and interpretation.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to demystify the best practices of a community school in Nepal, ensuring the sustainability of the nationally prioritized MDMP, with a particular focus on establishing long-term practices for providing healthy meals in a community school. The study’s findings indicate that the MDMP can be sustainably implemented in collaboration with school stakeholders. This collaboration could strengthen the opportunity to involve a multitude of stakeholders, including teachers, parents, school administration, and local government, in sustaining the federal government’s MDMP. The study’s findings indicate that the school can implement a three-way cost-sharing and teacher-supported service model to maintain the MDMP. The results of the study suggest that teachers’ involvement in the MDMP, the formation of the SMDMMC, local government subsidies, collaboration with the entire school stakeholders, and plans for sustainability are key actionable strategies for sustaining the MDMP. The study suggests that further research focusing on PAR methodology is promosing to foster a culture of collaboration and active participation among school stakeholders and the local government, aiming to achieve both health and educational outcomes through school-based interventions.
