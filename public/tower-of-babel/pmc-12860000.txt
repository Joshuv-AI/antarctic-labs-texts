Tuberculosis treatment outcomes and associated factors among patients on anti- TB treatment in Nekemte public health facilities, oromia regional State, Western Ethiopia
Authors: Robsan Gudeta Getachew, Rut Oljira, Ayantu Getahun, Lemane Dereje, Milion Gebrewold Abdi, Tadesse Tolossa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) has been recognized as one of the major public health problems in Ethiopia over the past 50 years. It is the leading cause of morbidity, the third leading cause of hospitalization, and the second leading cause of death. Successful treatment outcomes are crucial in order to improve the Tuberculosis management program. This study is aimed to assess treatment outcomes of TB and the factors that influence the observed treatment outcomes in TB patients of Nekemte public health facility.MethodAn institutional-based cross-sectional study design was conducted in Nekemte town public health care facilities from November 1 to November 30, 2021. The data were collected by using a checklist from TB registration logbook, patient medical cards, and laboratory requests. The data was entered into Epi data version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 24. The variables associated with the treatment outcomes were selected using bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis. The level of significance was expressed by the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). A variable with a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultSuccessful treatment outcomes of the TB patients were 77.3% (95%CI: 74.6, 79.8). Urban residency (AOR = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.8–4.34), absence previous treatment history (AOR = 3.5, 95% CI: 1.34–9.164), absence of history of comorbidity (AOR = 13.9, 95% CI:7.1-27.33), and patients who had DOTs supporter (AOR = 7.7, 95% CI: 3.9 -15.11) were significantly associated with successful TB treatment outcomes.Conclusion and recommendationThe study found that TB treatment success rates were below national and global targets. Factors such as urban residency, absence of previous TB treatment, absence of comorbidity, DOTs supporter was found to be the significant predictors of TB treatment outcome. To improve success rates, it is recommended to expand access to trained DOTs supporters, particularly in rural areas, and prioritize monitoring of high-risk populations, including patients with a history of TB treatment, additionally, educate family members on supporting treatment adherence and addressing co-morbidities through integrated care models will contribute to improved treatment adherence and success.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a treatable and preventable disease caused by bacteria (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) that primarily affects the lungs [1]. Each year, approximately 10 million individuals are infected by tuberculosis, with reported cases reaching 7.1 million through national TB programs. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, tuberculosis stood as the primary infectious agent leading to mortality, surpassing even HIV/AIDS [1–3]. It has been recognized as one of the major public health problems in Ethiopia over the past 50 years. It is the leading cause of morbidity, third leading cause of hospitalization, and second leading cause of death [4].

In 2019, an estimated 10.0 million people contracted tuberculosis globally, with adults comprising 88% of those affected and children making up the remaining 12%. Sub-Saharan Africa shares approximately 2.5 million cases of TB in 2017, resulting in 665,000 deaths attributed to the illness [5, 6].

Ethiopia has adopted and implemented strategies such as Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) number three, which aims to improve everyone’s health and well-being, with one of the goals being to end the global TB and HIV epidemics by 2030 and have been using the directly observed therapy short-course (DOTS) since 1990 [7]. In order to reduce morbidity and mortality from TB and to stop transmission, early identification and treatment are essential .The national tuberculosis control program now has 100% geographic coverage, and DOTS is available in 92% of public hospitals and health institutions which, helps to ensure, the right drugs are taken at the right time for the full duration of treatment, and has a paramount importance in increasing TB treatment success rate [8]. Treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) has improved with the introduction of shorter, all-oral regimens such as BPaL and its variations (BPaL/M), as outlined by (WHO, 2022). These approaches, now adopted in countries like Ethiopia, shorten therapy duration, enhance patient outcomes, and overcome challenges linked to extended and injectable treatments to improve treatment success rate [9].

Despite the implementation of DOTS program and implementation of all oral short regimens in Ethiopia, different reports have indicated the existence of challenges in improving TB treatment outcomes. Social and economic factors are directly related to TB epidemiology, which makes prevention, treatment, and control more difficult [1]. Early diagnosis and effective therapy can avoid many TB-related morbidities, although managing these conditions is not always straightforward [10]. A crucial component of the therapy is assessing the effectiveness of the TB treatment and determining the elements that contribute to it. Although the success percentage of the disease’s treatment differs from nation to nation, 83% has been observed internationally. In the African Region the success rate for therapy was 79% [11].

Ethiopia is, subsequently, profoundly endemic for TB, where it is the primary cause of illness and mortality. Tracking treatment progress and identifying elements that have a direct impact on the whole treatment process require an understanding of the treatment result and the factors that contribute to it. The effectiveness of a health care system’s TB treatment is gauged by the findings of treatment outcome studies. The epidemiological surveillance system should ideally routinely monitor the results of all patients’ treatments. This would enable the detection and correction of system flaws prior to an increase in the frequency and percentage of resistant isolates. Therefore, evaluations of treatment results and specific contributing reasons to poor treatment outcomes are crucial in order to improve the TB management program. Therefore, this study is aimed at assessing treatment outcomes of TB and the factors that influence the observed treatment outcomes in TB patients at Nekemte public health facilities.

Methods

Study area and design

Facility based retrospective cross-sectional study was employed in Nekemte town public health care facilities. Nekemte is the capital city of the East Wollega zone which is found at 331 km from Addis Ababa. There are four healthcare facilities in Nekemte Town. These are one teaching referral hospital (WURH), one specialized hospital (NSH), and two health centers; (Nekemte and Cheleleki Health Center). These two hospitals serve as a referral hospital for a population of the western part of the country and provide healthcare services for more than 2 million people in the surrounding area. The study period was from 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2021 and the data was retrieved from two hospitals and two health centers from November 1 to November 30, 2021.

Population

All TB patients who were placed under DOTS and started TB treatment follow-up in Nekemte public health care facilities from 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2021 were the source population. All patients over the age of 18 who began DOTS treatment follow-up and their treatment outcome recorded were included in the study. Patients who move in from another health facility without complete information or whose card was not available were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

A total of 951 patients received TB treatment during the study period from July 1, 2017, to June 30, 2021. The study included all patient records from public health facilities in Nekemte town. The study population was identified using patient medical records and the TB registration logbooks. Out of the 951 records, 32 were unavailable, 45 were excluded due to patients being under 18 years of age, and 74 were excluded because of incomplete information, such as missing TB treatment outcomes. Consequently, 800 patient records with complete data were included in the final analysis. These records encompassed profiles of all patients who received TB treatment follow-up and profiles of all patients enrolled to TB treatment follow-up during the specified period.

Data collection tool and procedure

The data for this study were collected using a checklist developed by reviewing similar literature and based on the availability of information in the TB registration logbook, patient medical records, and laboratory request forms. The checklist was structured into three sections: demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory-related details. Data collection focused on TB registration logbooks and patients’ medical records from public health facilities in Nekemte town.

The outcome variable, successful treatment outcome was confirmed by reviewing medical records or TB registration logbooks, which were maintained by health professionals working in TB clinics. Data were gathered from two hospitals and two health centers in Nekemte town by a team of seven trained nurses, each holding a Bachelor of Science degree and experienced in data collection from TB clinics outside the study area. The data collection process was overseen by two trained supervisors who ensured the quality and accuracy of the collected information.

Operational definitions and terms

Successful treatment outcomes

Include cases classified as “cured” and “treatment completed” [9].

Unsuccessful treatment outcomes

Include cases classified as “treatment failure”, “transferred out”, “lost to follow-up (LTFU ’’and “died”) [9].

Cured

Patients who completed with the negative bacteriological result at the end of the treatment.

Treatment completed

Patients who completed but without bacteriological results at the end of the treatment.

Died

Patient who died from any cause during the course of the treatment.

Treatment failure

Patients whose sputum smear or culture is positive at 5 months or later during treatment.

Lost to follow- up

Patients who did not start treatment or whose treatment was interrupted for two or more consecutive months.

Transferred out

Patients transferred to another recording and reporting unit, with their treatment outcomes remaining unknown.

Project workers

Individuals actively involved in implementing the TB project, including field staff, supervisors, healthcare professionals, and other personnel tasked with carrying out project-related activities [9].

Congregated workers

Individuals employed or residing in environments with a higher likelihood of TB transmission due to close contact and frequent interactions, such as workplaces, correctional facilities, dormitories, or other shared living or working spaces [9].

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, all variables’ availability was verified before collecting data from the TB registration book. A pre-test was conducted in Nekemte specialized hospital on 5% of patient records to assess question clarity, terms, and time needed. Seven trained nurses handled data collection. To prevent bias, data collectors were recruited from outside the study setting. Two supervisors oversaw data collection. One day of training were provided to collectors and supervisors. The principal investigator checked checklist completeness and consistency daily during data collection.

Methods of data analysis

Data was entered into the computer using Epi-data software version 3.2 and exported to SPSS software version 22 for analysis. Before analysis, data were cleaned and edited by using simple frequencies and cross-tabulation. Appropriate descriptive statistics such as mean (with standard deviation), median (with interquartile range [IQR]), and frequency (with percentages) were used to describe the study population in relation to relevant variables. Binary logistic regression was used to calculate the crude odds ratio (COR) with a 95% confidence interval. Each independent variable with a p-value ≤ 20% (0.2) was entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to determine the presence of a statistically significant association with the outcome variable. Multicollinearity among selected independent variables was checked through variance inflation factor (VIF). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The assumption on the fitness of goodness of the final model was checked by Hosmer and Lemeshow test.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ethical committee of the Wallaga University Institute of health Science, Institutional Health Research Ethics Review Committee (IHRERC). Letter of the permission was obtained from the Wallaga University Public health department to get permission from Nekemte town health Office. Then, a supportive letter was obtained from the town health office to get permission from the administrative office of the public health care facilities. Informed voluntary, written, and signed consent was obtained from each head of the healthcare facility. To assure confidentiality, the name of the patients was not recorded in the data extraction check list rather the identifiers of patients participated in the study was coded and data was kept secured. The recorded data was not accessed by a third person, except the principal investigator.

A waiver was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) that reviewed the proposal, indicating that the study meets the established criteria for exemption.

Result

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 800 registered TB cases were included in the study. Of the participants, 60% were males, and 63.8% were found in the age group between 18 and 34 years. The mean age of the participants was 32.6 years ± 13.25 SD. Five hundred ninety (67.4%) study participants lived in urban areas. Among the study participants, 594 (74.3%) were grouped into the general population. Six hundred seven (75.9%) were linked to TB services from public health facilities, and 132 (16.5) were from PPM sites for TB diagnosis or to initiate anti-TB treatment (Table 1).

Clinical and laboratory characteristics

Out of all the participants, 731 (91.5%) of the TB cases were new patients, and 663 (82.9%) had a history of contact with TB patients. Additionally, 83 participants (10.4%) were individual at most risk group who had tuberculosis. From those high risk group of patients, 56 (67.5%) had an HIV co-infection. and 20.5% were being partners of PLHIV. Diagnosis methods included AFB and Gene X-pert for 405 (50.6%) participants, followed by X-ray for 189 (23.6%) cases. Extra pulmonary TB was found in 251 (31.4%) participants, with TB lymphadenitis being the most common type (92; 36.6%). Only 51 (6.4%) were classified as severely acute malnourished. Positive HIV result was observed in 77 (9.6%) participants, while 714 (89%) had DOTs support (Table 2).

TB treatment outcomes

Of the total registered 800 cases, 618 (77.25%, 95%CI: 74.6, 79.8). had a successful treatment outcome and 182 (22.75%) had an unsuccessful treatment outcome. Three hundred thirty one (41.4%) patients completed their treatment, 287(36%) were cured, 30 (3.8%) failed treatment, 42 (5.3%) were transferred out, 77(9.8%) were lost to follow up and the remaining 33 (4.1%) died (Fig. 1).

Factors associated with TB treatment outcome

In bivariable analysis, age, residence, patient category, diagnosis methods, history previous treatment, co-morbidity, history of hospitalization, and treatment supporter were considered as candidate variable for multivariable logistic regression at p value < 0.25. On multivariable logistic regression analysis, patient residency, co-morbidity, history of previous treatment, and treatment supporter were significantly associated with tuberculosis treatment outcome.

Adjusting the effect of other variables in the model, the odds of having a successful treatment outcome were 2.8 times higher among patients living in urban areas compared to those living in rural areas (AOR = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.8–4.34). The odds of having a successful treatment outcome were 3.5 times higher in patients without a history of prior TB treatment than in patients who had previous history of treatment (AOR = 3.5, 95% CI: 1.34–9.16). The odds of having a successful treatment outcome were 13.9 among patients without a history of co-morbidity than those who had history of co-morbidity (AOR = 13.9, 95% CI: (7.1-27.33). Moreover, the odds of a successful treatment outcome were 7.7 times higher in patients who had a treatment supporter than in patients who did not have a treatment supporter (AOR = 7.7 95% CI: 3.9 -15.11) (Table 3).

Discussion

According to findings of this study, the successful treatment rate was 77.3% which is found to be below the expected global treatment success rate of 85% (5). The discrepancy might be due to poor counseling and implementation of DOTs strategy, poor health care utilization behavior and high loss to follow up of patients in Ethiopia. However, this finding is higher when compared with the findings from studies conducted in Debre-Markos referral hospital, Ethiopia University Hospital, Gambella, Gimbi town and Ghana [12–16]. These may include differences in healthcare infrastructure, the availability and quality of DOTs support, patient adherence strategies, and regional healthcare practices. Additionally, variations in demographic and socio-economic characteristics could also influence treatment outcomes.

Results of this study show that being an urban resident is significantly associated with successful treatment outcome of TB. This finding is supported by the studies conducted in Gimbi town, Gonder university teaching hospital, and North West of Ethiopia [15, 17, 18]. Furthermore, finding studies conducted in Debremarkos referral hospital, East Wollega zone, Felege Hiwot referral hospital and south west Ethiopia [13, 19–21] had clearly indicated that rural residence is significantly associated with poor treatment outcome. The reason for this finding could be that urban residents have more information and knowledge regarding the importance of treatment adherence when compared to rural residents. In addition, urban residents can easily access health facilities, and they are more likely to complete their treatment session successfully than rural residents.

Having no history of previous TB treatment is another significant determinant of having successful treatment outcome and it is supported by the study finding from Harar hospital [22]. Studies conducted in Asella teaching hospital, Bale Robe general hospital, Felege Hiwot referral hospital, Lusaka, Zambia and Bahawalpur, Pakistan also strengthens this findings by indicating that having history of previous TB treatment is significant determinant of poor treatment outcome [2, 20, 22–24]. The justification for this finding could be that those who have been previously treated for TB have a higher chance of acquiring MDR-TB which has poor treatment outcome.

Findings of this study clearly showed that not having co-morbidity is another important factor associated with successful Tb treatment outcome. This finding is in line with the findings from the study conducted in Adama [25]. On the other hand studies conducted in Bule hora General Hospital, Ethiopian university Hospital, Ghana and Southwestern Nigeria had revealed that having co-morbidity especially with HIV/AIDS is significantly associated poor treatment outcome [3, 11, 16, 26]. The scientific justification for this finding is that patients presented with co-morbidities are most likely to use different treatments and medications which increase their susceptibility to adverse drug reactions leading to treatment failures. Taking certain HIV and TB medicines at the same time can also increase the risk of drug-drug interactions and side effects leading to reduced quality of life, impaired function, worse physical and mental health and death. In addition, HIV-induced immunosuppression weakens the body’s control over latent TB, leading to the reactivation of the infection and transformation into active and contagious TB disease. On the other hand, individuals on ART are at risk of developing TB-associated immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome which leads to exaggerated inflammatory responses to MTB antigens causing morbidity and mortality among HIV/TB co-infected patients.

Having DOTs supporter is also one of the significant determinants of having successful TB treatment. Those who have DOTs supporters are seen to have more successful treatment outcome when compared to those who don’t have DOTs supporters. This finding is strengthened by the findings from studies conducted in Gibe Woreda Ethiopia, and Uganda that concludes family and community support during TB treatment as significant determinant of good treatment outcome [2, 21]. Treatment supporters can positively affect treatment outcome by accompanying the patient to health facilities and by encouraging them to take the TB drugs regularly and for the full duration of the treatment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it relies on a retrospective review of records, which may be affected by incomplete data or unreported outcomes. Moreover, key demographic and clinical variables that could influence treatment outcomes were not comprehensively documented. The study also did not investigate underlying factors contributing to the suboptimal TB treatment success rate, such as patient adherence, healthcare accessibility, socio-economic challenges, or regional variations in healthcare practices. Additionally, the generalizability of the findings may be constrained by differences in local healthcare systems and treatment protocols. Future research is essential to address these aspects and provide a more in-depth understanding of TB treatment outcomes.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study found that the TB treatment success rate falls below national and global targets, with factors such as urban residency, DOTs support, no previous TB treatment, and absence of comorbidity was found to be the significant predictors of TB treatment outcome. The findings highlight the importance of healthcare provider assistance and family member education in supporting medication adherence. To improve treatment outcomes, it is recommended to expand access to DOTs supporters, especially in underserve areas, educate family members on supporting patients, address barriers faced by rural populations, and develop integrated care models for patients with co-existing health issues. These measures are essential for achieving higher treatment success rates.
