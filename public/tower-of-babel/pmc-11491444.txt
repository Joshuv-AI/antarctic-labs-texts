Parametric resonance brain model
Authors: Salvatore Magazù, Maria Teresa Caccamo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paper introduces a parametric resonance model for characterizing some features of the brain’s electrical activity. This activity is assumed to be a fundamental aspect of brain functionality underpinning functions from basic sensory processing to complex cognitive operations such as memory, reasoning, and emotion. A pivotal element of the proposed parametric model is neuron synchronization which is crucial for generating detectable brain waves. The analysis of the frequency content of brain waves, categorized as delta (0÷4 Hz), theta (4÷7 Hz), alpha (8÷12 Hz), beta (13÷30 Hz), and gamma (30÷100 Hz) reveals, notably, that the mean frequency of each of these brain wave classes is, in sequence, approximately the double of that of the previous one. Based on this observation, the proposed parametric resonance model suggests a cascade of amplification effects. Following the proposed model, in the transition from wakefulness to sleep, the brain wave bands are energized at double frequency by higher frequency neighboring bands; on the contrary, in the sleep to awake transition, brain waves are energized at a half frequency by their lower frequency neighbor waves. Finally, the trend of increasing amplitude values from higher to lower frequencies lends empirical support to the parametric resonant brain model validity.Subject terms: Biological physics, Biophysics

Introduction

The brain’s electric activity is a fundamental aspect of its function, underlying activities from basic sensory processing to complex cognitive functions, including memory, thought, and emotion1,3. At the heart of the brain’s electrical activity are neurons, specialized cells capable of transmitting electrical signals. Each neuron generates electric activity primarily through the movement of ions across its membrane. This generates a potential difference across the neuron’s membrane, which, when significant enough, can lead to the generation of an action potential, or a nerve impulse. This action potential travels along the axon of the neuron and leads to the release of neurotransmitters, thus conveying information to other neurons4.

When many neurons in a particular area of the brain fire simultaneously, they generate a synchronized electric field that can be detected and measured5,6. This activity contributes to what is called the Local Field Potential (LFP) which provides a measure of the summed electric activity of neurons within small regions and are crucial for understanding brain functions at a network level. More in details, the key components contributing to LFPs include: (i) synaptic potentials which are the primary contributors to LFPs; (ii) subthreshold oscillations connected with neurons that often display rhythmic fluctuations in their membrane potentials due to ion channel activities; (iii) action potentials that by themselves contribute less to LFPs compared to synaptic activity.

LFPs are typically measured both by using EEG which records global brain activity from the scalp and reflects the summation of the synchronous activity of neurons or by using microelectrodes inserted into the brain tissue which provide a more localized measure of brain activity. These measurements allow to characterize the dynamics of specific brain regions and furnish information on how these areas activate themselves. By examining fluctuations in LFPs, it is possible to infer details about the types of information being processed within a brain area and how different neuronal populations interact. LFPs have been used to correlate specific patterns of brain activity with various cognitive functions such as decision making, attention, and memory. These studies often relate changes in LFP frequency bands (like theta, alpha, beta, and gamma) to different types of cognitive activities7,8.

When the coupling strength parameter K is small, neurons tend to fire independently according to their natural frequencies; as K increases, the interaction among neurons becomes stronger, leading them to start synchronizing their firing; beyond a critical threshold of K, a significant fraction of neurons may begin to fire in unison, leading to a coherent neuronal oscillation across the network. In a fully synchronized state, the phase difference between any pair of neurons becomes constant or even zero, meaning that all neurons fire in a highly coordinated way. This state of synchronization is supposed to correspond to various rhythmic activities observed in the brain, such as alpha, beta, and gamma waves.

In the last decades a rich body of research was addressed to neural oscillations. In particular, the foundational work of Buzsáki et al.11 investigated how different oscillatory frequencies are connected with cognitive processes. Other studies12–14 have also provided a groundwork for the hypothesis that brain waves modulate each other through resonance mechanisms, a concept explored in the model proposed in the present work.

In particular, the mathematical basis for modeling neural oscillations as resonant phenomena was supported by Izhikevich12 who introduced polychronous neural groups.

Furthermore, recent advances in neuroimaging13 have allowed for a more detailed mapping of brain connectivity, particularly through techniques such as functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) and Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI); these studies highlighted the importance of DTI for understanding how different brain regions communicate.

Finally, the concept of organizing brain regions based on their resonance properties aligns with the “connectome harmonics” framework, as explored by Atasoy et al.14; in their contribution the Authors describe how the eigenmodes of brain connectivity matrices contribute to large-scale brain dynamics, providing a mathematical basis for understanding structured resonance patterns.

The present work proposes a brain parametric resonance model tailored to characterize cerebral electrical activity. The model posits neuron synchronization as a critical mechanism for generating observable brain electrical waves, especially evident when substantial neuron populations within specific cerebral regions exhibit synchronized activity. The model is based on a brain wave frequency analysis and suggests a hierarchical frequency amplification.

As a rule, parametric resonance describes the phenomenon where the system’s response is markedly amplified at specific frequencies and is extensively employed in various scientific disciplines. Applied to cerebral dynamics, brain waves are modeled as oscillators whose amplitude can be resonantly augmented by their mutual interactions. In particular, brain waves can be amplified by brain waves with different frequency. The model is corroborated by different empirical observations and provides a coherent framework for understanding the connection among frequency ranges of brain waves.

Parametric resonance

This section offers a straightforward explanation of the parametric resonance effect using a simple device: a pendulum whose length varies sinusoidally with different frequencies15–17. In such scenarios, due to parametric resonance, within specific frequency ranges, the pendulum’s energy accumulates over time at a rate proportional to its total energy, thereby increasing the oscillation amplitudes exponentially. From a general viewpoint, oscillations are considered free when the oscillating system operates without any external or internal influence; this means that in the presence of friction, the amplitude of the oscillation amplitude gradually decreases to zero over time. When an external periodic force is applied to an oscillator, the oscillations are termed forced; in this situation, after an initial transitory phase, the oscillations stabilize themselves and match the frequency of the external force; furthermore, their amplitude can significantly increase, leading to a resonance effect, when the frequency of the external force is close to the oscillator’s natural frequency. In addition to this effect, a proper periodic adjustment of the system’s internal parameters can significantly enhance the oscillation amplitude, leading to a parametric resonance18,19. Delta waves have a smooth and regular pattern, have the lowest frequency and have the highest amplitude; they are dominant during dreamless sleep; they are also a hallmark in certain stages of brain development, particularly in infants22.

Theta waves (4÷7 Hz) are slightly faster and are present during certain sleep stages, particularly stages 1 and 2 of non-REM sleep; these waves are also seen during deep relaxation and in some meditative states; theta rhythms can indicate a brain engaged in imagery, intuition, and memory. Alpha waves are typical of a relaxed, awake state with closed eyes; these waves are often found when the brain is calm and resting, not processing much information; they can be increased through mindfulness and meditation and are diminished during open-eyed focused mental activity. Beta waves are seen during active, engaged mental activity, such as problem-solving or decision-making; they are the patterns of the working or thinking brain involved in logical thought and conscious decision. Gamma waves have the smallest amplitude on the EEG when compared to the other waves, but they are the fastest; they are associated with higher-level cognitive functions, such as perception and consciousness; these waves are supposed to be involved in integrating information from different areas of the brain and have been associated with heightened sensory perception and insight. Furthermore, each wave pattern differs not only in frequency but also in form and amplitude23,24.

Let’s start the discussion observing that brain waves are not mutually exclusive phenomena but rather coexist across different regions of the brain, varying in intensity and dominance depending on the psychological and physiological state of an individual. Research in neuroscience has revealed that multiple types of brain waves can be active simultaneously, indicating a complex interplay that underpins various cognitive and emotional processes25,26.

In particular, the human brain exhibits a dynamic landscape where different brain regions can generate distinct types of neural oscillations concurrently. For instance, while the frontal cortex may display beta waves during active problem-solving tasks, the posterior regions might simultaneously produce alpha waves indicative of a relaxed state. The brain’s ability to perform complex cognitive functions is supported by its capacity to modulate the intensity and frequency of these waves as per the task requirements. During activities requiring heightened attention and sensory processing, an increase in beta and gamma wave activity is observed in task-relevant cortical areas. Conversely, regions less involved may continue to exhibit lower frequency theta or alpha waves. Transitions between different mental states are characterized by shifts in the dominant brain wave frequencies. For example, the transition from wakefulness to sleep involves a shift from higher frequency beta waves to the slower theta and delta waves. Even during sleep, these waves coexist in complex patterns that vary with the sleep stage, reflecting the brain’s layered processing activities. The interaction among different brain waves is further evidenced by phenomena such as cross-frequency coupling, where the phase or amplitude of one frequency band modulates another. This coupling is crucial for cognitive flexibility and enables the brain to coordinate widespread neural networks efficiently. These findings underscore the non-mutually exclusive nature of brain waves and highlight their simultaneous activity as a fundamental aspect of brain function. This complexity allows the brain to integrate sensory information, regulate consciousness, and maintain cognitive flexibility across various tasks and states.

The data reported in the two tables can be interpreted through a parametric resonance model tailored to highlight regular features within the intricate dynamics of brain function. Central to this model is the phenomenon of neuron synchronization, where large numbers of neurons firing in unison within specific regions create detectable synchronized electric fields. This synchronization is crucial for the generation of coherent brain waves, which facilitate various brain functions by enabling efficient communication between different brain regions.

In the context of this model, brain wave frequencies - specifically delta (0÷4 Hz), theta (4÷7 Hz), alpha (8÷12 Hz), beta (13÷30 Hz), and gamma (30÷100 Hz) waves - are analyzed. These frequency bands not only delineate different states of brain activity but also exhibit a hierarchical frequency modulation with a specific scaling factor that suggests a cascading influence from higher to lower frequencies. This pattern aligns with the concept of parametric resonance, where the oscillation of one wave can be significantly amplified by another wave operating at a double frequency.

The model hypothesizes that, for example, higher frequency brain waves can modulate and amplify the amplitude of lower frequency waves through the parametric resonance mechanism. Furthermore, the increase in the amplitude of brain waves from higher frequencies to lower ones, suggests an interconnected and layered structure of brain wave interaction. The observed increasing amplitude values, moving from higher to lower frequency brain waves, provide empirical support for this model. This amplification pattern suggests a structured resonance cascade, where each wave frequency potentially plays a role in modulating and enhancing the subsequent lower frequency wave. For example, the doubling in frequency from delta to theta to alpha suggests that each wave could be acting as a driver for the next, enhancing its amplitude through resonance effects.

This behavior can be interpreted in terms of the fact that higher frequency brain waves, such as beta and gamma waves, are associated with more intense cognitive processing and hence to greater energy consumption. For example, gamma waves are often linked to concentrated mental activity, processing of information, learning, and memory, which require significant metabolic energy. Furthermore, it should be considered that the human brain is optimized for energy efficiency; as mental activity intensifies and higher frequency waves are generated, energy use increases but not indefinitely. Brain may have mechanisms to optimize and limit energy consumption, leading to the observed plateau for in the plot. This can prevent excessive energy use and potential metabolic exhaustion. In other terms the plateau could reflect a biological ceiling for energy expenditure in terms of brain wave production. The brain has a limited metabolic budget; despite increases in cognitive demand, it may not proportionally increase energy consumption beyond a certain threshold. This ceiling ensures the brain does not exceed its energy supply, which is critical for its health and function.

In other terms, when a subject transits from wakefulness to sleep, the high-frequency beta and gamma waves that are associated with alertness and active cognition diminish, whereas as one progresses into deeper stages of sleep, there is an increase in the dominance of lower-frequency theta and delta waves; this is especially true during deep (slow-wave) sleep when delta waves are the most prominent.

The obtained results are consistent with earlier works that highlight the relationship between brain wave activity and energy consumption during various cognitive and resting states27,28. Studies have shown that the brain’s electrical activity, particularly during transitions between sleep and wakefulness, correlates with significant energy demands. For instance, high-frequency brain waves like gamma and beta are typically associated with increased cortical activity, which requires more energy, whereas low-frequency waves like delta dominate during sleep, coinciding with lower energy consumption.

However, it should be stressed that several mechanisms can be responsible to regulate brain energy consumption and prevent excessive metabolic demands29; these mechanisms include, for example: (i) neural efficiency, for which during low-demand states (e.g., sleep), the brain reduces activity in high-energy processes such as synaptic transmission and cortical excitability, shifting toward energy-saving neural pathways dominated by slow-wave activity (delta and theta); (ii) neurotransmitter modulation, for which neurotransmitters are involved in reducing neural excitability, thus lowering the brain’s metabolic demands; (iii) homeostatic regulation, for which the brain actively manages energy by employing homeostatic mechanisms, such as regulating ATP levels and modulating ion channel activity, to ensure that energy consumption aligns with functional requirements30,31.

Recent research supports the notion that the transition from wakefulness to sleep generally occurs faster than the reverse. During sleep onset, EEG studies show rapid shifts from alpha to theta waves, indicating a quicker descent into light sleep stages31. In contrast, the process of waking up involves a more gradual shift, where delta wave activity decreases slowly, accompanied by hormonal changes such as a reduction in melatonin and a rise in cortisol, which promotes wakefulness32–36.

The proposed parametric resonance brain model suggests different rates in the transition from low to high frequency brain waves and in the opposite transition.

Following the model, the transition from an awake state to a sleep state may occur more quickly than the transition from sleeping to becoming fully awake; this inference is justified by the fact that each brain wave band is energized faster (i.e. at a double frequency) and more (by more energetic waves). On the contrary, following the model, the transition from sleep to awake is slower; this is justified by the fact that each frequency brain wave is energized slower (i.e. at a half frequency) by less energetic waves.

Generally speaking, as far as the transition from awake to sleep is concerned37, known as sleep onset, this is characterized by a rapid shift in brain wave activity. Initially, while awake and actively thinking or engaged, the brain exhibits high-frequency waves, such as beta waves (13÷30 Hz). As the body begins to relax and the mind calms, there is an increase in alpha waves (8÷12 Hz), indicative of a relaxed, wakeful state. As the individual drifts into light sleep, these give way to theta waves (4÷7 Hz). This transition can occur relatively quickly, often within a few minutes, making it a quicker shift compared to waking up.

In the framework of the parametric resonance brain model, the circumstance that the transition from being awake to falling asleep occurs more quickly than the transition from sleeping to becoming fully awake is justified by the fact that each brain wave band is energized faster (i.e. at a double frequency) and more (with a higher energy) by its near higher frequency brain wave band, i.e. beta waves are energized by gamma waves, alpha waves by beta waves, theta waves by alpha waves and, finally, delta waves by theta waves. This is due that each intermediate frequency brain wave band is energized at a double frequency by more energetic waves.

On the other hand, the transition from sleep to awake involves a more gradual increase in brain wave frequencies. During deep sleep, the brain predominantly generates delta waves (0÷4 Hz), the slowest of the brain waves. As the sleep cycle progresses towards ending, the brain activity escalates towards REM (Rapid Eye Movement) sleep, where the brain wave pattern resembles that seen in wakefulness, with a mixture of theta, alpha, and beta waves. As a matter of fact, the process of waking up involves not only the cessation of delta wave production but also a gradual hormonal shift, including the reduction of melatonin, which supports sleep, and an increase in cortisol, which promotes wakefulness. This transition is typically slower and can take a considerable part of the morning to complete, especially if the person is awoken from a deep sleep stage38,39.

In the framework of the parametric resonance brain model, the fact that the transition from sleep to awake is slower is justified by the fact that each frequency brain wave is energized slower (i.e. at a half frequency) by its near lower frequency brain wave, i.e. theta waves are energized by delta waves, alpha waves by theta waves, beta waves by alpha waves and, finally, gamma waves by beta waves. This is due that each intermediate frequency brain wave band is energized at a half frequency by less energetic waves40.

In summary, within the proposed model, the brain wave energy transfer could be facilitated by multiple parametric resonance effects among different waves characterized by different frequency ranges. Therefore, the brain actively regulates the parameters governing neural oscillations, and these regulatory actions can lead to parametric resonance effects, which may contribute to the shifts in brain wave frequencies seen during the sleep-wake cycle.

As a matter of fact, it should be taken into account that while the parametric resonance model offers a novel and promising approach to understanding some features brain wave interactions, it faces limitations related to the complexity of brain dynamics, empirical validation, generalizability across cognitive states, and potential therapeutic applications. Addressing these limitations through further research and empirical studies will be essential for refining the model and expanding its practical utility.

In other terms, the proposed parametric resonance model presents a simplified view of neural oscillations and brain wave interactions. While the model captures some aspects of brain wave dynamics, it does not account for the complexity of brain networks, which involve a vast array of interconnected neurons and oscillators that interact across multiple time scales and regions. The brain’s electrical activity is influenced by numerous factors, such as neurotransmitter levels, synaptic plasticity, and structural differences, which are not represented in the model41–43. Therefore, it requires more extensive validation through real-time neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies.

The study assumes a hierarchical relationship in which higher-frequency brain waves (gamma, beta) energize lower-frequency waves (alpha, theta, delta) through a doubling and halving process; while this assumption is interesting, it simplifies the intricate dynamics of neural synchronization and coupling; brain waves, in fact, often interact in complex, non-linear ways, which may not always follow the strict doubling and halving relationships proposed.

The study suggests that the model applies across various brain states (e.g., wakefulness, sleep, meditative states), but the generalizability of the model is limited; different cognitive tasks, emotional states, and environmental conditions can significantly alter brain wave patterns and synchronization mechanisms.

All in all, the proposed model opens some avenues for exploring the complex interplay between different brain wave frequencies and their roles in the holistic functioning of the brain, providing a robust framework for future neurological research and therapy. Understanding these interactions could offer an insight into how different brain states are initiated and maintained. It could also lead to innovations in treating neurological disorders by targeting specific frequencies with therapies that adjust or normalize harmful resonance patterns. Future research will focus on detailed empirical studies to map these resonance patterns more precisely, using advanced neuroimaging techniques to observe these phenomena in real-time and across different cognitive states.
