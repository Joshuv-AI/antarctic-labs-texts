Unveiling the power of TIIC: A prognostic tool for esophageal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Shaolin Gao, Bing Du, Yeju He, Haiyong Zhu, Shujun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A novel TIIC signature score predicts esophageal adenocarcinoma prognosis with high accuracy.•Integrating 20 machine learning algorithms establishes a reliable, data-driven prognostic model.•High TIIC scores correlate with poor survival outcomes and enhanced tumor genomic instability.•TIIC signature score offers insights into immune response and immunotherapy potential for EAC patients.Keywords: Esophageal adenocarcinoma, Tumor-infiltrating immune cell, Prognosis, Machine learning algorithms

Introduction

Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) continues to present a significant unmet medical need, characterized by elevated rates of morbidity and mortality [1,2]. Notably, risk factors of EAC include high body mass index and gastrointestinal reflux disease [3,4]. Patients diagnosed with EAC typically exhibit advanced-stage disease, and the five-year survival rates for those who can undergo potentially curative treatments are only moderately satisfactory. Scholars have substantiated that the combination of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy confers a survival benefit [3]. However, this advantage is limited to a minority of patients. In the majority of instances, neoadjuvant treatments prove to be ineffectual, burdensome, and postpone definitive surgical intervention [5,6].

The therapeutic landscape for gastrointestinal tumors (GI) has recently undergone a paradigm shift. Beyond conventional neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CROSS regimen) and perioperative FLOT chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have now entered clinical practice. Landmark trials have established the role of nivolumab as adjuvant therapy in resected EAC 1010 and of combination ICI plus first-line chemotherapy in advanced disease [7,8]. However, real-world responses are heterogeneous: only a subset of patients achieves durable benefit, while others experience primary resistance or immune-related toxicities without oncologic advantage. Emerging evidence suggests that the density and functional state of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs)—rather than PD-L1 expression alone—may determine immunotherapy responsiveness. This realization has motivated our group and others to dissect the TIME at single-cell resolution. In this context, several recent studies have refined the role of novel systemic therapies in GI tumors [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], yet a clinically implementable TIIC-based prognostic index for EAC has remained elusive.

This study leverages publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk transcriptome data to delineate the cellular heterogeneity of EAC tumors. Capitalizing on these insights, we constructed a novel prognostic model centered on TIICs using advanced machine learning, aiming to accurately evaluate survival prospects in EAC patients.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

RNA expression profiles and matched clinical data for 79 EAC samples were retrieved from the TCGA database. This internal cohort was randomly allocated into training and verification sets at a 1:1 ratio. To externally validate the model's robustness and accuracy, the LUD2015–005 dataset (n = 33) [14] was employed as an independent RNA-seq validation cohort. All expression data were normalized to TPM format and subsequently log2-transformed for downstream analyses. The chip dataset of GSE19417 (n = 48) was considered as the validation set. The normalizeBetweenArrays function in the “limma” R package was applied for data correction of the chip data.

Processing of scRNA-seq data

The scRNA-seq dataset of GSE222078 contained 8 EAC tumor samples and 2 paracancer samples. We conducted “Seurat” R package (version 4.1.3) for dimension reduction clustering analysis. In the context of cellular quality control, it is recommended that the mitochondrial content remains below 10%. Stringent quality control was applied by retaining cells with UMI counts between 200 and 50,000 and gene counts from 200 to 5000. Subsequent preprocessing included normalization, identification of the top 2000 highly variable genes, and scaling to regress out cell cycle effects, which were performed using the NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions in Seurat, respectively. Batch effects were then mitigated with the Harmony algorithm. Finally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across clusters or cell types were identified with the FindAllMarkers function, applying thresholds of |log∼2∼FC| > 0.25, an expression fraction > 0.1, and an adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Acquisition of TIIC-related genes

To construct the TIIC signature score, we established a computational framework that integrated both scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data from EAC tissues. The analysis commenced with the selection of the top 15% most highly expressed RNAs as candidates for immune-related molecules. In this formula, N denotes the total number of immune cell types, while the index i corresponds to a specific cell type, with XRNA,i representing the normalized maximum expression abundance of the RNA in that cell type. TSI ranges from 0 to 1, where immune cell universal RNA is defined when TSI equals 0 and immune cell specific RNA is defined when TSI equals 1. Highly expressed RNAs in all immune cell types are classified as immune-related universal RNAs (iuRNAs). iuRNAs that are significantly up-regulated in immune cell types and down-regulated in tumor cells are defined as TIIC-RNA [15,16].

An ensemble machine learning framework was then employed to refine the selection of the most predictive TIIC-RNAs. This framework integrated six distinct algorithms: Boruta, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator logistic regression (LassoLR), support vector machines (SVM), random forest (RF), and predictive analysis for microarrays (PAMR).

Cell annotation

Cellular identities were defined using the Sc-Type software by annotating canonical gene markers for major cell lineages, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, T cells, NK cells, B cells, myeloid cells, and mast cells.

Function enrichment analysis

To comprehensively characterize the tumor immune landscape, we applied multiple computational approaches. Immune cell infiltration was quantified using the TIMER algorithm (6 cell types), ssGSEA (28 cell types), MCP-counter (10 cell types), and the ESTIMATE suite. Functional profiling was performed through Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on GO and KEGG terms, supplemented by enrichment analysis via Metascape. Metabolic activity was assessed by scoring 114 literature-curated metabolic pathways and additional pathways from the KEGG database using GSVA.

Prediction of immunotherapy response

Immunotherapy response prediction was analyzed with Nathanson (melanoma), GSE35640 (melanoma), GSE91061 (melanoma), GSE78220 (melanoma), IMvigor210 (uroepithelial carcinoma, UC), Braun (renal cell carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, RCC), GSE179351 (colorectal adenocarcinoma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, COAD and PAAD), GSE165252 (esophageal adenocarcinoma, ESCA), GSE103668 (triple-negative breast cancer, TNBC) and GSE126044 (non-small cell lung cancer, NSCLC) datasets. TIIC signature scores were calculated for each dataset to predict immunotherapy response. TIDE online tools (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) was conducted to predict the immune response score in the TCGA dataset.

GSEA analysis of differential genes

Differential expression analysis between the two groups was conducted using the "limma" R package, with thresholds set at |log∼2∼FC| > 0.5 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) focused on up-regulated genes was subsequently performed with the "clusterProfiler" package, utilizing KEGG and GO-BP gene sets obtained from the MSigDB database. Enrichment results with a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value of <0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Comparison of genomic variation landscape

Somatic mutation patterns between the two groups were analyzed using the "maftools" R package, which included visualization of the top 30 most frequently mutated genes and comparison of mutation frequencies via Chi-square tests. Concurrently, copy number variation (CNV) data were processed with GISTIC 2.0 to identify significantly recurrent arm-level and focal amplifications and deletions. Genomic instability was further quantified by calculating the fraction of genome altered (FGA), gained (FGG), and lost (FGL). All resulting CNV profiles were visualized using the "ggplot2" R package.

TIIC-related risk characteristics generated by machine learning methods

Candidate prognostic TIIC-RNAs were initially identified through univariate Cox regression analysis. The selection was subsequently refined using a panel of three machine learning algorithms: random survival forest (RSF), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression (LassoCox), and CoxBoost. 20 machine learning algorithms were utilized for scoring to determine the most reliable model based on the comprehensive C-index, including RSF, conditional random forest (CForest), LassoCox, elastic network regression (Enet), Ridge, gradient lift using regression trees (BlackBoost), parametric survival model regression (SurvReg), and conditional inference trees (CTree), CoxPH, ObliqueRSF, StepwiseCox, SurvivalSVM, generalized enhanced regression model (GBM), Ranger, Cox model and partial least squares regression (PlsRcox) of related techniques, gradient lifting is performed using component linear models (GlmBoost), supervised principal components (SuperPC), akritas conditional nonparametric survival estimators (Akritas), CoxBoost, and recursive partition and regression trees (Rpart). We developed TIIC signature score based on prognostic TIIC-RNAs.

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for EAC were sourced from the GCTA database, and a Manhattan plot was generated for visualization using the "CMplot" R package. For gene colocalization analysis, we subsequently employed the SMR software, integrating the EAC GWAS data with cis-eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium (EQTLgen-CIS-EQTLS) using the 1000 Genomes European population as a reference. Furthermore, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed with the "TwoSampleMR" R package to assess potential causal relationships between SNP sites in prognostic model genes, gastro-oesophageal reflux, and EAC.

Cell culture

The human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cell lines TE-1 and KYSE-30, as well as the normal esophageal epithelial cell line HET-1A, were obtained from the Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. All cells were routinely passaged and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Western blot analysis

Protein expression in cultured cells was evaluated by Western blotting. Total proteins were extracted by lysing the samples in RIPA buffer (Proteintech, China) supplemented with 1% PMSF (Solarbio, China) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Proteintech, China). Protein concentrations were subsequently determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 4× SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Solarbio, China) and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. The prepared protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and then electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Biosharp, China) at room temperature for 1 h to reduce nonspecific binding, followed by three washes with TBST. The membranes were then incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After extensive washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following additional TBST washes, immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Yeasen, China) and captured with a Tanon-5200 imaging system (Shanghai, China). GAPDH was used as the internal loading control, and the relative band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from clinical tissue specimens using TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the HiFi II First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was subsequently performed on an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). GAPDH was used as the endogenous reference gene to normalize the mRNA expression levels of target genes, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Clone formation assay

The treated cells were first seeded into 6-well or 12-well plates and cultured in DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS under standard incubator conditions for approximately 2 weeks. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet. The number of colonies in each well was subsequently quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasive capacity was assessed using Matrigel-precoated Transwell chambers with 8 μm pores (Corning). Briefly, 2 × 104 cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 36 h, cells that had migrated through the membrane were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and subsequently counted.

Statistical analysis

All statistical computations and data analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.3). Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson's correlation analysis. For group comparisons, categorical variables were evaluated with the Chi-square test, while continuous variables were analyzed using the Student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. The optimal cutoff value for survival analysis was determined with the "survminer" package. Subsequently, both univariate/multivariate Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, along with log-rank tests, were performed utilizing the "survival" R package.

Results

Identification of TIIC-RNA

The top 15% of RNAs (total 3609 RNAs) in each immune cell were identified as potentially immune-related RNAs, When TSI score < 0.3, 428 immune-related RNAs were further noted as iuRNAs. The expression level of cell markers in each immune cell was shown in Fig. 1C. The spatial distribution of immune and neoplastic cells within the TME was delineated by t-SNE visualization (Fig. 1D). Subsequent differential expression analysis between these compartments identified 68 immune cell-upregulated genes, which were designated as TIIC-RNAs (Fig. 1E). To refine this list, we applied a suite of six machine learning algorithms—Boruta, XGBoost, LassoLR, SVM, RF, and PAMR—which collectively pinpointed 34 most valuable TIIC-related RNAs for downstream model construction (Fig. 1F).

Construction of prognostic model

From the initial candidates, seven TIIC-RNAs with prognostic potential were identified in the TCGA dataset (Fig. 2A). Their survival relevance was further evaluated using three distinct machine learning algorithms: CoxBoost (Fig. 2B), LassoCox (Fig. 2C, D), and Random Forest (Fig. 2E), which collectively determined the final prognostic TIIC-RNAs (Fig. 2F).

(C-D) Clustering analysis of 7 prognostic genes by LassoCox algorithm. (E) Clustering analysis of 7 prognostic genes by RSF algorithm. To construct the most robust model, we conducted a comprehensive benchmark of 20 machine learning algorithms based on the aggregated C-index across validation datasets. This process led to the development of the TIIC signature score, with the Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithm emerging as the top performer. The prognostic power of the score was consistently affirmed, as patients classified with high TIIC scores exhibited significantly poorer overall survival across all cohorts, including the TCGA training, test, and entire sets, as well as the independent LUD2015–005 and GSE19417 datasets (Fig. 3A). Time-dependent ROC analysis further confirmed its predictive accuracy, with high AUC values for 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival in the TCGA training set (0.96, 0.91, 0.92). This robust performance was maintained in external validation sets: TCGA test set (0.692, 0.804, 0.879), TCGA total set (0.83, 0.833, 0.9), GSE19417 (0.676, 0.677, 0.537), and LUD2015–005 (0.57, 0.725, 0.643) (Fig. 3B).

Validation of TIIC signature score. (A) Kaplan-Meier curve of TIIC signature score in TCGA training set, TCGA test set, TCGA entire set, LUD2015–005 and GSE19417. (B) Time-dependent ROC curves of TIIC signature score for 1-year, 2-year and 3-year OS in TCGA training set, TCGA test set, TCGA entire set, LUD2015–005 and GSE19417.

Comparison of prognostic value between TIIC signature score and clinical features

In the TCGA dataset, TIIC signature score was significantly correlated with survival status, tumor stage, and TNM staging system (Fig. 4A). C-index of TIIC signature score showed better performance in terms of age, sex, tumor stage, and TNM staging system (Fig. 4B). In a comprehensive comparison with 22 established prognostic models, our TIIC signature exhibited superior performance, as evidenced by a higher C-index across all validation datasets, including the TCGA subsets, LUD2015–005, and GSE19417 (Fig. 4C).

Prediction of biological mechanisms associated with TIIC signature score

The TIIC signature score was significantly associated with the activation of key oncogenic pathways, including spliceosome, mismatch repair, and nucleotide excision repair (Fig. 5A). Analysis of eight significantly altered pathways (Fig. 5B) and Metascape enrichment (Fig. 5C) consistently indicated that the high-TIIC group was enriched for cell cycle and mitosis. Conversely, GSEA revealed a contrasting enrichment pattern, with the low-TIIC group showing significant involvement in antimicrobial and humoral immune responses (Fig. 6A).

Correlation of TIIC signature score with immune-related features

The TIIC score exhibited an inverse correlation with the activity of most immune cells (Fig. 6B). Stratified analysis showed that the low and high TIIC groups exhibited divergent correlation profiles with molecular features. Specifically, the score showed positive associations with immune checkpoint expression and CNV, but a negative association with methylation in the low-score group; these relationships were nearly diametrically opposed in the high-score group (Fig. 6C).

Predictive value of TIIC signature score for immunotherapy response

EAC patients with low TIIC signature score showed lower survival outcomes in the Nathanson dataset (p = 0.0049) in Fig. 7A. The proportion of responders was higher in the low TIIC signature score group than in the high TIIC signature score group (p = 0.3, Fig. 7B).

Predictive metabolic characteristics associated with TIIC signature score

The TIIC signature score showed a positive association with pro-tumorigenic metabolic pathways (Fig. 7C). Conversely, a specific lipid metabolic pathway known to potentiate anti-tumor immunity was significantly more active in the low-TIIC group (Fig. 8A). Further analysis of individual metabolic processes revealed that the TIIC score correlated positively with cysteine and methionine metabolism, but exhibited negative correlations with arachidonic acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, and inositol phosphate metabolism (Fig. 8B).

Differences in SNV mutation and tumor immune microenvironment (TIME)

Comparative genomic analysis revealed distinct mutational landscapes between the two TIIC-based groups (Fig. 9A). Visualization of the top 30 mutated genes identified TP53 (78.2%), TTN (48.7%), and SYNE1 (30.8%) as the most frequently altered, with CSMD3 (19.2%) showing a significant difference in mutation frequency between groups (Fig. 9B). Consistent with this genomic instability, the high-TIIC group exhibited significantly elevated levels of chromosomal instability, as quantified by the fraction of genome altered (FGA), gained (FGG), and lost (FGL) (Fig. 9C). A focal analysis further identified chromosome 7 as a site of significantly differential CNV alterations between the groups (Fig. 9D).

CCNI was upregulated in esophageal cancer cells and promoted malignant cellular phenotypes

CCNI was selected for functional validation due to its most pronounced upregulation among TIIC-related prognostic genes in esophageal cancer cell lines. qRT-PCR and Western blotting confirmed that CCNI expression was markedly elevated in TE-1 and KYSE-30 cells compared with normal HET-1A cells (Fig. 11A–C). siRNA-mediated knockdown of CCNI significantly suppressed cell migration, invasion, and colony formation in both cell lines, as demonstrated by Transwell and colony formation assays (Fig. 11I–K, M). Additionally, CCNI silencing induced apoptosis-related molecular changes, characterized by upregulated Caspase-3/Bax and downregulated BCL2 expression (Fig. 11L, N). Collectively, these findings indicate that CCNI promotes malignant phenotypes in esophageal cancer cells and support its role as a potential oncogenic regulator within the TIIC-associated network.

Discussion

Researchers have recently applied machine learning techniques to predict and assess cancer, specifically focusing on breast cancer detection using a novel algorithm called BCD-WERT, introduced by Abbas et al. [17]. Previous studies have demonstrated promising outcomes when employing machine learning in the context of EAC. However, despite the utilization of algorithms by certain researchers to investigate EAC, an optimal prognostic model with a high level of stability in predicting the overall survival of EAC patients has not yet been developed.

The progression of cancer is impacted by the immune response directed towards tumor cells, whether it occurs locally or systemically [18]. Immune-related signatures in anti-tumor immune responses can be employed to forecast the immune status and prognosis of patients diagnosed with EAC [19]. Beyond the well-established role of inflammatory cytokines, TIME is primarily composed of TIICs and stromal cells, both of which are critically implicated in diagnosis and prognosis [20]. Advancements in high-throughput technologies have further solidified TIICs as pivotal determinants of clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses in oncology [21]. Capitalizing on this understanding, we developed a novel TIIC signature score that demonstrates high prognostic accuracy for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival in EAC patients. To contextualize its performance, we conducted a comprehensive benchmarking against 22 previously published prognostic models. Our TIIC model consistently exhibited superior discriminative power, achieving the highest C-index across all evaluated datasets, including the TCGA training, test, and entire cohorts, as well as the independent LUD2015–005 and GSE19417 validation sets. The results of this study indicate significant correlations between TIIC signature score and various clinical factors, including survival status, tumor stage, and TNM staging system. An increase in TIIC score was found to be associated with a decrease in the activity of most immune cells. Additionally, TIIC signature score demonstrated a positive correlation with metabolic pathways that facilitate tumor growth, specifically in relation to cysteine and methionine metabolism. Conversely, negative correlations were observed between TIIC score and arachidonic acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, and inositol phosphate metabolism.

Our study identified key pathways, such as KEGG_SPLICEOSOME and KEGG_MISMATCH_REPAIR (MMR), that were significantly associated with TIIC signature scores. Spliceosome dysregulation has been increasingly implicated in cancer, including EAC. Alterations in RNA splicing processes can drive oncogenesis by producing oncogenic isoforms or inactivating tumor-suppressive variants. The association between high TIIC scores and spliceosome-related pathways suggests that immune infiltration may lead to shifts in transcriptomic diversity, promoting tumor adaptability and resistance to therapy. For instance, spliceosome alterations may result in evasion of immune detection by influencing the expression of checkpoint molecules or neoantigens, directly impacting immune surveillance. MMR is essential for maintaining genomic stability by correcting replication errors. Dysfunctional MMR is often linked to microsatellite instability (MSI), which can promote the generation of mutation-derived neoantigens that enhance immune cell recruitment [22]. Our results suggest that increased TIIC scores are associated with heightened MMR activity, implying that while more immune cells infiltrate the tumor, they may paradoxically be ineffective due to the tumor's enhanced ability to repair immune-induced DNA damage. This highlights a potential mechanism through which immune infiltration is linked to poor prognosis, as the tumor microenvironment adapts to immune pressure by maintaining genomic integrity.

In the process of the construction of TIIC signature score based on various machine learning methods, we firstly identified TIIC-RNA according to TSI. It is calculated to characterize the correspondence between cell lines and tumor characteristic gene expression [23]. Subsequently, we observed that when the TSI score is <0.3, a total of 428 immune-related RNAs were identified as iuRNAs. Further research is needed to ascertain the therapeutic efficacy of targeting components of the TIME specifically in EAC. To determine the most dependable model, twenty machine learning algorithms were employed, considering the comprehensive C-index of validated datasets. Consequently, a TIIC signature score was formulated. The new model based on RSF algorithm provided a better C-index among the 20 machine learning algorithms. Notably, patients with lower TIIC risk scores exhibited elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules, suggesting a more immunoresponsive microenvironment. Furthermore, gene colocalization analysis for two key prognostic genes, CCNI and PTGES3, provided mechanistic insights. Extending this genetic inquiry, Mendelian randomization identified a significant association at the SNP locus rs8130507, linking gastro-oesophageal reflux to EAC susceptibility.

Our TIIC signature offers a dynamic, RNA-based metric that could be serially monitored to inform such sequencing decisions. From a translational standpoint, we propose that a low TIIC score (associated with higher immune checkpoint expression and better predicted response) identifies patients who might safely delay or avoid cytotoxic chemotherapy in favor of ICI-based regimens, thereby reducing overtreatment. Conversely, a high TIIC score, despite its association with poor prognosis, may enrich for patients who could benefit from microenvironment-modulating agents (e.g., CSF-1R inhibitors or TGFβ blockers) prior to ICI administration [24,25].

We acknowledge that several hurdles must be overcome before clinical translation. First, the signature must be validated in prospective, multicenter cohorts with standardized RNA sequencing protocols. Second, a clinically deployable assay (e.g., a NanoString panel or qPCR-based kit) should be developed to replace discovery-phase transcriptomics. Third, we recognize a pressing need for ‘dynamic TIIC monitoring’—serial biopsies or liquid biopsies to capture how the TIIC score evolves during neoadjuvant therapy. By openly addressing these challenges, we hope to accelerate the bench-to-bedside trajectory of the TIIC signature.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the TIIC signature score emerges as a promising biomarker to stratify EAC patients, potentially identifying those who would derive the greatest benefit from immunotherapy.
