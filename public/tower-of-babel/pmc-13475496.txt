Solid interface electrochemistry between LiCoO2 and solid-state electrolytes
Authors: Kangzhe Yu, Lei Su, Weijun Tuo, Jiuqing Gui, Jinying Xiong, Jie Liu, Zhihan Liu, Bingbing Tian, Shangquan Zhao, Xianyong Zhang, Xia Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Strategies to optimize the cathode/solid electrolyte interphase (CSEI) have been developed to improve the high voltage durability of LiCoO2 (LCO), yet the underlying interfacial mechanism remains unclear. Here, we construct a stable oxyhalide-derived CSEI for all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) to identify the key interfacial features required for high-voltage operation. At 4.6 volts, commercial LCO coupled with Li6PS5Cl (LPSC) suffers from severe interfacial instability, sluggish Li+ transport, and continuous side reactions. By introducing LiNbOCl4 (LNOC), an in situ formed CSEI rich in Li-Cl/Nb-O/Nb-O-Cl species is established, lowering the interfacial energy barrier to 0.363 electron volts and enhancing interfacial toughness and ionic conductivity. This stabilized interface suppresses lattice oxygen activity, accelerates Li+ transport, and improves the reversibility of O3/H1-3 phase transitions. Consequently, LCO|LNOC|LPSC|Li-In ASSBs deliver 95.8% capacity retention after 500 cycles at ∼1.0 C-rate with an LCO loading of 15.31 milligrams per square centimeter. The pouch cell achieves 90% initial Coulombic efficiency and stable cycling over 50 cycles.

INTRODUCTION

To date, rechargeable batteries play a key role in clean energy storage and electric vehicle applications. Although traditional Li-ion batteries have been omnipresent in our daily life, the used flammable organic liquid electrolytes have raised widespread safety concerns (1–4). Therefore, the ever forgotten all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) have stepped again into the focus on developing high energy density and safe energy storage devices, where the substitution of solid-state electrolytes (SEs) for liquid electrolytes and the construction of compatible interface become the key aspects at present, which is expected to achieve higher energy density, making them a promising development direction (5–7). Among the alternative SEs, the sulfide SE is proposed to be an attractive candidate due to its ionic conductivity up to 10−2 S cm−1 at room temperature and good mechanical ductility (8, 9). In pursuit of high energy density, high-voltage layered cathodes, such as LiCoO2 (LCO), LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NCM), and lithium-rich layered oxides, are often integrated with good electrochemistry. However, the interphase between cathode and sulfide SE presents multiple challenges, which include the electrochemical oxidation of sulfide SE due to its inherently narrow electrochemical window, unwanted electrochemical reactions between cathode and sulfide SE due to thermodynamic instability, as well as the structural degradation of cathode due to O loss and spinel/rock-salt phase formation (10–12). All these problems demonstrate adverse effects on advancing ASSBs and need to be solved urgently.

At present, LCO is a promising cathode candidate for ASSBs. However, when the charging potential increases (≥4.6 V), the Co cations in an LCO cathode will undergo severe parasitic side reactions with LPSC SEs, generating insulated by-products. Meanwhile, the electrochemically active oxygen species usually cause irreversible lattice oxygen loss, resulting in harmful surface degradation and electrochemical performance attenuation. To address these challenges, surface modification of sulfide SE is a common solution, but it often comes at the expense of ionic conductivity. For example, Shao et al. (13) prepared Li6.25PS4O1.25Cl0.75 by doping O element, and the high-voltage LCO-based ASSBs exhibited good electrochemical compatibility and stability. However, the ionic conductivity of the modified electrolyte is lower than that of the original sulfide SEs, reducing the Li+ ion transport kinetics. In addition to the modified sulfide SEs, interface engineering has been widely adopted. Guo et al. (14) prepared the Li2TiO3-coated and Ti-doped NCW (LiNi0.9Mn0.09W0.01O2) cathode, which improves the structural stability from bulk to surface and the parasitic reaction with SE constructs a stable interphase during cycling. Moreover, Pan et al. (15) proposed a modification strategy to enhance the structural stability and interface dynamics of cathode with a Li2ZrO3 layer, resulting in a stable cyclic performance over 200 cycles. However, these modifications substantially increase the interface impedance, thereby deteriorating the transport performance of Li+ ion. In contrast to the conventional Nb-oxide interlayers that mainly act as passive protective layers with limited ionic transport, the Li-Nb-O-Cl (LNOC) oxyhalide introduced here enables a dynamic interfacial evolution, involving the in situ formation and subsequent stabilization of an ionically conductive interphase. Furthermore, almost all electrolytes based on LPSC reported so far show insufficient stability above 4.3 V, and the severe side reactions at the cathode/electrolyte interface under this high voltage are generally regarded as the main cause of the electrochemical performance degradation. Therefore, there is an urgent need to design a composite cathode system with both compatibility and high voltage tolerance to effectively integrate the LCO cathode with SEs. These systems are crucial for clarifying the fundamental mechanism of electrochemical degradation. They provide a key path for developing more effective interface modification strategies (such as in situ construction of self-passivated interface phases) to suppress harmful effects and achieving high energy density ASSBs.

In this context, the interphase incompatibility between LCO and Li6PS5Cl(LPSC) sulfide SEs is revealed by an electrochemically unfavorable cathode/solid electrolyte interphase (CSEI) at high voltages, which remarkably impeded Li+ ion diffusion kinetics and remarkably increased the interface resistance. To this end, using LiNbOCl4 (LNOC) SE, a previously unidentified CSEI configuration with rich Li-Cl/Nb-O/Nb-O-Cl bonds is in situ established at the interface region, which delivers low interface energy barrier of 0.363 eV to ensure the considerable long-cycle stability. The CSEI at the LCO|LNOC interface is primarily formed during the initial formation charge, where the rapid interfacial reactions between LNOC and highly oxidized LCO surface establish a functional interphase framework. Subsequently, during the early cycling, this interphase undergoes gradual chemical and structural reconstruction, leading to densification and stabilization, as evidenced by the progressive thickening in high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and the continuously improved interfacial kinetics in electrochemical measurements. In addition, electronic-structure analysis suggests a partial Nb reduction associated with interfacial charge redistribution, which may contribute to the stabilization of oxygen chemistry. Theoretical evaluation shows that this cathode/SE interphase is conducive to the fast-ionic transport. The interface between LCO and LNOC with excellent thermal stability substantially improves the toughness and ionic conductivity of LCO cathode, stabilizes the surface lattice oxygen, and enhances the Li+ ion interphase transport dynamics and reversibility of O3/H1-3 phase transition. The resultant LCO|LiNbOCl4|LPSC|Li-In (marked as LCO|LNOC) ASSB exhibits a highly reversible capacity of 189.7 mAh g−1 at 18 mA g−1, with initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of 97.7% and capacity retention of 95.8% after 500 cycles at 180 mA g−1. This strategy provides a rewarding avenue toward the improved compatibility between cathode/SE composite cathodes for future improvement of high-voltage ASSBs.

RESULTS

Li+ diffusion kinetics in LCO|SE interphase

To explore interfacial electrochemistry and compatibility, we developed LCO and SEs (LPSC or LNOC) composite cathodes. Figures S1 to S4 in the Supplementary Materials demonstrate the structure, ionic conductivities, and Li+ ion diffusion barriers of both as-prepared phase-pure LNOC and LPSC SEs, where the activation energy of LNOC is about 0.256 eV, about 60% of LPSC (0.383 eV). Figure 1 (A and B) and fig. S2 illustrate the cross sections of LCO|LNOC and LCO|LPSC|Li-In (marked as LCO|LPSC) ASSBs, respectively, where the interface structure and reaction process of LCO with LPSC and LNOC can be retrieved clearly. To gain insights into the interfacial characteristics at the atomic level, we used complementary theoretical approaches: density functional theory (DFT) calculations to probe the electronic structure and the bond valence site energy (BVSE) method (16–19) to map the probable Li+ migration pathways and estimate the associated energy barriers (Fig. 1, C to E) (20). The total density of states reveals a distinct contrast between the two interfaces (Fig. 1C). The LCO|LNOC interface exhibits a finite density of states at the Fermi level, indicative of a metallic character. In contrast, the LCO|LPSC interface maintains a bandgap of ∼0.4 eV, characteristic of a semiconductor. This fundamental difference in electronic structure suggests that interfacial charge transfer would be more facile at the LCO|LNOC interface. To understand the origin of this metallicity, we examined the orbital-projected density of states for the LCO|LNOC system (fig. S5, A and B). A pronounced overlap and strong hybridization between the Nb-d, Cl-p, and O-p orbitals are observed in the valence band. This pronounced orbital hybridization is responsible for the closed bandgap and the formation of a conductive interfacial phase. The more conductive nature of the LCO|LNOC interface is correlated with enhanced kinetics for surface lithium transport and storage, which contributes directly to the superior rate capability of the composite cathode.

To complement the electronic structure analysis, the BVSE method was used to identify the preferred Li+ migration pathways and to estimate the corresponding activation energy barriers. The BVSE analysis reveals distinct interfacial transport properties. The calculated landscape for the LCO|LPSC interface (fig. S5, E and H) shows constrained diffusion channels. In contrast, the LCO|LNOC interface presents wider diffusion channels and a more favorable coordination environment for Li+ migration. The most favorable Li+ migration trajectory in the LCO|LPSC interface is identified as the [-Li5-Li29-] chain along the [003] direction, with a high energy barrier of ∼1.21 eV (Fig. 1D and fig. S5, E and H). For the LCO|LNOC interface, the lowest-energy two-dimensional (2D) path is the [-Li1-Li5-] trajectory on the ab plane. Furthermore, Li+ can also migrate via the [-Li9-Li26-] chain, which together forms a connected transport network with a substantially lower overall barrier of only 0.52 eV. The atomic structures used for these static calculations are based on experimentally refined models at open-circuit voltage (OCV). The low weighted profile R-factor (Rwp) values (<5%) from Rietveld refinement (fig. S5, D and G, and tables S1 and S2) validate the reliability of the underlying bulk structural models. At the same time, we acknowledge that interfacial structures may evolve during operation. To account for dynamic effects, we performed ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations (fig. S5, C, F, and I). Unlike the static BVSE approach, AIMD models the dynamic Li+ diffusion behavior at finite temperature. The calculated Li+ ion probability density distribution shows that in the LCO|LNOC system, its diffusion path is more continuous and uniform than that in the LCO|LPSC system, as shown in fig. S5 (F and I). Moreover, the activation energy obtained from AIMD is only 0.13 eV, lower for the LCO|LNOC system, which is consistent with the trends in the BVSE calculations and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements. Note that although the discrepancies of Li+ transport exist, the consistent trends indicate that the enhanced kinetics in LCO|LNOC system results from the combined effect of optimized intrinsic migration paths and improved interface transmission.

As shown in Fig. 1 (F to K), the interfacial Li+ ion diffusion kinetics of the two composite cathodes were examined using the temperature-dependent EIS. Note that the resistance of Li anode and its interphase is almost negligible compared to that of the cathode side (21–23), and the ASSBs at 30% state of charges (SOCs) are selected to analyze the EIS results. Figure 1H shows that the radius of the low-frequency semicircle substantially increases with the temperature decrease regarding the high-frequency semicircle. To accurately quantify the kinetic processes, the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) is obtained to clarify the separate contributions from EIS results inside the corresponding cathodes. As shown in Fig. 1 (G and J) and table S3 in the Supplementary Materials, five DRT peaks (R1 to R5) correspond to different kinetic processes, where the resistances of interface Li+ ion transport (RCSEI) and surface charge transfer (Rct) correspond to the respective R3 and R4 peaks, after ignoring the anodic side effects (24). However, accurately determining RCSEI and Rct is still challenging using this semiquantitative DRT analysis. To the best of our knowledge, the Li+ ion transport is a rate-limited step due to the lower ionic conductivity regarding the electronic conductivity. Hence, using these two resistances to evaluate the interphase resistance (Rint = RCSEI + Rct), the activation energy (Eint) of LCO|LPSC interphase is derived to be 0.489 eV, as shown in Fig. 1 (F and K) and fig. S6. For the LCO|LNOC ASSBs, the high-frequency semicircle at low temperatures and the low-frequency semicircle at high temperatures respectively dominated the electrochemical diffusion process, as shown in Fig. 1I and fig. S6. However, the peak intensity in the high-frequency region is still lower than that of the LCO|LPSC ASSBs. The DRT results indicate that within the temperature range of −15° to 35°C, the peak differences of R3 and R4 are dominant, and the R3 and R4 peak intensities of LCO|LNOC are much lower than those of LCO|LPSC. Since the interface resistance impedance of the LCO|LNOC ASSBs is lower than that of the LCO|LPSC ASSBs, the energy barrier of LCO|LNOC interphase is consequently reduced to 0.363 eV, and the LCO|LNOC ASSB demonstrates better tolerance to deliver higher capacity in the whole temperature range.

Electrochemical performance

The Li-In/SE/composite cathode ASSBs were then assembled to evaluate the electrochemical performance, as shown in Fig. 2A. Figure S7 shows the schematic diagram of the assembled ASSBs along with the corresponding linear sweep voltammetry curves (LSVs), where the LNOC SE presents an electrochemically stable behavior up to 7.0 V, excepting the weak oxidation around 4.3 V by self-activation to be compatible with the interface construction. While the LPSC SE shows an oxidation potential around 3.6 V and then gradually rise after ∼4.0 V to indicate its intrinsically electrochemical activity and interface instability. Figure 2B and fig. S8 show the charge/discharge curves of LCO|LNOC and LCO|LPSC ASSBs at 0.1 C (1 C = 180 mA g−1) in the voltage range of 3 to 4.6 V. The discharge capacity of LCO|LPSC decreases rapidly to show the as-expected structural/interfacial degradation during cycling. In contrast, that of LCO|LNOC ASSB delivers the normal electrochemical process with higher energy density with respect to that of organic electrolyte–based lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), much better than the LCO|LPSC ASSBs. We can observe that in the electrochemical tests, no obvious H1-3 phase transition platform similar to that of liquid batteries was found. This is mainly because in the ASSBs, at high loading mass, the solid-solid interface reactions and transport limitations weaken the resolution of the phase transition signals. This phenomenon is also consistent with the reports in existing literature regarding the weakening of phase transition signals in solid-state systems (the specific phase transition analysis will be described in the subsequent XRD section) (25–27). In addition, the LCO|LNOC ASSB shows an unprecedently higher ICE of 97.7% and lower polarization voltage than that of LCO|LPSC one, indicating a fast-established and electrochemically feasible solid-solid electrode/SE interface with suppressed side reactions, as shown in Fig. 2 (B and C). Moreover, Fig. 2D indicates that the oxidation potential between LPSC and LNOC does not occur even up to 7.0 V during LSV measurement, demonstrating the high-voltage stability of LPSC-LNOC nanocomposite in ASSBs. This chemical/electrochemical interface stabilities endow the LCO|LNOC cell with an excellent rate capability of 165.6 mAh g−1 at 1 C, much higher than that of LCO|LPSC ASSBs, as shown in Fig. 2 (E and F).

To better evaluate the intrinsic ionic diffusion capability, galvanostatic intermittent titration techniques were performed to determine the macroscopic diffusion coefficient (DLi+), as shown in fig. S9. The obtained Li+ ion diffusion coefficient is 1.58 × 10−8 cm2 s−1 of LCO|LNOC, approximately one order of magnitude higher than 0.13 × 10−8 cm2 s−1 of LCO|LPSC. To further clarify the interface dynamic evolution, the rate-dependent cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed in a voltage range of 0.1 to 1.0 mV s−1. By fitting the Randles-Sevcik equation (28), the average slopes of anode and cathode peaks are 0.54 and 0.51, respectively, indicating that the redox reaction kinetics is mainly controlled by the rapid Li+ ion diffusion process in LCO|LNOC ASSBs, as shown in fig. S10. This enhanced Li+ ion diffusion is closely associated with the optimized interfacial electronic environment induced by Nb-related interactions, together with the formation of a stable CSEI layer (20), which further accounts for the good electrochemical performance even at high rates. While the Li+ ion diffusion fluctuates obviously to imply the serious side reactions inside the CSEI of LCO|LPSC ASSBs as shown in fig. S10 (C and D), in consistence with the report by Sun et al. (28). Moreover, the spatial distribution of Li+ ions was simulated using a single-particle model, as shown in Fig. 2G. At the initial charging, the Li+ ions are preferentially extracted from the particle surface, resulting in a particle surface concentration lower than that of the bulk phase. With the consecutive lithium removal, the Li+ ion concentration shows a distinct gradient in LCO|LPSC, especially at the high-voltage stage. A strong concentration difference can induce stress distribution within the crystal and accelerate the particle cracks. In contrast, the Li+ ion concentration of LCO|LNOC changes uniformly throughout the cycling process owing to the optimized surface by LNOC phase, which promotes rapid and uniform lithium removal to thereby reduce the accumulation of mechanical stress.

Moreover, the cycling profile is demonstrated on the basis of the optimized surface chemistry. Figure 2H and fig. S11 compare the cyclability of LCO|LPSC and LCO|LNOC ASSBs, where the LCO|LNOC ASSB delivers a highly capacity retention of 95.8% after 500 cycles, while the capacity of LCO|LPSC ASSBs demonstrates a serious plunge in 200 cycles at 1.0 C. Of special interest is that such solid-solid interface-compatible strategy works either under a lower cutoff voltage of 4.3 V, as shown in figs. S12 to S14, or with even high-nickel layered cathode system, corresponding to ultrahigh energy density as shown in fig. S14 (E and F), which demonstrates the universal application in ASSBs. The capacity retention of ASSB with high-nickel cathode is still higher than 80% even after 500 cycles at 1.0 C. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis indicates that there is a stress concentration process inside the cycled LCO|LPSC particles, which accelerates the crack formation in an LCO cathode, as shown in Fig. 2H. Furthermore, an all-solid-state pouch cell was assembled to assess the engineering applicability of LCO|LNOC ASSB, as shown in Fig. 2 (I to K), which could still deliver a high initial efficiency at 0.1 C, as shown in Fig. 2J. Moreover, after 50 cycles at 0.2 C, the capacity retention of the pouch cell still exceeded 80%, showcasing excellent cycling stability, as shown in Fig. 2K. These results collectively prove that this low interface energy barrier design presents the promising application for practical industrial-scale solid-state batteries. Overall, through the interface reconstruction, the LCO|LNOC ASSB exhibits excellent cycle stability and rate performance due to the optimized CSEI layer.

Interface stability mechanism

Under 4.6 V, the highly oxidized Co4+/On- (0 < n < 2) ions on the LCO surface will probably result in severe structural distortions, leading to the surface Co/O ion loss and degradation (29–31). Using DFT simulations, the calculated differential charge density shows that there are obvious electron transfer between Co, Nb, and the surrounding O ions, and the electron transfer is enhanced obviously near the local Nb ions in the LCO/LNOC interface. Compared with Co-O bonds, the Nb-O bonds have higher electron affinity and bonding energies, which is conducive to stabilizing the lattice oxygen, especially at high cutoff voltages during cycling, as shown in Fig. 3 (A and B) and fig. S15. Therefore, with a Nb-O rich CSEI layer, the interfacial side reactions can be substantially reduced. Then, the thermal stability is shown in Fig. 3C and fig. S16 using thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC). For the LCO/LPSC composite cathode, the endothermic temperature decreases from 170.9°C at 0% to 130.5°C at 100% SOCs. In contrast, the endothermic temperature of LCO/LNOC composite cathode decreases from 192.9° to 134.9°C, which is slightly higher than that of the LCO/LPSC composite cathode. Moreover, at 100% SOC, the DSC curves go down more quickly in the LCO/LPSC composite cathode, indicating the faster heat release and decomposition-based weight loss, which implies the totally different reaction process at high delithiated states.

As shown in Fig. 3 (D and E), the differential electrochemical mass spectrometry is used to monitor the reactions at the LCO|SE interfaces, where during the formation cycle, the release of gases, such as CO2, CO, and O2 from LCO|LNOC, is substantially lower than those from LCO|LPSC ASSBs. This suggests the improved interface reaction kinetics by the stable CSEI layer in LCO|LNOC ASSBs, where the surface-active species, e.g., the Co-O dangling bonds/locally defective structures of LCO, are substantially saturated to effectively inhibit the decomposition of interphases and SE. The deposition of Nb-O/Nb-Cl/Nb-O-Cl species on the LCO surface provides an effective physical separation from the solid electrolyte, thereby suppressing interfacial side reactions and gas evolution. The interfacial properties of LCO|LNOC at different delithiation states were further investigated by ex situ x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 3 (F and G), the peaks at 209.4 and 206.5 eV correspond to the Nb 3d3/2 and 3d5/2, respectively, which include contributions from both Nb5+ and Nb4+-like species (20). Notably, the O 1s spectra indicates changes in the anionic redox–related species [O(2-n)− at ∼530.5 eV] at 4.6 V. Meanwhile, the slight relative intensity variations in the Nb5+ and Nb4+-like components are observed during cycling. However, these changes do not indicate a dominant Nb5+ and Nb4+ redox process. Instead, they suggest the localized and reversible electronic modulation at Nb sites associated with the dynamic evolution of the CSEI. This electronic adjustment is likely coupled with interfacial charge redistribution, which helps buffer excessive charge accumulation, suppress irreversible oxygen oxidation, and contribute to the stabilization of the interphase. To further investigate the electronic structure evolution of Nb species, the synchrotron-based x-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy was collected at different states of charge (SOCs, OCV, 100% SOC, and 0% SOC), as shown in fig. S17A. During cycling, a minor shift (probably within the operational error) in the absorption edge of Nb K-edge was observed, indicating that the electronic structure of Nb ion has been influenced slightly. The extended x-ray absorption fine structure R-space analysis shows that the first coordination shell of niobium is still composed of niobium-oxygen bonds, which is very similar to that of Nb2O5, as shown in fig. S17B. Note that the overall spectral features still retain the characteristics of the high-valence state, while the minor changes at the edge indicate that a small portion of Nb sites may not be fully oxidized, reflecting the local and dynamic charge redistribution within the phase interface. From O 1s XPS, the content of O(2-n)− related species at highly charged state decreases substantially, as shown in Fig. 3F, in line with the reported results (32–34). This phenomenon further indicates that there is a strong interaction between Nb ions and oxidized O(2-n)− substances. This electron regulation is coupled with the lattice oxygen evolution. Through local electron buffering, it inhibits oxygen oxidation and promotes the dynamic stability of CSEI, rather than forming the dominant Nb5+/Nb4+ redox process.

Figure 3 (I to O) and figs. S18 and S19 show the interface impedance changes during the formation cycle of ASSBs by in situ EIS combined with the DRT analysis (24, 35). As shown in Fig. 3H, the impedance changes in LCO|LPSC interface occur mainly in the medium/low-frequency regions, where it increases from 1.0 to 1.5 kilohm when charged to 4.6 V versus Li/Li+. This substantial impedance increase is attributed to the irreversible oxygen oxidation at the formation charge, which intrigues the active oxygen ions and subsequent oxidation of LCO|LPSC interface. These interface changes will obviously hinder the transport of Li+ ion and lead to the further interface deteriorations. In contrast, the impedance of LCO|LNOC shows a small change in the middle/low-frequency regions, increasing only from 50 to 250 ohm, which is substantially lower than that of LCO|LPSC interface, as shown in Fig. 3L. This result assures the fast Li+ ion transport kinetics, especially at 4.6 V, with the reduced interface impedance during long cycling at the LCO|LNOC interface. Further, DRT analysis demonstrates that the impedance rises sharply over the charged 4.4 V at the LCO|LPSC interface, while it shows a gradual rise even at charged 4.6 V, clearly revealing the suppressed impedance due to an ultrastable CSEI layer at the LCO/LNOC interface, as shown in Fig. 3 (I and M). The subsequent EIS aging tests indicate that the interface resistance (Rint) of LCO|LPSC-based ASSB increases rapidly from 2.0 to 4.0 kilohm while that of LCO|LNOC ASSBs only increases to 600 ohm, as shown in Fig. 3 (J and N). The corresponding DRT results manifest that the resistance fluctuation of Rint with the relaxation time at the 10−1 to 10−2 range indicates a much more unstable interphase structure, thus the sluggish Li+ transport at the LCO|LPSC interface. However, the Rint at the LCO|LNOC interface is substantially lower with fast Li+ ion diffusion kinetics and remains stable after 60 hours, as shown in Fig. 3 (K and O) and fig. S19.

Stress distribution in LCO|LNOC interface

To understand the enhanced cycling stability, the structural reversibility of LCO was evaluated further, as shown in Fig. 4. It is well established that the layered LCO undergoes O3/H1-H3 phase transitions when charged above 4.5 V, which are closely associated with structural instability (36). Because of the challenges associated with implementing in situ XRD measurements in solid-state battery systems, an optimized ex situ XRD approach was used here. As revealed by the ex situ XRD, both LCO|LPSC and LCO|LNOC systems exhibit the characteristic evolution of the (003) reflection during cycling, as shown in Fig. 4 (A and D). Upon charging to ∼4.4 V, the (003) peak shifts toward lower angles, indicating an expansion of the interlayer spacing along the c axis driven by enhanced electrostatic repulsion between CoO6 slabs upon delithiation. With further charging to 4.6 V, the (003) peak shifts back toward higher angles, reflecting a pronounced contraction at deep delithiation states and reversibly returns during discharge. Notably, in the LCO|LPSC system, the clear splitting of the (003) reflection at ∼4.4 V and its persistence up to 4.6 V provide direct evidence of multiple phase coexistence, characteristic of the H1-H3 phase transition in layered LCO (37, 38). In sharp contrast, no observable peak splitting is detected in the LCO|LNOC system but only a slight and continuous peak shift, indicating that the H1-H3 phase transition is delayed effectively. Moreover, the overall peak shift in LCO|LNOC is substantially smaller than that in LCO|LPSC, suggesting the reduced lattice expansion and contraction during cycling, as shown in Fig. 4 (B and E) and tables S4 and S5. These results collectively indicate that the intermediate phase of CSEI layer at LCO|LNOC interface effectively reduces structural inhomogeneity and suppresses excessive lattice fluctuations, thereby enhancing the structural stability of LCO at high voltages. The dQ/dV curves present that within 200 cycles, the redox peaks of LCO|LNOC ASSB display much more reversible and consistent than those of LCO|LPSC ASSB, indicating a stabilized LCO|LNOC composite cathode during cycling, as shown in Fig. 4 (C and F). This irreversible cell volume expansions/contractions lead to more cracks of the cycled LCO particles in LCO|LPSC ASSB, as shown in Fig. 4G and reported result (39, 40). Furthermore, the SEM cross-sectional analysis was conducted to examine the interfacial integrity of the double-layer electrolyte after cycling (fig. S21A). No obvious cracks or elemental interdiffusion were observed in the middle region of the LNOC|LPSC interface, indicating that the double-layer structure maintains good structural stability after cycling. The Young’s modulus of LPSC is ∼20 GPa, whereas that of LNOC is substantially lower (∼4 GPa) (41, 42). This contrast enables LPSC to serve as a mechanically robust framework to provide structural support, while the LNOC acts as a more compliant interfacial layer that accommodates strain and buffers volume changes during cycling. In addition, the thermogravimetric analysis confirms that no obvious side reactions or decomposition occur between LPSC and LNOC, further supporting the chemical compatibility and stability of the bilayer interface, as shown in fig. S21B. After all, these results demonstrate that the LNOC|LPSC interface remains both mechanically and chemically stable in ASSBs.

To analyze the CSEI composition, the XPS was used to study the chemical states of the cycled composite cathodes and pristine LCO powder, as shown in Fig. 4 (H to J) and fig. S20. Before testing, the cathode surfaces were etched by Ar+ ion gun to remove possible surface contaminations. The O 2p XPS of pristine LCO is shown in Fig. 4H for reference, and the XPS peaks at 530.5, 531.8, and 533.1 eV correspond to the crystal O ions adjacent to transition metal (TM) ions (TM-O bonds) and the surface adsorbed oxygen species, respectively (23, 43, 44). After 100 cycles, the XPS peak areas of the TM-O in LCO|LNOC are much larger than those in LCO|LPSC, which indicates the electrochemically more stable LCO surface without obviously compromising the outmost surface TM-O species in LCO|LNOC ASSBs. Moreover, the Co 2p XPS corresponds to a normal Co3+ ion after 100 cycles of all LCO cathodes, as shown in Fig. 4I. While the S 2p XPS of LPSC shows a double peak with the normal S 2p3/2 state at 161.4 eV, as shown in Fig. 4J and fig. S20. After 200 cycles, the S 2p XPS peak of LCO|LPSC cathode presents two additional double peaks at 163.2 and 164.9 eV, corresponding to the S-S/CoSx by-products, which play the negative roles in the Li+ ion transport within the composite cathode as attested from the aforementioned electrochemistry. It is also challengeable in directly using LPSC to match with high-voltage cathodes.

Interface establishment in LCO|LNOC ASSB

The time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry was performed to scrutinize the spatial distribution of various chemicals in CSEI and further validate this unique LCO|LNOC interface. The 3D variation of Nb-O/Nb-Cl segments indicate that there is an Nb-O/Nb-Cl–rich interface layer along the edge of LCO particles, which can saturate the highly active oxygen ions as many as possible upon charging, as shown in Fig. 5 (A and B). Moreover, the interface mapping results also show that there is an apparently higher content of Nb with respect to those of Co and O elements. This proves that the employment of Nb-based Cl-rich filler can effectively decrease the electrochemically peeling of Co and O ions from the active LCO cathode surface upon cycling, and as a result, a lattice oxygen stable structure between LCO|LNOC interfaces is in situ self-constructed during the cycling. Moreover, the spatial distributions of Co+(Co3+ ions in LCO), S−, and Nb− species can be used to precisely locate the LCO, LPSCl, and LNOC species, respectively, where a complex interface reaction and ionic diffusion kinetics are disclosed accordingly. Various parasitic substances and processes can be observed, including the CoCl3−, LiCl−, NbO−, NbCl3−, LiCl−, and CoCl3− segments, due to the reaction of LNOC and LCO under high voltages. Of special interest is that the signals of NbCl3− and NbO− radicals are examined on the surface, especially on the 3D structural surface, which further confirm the formation of a uniform thin CSEI layer inside the LCO|LNOC composite cathode, as shown in Fig. 5A. These results strongly prove that the CSEI of LCO|LNOC contains Li-Cl/Nb-O/Nb-Cl/Nb-Cl-O components, which can substantially reduce the chemical degradation and ensure the rapid transmission of Li+ ion in the composite cathode. On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 5B, the intensities of O2P− and SO− segments mainly present inside the 3D structure, indicating the thick enough CSEI layer that is not capable of stabilize LCO in long-term cycling of LCO|LPSC cathode. After all, even in the formation cycle, there are many electric/electrochemical processes progressing, including the decomposition and reaction of interface fillers (LNOC and LPSC), construction of electron/ion conduction network, charged particles diffusion (Nb-O/Nb-Cl segments), and physical/structural stability (interface inflation and gas release) to establish the expected electrochemical interphase, which bridges the electrode and the electrolyte, and further make the ASSBs different from the semisolid or organic liquid–based Li+ ion batteries.

The morphological and structural changes of the long-term cycled LCO|LPSC and LCO|LNOC composite cathodes were then directly analyzed using HRTEM, and the images were acquired after 50, 100, and 500 cycles, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5 (C and D). In LCO|LNOC composite cathodes, a thin CSEI layer (∼1.7 nm) is formed on the LCO surface in the initial cycles. After 500 cycles, the CSEI thickness is increased to ∼2.13 nm, and this robust CSEI layer should play a key role in keeping the good layered structure of LCO cathode, as shown in Fig. 5C. But for LCO|LPSC interphase, the CSEI layer thickness grows in disorder, probably by the fierce reactions between LPSC and LCO, as well as the surface exfoliation. After 500 cycles, the CSEI layer is increased to about 10 nm with apparent fluctuations, and the LCO surface structure is probably transformed into a rock-salt phase, as shown in Fig. 5D. This phase transition process can propagate from the surface into the bulk (∼50 nm), indicating the severe structural distortions of LCO cathode. Furthermore, by comparing EIS with different numbers of cycles, it was found that the interfacial impedance of LCO|LNOC is much lower than that of LCO|LPSC, as shown in Fig. 5 (E and F). It is found that the (003) and (018)/(110) peaks clearly present in the cycled LCO|LNOC to show the structural stability/reversibility of LCO, while they are nearly absent in LCO|LPSC, to indicate the deteriorated structural changes and particle pulverization upon cycling, as confirmed by the XRD patterns of cycled composite electrodes in Fig. 5G. After all, the robust thin CSEI layer in LCO|LNOC can effectively inhibit the side reactions at the interface, indicating that the continuously enhanced CSEI layer is essential to improve the structural stability of LCO at 4.6 V, as well as the concerned interface compatibility (45–47).

DISCUSSION

In summary, by designing a composite cathode containing only sulfide solid electrolytes (SSEs; LPSC and LNOC) and LCO, the interface incompatibility between a cathode and an SE is well resolved. At the LCO/LNOC interface, the interaction between Nb and O self-constructs a lattice-oxygen stable interface, reducing irreversible oxygen loss, stabilizing the surface structure, and simultaneously forming a CSEI layer rich in Li-Cl/Nb-Cl/Nb-O/Nb-Cl-O on the surface, which can effectively inhibit the phase transition and structural collapse of LCO at high voltage. Therefore, the LCO|LNOC ASSB delivers good electrochemical performance, achieving the high reversible capacity of 189.7 mAh g−1 and ICE of 97.7%, as well as the capacity retention of 95.8% after 500 cycles. Moreover, the all-solid-state pouch battery assembled by combining LNOC and LCO cathodes demonstrates a formation Coulombic efficiency of more than 90% at 0.1 C and a stable cycling performance of more than 50 cycles at 0.2 C, highlighting the practical feasibility. This rational design of the high-voltage LCO cathode/SSE interface provides effective insights into interface compatibility and new strategies for improving the reversibility of lattice oxygen redox and energy density of ASSBs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Preparation of SEs

To prepare LiNbOCl4 SEs, the starting materials were LiOH (Sigma-Aldrich, >99.9%) and NbCl5 (Sigma-Aldrich, >99.9%). They were weighed and mixed in a molar ratio of LiOH/NbCl5 = 1/1 in an Ar-filled glove box. Afterward, the mixtures were then loaded into ZrO2 pots containing ZrO2 balls (φ5 mm) and mechanochemically milled by planetary ball milling for 40 hours at 500 rpm. In this synthetic process, the following stoichiometric reaction takes place, viz., LiOH + NbCl5 → LiNbOCl4 + HCl (gas). The final targeted product of LiNbOCl4 SE powder was got at the end.

Preparation of LPSC powder

The SSE Li6PS5Cl was synthesized via high-energy mechanical ball milling. All procedures were conducted in an Ar-filled glove box with controlled atmosphere, where both H2O and O2 levels were kept below 0.1 parts per million (ppm). The stoichiometric amounts of Li2S (Macklin, purity >99.9%), LiCl (Macklin, >99.9%), and P2S5 (Macklin, >99.9%) were precisely weighed according to a molar ratio of 5:2:1. The powders were then loaded into a ball-milling jar along with ZrO2 balls at a ball-to-powder ratio of 25:1. The jar was hermetically sealed with Parafilm to prevent atmospheric contamination. Subsequent mechanical milling was performed using a planetary ball mill operated at 500 rpm for 20 hours. The resultant amorphous powder was uniaxially pressed into pellets under a pressure of 2 tons (∼250 MPa). These pellets were vacuum-sealed in quartz tubes and subjected to a heat treatment process in a muffle furnace. The annealing protocol was consisted of heating to 550°C at a rate of 5°C min−1, keeping for 12 hours, followed by a natural cooling process to room temperature. The crystallized Li6PS5Cl product was lastly ground and sieved through a 400-mesh screen for further use.

Preparation of ASSBs

The composite cathode of LCO|SE|Li-In ASSB consisted of LCO powder, LPSC SE, and vapor-grown carbon fiber (VGCF), which were mixed in a 70:29:1 (wt %) for 30 min through a mortar and pestle. The Li-In alloy anode was prepared by mechanical pressing a piece of In foil (∼25 mg, 99.999%) with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 50 μm and a piece of Li foil (∼1 mg) with a diameter of 8 mm and a thickness of 30 μm together. The fabrication of ASSBs has the following procedures.

1) The 90 mg of LPSC powder was added into the Swagelok model cell and pressed under 125 MPa (1 ton) for 1 min to form a flat surface;

2) The 15.0 mg of composite cathode material was uniformly spread onto the surface of SSE layer and pressed together under 873 MPa (7 tons) for 1 min;

3) The In foil was placed on the other side of SSE layer;

4) The Li foil was placed on the surface of In foil;

5) Last, the four-layer pellet was formed by pressing at 250 MPa (2 tons) for 1 min. Then, the four-layer pellet cell was sandwiched between two stainless steel rods and sealed in a Swagelok model cell.

The galvanostatic charge-discharge was conducted on a LAND CT2001A battery testing system (Wuhan LAND Electronics Co. Ltd.). The voltage window was set as 3.0 to 4.6 V (versus Li+/Li). The low temperature performance was evaluated by operating LCO|SE|Li-In ASSBs at each temperature from −15° to 65°C, respectively. The holding time for each temperature was at least 8 hours.

Fabrication of pouch-type full cell

The cathode was prepared using a solvent-free method. The specific steps were as follows. The LCO, LNOC, and VGCFs were mixed in a mass ratio of 80:18.5:0.5, followed by the addition of 1.0 wt % polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). The mixture was repeatedly fibrillated and pressed into a film, which was then transferred onto a carbon-coated aluminum foil. The cathode film was formed using a rolling process with a 90-μm stainless steel spacer to control the thickness and densification. The anode was prepared using a similar procedure, where graphite and an LNOC electrolyte were mixed in a mass ratio of 1.5:1, followed by the addition of 1 wt % PTFE.

The solid electrolyte membrane was also fabricated via a solvent-free process. Specifically, the side facing the cathode consisted of an LNOC-based electrolyte layer containing 1.0 wt % PTFE, while the other side facing the anode was an LPSC solid electrolyte membrane with 1.0 wt % PTFE. The electrolyte layers were rolled using a 100-μm stainless steel spacer to ensure uniform thickness. The resulting bilayer electrolyte structure provides a thickness ratio of approximately 1:2 between the cathode and electrolyte layers.

The electrodes and electrolyte sheets were cut using an air-powered punch press, with dimensions of 40 mm by 60 mm for the cathode and 42 mm by 62 mm for both the anode and electrolyte sheets. The tabs were connected to the current collectors via ultrasonic welding. All components were stacked and sealed in a laminated pouch under vacuum, followed by isostatic pressing at 450 MPa for 90 s and subsequent hot pressing at 60°C to ensure intimate interfacial contact. During electrochemical testing, a constant external stack pressure of 30 MPa was applied to the pouch cell to maintain stable solid-solid contact. All preparation and assembly processes were carried out in an Ar-filled glove box (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).

Methods

Material characterization

Powder XRD patterns were collected with an Ultima IV. The XRD cells containing hermetically sealed SSE samples with a polyimide film were mounted on the XRD diffractometer and measured at 40 kV and 40 mA from 10° to 80° at a scan rate of 2.4° min−1. XPS was performed using a PHI 5000 Versa Probe III with a 200-μm monochromatic Al Kα x-ray source and an inert atmosphere transfer system. The charge compensation was achieved by dual beam charge neutralization, and the binding energy was corrected by setting the binding energy of the hydrocarbon C 1s peak to 284.8 eV. SEM (TESCAN MIRA3) equipped with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS; Bruker) add-on was used to characterize the morphologies and elemental distributions, respectively.

Electrochemical characterization

Before measuring the ionic conductivity, the powders were loaded into electrically insulating cylindrical cells made of Al2O3 with a diameter of φ10 mm and compressed at 240 MPa without heat treatment. Following this, the carbon electrodes were added to both sides, and ac impedance tests were conducted using symmetrical C/SSEs/C cells. Electrochemical workstation (IVIUM nSTAT) was used to measure EIS in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 1 Hz at an amplitude of 10 mV. Conductivity values were determined using the standard formula σ = L/RS, where L represents the particle thickness, S represents the pellet area, and R is the resistance determined by the value of Z′ at the inflection point in the Nyquist plot. The In/SSE/In symmetric cells were used for variable-temperature ac impedance testing, which was monitored by the HHTC−30-40-F and measured continuously over a range of temperatures. Two stainless steel sheets were used as closed electrodes to measure the produced halide electrolyte’s conductivity using dc polarization. Specifically, ∼100 mg of milled halide powder was loaded into a 10-mm-diameter Al2O3 mold and cold pressed into pellets for 5 min to obtain a symmetric SS/halide electrolyte/SS structure, which was loaded into a sealed jar filled with Ar. Last, the assembled cell was subjected to current response measurements under the action of a 0.5-V voltage for 12 hours. For the all-solid-state half-cells, Li-In electrodes were used as both the counter and reference electrodes. To create the composite working electrode, the LCO, LNOC, and VGCF powders were mixed in a 70:29:1 weight ratio and then manually milled them for 30 min. To begin cell assembly, ∼50 mg of LPSC SE was put into an Al2O3 cylinder and compacted at 120 MPa for 1 min using a hydraulic press. Taking ∼100 mg of the synthesized halide powders coats evenly on one side of LPSC pellet and presses at 120 MPa for 1 min to create the SSE layer. On the other side of the compact halide pellet, ∼8.3-mg composite cathode powders (active mass loading of ∼7.3 mg cm−2) were uniformly distributed and pressed at 240 MPa for 5 min. Last, a Li-In electrode was placed to one side of LPSC and squeezed at 20 MPa. The ASSBs underwent an external pressure of 30 MPa.

The EIS was tested by Zahner Zennium pro. The multichannel battery testing device (LANHE CT2001A) was used to test the batteries’ charge-discharge cycles over a voltage range of 2.4 to 4.2 V versus Li-In.

To analyze the electrochemical stability window of the as-prepared SSEs, the CV was conducted using an electrochemical workstation (IVIUM nSTAT), consisting of Li-In/Li6PS5Cl/halide SSEs/SSEs + graphite configuration at room temperature. The scan rate was set to 1.0 mV s−1. The composite working electrode was prepared by grinding the mixture of halide electrolytes and graphite in a weight ratio of 1:1. The characterization of each cell started from the OCV, went through the oxidative reversal potential, and returned back. Each operation examined three sequential reversal potential cycles. The mass loading of working composite electrode is 50 mg.

The ex situ XRD measurements were carried out to probe the structural evolution of the LCO cathode at different SOCs. To minimize the deviation between the electrochemical state and the structural characterization, a modified sampling protocol was adopted. Specifically, the cells were charged using a constant current–constant voltage protocol to the target voltage (e.g., 4.4 or 4.6 V), followed by a constant-voltage holding step for ∼10 min to stabilize the electrochemical state. After reaching the desired state, the cells were rapidly transferred into an Ar-filled glove box (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm) and disassembled without resting to minimize voltage relaxation and Li redistribution. The harvested cathode samples were carefully extracted and directly sealed with Kapton film to prevent air exposure. The sealed samples were then mounted on a multisample XRD holder and measured without delay. The XRD patterns were collected using a Cu Kα radiation source, with particular attention to the (003) reflection of LCO. The total time from cell disassembly to XRD measurement was minimized (typically within several minutes) to preserve the high-SOC structural state. This protocol effectively reduces artifacts associated with ex situ measurements, such as voltage relaxation and structural deviation, and ensures that the obtained XRD patterns more accurately reflect the intrinsic structural evolution at high voltage.
