Invited Perspective: Is It Time to Revisit the Allowable Maximum Contaminant Limit for Arsenic in Public Drinking Water?
Authors: Jaymie Meliker, Meroona Gopang
Published: 2024

As we have discussed previously,1 both considerable interest and some controversy surround the assessment of health risks associated with exposure to moderately elevated levels of arsenic in drinking water (up to 100μg/L). In 1999, the National Research Council (NRC) Subcommittee on Arsenic in Drinking Water, for instance, wrote, “No human studies of sufficient statistical power or scope have examined whether consumption of arsenic in drinking water at the current MCL [maximum contaminant level; then 50μg/L] results in an increased incidence of cancer or noncancer effects.”2 Yet, the subcommittee simultaneously concluded that that guideline did not protect public health “and, therefore, requires downward revision as promptly as possible.”2 The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) recommended a reduction in the MCL to 10μg/L for arsenic in US public drinking water supplies, which went into effect in 20063 and was identical to the provisional guideline recommended by the World Health Organization.4

Over the past 20 years, several studies have suggested an increase in cardiovascular risk associated with drinking water arsenic at concentrations ranging from 10 to 50μg/L,1,5–11 supporting the EPA’s move to an MCL below 50μg/L. Elucidating risk at exposures below 10μg/L, however, has remained a challenge due to relatively small study populations in individual-level studies, and difficulties categorizing people into groups with narrow exposure ranges (e.g., 5–10μg/L) over a long duration.

The study by Medgyesi et al.12 published in this issue of Environmental Health Perspectives begins to overcome these challenges by leveraging the California Teachers Study, which included over 98,000 women with 1.8 million person-years of follow-up, and over 6,000 ischemic heart disease (IHD) cases and 9,000 cardiovascular disease (CVD) cases. Although no exposure reconstruction is perfect, Medgyesi et al.12 admirably included self-reported residential histories, historic data on community water supplies’ arsenic levels and service boundaries, different time windows of exposure, and a series of sensitivity analyses.

Medgyesi et al.12 reported significantly elevated hazard ratios for IHD at both 5.00–9.99μg/L and ≥10μg/L. They noted effects were weaker for a measure of CVD, which included IHD and stroke outcomes, raising questions as to whether stroke outcomes are as sensitive as IHD to lower levels of arsenic exposure, consistent with some studies.6,13,14 They also observed the strongest risks linked with exposures over the 10 years prior to an IHD outcome, consistent with a study in Chile that found mortality rates for acute myocardial infarction were highest during and immediately after a period of high arsenic exposure (1958–1970).15

Collectively, the strengths of Medgyesi et al.,12 combined with emerging consistencies in the recent epidemiologic literature, point to the likelihood that there is a cardiovascular risk from drinking water arsenic below 10μg/L. In fact, in vivo research recently showed that low concentrations of arsenic (down to 10 ppb) induced atherosclerosis in ApoE−/− mice.16 When coupled with mechanistic evidence that arsenic induces endothelial dysfunction, increases reactive oxygen species, and impairs nitric oxide balance (all of which can enhance atherogenesis),17 this provides additional evidence supporting the idea that low levels of arsenic exposure can increase CVD risks. We therefore cannot help but wonder whether the current MCL needs to be revisited to protect the public’s health and safety.

By its very nature, revising an MCL is a contentious process. EPA and advisory members must contend with uncertainties in risk from low-level exposure. Although Medgyesi et al.12 provide some of the most compelling evidence of risk below 10μg/L, there remains uncertainty regarding the appropriate threshold for a revised MCL: Should it be 5μg/L, 3μg/L, 1μg/L, or even lower—or should it remain at 10μg/L? Furthermore, public water suppliers face real costs if the MCL is reduced. Arsenic is a natural contaminant in groundwater with elevated concentrations found throughout many parts of the US.2 Last time the MCL for arsenic was reduced in 2006, the EPA dispersed grants to assist small water suppliers who needed to update their water treatment technologies to comply with the updated standard; it is likely this type of assistance for technological upgrades would be needed again.

Financial considerations and scientific uncertainty, however, need to be balanced with the public’s health and well-being, as well as the need for the public to feel confident that the EPA is protecting us from harm in our drinking water. The American public is at a crossroads of distrust in government; according to a survey by the Pew Research Center, 16% of respondents reported in 2023 that they trust the government “just about always” or “most of the time,” which was among the lowest measure in nearly seven decades of polling.18 We believe the EPA, in reviewing our drinking water MCLs, can play a pivotal role in helping to rebuild trust in government.

The EPA has already begun to go down this path, recommending the first new MCL for a drinking water contaminant since 2000: We now have provisional MCLs for different per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances at 10 ng/L (or 0.01μg/L),19 and discussions about regulating 1,4-dioxane at levels near or below 1μg/L as well.20 In addition, New Jersey and New Hampshire have recently reduced the MCL for arsenic in drinking water to 5μg/L in light of increasing evidence of low-dose arsenic-related CVD risk.21 The EPA’s responsibility is to protect the health and welfare of the population and to assess the competing risks and benefits of different guidelines; it is time for the EPA to seriously consider a reevaluation of the arsenic MCL for public drinking water.
