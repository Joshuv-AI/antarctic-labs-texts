Tensor-variate Gaussian process regression for efficient emulation of complex systems: comparing regressor and covariance structures in outer product and parallel partial emulators. Thanks: Submitted to the editors 2024-12-12. This work was supported by the Chemical and Biological Technologies Department (contract HDTRA1-17-C-0028) and the UK Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (grants EP/R009902/1 and EP/S019472/1).
D. Semochkina, S. E. Jackson, D. C. Woods

Abstract
Multi-output Gaussian process regression has become an important tool in uncertainty quantification, for building emulators of computationally expensive simulators, and other areas such as multi-task machine learning. We present a holistic development of tensor-variate Gaussian process (TvGP) regression, appropriate for arbitrary dimensional outputs where a Kronecker product structure is appropriate for the covariance. We show how two common approaches to problems with two-dimensional output, outer product emulators (OPE) and parallel partial emulators (PPE), are special cases of TvGP regression and hence can be extended to higher output dimensions. Focusing on the important special case of matrix output, we investigate the relative performance of these two approaches. The key distinction is the additional dependence structure assumed by the OPE, and we demonstrate when this is advantageous through two case studies, including application to a spatial-temporal influenza simulator.



Keywords

Emulation, Gaussian process regression, Separable covariance

1
Introduction

Scientific processes are commonly modeled using mathematical models implemented in computer codes, or simulators, that encapsulate the key features of the system and facilitate prediction and decision-making
[
9
]
.
When these simulators are computationally expensive, it is common to approximate them using statistical emulators, most commonly a Gaussian process, constructed from computer experiments
[
35
]
. When the simulator output is dynamic, evolving over dimensions such as space or time, development of efficient and effective emulators is more challenging.

As motivation, we study a simulator of the spatio-temporal dynamics of an influenza epidemic, developed by the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA). The code implements an SEIR model
[
19
]
that describes the time progression of a closed population through the states
Susceptible
,
Exposed
,
Infected
and
Recovered
. The model also has a discrete spatial dimension, made up of a set of “patches” representing different urban communities. Movement between these patches is controlled via a commuter matrix which simulates flows of people for work, education and leisure. While there is a well-understood, although intractable, set of differential equations governing this system, for the purposes of this paper we treat the simulator as a block box modeling the number of infected individuals and each simulator run having as output a matrix of spatio-temporal responses (numbers of infections for each time and patch).

Previous work
[
20
,
12
,
7
,
31
]
on emulating simulators with dynamic or, relatedly, multivariate output has focused on vector-valued responses, where the output from each run is unstructured or the structure is preserved in, or collapsed onto, a single dimension. Most commonly, the covariance kernel for the Gaussian process emulator is formed as the product of two separate kernels describing, respectively, relationships between simulator inputs and simulator output indices. Two approaches which have seen particular application are (i) the Outer Product Emulator
[
34
]
(OPE), which extends the separability to include the mean function with a closely related model applied to spatio-temporal simulators
[
4
]
; and (ii) the Parallel Partial Emulator
[
18
]
(PPE), which assumes separate emulators across the dynamic output indices with common regression functions, but distinct regression parameters, and common kernel functions with shared parameters.

Motivated by work in the machine learning literature
[
40
,
11
,
41
,
10
]
, in this paper, we cast OPE and PPE as special cases of a tensor-variate Gaussian process (TvGP) which allows modeling of outputs of arbitrary dimension represented as tensors using separable covariance kernels (Section
2
). We then perform an empirical comparison of the effectiveness of OPE and PPE via two case studies: the SEIR patch model described above (Section
3
) and a synthetic environmental simulator
[
5
]
of a pollutant spillage from a chemical accident (Section
4
). A primary aim of these studies is to assess the contribution of the different assumptions made for the two emulators, and to attempt to gain insight about situations when imposing beliefs about correlation structure is important. As such, a discussion of the results and the differences between the two emulators are also provided (Section
5
and Appendix
B
). Code for implementing the methods in this paper and reproducing the results is available at
https://github.com/samjacksonstats/TVGP
.

2
Multivariate Emulation

2.1
Separable emulators

Consider a generic multivariate simulator, represented by the function
\mathbf{f}
, which takes a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
of input parameters, and outputs a vector
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. We choose to represent our beliefs about the behavior of the simulator output for any input
\mathbf{x}
in the following form
[
16
]
:

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\bm{\mu}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(1)

where

\bm{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
is a vector-valued mean function, typically expressed as a linear predictor, and
\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x})
is an
r
-dimensional stochastic residual process aiming to capture residual variation across
\mathcal{X}
.

A prior belief specification across the collection
\{\bm{\mu}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\}
leads to a prior specification about simulator output
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
at any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Such specification is not trivial; however, it is common for the specification to assume
{\rm E}[\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x})]=0
,
{\rm Cov}\left[\bm{\mu}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x})\right]=\mathbf{0}
, and a separable covariance structure between the value of
\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, at two inputs
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, of the following form
[
12
]
:

{\rm Cov}\left[\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]=\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,;\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\Sigma\,.

(2)

This assumption has been shown to be very effective for constructing emulators for multivariate simulators with large
r
. Covariance between the output components at any given input is given by the product of the positive semi-definite coregionalization matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
and
the stationary kernel function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, parameterised by
\bm{\theta}_{0}
(although we will drop the
\bm{\theta}_{0}
in this expression from here-on in, assuming that such a parameterization is implicit). A common choice is the Gaussian correlation function
[
27
,
3
]
:

\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\sum_{h=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{x_{h}-x^{\prime}_{h}}{\theta_{0,h}}\right\}^{2}\right),

(3)

which requires specification of correlation length parameters
\theta_{0,h},h=1,\dots,p
.

One particular specification of a prior stochastic process for
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
with these properties is a multivariate Gaussian process (MvGP)
[
12
,
31
]
,

\mathbf{f}^{T}(\cdot)\sim\mathcal{M}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(\bm{\mu}^{\top}(\cdot),\,\kappa(\cdot,\cdot),\Sigma)\,.

(4)

This process is a multivariate generalisation for
r
-dimensional outputs of the usual one-dimensional Gaussian process
[
32
]
, and captures many of the representations developed throughout the literature.

2.2
Tensor-variate Gaussian processes

There are several commonly observed structures in
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
, for example with entries corresponding to a spatial location in two dimensions or defined via space-time coordinates in higher dimensions. In Sections
3
and
4
we emulate simulators with two-dimensional space-time coordinates, that is, input
\mathbf{x}
produces outputs

\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{pmatrix}f_{11}(\mathbf{x})&\ldots&f_{1r_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{r_{1}1}(\mathbf{x})&\ldots&f_{r_{1}r_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\end{pmatrix}.

Here
f_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x};s_{i_{1}},t_{i_{2}})
represents model output for spatial grid point
s_{i_{1}}\in\mathcal{S},i_{1}=1,\ldots,r_{1}
at time
t_{i_{2}}\in\mathcal{T},i_{2}=1,\ldots,r_{2}
corresponding to the model input parameters
\mathbf{x}
. Sets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
can be thought of as discretised sets of points within continuous spatial and temporal domains
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{T}
respectively. That is,
\mathcal{S}
is a set of coordinate points of interest over a geographical region represented by
\mathbb{S}
, and
\mathcal{T}
is a set of time increments (hours or days) over time
\mathbb{T}
.
More generally, if the output arises on a regular grid of higher-order coordinates, the simulator output can be represented by an
m
-dimensional array or tensor
\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq f_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\,:\,\times_{z=1}^{m}r_{z}
, with
i
th dimension having size
r_{z}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\top}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
.

Such output can be modeled using Equation (
4
) via a vectorization operation
[
30
]

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\textrm{vec}\left({\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x})}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{\mathbf{r}}}f_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\,\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathbf{r}}_{i_{1}:i_{m}}\,,

(5)

where
\mathbf{i}_{\mathbf{r}}=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\,:\,1\leq i_{z}\leq r_{z},\,1\leq z\leq m\}
,
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathbf{r}}_{i_{1}:i_{m}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{r_{1}}_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\varepsilon}^{r_{m}}_{i_{m}}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}^{r}_{i}
is a
r
-dimensional unit vector with
i
th entry 1. However, the coregionalization matrix would have dimension
r=\prod_{z=1}^{m}r_{z}
, possibly making inference and prediction from Equation (
4
) computationally infeasible except in special cases such as output independence and diagonal
\Sigma
, as utilized by the PPE.

Alternatively, if an assumption of separability between the output dimensions is reasonable, the coregionalization matrix can be decomposed as

\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{m}\,,

(6)

leading to a
tensor-variate Gaussian process
(TvGP)

\mathscr{F}(\cdot)\sim\mathcal{T}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{M}(\cdot),\,\kappa(\cdot,\cdot),\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m})\,,

(7)

with
\mathscr{M}\,:\,\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{m}}
a
m
-dimensional tensor-variate function. Such a process can be defined via a linear combination of independent univariate standard normal random variables,
\mathbf{u}^{\rm T}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
,
u_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, via

\textrm{vec}\left({\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x})}\right)=\textrm{vec}\left({\mathscr{M}(\mathbf{x})}\right)+\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}\,,

where
\Sigma
has the Kronecker product form from Equation (
6
) and any matrix square root can be used. In an analogy to Equation (
1
), such a process can also be expressed as

\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathscr{M}(\mathbf{x})+\mathscr{E}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(8)

where
\mathscr{E}\coloneqq e_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\,:\,\times_{z=1}^{m}r_{z}
and
\mathscr{E}\sim\mathcal{T}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{Z},\,\kappa(\cdot,\cdot),\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m})
, with
\mathscr{Z}
the tensor with all components equal to 0.

A consequence of assuming this prior is that, for a collection of
n
input vectors
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}
, the
n\times r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{m}
output tensor
\mathscr{F}=\{\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}),\ldots,\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(n)})\}
follows a tensor, or multi-linear, normal distribution
[
30
]
of dimension
m+1

\mathscr{F}\sim\mathcal{T}\mathcal{N}_{n,r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}}(\mathscr{M},K,\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m})\,,

with
m+1
dimensional mean tensor
\mathscr{M}=\{\mathscr{M}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}),\ldots,\mathscr{M}(\mathbf{x}^{(n)})\}
and
K
having
jk
-th entry
\kappa(\mathbf{x}^{(j)},\mathbf{x}^{(k)})
. Vectorization of
\mathscr{F}
results in a multivariate normal distribution

\textrm{vec}\left(\mathscr{F}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}_{nr}(\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{M}),K\otimes(\otimes_{i=1}^{m}\Sigma))\,.

(9)

TvGP regression is defined via the joint distribution of
\mathscr{F}
and
\tilde{\mathscr{F}}=\{\mathscr{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)}),\ldots,\mathscr{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(n^{\prime})})\}
at new inputs
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(n^{\prime})}
, given by

\{\mathscr{F},\tilde{\mathscr{F}}\}\sim\mathcal{TN}_{n+n^{\prime},r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}}\left(\mathscr{M}_{+},K_{+},\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m}\right)\,,

with
\mathscr{M}_{+}=\{\mathscr{M},\tilde{\mathscr{M}}\}
,
\tilde{\mathscr{M}}=\{M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)}),\ldots,M(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(n^{\prime})})\}
,

K_{+}=\begin{bmatrix}K&L\\
L^{T}&\tilde{K}\end{bmatrix}\,,

\tilde{K}_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}=\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j^{\prime})},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})
(
j^{\prime},k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,n^{\prime}
) and
L_{jk^{\prime}}=\kappa(\mathbf{x}^{(j)},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})
(
j=1,\ldots,n;\,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,n^{\prime}
). Then, conditional on
\mathscr{F}
we have the posterior predictive distribution

\tilde{\mathscr{F}}|\mathscr{F}\sim\mathcal{TN}_{n^{\prime},r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}}\left(\tilde{\mathscr{M}}^{\star},\tilde{K}^{\star},\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m}\right)\,,

with
\tilde{\mathscr{M}}^{\star}=\{\mathscr{M}^{\star}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)}),\ldots,\mathscr{M}^{\star}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(n^{\prime})})\}
,

\mathscr{M}^{\star}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\mathscr{M}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})+[\mathscr{F}-\mathscr{M}]\times_{1}\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{T}K^{-1}\,,

(10)

\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=(\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{(1)}),\dots,\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{(n)}))^{T}
,
\tilde{K}^{\star}_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}=\kappa^{\star}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j^{\prime})},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})
and

\kappa^{\star}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})=\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{T}K^{-1}\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\,.

Here,
\times_{c}
is the
c
-mode tensor-matrix product defined for tensor
\mathscr{P}
and matrix
\Lambda
, with entries
p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}
and
\lambda_{ab}
respectively, as

(\mathscr{P}\times_{c}\Lambda)_{i_{1}\ldots i_{c-1}ai_{c+1}\ldots i_{m}}=\sum_{b}\lambda_{ab}p_{i_{1}\ldots i_{c-1}bi_{c+1}\ldots i_{m}}\,.

Hence we have the posterior process

\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{x})|\mathscr{F}\sim\mathcal{TVGP}(\mathscr{M}^{\star}(\cdot),\kappa^{\star}(\cdot,\cdot),\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m})\,.

The statistical surrogate model of
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
is usually not specified with fixed chosen values for the elements of
\Sigma
and
\theta
. If a prior distributional specification is assumed across this collection, the updated posterior distributional form
\mathscr{F}
is no longer a Gaussian process, and numerical inference methods must be used. Typically, a vectorization of the mean tensor
\mathscr{M}(\cdot)
is formed as a combination of a linear predictor and unknown parameters (Appendix
B
). These mean parameters are integrated over with respect to a prior distribution, retaining a posterior predictive Gaussian process. Covariance parameters are often estimated as, e.g., posterior modes and plugged into the posterior predictive density
[
17
, ch. 5]
.

2.3
Emulation of Dynamic Simulators

A straightforward approach for emulation of spatio-temporal simulators is to treat the
r=r_{1}\times r_{2}
space-time outputs
as a general multivariate collection of random variables and emulate directly using the MvGP from Equation (
4
)
[
29
,
12
]
. In this case, the indexing of the model output enters only indirectly through specification of the prior variance matrix for the outputs. Such an emulator is simple to implement, however, computationally expensive for large values of
r
. It may also be difficult to specify or elicit the required parameters involved in capturing the covariances between all of the outputs.

An alternative approach is to construct a separate emulator for each space-time output, indexed by
i=1,\dots,r

[
12
]
:

f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\mu_{i}(\mathbf{x})+e_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

(11)

with
e_{i}
given independent, zero-mean, Gaussian process priors. As a result, only data for the
i
th output is utilised when emulating output at spatial-temporal coordinate
i
. Prior correlation across space-time output is not captured. Additionally, constructing each emulator completely independently, including specification of parameters, is computationally intensive; commonly,
r_{1}
and/or
r_{2}
may be in the order of (tens of) thousands; hence, even if using parallel computation, estimation will be expensive. Even for smaller grid sizes, computation can be reduced by orders of magnitude through the use of a TvGP.

Extensions or variants of these two approaches have proved popular in applications
[
33
,
37
,
28
,
14
]
. In the next sections, we place two such methods in the context of tensor-variate Gaussian processes with
m=2
dimensional tensor (or matrix) output
\mathscr{F}=f_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\,:\,r_{1}\times r_{2}
.

2.4
Outer Product Emulators

An Outer Product Emulator (OPE) for multivariate responses
[
34
]
extends the separable emulator (
4
) by assuming a mean function constructed from a further separable structure in the regressors. Such structure can be extended to higher-dimensional outputs, defined over the inputs and all output locations within the TvGP expressed as Equation (
8
). Each component is modeled as

f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}+e_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x}),\quad i_{z}=1,\ldots,r_{z};\ z=1,\ldots,m,

where
\mathbf{g}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}_{0}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\otimes(\otimes_{z=1}^{m}\mathbf{g}_{z}^{\top}(\bm{\xi}_{zi_{z}})),\ \bm{\beta}=\bm{\beta}_{0}\otimes(\otimes_{z=1}^{m}\bm{\beta}_{z})
, with
\bm{g}_{0}(\cdot)
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}
being
v_{0}
-vectors of regressors and parameters related to the input variables, and
\bm{g}_{z}(\cdot)
and
\bm{\beta}_{z}

v_{z}
-vectors corresponding to the output locations in dimension
z
. Hence, the TvGP has mean tensor

\mathscr{M}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}^{\top}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}\,:\,\times_{z=1}^{m}r_{z}\,.

(12)

For the
m=2
dimensional spatio-temporal case, we define
\bm{\xi}_{1i_{1}}=s_{i_{1}}\in\mathcal{S}
as the spatial locations and temporal locations
\bm{\xi}_{2i_{2}}=t_{i_{2}}\in\mathcal{T}
and

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})&=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{g}_{11}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}&\cdots&\mathbf{g}_{1r_{2}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{g}_{r_{1}1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}&\cdots&\mathbf{g}_{r_{1}r_{2}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=G(\mathbf{x})\left[I_{r2}\otimes\bm{\beta}\right]\end{aligned},

where

G(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{g}_{11}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})&\cdots&\mathbf{g}_{1r_{2}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{g}_{r_{1}1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})&\cdots&\mathbf{g}_{r_{1}r_{2}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}\,,

and
I_{r_{2}}
is an
r_{2}\times r_{2}
identity matrix.

The TvGP is completed by specification of coregionalization matrix
\Sigma=\otimes_{z=1}^{m}\Sigma_{z}
for residual tensor
\mathscr{E}
. For emulators with spatio-temporal output,

\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\otimes\Sigma_{2}\,,

with
\Sigma_{1}
an
r_{1}\times r_{1}
spatial coregionalization matrix and
\Sigma_{2}
an
r_{2}\times r_{2}
temporal coregionalization matrix. Advantage can be taken of naturally defined metric spaces on the output locations to borrow strength across different space-time coordinates via suitable prior specifications. Here, we assume
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}W_{1}\otimes W_{2}
. Hence
\Sigma_{1}=W_{1}
, with
r_{1}\times r_{1}
matrix
W^{s}
having entries defined via a kernel specifying response correlation on the spatial output locations, and
\Sigma_{2}=\sigma^{2}W_{2}
, where
r_{2}\times r_{2}
matrix
W^{t}
is similarly defined via a kernel for response correlation on the temporal output locations. The single scale parameter is
\sigma^{2}
. Efficient computational methods are available for the OPE with matrix-variate output as appropriate for spatio-temporal simulators; see
[
4
]
and Appendix
B.1
, which demonstrates that a vectorization of the tensor-variate OPE maintains the usual form of a vector-variate OPE.

2.5
Parallel Partial Emulators

A Parallel Partial Emulator (PPE), introduced in
[
18
]
, involves fitting separate, shared-characteristic emulators at each output grid point:

f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}_{0}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\beta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}+e_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(13)

The emulators have a common
v_{0}
-vector of basis functions
\mathbf{g}_{0}(\cdot)
, but distinct
v_{0}
-vectors of regression coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}
. Emulators at different output locations are assumed conditionally independent, a priori, and hence
\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}\left(\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{r}\right)
. The PPE can be expressed as a TvGP with a single output dimension of size
r=\prod_{z=1}^{m}r_{z}
, that combines all output indices. This is equivalent to MvGP (
4
). The emulator has mean vector

\mathscr{M}(\mathbf{x})=\left[I_{r}\otimes\mathbf{g}_{0}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\right]\textrm{vec}\left(\mathscr{B}\right)\,,

(14)

with
\mathscr{B}=\beta_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}j}\,:\,[\times_{z=1}^{m}r_{z}]\times v_{0}
. Here
\mathscr{B}
is a tensor representation of the output-specific (
i_{1}\cdots i_{m}
) coefficients of the common
v_{0}
regressors and
\beta_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}j}
is the
j
th element of
\bm{\beta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}
.

The shared structure of the linear predictor and kernel function, including common parameters
\bm{\theta}
results in the PPE inheriting properties of the simulator, including smoothness
[
18
]
. Completely independent emulators for each space-time coordinate would not have this property.

2.6
Priors and estimation

Connections between the OPE and PPE can be established via the choice of hyperparameter priors
[
34
,
12
,
18
]
, i.e., for
\bm{\beta}
and
\Sigma_{z}
; see Appendix
B.2
. Here, for the OPE we use a conjugate normal-inverse gamma joint prior for
\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}

[
34
]
, leading to a marginal multivariate
t
-distribution prior for
\bm{\beta}

In the mean structure, for both emulators, we specify input regressors
\mathbf{g}_{0}^{\top}=(1,\mathbf{x}^{\top})
. For the OPE we also specify temporal regressors
\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\top}=(1,t)
. The input correlation for both emulators was specified as Gaussian from Equation (
3
). For the OPE, the Gaussian correlation function was also used to parameterize the temporal correlations, with

(\Sigma_{2})_{i_{2}i_{2}^{\prime}}=\sigma^{2}\exp\left(-\{t_{i_{2}}-t_{i_{2}^{\prime}}\}^{2}/\theta_{2}\right)\,,

and hyperparameter
\theta_{2}>0
. The choice of spatial regressors and correlation function for the OPE varies between our two examples.

The OPE is completed via choice of parameters for the normal-inverse gamma prior discussed above; we use
\sigma^{-2}\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)
and
\bm{\beta}|\sigma^{2}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}I_{v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}})
. Hence, the marginal prior distribution for
\bm{\beta}
is a scaled
t
-distribution with two degrees of freedom, and enforces shrinkage towards zero for the posterior of the regression parameters. All correlation parameters were estimated via maximum likelihood, which corresponds to minimizing the Mahalanobis distance, using the L-BFGS-B algorithm
[
8
]
.

For the PPE, vague prior beliefs are assumed for
\bm{\beta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
, leading to posterior mean and variance estimators for
\bm{\beta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
equivalent to generalized least squares estimators
[
22
]
. We obtain point estimates for
\sigma_{i}^{2}
and common correlation parameters
\bm{\theta}
via maximum likelihood
[
1
,
24
]
, with the latter obtained numerically.

Two different computer experiments were designed and performed to generate training data, with
n=20
(
\mathcal{X}_{20}
) and
n=50
(
\mathcal{X}_{50}
). Both designs were found using the MaxPro
[
25
]
space-filling criterion, with input dimensions each scaled to
[-1,1]
. All statistical modeling also used this scaling, which was also applied to spatial and temporal locations. A further MaxPro design,
\mathcal{X}_{D}
of size
n^{\prime}=150
, was constructed and run for diagnostic purposes.

3
Application: Dynamic Influenza Simulator

3.1
The Simulator

Our first application is to the compartmental SEIR simulator for influenza introduced in Section
1
. The spatial patches
[
39
]
represent population distributions among 15 fictional regions. The population in each patch moves through the four states (
S
,
E
,
I
and
R
) over time according to a system of deterministic differential equations, governed by a set of rate parameters which are consistent across all patches. Previous work on emulating SEIR simulators has not included such a spatial component
[
13
]
.

Interactions between infection levels in neighboring patches are represented by a non-symmetric (commuter) matrix, through which movement between the different patches is modeled
[
36
]
, with row
a
column
b
representing the flow of people from patch
a
to patch
b
. Two daily timesteps are assumed, simulating travel from a “home” patch to a “work” patch and back again. The susceptible population in patch
a
can become infected if they mix with (i) infected individuals from patch
a
; (ii) infected individuals in patch
a
who have moved from patch
b

b
if they themselves commute to patch
b
.

Here, spatio-temporal emulators are built for the number of infected individuals in each patch over time. The simulator is initialized with 100 infected individuals in a single fixed patch with the remaining population across all patches being susceptible. The input parameters to the simulator are rates
x_{1}=\beta\in[1,2]
,
x_{2}=\alpha\in[0.25,1]
and
x_{3}=\gamma\in[0.25,1]

to
E
,
E
to
I
and
I
to
R
, respectively. Initial conditions for the simulator and the ranges for these inputs were chosen by the epidemiological modelers at UKHSA.

3.2
Emulation

Tensor-variate GP emulators were constructed for this simulator, under each of the OPE (Section
2.4
) and PPE assumptions (Section
2.5
). The simulator output at each space-time location,
y_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\mathbf{x})
, was shifted and log transformed before emulation; that is,
f_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\mathbf{x})=\log\left(y_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\mathbf{x})+1\right)
. Each simulator run produced numbers of infected individuals across
r_{1}=15
spatial patches and
r_{2}=150
discrete time steps.

The OPE and PPE were constructed using the priors described in Section
2.6
. The fixed initial conditions and commuter matrix led, for all combinations of input variables, to the same temporal order of patches obtaining their respective maximum number of infections. This ordering was used to define a new spatial coordinate for each patch,
\tilde{s}_{i_{1}}
, taking values in
\{1,\ldots,15\}
, which are common across all simulator runs. Hence these quantities can be used to define
\Sigma_{1}
for the OPE. This matrix was parameterized using a Gaussian correlation function and therefore for the OPE

(\Sigma_{1})_{i_{1}i_{1}^{\prime}}=\exp\left(-\{\tilde{s}_{i_{1}}-\tilde{s}_{i_{1}^{\prime}}\}^{2}/\theta_{1}\right)\,,

with hyperparameter
\theta_{1}>0
. For the prior mean structure, we use
\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\top}(\mathring{s}_{i_{1}})=(1,\mathring{s}_{i_{1}}),
where
\mathring{s}_{i_{1}}
is a scaled patch size.

4.1
The Simulator

As a second example, consider an environmental simulator modeling a pollutant spill caused by a chemical accident
[
5
]
. A mass
x_{1}\in[7,13]
of pollutant is spilled at each of two locations in a long, narrow channel, denoted by the space-time vectors
(0,0)
and
(x_{2},x_{3})
. The first spill is fixed; however, the time and spatial location of the second spill are inputs. The location of the second spill is almost the entire domain,
x_{2}\in[0.01,3]
, but the time of the second spill is quite restrictive,
x_{3}\in[30.01,30.295]
. A fourth input variable,
x_{4}\in[0.02,0.12]
, is the constant diffusion rate in the channel.

The simulator output, log pollutant concentration at spatial location
s_{i_{1}}\in[0,3]
and time
t_{i_{2}}\in[0,60]
is defined as the following closed-form function of
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{4})^{\top}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\mathbf{x})&=\log\left\{\sqrt{4\pi}C(\mathbf{x};\,s_{i_{1}},t_{i_{2}})+1\right\}\,\quad i_{1}=1,\ldots,r_{1};\,i_{2}=1,\ldots,r_{2}\,,\\
C(\mathbf{x};\,s,t)&=\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{4\pi x_{4}t}}\exp\left(\frac{-s^{2}}{4x_{4}t}\right)+\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{4\pi x_{4}(t-x_{3})}}\exp\left(\frac{-(s-x_{2})^{2}}{4x_{4}(t-x_{3})}\right)\mathbb{I}(x_{3}>t)\,,\end{split}

(15)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function. Figure

shows the simulator output as a function of

and

for the values
x_{1}=10,x_{2}=1.505,x_{3}=30.1525
and
x_{4}=0.07

Note that the time and location of the first release are fixed and the time of the second release varies slightly around the halfway point of the time interval. Therefore, for

5
Conclusions

Tensor-variate Gaussian processes are a powerful tool for emulation of multi-output simulators, which generalize previous approaches. Applications of such simulators are common in areas such as biomedicine
[
38
]
and engineering
[
21
]
, in addition to spatio-temporal modeling. There are also synergies with multi-task learning using Gaussian processes
[
6
]
.

The important special cases of OPE and PPE have been compared across two case studies, using a practically important simulator from public health and a synthetic environmental simulator. PPE has modeling advantages when the simulator outputs are unstructured, or the structure adds little to the understanding of the system, particularly if larger computer experiments can be performed. When an advantage can be taken of correlation in the output dimensions, OPE can provide more accurate predictions, especially in cases where output structure can compensate for high variation with respect to the simulator inputs. This higher accuracy does come at a higher computational cost, especially for larger numbers of output locations. However, for many applications, computational expense will be dominated by the computer experiment on the simulator, rather than the cost of fitting the emulator.

Extensions of this work could address the additional computation complexity of the TvGP regression, particularly the OPE, through kernel choice for the coregionalization matrices (e.g., tapered kernels
[
26
]
) or include heteroskedastic errors across output dimensions. Variable selection could be considered for general TvGP regression and for the OPE in particular, including the choice of regressors in the output locations. TvGP regression could also be applied to provide prediction for output locations that differ from those of the simulator, provided a continuous output domain can be defined.

Appendix A
Summaries of predictive performance

The following quantities are used to summarize emulator predictive performance. In each case,
\mu_{f}(\cdot)
is the emulator mean,
v_{f}(\cdot)
the emulator variance and
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})},f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})\right\}_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n^{\prime}}
is a set of test points (inputs and simulator outputs).

1.

Mean Absolute Standardised Prediction Error (MASPE)
[
23
]
:

\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{\left|f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})-\mu_{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})\right|}{\sqrt{\nu_{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})}}\,.

(A.1)

2.

Root Mean Squared Prediction Error (RMSPE)
[
2
]
:

\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left\{f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})-\mu_{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})\right\}^{2}}\,.

(A.2)

3.

Mean Generalised Entropy Score (MGES)
[
15
]
:

-\,\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left\{\frac{\left[f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})-\mu_{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})\right]^{2}}{\nu_{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})}+\log\left(\nu_{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k^{\prime})})\right)\right\}\,.

(A.3)

MASPE is a measure of emulator validity; heuristically we expect this value to be around 1 (assuming normal errors this value should be
\sqrt{2/\pi}
based on properties of the half-normal distribution). RMSPE permits comparison of emulator accuracy. MGES is larger (better) for approximations that are both accurate and valid.

Appendix B
Supporting comparison of the emulators

B.1
Vectorized mean tensors

Vectorization of the mean tensor
\mathscr{M}
, applying definition (
5
) with the OPE mean (
12
), leads to a mean vector

\textrm{vec}\left({\mathscr{M}}\right)=\left(\otimes_{i=0}^{m}G_{i}\right)\bm{\beta}\,,

(A.1)

with

G_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}^{(n)})\end{bmatrix}\,.

Similarly, assuming the PPE mean (
14
) leads to a mean vector

\textrm{vec}\left({\mathscr{M}}\right)=\left(G_{0}\otimes I_{r}\right)\textrm{vec}\left(\mathscr{B}\right)\,.

(A.2)

Hence, when vectorized via (
9
), both emulators can be cast in the outer product emulator framework; the mean vector takes the form of a linear predictor with both the model matrix and the variance-covariance matrix having Kronecker form and efficient computation can be achieved
[
34
]
. In fact, the independence of the parallel emulators in the PPE leads to even more efficient computation.

B.2
The role of spatio-temporal coregionalization

From Equation (
10
) it is clear that, conditional on the mean tensor
\mathscr{M}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, the posterior predictive mean at new input
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
is independent of the coregionalization matrix
\Sigma=\otimes_{i=1}^{m}\Sigma_{i}
. In fact, for the PPE with a non-informative prior on regression parameters, it can be shown that this independence also holds unconditionally
[
18
]
. We restate the result here using the notation from this paper.

Lemma B.1
.

Assume a TvGP implying (
9
) with vectorized mean tensor (
A.2
) and constant prior on
\textrm{vec}(B)
. The generalized least squares estimator for
\textrm{vec}(B)
, or equivalently the posterior mean, is independent of the coregionalization matrix
\Sigma=\otimes_{i=1}^{m}\Sigma_{i}
and has the form

\widehat{\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{B})}=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}G_{0}\right)^{-1}G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}\otimes I_{r}\right]\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})\,.

Proof.

For the most general tensor PPE we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{B})}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}\otimes I_{r}\right)\left(K^{-1}\otimes\Sigma^{-1}\right)\left(G_{0}\otimes I_{r}\right)\right]^{-1}\left(G_{0}^{\top}\otimes I_{r}\right)\left(K^{-1}\otimes\Sigma^{-1}\right)\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})

\displaystyle=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}G_{0}\right)^{-1}\otimes\Sigma\right]\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}\otimes\Sigma^{-1}\right)\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})

\displaystyle=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}G_{0}\right)^{-1}G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}\otimes I_{r}\right]\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})\,,

which does not involve
\Sigma
.
∎

The form of the GLS estimator from Lemma
B.1
also demonstrates the independence of the estimators for each output location.

In contrast, the GLS estimator for the tensor OPE does not simplify to such an extent, even with non-informative priors on the regression coefficients, as demonstrated via Lemma
B.2
.

Lemma B.2
.

Assume a TvGP implying (
9
) with vectorized mean tensor (
A.1
) and constant prior on
\bm{\beta}
. The generalized least squares estimator for
\bm{\beta}
, or equivalently the posterior mean, depends on the coregionalization matrix
\Sigma=\otimes_{i=1}^{m}\Sigma_{i}
and has the form

\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}G_{0}\right)^{-1}G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}\otimes\left(G_{1:m}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}G_{1:m}\right)^{-1}G_{1:m}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\right]\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})\,,

with
G_{1:m}=\otimes_{i=1}^{m}G_{i}
.

Proof.

For the most general tensor OPE we have

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}\otimes G_{1:m}^{\top}\right)\left(K^{-1}\otimes\Sigma^{-1}\right)\left(G_{0}\otimes G_{1:m}\right)\right]^{-1}\left(G_{0}^{\top}\otimes G_{1:m}^{\top}\right)\left(K^{-1}\otimes\Sigma^{-1}\right)\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})

\displaystyle=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}G_{0}\right)^{-1}\otimes\left(G_{1:m}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}G_{1:m}\right)^{-1}\right]\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}\otimes G_{1:m}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\right)\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})

\displaystyle=\left[\left(G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}G_{0}\right)^{-1}G_{0}^{\top}K^{-1}\otimes\left(G_{1:m}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}G_{1:m}\right)^{-1}G_{1:m}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\right]\textrm{vec}(\mathscr{F})\,.

Hence the GLS equations are a Kronecker product of equations across the input and output regressors involving
\Sigma
.
∎

Therefore, for a tensor OPE the mean prediction has the property of smoothing across output locations, which can be beneficial when there are
strong output dependencies.
