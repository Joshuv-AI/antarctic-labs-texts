Addressing the procedural mismatch: individualized learning plans to align internal medicine residency training with pulmonary and critical care medicine fellowship procedural expectations
Authors: Carleen R Spitzer, Emily M Olson, Ilana Krumm, Jason M Arnold, Lekshmi Santhosh
Published: 2026

Introduction

The procedural competency requirements for internal medicine (IM) residency graduates have drastically changed over the last 30 years. Until 2002, graduates had to complete at least 5 central venous catheters (CVCs) to become board certified, and many exceeded this number.1 In contrast, current IM residents must merely “have exposure to the performance of, and the opportunity to develop competence in, invasive procedures … [in] preparation for their subsequent subspecialty fellowship or chosen career path” prior to graduation.2 The omission of required procedure volumes, and the ambiguity of the current requirements, has led to variability in IM residency programs’ approaches to procedure training and graduation expectations.

Simultaneously, IM residents may have fewer procedural opportunities due to a variety of factors including the adoption of peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), peripheral administration of low-dose vasopressors, and the widespread implementation of dedicated procedure teams.3-6 As expectations and opportunities for procedural exposure decrease, residents’ procedural competency is at risk of decreasing as well. For example, inexperienced residents—as defined by performing fewer than 50 central venous catheterizations—have more frequent failure and complication rates compared to experienced residents and practicing physicians.7

Though procedure services and the adoption of PICCs can mitigate some of the challenges created by having a less procedurally competent resident workforce,8,9 they are not suited to address the downstream effects and can potentiate the discrepancy between trainee procedural competency and need. This mismatch is perhaps most notable when examining the procedural competency of incoming pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) fellows. In an IM residency environment where procedural competency in invasive procedures is declining nationally, the PCCM educator community needs to proactively strategize ways to address the mismatch that exists given the decline in IM residents’ procedural opportunities and the need for incoming PCCM fellows who are procedurally competent. In this Perspective, we explore the use of individualized learning plans (ILPs) as a potential tool to address this gap and allow for individualized higher-intensity training for those IM residents who self-identify as PCCM-bound.

Trainee procedural competency and comfort

Despite faculty’s lamentations about the competence of “kids these days,”10 few data are available to quantify IM residents’ procedural competence upon graduation and how it has changed over time. Though we do not have global, objective procedural assessments at the time of graduation, data suggest that most practicing residents are not yet expert proceduralists. For example, data gathered during a simulated training session demonstrated that most surgical residents committed at least one error when inserting a CVC,11 and 64% of IM residents reported having experienced a procedural complication during training.12

Procedural competence is paramount, and self-reported confidence does not equate with observed procedural competency (nor vice versa).13 However, procedural comfort is also important. Just as trainees’ lack of procedural experience is correlated with increased complication rates,7 lack of exposure is also correlated with procedural discomfort,14 and many residents report supervising procedures before they feel ready to do so.12

Even among new PCCM fellows, procedural experience is not guaranteed. A national Association of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine Program Directors (APCCMPD) survey in 2025 found that at the transition to fellowship in July, most—but not all—PCCM fellows indicated they were comfortable performing procedures either independently or with a supervisor available to be called if needed (Table 1; data presented at 2025 APCCMPD Annual Conference and June 2025 Member Check-in [unpublished]). However, there is significant variability in procedure acquisition, with some fellows indicating they had not performed any procedures prior to fellowship. Though these data suggest that most fellows feel comfortable completing CVCs, arterial lines, and paracenteses, it cannot and should not be assumed that all incoming PCCM fellows are comfortable with this skill. The data regarding thoracentesis and lumbar puncture were even more striking, with most fellows indicating they never performed these before or needed direct supervision. This pattern highlights a possible shift from thoracentesis and lumbar puncture being considered “general medicine” procedures. Understandably, not all fellows feel comfortable supervising procedures at the start of their fellowship. Though we know PCCM fellows feel prepared to complete bedside procedures independently by the time they graduate,15 the lack of perceived comfort with procedural skills at the beginning of fellowship warrants further exploration.

One potential approach to helping first-year PCCM fellows achieve the procedural competency not reached in IM residency is to grant them the right of first refusal for all procedures in the intensive care unit. However, this approach can further limit opportunities for IM residents, thereby propagating a vicious cycle.

Similarly, PCCM fellows may have fewer opportunities to ­complete pulmonary-specific procedures due to the rise of subspecialty procedure services, subspecialty fellowships like interventional pulmonary (IP), competing trainees with similar skillsets (ie, anesthesia, critical care trainees), and advanced practice provider teams.16

For example, with the growing footprint of IP and the transition of IP training to Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) accreditation, there is uncertainty on how IP fellowships may impact PCCM fellows.17 Previous work has shown that pulmonary procedures and management of complex pleural disease are not uniformly emphasized across PCCM fellowship training. However, PCCM fellows in programs with IP were more likely to have increased exposure and satisfaction with training in advanced pulmonary procedures but did not have an increased likelihood of achieving target procedure numbers.18,19 While surveyed PCCM fellows have cited IP fellowships as both a benefit and a barrier to procedural opportunities,20 this variability, impacted by both individual initiative and institutional culture, may further widen disparities in advanced procedural training.

The path forward: next steps for bridging the gap

Given the decreasing procedural opportunities and the delay in procedural competency for PCCM-bound trainees, interventions at both the IM residency and PCCM fellowship levels are needed to ensure timely trainee competency and comfort as well as patient safety.

Interventions at the IM residency level

At the IM residency level, several interventions could address the mismatch between procedure opportunities and skill acquisition. First, every effort should be made to provide incoming trainees with basic procedural education during the first few months of training21 in addition to just-in-time training when procedural opportunities arise. In the age of simulation, trainees have the opportunity to practice their procedural skills without risking harm to patients in the clinical environment and can benefit from spaced repetition to minimize skill decay. Once basic procedural education and simulation opportunities are provided, programs can tailor procedural expectations for each resident by incorporating these into residents’ ILPs.22 ILPs have been required by the ACGME for IM trainees since 2017.23 These plans provide a framework to discuss residents’ career goals, strengths, and areas for growth and to ensure that trainees’ residency experiences are preparing them for their future careers. By embedding procedural expectations into residents’ ILPs, programs can ensure that each trainee has access to the opportunities for skill acquisition that they will need to be successful in the next stage of their career (Figure 1). Though there are not standardized procedural expectations for IM residents based on their career aspirations, several authors have put forth their own recommendations.24,25 Recently, Singas and colleagues surveyed current fellowship program ­directors (PDs) to try to ascertain what (if any) procedural ­competency residents should attain prior to entering fellowship. Not surprisingly, procedural preferences varied widely across PDs from the 15 subspecialties surveyed, with PDs in more procedurally heavy specialties such as cardiology and PCCM favoring arterial lines, CVCs, and vascular ultrasound (among others), whereas the majority of PDs in allergy/immunology, endocrine, geriatrics, hematology, hospice/palliative medicine, infectious diseases, and rheumatology did not expect incoming fellows to demonstrate competence in any of the procedures queried.25

Based on current Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education expectations, many programs require all internal medicine residents to perform a certain number of each procedure regardless of future career plans or interest in performing procedures. An equal flow of procedures into both “procedure-light” and “procedure-heavy” tracks does not account for the fact that different trainees will require different opportunities based on their anticipated career paths. In a future state where individualized learning plans (ILPs) are implemented, procedures are shunted to residents interested in procedures, thus incorporating precision medical education into procedure allocation. Some key procedures are still filtered to “procedure-light” trainees to ensure safe patient care.

These results underscore the value of adjusting expectations for IM residents based on their future career paths as both residents and future fellowship programs could benefit from this individualized approach. For example, a resident pursuing primary care should still be exposed to CVC insertion, but a PCCM-bound resident should be able to demonstrate CVC insertion competency—ideally even under more technically challenging clinical scenarios. This approach ensures not only that newly graduated residents have the procedural skills that they will need upon entering their fellowships or jobs but also that trainees have programmatic support to achieve their individual goals.

Implementing procedure expectations into ILPs

At The Ohio State University IM residency program, we implemented procedure expectations into trainees’ ILPs for the first time during the 2025-2026 academic year. However, before proceeding with this ILP-centric approach to procedure expectations, we needed to consider several things. First, we assessed the current clinical needs to ensure that patient care would not be negatively affected by this change. For instance, at our institution, IM residents complete many of the CVCs on general medicine ward services and in the intensive care unit, including during nighttime hours. Therefore, if we stipulated that only residents with a procedural interest were required to demonstrate CVC insertion competency, individuals without a procedural interest may “opt out” of these procedures, and patient care could be compromised. To prevent this, we explicitly stated that all residents should complete the procedures required to take optimal care of their patients (or identify an alternate provider or service who is immediately available to do so). We included this verbiage in our formal description of how procedures should be included into ILPs and messaged this at intern orientation, our house staff education committee meetings, and house staff business meetings.

While it is of upmost importance that residents continue to complete all the procedures necessary for optimal patient care, we also needed to confirm that the necessary resources existed to enable each resident’s success. Prior to this academic year, residents merely had to demonstrate competence in one invasive bedside procedure prior to graduation. However, with this new approach, the bar may be raised for some individuals. For example, whereas a PCCM-bound resident would previously meet our internal procedure requirement if they were deemed competent to complete paracenteses, with the implementation of the procedure-based ILPs, now we would expect this same resident to gain competency in CVC and arterial line placement as well. During semi-annual reviews and Clinical Competency Committee meetings, each resident’s progress toward their procedural goals will be evaluated and attention paid to ensure that their upcoming rotations will provide the procedural opportunities needed. If individual residents are not meeting their procedure goals, a deeper dive is indicated to determine if this is an individual-specific issue or if systems-level issues are contributing to inadequate exposure. Once barriers are identified, solutions can be developed to address the identified challenge (eg, rotation with procedure service, subspecialty elective).

However, before resources can be identified, procedural expectations for different career plans must be clearly delineated. This can be done in several different ways: setting expectations based on groups (ie, outpatient-focused specialty, inpatient practice, procedure-heavy specialty), developing explicit tracks with varied requirements (ie, critical care track, primary care track), or listing expectations for each individual career or subspecialty. Though programs should consider what procedural competencies different subspecialty fellowship directors value,25 different IM residency programs’ expectations should also vary depending on their institutions’ unique needs and the existing culture surrounding procedures. At The Ohio State University, we developed specialty-specific procedure expectations—seeking input from core faculty, specialty program directors, and IM chief residents in addition to considering the residents’ clinical practice environment and available institutional resources. Interventions at the PCCM fellowship level

Unlike IM residency programs, ILPs are not required for PCCM fellowships. However, given the variable procedural expertise of entering fellows, ILPs may be a non-stigmatizing way to acknowledge variability among trainees while working to raise everyone to the same minimum standard. For example, at the beginning of fellowship, each incoming fellow would have their own procedure-related ILP. This ILP would be based on their initial confidence and competence performing and supervising procedures, with confidence assessed either via survey or structured interview with program leadership and competence determined by their technical performance in the simulated setting or in the clinical environment. After this minimum standard is reached, ILPs could be leveraged to ensure that fellows are gaining the procedural experiences that they will need to graduate (eg, bronchoscopies logged) as well as those tailored to their individual career goals. This approach could be particularly useful for trainees who are interested in pursuing a subsequent IP fellowship or who are hoping to complete less common procedures as a part of their future career paths (eg, right heart catheterizations for those interested in pulmonary hypertension).

What are the challenges?

Though embedding procedures into trainees’ ILPs guarantees an individualized approach to procedural competency, there are challenges and even potential educational threats with this method. First, the incorporation of procedures into ILPs represents a significant shift from the way most programs currently approach procedure expectations. With the adoption of this approach, programs will need to carefully monitor any delays in patient care that could be attributed to a change in procedure allocation. For example, hospitals staffed by IM resident trainees often do not have built-in precautions should a trainee attempt to “opt out” of a required procedure. Concretely, we cannot have a situation where all of the residents who are on nights in the intensive care unit say that central lines are not “aligned with their ILP goals” when a decompensating patient needs a central line emergently. Moreover, we need to carefully monitor residents’ procedure numbers to ensure that some less common procedures like lumbar punctures and pelvic exams do not suffer from skill decay over time.

From a training program perspective, this new approach may require adjustments to individual trainees’ schedules as well as additional resources to ensure that each resident receives the procedural opportunities that are most applicable for their future careers. For example, a resident interested in primary care may have competency in CVC insertions but not pelvic exams. With the inclusion of procedures into ILPs, programs would need to identify ways to increase pelvic exam opportunities—perhaps through a women’s health elective or by having the resident rotate with a specific primary care provider whose practice would be expected to provide this exposure.

Last, careful attention should be paid to avoid propagating unintentional gender and/or racial bias. While procedural training should be highly encouraged for trainees who are PCCM- or cardiology-bound while in IM residency, many residents may not have yet self-identified as “procedurally interested,” and we need to be careful not to inadvertently gatekeep procedural training from these residents. Research shows that women may be less likely to self-advocate for procedures,26 and gender disparities still persist in PCCM fellowships.27 Therefore, though residents who know they are PCCM- or cardiology-bound need to secure enough procedures to attain competency, every effort should be made to ensure that procedural opportunities remain available to all trainees regardless of their anticipated career path.

Conclusions

The incorporation of procedures into ILPs represents a novel framework that is well-positioned to address the current mismatch between procedural competency expectations and reality. Through this lens, program leaders can provide precision medical education that will best equip IM residents for success in their future careers. Further directions could include pre-/postprogram evaluations of programs who have implemented ILPs and further analysis of procedural competency and comfort in the era of declining procedural volumes.
