NP-completeness of the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -embedding problem for simple graphs as sphere-of-influence graphs
Stanislav Jabuka

Abstract
. In graph theory an interesting question is whether for a fixed choice of p ∈ [ 0 , ∞ ] p\in[0,\infty] , all simple graphs appear as sphere-of-influence graphs in some Euclidean space with respect to the ℓ p \ell_{p} metric. The answer is affirmative for p = ∞ p=\infty , negative for any p ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) p\in(0,\infty) , and unknown for p = 1 p=1 . The result of this work shows that for the case of p = 1 p=1 , this embeddability question is a (Promise) NP-Complete problem.

05C12, 68R10, 68R12

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Let
(M,d)
be a metric space and
X=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}\subset M
be a finite subset. The
radius of influence
r_{i}
of
X_{i}
is
r_{i}=\min_{j\neq i}d(X_{i},X_{j})
, and the
sphere of influence
of
X_{i}
is
S_{i}=\{x\in M\,|\,d(X_{i},x)<r_{i}\}
.
The
sphere-of-influence graph
, or SIG for short, of the subset
X\subset M
and induced by the metric
d
, is the graph with vertices
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, two of which share an edge if and only if
d(X_{i},X_{j})<r_{i}+r_{j}
(or equivalently, if their spheres of influence overlap). A central question in the theory of SIGs is whether any simple graph
G
is isometric to a SIG in
(\mathbb{R}^{m},d_{p})
with the dimension
m\geq 1
arbitrary, and with
d_{p}
being the
\ell_{p}
-metric

d_{p}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left(|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{p}+\dots+|x_{n}-y_{n}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}&\quad;\quad p\in[1,\infty),\cr\max\{|x_{1}-y_{1}|,\dots,|x_{n}-y_{n}|\}&\quad;\quad p=\infty.\end{array}\right.

The answer is ‘yes’ if
p=\infty

[
5
,
14
]
, it is ‘no’ if
p\in(1,\infty)

[
12
]
and is unknown if
p=1
. We show that the case of
p=1
leads to a (Promise) NP-Complete problem, lending credence to the difficulty of the problem.

1.2.
\ell_{1}
-embeddability and the cut cone
CUT_{n}

Consider a finite metric space
(V_{n},d)
with
V_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
. A
cut of
V_{n}
is a subset
C\subset V_{n}
, which we view as “cutting”
V_{n}
into the disjoint pieces
V_{n}=C\cup(V_{n}-C)
. A cut creates a notion of distance on
V_{n}
, with two elements that lie in the same subset induced by the cut, either
C
or
V_{n}-C
, being distance
0
apart, and with two elements lying in opposite components of the cut being distance
1
apart. This simple idea defines a semi-metric
\delta_{C}
on
V_{n}
by

(1)

\delta_{C}(i,j)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\quad;\quad|C\cap\{i,j\}|=1,\cr 0&\quad;\quad|C\cap\{i,j\}|=0\text{ or }2.\end{array}\right.

We call
\delta_{C}
the
cut-metric
corresponding to
C
. Obviously
\delta_{C}=\delta_{V_{n}-C}
. Let
CUT_{n}
denote the
cut cone
, defined as

\displaystyle\text{CUT}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{C\subset V_{n}}w_{C}\,\delta_{C}\,\big|\,w_{C}\geq 0\right\}.

A metric
d
on
V_{n}
lies in the cut cone if, by definition, it can be written as a linear combination of cut-metrics with non-negative coefficients:

d=\sum_{C\subset V_{n}}w_{C}\cdot\delta_{C},\quad w_{C}\geq 0\text{ for all }C\subset V_{n}.

The following beautiful theorem was proved in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

The metric space
(V_{n},d)
is isometric to a subspace of
(\mathbb{R}^{m},d_{1})
if and only if
d\in CUT_{n}
.

This theorem shifts the question of embeddability of a finite metric space
(V_{n},d)
into
(\mathbb{R}^{m},d_{1})
, to the question of membership of
d
in the cut cone
CUT_{n}
. The latter is known to be NP-Complete decision problem:

Theorem 1.2

(Avis-Deza
[
3
]
)
.

The problem of determining if a metric
d
on
V_{n}
belongs to the cut cone
CUT_{n}
, is an NP-Complete decision problem.

Building on this, and using the notions of promise decision problems and polynomial reduction, we prove what is the main result of this paper.

Main Theorem
.

The problem of determining if a simple graph
G
with vertices
V_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
, admits an embedding
\varphi:V_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that the sphere-of-influence graph of
\{\varphi(1),\dots,\varphi(n)\}
with respect to the
\ell_{1}
-metric
d_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is isomorphic to
G
, is a Promise NP-Complete problem.

Remark 1.1
.

The notion of a
Promise NP-Complete
problem is a technical term explained in Section
2.2
. For now it suffices to say that every Promise NP-Complete problem reduces in polynomial time to any NP-Complete problem, and that therefore both types of problems are of equivalent computational complexity.

2.
Background on Computational Complexity

The exposition in this section follows standard texts in computation complexity theory. For decision/language problems we benefited from
[
1
,
11
,
15
,
16
]
, while for promise problems we followed
[
8
,
9
]
.

2.1.
Promise and Decision Problems

As is customary in computational complexity theory, we let
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
denote the “universal alphabet” and
\Sigma^{\ast}=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\left(\cup_{n\geq 1}\Sigma^{\times n}\right)
be its Kleene star, with
\varepsilon
the empty word.

A
promise problem
B
is a pair of disjoint subsets
B_{\mathrm{Yes}},B_{\mathrm{No}}\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
, with the possibility that
B_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup B_{\mathrm{No}}\neq\Sigma^{\ast}
. The
domain of the promise
or simply the
promise
, denoted Dom
(B)
, is Dom
(B)=B_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup B_{\mathrm{No}}
. Promise problems were introduced in
[
6
]
with a view toward applications in Public-Key Cryptography. They have since received considerable attention and applications elsewhere, see for instance
[
9
]
.

A
decision problem
A
is a promise problem for which Dom
(A)=A_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup A_{\mathrm{No}}=\Sigma^{\ast}
. A decision problem is also referred to as a
language recognition problem
with the language
L
being
L=A_{\mathrm{Yes}}
, in which case the decision to make is whether word
x\in\Sigma^{\ast}
belongs to the language
L
.

We will assume that the reader is familiar with the concept of a
deterministic Turing machine
, see for example
[
15
, Chapter 2]
.

2.2.
P versus NP, and Promise P versus Promise NP

Following
[
9
, Definitions 2.4 and 2.5]
, we make this definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a decision problem.

(i)

We say that
A
is of
polynomial complexity
, or in
class P
, if there exists a deterministic Turing machine
M
on
\Sigma^{\ast}
that halts in polynomial time, and accepts an
x\in\Sigma^{\ast}
if and only if
x\in A_{\mathrm{Yes}}
.

(ii)

A
is
non-deterministically polynomial
or in
class NP
if there exists a polynomial
p
and a polynomial-time algorithm
V
such that for every
x\in A_{\mathrm{Yes}}
there exists a
y\in\Sigma^{\ast}
of length at most
p(|x|)
and with
V(x,y)=1
(such a
y
is called an NP-witness for
x
). Additionally, for every
x\in A_{\mathrm{No}}
and for any
y\in\Sigma^{\ast}
,
V(x,y)=0
(saying that no witness
y
can accept an element in
A_{\mathrm{No}}
).

Similar notions of computational complexity can be formulated for promise problems, see
[
9
, Definition 2.31.]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
B
be a promise problem.

(i)

We say that
B
is of
promise polynomial complexity
, or in
class Promise P
, if there exists a deterministic Turing machine
M
on
\Sigma^{\ast}
that halts in polynomial time for any
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(B)
and accepts an
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(B)
if and only if
x\in B_{\mathrm{Yes}}
. There is no requirement for the behavior of
M
on
\Sigma^{\ast}-\mathrm{Dom}(B)
.

(ii)

B
is
promise non-deterministically polynomial
or in
class Promise NP
if there exists a polynomial
p
and a polynomial-time algorithm
V
such that for every
x\in B_{\mathrm{Yes}}
there exists a
y\in\Sigma^{\ast}
of length at most
p(|x|)
and with
V(x,y)=1
. Additionally, for every
x\in A_{\mathrm{No}}
and for any
y\in\Sigma^{\ast}
,
V(x,y)=0
. No requirements are made for how the algorithm
V
behaves on
(x,y)
with
x\notin\mathrm{Dom}(B)
.

Remark 2.1
.

Decision problems are special cases of promise problems, and Definitions
2.1
and
2.2
are compatible in the sense that if a decision problem is viewed as a promise problem, then it belongs to the class Promise P if and only if it belong to the class P, and similarly for NP.

2.3.
Karp Reductions

For our purposes the more specialized Karp reductions will suffice, in favor of simplicity, compared to the more general Cook reductions. Karp reductions were introduced by Karp
[
13
]
in 1972 with the goal of reducing a large variety of computational problems to language recognition problems. Our definition expands
[
9
, 2.11]
from decision to promise problems.

Definition 2.3
.

Consider a pair of promise problems

A:\,A_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup A_{\mathrm{No}}\subset\Sigma^{\ast},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B:\,B_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup B_{\mathrm{No}}\subset\Sigma^{\ast}.

A
promise-preserving Karp reduction
from
A
to
B
is a function
f:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
, computable in polynomial time, such that:

(i)

f\left(\mathrm{Dom}(A)\right)\subset\mathrm{Dom}(B)
.

(ii)

x\in A_{\mathrm{Yes}}
if and only if
f(x)\in B_{\mathrm{Yes}}
.

If such a function exists, we write

A\leq_{p}B,

with the subscript “p” referring to “polynomial time”. Points (i) and (ii) in the preceding definition imply that
x\in A_{\mathrm{No}}
if and only if
f(x)\in B_{\mathrm{No}}
. The notation
A\leq_{p}B
is meant to suggest that problem
B
is at least as hard as problem
A
, up to polynomial time. For the same deterministic Turing machine
M
that accepts/rejects elements from Dom
(B)
, can be used to accept/reject elements in Dom
(A)
by applying
M
to
f(x)
for any choice of
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(A)
.

Since decision problems are viewed here as special cases of promise problems, the notion of promise-preserving Karp reduction applies to them as well, with condition (i) from Definition
2.3
becomes trivially true. Since that condition is the promise-preserving condition, we simply refer to
Karp reduction
in the case of decision problems.

A decision problem
B
is called
NP-Hard
if
A\leq_{p}B
for any NP-Problem
A
, and it is called
NP-Complete
if it is NP and NP-Hard. The first ever NP-Complete problem was the SAT problem discovered by Stephen Cook
[
4
]
in 1971 (and independently discovered by Leonid Levin from the Soviet Union in 1973). By now there are many dozens of known NP-Complete problems, see for instance
[
7
,
10
]
.

Similarly, a promise problem
B
is called
Promise NP-Hard
if
A\leq_{p}B
for any Promise NP Problem
A
, and it is called
Promise NP-Complete
if it is Promise NP and Promise NP-Hard.

Remark 2.2
.

From the definitions alone, it is be obvious that if
A
and
B
are two decision problems of which
A
Karp reduces to
B
and
B
is either of class P or class NP, then so is
A
. Similarly, if
A
and
B
are two promise problems of which
A
promise-preserving Karp reduces to
B
and
B
is either of class Promise P or class Promise NP, then so is
A
.

3.
Proof of the Main Theorem

Fix
n\geq 3
, let
V_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
CUT_{n}
be the cut cone of metrics on
V_{n}
. Consider the two problems below, both of which we view as promise problems, even as the first is also a decision problem:

1.

Problem A: Cut-Cone Membership Decision Problem.

Choose
\Sigma^{\ast}
to be the set of all metrics
d
on
V_{n}
, and consider the decision problem
A_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup A_{\mathrm{No}}=\Sigma^{\ast}
with

A_{\mathrm{Yes}}=\{d\,|\,d\in CUT_{n}\}
,

A_{\mathrm{No}}=\{d\,|\,d\notin CUT_{n}\}
.

2.

Problem B: Cut-Cone Membership with SIG-Promise Problem.

Choose
\Sigma^{\ast}
to be the set of all pairs
(G,d)
with
G
a simple graph with vertices
V_{n}
, and consider the promise problem
B_{\mathrm{Yes}}\cup B_{\mathrm{No}}\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
with

B_{\mathrm{Yes}}=\{d\,|\,d\text{ is a SIG metric on }V_{n}\text{ for $G$, and }d\in CUT_{n}\}
,

B_{\mathrm{No}}=\{d\,|\,d\text{ is a SIG metric on }V_{n}\text{ for $G$, and }d\notin CUT_{n}\}
.

Of these, the first problem is known to be NP-Complete
[
3
]
, which by Remark
2.1
means it is also Promise NP-Complete. We proceed to show that each can be Karp reduced to the other in polynomial time, in a promise-preserving way.

A
\leq_{p}
B

Given
d\in A_{\mathrm{Yes}}
, that is, given a metric
d
on
V_{n}
with
d\in CUT_{n}
, let
G_{d}
be the sphere-of-influence graph associated to
d
. This graph can be computed in polynomial time in
n
as it requires finding the
n
radii of influence
r_{i}=\min_{j\neq i}d(i,j)
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and then computing the
{n\choose 2}
quantities
r_{i}+r_{j}-d(i,j)
,
1\leq i<j\leq n
(which if positive lead to an edge between
i
and
j
, and otherwise don’t). It is trivial to see that
d
is a SIG metric for
G_{d}
so that
(G_{d},d)\in B_{\mathrm{Yes}}
.

Similarly, if
d\in A_{\mathrm{No}}
, then with the same
G_{d}
as above,
d
is a SIG metric on
V_{n}
for
G_{d}
but
d\notin CUT_{n}
, and so
(G_{d},d)\in B_{\mathrm{No}}
.

In summary, the function
d\mapsto(G_{d},d)
is a polynomial time, promise-preserving Karp reduction from Problem A to Problem B.

B
\leq_{p}
A

If
(G,d)\in B_{\mathrm{Yes}}
then
d\in CUT_{n}
and therefore
d\in A_{\mathrm{Yes}}
, while if
(G,d)\in B_{\mathrm{No}}
then
d\notin CUT_{n}
and thus
d\in A_{\mathrm{No}}
. The forgetful function
(G,d)\to d
is a polynomial time, promise-preserving Karp reduction from Problem B to Problem A. This completes the proof of the Main Theorem.
