Comparison of clinical practices during the transitional and young adult phases between patients with oligoarticular/polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis and those with rheumatoid arthritis in Japan
Authors: Sho Mori, Kosuke Shabana, Toshihiro Matsui, Tomo Nozawa, Yuko Sugita, Minako Tomiita, Yasuo Nakagishi, Yuichi Yamasaki, Hiroaki Umebayashi, Masato Yashiro, Naomi Iwata, Junko Yasumura, Hiroyuki Wakiguchi, Takeshi Yamamoto, Shunichiro Takezaki, Yuka Okura, Tadafumi Yokoyama, Masaki Shimizu, Masahiro Hirayama, Shigeto Tohma, Nami Okamoto, Masaaki Mori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a chronic inflammatory condition that frequently persists into adulthood, posing long-term challenges in disease control and quality of life. However, clinical management during the transitional and young adult phases remains insufficiently characterized, especially in comparison with adult-onset rheumatoid arthritis (RA). This study aimed to compare disease activity, medication use, and treatment practices between patients with oligoarticular/polyarticular JIA and those with RA, focusing on individuals aged 16–30 years.MethodsData were derived from two nationwide multicenter databases in Japan—NinJa (National Database of Rheumatic Diseases in Japan) for RA and CoNinJa (a pediatric counterpart of NinJa) for JIA. A total of 176 JIA and 152 RA patients, all aged 16–30 years, were analyzed. Clinical parameters, disease activity indices, and medication profiles were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test.ResultsCompared to RA patients, JIA patients demonstrated significantly lower disease activity (median SDAI 0.6 vs. 2.4) and higher remission rates, particularly Boolean remission (70% vs. 44%) (p < 0.001). MTX usage was less frequent in JIA (49% vs. 68%, p < 0.001), whereas biologic use was notably more common (69% vs. 38%, p < 0.001), with 31% involving off-label prescriptions. Among patients in CDAI remission, biologic monotherapy was observed more frequently in JIA (29% vs. 7%, p < 0.001). Discontinuation of MTX was most commonly attributed to disease improvement (58%) or gastrointestinal intolerance (nausea, 29%). Subcutaneous tocilizumab, though unapproved for JIA in Japan, had the lowest discontinuation rate (4%), suggesting favorable tolerability.ConclusionsDespite an overlap in age, patients with JIA and RA exhibit distinct disease characteristics and therapeutic patterns. These differences underscore the need to expand approved treatment options for JIA, promote equitable access to biologics, and strengthen transitional care frameworks. Further research is warranted to explore long-term outcomes, reproductive health considerations, and socioeconomic barriers that influence treatment continuity in young adults with childhood-onset arthritis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-025-01164-6.Keywords: Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Rheumatoid arthritis, Disease activity, Biologics, Methotrexate

Background

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a chronic arthritis of unknown etiology that develops in children under 16 years of age, with an estimated incidence of 1.6 to 23 per 100,000 children, while prevalence varies from 3.8 to 400 per 100,000 [1]. JIA is often associated with chronic disability, reduced participation in social activities, and significant morbidity due to both articular and extra-articular manifestations [2]. Despite significant improvements in treatment outcomes with biologics, the course of JIA frequently extends into adulthood. Approximately half of the patients experience persistent active disease or disease flare-ups after adolescence [3]. Many JIA patients require ongoing treatment into adulthood, with a growing number transitioned to adult rheumatology departments during the young adult phases.

The International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification categorizes JIA into seven subtypes [4]. These subtypes, particularly polyarticular JIA, share certain aspects of their pathophysiology and treatment strategies with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). However, treatment guidelines for adult oligoarticular/polyarticular JIA patients remain undefined, and the recommended therapies are often extrapolated from pediatric JIA and adult RA populations [5, 6].

Furthermore, significant differences exist between JIA and RA, particularly regarding approved medications and access to medical subsidies in Japan, where pediatric and intractable disease support programs influence treatment accessibility. These distinctions underscore the need for a deeper understanding of the challenges faced by JIA and RA patients during these critical phases of care.

This study aimed to investigate the clinical practices in oligoarticular/polyarticular JIA patients during the transitional and young adult phases, by comparing them with RA patients in the same life stage, using data from the National Database of Rheumatic Diseases in Japan (NinJa) [7, 8] and its pediatric counterpart, CoNinJa (Children’s version of NinJa) [9]. The analysis focused on disease activity and prescribed medications.

Methods

Data collection

RA was diagnosed according to either the 1987 ACR criteria or the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria. The 1987 criteria were applied in young adult patients whose disease onset occurred before the 2010 criteria were introduced and whose data were first registered in NinJa prior to 2010, when the 1987 criteria were the prevailing standard in Japan.

Data were collected from CoNinJa and NinJa, nationwide observational cohort databases for JIA and RA in Japan, focusing on patients aged 16 to 30 years in 2016. Pediatric departments from 15 institutions participated in the CoNinJa project, while adult departments from 51 institutions participated in the NinJa project. Diagnoses were based on standard criteria for JIA [10] and RA [11, 12]. The CoNinJa dataset was used to identify patients aged 16–30 years at the time of registration with a disease classification of “oligoarthritis,” “rheumatoid factor (RF)-positive polyarthritis,” or “RF-negative polyarthritis.” In the NinJa database, patients were identified using the registration age (16–30 years) and diagnostic codes for RA as defined by the 1987 ACR or 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria. CoNinJa and NinJa collect annual data on important clinical events and detailed patient information, including demographics, medication use, and disease activity assessments. Important clinical events (e.g., hospitalizations, surgeries, malignancies) were defined according to the NinJa/CoNinJa protocols and are not identical to MedDRA’s definition of ‘important medical events’. Data curation and validation were conducted at the National Hospital Organization (NHO) Sagamihara National Hospital for NinJa, the Institute of Science Tokyo for CoNinJa, respectively.

Study variables

We analyzed patient backgrounds (disease type, sex, age at onset, age at registration, disease duration), disease activity metrics (e.g., erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], C-reactive protein [CRP], patient and physician global assessments via visual analog scales [VAS] scores, numbers of tender and swollen joints, 28-joint Disease Activity Score using ESR [DAS28-ESR] [13], DAS28 using CRP [DAS28-CRP] [14], Simplified Disease Activity Index [SDAI] [15], Clinical Disease Activity Index [CDAI] [16], and Boolean 1.0 remission rates [17]), and medication use. Median values (25th–75th percentiles) were reported.

Medication use was categorized as conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), biologic (b) DMARDs and targeted synthetic (ts) DMARDs. For biologics, we recorded concomitant MTX use, monotherapy, and whether the agent was approved for JIA or used off-label. We also analyzed medication use history and reasons for discontinuation. Incidentally, in Japan, subcutaneous tocilizumab was not approved for JIA during the study period; its use in JIA patients was therefore off-label.

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered significant.

Ethical considerations

The CoNinJa study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Board of Institute of Science Tokyo (Tokyo Medical and Dental University), the primary study center (approval No: M2016-256). Additional approvals were obtained from the ethics committees of the 15 other participating medical institutions. Guardians and patients were informed through an opt-out procedure. The NinJa study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the NHO Sagamihara National Hospital (approval No: 2014031816) and the ethics committees of all participating institutions. In accordance with the decisions of the respective ethics committees, the study was conducted either with written informed consent obtained from patients or through an opt-out approach.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 176 patients with oligoarticular/polyarticular JIA and 152 patients with RA, all aged 16–30 years at the time of registration, were included in the analysis. The proportion of male patients was higher in the JIA group, with 39 of 176 patients (22%), compared to 17 of 152 patients (11%) in the RA group. The median (25th–75th percentile) age at disease onset was 11 years (7–14) in the JIA group and 21 years (19–24) in the RA group (Table 1). The median age at registration was 19 years (17–22) in the JIA group and 27 years (24–29) in the RA group. The JIA group also had a significantly longer disease duration, with a median of 9 years (6–14), compared to 4 years (2–7) in the RA group (Table 1).

To construct these cohorts in detail, 149 of 695 patients in the CoNinJa dataset initially met the JIA inclusion criteria. Of these, 44 (30%) had oligoarthritis, 71 (48%) had RF-positive polyarthritis, and 34 (23%) had RF-negative polyarthritis. From the NinJa dataset, 179 patients were identified out of 15,337 based on their age at registration (16–30 years). Among the CoNinJa cases, four patients experienced disease onset at age 16 or older and were therefore classified as RA. Conversely, among the NinJa cases, 31 patients experienced disease onset at age 15 or younger and were classified as JIA (oligoarthritis: 2 [7%], RF-positive polyarthritis: 12 [39%], RF-negative polyarthritis: 8 [26%], unknown: 9 [29%]).

For the final analysis, the JIA group consisted of 145 cases from CoNinJa and 31 cases from NinJa, while the RA group consisted of 148 cases from NinJa and 4 cases from CoNinJa (Supplementary Fig. 1). This unified cohort enabled a direct comparison of background characteristics, disease activity, and medication usage between young adults with JIA and those with RA.

Disease activity

ESR (mm/hour) and CRP (mg/dL) levels were significantly lower in the JIA group compared to the RA group [5.5 (3–9) vs. 9.5 (5–16) and 0.02 (0-0.05) vs. 0.09 (0.03–0.3), respectively] (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Patient VAS (0–10 cm scale) and physician VAS were also significantly lower in the JIA group. Similar results were observed for the number of tender and swollen joints, in both the 28-joint and 68-joint assessments (Table 2).

In the assessment of disease activity using composite measures, JIA patients showed significantly lower scores compared to RA patients, regardless of whether DAS28-ESR [1.4 (0.8–1.9) vs. 2.0 (1.4–2.8)], DAS28-CRP [1.1 (1.0-1.7) vs. 1.6 (1.2–2.3)], SDAI [0.6 (0.01–3.1) vs. 2.4 (0.9–6.5)], or CDAI [0.5 (0-2.9) vs. 2.5 (0.8–6.4)] was used (p < 0.001). Boolean 1.0 remission rates were significantly higher in the JIA group at 70%, compared to 44% in the RA group (p < 0.001). The remission rates assessed using various disease activity indices were as follows: DAS28-ESR (< 2.6) was 88% for the JIA group vs. 68% for the RA group, DAS28-CRP (< 2.3) was 90% vs. 75%, SDAI (≤ 3.3) was 76% vs. 53%, and CDAI (≤ 2.8) was 75% vs. 51%. Across all indices, the remission rates were significantly higher in the JIA group (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Medication use

There were no significant differences between the JIA and RA groups in the regular use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or glucocorticoids (Table 3). NSAIDs were used by 16% (28/176) of JIA patients and 21% (32/152) of RA patients, while glucocorticoids were used by 17% (29/176) and 22% (34/152), respectively. Although the overall usage rate of csDMARDs did not differ between the groups, methotrexate (MTX) was used significantly less frequently in the JIA group at 49% (86/176) compared to 68% (104/152) in the RA group (p < 0.001).

Biologics were used significantly more frequently in the JIA group, with 69% (121/176) of patients compared to 38% (57/152) in the RA group (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Regarding the selection rates among biologics users, tocilizumab (52%, 63/121, vs. 25%, 14/57) and adalimumab (17%, 21/121, vs. 4%, 2/57) were significantly higher in the JIA group compared to the RA group, whereas etanercept (16%, 19/121, vs. 35%, 20/57) and infliximab (4%, 5/121, vs. 14%, 8/57) were significantly lower (Table 3). The concomitant use rate of MTX with biologics was 50% (61/121) in the JIA group, compared to 67% (38/57) in the RA group (p = 0.052). Non-use of MTX was more common among tocilizumab users in both groups. The proportion of patients receiving biologic monotherapy was significantly higher in the JIA group at 41% (50/121), compared to 23% (13/57) in the RA group (p = 0.019). A Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor (tofacitinib) was used in one patient in the JIA group and in four patients in the RA group.

Unapproved medication use in JIA

Some JIA patients were found to be using medications that are approved for RA but not for JIA (Table 3). Regarding csDMARDs, tacrolimus was used by 6% (10/176), salazosulfapyridine by 2% (3/176), iguratimod by 2% (4/176), and bucillamine by 2% (3/176). Additionally, regarding biologics, 31% (37/121) of their usage involved unapproved applications in JIA. For tocilizumab and abatacept, which are approved for intravenous administration but not for subcutaneous administration, subcutaneous formulations were used in 20% (24/121) for tocilizumab and in 1% (1/121) for abatacept. Among tocilizumab users, 38% (24/63) were receiving the subcutaneous formulation. The use of infliximab, golimumab, and certolizumab was also observed in the JIA group.

Medication use in patients achieving remission

When comparing the treatment regimens of patients achieving CDAI remission in the JIA and RA groups, the JIA group showed a significantly lower usage rate of MTX (45%, 40/89, vs. 72%, 53/74) and a significantly higher usage rate of biologics (72%, 64/89, vs. 38%, 28/74) (p < 0.001) (Table 4). Furthermore, among biologics users, the JIA group had a significantly higher proportion of monotherapy users (29%, 26/89, vs. 7%, 5/74) (p < 0.001).

Medication use history and reasons for discontinuation in the JIA-CoNinJa group

The JIA-CoNinJa Group also investigated the history of medications and the reasons for discontinuation. In this group, 76% (110/145) of patients had a history of glucocorticoid use, and 3% (5/145) received intra-articular steroid injections. MTX had been used in all patients, and while 49% (67/136) were using it at the time of evaluation, 51% (69/136) had discontinued it mainly due to disease improvement (58%), nausea (29%), or ineffectiveness or insufficient efficacy (3%) (Table 5). Rare adverse events leading to discontinuation, including interstitial pneumonia, malignant lymphoma, and hepatic disorder, were reported in one case each. The three rare adverse events leading to discontinuation all occurred in the JIA-CoNinJa group: one case of interstitial pneumonia during MTX therapy, one case of malignant lymphoma during MTX therapy, and one case of hepatic disorder during MTX therapy. No such rare adverse events were reported in the RA group.

Intravenous tocilizumab was administered to 49 patients, with a discontinuation rate of 32% (15/49) (Table 5). The primary reason for discontinuation was disease improvement (40%, 6/15), and no cases of discontinuation due to adverse events were reported. In contrast, subcutaneous tocilizumab had the lowest discontinuation rate at 4% (1/23). The discontinuation rate for etanercept was 59% (19/32), with primary failure or insufficient efficacy being the most common reason (47%, 9/19), a rate higher than that observed for other biologics. Adalimumab had a discontinuation rate of 50% (19/38), with secondary failure being the most frequent cause (58%, 11/19), representing the highest rate of secondary failure among the biologics. Infliximab had the highest discontinuation rate among the biologics, at 71% (10/14), with adverse events being the leading contributing factor (40%, 4/10) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study examined differences in clinical practices, disease activity, and medication usage between oligoarticular/polyarticular JIA and RA patients during the transitional and young adult phases. Using data from CoNinJa and NinJa, several key findings were identified, offering valuable insights into the management of these patient populations.

Our findings revealed that JIA patients had significantly lower disease activity and higher remission rates compared to RA patients. Currently, both JIA and RA treatment strategies adhere to the Treat-to-Target (T2T) approach, with remission as the primary goal [18, 19]. For JIA, remission is defined by the Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score (JADAS)-27 [20] or inactive disease as defined by the Wallace criteria [21], which are more stringent and challenging than the remission criteria commonly used for RA. The observed lower disease activity in JIA may reflect the more aggressive treatment strategies recommended for JIA compared to RA. However, differences in disease duration, treatment regimens (discussed later), and the heterogeneity of JIA, which includes three distinct subtypes, necessitate cautious interpretation of these results.

In the analysis of medication use, significant differences were observed between JIA and RA patients. JIA patients had a lower usage rate of MTX and a higher usage rate of biologics, consistent with previous reports [22]. While not statistically significant, the rate of MTX combination therapy with biologics was lower in JIA, highlighting differences in treatment strategies between the two groups. Although all JIA patients in this study had a history of MTX use, only half were still using it at the time of the survey. The most common reason for MTX discontinuation was “disease improvement” (58%). Studies have shown that discontinuing MTX rather than biologics after achieving remission with both therapies is associated with a lower relapse rate [23], and our findings may support this observation. Additionally, “nausea” was reported as a reason for MTX discontinuation in 29% of cases, a slightly higher rate than previous reports [24]. The absence of injectable MTX formulations approved for JIA in Japan may contribute to these findings, emphasizing the need for such approvals.

During the transitional and young adult phases, family planning and pregnancy significantly influence treatment choices. In this study, JIA patients had a lower usage rate of MTX—a drug contraindicated during pregnancy and breastfeeding—and more frequently used biologic monotherapy, including tocilizumab. Biologics not contraindicated for pregnancy planning may facilitate MTX discontinuation, potentially extending the window of reproductive opportunity. Biologics with low placental transfer are particularly desirable; however, certolizumab pegol is not yet approved for JIA in Japan, necessitating careful consideration for its expanded indication. Additionally, the lack of data on pregnancy intentions, actual pregnancy, and childbirth outcomes underscores the need for further research on reproductive health in JIA and RA patients.

Economic factors also play a significant role in treatment choices. In Japan, JIA patients under 20 years old receive government-subsidized medical expenses under the Specified Pediatric Chronic Diseases Program. After reaching 20 years of ageon, they can continue receiving subsidies through the Designated Intractable Diseases Program, creating a favorable environment for the use of expensive biologics. Conversely, RA patients aged 20 and older often face higher out-of-pocket costs, potentially limiting their access to biologics despite clinical benefits. This disparity underscores the need for reforms in the medical subsidy system to ensure equitable access to advanced treatments for young RA patients.

The analysis of biologic discontinuation rates showed that etanercept, adalimumab, and infliximab had discontinuation rates of 50–71%, primarily due to primary or secondary failure (60–78%). In contrast, tocilizumab exhibited a low discontinuation rate, with “disease improvement” as the most common reason. Notably, the discontinuation rate for subcutaneous tocilizumab, which is not approved for JIA in Japan, was as low as 4%, reflecting its high efficacy and tolerability. Some cases of biologic discontinuation due to disease improvement suggest the possibility of biologic-free remission in JIA patients. However, this study lacked data on the sequence of biologic usage and concomitant medications at the time of discontinuation, limiting comparisons of the relative advantages of each biologic. Ongoing longitudinal data collection by the CoNinJa project aims to address these questions. This regulatory limitation highlights the unmet need for broader approval of subcutaneous tocilizumab in pediatric populations.

The analysis of medications used by JIA patients revealed that, despite being unapproved for JIA, drugs commonly used for RA, such as tacrolimus, salazosulfapyridine, and iguratimod, were also utilized in JIA patients. Similarly, many biologics approved for RA were frequently employed in JIA treatment. These findings indicate that treatment approaches for JIA patients beyond the transitional phase closely resemble those for RA. In Western countries, salazosulfapyridine [25] and leflunomide [26] have long been used for JIA. In Japan, tacrolimus was recently approved under a public knowledge application. However, the limited options for csDMARDs in JIA likely contribute to the high usage rate of biologics. The observed improvements with existing csDMARDs suggest their potential utility, warranting further evaluation, particularly from a health economic perspective. Discussions on expanding biologics’ indications for JIA, particularly for drug-resistant cases and patients planning for childbirth, are necessary.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it is subject to biases inherent in observational studies. Differences in disease duration among patients, the inclusion of various JIA subtypes, and the absence of detailed data on pregnancy intentions or comprehensive life-scale indicators, such as the Health Assessment Questionnaire, may have influenced the results. Furthermore, the data from 2016 may not fully reflect recent treatment trends. Supplementary analysis of the 2019 patient cohort, though based on a smaller sample size, revealed no significant differences in disease activity or biologic usage rates compared to the 2016 cohort (data not shown). Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the unique clinical practices and challenges faced by JIA and RA patients during this critical transitional period. Specifically, the differences in disease duration between JIA and RA, heterogeneity of JIA subtypes, absence of pregnancy-related data, and the retrospective design should be acknowledged as potential biases.

In conclusion, JIA patients in the transitional and young adult phases exhibited lower disease activity and a higher reliance on biologics compared to RA patients, reflecting differences in treatment goals and access to advanced therapies. These findings underscore the need to expand biologic indications for JIA and reform subsidy systems to support equitable access for young RA patients. Further research should address reproductive health needs and socioeconomic barriers to optimize care and improve the quality of life for both patient groups.
