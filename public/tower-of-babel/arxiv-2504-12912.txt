On the Geometry of Solutions of the Fully Nonlinear Inhomogeneous One-Phase Stefan Problem
Fausto Ferrari, Davide Giovagnoli, David Jesus

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we characterize the geometry of solutions to one-phase inhomogeneous fully nonlinear Stefan problem with flat free boundaries under a new nondegeneracy assumption.
This continues the study of regularity of flat free boundaries for the linear inhomogeneous Stefan problem started in
[
9
]
, as well as justifies the definition of flatness assumed in
[
15
]
.

1
Introduction

In this paper we obtain geometric results concerning the free boundary of solutions of a fully nonlinear one-phase Stefan problem governed by an inhomogeneous equation. That is, for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
T>0
, we deal with nonnegative viscosity solutions
u:\Omega\times(0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of the following free boundary problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-F(D^{2}u)=f&\text{in}\,\,\Omega^{+}(u):=\left(\Omega\times(0,T]\right)\cap\{u>0\},\\
\partial_{t}u=\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{F}(u):=\left(\Omega\times(0,T]\right)\cap\partial\{u>0\},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
F
is a uniformly elliptic operator,
f\in C(\Omega\times[0,T])\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times[0,T])
and
\mathcal{F}(u)
is the
free boundary
of the solution. More precisely we relate the geometric condition of free boundary flatness in the evolutive framework to a suitable notion of solution flatness in the inhomogeneous case. This result, together with the methods introduced in
[
8
]
and further developed in
[
9
,
15
]
, allows to conclude the analysis of the smoothness of flat free boundaries for solutions to (
1.1
).

The Stefan problem attracts the attention of many researchers since it models fundamental physical phenomena, such as the process of melting ice or freezing water, and it plays a pivotal role within the context of free boundary problems.

We recap some results concerning the viscosity formulation of the Stefan problem. For a more extensive discussion we refer to
[
5
]
. We point out the trilogy of articles
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
that collect important contributions on the regularity in the homogeneous two-phase Stefan problem. In
[
1
]
it is proved the optimal Lipschitz regularity across the free boundary and in
[
2
,
3
]
the authors focus their attention on the so-called strong results, which involve obtaining an improvement of regularity starting from an initial one. The parabolic structure of the problem permits the construction of some counterexamples (see for instance
[
5
, Section 8.3]
) for which Lipschitz free boundaries do not instantaneously regularize. However, if the Lipschitz constant is small enough or a nondegeneracy condition to prevent the heat fluxes to vanish on
\mathcal{F}(u)
is prescribed, further regularity of the free boundary can be obtained. Moreover, if only a nondegeneracy condition is assumed, in
[
3
]
, the authors proved that it is possible to reach the same conclusion for sufficiently ”flat” free boundaries, which do not even need to be graphs of functions.
For results in the variable coefficients case see
[
10
,
11
]
. We also point out the interesting paper
[
12
]
about the Hele-Shaw flow, which is a closely related topic.

In
[
8
]
, the authors adapted the viscosity elliptic approach introduced in
[
7
]
to deal with the parabolic case. They addressed the regularity in a parabolic homogeneous one-phase scenario, employing the hodograph transform and viscosity theory. In particular, they introduced a new notion of a flat free boundary within a parabolic framework.

All of the aforementioned results on the Stefan problem consider only the homogeneous situation. However, the tools introduced in
[
8
]
are flexible enough to consider a source term, provided that suitable difficulties are addressed. In this order of ideas, we describe the difficulties tackled in this paper, using the physical example of ice melting as a reference.
Recall that for the one-phase scenario, the condition on the free boundary,
\partial_{t}u=|\nabla u|^{2}
, only describes the melting of ice, since the time derivative is nonnegative, and so the interface between the two states is pushed over time towards the ice side.
Hence, the positive part of
f
adds heat to the system following the natural direction of the boundary condition. On the other hand, a more delicate situation appears if we allow the source term
f
to change sign, since this introduces a competition between the effect of the law of conservation of energy at the free boundary and the external source.
The presence of a negative source term slows down the melting process of ice, potentially leading to a degenerate situation where no regularization is expected.
Indeed, this informal discussion translates into a very precise technical difficulty that appears when
f
is allowed to be negative, which is the fact that the Hopf Lemma, as it is stated in the homogeneous setting, is false in this case. Hence, a stronger nondegeneracy condition has to be assumed.

Our goal falls within the scope of proving that flat free boundaries are smooth under minimal assumptions. As in
[
8
]
, the first step in this strategy is to characterize the geometry of the graph of
u
near the
\varepsilon_{0}
-flat free boundary. We say that the free boundary is
\varepsilon_{0}
-flat if, at each time, within a small ball centered at a point on the free boundary, it is contained in a narrow strip (see Definition
2.2
).
The main result, conveniently formulated for the parabolic rescaling of (
1.1

\partial_{t}u=\lambda\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\left(B_{2}\times(-(K\lambda)^{-1},0]\right)\cap\partial\{u>0\},\end{cases}

We now comment on the nondegeneracy condition (
1.3
). First, we emphasize that it depends on the
L^{\infty}-
norm of
f^{-}
. This fact appears to be sensible, since the model describes the melting of ice and the free boundary condition effect needs to prevail with respect to the contribution of the negative part of the source term. In fact, this ensures that close to the free boundary, the solution is “large enough to balance the effect of
f^{-}
”. This assumption prevents the water from freezing in the presence of a negative source term, which would conflict with the condition at the free boundary.

Furthermore, we remark that if
u
is Lipschitz continuous with sufficiently small Lipschitz norm, the integral condition (
1.3
) is equivalent to the more standad pointwise one

u(x_{0},t_{0})\geq K^{-1}(1+\left\lVert f^{-}\right\rVert_{\infty}).

Instead of assuming the Lipschitz continuity of
u
, we can replace (
1.3
) with the alternative assumption

u(x_{0},t_{0})\geq K^{-1}(1+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}),

(1.5)

however it does not capture the difficulty associated with a negative source term, as discussed before. Notice that (
1.5
) reduces in the homogeneous case to the nondegeneracy condition considered in
[
8
]
. For a detailed discussion of this point, we refer to Section
4

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
K>1
. For

and

which depend only on

such that if

is a viscosity solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\mathcal{M}_{K}^{-}(D^{2}u)\geq-\kappa&\mbox{ in }B_{1}\times(0,T]\\

u\geq 0&\mbox{ in }\partial_{p}(B_{1}\times(0,T]).\end{cases}

(1.6)

Then

u(x,t)\geq\mu\,\dist(x,\partial B_{1})\quad\mbox{ in }B_{1}\times[T_{1},T].

(1.7)

2.1
Notation and definitions

Let
(x,t)\in\R^{n+1}
, we call
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the space variable and
t\in\R
the time variable. A point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
will sometimes be written as
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
, where
x^{\prime}\in\R^{n-1}
and
x_{n}\in\R
.
Given a function
u(x,t)
, we denote by
\nabla u
the gradient of
u
with respect to
x
,
D^{2}u
the Hessian with respect to
x
,
\nabla_{x^{\prime}}u
the gradient with respect to
x^{\prime}
,
\partial_{x_{n}}u
and
\partial_{t}u
the partial derivatives of
u
with respect to
x_{n}
and
t
, respectively.

A
parabolic cylinder

\mathcal{P}
is any set of the form

\mathcal{P}=U\times(t_{1},t_{2}],

where
U
is a smooth bounded domain in
\R^{n}
and
t_{1}<t_{2}
. We denote by
\partial_{p}\mathcal{P}
the
parabolic boundary
of
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,
\partial_{p}\mathcal{P}=\left(U\times\{t_{1}\}\right)\cup\left(\partial U\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\right)
.
In particular, we define the
standard parabolic cylinder
of radius
r
as

\mathcal{P}_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})=B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0},t_{0}+r^{2}]

We will omit the center if it is the origin.
Given
(x,t),(y,s)\in\R^{n+1}
, we define the
parabolic distance
as

d_{p}((x,t),(y,s)):=\big(|x-y|^{2}+|t-s|\big)^{1/2}.

Let
C^{0}(\mathcal{P})
be the set of continuous function in
\mathcal{P}
. For
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
u\in C^{0}(\mathcal{P})
, define the parabolic Hölder semi-norm as

[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t)\neq(y,s)\end{subarray}\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{\left\lvert u(x,t)-u(y,s)\right\rvert}{d_{p}((x,t),(y,s))^{\alpha}}.

The parabolic Hölder norm is defined as

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}:=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})}+[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}.

Further we define the Hölder semi-norm in time to be

[u]_{C_{t}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t_{1})\neq(x,t_{2})\end{subarray}\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{|u(x,t_{1})-u(x,t_{2})|}{|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\alpha}}.

This is necessary to introduce the
C^{1,\alpha}(\mathcal{P})
-norm as

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}:=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})}+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})}+[\nabla u]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}+[u]_{C_{t}^{(1+\alpha)/2}(\mathcal{P})}.

We denote by
C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})
(resp.
C^{1,\alpha}(\mathcal{P})
) the space of continuous (resp. differentiable in space) functions on
\mathcal{P}
which are bounded in the
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}
(resp.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}
) norm.
Throughout the paper, we will denote by K a large universal constant.
The operator
F:\mathcal{S}(d)\to\mathbb{R}
is uniformly
K
-elliptic if it satisfies

K^{-1}|N|\leq F(M)-F(M+N)\leq K|N|,

for every
M,N\in\mathcal{S}(d)
, with
N\geq 0
.
Recall the definition of extremal Pucci operators with ellipticity constants
K^{-1}
and
K

\mathcal{M}_{K}^{+}(N)=\sup_{K^{-1}I\leq A\leq KI}\quad\tr(AN),\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{M}_{K}^{-}(N)=\inf_{K^{-1}I\leq A\leq KI}\quad\tr(AN).

(2.1)

Let
f
be a continuous function, we denote by
\underline{S}_{K}(f)
the space of upper semi-continuous functions
u
in
\mathcal{P}
such that
\partial_{t}u-M_{K}^{+}(D^{2}u)\leq f
in
\mathcal{P}
. The classes
\overline{S}_{K}(f),S_{K}(f)
and
S_{K}^{*}(f)
are defined as usual.
In the rest of the paper the constant
\alpha_{0}
always refers to the universal exponent corresponding to the

regularity of solutions to the problem
\partial_{t}u-F(D^{2}u)=0

]

In what follows
c,\,C,\,C_{1},\dots
denote universal constants that may vary from line to line.

Proof of Proposition

Consider the barrier function

Note that

Defined in this way we also have that

\displaystyle h(x,0)\leq h(\frac{\delta}{2},0)=0,\quad\text{for }|x|\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\qquad h(x,0)\leq h(0,0)=1\quad\text{for all }x.

Thus
h\leq u
on
\partial_{p}(B_{1}\times(0,T])
. We need to prove that exists a

where

Computing the spatial derivatives of

By the property of the Pucci extremal operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{K}^{-}(D^{2}h)\geq\frac{E}{D}\frac{2K^{2}}{(t+a)^{2}}\left[2\mathcal{M}_{K}^{-}\left(x\otimes x\right)-(t+a)n\right]=\frac{E}{D}\frac{2K}{(t+a)^{2}}\left[2\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}-nK(t+a)\right].

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\int_{t-t_{0}}^{t-(t_{0}-\eta^{2})}\rho^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}(t-t_{0}-s)+1\right)\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\bar{a}(s)\,ds

taking
r=\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}(t-t_{0}-s)+1
and
c_{1}=\int_{-1}^{0}\rho^{\prime}(r)\,dr=-\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{\prime}(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\int_{-1}^{0}\rho^{\prime}(r)\bar{a}\left(t-t_{0}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}(r-1)\right)\,dr+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{\prime}(r)\bar{a}\left(t-t_{0}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}(r-1)\right)\,dr\leq

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\sup_{(t-t_{0},t-t_{0}+\eta^{2})}\bar{a}\int_{-1}^{0}\rho^{\prime}(r)\,dr+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\inf_{(t-t_{0},t-t_{0}+\eta^{2})}\bar{a}\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{\prime}(r)\,dr=\frac{c_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\operatorname{osc}_{(t-t_{0},t-t_{0}+\eta^{2})}\bar{a}\leq C\eta^{\gamma-2}

and from the convergence of
\bar{a}_{\eta}
with
\bar{a}
we have

\bar{a}_{\eta}(t)\left(x_{n}-b(t)-C\eta^{1+\gamma}\right)^{+}\leq u(x,s)\leq\bar{a}_{\eta}(t)\left(x_{n}-b(t)+C\eta^{1+\gamma}\right)^{+}\quad\text{in }B_{2C_{0}\eta}\times[t-\eta^{2},t]

(3.24)

with the following bounds on the coefficients

c(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})\leq\bar{a}_{\eta}(t)\leq C,\quad\left\lvert\bar{a}_{\eta}^{\prime}(t)\right\rvert\leq C\eta^{\gamma-2},\quad\left\lvert b(t)\right\rvert\leq C_{0}\eta.

In the following
\bar{a}_{\eta}
will be denoted with
\bar{a}
.

Now, in order to conclude the argument we perturb
b
by constructing
\tilde{b}
which satisfies the ODE
\tilde{b}^{\prime}=-\lambda\bar{a}
and
\tilde{b}(0)=0
. In particular we will show that for
t\in[-\lambda^{-1}\eta,0]
,

|b(t)-\tilde{b}(t)|\leq C\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}.

(3.25)

The method consists in perturbing the family of evolving planes
\bar{a}(t)(x_{n}-\tilde{b}(t))^{+}
into a subsolution/supersolution. For this we define

d(t):=\tilde{b}(t)+C_{1}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\lambda t,

with
C_{1}
large to be fixed. Now, obtaining
(
3.25
)
is equivalent to show that

b(t)\geq d(t)-2\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}.

(3.26)

In this direction we define the function

v(x,t):=(1-C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\bar{a}(t)\left(h(x-d(t)e_{n})\right)^{+}\quad\text{with}\quad h(x):=x_{n}-\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}\left(\left\lvert x^{\prime}\right\rvert^{2}-C_{3}x_{n}^{2}\right)

where the constants
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
are large and will be assigned later in the proof. Choose the constant
C_{2}
universal in such a way that

v\leq\bar{a}(t)(x_{n}-d(t))^{+},

(3.27)

they also coincide in
d(t)e_{n}
by construction and moreover, when
x\in\partial B_{2\eta}(d(t)e_{n})\cap\{v>0\}
, the difference is greater than
\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}.
Next, we can check that
v
is a strict subsolution of (
1.2
) in the domain
\Omega:=\cup_{t\in[-\lambda^{-1}\eta,0]}B_{2\eta}(d(t)e_{n})\times\{t\}
. First, in
\{v>0\}
, we have

\displaystyle d^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lambda(-\bar{a}+C_{1}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\qquad\nabla h=(-2\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x^{\prime},1+2C_{3}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x_{n})

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v

\displaystyle=(1-C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\left[\bar{a}^{\prime}h+\bar{a}h_{n}d^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\left[\bar{a}^{\prime}h-\bar{a}(1+2C_{3}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}(x_{n}-d)\lambda(-\bar{a}+C_{1}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\right]

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{K}^{+}(D^{2}v)

\displaystyle=(1-C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\bar{a}\left(2KC_{3}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}-2K^{-1}(n-1)\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}\right).

We obtain the following bound

|\partial_{t}v|\leq C|\bar{a}^{\prime}|\eta+C|d^{\prime}|\leq C\eta^{\gamma-1}.

Hence choosing
C_{3}
large enough, depending on
f
, we have that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{M}_{K}^{+}(D^{2}v)

\displaystyle\leq C\eta^{\gamma-1}-\frac{c}{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}(2KC_{3}-2K^{-1}(n-1))

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}\left(C\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}-\frac{c}{2}KC_{3}\right)\leq-\frac{c}{4}KC_{3}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}<-\lambda f^{-}.

Regarding the condition on the free boundary,

v_{t}=(1-C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\bar{a}(-d^{\prime})h_{n},\quad\quad|\nabla v|^{2}\geq(1-2C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})\bar{a}^{2}.

Since

h_{n}=1+O(\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}),\quad\quad\quad(-d^{\prime})\bar{a}=\lambda\bar{a}^{2}-C_{1}\lambda\bar{a}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}},

we can choose
C_{1}>2C_{2}C
, where
C
is the upper bound of
\bar{a}
, such that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v\leq\lambda\bar{a}^{2}\left(1-\frac{C_{1}}{\bar{a}}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\right)\leq\lambda\bar{a}^{2}\left(1-\frac{C_{1}}{C}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\right)<\lambda\bar{a}^{2}\left(1-2C_{2}\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\right)\leq\lambda|\nabla v|^{2}.

Arguing by contradiction, if

b(t_{0})<d(t_{0})-2\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}
for some
t_{0}\in[-\lambda^{-1}\eta,0]
,

then by (
3.24
) and (
3.27
) we find that
v<u
at time
t=t_{0}
in
B_{2\eta}(d(t_{0})e_{n})\cap\overline{\{v>0\}}
.
On the other hand
v=u
at the origin
(0,0)
.
This means that as we increase
t
from
t_{0}
to
0
,
the graph of
v(\cdot,t)
in
B_{2\eta}(d(t)e_{n})\cap\overline{\{v>0\}}
will touch from below the graph of
u
for a first time
t
,
and the contact must be an interior point to
B_{2\eta}(d(t)e_{n})
due to the properties (
3.24
),(
3.27
) of
u
and
v
(in particular the difference between
\bar{a}(t)\,(x_{n}-d(t))^{+}
and
v
is greater than
\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}
on
\partial B_{2\eta}(d(t)e_{n})
).
This contact point is either on the free boundary
\partial\{v>0\}
or on the positivity set
\{v>0\}
and we reach a contradiction since
v
is a strict subsolution. The claim (
3.26
) is proved, hence

b(t)\geq\tilde{b}(t)-C\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}\quad\mbox{if}\quad\quad t\in[-\lambda^{-1}\eta,0].

The opposite inequality is obtained similarly and the claim (
3.25
) holds. Then from (
3.24
) and (
3.25
) we deduce that for all
\eta\leq c
small

\bar{a}(t)\left(x_{n}-\tilde{b}(t)-\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{4}}\right)^{+}\,\,\leq\,\,u\,\,\leq\,\,\bar{a}(t)\left(x_{n}-\tilde{b}(t)+\eta^{1+\frac{\gamma}{4}}\right)^{+}

in
B_{\eta}\times[-\lambda^{-1}\eta,0]
with
\frac{\gamma}{4}=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{4(2+\alpha_{0})}\in(0,1)
universal and

c(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})\leq\bar{a}(t)\leq C,\quad|\bar{a}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\eta^{\frac{\gamma}{4}-2},\quad\quad\tilde{b}^{\prime}(t)=-\lambda\bar{a}(t),\quad\tilde{b}(0)=0.

\square

4
The equivalence of the integral and pointwise nondegeneracy condition

The nondegeneracy condition considered in this paper is equivalent to the usual one that appears in the literature (for example in
[
2
,
8
]
) under the assumption that the Lipschitz norm of the solution is sufficiently small. We prove one of the implications in the following lemma, since the other implication is immediate.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
u
is a Lipschitz continuous function with respect to the parabolic distance, that is,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t),(y,s)\in B_{1}\times[-1,0]\end{subarray}}|u(x,t)-u(y,s)|\leq Ld_{p}((x,t),(y,s))

with
L\leq 2K^{-1}(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})
and for every
\lambda>0
there exists a point
x_{0}\in\partial B_{\frac{3}{4}\lambda}
such that for every
t_{0}\in(-(K\lambda)^{-1},0)

u(x_{0},t_{0})\geq K^{-1}(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})\lambda,

then for every
0<p_{0}<1
and
r<\frac{1}{4}\lambda
we have

\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0},t_{0}+r^{2})}u^{p_{0}}\,dxdt\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{0}}}\geq\frac{K^{-1}}{2}(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})\lambda

Proof.

First we note that
u^{p_{0}}
is
C^{0,p_{0}}
in the parabolic sense. This is a consequence of the fact that the function

g(y)=1-y^{p_{0}}-(1-y)^{p_{0}}

is convex. Thus we compute

\displaystyle u^{p_{0}}(x_{0},t_{0})\leq

\displaystyle\,\left|u^{p_{0}}(x_{0},t_{0})-\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0},t_{0}+r^{2})}u^{p_{0}}\right|+\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0},t_{0}+r^{2})}u^{p_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0},t_{0}+r^{2})}|u^{p_{0}}(x_{0},t_{0})-u^{p_{0}}(x,t)|+\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0},t_{0}+r^{2})}u^{p_{0}}\right).

Therefore since
L\leq 2K^{-1}(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times(r^{2},2r^{2})}u^{p_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{0}}}\geq\left[K^{-p_{0}}(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})^{p_{0}}-\left(\frac{L}{4}\right)^{p_{0}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p_{0}}}\lambda\geq\frac{K^{-1}}{2}(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})\lambda.

\Box

Remark 4.2
.

Without requiring any further regularity on
u
it is possible to consider, instead of (
1.8
), the pointwise nondegeneracy condition with the full
L^{\infty}
-norm of
f
, namely for every
t_{0}\in(-(K\lambda)^{-1},0)

u(x_{0},t_{0})\geq K^{-1}(1+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty})\lambda.

(4.1)

This can be done replacing the Weak Harnack inequality in (
3.14
) with the Harnack inequality in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
As discussed in the introduction, the nondegeneracy condition (
1.8
) more accurately captures the main obstacle of the inhomogeneous Stefan problem, since it depends only on the negative part of
f
.
