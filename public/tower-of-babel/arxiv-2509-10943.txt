Abstract
. We study those measures whose doubling constant is the least possible among doubling measures on a given metric space. It is shown that such measures exist on every metric space supporting at least one doubling measure. In addition, a connection between minimizers for the doubling constant and superharmonic functions is exhibited. This allows us to show that for the particular case of the euclidean space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , Lebesgue measure is the only minimizer for the doubling constant (up to constant multiples) precisely when d = 1 d=1 or d = 2 d=2 , while for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 there are infinitely many independent minimizers. Analogously, in the discrete setting, we can show uniqueness of the counting measure as a minimizer for regular graphs where the standard random walk is a recurrent Markov chain. The counting measure is also shown to be a minimizer in every infinite graph where the cardinality of balls depends solely on their radii.

Introduction

Given a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, a Borel regular measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
is said to be doubling if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r>0

\mu(B(x,2r))\leq C\mu(B(x,r))

and
0<\mu(B(x_{0},r_{0}))<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,
r_{0}>0
. If so,
0<\mu(B(x,r))<\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r>0
.
Here and throughout the paper
B(x,r)=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:d(y,x)<r\}
.

Metric spaces equipped with a doubling measure (also known in the literature as homogeneous spaces
[
CW71
]
) are central in analysis and geometry because they provide a controlled notion of “volume growth” that resembles Euclidean space. The doubling condition is crucial for extending classical tools, such as covering theorems, maximal functions, Poincaré inequalities and differentiation theory, to more general geometrical contexts (see the monographs
[
BB11
,
Hei01
]
for these and further developments). This makes them a natural setting for analysis on fractals, manifolds, and metric spaces arising in a wide range of topics, including for instance geometric group theory or computer science.

Given a doubling measure
\mu
, let
C_{\mu}
denote its doubling constant, which is the best possible
C
in the display above. In other words,

C_{\mu}=\sup_{x,r}\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}.

Now, if
\mathcal{X}
admits a doubling measure, we can also denote the
least doubling constant
of
\mathcal{X}
by

C_{\mathcal{X}}=\inf_{\mu}C_{\mu},

where the infimum ranges over all doubling measures on
\mathcal{X}
, and we say that
C_{\mathcal{X}}=\infty
if
\mathcal{X}
does not support any doubling measure.

Although a relatively natural quantity, the research on
C_{\mathcal{X}}
started only recently in
[
ST19
]
, where it was shown that
C_{\mathcal{X}}\geq 2
whenever
\mathcal{X}
does not reduce to a singleton. The precise value of
C_{\mathcal{X}}
is related to metric dimensions and can be relevant for instance for controlling the norm of extension operators on spaces of Lipschitz functions (see
[
BB06
]
), among other applications.

The least doubling constant of a metric space and the set of measures minimizing this constant can reveal relevant information about the space. It is easy to observe that, for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the distance induced by any
p
-norm (
1\leq p\leq\infty
), one has
C_{(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})}=2^{d}
(see
[
ST19
, Proposition 5.1]
), and this value is achieved in particular with the Lebesgue measure. The question that motivates our research in this paper is whether Lebesgue measure is the only minimizer, up to scalar multiples.

In the discrete setting, a connection between the least doubling constant of a graph (equipped with the shortest path distance) and the spectral radius of its (possibly infinite) adjacency matrix has been found in
[
DCST23
]
. To be more precise, for a simple connected locally finite graph
G
and a measure
\mu
on
G
one can consider the local doubling constant

C_{\mu}^{0}=\sup_{x\in G}\frac{\mu(B(x,2))}{\mu(B(x,1))},

and the least local doubling constant
C_{G}^{0}=\inf_{\mu}C_{\mu}^{0}
. For every graph,
C_{G}^{0}
coincides with
1+r(A_{G})
, where
r(A_{G})
is the spectral radius of the adjacency matrix of
G
; moreover, in the case of finite graphs, where
r(A_{G})
coincides with the largest eigenvalue of
A_{G}
, the corresponding eigenvector (also known as Perron eigenvector) induces the unique measure minimizing
C_{G}^{0}
. In general,
C_{G}^{0}\leq C_{G}
with equality under certain conditions. In particular, it can be shown that certain families of graphs, including all graphs with diameter 2, can be shown to have a unique minimizer up to multiplicative constants. For the particular case of linear graphs with
n
vertices, this is the case as long as
n\leq 8
, while for
n\geq 9
the situation is more elusive. In the infinite linear graphs
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Z}
the least doubling constant is 3, and although the counting measure is the unique minimizer for
\mathbb{Z}
, this is not the case for
\mathbb{N}
(see
[
DCST25
]
for details).

In this paper, our starting point will be a simple argument, based on Christ’s dyadic systems of cubes, that shows minimizers for the least doubling constant exist on every metric space which supports a doubling measure. Recall that for complete metric spaces the existence of doubling measures is equivalent to the space being geometrically doubling
[
LS98
]
(see also
[
VK88
]
), in the sense that there is a constant
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
r>0
, every ball of radius
2r
can be covered by at most
N
balls of radius
r
.

Once minimizing measures for the doubling constant are always shown to exist, it is natural to wonder how large this set of minimizers can be and what structure it might have. For instance, are all minimizing measures absolutely continuous with respect to each other? It is pertinent to recall here that, although doubling measures can be supported on very small sets, there are certain limitations (see
[
ST19
, Proposition 2.5]
,
[
Ste93
, I.8.6]
,
[
Wu98
]
). In some cases, including the canonical example of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an argument from
[
Jon95
]
will allow us to show that minimizing measures are actually mutually absolutely continuous with respect to each other. This fact will be key in the following:

Theorem A
.

For
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
, Lebesgue measure is the unique doubling minimizer (up to scalar multiples) as long as
d=1,2
, whereas for
d\geq 3
the set of doubling minimizers is an infinite-dimensional cone.

The proof of this result will be based on the fact that a minimizer on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
must be a measure with density
f
(with respect to Lebesgue measure) such that

\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int_{B(x,r)}f(y)dy\geq\frac{1}{|B(x,2r)|}\int_{B(x,2r)}f(y)dy.

In particular,
f
must be superharmonic and positive. Since there are no nonconstant functions with that property unless
d\geq 3
, that will settle the question.

We give alternative arguments when
d\in\{1,2\}
that prove that the only minimizer is indeed the Lebesgue measure. These are based on discretizations of the minimizers and combine known result in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with an application of Polya’s random walk theorem. The procedure works for the norms
\|\cdot\|_{1}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
by the nature of the discretization argument, taking advantage of the fact that the corresponding balls have flat boundaries in both cases. In the discrete setting, we also show that the counting measure
\#_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a minimizer in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in every dimension, although it may not be the only one when
d\geq 3
. This also yields a procedure to compute the explicit value of the constant
C_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
in any dimension. The result is a particular case of the following theorem.

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a infinite graph such that
|B(x,r)|=|B(y,r)|
for all
x,y\in G
and
r>0
. Then
\#_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X})
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section
1
deals with general metric spaces and contains the proof of existence of doubling minimizers, as well as several other basic facts. In Section
2
, we prove Theorem
B
and we discuss examples of discrete measure spaces for which we can identify the family of doubling minimizers via its local structure. This will include, in particular, the cases of
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Finally, Section
3
contains the proof of Theorem
A
and some further results about doubling minimizers for different metrics on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

1.
General metric spaces

Unless specified otherwise, we will assume the metric spaces we deal with support some doubling measure. For every metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, the quantity
C_{\mathcal{X}}
is defined as an infimum. We first show that, in fact, it is always attained. For the discrete case, this was shown in
[
DCST25
, Proposition 2]
. For the proof in the general case, we need to use the existence of a system of dyadic cubes on
\mathcal{X}
. This is guaranteed as long as
\mathcal{X}
is geometrically doubling, as shown in
[
Chr90
]
. In turn, being geometrically doubling is a necessary condition for
C_{\mathcal{X}}<\infty
, and sufficient under the hypothesis of compactness or completeness (
[
VK88
]
,
[
LS98
]
). Our construction is borrowed from
[
HK12
]
. The result can be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

[Theorem 2.2 in
[
HK12
]
]
Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a geometrically doubling metric space. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, there exist a set of points
\{z_{j}^{k}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and a partition
\mathscr{D}_{k}=\{Q_{j}^{k}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{X}
such that the following properties hold:

(1)

If
\ell\geq k
,
Q_{j}^{k}\cap Q_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell}\in\{\emptyset,Q_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell}\}
.

(2)

There exists a constant
C_{0}
such that
B(z_{j}^{k},2^{-k})\subseteq Q_{j}^{k}\subseteq B(z_{j}^{k},2^{-k}C_{0})
for all
j,k
.

As usual, we denote

\mathscr{D}=\bigcup_{k}\mathscr{D}_{k}.

As a consequence of (2) in Theorem
1.1
, every ball
B\subset\mathcal{X}
is a disjoint union of elements from
\mathscr{D}
. In particular, a Borel regular measure is determined by its values on the elements from
\mathscr{D}
. In fact, by the properties of the system of dyadic cubes (see
[
HK12
, Lemma 3.5]
) such a measure will be determined by its value on
\bigcup_{k\geq k_{0}}\mathscr{D}_{k}
for any fixed
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space such that
C_{\mathcal{X}}<\infty
. Then, there is a measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
such that

C_{\mu}=C_{\mathcal{X}}.

Proof.

Let
\{\tilde{\mu}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of measures on
\mathcal{X}
such that
C_{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\to C_{\mathcal{X}}
. We normalize them by defining

\mu_{n}:=\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{n}}{\tilde{\mu}_{n}(Q_{1}^{0})},

so that
\mu_{n}(Q_{1}^{0})=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Rescaling does not modify the doubling constant, so
C_{\mu_{n}}\to C_{\mathcal{X}}
as well.

Let us now fix
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
and see that the sequences
\{\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{k})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are bounded. Indeed, for each
j
there is an
R=R(j)>0
such that
Q_{j}^{0}\subset B(z_{1}^{0},R)
. Define
\gamma=\ulcorner\log_{2}(R)\urcorner
. We have that

\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{0})\leq\mu_{n}(B(z_{1}^{0},R))\leq(C_{\mu_{n}})^{\gamma}\mu_{n}(Q_{1}^{0})\leq(\sup_{n}C_{\mu_{n}})^{\gamma}.

The sequence
\{C_{\mu_{n}}\}
is convergent and hence bounded, so the sequences of measures of dyadic cubes of scale
k=0
are indeed bounded. If
k>0
, then for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
there is
j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Q_{j}^{k}\subset Q_{j^{\prime}}^{0}
, and so
\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{k})\leq\mu_{n}(Q_{j^{\prime}}^{0})
. Therefore, the sequence
\{\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{k})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Hence, for each
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a subsequence
\mathbb{M}_{j,k}
such that
\{\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{k})\}_{n\in\mathbb{M}_{j,k}}
converges. Cantor diagonalization yields a subsequence
\mathbb{M}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that
\{\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{k})\}_{n\in\mathbb{M}}
converges for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and so we can define

\mu(Q_{j}^{k}):=\lim_{n\in\mathbb{M}}\mu_{n}(Q_{j}^{k}).

This defines
\mu
over
\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathscr{D}_{k}
. Next, all open balls are a disjoint, countable union of cubes, so this determines the measure unequivocally on
\mathcal{X}
. We finally claim
C_{\mu}=C_{\mathcal{X}}
. To see this, consider a ball
B(x,r)\subset\mathcal{X}
and take a countable disjoint family
\{Q_{i}\}\subset\mathscr{D}
such that

B(x,r)=\bigcup_{i}Q_{i}.

Then

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,r))=\sum_{i}\mu(Q_{i})=\sum_{i}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{M}}\mu_{n}(Q_{i})=\lim_{n\in\mathbb{M}}\mu_{n}(B(x,r)).

Hence, given
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
r>0
, we indeed have

\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}=\lim_{n\in\mathbb{M}}\frac{\mu_{n}(B(x,2r))}{\mu_{n}(B(x,r))}\leq C_{\mathcal{X}}.

∎

Given a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, let us denote the set of doubling minimizers by

\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)=\{\mu:C_{\mu}=C_{\mathcal{X}}\}.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space.

(i)

\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
is a convex cone.

(ii)

\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
is invariant under isometries.

Proof.

(i) Clearly the doubling constant is invariant under scalar multiplication,
C_{\alpha\mu}=C_{\mu}
for every
\alpha>0
. Take now
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
and let
\mu=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
. For
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}(B(x,2r))+\mu_{2}(B(x,2r))}{\mu_{1}(B(x,r))+\mu_{2}(B(x,r))}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\frac{\mu_{1}(B(x,2r))}{\mu_{1}(B(x,r))},\frac{\mu_{2}(B(x,2r))}{\mu_{2}(B(x,r))}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{C_{\mu_{1}},C_{\mu_{2}}\}=C_{\mathcal{X}}.

This shows that
\mu\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
as claimed.

(ii) Let
\phi
denote an isometry on
(\mathcal{X},d)
(i.e., a bijective map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
such that
d(x,y)=d(\phi x,\phi y)
for every
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
). Given
\mu\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
, let
\mu_{\phi}(A)=\mu(\phi(A))
for every Borel set
A\subset\mathcal{X}
. Clearly, we have

C_{\mu_{\phi}}=\sup_{x,r}\frac{\mu(\phi(B(x,2r)))}{\mu(\phi(B(x,r)))}=\sup_{x,r}\frac{\mu(B(\phi(x),2r))}{\mu(B(\phi(x),r))}=C_{\mu}.

∎

As is customary, for a locally integrable function
f
defined on a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
, and
A\in\Sigma
, we denote the corresponding average as

\fint_{A}fd\mu:=\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\int_{A}fd\mu.

Given a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
and
\mu
a Borel regular measure on
(\mathcal{X},d)
, we will say a function
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
superharmonic with respect to
\mu
if for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r>0

\fint_{B(x,2r)}fd\mu\leq\fint_{B(x,r)}fd\mu.

(1.1)

This extends the notion of
(strongly) harmonic functions
on a metric measure space
(\mathcal{X},d,\mu)
, which are defined by the property

f(x)=\fint_{B(x,r)}fd\mu,

for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and every
r>0
. These were introduced and analyzed in
[
AGG19
,
GG09
]
, and clearly satisfy (
1.1
).

The following fact (whose straightforward proof is omitted) shows that a wealth of superharmonic functions allows us to construct new minimizers from known ones.

Proposition 1.4
.

If
\mu\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
, and
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
with Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
being superharmonic with respect to
\mu
, then
\nu\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X},d)
.

A natural question here is whether all doubling minimizers can be described by a density with respect to a given minimizer. Note that it is well known that there exist mutually singular doubling measures on
[0,1]

[
AB56
, Theorem 3]
. In fact, there are examples of doubling measures in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that assign positive measure to a rectifiable curve
[
Gar10
]
(although all of them must vanish on hypersurfaces). We will show next the absolute continuity of minimizers under very specific conditions, related to the Hausdorff measure of the space and its minimal doubling constant. These follow from the work of Sjödin
[
Sjö97
]
which in turn generalizes the work of Jonsson
[
Jon95
]
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us first recall the following.

Definition 1.5
.

A Borel measure
\mu
on a metric space
\mathcal{X}
is called a
d
-measure (
d>0
) if there exist a constant
C\geq 1
and
r_{0}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
r\leq r_{0}

\frac{1}{C}r^{d}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{d}.

(1.2)

This is slightly weaker than the well-known notion of Ahlfors
d
-regular measure. In particular, note that a metric space which supports a
d
-measure must have Hausdorff dimension equal to
d

[
Sjö97
, Proposition 1.1]
. We will also need the following.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel measure on a metric space
\mathcal{X}
, and let
C\geq 1
,
d\geq 0
. We say that
\mu
is
(C,d)
-homogeneous if it satisfies

\mu(B(x,\lambda r))\leq C\lambda^{d}\mu(B(x,r))

(1.3)

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
r>0
, and
\lambda\geq 1
.

A
(C,d)
-homogeneous measure
\mu
is, in particular, doubling with
C_{\mu}\leq C2^{d}
. Conversely, a doubling measure is
(C_{\mu},\log_{2}C_{\mu})
-homogeneous. From this notion, one can define the Volberg–Konyagin dimension (see
[
LS98
]
) as

\dim_{VK}(\mathcal{X}):=\inf\{d:\textrm{there exists a }(C,d)\textrm{-homogeneous measure }\mu\textrm{ on }\mathcal{X}\}.

In particular, we have
\dim_{VK}(\mathcal{X})\leq\log_{2}C_{\mathcal{X}}
. Volberg-Konyagin dimension can be thought of as a measure theoretic counterpart of the well-known notion of Assouad’s dimension of a metric space. In fact, it is easy to see that the Hausdorff dimension of a metric space is always bounded above by
\dim_{VK}(\mathcal{X})
. Therefore, on a metric space of Hausdorff dimension
d
we have that
C_{\mathcal{X}}\geq 2^{d}
. The following relevant observation, which applies for instance to the particular case of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, follows from
[
Sjö97
]
.

Proposition 1.7
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a metric space with a
d
-measure
m
such that
C_{\mathcal{X}}=2^{d}
, then all doubling minimizers on
\mathcal{X}
and
m
are absolutely continuous with respect to each other.

Proof.

Let
\mu\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{X})
, that is
C_{\mu}=2^{d}
. Therefore, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
r>0
and
\lambda\geq 1
, we have

\mu(B(x,\lambda r))\leq(2^{d})^{\lceil\log_{2}\lambda\rceil}\mu(B(x,r))\leq(2^{\log_{2}(\lambda)+1})^{d}\mu(B(x,r))=2^{d}\lambda^{d}\mu(B(x,r)).

This means that
\mu
is
(2^{d},d)
-homogeneous. By
[
Sjö97
, Theorem 1]
, it follows that
\mu
and
m
are absolutely continuous with respect to each other.
∎

We finish this section with a method for constructing doubling measures whose doubling constant can be arbitrarily close to any given possible value. To be more precise, we have the following:

Proposition 1.8
.

The set
\{C_{\nu}:\nu\textrm{ doubling measure in }\mathcal{X}\}
is dense in
[C_{\mathcal{X}},\infty)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be a doubling minimizer on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\{B(x_{j},r_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of balls such that

\frac{\mu(B(x_{j},2r_{j}))}{\mu(B(x_{j},r_{j}))}\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}C_{\mu}=C_{\mathcal{X}}.

Given
C>C_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

C_{\mathcal{X}}-\frac{\mu(B(x_{n},2r_{n}))}{\mu(B(x_{n},r_{n}))}<\varepsilon.

For convenience, let us denote
c=\frac{\mu(B(x_{n},2r_{n}))}{\mu(B(x_{n},r_{n}))}
. Now, take
\eta\in(0,1)
such that

\frac{c-\eta}{1-\eta}=C.

Note that
\eta\mapsto(c-\eta)/(1-\eta)
is a continuous function on
[0,1)
, and since
c\leq C_{\mathcal{X}}<C
, there must exist such
\eta
.

Now, let us define the measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
given by

\nu(F)=(1-\eta)\mu(F\cap B(x_{n},r_{n}))+\mu(F\cap B(x_{n},r_{n})^{\mathsf{c}}),

for each Borel set
F\subset\mathcal{X}
.

Clearly, we have

\frac{\nu(B(x_{n},2r_{n}))}{\nu(B(x_{n},r_{n}))}=\frac{\mu(B(x_{n},2r_{n}))-\eta\mu(B(x_{n},r_{n}))}{(1-\eta)\mu(B(x_{n},r_{n}))}=\frac{c-\eta}{1-\eta}=C,

hence
C_{\nu}\geq C
. Now let
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r>0
. We distinguish two cases:

B(x,r)\cap B(x_{n},r_{n})=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad B(x,r)\cap B(x_{n},r_{n})\neq\emptyset.

In the first case, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\nu(B(x,2r))}{\nu(B(x,r))}=\frac{\nu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}\leq\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}\leq C_{\mathcal{X}}<C.

While if
B(x,r)\cap B(x_{n},r_{n})\neq\emptyset
, then let us set
S_{1}=B(x,r)\cap B(x_{n},r_{n})
,
S_{2}=B(x,r)\cap B(x_{n},r_{n})^{\mathsf{c}}
and
S_{3}=B(x,2r)\setminus S_{1}
. Thus,
B(x,r)=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
and
B(x,2r)=S_{1}\cup S_{3}
, where both unions are disjoint. Then we have

\displaystyle\nu(B(x,2r))

\displaystyle=\nu(S_{1})+\nu(S_{3})=(1-\eta)\mu(S_{1})+\nu(S_{3})

\displaystyle=-\eta\mu(S_{1})+\mu(S_{1})+\nu(S_{3})\leq-\eta\mu(S_{1})+\mu(B(x,2r))

\displaystyle\leq-\eta\mu(S_{1})+C_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(B(x,r))=-\eta\mu(S_{1})+C_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(S_{1})+C_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(S_{2})

\displaystyle=(C_{\mathcal{X}}-\eta)\mu(S_{1})+C_{\mathcal{X}}\nu(S_{2})=\frac{C_{\mathcal{X}}-\eta}{1-\eta}\nu(S_{1})+C_{\mathcal{X}}\nu(S_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{X}}-\eta}{1-\eta}\nu(B(x,r)).

This implies that

C\leq C_{\nu}\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{X}}-\eta}{1-\eta}\leq C\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{c-1}\Big).

Since we can take
\varepsilon
arbitrarily small, this suffices to prove the statement.
∎

Remark 1.9
.

Note that in the case the metric space admits a doubling minimizer
\mu
which attains its doubling constant, that is

\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}=C_{\mu}=C_{\mathcal{X}}

for some
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
r>0
, then one can take
\varepsilon=0
in the argument above to show that
\{C_{\nu}:\nu\textrm{ doubling measure in }\mathcal{X}\}=[C_{\mathcal{X}},\infty)
. For instance, this is the case for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the distance induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Although, in general, a doubling minimizer need not attain its doubling constant, we do not know of any explicit example where the set of doubling constants of measures on a metric space
\mathcal{X}
does not coincide with the whole interval
[C_{\mathcal{X}},\infty)
.

2.
Discrete measure spaces

We turn our attention to discrete measure spaces. We use standard terminology for graphs. We assume
\mathcal{X}
is the set of vertices of a simple, connected graph with edge set
E(\mathcal{X})
. Given
x\in\mathcal{X}
, its neighbors form the set

N(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:(x,y)\in E(\mathcal{X})\},

and we call
|N(x)|
the degree of
x
. On
\mathcal{X}
we always consider the shortest path distance, so that
N(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:d(x,y)=1\}
. Measures
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
are in one-to-one correspondence with densities
f_{\mu}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with respect to the counting measure
\#_{\mathcal{X}}
. We shall pay special attention to the local behavior of measures
\mu
. To that end, define

C_{\mu}^{0}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\mu(B(x,2))}{\mu(B(x,1))},

and say that the local case is critical for
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
if
C_{\mu}=C_{\mu}^{0}
. The hypothesis of the local case being critical will allow us to obtain several positive results in the sequel. The discrete (combinatorial) Laplacian of a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\Delta f(x):=\frac{1}{|N(x)|}\sum_{y\in N(x)}(f(x)-f(y))=f(x)-\frac{1}{|N(x)|}\sum_{y\in N(x)}f(y).

We say
f
is harmonic if
\Delta f(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If
\Delta f(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
we call it superharmonic. In the graph setting, the notion of (super)harmonicity is a local one, and hence it can differ significantly from the generalization of the one used in the Introduction for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us now assume that
\mathcal{X}
is regular, that is, there exists
N_{0}
such that
|N(x)|=N_{0}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If the local case is critical for
(\mathcal{X},\#_{\mathcal{X}})
, any doubling minimizer
\mu
must satisfy
C_{\mu}^{0}\leq C_{\#_{\mathcal{X}}}^{0}
. But this means that

1+\frac{\sum_{y\in N(x)}\mu(y)}{\mu(x)}\leq\sup_{x\in G}\frac{\mu(x)+\sum_{y\in N(x)}\mu(y)}{\mu(x)}=C_{\mu}^{0}\leq C_{\#_{G}}^{0}=1+|N(x)|,

which in turn implies

\frac{\sum_{y\in N(x)}\mu(y)}{|N(x)|}\leq\mu(x)\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

The above discussion implies that, when the local case is critical, if
\mu
is a doubling minimizer, then
f_{\mu}
must be a positive, superharmonic function. We next use the well-known link between the existence of nontrivial positive superharmonic functions and transitivity of random walks to find obstructions to the existence of doubling minimizers in certain spaces. In particular, we will use the following fact: if
\mathcal{X}
is a graph where the standard random walk is a recurrent Markov chain, then the only positive superharmonic functions on
\mathcal{X}
are constants
[
Woe00
, Theorem 1.16]
. By Theorem 1 in
[
Lyo83
]
, this always happens when
\mathcal{X}
is a regular graph such that there exists
c>0
so that

|B(x,n)|\leq cn^{2}.

(2.1)

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a regular graph such that the local case is critical for
(\mathcal{X},\#_{\mathcal{X}})
. If the standard random walk on
\mathcal{X}
is recurrent, then
C_{\mathcal{X}}=\deg(\mathcal{X})+1
and
DM(\mathcal{X})=\{\alpha\#_{\mathcal{X}}\}_{\alpha>0}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{X}
is regular, then

\frac{|B(y,2)|}{|B(y,1)|}=\deg(\mathcal{X})+1

for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, and therefore the local case being critical implies
C_{\#_{\mathcal{X}}}=\deg(\mathcal{X})+1
. On the other hand, given
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
with
C_{\mu}=C_{\mathcal{X}}
, we must have

\frac{\mu(B(y,2))}{\mu(B(y,1))}\leq C_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\deg(\mathcal{X})+1

for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, and so
f_{\mu}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
must be a superharmonic function. But the random walk on
\mathcal{X}
is recurrent, so all positive superharmonic functions must be constant.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In
[
DCST23
]
, it is shown that
C_{\mathcal{X}}\geq r(A_{\mathcal{X}})+1
, where
r(A_{\mathcal{X}})
is the spectral radius of the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{X}
, and the equality holds for certain finite graphs. In fact,
[
DCST23
, Proposition 18]
provides a criterion in terms of the Perron eigenvector of the adjacency matrix of a finite graph
\mathcal{X}
that characterizes when
C_{\mathcal{X}}=r(A_{\mathcal{X}})+1
. Lemma
2.1
can be seen as a reinforcement of the conclusions there in terms of the properties of the random walk in
\mathcal{X}
.

Although without retrieving uniqueness, Theorem
B
allows us to conclude the counting measure is a doubling minimizer regardless of whether the local case is critical or not. We now provide its proof.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Suppose for a contradiction there is a measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
such that
C_{\mu}<C_{\#_{\mathcal{X}}}
. Take a sequence of radii
n_{k}
such that

\frac{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n_{k}))}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,n_{k}))}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}C_{\#_{\mathcal{X}}}.

Fix some
k
sufficiently large so that
C_{\mu}<\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n_{k}))/\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,n_{k}))
and set
n=n_{k}
. Then, for some
\gamma<1
,

\frac{\mu(B(x,2n))}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n))}<\gamma\frac{\mu(B(x,n))}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,n))}

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Fix a point
0\in\mathcal{X}
. The rest of the argument relies on the following observation: fixed
r>0
, for every
N\geq r
and
x\in B(0,N-r)
we have

\{y\in B(0,N):x\in B(y,r)\}=B(x,r).

Furthermore, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have

\{y\in B(0,N):x\in B(y,r)\}\subset B(x,r).

Therefore, for
N\geq 2n
we get

\displaystyle\mu(B(0,N-2n))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in B(0,N-2n)}\frac{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n))}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n))}\mu(\{x\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in B(0,N-2n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in B(0,N):\\
x\in B(y,2n)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(\{x\})}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in B(0,N)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B(0,N-2n)\cap B(y,2n)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(\{x\})}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n))}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in B(0,N)}\sum_{x\in B(y,2n)}\frac{\mu(\{x\})}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(x,2n))}=\sum_{y\in B(0,N)}\frac{\mu(B(y,2n))}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(y,2n))}

\displaystyle<\gamma\sum_{y\in B(0,N)}\frac{\mu(B(y,n))}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(y,n))}=\frac{\gamma}{\#_{\mathcal{X}}(B(0,n))}\sum_{y\in B(0,N)}\sum_{x\in B(y,n)}\mu(\{x\})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\mu(B(0,N+n)).

In particular, taking
N=2M-n
above, we get
\mu(B(0,2M))\geq\gamma^{-1}\mu(B(0,2M-3n))
if
2M\geq 3n
. Iterating this yields:

\mu(B(0,2M))\geq\frac{1}{\gamma}\mu(B(0,2M-3n))\geq\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)^{k}\mu(B(0,2M-3nk))

if
k\leq\frac{2M}{3n}
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is infinite, we may take
M
arbitrarily big to achieve this. Taking
k=\lfloor M/3n\rfloor
, choosing
M
large enough yields a contradiction with
\mu
being doubling as

\mu(B(0,2M))\geq\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)^{\lfloor M/3n\rfloor}\mu(B(0,M)).

∎

2.1.
The case of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

For the remainder of this subsection, we denote by
B_{d}(x,k)
the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-ball with center
x
and radius
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We focus on the counting measure, which only requires computing the size
B_{d}(0,n)
, by translation invariance. It is easy to see that
|B_{1}(0,k)|=2k-1
for each
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This allows one to compute the size of the balls in dimension 2.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
|B_{2}(0,k)|=2k^{2}-2k+1=k^{2}+(k-1)^{2}
.

Proof.

Define

H_{0}:=B_{2}(0,k)\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2}):y_{1}=0\}=\{(0,y_{2}):|y_{2}|<k\}.

We have
|H_{0}|=|B_{1}(0,k)|
. Similarly, if

H_{1}^{+}:=B_{2}(0,k)\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2}):y_{1}=x_{1}+1\}=\{(1,y_{2}):|y_{2}|<k-1\},

then
|H_{1}^{+}|=|B_{1}(0,k-1)|
, and the same happens to

H_{1}^{-}=B_{2}(0,k)\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2}):y_{1}=-1\}.

Analogously, given
1\leq j\leq k-1
we may set

H_{j}^{\pm}:=B_{2}(0,k)\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2}):y_{1}=\pm j\}=\{(\pm j,y_{2}):|y_{2}|<k-j\},

and
|H_{j}^{+}|=|H_{j}^{-}|=|B_{1}(0,k-j)|
. It is easy to see that

B_{2}(0,k)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{k-1}(H_{j}^{+}\sqcup H_{j}^{-})\sqcup H_{0},

which yields

\displaystyle|B_{2}(0,k)|

\displaystyle=|B_{1}(0,k)|+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}|B_{1}(0,k-j)|

\displaystyle=2k-1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(2j-1)=2k^{2}-2k+1.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

The technique used to prove Lemma
2.3
gives us an inductive algorithm to compute
P_{d}(k):=|B_{d}(0,k)|
for each
d
and
k
. Indeed, if
P_{d}(k)
is known, we may set

H_{0}:=B_{d+1}(0,k)\cap\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1}):y_{1}=0\},

and
|H_{0}|=|B_{d}(0,k)|
. Similarly, for
0\leq j\leq k-1

|H^{\pm}_{j}|=|\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1}):y_{1}=\pm j\}|=|B_{d}(0,k-j)|,

and we still have the decomposition

B_{d+1}(0,k)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{k-1}(H_{j}^{+}\sqcup H_{j}^{-})\sqcup H_{0}.

Therefore,

|B_{d+1}(0,k)|=|B_{d}(0,k)|+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}|B_{d}(0,k-j)|=P_{d}(k)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}P_{d}(k-j).

Assuming
P_{d}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
d
, the sum in the formula above gives a polynomial of degree
d+1
via Faulhaber’s formula. We next list what one gets in dimensions
3
and
4
:

\displaystyle|P_{3}(k)|

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}k^{3}-2k^{2}+\frac{8}{3}k-1,

\displaystyle|P_{4}(k)|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}k^{4}-\frac{4}{3}k^{3}+\frac{10}{3}k^{2}-\frac{8}{3}k+1.

We can now try to compute

C_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}=C_{\#_{d}}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|P_{d}(2k)|}{|P_{d}(k)|}.

At least in the case
1\leq d\leq 4
the supremum above is attained. Indeed, for each
d
the function
P_{d}(2r)/P_{d}(r)
is eventually decreasing, so one only has to check a finite number of values to calculate its maximum. Figure
2.1
depicts the functions
P_{d}(2r)/P_{d}(r)
for
1\leq d\leq 4
and their maxima.

Figure 2.1.
d=1
: green;
d=2
: blue;
d=3
: red;
d=4
: gray.

It can be checked that
C_{\mathbb{Z}}=3
,
C_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}=5
,
C_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}=231/25=9.24
and
C_{\mathbb{Z}^{4}}=11969/681\approx 17.58
. In any case,
C_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
has to grow exponentially: since
P_{d}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
d
, we must have

C_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{P_{d}(2k)}{P_{d}(k)}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{(2k)^{d}}{k^{d}}=2^{d}.

One important consequence of the above computations about the size of the balls in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is that both
C_{\#_{1}}
and
C_{\#_{2}}
are attained at small balls. This allows us to finish the discussion characterizing all doubling minimizers in those two cases.

Corollary 2.5
.

\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{Z})=\{\alpha\cdot\#_{\mathbb{Z}}:\;\alpha>0\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=\{\alpha\cdot\#_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}:\;\alpha>0\}.

Proof.

By the computations above, the local case is critical for both
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, while the random walk is recurrent in
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, so there are no nonconstant positive superharmonic functions. Therefore, in both cases the only minimizer is the counting measure, or a constant multiple of it.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The case of
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{Z}
had already been studied in
[
DCST25
]
. The following elementary argument shows that the counting measure is the only doubling minimizer: if
\mu
is a measure on
\mathbb{Z}
with
C_{\mu}\leq 3
, suppose there is
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mu(\{j-1\})-\mu(\{j\})>0
. Since
B(j,1)=\{j\}
and
B(j,2)=\{j-1,j,j+1\}
, we have

3\mu(\{j\})\geq\mu(\{j-1\})+\mu(\{j\})+\mu(\{j+1\})=2\mu(\{j\})+\varepsilon+\mu(\{j+1\}),

because
C_{\mu}\leq 3
. Therefore,
\mu(\{j+1\})\leq\mu(\{j\})-\varepsilon
. But looking now at the balls
B(j+1,1)
and
B(j+1,2)
, it follows that
\mu(\{j+2\})\leq\mu(\{j+1\})-\varepsilon\leq\mu(\{j\})-2\varepsilon
. Iterating this process we get

\mu(\{j+n\})\leq\mu(\{j\})-n\varepsilon.

Therefore, for
n>\mu(\{j\})\varepsilon^{-1}
we find
\mu({j+n})<0
, and so
\mu
is not non-negative. This argument is simpler than the one offered above or that in
[
DCST25
]
, but it does not carry over to
\mathbb{N}
, where other minimizing examples can be found.

We end this subsection with a natural conjecture:

Open Question 2.7
.

As explained above, in dimensions
1
and
2
the local case is critical, but in higher dimensions that is not always the case. Additionally, if
d\geq 3
, then the natural random walk is transitive, and so there are nonconstant positive superharmonic functions in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. That could be interpreted as evidence that there should be more minimizers other than the counting measure. One such function for
d=3
seems to be
f(x)=\|x\|_{2}^{-1/2}
. If we set
\mu=fd\#_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}
, it is difficult to estimate
C_{\mu}
, but some preliminary computational work shows that

\frac{\mu(B_{3}(x,2n))}{\mu(B_{3}(x,n))}<9.24=C_{\#_{3}},

for
x
and
n
not too large. The maximum seems to be attained at
n=3
and approaches
9.24
as
\|x\|
grows, which makes us think that
\mu
could be a doubling minimizer. In analogy with our results in the continuous setting, we conjecture that that there are plenty of independent doubling minimizers in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 3
. Whether this is the case is, to the best of our knowledge, open at the moment.

.

3.
Results for
\mathbb{R}^{d}

We now address the question of what are the doubling minimizers in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A simple geometric argument in
[
ST19
, Proposition 5.1]
shows that
C_{(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})}=2^{d}
for every
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and it follows that Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{d}\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Our problem is then reduced to studying whether there are any other measures minimizing the doubling constant. We are able to answer this question in all dimensions for the Euclidean norm, whereas only some special cases and partial results are obtained for other
\ell^{p}
norms.

Analogous uniqueness results to those for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
when
d\leq 2
hold in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
when
d\leq 2
, the Lebesgue measure playing the role of counting measure. In the two-dimensional case, we shall reduce the problem to the study of densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure by means of Proposition
1.7
. However, we are able to show uniqueness directly through a discretization argument in the one-dimensional case.

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{R})=\{\alpha\lambda\}_{\alpha>0}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be a doubling measure on
(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
such that
C_{\mu}=2
. We claim that

\mu(B(x,3r))\leq 3\mu(B(x,r))\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R},r>0.

(3.1)

Indeed,

\displaystyle 4\mu(B(x,r))

\displaystyle\geq 2\mu(B(x,2r))=2(\mu(B(x+r,r))+\mu(B(x-r,r)))

\displaystyle\geq\mu(B(x+r,2r))+\mu(B(x-r,2r))

\displaystyle=\mu(B(x,r))+\mu(B(x,3r)).

Next, for
k,j\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
I_{j}^{k}=[2^{-k}j,2^{-k}(j+1))
denote the standard dyadic intervals. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
we consider the natural discretization
\mu_{k}
of
\mu
in
\mathbb{Z}
given by

\mu_{k}(\{j\})=\mu(I_{j}^{k}).

(3.2)

Let us see that
C_{\mu_{k}}=3
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, take
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
r>0
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
n-1<r\leq n
. By (
3.1
) we have

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(B(j,2r))

\displaystyle\leq\mu((2^{-k}(j-2n+1),2^{-k}(j+2n))=\mu\left(B\left(2^{-k}\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right),\frac{(4n-1)2^{-k}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3\mu\left(B\left(2^{-k}\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right),\frac{2^{-k}(4n-1)}{6}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=3\mu\left(2^{-k}\left(j-\frac{2n}{3}+\frac{2}{3}\right),2^{-k}\left(j+\frac{2n}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3\mu(2^{-k}(j-n+1),2^{-k}(j+n))\leq 3\mu_{k}(B(j,r)).

By Corollary
2.5
,
\mu_{k}
is a multiple of
\#_{\mathbb{Z}}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
\alpha=\mu([0,1))
. Then, for
k\leq 0
it follows that

\mu(I_{j}^{k})=\mu_{k}(j)=\mu_{k}(0)=\mu(I_{0}^{k})=\mu\Bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{2^{-k}}I_{i}^{0}\Bigg)=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{-k}}\mu_{0}(i)=2^{-k}\alpha.

Similarly, if
k>0
:

\alpha=\mu\Bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{2^{k}}I_{i}^{k}\Bigg)=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{k}}\mu(I_{i}^{k})=2^{k}\mu(I_{j}^{k}).

Hence,
\mu(I_{j}^{k})=\alpha 2^{-k}=\alpha\lambda(I_{j}^{k})
for all
j
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. As the dyadic intervals generate the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, this suffices to prove the result. ∎

We also have the uniqueness of the counting measure as a minimizer in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, so it is reasonable to expect that the same argument of
3.1
yields the uniqueness of the Lebesgue measure as a minimizer in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. However, one should keep in mind that in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
there are different (equivalent) norms. The
\ell_{1}
-norm, given by
\|(x,y)\|_{1}=|x|+|y|
is the one which has graph-norm like discretizations. Hence, it will be for this norm, and for
\|(x,y)\|_{\infty}=\max\{|x|,|y|\}
by rotation, that one can infer the uniqueness of the Lebesgue measure as a minimizer from that of the counting measure in the discrete case as follows.

Proposition 3.2
.

\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{1})=\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})=\{\alpha\lambda\}_{\alpha>0}.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be a doubling measure in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{1})
such that
C_{\mu}=4
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
we define the dyadic
\|\cdot\|_{1}
-ball

Q_{(x_{0},y_{0})}^{k}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x-2^{-k}x_{0}|+|y-2^{-k}y_{0}|<2^{-k}\}.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we consider the discretization
\mu_{k}
of
\mu
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
given by

\mu_{k}(\{(x,y)\})=\mu(Q_{(x,y)}^{k}).

We will show that these discretizations are multiples of the counting measure. Recall that a doubling measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
charges no measure on hypersurfaces (see, for instance,
[
Ste93
, I.8.6]
). In particular,
\mu\left(\partial Q_{(x_{0},y_{0})}^{k}\right)=0
, and so it makes no difference to consider open balls instead of half-open balls forming a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(\{(x,y)\})+\mu_{k}(\{(x+1,y)\})

\displaystyle+\mu_{k}(\{(x-1,y)\})+\mu_{k}(\{(x,y+1)\})+\mu_{k}(\{(x,y-1)\})

\displaystyle=\mu(B((2^{-k}x,2^{-k}y),2^{-k+1}))+\mu(B((2^{-k}x,2^{-k}y),2^{-k}))

\displaystyle\leq 5\mu(B((2^{-k}x,2^{-k}y),2^{-k}))=5\mu_{k}(\{(x,y)\}).

Hence, by Proposition
1.7
, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}

\mu_{k}
is a multiple of the counting measure. Set
\alpha=\mu_{0}(\{(0,0)\})
. Observe that, for
k\leq 0
, there are
2^{-2k}
disjoint sets of the type
Q^{0}_{(x,y)}
covering
Q^{k}_{(0,0)}
(up to sets of
\mu
-measure
0
). Then, as
\mu_{0}
assigns the same measure to all points, we have

\mu_{k}(\{(0,0)\})=\alpha 2^{-2k}=\alpha\lambda\left(Q^{k}_{(0,0)}\right).

Similarly, for
k>0
, there are
2^{2k}
sets of the type
Q^{k}_{(x,y)}
covering
Q^{0}_{(0,0)}
. Considering
\mu_{k}
assigns the same measure to all points, we also have

\mu_{k}(\{(0,0)\})=\alpha 2^{-2k}=\alpha\lambda\left(Q^{k}_{(0,0)}\right).

As the dyadic
\|\cdot\|_{1}
-balls generate the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, the result follows for
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{1})
. As
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
is isometric to
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{1})
by a rotation, the same conclusion holds in that case.
∎

To study the set of minimizers in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
, a direct translation from the discrete case is no longer possible if
d>1
. However, the connection with positive superharmonic functions will prove to be fruitful again. First recall that, by Proposition
1.7
, all minimizers in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The problem is then reduced to studying which positive measurable functions
u
yield a doubling minimizer
d\mu=ud\lambda
. Given
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
d\mu=ud\lambda
,
C_{\mu}=2^{d}
if and only if

\fint_{B(x,r)}u\;d\lambda=\frac{1}{\lambda(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}u\;d\lambda\geq\frac{1}{\lambda(B(x,2r))}\int_{B(x,2r)}u\;d\lambda=\fint_{B(x,2r)}u\;d\lambda

(3.3)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
. That is to say, a measure is a doubling minimizer if and only if its density with respect to the Lebesgue measure satisfies the superharmonic mean value property (
3.3
). The necessity of this condition is particular to this case because the Lebesgue measure reaches its doubling constant in every ball. As we shall see, the only positive functions satisfying this property when
d=2
are constants, whereas there is a large family of examples for
d\geq 3
. Our argument for
d=2
is based on a comparison with the fundamental solution to the Laplacian,
\log\|x\|
, borrowed from the classical partial differential equations theory. However, some tweaks must be introduced, as we cannot assume any regularity in our functions
a priori
.

Theorem 3.3
.

\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{2})=\{\alpha\lambda\}_{\alpha>0}.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be a doubling measure in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
C_{\mu}=4
. Then there exists a measurable function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying (
3.3
) and such that
d\mu=ud\lambda
. We shall prove that
u
is constant
\lambda
-almost everywhere.

From (
3.3
) we may infer that the classical mean value property for superharmonic functions holds
\lambda
-almost everywhere, i.e.,

u(x)\geq Mu(x):=\sup_{r>0}\fint_{B(x,r)}u\;d\lambda\quad\quad\text{for }\lambda-\text{almost every }x.

(3.4)

Indeed, suppose that the set

A=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\exists r_{x}>0,\text{ such that }\fint_{B(x,r_{x})}u\;d\lambda>u(x)\right\}

satisfies
\lambda(A)>0
(note
A
is measurable because so is the function
Mu
, by the properties of the centered maximal function).
For every
x\in A
there exist
r_{x}>0
such that

u(x)<\fint_{B(x,r_{x})}u\;d\lambda.

Then, by (
3.3
) it follows that

u(x)<\fint_{B(x,r_{x})}u\;d\lambda\leq\fint_{B(x,\frac{r_{x}}{2^{n}})}u\;d\lambda

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, by Lebesgue differentiation theorem, there is
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\lambda(S)=0
such that for
x\notin S

\fint_{B(x,\frac{r_{x}}{2^{n}})}u\;d\lambda\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}u(x).

Consequently, for
x\in A\backslash S
we would have that

u(x)<\fint_{B(x,r_{x})}u\;d\lambda\leq u(x),

which is a contradiction as
\lambda(A\backslash S)>0
. Hence,
u
satisfies (
3.4
) almost everywhere. Moreover, fixed
R>0
, we may modify
u
on a set of Lebesgue measure
0
so that it satisfies

u(x)\geq\fint_{B(x,2^{n}R)}u\;d\lambda

(3.5)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For this purpose, simply set

u(x):=\fint_{B(x,R)}ud\lambda.

From now on, we will assume we are using such a version of
u
(defined in all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
instead of only
\lambda
-almost everywhere), which is still a density for
\mu
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Now, we define

\alpha=\inf_{x\in\partial B(0,1)}u(x)\geq 0.

We will denote
\tilde{u}=u-\alpha
and
f(x)=-\log(\|x\|)
, the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
f
is harmonic, so

f(x)=\fint_{B(x,r)}f\;d\lambda

(3.6)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
,
r>0
. Now, fix
\varepsilon>0
and denote
f_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon f(x)
. Of course,
f_{\varepsilon}
also satisfies the mean value property (
3.6
). As

\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}f_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\infty,

and
\tilde{u}\geq-\alpha
everywere since
u
is non-negative, there is an
M=M(\varepsilon)>0
such that
\tilde{u}(x)\geq f_{\varepsilon}(x)
when
\|x\|\geq M
. Next,

g_{\varepsilon}(x):=\tilde{u}(x)-f_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq\fint_{B(x,r)}\tilde{u}\;d\lambda-\fint_{B(x,r)}f_{\varepsilon}\;d\lambda,

(3.7)

for all
r>0
and all
x\neq 0
. Let us see
g_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
almost everywhere. Indeed, we have that

g_{\varepsilon}(x)=\tilde{u}(x)+\varepsilon\log(\|x\|)\geq-\alpha+\varepsilon\log(\|x\|)\xrightarrow{\>\|x\|\to\infty\>}=\infty,

so if
g_{\varepsilon}(x)<0
, for
R
sufficiently large it holds that

g_{\varepsilon}(x)<0<\fint_{B(x,R)}g_{\varepsilon}\;d\lambda,

which contradicts (
3.7
). Therefore,
g_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
almost everywhere. Making
\varepsilon
tend to
0
we get that

\tilde{u}(x)\geq f_{\varepsilon}(x)\xrightarrow{\>\varepsilon\to 0\>}0

almost everywhere and, hence,
u\geq\alpha
almost everywhere. We claim that
u=\alpha
almost everywhere. Indeed, assuming otherwise, there exists
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\lambda(D)>0
and such that
u(x)>\alpha
for all
x\in D
. Moreover, there must be a ball
B(0,R^{\prime})
such that
m(D\cap B(0,R^{\prime}))>0
, and we may pick
R^{\prime}>R-2
. We have that

\fint_{B(0,R^{\prime})}u\;d\lambda=\beta+\alpha

for some
\beta>0
. Let
x\in\partial B(0,1)
. As
B(0,R^{\prime})\subset B(x,R^{\prime}+2)
, it holds that

\fint_{B(x,R^{\prime}+2)}u\;d\lambda\geq\frac{\lambda(B(0,R^{\prime}))}{\lambda(B(x,R^{\prime}+2))}(\alpha+\beta)+\frac{\lambda(B(x,R^{\prime}+2)\setminus B(0,R^{\prime}))}{\lambda(B(x,R^{\prime}+2))}\alpha.

The above expression does not depend on
x
. However, by definition of
\alpha
, for all
\delta>0
there is a point
x_{\delta}\in\partial B(0,1)
such that
0<u(x_{\delta})-\alpha<\delta
. Picking
\delta
sufficiently small we have that

u(x_{\delta})<\fint_{B(x_{\delta},R^{\prime}+2)}u\;d\lambda.

But this is a contradiction with (
3.5
). Hence,
u=\alpha
almost everywhere and so
\mu=\alpha\lambda
.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
A
, we only need to show the abundance of minimizers in higher dimensions. In contrast with Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
, we will exhibit an uncountable family of doubling minimizers distinct from the Lebesgue measure in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
when
d\geq 3
. This is a direct consequence of the existence of positive, nonconstant, superharmonic functions. Indeed, fixed
d\geq 3
, the function

f_{k}(x)=\begin{cases}k^{d-2},&\textrm{if }x\in B(0,k),\\
\|x\|^{d-2},&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

is superharmonic and continuous in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
for every
k>0
. In particular, this implies that

\fint_{B(x,2r)}f_{k}d\lambda\leq\fint_{B(x,r)}f_{k}d\lambda

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
. Define
d\mu_{k}=f_{k}d\lambda
and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
r>0
.
For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
. We have

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(B(x,2r))

\displaystyle=\int_{B(x,2r)}f_{k}d\lambda=\lambda(B(x,2r))\frac{\int_{B(x,2r)}f_{k}d\lambda}{\lambda(B(x,2r))}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}\lambda(B(x,r))\frac{\int_{B(x,r)}f_{k}d\lambda}{\lambda(B(x,r))}=2^{d}\mu_{k}(B(x,r)).

Hence,
\mu_{k}\in\mathrm{DM}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
for every
k>0
. Note that the doubling constant is preserved by isometries, so
f_{k}(\cdot-y)d\lambda
is a doubling minimizer for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, any linear combination of doubling minimizers yields a doubling minimizer as well.

The questions of whether there are doubling minimizers other than the Lebesgue measure in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
for
p\neq 2
and
d\geq 3
, and whether the Lebesgue measure is the only minimizer when
d=2
and
1<p<2
or
2<p<\infty
remain open. There are reasons to believe that the latter statement is true: if there is a minimizer
\mu
in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{p})
different from the Lebesgue measure, it would have to be of the form
d\mu=fd\lambda
, where
f\not\in C^{2}
. This is a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Let
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a superharmonic function in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
with respect to
\lambda
. Then
\Delta u\leq 0
and
u
is superharmonic in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2})
with respect to
\lambda
.

Proof.

We will carry out the proof for
d=2
. The same argument works in higher dimensions. Let
u
be as in the statement. Its Taylor expansion around
(0,0)
is

\displaystyle u(x,y)

\displaystyle=u(0,0)+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(0,0)x+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(0,0)y

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(0,0)x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}(0,0)y^{2}+\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial y}(0,0)xy+o(\|(x,y)\|_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=:u(0,0)+u_{x}x+u_{y}y+\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u_{yy}y^{2}+u_{xy}xy+o(\|(x,y)\|_{2}^{2}).

Using the symmetries of the balls
B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)
,
(x,y)\in B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)
if and only if
(\sigma_{1}x,\sigma_{2}y)\in B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)
for every
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{-1,1\}
and
(y,x)\in B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)
, we get

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}u

\displaystyle=u(0,0)+u_{x}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}x+u_{y}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}y+\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}x^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}u_{yy}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}y^{2}+u_{xy}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}xy+o(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=u(0,0)+\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u_{yy}\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}y^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=u(0,0)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u(0,0)\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}x^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2}),

where all integrals are with respect to Lebesgue measure. Note that

\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}x^{2}\,d\lambda\gtrsim\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon/2)}x^{2}\,d\lambda+\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)\setminus B_{p}(0,\varepsilon/2)}x^{2}\,d\lambda\geq\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)\setminus B_{p}(0,\varepsilon/2)}x^{2}\,d\lambda\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}.

Then, by the mean value property for superharmonic functions,

\Delta u(0,0)\leq 2\cdot\frac{o(\varepsilon^{2})}{\fint_{B_{p}(0,\varepsilon)}x^{2}}\lesssim 2\cdot\frac{o(\varepsilon^{2})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0.

We have only used the mean value property and the symmetries of the balls, so the same argument shows that
\Delta u(x,y)\leq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is well known that, in this case,
u
is superharmonic in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|_{2})
with respect to
\lambda
.
∎
