A Technique for Patellar Tendon Repair Using a Suture‐Free Nonwoven Polyethylene Terephthalate Implant
Authors: McKenzie A. Mayer, Michael Massa, Keara M. Gilligan, Samantha L. Reiss, Oscar Vazquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurgical repair remains the standard of care for complete patellar tendon ruptures. However, healing is often challenging due to the tendon's poor biological environment and high tensile stress at the repair site. Bioinductive implants have been introduced to enhance tendon healing; however, they often lack early structural reinforcement. This report presents the FiberLocker System (ZuriMED Technologies AG, Zurich, Switzerland) which includes a FiberLocker Instrument and a SpeedPatch as a suture‐free augmentation method for patellar tendon repair. The FiberLocker Instrument embeds the SpeedPatch polyethylene terephthalate, a synthetic nonwoven scaffold composed of a high‐strength polymer, into the native tendon. Unlike traditional techniques that rely on sutures passed through tendon tissue, this system employs a reciprocating microblade mechanism that directly integrates polyethylene terephthalate fibers into the native tendon. This interwoven patch‐tendon construct increases the surface area for load distribution, enhances biomechanical support at the time of repair, and reinforces the repair site without the need for additional sutures.

Augmented repair of a left patellar tendon rupture is performed with the patient in the supine position. A midline incision is made over the patellar tendon, and a complex coronal split tear with anterior proximal and posterior distal tendon stumps is identified. Primary repair is completed using Krakow SutureTape sutures passed through three transosseous patellar tunnels, followed by distal fixation with suture anchors adjacent to the tibial tubercle. Interrupted Dynacord sutures are then placed to unite the tendon stumps and repair the retinacula. A polyethylene terephthalate (PET) SpeedPatch is applied over the anterior repair surface using the FiberLocker System, which interweaves loose patch fibers into the tendon to reinforce the repair construct. Complete patellar tendon tears most commonly occur during a forceful eccentric contraction of the quadriceps with the knee in flexion and typically present as proximal avulsions at the inferior pole of the patella. 1 , 2 Complete tears require surgical repair and are often supplemented with graft augmentation due to the poor healing environment and high tensile forces present across the repair site. 3 , 4 The addition of bioinductive implants are a promising adjunct to enhance tissue regeneration. 3 However, some bioinductive implants lack the immediate structural support that other implants may provide. 5 This is particularly relevant in complex tears, where insufficient early reinforcement may compromise repair integrity. Additionally, regardless of augmentation type, multiple fixation points are often required to secure the graft to the tendon. This adds complexity to the procedure, and the placement of sutures directly through the tendon and graft may affect tendon quality. 5

Therefore, we describe an operative technique for augmented patellar tendon rupture repair using a PET patch that is interwoven into the repaired tendon with a suture‐free powered device with a reciprocating microblade (FiberLocker System; ZuriMED Technologies AG, Zurich, Switzerland).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

After confirmation of a complete patellar tendon rupture on preoperative radiographs (Insall‐Salvati ratio 1.33), the patient is indicated for operative repair (Figure 1). The patient is placed in the supine position on a standard operating room table. Adductor canal block and infiltration between the popliteal artery and capsule of the knee (iPACK) blocks are performed for the operative lower extremity, followed by induction of general anesthesia with placement of a laryngeal mask airway. The operative extremity is then prepped and draped in sterile fashion. The rest of the repair is narrated and visualized in Video 1. A midline incision is made using a scalpel with a No. 10 blade. The soft tissue is dissected down to the injury, and a hematoma is irrigated and debrided. On direct visualization, the patellar tendon and both the medial and lateral retinacula are completely torn. The patellar tendon injury is a complex coronal split tear, with one stump anterior and proximal and the other posterior and distal (Figure 2).

SutureTape (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed in a Krakow fashion in both the tibial stump from proximal to distal and the patellar stump from distal to proximal, resulting in two independent rows and four free suture ends from each stump (Figure 3). 3 , 6 , 7 The inferior aspect of the patella is then debrided and decorticated. Three parallel, equidistant 2.4‐mm transosseous drill holes are created in the patella from distal to proximal. 3 , 6 , 7 The sutures are passed through the patella using a Hewson Suture Retriever (Smith & Nephew, Watford, Hertfordshire, UK). The most lateral suture is passed through the lateral patellar tunnel, whereas the most medial suture is passed through the medial tunnel. The two central sutures, consisting of one limb from the medial row and one from the lateral row, are passed together through the central patellar tunnel. All sutures are then tied over the patella and quadriceps tendon in standard fashion. 3 , 6 , 7 Afterwards, the two medial sutures from the proximal stump are secured to a 4.7 OSSIOfiber Compression Anchor (OSSIO, Caesarea, Israel) medial to the tibial tubercle, and the two lateral sutures are similarly secured to a second anchor lateral to the tibial tubercle. OSSIOfiber anchors are selected for their high stiffness, which facilitates reliable insertion into dense cortical bone. They also show strong pullout strength and gradually undergo ossification, allowing them to integrate and be replaced by native bone over time. This completes both proximal and distal fixation of both halves of the patellar tendon. 7 Several #2 Dynacord sutures (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA) are then placed as anterior‐to‐posterior interrupted stitches to unite the tendinous portion of the torn patellar tendon (Figure 4). The lateral and medical retinacula are repaired with an additional interrupted #2 Dynacord suture to reconstruct the entirety of the extensor mechanism. 3

The PET patch (SpeedPatch) is then applied over the anterior aspect of the repair. At a frequency of 42 Hz, the powered reciprocating microblade (FiberLocker Instrument) embeds groups of loose, nonwoven fibers within the patch into the tendon (Table 1). This creates a suture‐free interwoven fiber‐tendon network that overlays the primary repair. The device is applied for 90 seconds over the entire patch surface to maximize the force distribution over a broader area, thereby increasing construct strength at the time of repair (Figure 5). 5 , 8 , 9

Lastly, the surgical site is copiously irrigated with normal saline and 0.5 L of XPERIENCE Advanced Surgical Irrigation (XP; Next Science, Jacksonville, FL) citric acid wash to help prevent infection. The wound is closed deeply with 0 Vicryl (Ethicon, Cincinnati, OH, USA), followed by 2‐0 Vicryl for the subcutaneous layer, and running 2‐0 Monocryl (Ethicon, Cincinnati, OH), for the subcuticular layer. Steri‐Strips (Solventum, St. Paul, MN), Xeroform (Cardinal Health, Dublin, OH), 4x4 sterile gauze pads, an abdominal pad, and an Ace wrap are applied. The patient is placed in a knee immobilizer, awakened from anesthesia, and transferred to the recovery room in stable condition. Postoperative radiographs of the operative knee show restoration of patellar height with maintained tibiofemoral alignment (Figure 6).

Postoperative Management

The knee is immobilized in a hinged T‐scope Premier Post‐Op Knee Brace (Breg, Carlsbad, CA) locked in extension for 6 weeks. The patient is allowed to weight‐bear as tolerated while in the locked knee brace. Early self‐directed range‐of‐motion exercises are initiated on postoperative day 2, with gradual progression. Formal physical therapy begins at approximately 4 weeks postoperatively, focusing on passive extension and active‐assist flexion. Flexion is then advanced over the first 6 weeks while maintaining brace protection. At 6 weeks, the brace may be gradually unlocked for weightbearing during therapy as quadriceps control improves. Progressive strengthening is initiated with emphasis on quadriceps activation and closed‐chain exercises. Between 8 and 12 weeks, range of motion is advanced toward full flexion, and strengthening exercises are intensified. At approximately 16 weeks postoperatively, the patient may begin light jogging and advance functional activities, provided adequate strength, range of motion, and clinical stability are achieved. Brace discontinuation is permitted once appropriate quadriceps control is shown. The patient can begin incorporating full‐depth closed‐chain exercises 6 months after surgery. 10 , 11 , 12

DISCUSSION

This technique describes an open repair of an acute, complex patellar tendon rupture augmented with the ZuriMED FiberLocker System. This approach creates a suture‐free, interlocked patch‐tendon interface intended to provide time‐zero mechanical reinforcement and enhanced strength to the patellar tendon repair. 5 , 8 , 9

In the current literature, several augmentation strategies have been described involving various techniques and augment properties. 2 For example, bioinductive implants are designed to support tissue regeneration but generally show lower tensile stiffness compared with synthetic constructs. 13 In contrast, the PET patch used in this technique displays biomimetic‐like properties, but does not directly stimulate new tissue formation. Animal studies have shown that the patch supports cellular ingrowth and infiltration into the porous scaffold, promoting collagen‐like tissue integration. 9 Additionally, the nonwoven PET properties of the patch allow for superior time‐zero biomechanical properties. For example, in rotator cuff repair, the PET patch has been shown to increase cyclic stiffness, improve ultimate tensile strength, and reduce elongation at time‐zero. 8 Although these findings are currently limited, they provide a rationale for further investigation of the SpeedPatch in complex acute patellar tendon repairs, where enhanced construct strength may be beneficial.

Several advantages of this technique merit consideration. The intuitive design of the ZuriMED FiberLocker System with SpeedPatch PET allows for easy use and consistent reproducibility. Surgeons simply guide the device's reciprocating microblade around the patch, allowing individual PET fibers to interlock directly with the native tendon tissue. 5 , 8 , 9 This simplistic technique is associated with a relatively minimal learning curve compared with more complex augmentation techniques, and its suture‐free mechanism eliminates the need for additional fixation steps. For example, completing augmentation with hamstring autograft is a commonly reported technique but requires graft harvest preparation, insertion into a distal patellar pole socket, and suturing to the native tendon. 2 Internal bracing techniques similarly require additional steps, including knotless anchors, patellar bone tunnels with tibial cortical button fixation, and suture tape constructs whipstitched through the tendon. 2 The present technique allows mid‐substance reinforcement through direct fiber integration in approximately 90 seconds, without the need for additional sutures or hardware. 5 , 8 , 9 This therefore simplifies the procedure and may potentially improve operative efficiency (Table 2).

Nonetheless, this technique is not without risks and limitations. The SpeedPatch PET construct is applied as a mid‐substance overlay and does not directly reinforce the bone‐tendon fixation interface. As such, overall construct stability remains dependent on secure primary fixation using transosseous tunnels and/or suture anchors. This augmentation should therefore be viewed as adjunctive tendon‐level reinforcement rather than a substitute for primary repair. Additionally, adequate fixation of the SpeedPatch PET requires healthy, well‐prepared tendon tissue to ensure secure integration. Thus, extensively degenerative or severely frayed tendon tissue may limit adequate fiber interlocking and prevent secure SpeedPatch attachment (Table 2).

In conclusion, this technique report highlights the use of the ZuriMED FiberLocker System for patellar tendon augmentation. Further studies should assess outcomes in larger cohorts and longer follow‐up to better define the technique's safety and efficacy.
