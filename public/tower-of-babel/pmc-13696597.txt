Application of a nursing care bundle combined with a clinical nursing pathway in liver transplantation and its impact on patient self-management: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Lili Shi, Zhiying Lei, She Wang, Qiuyun Huang, Xiuli Peng, Xiongsheng Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the effects of a nursing care bundle combined with a clinical nursing pathway on self-management ability, postoperative biochemical markers, medication adherence, psychological status, and quality of life among liver transplant recipients.MethodsThis study was a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial. A total of 103 patients who underwent liver transplantation were initially screened. Three individuals were excluded due to severe postoperative complications (n = 1), incomplete medical records (n = 1), or inability to adhere to follow-up procedures (n = 1). The remaining 100 patients completed follow-up and were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the control group (n = 50) or the intervention group (n = 50) using a random number table. The control group received routine perioperative nursing care and standard follow-up after discharge. The intervention group received a structured nursing care bundle integrated with a clinical nursing pathway under the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) framework. Primary outcome included self-management ability [assessed using the Chinese version of the Exercise of Self-Care Agency Scale (ESCA)]. Secondary outcomes included postoperative biochemical indicators at 1 and 6 months after discharge [including fasting blood glucose (FBG), 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2 h-PBG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), serum creatinine (SCr), total bilirubin (TBil), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and therapeutic drug concentrations], medication adherence [assessed by the Basel Assessment of Adherence to Immunosuppressive Medications Scale (BAASIS)], psychological status [measured by the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)], and quality of life (evaluated using the GQOLI-74 questionnaire).ResultsAt both 1 and 6 months following discharge, patients in the intervention group demonstrated better biochemical outcomes, including lower FBG, 2 h-PBG, ALT, SCr, TBil, and BUN levels, as well as more stable drug concentrations compared with the control group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, ESCA scores, BAASIS scores, and GQOLI-74 scores were all higher in the intervention group than in the control group (P < 0.05). SDS and SAS scores were reduced in the intervention group relative to the control group (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe integration of a nursing care bundle with a clinical nursing pathway has a positive effect on improving self-management ability and medication adherence, optimizing postoperative biochemical indicators, alleviating negative emotions, and enhancing quality of life in liver transplant patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04135-x.Keywords: Nursing care bundle, Clinical nursing pathway, Liver transplantation, Self-management ability, Medication adherence, Quality of life

Introduction

Liver transplantation is an established, evidence-based treatment for acute liver failure and end-stage liver disease, and has gradually become a routine surgical procedure worldwide over the past several decades [1, 2]. Continuous progress in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive therapy, and perioperative management has improved transplant outcomes [3]. As post-transplant survival increases, long-term medical, psychological, and behavioral management needs have also grown. Primary care providers now encounter liver transplant recipients more frequently in the early recovery phase, and understanding the common risks and complications faced by these patients is essential for improving overall care quality [4].

After solid-organ transplantation, patients must comply with lifelong immunosuppressive medication, adjust lifestyle behaviors, and cope with various physiological and psychological pressures—demands that require strong self-management skills [5]. While telemedicine has demonstrated potential in supporting home-based self-care after liver transplantation, its clinical application remains limited, and several practical issues remain unresolved [6]. Care bundles, consisting of several coordinated evidence-based interventions, have been widely adopted in critical care, perioperative management and chronic disease nursing, and have shown significant benefits in standardizing care and improving patient outcomes [7, 8]. Although care-bundle interventions have demonstrated benefits in diverse clinical settings (e.g., perioperative management, acute kidney injury prevention, neurosurgical care) [9–11], evidence on their use in liver transplant recipients remains very limited.

Clinical nursing pathways—also referred to as critical pathways—offer a structured, guideline-driven framework for coordinating and standardizing nursing care [12]. They help reduce unwarranted variation, improve care efficiency, and enhance patient outcomes across different clinical populations [13]. Recent studies have also embedded targeted care bundles within structured clinical pathways or disease-specific management programs, such as Clostridioides difficile infection pathways incorporating a measures bundle that reduced recurrence, readmissions, and mortality [14], and postoperative nursing pathways built around a 48-hour high-risk surgical bundle that improved survival in major abdominal surgery [15]. However, evidence on the combined use of a care bundle and a clinical nursing pathway in liver transplant recipients remains very limited. Therefore, this study evaluated the effects of integrating these two nursing strategies on multiple postoperative dimensions—including self-management ability, biochemical indicators, medication adherence, emotional well-being, and quality of life—to provide practical, evidence-based guidance for optimizing nursing protocols in liver transplantation.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, and all patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) for research involving human subjects, as well as the CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (2016 edition). The design, implementation, data collection, and analysis of the study protocol all complied with the core requirements of ethical review.

Study design

This study was a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial. This report complies with the CONSORT guidelines (Supplementary file).

Study subjects

A randomized controlled trial was conducted on 100 patients with end-stage liver disease who underwent liver transplantation in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University from May 2022 to May 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for end-stage liver disease and the indications for liver transplantation specified in the relevant guidelines of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) [16]; (2) undergoing liver transplantation for the first time; (3) having clear consciousness and intact communication ability; and (4) voluntarily participating in this study and signing written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) severe comorbid complications (e.g., severe organ failure or malignancy); (2) presence of mental illness or cognitive dysfunction that prevents compliance with follow-up requirements; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) known allergy to medications used in this study; (5) participation in other clinical trials; (6) incomplete clinical data.

Sample size calculation

Using the Exercise of Self-Care Agency Scale (ESCA) scores as the primary outcome, based on pilot study results, we assumed a 6-month post-discharge ESCA total score of (102.2 ± 11.5) in the control group and (112.6 ± 16.1) in the intervention group. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05 (two-tailed), and the statistical power (1-β) was set at 0.90. The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing two independent means: , where n represents the required sample size per group, σ denotes the pooled standard deviation, taken as 14.0, δ is the mean difference between groups, taken as 10.4, and Zα/2 and Zβ are the corresponding standard normal quantiles for α and β, with Zα/2 = 1.96 and Zβ = 1.28. By substituting the pilot data into the formula and verifying the results using PASS 15.0 software, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 38 patients per group. Considering a potential 20% attrition rate during follow-up, the final planned sample size was increased to 50 patients per group, meeting statistical requirements. Ultimately, 100 eligible patients were randomized, with 50 assigned to the control group and 50 to the intervention group.

Randomization and blinding

Random allocation sequences were generated using a random number table, with patients allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the control group (A) or the intervention (B) group. Group assignments were sealed in opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes and revealed sequentially upon enrollment. Due to the nature of nursing interventions, blinding of nurses and patients was not feasible; however, outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group allocation. The study execution team handled intervention implementation, while an independent team managed outcome assessment and data analysis, with no cross-communication regarding group information. The generation of the random group allocation sequence, allocation concealment, and implementation were carried out by third-party researchers not involved in this study.

Nursing methods

The nursing period extended from three days prior to surgery through six months after transplantation, covering the entire continuum of care—from preoperative evaluation and preparation, intraoperative and perioperative management, early postoperative rehabilitation, and discharge transition to long-term follow-up.

Control group: Patients assigned to the control group received routine perioperative nursing, which emphasized consistent execution of standard nursing procedures. The main components were as follows:

Preoperative phase (admission to one day before surgery): Upon admission, patients received routine education via group sessions or verbal explanations, introducing the ward environment, medical staff, and general steps of the transplant procedure. Psychological reassurance was provided using simple comforting statements such as “There is no need to worry; the surgery is safe.”

Intraoperative phase: Nurses collected basic medical record information and completed routine laboratory and diagnostic examinations—such as blood tests, liver and renal function tests, and electrocardiograph—to confirm whether patients met surgical requirements. Nursing activities followed traditional patterns, focusing on assistance during procedures and monitoring vital signs. Preoperative preparation included standard measures such as shaving the operative area, enforcing fasting and fluid restriction, and administering prescribed medications. Aseptic checks were independently performed by the responsible nurse, who inspected sterile packages and sterilization indicators. Operating-room nurses performed skin disinfection on the day of surgery. Postoperative medication reminders were briefly provided, typically summarized as, “Take your medicine on schedule after surgery.”

Intervention group: On the basis of the standard perioperative nursing provided in the control group, the intervention group received an integrated program combining a care bundle with a clinical nursing pathway guided by the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) concept. The specific measures were as follows:

Establishment of a specialized nursing team: An interdisciplinary team was established, consisting of attending physicians, senior nurses, rehabilitation therapists, dietitians, and psychologists. Taking into account each patient’s clinical condition, surgical plan, and individual needs, the team collaboratively developed the clinical nursing pathway and corresponding care-bundle checklists. Regular team training sessions and case discussions were conducted to ensure that all procedures were implemented in a standardized and coordinated manner.

Implementation of the clinical nursing pathway:① Preoperative phase (1–3 days prior to surgery): Patients underwent a comprehensive assessment covering physical health, psychological status, disease understanding, and available family support. Health education was delivered through one-on-one explanations, instructional videos, and printed brochures, emphasizing surgical steps, key aspects of postoperative recovery, correct use of immunosuppressants, and recognition of adverse effects. Targeted psychological counseling was provided to address anxiety or fear, and recovered patients were invited to share personal experiences when appropriate to improve treatment confidence. Patients were also guided to complete preoperative examinations and bowel and skin preparation. ② Intraoperative phase: A strict “three-party safety verification” protocol was followed, during which nurses, anesthesiologists, and surgeons jointly confirmed crucial information, including the patient’s identity, surgical site, and type of procedure. The layout of the instrument table was optimized based on workflow requirements, placing commonly used instruments in clearly categorized sections for quick retrieval. A “double four-count system” was applied to all sterile items and instruments, conducted twice before, during, and after surgery, to prevent errors or omissions. To reduce hypothermia, warming blankets were used to maintain body temperature at ≥ 36 °C throughout the procedure. Vital signs such as heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and hemodynamic parameters were continuously monitored, with any abnormal findings promptly communicated and addressed in collaboration with the surgical team. ③ Postoperative phase (1–7 days post-surgery): Individualized rehabilitation plans were developed for each patient. Six hours following surgery, patients were instructed to perform in-bed turning and ankle-pump exercises; at 24 h, they were encouraged to sit up and begin gradual mobilization, progressing to standing and ambulation at the bedside to support gastrointestinal recovery and enhance circulation. Biochemical markers and blood concentrations of immunosuppressants were closely monitored, and nursing strategies were adjusted according to test results. Pain was managed using a combination of pharmacologic analgesics (e.g., acetaminophen) and non-pharmacologic measures such as cold compresses and music therapy, aiming to maintain pain scores below 3. Dietitians provided individualized nutritional plans tailored to digestive recovery, guiding patients in transitioning from liquid to semi-liquid and then to regular diets to ensure adequate nutrient intake. ④ Discharge preparation phase (1–2 days before discharge): Before discharge, patients and their family members received structured training on post-discharge self-management. Key topics included medication management (taking immunosuppressants on schedule and at the correct dosage to prevent omissions or errors), wound care (keeping the incision clean and dry and monitoring for redness, swelling, or discharge), and symptom surveillance (seeking medical attention promptly in the event of fever, jaundice, abdominal pain, or other warning signs). A follow-up file was created for each patient, specifying follow-up timelines, methods, and required assessments.

Care bundle measures:① Medication-management bundle: An individualized medication schedule was created for each patient, specifying the exact timing, dosage, administration method, and key precautions for immunosuppressants and other adjunctive medications. Wound-care procedures included daily assessment of incision healing and timely replacement of dressings. Patients received guidance on maintaining personal hygiene—such as frequent handwashing, regular clothing changes, and avoiding known sources of infection. Vital indicators including body temperature and complete blood counts were monitored regularly to detect potential infection at an early stage. ③ Psychological-support bundle: A weekly psychological assessment was conducted post-surgery. Screening for negative emotional states was performed using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS). Patients exhibiting symptoms of anxiety or depression received one-on-one counseling from psychologists to help alleviate mental stress. Outcome measures

Primary outcome: Self-management ability: Patients’ self-management competencies at discharge and 6 months post-discharge were assessed using the Chinese version of the ESCA [17] [Since the differentiated intervention in this study mainly occurred during the follow-up phase after discharge rather than during hospitalization, the ESCA score at discharge was regarded as the “baseline level” for home self-management of both groups after discharge, and the ESCA score before the intervention (at admission) was not measured]. The scale comprises 43 items across four subdomains—self-care skills (12 items), sense of responsibility for self-care (6 items), self-concept (8 items), and health knowledge (17 items). Scores range from 0 to 172, with higher values reflecting stronger self-management capacity.

Secondary outcomes:

Postoperative biochemical indicators: At 1 and 6 months following discharge, fasting venous blood samples were obtained. Following an 8–12-hour fast, 5 mL of blood was drawn from the cubital vein, allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10–15 min. The separated serum was stored at − 80 °C for no longer than 6 months and sent to our hospital’s laboratory for testing. Biochemical testing included: fasting blood glucose (FBG, mmol/L) and 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2 h-PBG, mmol/L) were quantified using a glucose oxidase–based assay. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), total bilirubin (TBil, µmol/L), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mmol/L) were assessed through enzymatic colorimetry, whereas serum creatinine (SCr, µmol/L) was measured with an enzymatic assay. All biochemical parameters were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer.

Adverse events during follow-up were recorded for both groups. A standardized adverse event monitoring system was established. During the patient follow-up (1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after discharge), trained researchers asked patients using a unified adverse event assessment questionnaire through a combination of outpatient follow-up, telephone follow-up, and home visits. During the follow-up process, patients and their families were actively informed about the definition and reporting methods of adverse events and encouraged to promptly report any physical discomfort symptoms.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. The distribution of continuous variables was examined with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all measured indicators met the criteria for normal distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as percentages, with between-group differences assessed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between the two groups were conducted with independent-samples t-tests. For within-group comparisons, paired t-tests were applied. Continuous variables collected at different time points were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), preceded by Mauchly’s test of sphericity. If the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Study flow of participants

A total of 103 liver transplant recipients were initially screened for eligibility. According to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 3 individuals were excluded—one due to serious postoperative complications, one owing to incomplete clinical information, and one because follow-up requirements could not be met. The remaining 100 eligible patients were subsequently randomized, with 50 allocated to the control group and 50 to the intervention group. All participants received their assigned nursing interventions as planned. Throughout the study period, no participant withdrew or was lost to follow-up; thus, all 100 patients completed follow-up assessments and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Postoperative biochemical indicators

At both 1 month and 6 months following discharge, the intervention group demonstrated lower levels of FBG, 2 h-PBG, ALT, SCr, TBil, and BUN compared to the control group. During the follow-up period, there was one case of pulmonary infection, two cases of poor wound healing after transplantation, and two cases of postoperative fever with unknown cause in the control group. In the intervention group, there was one case of postoperative fever with unknown cause and two cases of postoperative wound infection.

Discussion

The nursing care bundle combined with clinical nursing pathway intervention adopted in this study was developed based on the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery framework. The care bundle comprised four core modules (medication management, infection prevention, psychological support, and follow-up management), while the clinical nursing pathway standardized the perioperative period into four phases (preoperative, intraoperative, postoperative, and discharge preparation). This combined intervention was chosen because conventional nursing cannot fully address the multidimensional needs of liver transplant patients, including long-term immunosuppressant use, complication management, psychological stress, and lifestyle changes. Although both strategies have shown benefits individually, evidence for their combined application in liver transplantation remains limited. Multiple evidence-based studies support this intervention: bundle interventions have been shown to reduce infection risk and improve medication adherence in organ transplantation [7, 22], and structured clinical nursing pathways optimize perioperative management and reduce complications [23]. Furthermore, the integration of the two strategies creates a synergistic effect, with the pathway providing the framework and the care bundle focusing on key links, both centered on “standardization + individualization” [22]. Findings from the present study indicate that the integration of a nursing care bundle with a clinical nursing pathway demonstrates positive impacts on patient prognosis across multiple dimensions.

Abnormal biochemical indicators are frequently encountered during the postoperative course of liver transplantation and may reflect biliary complications, vascular issues, immune-mediated allograft injury, or metabolic dysregulation [24]. Effective perioperative management is therefore essential for preventing early graft dysfunction and ensuring long-term graft survival [25]. A review by Campbell et al. highlighted that modern post-liver transplantation management emphasizes a multidisciplinary collaborative model centered on ERAS principles, including early extubation, restrictive fluid strategies, and goal-directed hemodynamic management, which contributes to improved early graft function [26]. In this study, patients receiving the combined intervention exhibited better biochemical profiles at 1 and 6 months post-discharge. This suggests that structured medication management, infection prevention, and rehabilitation guidance—key components of the care bundle and clinical nursing pathway—may contribute to improved hepatic metabolic recovery, reduced inflammatory burden, and enhanced graft stability, although causal relationships cannot be definitively established.

Psychological vulnerability is common after transplantation; patients often struggle with diminished self-efficacy, fear of complications, uncertainty about the future, and emotional fatigue [27]. Such psychological distress can negatively affect autonomic function, hemodynamics, and overall health trajectories [28]. Strong self-management abilities are therefore crucial for transplant recipients, enabling them to comply with complex monitoring schedules and long-term immunosuppressive regimens [29]. The improvement in ESCA scores observed in the intervention group suggests that the combined intervention may have promoted increased patient engagement and autonomy while enhancing knowledge acquisition and health responsibility. The structured psychological-support bundle was further associated with reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms, consistent with literature emphasizing the importance of early mental health assessment and psychosocial intervention in transplant populations [30, 31]. Although intraoperative interventions within the nursing bundle did not directly improve patients’ self-management ability immediately, they played a supportive role by reducing postoperative complications (e.g., through strict infection prevention measures), thereby minimizing disruptions to self-management caused by adverse events. The significant improvement in self-management ability was primarily observed at the 6-month follow-up, and the intraoperative components served as an essential foundation for achieving this long-term goal.

Medication adherence is a key determinant of graft survival, as widely recognized in transplantation medicine. Nonadherence to immunosuppressive therapy increases the risks of rejection, graft loss, and mortality [32]. While pharmacological modifications such as extended-release tacrolimus can facilitate adherence [33], behavioral and educational interventions remain indispensable. In this study, the intervention group achieved higher BAASIS scores at both follow-up time points, suggesting that personalized medication schedules, electronic reminders, and continuous follow-up management may help sustain long-term adherence. This finding aligns with studies emphasizing the multifactorial nature of adherence and the need for integrated behavioral support [34].

Quality of life is increasingly recognized as a core outcome in the long-term management of liver transplant recipients [35]. The improvements across all GQOLI-74 dimensions in the intervention group underscore the comprehensive benefits of the combined nursing approach—addressing not only physiological recovery but also psychological resilience, social reintegration, and material well-being. Nevertheless, existing evidence indicates that demographic factors such as age at transplantation may influence emotional and quality-of-life outcomes [36], highlighting the importance of individualized care within structured pathways.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, outcomes such as self-management ability, emotional status, and quality of life were assessed using self-reported instruments, which may be susceptible to recall bias and subjective emotional influences. Although validated scales were employed, the inherent subjectivity of self-reported measures cannot be entirely eliminated. Objective behavioral indicators—such as electronic monitoring of medication adherence or wearable-based activity tracking—were not incorporated, which may limit the comprehensiveness of outcome assessment. Second, the single-center design and moderate sample size may restrict the external generalizability of the findings. Variations in institutional protocols, patient characteristics, and healthcare resource allocation across different centers could influence the applicability of this combined nursing model. Furthermore, long-term outcomes beyond six months were not evaluated, leaving the sustainability of the observed benefits uncertain. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods are therefore warranted to further validate and refine this integrated nursing approach. Additionally, this study was not prospectively registered in a publicly accessible clinical trial registry prior to participant enrollment. Although ethical approval was obtained and written informed consent was secured from all participants, the absence of prospective registration may limit transparency regarding predefined endpoints and analytic strategies, and may increase the potential risk of reporting bias. Prospective trial registration is an important component of research transparency and methodological rigor, and future studies should ensure registration in recognized clinical trial registries to enhance credibility and reproducibility. Moreover, the absence of pre-intervention (at admission) baseline measurements limits the ability to more precisely distinguish between-group differences and assess true intervention-related changes over time. This methodological constraint may impose certain limitations on the interpretation of results and the robustness of statistical inference. In future research, study designs should incorporate more rigorous baseline assessments and standardized pre-intervention measurements to enhance internal validity and strengthen causal interpretation.

In conclusion, the integration of a nursing care bundle with a structured clinical nursing pathway appears to be associated with improvements in self-management ability, medication adherence, postoperative biochemical indicators, emotional well-being, and overall quality of life among liver transplant recipients. This combined nursing model offers a structured and systematic postoperative management framework for clinical practice and provides practical insights into standardizing perioperative care and post-discharge follow-up processes. Nevertheless, given the methodological limitations noted above, further multicenter investigations with broader populations, longer follow-up durations, and incorporation of objective behavioral and physiological monitoring are necessary to confirm the long-term efficacy and generalizability of this model. Strengthening baseline control strategies and integrating multidimensional outcome assessments may facilitate a deeper understanding of the intervention mechanisms and support the continued optimization of evidence-based nursing protocols in liver transplantation.
