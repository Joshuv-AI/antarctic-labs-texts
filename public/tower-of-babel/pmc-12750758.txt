Dissemination of study results to participants in mental health research: a meta-research review of studies published in high-impact psychiatry journals
Authors: Georgia Pierson, Elsa-Lynn Nassar, Claire Adams, Jill Boruff, Julia Nordlund, Sophie Hu, Danielle B Rice, Mariana Thombs-Vite, Natalie Co, Vanessa Cook, Brett D Thombs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe surveyed authors of publications in high-impact psychiatry journals to assess the (1) proportion that disseminated results to study participants or others with lived experience, and, among those who disseminated, (2) methods (eg, email) and (3) tools (eg, plain-language summary) used.DesignMeta-research review.Data sourcePubMed search on 14 December 2022 and emails to study authors for information on dissemination.Eligibility criteriaEligible studies collected primary human data and were published in psychiatry journals with 2021 impact factor ≥10.Data extraction and synthesisStudy information was extracted by one investigator and validated by a second investigator, with conflicts resolved by consensus, with a third investigator consulted as necessary. We emailed authors approximately 2 years post-publication to ensure sufficient time had passed to share results. We estimated the proportion of authors that may have disseminated results to participants or others with lived experience, assuming that non-respondents (1) did not disseminate, (2) were half as likely to disseminate as respondents or (3) disseminated in the same proportion as respondents.ResultsOf 141 studies, 94 (67%) authors responded. Among respondents, 21 (22%) reported disseminating to study participants, and an additional 9 (10%) reported disseminating lay materials to people with lived experience (total of 30 studies, 32%). Overall, we estimated that 15% (95% CI 10% to 22%) to 23% (95% CI 17% to 30%) of authors may have disseminated results directly to study participants and 21% (95% CI 15% to 29%) to 32% (95% CI 25% to 40%) to participants or others with lived experience. Among the 30 that reported disseminating, the most common methods were sending mail or emails to study participants (17 studies, 57%) and posting on social media (15 studies, 50%). The most common tools were plain-language summaries (22 studies, 73%) and webinars or other meetings (15 studies, 50%).ConclusionsDissemination of results to participants in mental health research is uncommon. Funding agencies, ethics committees, journals and academic institutions should support dissemination.Keywords: PSYCHIATRY, Methods, ETHICS (see Medical Ethics)

Background

The Declaration of Helsinki stipulates that ethical research requires meaningful engagement with potential participants and their communities and that aggregate research results should be shared with study participants.1 National research ethics policies similarly mandate that study results be shared with participants,2 3 and research funding agencies additionally encourage sharing of research findings with others with relevant lived experience.4,7

Many people volunteer to participate in research, despite associated risks and burdens, due to a desire to help others and make positive use of their lived experience.1,38 Most research participants report wanting to learn about study results.8,10 Effectively communicating research results to study participants could help them understand how their participation has contributed to science and may benefit others; provide them with a sense of ownership of study results; encourage them to participate in future research or recommend participation to others; and increase trust in researchers, the research process and study findings.8,11

Most health researchers support sharing research results with study participants,11 12 but this may not be done consistently in practice.11,14 A 2016 survey of 1818 authors of clinical trials indexed in PubMed in 2014–2015 found that 27% of authors who responded reported having shared results with participants. However, only 16% of invited researchers responded, and study authors estimated that the actual proportion that shared results may have been between 5% and 21%.13 A 2017 survey of 414 health researchers from medical universities in the USA found that 65% believed study results should always be shared, but only 43% reported consistently doing so. Study authors were not asked, however, about the dissemination of results from a specific study, and the number of study authors invited to complete the survey was not reported.12

Objective

No studies have evaluated whether mental health researchers disseminate study results to participants. We surveyed authors of primary human research published in high-impact psychiatry journals to evaluate the (1) proportion who reported that they disseminated results to participants or others with relevant lived experience; and, if dissemination occurred, (2) dissemination methods (eg, email, mail, social media) and (3) tools (eg, academic summary, plain-language summary, website) used.

Methods

We conducted a meta-research review. This was a secondary analysis of primary human research studies published in 2022 that were previously included in a meta-research review on patient and public engagement.15 We resurveyed authors about whether they had disseminated results to study participants approximately 2 years post-publication to ensure that sufficient time had passed to share results. We posted a study protocol prior to study initiation (https://osf.io/akrcz/files/osfstorage). There are no reporting guidelines for meta-research studies,16 so we reported our study based on the relevant Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses17 items. Since some of our methods were based on our previous study,15 we followed reporting guidance from the Text Recycling Research Project.18

Identification of eligible studies

Eligible studies were primary human research studies published in journals included in the category Psychiatry in the Clarivate Journal Citation Reports that had a 2021 impact factor ≥10. This category was selected because it relates directly to mental health, whereas other categories, such as Psychology and Medicine, General & Internal, include many studies that are not about mental health research. See online supplemental table 1 for eligible journals included in searches.

Primary human research studies were defined as studies in which one or more of the study authors were involved in data collection. Studies that used clinical records without requiring patient consent, data from clinical or quality control registries, healthcare administration databases, national census databases, shared databases collected by other researchers or biobanks were excluded. Brief or concise reports were excluded because the initial phase of our study evaluated reporting of patient engagement, and these article types have limited word counts.

Eligibility criteria were designed to (1) include articles published in high-impact journals, which are more likely to be funded and authored by researchers who have resources to engage people with lived experience and disseminate results; (2) exclude studies that did not involve primary data collection and would not be required to disseminate results to participants; and (3) include studies with initial versions published online at least 2 years ago, which ensured sufficient time had passed for authors to share results with participants.

Selection of eligible studies

We used MEDLINE (PubMed interface) to efficiently search for and download information from eligible studies from eligible journals on 14 December 2022, from database inception to present, using a search designed by an experienced health sciences librarian. See online supplemental methods 1 for the search strategy. Citations were reviewed in reverse chronological order by online publication date until the desired sample size (N=141) was reached. The PubMed search was conducted via DistillerSR systematic review software (Evidence Partners, Ottawa, Canada), and citations were uploaded to the platform. Two investigators independently assessed study eligibility at the title and abstract level. If either considered a study potentially eligible, it proceeded to full-text review, where two investigators independently assessed it for eligibility. Conflicts were resolved by consensus, with a third investigator consulted as necessary. See online supplemental methods 2 for title and abstract and full text screening forms.

Sample size

We based our sample size calculations on results from a survey of investigators from clinical trials published in 2014 or 2015 in which 27% reported that they shared trial results with participants. Due to the low survey response rate, the authors estimated that 5%–21% of trials may have shared results.13 We calculated 95% CIs around assumed dissemination percentages using the Agresti and Coull method.19 We calculated that including 141 studies would result in a 95% CI width of <8% for 5% dissemination and a 95% CI width of 15%, our target maximum width, for 30% dissemination (online supplemental table 2).

Data extraction

For each included primary study, we extracted: (1) article information, including first author’s surname; publication month and year, with ‘online ahead of print’ designations subsequently updated to final publication month and year; journal name; funding source(s); country of corresponding author; country or countries where study participants were recruited; (2) study design; (3) study aim; (4) recruitment setting; study population; number of participants; (5) study topic (eg, interventions; outcomes or prognosis); (6) whether engagement of people with lived experience was reported in the article; if yes, (7) all of the stages of the research process in which people with lived experience were engaged (research planning; conduct; interpretation; dissemination); (8) the level in which people with lived experience were engaged (consult; involve; partner). We defined stages and engagement levels based on prominent international and national frameworks on engagement.15 Data were extracted by one investigator in DistillerSR and validated by a second investigator using the DistillerSR Quality Control function. Conflicts were resolved by consensus, with a third investigator consulted as necessary. See Adams et al15 for the data extraction form.

Author surveys

We emailed corresponding authors of included studies and invited them to complete an online survey via Qualtrics to provide information about whether they disseminated results to study participants and the methods (eg, email, mail, social media) and tools (eg, academic summary or abstract, plain-language summary, website) they used. Dissemination was defined as (1) directly sharing study results in any format with participants, (2) publicly sharing results using tools designed for people with relevant lived experience but not directly disseminating to study participants (eg, posting on social media, presenting at a community forum) or (3) both. Publishing articles in journals or sharing academic abstracts or full reports publicly (eg, on social media) without directly sharing with study participants or providing lay materials was not considered dissemination to study participants or others with relevant lived experience. See online supplemental methods 3 for our email template and survey items. Corresponding authors were emailed once per week, from 26 February to 16 March 2025, until a response was received. If a corresponding author’s email was invalid, we searched for an alternate email address and emailed co-authors if one was not found. If no response was obtained from a corresponding author by 12 March 2025, we then additionally emailed study coauthors up to two additional times.

Analysis

We evaluated the proportion of authors who completed our survey and reported that they disseminated study results to participants. We estimated the overall proportion of studies whose authors may have disseminated results to participants or others with lived experience based on three assumptions: (1) that non-respondents did not disseminate results, (2) that the proportion of non-respondents who disseminated was half that of respondents and (3) that the proportion who disseminated was the same among survey respondents and non-respondents.

Among studies whose authors reported that they disseminated results to study participants or others with lived experience, we evaluated the proportion that disseminated with each method (eg, email, mail, social media) and tool (eg, academic summary or abstract, plain-language summary, website). We assumed that authors who had not yet disseminated results to study participants would not do so since >2 years had passed since the included studies were published. All proportions are presented with 95% CIs.19

We presented subgroup results for region or country, study design, population, study topic, funding and engagement of people with lived experience. We defined subgroup classifications a posteriori based on frequency data. We did not conduct statistical comparisons between subgroups as our study was not designed or powered for such analyses.

Patient and public involvement

Because of the methodological focus of our study and because we collected data about published research studies and not people with lived experience, we did not involve people with lived experience in its design, conduct, interpretation or dissemination.

Amendments to protocol

Our initial protocol included a review of policies and consent form templates from ethics committees of included studies to determine if they included statements about disseminating research results to study participants. Pilot testing of procedures revealed that this material was not available on websites for many institutions, that institutional contacts would be required and that translation of documents from many languages would be needed for documents that could be obtained. Our resources were limited; therefore, we removed this from our protocol.

Results

Our search initially identified 7995 titles and abstracts. After screening in reverse chronological order to obtain our target sample of 141 studies, we excluded 184 titles and abstracts and reviewed 207 studies at the full-text level, where we excluded 66 additional articles. This resulted in the inclusion of 141 studies (see online supplemental figure 1), first published online between October and December 2022 (final publication dates between October 2022 and October 2023). Journals with 10 or more included studies were: Psychiatry Research (n=27), Psychological Medicine (n=24), Molecular Psychiatry (n=15), Asian Journal of Psychiatry (n=13), International Journal of Social Psychiatry (n=13) and Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery & Psychiatry (n=11) (online supplemental table 3).

As shown in table 1, most study corresponding authors were affiliated with institutions in Europe and the UK (N=54 of 141, 38%), followed by North America (N=38, 27%) and China (N=24, 17%). Cross-sectional observational study designs were most common (N=58, 41%), followed by longitudinal cohorts (N=36, 26%) and interventional studies (N=34, 24%). Most study samples were non-psychiatric samples (N=43, 31%), individuals with severe mental disorders (N=30, 21%) and individuals with common mental disorders (N=27, 19%). The most common study topics were factors associated with mental disorders, mental disorder prognosis or predictors of intervention outcomes (N=48, 34%); neuroimaging, genetics and biomarker research (N=42, 30%); and the evaluation of intervention effects (N=30, 21%). Most studies reported receiving non-industry funding (N=117, 83%). Few studies previously reported having engaged people with lived experience (N=25, 18%). Proportion of studies that disseminated to study participants

As shown in table 2, among the authors of the 141 included studies, 94 (67%) responded to our survey. Of the 94 survey respondents, 30 (32%) reported dissemination to study participants or others with relevant lived experience, including 18 (19%) who reported that they disseminated lay information to study participants, 3 (3%) who disseminated academic material (eg, journal publication, academic summary) to study participants and 9 (10%) who disseminated lay material but did not disseminate any material directly to study participants (eg, patient organisation newsletter, website post without sending link to study participants). Among the 18 studies that disseminated lay material directly to study participants, 11 also disseminated more broadly to others with lived experience (eg, social media posts; not shown in table 2). Thus, a total of 20 studies (21%) disseminated lay material to people with lived experience, not including study participants.

We estimated that, overall, 21% (95% CI 15% to 29%) to 32% (95% CI 25% to 40%) of the 141 included studies may have disseminated either lay or academic versions of results to study participants or others with relevant lived experience, depending on whether we assume non-respondents did not disseminate to study participants or if we assume they disseminated in the same proportion as respondents (see table 2). If we assume that non-respondents are disseminated at half the rate of respondents, we could estimate an overall proportion of 27% (95% CI 20% to 35%) (not shown in table 2). We estimated that 15% (95% CI 10% to 22%) to 23% (95% CI 17% to 30%) of studies disseminated study results directly to study participants, using either lay or academic material, depending on assumptions about non-respondents to our survey (not shown in table 2).

Sample sizes within subgroups were small. We estimated, however, that 8%–11% of studies conducted in North America disseminated to study participants, while estimated proportions were 26%–39% for studies conducted in Europe or the UK, and 25%–54% for studies conducted in China. We estimated that 33%–42% of studies on non-psychiatric populations and 30%–52% of studies on people with common mental disorders disseminated results to participants. For the study topic, studies on the effects of mental health interventions had the highest estimated proportion of studies that may have disseminated results (estimated 33%–47%). An estimated 32%–44% of studies whose authors previously reported having engaged people with lived experience disseminated results to participants, while we estimated that 19%–23% of studies that did not engage people with lived experience disseminated.

Methods and tools used to disseminate to study participants

As shown in table 3, 30 study authors reported that they disseminated research results to study participants or others with relevant lived experience. Among the 30 studies that disseminated in any way, 17 (57%) sent results directly to participants (eg, via email or mail), 4 (13%) posted results on a website and sent a link to participants and 2 (7%) used other methods to directly disseminate to participants. Among methods for disseminating to people with lived experience that did not target study participants, 10 studies (33%) shared via patient or community organisations, 15 (50%) posted on social media, 5 (17%) posted on a website without notifying study participants and 6 (20%) used other methods. Some studies used more than one method.

Discussion

We examined 141 articles on primary human mental health research published in high-impact psychiatry journals. We found that dissemination to study participants was uncommon despite being required by the Declaration of Helsinki and other major human research ethics guidelines.1,3 Of 94 study authors who responded to our survey, 30 (32%) reported that they disseminated results to study participants (21 studies) or to others with relevant lived experience but not directly to study participants (nine studies). We estimated that 15%–23% of studies may have disseminated results directly to study participants, depending on whether we assumed that survey non-respondents did not disseminate or that they disseminated in the same proportion as respondents. We estimated that 21%–32% may have been disseminated to study participants or others with lived experience. We did not conduct statistical tests on subgroups because our study was not designed or powered for that purpose. However, fewer authors of studies conducted in North America disseminated results to participants than authors of studies conducted in Europe or the UK or China. More authors of studies on non-psychiatric populations or people with common mental disorders disseminated results to participants than those of authors of studies on other populations. Greater proportions were also present for authors of interventional studies and those who previously reported engaging people with lived experience in their study.

Of the 94 studies whose authors responded to our survey, 18 (19%) shared lay materials (eg, plain-language summary, infographic) directly with participants (eg, via email, mail) and 3 (3%) shared only journal publications or academic summaries or abstracts directly with study participants. An additional nine studies (10%) shared lay materials more broadly (eg, social media, websites) without notifying study participants. Among those who disseminated using one or more methods and tools, the most common methods reported were sending results directly to participants (eg, via email or mail; 57%) and posting on social media (50%; some studies used more than one method). The most common tools used were plain-language summaries (73%), followed by workshops, webinars or other types of meetings (50%), and full reports or journal publications (47%).

To the best of our knowledge, no other studies have assessed the proportion of authors of mental health studies who have disseminated study results to participants. Our finding that between 15% and 23% of studies published in high-impact psychiatry journals may have disseminated results directly to participants is similar to results from the only other study we found that queried authors about the dissemination of results from a specific study. A 2016 survey of 1818 authors of clinical trials indexed in PubMed from 2014 to 2015 found that only 27% reported sharing results with participants, with an estimated actual proportion of 5%–21% when accounting for non-respondents (84% non-response rate).13 However, the focus of that study on clinical trials in any area of healthcare and its low author response rate limits comparability with our findings.

Strengths and limitations

Our study is the first to assess the extent to which authors of mental health studies disseminate their results to study participants. We contacted study authors directly and carefully reviewed survey responses with procedures that were established a priori. We posted a protocol to support transparency. The proportion of authors who responded to our survey was much higher than the only other study we identified that queried authors about disseminating results from a specific study.13

Our study has several limitations. First, we only included studies published in high-impact psychiatry journals, which may limit generalisability. However, it seems unlikely that dissemination of mental health studies published in non-psychiatry journals would differ substantively. Furthermore, we do not believe that dissemination to study participants would be higher in lower-impact journals, where authors often have less funding and fewer resources to support dissemination. However, this may not be the case, and further research could include a broader sample of journals. Second, our sampling strategy, which was based on recency of publication, resulted in more studies from journals that publish issues more frequently or more recently than if we had sampled studies on a per-journal basis. It is possible that the proportion of studies whose authors disseminate results to study participants may differ between journals, which we would not have captured. Third, we did not have a large enough sample to conduct hypothesis tests by subgroups, although we were able to identify subgroups that appeared to be substantially more or less likely to disseminate. Fourth, we made assumptions about the percentage of study authors who did not respond to our emails who may have disseminated, including assuming that non-respondents (1) did not disseminate, (2) were half as likely to disseminate as respondents or (3) disseminated in the same proportion as respondents. We believe that the true percentage likely lies between no dissemination and dissemination in the same percentage, but we could not be more precise. Fifth, there are no study quality or risk of bias tools designed to evaluate characteristics associated with risk of non-dissemination, and we included studies with many different designs, which precluded comparisons based on existing tools, which are design-specific.

Clinical implications

Researchers appear to recognise the value of sharing study results with participants, but have described ethical and practical concerns they perceive to be barriers.12,14 Perceived barriers include concerns that participants may misunderstand results and make harmful decisions about their health or treatments, that negative results may cause emotional distress or reinforce stigma, and that substantial time and resources would be required for dissemination.12,14

These concerns are addressable. Ethics guidelines1,3 require that, prior to participating in a study, potential participants receive a plain-language explanation of study aims, methods, anticipated benefits and potential risks and burdens, and other study elements so that they can provide informed consent. If study participants can provide informed consent to participate, they can also understand study results and their implications if these are similarly provided in an accessible language that clearly describes what results mean for study participants and the limitations of conclusions.

Sharing research results via tools designed specifically for study participants presents an opportunity to carefully describe what a study has found and what it means for participants in a way that avoids or even counters potential stigma. Most adults in high-income countries seek information on their health or medical treatments via the internet,20,22 and the internet is more likely to be the primary source of health information for many people than a qualified health professional.23 Not providing results in a manner that supports understanding for study participants may increase the likelihood that interested study participants seek results from less credible sources or from sources that are not designed for members of the public. Additionally, if results are not provided, participants may lose trust in researchers and research institutions and assume their contributions had no impact or were not used as intended. Consistent dissemination of study results to participants could increase trust in research and encourage future research participation by providing evidence to study participants that their contributions matter and are impacting healthcare.8,11

Research teams routinely spend thousands of hours planning, conducting and disseminating their studies. Dissemination to study participants requires a relatively small amount of time and resources if a simple strategy, such as emailing a plain-language summary to participants, is used. Budgets for this can be included in grant proposals. Research funders and other organisations provide easy-to-use guidance and templates for developing plain-language summaries24 25 and other easy-to-implement tools, such as infographics.26 27 Cochrane Collaboration guidance for plain-language summaries suggests that approximately 5 hours may be needed to draft the summary, even if the draft is developed by somebody not familiar with the research, followed by review, ideally by a research partner with relevant lived experience.24 Some researchers are also exploring the use of artificial intelligence tools that may reduce the time required even further.28

Increasing dissemination to participants in mental health research will require action from ethics committees, funding agencies, universities and other academic institutions, together with members of the patient community. Major ethics policies already mandate dissemination, but the extent to which institutional guidance for researchers supports this is unclear. Although we did not systematically assess this, we noted that few websites of ethics committees that approved the studies included in our review included policy statements, consent form templates or consent form checklists with statements supporting or requiring dissemination of results to study participants. A 2019 study requested and analysed 121 consent form templates from 65 Canadian research universities and hospital-based research institutions and found that only 51% included a statement about providing research results to participants.29 Institutional ethics committees can adopt stronger policies to highlight dissemination to study participants as an ethical obligation and require dissemination as part of ethical approval. Funding agencies can also play a role by designating funds for dissemination, providing education, tools and resources to help researchers plan and implement dissemination, and monitoring implementation.

Universities and other academic institutions could encourage dissemination by evaluating studies that share results with study participants more positively when making decisions regarding promotions and tenure. A 2020 study that examined policies from 92 top international universities found that traditional criteria used for promotion and tenure, such as the number of publications, authorship position, journal impact factors and grant funding, may discourage research that maximally serves the public interest. The authors suggested that institutions should recognise and reward non-traditional metrics, such as data sharing, study pre-registration and compliance with reporting guidelines, as these practices promote research that is more transparent, verifiable and replicable.30 Disseminating results to study participants is consistent with these goals. Future research should examine interventions that can be used to support the dissemination of research results to study participants, including steps that can be taken by institutions, ethics boards and funding agencies. Research is also needed on dissemination methods and tools that may most effectively reach participants and communicate results in a way that is easy to access and understandable.31
