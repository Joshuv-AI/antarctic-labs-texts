Introduction

Schwannoma or neurilemmoma is a benign tumor originating from Schwann cells, commonly formed around peripheral nerves. Schwannomas account for approximately 29 % of total primary spinal tumors. [1] Although benign in nature, schwannomas may undergo degenerative changes such as cyst formation, hemorrhage, calcification, and hyalinization. In the diagnosis of cystic spinal lesions, schwannoma is generally not considered as most of them are solid and heterogeneous. [1] In such situations, establishing a diagnosis for cystic schwannomas can be challenging. Additionally, cases of giant cystic schwannoma within the extramedullary intradural compartment (IDEM) compartment can cause pain along with neurological symptoms due to the gradual pressure on the spinal cord [2]and are still rarely reported in the medical literature. [1] This study aims to present a comprehensive analysis of patients diagnosed with giant cystic schwannoma, providing a deeper understanding of its characteristics, clinical presentations, diagnosis, and management strategies.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male patient presented with lumbar pain occasionally radiating to the left thigh, without associated numbness. Bowel and bladder function were within normal limits, with no reported erectile dysfunction. Upon physical examination, the patient's vital signs were within normal ranges. The patient's neurological status indicated a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 4–5-6. Motor and sensory examinations at various levels of nerves revealed normal findings. Physiological reflexes were all normal, with a score of +2/+2.

Laboratory investigations yielded values within normal limits. A contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbosacral region identified a complex cystic lesion within the spinal canal. This lesion exhibited well-defined and regular margins, with septae inside it appearing isohypointense on T1-weighted images (T1WI), while T2-weighted images (T2WI)/T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences (T2FS) showed hyperintense signals. An enhancement of signals occurred in the walls and septae following contrast administration. The lesion extended from the inferior endplate of L4 to the midbody of S1, consistent with a diagnosis of cystic schwannoma (Fig. 1). This lesion exerted pressure on the cauda equina nerve roots bilaterally. Additionally, there was suspicious neurogenic bladder distension.

The patient underwent a surgical procedure, including laminectomy, decompression at the L4-L5 level, and tumor removal using appropriate instruments by a spine surgeon and senior surgical resident. The patient was placed in the prone position. Following the sterilization of the surgical area, the surgical field was draped, and the tissues beneath the skin and aponeurosis were dissected. The location of the posterior arches of the dorsal vertebrae was confirmed using fluoroscopy at the level of the L4-L5 lesion. An incision was made along the midline from L2 to S1. Screws were inserted and secured with rods at the L4 and L5 levels. Laminectomy was performed at the L4-L5 level, allowing visualization of the dura mater opening. Through careful dissection, the tumor was removed until reaching the spinal canal. Comprehensive tumor excision was carried out, followed by irrigation and hemostasis. The dura mater was then reapproximated, and the tissue layers were restored to their original position. Finally, the surgical incision was closed, and sterile dressings were applied. Histopathological testing of the excised mass confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma.

The patient was subsequently transferred to the recovery room without complications, where post-anesthetic monitoring and surveillance began. The patient experienced post-operative symptom improvement with complete resolution of symptoms, including relief of back pain, within 7–10 days after surgery. Additionally, the patient exhibited normal motor and sensory strength.

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria. [3]

Discussion

Schwannomas, also referred to as neurilemmomas, commonly present as solitary, well-circumscribed, enclosed within a capsule, and are characterized by either solid or variably solid compositions. Cases of large solitary cystic degeneration, as seen in our case, are relatively rare and often posing preoperative diagnostic dilemma. [4] Various theories have been proposed to elucidate the cystic changes observed in schwannomas. It has been suggested that degeneration of the Antoni B component of schwannoma may lead to cyst formation, subsequently evolving into larger cysts. [5] Additionally, central ischemic necrosis/hemorrhage may result from tumor growth, contributing to cyst formation within the tumor. [5]

The clinical presentation of giant cystic schwannoma in the spine can vary significantly, including dysuria, leg pain, radicular pain, paresthesia, lumbar back pain, and acute abdominal pain. When compression of the spinal cord is more severe, symptoms may include weakness in the lower extremities or myelopathy. [6] Additionally, if the schwannoma is located near critical areas such as the conus medullaris or cauda equina in the spine, patients may experience bladder and bowel dysfunction due to nerve compression. [7] However, schwannomas located elsewhere in the lumbar spine can also cause similar dysfunction if they compress relevant nerves.

Several studies have indicated that contrast-enhanced MRI aids in distinguishing cystic schwannoma from other cystic lesions such as epidermoid, arachnoid, and neurenteric cysts. [8] The differential diagnosis for this case includes an arachnoid cyst. On MRI findings, schwannomas typically exhibit iso- or hypointense signals on T1WI and hyperintense signals on T2WI, with brighter areas indicating the presence of Antoni A, while darker areas indicate Antoni B. [4,6,9] Intense hyperintensity observed on T2WI images often corresponds to cystic areas, while hypointensity may indicate the presence of hemorrhage, high cellularity, or collagen deposition. [5]

Based on the study conducted by Savardekar [5], in cases of cystic spinal schwannoma, the lesion walls tend to be thicker and more irregular compared to other cysts, including arachnoid cysts. Additionally, the presence of a thin rim enhancement observed in cysts can be a significant consideration for the diagnosis of cystic schwannoma over arachnoid cysts. Rim enhancement in arachnoid or hydatid cysts typically occurs only in the presence of complications such as rupture or infection. During MRI examination, arachnoid cysts typically exhibit hypointensity with smooth walls on T1WI. Conversely, on T2WI, they appear hyperintense with smooth walls. [5].

The typical histological staining of benign schwannoma reveals two growth patterns, namely Antoni A and Antoni B. In the Antoni A pattern, palisading nuclei and verocay bodies associated with prominent extracellular matrix and laminin secretion are the dominant characteristics, while loose organization with myxomatous and cystic changes is the main feature of Antoni B. [10]

The optimal treatment for cystic giant schwannoma typically entails complete excision of the lesion, resulting in favorable outcomes. Recurrence of conventional spinal schwannomas is reported in fewer than 5 % of surgical patients, generally occurring several years following the initial surgical resection and often linked to incomplete tumor removal. [11] Laminectomy at the level of the lesion, followed by total excision, is the procedure performed to treat these lesions in this case. Therefore, long-term monitoring becomes crucial. [11] The integration of advanced surgical techniques and intraoperative monitoring has improved the precision of tumor resection and preservation of neurological function.

Conclusion

The giant cystic lumbar schwannoma, although rare, necessitates careful diagnosis and management. The utilization of contrast-enhanced MRI aids in distinguishing it from other lesions. In cases of cystic spinal schwannoma, the lesion walls tend to be thicker and more irregular compared to other cysts, including arachnoid cysts. Histopathological examination should be utilized to identify these lesions intraoperatively. Surgical excision is the primary treatment, and complete excision should be attempted whenever feasible.
