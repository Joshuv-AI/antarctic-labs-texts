Extra-Gradient Method with Flexible Anchoring: Strong Convergence and Fast Residual Decay
Radu I. Boţ, Enis Chenchene

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a novel Extra-Gradient method with anchor term governed by general parameters. Our method is derived from an explicit discretization of a Tikhonov-regularized monotone flow in Hilbert space, which provides a theoretical foundation for analyzing its convergence properties. We establish strong convergence to specific points within the solution set, as well as convergence rates expressed in terms of the regularization parameters. Notably, our approach recovers the fast residual decay rate O ⁡ ( k − 1 ) O(k^{-1}) for standard parameter choices. Numerical experiments highlight the competitiveness of the method and demonstrate how its flexible design enhances practical performance.



Key Words.
monotone equations, Tikhonov regularization, strong convergence, convergence rates, extra-gradient method, Lyapunov analysis, explicit discretization

Introduction

Tikhonov regularization has a long history in optimization. Originally introduced in the context of inverse problems as a powerful tool to stabilize ill-conditioned linear systems, it has quickly become popular as a simple mechanism to induce desirable properties in dynamical systems, such as strong convergence to a particular solution
[
12
]
and acceleration
[
6
]
. Let
H
be a real Hilbert space and consider the following problem:

\text{Find}\ x\in H:\ M(x)=0\,,

(1.1)

where
M\colon H\to H
is a monotone and
L
-Lipschitz continuous operator. One might be tempted to approach (
1.1
) by considering the asymptotic behaviour of the following dynamical system

\dot{x}(t)+M(x(t))=0\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,,

(1.2)

and
t_{0}>0
. However, trajectories of (
1.2
) are well known to only converge weakly and
ergodically
to a zero of
M

[
3
]
. Tikhonov regularization
[
12
]
is one possible modification that allows us to overcome this issue. It consists in introducing a
regularization
function
\varepsilon\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
and considering the regularized system

\dot{x}(t)+M(x(t))+\varepsilon(t)\left(x(t)-v\right)=0\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,,

(1.3)

where
v\in H
is the so-called
anchor term
. If
\varepsilon
is constant, (
1.3
) is a special case of (
1.2
) with respect to the strongly monotone operator
M_{\varepsilon}:=M+\varepsilon(I-v)
,
I\colon H\to H
being the identity operator. This dynamical system does indeed allow us to get a converging trajectory, but the limit is a zero of
M_{\varepsilon}
, hence suggesting
\varepsilon(t)\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
. If
\varepsilon
vanishes too fast, e.g.,
\varepsilon\in L^{1}([t_{0},+\infty),\mathbb{R})
, the system (
1.3
) is qualitatively equivalent to (
1.2
), see
[
2
]
. It generates trajectories weakly converging to a zero of
M
provided that, e.g.,
M
is demipositive
[
9
]
. The interesting case is thus

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\varepsilon(t)=0\,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\varepsilon(t)dt=+\infty\,.

(1.4)

In this setting, strong convergence of
x(t)
to the projection of
v
onto
\zer M
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
has been established under a list of additional parameter assumptions. Based on previous results by Browder
[
8
]
in 1967, Reich in the seventies obtained the first result in this direction in
[
27
]
, which requires
\varepsilon
to be nonincreasing. Israel and Reich in
[
16
]
later replaced this assumption with
\frac{|\dot{\varepsilon}(t)|}{\varepsilon^{2}(t)}\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
. However, the latter does not include choices such as
\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\alpha}{t}
for
\alpha>0
and all
t\geq t_{0}
. This case was covered by Cominetti, Peypouquet, and Sorin in
[
12
]
replacing the condition with
\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}|\dot{\varepsilon}(t)|dt=+\infty
, and by Boţ and Nguyen in
[
6
]
who instead proposed
\frac{|\dot{\varepsilon}(t)|}{\varepsilon(t)}\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
.

In parallel and independently, Halpern in 1967 in
[
15
]
initiated a flourished research line on the so-called
anchored
iterative methods to find fixed points of non-expansive operators. For a nonexpansive operator
T\colon H\to H
, these are numerical algorithms of the form

x^{k+1}:=\varepsilon^{k}v+(1-\varepsilon^{k})T(x^{k})\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0,

(1.5)

for
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
satisfying, analogously to (
1.4
), at least

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\varepsilon^{k}=0\,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{k}=+\infty\,,

(1.6)

among additional assumptions. Lions in
[
21
]
in the seventies complemented (
1.6
) by imposing
\frac{|\varepsilon^{k+1}-\varepsilon^{k}|}{(\varepsilon^{k})^{2}}\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
. However, this condition does not allow for choices of the form
\varepsilon^{k}=\frac{\alpha}{k+\beta}
for all
k\geq 1
and
\alpha,\beta>0
. Wittman
[
34
]
later closed this gap with the assumption:
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}|\varepsilon^{k+1}-\varepsilon^{k}|<+\infty
. Perhaps inspired by the continuous-time approach, Reich in
[
28
]
established that strong convergence of
(x^{k})_{k}
also holds when
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
is nonincreasing. Leaving the monotonicity of the sequence, Xu in
[
35
]
recognized that, in fact, a sufficient condition for the convergence of the sequence is
\frac{|\varepsilon^{k+1}-\varepsilon^{k}|}{\varepsilon^{k}}\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
. This flagrantly evident connection between the two theories (Tikhonov regularization for continuous time dynamics and Halpern/anchoring mechanisms for discrete time schemes) has seemingly been elusive for almost
60
years until the first attempts of reconciliation were established in
[
6
,
32
]
. In fact, (
1.5
) can be derived and analyzed as a discretization of the Tikhonov flow (
1.3
), see
[
6
]
.

The growing interest in Tikhonov or anchoring mechanisms of type (
1.5
) is not only due to their desirable property of generating sequences that converge strongly to specific points in the set of solutions, but also to their inherent acceleration. As first observed by Sabach and Shtern in
[
31
]
, the condition introduced by Xu in
[
35
]
can also be used to obtain a rate of
O(k^{-1})
for the fixed-point residual
\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
for the choices of
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
of the form
\varepsilon^{k}=\frac{2}{k+2}
for all
k\geq 0
. This result was further tightened by Lieder in
[
20
]
, who improved the constant by Sabach and Shtern by a factor of
4
for
\varepsilon^{k}=\frac{1}{k+2}
for all
k\geq 0
, and established a tightness result. Later, Ryu and Park in
[
24
]
proved that this choice is indeed optimal with respect to a large class of methods in a sense that is very close in spirit to Nemirovski’s classical result
[
23
]
for convex optimization. Not surprisingly, these acceleration results can be adapted to the continuous time setting
[
6
,
32
]
.

]
(EG) and Popov’s

[

, and the Fast Extra-Gradient (FEG) method introduced by Lee and Kim in

. The main drawback of EG-type schemes is the double evaluation of the operator, on both

[

. In contrast to EAG and FEG, for APV one has

, hence requiring only one evaluation of

, but with one additional variable to store per iteration. All these methods exhibit the rate

as

), see
[
37
]
.

In this paper, we further leverage the connection between Tikhonov regularization and anchored methods to design a new Extra-Anchored-Gradient type method for a general sequence of parameters
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
. The proposed method reads as in Algorithm
1
. In line with
[
31

6
]

1.6
),

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{|\varepsilon^{k+1}-\varepsilon^{k}|}{\varepsilon^{k}}=0\,\quad\text{as}\quad k\to+\infty\,,

(1.7)

\theta\in\big(0,\frac{1}{L}\big)
, this method generates a sequence
(x^{k})_{k}
that converges strongly to the projection of
v
onto the solution set of (
1.1
) with a rate depending on the sequence
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
. We show that with particular choices of
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
such as
\varepsilon^{k}=\frac{\alpha}{\theta(k+\beta)}
with
\alpha>1
and
\beta>0
, the rate particularizes to
O(k^{-1})
as

, and discuss several other possible choices. We obtain the new method as an explicit discretization of (
1.3
) and follow the continuous time approach to establish strong convergence of the iterates and derive convergence rates. A notable feature of our approach is that we obtain strong convergence of
(x^{k})_{k}
without relying on the Halpern iteration as proposed for EAG and FEG in
[
37
]
. Eventually, we demonstrate the practical efficiency of Algorithm

with several numerical examples. These illustrate in particular that Algorithm

can outperform other variants such as EAG, FEG and APV.

1.2
Background and Preliminaries

We say that the operator
M\colon H\to H
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, where
L\geq 0
, if

\|M(x)-M(x^{\prime})\|\leq L\|x-x^{\prime}\|\,\quad\text{for all}\ x,x^{\prime}\in H.

(1.8)

We further suppose that
M
is
monotone
. A multi-valued operator
M\colon H\to 2^{H}
is said to be monotone if

\left\langle m-m^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq 0\,\quad\text{for all}\ (x,m)\,,(x^{\prime},m^{\prime})\in\gra M\,,

(1.9)

where the set
\gra M\subseteq H\times H
is the
graph
of
M
and is defined by
\gra M:=\{(x,m)\in H\times H\mid m\in M(x)\}
. A monotone operator is called
maximal monotone
if its graph is maximal with respect to the inclusion of sets among the class of monotone operators. A single-valued monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator is also maximal monotone
[
4
, Corollary 20.28]
. In particular,
M
is strong-to-weak continuous
[
4
, Corollary 21.21]
. We refer to
[
4
]
for a comprehensive introduction to the theory of monotone operators in Hilbert spaces.

For a monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator
M\colon H\to H
, we are interested in finding a
zero
of
M
, i.e. an element in the set
\zer M:=\{x\in H\mid M(x)=0\}
, which is sometimes referred to as a
root
of
M
. This problem is ubiquitous in applications. If
M
is allowed to be multi-valued, this encompasses from classical first-order optimality conditions for convex minimization problems (Fermat’s principle) to generalized conditions of optimality for sums of non-smooth convex functions
[
10
,
7
]
. Here, we are mainly interested in minimax games

\min_{x\in H}\max_{y\in K}\ \mathcal{L}(x,y)\,,

(1.10)

where
{\mathcal{L}}\colon H\times K\to\mathbb{R}
is called
Lagrangian
, and
H,K
are Hilbert spaces, a problem that is gaining increasing interest in machine learning due to its applications in Adversarial Training
[
13
]
. We say that
{\mathcal{L}}
is
convex-concave
if
x\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)
is convex for all
y\in K
and
y\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)
is concave for all
x\in H
. A
saddle point
of
{\mathcal{L}}
is any couple
(x^{*},y^{*})\in H\times K
such that
{\mathcal{L}}(x^{*},y)\leq{\mathcal{L}}(x^{*},y^{*})\leq{\mathcal{L}}(x,y^{*})
for all
(x,y)\in H\times K
. When
{\mathcal{L}}
is differentiable and convex-concave, saddle points of
{\mathcal{L}}
correspond to zeros of the so-called
saddle operator

\mathcal{G}_{{\mathcal{L}}}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)\\
-\nabla_{y}{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)\end{pmatrix}\,\quad\text{for}\ x\in H\,,y\in K\,.

(1.11)

The saddle operator
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}
is, in fact, monotone
[
29
]
. It is
L
-Lipschitz continuous if
{\mathcal{L}}
is
L
-smooth
, i.e.,
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous. Therefore, minimax games with
L
-smooth Lagrangians fit our problem formulation (
1.1
) with
M={\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}}
.

1.3
Continuous Time Analysis

In this section, we briefly recall the continuous time analysis of the Tikhonov flow (
1.3
) with the careful adaptation recently introduced by Boţ and Nguyen in
[
6
]
, which allows establishing a fast convergence rate for the residual. Despite our main focus being on numerical algorithms, it is important to include this analysis in this paper, since our discrete-time approach is profoundly based on the continuous-time approach, which emerges as an excellent assistant to:

1.

Guide the convergence proof: Our Lyapunov analysis follows exactly the arguments in the continuous time approach, which is significantly simpler than its discrete time counterpart. We shall say that our approach is
continuous time assisted
.

2.

Design converging algorithms: It will become clear in our analysis that certain design choices of our methods are dictated by the Lyapunov analysis itself. Specifically, these are often established to control undesired terms that do not appear in the continuous time approach.

Let
M\colon H\to H
be a monotone and
L
-Lipschitz continuous operator with
\zer M\neq\emptyset
, and consider the system (
1.3
). We assume that the regularization function
\varepsilon\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
is continuously differentiable. For every starting point, the system admits a unique locally absolutely continuous solution
t\mapsto x(t)
, therefore
t\mapsto M(x(t))
is also locally absolutely continuous. We also observe that, without loss of generality, we shall assume
v=0
. The general case can then be retrieved with a suitable shift
[
5
, Subsection 2.3]
. Following the standard approach, we divide our analysis into two main sections. First, we study the functional
\varphi\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\varphi(t):=\frac{1}{2}\|x(t)-x^{*}\|^{2}\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\ \mbox{and}\ x^{*}\in\zer M\,.

(1.12)

Using classical techniques, for example,
[
12
]
, we establish in Lemma
1.1
a fundamental result that allows us to prove that, whenever
x^{*}
is the minimum norm solution of (
1.1
), then
\varphi(t)\to 0
for
t\to+\infty
, provided that, for example,
M(x(t))\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
. Specifically, this will be a consequence of the vanishing (with a convergence rate) of the second main functional, we study in this paper,
\psi\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\psi(t):=\frac{1}{2}\|M(x(t))+\varepsilon(t)x(t)\|^{2}\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

(1.13)

In particular, following
[
6
]
, we establish in Lemma
1.2
a fundamental inequality that allows us to obtain rates for
\psi
expressed in terms of the regularization parameter function
\varepsilon
, including
\psi(t)=O(t^{-2})
as
t\to+\infty
, for
\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\alpha}{t}
for all
t\geq t_{0}
, and
\alpha>1
.

In the following result, we reproduce the argument presented, e.g., in
[
12
]

Lemma 1.1

(Proposition 6 in
[
12
]
)
.

Let

\dot{\varphi}(t)+\varepsilon(t)\varphi(t)\leq\varepsilon(t)\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x(t)\|^{2}\Big)\,\quad\text{for a.e.}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

(1.14)

Proof.

Differentiating
\varphi
in time we get for a.e.
t\geq t_{0}

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{x}(t),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle=-\left\langle M(x(t))+\varepsilon(t)x(t),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle

(1.15)

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon(t)\left\langle x(t),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle=\varepsilon(t)\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x(t)\|^{2}\Big)-\varepsilon(t)\varphi(t)\,,

which yields the claim.
∎

The second result studies the functional
\psi

1.13
) with the adaptation proposed in
[

Lemma 1.2

(Theorem 2.5 in
[
6
]
)
.

Let
x\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to H
be a solution trajectory to (
1.3
). Suppose that
t\mapsto x(t)
is uniformly bounded on
[t_{0},+\infty)
. Then, for each
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
C_{\delta}\geq 0
such that

\dot{\psi}(t)+2(1-\delta)\varepsilon(t)\psi(t)\leq C_{\delta}\varepsilon(t)\left(\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}(t)}{\varepsilon(t)}\right)^{2}\,\quad\text{for a.e.}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

(1.16)

Proof.

Observe first that
\psi(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}
for a.e.
t\geq t_{0}
. Take the time derivative of
\psi
to get for a.e.
t\geq t_{0}

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\ddot{x}(t),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle=-\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}M(x(t))+\varepsilon(t)\dot{x}(t)+\dot{\varepsilon}(t)x(t),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle

(1.17)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}M(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle-2\varepsilon(t)\psi(t)+\dot{\varepsilon}(t)\left\langle x(t),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle\,.

We now apply the Young inequality on the last term in the right hand-side of (
1.17
) to bound it as follows

\dot{\varepsilon}(t)\left\langle x(t),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle\leq\varepsilon(t)\left(\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}(t)}{\varepsilon(t)}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{4\delta}\|x(t)\|^{2}+2\delta\varepsilon(t)\psi(t)\,\quad\text{for a.e.}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

(1.18)

To conclude, we use the monotonicity of
M
, which gives
\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}M(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle\geq 0
for a.e.
t\geq t_{0}
, and the boundedness of the trajectory, to obtain (
1.16
) with
C_{\delta}:=\frac{1}{4\delta}\sup_{t\geq t_{0}}{\|x(t)\|^{2}}\geq 0
.
∎

These two results together with the Gronwall inequality lead us to the following statement.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
x
be a solution trajectory to (
1.3
) with
\varepsilon\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\varepsilon(t)=0\,,\quad\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\varepsilon(s)ds=+\infty\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|\dot{\varepsilon}(t)|}{\varepsilon(t)}=0\,.

Then,
x(t)

t\rightarrow+\infty

\psi(t)\leq\frac{\psi(t_{0})}{\gamma(t)}+\frac{C_{\delta}}{\gamma(t)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma(s)\varepsilon(s)\left(\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}(t)}{\varepsilon(t)}\right)^{2}ds\,\quad\text{for all}\ t\geq t_{0}\,,

(1.20)

and any
\delta\in(0,1)
, where
\gamma(t):=\exp\Big(2(1-\delta)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\varepsilon(s)ds\Big)
and
C_{\delta}:=\frac{1}{4\delta}\sup_{t\geq t_{0}}{\|x(t)\|^{2}}\geq 0

Proof.

From Lemma
1.1
and Lemma
B.1

t\mapsto x(t)
is uniformly bounded on
[t_{0},+\infty)
. Thus, Lemma
1.2

1.20

1.19
) and Lemma
B.1

M(x(t))\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
. This shows that all cluster points of
x(t)
as
t\to+\infty
lie in
\zer M

∎

The rate reduces to the expected

rate for choices of

Corollary 1.4
.

Let

2.

If

, then

Proof.

Let

∎

Remark 1.1

(The case
\alpha=1
)
.

The case
\alpha=1
can also be shown to give the rate

as

. This has been studied in
[

with a somewhat more involved analysis and in
[

in the context of a similar approach with general parameters, but requiring the additional assumption that

preserves constant sign on

applies the Gronwall inequality as in the proof of Theorem

2
A General Framework for the Extra-Anchored-Gradient method

In this section, we study the asymptotic behavior of the Algorithm
1
. First, let us observe once again that without loss of generality, we shall assume
v=0
. The general case can then be retrieved with an appropriate shift. The iterative scheme produces for a starting point
x^{0}\in H
two sequences
(x^{k})_{k}
and
(y^{k+1})_{k}
defined for all
k\geq 0
by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y^{k+1}:=(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})x^{k}-\theta M(x^{k})\,,\\
&x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}x^{k+1}-\theta M(y^{k+1})\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

where
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
is a non-negative sequence of parameters satisfying:

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\varepsilon^{k}=0\,,\quad\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{k}=+\infty\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{|\varepsilon^{k+1}-\varepsilon^{k}|}{\varepsilon^{k}}=0\,.

(2.2)

The second identity in (
2.1
) can be written as

\frac{\Delta x_{k}}{\theta}+M(y^{k+1})+\varepsilon^{k+1}x^{k+1}=0\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0\,,

(2.3)

which shows that (
2.1
) can be understood as an explicit discretization of (
1.3
),
y^{k+1}
being an extrapolation point. If
\varepsilon^{k}=0
for all
k\geq 0
, the method reduces to EG
[
18
]
. Note that, unlike Popov-type schemes, the variable
y^{k+1}
is in fact only temporary, and thus the method only requires storing one variable
x^{k}
. Using the terminology of
[
30
]
, we shall say that the method has
minimal lifting
and is not
frugal
.

Following the continuous time setting, we divide the analysis into two main parts. This time, we start with the analysis of the sequence
(\psi^{k})_{k}
defined by

\psi^{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\|M(x^{k})+\varepsilon^{k}x^{k}\|^{2}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0\,,

(2.4)

for which we provide in Lemma
2.1
below an analogous result to the continuous time setting in Lemma
1.2
. Then, we study the sequence
(\varphi^{k})_{k}
defined by

\varphi^{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0\ \text{and}\ x^{*}\in\zer M\,,

(2.5)

for which we provide in Lemma
2.2
below the discrete time analogous of Lemma
1.1
. These two preliminary results are collected in Section
2.1
.

2.1
Technical Lemmas

To strengthen the connection with the continuous time approach and make the rationale of the proof of Lemma
2.1
below easier to follow, we introduce two important simplifications. First, leveraging the analogy between finite differences and derivatives, given a sequence
(a^{k})_{k}
we denote
\Delta a_{k}:=a^{k+1}-a^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. In this sense, it will be useful to recall some discrete calculus rules listed in Appendix
A
. Secondly, we introduce an auxiliary sequence
(z^{k})_{k}
defined by

z^{k}:=M(x^{k})+\varepsilon^{k}x^{k}\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0\,.

(2.6)

The sequences
(x^{k})_{k}
,
(y^{k+1})_{k}
, and
(z^{k})_{k}
are related by the following fundamental relations, which, although unmotivated at first sight, arise naturally from our Lyapunov analysis

\displaystyle\Delta x_{k}+\theta z^{k}=-\theta\Delta(\varepsilon x)_{k}+\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k}z^{k}+\theta N^{k}\,,

(2.7)

where

\displaystyle N^{k}:=M(x^{k})-\theta\varepsilon^{k}z^{k}-M(y^{k+1});

(2.8)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}=x^{k}-\theta z^{k}\,.

(2.9)

Lemma 2.1

(
\psi
-Analysis)
.

Let
(x^{k})_{k}
and
(y^{k+1})_{k}
be generated by (
2.1
) with
\theta\in\big(0,\frac{1}{L}\big)
and
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
satisfying (
2.2
). Suppose that
(x^{k})_{k}
is bounded. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist
C_{\delta}\geq 0
and
k_{0}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{k}+2(1-\delta)\theta\varepsilon^{k}\psi^{k}\leq C_{\delta}\theta\varepsilon^{k}\left(\frac{\Delta\varepsilon_{k}}{\theta\varepsilon^{k}}\right)^{2}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq k_{0}\,.

(2.10)

Proof.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. To shorten notation, we write
M^{k}:=M(x^{k})
for all
k\geq 0
. Recall that
z^{k}=M^{k}+\varepsilon^{k}x^{k}
and hence
\psi^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\|z^{k}\|^{2}

k\geq 0

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Delta M_{k}+\varepsilon^{k}\Delta x_{k}+\Delta\varepsilon_{k}x^{k+1},z^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Delta M_{k},z^{k}\right\rangle-2\varepsilon^{k}\theta\psi^{k}+\varepsilon^{k}\left\langle\Delta x_{k}+\theta z^{k},z^{k}\right\rangle+\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\left\langle x^{k+1},z^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\theta}\left\langle\Delta M_{k},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle-2\varepsilon^{k}\theta\psi^{k}+\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\left\langle x^{k+1},z^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\theta}\left\langle\Delta M_{k},\Delta x_{k}+\theta z^{k}\right\rangle+\varepsilon^{k}\left\langle\Delta x_{k}+\theta z^{k},z^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}}_{(\hypertarget{label:psi_I}{\text{$I$}})}\,.

We study (

satisfying the identity (

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\theta}\left\langle\Delta M_{k},\Delta x_{k}+\theta z^{k}\right\rangle+\varepsilon^{k}\left\langle\Delta x_{k}+\theta z^{k},z^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta M_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k}z^{k}+N^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|N^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\eqref{label:psi_N}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\|\Delta(\varepsilon x)_{k}\|^{2}+\theta\varepsilon^{k}\frac{\delta}{2}\psi^{k}

(2.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{2}}{(1+\theta\varepsilon^{k})^{2}}\|\Delta\varepsilon_{k}x^{k+1}-\theta\varepsilon^{k}(M(y^{k+1})-M^{k})-\theta\varepsilon^{k}z^{k}\|^{2}+\theta\varepsilon^{k}\frac{\delta}{2}\psi^{k}

-\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\left\langle x^{k+1},x^{k+1}-x^{*}\right\rangle=\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\left(\|x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}\right)-\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varphi^{k+1}\,.

(2.26)

We now deal with the remainder in (
2.25
), where we denote by
C:=\frac{(\theta L)^{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\delta_{1}}\right)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle-\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\underbrace{\frac{C(\theta\varepsilon^{k})^{2}}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}x^{k+1}\|^{2}}_{(\hypertarget{label:phi_N1}{\text{$N.1$}})}+\underbrace{\theta\left\langle M(y^{k+1}),\theta\Delta(\varepsilon x)_{k}-\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle}_{(\hypertarget{label:phi_II}{\text{$II$}})}\,.

(2.27)

We start from (
). Using the definition of
x^{k+1}
(see (
2.3
)), we get

\displaystyle\eqref{label:phi_II}

\displaystyle:=\theta\left\langle M(y^{k+1}),\theta\Delta(\varepsilon x)_{k}-\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\theta M(y^{k+1}),-(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})\Delta x_{k}+\theta\Delta\varepsilon_{k}x^{k+1}\right\rangle

(2.28)

\displaystyle=\left\langle-\Delta x_{k}-\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}x^{k+1},-(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})\Delta x_{k}+\theta\Delta\varepsilon_{k}x^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}-\theta\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\left\langle\Delta x_{k},x^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})\left\langle x^{k+1},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle-\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\left\langle x^{k+1},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\theta\left(\Delta\varepsilon_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varepsilon^{k}\right)\left\langle x^{k+1},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle-\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}}_{(\hypertarget{label:phi_N2}{\text{$N.2$}})}\,.

Plugging (
) into (
2.27
) and denoting by
(\hypertarget{label:phi_N}{\text{$N$}}):=\eqref{label:phi_N1}+\eqref{label:phi_N2}
, we get

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:remainder_phi}=

\displaystyle-\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+(1-\theta\varepsilon^{k})\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\left\langle x^{k+1},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle+\eqref{label:phi_N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\theta\varepsilon^{k}\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}\right)+\eqref{label:phi_N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}-\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k}}{2}\left(1-\eta_{1}^{k}\right)\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\eqref{label:phi_N}\,,

where
\eta_{1}^{k}:=\frac{\Delta\varepsilon_{k}}{\varepsilon^{k}}
is an infinitesimal term as
k\to+\infty
from (
2.2
). We deal with (
) as follows

\displaystyle\eqref{label:phi_N}=

\displaystyle\frac{C(\theta\varepsilon^{k})^{2}}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}x^{k+1}\|^{2}-\theta\left(\Delta\varepsilon_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varepsilon^{k}\right)\left\langle x^{k+1},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle-\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\theta\varepsilon^{k+1})^{2}-2\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\right)\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\theta\left(\Delta\varepsilon_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varepsilon^{k}\right)\left(\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\left\langle x^{k},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle\right)+\frac{C(\theta\varepsilon^{k})^{2}}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\theta\varepsilon^{k+1})^{2}-2\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}+\left\langle\Delta x_{k},x^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\theta\left(\Delta\varepsilon_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varepsilon^{k}\right)\left(\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}+\left\langle x^{k},\Delta x_{k}\right\rangle\right)+\frac{C(\theta\varepsilon^{k})^{2}}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left((\theta\varepsilon^{k+1})^{2}-2\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}+C(\theta\varepsilon^{k})^{2}\right)\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left((\theta\varepsilon^{k+1})^{2}-2\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}-2\theta\Delta\varepsilon_{k}-2\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\varepsilon^{k}\right)\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left|(\theta\varepsilon^{k+1})^{2}-2\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}-\theta\Delta\varepsilon_{k}-\theta^{2}\varepsilon^{k+1}\varepsilon^{k}\right|\left(\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{k}\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\|x^{k}\|^{2}+\eta_{2}^{k}\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k}}{2}\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}\,,

where
\eta^{k}
and
\eta_{2}^{k}
are the coefficients that make the last identity hold true for all
k\geq 0
. Applying once again (
2.2
), it is easy to note that these sequences are indeed infinitesimal, i.e., they converge to zero as
k\to+\infty
. Plugging (
2.27
) and (
2.26
) into (
2.25
) we observe that the term
\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\|x^{k+1}\|^{2}
cancels and thus we get for all
k\geq 0

\Delta\varphi_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varphi^{k+1}\leq\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\left(\|x^{*}\|^{2}-(1-\eta^{k})\|x^{k}\|^{2}\right)-\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k}}{2}\left(1-\eta_{1}^{k}-\eta_{2}^{k}\right)\|\Delta x_{k}\|^{2}\,.

(2.29)

Taking
k_{1}^{\prime}\geq 0
such that
\eta^{k}\leq 1
and
\eta_{1}^{k}+\eta_{2}^{k}\leq 1
for all
k\geq k_{1}^{\prime}
, we deduce:

\Delta\varphi_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varphi^{k+1}\leq\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\left(\|x^{*}\|^{2}-(1-\eta^{k})\|x^{k}\|^{2}\right)\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq k_{1}^{\prime}\,.

(2.30)

Observe now that
\Delta\varphi_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varphi^{k+1}=(1+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1})\Delta\varphi_{k}+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}\varphi^{k}
. Hence, plugging this into the left hand-side of (
2.30
), dividing by
1+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}>0
and using that
\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{2}\leq\frac{\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}{1+\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}}\leq\theta\varepsilon^{k+1}
for all
k\geq k_{1}^{\prime\prime}
and some
k_{1}^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
large enough, we obtain the claim with
k_{1}:=\max\{k_{1}^{\prime},k_{1}^{\prime\prime}\}
.
∎

2.2
Main Convergence Result

As in the continuous time approach, we combine the two preliminary lemmas on
(\varphi^{k})_{k}
and
(\psi^{k})_{k}
to get a converging result with a general rate expressed in terms of
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
. We do so by utilizing the discrete Gronwall inequality. In Section
2.3
below, we turn this inequality into asymptotic rates for several choices of the parameters relying on Chung’s Lemma, see Appendix
C
.

Theorem 2.3

(Convergence statement)
.

Let
(x^{k})_{k}
,
(y^{k+1})_{k}
be generated by Algorithm
1
with
\theta\in\big(0,\frac{1}{L}\big)
and
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
satisfying (
2.2
). Then
(x^{k})_{k}
converges strongly to the minimum norm solution to (
1.1
) as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, and the convergence rate of the residual results from the following statement: For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist
\widetilde{C}_{\delta}\geq 0
and
k_{0}\geq 0
such that

\psi^{k+1}\leq\frac{\psi^{k_{0}}}{\gamma^{k}}+\frac{\widetilde{C}_{\delta}}{\gamma^{k}}\sum_{j=k_{0}}^{k}\gamma^{j}\varepsilon^{j}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{j+1}-\varepsilon^{j}}{\theta\varepsilon^{j}}\right)^{2}\ \quad\text{for all}\ k\geq k_{0}\,,

(2.31)

where
(\gamma^{k})_{k}
is the sequence defined by
\gamma^{k}:=\prod_{l=k_{0}}^{k}(1-2(1-\delta)\theta\varepsilon^{l})^{-1}
.

Proof.

The result is based on Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.1
. From Lemma
2.2
, we deduce that
(x^{k})_{k}
is bounded. Thus, Lemma
2.1
applies and yields, together with Lemma
B.2
, the abstract rate (
2.31
), where
\widetilde{C}_{\delta}:=\frac{C_{\delta}}{2(1-\delta)}
, and
k_{0}\geq 1
is such that, in addition to (
2.10
),
\varepsilon^{k}<\frac{1}{2(1-\delta)}
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. In particular, since (
2.2
) holds, we get
\psi^{k}\to 0
for
k\to+\infty
by applying the second statement of Lemma
B.2
. The strong convergence of
(x^{k})_{k}
to the element with the minimum norm in
\zer M
follows from the same argument as in Theorem
1.3
, by using Lemma
2.2
and the second statement of Lemma
B.2
.
∎

Remark 2.2

(Convergence of
(y^{k})_{k}
)
.

Since
(x^{k})_{k}
converges strongly to the minimum norm solution to (
1.1
) as
k\rightarrow+\infty
and
M(x^{k})\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
,
(y^{k+1})_{k}
converges also strongly to the same point.

2.3
Choices of
\varepsilon^{k}

An immediate consequence of our approach is a straightforward analysis of Algorithm
1
with several choices of the parameter
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
. We only have to turn (
2.31
) into explicit rates in each case. We start with the most natural choice.

Linear:

In this section, we make the following choice:

\varepsilon^{k}:=\frac{\alpha}{\theta(k+\beta)}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0,\ \mbox{and}\ \alpha,\beta>0\,,

(2.32)

which can be understood as a discrete time counterpart of
\varepsilon(t):=\frac{\alpha}{t}
. Specifically, since
\theta
represents the temporal step size, the term
t^{k}:=\theta(k+\beta)
represents the discrete time, with
\theta\beta=t^{0}
as the starting time
t_{0}
in (
1.3
). The convergence result aligns with its continuous time counterpart stated in Corollary
1.4
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
(x^{k})_{k}
,
(y^{k+1})_{k}
be generated by Algorithm
1
with
\theta\in\big(0,\frac{1}{L}\big)
and
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
defined as in (
2.32
) with
\alpha,\beta>0
. Then
(x^{k})_{k}
converges strongly to the minimum norm solution to (
1.1
) as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, the residual showing the following convergence rate:

1.

If
\alpha>1
, then
\|M(x^{k})\|=O(k^{-1})
as
k\to+\infty
;

2.

If
\alpha\leq 1
, then
\|M(x^{k})\|=O(k^{-\alpha(1-\delta)})
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, as
k\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
0<\hat{\delta}<\delta
. Lemma
2.1
yields
C_{\hat{\delta}}\geq 0
and
k_{0}\geq 0
, such that

\Delta\psi_{k}+2(1-\hat{\delta})\varepsilon^{k}\theta\psi^{k}\leq 2(1-\hat{\delta})\varepsilon^{k}\theta h^{k}\,\quad\mbox{for all}\ k\geq k_{0},

(2.33)

where
h^{k}:=\frac{C_{\hat{\delta}}}{2(1-\hat{\delta})}\left(\frac{\Delta\varepsilon_{k}}{\theta\varepsilon^{k}}\right)^{2}
. Using the definition of
\varepsilon^{k}
and of
h^{k}
, we get
h^{k}=\frac{C_{\hat{\delta}}}{2(1-\hat{\delta})\theta^{2}(k+\beta+1)^{2}}
, and thus

\displaystyle\psi^{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{2\alpha(1-\hat{\delta})}{k+\beta}\right)\psi^{k}+\frac{\alpha C_{\hat{\delta}}}{(k+\beta)\theta^{2}(k+\beta+1)^{2}}

(2.34)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-\frac{2\alpha(1-\hat{\delta})}{k}\underbrace{\frac{k}{k+\beta}}_{=:\xi^{k}}\bigg)\psi^{k}+\frac{\alpha C_{\hat{\delta}}}{\theta^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{3}}\frac{k^{3}}{(k+\beta)(k+\beta+1)^{2}}\,\quad\mbox{for all}\ k\geq k_{0}\,.

Since
\xi^{k}\to 1
as
k\to+\infty
, there exists
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
such that

\psi^{k+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{2\alpha(1-\delta)}{k}\right)\psi^{k}+\frac{\alpha C_{\hat{\delta}}}{\theta^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{3}}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq k_{1}.

(2.35)

We are in the setting of Lemma
C.1
. If
\alpha>1
, choosing
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
2\alpha(1-\delta)>2
, we get
\psi^{k}=O(k^{-2})
as
k\to+\infty
. If
\alpha\leq 1
, then
2\alpha(1-\delta)<2
and thus
\psi^{k}=O(k^{-2\alpha(1-\delta)})
. To turn these into rates for
\|M(x^{k})\|
, observe that
(2\psi^{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(k^{-1})\geq\|M(x^{k})\|
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, since
x^{k}
is bounded and
\varepsilon^{k}=O(k^{-1})
as
k\to+\infty
.
∎

Power:

Our second choice reinforces the influence of the anchor term as follows

\varepsilon^{k}:=\frac{\alpha}{(k+\beta)^{\eta}}\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0,\ \mbox{and}\ \alpha,\beta>0\,,\ \eta\in(0,1)\,.

(2.36)

Simple calculation shows that in this case the conditions in (
2.2
) are also satisfied. However, the convergence rate we are able to obtain gets worse.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
(x^{k})_{k}
,
(y^{k+1})_{k}
be generated by Algorithm
1
with
\theta\in\big(0,\frac{1}{L}\big)
and
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
defined as in (
2.36
). Then
(x^{k})_{k}
converges strongly to the minimum norm solution to (
1.1
) as
k\to+\infty
, the residual showing the following convergence rate

\|M(x^{k})\|=O(k^{-\eta})\,\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,.

(2.37)

Proof.

The proof follows the same argument of Corollary
2.4
and therefore we only state the differences. In this case, we have once again
h^{k}=O(k^{-2})
for
k\to+\infty
and thus (
2.10
) writes as

\psi^{k+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{c}{k^{\eta}}\right)\psi^{k}+\frac{C}{k^{2+\eta}}\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq k_{1}\,,

(2.38)

for some
c,C>0
and
k_{1}\geq 0
large enough. We now make use of Lemma

to obtain
\psi^{k}=O(k^{-2})
as
k\to+\infty
. Since
(2\psi^{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(k^{-\eta})\geq\|M(x^{k})\|
, we get
\|M(x^{k})\|=O(k^{-\eta})
as

.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The asymptotic behaviour of the abstract rate in (
2.31
) is the same as the one inferred for
(\psi^{k})_{k}
. Indeed, if we denote by
r^{k+1}
the right hand-side of (
2.31
), which satisfies
\psi^{k+1}\leq r^{k+1}
for all

, it is easy to note that the sequence

satisfies the following recursion

r^{k+1}=(1-2(1-\delta)\varepsilon^{k}\theta)r^{k}+C_{\delta}\theta\varepsilon^{k}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{k+1}-\varepsilon^{k}}{\theta\varepsilon^{k}}\right)^{2}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq k_{0}\,.

(2.39)

Therefore, applying Chung’s Lemma on this expression, we derive the same results as in Corollary
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
.

Miscellaneous:

Our approach to deriving convergence rates leverages inequality (
2.10
) in combination with Chung’s Lemma. Originally introduced by Chung in
[
11
]
to study the stochastic gradient descent method, this lemma has since become a key tool in the stochastic optimization literature. It has been extensively used, and efforts have been made to derive nonlinear and non-asymptotic extensions

1

17
]
. We can leverage these results to obtain several analytical bounds on
(\psi^{k})_{k}
. However, due to space constraints, we limit our focus here to present the explicit expansion (

We observe in particular that the choice

behaves like

for

and
\beta>0
and in particular raises

for

and

does not seem to be present for

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a new Extra-Anchored-Gradient-type scheme with a general choice of parameter sequences
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
, which only has to satisfy (
2.2
). Our approach is profoundly based on the continuous time analysis of Tikhonov regularized monotone operator flows, which guide our convergence proof while suggesting critical design choices of the algorithm itself. In particular, it allows us to establish the strong convergence to a specific point in the solution set without relying on other systems dynamics. Although we allow for a general class of parameters, the main drawback of our approach is the lack of an explicit constant in the abstract rate (
2.31
). We suggest a possible approach to address this issue in Remark
1.1
. Indeed, despite our numerical experiments suggest that Algorithm
1
with
\varepsilon^{k}=\frac{\alpha}{\theta(k+\beta)}
and
\alpha\simeq 1
seems to slightly improve over FEG, it remains an open question whether its worst-case rate actually improves upon the one of FEG with this specific choice of
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
.

Discrete Calculus Rules

Let
a:=(a^{k})_{k}
,
b:=(b^{k})_{k}
, and
c:=(c^{k})_{k}
, be three real sequences. The product of two sequences
a
and
b
is denoted by
ab:=(a^{k}b^{k})_{k}
. As usual, if
a=b
, we denote
a^{2}=ab
. For all
k\geq 0
it holds

\displaystyle\Delta(ab)_{k}=\Delta a_{k}b^{k}+a^{k+1}\Delta b_{k}\,,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Delta(abc)_{k}=\Delta a_{k}b^{k}c^{k}+a^{k+1}\Delta b^{k}c^{k}+a^{k+1}b^{k+1}\Delta c_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta a^{2}_{k}=2(a^{k+1}-a^{k})a^{k}+(\Delta a^{k})^{2}=2(a^{k+1}-a^{k})a^{k+1}-(\Delta a_{k})^{2}\,.

This formalism can be readily extended to sequences in a Hilbert space
H
, with products replaced by inner products. For instance, if
(z^{k})_{k}
is a sequence in
H
, and
\zeta^{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\|z^{k}\|^{2}
for all
k\geq 0
, we have for all
k\geq 0

\Delta\zeta_{k}=\left\langle\Delta z_{k},z^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}=\left\langle\Delta z_{k},z^{k+1}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}\,.

(A.2)

Similar rules as those in (
A.1
) apply for products of sequences in
H
and in
\mathbb{R}
, for instance if
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
is a real sequence, then for all
k\geq 0

\Delta(\varepsilon z)_{k}=\Delta\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}+\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta z_{k}=\Delta z_{k}\varepsilon^{k}+z^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\,.

(A.3)

B
Gronwall in Continuous and Discrete Time

The following result is an application of Grownwall’s inequality, see for instance
[
12
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
f\colon\left[t_{0},+\infty\right)\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally absolutely continuous function,
h\colon\left[t_{0},+\infty\right)\to\mathbb{R}
a bounded function and
\varepsilon\colon\left[t_{0},+\infty\right)\to(0,+\infty)
a locally integrable function. Suppose that

\dot{f}(t)+\varepsilon(t)f(t)\leq\varepsilon(t)h(t)\quad\mbox{for a.e.}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

(B.1)

Denote by
\gamma(t):=\exp\Big(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\varepsilon(s)ds\Big)
. Then,

f(t)\leq\frac{f(t_{0})}{\gamma(t)}+\frac{1}{\gamma(t)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\varepsilon(s)\gamma(s)h(s)ds\,\quad\text{for all}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

(B.2)

In particular, if
\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\varepsilon(t)dt=+\infty
, then

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}f\left(t\right)\leq\limsup_{t\to+\infty}h\left(t\right).

(B.3)

The following result is the discrete counterpart of Lemma
B.1
. For this result, we only present the derivation of the explicit expansion (
B.5

Lemma B.2
.

Let
(f^{k})_{k}
and
(h^{k})_{k}
be two sequences in
\mathbb{R}
and
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k}
be a nonnegative sequence satisfying

(B.4)

Suppose further that

f^{k+1}\leq\frac{f^{0}}{\gamma^{k}}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\gamma^{j}\varepsilon^{j}h^{j}\,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0\,,

(B.5)

where

(B.6)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon^{k})f^{k}+\varepsilon^{k}h^{k}

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\ell=0}^{k}(1-\varepsilon^{j})f^{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\prod_{\ell=j+1}^{k}(1-\varepsilon^{\ell})\varepsilon^{j}h^{j}\,,
