Effects of simulated seawater intrusion-induced salt stress and the recovery process on growth, antioxidant system, and photosynthesis of rice, and the alleviating effect of Pro-Ca
Authors: Jiangyuan Ya, Dianfeng Zheng, Qicheng Zhang, Wenkang Huang, Qianqian Shang, Hang Zhou, Naijie Feng, Shaobiao Duan, Junhua Zhang, Zhiyuan Sun, Rui Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSaltwater intrusion is a common threat to coastal agriculture. This study investigated the mitigating and restorative effects of exogenous prohexadionecalcium (Pro-Ca) on rice under simulated saltwater intrusion. Salt-tolerant Chaoyou 1000 and salt-sensitive Huanghuazhan were treated with 100 mg·L⁻¹ Pro-Ca at the four-leaf-one-bud stage. Physiological responses were analyzed during stress (D1–D4) and recovery (FD4).ResultsSalt stress significantly inhibited rice growth, reduced biomass, induced oxidative damage, and impaired photosynthesis and photosystem II (PSII) activity, with maximum damage at D4. Exogenous Pro-Ca enhanced stress tolerance by activating antioxidant enzymes and the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle, effectively scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protecting cell membrane integrity. Additionally, Pro-Ca maintained PSII activity and improved light use efficiency, ensuring photosynthetic function. During recovery (FD4), Pro-Ca-treated plants showed superior physiological recovery compared to salt-stressed controls.ConclusionsThe exogenous application of Pro-Ca effectively alleviates salt stress damage caused by seawater intrusion in rice. It enhances salt tolerance by strengthening the antioxidant defense system and maintaining photosynthetic function, while significantly promoting the recovery of physiological status during the post-stress rehabilitation phase.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08668-7.

Introduction

Seawater intrusion is a natural phenomenon that occurs frequently in coastal regions worldwide, drawing widespread research attention. When the Earth, Moon, and Sun align, tidal forces intensify, causing abnormal sea level rises that facilitate seawater penetration inland. Astronomical tides often combine with storm surges to create more severe intrusion events [1, 2]. Seawater reaches land via surface channels, damaging agricultural, aquaculture, and residential areas in low-lying coastal zones, while also impeding inland river drainage and increasing flood vulnerability: a worsening ecological issue along China’s coastlines [3]. Short-term high-salinity immersion causes the rapid accumulation of sea salt in soil, contaminating water sources and accelerating farmland salinization. Such intrusion becomes particularly severe following extreme weather events like typhoons, with often catastrophic impacts on local agriculture [4, 5]. Consequently, seawater intrusion stress has emerged as a critical constraint on ecological restoration and crop yields [6].

Rice is a crucial food crop as it supplies more than half of the world’s population [7]. Cyclical tides and rising sea levels subject rice fields near estuaries to seawater intrusion, which increases soil salinity and exposes rice to high salt stress. Rice is a salt-sensitive crop [8], with salt stress conditions inhibiting its growth and development [9], reducing its photosynthetic efficiency and biomass accumulation, and ultimately leading to yield reduction [10]. Therefore, improving salt tolerance in rice not only increases the potential for saline soil utilization but also supports sustainable agricultural development [11].

Salt stress affects rice differently at each stage of its growth and development cycle [12]. Studies have shown that the seedling and reproductive stages are more susceptible to salinity than the vegetative stage, and that the rice seedling stage is highly sensitive to moisture and soil conditions [13–15]. The high-salinity soil environment caused by seawater intrusion can directly affect the growth and survival of rice seedlings. When seawater floods paddy fields, soil salinity rises sharply, severely threatening rice growth. Farmers typically use freshwater irrigation to reduce salinity, but this method is time-consuming, labor- and resource-intensive, and often ineffective. Therefore, formulating efficient and environmentally friendly exogenous control measures to enhance rice salt tolerance has become a key research direction.

The application of chemical control technologies is an effective way to mitigate abiotic stress and increase crop yield. Highly efficient in small doses and easy to use, these approaches have been demonstrated to enhance plant stress resistance by regulating various physiological mechanisms [16, 17]. Pro-Ca is considered an eco-friendly, new type of plant growth retardant with low toxicity, no residue, and easy absorption [18]. To date, most research on Pro-Ca has focused on its effects of shortening plant stem length, improving lodging resistance, and increasing crop yield. However, the stress resistance it imparts to plants has also attracted increasing attention. Bekheta et al. [19] found that using exogenous Pro-Ca for seed soaking could alleviate the inhibitory effects of salt stress on faba bean growth and development by regulating its physiological metabolic processes and increasing dry matter accumulation. Feng et al. [20] discovered that foliar spraying with exogenous Pro-Ca could mitigate the damage of salt stress to soybean seedlings by regulating seedling phenotypes, improving photosynthesis, and activating the antioxidant defense system and osmotic regulation. While existing studies confirm that exogenous Pro-Ca regulates plant responses to salt stress, most have focused on static conditions (at a single intensity and time point), ignoring continuous physiological dynamics during stress fluctuation and the subsequent recovery period. This research gap limits our understanding of rice’s real-time adaptive responses and the accurate evaluation of Pro-Ca’s long-term mitigating effects.

Seawater intrusion is typically characterized by a dynamic process involving a sudden increase in salinity, followed by gradual alleviation. To enhance the practical representativeness of the study, the effects of seawater on the physiological mechanisms of rice should be investigated using an experimental strategy that mimics this natural gradient variation. To simulate the dynamic salt stress triggered by seawater intrusion in coastal rice areas, a hydroponic system for controlled experiments was used. An experimental platform was established in Zhanjiang, Guangdong Province, featuring two representative rice varieties: Huanghuazhan and Chaoyou 1000. After spraying Pro-Ca at the four-leaf stage, a salt stress of 0.6 ppt NaCl was applied. A gradient concentration treatment (0.6 ppt→0.3 ppt→0.15 ppt→0 ppt) was then used to simulate the natural process of seawater intrusion, alleviation, and recovery. The dynamic physiological responses of the rice varieties were systematically evaluated during the four consecutive days of salt stress and the subsequent 4-day recovery period.

Methods

Test materials

Plant materials: Two rice varieties, Chaoyou 1000 (CYQH) and Huanghuazhan (HHZ), were provided by the Germplasm Resource Bank of the Institute of Agricultural Biotechnology, Guangdong Ocean University.

Experimental treatment: The Pro-Ca stock solution was provided by the laboratory of the Institute of Coastal Agricultural Biotechnology, Guangdong Ocean University, and was applied at a concentration of 100 mg·L–1.

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted at 2024 in the greenhouse of the Institute of Agricultural Biotechnology, Guangdong Ocean University. Healthy, full, and uniformly sized rice seeds were selected and surface-sterilized by immersion in 2% hydrogen peroxide solution for 30 min, followed by thorough rinsing with distilled water. After soaking in distilled water for 24 h, the seeds were placed in clean seedling trays for germination (48 h) and then transferred to hydroponic boxes. On day 1, seedlings were incubated in water for 24 h, and on day 2 the solution was replaced with 1/4-strength Hoagland nutrient solution. On day 7, the nutrient solution was replaced with 1/2-strength Hoagland solution, and it was thereafter renewed every 7 days. Seedlings were first cultured in 96-well black hydroponic boxes until the 3-leaf-1-heart stage. At this stage, seedlings were transferred into round plastic buckets (35.0 cm top diameter, 13.5 cm bottom diameter, 30.0 cm height) filled with nutrient solution. Foliar spraying of 100 mg·L–1 Pro-Ca solution was applied at the 4-leaf-1-heart stage. After 48 h, salt stress was imposed using 0.6 ppt NaCl. The experimental treatments were as follows: (1) CK: foliar spraying with water only. (2) S: foliar spraying with water + 0.6 ppt NaCl stress for 1, 2, 3, or 4 days. (3) SP: foliar spraying with 100 mg·L–1 Pro-Ca + 0.6 ppt NaCl stress for 1, 2, 3, or 4 days. To simulate the process of desalination following seawater intrusion, the NaCl concentration was gradually reduced in steps (0.6 ppt→0.3 ppt→0.15 ppt→0 ppt). Sampling was performed 24 h after the NaCl concentration was reduced to 0 ppt (fresh water), and again after 4 days of recovery under desalination conditions (Fig. 1).

Indicators and methods

Morphological indicators and determination of biomass

For morphological analysis, sampling was conducted at the end of the stress and recovery periods. In each treatment group, 20 representative seedlings were randomly selected for analysis. The selected seedlings were washed with tap water, rinsed with distilled water, and the surface moisture was blotted dry with paper towels. Subsequently, each seedling was separated into its shoot and root components. Plant height, root length, and stem base width were measured using a ruler and a vernier caliper (0.1 mm). The fresh weights of the shoots and roots were determined using an electronic balance (0.0001 g). To measure dry weight, the samples were placed in an oven at 105 °C for 30 min for enzyme deactivation, then dried at 80 °C to a constant weight. The dry weights of the shoots and roots were subsequently recorded. Leaf area was measured using a portable leaf area meter (LI-3000 C, LI-COR, USA)0.2.3.2. Determination of antioxidant enzyme activity To prepare the crude enzyme extract, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue (previously frozen in liquid nitrogen) was homogenized in pre-cooled phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8). The activity of various antioxidant enzymes was then determined as follows. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photochemical reduction method [21]. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by monitoring the rate of H₂O₂ degradation [22]. Peroxidase (POD) activity was measured using the guaiacol method [23]. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was determined by the rate of AsA-dependent H₂O₂ decomposition. Glutathione reductase (GR) activity was measured by the rate of GSSG reduction [24]. Dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR) and monodehydroascorbate reductase (MDHAR) activity was assayed based on the AsA regeneration system [25].

Determination of non-enzymatic antioxidant content and soluble protein

Ascorbic acid (AsA) content was determined using the colorimetric method based on dichloroindophenol sodium salt [26]. Glutathione (GSH) content was measured using the 5,5’-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) colorimetric method. Soluble protein content was determined according to the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method.

Determination of degree of membrane lipid peroxidation and content of ROS production indicators

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method [23]. A conductivity meter was used to measure the permeate conductivity value and calculate the relative conductivity. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content was determined using the potassium iodide method [27]. Superoxide radical (O2•–) content was determined using the hydroxylamine oxidation method [21].

Photosynthetic and gas exchange parameters

The net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr) of fully expanded functional leaves were measured between 09:00 and 11:00 h using a portable photosynthesis system (LI-6800, LI-COR, USA) under the following conditions: photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD), 1000 µmol·m–2·s–1; CO2 concentration, 400 µmol·mol–1; leaf chamber temperature, 28 °C; relative humidity, 60%.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were determined using a chlorophyll fluorometer system (OS5P, OPTI-Sciences, USA). The maximum quantum efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), electron transport rate (ETR), and effective quantum yield of PSII [Y(II)] for each treatment were measured and calculated under dark conditions, 30 min after the rice plants were placed in darkness [28].

Data processing

Data were organized and collected in Excel 2016. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27, with multiple comparisons conducted via Duncan’s test. Graphs were plotted using Origin 2021.

Results

Morphological index

Salt stress significantly inhibited the morphological development of both rice varieties, with distinct varietal differences in inhibition degree (Tables 1 and 2). In CYQH, plant height, root length, stem diameter, and leaf area under salt stress decreased by 4.6%–10.5%, 3.7%–12.8%, 5.8%–9.4%, and 8.2%–21.7%, respectively, during the stress period (D1–D4) compared to the control, and remained 18.9%, 18.6%, 15.0%, and 18.4% lower at the recovery stage (FD4). In HHZ, decreases of 0.4%–8.7%, 6.1%–21.0%, 10.6%–20.7%, and 10.5%–46.8% were observed in these parameters during the stress period, particularly in leaf area, which exhibited a substantial reduction of 46.8% at D4, indicating that HHZ suffered more severe inhibition. The exogenous application of Pro-Ca significantly alleviated salt-induced damage: In CYQH, Pro-Ca treatment restored plant height, root length, stem diameter, and leaf area to 92.8%–96.5%, 93.8%–106.9%, 91.9%–99.0%, and 91.3%–96.4% of control levels during the stress period, and to 93.6%, 90.0%, 95.1%, and 91.8% at the recovery stage, respectively. In HHZ, restoration rates of 94.0%–100.7%, 85.2%–96.6%, 85.5%–96.1%, and 59.3%–94.1% were achieved during the stress period; at the recovery stage, plant height, root length, stem diameter, and leaf area reached 95.0%, 87.3%, 92.7%, and 80.8% of control levels, respectively. Notably, root length in CYQH under Pro-Ca treatment exceeded control levels at D1 (106.9%), whereas leaf area in HHZ recovered to only 59.3% of the control at D4.

Biomass

Salt stress significantly reduced biomass accumulation in both rice varieties, with more pronounced inhibition in HHZ than in CYQH (Tables 3 and 4). In CYQH, under salt stress, root fresh weight, shoot fresh weight, root dry weight, and shoot dry weight decreased by 13.3%–27.0%, 5.2%–39.8%, 19.4%–38.3%, and 15.4%–25.0%, respectively, during the stress period (D1–D4) compared to the control, and remained 21.6%, 26.9%, 29.8%, and 18.8% lower at the recovery stage (FD4). In HHZ, these parameters decreased by 5.7%–12.1%, 12.5%–14.5%, 26.2%–35.6%, and 16.7%–23.1% during the stress period, and at FD4 were still 17.1%, 31.9%, 30.4%, and 20.0% below control levels, indicating that HHZ suffered greater inhibition, particularly in shoot fresh weight and root dry weight. The exogenous application of Pro-Ca alleviated the salt-induced reduction in biomass. In CYQH, Pro-Ca treatment restored root fresh weight, shoot fresh weight, root dry weight, and shoot dry weight to 83.8%–103.3%, 64.3%–95.6%, 68.1%–91.7%, and 75.0%–92.3% of control levels during the stress period, and to 86.5%, 77.2%, 76.6%, and 93.8% at FD4, respectively. In HHZ, Pro-Ca restored these parameters to 90.9%–96.9%, 88.0%–91.9%, 70.5%–81.0%, and 81.8%–91.7% during stress, and to 85.4%, 74.7%, 78.3%, and 80.0% at recovery.

Antioxidant enzyme activity

The S treatment significantly enhanced the activity of SOD, POD, APX, and CAT in both rice varieties compared to the CK treatment (Fig. 2). Throughout the stress period (D1–D4), SOD activity increased by 16.20–46.00% in CYQH and by 10.46–32.62% in HHZ, peaking in D4. Statistically significant differences were observed between the S and SP treatments in CYQH at D1 and D2, but not in HHZ. Similar increases were detected in POD, APX, and CAT activity. Notably, APX activity exhibited a more substantial increase in CYQH (42.26–47.16%) than in HHZ (9.49–23.86%), emphasizing the stronger antioxidant capacity of CYQH. The exogenous application of Pro-Ca under salt stress (SP) further elevated the activity of all four antioxidant enzymes from D1 to D4 relative to the S treatment. In HHZ, the most prominent increases occurred in SOD (12.87% at D1), APX (23.75% at D2), POD (18.14% at D3), and CAT (25.00% at D3). In CYQH, significant enhancements were observed in POD activity at D2 (12.04%) and D4 (17.97%), SOD at D3 (10.00%), and APX at D4 (14.73%). By the recovery phase (FD4), the differences between the S and SP treatments in HHZ were no longer significant. Furthermore, the elevated enzyme activity under the SP treatment at D4 decreased considerably and approached CK levels in both varieties. These findings demonstrated that exogenous Pro-Ca effectively enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity under salt stress, thereby mitigating oxidative damage and supporting recovery.

AsA-GSH cycle–related enzyme activity

Salt stress significantly influenced the activity of key enzymes associated with the AsA–GSH cycle in rice leaves (Fig. 3). Under salt treatment (S), the activity of GR, MDHAR, and DHAR in both the CYQH and HHZ cultivars continuously increased throughout the stress period (D1–D4), indicating that the enzymatic responses were enhanced over time. Compared with the CK treatment, salt stress (S) increased GR activity by 52.03–81.76% in CYQH and 25.17–53.49% in HHZ, DHAR activity by 48.45–76.32% in CYQH and 22.81–76.10% in HHZ, and MDHAR activity by 25.26–50.00% in CYQH and 24.18–51.78% in HHZ. The highest increases were observed at D4. Notably, the application of exogenous Pro-Ca under salt stress (SP) further enhanced the activity of these enzymes. Compared with the S treatment, the SP treatment increased GR activity by 19.00–32.88% in CYQH and 15.70–30.30% in HHZ, DHAR activity by 10.82–17.00% in CYQH and 15.24–22.81% in HHZ, and MDHAR activity by 14.49–27.69% in CYQH and 13.68–29.21% in HHZ. The maximum increases also occurred at D4. During the recovery phase (FD4), the upregulating effect of Pro-Ca persisted. Under SP treatment, the activity of GR, DHAR, and MDHAR in both cultivars remained significantly higher than for the S group. Although the activity of GR, MDHAR, and DHAR in CYQH decreased at FD4 compared to D4, it was still 59.78%, 54.3%, and 37.6% higher than the CK levels, respectively. Similarly, in HHZ, the activity of GR, DHAR, and MDHAR was 35.0%, 46.5%, and 60.8% higher than for CK at FD4. These results strongly suggest that the defense system activated by Pro-Ca during salt stress remained functional during recovery, facilitating a return to near-normal physiological conditions and thus contributing to the mitigation of oxidative damage.

Non-enzymatic antioxidant content

Salt stress (S treatment) significantly reduced the levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants in rice leaves (Fig. 4). Throughout the stress period (D1–D4), the content of glutathione (GSH), ascorbate (AsA), and soluble protein declined in both CYQH and HHZ. This suggested that salt stress not only depleted antioxidant reserves but also impaired protein synthesis. Specifically, the GSH content in CYQH decreased by 29.98–34.78% compared to CK, with the maximum reduction observed at D4. In HHZ, the decrease in GSH was more pronounced, ranging from 30.40 to 41.97%, and decreasing most sharply at D1. Similarly, AsA content declined by 25.26–26.31% in CYQH (lowest at D4) and by 24.62–35.62% in HHZ (lowest at D3). Soluble protein content in CYQH showed a slight reduction, ranging from 0.89 to 8.24%, with the lowest value at D2. In contrast, HHZ experienced a more substantial 25.59–31.87% decrease in soluble protein, also reaching a minimum at D2, indicating greater metabolic inhibition in this cultivar. The application of Pro-Ca (SP treatment) significantly alleviated the salt-induced depletion of these non-enzymatic antioxidants GSH, AsA, and soluble protein. In compared to the S treatment, AsA content in CYQH increased by 4.95–7.47%, GSH by 10.08–15.43%, and soluble protein by 4.95–7.47% in the SP treatment. In HHZ, the increases were even more notable: AsA rose by 6.83–11.90%, GSH by 15.20–22.18%, and soluble protein by 22.25–26.26%. During the recovery phase (FD4), the antioxidant content under SP treatment remained at higher levels in both cultivars compared to the S group. Notably, the SP treatment facilitated the rebound of AsA, GSH, and soluble protein in CYQH relative to D4 levels, and a similar restorative trend was observed in HHZ.

Membrane damage and ROS content

Salt stress (S treatment) induced a time-dependent increase in membrane damage and ROS accumulation in both rice cultivars (Fig. 5). The S treatment significantly elevated leaf relative conductivity and MDA content, which are key indicators of membrane injury and lipid peroxidation, by 31.83–148.60% in CYQH and 34.54–73.15% in HHZ compared to CK. These changes reflected severe oxidative disruption of plasma membrane integrity and function. Notably, the exogenous application of Pro-Ca (SP treatment) significantly reduced these oxidative damage markers, lowering relative conductivity by 6.81–18.17% and MDA by 8.16–48.97% relative to the S treatment. This mitigation was likely attributable to the role of Pro-Ca in enhancing membrane stability and reducing peroxidation through the upregulation of antioxidant synthesis or direct membrane protection. Salt stress also triggered substantial ROS accumulation, with O2•⁻ increasing by 95.2–227.4% and H2O2 by 10.4–39.7% compared to CK. The SP treatment effectively suppressed ROS levels, reducing O2•⁻ by 12.6–23.5% and H2O2 by 9.4–16.5%. This suggested that Pro-Ca may stimulate antioxidant enzyme activity or improve redox homeostasis, thereby limiting O2•⁻ and H2O2 buildup. After recovery (FD4), SP-treated plants exhibited near-complete restoration of ROS to basal levels and significantly faster membrane recovery compared to S-treated plants, supporting the role of Pro-Ca in promoting cellular repair mechanisms and reestablishing oxidative balance. These physiological responses highlighted the protective function of exogenous Pro-Ca in preserving membrane integrity and reducing oxidative stress under salt stress conditions.

Photosynthesis

Salt stress (S treatment) significantly reduced the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), and transpiration rate (Tr), while increasing the intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) in both rice varieties (Fig. 6). In comparison to CK, the S treatment suppressed Pn by 21.9–59.1% in CYQH and by 21.0–66.7% in HHZ, with the most pronounced inhibition occurring at D4. The greater reduction in HHZ suggested that this cultivar experienced more severe photosynthetic impairment under salinity. Consistent with the Pn results, Gs and Tr also declined under salt stress. In CYQH, Gs decreased by 19.0–33.3% and Tr by 23.3–37.3%, whereas in HHZ, Gs dropped by 33.3–66.7% and Tr by 21.4–37.5%, indicating stomatal closure and restricted gas exchange. Concurrently, Ci increased by 11.5–22.6% in CYQH and 28.8–52.7% in HHZ, implying that non-stomatal limitations—such as biochemical constraints in carbon fixation—also contributed to the photosynthetic decline. Exogenous prohexadione calcium (SP treatment) significantly alleviated the salt-induced inhibition of photosynthesis. The SP treatment enhanced Pn relative to the S treatment, with increases of 18.1% in CYQH and 19.4% in HHZ at D3. Similarly, Gs and Tr were markedly improved: at D3, Gs rose by 23.0% and Tr by 18.5% in CYQH, while in HHZ, Gs rose by 41.0% and Tr by 27.0%. Notably, Ci values were lower under the SP treatment compared to the S treatment, suggesting that Pro-Ca potentially mitigated metabolic limitations by enhancing carboxylation efficiency and Rubisco activity. During recovery (FD4), plants under the S treatment still exhibited significantly lower Pn, Gs, and Tr values, and higher Ci, in comparison to CK, indicating persistent photosynthetic dysfunction. In contrast, the SP group showed near-complete recovery of all parameters to CK levels, emphasizing the role of exogenous Pro-Ca in restoring photosynthetic function and mitigating both stomatal and non-stomatal limitations under salt stress.

Salt stress (S treatment) significantly inhibited photosynthetic electron transport and PSII efficiency in both rice cultivars (Fig. 7). The effective quantum yield of PSII (Y(Ⅱ)) also declined, with reductions of 3.29–27.97% in CYQH and 7.37–26.66% in HHZ. This further confirmed the adverse effects of salinity on photochemical efficiency.

Discussion

Excessive salinity impedes various physiological and biochemical processes in plants, thereby restricting growth and development [29]. Changes in biomass accumulation serve as a comprehensive indicator of a plant’s responsiveness to adverse environmental conditions [30]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that high salinity significantly suppresses growth and biomass production in species such as wheat [31], tomato [32], and rapeseed [33]. Both aerial and root tissues are vulnerable to saline conditions, which often leads to substantial reductions in overall biomass [34]. In the present study, salt stress markedly inhibited plant height, root length, stem basal diameter, and leaf area in both the CYQH and HHZ rice varieties. The application of Pro-Ca effectively mitigated salt-induced growth impairment (Tables 1 and 2). Notably, HHZ exhibited more severe damage than CYQH, likely attributable to its higher sensitivity to salinity [35]. Furthermore, biomass accumulation in the Pro-Ca-treated (SP) group exceeded that in the salt-stressed (S) group after the recovery period (FD4), indicating that Pro-Ca not only alleviated salt-induced growth suppression but also enhanced regenerative growth and biomass recovery following stress [36] (Tables 3 and 4). These results also suggest that Pro-Ca may be more effective in alleviating salinity-induced inhibition of dry matter accumulation in salt-sensitive cultivars than in salt-tolerant ones.

This study demonstrated that salt stress triggers a substantial accumulation of ROS, including O2•⁻ and H2O2, in rice leaves. The marked increases in MDA content and electrolyte leakage—both end products of membrane lipid peroxidation—provide direct evidence that excessive ROS inflicts severe damage to cellular membranes, consistent with earlier reports (Fig. 5). Of the two cultivars examined, HHZ exhibited more pronounced ROS accumulation and membrane damage, with higher peak values in MDA and electrolyte leakage than CYQH, emphasizing its weaker salt tolerance. In response to oxidative stress, rice plants activated intrinsic antioxidant defense mechanisms. The activity of antioxidant enzymes SOD, POD, APX, and CAT was significantly elevated under saline conditions (Fig. 2). In contrast, the activity of non-enzymatic antioxidants AsA and GSH decreased, reflecting their consumption as ROS scavengers (Fig. 4). However, under prolonged stress, both enzymatic and non-enzymatic systems exhibited a gradual decline, indicating a partial failure in antioxidant capacity under sustained oxidative pressure. The application of Pro-Ca played a critical protective role under these conditions. Pro-Ca treatment significantly suppressed ROS accumulation, reduced MDA content, and minimized electrolyte leakage, thereby preserving membrane integrity. The protective mechanism involved a synergistic enhancement of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense systems: Pro-Ca not only increased the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, POD, APX, and CAT, but also maintained AsA and GSH pools by upregulating central enzymes in the AsA–GSH cycle, such as GR, DHAR, and MDHAR. These actions facilitated the efficient recycling of antioxidants, confirming that Pro-Ca primarily mitigates oxidative damage by reinforcing the ROS-scavenging pathway. Furthermore, the efficacy of Pro-Ca was cultivar-dependent. Although beneficial to both genotypes, its effect was more pronounced in HHZ—the more salt-sensitive cultivar—which exhibited greater improvements in the activity of certain antioxidants and stress recovery metrics. This suggests that Pro-Ca confers stronger protection in genotypes with higher inherent vulnerability to salinity. Notably, the regulatory influence of Pro-Ca persisted into the recovery phase following stress removal. Physiological parameters in the Pro-Ca–treated (SP) group remained at significantly higher levels compared to the salt-stressed (S) group, demonstrating the sustained role of Pro-Ca in facilitating physiological recovery and maintaining metabolic homeostasis after stress alleviation.

Photosynthesis, a fundamental process that occurs in plant chloroplasts, enables the conversion of solar energy into chemical energy to support plant growth and development [37]. Numerous studies have confirmed that salt stress adversely affects rice photosynthesis through both stomatal and non-stomatal limitations, ultimately leading to yield reduction [38]. In this study, the impact of salt stress and exogenous Pro-Ca application was evaluated by measuring key gas exchange parameters, including Pn, Gs, Ci, and Tr (Fig. 6). The results revealed that salt stress significantly reduced all four parameters, while foliar spraying of Pro-Ca under stress conditions markedly alleviated this reduction. The notable increase in Gs following Pro-Ca treatment suggests a mitigation of suppressed CO2 diffusion from the atmosphere into the leaf interior [39], thereby enhancing carbon availability for photosynthesis. The concomitant rise in Pn conld be attributed to improved stomatal opening and increased intracellular CO2 concentration.

In addition to stomatal limitations, non-stomatal factors also contributed to the decline in photosynthesis under saline conditions. These included excessive Na⁺ accumulation, decreased K⁺ content, reduced photosynthetic pigment levels, disruption of chloroplast ultrastructure, and inhibition of key photosynthetic enzymes [40]. During the recovery period (FD4), although photosynthetic parameters in the salt-stressed (S) group exhibited partial recovery, they remained significantly lower than those of the control (CK), indicating persistent stress-induced damage. In contrast, the Pro-Ca–treated (SP) group not only maintained higher Pn, Gs, Ci, and Tr values during the stress period (D1–D4) but also recovered more rapidly, reaching levels comparable to CK by FD4. These results provide strong evidence that Pro-Ca effectively protects the photosynthetic apparatus, maintains high photosynthetic efficiency, and facilitates post-stress recovery. Furthermore, the salt-sensitive cultivar HHZ exhibited more pronounced reductions in photosynthetic gas exchange under salt stress compared to the tolerant cultivar CYQH. Importantly, the Pro-Ca application led to greater improvements in HHZ than CYQH, indicating that the regulatory effect of Pro-Ca on photosynthesis is genotype-dependent and more substantial in salt-sensitive genotypes.

Compared to gas exchange parameters, chlorophyll (Chl) fluorescence parameters provide a more direct reflection of light energy absorption and utilization by PSII, offering precise insights into the location and degree of stress-induced damage within the photosynthetic apparatus. As non-invasive indicators, these parameters are highly sensitive for evaluating photosynthetic efficiency and PSII activity under adverse conditions. In this study, prolonged salt stress (D1–D4) resulted in a significant decline in ETR, Fv/Fm, and Y(Ⅱ) values in both CTGH and HHZ seedlings (Fig. 7). These results indicate that high salinity severely impaired the PSII reaction center, inhibited photosynthetic electron transport, and reduced light energy conversion efficiency [41]. The inhibitory effects persisted into the recovery phase (FD4) following stress removal. However, the foliar application of exogenous Pro-Ca under salt stress (SP treatment) markedly alleviated the suppression of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters. Throughout both the stress and recovery periods, ETR, Fv/Fm, and Y(Ⅱ) values in the SP-treated plants remained significantly higher than those under salt stress alone (S treatment). This demonstrated that Pro-Ca effectively preserved the structural and functional integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus, thereby sustaining high photosynthetic energy use efficiency. Previous studies have shown that Pro-Ca enhances photosynthetic performance under stress by regulating the expression of photosynthetic genes, maintaining chlorophyll content, and reinforcing the antioxidant system [39, 42]. The improved chlorophyll fluorescence parameters observed in both cultivars, particularly in the salt-sensitive HHZ, further support the role of Pro-Ca in mitigating salt-induced photoinhibition and facilitating functional recovery of PSII.

Conclusions

This study aimed to elucidate the multifaceted adverse effects of salt stress—simulating seawater intrusion—on rice seedling growth and to evaluate the efficacy of exogenous prohexadione calcium (Pro-Ca) as a strategy for enhancing salt tolerance. The results demonstrated that salt stress markedly suppressed rice morphological development and biomass accumulation, with leaf area being the most severely affected. Photosynthetically, salt stress not only limited CO₂ uptake through stomatal closure but also significantly reduced the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) by impairing the PSII reaction center and disrupting photosynthetic electron transport. Concurrently, salt stress triggered a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the notable increases in malondialdehyde (MDA) content and electrolyte leakage provided clear evidence of membrane lipid peroxidation and cellular damage. The Pro-Ca application enhanced salt tolerance through the synergistic regulation of multiple physiological pathways. Its protective mechanisms were multifaceted: it effectively scavenged ROS and mitigated oxidative damage by reinforcing the antioxidant system—elevating the activity of key enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and catalase (CAT), and sustaining the efficient operation of the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle. Simultaneously, Pro-Ca preserved the structural and functional integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus, maintaining high PSII activity (as reflected in Fv/Fm and Y(II)), electron transport efficiency (ETR), and gas exchange parameters (Pn and Gs). During the recovery period following stress removal, plants treated with Pro-Ca exhibited faster and more complete recovery across morphological, photosynthetic, and antioxidant parameters compared to those under salt stress alone (Fig. 8). Furthermore, the salt-tolerant cultivar Chaoyou 1000 demonstrated inherently stronger salinity resistance, whereas Pro-Ca exerted more pronounced protective and restorative effects in the salt-sensitive Huanghuazhan, highlighting its genotype-specific efficacy. The findings of this study provide important insights into the physiological responses of rice to dynamic salt stress. They provide theoretical and practical support for the application of plant growth regulators, such as Pro-Ca, to enhance salt tolerance and stabilize yield in coastal rice cultivation systems.
