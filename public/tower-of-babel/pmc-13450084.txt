Combined ascending aortic and hemi-arch replacement with coronary bypass in a young woman with systemic lupus erythematosus and prior inferior STEMI
Authors: Moath Hattab, Mohammed Abufara, Fadi Abualhommos, Nizar Awwad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) confers markedly increased cardiovascular risk, including premature coronary artery disease and rare but high-risk aortic aneurysms. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is associated with markedly increased cardiovascular risk, including premature coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction (MI), and, less commonly, aortic aneurysm and aortitis. Young women with SLE have a several-fold higher risk of MI compared with age-matched controls, driven by chronic inflammation, steroid exposure, and traditional risk factors. Aortic pathology in SLE, including non-dissecting thoracic aneurysms and lupus aortitis, has been described but remains rare and carries substantial risk of rupture or dissection if not treated [1].

Current aortic disease guidelines recommend elective repair of sporadic ascending aortic aneurysms at diameters around 5.0–5.5 cm, with lower thresholds in heritable aortopathies or when additional risk factors exist. However, there is little specific guidance for patients with SLE who simultaneously have advanced CAD and require complex combined cardiac surgery [2, 3].

We report a 47-year-old woman with longstanding SLE, prior inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and right coronary artery (RCA) stenting, who presented with unstable angina and was found to have a large ascending aortic/arch aneurysm and significant in-stent restenosis (ISR). She underwent combined supracoronary ascending aortic and hemi-arch replacement with saphenous vein bypass to the posterior descending artery (PDA).

The distinctive feature of this case is not simply thoracic aortic aneurysmal disease in a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus, but the coexistence of a surgically significant ascending/proximal arch aneurysm with symptomatic coronary in-stent restenosis requiring revascularization, successfully managed with a single-stage supracoronary ascending aortic and hemi-arch replacement together with coronary artery bypass grafting.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old woman with SLE (with arthritis, lung involvement, and lupus cerebritis) and prior inferior STEMI in 2023 treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and stenting of the proximal and mid RCA presented to a regional hospital with 2 days of new epigastric and retrosternal chest pain. The pain was described as heaviness and burning, 6/10 in intensity, radiating retrosternally and similar to her prior MI symptoms. It was associated with exertional dyspnea, worse on walking uphill, and orthopnea. At presentation, there was no clear evidence of a major systemic lupus flare. Clinically, she had no malar rash, oral ulcers, nephritic symptoms, or new neurologic manifestations. Her lupus was considered clinically quiescent to mildly active, with chronic musculoskeletal symptoms but no overt end-organ flare. Laboratory evaluation showed anti-dsDNA positivity at low titer, complement levels that were within or near the lower limit of normal (C3 88 mg/dL, C4 16 mg/dL), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 34 mm/h, and C-reactive protein (CRP) 6 mg/L. These findings did not support severe active inflammatory lupus at the time of surgery.

Her baseline functional capacity had been chronically limited by recurrent arthritis and repeated respiratory exacerbations, but she reported a clear recent deterioration. These chest infections consisted of approximately 3–4 episodes per year over the preceding years, characterized by cough, increased sputum production, and dyspnea, mostly treated empirically as lower respiratory tract infections in the outpatient setting. Given the CT findings of mild right middle lobe bronchiectatic change and the absence of consolidation at current presentation, these episodes were considered more likely related to chronic airway disease with intermittent infective exacerbations rather than acute lupus pneumonitis. Long-term immunosuppressive therapy may also have contributed to susceptibility to recurrent respiratory infections. Past medical history was negative for known hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, or prior documented hyperlipidemia before her coronary event, although she was receiving lipid-lowering therapy after her prior STEMI. She had no smoking history. Her body mass index was 27 kg/m². Family history was notable for premature ischemic heart disease in a first-degree relative. She was single, a non-smoker, and lived with her family.

Her chronic medications included aspirin 100 mg daily, clopidogrel 75 mg daily, ezetimibe 10 mg daily and atorvastatin 40 mg daily, hydroxychloroquine 200 mg daily, prednisolone 10 mg daily, mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice daily, colchicine 0.5 mg twice daily, proton-pump inhibitor therapy, calcium supplements, and alfacalcidol.

In preparation for major cardiac surgery and in coordination with rheumatology, prednisolone was stopped pre-operatively and replaced with stress-dose hydrocortisone 50 mg IV three times daily, with the plan to transition back to her baseline steroid regimen postoperatively. At the referring hospital, vital signs were stable. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with no new ischemic changes. Serial troponins were negative. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function with prior inferior wall involvement and an ascending aortic diameter of approximately 4.7 cm. In view of recurrent chest pain (resembling ischemic type) and a high cardiovascular risk, she was transferred to our tertiary center as unstable angina for coronary angiography and for further evaluation.

Coronary angiography showed a normal left main coronary artery (LMCA). The left anterior descending (LAD) artery had no significant obstructive disease. The left circumflex artery (LCX) had an approximately 50% mid-segment stenosis, which was not considered hemodynamically significant and was managed medically during the current admission. The RCA had prior proximal and mid stents but demonstrated critical mid-segment in-stent restenosis (> 95%) in the region supplying the posterior descending artery (PDA), which was considered the clinically relevant lesion. Angiography estimated the ascending aorta diameter at 55 mm (Fig. 1).

Contrast-enhanced CT angiography of the aorta confirmed a fusiform aneurysm of the ascending aorta and proximal arch measuring up to 5.3 cm in maximal diameter, consistent with an ascending aortic aneurysm. The descending thoracic and abdominal aorta were normal in caliber, with only mild intimal thickening and heavy calcifications and no dissection or significant branch vessel stenosis. The lungs showed bilateral atelectatic changes and mild bronchiectasis in the right middle lobe, with a few sub-centimeter pulmonary nodules, but no consolidation or mass. Abdominal organs were unremarkable (Fig. 2).

Given her age, SLE, large ascending/arch aneurysm, and significant RCA ISR in the setting of recurrent angina, the multidisciplinary heart team recommended elective combined surgical repair.

Management

After optimization of medical therapy and perioperative planning with rheumatology, including adjustment of her immunosuppressive regimen (discontinuation of mycophenolate mofetil two months before surgery, continuation of hydroxychloroquine, and replacement of oral prednisolone with intravenous hydrocortisone 50 mg every 8 h for perioperative steroid coverage), the patient was scheduled for combined ascending aortic/hemi-arch replacement and CABG. Chronic antiplatelet and immunosuppressive therapy were carefully managed perioperatively according to institutional protocol.

Under general anesthesia, a median sternotomy was performed. The pericardium was opened in an inverted T fashion and suspended. Following systemic heparinization, cardiopulmonary bypass was initially established by central arterial cannulation through the ascending aorta and venous drainage via a right atrial two-stage cannula. The patient was cooled to 24 °C, the aorta was cross-clamped, and myocardial protection was achieved with cold cardioplegia delivered into the aortic root. A supracoronary replacement of the ascending aorta was performed using a 30-mm prosthetic graft reinforced with two Teflon strips and biologic glue. Hemi-arch replacement was then undertaken under hypothermic circulatory arrest with retrograde cerebral perfusion. The supra-aortic arch vessels were not individually looped or clamped. After completion of the distal arch anastomosis, arterial inflow was re-established through the interposition graft, which was cannulated directly using a 3 − 0 polypropylene purse-string suture, and cardiopulmonary bypass was resumed. Peripheral arterial cannulation was not used because preoperative imaging demonstrated heavy calcification of the peripheral arteries and descending aorta, raising concern for complications such as dissection or embolic showering from atherosclerotic and calcific debris; therefore, central arterial cannulation was preferred.

The great saphenous vein was harvested and anastomosed end-to-side to the PDA using 7 − 0 polypropylene. The proximal anastomosis to the neo-ascending aorta graft was fashioned with 5 − 0 polypropylene on the cross-clamped graft. Following deairing, the aortic cross-clamp was removed and the heart resumed sinus rhythm spontaneously.

The patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass on low-dose inotropic support. Protamine was administered, meticulous hemostasis was achieved, and chest drains were placed in the right pleural and retrosternal spaces. The sternum was closed in standard fashion. She was transferred intubated to the cardiac intensive care unit in a hemodynamically stable condition. The postoperative course was uncomplicated and recovery was uneventful. Postoperative CT thorax performed on postoperative day 6 and transthoracic echocardiography performed on postoperative day 7 demonstrated satisfactory reconstruction of the ascending aorta and arch with no residual aneurysmal segments and preserved left ventricular systolic and valvular function (Fig. 3). Histopathologic examination of the resected aortic tissue was not available for review. Therefore, it was not possible to definitively determine whether the aneurysm reflected active inflammatory aortitis related to SLE, chronic immune-mediated vascular injury, or predominantly degenerative medial change. Hydrocortisone was tapered postoperatively, and she was transitioned back to her maintenance prednisolone regimen prior to discharge, while hydroxychloroquine was continued and mycophenolate mofetil remained on hold pending outpatient rheumatology reassessment. She was discharged on postoperative day 10 on guideline-directed dual antiplatelet therapy and high-intensity statin therapy.

Discussion

This case illustrates the complex interaction between autoimmune disease, coronary atherosclerosis, and aortic pathology in a relatively young woman with SLE. Patients with SLE have a two-to-five-fold increased risk of cardiovascular events and MI compared with the general population, this is due to chronic systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, corticosteroid exposure, and other risk factors. SLE-associated aortic aneurysm remains rare in the literature, with most evidence limited to case reports and small pooled reviews, while cases requiring combined aortic replacement and concomitant coronary bypass appear to be even less commonly reported. To our knowledge, reports describing combined ascending/hemi-arch replacement with concomitant CABG in a relatively young woman with longstanding SLE are exceptionally uncommon. The patient in our case had already suffered from an inferior STEMI requiring RCA stenting in her mid-forties, consistent with this accelerated atherosclerotic phenotype [1, 2].

Aortic aneurysm and aortitis are increasingly recognized but rare manifestations of SLE. While SLE vasculitis classically targets small and medium‑sized vessels, including cutaneous, renal, cerebral, and coronary microcirculation, large‑vessel aortic involvement carries substantially greater risk of dissection and rupture. Crucially, SLE‑associated aortic aneurysms differ from conventional degenerative aneurysms: rather than the intimal thickening and atheromatous plaque deposition of atherosclerotic disease, SLE‑related aortopathy is characterized by immune‑mediated medial cystic degeneration, smooth muscle cell loss, elastic lamellae disruption, and perivascular lymphocytic infiltrates in the adventitia. Although aortic histopathology was unavailable in our case, the isolated ascending and proximal arch involvement in a young woman with longstanding SLE, in the absence of hypertension or a heritable connective tissue disorder, is highly suggestive of an inflammatory‑degenerative phenotype [3–6].

Our patient’s aneurysm size (5.3–5.5 cm) falls within the range where current aortic guidelines support elective repair of ascending aneurysms. The threshold for intervention may be lower in patients with additional risk factors such as heritable aortopathy or systemic inflammatory disease, given reports suggesting rupture at smaller diameters in inflammatory aortopathy. In this patient, a single-stage strategy offered the advantage of treating both the surgically significant ascending/proximal arch aneurysm and the clinically relevant coronary lesion during one operation, thereby avoiding interval risk of recurrent ischemia or aortic complication while awaiting a second procedure. The trade-offs of this approach include greater technical complexity, longer operative time, and potentially higher perioperative physiologic stress. By contrast, a staged strategy may reduce the complexity of each individual procedure but would expose the patient to cumulative procedural risk, another hospitalization, and a delay in definitive treatment. In a symptomatic SLE patient already requiring surgical revascularization for RCA ISR, the decision to proceed with combined supracoronary ascending and hemi-arch replacement plus CABG was appropriate and avoided the need for staged procedures [7, 8].

This case also underlines the importance of comprehensive imaging. Initial transthoracic echocardiography raised suspicion of ascending aortic dilatation, but CT angiography provided precise measurements, showed arch involvement, and excluded concomitant descending or abdominal pathology, and such information is crucial for surgical planning. Coronary angiography demonstrated tight ISR in the RCA, justifying a surgical graft to the PDA in preference to repeat PCI in the setting of planned sternotomy and aortic replacement.

Another important consideration is the perioperative management of immunosuppression in SLE. In this case, mycophenolate mofetil was appropriately stopped two months preoperatively, hydroxychloroquine was continued, and stress-dose hydrocortisone was used in place of prednisolone, balancing the risk of infection and preventing adrenal insufficiency and lupus flare at the same time. A coordinated perioperative plan with rheumatology is essential; this case emphasizes that complex cardiac surgery can be performed safely in appropriately optimized SLE patients [9].

The main clinical lesson from this case is not only the coexistence of AAoA and CAD in a patient with SLE, but the need to recognize SLE as a systemic vasculopathic state capable of affecting both the coronary and aortic circulation at a relatively young age, even in the absence of multiple cardiovascular risk factors or a lupus flare. Our case highlights the importance of having a low threshold for multimodality cardiovascular assessment in SLE patients with chest pain or unexplained exertional symptoms. In clinical practice, this should include coronary risk assessment, baseline and interval echocardiographic evaluation when aortic dilation is suspected, and CT or MRI imaging when symptoms, examination, or echocardiography raise concern for thoracic aortic disease. For patients with established aortic dilation, surveillance intervals should be individualized according to aortic diameter and growth rate. In a patient such as ours, repeat imaging at 6 months after surgery and annually thereafter would be reasonable, together with strict blood pressure control, lipid optimization, and rheumatologic follow-up

Conclusion

This case emphasizes that SLE may be associated with concurrent premature coronary artery disease and clinically significant thoracic aortic aneurysm, even in relatively young patients with limited traditional cardiovascular risk factors and no clear severe lupus flare at presentation. Its key clinical implication is that chest pain in SLE should prompt further cardiovascular evaluation beyond acute coronary syndromes alone, particularly when symptoms are recurrent or unexplained. Multimodality imaging was essential in defining both the coronary and aortic pathology and in guiding treatment. This case also demonstrates that single-stage ascending/hemi-arch replacement with concomitant coronary bypass is feasible in selected, carefully optimized SLE patients. After repair, follow-up should include periodic aortic imaging surveillance, aggressive cardiovascular risk-factor modification, and coordinated cardiology-rheumatology follow-up. An important limitation of this report is the absence of histopathologic examination of the resected aortic tissue, which prevents definitive distinction between lupus-associated inflammatory aortopathy and a more typical degenerative aneurysmal process. Nevertheless, the case remains clinically instructive in emphasizing the need to consider large-vessel cardiovascular involvement in selected patients with longstanding SLE.
