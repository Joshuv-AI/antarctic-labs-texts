Almost Commutative Terwilliger Algebras of Group Association Schemes I: Classification
Nicholas L. Bastian, Stephen P. Humphries

Abstract
Terwilliger algebras are a subalgebra of a matrix algebra that are constructed from association schemes over finite sets. In 2010, Rie Tanaka defined what it means for a Terwilliger algebra to be almost commutative. In that paper she gave five equivalent conditions for a Terwilliger algebra to be almost commutative. In this paper, we provide a classification of which groups result in an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra when looking at the group association scheme (the Schur ring generated by the conjugacy classes of the group). In particular, we show that all such groups are either abelian, or Camina groups. Following this classification, we then compute the dimension and non-primary primitive idempotents for each Terwilliger algebra of this form for the first three types of groups whose group association scheme gives an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra. The final case will be considered in a second paper.



Keywords
:
Terwilliger algebra, Camina group, association scheme, group association scheme, Schur rings, centralizer algebra

2
Terwilliger Algebras

In this section we shall discuss some results relating to Terwilliger algebras. For more information about them see
[
46
]
. Let
\Omega
be a finite set. Suppose
A_{0},A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\in M_{|\Omega|}(\mathbb{C})
are
0,1
matrices with rows and columns indexed by the elements of
\Omega
. Let
A^{t}
denote the transpose of the matrix
A
. If additionally

1.

A_{0}=I_{|\Omega|}
;

2.

for all
i=1,2,\dots,d
, we have
A_{i}^{t}=A_{j}
for some
j=1,2,\dots,d
;

3.

for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
we have
A_{i}A_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{ij}^{k}A_{k}
;

4.

none of the
A_{i}
is equal to
0_{|\Omega|}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{d}A_{i}
is the all
1
matrix;

then we say that
\mathcal{A}=(\Omega,\{A_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
is an
association scheme
. We call the matrices
A_{0},A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}
the
adjacency matrices
of the association scheme and the constants
p_{ij}^{k}
are called the
intersection numbers
. If we have
A_{i}A_{j}=A_{j}A_{i}
for all
i,j
, then we say that we have a
commutative association scheme
.

The association schemes that we will be interested in come from a Schur ring, which is an example of an association scheme and which we now define. Let
G
be a group and
R
be a commutative ring. For any
C\subseteq G
, we let
\overline{C}=\sum_{g\in C}g\in R[G]
. We call this a
simple quantity
. Given any
C\subseteq G
, we let
C^{*}=\{g^{-1}\colon g\in G\}
.

Now given a group
G
and ring
R
, let
\mathcal{P}
be a partition of
G
of finite support, that is, if
C\in\mathcal{P}
then
|C|<\infty
. We say that
\mathfrak{S}=\text{Span}_{R}\{\overline{C}|C\in\mathcal{P}\}
is a
Schur ring
if:

1.

\{e\}\in\mathcal{P};

2.

if
C\in\mathcal{P}
, then
C^{*}\in\mathcal{P};

3.

for all
C,D\in\mathcal{P}
,

\overline{C}\cdot\overline{D}=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda_{CDE}\overline{E}
,
where all but finitely many
\lambda_{CDE}=0
.

Schur rings have been studied on their own extensively; see
[
5
,
33
,
54
]
. They give rise to association schemes in the following way. Given a Schur ring
\mathfrak{S}
over the finite group
G
with partition set
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{0}=\{e\},P_{1},P_{2},\cdots,P_{d}\}
, we define
|G|\times|G|
matrices
A_{i}
,
0\leq i\leq d
, by

(A_{i})_{xy}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{ if }yx^{-1}\in P_{i}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

With this construction
(G,\{A_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
is an association scheme. If the Schur ring is commutative then the association scheme is commutative. The structure constants
\lambda_{ijk}
coming from
\overline{P_{i}}\cdot\overline{P_{j}}=\sum_{P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda_{ijk}\overline{P_{k}}
are the intersection numbers of the association scheme. In particular, partitioning
G
by the conjugacy classes of
G
, we get a Schur ring. This in turn gives rise to a commutative association scheme, called the
group association scheme
.

Returning to arbitrary association schemes, we notice that
\mathfrak{A}=\text{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{A_{0},A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\}
is an algebra called the
Bose-Mesner algebra
of the association scheme.

We let
E_{0},E_{1},\dots,E_{d}
be the primitive idempotents of
\mathfrak{A}
. As
\mathfrak{A}
is closed under component-wise multiplication (Hadamard product, denoted
\circ
), we have
E_{i}\circ E_{j}\in\mathfrak{A}
. Then there are complex numbers
q_{ij}^{k}
, called the
Krein parameters
, such that

E_{i}\circ E_{j}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{k=0}^{d}q_{ij}^{k}E_{k}.

We further define diagonal matrices
E_{i}^{*}(x)

with base point
x\in\Omega
by the rule

(E_{i}^{*}(x))_{yy}=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&(x,y)\in R_{i}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}

Essentially what we are doing here is taking the
x
th row of
A_{i}
and putting it along the main diagonal to form
E_{i}^{*}(x)
. We call
\mathfrak{A}^{*}(x)=\text{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{0}^{*}(x),E_{1}^{*}(x),\cdots E_{d}^{*}(x)\}
the
dual Bose-Mesner Algebra
of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to
x
.

Now we define the
Terwilliger algebra
with base point
x
of the association scheme
\mathcal{A}=(\Omega,\{A_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
to be the subalgebra of
M_{|\Omega|}(\mathbb{C})
generated by
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{*}(x)
. We denote this by
T(x)
. For the group association scheme we always take the base point to be the identity
e
in the group. In this case we let
T(G)
denote the Terwilliger algebra for the group association scheme.

Let

T_{0}(x)=\text{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{i}^{*}(x)A_{j}E_{k}^{*}(x)\colon 0\leq i,j,k\leq d\}.

From
[
2
]
we have
\dim T_{0}(x)=|\{(i,j,k)\colon p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0\}|
. We shall say that a Terwilliger algebra is
triply regular
if
T(x)=T_{0}(x)
.

If
|\Omega|>1
, then the Terwilliger algebra
T(x)
is non-commutative and semi-simple for all
x\in\Omega

[
46
]
. As such, it has a Wedderburn decomposition. Given an association scheme
(\Omega,\{A_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
, the Terwilliger algebra
T(x)
for this association scheme always has an irreducible ideal of dimension
(d+1)^{2}
; see
[
39
]
. This Wedderburn component is called the
primary component
and will be denoted by
V
.

Closely related to the Wedderburn decomposition of the Terwilliger algebra are those
T(x)-
modules that are irreducible when
T(x)
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{|\Omega|}
by left multiplication. We note that with this action
\mathbb{C}^{|\Omega|}
decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible
T(x)-
modules. Letting
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\Omega|}
have
1
in position
x
and
0
elsewhere, it is shown in
[
46
]
that
\mathfrak{A}\hat{x}
is an irreducible
T(x)-
module of dimension
d+1
. We call this irreducible module the
primary module
.

We now say a Terwilliger algebra
T(x)
is
almost commutative
if every non-primary irreducible
T(x)-
module is
1-
dimensional. There is a classification of such Terwilliger algebras when the underlying association scheme is commutative:

Theorem 2.1

(Tanaka,
[
45
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(\Omega,\{R_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq d})
be a commutative association scheme. Let
T(x)
be the Terwilliger algebra of
\mathcal{A}
for some
x\in\Omega
. The following are equivalent:

1.

Every non-primary irreducible
T(x)-
module is
1-
dimensional for some
x\in\Omega
.

2.

Every non-primary irreducible
T(x)-
module is
1-
dimensional for all
x\in\Omega
.

3.

The intersection numbers of
\mathcal{A}
have the property that for all distinct
h,i
there is exactly one
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0

(0\leq h,i,j\leq d)
.

4.

The Krein parameters of
\mathcal{A}
have the property that for all distinct
h,i
there is exactly one
j
such that
q_{ij}^{h}\neq 0

(0\leq h,i,j\leq d)
.

5.

\mathcal{A}
is a wreath product of association schemes
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
where each
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is either a
1-
class association scheme or the group scheme of a finite abelian group.

Moreover, a Terwilliger algebra satisfying these equivalent conditions is triply regular.

For the definition of the wreath product of association schemes see
[
22
]
. We note that a Terwilliger algebra for a commutative association scheme is almost commutative if it satisfies the five equivalent conditions in Theorem
2.1
.
The focus of this paper is to determine exactly for which groups the Terwilliger algebra of the group association scheme is almost commutative. The main result is Theorem
4.1
. As all group association schemes are commutative, we note that Theorem
2.1
applies to the Terwilliger algebras under consideration.

Camina Groups

Camina groups were first studied by Camina in
[
8
]
as a generalization of Frobenius groups and extra special groups. Results on Camina groups can be found in the survey
[
29
]
.

A group
G
is a
Camina group
if every conjugacy class of
G
outside of
G^{\prime}
is a coset of
G^{\prime}
in
G
. An important result here is:

Theorem 3.1

(Dark and Scoppola,
[
15
]
)
.

Let
G
be a Camina group. Then one of the following is true:

•

G
is a Frobenius group whose Frobenius complement is cyclic.

•

G
is a Frobenius group whose Frobenius complement is
Q_{8}
, the quaternion group of order
8
.

•

G
is a
p-
group for some prime
p
of nilpotency class
2
or
3
.

We will also need:

Proposition 3.1

(Macdonald,
[
34
]
)
.

Let
G
be a Camina
p-
group of nilpotency class
3
. Then the lower central series of
G
is
\{e\}\leq Z(G)\leq G^{\prime}\leq G
. Furthermore,
[G\colon G^{\prime}]=p^{2n}
and
[G^{\prime}\colon Z(G)]=p^{n}
for some even integer
n
. Additionally,
G/G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime}/Z(G)
are elementary abelian groups.

Proposition 3.2

(Macdonald,
[
34
]
)
.

Let
G
be a Camina p-group. Then
Z(G)
is an elementary abelian group.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let
G
be a non-abelian Camina group. Then
Z(G)\leq G^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
29
]
)
.

Suppose that
G
is a Camina group and
N\trianglelefteq G^{\prime}
. Then
G/N
is a Camina group.

Another result that will be of particular interest to us is:

Proposition 3.4

(Macdonald, Theorem 5.2(i),
[
34
]
)
.

Let
G
be a Camina
p-
group. Let
\gamma_{2}(G)=[G,G]
and
\gamma_{3}(G)=[\gamma_{2}(G),G]=Z(G)
be the second and third terms in the lower central series of
G
. Then

x^{G}=x\gamma_{2}(G)\text{ if }x\in G\setminus\gamma_{2}(G),

x^{G}=x\gamma_{3}(G)\text{ if }x\in\gamma_{2}(G)\setminus\gamma_{3}(G),

x^{G}=\{x\}\text{ if }x\in\gamma_{3}(G).

A result for Camina groups that are also Frobenius groups that we use is:

Theorem 3.2

(Cangelmi and Muktibodh,
[
9
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then,
G
is a Camina group and
G^{\prime}
is the union of two conjugacy classes if and only if either
G
is a Frobenius group with Frobenius kernel
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{r}
and Frobenius complement
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r}-1}
, or
G
is an extra-special
2-
group.

Generalizing a Camina group we have for
H\leq G
, the pair
(G,H)
is a
Camina pair
if for all
g\in G\setminus H
,
g
is conjugate to every element of
gH
. Note
(G,G^{\prime})
is a Camina pair if and only if
G
is a Camina group. More information on Camina pairs can be found in
[
29
]
.

Our interest in Camina pairs stems from the following result about equivalent conditions to
(G,H)
being a Camina pair. This result was proven over the course of several papers
[
8
,
14
,
17
,
56
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1<K\triangleleft G
. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

(G,K)
is a Camina pair.

2.

If
x\in G\setminus K
, then
|C_{G}(x)|=|C_{G/K}(xK)|
.

3.

If
xK
and
yK
are conjugate and nontrivial in
G/K
, then
x
is conjugate to
y
in
G
.

4.

If
C_{1}=\{e\},C_{2},\cdots,C_{m}
are the conjugacy classes of
G
contained in
K
and
C_{m+1},\cdots,C_{n}
are the conjugacy classes of
G
outside
K
, then
C_{i}C_{j}=C_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq m
and
m+1\leq j\leq n
.

4
Almost Commutative Terwilliger Algebras for Group Association Schemes

For the remainder of this paper we let
T(G)
to be the Terwilliger algebra for the group association scheme of the group
G
with the base point being the identity of the group. Let
C_{0}=\{e\},C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{d}
be the conjugacy classes of
G
. We shall show the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then
T(G)
is almost commutative if and only if
G
is isomorphic to one of the following groups

•

A finite abelian group;

•

The group
(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
;

•

(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{r}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r-1}}
, for some prime
p
and
r>0
;

•

A non-abelian Camina
p-
group, for some prime
p
.

We begin by proving that for each of the groups in Theorem
4.1
,
T(G)
is an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then
T(G)
is almost commutative.

Proof.

Given any
C_{i}=\{x\}
and
C_{h}=\{y\}
with
x\neq y
we have that,
C_{j}=\{x^{-1}y\}
satisfies
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=y=\overline{C_{h}}
. So only
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
for this specific value of
j
. Hence, by Theorem
2.1
(3),
T(G)
is almost commutative.
∎

Next we consider
(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
. As this is a single group, we can directly compute its conjugacy classes to show the result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
. Then
T(G)
is almost commutative.

Proof.

A presentation of the group
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}=\langle x,y\rangle\rtimes\langle a,b,c\rangle
is

\langle a,b,c,x,y\colon a^{2}=b^{2}=c,c^{2}=e,x^{3}=y^{3}=e,b^{a}=bc,x^{a}=xy^{2},x^{b}=y,x^{c}=x^{2},y^{a}=x^{2}y^{2},y^{b}=x^{2},y^{c}=y^{2}\rangle.

Through direct computation one can find that the six conjugacy classes of
G
are

C_{0}=\{e\},C_{1}=\{cy^{2},cx^{2},cx,cx^{2}y,c,cxy,cx^{2}y^{2},cy,cxy^{2}\},C_{2}=\{xy,x^{2}y^{2},y,xy^{2},y^{2},x^{2},x,x^{2}y\},

C_{3}=\{aby,abxy^{2},abcx^{2},ab,abxy,abx^{2}y^{2},abcy^{2},abx,abx^{2}y,abcy,abx^{2},abcxy^{2},abc,abcxy,abcx^{2}y^{2},aby^{2},abcx,abcx^{2}y\},

C_{4}=\{acx^{2},a,axy,ax^{2}y^{2},ax,acy^{2},ax^{2}y,acy,ax^{2},acxy^{2},ac,acxy,acx^{2}y^{2},ay^{2},acx,acx^{2}y,ay,axy^{2}\},

C_{5}=\{bxy^{2},bcx^{2},b,bxy,bx^{2}y^{2},bcy^{2},bx,bx^{2}y,bcy,bx^{2},bcxy^{2},bc,bcxy,bcx^{2}y^{2},by^{2},bcx,bcx^{2}y,by\}.

We shall now compute the products
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}
. Note that
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{C_{j}}\cdot\overline{C_{i}}
, so it will suffice to compute the products for
i\leq j
. Doing so we find that
\overline{C_{0}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{C_{j}}
for
0\leq j\leq 5
and that

\overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{1}}=9\overline{C_{0}}+9\overline{C_{2}},\ \overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{2}}=8\overline{C_{1}},\ \overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{3}}=9\overline{C_{3}},\ \overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{4}}=9\overline{C_{4}},\ \overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{5}}=9\overline{C_{5}},\ \overline{C_{2}}\cdot\overline{C_{2}}=8\overline{C_{0}}+7\overline{C_{2}},

\overline{C_{2}}\cdot\overline{C_{3}}=8\overline{C_{3}},\ \overline{C_{2}}\cdot\overline{C_{4}}=8\overline{C_{4}},\ \overline{C_{2}}\cdot\overline{C_{5}}=8\overline{C_{5}},\ \overline{C_{3}}\cdot\overline{C_{3}}=18\overline{C_{0}}+18\overline{C_{1}}+18\overline{C_{2}},\ \overline{C_{3}}\cdot\overline{C_{4}}=18\overline{C_{5}},\ \overline{C_{3}}\cdot\overline{C_{5}}=18\overline{C_{4}},

\overline{C_{4}}\cdot\overline{C_{4}}=18\overline{C_{0}}+18\overline{C_{1}}+18\overline{C_{2}},\ \overline{C_{4}}\cdot\overline{C_{5}}=18\overline{C_{3}},\ \overline{C_{5}}\cdot\overline{C_{5}}=18\overline{C_{0}}+18\overline{C_{1}}+18\overline{C_{2}}.

Looking at these products we see that given
C_{i}
and
C_{h}
with
C_{i}\neq C_{h}
there exists a unique
C_{j}
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. Therefore,
T(G)
is almost commutative by Theorem
2.1
(3).
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a Camina group in which
G^{\prime}
is the union of two conjugacy classes. Then
T(G)
is almost commutative.

Proof.

Here
G^{\prime}=C_{0}\cup C_{1}
with
C_{1}=G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
. We show given distinct
i
and
h
there exists a unique
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. We do so by splitting into cases based on if
C_{i}\subseteq G^{\prime}
or not.

Case 1:

C_{i}\subseteq G^{\prime}
. If
C_{i}=\{e\}
, then
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{C_{j}}
. In this case
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
if and only if
C_{j}=C_{h}
. Hence, there is a unique
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
in this case. If
C_{i}=G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
we consider two subcases.

Subcase 1.1:
C_{h}\subseteq G^{\prime}
. Then as
C_{h}\neq C_{i}=G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
we have
C_{h}=\{e\}
. For any
C_{j}
not in
G^{\prime}
we have by Lemma
3.2
that
C_{i}C_{j}=C_{j}
. So
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=|C_{i}|\overline{C_{j}}
. Then in this case as
C_{j}\neq C_{h}
we have
p_{ij}^{h}=0
. Thus for
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
either
C_{j}=\{e\}
or
C_{j}=G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
. Since
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot e=\overline{C_{i}}\neq\overline{C_{h}}
we have
p_{ij}^{h}=0
in this case. The only class we have left to consider is
C_{j}=G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
. In this case
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=(\overline{G^{\prime}}-e)(\overline{G^{\prime}}-e)=|G^{\prime}|\overline{G^{\prime}}-2\overline{G^{\prime}}+e=(|G^{\prime}|-2)\overline{G^{\prime}}+e
. Thus,
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
, and there is exactly one
j
such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
.

Subcase 1.2:
C_{h}\subseteq G\setminus G^{\prime}
. Then for any
C_{j}\subseteq G^{\prime}
we have
C_{i}C_{j}\subseteq G^{\prime}
as
C_{i},C_{j}\subseteq G^{\prime}
. Then
p_{ij}^{h}=0
in this case. For any
C_{j}\subseteq G\setminus G^{\prime}

. Then for

Case 2:

Subcase 2.1:

. If

\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}
are in
(gk)G^{\prime}
. As
C_{h}\subseteq G^{\prime}
we have
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
if and only if
gkG^{\prime}=G^{\prime}
. In this case

, so

are unique there is a unique

and

.

Subcase 2.2:
C_{h}\subseteq G\setminus G^{\prime}

C_{h}=xG^{\prime}
for

, with

C_{j}\subseteq G^{\prime}
then
C_{i}C_{j}\subseteq gG^{\prime}
and
p_{ij}^{h}=0
. Now if
C_{j}
is outside of
G^{\prime}
,
C_{j}=kG^{\prime}
and
C_{i}C_{j}=gkG^{\prime}
. Hence, all the terms in
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}
are in

. Then

if and only if

if and only if
C_{j}=kG^{\prime}=g^{-1}xG^{\prime}
. Therefore, there is a unique

such that
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
.

Hence,
T(G)
is almost commutative by Theorem
2.1
(3).
∎

At this point, we have proved that for each of the four types of groups

,

is almost commutative. For the converse we need:

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
T(G)
is almost commutative. Then for
x,y\in G
with
x^{G}\neq(y^{-1})^{G}
, we have
x^{G}y^{G}=(xy)^{G}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in G
such that
x^{G}\neq(y^{-1})^{G}
. Let
x^{G}=C_{i}
and
y^{G}=C_{j}
. Then
C_{j}\neq C_{i^{\prime}}
. Since
C_{j}\neq C_{i^{\prime}}
, and
T(G)
is almost commutative, there exists a unique
h
with
p_{i^{\prime}h}^{j}\neq 0
, so
C_{j}\subseteq C_{i^{\prime}}C_{h}
. Then for
z\in C_{j}
, there are
a\in C_{i^{\prime}}
and
b\in C_{h}
such that
z=ab
. Then
b=a^{-1}z
, so
b\in C_{i}C_{j}
. Thus,
C_{h}\subseteq C_{i}C_{j}
.

Now suppose that
C_{m}\subseteq C_{i}C_{j}
. Then for
a\in C_{m}
, there exists a
b\in C_{i}
and
c\in C_{j}
such that
a=bc
. Then
c=b^{-1}a
, so
c\in C_{i^{\prime}}C_{m}
. Thus
C_{j}\subseteq C_{i^{\prime}}C_{m}
. This implies
p_{i^{\prime}m}^{j}\neq 0
. By the uniqueness of
p_{i^{\prime}h}^{j}
, we have
C_{m}=C_{h}
. So
C_{h}
is the only class in
C_{i}C_{j}
. We know that
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\sum_{\ell}p_{ij}^{\ell}\overline{C_{\ell}}
. For all
p_{ij}^{\ell}\neq 0
we have
C_{\ell}\subseteq C_{i}C_{j}
. Since
C_{h}
is the unique class in
C_{i}C_{j}
we must have
C_{i}C_{j}=C_{h}
.

Let us consider what
C_{h}
could be. We know that
x\in C_{i}
and
y\in C_{j}
so
xy\in C_{h}
as
C_{i}C_{j}=C_{h}
. Therefore,
C_{h}=(xy)^{G}
. Thus
x^{G}y^{G}=(xy)^{G}
.
∎

The following result now shows which groups

satisfy the conclusion of Lemma
4.1
.

(Dade and Yadav,
[
16
]
)
.

A finite group

such that

if and only if

is isomorphic to exactly one of the groups in the following list:

A finite abelian group.

•

A non-abelian Camina

•

The group

.

Combining Theorem

and Lemma

we see for a group

, if

is almost commutative, then

5
Wedderburn Decomposition for Almost Commutative Group Association Schemes

Now that we have found exactly which groups have a group association scheme that produces an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra, we are going to find the Wedderburn decomposition of each of these Terwilliger algebras.

For finite abelian groups, we have:

Proposition 5.1

(Bannai and Munemasa,
[
2
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then
\dim T(G)=|G|^{2}
.

We note that the primary component of the Wedderburn decomposition has dimension equal to the number of conjugacy classes squared. For an abelian group this is
|G|^{2}
. Hence, the Wedderburn decomposition of
T(G)
is just
V
, where
V
is the primary component.

For
(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
we have:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
. Then
\dim T(G)=44
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
,
T(G)
is almost commutative. Then by Theorem
2.1
,
T(G)
is triply regular. Therefore,
T(G)=T_{0}(G)
and
\dim T(G)=|\{(i,j,k)\colon p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0\}|
. Within the proof of Theorem
4.2
we computed every product of the form
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}
for
i\leq j
. Then in computing the number of
(i,j,k)
such that
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
we count the number of
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
that we got in the products in the proof of Theorem
4.2
. In this counting, for each
i\neq j
we get two nonzero triples, namely
(i,j,k)
and
(j,i,k)
where
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
. Doing this we find
|\{(i,j,k)\colon p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0\}|=44
, so
\dim T(G)=44
.
∎

We next determine the dimension of
Z(T(G))
, the center of
T(G)
. This is equal to the number of Wedderburn components
T(G)
has. We use the method of
[
1
]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
. Then
\dim Z(T(G))=9
.

Proof.

Since
T(G)
contains all the
E_{i}^{*}
matrices,
Z(T(G))
consists of block diagonal matrices and

Z(T(G))\subseteq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}Z(E_{i}^{*}T(G)E_{i}^{*}).

To find
\dim Z(T(G))
, we explicitly determine a basis for
Z(T(G))
. To do so, we first determine a basis for
Z(E_{i}^{*}T(G)E_{i}^{*})
for each
i=0,1,\cdots,d
. Let
\{b_{j}\}
be the union of these bases. Thus if
y\in Z(T(G))
,
y=\sum c_{j}b_{j}
, for some
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The system of linear equations
\{x_{i}y=yx_{i}\}
, ranging over all elements
x_{i}\in\{E_{i}^{*}A_{j}E_{k}^{*}\colon p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0\}
, the basis of
T(G)=T_{0}(G)
, can then be solved to yield the scalars
c_{j}
and the required basis for
Z(T(G))
. As we are looking at
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
this calculation can be done using Magma
[
7
]
, to find
\dim Z(T(G))=9
.
∎

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{2}\rtimes Q_{8}
. Then the Wedderburn decomposition of
T(G)
is

T(G)\cong V\oplus Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2}\oplus Z_{3}\oplus\cdots\oplus Z_{8}

where each
Z_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 8
is a
1-
dimensional ideal of
T(G)
and
V
is the primary component.

Proof.

Recall that
\dim Z(T(G))
equals the number of irreducible
2-
sided ideals of
T(G)
. Then by Proposition
5.3
,

For Frobenius groups
(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}-1}
, we have:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}-1}
for some prime
p
and
n\geq 1
. Then
\dim T(G)=p^{2n}+p^{n}-1
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.1
,
T(G)
is almost commutative. Then by Theorem
2.1
,
T(G)
is triply regular. Therefore,
T(G)=T_{0}(G)
and
\dim T(G)=|\{(i,j,k)\colon p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0\}|
.

Since
G
is a Camina group, its classes outside of
G^{\prime}
are all nontrivial cosets of
G^{\prime}
. By Theorem
3.2
, the classes of
G
inside
G^{\prime}
are
\{e\}
and
G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
. Note
G^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}
and
G/G^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}-1}
. Then there are
p^{n}-2
classes of
G
outside of
G^{\prime}
. So the number of classes of
G
is
2+(p^{n}-2)=p^{n}
. We count the number of triples by considering cases based the types of classes. Let
C_{0}=\{e\}
and
C_{1}=G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}
.

Case 1:
C_{i}=C_{0}
. Now
\overline{C_{0}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{C_{j}}
for all
j
, thus there are
p^{n}
triples
(0,j,j)
(or
(j,0,j)
) such that
p_{0j}^{j}\neq 0
(or
p_{j0}^{j}\neq 0
), giving a total of
2p^{n}
triples that involve the class
C_{i}=\{e\}
,
p_{ij}^{h}\neq 0
. This double counts the triple
(0,0,0)
. Hence, there are
2p^{n}-1
nonzero triples that involve
C_{0}=\{e\}
.

Case 2:
C_{i}=C_{1}
. We consider
\overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}
, where
j>1
. Then
\overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{(G^{\prime}-e)}\cdot\overline{gG^{\prime}}=|G^{\prime}|\overline{gG^{\prime}}-\overline{gG^{\prime}}=(|G^{\prime}|-1)\overline{gG^{\prime}}=(p^{n}-1)\overline{C_{j}}
. So
(1,j,j)
is a triple with
p_{1j}^{j}\neq 0
. Similarly,
(j,1,j)
has
p_{j1}^{j}\neq 0
. There are
p^{n}-2
possible choices for
C_{j}
, so we have found
2(p^{n}-2)
triples. Now
\overline{C_{1}}\cdot\overline{C_{1}}=\overline{G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}}\cdot\overline{G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}}=|G^{\prime}|\overline{G^{\prime}}-2\overline{G^{\prime}}+\{e\}=(p^{n}-2)\overline{G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}}+(p^{n}-1)\overline{\{e\}}
. Then the triples
(1,1,0)
and
(1,1,1)
also have nonzero
p_{11}^{0}
and
p_{11}^{1}
. Thus, when
C_{1}
is a class in the product we get
2p^{n}-4+2=2p^{n}-2
nonzero triples.

Case 3:

C_{i}=gG^{\prime}
where
g\not\in G^{\prime}
. We may consider
C_{j}=hG^{\prime}
as we have already counted all the classes that are not nontrivial cosets of
G^{\prime}
in
G
. For all
C_{j}\neq g^{-1}G^{\prime}
, we have
C_{i}C_{j}=ghG^{\prime}\neq G^{\prime}
. This implies
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=|G^{\prime}|\overline{ghG^{\prime}}=|G^{\prime}|\overline{C_{k}}
for some conjugacy class
C_{k}
. Thus,
(i,j,k)
is the only triple with our choice of
i,j
such that
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
. With
C_{i}
fixed, we have a total of
p^{n}-2-1
choices for
C_{j}
such that
C_{j}\neq g^{-1}G^{\prime}
. As each choice of
C_{j}
gives a unique
C_{k}
such that
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
we get a total of
p^{n}-3
triples
(i,j,k)
in this case. If
C_{j}=g^{-1}G^{\prime}
, then
\overline{C_{i}}\cdot\overline{C_{j}}=\overline{gG^{\prime}}\cdot\overline{g^{-1}G^{\prime}}=|G^{\prime}|\overline{G^{\prime}}=|G^{\prime}|\overline{C_{0}}+|G^{\prime}|\overline{C_{1}}
. Thus, the triples
(i,j,0)
and
(i,j,1)
have nonzero
p_{ij}^{0}
and
p_{ij}^{1}
in this case. We then have found a total of
p^{n}-1
different
(i,j,k)
such that
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
for our fixed
C_{i}
. We have a total of
p^{n}-2
choices for
C_{i}
, hence there are a total of
(p^{n}-1)(p^{n}-2)
triples
(i,j,k)
such that
p_{ij}^{k}\neq 0
. This gives

\dim(T(G))=2p^{n}-1+2p^{n}-2+(p^{n}-2)(p^{n}-1)=p^{2n}+p^{n}-1.\qed

For the nonprimary Wedderburn components of
T(G)
,

so all the elements of

are first, then all those in

can be thought of as a block matrix where the

of

where

has all
1
’s in the

.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let

From the proof of Corollary
5.2

\{e\},G^{\prime}\setminus\{e\}

.

1

for all

.

We now show that

. For the

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
G=Z_{p}^{n}\rtimes Z_{p^{n}-1}
for some prime
p
and
n\geq 1
. Suppose
C_{0}=\{e\},C_{1},\cdots,C_{p^{n}-1}
are the classes of
G
. Defining

as in Proposition
5.4
, we have that for each

,
\frac{|C_{i}|-1}{|C_{i}|}B_{i}
is a non-primary primitive idempotent of

.

Proof.

We note that direct computation yields for all

,
B_{i}^{2}=(1+\frac{1}{|C_{i}|-1})B_{i}
, giving for each

,

.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
G=Z_{p}^{n}\rtimes Z_{p^{n}-1}
for some prime
p
and
n\geq 1
. Suppose
C_{0}=\{e\},C_{1},\cdots,C_{p^{n}-1}
are the conjugacy classes of
G
. Then the Wedderburn decomposition of
T(G)
is

T(G)\cong V\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{p^{n}-1}.

Proof.

Recall
V
is an irreducible two sided ideal of
T(G)
with dimension equal to the square of number of conjugacy classes of
G
. From the proof of Theorem
5.2
,
G
has
p^{n}
classes. Then
V
has dimension
p^{2n}
. From Proposition
5.4
,
W_{i}
is a
1-
dimensional ideal of
T(G)
for all
1\leq i\leq p^{n}-1
. We note that since the row sum of each row of the basis element of
W_{i}
is
0
, that each
W_{i}
is orthogonal to
V
under the Hermitian inner product. As the basis elements for each
W_{i}
are nonzero in different blocks,
W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are orthogonal for all
i\neq j
.
Then
\dim(V\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{p^{n}-1})=p^{2n}+p^{n}-1
.
As each of the
W_{i}
and
V
are ideals of
T(G)
we have
V\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{p^{n}-1}\subseteq T(G)
.
From Theorem
5.2
,
\dim(T(G))=p^{2n}+p^{n}-1
. Then
V\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{p^{n}-1}
is a subalgebra of
T(G)
with the same dimension. We thus have
T(G)\cong V\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{p^{n}-1}
.
∎
