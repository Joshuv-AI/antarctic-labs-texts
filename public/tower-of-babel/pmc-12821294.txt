Integrated molecular subtyping and functional analysis of circ_0001946/miR-7-5p axis reveals a glucose metabolism-linked regulatory mechanism in ovarian cancer
Authors: Yanlei Dong, Guiyu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian cancer (OvCa) remains one of the most lethal gynecological malignancies, primarily due to late-stage diagnosis, chemoresistance, and substantial molecular heterogeneity. Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal roles of microRNAs (miRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs) in tumor progression. Among them, miR-7-5p has been implicated in tumor suppression and the regulation of metabolic pathways. However, its functional interaction with circ_0001946 in OvCa has yet to be elucidated.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the interplay between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 in OvCa, with a particular focus on their regulatory roles in apoptosis, proliferation, tumorigenesis, and glucose metabolism.MethodsUsing integrated bioinformatics analyses of TCGA and GEO datasets, we identified glucose metabolism-related molecular subtypes. Functional enrichment, immune landscape profiling, and LASSO regression were employed to validate these subtypes and develop prognostic risk models. A series of in vitro and in vivo experiments–including qPCR, CCK-8 assay, flow cytometry, colony formation, dual-luciferase reporter assay, and xenograft tumor models in nude mice–were conducted to evaluate the expression and functional effects of miR-7-5p and circ_0001946.ResultsTwo molecular subtypes, Subtype-A and Subtype-B, with distinct glucose metabolism and immune profiles were identified. Subtype-A correlated with advanced-stage disease and chemoresistance, while Subtype-B exhibited better prognosis, higher immune infiltration, and activation of metabolic pathways. Circ_0001946 was upregulated in OvCa tissues and cell lines and acted as an oncogene by sponging miR-7-5p. Functional assays revealed that downregulation of miR-7-5p or overexpression of circ_0001946 promoted cell proliferation, inhibited apoptosis, and accelerated tumor growth. Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed a direct interaction between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946. Clinical tissue analysis further showed elevated miR-7-5p expression in para-cancerous tissues.ConclusionsThe circ_0001946/miR-7-5p regulatory axis contributes to OvCa progression by suppressing apoptosis and promoting cellular proliferation. Targeting this axis presents a promising therapeutic avenue. Additionally, molecular subtyping based on glucose metabolism-related genes offers valuable prognostic information and supports the advancement of personalized treatment strategies in OvCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04140-2.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Micro RNAs, Hsa-miR-7-5p, Circ_0001946, Glucose metabolism, OVCAR3, SKOV3

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OvCa) remains one of the most lethal gynecologic malignancies, primarily due to its asymptomatic progression and diagnosis at advanced stages. Globally, it ranks as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, with the majority of cases identified only after the disease has metastasized beyond the ovaries. The clinical management of OvCa is further complicated by its molecular and histological heterogeneity, which encompasses several distinct subtypes with differing biological behaviors and treatment responses [1, 2]. Despite advances in cytoreductive surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy, the overall prognosis remains poor, with five-year survival rates below 50%. Delayed detection, frequent recurrence, and the development of chemoresistance underscore the urgent need for novel biomarkers and targeted therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes [3, 4].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as key regulators of gene expression at the post-transcriptional level, acting either as oncogenes or tumor suppressors. By modulating diverse cellular processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis, miRNAs hold great promise as diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic tools in oncology [5, 6]. Among these, hsa-miR-7-5p (miR-7-5p thereafter) has attracted particular interest. In lung cancer, miR-7-5p has been shown to suppress metastasis under acidic microenvironmental conditions through regulation of the miR-7-5p/TGF-β2 axis, highlighting its critical role in tumor adaptation and progression [7]. In colorectal cancer, downregulation of miR-7-5p has been associated with radioresistance. Its upregulation not only restored radiosensitivity but also diminished cancer stem cell properties, pointing to the miR7-5p/KLF4 axis as a potential therapeutic target [8]. Similarly, in breast cancer, miR-7-5p inhibits cell migration and invasion by targeting the receptor-like tyrosine kinase (RYK), thereby suppressing activation of the downstream c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathway and impairing the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [9]. These studies collectively underscore the tumor-suppressive functions of miR-7-5p across various cancer types, particularly in modulating the tumor microenvironment, therapy responsiveness, and metastatic potential.

Beyond its oncologic relevance, miR-7-5p is also a critical regulator of glucose metabolism. It influences key metabolic pathways by targeting enzymes and signaling molecules involved in insulin sensitivity, glucose uptake, and energy homeostasis [10–12]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), miR-7-5p directly targets O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT), reducing anaerobic glycolysis and limiting tumor growth [13]. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, its expression is downregulated by the circular RNA (circRNA) CDR1as, which promotes E2F3-mediated tumor progression and glucose metabolism [14]. Furthermore, miR-7-5p regulates the GLP-1/β-arrestin 1 pathway in pancreatic β-cells, thereby modulating insulin secretion and offering therapeutic potential for type 2 diabetes [15]. These findings position miR-7-5p as a multifaceted regulator with significant implications in both oncogenic and metabolic disorders.

The molecular subtyping of OvCa is essential for advancing precision medicine. It enables the stratification of patients based on distinct molecular features, supports the development of tailored therapeutic strategies, improves prognosis accuracy, and facilitates the discovery of novel drug targets. In this study, we identified two molecular subtypes of OvCa by investigating downstream glucose metabolism-related genes of miR-7-5p using next-generation sequencing data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, we characterized their clinicopathological features, prognostic differences, metabolic profiles, and immune phenotypes. This approach aims to contribute to the development of individualized treatment strategies and enhance our understanding of the genetic and metabolic heterogeneity underlying OvCa.

Materials and methods

Data mining

Processed RNA sequencing data were obtained from the UCSC Xena platform, which integrates datasets from TCGA, Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET), and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) projects. These datasets included OvCa tissue samples and normal ovarian epithelial tissues. Additionally, three independent datasets–GSE160626, GSE214302, and GSE268514–were retrieved from the GEO database, each providing transcriptomic profiles relevant OvCa research.

From the UCSC Xena database, a total of 428 OvCa samples and 88 normal ovarian epithelial samples were extracted. The GEO databases included 17 samples from GSE160626, 19 from GSE214302, and 16 from GSE268514. Following data collection, batch effects across databases were corrected using the SVA package (version 3.52.0) [16]. Differential expression analysis was subsequently performed using the limma package (version 3.60.6) [17]. All statistical analysis and visualizations were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1) [18] and RStudio (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA) [19] for Windows operating system.

Identification of metabolism-related subtypes in ovarian cancer

To identify glucose metabolism-associated molecular subtypes in OvCa, unsupervised clustering analysis was conducted using the ConsensusClusterPlus package (version 1.68.0) [20]. Clustering was performed based on the expression of key genes involved in glucose metabolism, using the following parameters: reps = 50, pItem = 0.8, pFeature = 1, and distance = pearson. Hierarchical clustering revealed the presences of two distinct subtypes across the cohort.

Functional enrichment analyses and gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

To investigate the biological functions of glucose metabolism-related genes, functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID), with a focus on Gene Ontology (GO) annotations [21]. GO terms with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In addition, curated gene sets related to glucose metabolism were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB version 7.0) [22], and glycolysis-related genes were obtained from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [23].

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to assess differences in biological processes between the identified subtypes, while gene variation analysis (GSVA) was conducted to evaluate pathway activity variations using the GSVA algorithm [24]. Results were visualized using the heatmap generated with the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12), enabling a comparative analysis of pathway activity across subgroups [25].

Immune microenvironment analyses

To evaluate the tumor microenvironment, we applied the Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumor tissues using the Expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithm (version 2.0.0), which calculates stromal scores, immune scores, and tumor purity based on transcriptomic data [26]. This analysis provided insights into immune cell infiltration and stromal content across the molecular subtypes.

Construction of risk models for metabolic subtypes

To establish prognostic risk models for the identified metabolic subtypes, we conducted least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis using the glmnet package [27]. Genes identified as critical in the TCGA and GEO datasets were input into the LASSO model. The risk score for each patient was calculated as a weighted sum of gene expression levels, with weights corresponding to the regression coefficient assigned to each gene.

In vitro validation – quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

The qPCR protocol consisted of sample lysis, RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and amplification. Cells (1–5 × 107) or fresh tissue (20 mg) were lysed using TRIzol reagent, and RNA was isolated using standard chloroform-isopropanol-ethanol extraction. The extracted RNA was dissolved in DEPC-treated water and reverse transcribed at 55 °C for 45 min using the M5 First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, which included primer mix, dNTPs, and reverse transcriptase.

The qPCR amplification was conducted using PerfectStart™ Green qPCR SuperMix, with 0.4 µM of each primer and 1 µL of cDNA template in a final reaction volume of 20 µL. Amplification was carried out under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. A final melt curve analysis was performed from 55 °C to 95 °C to confirm amplification specificity.

Primers targeting circ_0001946, miR-7-5p, BAX, BCL2, and CASP3 were used. Quantitative analysis was performed using the Bio-Rad iQ5 Real-Time PCR system, allowing precise detection and quantification of gene expression levels. Primer sequences used in the study are provided in Table [1] [28].

Cell transfection assay

Transient transfection of T-cells using small interfering RNA (siRNA) and plasmid DNA was conducted using standardized protocols. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates with 2–4 mL of complete culture medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Upon reaching 50–70% confluency, transfection mixtures were prepared in sterile conditions. For siRNA transfection, Solution A consisted of 100 µL Opti-MEM with 100 pmol siRNA, and Solution B comprised 100 µL Opti-MEM with 5 µL RNAiMAX. For mimic transfection, Solution A included 100 µL Opti-MEM with 100 pmol mimics, while Solution B contained 100 µL Opti-MEM with 5 µL Lipo3000. For plasmid DNA, Solution A contained 100 µL Opti-MEM with 4 µg DNA, and Solution B included 100 µL Opti-MEM with 10 µL Lipo3000.

Solutions A and B were mixed, gently vortexed, and incubated at room temperature for 15–30 min. Cells were washed with PBS, and 1 mL of Opti-MEM was added to each well before dropwise addition of the transfection mixture. After gentle mixing, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 2–3 days [29].

Tumor formation in nude mice

All animal experiments were conducted at the Qilu Hospital of Shandong University Experimental Animal Center, which operates in compliance with China’s Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals (2017 version) and national welfare standards, following internationally benchmarked practices derived from the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (ILAR, 8th Ed., 2011). The study adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and relevant national and international ethical standards. It was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Shandong University (Approval number: KYLL-2023-297).

SPF-grade female nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Centre. They were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under standard laboratory conditions of temperature 22 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Mice were acclimated for one week. Human OvCa cell lines SK-OV-3 and NIH: OVCAR-3 (Wuhan Pronocell) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 20% fetal bovine serum, penicillin, and streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Once cells reached 80–90% confluency, they were trypsinized, centrifuged, washed with PBS, and resuspended at 2 × 106 cells/mL. A total of 0.2 mL of the suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right axillary region of each mouse following iodine disinfection. The injection site was compressed using sterile cotton, and mice were monitored for health status and injection site reactions [30]. Mice were assigned to six groups as outlined in Table 2.

Cell counting (CCK-8) assay

Cells at approximately 80% confluency were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin and seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/mL (100 µL per well) in six replicates. After 24, 48, 72, and 96 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to each well. Following a 2-hour incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader. A cell viability curve was constructed with time (X-axis) versus optical density (OD) values (Y-axis) [31].

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Predicted binding sites between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 were identified using GeneBank and TargetScan. Wild-type (WT) and mutant-type (MUT) luciferase reporter plasmids for circ_0001946 and miR-7-5p, as well as miR-7-5p mimics and negative controls (NC), were synthesized by GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Adherent cells were lysed using reporter gene lysis buffer, while suspended cells were centrifuged before lysis. Lysates were centrifuged at 10,000–15,000×g for 3–5 min, and the supernatant was used for analysis. Firefly luciferase assay reagent and Renilla luciferase buffer were prepared, with Renilla substrate diluted 1:100. A dual-luciferase reporter assay system (E2910; Promega, WI, USA) and a GloMax®20/20 luminometer were used to measure luminescence.

For each assay, 20–100 µL of lysate was mixed with 100 µL of firefly luciferase reagent, and luminescence was recorded. Subsequently, 100 µL of Renilla working solution was added, and the relative light units (RLU) were measured. Firefly RLUs were normalized to Renilla RLUs for comparison across groups.

Western blot analysis

Cells were trypsinized, pelleted by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min), washed with PBS (3×), and lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Zhongshan Jinqiao, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (Sigma, China). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C), and protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Bio-Tek ELx800 microplate reader at 450 nm). A BSA standard (0.5 mg/mL) was serially diluted to generate a standard curve, and samples were measured in duplicate using the BCA working solution (50:1 mixture of BCA reagent to Cu reagent).

Equal amounts of protein (20–50 µg per sample) were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels (Mini-PROTEAN Tetra, Bio-Rad), and electrophoresed at 70 V initially, then at 120 V until complete. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer (300 mA, ~ 4 °C). Transfer time was 1–1.5 h for 20–100 kDa proteins and 1.5–2.5 h for 100–200 kDa proteins.

Membranes were blocked with 10% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min at 37 °C. Chemiluminescent substrate was applied, and signals were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were collected following treatment and digested with trypsin (without EDTA), neutralized with serum-containing medium, and washed in PBS. Cells were resuspended in 100 µL of 1× binding buffer and stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC/PI solution for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After centrifugation and resuspension in 500 µL PBS, apoptotic cells were quantified using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer.

Plate colony formation assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phases were trypsinized, counted, and seeded at 200 cells per well in 6-well plates. After one week of incubation, colonies were fixed with 1 mL methanol for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 3 min. Colonies were imaged and counted using a light microscope. Clusters visible to the naked eye were considered colonies. Colony formations rate was calculated as: Colony formation rate (%) = (Number of colonies / Number of seeded cells) × 100.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted in six independent samples for each experimental condition. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to measure and represent the differences between multiple groups. The data was compared statistically by the log-rank test, Fisher’s exact test, and Student’s t-test. The Kaplan–Meier plotter was used for survival analysis. The LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) algorithm regression method was implemented to build the risk model. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and visualizations were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1) and RStudio (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Identification of key downstream metabolic genes regulated by miR-7-5p in ovarian cancer

To identify potential downstream targets of miR-7-5p involved in glucose metabolism, we cross-referenced predicted targets from PicTar, TarBase, and TarScan with genes associated with abnormal glucose metabolism from the MSigDB. This integrative analysis yielded six consistently overlapping genes: ITCH, LEMD3, PDE4D, GLI3, PLAG1, and IRS2 (Fig. 1A).

GSVA revealed that, beyond their role in glucose metabolism, these six genes were significantly enriched in biological processes such as RNA transcription regulation, DNA binding, protein phosphorylation, and protein ubiquitination (Fig. 1B), suggesting their broader relevance in tumor biology.

We constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database, which demonstrated strong interconnectivity among these genes (Fig. 1C). These interactions were validated using gene expression data from the TCGA-TARGET-GTEx cohort, confirming tight co-expression patterns (Fig. 1D).

Expression analysis indicated that ITCH, LEMD3, GLI3, PLAG1, and IRS2 were significantly downregulated in OvCa tissues compared to normal tissues, while PDE4D showed an opposite trend (Fig. 1E).

Genomic alteration data from the cBioPortal revealed frequent amplification mutations in these genes, except for PDE4D, which was characterized by deletion mutations in OvCa samples (Fig. 1F).

Survival analysis using the Kaplan–Meier plotter (TCGA + GEO databases) indicated that high expression of IRS2, ITCH, and GLI3 was significantly associated with worse overall survival, suggesting oncogenic potential (log-rank test, p-values 0.0002, 0.03, and 0.0024, respectively). In contrast, low expression of PDE4D correlated with poorer prognosis, indicating a possible tumor-suppressive role. No significant survival associations were observed for LEMD3 and PLAG1 (log-rank test, p-values 0.18 and 0.24, respectively) (Fig. 2).

Identification of clinical and prognostic characteristics in two molecular subtypes

Using unsupervised consensus clustering of transcriptomic data from OvCa tissues in the TCGA and GEO datasets, we classified patients into two distinct subtypes: Subtype-A and Subtype-B (Fig. 3A). Integrated survival analysis from both cohorts revealed that Subtype-B was associated with significantly improved overall survival (p = 0.033) and progression-free survival (p < 0.0001) following platinum-based chemotherapy, in comparison to Subtype-A (Fig. 3B and C). Further evaluation of clinicopathological features demonstrated that Subtype-A predominantly comprised younger women (< 50 years) (p = 0.0003), with a higher proportion of advanced-stage disease (FIGO stage III-IV) (p = 0.0027), high-grade tumors (p < 0.0001), and resistance to platinum-based treatment (p < 0.0001)–all significantly different from Subtype-B (Fig. 3D and F). These findings suggest that distinct metabolic processes, particularly those involving glucose metabolism, may underlie the clinical heterogeneity of OvCa and are closely linked to prognosis and treatment response, underscoring the importance of molecular subtype classification.

Distinct metabolic and immune characteristics identified by GSVA in ovarian cancer subtypes

To further elucidate the biological differences between Subtype-A and Subtype-B, we conducted GSVA. Subtype-B showed positive enrichment for multiple glucose- and lipid-related metabolic pathways, including KEGG_CITRATE_CYCLE_TCA_CYCLE, KEGG_CYSTEINE_AND_METHIONINE_METABOLISM, KEGG_DNA_REPLICATION, KEGG_FRUCTOSE_AND_MANNOSE_METABOLISM, KEGG_GALACTOSE_METABOLISM, KEGG_GLUTATHIONE_METABOLISM, and KEGG_GLYCOLYSIS_GLUCONEOGENESIS (Fig. 4A). In contrast, Subtype-A demonstrated a significantly lower GSVA score for KEGG_PEROXISOME, a marker associated with ferroptosis mediated by lipid peroxidation.

Based on the risk score, patients in the TCGA cohort were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the high-risk group had significantly worse overall survival compared to the low-risk group (log-rank test, p = 0.0003) (Fig. 5B). Gene expression analysis indicated that markers of fatty acid synthesis–ACACA, ACACB, ACSF3, OXSM, HSD17B8, and HTD2–were significantly upregulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 5C). GSEA further showed enrichment of key oncogenic pathways in the high-risk group, including the KRAS (p = 0.018), Hedgehog (p = 0.04), and Wnt/β-catenin (p = 0.019) signaling pathways (Fig. 5D). Clinically, the high-risk group was characterized by more advanced FIGO stage (p = 0.0341), higher WHO grade (p = 0.0001), and a greater prevalence of TP53 (p = 0.0096) and BRCA1 mutations (p = 0.001) (Fig. 5E).

qPCR validation in cell lines and clinical samples

qPCR analysis in OvCa cell lines and clinical samples supported our computational findings. Notably, circ_0001946 was significantly upregulated in OvCa tissues derived from both OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cell lines (Fig. 6A and C). In contrast, miR-7-5p mRNA levels were markedly reduced in cancer-treated groups compared to both the normal control and mimic-transfected groups (Fig. 6B and D). Moreover, circ_0001946 expression was significantly more elevated in the OVCAR3/diamminedichloroplatinum (DDP) (cisplatin-resistant) group compared to parental OVCAR3 cells, whereas it was decreased in both SKOV3 and SKOV3/DDP groups (Fig. 6E; p < 0.05). Consistent with this, the relative expression of miR-7-5p was significantly lower in the OVCAR3/DDP, SKOV3, and SKOV3/DDP groups compared to the OVCAR3 group, reinforcing a regulatory relationship (Fig. 6F). In clinical tissue samples, circ_0001946 expression was significantly increased in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 6G). In contrast, miR-7-5p expression was significantly lower in para-cancerous tissues than in cancer tissues (Fig. 6H). These results highlight complex regulatory interactions in OvCa involving circ_0001946 and miR-7-5p, with potential implications for disease progression and treatment resistance.

Tumor formation in animal models and cell viability assay

The OVCAR3 and SKOV3 OvCa cell lines are widely utilized in preclinical models due to their ability to form xenografts in murine hosts. Among them, SKOV3 cells are particularly noted for their high tumorigenic potential, producing substantial tumor masses following both subcutaneous and intraperitoneal injections. Although OVCAR3 cells are also capable of inducing tumor formation, their tumorigenicity is generally considered lower than that of SKOV3 cells.

In our study, we systematically evaluated tumor sizes across different experimental groups. The OVCAR3-inh-miR-7-5p (inhibition of miR-7-5p) group developed tumors with a mean weight of 290 mg, while the OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946 (overexpression of circ_0001946) group produced smaller tumors, averaging 150 mg. In contrast, the SKOV3-derived xenografts exhibited markedly larger tumors, with weights of 800 mg in the SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p group and 300 mg in the SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946 group–both significantly larger than those in the control group (Figs. 8A–C).

The CCK-8 assay demonstrated significantly increased cell viability in the OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946, SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946, OVCAR3-inh-miR-7-5p, and SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p groups, as shown in Fig. 8D and E (p < 0.01). These findings indicate that suppression of miR-7-5p or overexpression of circ_0001946 enhances the proliferative capacity of both SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells. This highlights a potential role for miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 in modulating OvCa cell viability and tumor progression.

Downregulation of caspase activity in modified cell lines

To further explore the role of miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 in apoptosis regulation, we assessed the protein expression levels of key apoptotic markers–Caspase-3, Caspase-7, and Caspase-9–using ELISA. In OVCAR3-derived cells, both the oe-circ_0001946 and inh-miR-7-5p groups exhibited a marked downregulation of all three caspases compared to the control and mimic groups (Figs. 9A–C). Similarly, in SKOV3 cells, reduced levels of Caspase3, Caspase-7, and Caspase-9 were observed in the oe-circ_0001946 and inh-miR-7-5p groups relative to controls (Figs. 9D–F). These results suggest that overexpression of circ_0001946 or inhibition of miR-7-5p leads to the suppression of apoptotic pathways in OvCa cells. Collectively, our findings provide strong evidence that the circ_0001946/miR-7-5p axis may serve as a key regulatory mechanism contributing to increased cell survival and tumor development in OvCa.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was performed to evaluate the effects of miR-7-5p mimic, oe-circ_0001946, and inh-miR-7-5p on cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and surface marker expression in OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cell lines (Fig. 10A). Notably, the apoptotic rate was significantly reduced in both the OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946 and OVCAR3-inh-miR-7-5p groups compared to the mimic and natural control groups (Fig. 10B). Moreover, there is also a substantial decrease in the rate of apoptosis in the SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946 and SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p groups compared to the mimic and natural control groups (Fig. 10C). These findings indicate that circ_0001946 overexpression and miR-7-5p inhibition may suppress apoptotic signaling in OvCa cells.

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was used to quantify the expression levels apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins in the experimental groups. Total Caspase-3 expression remained relatively unchanged across all groups compared to the OVCAR3-NC group, suggesting a consistent baseline expression. However, the levels of cleaved Caspase-3 and cleaved Caspase-9 were notably elevated in the OVCAR3-miR-7-5p-mimic, OVCAR3-si-circ_0001946, and SKOV3-miR-7-5p-mimic groups, indicating enhanced activation of apoptotic pathways under these conditions. Conversely, these cleaved caspase levels were reduced in the OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946, SKOV3-NC, SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p, and SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946 groups, with no significant changes detected in the SKOV3-si-circ_0001946 group (Fig. 11A).

A The expression of Caspase-3, cleaved Caspase-3 (C-Caspase 3), Caspase-9, Bcl-2, Bax, and α-tubulin by Western blot. B Relative protein levels of Caspase-3, C-Caspase-3, Caspase-9, Bcl-2, and Bax in OVCAR3 and SKOV3

The expression of Bcl-2, an anti-apoptotic marker, was decreased in the OVCAR3-miR-7-5p-mimic and OVCAR3-si-circ_0001946 groups, suggesting a pro-apoptotic shift. In contrast, increased Bcl-2 expression was observed in the OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946, SKOV3-NC, SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p, SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946, and SKOV3-si-circ_0001946 groups. The SKOV3-miR-7-5p-mimic group exhibited no significant change in Bcl-2 levels (Fig. 11A).

Additionally, the pro-apoptotic marker Bax was significantly upregulated in the OVCAR3-miR-7-5p-mimic, OVCAR3-si-circ_0001946, and SKOV3-miR-7-5p-mimic groups, further supporting enhanced apoptotic signaling in these models (Fig. 11A). In contrast, Bax levels were reduced in the OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946, SKOV3-NC, SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p, and SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946 groups. Meanwhile, no notable alterations were observed in the SKOV3-si-circ_0001946 group (Fig. 11A). The detailed interaction between apoptotic and anti-apoptotic factors highlights the complexity of the regulatory mechanisms involved in these experimental models.

Protein quantification analysis also confirmed a significant increase in the expression levels of Caspase-3, cleaved Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and Bax in the OVCAR3-miR-7-5p-mimic, OVCAR3-si-circ_0001946, and SKOV3-miR-7-5p-mimic groups. On the other hand, the SKOV3-inh-miR-7-5p, SKOV3-oe-circ_0001946, OVCAR3-inh-miR-7-5p, and OVCAR3-oe-circ_0001946 groups showed higher protein levels of Bcl-2 (p < 0.05 for all; Fig. 11B). These results highlight the dynamic interplay between apoptotic and anti-apoptotic factors and their regulation by miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 in OvCa.

Colony formation and dual-luciferase reporter assays

The colony formation assay revealed a significant increase in cell proliferation in the inh-miR-7-5p and oe-circ_0001946 groups, with approximately a 70% increase in colony numbers compared to the natural control. This enhancement was consistently observed in both the OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cell lines, underscoring the pro-proliferative effects of miR-7-5p inhibition and circ_0001946 overexpression (Fig. 12A).

A The colony formation in each group in both OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cell lines. B Relative luciferase activity with respect to wild type and mutant conditions

To further investigate the regulatory interaction between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. Co-transfection of miR-7-5p mimic with a WT circ_0001946 luciferase reporter plasmid significantly increased luciferase activity compared to the control group co-transfected with miR-NC and WT reporter. These findings confirm the direct interaction between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946, supporting a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism (Fig. 12B).

Discussion

Ovarian cancer remains a major global health challenge and is the most lethal gynecological malignancy worldwide. Its incidence is notably higher in developed countries, and the disease is often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to a lack of early symptoms. Consequently, the prognosis remains poor. Despite advances in surgical techniques and chemotherapeutic regimens, survival rates have not significantly improved in recent decades [32, 33]. Treatment continues to be hindered by high recurrence rates, chemoresistance, and the absence of effective early diagnostic tools [34]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies and a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying OvCa progression.

miRNAs play crucial roles in regulating gene expression, significantly influencing tumor development and progression. By targeting specific mRNAs for degradation or translational repression, miRNAs regulate key cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [35]. In the context of cancer, miRNAs are increasingly recognized for their roles in metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark that allows tumor cells to thrive under stressful conditions such as hypoxia. miR-7-5p, for instance, has been implicated in modulating glucose metabolism, supporting the Warburg effect and promoting tumor cell survival [36, 37]. Understanding the interplay between miRNAs and metabolic pathways opens new avenues for targeting cancer-specific vulnerabilities.

In this study, a bioinformatics approach identified two OvCa subtypes with distinct glucose metabolic profiles. Subtype B, characterized by reduced glucose metabolism, was associated with better patient outcomes and heightened immune infiltration–traits linked to immune activation. Conversely, Subtype A showed higher metabolic activity and may benefit from targeted metabolic interventions. Notably, Subtype B was also associated with TP53 mutations and mesenchymal activation, further indicating distinct biological behavior. A risk model developed using four gene signatures effectively predicted these subtypes and demonstrated good prognostic value, highlighting the relevance of glucose metabolism in OvCa progression and resistance.

Several of the identified metabolic signatures are known to influence tumorigenesis. For example, the itchy E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (ITCH) is involved in protein degradation pathways and has been implicated in stabilizing oncogenic factors such as Notch3-ICD in NSCLC [38]. Similarly, LEMD3, a nuclear membrane protein, negatively regulates TGF-β, activin, and BMP signaling and is mutated in various connective tissue disorders [39]. PDE4D, another identified factor, promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression by interacting with Yes-associated protein (YAP) and may serve as a potential therapeutic target [40]. GLI3, a regulator of the Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) pathway, and PLAG1, which activates the IGF2 pathway, have also been implicated in promoting proliferation and metastasis in ovarian and other cancers [41, 42]. Insulin receptor substrate-2 (IRS2), upregulated through the YAP-TEAD axis, is another key player in tumor cell survival, particularly in esophageal cancer [43].

Our findings demonstrate that circ_0001946 and miR-7-5p play pivotal role in OvCa progression, influencing apoptosis, cell viability, and tumorigenesis. Elevated expression of circ_0001946 in OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cell lines aligns with the oncogenic role of circRNAs, which often function as miRNA sponges [44]. The observed downregulation of miR-7-5p in tumor tissues is consistent with its tumor-suppressive functions, as documented in various cancers [44, 45].

The potential interaction between circ_0001946 and miR-7-5p suggests a ceRNA mechanism, wherein circ_0001946 sequesters miR-7-5p and reduces its regulatory capacity. This mechanism, previously described in hepatocellular carcinoma and other malignancies, contributes to the dysregulation of oncogenic pathways [46]. The resulting reduction in pro-apoptotic factors such as Bax and cleaved Caspase-3, coupled with increased Bcl-2, in OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cell lines supports an anti-apoptotic role for circ_0001946, whose overexpression promotes cell survival by inhibiting apoptosis. This is consistent with studies demonstrating that circRNAs can inhibit apoptosis by modulating the Bcl-2/Bax axis [47].

The results of CCK-8 and colony formation assays further support the pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic roles of circ_0001946 overexpression and miR-7-5p downregulation. Similarly, ELISA and Western blot analyses showed decreased levels of cleaved caspases in cells overexpressing circ_0001946, reinforcing its role in apoptosis inhibition [48, 49]. The dual-luciferase assay confirmed a direct interaction between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946, consistent with the ceRNA hypothesis [50]. Of note, we noted an increase in the luciferase activity after co-transfection with miR-7-5p mimic and WT circ_0001946 reporter plasmid. Perhaps this results from the need for greater production of circ_0001946 (i.e., through accelerated back splicing) by OvCa cells to keep miR-7-5p sequestrated in the same way that the synthesis of an inhibitory molecule is upregulated in the presence of its target [51]. However, such a phenomenon has not been described before. A more plausible explanation is that miR-7-5p may fail to effectively bind and/or interact with the luciferase gene in cancer conditions, thereby not affecting circ_0001946 expression. Various mechanisms that mediate cancer cells’ resistance to miRNAs, such as genes deletion, transcription factor dysregulation, and DNA hypermethylation, may promote the inefficacy of miR-7-5p in OvCa cells expressing circ_0001946 [52]. Functional assays, including flow cytometry and tumor xenograft models, revealed increased tumor growth in miR-7-5p-inhibited groups, emphasizing its role as a tumor suppressor.

Interestingly, analysis of clinical tissues revealed lower levels of circ_0001946 compared to cell line data, potentially due to tumor heterogeneity or differences between in vitro and in vivo environments [51]. The increased expression of miR-7-5p in adjacent non-cancerous tissues further supports its suppressive function in vivo. We assume that the miR-7-5p higher expression in para-cancerous tissue could be a feature of upregulated antineoplastic activity in stromal cells, aiming to limit tumor growth. Notably, miRNAs serve as markers of anti-tumor inflammatory responses, enhancing the efficacy of cytotoxic CD8 + T cell/natural killer cell responses, as well as CD4 + T helper cell polarization, in the tumor microenvironment [53]. Th17 cells, a critical player in immune response against OvCa, were shown to produce higher levels of miRNAs when activated in ovarian tissue [54]. Moreover, Mitra et al. found that miR-155 was upregulated in fibroblasts surrounding OvCa cells while other miRNAs were downregulated (i.e., miR-31 and miR-214). We conclude that OvCa cells may use miRNAs to reprogram the phenotype and behavior of normal fibroblasts in the sense of supporting their survival and proliferation [55]. However, it is unlikely to be the case in our study given the observed tumor-altering properties of miR-7-5p.

Despite these promising findings, this study has limitations. The initial analyses relied on public gene expression datasets, which may introduce platform-related biases. Additionally, our retrospective study design necessitates further prospective validation. Although the data were derived from established cancer tissue repositories, in vivo confirmation of molecular mechanisms is required. Rescue experiments, such as overexpressing circ_0001946 in conjunction with miR-7-5p mimic, would significantly strengthen the suggested competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) interaction. Moreover, this study focused mainly on assessing the effects of miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 on OvCa cell lines while confirming the direct binding between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946 through dual-luciferase reporter assays. However, predicting potential binding sites through bioinformatics databases could have allowed better evaluation of the molecular interaction between miR-7-5p and circ_0001946. Lack of validation of our findings in an external database is another limitation. Nevertheless, the study offers important insights into the roles of metabolic pathways and specific genetic regulators in OvCa development.

Collectively, our study highlights a novel circ_0001946/miR-7-5p regulatory axis that promotes OvCa cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis. These findings enhance our understanding of circRNA-mediated networks and offer promising therapeutic targets. Future studies should explore the clinical relevance of targeting this axis, particularly in chemo-resistant OvCa subtypes.

Clinical implications and future directions

The contrasting expression patterns of circ_0001946 and miR-7-5p between clinical samples and cell lines suggest the existence of tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms. These discrepancies highlight the complexity of circRNA-miRNA interactions in different biological contexts and warrant further investigation. Targeting the circ_0001946/miR-7-5p axis could provide a novel therapeutic approach for OvCa, especially by silencing circ_0001946 or restoring miR-7-5p function to reactivate apoptotic pathways and suppress tumor growth. However, additional mechanistic studies are essential to validate these effects across various OvCa subtypes and to address the observed differences between in vitro and in vivo systems.

Conclusions

This study emphasizes the significant roles of miR-7-5p and its regulatory circRNA, circ_0001946, in OvCa progression. Their involvement in modulating glucose metabolism, cell proliferation, and apoptosis underscores their therapeutic potential. The circ_0001946/miR-7-5p axis appears to enhance tumorigenesis by suppressing apoptotic pathways, supporting its relevance as a biomarker and potential treatment target. Moving forward, in vivo studies and clinical trials should investigate circRNA-based therapeutic strategies, particularly in drug-resistant OvCa. These efforts could pave the way for improved prognostic tools and more effective, personalized treatment approaches.
