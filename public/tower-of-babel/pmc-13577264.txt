Discovery and engineering of bispecific TCR-mimic antibodies targeting peptide–HLA complex
Authors: Jennifer Watkins-Yoon, Wei-Cheng Lu, Elyse M Petrunak, Noah J Tubo, Sara Halmos, William McConaughy, Rodolfo Ciuffa, Cédric Weber, Simon Friedensohn, Sune Justesen, Marie Skovgaard, Rennard Rachel, Caity Goshert, Lucy Liu, Piotr Bobrowicz, Jeffrey J Molldrem, Dongxing Zha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTCR-mimic (TCRm) antibodies represent a promising new frontier in cancer immunotherapy, enabling the targeting of intracellular targets previously considered undruggable with antibodies. We developed an integrated platform for generating human antibodies that mimic T-cell receptors (TCRs) and recognize peptide-HLA complexes with high specificity and affinity. These antibody binders were then engineered into highly potent bispecific antibodies capable of redirecting T cells to kill tumor cells. By leveraging high-quality pHLA immunogens, fully human transgenic mice, advanced single B-cell discovery, and whole proteome off-target screening (X-Scan) techniques, this end-to-end platform establishes a new benchmark in TCRm discovery. In this study, we highlight the successful identification and engineering of antibodies targeting the Wilms Tumor 1 (WT1) antigen, demonstrating superior performance compared to competitors’ TCRm antibodies.KEYWORDS: Bispecific ab, pHLA, T cell engager, TCR mimic antibodies, TCRm, WT1

Introduction

By harnessing the power of the immune system to fight cancer, immunotherapies have transformed the oncology treatment landscape. The majority of progress in cancer immunotherapy thus far has been made in antibody-based drugs, which have been hugely successful: monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapies made up five of the ten top-selling oncology drugs of 2024.1 mAbs can target soluble molecules and disease-related antigens present on the cell surface with high specificity, making them a valuable modality for directed cancer treatment.

Despite their impact, conventional antibody-based cancer therapies are limited to targeting antigens present on the cell surface. However, cell surface antigens represent only about ~30% of the proteome, meaning that most potential cancer-associated protein targets are inaccessible to mAbs.2,3 Targeting of peptide-human leukocyte antigen (pHLA) complexes on tumor cells represents a promising alternative approach to developing therapeutics directed at intracellular protein targets. These proteins can be degraded by the proteasome and processed into peptide fragments that will be then presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, known as the HLA system in humans, at the surface of cells. T-cell receptors (TCRs) present on cytotoxic T cells recognize these pHLA complexes, resulting in cell lysis.4

pHLA complexes are a broad and largely untapped class of potential therapeutic targets.5 An estimated 5000+ proteins have been identified as being specific to cancer cells, including tumor drivers, neoantigens, lineage antigens, cancer testis antigens, and, most recently, so-called dark genome antigens referring to non-canonical, cryptic, or non-coding-region-derived peptides.6–8 Some proteins, such as PRAME and melanoma-associated antigens (MAGEs), are expressed only in cancer cells except low level expression in testis, while some involve more ubiquitous proteins with cancer-specific mutations (KRAS, TP53).9–12 In designing targeted therapies, high specificity to antigens expressed only in cancer cells is extremely important for ensuring the safety of modalities that rely on tumor cell killing.

While TCR biology could be leveraged to reach a broader range of intracellular tumor-specific targets, several factors limit their direct therapeutic application. Each TCR is capable of recognizing thousands of different pHLA pairs in order to support an adequate protective immune response against evolving pathogens and oncogenic mutations.13 This potential for cross-reactivity complicates the development of TCR-based therapies, as off-target effects due to recognition of pHLA complexes on healthy cells can compromise safety.14 Additionally, isolation of tumor-specific TCRs requires screening of populations and various engineering methods that render the process inefficient. TCRs have low (typically micromolar) binding affinities toward their target pHLA complex. Affinity maturation is required to achieve adequate potency, which can impact their specificity profile.15

TCR-mimic (TCRm) antibodies that recognize pHLA targets are an attractive alternative to conventional TCRs in therapeutic applications.16 TCRm antibodies typically have binding affinities roughly 1000 times higher than TCRs, and their binding mode can differ from canonical TCR binding.17 TCRm antibody fragments can be engineered into various functional formats such as bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs), which simultaneously bind tumor-associated antigen and T cells to bring them into proximity for effective cell-killing.18 This approach shows immense potential for developing precise and potent immunotherapies. For example, tebentafusp, a TCR/anti-CD3 bispecific antibody (bsAb) targeting the gp100 peptide presented on HLA-A *02:01, demonstrated a significant clinical impact in metastatic uveal melanoma.19 Importantly, this cancer is commonly recognized as an immune-deserted or immunologically “cold” tumor due to the lack of surrounding T cells, which results in poor response rates to traditional immunotherapeutic approaches. The clinical findings associated with tebentafusp demonstrate the promising potential of T-cell engagers targeting pHLA complexes on cancer cells. Most recently, IMC-F106C, a TCR bispecific targeting PRAME and CD3, demonstrated positive outcomes in clinical trials and has advanced to a pivotal trial in combination with nivolumab.20

In this study, we built a discovery workflow for TCR-mimic Abs that screens the diversity generated upon immunization of ATX-Gx mice platform developed by Alloy Therapeutics with pHLA monomers to isolate peptide-HLA-specific fully human Abs. The specificity of these TCRm Abs for a panel of selected pHLAs was interrogated using phage ELISA and X-scan methods to map potential off-target binding across the entire human peptidome. X-scan is a high-resolution screening technology designed to assess off-target specificity of TCR‑mimic (TCRm) antibodies. By introducing single amino acid substitutions across the peptide presented in the MHC complex and displaying these variants on phage, X-scan evaluates binding tolerance through TCRm interaction. Next-generation sequencing and proteome-wide motif scanning are then used to identify potential cross-reactive peptides within the human proteome.

We used WT1126–134 peptide (RMFPNAPYL) presented on HLA-A *02:01 as a proof-of-concept target to validate our workflow. Previously discovered antibodies targeting this pHLA complex have shown specificity issues.21 Indeed, previously developed TCRm antibodies targeting WT1126–134 have demonstrated poor results in the clinic, primarily due to their lack of specificity. We discovered highly specific Abs that recognize WT1126–134 /HLA-A *02:01 complex and not any of the non-relevant peptide complexes we tested. Scanning through a panel of pHLA-A *02:01 displayed on phage ELISA and X-scan data showed that our Ab lead was highly specific for WT1126–134 peptide in complex with HLA-A *02:01.

Using these findings, we then generated T-cell engagers consisting of one arm targeting CD3εδ domain and the other arm targeting WT1126–134/HLA complex. The CD3 ×WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 bispecific antibody demonstrated specific cytotoxicity against WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01-positive tumor cells expressing endogenous levels of the pHLA target. These data demonstrated the viable potency and specificity of our TCR-mimic T-cell engagers, warranting further development efforts.

Results

Antibody diversity generation and screening for pHLA binders

To generate highly specific TCRm antibodies targeting WT1126–134 (RMFPNAPYL) in complex with HLA-A *02:01, ATX-GK mice (n = 12) were immunized weekly via subcutaneous injection with pHLA monomer WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 in combination with anti-GITR monoclonal antibody (cohort 2) or without (cohort 1). ATX-Gk mice are Alloy Therapeutics-owned proprietary transgenic animals engineered to express human antibody heavy- and light-chain variable regions. All mice mounted a robust immune response against WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 (Figure 1A). After five pHLA antigen immunizations animals were sacrificed, and lymphoid tissues were used for isolation of antigen-specific B cells by FACS (Figure 1B) and to generate immune-phage libraries. To deselect HLA-A *02:01-specific B cells, antigen-specific B cells were sorted based on their binding to WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex and their lack of binding to CMVpp65/HLA-A *02:01 complex (Figure 1B). WT1‑specific B cells represent between 0.5% and 6.5% of the total IgG+ memory B cell population (Figure 1C). Individual cells were sorted into 96 wells and antibody variable regions were sequenced. 95 hits representing 78 unique heavy chain CDR3 were cloned as human IgG1 antibodies, expressed, and supernatants were collected.

Using ELISA, we then assessed binding of these various hits to the following pHLA monomers: WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 and a mix of peptides/HLA-A *02:01 complexes (Figure 1D). 11% of the expressed hits displayed binding to WT1 (RMFPNAPYL) peptide in complex with HLA-A *02:01 with no detectable binding to 02:01 with the mixed peptide/HLA-A *02:01 complexes (Figure 1D). These 11 specific antibodies represent 3 distinct clonotypes.

Furthermore, spleens of immunized ATX-Gk mice were used to generate phage immune libraries that were screened according to the following panning strategy. After negative selection using a mix of non-relevant peptides bound to HLA-A *02:01, phages were panned using WT1126–134/HLA *02:01 complex. This process was repeated for two additional rounds of panning. SS320 Fab-containing lysates binding to WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 and not binding to a panel of mixed peptide-HLA-A *02:01 were detected by ELISA (Figure 1E). Specific binders were cloned as human IgG1 antibodies, expressed, and purified. Binding with recombinant antibodies was confirmed by ELISA. Using this method, we isolated 15 specific antibodies representing 2 distinct clonotypes.

Anti-WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 TCR mimic antibodies specifically bind to WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01

We further interrogated the binding properties of the 26 specific antibodies isolated by B-cell sorting or immune phage libraries.

First, we determined affinity for soluble pHLA monomer WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 and negative controls using a high-throughput surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-based assay (Carterra). All tested antibodies showed binding to the monomer formed by WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 with affinity ranging from 10−7 to 10−10 M (Figure 2A). In this assay, we also tested the specificity of these 26 antibodies by measuring binding to non-relevant peptides loaded on MAGE-A4/HLA-A *02:01 complex, and a panel of selected negative pHLA complexes. 25/26 antibodies did not show binding to MAGE-A4/HLA-A *02:01 monomer on Carterra (data not shown). In this assay, we included three control antibodies: 33H9, 11D06, and ESK1 (KD for WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 monomer ranging from 2.7e−6 to 8.7e−8 M). All showed binding to WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 (Figure 2A) and did not show binding to MAGE-A4/HLA-A *02:01 (data not shown). Newly discovered binders do not bind non-relevant peptide-HLA-A *02:01 complexes in SPR experiments indicating that these binders are specific for this WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex. In addition, we performed epitope binning of WT1-specific binders using the commercial HLA-A *02:01-specific antibody BB7.2 and identified two major epitope classes (Figure 2B). The BB7.2 antibody served as a benchmark to distinguish binder interactions at or near the HLA-peptide interface. WT1 binders are distributed into distinct epitope bins. Binders from Bin 1 showed some level of competition with the anti-HLA-A *02:01 BB7.2 antibody while binders from Bin 2 did not. These data suggest these WT1 binders recognize different structural determinants on the pHLA complex.

To further interrogate the specificity of these WT1 binders, we assessed binding of these binders to WT1 peptide, M13L peptide, and PIGQ peptide loaded on HLA-A *02:01-expressing T2 cells. T2 cells are deficient in endogenous peptide loading onto MHC, allowing for exogenous “pulsing” of target peptides onto surface MHC. M13L (RMFPTPPSL) and PIGQ (RMFPGEVAL) peptides were selected because they share high sequence homology with WT1(RMFPNAPYL) peptide and are known off-target peptides for drugs developed against WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex. In this assay, all tested antibodies demonstrated binding to T2 cells pulsed with WT1126–134 peptide (Figure 2C). Three of 26 antibodies tested showed weak binding to non-pulsed T2 cells, 13/26 antibodies showed binding to the off-target peptide M13L, and 14 of 26 showed binding to T2 cells pulsed with PIGQ peptide. Interestingly, while 33H9 and ESK1 demonstrated binding to both off-target peptides (M13L and PIGQ) and 11D06 showed binding to M13L peptide, 8 of 26 antibodies did not bind to T2 cells pulsed with either the M13L or PIGQ peptides, indicating that these antibodies could be highly specific for the WT1126–134 peptide-HLA complex. From this assay, the selected top 8 leads have superior specificity in binding to the target peptide presented in HLA-A *02:01.

For the top 8 leads, we tested binding at 100 nM antibody concentration to SKM-1 cells that endogenously express WT1 antigen and HLA-A *02, to K562 cells that express WT1 antigen but lack HLA, and to T2 cells that do not express the WT1 antigen but express HLA-A *02:01 (Figure 2D). The top 8 leads and the 2 positive control antibodies tested 11D06 and ESK1 showed binding to SKM-1 cells and minimal binding to K562 and T2 cells indicating that these hits are specific to the WT1126–134/HLA-A *02 complex.

Last, we measured binding EC50 of the 8 TCRm leads to T2 cells pulsed with WT1126–134 peptide (Figure 2E) with antibody concentrations ranging from 100 nM to 0.5pM. For 3 of the antibodies (ATX-TCRm-07, ATX-TCRm-08, and ESK1) plateau was not reached at 100 nM and EC50 could not be calculated. For the remaining antibodies, EC50 ranged from 0.181 nM to 1.02 nM (Figure 2F).

Based on binding (KD and EC50) and specificity toward WT1126–134 peptide, we selected 6 TCRm leads to be further tested for off-target binding.

TCRm antibody specificity test

Off-target effects of TCRs or TCRm antibodies can lead to life-threatening complications, and various assays have been developed to assess specificity and minimize these risks.22 To efficiently evaluate the specificity of selected TCRm binders during the discovery phase, we developed two specificity assays: 1) a phage-based specificity assay using a panel of soluble pHLAs and 2) an X-scan assay using phage display technology. Previous studies have demonstrated that pHLA can be displayed on phage as a trimer (linking a peptide with β2 m and the α chain) without altering the pHLA presentation conformation.23,24 In the first assay, we generated approximately 40 different pHLA trimers on phages and established an ELISA-based method to rapidly test for nonspecific binding of TCRm antibody candidates. Nine TCRm antibodies were tested (six from our discovery, three controls from literature or patent described above) (Figure 3A). We observed that 33H9 and ESK1 exhibited weaker binding to the WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 monomer compared to our TCRm antibodies. We hypothesize that the low affinity of these control antibodies for the WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 monomer, perhaps due to additional interference from the linker region, may have affected their interaction with the WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 trimer, particularly in the region near or at the linker. Although weak binding was detected for NRP-1 (GMLGMVSGL) and Survivin (LMLGEFLKL) with the ATX-TCRm-06 antibody, these signals were significantly weaker than for WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 as measured by absorbance. Given that these peptides are cancer-relevant, they are unlikely to present significant concerns for therapeutic applications. In conclusion, no concerning off-target binding signals were observed for our top TCRm antibodies tested. After confirming the specificity of selected leads with the initial assay, we further extended screening to the wider human proteome using a phage display-based X-scan specificity assay developed in-house, enabling rapid assessment of TCRm antibody specificity and efficient characterization of multiple antibodies simultaneously (Figure 3B).

For WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01, the phage display-based X-scan pool consisted of phages displaying a library of 172 different single chain pHLAs, each differing from the WT1 peptide by a single amino acid. The phage pool was incubated with anti-WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 TCRm antibody-coated beads to assess the interaction between pHLAs and anti-WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 TCRm antibodies. After two rounds of panning, phages displaying interactions with TCRm anitbodies were isolated and sequenced by NGS to generate the interaction epitope profile (Figure 3B).

We characterized nine TCRm antibodies, and the SeqLogo data revealed four distinct interaction epitope subsets (Figure 3C). The first subset, represented by ATX-TCRm-06, interacted with positions 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8 of the WT1126–134 peptide while tolerating substitutions at position 5. The second subset, consisting of ATX-TCRm-01, ATX-TCRm-03, and ATX-TCRm-04, interacted with positions 3, 4, 5, and 7 of the WT1126–134 peptide and tolerated amino acid changes at positions 6 and 8. This second subset exhibited an interaction profile similar to that of control antibody 33H9. The third subset of antibodies, including ATX-TCRm-07 and ATX-TCRm-08, binds to positions 3, 4, and 7 of the peptides. The fourth subset, represented by 11D06 and ESK1, tolerated a larger number of mutations at each position of the WT1126–134 peptide. In conclusion, subsets 1 and 2, which recognize 5 or 4 positions of the WT1126–134 peptide, showed greater specificity and may be stronger TCRm candidates compared to subsets 3 and 4.

Whole human proteome database blast (homology search) with an internal proprietary algorithm using X-scan data revealed a marked difference in binding specificity among the TCRm antibodies tested. The target peptide WT1126–134 RMFPNAPYL was identified as a potential binder for four TCRm antibody subsets and the control antibody 33H9. However, this blast also suggested potential interactions between another two control antibodies (11D06 and ESK1) and a broader range of peptides (four to twelve, Figure 3D). Given this specificity data, we selected ATX-TCRm-06 to be engineered as a CD3-T-cell engager.

Bispecific antibody engineering and functional characterization

For bispecific Abs engineering, we have tested two formats of bispecific formats illustrated as Figures 4A and 5A with Fc domain or an anti-HSA domain for half-life extension. The first format was anti-WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 TCRm binder ATX-TCRm-06 engineered into a bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) format as illustrated in Figure 4A. The bispecific Ab format is a heterodimer incorporating scFv and Fab binding moieties. The CD3 binder DM38 used in this study is derived from humanized anti-CD3 SP34 clone with additional affinity maturation and swapping of light chain isotype from mouse lambda to human kappa. As described in the Materials and Methods section, the Fc domain is fully human, containing a knob-in-hole mutation according to Shatz et al. 2013 and an additional mutation at N297A to eliminate effector functions mediated by Fc gamma receptors.25 The scFv arm is linked to Fc with Knob mutation and Fab is linked with Hole mutations. Bispecific antibody expression was evaluated for both TCRm and CD3 in scFv format. In this construction, TCRm in Fab format was better expressed based on the purity post Protein A affinity purification. We achieved above 90% purity when TCRm ATX-TCRm-06 was converted to Fab. As demonstrated through representative bioanalytical data in Figure 4A, the BsAb CD3 ×WT1(ATX-TCRm-06) exhibited binding for CD3 and WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex with KD of 1.46E-08 M and 3.50E-08 M, respectively.

To test the therapeutic functionality of the BsAb CD3xWT1 (ATX-TCRm-06), a co-culture system was utilized with reporter T cells as effector cells (Jurkat NFAT-RE-Luciferase) and T2 cells as target cells (Figure 4B). T2 cells were pulsed with either WT1126–134 peptide or the off-target peptide (M13L) that can also bind HLA-A *02:01 and were co-cultured along with Jurkat reporter cells and WT1xCD3 bispecific antibody. As evidenced by luciferase expression, the BsAb CD3xWT1 (ATX-TCRm-06) only induced T-cell activation in the presence of target cells that had the correct pHLA complex on the surface (Figure 4B). In the presence of either T2 cells without exogenous peptide or cells pulsed with off-target peptide M13L, the WT1xCD3 (ATX-TCRm-06) BsAb did not induce activation of the Jurkat reporter cells, confirming that ATCRm-06 is specific for the WT1126–134 peptide.

Next, we sought to show that the bispecific antibody could induce killing of WT1-expressing target cells by primary human CD8+ T cells. T2 cells were pulsed with a wide range of WT1126–134 peptide concentrations and then co-cultured with CD8+ T cells and WT1xCD3 BsAb to measure cytotoxic activity (Figure 4C,D). Cytotoxicity was measured using a flow cytometry-based method that quantified absolute cell counts. Following co-culture with BsAb, primary human CD8+ T cells were able to efficiently target and kill WT1126–134+ T2 cells, even at low concentrations of WT1126–134 peptide. The killing of T2 target cells approached 100% at peptide pulsing concentrations greater than 50 nM. In the absence of BsAb, no target cell killing was observed, even at high concentrations of pulsed peptide (>500 nM).

Furthermore, we evaluated T cell–dependent cellular cytotoxicity against WT1+/HLA-A *02+ endogenous cancer cell lines using a second bispecific antibody format, as illustrated in Figure 5A. Two cell lines, TF1 and SET-2, were identified based on data from the DepMap portal database (https://depmap.org/portal/). This bispecific antibody format features a different geometry compared with the first bispecific antibody tested in the T2 killing assay. Moreover, it incorporates a high-affinity CD3 binder derived from UCHT1 clone. Both bispecific antibodies, ATX-TCRm-BsAb1 and ATX-TCRm-BsAb2, specifically killed TF1 and SET-2 target cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 5B,C). This efficient cytotoxicity was reproducible using T cells from a second HLA-A *02+ donor (Figure 5D,E). Importantly, an isotype control bispecific antibody with the same CD3 binder and identical geometric format did not induce killing of TF1 or SET-2 cells. The active bispecific antibodies demonstrated sub-nanomolar EC50 values.

Together, these results demonstrate that the TCRm bispecific antibodies mediate potent cytotoxicity against WT1+/HLA-A *02+ cells in both T2 peptide-pulsing assays and endogenous cancer cell lines, and that this cytotoxicity is not reproduced by an isotype control bispecific of identical format and CD3 affinity.

Discussion

Monoclonal antibodies have emerged as a highly successful therapeutic modality across a range of diseases, particularly in oncology, with over 150 antibodies approved for clinical use. However, these therapies are limited to targeting cell surface or secreted proteins, which comprise only ~30% of the human proteome – leaving the vast majority of intracellular targets inaccessible.2,3 TCR-based approaches that recognize peptide-MHC (pHLA) complexes offer a powerful opportunity to access this intracellular antigen space with greater tumor specificity and reduced off-target effects in normal tissues.

Moreover, soluble T-cell engagers that incorporate TCR specificity, such as tebentafusp and IMC-F106C, have demonstrated both clinical efficacy and safety – particularly in solid tumors, where conventional surface-directed T-cell engagers have largely failed to make significant clinical impact.

TCRm antibodies represent a novel way of targeting intracellular targets. Discovery of highly specific TCRm antibodies relies on the generation of a repertoire of high-affinity antibodies paired with a robust specificity screening. Our platform combines immunization of ATX-GK mice, pHLA monomer generated by E. coli through refolding and purification, and bispecific engineering into a TCRm ×CD3 asymmetric T-cell engager format,

In this study, we generated monoclonal antibodies that mimic TCR recognition of pHLA complexes, using the WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex as a case study. We discovered high-affinity and high-specificity TCRm antibodies targeting WT1126–134 peptide according to functional assays and X-scan assays of the entire human proteome. Our lead molecule ATX-TCRm-06 binds to WT1126–134/pHLA-A *02:01 monomer with sub-nanomolar affinity (KD = 0.1 nM). Remarkably, ATX-TCRm-06 did not show binding to T2 cells pulsed with M13L (RMFPTPPSL) and PIGQ (RMFPGEVAL) peptides while control antibodies ESK1, 33H9, and 11D06 showed binding to one or two of these peptides. In addition, we demonstrated that a CD3-T-cell engager designed with ATX-TCRm-06 could effectively recruit and activate T cells to selectively kill WT1126–134 expressing target cells in the context of HLA-A *02:01 and not kill T2 cells pulsed with M13L peptides. These cytotoxicity data demonstrate that ATX-TCRm-06 binders are highly specific to WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex.

Cell-based X-scan has been employed to characterize the specificity of TCR and TCRm antibodies by analyzing the interaction epitope profile (SeqLogo) and potential off-target interactions using a proteome database. While this approach offers the advantage of natural peptide presentation, it is time- and labor-intensive. To overcome these limitations, we developed a phage display-based X-scan assay, enabling rapid assessment of TCRm antibody specificity and efficient characterization of multiple antibodies simultaneously. These specificity screening methods allow us to assess a diverse range of potential off-target peptides presented by HLA in the context of the entire human proteome. The top TCRm leads identified through our workflow did not show binding to other peptides in these assays. Moreover, X-scan allows for the identification of key contact residues between the antibody and the peptide on HLA. The control antibody ESK1 binding is mainly driven by the first residue. Conversely, binding of our lead molecule, ATX-TCRm-06, engages 5 residues on the WT1126–134 peptide, which is less tolerant to mutation.

These data demonstrate that specificity can be achieved with TCRm antibodies, shifting the paradigm between TCR and TCRm therapies. In comparison to TCR-based therapeutics, TCRm carries inherent advantages for facilitating scale-up and manufacturing. It was previously assumed that TCRs offer greater specificity after engineering, as in the case of tebentafusp, due to the difference in epitope binding footprint between TCR and TCRm.26 However, here we demonstrate that TCRm antibodies discovered through our platform can achieve high specificity by binding a similar epitope footprint to TCRs in the middle of the peptide of interest, conferring this high specificity.

Due to the low copy number of peptide/HLA complexes at the surface of tumor cells, TCRm require engineering as T-cell engagers or CAR T-cell therapies to reach necessary potency.27,28 We demonstrated that this ATX-TCRm-06 binder can produce specific tumor cell killing when engineered as a bispecific T-cell engager with a potent CD3 arm derived from the clinically validated SP34. In addition, we generated a second bispecific antibody format incorporating a high-affinity CD3 binder in an scFv–Fab configuration, analogous to tebentafusp targeting gp100. Two distinct TCRm binders engineered in this format demonstrated high potency and specificity against cancer cell lines with endogenous WT1 and HLA-A *02:01 expression. These encouraging results warrant further preclinical and clinical investigation to compare TCRm-based bispecific T‑cell engagers with each other and potentially with TCR-based therapeutics.

TCRm-based T-cell engagers represent a new class of antibody-based modality with great therapeutic potential to make many undruggable targets accessible to immunotherapies. Many targets related to cancer biology, including neoantigens, tumor driver antigens, lineage antigens, cancer testis antigens, and dark genome antigens, are localized intracellularly and cannot be accessed by traditional immunotherapies. Because these targets are highly and specifically expressed in tumors, novel immunotherapeutic modalities directed at these antigens could spare normal tissue and produce robust antitumor effects. By leveraging the power of T cells to exert potent effector functions, new therapeutic modalities could bring a greater therapeutic index and deliver novel therapy for high unmet clinical needs.

A limitation of this study is that direct cytotoxicity testing of ATX-TCRm-BsAb1 and ATX-TCRm-BsAb2 against WT1-negative, HLA-A *02:01-positive target cells were not performed. While reporter activation, cell-surface binding, and isotype-control cytotoxicity data are each consistent with strictly target-dependent activity, formal confirmation of selectivity in a primary T-cell killing assay against a WT1-negative HLA-A *02:01+ line remains to be established and is the subject of ongoing work.

Materials and methods

Peptide HLA *02:01 monomer and tetramer production

pHLA monomers generated by Immunitrack were shipped frozen to Alloy Therapeutics. pHLA monomer and tetramers were produced in-house by Immunitrack, as described previously in Leisner et al.29 Briefly, for HLA-I monomers or tetramers, E.coli-produced heavy chains were diluted into a reaction buffer (50 mM tris-maleate pH 6.6, 0.1% pluronic F86 NF BASF, a surfactant compatible with cellular use), with excess β2-microglobulin and excess peptide of interest and incubated at 18°C for 48 h. For tetramerization, 1:4 molar ratio of Streptavidin-APC or Streptavidin-PE was added sequentially to the peptide-HLA monomers over 1 h. The resulting PE- or APC-tetramers were stored at −20°C before use.

Animals

Female ATX-GK transgenic mice (Alloy Therapeutics), 6–8 weeks of age and approximately 20 g at the first immunization, were used (n = 12; cohort 1, n = 4; cohort 2, n = 8). ATX-GK mice express human antibody heavy- and light-chain variable regions; all animals were experimentally naive, immunocompetent and specific-pathogen-free. Mice were housed in the vivarium at Alloy Therapeutics (Waltham, MA) under standard conditions with ad libitum food and water and environmental enrichment and were acclimatized for at least 7 days before the first procedure.

Ethical approval and immunization

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Mispro Biotech Services Corporation (protocol 2022-ALY-01) and were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Immunizations were carried out under isoflurane anesthesia (3–5% induction, 1–3% maintenance). Animals were monitored throughout the immunization schedule, including weekly body weights, and were euthanized if predefined humane endpoints were met, including ≥ 20% body weight loss from baseline. At study completion, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation or isoflurane overdose followed by a secondary physical method, in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. ATX-GK mice (n = 12) were immunized subcutaneously weekly with 10 µg of WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01. After 5 weekly injections of WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex, mice were bled, and sera were prepared for testing by ELISA for binding to WT1126–134/HLA-A02:01 complex and CMVpp65/HLA-A *02:01 complex. After titer was measured by ELISA, the study was terminated, and spleens and lymph nodes were collected for isolation of antigen-specific B cells and generation of immune libraries for phage display. One section of the spleen of immunized mice was stored in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for library preparation while remaining cells were prepared as a cell suspension. Cell suspensions were also prepared with lymph nodes of immunized animals.

Immunization titers by ELISA

Briefly, 96-well Costar ELISA plates (Corning) were coated overnight at 4 º C with 1 µg/mL of WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 complex or 1 µg/mL of CMVpp65/HLA-A *02:01 complex. After 3 washes with PBS Tween, all wells were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sera from immunized animals collected at day 21 was serially diluted in 3% BSA ranging from 1:100 to 1:1,000,0000 dilution. After 3 washes with PBS Tween, sera dilutions were transferred to the ELISA plate and incubated for one hour at room temperature. After 3 washes with PBS Tween, secondary antibody was added to each well. After 1 h incubation at room temperature, plates were washed three times with PBS Tween and TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each well. After 5 min in the dark, stop solution was added to all wells and OD was measured at 450 nM with BioTek Synergy Neo2 Multi-Mode Plate Reader (#NEO2SM, Agilent Technologies).

Isolation of antigen-specific B cells and sequencing of antibody variable regions

Cells were then stained with a cocktail of antibodies (CD4, CD8, F6/40, CD19, IgM, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b) to isolate IgG+ B cells, WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 tetramer complex labeled in PE, and CMVpp65/HLA-A *02:01 tetramer complex labeled in Dylight 650. On FACS Aria II (BD Biosciences), we gated on Live cells/CD4 negative/CD8 negative, Gr-1 negative/F640-negative/IgM-negative/IgG-positive/CD19 positive cells that were positive for PE but negative for Dylight 650. Cells were sorted into individual wells in lysis buffer and stored at −80°C.

After thawing the single-cell plates, reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript IV single strand (Life Technologies #18091050). Once cDNA was generated, nested PCRs were performed to amplify variable regions from heavy and light chains. PCR products were sequenced using Sanger sequencing. Sequencing output was analyzed using Geneious Biologics software.

Variable region cloning of selected B cells and protein production

mRNA was extracted from sorted single B cells and reverse transcribed into cDNA using standard molecular biology protocols and Alloy proprietary primers. Heavy- and light -chain variable regions were amplified by PCR. PCR amplicons for next-generation sequencing (NGS) and Sanger sequencing were then performed by internally developed protocols.

Generation of immune-phage libraries and panning for WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01

Heavy- and light-chain variable regions were amplified from the spleens of high-titer immunized mice by reverse transcription followed by PCR amplification using gene-specific primers. The variable regions were cloned into phage-display vectors designed to express Fabs on phage g3p protein and transformed into TG1 bacteria. Phage libraries expressing unique Fabs were amplified via co-infection with M13 helper phage and purified by PEG precipitation.

Phage were allowed to bind to biotinylated negative mixed peptide-HLA-A *02:01 captured on streptavidin magnetic beads to deplete the library of nonspecific binders. The non-bound phage was then incubated with biotinylated WT1126–134 /HLA-A *02:01 captured on streptavidin magnetic beads with an excess of the negative mixed peptide in solution. Remaining phage bound to WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 beads after several stringent washes were eluted using a basic triethylamine solution and neutralized with Tris buffer pH 8.0. Eluted phages were reinfected into TG1 bacterial cells and amplified and purified as before. This panning step was repeated for a second round.

Polyclonal pools of phage from pre- and post-panning outputs were tested by ELISA to gauge enrichment of WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01-specific phage and depletion of negative mixed peptide-HLA-A *02:01 reactive phage. Post-panning phage were diluted and infected into SS320 cells, and four 96-well plates of monoclonal bacteria were selected to express soluble Fabs. SS320 Fab containing lysates binding to WT1 WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 and not to negative mixed peptide-HLA-A *02:01 were detected by ELISA. Variable heavy- and light-chain regions of positive binders were sequenced from single infected bacterial colonies using a rolling circle amplification and standard Sanger sequencing.

Antibody expression

The designed construct was cloned into vectors designed to express full length antibodies as aglycosylated IgGs in HEK293 cells under the control of a CMV promoter as described above and handed off for production. Antibody expression vectors were complexed with polyethylenimine and transfected into HEK293 cultures. After 5 days of growth at 37°C, 8% CO2, antibodies were captured on magnetic protein A resin. After several washes with PBS, antibodies were eluted in an acidic buffer (100 mM citrate, pH 3) and neutralized with HEPES to pH ~ 6. Elution aliquots were then assessed for purity via analytical size exclusion chromatography (SEC) (YMC-200 4.6 × 150 mm, 3 µM) and the highest purity fractions were subsequently pooled. Preparative size exclusion chromatography (SEC; s200 Increase column run in PBS pH 6.5) was then performed to remove higher molecular weight impurities. Elution aliquots were assessed and pooled as described previously. Samples were then sterile filtered and stored for later use at −80°C or 4°C for long- or short-term storage, respectively.

Kd determination with Carterra binding

Kinetic experiments were performed on Carterra LSA with a running buffer (HBSTE, 10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.05% Tween 20). Immobilization of goat anti-human IgG capture antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) onto a HC30M sensor was performed using a standard amine-coupling procedure. The sensor chip was activated by 0.2 M EDC/0.05 M sulfo-NHS. Subsequently, the capture antibodies were immobilized at 15 µg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate pH 4.5, followed by quenching with 0.5 M ethanolamine. Test antibodies were captured on the anti-human Fc capture chip prepared with a HC30M chip.

For kinetics analysis, WT1 pHLA monomer from Immunitrack or prepared internally at a concentration from 0.076 nM to 1500 nM (a serial 3-fold dilution) was injected sequentially. For each concentration, there was 5 min association followed by 15 min dissociation. Results were processed and analyzed in Carterra LSA Kinetics Software. The kinetic data was referenced with the interstitial reference spots and double-referenced to a buffer cycle, then fit globally to a 1:1 binding model to determine apparent association and dissociation kinetic rate constants (ka and kd values). The ratio kd/ka was used to derive the KD value of each antigen/mAb interaction, i.e. KD = kd/ka.

Epitope binning

A high-throughput epitope binning experiment was performed on real-time label-free biosensors (Carterra LSA) to sort large panels of mAbs into bins based on their ability to block antigen binding of other mAbs. In a pairwise epitope binning analysis, WT1 monomer and antibody 2 (analyte antibody) were sequentially applied to the sensor chip (HC200M) covalently pre-loaded with antibody 1 (ligand antibody). An increase in response upon exposure to the analyte antibody indicates non-competition between the two antibodies, whereas a lack of change in the signal indicates competition. Antibodies having the same blocking profiles toward others in the test set are grouped into one bin.

Community network plots were used to explore clustering of mAbs that share similar but not necessarily identical competition profiles. Rather than relying strictly on the sandwiching/blocking assignments in the heat map as the bin network plots do, hierarchical clustering is applied to the sorted heat map to generate dendrograms, which progressively group mAbs.

T2 cell binding assay

T2 cells were cultured with 5 µg/mL β2 macroglobulin and 50 µM WT1 or control peptides overnight. On the next day, the cells were tested in a binding assay with anti-WT1 antibodies at concentrations from 100 nM to 0.6 pM (a serial 3-fold dilution) for 45 min on ice. Cells were then incubated with the secondary antibody R-Phycoerythrin AffiniPure Goat Anti-Human IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch #109–115-098). The data was acquired on Intellicyt iQue3 VBR or FACSCanto II. Median fluorescence intensities (MFI) were plotted against antibody concentrations. EC50 was derived from fitting to a four-parameter dose-response curve.

Peptide-specificity ELISA assay

High-binding ELISA plates (Nunc MaxiSorp 384-well, Invitrogen) were coated with TCRm antibodies (anti-WT1/HLA-A *02:01, 100 ng/well) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After three washes with PBST, the plates were blocked with 100 μL of 4% PBSM for 2 h at room temperature. Phages displaying different single-chain pHLA were diluted in 2% PBSTM, and their titers were normalized to 6.6 × 106 cfu/μL. After blocking, 1.6 × 108 cfu of each phage solution was applied to the wells and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Following the binding step, the plates were washed three times with PBST, and a diluted anti-G8P-HRP solution (1:5000, Antibody Design Laboratories) was added to each well for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional washes with PBST, TMB (Promega) was added and incubated for 1–5 min until blue color development was observed. The reaction was quenched with acidic ELISA TMB Stop Solution (Promega), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy Neo2 Multi-Mode Plate Reader.

Phage display-based X-scan assay

The X-scan library was displayed on the surface of phage as single-chain trimers (peptide-linker-β2 m-linker-HLA-A *02:01 with the Y84A mutation). The Y84A mutation was introduced into the HLA-A2 heavy chain to accommodate the linker. Nine oligonucleotide pools were designed and cloned into a phagemid vector containing the single-chain HLA-A2 sequence. Diversity at the peptide region was encoded by sequentially introducing 20 amino acids at each position of the WT1 peptide (RMFPNAPYL), resulting in a theoretical diversity of 172. The cloned phagemid library was then electroporated into E. coli TG1 cells and cultured in 2XYT medium containing glucose and carbenicillin until reaching an OD600 of 0.5. CM13 helper phage (Antibody Design Laboratories) was added to the culture, which was incubated for 45 min at 37°C. The culture was then pelleted, resuspended in 2XYT medium containing carbenicillin and kanamycin, and incubated overnight at 30°C with shaking. Phage particles were precipitated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and resuspended in PBS for titering. Two hundred nanomolar biotinylated TCRm antibodies were captured on streptavidin-coated beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated with 1 × 1011 phage particles for 1 h at room temperature with rotation. After incubation, the beads were pulled down using a magnet and washed six times with PBST, followed by three washes with PBS. Phage particles were then eluted with TEA and quenched with 1 M Tris buffer pH 8. The eluted phage particles were used to infect E. coli TG1 cells, which were then plated on 2XYT plates. Colonies were collected and resuspended in 2XYT containing glucose, carbenicillin, and 20% glycerol. After two rounds of panning, phagemid DNA from the different pools (original X-scan pool, first panning pool, second panning pool) was extracted and analyzed by NGS (Genewiz).

Bispecific generation: sequence selection and cloning

To initiate bispecific generation, a lead pair of sequences was established. An anti-CD3 scFv (VH-VL orientation) was selected (clone DM38) to pair with an anti-WT1 TCRm Fab sequence (ATX-TCRm-06). This molecule was designed in the same fashion as 1:1 Knob-in-Hole bispecifics (monovalent Fab-scFv-Fc) with the anti-WT1 Fab sequence on the Hole arm and anti-CD3 scFv (VH-VL) sequence on the Knob arm. The Knob arm Fc contained a 6x Histidine tag on the C-terminus of the Fc for ease of purification.

Sequences were compiled of the variable heavy- and light-chain pairs and scFv sequences. Synthetic DNA was ordered through Integrated DNA Technologies, with designs for the Knob-in-Hole bispecifics (monovalent Fab-scFv-Fc). KIH (Knob-in-Hole) mutations plus disulfide stabilizers used in the constant region designs were as follows: Knob: T366W (S354C) & Hole: T366S. L368A. Y407V (Y349C). The constant region isotype for the clone was hIgG1-Aglycosylated (N297A). For the anti-CD3 sequence a VH-VL orientation was used with a (G4S)4 linker between the VH and VL of the scFv. A disulfide stabilizer (Hc44C/Lc100C) was also included in the VH and VL scFv. Both the Fc and scFv disulfide stabilizers were included to maximize molecular stability during production.

The construct was cloned using Gibson Assembly via the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Cloning Kit (NEB). After Gibson Assembly the construct was transformed using DH5a competent cells (NEB) and plated onto LB-CARB trays (Teknova), then grown overnight at 37°C with shaking. Single colonies were then picked from each construct on the LB-CARB trays for inoculation into LB-CARB Media (Teknova, prepared fresh) and grown overnight at 37°C with shaking in 15 mL Falcon tubes, then seeded in 5 mL LB-CARB media.

The cultured cells tubes were pelleted, media was discarded, and plasmid purification was initiated using the Qiagen Plasmid Plus Midiprep purification kits (for yield), using a vacuum manifold and centrifugation. Plasmid DNA concentration was assessed (NanoDrop) and sent for confirmatory Sanger sequencing. 24 h following the sequencing run, the plasmid DNA constructs were sequence confirmed and transferred for purification.

A second format of bispecific Abs derived from ATX-TCRm-01 and ATX-TCRm-06 binders to HLA-A *02:01/WT1 were engineered, comprising a CD3 antibody derived UCHT1 as an scFv with a (G4S) 4 linker between heavy and light variable regions fused through a (G4S)2 linker to the N-terminus of the light chain of a WT1126–134/HLA-A *02:01 targeting-Fab. The bispecific Abs were incorporated into a half-life extension domain fused through a (G4S) 2 linker to a single domain fragment targeting HSA at the C-terminus of the Fab heavy chain. Both specific Abs were produced by transient mammalian expression in a Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell line, captured from clarified conditioned media using CH1 affinity chromatography, and polished by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column.

Cell lines and primary cell cultures

NFAT Luciferase Reporter Jurkat cells (#60621, BPS Bioscience) were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium (ATCC modification, #A1049101, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; #S11550H, R&D Research) and 1 mg/mL Geneticin (for reporter Jurkat cells; #10131035, Gibco) using standard cell culture techniques. Cells were grown in suspension at 37°C, 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere, and the cell density was kept below 2 × 106 cells/mL in culture media. The transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP) deficient T2 cells (CRL-1992, ATCC) were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM; #30–2005, ATCC) and 20% FBS.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donor leukopaks (STEMCELL Technologies) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep (#07851, STEMCELL Technologies). The isolated PBMCs were frozen in CryoStor® CS5 Cell Freezing Media (#07933, STEMCELL Technologies) at 50 × 106 cells/mL concentration for further use. CD8+ T cells were isolated from the frozen PBMCs by using EasySep™ Human CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (#17953, STEMCELL) and cultured in X-Vivo 15 (#04-418Q, Lonza) supplemented with 10% FBS, recombinant human IL-2 (5 ng/mL; #589106, Biolegend) and IL-7 (2.5 ng/mL; #581904, Biolegend). Upon isolation, CD8+ T cells were cultured at 1 × 106 cells/mL density and activated with ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28/CD2 T cell Activator (#10990, STEMCELL Technologies) at 25 μL Immunocult per 1 × 106 cells dose. On day 1, the cell culture media was doubled with more T-cell media. The cell culture media was doubled again on days 2 and 5 of activation. On day 7, the cells were collected, and the dead cells were removed by density gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep. After the centrifugation, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, #10010023, Gibco), and the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL in T‑cell media. On day 9, the cells were counted again, and the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL in T-cell media. The expanded CD8+ T cells were collected on day 12 of activation and frozen in CryoStor® CS5 Cell Freezing Media at 15–30 × 106 cells/mL. TF-1 (ATCC) and SET-2 (DSMZ) were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mM HEPES, and 1X Glutamax (Gibco).

Reporter cell assay with NFAT-RE Jurkat cells and peptide-pulsed T2 cells

T2 cells were collected and washed with PBS. The cells were resuspended in serum-free IMDM media at 1 × 106 cells/mL concentration. Beta2-microglobulin (beta2m) was added to the cells at 5 µg/mL concentration to stabilize the MHC complex on the cell surface. Synthetic peptides corresponding to Wilms Tumor 1 (WT1126–134, RMFPNAPYL) and Mediator of RNA polymerase II transcription subunit 13-like (Med13L863-871, RMFPTPPSL) were ordered from Pierce Custom Peptides (Thermo Fisher) and solubilized in 25% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; #D12345, Invitrogen) in water (v/v) at 3 mM concentration. T2 cells were either pulsed with 50 µM of the peptides for up to 2 hours at 37°C.

White-walled and flat-bottomed 96-well plates (#3917, Corning) and RPMI-1640 and 10% FBS media were used for the reporter cell assays. The target cell (T2) count was fixed at 50,000 cells/well and the effector cell count (NFAT-RE Jurkats) was fixed at 100,000 cells/well (E:T = 1:1). The co-culture was also treated with a fixed concentration of the CD3 ×WT1 bispecific antibody (ATX-P-5663) or the control CD3 scFv-Fc molecule at 100 nM. The cultures were incubated for 6 hours and the luciferase activity within the cells was assessed by using the One-Step™ Luciferase Assay System (#60690, BPS Bioscience) following the manufacturer’s protocol. A BioTek Synergy Neo2 Multi-Mode Plate Reader was used to measure the luminescence signal.

Cytotoxicity assays with CD8 T-cells and peptide-pulsed T2 cells

Cytotoxic effects of the CD8 T cells in the presence of the bispecific molecules were assessed by flow cytometry. To prepare the target cells, the T2 cells were collected and stained with the Cytolight Rapid Green Dye (#4705, Sartorius), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The labeled T2 cells were then pulsed with a serial dilution of the WT1 peptide (WT1126–134, RMFPNAPYL) ranging from 5 µM-500 pM for up to 2 hours at 37°C. For the effector cells, previously isolated and expanded CD8 T cells were thawed and rested in RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS +5 ng/mL IL-2 media overnight.

The target and effector cell count were fixed at 40,000 cells/well with E:T = 1: 1). After the overnight incubation of the co-cultures (16–22 hours) in the presence or absence of the bispecific molecules at 100 nM, the assay plate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed with the flow buffer once and then stained with the BV421-CD8 antibody and the APC-conjugated viability dye as described above. After washing the cells and just before the acquisition, CountBrightTM Absolute Counting Beads (#C36950, Invitrogen) were added to the wells (30 µL/well) and mixed with the samples. For calculating the remaining target cells that were left in each well, the following formula was used: Absolute target cell count = (Counting beads count in 30 µL x target cell count)/counting beads count.

Lentivirus production and cancer cell engineering

Lentivirus was produced via transient transfection of 293T cells. Briefly, psPAX (gag-pol), pMD2.G (VSV-G) and transfer plasmid (EF1a-eGFP-IRES-Blasticidin resistance) were pooled in a 3:1:4 ratio, respectively, and Genejuice transfection reagent (Millipore Sigma) was used to transfect 293T cells following the manufacturer’s protocol. 72 hours after transfection, culture supernatants were harvested, filtered through a 0.45um PES membrane, and snap frozen at −80°C. To create eGFP expressing target cells, TF-1 (ATCC) and SET-2 (DSMZ) were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mM HEPES, and 1X Glutamax (Gibco). Cells were spinfected by addition of viral supernatant +8ug/ml polybrene, followed by centrifugation at 1200xg for 1 hour at 18 °C. 72 hours after transduction, transduced cells were selected using Blasticidin S HCl (Thermofisher). GFP+ cells were further purified via FACS.

Cytotoxicity assays with CD8 T cells and cancer endogenous cell lines

Cytotoxicity experiments were conducted in XVIVO 15 media (Lonza) supplemented with 10% human serum (Gemini Bio). 50,000 target (GFP-TF-1 or GFP-SET-2) cells were seeded into the wells of a round bottom, 96 well tissue culture plate, and co-cultured with 50,000 previously expanded human CD8 T cells. BiTEs were added to final concentrations ranging from 1pM to 10 nM, and cells were cultured for 3 days. Subsequently, a fixed number of Count bright Absolute Counting beads (Invitrogen) were added to each well, and live GFP+ target cells were enumerated via flow cytometry. Percent killing was calculated by comparing the number of live target cells in test wells to the number of target cells in control wells (which contained both targets and effector cells but not BiTE) using the following formula: % Killing = 1- ((# Target cells in test condition)/(# of target cells in control well))*100
