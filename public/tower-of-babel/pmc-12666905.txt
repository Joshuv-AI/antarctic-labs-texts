Tachycardia alert from an implantable loop recorder after brain magnetic resonance imaging—The importance of patient history
Authors: Jill Boone Schaeffer, Jill Martin, Sandeep Bansal
Published: 2025

Introduction

Implantable loop recorders (ILRs) are increasingly prescribed for continuous rhythm monitoring after cryptogenic stroke. These devices transmit rhythm abnormalities to the device clinic via home monitoring. When atrial fibrillation is detected, anticoagulation can be prescribed to prevent further strokes. ILRs are programmed at implantation to also identify other abnormal rhythms, such as tachycardia or bradycardia.

Case report

In March 2025, a 75-year-old man experienced an ischemic stroke in the left posterior cerebral artery, confirmed via imaging. He had presented to the emergency department with symptoms of nausea, right visual field loss, and left-sided weakness. Medical therapy was initiated, including aspirin, a statin, and clopidogrel, as tenecteplase could not be administered because of presentation beyond the therapeutic window. On the day of discharge, an Abbott Assert-IQ ICM (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] conditional) was implanted to monitor for atrial fibrillation as a potential cause of the stroke.

Two weeks after discharge, the patient was readmitted because of left-sided facial tingling and noted cognitive impairment since the stroke. Brain MRI (1.5 T) was performed, which revealed continued evolution of the left posterior cerebral artery infarct with petechial hemorrhage in the infarct bed but no new areas of restricted diffusion. He was discharged home.

The same day, the device clinic received an alert transmission for a tachycardic event. The electrograms showed the presenting rhythm (Figure 1A) and a wide complex tachycardia, raising concerns for ventricular tachycardia (VT) lasting 28 seconds with an average rate of 202 beats/min (Figure 1B). Upon review by our device clinic team and an electrophysiology physician, the patient was advised to return to the hospital for arrhythmia evaluation. Diagnostic testing, including laboratory work and pharmacologic stress testing, did not reveal any significant abnormality. The patient denied any cardiac symptoms.

The electrophysiology inpatient team was consulted regarding the rhythm. Electrograms were fully reviewed, and the patient and family were interviewed. During those discussions, it was realized that the patient had been hospitalized at the time of the event. A review of telemetry tracings from the prior hospitalization revealed no VT or supraventricular tachycardia. Centralized telemetry records indicated that the alert occurred while the patient was undergoing MRI. Furthermore, the ladder-like appearance on the device scatterplot as well as the initiation of the tachycardia revealed the possibility of a nonphysiologic artifact (Figure IB). The rhythm tracings, initially concerning for VT, were thus identified as artifacts caused by the MRI scan.

Discussion

Several reports have highlighted artifacts resembling wide or narrow tachycardias during MRI scans.1, 2, 3 Modern ILRs are labeled as MRI conditional; however, even these devices can experience temporary interference during scanning. The radiofrequency pulses and gradient magnetic fields used in MRI can cause electromagnetic interference in ILRs, resulting in false arrhythmia detection, temporary signal dropout, or noise artifacts.4 MRI guidelines for this ILR recommend postscan interrogation to review event detection and programmed parameters and to clear any data that may have been collected during the MRI scan.5 This postscan interrogation of ILRs is not routinely performed at our institution. This case has prompted discussion regarding communication between the MRI suite and our device clinic to raise awareness of possible alerts that may be received after MRI scanning.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of reviewing event timing and thoroughly investigating alert notifications in patients with ILRs. Proper evaluation can prevent unnecessary interventions and hospitalizations, ensuring accurate diagnosis and patient safety. Although perceived to be rare, artifact from MRI or other devices mimicking tachycardia is likely underreported.
