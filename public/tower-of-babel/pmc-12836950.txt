Assessing reporting completeness and characteristics in decentralized randomized controlled trials: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Juntao Wang, Shuhui Li, Chen Zhao, Juan Wang, Weifeng Xiong, Lihan Hu, Xuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the development of remote technology, decentralized clinical trials have rapidly increased in recent years. Despite the comprehensive reporting guidance provided by the CONSORT 2025 guideline, decentralized randomized controlled trials still face specific reporting challenges. Therefore, we aim to evaluate the reporting characteristics and completeness of decentralized randomized controlled trials, using a specially designed checklist that has been extended according to the CONSORT 2025 original items.MethodsWe will search six databases (Embase, Ovid MEDLINE®, PubMed, CNKI, VIP, Wanfang) for decentralized randomized controlled trials published in Chinese or English during 2023. A pre-designed form will be used to extract general characteristics. Reporting completeness will be assessed using a custom 26-item “CONSORT-DRCT” checklist. This checklist includes 17 items focused on design, implementation, quality control, and key technologies related to decentralized elements, while 9 items pertain specifically to intervention details (5 for pharmacological interventions and 4 for non-pharmacological interventions). To ensure consistency, a standard operating procedure for scoring reporting completeness will be developed. Each item will be evaluated independently by two reviewers, and inter-rater agreement will be calculated to assess consistency between reviewers. Data will undergo descriptive analysis, with subgroup analyses performed where applicable to explore factors influencing reporting completeness.DiscussionThis protocol outlines a methodological framework for conducting the review, which aims to identify the current reporting characteristics and completeness of decentralized randomized controlled trials. The results may offer valuable insights into the development of a CONSORT extension guideline for decentralized randomized controlled trials.Systematic review registrationThis study has been registered with the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/c9pdx/).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-03045-1.

Background

According to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), a decentralized clinical trial (DCT) is defined as “some or all of the trial-related activities occur at locations other than traditional clinical trial sites” [1]. DCTs are innovative approaches to clinical research that leverage digital technologies to address the limitations of traditional site-based trials [2]. They are preferred in study designs with low-to-medium complexities and especially useful in cases where patients present with chronic diseases or clinical conditions, have limited ability to ambulate freely, use self-administrable investigational products, and have a low safety risk profile [3]. The survey findings suggest that cancer clinical trials leveraging remote technology and decentralization tools to reduce patient time and travel burden associated with participation may increase the patient consent rate [4, 5]. The REMOTE (Randomized Evaluation of Methods and Outcomes using Telemedicine for Efficiency) trial conducted by Pfizer in 2011 is often regarded as one of the first fully DCTs in the pharmaceutical industry [6, 7]. The COVID-19 pandemic has had a significant impact on clinical research, prompting many ongoing and newly initiated clinical trials to adapt by decentralizing key elements such as enrollment processes, management of study interventions, and safety monitoring [8, 9]. As of 2024, the world’s largest DCT is widely recognized as the COVID-19 cohort follow-up study under the NIH-led RECOVER project. This study uses decentralized methods—including remote enrollment, electronic consent, online surveys, wearables, and at-home sample collection—to gather health data from diverse populations. Aiming to enroll over 100,000 participants across the U.S., RECOVER seeks to identify the causes and treatments for long-term COVID-19 symptoms [10].

In response to the operational shifts prompted by the COVID-19 pandemic, regulatory agencies worldwide have issued new guidelines and frameworks. In March 2020, the U.S. FDA issued its first regulatory guidance, detailing that remote follow-up visits, electronic informed consent, and other DCT elements could be adopted during the pandemic. In the years that followed, multiple countries subsequently released laws, regulations, or regulatory frameworks related to DCTs, as shown in Fig. 1 [11–15]. In May 2023, the FDA released the world’s first dedicated draft guidance on DCTs, titled Decentralized Clinical Trials for Drugs, Biological Products, and Devices. The final version was published in September 2024, providing the first systematic regulatory framework for DCTs [16]. This document clearly defines DCTs, applicable scenarios, and regulatory requirements, providing a robust framework for the comprehensive implementation and operation of decentralized clinical trials. China is another country that began conducting DCTs relatively early. However, as of 2024, all provisions related to DCT elements are mainly scattered across documents such as the Technical Guidelines for Patient-Centered Drug Clinical Trial Design (Interim) [17, 18]. In a significant step, the Center for Drug Evaluation of China’s NMPA issued the Technical Guidelines for the Application of Decentralized Clinical Trials in Drug Development for Rare Diseases in May 2024, marking the first guidance specifically focused on the application of decentralized clinical trials in a particular disease area. Notably, this guideline is exclusively applicable to rare diseases. These documents primarily contain fragmented regulations focusing on individual aspects of this new clinical trial model—such as remote monitoring, remote visits, and the use of digital technologies—rather than providing comprehensive guidance for DCTs.Fig. 1A Chronology of global DCT regulatory frameworks. As DCT methods are used in chronic disease research, rare disease trials, and rapid responses to epidemics, deficiencies in reporting may compromise the validity of evidence and its applicability in clinical practice. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are considered the gold standard in interventional study design and are classified as grade A clinical evidence. The completeness of reporting for RCTs is primarily guided by the CONSORT statement. The CONSORT statement was initially released in 1996 and updated in 2001 and 2010 [19–21], and its latest version (2025 edition) comprises 30 essential items designed to standardize RCT reporting [22]. The latest CONSORT-2025 statement establishes an essential and comprehensive framework for reporting all RCTs. Building upon this foundation, the unique characteristics of decentralized randomized controlled trials—such as their reliance on technology and remote execution—call for tailored reporting considerations to ensure their reliability and applicability. Firstly, unique aspects of remote trials—such as the use of technological tools, quality control, and decentralized execution processes—urgently require further clarification in reporting. In addition, reports should clearly indicate whether the trial is fully remote or uses a hybrid model, to ensure methodological transparency and the credibility of results. The CONSORT statement appropriately calls for reporting the trial environment (such as community or hospital) and location. To adapt this principle to DCTs, it becomes crucial to specify details unique to remote settings, particularly the nature of the home-based environment. This omission may lead to inconsistencies in describing trial conduct in diverse environments, such as home-based settings. Furthermore, reports should specify whether participants need technical skills to participate in remote trials, and how these skills are assessed and supported. Finally, details related to pharmacological or non-pharmacological interventions—such as training, logistics, communication, and adherence monitoring—are critical for successful trial implementation, but current CONSORT guidelines do not provide specific requirements for DCTs.

There is currently no official CONSORT extension guideline specifically for DCTs, and a systematic assessment of current reporting practices is also lacking. To address this gap, this protocol outlines a methodological framework for conducting a review aimed at identifying the reporting characteristics and completeness of current DCTs, thereby providing a reference for the potential development of a CONSORT extension for DCTs in the future. We selected decentralized randomized controlled trials published in 2023, not only because our study commenced in April 2024, but also because 2023 represents a transitional period in global COVID-19 pandemic management. This period not only sustained the technological momentum of accelerated DCT application during the pandemic but also witnessed the systematic restoration of traditional clinical trial models [23]. Reporting of DCTs from 2021–2022 largely adhered to COVID-19 pandemic–era reporting standards and differed considerably from typical DCT reporting [24]. After the WHO announced in 2023 that COVID-19 no longer constituted a global public health emergency, subsequently published DCTs have been more representative and more amenable to analysis. By focusing on this transitional year, our review aims to generate foundational data to support future guideline development. To avoid ambiguity, we note that while this section summarizes regulatory developments up to September 2024 (and cites CONSORT 2025), these documents were used solely to contextualize the policy landscape and to inform the construction of our DCT reporting checklist. They did not inform study identification or inclusion; our dataset is strictly limited to DCTs published in 2023.

Methods

Study registration and reporting

We will prospectively register this protocol on PROSPERO prior to the start of screening. If PROSPERO does not accept the review type, we will register on INPLASY and provide the registration ID and DOI. This review has been registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/c9pdx/), and any protocol amendments will be dated and updated in both the registry record (PROSPERO or INPLASY) and the OSF page. The study protocol has been reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, along with the PRISMA-P checklist (see Additional file 1) [25]. This review uses only publicly available reports and does not involve interaction with human participants or access to identifiable information. According to institutional policy, it is exempt from human-subjects ethics review. No informed consent is required.

Publications in English or Chinese published between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, will be systematically screened for eligibility. To develop and finalize the DCT reporting checklist, we consulted CONSORT 2025 and relevant regulatory/guidance documents available up to September 2024. These sources were used for tool development only and did not influence study identification or selection. Eligible trials must pre-specify and implement at least one of the following decentralized elements in the protocol or methods section, replacing or augmenting on-site conduct: (1) Remote informed consent or eConsent (including video/online processes). (2) Remote recruitment and eligibility assessment (online screening, video assessments, remote verification). (3) Remote intervention delivery (e.g., tele-visits/video consultations, home-based therapy/training, direct-to-patient or pharmacy dispensing of investigational products). (4) Remote outcome assessment or follow-up (tele-visits, online surveys/ePROs, home sampling/home phlebotomy, local lab testing with electronic data transfer). (5) Wearables/mobile apps/patient portals used for continuous or intermittent remote data capture/monitoring. (6) Home visits by study staff or completion of key trial activities at community/pharmacy/local facilities with electronic linkage to the central site. Minimal, retention-only contacts do not qualify. Non-qualifying example: trials that only use phone or text for scheduling or retention, while all key activities (consent, intervention, assessments) occur on-site.

Search strategy

Comprehensive searches have been conducted in six electronic databases: Embase, Ovid MEDLINE®, PubMed, CNKI (Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure), VIP Database, and Wanfang Data. The search strategy was restricted to studies published between 1 January 2023 and 31 December 2023, with language limitations to English or Chinese. To ensure reproducibility, we provide complete electronic search strategies in Additional file 2, including controlled vocabulary (e.g., Emtree/MeSH), field tags (e.g., Ovid.mp/.tw, PubMed MeSH and Text Word, and subject fields in Chinese databases), Boolean operators, language and publication date limits (English or Chinese; 1 January–31 December 2023), and human/animal filters (e.g., PubMed “NOT (animals[MeSH] NOT humans[MeSH])”; Ovid limit to humans). The final searches were run on 10 May 2024.

Screening and study selection

All identified citations will be collected and uploaded in EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) and deduplicated in two stages. Stage 1 will use automated matching on title, authors, year, DOI/PMID, and journal; Stage 2 will involve manual verification by two independent reviewers to resolve remaining duplicates. Two independent reviewers will screen the titles and abstracts of the identified studies. After completing the initial screening of titles and abstracts, they will proceed to review the full texts. Potentially eligible studies will be retrieved in their entirety and thoroughly evaluated against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion during full-text screening will be documented and reported in the results section. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or consultation with the third reviewer. The third reviewer will also independently verify the final list of included studies and the documented reasons for exclusion at the full text stage. Data extraction

A reviewer will use the standardized extraction form provided in Additional file 3 to extract data from the full text, which will be independently verified by a second reviewer. The extracted study characteristics data will be categorized into three key aspects:General Information: This includes language, journal and impact factor, corresponding author information, country/region of the corresponding author, funding, and protocol availability.Trial Features: This encompasses randomization methods, allocation concealment mechanisms, clinical trial phase, allocation ratio and framework, and study purpose. Additionally, it includes blinding, participant characteristics (such as sample size, disease type, and age), intervention details (including TCM-related intervention, type of control, and duration of treatment), follow-up period, and safety monitoring.Unique aspects of DCTs: This involves the type of DCT (fully/hybrid remote), the types of remote methods and tools used across various trial steps (such as recruitment, screening, baseline, intervention, follow-up), and methods for monitoring remote adherence. The extraction form will be iteratively revised to reflect emerging data requirements during the extraction process. Prior to commencing comprehensive data extraction, researchers will undergo training in standardized procedures. Subsequently, we will randomly select a sample of 20 included studies for a data extraction trial to calibrate consistency in interpretation. Issues of inconsistency or inaccuracy in data extraction will be addressed through a tiered consultation process: initially through communication with a senior researcher, and if the issues persist, through consultation with the entire research team to reach consensus.

Reporting completeness assessment

The research team developed a checklist specifically for assessing the reporting completeness of DCTs based on the CONSORT 2025 checklist, known as the CONSORT-DRCT checklist (see Table 1). Notably, while the assessment tool draws on CONSORT 2025 and guidance available up to September 2024, the trial dataset is limited to DCTs published in 2023. Rather than extending every CONSORT 2025 item, we selected those most relevant to decentralized design, conduct, and reporting; these selected items were then, where appropriate, formulated into one or more assessment questions in the final CONSORT-DRCT checklist, enabling a systematic evaluation of reporting completeness. The checklist contains 26 items, focusing on decentralized design in trials and its impact. We have designed detailed judgment rules for each item. For example, Q1 (Does the title contain specific descriptors indicating DCTs?), “Fully reported” is considered if the reader can determine from the title that the article uses a DCT design. This is the case if the title includes the following: (1) words directly indicating decentralization, such as “decentralized”; or (2) words indicating the use of digital technology, such as “digital”, “digitalization”, “eHealth”, “mHealth”, “computer”, “texting”, “short message service (SMS)”, “social media”; or (3) words indicating remote aspects, such as “tele-”, “online”, “remote”. "Partially or not reported" is considered when the title does not include words related to decentralization, digital technology, or remote aspects. In the checklist, aside from the 17 items concerning the general characteristics of DCTs, we have established two evaluation standards specifically for the intervention section, differentiating between pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions: 5 items (QP) are dedicated to pharmacological interventions, and 4 items (QN) pertain to non-pharmacological interventions. Generally, each item can be scored in one of three ways: “1” for “Fully reported,” “0” for “Partially or not reported,” and “NA” for “Not applicable.” For example, QN3 (Does the study describe how decentralized non-pharmacological therapies were controlled and quality-checked in the “Methods” section?), “Fully reported” is considered if the reader can understand from the methods section the quality control methods in decentralized non-pharmacological intervention. This specifically includes: 1. the presence of standard operating procedures (SOPs) for the intervention; or 2. the involvement of dedicated quality control personnel; or 3. the implementation process is based on standardized references (such as research literature or joint statements). “Partially or not reported” is considered when the reader cannot understand from the methods section the quality control methods in decentralized non-pharmacological intervention. “Not applicable” refers to studies that did not use decentralized non-pharmacological intervention. For details on the SOPs for completeness assessment items, content, and scoring rules (see Additional file 4).

Before the evaluation begins, all evaluators will receive training from senior researchers and participate in pilot assessments. A random sample comprising approximately 20% of the total included articles will be selected for reporting completeness evaluation to calibrate inter-rater reliability and refine the evaluation criteria. Inter-rater reliability will be quantified using unweighted Cohen’s kappa (κ) and raw percent agreement, each with 95% confidence intervals. During the pilot, we will compute item-level and overall κ; a priori, κ ≥ 0.70 and raw percent agreement ≥ 80% will be considered acceptable. If κ < 0.70 for any critical item, we will revise the guidance, retrain reviewers, and repeat the pilot until thresholds are met. Disagreements will be resolved by consensus or adjudication by a senior reviewer, with reasons documented. The evaluation forms, scoring rules, and SOPs will be iteratively revised based on the pilot results. During the formal completeness assessment phase, each item will be evaluated by two reviewers in a paired, blinded manner, and inter-reviewer agreement will be reported as both percent agreement at the item level and overall.

Data analysis

The extracted data will be collated, synthesized, and summarized according to the analytical framework of the study. Categorical variables will be summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables will be presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). All data will be collected and recorded using Microsoft Excel 365. Descriptive statistics will be calculated using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

First, we will provide a detailed description of the characteristics of the included DCTs. Furthermore, the reporting completeness of all included trials will undergo quantitative analysis. We will assess reporting using the 26-item CONSORT-DRCT checklist. Each item is scored as fully reported = 1, partially reported or not reported = 0; NA items are excluded from the denominator. The overall study score (%) is computed as (sum of scores for applicable items/number of applicable items) × 100. We will also compute domain scores for decentralized elements (17 items) and intervention-specific items (pharmacological QP or non-pharmacological QN, as applicable). For trials with both pharmacological and non-pharmacological components, both sets of intervention items are applicable and reported. Reporting completeness tiers are prespecified as excellent (> 90%), moderate (65–90%), and suboptimal (< 65%). This reporting-level category was custom-developed, informed by methodologies from similar prior studies [26, 27]. Statistical comparisons will use appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. Second, where appropriate, we will conduct subgroup analyses. Grouping factors may include, but are not limited to the following: (1) sample size of included studies; (2) fully decentralized versus hybrid models; (3) single-component versus multicomponent interventions; (4) presence of a study protocol; (5) type of intervention delivered to the experimental group; (6) method of follow-up visits for the experimental group; (7) use of blinding; (8) journal quality; (9) language. In the subgroup analysis, the total scores of the 9 interventions (QP, QN) will be averaged according to the actual number of reported items to avoid bias caused by differing numbers of applicable items.

We will assess normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots and homoscedasticity with Levene’s test. For two-group comparisons, we will use Student’s t tests when assumptions are met, Welch’s t tests for unequal variances, or Mann–Whitney U tests otherwise. Missingness in reporting items was scored as 0 by design; non-applicable (NA) items were excluded from the denominator. As this scoping review is primarily descriptive, no formal hypothesis testing across groups is planned; p values, if presented, will be labeled as exploratory and not used for inferential claims. Effect sizes will be reported as above, each with 95% confidence interval. Analyses will be performed in SPSS v27.0.

Discussion

To further enhance the effectiveness of DCT trial outcomes and promote the adoption of DCTs, it is essential to comprehensively assess their reporting completeness. Currently, there is no CONSORT extension specifically for evaluating the reporting completeness of DCTs. Therefore, this study has developed a CONSORT-DRCT checklist to systematically assess the reporting completeness of decentralized interventions within RCTs. The findings of future research using this checklist are expected to serve two purposes: first, to analyze the current reporting completeness of DCTs, thereby contributing to improved reporting transparency and strengthening the validity of decentralized trial results; and second, if reporting completeness is found to be suboptimal, to highlight the need for developing a dedicated DCT extension and identify key areas of focus in future extensions.

One of the innovative aspects of this study is the evaluation of reporting completeness based on an expanded checklist adapted from the latest CONSORT 2025 statement. Compared to CONSORT 2010, the new CONSORT 2025 introduces updated guidelines that align with current trends in medical trials. For example, it adds an “open science” section with specific requirements for trial registration (Item 2), access to trial protocols and statistical analysis plans (Item 3), individual participant data sharing (Item 4), and conflicts of interest (Item 5). These additions reflect CONSORT 2025’s emphasis on the emerging trends of networking, digitalization, and scientific data sharing. With advancements in information technology, DCTs will also have broader platforms to demonstrate their advantages. Furthermore, to ensure the reliability and rigor of the research, this protocol provides detailed and standardized procedures to minimize bias in the study results. The study follows PRISMA guidelines to establish a scientific screening process that effectively excludes irrelevant literature and studies that do not meet the inclusion criteria, thereby ensuring the representativeness of the results. Data extraction and completeness assessment for each included study are conducted independently by two researchers, all of whom undergo standardized training and pilot testing to ensure the accuracy of results. A notable strength of this study is the development of specific SOPs as evaluation standards for DCTs. In addition to facilitating the standardized assessment of reporting completeness, these SOPs may also serve as a reference for future extensions of the CONSORT statement for decentralized interventions.

Although this study aims to comprehensively evaluate the reporting completeness and characteristics of current DCTs, there are several limitations to consider. First, the study excludes literature published in languages other than English and Chinese, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the findings. Second, as decentralized trials are still in their early stages, this study does not focus on RCT reports for specific diseases, but rather broadly includes decentralized trial reports across a range of disease areas. Given the potential differences in the application of decentralized methods among various diseases, the results may not precisely reflect the reporting completeness and characteristics of decentralized trials for individual diseases.

Conclusion

This protocol outlines a comprehensive assessment of the reporting completeness of DCTs published in 2023, with a particular focus on their adherence to an extended checklist based on existing reporting guidelines. By mapping and synthesizing the landscape of DCT reports, this review will characterize their study features and evaluate their reporting completeness across different types of interventions. Ultimately, the findings are expected to identify current reporting practices and gaps in DCT reporting, providing valuable evidence to inform future extensions or adaptations of the CONSORT statement for decentralized trials, and to guide improvements in reporting standards within this emerging field.
