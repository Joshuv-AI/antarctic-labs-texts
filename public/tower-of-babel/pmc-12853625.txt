Decoding epigenetic and transcriptional landscapes: DNA methylome-transcriptome integration reveals novel drivers in 4NQO-Induced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma mouse model
Authors: Yanli Qian, Runhua Lin, Zhiwei Liu, Xinxin Zhang, Dongming Lin, Min Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpigenetic alterations, particularly DNA methylation, and dysregulation of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) are increasingly recognized as critical factors in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) pathogenesis. Understanding the dynamic interplay between DNA methylation changes and TIME evolution during ESCC progression remains essential. We established a 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide (4NQO)-induced ESCC mouse model, capturing distinct pathological stages: normal esophageal epithelium (Normal), esophageal simple hyperplasia (ESSH), intraepithelial neoplasia (IEN), and ESCC. Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was performed using the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip (285 K), coupled with transcriptome analysis via bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for Cd45 (leukocyte common antigen) validated immune cell infiltration.ResultsDNA methylation profiling revealed progressive genome-wide hypomethylation during ESCC development, with hypomethylated probes significantly enriched in immune response pathways. Notably, ESSH exhibited a methylation profile similar to IEN and ESCC. RNA-seq identified escalating numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Immune deconvolution analysis and IHC of Cd45 demonstrated dynamic changes in TIME composition from ESSH onwards. Furthermore, dynamic expression clustering identified an innate immune response-related gene cluster highly expressed in ESSH. Integrative analysis yielded 495 methylated regulatory genes, significantly enriched in leukocyte cell-cell adhesion and T cell activation pathways (e.g., Ptprc/Cd45, Il12rb1, Tox), with peak activity in ESSH.ConclusionsThese findings highlight ESSH as a critical window where epigenetically driven immune changes facilitate ESCC progression. Targeting these early epigenetic-immune interactions may offer a novel strategy for ESCC early detection and combination therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02035-3.Keywords: Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, DNA methylation, RNA-seq, Tumor immune microenvironment

Background

The incidence and mortality rates of esophageal cancer (EC) rank seventh and sixth, respectively, with pathologically predominant esophageal squamous cell cancer (ESCC) worldwide [1]. Importantly, the early diagnosis, screening, and detection of ESCC have been gradually valued. However, it remains a big challenge to get the early lesions of ESCC carcinogenesis in clinics leading to difficulty in early screening. The most common methods to explore the mechanisms of different tumors or other diseases are animal models. Thus, in our previous study [2], the C57 mouse of 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide (4NQO)-induced ESCC model was constructed successfully to explore the mechanism of the initial and progression of ESCC through mimicking the human ESCC [3]. Studies regarding transcriptomics, single-cell transcriptomics, and whole-exome sequencing [2, 4] have already reported about the ESCC mouse model. Genomic changes, like copy number variations (CNVs) and mutations, have been observed in precancerous lesions before the emergence of ESCC [2, 5]. Abnormal DNA methylation alterations play essential roles in the progression to ESCC, providing new insights on detecting circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) within cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in early diagnosis and its clinical implications in systemic chemotherapy [6, 7]. Conventionally, HumanMethylation450k, 850k, and 935k Beadchips become the most popular, high-quality, economical techniques for DNA methylation study [8, 9]. However, the epigenetic alterations of ESCC mouse model have not been performed fully as the technical restrictions. Only recently, it is possible to make the BeadChip technology also applicable to other mammals for epigenetics study [10] and Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip (285K) that interrogates 280,754 unique CpG sites in the C57BL/6J mouse genome was released to a newly developed DNA methylation microarray [11, 12].

Epigenetic regulation of gene expression occurs commonly in tissues. C-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 20 (CCL20) [13], Histone lysine demethylase LSD1 (KDM1A) [14], and long-interspersed nucleotide element-1 (LINE-1) [15] were identified associated with DNA methylation alteration and immune disorder in esophageal cancer progression. Universally, disorder of tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) was detected in most solid tumors and it plays an important role in immunotherapy. We found previously that Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) protein expression displayed an increasing trend from normal esophagus, simple hyperplasia (ESSH), intraepithelial neoplasia (IEN), to ESCC, indicating innate and adaptive immunity influenced in ESCC progression [16]. Elucidating the dynamics of DNA methylation changes and their relationship with TIME evolution throughout ESCC progression is crucial for understanding disease mechanisms and identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities.

To date, the application of this specific microarray platform to investigate DNA methylation dynamics during ESCC development remains unreported. We first utilized the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip to uncover the DNA methylation changes during the carcinogenesis process of ESCC via 4NQO-induced mouse model and to preliminarily investigate the epigenetic-immune interactions combined Methylation BeadChip and RNA sequencing analysis.

Methods

Mouse model of esophageal tumorigenesis

All the animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shantou University Medical College (SUMC2020-368). In the previous study [2], we have successfully established the ESCC mouse model using the C57BL/6J mouse treated with 16 weeks of 4NQO drug according to Tang’s method [3]. The powder of 4NQO (Sigma Aldrich, MO, USA) was dissolved in propylene glycol to make a 1 mg/mL stock solution storing at 4℃ under light protection. The mice were treated with 100 µg/mL 4NQO working solution at 6 weeks postnatal, i.e., from 6 weeks and the drug was fed to the mice every day for 16 weeks. Then, all the mice were given normal feed and water only for 12 weeks. During this time, the drug was kept shaded from light and changed weekly, during which time the drug was stable in solution.

Sample collection

During the 28 weeks of modeling period, the esophagus was isolated from anesthetized and sacrificed mice after daily 4NQO-treatment for 0w (n = 6), 4w (n = 7), 16w (n = 7), 28w (n = 7) in total, respectively. The esophagus was opened longitudinally, observed grossly, and photographed. 4-µm sections were prepared following the fixation, dehydration, and embedding in paraffin. The histological changes of esophagus were evaluated by Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) staining and determined by two certified pathologists according to the 2019 WHO classification of tumors of the digestive system and the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control—American Joint Committee on Cancer (UICC-AJCC) TNM staging system. Genomic DNA and RNA were extracted from distinct sets of esophageal tissues harvested at different time points after 4NQO treatment. For genomic DNA analysis, tissues were collected at 0w (Normal, n = 3), 4w (ESSH stage, n = 3), 16w (IEN stage, n = 3), and 28w (ESCC stage, n = 3). For RNA analysis, tissues were obtained at 0w (Normal, n = 3), 4w (ESSH stage, n = 4), 16w (IEN stage, n = 4), and 28w (ESCC stage, n = 4).

We defined four different esophageal lesions as follows: (1) Normal esophageal squamous epithelium (Normal): Well-oriented stratified epithelium consisting of the basal zone and superficial zone. (2) Esophageal simple hyperplasia (ESSH): Basal cell hyperplasia but abnormalities were confined to the lower third of the epithelium. (3) Intraepithelial neoplasia (IEN): Defined as a loss of polarity in the epithelial cells, nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromatic, and increased or abnormal mitoses. (4) Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC): Defined as a lesion with invasion into the sub-epithelial tissues.

Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip data processing

Each three esophagus tissues from Normal, ESSH, IEN, and ESCC groups were sent to Xu ran Biological (Shanghai, China). A total of 12 genomic DNA samples were extracted. Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip from Illumina was used to analyze the DNA methylation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA was bisulfite converted using the EZ DNA Methylation™ Kit (BMT3-D5002, America). The raw intensity data (IDAT) files were generated from the Illumina iScan platform, and fluorescence signal detection and analysis were made using Illumina GenomeStudio (version 2011). The R-package Sensible Step-wise Analysis of DNA Methylation BeadChips (SeSAMe, version 1.24.0) [17] was performed to analyze the data with the recommended preprocessing code “TQCDPB”. Array-related information is available at http://zwdzwd.github.io/InfiniumAnnotation#mouse [18, 19]. Principal-component analysis of β values was performed using the “FactoMineR” package (version 2.11). Differential DNA methylation analysis between groups was performed in “limma” package (version 3.62.2). We finally get 256,676 probes filtering single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), multi-mapping loci, and CpG sites on the X and Y chromosomes in subsequent analyses. Differential Methylated Probes (DMPs) were defined as the |Δβ| cutoff value > 0.1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

To analysis of the distribution of DMPs, we obtained partial methylation domain (PMD) methylation levels from the dataset published by Zhou et al. [20] (https://zwdzwd.github.io/pmd).

Bulk-RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

We collected three normal esophagi, each with four esophagus tissues of ESSH, IEN, and ESCC stages. RNA was extracted and passed the quantity and quality criteria. Then RNA-seq was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Novogene (Beijing, China). Illumina paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp) were generated and raw reads were pre-processed before downstream analyses by removing reads with adapters, unknown bases, and low-quality reads (bases with Phred score ≤ 20 accounting for more than 50% of the read length). Q20, Q30, and GC content were also calculated.

Hisat2 was used to align against the mm10 reference genome. The function “varianceStabilizingTransformation (VST)” in the R package DESeq2 [21] was used to normalize and scale the read counts. Genes with |log2 (Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Concerning the sequencing depth and gene length, fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) was calculated for gene expression.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

4-µm esophageal sections were made after esophagus fixated, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 after deparaffinized and rehydrated. Then, Cd45 Rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology, #70257, 1:300 dilution) was incubated at 4 °C overnight, followed by antigen retrieval (0.01 M citrate buffer, pH 6.0). The next day, sections were incubated with secondary antibody against HRP-conjugated-rabbit/mouse IgG at 37 °C for 30 min, and visualized using 3, 3-diaminobenzidine. Finally, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Membrane staining for cells was evaluated as positive expression of Cd45. The percentage of positive cells was counted using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Each slide was examined in 5 views randomly and scored by two experienced pathologists.

Landscape of immune infiltration in the development of ESCC

To estimate the abundance of infiltrating immune cells, the CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) approach was applied to calculate the relative proportion of immune cells in all samples. LM25 [22], a gene signature matrix that could distinguish 25 types of mouse immune cells, was used as the annotation gene set. R package “ggpubr” (version 0.6.0) was used to visualize the proportion change of 25 types of mouse immune cells in four groups.

Dynamic expression clustering and core gene identification

Based on Bulk-RNA sequencing data, a total of 6,147 genes (the union of DEGs in three groups) in Mfuzz package (version 2.66.0) were implied to identify core gene clusters according to the dynamic expression of ESCC development, of which the core algorithm is Fuzzy C-Means Clustering (FCM). The optimal number of clusters based on the inflection point was determined by ClusterGVis package (version 0.1.2). Additionally, we did the analyses across k = 3–10 to identify the most suitable value. Membership cutoff for defining core genes was set as acore ≥ 0.5. The FPKM values for each gene across the four groups were used as the input data, and assigned each gene to a specific cluster based on its expression value in the 4 groups after the R function “standardise”.

Statistical analysis

The visualization and analysis of these data was implemented in R version 4.4.2. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.14.4). The cutoff q-value with 0.2 and p-adjusted with 0.05 were set. Data visualization was performed with the R packages ggplot2 (version 3.5.1) and pheatmap (version 1.0.12). Additional R package venn (version 1.12) was used.

Multiple sample test methods use the Kruskal-Wallis test and P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical details are displayed in the figure legends.

Results

Altered DNA methylation profiling in the progression to the ESCC in mouse model

Based on the construction of 4NQO induced ESCC mouse model, we collected normal, ESSH, IEN, and ESCC esophagus tissues at 0, 4, 16, and 28 weeks after 4NQO treatment, respectively. Tissues from different pathological stages were performed on Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip (285K) and bulk-RNA sequencing to explore the change of global DNA methylation and the infiltration of immune cells (Fig. 1A).

We first depict genome-wide DNA methylation dynamics using the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip during the development of ESCC. A total of 256,676 probes were finally obtained after the default preprocessing via SeSAMe package and Principal-component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct methylation profiles across the four pathological stages (see Additional file 1 A). These probes are predominantly located in promoter (TSS1500, TSS200, 5’UTR, and 1st Exon) and gene body regions, followed by intergenic region (IGV) and 3’UTR region (see Additional file 1B). It also showed different β value distributions in different genome regions in the progression to ESCC (see Additional file 1 C).

Differential methylation analysis was performed between normal group and ESSH, IEN, ESCC groups, respectively. Following this analysis, we identified 8,742 DMPs, 26,335 DMPs, and 44,172 DMPs in ESSH, IEN, and ESCC groups compared to normal esophagus. (Fig. 1B-D). Manhattan plot showed an increasing trend in DMPs located on chromosomes (see Additional file 1D). Notably, hypermethylated probes predominated in the ESSH (83.9%) and IEN (81.5%) stages, whereas hypomethylated probes were predominant in ESCC (52.5%) (Fig. 1B-D, see Additional file 2). The same trend was observed in promoter region, body region, intergenic region (IGV), and 3’UTR (Fig. 1E-G).

The dramatic increase in hypomethylation at ESCC stage is quite interesting. A recent study identified partial methylation domain (PMD) demethylation as a common feature across multiple cancer types. We obtained PMD methylation levels from the dataset published by Zhou et al. (https://zwdzwd.github.io/pmd) and annotated the differentially methylated probes (DMPs) identified in ESCC onto PMD and highly methylated domain (HMD) regions. The results clearly showed that DMPs located within PMDs constituted a minority of all DMPs (see Additional file 3 A). Notably, violin plot revealed that DMPs in PMD were predominantly hypomethylated, whereas those in HMD were mainly hypermethylated (see Additional file 3B, P < 0.001). Based on these findings, we proposed that demethylation within PMD may contribute to ESCC progression.

The global hypomethylation change influences immune response in ESCC mouse model

There are 3,487 hypermethylated probes and 735 hypomethylated probes intersected among ESSH, IEN, and ESCC groups (Fig. 2A and C). Unsupervised cluster analysis of DMPs showed the ability of 735 hypomethylated probes to distinguish different stages of ESCC is better than that of hypermethylated probes (Fig. 2B and D). Furthermore, these hypomethylated probes were specifically enriched in immune response-regulating cell surface receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 2E). Moreover, based on these 4,222 DMPs (3,487 hypermethylated and 735 hypomethylated probes) persisting throughout the ESCC development, ESSH stage exhibited similar DNA methylation profiling to IEN and ESCC stages, indicating ESSH stage already presents the DNA methylation changes (including hypermethylation and hypomethylation) and developmental basis of carcinogenesis under the strong tumorigenic induction (Fig. 2F).

Above analysis revealed a progressive increase in DMP number alongside a shift towards hypomethylation dominance during ESCC development, underscoring the key role of global hypomethylation in modulating immune responses.

Disorder of immune microenvironment during the development of ESCC

We demonstrated that hypomethylation probes involved in immune response signaling pathway and immune cells acted significant roles during the ESCC development. Immunohistochemistry of Cd45 (leukocyte common antigen, LCA) showed the increasing infiltration of inflammatory cells commencing at the ESSH stage (Fig. 3A-B). Cd45-positive cells were primarily localized within the submucosa, with occasional intra-epithelial presence.

We performed RNA sequencing to explore the change of transcriptome of esophageal squamous cancer induced by chemical carcinogen 4NQO. RNA-seq revealed the increased trend of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) during the development of ESCC and 1,944 DEGs, 3,571 DEGs, and 4,353 DEGs were identified in ESSH, IEN, and ESCC compared to normal esophagus (Fig. 3C). Consistent with the differential methylation analysis, the number of DEGs increased during ESCC progression. Significant transcriptomic alterations were observed, with upregulated genes being predominant at the ESSH stage. Moreover, the distribution patterns of DEGs in IEN and ESCC stages were notably similar. To illustrate the change of microenvironment, we calculated and displayed the proportion of 25 types of mouse immune cells. More than half of the mouse immune cell types (13 out of 25, Fig. 3D) underwent substantial changes during ESCC development, with T cells being the most markedly affected (7 out of 13, Fig. 3D). Especially, we found that CD8 memory T cells, Th1 cells, M1 macrophages, and Treg cells dramatically increased in ESSH stage, however, they showed an exhausted tendency in ESCC stage (Fig. 3D, see Additional file 4 A).

Dynamic expression clustering identified five core gene clusters during the development of ESCC

Combining the biological significance during the process of ESCC development, dynamic expression clustering of bulk-RNA sequencing data identified five core gene clusters reflecting distinct temporal expression patterns throughout ESCC progression (see Additional file 4B-C, see Additional file 5), which showed different expression spectra in the progression of ESCC (Fig. 4A-B). The genes in cluster 1 (C1, n = 1390), cluster 2 (C2, n = 1395), and cluster 5 (C5, n = 1121) were observed to be significantly highly expressed in ESCC, normal, and ESSH groups, respectively. While the expression of cluster 3 (C3, n = 1024) gradually decreased and cluster 4 (C4, n = 1217) displayed an increasing trend. Next, we renamed these five core gene clusters via Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (see Additional file 4D-F), including muscle system processes related cluster (C1), energy system related cluster (C2), and extracellular matrix related cluster (C3). Notably, we noticed C4 (k = 5 is identified, k = 3 is not) is involved in DNA replication/repair and C5 is involved in innate immune response intensively (Fig. 4C-D), which are significant features during carcinogenesis.

Comprehensive integrated analysis of DNA methylation and RNA expression

Analysis using the 285K BeadChip and RNA sequencing revealed distinct DNA methylation changes and immune responses already present at the ESSH stage. To systematically identify methylation-regulated genes, we integrated 8,742 DMPs identified in ESSH with their corresponding Δβ-values and log₂(Fold Change) values. Since the direction of gene expression regulation depends heavily on the genomic context of DNA methylation, we categorized DMPs based on their location: those in promoter regions (TSS1500, TSS200, 5’UTR, and 1st exon) were linked to negative regulation of expression, while those in gene body regions were associated with positive regulation (Fig. 5A). Using cutoffs of |log₂(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 for RNA-seq and |Δβ| > 0.1 for the 285K array, we identified 117 genes with promoter hypomethylation and upregulated expression, and 91 genes with promoter hypermethylation and downregulated expression (Fig. 5B). Similarly, 269 upregulated genes and 18 decreased genes were identified in body region (Fig. 5C).

Subsequent GO enrichment analysis of these 495 methylated regulatory genes revealed significant enrichment in leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, regulation of leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, and regulation of T cell activation, showing the significant influence of leukocyte cells and T cells during the development of ESCC. (Fig. 5D). The expression profile of 33 candidate genes included in these top 3 biological processes revealed a pronounced upregulation immune response in ESSH stage, however, a slight decrease in IEN and ESCC stage, which might indicate the suppression of immune response (Fig. 5E). Among 33 candidate genes, Ptprc was also referred to as Cd45 has been validated previously via immunohistochemistry, and Il12rb1 and Tox were significantly regulated in T cells.

Discussion

Leveraging the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip alongside bulk-RNA sequencing, this study delineates the dynamic landscape of DNA methylation alterations and immune microenvironment dysregulation across the spectrum from normal esophagus, ESSH, IEN, to ESCC in a 4NQO-induced mouse model. Our findings pinpoint specific epigenetic and immune alterations occurring as early as the ESSH precancerous stage (Fig. 6).

The diagram of DNA methylation changes and disorder of tumor immune microenvironment during the development of ESCC mouse model

To explore the dynamic changes of esophageal carcinogenesis, we constructed a mouse model of 4NQO-induced ESCC and identified four different lesion stages during the progression to ESCC [2], including normal esophagus, ESSH, IEN, and ESCC stages, as precancerous samples in clinics are difficult to obtain. Considering the scarcity of mouse methylation chips on the market today, we first utilized the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip [12, 23, 24] released in recent years to depict the DNA methylation changes during the development of ESCC mouse model, identifying 8,742 DMPs, 26,335 DMPs, and 44,172 DMPs in ESSH, IEN, and ESCC stages, respectively. With the progression to ESCC, the number of DMPs increased dramatically compared to normal esophagus (Fig. 1B-D, see Additional file 1D). In contrast to previous studies primarily examining static DNA methylation profiles in established ESCC tissues [25–27], our longitudinal approach emphasized the dynamic changes of DNA methylation in the process of esophageal carcinogenesis. Additionally, we found that hypermethylated probes accounted for more than 80% in ESSH (83.9%) and IEN (81.5%) stages while hypomethylated probes were predominantly in ESCC stage (52.5%), revealing that the hypomethylated probes dominate the development of ESCC, consistent with changes in human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [7]. Present studies also showed global DNA hypomethylation and promoter CpG island hypermethylation in ESCC [7, 25], and global methylation loss correlated with immune evasion signatures [28]. However, the proportion of hypermethylated versus hypomethylated probes in ESCC did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1G). This observation may stem from inherent differences between murine and human genomes, particularly the substantially lower density of CpG sites interrogated by the Mouse Methylation BeadChip (~ 285,000) [23] compared to common human arrays (>850,000), potentially limiting detection sensitivity.

Analysis of shared DMPs across stages identified 4,222 probes common to ESSH, IEN, and ESCC. Cluster analysis demonstrated that the methylation profile of ESSH closely resembled those of IEN and ESCC based on these shared DMPs (Fig. 2F), strongly suggesting that profound DNA methylation alterations conducive to carcinogenesis are already established by the ESSH stage. Promoter methylation of p16, p14, FHIT, MGMT, and TFF1 was already detected in dysplastic lesions in esophagus tissue [6, 29, 30]. Significantly, GO enrichment analysis of hypomethylated genes revealed pronounced dysregulation of the immune response-regulating cell surface receptor signaling pathway, indicating the early involvement of epigenetically-mediated immune dysfunction in ESCC development. Aberrant methylation occurred in almost every significant molecular pathway associated with systemic chemotherapy, such as immune response regulation pathways [31]. Meanwhile, recent studies showed that DNA methylation biomarkers might be important and promising in diagnosis and immune therapy in the future [6, 25, 32].

Immunohistochemistry of Cd45 indicated a disorder of immune cells in precancerous tissues. Therefore, we explored the immune environment evolution in the progression to ESCC via bulk-RNA sequencing and deconvolution analysis. Deconvolution analysis demonstrated that T cell was dramatically influenced during the development of ESCC. A key strength of this study is the longitudinal sampling across defined stages of carcinogenesis, enabling dynamic expression clustering to resolve dynamic expression patterns. Dynamic expression clustering identified a characteristic innate immune response-related gene cluster in ESSH stage, in line with the alteration of DNA methylation. Thus, we propose that DNA methylation alterations during ESSH orchestrate changes in specific immune-related genes and cell populations (e.g., T cells), thereby fostering an immune-permissive microenvironment that facilitates malignant progression to ESCC. Altogether, epigenetic modifications enabled new insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning T cell function [33–35]. Based on the above findings, we next preliminarily explored the association between DNA methylation changes and regulated immune cells. It is confirmed that the methylation site in the promoter region negatively regulates gene expression, while the gene body region is positively regulated [36]. Combined with the DNA methylation and RNA expression changes at ESSH stage, we found 495 methylated regulatory genes and functional enrichment of these genes implicated upregulated leukocyte and T cell activities. Integrated analysis showed that T cell was activated at early lesion stages, and the cell type was regulated in terminal stages, resulting in different differentiation trajectories.

These 33 identified genes are at least partially regulated by DNA methylation, which controls T cell activation or differentiation, prompting the esophageal carcinogenesis. Several studies [37–40] have demonstrated a conserved epigenetic profile between murine and human exhausted T cells (TEX). The interaction between TIME and T cells manifests as time-dependent and it may be reversible for the early T cell epigenetic program [37]. Combining our research with the above research reports, we believe that T cells present different epigenetic regulation and related expression characteristics in different stages of carcinogenesis. During ESCC progression, the dynamic changes in Ptprc mRNA expression observed in RNA-seq diverged from the Cd45 protein accumulation pattern detected by IHC (Figs. 3A–B and 5E). This apparent discrepancy can be attributed to several biological and methodological factors. Biologically, mRNA levels do not always correlate directly with protein abundance, due to post-transcriptional regulation and differences in protein turnover rates. Moreover, a decrease in overall Ptprc transcript levels could reflect reduced transcription per cell, even in the context of an expanding Cd45 + immune cell population. The most plausible explanation, however, lies in the methodological differences between the two assays. RNA-seq captures the average Ptprc expression across the entire esophageal tissue—a complex mixture of epithelial, stromal, and immune cells—while IHC specifically and semi-quantitatively visualizes Cd45+ immune cells that have infiltrated the epithelial and stromal compartments.

In summary, this study provides the first comprehensive genome-wide DNA methylation atlas using the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip. Both DNA methylation patterns and gene expression profiles have undergone specific changes, inducing the process of esophageal carcinogenesis, and we identified T cells regulated by DNA methylation at ESSH stage. Our results identify the ESSH stage as a pivotal window for epigenetic-immune dysregulation and uncover novel targets for early detection biomarkers and rationale for combining epigenetic modulators with immunotherapies in ESCC treatment.

Conclusion

Our study highlights that ESSH as a critical window where epigenetically driven immune changes facilitate ESCC progression. Targeting these early epigenetic-immune interactions may offer a novel strategy for ESCC early detection and combination therapy.
